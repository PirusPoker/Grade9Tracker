## What it is

A composer rarely writes a long melody from nothing. They take a small idea, a **motif**, and build a piece from it with **devices**: repeat it, move it to another pitch, pass it to another instrument, or lay it underneath as a repeating pattern. The spec lists the devices for AoS1, and this lesson covers the **melodic and rhythmic** ones. Harmonic devices (drone, pedal, broken chord, Alberti bass, cadences and modulation) are in AoS1d. In the exam, devices come up as "name the device", "underline a feature", "tick three features heard" and "explain the difference between…".

**Melodic devices**

| Device | What happens | Written example (text notation) |
|---|---|---|
| **Repetition** | an idea is repeated exactly, in the same part and at the same pitch | bar 1: C E G E; bar 2: C E G E |
| **Sequence** | an idea is repeated **in the same part at a higher or lower pitch**, usually straight after itself (ascending or descending) | bar 1: C D E; bar 2: D E F; bar 3: E F G |
| **Imitation** | an idea is **copied by a different part** soon after, often at a different pitch, while the first part carries on | violin: D E F sharp G…; two beats later the cello plays D E F sharp G an octave lower |
| **Canon** | strict imitation lasting a whole melody: each part plays the same tune, entering one after another, so the parts overlap | part 1 starts; part 2 starts the same tune one bar later |
| **Round** | a canon for voices at the same pitch that can loop back to the start (e.g. a nursery-rhyme round) | voices enter in turn, each singing the whole tune |
| **Conjunct movement** | the melody moves **by step** (smooth, stepwise) | E F G A G F E |
| **Disjunct movement** | the melody moves **by leap** (angular) | C G E high C, G |
| **Ornamentation** | small decorations added to melody notes | trill (rapid alternation with the note above), mordent, turn, appoggiatura, grace note |
| **Melodic motif** | a short, recognisable group of pitches used to build a piece | the Badinerie's motif X: a descending B minor broken chord |
| **Answering phrase** | a phrase that replies to the one before, like question and answer | phrase 1 ends on V (question); phrase 2 ends on I (answer) |

**Rhythmic devices**

| Device | What happens | Written example |
|---|---|---|
| **Anacrusis** | an upbeat: one or more notes **before the first strong beat** of the first full bar | in 3/4, the tune starts on beat 3 of an incomplete bar |
| **Dotted rhythm** | a long, dotted note followed by a short one, giving a jagged, bouncy feel | dotted quaver + semiquaver, repeated |
| **Syncopation** | accents placed **off the beat** or between beats, against the pulse | in 2/4: quaver, crotchet, quaver (the long note falls between beats) |
| **Rhythmic motif** | a short rhythm pattern used again and again to build a piece | quaver, two semiquavers, quaver |
| **Ostinato** | a pattern (rhythmic or melodic) **repeated many times in a row**, usually while other music changes above it; in pop it is a **riff**, and a bass ostinato in Baroque music is a **ground bass** | cello repeats D A B F sharp every bar |

**Structural devices.** **Repetition** gives unity, and **contrast** (a new idea, key, rhythm or instrument) gives variety. **Regular phrasing** means phrases of equal length (4 + 4, 8 + 8). It is typical of Classical music and dance music, and makes a melody feel balanced and predictable. Irregular phrasing (for example 3 + 5) sounds less settled.

**Developing a motif.** Mark schemes reward you for saying how a motif is changed. The Eduqas Badinerie analysis shows several of these ways at work:
- **sequence**: repeat it higher or lower;
- **inversion**: turn it upside down, so falling becomes rising; motif X is inverted in Section B;
- **fragmentation**: use only part of it;
- **modified ending**: keep the start and change the last notes;
- **augmentation** or **diminution**: longer or shorter note values;
- **passing it between instruments**: in the Badinerie the motif is divided between two instruments.

**The three most confused pairs.**
- **Sequence against imitation.** Both repeat an idea at a new pitch. A **sequence** is in the **same part**; **imitation** is in a **different part**. Ask yourself who plays the repeat.
- **Imitation against canon.** Imitation can be brief (a few notes copied). A **canon** is **strict and continuous**: the whole line is copied.
- **Ostinato against repetition.** Any idea can be repeated once. An **ostinato** is a pattern that repeats **continuously**, usually as an accompaniment.

**Listening strategy.** Use the topic's videos (Mr Luke's Music Room on melodic devices, Picardy on sequence and imitation, and MusicHelpGuy on ostinato). Train three habits:
1. **Follow one idea.** When a short motif catches your ear, count how often it returns and whether it comes back higher, lower, in another instrument or upside down.
2. **Tap the pulse.** If the accents fall between your taps, it is syncopation. If the tune starts before your first strong tap, it is an anacrusis.
3. **Listen to the bass.** A bass that keeps repeating the same short figure is an ostinato or ground bass.

In a "tick three features" question, cross out the options you are sure are absent before choosing.

::: terms
- **Motif**: a short melodic or rhythmic idea that is used repeatedly to build a piece.
- **Sequence**: the immediate repetition of a phrase in the same part at a higher or lower pitch.
- **Imitation**: when one part copies a phrase just played by another part, often at a different pitch.
- **Canon**: strict imitation in which each part plays the whole melody, entering one after another so the lines overlap.
- **Ostinato**: a short rhythmic or melodic pattern repeated continuously, often as an accompaniment.
- **Anacrusis**: one or more upbeat notes that come before the first strong beat of a piece or phrase.
- **Syncopation**: accenting notes off the beat or between beats, against the natural pulse.
- **Dotted rhythm**: a pattern of a dotted (lengthened) note followed by a short note, giving a jagged feel.
- **Conjunct**: melodic movement by step, to the next note up or down the scale.
- **Disjunct**: melodic movement by leap, jumping more than a step between notes.
- **Ornamentation**: decoration of a melody with extra notes such as trills, mordents and appoggiaturas.
- **Regular phrasing**: phrases of equal length, such as four bars answered by four bars.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the options first.** In underline and tick items, the answer is one of the words printed, so decide which ones to listen for.
2. **Ask who repeats the idea.** Same part at a new pitch: sequence. Another part: imitation. Every part in turn, strictly: canon. Continuous repeats underneath: ostinato.
3. **Check the rhythm against the pulse.** Off-beat accents are syncopation; long–short jagged pairs are dotted rhythm; notes before the first downbeat are an anacrusis.
4. **Check the shape.** Mainly steps: conjunct. Mainly leaps: disjunct. Decorations: name the ornament (trill, appoggiatura, mordent).
5. **In "describe" answers, locate and explain.** "An ascending sequence in the violins in bars 5–8" beats "sequence".
6. **In "explain the difference" answers, state both sides**: "a sequence is in the same part…, whereas imitation is in a different part…".
:::

::: example Name the device
In bar 1 the flute plays C D E F. In bar 2 the flute plays D E F G, and in bar 3 E F G A. Name the device. (1)

Sequence (ascending sequence) (1).
:::

::: example Sequence or imitation?
Explain the difference between sequence and imitation. (2)

A sequence repeats a phrase in the same part at a higher or lower pitch (1). Imitation is when a different part copies the phrase, often at a different pitch, while the first part continues (1).
:::

::: example Complete a sequence
A melody in G major has these pitches in bar 1, all quavers except the last, which is a crotchet: G A B G C. Bar 2 is a sequence of bar 1, one step higher, using only notes of the G major scale. Write the five pitches of bar 2. (5)

A (1), B (1), C (1), A (1), D (1).
:::

::: example Features in a described extract
An extract is played by a jazz big band. The drummer repeats the same tom-tom pattern throughout. The saxophones play a melody with accents between the beats, and some melody notes are flattened thirds and sevenths. From the list below, give three features found in this description: blue notes / drone / ostinato / power chords / syncopation / tremolo. (3)

Blue notes (1), ostinato (1), syncopation (1).
:::

::: example Anacrusis and dotted rhythm
A march in 4/4 begins with a single quaver before the first bar line. Many bars contain a dotted quaver followed by a semiquaver. Name these two rhythmic devices and describe the effect of the second. (3)

Anacrusis (1). Dotted rhythm (1). It gives a jagged, bouncy or martial feel that suits marching (1).
:::

::: q
Give the technical name for a note or notes that come before the first strong beat of a piece. (1)
---
Anacrusis (accept upbeat / pick-up).
:::

::: q
The cello repeats the notes D A B F sharp in every bar for 16 bars while the violins play a changing melody. Name the device. (1)
---
Ostinato (accept ground bass). Do not accept "repetition" alone.
:::

::: q
Describe the difference between conjunct and disjunct movement. (2)
---
Conjunct moves by step (1); disjunct moves by leap / larger intervals (1).
:::

::: q
Two voices sing the same melody. The second voice starts one bar after the first and sings the whole tune, so the two lines overlap all the way through. Name the device. (1)
---
Canon (accept round if at the same pitch). Do not accept imitation alone.
:::

::: q
Name two ornaments. (2)
---
Any two: trill / mordent / turn / appoggiatura / acciaccatura / grace note.
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by syncopation. (1)
---
Accenting notes off the beat / between the beats / against the regular pulse.
:::

::: watch
- **Sequence is the same part; imitation is a different part.** This distinction is tested almost every year.
- **Ostinato is not just "repetition"**: it repeats continuously as a pattern, and naming it gets the mark where "repeated" may not.
- **Anacrusis is the upbeat at the start**, not any short note; say "before the first strong beat".
- **Syncopation is off-beat accenting**, not simply "fast rhythm" or "complicated rhythm".
- In **tick-three questions**, ticking a fourth box can cost marks; choose exactly the number asked.
- Give **locations** (bar numbers, instrument, section) whenever a describe question allows, because some mark schemes require them.
:::

::: test
Describe the effect of dotted rhythms in a march. (1)
---
Jagged / bouncy / crisp / martial / energetic feel that emphasises the strong beats. Any one.
:::

::: test
A melody in C major reads, in crotchets: E D C G | F E D A. Describe the melodic device in bar 2 and the direction of movement. (2)
---
Sequence (1): bar 2 repeats bar 1 one step higher / ascending sequence (1).
:::

::: test
A rhythm in 4/4 is written: crotchet, quaver, crotchet, quaver, crotchet. Explain why it is syncopated. (2)
---
The crotchets after each quaver begin between the beats (on the "and") (1), so the longer, stressed notes fall off the beat / against the pulse (1).
:::

::: test
Describe three ways in which a composer could develop a melodic motif. (3)
---
Any three: repeat it / put it in sequence / invert it / fragment it / change its ending / augmentation or diminution / give it to a different instrument / imitation / change its harmony or key.
:::

::: test
A melody in G major is written as: bar 1 B C D B, bar 2 A B C A, bar 3 G A B G. Is the melody (a) conjunct or (b) disjunct? Name the device used across the three bars. (2)
---
(a) Mostly conjunct (1); descending sequence (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how an ostinato can help to build the structure of a piece. (2)
---
It provides a constant, repeated foundation that gives unity (1) while other parts change above it, allowing contrast and variety without losing coherence / building tension (1).
:::
