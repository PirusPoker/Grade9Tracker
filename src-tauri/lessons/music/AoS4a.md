## What it is

Area of Study 4 is **Popular Music**: pop, rock and pop, bhangra and fusion. Two questions on the paper come from it: Question 7 on the set work, Toto's *Africa*, and Question 8 on an unfamiliar song. This lesson covers the **structures and features** listed in the spec:
- 32-bar song form, strophic form and the 12-bar blues;
- verse, chorus, middle 8, bridge, fill, instrumental break, intros and outros;
- riffs, improvisation, syncopation and driving rhythms;
- standard chord progressions, primary and secondary chords, and cadences.

Question 8 often prints the **section plan** (e.g. Introduction 4 bars, Verse 1 16 bars, Chorus 1 8 bars) or the lyrics. It then asks about what happens in each section, so you must know what each section normally does.

The app can't play audio. Use the topic's videos, which walk through song forms and the 12-bar blues, and **count bars aloud** while you listen. Most pop songs move in 4- and 8-bar units.

### The sections of a song

| Section | What it does | How to recognise it |
|---|---|---|
| **Intro** | Opens the song, sets the key, tempo and style, often introduces a **riff** | Usually instrumental, often 4 or 8 bars |
| **Verse** | Tells the story | **Same music, different words** each time; lower, calmer, often thinner texture |
| **Pre-chorus** | Builds up to the chorus | Rising melody, a new chord pattern, crescendo |
| **Chorus** (refrain) | The main message, and the catchiest part | **Same music and same words** each time; usually higher, louder, thicker, with the **hook** and often backing vocals |
| **Middle 8** | A contrasting section, often 8 bars, usually after the second chorus | New melody and chords, sometimes a new key |
| **Bridge** | A link or transition between two sections (some writers use "bridge" to mean the middle 8) | Short, connecting |
| **Instrumental break / solo** | Voices stop and an instrument takes the melody, often **improvised** | Guitar, saxophone or synth solo |
| **Link** | A short passage joining sections, often the intro music again | Instrumental |
| **Fill** | A short burst (often on the drums) in the gap at the end of a phrase or section | One or two bars, leads into the next section |
| **Outro / coda** | Ends the song | Repeats the chorus or intro; may **fade out** or end suddenly |

A **hook** is the catchy phrase that sticks in the memory, and it is usually in the chorus. A **riff** is a short repeated musical pattern, usually instrumental, often played by guitar or bass.

### Song forms

**Strophic form (AAA...)**: every verse uses the **same music** with **new words**. There is no chorus. Folk songs, hymns and many ballads use it. The verse may end with a repeated line, called a **refrain**.

**32-bar song form (AABA)**: four sections of **8 bars**:
- **A**: the main melody (8 bars);
- **A**: the same melody with new words (8 bars);
- **B**: the **middle 8** or bridge, with contrasting melody and chords, often in a new key (8 bars);
- **A**: the main melody returns (8 bars).

The total is 4 × 8 = **32 bars**. It was typical of Broadway show tunes and pop songs of the 1920s–50s (the "Great American Songbook") and is also common in jazz standards.

**12-bar blues**: a repeating chord pattern of 12 bars using the three **primary chords**:

| Bars 1–4 | Bars 5–8 | Bars 9–12 |
|---|---|---|
| I I I I | IV IV I I | V IV I I |

In C major that is C C C C | F F C C | G F C C. Many versions use **V** in the last bar as a **turnaround** back to the start. The blues comes from African-American music. It uses **blue notes** (flattened 3rd, 5th and 7th), **swing** or shuffle rhythms and **improvisation**, and it underpins rock 'n' roll and much rock.

**Verse–chorus form**: the most common pop structure. A typical plan: Intro, Verse 1, Chorus, Verse 2, Chorus, Middle 8 or instrumental, Chorus (often repeated), Outro. *Africa* is in verse–chorus form.

### Chords, progressions and cadences

- **Primary chords** are **I, IV and V** (tonic, subdominant, dominant). In C major they are C, F and G.
- **Secondary chords** are **ii, iii and vi**. In C major they are Dm, Em and Am, which are minor in a major key.
- **Standard chord progressions** found in countless songs:
  - **I–V–vi–IV** (C–G–Am–F), the "four chord" pop progression;
  - **I–vi–IV–V** (C–Am–F–G), the 1950s doo-wop progression;
  - **vi–IV–I–V** (Am–F–C–G), a minor-flavoured rotation of the same four chords, used in *Africa*'s chorus (F#m–D–A–E in A major);
  - **I–IV–V** in the 12-bar blues.
- **Cadences** in pop: **perfect** (V–I, finished), **plagal** (IV–I, the "Amen" ending, common in rock), **imperfect** (ending on V, unfinished) and **interrupted** (V–vi, surprise).
- **Harmonic rhythm** is how often the chords change. In pop it is usually **one chord per bar**.

### Rhythmic features

- **Syncopation**: accents off the beat. It is almost everywhere in pop vocals and riffs.
- **Driving rhythm**: constant, energetic repeated quavers or semiquavers (e.g. a bass or hi-hat) that push the song forward.
- **Backbeat**: the snare drum accents beats **2 and 4** in 4/4, the basic rock and pop feel.
- **Improvisation**: music made up on the spot, typical of solos and the ad-libs at the end of a song.

### Listening strategy

1. **Count bars in 4s.** A change of melody, lyrics or texture every 4 or 8 bars marks a new section.
2. **Words plus music tell you the section.** New words over the same tune means a verse. The same words and tune means the chorus. A new tune altogether means the middle 8. No voice means the intro, a link, an instrumental break or the outro.
3. **Listen to the bass** to find the chord changes. Chord I sounds like "home".
4. Note what **changes** between sections: the texture, dynamics, pitch, backing vocals and instruments.

::: terms
- **Strophic**: a song form in which every verse is sung to the same music (AAA).
- **32-bar song form**: a song form of four 8-bar sections in the pattern AABA, with a contrasting middle 8 as B.
- **12-bar blues**: a repeating 12-bar chord pattern using chords I, IV and V, usually I-I-I-I, IV-IV-I-I, V-IV-I-I.
- **Verse**: a section repeated with the same music but different words each time, which tells the story.
- **Chorus**: the repeated section with the same music and words each time, containing the hook.
- **Middle 8**: a contrasting section, often 8 bars long, usually placed after the second chorus.
- **Bridge**: a short link or transition between two sections of a song (sometimes used to mean the middle 8).
- **Riff**: a short repeated musical pattern, usually instrumental, in popular music.
- **Hook**: a short, catchy musical phrase, usually in the chorus, designed to be memorable.
- **Fill**: a short improvised burst, often on drums, filling a gap at the end of a phrase or section.
- **Primary chords**: chords I, IV and V of a key: the tonic, subdominant and dominant.
- **Secondary chords**: chords ii, iii and vi of a key, which are minor chords in a major key.
:::

::: method
1. **"Name the section"**: use the words and music. The same words and tune means the chorus, new words to the same tune means a verse, and instrumental means the intro, link, instrumental or outro. Give the section's full name (e.g. "Chorus 2", "Instrumental").
2. **"State the length in bars"**: count in 4s. Most pop sections are 4, 8, 12 or 16 bars. An odd number, such as *Africa*'s nine-bar phrases, is a point worth making.
3. **Chord questions**: turn letter names into Roman numerals using the key (e.g. in G major: G=I, Am=ii, Bm=iii, C=IV, D=V, Em=vi). To find the chord "not heard", check each one against the bass line.
4. **"Explain riff"**: short + repeated + pattern/phrase (the mark scheme needs "short" if you say "melody").
5. **"Describe features of the melody"**: give one separate feature per mark. Use shape, movement (conjunct/disjunct), range and pitch, rhythm (syncopated, on the beat), word-setting (syllabic/melismatic), phrase lengths, and repetition.
6. **Comparisons** (verse 2 against verse 1): write in comparatives, e.g. "thicker texture", "backing vocals added", "shorter".
:::

::: example Naming the form
A song has three sections, each 8 bars long, with the same melody and new words each time, and no chorus. Name the form. (1)

**Strophic** (1).
:::

::: example 32-bar song form
A song has four 8-bar sections. Sections 1, 2 and 4 share a melody; section 3 has a new melody and moves to a new key. Name the form and the name for section 3. (2)

**32-bar song form / AABA** (1). Section 3 is the **middle 8 / bridge** (1).
:::

::: example Filling in the 12-bar blues
Write the chords of a 12-bar blues in G major, using letter names. (3)

G G G G (1) | C C G G (1) | D C G G (1).
:::

::: example Riff
Explain the meaning of the word riff. (1)

A **short, repeated** musical pattern or phrase, usually instrumental, in popular music (1).
:::

::: example Chord not heard
A song's introduction in D major uses the chords D, Bm, G and A. Which of these chords is not heard: I, IV, vi or ii? (1)

D = I, Bm = vi, G = IV, A = V, so **ii** (Em) is not heard (1).
:::

State two ways a chorus is usually different from a verse. (2)
---

::: q
Explain the difference between a fill and a riff. (2)
---
A riff is a short pattern repeated many times as part of the song (1); a fill is a short one-off burst (often drums) filling a gap at the end of a phrase or section (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Labelling sections by order, not content.** The third section is not automatically "Verse 2". Use words and music: same words means chorus, new words means verse.
- **Riff without "short".** "A repeated melody" can be marked wrong. Say "a **short** repeated pattern".
- **Mixing up the 12-bar order.** The pattern is I I I I, IV IV I I, V IV I I. Bar 10 is **IV**, not V.
- **Primary and secondary.** I, IV and V are primary; ii, iii and vi are secondary. They are minor in a major key.
- **Plain "middle".** Write "middle 8", "bridge" or "instrumental break" in full.
- **Describing the melody with non-melodic points.** "Loud" and "played on guitar" aren't melody features. Use conjunct, syncopated, syllabic, range and phrase length.
:::
