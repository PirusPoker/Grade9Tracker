## What it is

Area of study 1 places music in the **Western Classical Tradition, 1650–1910**. The spec does not want a detailed history of composers and dates. It wants you to hear an unfamiliar extract, name the **period** (Baroque, Classical or Romantic) and back your answer with **features**. On the paper this is usually a 1-mark "underline the period" item, a 2-mark "describe two features typical of this period", or part of a longer answer. The prepared extract for this area, Bach's *Badinerie*, is a Baroque piece, so Baroque features are also worth marks in Question 1 every year.

**The three periods at a glance.** The dates are approximate, and styles overlap at the edges. Beethoven, for example, begins as a Classical composer and ends pointing towards the Romantics.

| | **Baroque** (about 1600–1750; the spec starts at 1650) | **Classical** (about 1750–1810) | **Romantic** (about 1810–1910) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Composers** | Bach, Handel, Vivaldi | Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven | Schumann, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, Dvořák, Grieg, Verdi, Wagner |
| **Keyboard** | **harpsichord** or organ, usually playing the **basso continuo** | the **piano** replaces the harpsichord; the continuo dies out | a bigger, more powerful piano; the virtuoso pianist |
| **Orchestra** | small: mostly strings plus a few wind (oboe, bassoon, flute, sometimes trumpets and timpani), with continuo | larger and standardised: strings plus pairs of woodwind, **clarinets** added, horns, trumpets, timpani | **large**: extra brass (more horns, trombones, tuba), harp, more percussion, big string sections |
| **Melody** | long, flowing lines spun out of a short motif; **ornaments** (trills, appoggiaturas, mordents); **sequences** | **balanced, regular phrases** (often 4 + 4 bars, question and answer); clear, elegant tunes | **lyrical, expressive** melodies, long phrases, wide range, big climaxes |
| **Harmony and tonality** | diatonic, strong tonic–dominant relationships, clear perfect and imperfect cadences, suspensions | simple, diatonic, clear cadences, slow harmonic rhythm | **chromatic** harmony, rich extended chords, dissonance, modulation to distant keys |
| **Texture** | often **polyphonic** (imitation, fugue) as well as melody and continuo | mostly **homophonic**: melody and accompaniment, **Alberti bass** | rich, thick homophony; layered orchestral textures |
| **Dynamics** | **terraced**: sudden shifts between loud and soft, few markings | graded: crescendo, diminuendo, sforzando | extremes from pp (or quieter) to ff (or louder); sudden and gradual changes |
| **Tempo and rhythm** | steady, driving pulse ("motor rhythm"), one tempo per movement | steady tempo; clear metre | **rubato**, tempo changes within a movement |
| **Mood** | one mood (affect) held through a movement | contrast between themes, but controlled | intense emotion; mood can change quickly |
| **Typical genres and forms** | suite of dances, concerto grosso, oratorio, chorale, **trio sonata**, binary form | symphony, **string quartet**, solo sonata, solo concerto; sonata form, minuet and trio, rondo, variations | **programme music** (music that tells a story or paints a picture), symphonic poem, opera, song, symphony |

**Why the sound changed.** Each change has a reason you can use in an explain answer. The harpsichord plucks its strings, so it cannot make gradual dynamics; Baroque music therefore changes volume in blocks (terraced dynamics). The piano (short for *pianoforte*, "soft-loud") hits its strings with hammers, so Classical composers could write crescendos and accents at the keyboard. As public concerts grew and instruments improved (valves on brass, stronger pianos), Romantic composers wrote for bigger orchestras in bigger halls, and used them for bigger emotions and stories.

**Features you can hear versus facts you know.** In a listening exam, a feature only scores if it can be heard or seen in the score. "Bach wrote it" is a fact; "a harpsichord fills in chords above the cello's bass line" is a feature. The 2023 mark scheme for "describe two features of the Badinerie typical of Baroque music" credited: harpsichord, basso continuo, long flowing melodic lines, ornaments (trills, appoggiaturas), sequences, homophonic texture, terraced dynamics, binary form, and tonic–dominant harmony. Learn that list; it is the Baroque checklist.

**Telling neighbours apart.** The hardest calls are between neighbouring periods.
- **Baroque or Classical?** Listen to the keyboard (harpsichord or piano), the bass (a busy continuo line, or an Alberti-bass accompaniment), the phrasing (spun-out lines, or neat four-bar phrases) and the dynamics (terraced, or graded with crescendos).
- **Classical or Romantic?** Check the size and colour of the orchestra (heavy brass, harp), the harmony (plain diatonic, or chromatic and lush), the tempo (steady, or rubato) and the dynamic extremes.

**Listening strategy.** You can't hear extracts in this app, so use the topic's videos (INA Music's "Recognising Baroque, Classical & Romantic" and the Flipping Fantastic features videos). For each clip, run through a fixed checklist: **keyboard? orchestra size? texture? dynamics: terraced or graded? harmony: plain or chromatic? tempo: steady or rubato?** Say the period aloud before the video tells you. In the exam you hear each extract several times. Use the first hearing for the instrument and period, the second for texture and dynamics, and the third to check your answer.

::: terms
- **Baroque**: the period of Western music from about 1600 to 1750, the era of Bach, Handel and Vivaldi.
- **Classical**: the period from about 1750 to 1810, the era of Haydn, Mozart and early Beethoven, known for balance and clarity.
- **Romantic**: the period from about 1810 to 1910, known for large orchestras, chromatic harmony and intense expression.
- **Basso continuo**: a Baroque accompaniment in which a bass instrument plays the bass line and a keyboard or lute fills in chords above it.
- **Terraced dynamics**: sudden changes between loud and soft levels, with no gradual crescendo or diminuendo; typical of Baroque music.
- **Ornament**: a decoration added to a melody note, such as a trill, mordent or appoggiatura.
- **Alberti bass**: a Classical broken-chord accompaniment played low, high, middle, high (for example C G E G).
- **Balanced phrasing**: melodies built from phrases of equal length, often four bars answered by four bars.
- **Chromatic harmony**: chords that use notes outside the key, giving a rich, tense or colourful sound.
- **Rubato**: a flexible tempo in which the performer slightly speeds up and slows down for expression ("robbed time").
- **Programme music**: instrumental music that tells a story or describes a scene, place or character.
- **Harpsichord**: a Baroque keyboard instrument whose strings are plucked, so it cannot play gradual dynamics.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the keyboard first** if there is one: a harpsichord points to Baroque; a piano points to Classical or Romantic.
2. **Judge the size and colour of the ensemble**: small strings and continuo (Baroque), a tidy orchestra with clarinets (Classical), or massed brass, harp and percussion (Romantic).
3. **Check texture and bass**: continuo or imitation (Baroque), Alberti bass and melody-and-accompaniment (Classical), thick layered sound (Romantic).
4. **Check dynamics, harmony and tempo**: terraced (Baroque), graded and diatonic (Classical), extreme dynamics, chromatic harmony and rubato (Romantic).
5. **Answer with features, not dates or names.** For "describe two features", write two different elements, e.g. one on sonority and one on dynamics.
6. **For a 10-mark comparison**, work element by element and say what each feature shows about the period: "chromatic harmony, which would be unusual in the Classical period, so...".
:::

::: example Name the period
An extract is played by a small string orchestra. A harpsichord plays chords above a busy cello bass line. The melody is decorated with trills and moves through a series of sequences. The dynamics change suddenly from loud to soft. Name the period in which it was composed. (1)

Baroque (1): the harpsichord continuo, trills, sequences and terraced dynamics are all Baroque features.
:::

::: example Two features of Classical music
Describe two features typical of music from the Classical period. (2)

Balanced, regular phrases, often four bars answered by four bars (1). A homophonic texture with an Alberti bass accompaniment on the piano (1). (Also creditable: clear diatonic harmony and cadences; clarinets in the orchestra; graded dynamics with crescendos.)
:::

::: example Why the dynamics changed
Explain why Baroque keyboard music uses terraced dynamics while Classical keyboard music uses crescendos. (2)

The harpsichord plucks its strings with the same force however hard the key is pressed, so it can only change volume by switching manual or adding instruments: sudden, terraced changes (1). The piano strikes its strings with hammers, so pressing harder makes a louder note, allowing gradual crescendos and diminuendos (1).
:::

::: example Romantic rather than Classical
An orchestral extract begins very quietly with tremolo strings. The brass section, including trombones and tuba, builds to a fortissimo climax. The melody is long and expressive, the conductor slows down and speeds up freely, and the harmony includes many chromatic chords. Give three features that show this is Romantic rather than Classical. (3)

Large brass section including tuba and trombones, bigger than a Classical orchestra (1). Rubato: flexible tempo instead of a steady Classical pulse (1). Chromatic harmony rather than simple diatonic chords (1). (Also: extreme dynamics, pp to ff; long lyrical melody.)
:::

::: example Baroque features in the set work
The Badinerie was composed in the Baroque era. Describe two musical features of the piece that are typical of Baroque music. (2)

It is accompanied by basso continuo: the cello plays the bass line and the harpsichord fills in chords (1). The flute melody is decorated with ornaments such as trills and appoggiaturas, and uses sequences (1).
:::

::: q
Name the keyboard instrument most associated with the basso continuo in the Baroque period. (1)
---
Harpsichord (accept organ). Do not accept piano.
:::

::: q
Give two features of the orchestra in the Romantic period. (2)
---
Any two: larger than earlier orchestras / more brass, e.g. trombones, tuba, extra horns / harp / wider range of percussion / bigger string sections.
:::

::: q
An extract has a melody in two four-bar phrases, the second answering the first. The piano accompanies with a low–high–middle–high broken-chord pattern. Name the period and the accompaniment pattern. (2)
---
Classical (1); Alberti bass (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by programme music. (1)
---
Instrumental music that tells a story or describes a scene, character or idea (not just "music with a programme booklet").
:::

::: q
Describe two differences between Baroque and Classical textures. (2)
---
Baroque often polyphonic / contrapuntal / imitative, Classical mostly homophonic (1); Baroque uses a continuo bass with harpsichord, Classical uses melody and accompaniment such as Alberti bass (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the harmony of Romantic music differs from that of Classical music. (2)
---
Romantic harmony is more chromatic / uses notes outside the key (1) and more dissonant or extended chords and modulations to distant keys, whereas Classical harmony is mainly diatonic with clear cadences (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Features, not dates or names**: "it sounds old" and "it is by Mozart" earn nothing; "harpsichord continuo" earns a mark.
- **Harpsichord versus piano** is the quickest clue, so name the keyboard correctly: a plucked, twangy sound with no crescendo is a harpsichord.
- **Terraced dynamics** means sudden block changes; do not describe a Baroque piece as having "crescendos".
- **Alberti bass is Classical**, not Baroque; **continuo is Baroque**, not Classical.
- In "describe two features" answers, give two **different** features; "trills" and "ornaments" count once.
- Do not claim **rubato** or **chromatic harmony** for a Baroque piece; the Badinerie is diatonic with a steady pulse.
:::

::: test
The extract is from an orchestral piece describing a storm at sea. It opens pianissimo with tremolo violins and a low timpani roll. A long, expressive melody enters on the cellos and horns and rises to a high climax. The harmony contains many chromatic chords and dissonances, and the music modulates several times to distant keys. The full orchestra, including trombones, tuba, harp, cymbals and bass drum, builds to a fortissimo climax with sforzando accents. The tempo speeds up during the storm and slows with rubato as it dies away. Sudden changes of dynamics and texture follow one another quickly.
Explain how the features of this extract show that it is Romantic rather than Classical. Use musical vocabulary and refer to the musical elements. (10)
---
Mark in bands (AO4). 9–10: a perceptive answer that explains the features in detail and makes critical judgements about how each one marks the music as Romantic rather than Classical; accurate specialist vocabulary throughout. 7–8: a generally secure answer with a competent account of the features and general judgements; vocabulary mostly accurate. 5–6: an inconsistent answer that identifies some features with some judgement; some accurate vocabulary. 3–4: a limited answer with elementary detail and few relevant judgements; limited vocabulary. 1–2: very limited detail, little or no accurate vocabulary. 0: nothing of value.
Indicative content:
- Sonority: very large orchestra, with trombones, tuba, harp and extended percussion (cymbals, bass drum), far bigger than the Classical orchestra of pairs of wind with horns, trumpets and timpani; tremolo strings and timpani roll as colour and effect.
- Dynamics: extreme range from pianissimo to fortissimo; sforzandos; sudden contrasts. Classical dynamics are narrower and more controlled.
- Harmony and tonality: chromatic chords and dissonance, frequent modulation to distant keys, compared with Classical diatonic harmony and modulation mainly to closely related keys (dominant, relative).
- Melody: long, lyrical, expressive line with a wide range and a high climax, rather than neat balanced four-bar phrases.
- Tempo: accelerando in the storm and rubato, compared with the steady Classical pulse.
- Texture: thick, layered orchestral texture; rapid changes of texture.
- Context: programme music (a storm at sea), a typically Romantic genre; the music describes the scene, which Classical instrumental music rarely set out to do.
- Judgements such as "the chromatic harmony creates tension that a Classical composer would usually resolve quickly" lift answers into the top bands.
:::
