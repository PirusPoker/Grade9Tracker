## What it is

This topic covers three spec points for film music:
- **dynamics and contrast** are used to create special effects;
- **music technology** is used to enhance **sonority** (the sound);
- **minimalistic techniques** are used in film music.

Together they explain how a film score makes its biggest impacts: the jump-scare, the eerie electronic hum, and the hypnotic ostinato that builds tension for minutes at a time.

The app can't play audio. The topic's videos show the minimalist techniques clearly and the music-technology effects on real tracks. When you hear an effect, name it, then say what it does to the sound.

### Dynamics and contrast for special effects

| Device | What happens | Typical effect in film |
|---|---|---|
| **Sforzando (sfz)** | A single note or chord is suddenly strongly accented | A shock, a blow, a jump-scare |
| **Stinger** | A short, loud burst (chord, cymbal, brass hit) | Punctuates a reveal or a cut |
| **Subito piano** | Suddenly quiet straight after loud | Surprise, a held breath |
| **Crescendo** | A gradual increase in volume | Approaching danger, a build to a climax |
| **Diminuendo** | A gradual decrease in volume | Fading away, calm returning, death |
| **Silence / general pause** | All the music stops | Suspense, as the audience waits for what comes next |
| **Extreme dynamics** | Moving between pp and ff | Maximum drama, possible in a cinema's powerful sound system |
| **Contrast of register** | Very high against very low | Emptiness or threat |
| **Contrast of texture** | A solo line against a sudden tutti | A change of scene or power |
| **Contrast of tempo** | A slow opening followed by fast action | A calm scene broken |

Contrast works because the ear notices **change**. A loud chord after silence is more shocking than a loud chord after more loud chords. That is why film composers often write **very quiet** music just before a scare.

Special effects on orchestral instruments:
- **Strings**: tremolo; pizzicato; glissando; very high notes near the bridge that sound glassy; clusters of notes a semitone apart; double stopping.
- **Brass**: **muted** (sounds distant, sinister or comic); flutter-tongue; loud stabs; glissando on the trombone.
- **Woodwind**: flutter-tongue; trills; very low bassoon or contrabassoon; high piccolo shrieks.
- **Percussion**: timpani and snare **rolls** that crescendo; the **tam-tam** or gong; cymbal swells; tuned percussion for sparkle.

### Music technology in film

The spec says music technology "may be used to further enhance sonority". Modern scores are often **hybrid**: a live orchestra mixed with electronic sound.

| Technology | What it is | Film use |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesiser** | Makes sounds electronically (a **synthesised** sound) | Futuristic, alien or cold sounds; sustained pads; deep electronic bass |
| **Sampling / samples** | Recording a sound and replaying or reusing it | Adding real-world sounds (heartbeats, clocks, machinery), or orchestral samples when there is no budget for an orchestra |
| **Loop** | A sample or pattern repeated continuously | A driving rhythm bed or ostinato |
| **Sequencer / DAW** | Software that records, edits and arranges parts | Building layered electronic tracks, often synchronised to the picture |
| **Drum machine / electronic drums** | Programmed percussion | Modern action or thriller rhythms |
| **Reverb** | Simulated reflections of a space | Vastness (a cathedral, space), dreaminess, distance |
| **Delay / echo** | Repeats of a sound after a time gap | Disorientation, repetition, a haunted feel |
| **Distortion** | Deliberately "dirty", overdriven sound | Aggression, chaos, danger |
| **Panning** | Placing a sound between the left and right speakers (or around the room in surround sound) | Following the movement of an object across the screen |
| **Phasing** (as an effect) | A sweeping, swirling sound made by mixing a signal with a slightly delayed copy | Dreamlike, unstable or science-fiction |
| **Pitch shift / time stretch** | Changing the pitch or speed of a recording | Monstrous voices; slow-motion "braaam" sounds |
| **Multi-tracking / layering** | Recording parts separately and stacking them | Huge textures from few performers |
| **Amplification** | Making quiet sounds louder through microphones | Close, intimate sounds such as breathing and whispers |
| **Orchestral hit** | A sampled, sudden full-orchestra chord | Punchy dramatic accents |

A real exam question asked for **three ways music technology is used** in a film theme remixed by a rock band. Creditable answers included synthesised sounds, sampling, drum machine, reverb and echo, panning, looping, distortion and phasing. Learn the list, because each item is one mark.

### Minimalism

**Minimalism** began in the 1960s in the USA with composers such as **Steve Reich**, **Philip Glass**, **Terry Riley** and **John Adams**. It builds music from **very small cells** (short melodic or rhythmic patterns) that repeat many times and **change very gradually**. Philip Glass and Michael Nyman, among others, have written minimalist film scores, and the technique is now common in thrillers and documentaries.

Why it suits film:
- **Hypnotic repetition** creates a steady tension or a sense of time passing.
- **Gradual change** can build slowly over a long scene without drawing attention away from the picture.
- It is **flexible**: a pattern can loop for as long as the scene lasts and be cut at any point.
- Layered **ostinati** create energy and forward movement for travel, chases and machines.

The Eduqas spec lists these techniques. Learn a definition for each:

| Technique | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Melodic cell** | A tiny melodic idea of a few notes that forms the building block |
| **Ostinato** | A short pattern repeated continuously |
| **Layering** | Adding ostinato patterns one on top of another to build the texture |
| **Additive melody / rhythm** | A pattern grows by **adding** a note or beat each time (e.g. 1, 1-2, 1-2-3...) |
| **Subtractive** | The reverse: notes are removed one at a time |
| **Phasing / phase shifting** | Two or more parts play the same pattern, and one gradually moves slightly ahead, so they drift **out of sync** and later come back together |
| **Rotation** | A pattern starts on a different note of itself each time (e.g. ABCD, BCDA, CDAB) |
| **Rhythmic displacement** | A pattern is shifted so it starts on a different beat or part of the beat |
| **Isorhythm / overlap** | Ostinati of different lengths are layered, so they line up in constantly changing ways |
| **Metamorphosis** | A pattern is transformed, one small step at a time, into a new pattern |
| **Augmentation / diminution** | A cell is lengthened or shortened in note values |
| **Avoidance of exact repetition** | Something changes slightly on each repeat |
| **Note addition / substitution** | One note of a cell is added or replaced to create gradual change |

Other typical features of minimalism are consonant, **diatonic** harmony, often with a very **slow harmonic rhythm**, and sometimes a **drone** or **pedal**. There is a steady pulse, texture that changes by adding or removing layers, and dynamics that change gradually, such as long crescendos.

**Minimalism is not just "repetitive".** Every good answer names the **process**, such as phasing, an additive pattern or layered ostinati, and says **how it changes slowly**.

### Listening strategy

- **Dynamics**: listen for the **moment of contrast** and say what triggered it. A sudden silence or sforzando is almost always a mark.
- **Technology**: ask whether each sound could have come from an acoustic instrument. If not, it is synthesised or sampled. Listen for **space** (reverb), **repeats** (delay), **movement left-right** (panning) and **swirling** (phasing).
- **Minimalism**: follow one short pattern and count what changes, and when. Is a layer added? Does the pattern shift against another part? Is a note added?

::: terms
- **Sforzando**: a sudden, strong accent on a note or chord, marked sfz.
- **Muted**: playing with a mute, which softens and changes the tone of brass or strings.
- **Synthesised**: sound created electronically rather than by an acoustic instrument.
- **Sample**: a recorded sound or short section of music that is reused, often repeated or layered.
- **Reverb**: an effect that adds echoing reflections, making a sound seem to be in a large space.
- **Panning**: placing or moving a sound between the left and right speakers.
- **Phasing**: two identical patterns drifting gradually out of time with each other (or, as a studio effect, a swirling sound from a delayed copy).
- **Minimalism**: a style built from short repeated cells which change very gradually over time.
- **Melodic cell**: a very short melodic idea of a few notes that is repeated and gradually altered.
- **Additive melody**: a pattern that grows by adding a note or beat each time it repeats.
- **Layering**: building texture by adding parts, often ostinati, one on top of another.
- **Rhythmic displacement**: shifting a pattern so it begins on a different beat or part of the beat.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the stem** to see whether the question is about **dynamics/contrast**, **technology** or **minimalism**, and use that topic's vocabulary.
2. For a "**name the technique**" item, give the exact term: sforzando, tremolo, muted, pizzicato, phasing.
3. For "**explain three ways music technology is used**", give **three different technologies**, each with where or how it is used. Don't count echo and delay twice.
4. For **minimalism**, name the process and describe the gradual change: "an ostinato is repeated and layers are added one at a time, so the texture gradually thickens".
5. For **effects in film**, link device → effect: "the sudden silence makes the audience hold their breath before the shock".
6. In the **10-mark answer**, use contrast as your backbone. Describe how the dynamics, texture and sonority change through the extract and why that fits the scene.
:::

::: example Naming technology
A film theme has been remixed. It contains electronic drums, a guitar part that sounds rough and "dirty", a vocal line that sounds as if it is in a huge hall, and a keyboard sound that moves from the left speaker to the right. Explain three ways music technology has been used. (3)

A **drum machine / electronic drums** provide the beat (1). **Distortion** is applied to the guitar to make it sound aggressive (1). **Reverb** makes the voice sound as if it is in a large space (1). (Also: **panning** of the keyboard from left to right.)
:::

::: example Minimalist process
A film cue has a four-note marimba pattern repeated throughout. After eight bars a second marimba plays the same pattern, but it gradually moves ahead by one quaver until the two parts are out of sync, then realigns. Name the technique and describe one other minimalist feature that could be added. (2)

**Phasing / phase shifting** (1). Any one other feature, e.g. **layering** a new ostinato on top, an **additive** process growing the pattern note by note, or a gradual crescendo (1).
:::

::: example Contrast for a shock
A horror film cue has 30 seconds of very quiet, high violin notes, then a sudden fff chord for the whole orchestra, then silence. Explain how dynamics and contrast create the effect. (3)

The **very quiet** opening makes the audience listen closely and feel uneasy (1). The **sudden fff / sforzando** chord is a shock, matching a jump-scare on screen (1). The **silence** afterwards leaves the audience in suspense, not knowing what comes next (1).
:::

::: example Defining additive melody
Explain what is meant by an additive melody, and give an example using letter names. (2)

A melody that grows by adding one note (or beat) each time it repeats (1), e.g. C / C-E / C-E-G / C-E-G-A (1).
:::

::: example Why minimalism suits film
Give two reasons why minimalist techniques are useful to film composers. (2)

Repetition with slow change builds tension gradually over a long scene without distracting from the picture (1); patterns can be looped for exactly as long as the scene needs and stopped at any point (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"Repetitive" is not an answer for minimalism.** Name the process (phasing, additive, layering, rotation, displacement) and the slow change.
- **Panning is not volume.** It is left–right placement. Reverb and echo are not the same either, and in a list of three only credit "echo" once.
- **"Loud" is not a special effect.** Say sforzando, sudden fff, subito piano, crescendo or silence, and link it to the moment.
- **Synthesised versus sampled.** A synthesiser **creates** a sound electronically; a sample is a **recording** reused.
- **Studio phasing versus minimalist phasing.** In minimalism, phasing is two parts drifting out of sync. As a studio effect it is a swirling, sweeping sound. Make clear which one you mean.
- **Contrast needs two sides.** Say what the music changes **from** and **to**.
:::
