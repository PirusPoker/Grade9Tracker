## What it is

A **musical** (musical theatre) tells a story through **songs, spoken dialogue, dance and orchestral music**. For AoS2, the focus is on **vocal ensembles**: how solos, duets, trios, larger ensembles, the chorus and backing vocals are used, and how the **texture** changes when voices combine. Recent papers have used songs from *Oliver!* ("Who Will Buy?", 2025), Cole Porter's *Can-Can* ("I Love Paris", 2023, three versions compared) and Bernstein's *West Side Story* (a 10-mark question on a fight scene, 2023). Expect instrument and voice identification, "tick three vocal features", "describe how Version 2 differs", tempo, cadence and tonality items, and possibly the 10-mark extended answer.

**Vocal groupings**

| Number | Name | Typical dramatic use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **solo** | a character reveals private feelings: a ballad, or an "I want" song setting out their goal |
| 2 | **duet** | a love song, an argument, or two characters' differing views |
| 3 | **trio** | three characters, often each with their own line and attitude |
| small group | **ensemble** | several principal characters together, often with overlapping lines |
| whole cast | **chorus** / **company number** | crowd scenes, big openings and finales, "show-stoppers" |
| behind the lead | **backing vocals** | harmony, "ooh"/"aah" support, echoes and responses to the lead singer |

**How voices are combined (texture).**

| Texture | What happens | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Unison** | everyone sings the same melody (or in octaves, men and women) | unity, strength, a crowd or company in agreement |
| **Harmony** | voices sing different notes that fit together, often in **3rds or 6ths**, or in full chords | warmth, romance, togetherness (e.g. a love duet ending in harmony) |
| **Call and response** | a soloist sings a line and the chorus or backing singers answer | leader and group, a public or gospel-style feel |
| **Overlapping melodies** (counterpoint) | two or more characters sing **different tunes at the same time** | several viewpoints at once; conflict or excitement, often at the climax of an act |
| **Canon / imitation** | a phrase is copied by another voice before the first has finished | argument, echoing, a crowd joining in |
| **Solo becoming duet becoming trio** | voices added one by one | building intensity; drawing characters together |

**Musical-theatre features**
- **Overture**: an orchestral introduction before the show starts, usually a medley of the show's songs.
- **Underscore**: music played **under spoken dialogue** or action to set the mood.
- **Pit orchestra**: the band that accompanies the show from the pit in front of or below the stage. It may include strings, woodwind (often called "reeds", with players doubling several instruments), brass, percussion, keyboards or synths, and in modern shows a rock or pop band.
- **Recitative-like writing**: a vocal line that follows the **natural rhythms and accents of speech**, with a light accompaniment, used to move the plot on quickly.
- **Word-setting**:
  - **syllabic** (one note per syllable) keeps words clear;
  - **melismatic** (many notes on one syllable) is expressive and shows off the voice;
  - **word painting** makes the music illustrate the words, for example a rising line on "higher".
- **Reprise**: a song repeated later in the show, often with changed words or mood.
- **Finale**: the closing number of an act, usually a company number.

**Vocal techniques and voice types** (Appendix C)
- **Belt**: a powerful, chest-voice sound at the top of the range, typical of big musical-theatre songs.
- **Falsetto**: a light, high head voice, especially in male singers.
- **Vibrato**: a small, regular wobble in pitch that warms a sustained note.
- **Legato**: smooth and connected. **Staccato**: short and detached.
- **Syllabic** and **melismatic** word-setting (see above).
- **Scat**: jazz improvisation on nonsense syllables.
- **A cappella**: unaccompanied singing.
- **Voice types**: **soprano** (highest female), **alto** (lower female), **tenor** (high male) and **bass** (lowest male).

**What the exams have rewarded.** In the 2025 *Oliver!* question, the singer was accompanied by **harp**, **oboe** and **cello**. A harp feature scored with answers such as "broken chords or arpeggios", "spread chords" or "glissando", and **rubato** was defined as "stolen or robbed time: bending and stretching the tempo". In the 2023 *Can-Can* comparison, marks for "how Version 2 differs" came from changes of instrumentation, key, tempo, texture, style and rhythm. Examples were "stab chords", "violins in harmony", "a lower key", "slower", "thicker texture", "less improvisation" and "no walking bass". For Version 3, answers such as "live performance with audience", "electric piano", "thinner texture", "a female duet" and "backing vocals" scored.

**Listening strategy.** Use the topic's videos (Brandon Lawrence on types of musicals, Brett Boles on what makes a musical-theatre song, and StageAgent's "What is a Musical?"). With any cast recording, jot down **who sings** (solo, duet, chorus), **how they combine** (unison, harmony, call and response, overlapping), **the accompaniment** (instruments and what they play), and **how the words are set** (syllabic or melismatic, belt or legato). Then ask what each choice tells the audience about the character or situation. That last step is what earns marks in the extended answer.

::: terms
- **Musical**: a stage work telling a story through songs, spoken dialogue, dance and orchestral music.
- **Duet**: a song or passage for two singers; a trio is for three singers.
- **Chorus (company number)**: a number sung by the whole cast or a large group, often at openings and finales.
- **Backing vocals**: supporting voices behind the lead singer, adding harmony, "oohs" and responses.
- **Overture**: an orchestral introduction to a show, usually a medley of its songs.
- **Underscore**: music played underneath spoken dialogue or action to create mood.
- **Pit orchestra**: the instrumental ensemble accompanying a musical from the orchestra pit.
- **Belt**: a powerful, chest-voice style of singing high notes, typical of musical theatre.
- **Recitative**: a vocal style that follows the rhythms of speech, used to move the story along.
- **Syllabic**: word-setting with one note for each syllable.
- **Melismatic**: word-setting with several notes sung to one syllable.
- **Word painting**: music that imitates or illustrates the meaning of the words it sets.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the voices**: how many, which types (soprano, alto, tenor, bass), lead or backing.
2. **Say how the voices combine**: unison, harmony (in 3rds, in chords), call and response, overlapping melodies, imitation.
3. **Describe the vocal line**: syllabic or melismatic, legato or staccato, belt, vibrato, falsetto, range, conjunct or disjunct.
4. **Describe the accompaniment**: which instruments, and what they do (chords, broken chords, countermelody, stab chords, walking bass, underscore).
5. **For "how does Version 2 differ"**, go element by element with comparisons ("slower", "lower key", "thicker texture"). Do not use points the question has ruled out.
6. **For the 10-mark answer**, link each feature to character, emotion or action: "The two characters sing different melodies at the same time, which shows they are not listening to each other."
:::

::: example Name the grouping
In a song, the lead character sings the first verse alone, then another character joins her, and they sing the final chorus in harmony. Name the two vocal groupings heard. (2)

Solo (1), then duet (1).
:::

::: example Vocal features
A singer holds long notes with a warm, slight wobble, sings each syllable to one note, and keeps the line smooth and connected. From this list give three features heard: belt / falsetto / legato / scat / syllabic / vibrato. (3)

Legato (1); syllabic (1); vibrato (1).
:::

::: example Accompaniment feature
In a ballad the harp accompanies the singer by playing the notes of each chord one after another, from low to high. Describe one musical feature of the harp part. (1)

Broken chords / arpeggios (accept spread chords) (1).
:::

::: example Comparing versions
Version 1 of a show song is sung by a female singer accompanied by guitar, accordion, violin and double bass, with a walking bass in Section 2. Version 2 is sung by a male singer, in a lower key and at a slower tempo, accompanied by piano, drum kit and strings, with brass stab chords. Without mentioning that the singer is male, note four ways in which Version 2 is different. (4)

Lower key (1). Slower tempo (1). Different instrumentation: drum kit and piano, with no guitar or accordion (1). Brass stab chords (1). (Also: no walking bass in Section 2; thicker texture.)
:::

::: example Overlapping melodies
Explain the dramatic effect of two characters singing different melodies at the same time. (2)

It shows two points of view at once, for example conflict or misunderstanding between the characters (1); the busy, polyphonic texture builds excitement and tension, often at a climax (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Say how voices combine**, not only how many sing: "duet in harmony, in 3rds" beats "duet".
- **Do not mention excluded points**: if a question says "without mentioning that the singer is male", a point about a male voice scores nothing.
- **Instruments must be named precisely**: "oboe" not "woodwind"; "cello" not "strings" when one instrument is asked for.
- **Belt is not the same as loud**: it is a particular chest-voice technique.
- In the 10-mark answer, **link features to character and drama**. Lists of terms stay in the lower bands.
:::

::: test
Read the description and answer the question.
In a stage musical, two rival characters argue. The song opens with a fast, spiky orchestral introduction with syncopated stab chords from the brass and a pounding drum rhythm. Character A sings a short, jagged phrase with a wide leap and an accent on every word; character B answers immediately with a similar phrase a tone higher. The phrases get shorter and overlap as the argument grows, until both characters sing different melodies at the same time. The harmony becomes more dissonant and the key rises by a semitone. At the climax, both characters belt a long high note, fortissimo, over a tutti orchestra; then there is a sudden silence and a single, quiet chord from the strings.
Explain how the music portrays the argument between the two characters. Use musical vocabulary and refer to the musical elements. (10)
---
Mark in bands (AO4). 9–10: perceptive, detailed explanation of the features with critical judgements on how they portray the argument; accurate vocabulary. 7–8: generally secure, competent account, general judgements; mostly accurate vocabulary. 5–6: inconsistent, some features identified with some judgement. 3–4: limited, elementary detail, few relevant judgements. 1–2: very limited detail or vocabulary. 0: nothing of value.
Indicative content:
- Texture: call and response between A and B shows accusation and reply; phrases overlap and become polyphonic / counterpoint, as both characters talk over each other; tutti climax; sudden silence and thin ending.
- Melody: short, jagged, disjunct phrases with wide leaps sound aggressive; B's answer a tone higher (sequence) suggests one-upmanship and escalation; belting high notes at the climax show anger and power.
- Rhythm and tempo: fast tempo; syncopated stab chords and a pounding drum suggest conflict and agitation; accents on every word make the delivery forceful; shortening phrases increase urgency.
- Harmony and tonality: increasing dissonance mirrors rising hostility; the key rises by a semitone, raising tension.
- Dynamics: builds to fortissimo; sudden silence and a quiet final chord suggest shock or the end of the confrontation.
- Sonority: brass stab chords sound like blows; drums as heartbeat or tension; soft strings at the end contrast with the brass.
- Top-band answers judge effectiveness, e.g. "overlapping the melodies means neither character's words can be heard clearly, which shows neither is listening to the other".
:::
