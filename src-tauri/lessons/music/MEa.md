## What it is

**Melody** is the tune: a line of single pitches heard one after another, with its own rhythm and shape. In the Eduqas listening paper you are asked about melody more often than any other element: "Describe two features of the melody", "Name the interval", "Name the scale", "Describe the flute part". The marks go to precise vocabulary. "It goes up and down" earns nothing. "Conjunct, ascending, with a narrow range and a sequence" earns up to four marks.

Every melody can be described under the same headings. Learn them as a checklist so that you are never stuck for a second or third point.

| Heading | Ask yourself | Words that score |
|---|---|---|
| **Movement** | Steps or leaps? | **conjunct** (stepwise), **disjunct** (angular, leaping), **scalic**, **arpeggio / broken chord** |
| **Shape** | Which way does it go? | **ascending**, **descending**, arch-shaped, rises to a climax, falls at the end of the phrase |
| **Range** | Distance from lowest to highest note | **narrow** (within a 5th), about **an octave**, **wide** (more than an octave) |
| **Pitch** | Where does it sit? | **low pitch**, **high pitch**, moves higher in the second phrase |
| **Scale / tonality** | Which notes does it use? | **major**, **minor**, **pentatonic**, **chromatic**, **blue notes**, **microtones** |
| **Devices** | How is it built? | **motif**, **repetition**, **sequence**, **imitation**, **contrast**, **answering phrase**, **anacrusis** |
| **Decoration** | Is it ornamented? | **trill**, **ornamentation / decoration**, appoggiatura, grace note |
| **Phrasing** | How long are the phrases? | **regular** (balanced, 4 + 4 bars), **irregular** |
| **Rhythm and articulation** | How are the notes played? | on the beat, syncopated, dotted, **legato**, **staccato** |
| **Role** | What is it doing in the texture? | main melody, **countermelody**, **fanfare**, **thematic** (built from the main theme), **leitmotif** |

### Movement and shape
A **conjunct** melody moves by step, from one note to the next letter name (C to D to E). A **disjunct** or angular melody leaps by larger intervals (C to G to D). Most melodies mix the two, and "mostly conjunct with occasional leaps" is a good answer. A **scalic** melody runs up or down a scale in order: **ascending** scalic or **descending** scalic. An **arpeggio** or **broken chord** plays the notes of a chord one after another (C–E–G–C). The Badinerie's opening flute motif is a descending B minor broken chord, for example.

**Range** is the distance from the lowest to the highest note. Examiners accept "narrow range", "a range of an octave" or "a wide range of more than an octave". An exact interval such as "a range of a 9th" is better still, if you can work it out from a printed score. The Badinerie flute part covers about two octaves. A folk song or a bhangra vocal often stays within a 5th or 6th.

### Intervals
An **interval** is the distance between two notes. Count letter names **inclusively** to get the number: C up to G is C-D-E-F-G, five letters, so a **5th**. Then count **semitones** (the smallest step on a keyboard, from one key to the very next, black or white) to get the quality.

| Interval | Semitones | Example from C | Sound and use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Semitone** (minor 2nd) | 1 | C to C sharp | the smallest step; sounds tense and creeping (the famous shark motif in *Jaws* alternates two notes a semitone apart) |
| **Tone / major 2nd** | 2 | C to D | an ordinary step of a scale |
| minor 3rd | 3 | C to E flat | slightly sad; the bottom 3rd of a minor chord |
| **Major 3rd** | 4 | C to E | bright; the bottom 3rd of a major chord |
| **Perfect 4th** | 5 | C to F | open and strong; common as a rising "call" at the start of anthems and fanfares |
| tritone | 6 | C to F sharp | very unstable and dissonant |
| **Perfect 5th** | 7 | C to G | hollow, strong, heroic; the interval of a power chord and of a drone |
| minor 6th | 8 | C to A flat | yearning |
| **Major 6th** | 9 | C to A | warm and sweeping |
| minor 7th | 10 | C to B flat | the 7th in a dominant 7th chord |
| **Major 7th** | 11 | C to B | one semitone short of an octave; very tense, wants to rise |
| **Octave** | 12 | C to the next C | the same letter name, higher or lower; sounds like the same note doubled |

The intervals in bold are the ones named in the spec's list of terms. The others are there so that your semitone counting works for any pair of notes. A **microtone** is an interval smaller than a semitone. It is not found on a piano, but singers, string players and players of Indian instruments slide through microtones. You will hear them in bhangra vocals and in the bent notes of blues.

To learn the sound of each interval, link it to the opening of a tune you already know, for example a hymn, a national anthem or a film theme, and practise singing it up and down.

### Scales
- **Major scale**: tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone (C D E F G A B C). It sounds bright.
- **Minor scale**: its 3rd is a semitone lower than the major's (C D E flat ...). It sounds darker. The **harmonic minor** raises the 7th note (in A minor, G sharp), and that raised note is a clue to the key (see MLb).
- **Chromatic scale**: all twelve semitones in order. **Chromatic movement** means the melody slides by semitones, often using notes outside the key. It creates tension, menace or richness.
- **Pentatonic scale**: five notes. The major pentatonic uses degrees **1, 2, 3, 5 and 6** of the major scale, leaving out the 4th and the 7th. So C pentatonic is C D E G A, G pentatonic is G A B D E and F pentatonic is F G A C D. With no semitones, it sounds open and simple, and it is found in folk music from many cultures, in blues and rock riffs, and in *Africa*'s riffs.
- **Blue notes**: the **flattened 3rd, 5th and 7th** of a major scale, bent or "worried" by the performer. In C they are E flat, G flat and B flat. They are the sound of blues and jazz (AoS2d).

### How a melody is built
- A **motif** is a short, recognisable idea of a few notes that the composer repeats and develops.
- **Repetition** means the same music is played again. **Contrast** means different material, for example a new melody, key or rhythm.
- A **sequence** is the same idea repeated straight away **at a higher or lower pitch in the same part**. A rising sequence builds tension; a falling one relaxes it.
- **Imitation** is an idea copied by **a different part** soon after it is first played, often at a different pitch (see AoS1c).
- An **answering phrase** is a second phrase that replies to the first. A question phrase often ends on the dominant (open, unfinished) and the answer on the tonic (closed). Together they make a **balanced**, regular pair, also called question and answer.
- An **anacrusis** (upbeat) is one or more notes before the first strong beat of the first full bar. The Badinerie starts with one.
- A **countermelody** is a second, independent melody played at the same time as the main one. When it lies above the main tune it is often called a **descant**.
- **Thematic** material is built from the main theme. A **leitmotif** is a short theme linked to a character, object or idea (AoS3b). A **fanfare** is a bold, rhythmic flourish, usually for brass, built from arpeggio notes and heroic 4ths and 5ths, often with dotted rhythms.

### Ornaments and decoration
**Ornamentation** or **decoration** means extra notes that decorate a main note.
- **Trill** (written "tr"): a rapid alternation of the written note with the note above.
- **Mordent**: a single quick flick to the note above or below and back.
- **Turn**: the note above, the note, the note below, the note.
- **Appoggiatura**: a "leaning" note, usually a step above or below the main note, that clashes with the harmony and then resolves by step. It takes part of the main note's time.
- **Acciaccatura** (grace note): a very quick "crushed" note just before the main note.

Ornaments are typical of **Baroque** music, as in the Badinerie's trills and appoggiaturas. They also appear in bhangra vocals and Indian music, where the singer decorates the note as it falls at the end of a phrase.

### Phrasing
**Regular phrasing** means phrases of equal length, usually 2, 4 or 8 bars, that sound balanced; it is typical of the Classical style and of pop songs. **Irregular phrasing** means unequal lengths, for example the nine-bar verse phrases in *Africa*. When you describe a melody, the phrase lengths are often an easy extra mark.

### Listening strategy
The app cannot play sound, so practise with the videos for this topic (HPA Music's melody video, P Dillon on describing a melody, and Music Learning Club on intervals). While you listen:
1. **First hearing:** follow the overall shape. Does it rise or fall? Does it move by steps or leaps? Where is the highest point?
2. **Second hearing:** listen for repetition. Is a motif repeated at the same pitch (repetition) or at a new pitch (sequence)? Is it copied by another instrument (imitation)?
3. **Third hearing:** listen for detail such as ornaments, chromatic slides, blue notes, the range, and whether the first note is an anacrusis.
4. Hum the melody back. If you can sing it, you can describe it.

::: terms
- **Conjunct**: melodic movement by step, from one note to the next letter name; also called stepwise.
- **Disjunct**: melodic movement by leaps larger than a step; also called angular.
- **Scalic**: a melody that runs up (ascending) or down (descending) the notes of a scale in order.
- **Arpeggio**: the notes of a chord played one after another instead of together; also called a broken chord.
- **Range**: the distance between the lowest and highest notes of a melody or part.
- **Interval**: the distance in pitch between two notes, counted inclusively by letter name, e.g. C up to G is a 5th.
- **Semitone**: the smallest interval in Western music, the step from one piano key to the very next.
- **Microtone**: an interval smaller than a semitone, heard in Indian music, bhangra singing and bent blues notes.
- **Pentatonic**: a five-note scale; the major pentatonic uses degrees 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 of the major scale.
- **Blue notes**: the flattened 3rd, 5th and 7th of the scale, typical of blues and jazz.
- **Sequence**: a musical idea repeated straight away at a higher or lower pitch in the same part.
- **Answering phrase**: a phrase that replies to the one before it, usually ending more conclusively.
- **Trill**: an ornament made by rapidly alternating the written note with the note above it.
- **Anacrusis**: an upbeat, one or more notes before the first strong beat of the first full bar.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the question for the part and the section.** "Describe the viola part in Section B" means only that part, only there.
2. **Count the marks.** Each mark needs a separate feature. A 3-mark answer needs three different headings, not one idea described three ways.
3. **Work through the checklist:** movement, shape, range, pitch, scale, devices, ornaments, phrasing, rhythm and articulation. Pick the clearest features first.
4. **Use the exact term, with a location if you can:** "a rising sequence in bars 5–8" or "a trill on the last note of the phrase".
5. **For an interval,** count the letter names inclusively for the number, then the semitones for the quality (4 = major 3rd, 5 = perfect 4th, 7 = perfect 5th, 9 = major 6th, 11 = major 7th, 12 = octave).
6. **For a scale,** list the notes: five notes with no semitones = pentatonic; all twelve semitones = chromatic; flattened 3rd, 5th and 7th = blue notes.
7. **If told "do not refer to earlier answers",** check the earlier parts of the question and choose different features.
:::

::: example Name the interval
A melody leaps from D up to the A above it. Name the interval. (1)

D-E-F sharp-G-A is five letter names, so it is a 5th. From D to A is seven semitones (D, D sharp, E, F, F sharp, G, G sharp, A), which makes it a **perfect 5th** (1).
:::

::: example Name the scale
A melody uses only the notes C, D, E, G and A. Name the type of scale. (1)

**Pentatonic** / major pentatonic (1). The five notes are degrees 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 of C major. There is no F (4th) and no B (7th), so there are no semitones.
:::

::: example Describe a described melody
A melody begins on the upbeat. It climbs step by step through the first four bars, then repeats its opening two-bar idea one note higher. It ends with a quick decoration on the long final note. Describe two features of the melody. (2)

Any two, one mark each: it begins with an **anacrusis** (1); it is **conjunct** / ascending **scalic** (1); it uses a rising **sequence** (1); it ends with an **ornament** / a trill on the final note (1).
:::

::: example Sequence against repetition
Explain the difference between repetition and a sequence in a melody. (2)

**Repetition** is when an idea is played again at the **same pitch** (1). A **sequence** repeats the idea straight away in the same part but at a **higher or lower pitch** (1).
:::

::: example Describe a melody in three points
A film theme opens with a short three-note idea built on the notes of a minor chord, which leaps downwards. The idea returns several times, each time with its last note changed, and the phrases are four bars long. Describe the melody. (3)

Any three: it is built from a short **motif** (1); it is based on a **broken chord / arpeggio** (1); it is **disjunct** / angular (1); it is **descending** (1); it is in a **minor** key (1); it has **regular** / balanced **four-bar phrases** (1); the motif is **repeated with variation** / altered each time (1).
:::

::: q
A melody has two four-bar phrases. The first ends on the dominant and sounds unfinished; the second begins the same way but ends on the tonic. Describe two features of the phrasing. (2)
---
Regular / balanced phrasing, 4 + 4 bars (1); question and answer / an answering phrase (1); the second phrase begins with repetition of the first (1); the first ends with an imperfect cadence feel and the second a perfect cadence feel (1). Any two.
:::

::: watch
- **One feature per mark.** "Conjunct and moves by step" is one feature said twice.
- An interval needs **both parts** of its name: "perfect 5th", not "5th"; "major 3rd", not "3rd".
- Count interval numbers **inclusively**: C to E is a 3rd, not a 2nd.
- **Sequence** stays in the same part at a new pitch; **imitation** moves to a different part. Mixing them up is the commonest melody error.
- "High" and "low" need a comparison or a range to score well: "a wide range of about two octaves" beats "high".
- Don't write "tune goes up and down". Use **ascending** or **descending**, **conjunct** or **disjunct**, and say where in the extract.
- The pentatonic scale leaves out the **4th and 7th** degrees. Don't confuse it with the blues scale.
:::
