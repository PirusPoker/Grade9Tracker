## What it is

Harmonic devices are the ways composers use **chords and bass lines** to build a piece. The spec lists them for AoS1: **simple chord progressions including cadences, drone, pedal, broken chord/arpeggio, Alberti bass, and modulation to the dominant and relative minor**. A cadence question ("underline the cadence at bars 7–8") has appeared on every recent paper, often more than once, and the Badinerie questions regularly ask for cadences and keys. This topic is one of the most reliable sources of marks on the paper.

**Chords in a major key.** Build a triad on each note of the scale using notes of that key only. Capital numerals are major chords and small numerals are minor. **I, IV and V** are the **primary chords**; **ii, iii and vi** are **secondary chords**. **V7** is chord V with the seventh added above its root (the **dominant seventh**).

| Key | I | ii | iii | IV | V | V7 | vi |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C major | C | Dm | Em | F | G | G7 (G B D F) | Am |
| G major | G | Am | Bm | C | D | D7 (D F# A C) | Em |
| D major | D | Em | F#m | G | A | A7 (A C# E G) | Bm |
| F major | F | Gm | Am | B flat | C | C7 (C E G B flat) | Dm |
| B flat major | B flat | Cm | Dm | E flat | F | F7 (F A C E flat) | Gm |

In a **minor key**, chord V is normally made **major** by raising the 7th note of the scale: in A minor, V is E major (E G# B), not E minor. That raised note, the **leading note**, pulls strongly up to the tonic.

**Cadences** are the two chords at the end of a phrase, and they work like punctuation.

| Cadence | Chords | Sound | In C major |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect** | **V–I** (or V7–I) | finished, final: a full stop | G–C |
| **Plagal** | **IV–I** | finished but softer: the "Amen" at the end of hymns | F–C |
| **Imperfect** | **anything–V** (I–V, ii–V, IV–V) | unfinished, a question: a comma | C–G, Dm–G, F–G |
| **Interrupted** | **V–vi** (V to an unexpected chord) | a surprise: you expect I and get something else | G–Am |

The quick test: **what is the last chord?**
- It is **V**: imperfect.
- It is **I**: look at the chord before. **V** makes it perfect; **IV** makes it plagal.
- It is **vi** after **V**: interrupted.

**Chord progressions.** A chord progression (or chord sequence) is a series of chords. Common ones include:
- **I–IV–V–I**, the basic progression of countless folk songs, hymns and Classical tunes;
- **I–vi–IV–V**, a 1950s pop pattern;
- **vi–ii–V–I**, a chain of chords falling by fifths, which sounds strongly directed towards home;
- the **12-bar blues**, taught in AoS2d.
**Harmonic rhythm** is how often the chords change: one chord per bar is slow, two chords per bar is faster.

**Bass-line and accompaniment devices**

| Device | What it is | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| **Drone** | two notes, usually the **tonic and the fifth** (e.g. D and A), held or repeated throughout | bagpipes, folk music, Indian classical music, pastoral scenes |
| **Pedal** | **one note** held or repeated (usually in the bass) while the **harmony changes above it**, so some chords clash with it; usually the tonic or the dominant | builds tension before a return to the tonic (a dominant pedal) or grounds an ending (a tonic pedal) |
| **Inverted pedal** | a pedal note in the **top** part instead of the bass | a held high note in the violins over changing chords |
| **Broken chord / arpeggio** | the notes of a chord played one after another instead of together | an arpeggio usually runs up or down in order (C E G C); a broken chord may use any pattern |
| **Alberti bass** | a **broken-chord accompaniment** in the pattern **low–high–middle–high**, in steady quavers or semiquavers | Classical piano music (Mozart, Haydn). In C major: C G E G, then B G D G over chord V |

**Drone against pedal.** A drone is usually **two notes (a fifth)**, and the music above tends to fit it throughout. A pedal is **one note**, and the chords above keep **changing**, sometimes clashing with it.

**Modulation** means changing key during a piece. The two changes named in the spec are:

| From | To the **dominant** (key of V) | To the **relative minor** (key of vi) | New note that signals it |
|---|---|---|---|
| C major | G major | A minor | F# (dominant); G# (relative minor) |
| G major | D major | E minor | C#; D# |
| D major | A major | B minor | G#; A# |
| F major | C major | D minor | B natural; C# |

Minor keys modulate too: to the **relative major** (B minor to D major) or to the **dominant minor** (B minor to F sharp minor). The Badinerie does both. Section A goes from B minor, through A major, to **F sharp minor**; Section B goes from F sharp minor through E minor, **D major** (confirmed by a perfect cadence), G major and back to **B minor**. Both sections end with a perfect cadence.

**How to recognise a modulation.** In a score, look for a new accidental that keeps appearing (a new sharp usually means a move to the dominant or to a minor key's raised 7th), and for a cadence in the new key. By ear, the "home" note seems to shift. A move to the dominant sounds brighter and lifted; a move to the relative minor sounds darker, although many of the same notes are used.

**Listening strategy.** Use the topic's videos (Music Learning Club and P Dillon on cadences, MusicTheoryAcademy's four cadence types, and Music Matters on Alberti bass). Play or sing the four cadences in C until you can hear the difference between "finished" (perfect, plagal), "question" (imperfect) and "surprise" (interrupted). In the exam:
1. Wait for the **end of the phrase** named in the question.
2. Decide **finished or unfinished**.
3. If finished, decide whether it sounds strong (**perfect**) or soft, like an "Amen" (**plagal**).
4. If unfinished, decide whether it hangs on V (**imperfect**) or swerves somewhere unexpected (**interrupted**).
For pedals and drones, listen to the **lowest** part: does one note stay while everything else moves?

::: terms
- **Cadence**: the two chords at the end of a phrase, which make it sound finished or unfinished.
- **Perfect cadence**: chord V (or V7) followed by chord I; it sounds complete and final.
- **Imperfect cadence**: a cadence ending on chord V, such as I–V or ii–V; it sounds unfinished.
- **Plagal cadence**: chord IV followed by chord I; a softer ending, often sung to "Amen".
- **Interrupted cadence**: chord V followed by an unexpected chord, usually vi; it creates surprise.
- **Primary chords**: chords I, IV and V, built on the tonic, subdominant and dominant.
- **Dominant seventh**: chord V with an added seventh above its root, e.g. G7 (G B D F) in C major.
- **Pedal**: a note held or repeated, usually in the bass, while the harmony above it changes.
- **Drone**: two notes, usually the tonic and fifth, sustained or repeated throughout a passage.
- **Alberti bass**: a broken-chord accompaniment in the pattern low–high–middle–high, typical of Classical piano music.
- **Modulation**: a change of key within a piece, e.g. to the dominant or relative minor.
- **Harmonic rhythm**: the rate at which chords change, e.g. one chord per bar.
:::

::: method
1. **Find the key first** from the key signature, accidentals and final note. You cannot name chord V without knowing I.
2. **Name the two chords at the cadence** from the notes given (or from the question), then turn them into numerals.
3. **Apply the last-chord test**: ends on V, imperfect; V–I, perfect; IV–I, plagal; V–vi, interrupted.
4. **For modulation, look for a new accidental** that repeats plus a cadence confirming a new tonic. Name the relationship (dominant, relative minor or major) if the options are relationships, and the key "in full" (e.g. D major) if asked for a key.
5. **For bass devices, check what the bass does**: one note held with changing chords above (pedal), two notes a fifth apart (drone), chord notes one by one (broken chord), or the low–high–middle–high pattern (Alberti bass).
:::

::: example Name the cadence
A phrase in G major ends with the chords C major then G major. Name the cadence. (1)

Plagal (IV–I) (1).
:::

::: example Name the cadence from numerals
Which cadence is V–vi? (1)

Interrupted (1).
:::

::: example Chord names in a minor key
In the Badinerie (B minor), the chord on the last quaver of bar 3 contains the notes F#, A#, C# and E. Give its chord name and Roman numeral. (2)

F#7 (F sharp dominant seventh) (1); V7 (1).
:::

::: example Modulation clue
A piece in D major keeps using G# from bar 9 onwards, and bar 12 ends with the chords E major then A major. Underline the key the music modulates to: dominant / relative major / relative minor. Give the key in full. (2)

Dominant (1); A major (1). (G# is the leading note of A major, and E–A is a perfect cadence in A.)
:::

::: example Pedal against drone
Explain the difference between a pedal and a drone. (2)

A pedal is a single note held or repeated, usually in the bass, while the harmony above changes and may clash with it (1). A drone is usually two notes, the tonic and fifth, sustained throughout a passage, with music above that fits it (1).
:::

::: q
Write out an Alberti bass pattern for one bar of chord I in C major in quavers (4/4). (2)
---
C G E G C G E G (accept any bar showing low–high–middle–high on the notes C, E and G) (1 for correct chord notes, 1 for correct pattern).
:::

::: q
Explain why a dominant pedal creates tension. (2)
---
The bass stays on the dominant note while chords above change and clash with it (1), so the listener expects and waits for a resolution to the tonic (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Imperfect ends ON V; perfect goes FROM V to I.** Check which chord is last before you choose.
- **Plagal (IV–I) also ends on I**, so look at the chord before the last one.
- **Interrupted** is V to anything unexpected, usually vi. It does not end on I.
- **Name keys in full**: "D major", not "D"; "F sharp minor", not "F sharp". Mark schemes reject the bare letter.
- **Minor-key V is major** because of the raised 7th; do not call it a minor chord.
- A **pedal is one note** and a **drone is usually two**; Alberti bass is **Classical**, not Baroque.
:::

::: test
Describe how a listener might recognise that a piece has modulated to its relative minor. (3)
---
Any three: the mood becomes darker / minor (1); a new accidental appears, the raised 7th of the new key, e.g. G# in A minor (1); a cadence ends on the new tonic chord, e.g. E–Am (1); the melody centres on a new home note (1); the key signature stays the same, so the change is shown by accidentals (1).
:::
