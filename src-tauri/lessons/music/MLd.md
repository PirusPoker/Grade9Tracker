## What it is

**Dictation** means writing down music you hear. The spec says candidates will "complete the rhythm or pitch of a short section of music", and that **pitch dictation will be within the major scale**. Every real Component 3 paper has one dictation, usually worth **5 marks, one per note**. It sits inside a 12-mark question with a printed melody:
- **2022:** Schumann, pitches of bars 12–13, rhythm given.
- **2023:** Handel, pitches of bars 10–11, plus a missing accidental.
- **2025:** a Beatles song, pitches of bar 9, rhythm given.

You get the melody on the page with a gap and the rhythm (or the pitches) printed, and the extract is played **four times**.

The app cannot play sound, so this lesson teaches the **method** and trains the thinking with dictations **described in words**: "up a step, down a 3rd, repeat". Real aural dictation needs regular practice with sound. Use the topic videos (Baines Music on melodic and on rhythmic dictation) and ask your teacher for past-paper audio, which Eduqas releases only to schools.

### Why dictation is winnable
The exam gives you far more help than it seems:
- **The key signature**, so you know which seven notes are possible. "Within the major scale" means **no chromatic notes** in pitch dictation: every note belongs to the key.
- **The rhythm**, so you know exactly how many notes are missing and where they fall.
- **The notes before and after the gap**, which are your anchors.
- **The rest of the melody.** Melodies repeat and sequence, and the missing bar often matches an earlier bar exactly, or moves it up or down a step.
- **The harmony.** A note on a strong beat is usually in the chord at that point, and phrase ends fall on the tonic (perfect cadence) or the dominant (imperfect).

### Step 1: before the first playing (reading time)
1. **Name the key** from the key signature (MLb) and **write its scale** in the margin with numbers: in G major, G(1) A(2) B(3) C(4) D(5) E(6) F#(7) G(8).
2. **Mark the anchors:** the last note before the gap and the first note after it.
3. **Count the notes** you must fill in, and **tap the given rhythm** silently so you can follow it.
4. **Hunt for patterns.** Is there an earlier bar with the same rhythm? Mark it: the gap may repeat it, or be a sequence of it.
5. **Check the harmony** if chords are printed. The strong-beat notes are likely to be chord notes.

### Step 2: during the playings (the contour)
- **First playing:** draw the **shape** above the gap. Use arrows for up and down, "s" for a step, "L" for a leap and "=" for a repeated note. Don't try to find letter names yet.
- **Second playing:** fix the **anchor notes**. Is the first missing note a step from the note before it? Does the phrase end on the tonic?
- **Third playing:** measure each **leap**. Sing it: does it outline a chord (a 3rd or a 5th)? Most leaps in simple melodies are 3rds, 4ths, 5ths or octaves, and they usually land on chord notes.
- **Fourth playing:** check the whole bar and correct anything that breaks the shape.

### Step 3: after the last playing (checking)
- Every note must be **in the key**. Check the key signature: in F major, "B" is B flat.
- Do the notes **join smoothly** to the note after the gap?
- Does the phrase end on a stable note (**tonic**, 3rd or 5th of chord I, or on the dominant for an imperfect cadence)?
- Are the note heads clearly **on a line or in a space**? An ambiguous note head is marked wrong. Use ledger lines carefully.
- **Never leave a gap.** Each correct note scores independently, so an educated guess can still earn marks.

### Hearing steps, leaps and repeats
| Movement | What it sounds like | How to write it |
|---|---|---|
| **repeat** | the same pitch again | the same line or space |
| **step** (2nd) | the next note up or down the scale | the next line or space |
| **3rd** | a skip over one note, as in the first two notes of a major chord | line to line, or space to space |
| **4th** | a strong, open skip | line to space, or space to line, skipping two |
| **5th** | a wide, hollow leap, as from the tonic up to the dominant | line to line, or space to space, skipping three |
| **octave** | the same letter name, higher or lower | line to space or space to line, eight notes apart |

**Scale-degree singing** helps. Sing melodies using numbers (1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8) or sol-fa (doh re mi fa soh la ti doh). Once you can sing "5 to 1" and "3 to 1", you can recognise the common endings instantly: the **leading note (7) rises to 1** and the **2nd (2) falls to 1** at a perfect cadence.

### Rhythm dictation
When the **pitches** are printed and the rhythm is missing:
1. **Know the bar total** from the time signature: 4/4 = four crotchets, 3/4 = three, 6/8 = six quavers in two groups of three.
2. **Tap the pulse** and count how many notes fall on each beat.
3. **One note on a beat** = crotchet (simple time) or dotted crotchet (compound time). **Two equal notes** = quavers (simple). **Three equal notes** = quavers (compound) or a triplet (simple). **Four equal** = semiquavers (simple).
4. **Long-short** on one beat = dotted quaver + semiquaver. A note held across a beat = minim, dotted note or tie.
5. **Check that the bar adds up exactly**, and group the notes by beat (beaming).

### Related 1-mark items
Dictation questions often come with small score tasks:
- **Write in the missing accidental.** This is usually a note raised in a modulation or a chromatic decoration. Place the sign **before** the note on the same line or space.
- **Insert the time signature**: add up a full bar (MLa).
- **Give the full name of the key.**
- **Give the lowest or highest pitch.**
- **Underline the cadence** at the end of the phrase.

::: terms
- **Dictation**: writing down the pitch or rhythm of music heard, completing a gap in a printed melody.
- **Pitch dictation**: filling in missing note pitches when the rhythm is given; on this paper always within the major scale.
- **Rhythm dictation**: filling in missing note values when the pitches or the pulse are given.
- **Contour**: the up-and-down shape of a melody, sketched first before exact notes are found.
- **Anchor note**: a known printed note just before or after the gap, used to measure the missing notes.
- **Scale degree**: a note's number in the scale (1 = tonic, 5 = dominant, 7 = leading note).
- **Step**: a move to the next note of the scale, up or down, an interval of a 2nd.
- **Leap**: a move larger than a step, e.g. a 3rd, 4th, 5th or octave, often outlining a chord.
- **Leading note**: the 7th degree of the scale, which usually rises by step to the tonic at a cadence.
:::

::: method
1. **Before listening:** name the key, write the scale with numbers, mark the anchor notes, count the missing notes and look for an earlier bar that matches.
2. **First hearing:** sketch the contour (up, down, step, leap, repeat), not letter names.
3. **Second and third hearings:** fix the first note against the anchor, then measure each leap as a 3rd, 4th, 5th or octave.
4. **Use the harmony:** strong-beat notes belong to the chord; phrase ends go to the tonic or dominant.
5. **Final hearing:** check the whole shape, including the join to the note after the gap.
6. **After:** check every note is in the key (apply the key signature), note heads are clearly placed, and no note is left blank.
7. **Rhythm dictation:** count notes per beat, choose values, and make sure the bar adds up.
:::

::: example Pitches from a described contour
A melody is in C major. The note before the gap is E. The five missing notes (rhythm given) move: up a step, up a step, repeat, down a 3rd, down a step. Write the five pitches. (5)

From E: up a step **F** (1), up a step **G** (1), repeat **G** (1), down a 3rd **E** (1), down a step **D** (1).
:::

::: example Pitches using the key signature
A melody is in B flat major. The note before the gap is C. The five missing notes move: up a step, up a step, down a 4th, up a step, up a step. Write the five pitches. (5)

**D** (1), **E flat** (1), because B flat major has E flat in its key signature, then down a 4th from E flat to **B flat** (1), up a step to **C** (1), and up a step to **D** (1).
:::

::: example Rhythm from beats
A bar of 4/4 has five notes. One note falls on beat 1; beat 2 has two equal notes; the fourth note starts on beat 3 and lasts a beat and a half; the fifth fills the rest of the bar. Give the note values. (5)

**Crotchet** (1), **quaver** (1), **quaver** (1), **dotted crotchet** (1), **quaver** (1). Check: 1 + ½ + ½ + 1½ + ½ = 4.
:::

::: example Using a matching bar
In a melody in D major, bar 2 is D–E–F sharp–D. Bar 6 has the same rhythm and is described as "bar 2 moved up a step". Write the pitches of bar 6. (4)

A sequence up a step: **E** (1), **F sharp** (1), **G** (1), **E** (1).
:::

::: example The missing accidental
A melody in C major briefly moves to G major, and the note F in bar 6 needs to be raised by a semitone. Name the missing accidental and the resulting note. (2)

A **sharp** (1), giving **F sharp** (1), the leading note of G major.
:::

::: q
A melody in B flat major ends with a perfect cadence. Which note is the melody most likely to end on? (1)
---
B flat (the tonic).
:::

::: q
Explain why "within the major scale" helps you in a pitch dictation. (2)
---
Every missing note belongs to the key / there are no chromatic notes (1), so only seven letter names (with the key signature's sharps or flats) are possible (1).
:::

::: q
Give two things you should do before the first playing of a dictation question. (2)
---
Any two: identify the key and write out its scale (1); mark the notes before and after the gap (1); count the missing notes / tap the given rhythm (1); look for a matching or sequential bar elsewhere (1); check any chord symbols (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Apply the key signature.** A "B" in F major is B flat, and an "F" in G major is F sharp.
- **Never leave a gap.** Each note is marked separately.
- Get the **first note** right: everything else is measured from it.
- Place note heads **clearly** on a line or in a space. Ambiguous notes are marked wrong.
- **Look for repetition and sequence** elsewhere in the printed melody: the missing bar often copies one.
- In rhythm dictation, **the bar must add up**. Count it before moving on.
- Don't add accidentals in pitch dictation unless the question asks. The notes are within the major scale.
:::

---
