## What it is

**Film music** is music written or chosen to go with moving pictures. In Area of Study 3 you learn how a composer uses the **musical elements** to make an audience feel what the director wants them to feel. The questions test whether you can **hear a feature, name it with the correct term, and explain what it does to the scene**. Component 3 always has **two 12-mark film questions** (Questions 5 and 6), and one of the 12-mark questions on the paper contains the **10-mark extended answer**. In 2022 and 2025 that answer was a film question (a chase scene and the shark in *Jaws*). Film music is where the 10 marks most often sit, so this lesson teaches that answer in full.

The app can't play audio. Use this lesson for the knowledge and vocabulary, then do the listening with the topic's videos. They take you through atmosphere and tension cues. Pause each clip and say aloud **what you hear** and **what it makes you feel**, because the exam marks exactly that pairing.

### Where film music comes from: the commission

Film music is almost always **commissioned**. A director or studio pays a composer for music that fits one particular film. The commission sets limits that shape every choice:

| Part of the commission | What it means for the music |
|---|---|
| **The brief** | The director describes the mood, the characters and the story. The composer must match that intention, not their own taste. |
| **Timing** | Each **cue** (a piece of music for one scene) must last exactly as long as the scene. It must also **hit** key moments: a door opening, a punch, a cut to a new shot. These are called **hit points**, and the music is **synchronised** to the picture. |
| **Budget and forces** | The money decides whether the composer gets a full symphony orchestra, a small ensemble, a synthesiser, or a mix of acoustic and electronic sound. |
| **Genre** | A horror, western, romance, science-fiction or animated film each has sounds its audience expects. |
| **Stimulus** | The composer responds to a **stimulus**: the script (words), the storyboard or the edited footage (pictures), a location, a character. |

Some film music is **specially composed** (an orchestral score or songs written for the film). Some is **pre-existing**: songs from the period or place in which the film is set, or famous classical pieces. The finished **soundtrack** can contain both.

### Creating mood: element → effect

**Descriptive music** creates a mood or pictures an action. The composer uses **all** the elements, and your job is to link each one to its effect. Learn these pairings. They are the raw material of the 10-mark answer.

| Mood | Typical features |
|---|---|
| **Suspense / tension** | Slow or unclear pulse; quiet dynamics with sudden **sforzandos**; low sustained **pedal** notes or **drones**; **tremolo** strings; **dissonance** and **chromatic** harmony; the **semitone**; silences; an **ostinato** that slowly speeds up or gets louder (**crescendo**); a thin texture that gradually thickens |
| **Horror** | Clashing **cluster** chords; screeching high strings; very low brass; minor or unclear (atonal) tonality; sudden **stingers**; unpredictable rhythm |
| **Action / chase** | **Fast tempo** (Allegro); **driving rhythms**; repeated **ostinato**; **syncopation**; loud dynamics; busy **polyphonic** texture; **brass** and **percussion**; rising **sequences**; **stab chords** |
| **Heroism / triumph** | **Major** tonality; **brass fanfares**; rising **perfect 4ths and 5ths**; loud dynamics; strong march-like rhythms; full orchestra (**tutti**) |
| **Romance / tenderness** | Slow tempo; **legato**, **conjunct** melody; strings with **vibrato**; lush, rich harmony; soft to moderate dynamics; homophonic texture; solo woodwind such as the oboe or flute |
| **Sadness / reflection** | Minor tonality; slow tempo; descending melody; soft dynamics; thin texture; solo piano or cello; sustained notes |
| **Comedy** | Staccato woodwind (bassoon, piccolo); **pizzicato**; glissandos; unexpected pauses; jaunty dotted rhythms; **Mickey-mousing** |
| **Mystery / magic / space** | Unusual timbres (celesta, harp, glockenspiel, synthesiser); high sustained strings; reverb; shifting chords that don't settle; whole-tone or modal sounds |

**Contrast** is a tool in its own right. A sudden change from very quiet to very loud, from high to low, or from thin to thick tells the audience that something has happened. Film composers also exploit **range**. Very high and very low pitches at the same time, with nothing in the middle, can feel empty and threatening.

### Instrumental and vocal timbres create colour

**Timbre** (tone colour or sonority) is the quality of a sound. Film composers choose it deliberately:

- **Strings**: warm and expressive when legato with vibrato; nervous with **tremolo**; spiky with **pizzicato**; eerie with high **harmonics** or **glissando**.
- **Brass**: heroic and noble with **fanfares**; threatening in the low register (trombones, tuba); **muted** brass sounds distant or sinister.
- **Woodwind**: flute for innocence, air and nature; oboe for sadness or the countryside; clarinet for warmth; bassoon for comedy or menace.
- **Percussion**: timpani rolls build tension; the snare drum is military; the bass drum is doom; tuned percussion (glockenspiel, celesta, xylophone) sounds magical or childlike.
- **Voices**: a wordless **choir** can sound heavenly, epic or ghostly; a solo boy treble sounds innocent; chanting sounds ancient or ritual.
- **Electronic** sounds (synthesisers, samples) sound futuristic, cold or alien.

### Performers interpret, and audience and venue matter

**Performers interpret a composition.** The score gives the notes, but the conductor and players decide the exact tempo, balance, phrasing, **rubato**, **vibrato** and dynamic shading. For film, the orchestra usually records in a studio while the players watch the picture or follow a **click track** (a metronome in their headphones), so that the music synchronises. A famous soloist can be hired to bring a particular sound, and in the 2025 paper's tango cue the violin soloist's expressive playing was part of the effect.

**Audience and venue affect performance and composition.**
- In a cinema the music is heard through powerful **surround-sound** speakers, so very quiet and very loud sounds and extremely low bass all work.
- Music written under dialogue must leave room for the voices. That usually means sustained notes, a thin texture and a middle-to-low register.
- Film music is now also performed in **concert halls**, both as suites and as live-to-picture screenings. There the orchestra plays live while the film is shown, so the conductor must stay exactly in time with the picture.
- A children's animation, a horror film and a period drama each have a different audience, and the composer writes with that audience's expectations in mind.

### Listening strategy

1. In the first playing, just **feel the mood** and jot down one word for it: tense, heroic, sad.
2. In the second playing, run through the **elements checklist** and note one feature for each: **M**elody, **A**rticulation, **D**ynamics, **T**exture, **T**empo, **T**onality, **H**armony, **I**nstruments (timbre), **R**hythm/metre, **S**tructure. One way to remember the list is "MAD T-SHIRT" (Melody, Articulation, Dynamics, Texture, Structure, Harmony, Instruments, Rhythm, Tempo, Tonality).
3. In the third playing, listen for **changes**. When does it get louder, thicker, higher or faster? Changes are what describe the story.
4. **Read the scene description** printed in the question. Every feature you write should connect to something in it.

::: terms
- **Commission**: a request, usually paid, from a director or studio for music written for a particular purpose or film.
- **Stimulus**: the starting point a composer responds to, such as a script, storyboard, image or edited scene.
- **Cue**: a single piece of film music written to accompany one particular scene.
- **Underscore**: music played underneath the action or dialogue that the characters on screen cannot hear.
- **Diegetic music**: music that exists inside the story world, so the characters can hear it (a radio, a band on screen).
- **Stinger**: a short, sudden, loud chord or sound that marks a shock or a reveal.
- **Mickey-mousing**: music that copies the on-screen action exactly, such as a falling glissando when someone falls.
- **Hit point**: a precise moment in the picture, such as a crash or a cut, that the music is timed to coincide with.
- **Descriptive music**: music that paints a mood, place, character or action rather than being abstract.
- **Timbre**: the tone colour or quality of a sound, which lets you tell a flute from a violin playing the same note.
- **Tremolo**: rapid repetition of one note, or fast alternation between two, creating a trembling, tense effect.
- **Sforzando**: a sudden, strongly accented note or chord, often used as a shock in film.
- **Click track**: a metronome heard through headphones so performers stay synchronised with the film.
- **Dissonance**: notes that clash, creating tension that seems to need resolving.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the scene description first.** Underline the moods it asks for (e.g. "suspense and horror", "action and excitement"). Your answer must show both.
2. **Plan in a grid** with three columns: element, feature heard, effect on the scene. Aim for at least eight rows covering melody, harmony and tonality, tempo, rhythm, texture, dynamics, sonority, structure.
3. **Follow the extract in time order** where you can: "At the start... then... by the end...". Change over time is how music tells a story.
4. **Write feature + precise term + effect** in every sentence, e.g. "the low strings play a two-note **semitone** motif, which suggests something moving slowly underwater". A term with no effect, or an effect with no term, earns little.
5. **Be specific**, not generic. "Crescendo from pp to ff" beats "gets louder", and "trumpet fanfare built on rising 4ths" beats "brass".
6. **Make a judgement**: say *why* the choice is effective ("the silence makes the audience wait for the next attack, so the tension grows"). That is what lifts an answer into 9–10.
7. For the short 1–3 mark items, give **one separate feature per mark**, and don't repeat features you used in an earlier part if the question says so.
:::

::: example Linking a feature to a scene
In a film, a nervous character creeps through a dark house. The music has a very low sustained note in the double basses, high violin tremolo, and a slow two-note idea that repeats, getting louder. Explain how the music creates tension. (3)

The low **pedal note** in the double basses gives an unsettled, heavy feeling of something waiting in the dark (1). The high **tremolo** violins sound nervous and trembling, like the character's fear (1). The repeated two-note idea with a **crescendo** makes the audience feel the danger coming closer, building towards a shock (1).
:::

::: example Describing change between sections
A film cue has Section A (16 bars): a solo violin plays a hesitant, quiet melody in a minor key with pauses. Section B (16 bars): the full ensemble plays loudly, the piano joins with a strong dance rhythm, and the harmony changes chords more often. Describe three ways in which Section B is different from Section A. (3)

Section B is **louder** (1). The **texture is thicker**, with more instruments and the piano joining (1). The **harmonic rhythm is quicker**, with chords changing more often (1). (Also creditable: a stronger or clearer dance rhythm; a more confident mood.)
:::

::: example The commission
A composer is commissioned to write music for the opening credits of a western. Explain two ways in which the commission affects the music. (2)

The music must last exactly as long as the credits sequence, so its length and any big moments are timed to the picture (1). It must suit the genre and the director's brief, for example wide open harmonies and a heroic brass or guitar melody to suggest the landscape and the hero (1).
:::

::: example A 9–10 standard extended answer
A film scene shows astronauts drifting slowly towards a damaged spacecraft, then a sudden alarm and a race to escape. The music: begins with a very quiet, high sustained chord on synthesiser and strings, slow and without a clear pulse; a solo horn plays a slow rising melody of 4ths and 5ths; then a sudden loud stab chord, a fast ostinato in the low strings, pounding timpani and brass, a crescendo to a loud dissonant final chord. Explain how the music creates the mood of the scene. (10)

At the opening the **sustained, high chord** with **no clear pulse** makes time seem suspended, which suits the weightlessness of drifting in space. The **quiet dynamic** and the blend of **synthesiser and strings** give a cold, glassy timbre, which suggests the emptiness of space. The solo **horn** melody moves in rising **perfect 4ths and 5ths**. These are open, heroic intervals, so the astronauts seem brave and the scene has a sense of wonder, and the **slow tempo** and **legato** phrasing make the movement feel gentle.

The mood changes at once with a **sforzando stab chord**. This sudden **contrast** from pp to ff mirrors the alarm and shocks the audience. A **fast ostinato** in the low strings sets up a **driving rhythm** and a clear, urgent pulse, so we feel the race against time. **Timpani** and **brass** make the texture **thicker** and the timbre more aggressive, and the continual **crescendo** makes the danger feel as though it is growing. The music ends on a loud **dissonant** chord that does not resolve, which leaves the audience unsure whether the crew escape. The composer uses **contrast** in tempo, dynamics, texture and harmony to split the scene into calm and panic, which is highly effective because the shock is felt as well as seen.

(Band 9–10: detailed features across many elements, accurate vocabulary throughout, and every feature judged against the action.)
:::

::: q
Name two features of a melody that would make a hero sound brave. (2)
---
Any two: rising perfect 4ths/5ths; fanfare-like / based on a triad; major key; played by brass (trumpet/horn); loud; strong dotted / march rhythms; wide range / ends high.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between diegetic music and underscore. (2)
---
Diegetic music exists inside the story and the characters can hear it (1); underscore plays underneath the action for the audience only / characters cannot hear it (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways a composer could create suspense using dynamics. (2)
---
Any two: very quiet (pp) to make the audience strain; a gradual crescendo as danger approaches; sudden sforzando / stinger for a shock; sudden silence; subito piano after a loud chord.
:::

::: q
Why do musicians recording a film score often use a click track? (1)
---
So their tempo stays exactly synchronised with the picture / so the music hits the key moments (hit points).
:::

::: q
A cue for a sad farewell uses a slow tempo and a solo instrument. Suggest a suitable instrument and two other musical features. (3)
---
Instrument: solo cello / oboe / piano / violin (1). Features, any two: minor key; descending melody; legato; soft dynamics / p; thin texture; sustained notes; slow harmonic rhythm (2).
:::

::: q
In a comedy scene a character slips on a banana skin. Describe two features of music that would suit it. (2)
---
Any two: downward glissando (e.g. trombone/slide whistle) as he falls (Mickey-mousing); staccato bassoon/woodwind; pizzicato strings; a sudden loud crash/cymbal as he lands; jaunty dotted rhythm; major key; sudden pause.
:::

::: watch
- **Listing without linking.** "Minor key, tremolo, crescendo" scores little in the 10-mark answer. Every feature needs its effect on the scene ("which makes the audience feel...").
- **Vague terms.** "Gets louder" is weaker than "crescendo from pp to ff", and "the brass" is weaker than "a trumpet fanfare in rising 4ths".
- **Talking about the film, not the music.** Retelling the plot earns nothing. The marks are for musical features.
- **Covering only one mood.** If the question says "suspense **and** horror", the answer must show both, usually as a change through the extract.
- **Ignoring change.** Say what happens at the start, in the middle and by the end. Texture thickening, pitch rising and tempo speeding up are key marks.
- **Repeating earlier answers** when a part says "do not refer to your earlier answers", or giving the same feature twice in a 2-mark "describe two" item.
- **Loud and forte are not a change.** When a question asks how the music "becomes" more exciting, write crescendo or gets louder, not just "loud".
:::

::: test
A film opens on a submarine deep underwater as an enemy ship searches above. The music: begins in near-silence with a very low sustained note in the double basses and a quiet synthesised pulse like sonar; a slow, chromatic melody in the bass clarinet enters; high violins hold a quiet dissonant cluster; the pulse gradually speeds up, timpani enter with a crescendo, and the brass play loud stab chords; the music suddenly stops, leaving silence. Explain how the music creates the tension and danger of the scene. You should use musical vocabulary and refer to the musical elements and how they are used. (10)
---
Indicative content (credit features linked to the scene):
Melody: slow; chromatic; low register bass clarinet sounds dark and uncertain; little sense of a clear tune, suggesting hidden danger.
Harmony and tonality: dissonant cluster in the violins; unresolved chords; minor or unclear tonality creates unease.
Tempo and rhythm: no clear pulse at first (suspended time); synthesised pulse like sonar/heartbeat; gradual accelerando builds panic.
Dynamics: very quiet start makes the audience listen closely; crescendo with the timpani builds tension; loud stab chords are shocks; sudden silence leaves the audience holding their breath.
Texture: very thin at first (isolation, the deep sea); gradually thickens as the danger grows; climax then emptiness.
Sonority: low double-bass pedal gives weight and depth; synthesiser adds a cold, technological sound suggesting the submarine; high string cluster sounds eerie; brass and timpani sound aggressive.
Structure: gradual build to a climax, then an abrupt ending (the danger is unresolved).
