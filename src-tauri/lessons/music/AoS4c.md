## What it is

The spec names four kinds of popular music for Area of Study 4: **pop**, **rock and pop**, **bhangra** and **fusion** (of different styles). The unfamiliar song in Question 8 can come from any of them. You may be asked to recognise the style, to name typical instruments and rhythms, or to explain how a track **fuses** two styles, and a 10-mark answer on a fusion track is possible. The Appendix C list of styles also includes **rock, soul, hip-hop, reggae, ballad** and **pop**, so learn a fingerprint for each.

The app can't play audio. The topic's videos cover bhangra rhythms and instruments, and recognising fusion. As you listen, pick out one sound from each style that has been fused.

### Pop

**Pop** is commercial music aimed at a wide audience and at the charts. Typical features:
- **verse–chorus** structure with a catchy **hook** in the chorus;
- regular 4- and 8-bar phrases, usually in **4/4**, with a steady beat at a medium or upbeat tempo;
- simple, **diatonic** harmony and **standard chord progressions** (e.g. I–V–vi–IV), with a harmonic rhythm of about one chord per bar;
- a clear **lead vocal**, often with **backing vocals** in harmony; mostly **syllabic**;
- a strong role for **music technology**: synthesisers, drum machines, samples, auto-tune and heavy production;
- often a danceable beat, and songs about three to four minutes long.

A **ballad** is a slow, song-like piece, often a love song, usually with a lyrical melody and sustained accompaniment. In pop the **power ballad** builds from a quiet start to a big, loud final chorus.

### Rock and pop

**Rock** grew out of 1950s rock 'n' roll and the blues. It is louder and more guitar-based than pop. The spec's own list for rock and pop:
- clearly defined structures (verse–chorus, 32-bar, 12-bar);
- a chorus with a melodic **hook**, and verses with changing words;
- intro and outro, instrumental solos, improvisation and bridge ideas;
- **riffs**, clear chord progressions and **close harmony** in the vocals;
- guitar **licks** (short melodic figures), **strong bass lines**, sometimes a **walking bass**;
- **clear-cut rhythms** and a **strongly emphasised pulse** (backbeat on 2 and 4).

Rock sounds come from the **electric guitar** with **distortion** and **power chords**, a loud **drum kit**, a **bass guitar** often playing **driving** quavers, and an amplified vocal that may **belt**. **Soft rock**, the style of *Africa*, is gentler: melodic, polished, with keyboards and synthesisers and fewer distorted guitars. **Heavy rock** or metal is louder and more aggressive.

### Other styles to recognise (Appendix C)

| Style | Fingerprint |
|---|---|
| **Soul** | African-American style that grew from gospel and R&B. Passionate, **melismatic** vocals, backing vocals with **call and response**, a **horn section** playing stabs, and a tight groove |
| **Hip-hop** | **Rap** over beats; **samples** and **loops** from older records; drum machines; scratching; strong bass |
| **Reggae** | From Jamaica; **off-beat** chords (the "skank" on beats 2 and 4, or on the off-beats); heavy, melodic bass; relaxed tempo; the drums often stress beat 3 |
| **Ballad** | A slow, lyrical song, usually about love or loss |
| **Blues** | 12-bar blues chords, **blue notes**, swing or shuffle rhythm, call and response between the voice and guitar |

### Bhangra

**Bhangra** began as a **folk dance** music of the **Punjab** region (now divided between India and Pakistan), performed at harvest festivals and celebrations. In the late 20th century British Asian musicians in the **UK** fused it with **pop, disco, hip-hop and electronic dance music**, creating modern or **British bhangra**. The spec lists its features:

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Metre** | **4/4**, with each crotchet beat divided into **three** (a triplet or compound feel, so it swings and lilts) |
| **Chaal rhythm** | The characteristic bouncing, long-short dance rhythm played on the dhol, with a strong off-beat lift |
| **Dhol** | A large double-headed barrel drum hung from the shoulder and played with **two sticks**: a thicker, curved stick on the deep bass head and a thin cane on the high head. **Not the tabla**. |
| **Tempo** | **Fast**, with typical dance rhythms |
| **Vocals** | Melodies with a **small range** that often **fall at the end of a phrase**, with **ornamentation** (decorations, slides); **microtonal** intervals (smaller than a semitone); sung in **Punjabi** |
| **Shouts** | Energetic calls of **"Hoi!"** (and other shouts) from the performers |
| **Structure** | **Verse–chorus** |
| **Indian instruments** | **Tumbi** (a high-pitched, single-string plucked instrument), **sitar** (a long-necked plucked lute with a buzzing, resonant sound), and bowed strings such as the **sarangi** |
| **Riffs** | Short repeated instrumental patterns, often on the tumbi |
| **Modern features** | **Sampled bass lines**, **drum machines** and electronic beats, synthesisers, effects and music technology, fused with pop instruments |

The **tabla** is a pair of small hand drums from North Indian classical music. It is a different instrument, and mark schemes say so explicitly: dhol, not tabla.

### Fusion

**Fusion** is the combination of **two (or more) different musical styles** to create a new sound. The spec gives examples:
- **historical with modern**, e.g. a Baroque-style string part over a hip-hop beat, or classical crossover;
- **jazz, rock, classical or world** elements combined, e.g. **jazz-rock** (jazz fusion), **folk-rock**, **rap metal**, Latin pop;
- **contrasting current trends**, e.g. two popular styles together;
- **acoustic with electronic** timbres, e.g. a traditional instrument over synthesised beats.

**British bhangra** is itself a fusion of Punjabi folk with Western pop and dance music. *Africa* fuses American **soft rock** with features that evoke **African** music: pentatonic riffs, marimba-like sounds, layered percussion ostinatos and syncopation.

To explain a fusion, **sort the features by origin**. Say which features come from style A (e.g. dhol, chaal, tumbi, "Hoi!", microtonal ornaments) and which from style B (e.g. drum machine, synth bass, rap, 4/4 dance beat, verse–chorus), and then say how they are **combined**: layered at the same time, or alternating section by section.

### Listening strategy

1. **Drums first.** The dhol and chaal mean bhangra. A backbeat on the kit means pop or rock. Off-beat chords mean reggae. Programmed beats with samples mean hip-hop or electronic pop.
2. **Then the melody instrument and voice.** A tumbi or sitar, Punjabi vocals, ornaments and "Hoi!" point to bhangra. A distorted guitar solo points to rock. Melismatic gospel-style singing points to soul. Rap points to hip-hop.
3. **Then the technology**: synth bass, samples, effects.
4. For fusion, list the features in **two columns**, one for each style.

::: terms
- **Bhangra**: Punjabi folk dance music, fused in the UK with pop and dance music to create British bhangra.
- **Dhol**: a large double-headed barrel drum played with two sticks, the main drum of bhangra.
- **Chaal**: the bouncing, lilting long-short dance rhythm of bhangra, played on the dhol.
- **Tumbi**: a high-pitched, single-string plucked instrument from the Punjab, often playing riffs in bhangra.
- **Sitar**: a long-necked Indian plucked string instrument with a bright, buzzing, resonant tone.
- **Microtone**: an interval smaller than a semitone.
- **Fusion**: the combination of two or more different musical styles to create a new style.
- **Power chord**: a guitar chord of the root and 5th only (no 3rd), typical of rock.
- **Backbeat**: a strong accent on beats 2 and 4 of a 4/4 bar, the basic pulse of rock and pop.
- **Hook**: a short, catchy phrase, usually in the chorus, designed to be memorable.
- **Ballad**: a slow, lyrical song, usually about love or loss.
- **Soft rock**: a gentle, melodic, polished style of rock using keyboards and synthesisers, with little distortion.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the style** from its fingerprint features, and give the reasons if the question asks "how do you know?".
2. **Name instruments precisely**: dhol (not tabla), tumbi, sitar, drum machine, distorted electric guitar.
3. **Name the rhythm**: chaal (bhangra), backbeat or driving rock rhythm, swing, off-beat (reggae).
4. For **"describe the vocal line"**, give range (narrow or small), phrase shape (falls at the end), ornaments, microtones, syllabic or melismatic, language, shouts, call and response.
5. For **"explain how this track fuses..."**, sort the features into style A and style B, then show how they are combined and judge the effect (energy, danceability, a new sound).
6. In a 10-mark answer, cover **all the elements**: melody, rhythm and metre, tempo, harmony, texture, sonority and technology, and structure.
:::

::: example Recognising rock
A track has a distorted electric guitar playing power chords, a loud drum kit with snare on beats 2 and 4, a bass guitar playing constant quavers and a belted vocal. Name the style and give two features that support your answer. (3)

**Rock** (1). Any two: **distorted guitar**; **power chords**; **backbeat**; **driving** bass rhythm; loud dynamics; belt (2).
:::

::: example Explaining fusion
A track combines a sitar melody and tabla patterns with a drum machine, synth bass and a rapped verse. Explain why this is an example of fusion. (3)

It combines features of **Indian classical/traditional music**: the sitar and tabla (1). These are combined with features of **Western hip-hop / electronic music**: drum machine, synth bass and rap (1). Mixing two different styles **acoustic with electronic** creates a new sound, which is fusion (1).
:::

::: example Reggae
Describe two features of the rhythm and accompaniment in reggae. (2)

Chords played **off the beat** (the skank, often on beats 2 and 4) (1). A **heavy, prominent bass line** with a relaxed tempo (1). (Also: drums stressing beat 3.)
:::

::: watch
- **Tabla for bhangra.** The bhangra drum is the **dhol**. Writing tabla scores nothing.
- **"Indian music"** is too vague for a style. Say **bhangra** and back it with dhol, chaal and "Hoi!".
- **Calling every pop-with-something-else song "pop".** If there are two clear styles, the answer is **fusion**, and you should name both.
- **Treating rock as just "loud".** Give the evidence: distortion, power chords, riffs, backbeat, driving bass.
- **A fusion essay that lists features without sorting them.** Say which style each feature comes from and how they are combined.
- **Forgetting the metre subtlety.** Bhangra is written in 4/4 but each beat splits into three, which gives the lilting chaal.
:::
