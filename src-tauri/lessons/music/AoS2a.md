## What it is

**Texture** is how the lines of music are layered: how many parts are playing and how they relate to each other. The word comes from weaving. A texture can be **thin** (one or two strands) or **thick** (many), and the strands can move **together** or **independently**. Area of study 2, Music for Ensemble, is built around texture. The spec lists **monophonic, homophonic, polyphonic, unison, chordal, layered, melody and accompaniment, round, canon and countermelody**, and Appendix C adds **descant, 2-, 3- or 4-part textures, imitation, drone, Alberti bass, stab chords and walking bass**. Questions range from "name the texture" (1) through "describe how the texture changes" (2–4) to the 10-mark extended answer, which has been set on AoS2 (2023, a fight scene from *West Side Story*).

**The three big families**

| Texture | What you hear | Picture it | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monophonic** | a **single melodic line** with no accompaniment (one player, or several in unison) | one thread | plainchant, an unaccompanied solo, a solo drum or flute opening |
| **Homophonic** | a melody **supported by chords**; the parts move together or clearly accompany one tune | a tune on top of a cushion of chords | hymns, most pop songs, Classical piano music |
| **Polyphonic** (contrapuntal) | **two or more independent melodies at once**, each interesting in its own right | several threads woven together | Baroque fugue, a round, a canon, jazz with several improvisers |

**Types of homophony.**
- **Chordal** (sometimes called **homorhythmic**): all parts move in the **same rhythm**, like a hymn or a brass chorale.
- **Melody and accompaniment**: a tune with a separate accompaniment underneath. The accompaniment might be strummed chords, a broken-chord pattern, an **Alberti bass** or a **walking bass**.

**Textures and devices within those families**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Unison** | everyone plays or sings **the same notes at the same time** (strictly, at the same pitch; "in octaves" when the same notes sound in different octaves) |
| **Layered** | parts are added (or removed) one by one, each playing its own repeated idea, building up the sound in layers; common in minimalism, dance music and film |
| **Round** | a canon for voices at the same pitch, in which each part sings the whole tune and can start again (e.g. "London's Burning") |
| **Canon** | strict imitation: the same melody starts in each part at different times, so the lines overlap |
| **Imitation** | one part copies a short idea from another; it creates a polyphonic effect |
| **Countermelody** | a **second, different melody** played at the same time as the main tune |
| **Descant** | a decorative **higher** countermelody above the main tune, e.g. sopranos over the last verse of a hymn |
| **2-, 3-, 4-part texture** | the number of independent lines heard (e.g. a 4-part choir: soprano, alto, tenor, bass) |
| **Drone** | long held notes (usually tonic and fifth) underneath |
| **Stab chords** | short, loud, detached chords, often from brass, punctuating the music |
| **Walking bass** | a bass line moving steadily **in crotchets**, mostly by step, "walking" up and down; typical of jazz |
| **Call and response** | a phrase from one performer (the call) answered by another or by a group (the response) |
| **Antiphony** | two groups alternating, answering each other, often from different places |
| **Tutti** | "all": the whole ensemble playing together |

**Texture changes are what score.** Pieces rarely keep one texture throughout, and the most common exam request is to describe texture **with locations**. For example: "monophonic at the start (solo violin), then polyphonic in the middle as the melody enters in canon, becoming thicker and more layered at the end". In the 2022 paper, the mark scheme for a Romantic orchestral extract rewarded **monophonic or unison at the beginning**, **polyphonic or canon in the middle**, and **thicker, imitative or fugal at the end**, each with its location. In the 2025 big-band extract (*Sing, Sing, Sing*), the texture was credited as **thin at the start** (a soloist; the drum kit begins and ends the piece), **thick in the middle** (the full band) and **thin again at the end**.

**Why composers change texture.**
- **Contrast between sections.**
- **Drama**: a sudden thin texture exposes a single voice, and a sudden tutti overwhelms.
- **Clarity**: melody and accompaniment keeps the words clear in a song.
- **Excitement and building to a climax**: layering parts one by one increases tension.
- **Character**: a canon of voices overlapping can sound like a crowd or an argument.
These **effects** are what lift an extended answer into the top band.

**Texture and sonority together.** Questions often pair them: "Describe how texture and instrumentation are used (do not simply list instruments)". Say **who does what**:
- "trumpets play stab chords while the saxophones play the melody in unison";
- "the double bass plays a pizzicato walking bass under the piano's chords";
- "the full orchestra plays tutti for the climax".

**Listening strategy.** Use the topic's videos (P Dillon's texture revision, Music Learning Club's "Elements of Music 7 – Texture" and Baines Music's "What is Texture?"). For every clip, ask three questions in order:
1. **How many lines?** One line is monophonic or unison.
2. **Is there one tune or several?** One tune with support is homophonic; several independent tunes is polyphonic.
3. **Does it change?** Note the texture at the start, middle and end.
Listen for entries: when parts come in one at a time with the same tune, write "canon" or "imitation". When they add different ideas one at a time, write "layered".

::: terms
- **Texture**: the way musical lines are combined; how many parts there are and how they relate to each other.
- **Monophonic**: a texture of a single melodic line with no accompaniment.
- **Homophonic**: a texture in which a melody is supported by chords, with the parts moving together.
- **Polyphonic**: a texture of two or more independent melodic lines heard at once (also called contrapuntal).
- **Unison**: all parts performing the same notes at the same time.
- **Melody and accompaniment**: a texture with one clear tune supported by a separate accompaniment.
- **Layered texture**: a texture built up by adding separate parts or ideas one at a time.
- **Countermelody**: a second, independent melody played at the same time as the main melody.
- **Descant**: a decorative higher melody sung or played above the main tune.
- **Round**: a vocal canon at the same pitch in which each part sings the whole tune, entering in turn.
- **Stab chords**: short, sharp, detached chords used to punctuate the music, often by brass.
- **Walking bass**: a bass line moving steadily in crotchets, mostly by step, typical of jazz.
:::

::: method
1. **Count the lines** and decide on one line (monophonic or unison), one tune plus support (homophonic) or several tunes (polyphonic).
2. **Refine the term**: chordal or melody and accompaniment? Canon, round, imitation or countermelody? Layered? Descant?
3. **Track the changes** at the beginning, middle and end, and give a location for each point ("at the start", "when the chorus enters", "in the last four bars").
4. **For texture and instrumentation**, pair each instrument with its role and technique, never a bare list.
5. **For the 10-mark answer**, work through the elements (texture, sonority, dynamics, melody, harmony, rhythm, tempo, structure) and, for each feature, explain its **effect** on the scene or mood.
:::

::: example Name the texture
A choir sings a hymn. All four parts (soprano, alto, tenor and bass) move with the same rhythm, forming a chord on every syllable. Name the texture. (1)

Homophonic / chordal (accept homorhythmic) (1).
:::

::: example Describe texture changes
An extract opens with a solo violin playing a melody alone. In the middle, the flute plays the same melody while the violin plays a different tune at the same time. At the end, the whole orchestra plays together, with the violins playing the tune and the rest playing chords. Describe the use of texture, giving locations. (3)

At the start it is monophonic (solo violin) (1). In the middle it becomes polyphonic, as the violin plays a countermelody against the flute's tune (1). At the end it is homophonic, melody and accompaniment, with a thick tutti texture (1).
:::

::: example Canon or countermelody?
Explain the difference between a canon and a countermelody. (2)

In a canon every part plays the same melody but starts at a different time, so the lines overlap (1). A countermelody is a different melody played at the same time as the main tune (1).
:::

::: example Texture and instrumentation
In a big-band extract, the saxophones play the melody together, the trumpets play short, loud chords on off-beats, the double bass plucks steady crotchets moving by step, and the piano plays chords. Describe how texture and instrumentation are used. Do not simply list instruments. (3)

The saxophones play the melody in unison (1). The trumpets punctuate with syncopated stab chords (1). The double bass plays a pizzicato walking bass while the piano supplies chords, giving a homophonic melody-and-accompaniment texture (1).
:::

::: example Layering for effect
Explain how a layered texture can build excitement. (2)

Parts enter one at a time, each adding a new repeated idea, so the texture gradually thickens (1); the growing density and volume increase tension towards a climax (1).
:::

::: q
Name the texture of a single unaccompanied melody. (1)
---
Monophonic.
:::

::: q
Name the texture in which several independent melodies are heard at once. (1)
---
Polyphonic (accept contrapuntal).
:::

::: q
Name the high, decorative line sung above a hymn tune in its final verse. (1)
---
Descant.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between unison and homophony. (2)
---
Unison: all parts perform the same notes at the same time, so there is one line (1). Homophony: different notes forming chords under or with a melody (1).
:::

::: q
Describe a walking bass. (2)
---
Steady crotchets / on the beat (1); moving mainly by step, up and down the scale or chord (1). Accept pizzicato double bass as a typical sonority.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between call and response and antiphony. (2)
---
Call and response: a leader's phrase answered by another performer or group, often repeating or completing it (1). Antiphony: two groups alternating, answering each other, often from different positions (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"Thick" and "thin" alone rarely score**. Add a term (homophonic, polyphonic) or a location.
- **Give locations** when the question asks: beginning, middle, end, or a named section.
- **Homophonic is not "one sound"**: unison is one line, homophonic is chords.
- **Do not list instruments** in a texture-and-instrumentation answer; say what each does.
- **Canon is the same tune overlapping**; a **countermelody is a different tune**. Do not swap them.
- In the 10-mark answer, **every feature needs an effect**. Terms without explanation stay in the lower bands.
:::

::: test
The extract accompanies a stage scene in which two gangs meet and start to fight.
The scene begins with a quiet, low drum beat and a single muted trumpet playing a short, jagged motif. The motif is copied by the clarinets, then the strings, each entering before the last has finished. Layers are added one by one until the whole orchestra plays. Brass stab chords are heard on off-beats, accented and fortissimo. The music suddenly stops for a bar of silence, then the full orchestra returns in unison, playing a fast, descending run. At the climax, the tutti texture is thick and dissonant, with tremolo strings and loud cymbal crashes. The scene ends with one sustained, very quiet low note.
Explain how texture and sonority create the drama of the scene. Use musical vocabulary and refer to the musical elements. (10)
---
Mark in bands (AO4). 9–10: perceptive, detailed explanation of the features with critical judgement of how they portray the drama; accurate specialist vocabulary. 7–8: generally secure, competent account with general judgements; vocabulary mostly accurate. 5–6: inconsistent, some features identified with some judgement; some accurate vocabulary. 3–4: limited, elementary detail and few relevant judgements. 1–2: very limited detail and vocabulary. 0: nothing of value.
Indicative content:
- Texture: thin, almost monophonic opening (one muted trumpet over drum) creates suspense before the fight; imitation / canonic entries (clarinets, strings) suggest gang members joining and answering each other; layering thickens gradually as tension mounts; sudden silence creates shock and uncertainty; unison run gives aggressive unity; thick tutti at the climax represents chaos; final single low note is a thin, isolated ending suggesting the aftermath.
- Sonority: muted trumpet gives a tense, menacing colour; low drum as a heartbeat; brass stab chords, accented and off-beat, act like blows; tremolo strings create agitation; cymbal crashes add violence; low sustained note adds a dark, ominous end.
- Dynamics: quiet opening to fortissimo climax; sforzando / accents; sudden silence; very quiet end, a dramatic contrast.
- Melody and harmony: short jagged (disjunct) motif; fast descending run; dissonance creates conflict.
- Rhythm: off-beat accents / syncopation make it unpredictable; fast tempo in the fight.
- Judgements such as "the imitative entries make the instruments seem to argue, which mirrors the gangs confronting each other" reach the top band.
:::
