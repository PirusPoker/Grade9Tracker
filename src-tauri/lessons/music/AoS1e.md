## What it is

The **Badinerie** by **J. S. Bach** is the prepared extract (set work) for Area of study 1. Question 1 on the paper is always a 12-mark question on it. You are given part of the score, the extract is played three times, and you answer questions on bar numbers, chords, keys, cadences, ornaments, devices and the parts played by particular instruments. Because you can study the score in advance, this is the most "learnable" question on the paper. Every fact below comes from Eduqas's own background notes and analysis and from recent mark schemes.

**Background**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Composer** | Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750), a German composer of the **Baroque** era; other famous works include the Brandenburg Concertos, the Goldberg Variations, the St Matthew Passion and the Mass in B minor |
| **Work** | the **final movement** of **Orchestral Suite No. 2 in B minor**, BWV 1067, composed around **1738–39** |
| **The suite** | a collection of short pieces, mostly dances, played one after another. This suite has **seven** movements: Overture, Rondeau, Sarabande, Bourrée, Polonaise, Menuet and **Badinerie**, the last |
| **"Badinerie"** | an 18th-century name for a **quick, light, playful** movement in a suite |
| **Instrumentation** | solo **transverse flute** (held sideways, like the modern flute), **string orchestra** (1st violins, 2nd violins, violas, cellos, with double bass) and **harpsichord**. The score has five parts: flute, violin 1, violin 2, viola and cello |
| **Basso continuo** | the harpsichord player reads the cello line, plays it with the left hand and fills in chords with the right; the cello (and bass) and harpsichord together form the **continuo** |
| **Tempo** | **Allegro** (quick and lively), though not marked on the score |
| **Metre** | **2/4**, simple duple time, two crotchet beats per bar, throughout |
| **Dynamics** | mostly **forte**; very few markings on the score, which was typical of the period; some performances use **terraced dynamics** (sudden changes of volume) |
| **Texture** | **homophonic** (melody and accompaniment) |

**Form and key scheme: binary form, AABB.** The movement has two sections, each repeated. It starts with an **anacrusis** (it begins on beat 2 of an incomplete bar 0), so each section ends on beat 1 and the next begins on beat 2.

| Section | Bars | Length | Keys |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 0 (beat 2) to 16 (beat 1) | 16 bars | **B minor** (tonic) → **A major** around bars 7–8 → ends in **F sharp minor** (the **dominant minor**) |
| **B** | 16 (beat 2) to 40 (beat 1) | 24 bars | begins in **F sharp minor** → **E minor** → **D major** (the **relative major**, confirmed by a perfect cadence) → G major → D major → back to **B minor** |

Section B is the longer section: it travels through more keys before coming home. Section A moves from the tonic to the dominant, and Section B does the opposite, which is typical Baroque binary form.

**The two motifs.** The whole movement is built from two short ideas, and both begin with an anacrusis.
- **Motif X** is a **descending B minor arpeggio (broken chord)** in a **quaver and semiquaver** rhythm, played first by the flute. It is entirely **disjunct**.
- **Motif Y** is an **ascending semiquaver figure** mixing **broken chords and stepwise (conjunct) movement**.

**Bar-by-bar map** (full-movement bar numbers):

| Bars | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–2 | flute: motif X (first statement), B minor |
| 2–4 | flute: motif Y (first statement) |
| 4–6 | flute: motif X again |
| 6–8 | **cellos** take motif X, slightly modified (last crotchet becomes a quaver and two semiquavers); the music moves to **A major**; this is the first phrase of a **sequence** |
| 8–10 | cellos: motif X again with a new ending (four semiquavers); moves to **F sharp minor**; the answering phrase of the sequence |
| 10–12 | flute: motif Y with a modified ending (four semiquavers replace the last two quavers) |
| 12–16 | flute: motif Y extended and developed; Section A closes in **F sharp minor** |
| 16–18 | flute: motif X in F sharp minor |
| 18–20 | motif X modified, **inverted** (now ascending) and **divided between two instruments**; moves to **E minor** |
| 20–22 | flute: motif X, moving to **D major** (relative major) |
| 22–24 | flute: motif Y, a transposed copy of bars 10–12 |
| 24–28 | flute: transposed copy of bars 12–16; D major confirmed by a **perfect cadence** |
| 28–30 | cellos: motif X as in bars 6–8; first phrase of a second **sequence** |
| 30–32 | cellos: answering phrase; returns to the tonic, **B minor** |
| 32–34 | motif X developed into a **conversation between flute and 1st violin**, decorated with an **appoggiatura** and rapid **demisemiquavers** |
| 34–36 | flute: motif Y with a new beginning (a quaver and four demisemiquavers) |
| 36–38 | motif X modified, **inverted** and **divided between two instruments** again |
| 38–40 | cellos close Section B in B minor with a modified motif X (altered shape, less active rhythm) |

**Ornaments and devices (by bar).** You may be asked to "give a bar number where there is…".
- **Trills**: bars 8, 10, 15, 27, 30 and 32.
- **Appoggiaturas**: bars 33 and 40.
- **Sequences**: bars 6–10 and 28–32, both in the cellos.
- **Suspensions**: bars 8, 10 and 32.
- **Demisemiquavers**: in the flute and violin conversation from bar 32.

**Harmony**
- **Diatonic** throughout. **Perfect and imperfect cadences** are clearly presented, and both sections end with a **perfect cadence**.
- Chords are often in **inversion**, with occasional **V7 in third inversion** (the seventh in the bass).
- A **Neapolitan sixth** in **bar 35**: a major chord on the flattened second note of the key, so **C major** in B minor, usually in first inversion.
- **Suspensions** (a note held over a change of chord, creating a brief clash) in bars 8, 10 and 32.
- Chords set on real papers: bar 3, last quaver, **F#7** (F# A# C# E), which is **V7** in B minor; bar 6, last quaver, **B minor**; in Section B, **A7** (V7 of D major) and **B minor**.

**What each part does.**
- **Flute**: the main melody (motifs X and Y), a two-octave range, both conjunct and disjunct, quavers and semiquavers, trills, sequences, a detached (staccato) style.
- **Cello**: the bass line (continuo), but it also takes the melody in the sequences. It is mainly disjunct, moves in octaves, outlines chords and supplies the cadences.
- **1st violin**: joins in occasionally, most clearly in the conversation from bar 32.
- **2nd violin and viola**: fill in the harmony with less busy lines, mostly quavers, often in **rhythmic unison** with each other. The 2025 mark scheme described the viola as mostly disjunct, on the beat, outlining chords, detached, with rests, and with a range of about a 9th.
- **Harpsichord**: realises the chords over the cello bass (continuo).

**Exam tip on bar numbers.** A paper may print only one section, and a Section B extract may be renumbered from bar 1. Always use the bar numbers printed on the paper, and know the features well enough to find them.

**Listening strategy.** Watch the topic's videos with the score if you can get it (JHA Music, Langdon Academy, BBC Bitesize's Badinerie analysis, and Miss McCall's practice questions). Follow the flute line and point to each X and Y as it arrives; hear the cello take over for the sequences at bars 6 and 28; hear the flute and violin conversation at bar 32. Knowing where things happen is what lets you answer in the three minutes of silence after the last playing.

::: terms
- **Badinerie**: an 18th-century name for a quick, light, playful movement in a suite.
- **Suite**: a set of short pieces, usually dances, played one after another; Bach's Suite No. 2 has seven movements.
- **Transverse flute**: a flute held sideways to the player's right, as the modern flute is.
- **Basso continuo**: a bass line played by a low instrument such as the cello, with a keyboard (here harpsichord) filling in the chords.
- **Binary form**: a two-section form (AB), here with each section repeated (AABB).
- **Motif X**: the Badinerie's opening idea, a descending B minor broken chord in quavers and semiquavers.
- **Motif Y**: the Badinerie's second idea, an ascending semiquaver figure mixing broken chords and steps.
- **Appoggiatura**: an ornament note a step above or below the main note, which takes part of the main note's value (usually half).
- **Neapolitan sixth**: a major chord on the flattened second note of the key, usually in first inversion; C major in B minor, at bar 35.
- **Suspension**: a note held on while the harmony changes beneath it, causing a brief clash that then resolves.
- **Dominant minor**: the minor key built on the fifth note of a minor key; F sharp minor is the dominant minor of B minor.
- **Relative major**: the major key that shares a minor key's key signature; D major is the relative major of B minor.
:::

::: method
1. **Use the minute before the first playing** to read every part of the question and find each named bar on the printed score.
2. **For "give a bar number" items**, use the score: look for the symbol (tr for a trill, a small grace note for an appoggiatura, a natural sign, beams with three lines for demisemiquavers). Your memory of the bar map tells you where to look.
3. **For chords**, spell the notes on the given beat, find the root, and name the chord in full ("B minor", "F#7"). In B minor, F#/F#7 is V/V7; in D major, A/A7 is V/V7.
4. **For keys**, use the key scheme you have learned (A section: B minor → A major → F sharp minor; B section: F sharp minor → E minor → D major → G major → B minor) and confirm with accidentals.
5. **For "describe the X part" (2 marks)**, give two different features: pitch movement, rhythm values, role (melody, bass, harmony), articulation, range, a device.
6. **For "Baroque features"**, pick from harpsichord, continuo, ornaments, sequences, binary form, terraced dynamics, homophonic texture, flowing melodic lines, tonic–dominant key relationships.
:::

::: watch
- It is **binary (AABB)** and in **2/4**. Ternary, rondo, 3/4 or 4/4 lose easy marks.
- **Name keys and chords in full**: "F sharp minor", not "F sharp"; "B minor", not "B".
- Section A ends in the **dominant minor (F sharp minor)**, not F sharp major and not D major.
- **D major** is the **relative major**; it arrives in Section B, not Section A.
- In "describe the part" questions, **give two different features**. "Plays quavers" and "plays short notes" count once.
- **Motif X falls; motif Y rises.** In Section B, X is inverted, so it rises there.
:::

::: test
Explain why the Badinerie is in binary form rather than ternary form. (2)
---
It has two sections (A and B), each repeated, AABB (1); Section A ends away from the tonic, in F sharp minor, and Section B returns to B minor, rather than the opening section returning after a contrasting middle section (1).
