## What it is

**Form** (or **structure**) is the plan of a piece: how its sections are ordered, which ones come back and which ones are new. Every form is built from two forces. **Repetition** makes a piece hang together and helps the listener remember it. **Contrast** stops it becoming dull. Sections are labelled with letters: a new section gets a new letter, and a returning section keeps its letter. A section that comes back changed gets a small number or a dash (A1, or A').

The spec names six forms for AoS1: **binary, ternary, minuet and trio, rondo, variation and strophic**. On the paper, form turns up as a 1-mark "underline the structure", as 2–4 mark "describe how Section B / Version 2 is different" items, and as a way into the set work (the Badinerie is binary).

| Form | Letter plan | Keys | What to listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Binary** | **AB**, each half usually repeated: **AABB** | A starts in the tonic and **modulates** to a related key (the dominant in a major key; the relative major or dominant minor in a minor key). B starts there, may pass through other keys, and **returns to the tonic** | two halves of similar character, often built from the same motifs; the first half ends "away from home" |
| **Ternary** | **ABA** (or ABA1) | A in the tonic and complete in itself; B in a **contrasting key**; A returns in the tonic | a clear contrast in the middle (new tune, key, texture, sometimes tempo), then the opening comes back exactly or slightly varied |
| **Minuet and trio** | Minuet ‖:a:‖:b:‖, Trio ‖:c:‖:d:‖, Minuet again (usually without repeats) | trio often in a related key | a graceful dance in **3/4** at a moderate tempo; the trio is lighter, often with fewer instruments; overall ABA, so **ternary**, with each part itself **binary** |
| **Rondo** | **ABACA** (sometimes ABACABA) | A always in the tonic; the **episodes** (B, C) in contrasting keys | a catchy main theme (the refrain) keeps returning between contrasting episodes; often a lively last movement |
| **Theme and variations** | **A A1 A2 A3 …** | usually the tonic; one variation may switch major/minor | a simple theme, then repeats in which melody, rhythm, harmony, key, tempo, texture or instrumentation are changed while the theme stays recognisable |
| **Strophic** | **AAA** | one key | a song in which every verse has the same music with new words, as in hymns and folk songs |

**Other structural words** (Appendix C). **Introduction**: opening material before the main theme. **Coda**: a closing section added after the form is complete, usually confirming the tonic key. **Link** or **bridge**: a short passage joining two sections. **Phrasing**: regular (equal lengths, e.g. 4 + 4) or irregular. A piece with no repeated sections is **through-composed**. Mark schemes accept this for film extracts, but it is not one of the six AoS1 forms.

**Binary against ternary.** Both have two kinds of music, so the difference is in the ending. In **binary**, the A section is not complete in itself (it ends in a new key), and B finishes the journey home. In **ternary**, A is complete (it ends in the tonic), B is a real contrast, and the return of A is heard as a clear repeat of the opening. The Badinerie is binary: Section A (16 bars) goes from B minor to F sharp minor, and Section B (24 bars) comes back to B minor, with both halves repeated (AABB).

**Minuet and trio in detail.** The minuet was a courtly dance of the 17th and 18th centuries, and Classical composers used it as the third movement of symphonies and string quartets. The **minuet** has two repeated sections. The **trio** has two new repeated sections, often more lightly scored; the name comes from the older habit of writing this middle part for three instruments. Then the minuet is played again, usually without its repeats (the score says *Minuet da capo*). So it is ternary on the large scale and binary inside each part.

**How variations are made.** Knowing the tools of variation is also how you answer "describe four ways Version 2 differs from Version 1". Composers vary a theme through:
- **melody**, by adding ornaments, runs or decoration, or moving it to a different register;
- **rhythm**, with dotted rhythms, triplets, syncopation or diminution;
- **harmony**, with new chords or added chromatic notes;
- **tonality**, by switching between major and minor;
- **tempo** and **metre**;
- **texture**, by adding a countermelody or imitation, or thinning out to a solo;
- **instrumentation** and **dynamics**;
- **style**, for example turning a Classical theme into a jazz or Latin version.
The June 2025 paper compared a Beethoven orchestral movement with a Latin-style piano arrangement of it. The mark scheme credited points such as "faster", "louder", "fewer or different instruments", "syncopated", "different style", "blue notes", "wider pitch range" and "thicker texture". Every one of these is an element-based comparison.

**Listening strategy.** Use the topic's videos (Miss McCall's form and structure, Music Learning Club on structure, OpenLearn on the minuet and trio, and Dave Conservatoire on theme and variations). While a piece plays, jot a letter each time a new section starts, and write the same letter when a section returns. Mark a key change with "new key" and an instrumentation change with the instrument. After two hearings the letter string (AABB, ABA, ABACA) usually tells you the form. In comparison questions, divide your answer lines by element (tempo, dynamics, instruments, texture, key, rhythm, melody), and never write two points on the same element in the same direction.

::: terms
- **Form (structure)**: the overall plan of a piece: the order of its sections and which of them repeat.
- **Binary form**: a two-part form (AB), each half usually repeated; A moves to a related key and B returns to the tonic.
- **Ternary form**: a three-part form (ABA) in which a contrasting middle section is followed by a return of the opening.
- **Minuet and trio**: a Classical dance movement in 3/4: minuet, contrasting trio, then minuet again; ternary overall, binary within each part.
- **Rondo**: a form in which a main theme (A) keeps returning between contrasting episodes: ABACA.
- **Episode**: a contrasting section in a rondo, often in a different key, between returns of the main theme.
- **Theme and variations**: a form in which a theme is stated and then repeated several times with changes (A A1 A2 A3).
- **Strophic**: a song form in which every verse is sung to the same music (AAA).
- **Coda**: a closing section added to the end of a piece to round it off, usually in the tonic.
- **Da capo**: "from the head"; an instruction to go back and repeat from the beginning.
- **Repetition and contrast**: the two basic principles of form; repetition gives unity, contrast gives variety.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the section plan printed on the paper** (e.g. "Section 1 (8 bars), Section 2 (8 bars), Section 3 (8 bars)") before the first hearing.
2. **Label each section with a letter** as it plays: same music, same letter; changed music, the same letter with a number (A1); new music, a new letter.
3. **Match the letter string to a form**: AB/AABB binary; ABA ternary; ABACA rondo; A A1 A2 variations; AAA strophic; minuet–trio–minuet ternary.
4. **Use keys as evidence**: binary A ends away from the tonic; ternary and rondo main themes come back in the tonic.
5. **For "describe how B differs from A"**, write one point per element (tempo, dynamics, instruments, texture, tonality, rhythm, melody) and use comparative words: "faster", "minor instead of major", "thinner texture".
6. **For a 10-mark answer on form**, explain how each return and each contrast is made, and what effect it has on the listener.
:::

::: example Name the form
A piano piece has three sections. The first, in C major, is a gentle tune with a clear perfect cadence at its end. The second is in A minor with a different melody and a thicker texture. The third is exactly the same as the first. Name the form. (1)

Ternary (ABA) (1).
:::

::: example Binary or ternary?
Explain one difference between binary and ternary form. (2)

In binary form the first section usually ends in a new key, so it is not complete on its own (1); in ternary form the first section ends in the tonic and returns after a contrasting middle section (ABA rather than AB) (1).
:::

::: example Describe differences between versions
Version 1 of a piece is played by a string orchestra at a steady moderate tempo, quietly, in a minor key. Version 2 is played by a solo piano, a double bass and hand percussion. It is faster and louder, with syncopated Latin-American rhythms and some improvised decoration of the melody. Other than naming the piano, describe four ways in which Version 2 is different from Version 1. (4)

Faster tempo (1). Louder dynamics (1). Different instrumentation: double bass and untuned percussion instead of strings (1). Syncopated, Latin-American style rhythms (1). (Also creditable: improvised or altered melody; thicker or busier texture.)
:::

::: example Minuet and trio
Describe the structure of a Classical minuet and trio. (3)

The minuet has two sections, each repeated (binary) (1). The trio follows with two new repeated sections, usually lighter in texture and often in a related key (1). The minuet is then played again, usually without repeats, making the whole movement ternary (ABA) (1).
:::

::: example Rondo
A lively final movement opens with a cheerful tune in D major (A). A new tune in A major follows (B), then the opening tune returns. Next comes a stormy section in B minor (C), before the opening tune returns once more to end the piece. Give the letter plan and name the form. (2)

ABACA (1); rondo (1).
:::

::: q
Name the form described by the letters ABACA. (1)
---
Rondo.
:::

::: q
Name the form in which every verse of a song has the same music. (1)
---
Strophic.
:::

::: q
In binary form in a major key, to which key does the A section usually modulate? (1)
---
The dominant (e.g. C major to G major). Accept "dominant". Do not accept "a different key" alone.
:::

::: q
Give three ways a composer could vary a theme in a set of variations. (3)
---
Any three: change the melody by adding ornaments / change the rhythm, e.g. dotted notes or triplets / change the harmony / change from major to minor / change the tempo / change the metre / change the texture, e.g. add a countermelody / change the instrumentation / change the dynamics.
:::

::: q
Explain the purpose of a coda. (1)
---
A closing section added after the main structure to round off the piece / confirm the tonic key / give a sense of ending.
:::

::: q
A piece in G major has sections A (G major), B (D major), A, C (E minor), A. Name the keys of the episodes as relationships to the tonic. (2)
---
B: dominant (1); C: relative minor (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Minuet and trio** is ternary overall but binary within each part. Say both if the question wants detail.
- **Binary is not "two different tunes"**: the halves often share motifs. The key scheme (away, then home) is the defining feature.
- In "how does B differ from A" answers, **compare**: "B is louder" scores, but "B is loud" may not, because A might have been loud too.
- **Do not repeat an element** in the same answer: "faster" and "quicker tempo" are one point.
- **Do not refer to earlier answers**: if a question says so, points already credited elsewhere will not score again.
- **Rondo's A always returns in the tonic**. If the returning section is in a new key, think again.
:::

::: test
Explain the role of modulation in binary form. (2)
---

::: test
A song has four verses. Verses 1, 2 and 3 have the same music with different words. In verse 4 the key rises by a tone and a countermelody is added. Explain whether the song is strictly strophic. (2)
---
Mostly strophic, as verses share the same music with new words (1); but verse 4 is varied (key change / added countermelody), so it is modified strophic / not strictly strophic (1).

::: test
Read the description of a piece and answer the question below.
A Classical piano piece in F major opens with a cheerful 8-bar tune (A) in balanced 4-bar phrases over an Alberti bass. A contrasting section (B) follows in C major, with faster semiquaver runs and a thinner texture. The opening tune returns exactly. A second contrasting section (C) is in D minor; it is slower, legato and more chromatic, with a low, sustained bass. The opening tune then returns a final time, now decorated with trills and extra runs, and a short coda of repeated perfect cadences in F major ends the piece fortissimo.
Explain how the composer uses repetition and contrast to create the structure of this piece. Use musical vocabulary and refer to the musical elements. (10)
---
Mark in bands (AO4). 9–10: perceptive, detailed explanation of how repetition and contrast build the form, with critical judgements about their effect; accurate vocabulary throughout. 7–8: generally secure and competent account with general judgements; mostly accurate vocabulary. 5–6: inconsistent; identifies some features with some judgement; some accurate vocabulary. 3–4: limited, elementary detail, few judgements. 1–2: very limited, little accurate vocabulary. 0: nothing of value.
Indicative content:
- Structure: rondo, ABACA plus coda; the refrain A returns in the tonic each time, giving unity and a sense of homecoming.
- Repetition: A returns exactly the first time, so the listener recognises the main theme; balanced 4-bar phrases and the Alberti bass make it memorable and typically Classical.
- Contrast in B: modulation to the dominant (C major), faster semiquaver runs, thinner texture; contrast of rhythm and texture keeps interest while staying in a closely related key.
- Contrast in C: the relative minor (D minor), slower tempo, legato, chromatic harmony and a sustained bass; the strongest contrast, a change of mood before the final return.
- Final A is varied (A1): ornaments, trills and runs; repetition with variation avoids predictability and builds towards the end.
- Coda: repeated perfect cadences and fortissimo dynamics confirm F major and give a decisive ending.
- Judgements such as "placing the most distant contrast (minor key, slower tempo) just before the last return makes the return of the major-key refrain sound brighter" reach the top band.
:::

::: test
Explain why a composer might use a strophic form for a folk song or hymn. (2)
---
The same music for each verse is easy for performers and audiences to learn and remember / sing along (1); it lets the words carry the story while the music stays familiar (1).
:::
