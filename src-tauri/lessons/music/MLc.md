## What it is

A **chord** is two or more notes sounding together. The chords used in almost all the music on this paper are **triads**, three notes stacked in 3rds. The spec asks you to use **Roman numerals for chords I, ii, iii, IV, V and vi in a major key**, and **contemporary chord symbols** such as C, Dm, Em, F, G(7) and Am. Exam items include "Give the names of the chords marked *" (Badinerie, 2025: F#7 and B minor), "Fill in the missing chord names" (*Africa*, 2023: B, D#m, G#m, B/F#), "Underline the position of this chord", "Underline the chord which is not heard: I, IV, vi" and "Give the bar number where the chord changes".

### Building a triad
Take any note as the **root**, then add the note a **3rd** above and the note a **5th** above, skipping one letter name each time: C–E–G, D–F–A, G–B–D. Which kind of triad it is depends on the size of the 3rds:
- **Major triad:** a major 3rd (4 semitones) with a minor 3rd on top. C–E–G sounds bright.
- **Minor triad:** a minor 3rd (3 semitones) with a major 3rd on top. A–C–E sounds darker.
- **Diminished triad** (for reference): two minor 3rds. B–D–F sounds tense; it is chord vii in a major key.

### Roman numerals and chord symbols
Number each note of the major scale from 1 to 7 and build a triad on it using only notes of the key. The pattern is the same in every major key:

| Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quality | major | minor | minor | major | major | minor |
| Function | **tonic** | supertonic | mediant | **subdominant** | **dominant** | submediant |

**Upper-case numerals are major chords, and lower-case numerals are minor.** Writing "II" for a minor chord, or "iv" for the major subdominant, is marked wrong.

**Chord symbols** name the root and add "m" for minor: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am. A "7" adds the minor 7th above the root, so **G7** is G–B–D–F. A **slash** shows the bass note: **C/E** means a C chord with E in the bass.

| Key | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | V7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C major** | C | Dm | Em | F | G | Am | G7 |
| **G major** | G | Am | Bm | C | D | Em | D7 |
| **D major** | D | Em | F#m | G | A | Bm | A7 |
| **A major** | A | Bm | C#m | D | E | F#m | E7 |
| **E major** | E | F#m | G#m | A | B | C#m | B7 |
| **F major** | F | Gm | Am | B flat | C | Dm | C7 |
| **B flat major** | B flat | Cm | Dm | E flat | F | Gm | F7 |
| **E flat major** | E flat | Fm | Gm | A flat | B flat | Cm | B flat 7 |
| **A flat major** | A flat | B flat m | Cm | D flat | E flat | Fm | E flat 7 |
| B major (*Africa*'s verse) | B | C#m | D#m | E | F# | G#m | F#7 |

### Primary and secondary chords
- **Primary chords (I, IV and V)** are the three major chords. Between them they contain every note of the scale, so they can harmonise any simple major melody. They define the key and form most **cadences** (V–I perfect, IV–I plagal, ending on V imperfect).
- **Secondary chords (ii, iii and vi)** are the minor chords. They add colour and variety, and each shares two notes with a primary chord. Chord **vi** can stand in for I, which is how the **interrupted cadence** (V–vi) tricks the ear. Chord **ii** often leads to V (ii–V–I).

### The dominant 7th
**V7** is chord V with a fourth note added, a minor 7th above the root. In C major it is **G–B–D–F**. The F clashes with the G and wants to fall to E, while the B (the leading note) wants to rise to C. That double pull makes **V7–I** the strongest perfect cadence. In a **minor** key, V is still a **major** chord because it contains the raised 7th. In B minor, for example, V7 is **F#7 = F sharp, A sharp, C sharp, E**, the chord that the 2025 paper asked for on the last quaver of bar 3 of the Badinerie.

### Inversions and slash chords
The **bass note** (the lowest note) decides the position:

| Position | Bass note | Label | Example in C |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Root position** | root | I or **Ia** | C with C in the bass |
| **First inversion** | 3rd | **Ib** | C/E |
| **Second inversion** | 5th | **Ic** | C/G |
| **Third inversion** (7th chords only) | 7th | **V7d** | G7/F |

A slash chord tells you the inversion straight away: the note after the slash is the bass. **B/F#** is a B major chord (B, D sharp, F sharp) with F sharp, its 5th, in the bass, so it is **second inversion**: chord **Ic** in B major. This was the answer in the 2023 *Africa* question. Inversions give a smoother, stepwise bass line and make the harmony feel less final than root position. **Ic–V–I** is the classic approach to a final cadence.

### Naming a chord from its notes
1. Write the letter names out and **rearrange them into 3rds** (every other letter): E, C, G becomes C–E–G.
2. The **bottom note of the stack of 3rds is the root**. That gives the chord's name.
3. Check the **3rd** for major or minor, and look for a **7th**.
4. Check the **lowest sounding note** to find the inversion.
5. If the question is about a key, turn the root into a **Roman numeral**.

For example, the notes F sharp, A sharp, C sharp and E stack as F#–A#–C#–E: root F sharp, a major 3rd and a minor 7th, so the chord is **F#7**, which is V7 in B major or B minor.

### Chord progressions and harmonic rhythm
A **chord progression** is a sequence of chords. Common ones:
- **I–IV–V–I**: hymns, folk music, early rock and roll.
- **I–V–vi–IV**: countless pop songs.
- **vi–IV–I–V**: the same loop started on vi, as in *Africa*'s chorus in A major (F#m–D–A–E).
- **I–vi–IV–V**: 1950s doo-wop and ballads.
- **ii–V–I**: jazz.
- **12-bar blues** (I–I–I–I, IV–IV–I–I, V–IV–I–I): see AoS2d.

**Which chord fits a melody note?** Each note belongs to three triads. In C major the note G is in C (I), Em (iii) and G (V). The strongest choice is usually a primary chord, and at a cadence the bass will tell you which.

"Give the bar where the chord changes" is really a **harmonic rhythm** question: listen for the bass note moving.

### Listening strategy
Use the topic videos (Liberty Park Music on primary triads, Michael New on Roman numerals, Music Matters on inversions):
1. **Play the triads** of C, G and F majors on a keyboard app and sing each root. Hear I, IV and V as "home", "moving away" and "wanting to come home".
2. **Follow the bass line**: in most music the bass plays the chord's root, so a change of bass note usually means a change of chord.
3. **Major or minor:** listen for bright (I, IV, V) against darker (ii, iii, vi) chords.
4. **With a set-work score,** write the chord names above every bar you can work out from the notes on the page. The Badinerie and *Africa* chord questions reward this preparation directly.

::: terms
- **Triad**: a three-note chord made of a root with the notes a 3rd and a 5th above it.
- **Root**: the note on which a chord is built, which gives the chord its name.
- **Roman numeral**: a label for a chord by the scale degree of its root; upper case major, lower case minor.
- **Chord symbol**: a letter name for a chord, with m for minor and 7 for an added seventh, e.g. Am, G7.
- **Primary chords**: chords I, IV and V, the three major triads of a major key.
- **Secondary chords**: chords ii, iii and vi, the minor triads of a major key.
- **Dominant 7th**: chord V with a minor 7th added above the root, e.g. G–B–D–F in C major.
- **First inversion**: a chord with its 3rd in the bass, labelled b (Ib), e.g. C/E.
- **Second inversion**: a chord with its 5th in the bass, labelled c (Ic), e.g. C/G.
- **Slash chord**: a chord symbol with a named bass note after a slash, e.g. B/F#.
:::

::: method
1. **Find the key** first (key signature and final chord; see MLb).
2. **List the six chords of the key** (I ii iii IV V vi) with their letter names, using the pattern major-minor-minor-major-major-minor.
3. **For a chord given as notes,** stack them in 3rds, find the root, check major or minor and any 7th, then find the bass note for the inversion.
4. **Write the answer in the form asked for:** a chord name ("F sharp 7", "B minor") or a Roman numeral ("V7", "vi"). Match upper and lower case to the chord's quality.
5. **For a slash chord,** the note after the slash is the bass. Compare it with the chord's root, 3rd and 5th to name the inversion.
6. **For a missing chord in a progression,** look for a standard pattern (I–V–vi–IV, vi–IV–I–V, 12-bar blues) and check that the chord fits the melody note.
:::

::: example Name the chord from its notes
The chord marked * contains the notes F sharp, A sharp, C sharp and E. Give the chord name. (1)

Stacked in 3rds: F#–A#–C#–E. The root is F sharp, with a major triad plus a minor 7th, so the chord is **F#7** / F sharp dominant 7th (1).
:::

::: example Roman numeral in G major
Give the Roman numeral of the chord E minor in G major. (1)

G major's scale is G(1) A(2) B(3) C(4) D(5) E(6), and the minor chord on the 6th degree is **vi** (1).
:::

::: example Fill in a progression
A song in B major uses a four-bar chord pattern: bar 1 chord I, bar 2 chord iii, bar 3 chord vi, bar 4 chord I in second inversion. Give the chord symbols. (2)

**B, D#m, G#m, B/F#** (2). One mark for any two or three correct.
:::

::: example Position of a slash chord
The chord B/F# appears in B major. State whether it is in root position, 1st inversion or 2nd inversion, and explain how you know. (2)

**2nd inversion** (1): F sharp is the **5th** of the B major chord (B–D#–F#), and it is in the bass (1).
:::

::: example Which chord is not used

**(c) vi** (1). C is I, F is IV and G is V, so Am (vi) is not used.

::: watch
- **Upper case = major, lower case = minor.** "II" for a minor chord is wrong.
- A **chord symbol** needs the "m" if the chord is minor. "D" is D major; D–F–A is **Dm**.
- The bass note after the slash is **not** the chord's root. B/F# is a B chord, not an F sharp chord.
- **V is major even in a minor key**, because of the raised 7th: in B minor, V7 is F#7 with A sharp.
- To find a root, **stack the notes in 3rds** first. The lowest printed note may be an inversion.
- Write the chord in the form asked for: "chord name (e.g. D major)" and "Roman numeral" are different questions.
:::
