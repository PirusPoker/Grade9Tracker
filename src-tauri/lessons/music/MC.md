## What it is

**Musical contexts** are the circumstances in which music is created and heard: **why** it was written, **who** it was for, **where** it was performed and **when and where** in history and culture it belongs. The spec lists three areas:
1. **The purpose and intention** of composers, performers and those who **commission** music.
2. **The effect of the occasion, audience and choice of venue** on how music is composed and performed.
3. **How music is created, developed and performed in different social, historical and cultural contexts.**

Context is rarely a whole question on its own. It appears inside the other questions: "Underline the period in which this music was composed", "Name the style", "Give two features typical of Baroque music", "In this scene a nervous young woman learns to dance: explain how the music describes this scene", "Underline the year the song was released". The golden rule is that **a context point must be tied to a musical feature**. "It was written for dancing" becomes a scoring answer only when you add "so it has a steady pulse, a clear dance rhythm and short, balanced phrases".

### Purpose and intention
| Who | Their purpose | How it shows in the music |
|---|---|---|
| **Composer** | to express a feeling, tell a story, show off skill, fulfil a job | choice of key, instruments, form; programme music describes a story or picture |
| **Performer** | to interpret the notes: tempo, dynamics, phrasing, ornaments, rubato, improvisation | two performances of the same piece can differ greatly, as when a jazz singer improvises or an orchestra takes a slower tempo |
| **Commissioner** (patron, church, film studio, broadcaster, advertiser, festival) | pays for music with a specific brief: an occasion, a length, forces, a mood | a film cue must last exactly as long as the scene; a coronation anthem needs grandeur; an advert needs a short, catchy hook |

A **commission** is a request, usually paid, for a piece written to a brief. Film music is almost always commissioned (AoS3a). The director and composer agree where music is needed, and the composer writes to the exact timing of the pictures. In the Baroque period, musicians were often employed by a **court** or a **church** and wrote whatever their employer needed each week: dances, suites, cantatas and music for services.

### Occasion, audience and venue
| Context | Effect on the music |
|---|---|
| **Church or cathedral** | a long echo (reverberation) favours slow-moving harmony and sustained notes; organ and choir; sacred words in the liturgy |
| **Aristocratic court (Baroque and Classical)** | music for entertainment, dancing and dining: suites of dances, minuets; chamber groups and small orchestras; the composer's style had to please the patron |
| **Home and small room** | **chamber music** for a few players, often talented amateurs; intimate dynamics; the string quartet and the sonata (AoS2b) |
| **Public concert hall (from the late 18th century)** | paying audiences and larger halls led to larger orchestras, louder dynamics, virtuoso soloists and dramatic contrasts in the Romantic era |
| **Outdoor celebration** | loud wind, brass and percussion that carry in the open air; for example, Handel's *Music for the Royal Fireworks* used large wind and brass forces |
| **Theatre** | musicals and opera: a **pit orchestra** below the stage, songs that tell the story, **underscore** under spoken scenes, singers projecting over the band (belt) |
| **Cinema and television** | music fitted to the picture, **leitmotifs** for characters, mood set within seconds; the audience hears it but should not notice it too much |
| **Club and dance floor** | a steady, fast beat, repeated loops, strong bass, long tracks for dancing |
| **Stadium and festival** | **amplification**, big riffs, singalong choruses, simple powerful rhythms, visual spectacle |
| **Recording studio** | multi-tracking, effects, overdubs and editing: the recorded version can contain things no live band could play at once |
| **Religious or community celebration** | music tied to ritual and dance. Bhangra, for example, began as Punjabi harvest-festival dance music and later fused with Western pop in Britain (AoS4c) |

**Audience** matters too. A children's film needs clear, memorable themes. A dance crowd needs an unbroken beat. A concert audience will sit through a long, developing symphony. A radio single needs a hook within the first few seconds and a length of about three to four minutes.

### Social, historical and cultural contexts
Music reflects the society and technology of its time:
- **Instruments develop.** The piano replaced the harpsichord, valves transformed brass in the 19th century, and electric instruments and amplification appeared in the 20th.
- **Recording and technology** made popular music a mass product, and later allowed sampling, synthesisers and home production.
- **Cultures meet.** Jazz grew from African-American traditions combined with European harmony; bhangra fused Punjabi folk with Western pop in Britain; *Africa* is an American soft-rock song that borrows the sound of African music.

### Styles checklist
The spec's list of musical styles is a recognition list. For each style, learn three identifying features.

| Style | Recognition features |
|---|---|
| **Western Classical Tradition** | the umbrella term for art music from roughly 1650 to 1910, covering Baroque, Classical and Romantic |
| **Baroque** (c.1600–1750) | harpsichord and **basso continuo**; **terraced dynamics**; ornaments; sequences; one mood per movement; polyphony and imitation |
| **Classical** (c.1750–1820) | balanced, **regular phrases**; **homophony**; **Alberti bass**; the piano; a larger orchestra with clarinets; clear cadences |
| **Romantic** (c.1820–1910) | large orchestra with more brass and percussion; **chromatic harmony**; **rubato**; very wide dynamics; programme music; emotional, dramatic |
| **Chamber music** | a small group, one player per part: string quartet, sonata, trio |
| **Jazz** | **swing**, **improvisation**, syncopation, blue notes, walking bass, saxophones, trumpets, rhythm section |
| **Blues** | 12-bar blues chords, **blue notes**, call and response, emotional lyrics, guitar and voice |
| **Musical theatre** | songs that tell a story, pit orchestra, **belt** singing, solos, duets and chorus numbers |
| **Film music** | descriptive, mood-setting, leitmotifs, orchestral or electronic, timed to the picture |
| **Rock** | distorted electric guitars, **power chords**, riffs, loud drum kit with backbeat, powerful vocals |
| **Soul** | gospel-influenced passionate vocals, often with **melisma**; brass section; strong bass and drums; call and response with backing singers |
| **Hip-hop** | **rap** over a beat; **samples** and loops; drum machines; turntable scratching |
| **Reggae** | Jamaican; guitar or keyboard chops on the **off-beat**; a prominent bass line; relaxed tempo |
| **Ballad** | a slow, lyrical song, often about love or a story; expressive vocals |
| **Pop** | catchy hooks, verse-chorus structure, clear chord progressions, polished production |
| **Bhangra** | **dhol** and the **chaal** rhythm, tumbi riffs, shouts of "Hoi!", fused with pop beats and technology |
| **Fusion** | a combination of two different styles, for example classical with Latin, or folk with electronic |
| **Minimalism** | short repeated cells, layered ostinati, very gradual change, phasing |

### Strategy for the whole exam
The Component 3 paper lasts **about 1 hour 15 minutes**, has **eight questions of 12 marks** (96 in total), and is led entirely by a recording.
- Questions 1–2 are on AoS1, 3–4 on AoS2, 5–6 on AoS3 and 7–8 on AoS4.
- **Question 1 (Badinerie) and question 7 (*Africa*) are the prepared extracts,** printed with a score.
- **Before each extract** you get reading time, 30 seconds or a minute. Use it to underline the key words in every part, and to note the order of the sections (Version 1, Version 2 and so on).
- **Each extract is played three or four times,** with 30-second pauses. Tick the box each time you hear it so you know how many hearings are left. Aim to answer the 1-mark items on the first two hearings and use the later ones to check and to collect detail for the describe questions.
- **After the last playing there is a silence,** usually 2 or 3 minutes and longer for the extended answer, to finish writing. Use it for the questions about the printed score, which do not need the sound.
- **One question has a 10-mark extended answer** ("Explain how the music creates ..."). Plan it quickly by elements: melody, harmony and tonality, rhythm and tempo, texture, dynamics, sonority, structure. Link every feature to the effect.
- **One question includes a dictation** (5 marks, one per note). See MLd.
- **Underline or tick exactly the number asked for.** Two answers underlined when one is asked for scores zero, and on a one-word answer only the first word counts.
- **Never leave a gap.** There is no penalty for a wrong guess.

### Listening strategy
Watch Miss McCall's walk-through of a real Eduqas paper and Andrew Moxon's video on answering a listening question from the topic's video list. Then practise with a timer on any piece: 30 seconds to read three questions, three playings with 30-second gaps, and 2 minutes to finish. For context, ask of every piece you hear: **what was it for, where was it performed, and when?** Then find the musical evidence for each answer.

::: terms
- **Commission**: a request, usually paid, for a composer to write music to a particular brief or occasion.
- **Patron**: a wealthy person or institution that employs or pays a composer, as courts and churches did.
- **Purpose**: what the music is for, such as worship, dance, entertainment, a film scene or a ceremony.
- **Venue**: the place where music is performed, whose size and acoustics affect how it is written.
- **Audience**: the people the music is written for, whose expectations shape its length, style and complexity.
- **Occasion**: the event for which music is created, e.g. a coronation, wedding, festival or film premiere.
- **Chamber music**: music for a small group with one player per part, originally for performance in a room.
- **Amplification**: making sound louder electronically, which made stadium rock and large outdoor gigs possible.
- **Programme music**: instrumental music that describes a story, scene or picture, typical of the Romantic era.
- **Fusion**: a style that combines two or more different musical genres into something new.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the context in the question:** purpose, occasion, audience, venue, period or style.
2. **Link context to features:** "because it was for (X), the music has (feature), which (effect)".
3. **For "name the period or style",** decide from two or three features (instruments first, then harmony and texture), not from a feeling that it "sounds old".
4. **For "give two features typical of (style)",** give two separate, specific musical features from the checklist.
5. **For "explain how the music describes the scene",** connect a change in the music to a change in the scene, for example hesitant, then confident.
6. **In the exam as a whole,** use the reading time, tick each playing, answer the quick items first and use the final silence for score-based questions.
:::

::: example Venue and music
Explain how performing in a large cathedral could affect the way music is composed. (2)

The cathedral has a long **reverberation** / echo, so fast notes and quick chord changes would blur together (1). Composers therefore write **slow-moving harmony, sustained notes** and often use the organ and choir, whose sound fills the space (1).
:::

::: example Features of Baroque music
This piece was composed in the Baroque era. Describe two musical features typical of Baroque music. (2)

Any two: use of **harpsichord** / **basso continuo** (1); **ornaments** / trills (1); **sequences** (1); **terraced dynamics** (1); long, flowing melodic lines with **one mood** (1); binary form (1).
:::

::: example Describe the scene
A film scene shows a nervous young woman learning to dance in a new style. The music starts with a hesitant, quiet melody with pauses, then a middle section in which a confident tango rhythm becomes louder and fuller. Explain how the music describes this scene. (1)

The **hesitant, quiet opening reflects her nervousness, and the confident, louder tango section reflects her growing confidence** as she learns the steps (1).
:::

::: example Commission and effect
Explain two ways in which a commission for a film scene limits what the composer can write. (2)

The music must fit the **exact timing** of the scene and its cuts, so phrase lengths and climaxes are fixed by the pictures (1). The composer must match the **mood** the director wants and must not distract from dialogue, so dynamics and texture are often held back under speech (1).
:::

::: example Stadium rock
Explain how amplification affected the music written for stadium rock concerts. (2)

Amplification allowed a small band to fill a huge venue, so songs were built on **loud distorted guitars, powerful drums and big riffs** (1), with simple, repeated **singalong choruses** that a large crowd can join in with (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why chamber music usually uses a small number of players. (2)
---
It was written to be performed in a room / home / small space (1), often by amateurs or for a patron's private entertainment, so one player per part suited the space and the intimate audience (1).

::: q
Give two features that would identify a piece as reggae. (2)
---
Any two: off-beat guitar or keyboard chords / skank (1); prominent bass line (1); relaxed / moderate tempo (1); 4/4 with emphasis away from beat 1 (1); Jamaican origin / patois lyrics (1).
:::

::: q
In which year was *Africa* by Toto released as a single? (1)
---
1982 (it was recorded in 1981 for the album Toto IV).
:::

::: q
Explain how a composer might write differently for a public concert hall than for a small room at court. (3)
---
Any three: larger orchestra / more players to fill the hall (1); louder dynamics and wider dynamic range (1); virtuosic solo writing to impress a paying audience (1); more dramatic contrasts (1); longer works (1); whereas court music was often lighter entertainment or dance music for a small group (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Tie every context point to a musical feature.** "For a church" alone scores nothing.
- **Don't guess the period from "sounds old".** Name the evidence: harpsichord, Alberti bass, chromatic harmony.
- Baroque and Classical are commonly confused. **Harpsichord, continuo and terraced dynamics** mean Baroque; **Alberti bass and balanced phrases** mean Classical.
- **Answer exactly what is asked.** Underline one option if one is asked; two underlined scores zero.
- **Use every hearing.** Tick the boxes so you know when the last playing is coming.
- In "explain how the music describes the scene", show a **link**: musical feature, then what it represents.
:::
