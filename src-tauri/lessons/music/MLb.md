## What it is

A **key signature** is the group of sharps or flats written at the start of every line of music, straight after the clef. It tells the performer which notes are **always** sharp or flat in that piece, and so it tells you the **key**. The spec requires you to **read and write key signatures up to four sharps and four flats**. In the exam this appears as "Give the full name of the key of the extract (for example, C major)", "Name the relative minor of G major", "Write in the missing accidental" and "Name the key to which the music modulates". Always give the key **in full**: the 2023 mark scheme accepted "E major" and refused "E".

### The order of sharps and flats
Sharps are always added in the same order, and flats in the reverse order:
- **Sharps: F C G D** (A E B), remembered as "**F**ather **C**harles **G**oes **D**own **A**nd **E**nds **B**attle".
- **Flats: B E A D** (G C F), which spells "BEAD", or "**B**attle **E**nds **A**nd **D**own **G**oes **C**harles' **F**ather".

A key with three sharps always has F sharp, C sharp and G sharp, never any other three.

On the treble stave the sharps zig-zag. F sharp sits on the top line, C sharp in the third space, G sharp in the space above the stave and D sharp on the fourth line. The flats go B flat on the middle line, E flat in the top space, A flat in the second space and D flat on the fourth line. The bass clef uses the same zig-zag shapes on the matching letter names.

### The keys you need
| Key signature | Major key | Relative minor | Raised 7th in the minor |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | **C major** | **A minor** | G sharp |
| 1 sharp: F# | **G major** | **E minor** | D sharp |
| 2 sharps: F# C# | **D major** | **B minor** | A sharp |
| 3 sharps: F# C# G# | **A major** | **F sharp minor** | E sharp |
| 4 sharps: F# C# G# D# | **E major** | **C sharp minor** | B sharp |
| 1 flat: Bb | **F major** | **D minor** | C sharp |
| 2 flats: Bb Eb | **B flat major** | **G minor** | F sharp |
| 3 flats: Bb Eb Ab | **E flat major** | **C minor** | B natural |
| 4 flats: Bb Eb Ab Db | **A flat major** | **F minor** | E natural |

**Quick tricks**
- **Sharp keys:** the **last sharp is the 7th note** (the leading note) of the major key, so go **up a semitone**. The last sharp in two sharps is C sharp, and up a semitone is D, so the key is D major.
- **Flat keys:** the **second-to-last flat is the major key**. With three flats (B, E, A) the second-to-last is E flat, so the key is E flat major. Learn "one flat = F major" by heart.
- **Relative minor:** count **down three semitones** from the major tonic, or go to the **6th note** of the major scale. D major goes to B minor, and E flat major to C minor.
- **Relative major:** count **up three semitones** from the minor tonic. The pair **share the same key signature**.
- Moving round by 5ths, each new sharp key is a **perfect 5th higher** (C, G, D, A, E) and adds one sharp. Each new flat key is a **perfect 5th lower** (C, F, B flat, E flat, A flat) and adds one flat. This is the **circle of fifths**, and it is why the dominant key has one more sharp (or one fewer flat) than the tonic.

### Major or relative minor?
Every key signature fits **two keys**, a major and its relative minor. To decide which:
1. **Look for the raised 7th of the minor key.** In the minor, the 7th note is usually sharpened (the harmonic minor) to make a leading note, and it appears as an **accidental**. A piece with one sharp that keeps using **D sharp** is in **E minor**, not G major. With two flats, **F sharp** points to **G minor**. With three flats, **B natural** points to **C minor**.
2. **Check the last note** and the final chord, which is almost always the tonic. The first note or chord is often the tonic too.
3. **Check the sound:** bright means major, darker means minor (see MEb).

### Accidentals within the key
An **accidental** inside the music (a sharp, flat or natural not in the key signature) means one of three things:
- the **raised 7th** of a minor key;
- a **modulation**: new sharps suggest a move to the dominant, while naturals cancelling sharps suggest a move towards the subdominant or relative minor;
- **chromatic** decoration.

"Write in the missing accidental" questions usually test the raised 7th, or a note that must match the chord underneath.

### Keys in the set works
- **Badinerie:** two sharps. **B minor** is shown by the recurring **A sharp**, and it modulates to **F sharp minor**, whose raised 7th is E sharp.
- ***Africa*:** the verses are in **B major**, which has five sharps, one beyond the spec's list, but you should know it for the set work. The choruses are in **A major** (three sharps).

### Listening strategy
Key recognition is mostly a reading skill, but it links to your ears. Use the topic videos (Brad Harrison Music and MusicTheoryAcademy on key signatures, and Music Matters on working out the key of a piece):
1. **Drill the table** until you can go from signature to key and back in two seconds each way. Test yourself with flashcards in both directions.
2. **When a score is printed in the exam,** name its key during the reading time, before the music starts.
3. **While listening,** check major or minor against your reading: if a two-sharp melody sounds dark and keeps landing on B, it is B minor.
4. **Listen at the cadence** for the home note, and hum it. It should match the note you worked out from the page.

::: terms
- **Key signature**: the sharps or flats at the start of each stave, applying to every note of those letter names.
- **Order of sharps**: F, C, G, D, A, E, B; sharps are always added to a key signature in this order.
- **Order of flats**: B, E, A, D, G, C, F; the reverse of the order of sharps.
- **Relative minor**: the minor key with the same key signature as a major key, found three semitones below its tonic.
- **Relative major**: the major key with the same key signature as a minor key, found three semitones above its tonic.
- **Leading note**: the 7th note of a scale, a semitone below the tonic, which pulls strongly up to it.
- **Raised 7th**: the sharpened 7th degree used in minor keys, shown by an accidental, e.g. G sharp in A minor.
- **Accidental**: a sharp, flat or natural sign written in the music that is not part of the key signature.
- **Circle of fifths**: the arrangement of keys a perfect fifth apart, each adding one sharp or one flat.
:::

::: method
1. **Count the sharps or flats** and remember the order (F C G D or B E A D).
2. **Find the major key:** for sharps, go up a semitone from the last sharp; for flats, take the second-to-last flat (one flat = F major).
3. **Find the relative minor:** go down three semitones, the 6th note of the major scale.
4. **Decide major or minor:** look for the raised 7th as an accidental, then check the final note or chord.
5. **Write the key in full**, including "sharp" or "flat" and "major" or "minor": "F sharp minor", never "F#" or "F sharp".
6. **For a missing accidental,** work out whether it is the minor key's leading note or a note of the chord at that point.
:::

::: example Key from a signature
A melody has a key signature of three sharps, contains no accidentals and ends on A. Give the full name of the key. (1)

Three sharps is A major or F sharp minor. There is no E sharp (the raised 7th of F sharp minor), and it ends on A, so the key is **A major** (1).
:::

::: example Spotting the minor
A melody has a key signature of two flats. It often uses F sharp and ends on G. Give the full name of the key. (1)

Two flats is B flat major or G minor. F sharp is the raised 7th of G minor and the melody ends on G, so the key is **G minor** (1).
:::

::: example Sharps in a key
Name the sharps in the key signature of E major, in the order they are written. (1)

**F sharp, C sharp, G sharp, D sharp** (1).
:::

::: example Relative minor
Name the relative minor of E flat major. (1)

E flat down three semitones (D, D flat, C) gives **C minor** (1). Both keys have three flats.
:::

::: example Missing accidental
A melody in A minor rises G–A at its final cadence. The G has no sign in front of it. Which accidental is missing, and why? (2)

A **sharp** is missing: the note should be **G sharp** (1), the **raised 7th / leading note** of A minor, which leads up by a semitone to the tonic A (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why every key signature can represent two different keys. (2)
---
A major key and its relative minor share the same notes / key signature (1); e.g. C major and A minor both have no sharps or flats, so the tonic and the raised 7th are needed to decide (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Write the key in full:** "B minor", "E flat major". A single letter such as "E" was refused by the examiners.
- **Don't forget "sharp" or "flat"** in the key name: F sharp minor is not F minor.
- A key signature fits **two** keys. Check for the **raised 7th** accidental before choosing the major.
- The raised 7th of **E flat major's** relative minor (C minor) is a **B natural**, shown by a natural sign, not a sharp.
- Sharps go in the order **F C G D**. Writing C sharp alone as a key signature is impossible.
- The **dominant** key is not the **relative** key: D major's dominant is A major, and its relative minor is B minor.
:::
