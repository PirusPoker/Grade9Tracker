## What it is

**Tonality** and **harmony** are often asked together ("Describe how tonality and harmony are used", 3 marks), but they are different things.
- **Tonality** is the **key feel** of the music: which note sounds like home, and whether the music is major, minor or something else.
- **Harmony** is the **chords**: which notes sound together under the melody, how they move from one to the next, and how quickly they change.

Think of tonality as the map and harmony as the route taken across it.

### Tonality
| Tonality | What you hear | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Major** | built on the major scale; the 3rd above the home note is four semitones | bright, happy, confident, triumphant |
| **Minor** | built on the minor scale; the 3rd is three semitones, and the 7th is often raised | sad, serious, dark, dramatic, mysterious |
| **Pentatonic** | the melody and chords use only five notes (degrees 1, 2, 3, 5, 6) | open, simple, folk-like; no strong pull to a leading note |
| **Modal** (useful extra) | an older scale that is neither major nor minor | ancient, folk or film "otherworld" colour |
| **Atonal / unclear tonality** (useful extra) | no key centre at all | unsettling; used for horror and chaos in film |

The **tonic** is the home note and home chord of the key. A piece usually starts and almost always ends there. The music feels finished when it returns to the tonic.

**Modulation** is a **change of key** during a piece. The spec names two destinations:
- **Modulation to the dominant**: the key a perfect 5th above the tonic. C major goes to G major; D major goes to A major. In a minor key, the dominant key is usually minor too: the Badinerie's Section A moves from B minor to **F sharp minor**.
- **Modulation to the relative major or relative minor**: the key that shares the same key signature. A minor and C major are relatives; so are B minor and D major, and E minor and G major. The relative minor is three semitones **below** the major tonic, and the relative major is three semitones **above** the minor tonic.

The **subdominant** key (a perfect 4th up) is another common destination. You can hear a modulation as a change in the "home" feeling, often confirmed by a cadence in the new key. On a score you see it as **new accidentals** (G major moving to D major gains C sharp) or accidentals that are cancelled.

When you describe tonality, say **where** it changes. For example: "begins minor, becomes major in the middle, ends minor", or "minor, modulating to the relative major at line 3". A minor piece that ends on a **major** tonic chord uses a **tierce de Picardie** (a "Picardy third"). It is not on the spec list, but the examiners have credited it.

### Harmony
**Chords** are built by stacking notes a 3rd apart: a **triad** has a root, a 3rd and a 5th. Chords are numbered by the scale degree of their root (see MLc).
- **Primary chords** are **I** (the **tonic**), **IV** (the **subdominant**) and **V** (the **dominant**). These three major chords can harmonise almost any simple major melody.
- **Secondary chords** are **ii, iii and vi**, which are minor in a major key. They add colour and variety. Chord vi is especially common in pop.
- The **dominant 7th** (V7) is chord V with an added 7th, for example G–B–D–F in C major. The added note makes it pull strongly back to I.
- An **inversion** is a chord with a note other than the root in the bass: first inversion has the 3rd in the bass, second inversion the 5th.

**Diatonic** harmony uses only notes that belong to the key: clear, stable and predictable. **Chromatic** harmony uses notes from outside the key: rich, colourful, unstable, and typical of Romantic music and film scores.

**Consonance** is notes that sound settled together. **Dissonance** is notes that clash, such as a semitone or tritone sounding at once. Dissonance creates tension and horror. A Baroque suspension or appoggiatura is a brief dissonance that then resolves.

A **chord progression** or **chord sequence** is a series of chords. Standard ones include I–IV–V–I (hymns, folk, rock and roll), I–V–vi–IV (countless pop songs), vi–IV–I–V (*Africa*'s chorus) and the 12-bar blues (AoS2d).

**Harmonic rhythm** is **how often the chords change**. It is not the same as the speed of the melody.
- **Slow** harmonic rhythm is one chord every bar, or one every two bars. It sounds calm and spacious, and it is common in pop verses. *Africa*'s verse has one chord per bar.
- **Fast** harmonic rhythm is two or more chords per bar. It sounds busy and driving, and it is typical of Baroque music and approaching cadences.

The real paper gives three options, such as "one chord each bar / one chord every two bars / two chords each bar". Count how many beats each chord lasts.

**Drone**: two notes, usually the **tonic and the 5th**, held or repeated **throughout** a piece or section. It is found in bagpipe music, Indian classical music, folk music and some film scores.

**Pedal (pedal note)**: one note held or repeated, usually **in the bass**, while the harmonies above it change and sometimes clash with it.
- A **tonic pedal** sits on the home note.
- A **dominant pedal** sits on note 5 and builds expectation before a return to the tonic.
- An **inverted pedal** is held at the **top** of the texture instead.

A drone underlies everything; a pedal is usually a passage-length device.

**Power chords**: just the **root and the 5th**, often with the octave added, and **no 3rd**. They are therefore neither major nor minor. They are played on distorted electric guitar in rock, heavy metal and punk, and they sound thick, aggressive and powerful.

**Cadences** are the two-chord endings of phrases. They are taught fully in AoS1d, but you need the four names here: **perfect** (V–I, finished), **imperfect** (ends on V, unfinished), **plagal** (IV–I, the "Amen" ending) and **interrupted** (V–vi, a surprise).

**Extended chords** (with added 7ths, 9ths and so on) and **chords in inversion** are also creditable harmony points. The examiners accepted them in the 2025 jazz question.

### Describing tonality and harmony together
When a 3-mark question names **two elements**, the mark scheme caps you at 2 if you only write about one. Aim for at least one tonality point and one harmony point:
- **Tonality:** major or minor; where it modulates, and to which key; whether it ends in a different key; whether it is diatonic, chromatic or pentatonic.
- **Harmony:** the harmonic rhythm (slow or fast); primary or secondary chords; a named cadence; pedal or drone; dissonance; power chords; extended chords; inversions.

### Listening strategy
Use the topic videos (HPA Music on tonality and on harmony, P Dillon on tonality, Music Learning Club on harmony and tonality) and practise:
1. **Major or minor?** Hum the home note at the end, then the 3rd above it. If the 3rd is bright, it is major; if it is darker, it is minor. Check the start and the end separately.
2. **Harmonic rhythm:** tap the beat and count how many beats pass before the bass note changes. The bass usually moves with the chord.
3. **Pedal or drone:** listen underneath. Is one low note sticking while everything else moves?
4. **Modulation:** does the music settle somewhere new? Does the final cadence land on a different note from the opening?
5. **Dissonance:** does a chord make you wince? Say where it is and what it does, for example that it builds tension before the climax.

::: terms
- **Tonality**: the key feel of music, whether major, minor, pentatonic or with no clear key.
- **Tonic**: the home note and home chord of a key, chord I.
- **Dominant**: the fifth note of the scale and the chord built on it, chord V.
- **Subdominant**: the fourth note of the scale and the chord built on it, chord IV.
- **Modulation**: a change of key during a piece, often to the dominant or the relative major or minor.
- **Relative minor**: the minor key sharing a major key's signature, three semitones below its tonic (A minor for C major).
- **Diatonic**: using only the notes of the current key, with no chromatic alterations.
- **Dissonance**: notes sounding together that clash and create tension, needing resolution.
- **Harmonic rhythm**: the rate at which the chords change, e.g. one chord per bar.
- **Pedal**: a note held or repeated, usually in the bass, while the harmony changes above it.
- **Drone**: notes, usually the tonic and fifth, sustained or repeated throughout a passage or piece.
- **Power chord**: a chord of just the root and fifth (no third), played on distorted electric guitar in rock.
:::

::: method
1. **Separate the two elements.** Tonality is the key (major, minor, modulation); harmony is the chords (progression, harmonic rhythm, cadences, pedal, dissonance).
2. **Give locations.** "Begins in a minor key, becomes major in the middle, returns to minor at the end" can earn two or three marks, where "minor" earns one.
3. **Name the modulation by its relationship:** dominant, subdominant, relative major or relative minor. "It changes key" alone rarely scores.
4. **For harmonic rhythm,** count the beats per chord and choose the closest option.
5. **In a two-element 3-mark question,** write at least one point on each element. The mark scheme caps a one-element answer at 2.
6. **Link to effect where asked:** "chromatic, dissonant chords create tension", "a dominant pedal builds expectation before the return".
:::

::: example Name the modulation
A piece in D major moves to a new key halfway through; the new key signature has three sharps. Name the key and its relationship to D major. (2)

**A major** (1), the **dominant** (1). A is the 5th note of D major, and A major has three sharps: F sharp, C sharp and G sharp.
:::

::: example Describe the tonality
The music begins in a minor key, moves into a major key for the middle section, and returns to the opening minor key for the final section. Describe the tonality. (1)

**It begins minor, becomes major and ends minor** / it modulates from minor to major and back to minor (1).
:::

::: example Harmonic rhythm
In the verse of a song in 4/4, the chords are: bar 1 C, bar 2 Am, bar 3 F, bar 4 G. Describe the harmonic rhythm. (1)

**One chord each bar** / slow harmonic rhythm (1).
:::

::: example Pedal against drone
Explain the difference between a pedal and a drone. (2)

A **pedal** is a single note held or repeated (usually in the bass) for a passage while the chords above it change and may clash with it (1). A **drone** is usually two notes (the tonic and 5th) sustained or repeated throughout the piece or section, as a constant background (1).
:::

::: example Two elements in three marks
In a jazz extract, the music begins in E minor, moves to a major key in the middle, and ends in A minor. At first each chord lasts two bars; later the chords change twice in every bar, many with added 7ths, and each phrase ends V–I. Describe how tonality and harmony are used. (3)

Any three, with at least one from each element: begins minor / E minor (1); major in the middle (1); modulates and ends in a different key, A minor, the subdominant (1); slow harmonic rhythm at the start becoming fast, two chords per bar (1); extended chords / 7th chords (1); perfect cadences (1).
:::

::: q
Name the relative minor of G major. (1)
---
E minor (do not accept "E").
:::

::: q
Name the dominant key of F major. (1)
---
C major.
:::

::: q
Give the three primary chords of a major key by Roman numeral and name. (3)
---
I tonic (1); IV subdominant (1); V dominant (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by power chords and why they are not major or minor. (2)
---
Chords made of the root and 5th (and octave) only, typically on distorted electric guitar (1); there is no 3rd, and the 3rd is the note that makes a chord major or minor (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between diatonic and chromatic harmony. (2)
---
Diatonic harmony uses only notes of the key (1); chromatic harmony adds notes from outside the key / sharps, flats or naturals not in the key signature (1).
:::

::: q
A piece in C minor ends on a C major chord. Give the name of this device. (1)
---
Tierce de Picardie / Picardy third.
:::

::: q
A horror film cue has low sustained notes, clusters of notes a semitone apart and no clear home key. Describe how tonality and harmony create fear. (3)
---
Dissonance / clashing semitones creates tension and unease (1); no clear key / atonal / ambiguous tonality means the listener cannot predict what comes next (1); low sustained notes or a pedal give a menacing, static background (1); chromatic harmony (1). Any three, maximum 2 if only one element.
:::

::: watch
- **Name the key relationship,** not just "it changes key": dominant, relative major, relative minor.
- Give **full key names**: "B minor", not "B". The mark schemes reject a bare letter.
- **Harmonic rhythm** is the speed of the **chords**, not of the melody.
- A **pedal** is usually in the bass and the harmony moves over it; a **drone** is a constant background of usually two notes.
- **Power chords** are root and 5th only, so don't call them major or minor.
- In "tonality **and** harmony" questions, answering only one element caps the mark at 2 out of 3.
- Relative keys share a **key signature**; dominant keys are a **5th higher**. Don't mix them up.
:::
