## What it is

**Chamber music** is music for a **small ensemble with one player on each part**, originally performed in a room ("chamber") of a private house or palace rather than in a church, theatre or concert hall. It is one of the three repertoires named for AoS2, Music for Ensemble, and the spec picks out three groupings to know: the **string quartet**, the **basso continuo** and **sonatas**. In the exam, chamber music turns up as "name the ensemble", "underline the number of instruments", "name the string technique" and "describe the texture", and a basso continuo can appear in the Badinerie question too.

**Who played it, and where.** In the Baroque and Classical periods, chamber music was written for **patrons**: aristocrats who employed musicians or commissioned works for their households. Haydn, for example, spent many years employed by the Esterházy family. It was also written for **amateurs**, educated people who played at home for their own enjoyment, so many pieces were designed to be satisfying but playable. There is **no conductor**: the players lead each other by listening and eye contact. In the **Romantic** period chamber music moved increasingly into **public concert halls**, and groupings became more varied (the piano quintet, the horn trio). These context points connect to the MC (Musical contexts) topic.

**Naming the group by number**

| Number of players | Name | Common examples |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | **duet** (duo) | violin and piano; two pianists |
| 3 | **trio** | **piano trio** (piano, violin, cello); string trio; horn trio (horn, violin, piano) |
| 4 | **quartet** | **string quartet** (two violins, viola, cello); piano quartet |
| 5 | **quintet** | **piano quintet** (piano + string quartet); wind quintet (flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon, horn) |
| 6, 7, 8 | sextet, septet, octet | string sextet; string octet |

**The string quartet** is the most important Classical chamber group: **two violins, a viola and a cello**. A quartet is usually in **four movements**, with the first normally in **sonata form**. Other movements often include a slow movement and a minuet and trio (or scherzo). The traditional roles are:
- **1st violin**: often the melody, and the highest part;
- **2nd violin**: harmony, inner parts, sometimes doubling or answering the 1st violin;
- **viola**: inner harmony, a darker tone, a lower middle range;
- **cello**: the bass line, sometimes a singing melody in its tenor register.
Good quartet writing shares the material out. A theme passes from instrument to instrument (**imitation**), so the texture is often described as a **conversation** between equals.

**Basso continuo** (Baroque, about 1600–1750) is the accompaniment behind almost all Baroque ensemble music. It takes **two jobs**:
1. a **bass instrument** plays the written bass line (cello, viola da gamba or bassoon);
2. a **chordal instrument** (harpsichord, organ or lute) plays the bass line too and **improvises chords** above it, guided by figures (numbers) written under the bass notes, called **figured bass**.
The continuo gives the music its steady harmonic foundation. In the Badinerie, the cello plays the bass line and the harpsichord reads the cello part and fills in the chords.

**Sonatas.** A *sonata* is a piece to be played, not sung. It changes meaning with the period.

| Period | Sonata type | Performers |
|---|---|---|
| **Baroque** | **trio sonata** | **two melody instruments** (e.g. two violins, or flute and violin) **plus basso continuo**: three written lines, often four players |
| **Baroque** | **solo sonata** | **one melody instrument plus continuo** |
| **Classical** | **piano sonata** / **sonata for instrument and piano** | solo piano, or e.g. violin and piano; usually three or four movements, the first often in sonata form |

**String techniques** (Appendix C). These are named often, and each is worth 1 mark.

| Technique | What it is and how it sounds |
|---|---|
| **Arco** | played with the **bow** (the normal way) |
| **Pizzicato** | **plucked** with the finger; short, dry, light notes |
| **Tremolo** | **very rapid repeated** bow strokes on one note; shimmering or agitated |
| **Double stopping** | playing **two strings at once**, so two notes sound together |
| **Vibrato** | a slight, rapid wobble in pitch that warms the tone |
| **Muted** (con sordino) | a mute clipped on the bridge gives a soft, veiled sound |
| **Glissando** | sliding between two notes |
| **Divisi** | a section splits to play two or more different parts |

**Texture in chamber music.** Because every part can be heard clearly, composers vary the texture constantly:
- **melody and accompaniment**, for example the 1st violin's tune over repeated quavers in the inner parts and a pizzicato cello;
- **homophonic, chordal** passages in the same rhythm;
- **polyphonic** passages with **imitation** passed between players;
- **unison** or octaves for emphasis;
- **dialogue** between two instruments;
- in trio sonatas, two melody lines weaving around each other over the continuo.

**Listening strategy.** Use the topic's videos: "The string quartet explained", RevisionBuddy's chamber music revision, and the trio sonata introduction. Practise counting the players by register (high, middle and low lines) and by tone. A piano, a harpsichord or only strings? A harpsichord plus cello means continuo, so the music is Baroque. Four string lines means a string quartet. Piano with one string instrument is a Classical or Romantic sonata. For techniques, listen to how notes start: a dry "pop" is pizzicato, a shimmer is tremolo, and two notes from one violin is double stopping.

::: terms
- **Chamber music**: music for a small ensemble with one player per part, originally performed in a room of a house or palace.
- **String quartet**: an ensemble of two violins, a viola and a cello, or a piece written for them.
- **Basso continuo**: a Baroque accompaniment of a bass instrument playing the bass line and a keyboard or lute improvising chords above it.
- **Figured bass**: numbers written under a bass line to tell the continuo player which chords to play.
- **Trio sonata**: a Baroque piece for two melody instruments and basso continuo.
- **Solo sonata**: a Baroque piece for one melody instrument and basso continuo, or later a piece for solo piano or instrument and piano.
- **Patron**: a wealthy person or institution that employs a composer or commissions music.
- **Pizzicato**: plucking the strings of a bowed instrument with the finger.
- **Arco**: playing a string instrument with the bow.
- **Double stopping**: playing two strings of a string instrument at the same time.
- **Tremolo**: rapid repetition of a note with quick back-and-forth bow strokes, giving a shimmering or tense sound.
- **Piano quintet**: a piano with a string quartet, five players in all.
:::

::: method
1. **Count the performers** by listening for distinct lines and registers (high, middle, low), and by timbre. Then name the grouping: duet, trio, quartet, quintet.
2. **Identify the keyboard**: harpsichord points to continuo and Baroque; piano points to Classical or Romantic.
3. **Name the ensemble precisely**: "string quartet", not "strings"; "trio sonata with continuo", not "a trio".
4. **For texture**, say who has the melody, what the others do, and how this changes (imitation, dialogue, unison, melody and accompaniment).
5. **For techniques**, use the exact term: pizzicato, arco, tremolo, double stopping, muted, vibrato.
6. **For context questions**, link the setting (a small room, patrons, amateurs, no conductor) to musical features such as balanced parts, modest length and playability.
:::

::: example Name the ensemble
An extract is played by two violins, a viola and a cello. Name the type of ensemble. (1)

String quartet (1).
:::

::: example Role of the continuo
Explain the role of the basso continuo in a Baroque trio sonata. (2)

A bass instrument such as the cello plays the bass line, giving the harmonic foundation (1). A harpsichord (or organ) plays the bass and fills in chords above it from the figured bass, supporting the two melody instruments (1).
:::

::: example Name the technique
In a string quartet, the cello's notes are short, dry and plucked, while the violins shimmer on rapid repeated bow strokes. Name the two techniques. (2)

Pizzicato (cello) (1). Tremolo (violins) (1).
:::

::: example Describe the texture
In a string quartet movement, the first violin plays the melody while the second violin and viola play repeated quavers and the cello plays pizzicato bass notes. Later, the cello plays the melody and each of the other instruments copies it in turn. Describe the texture. (3)

At first it is homophonic, a melody and accompaniment texture with the 1st violin on the tune (1). The inner parts give a repeated-quaver accompaniment over a pizzicato bass (1). Later it becomes polyphonic, with imitation as the melody is passed from the cello to the other instruments (1).
:::

::: example Context
Explain how being written for performance in a room of a private house affected chamber music. (2)

The small space suited a small ensemble with one player per part and a quiet, intimate style (1). The performers were often amateurs or patrons, so the music was designed to be enjoyable to play and each part was interesting, like a conversation (1).
:::

::: q

---
Piano quintet.
:::

::: q
Name a chordal instrument that could play in a basso continuo. (1)
---
Harpsichord / organ / lute (accept theorbo). Not piano.
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by double stopping. (1)
---
Playing two strings at once on a string instrument, so that two notes sound together.
:::

::: q
Describe two differences between a Baroque trio sonata and a Classical violin sonata. (2)
---
Trio sonata: two melody instruments plus continuo, harpsichord (1). Classical sonata: one instrument and piano, no continuo / fully written-out piano part (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a string quartet usually plays without a conductor. (2)
---
It is small, with one player per part, so players can hear and see each other (1); they coordinate by listening and eye contact, and chamber music was originally informal, played in a room (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Basso continuo needs both parts**: the bass instrument and the chordal keyboard or lute. Naming only one may lose the second mark.
- A **trio sonata usually has four players** (two melody instruments, cello and harpsichord) but three written lines. Do not "count" it as a trio of players.
- Say **string quartet** for two violins, viola and cello. "String ensemble" or "strings" may not score.
- **Pizzicato is plucked, arco is bowed**; tremolo is rapid bowing on **one** note, not a trill.
- **Harpsichord means Baroque**; do not put a harpsichord in a Classical string quartet.
:::
