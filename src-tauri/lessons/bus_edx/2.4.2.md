## What it is

Business decisions should be based on **evidence**, and much of that evidence is numbers. This topic is about using **quantitative data** well, and knowing when it can mislead. Edexcel asks for:
1. **The use and interpretation of quantitative business data** to **support, inform and justify business decisions**, from four sources: **information from graphs and charts**, **financial data**, **marketing data** and **market data**.
2. **The use and limitations of financial information** in **understanding business performance** and **making business decisions**.

This topic is tested all over Paper 2. Section C nearly always gives a table or graph and asks you to **identify** a value from it (1 mark), then expects you to **use the figures** in the 9-mark justify and 12-mark evaluate. Data skills are worth marks in every section.

### Support, inform and justify

- **Inform**: data tells managers what is happening, such as which products are selling, whether costs are rising or whether the market is growing, so they can see that a decision is needed.
- **Support**: data gives evidence for one option over another, such as a higher ARR, a growing market or survey results showing demand.
- **Justify**: data lets managers explain and defend a decision to others (directors, shareholders, banks, employees) with facts rather than opinions.

### The four kinds of data

| Type | What it includes | Example uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Information from graphs and charts** | bar charts, line graphs, pie charts, tables | spotting trends over time, comparing regions or products, seeing shares of a total |
| **Financial data** | revenue, costs, gross and net profit, profit margins (2.4.1), cash-flow forecasts (1.3.3), break even (1.3.2b), ARR | judging profitability, cash position and whether an investment is worthwhile |
| **Marketing data** | market research results (1.2.3), sales by product or customer group, the response to promotions, website visits, social media engagement, customer satisfaction scores | deciding which products to promote, what price to charge, which customers to target |
| **Market data** | market size and growth, **market share**, competitors' prices and products, **changes in costs and prices** across the market | judging how well the business is doing against rivals and whether a market is worth entering |

Reading it: **London** has the highest sales (£21m) and the **South West** the lowest (£6m). Total sales are 14 + 9 + 21 + 6 + 8 = **£58m**. London's share of the total is 21 ÷ 58 × 100 = **36.21%**. The mean (average) sales per region is £58m ÷ 5 = **£11.6m**.

**Tips for graphs**: read the **axis labels and units** (£m or £000? units or %?); check whether the **vertical axis starts at zero** (if not, small differences look huge); look for the **trend** (rising, falling, steady, fluctuating) and any **unusual** point; read values carefully along gridlines.

### The calculations you need

Percentage change and averages are not in the spec's formula list, but they are in its quantitative skills, so expect them. **Formulae are not given in the exam.**

| Calculation | How to do it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Percentage change** | (new figure − original figure) ÷ original figure × 100 | sales rise from £40,000 to £46,000: £6,000 ÷ £40,000 × 100 = **15%** |
| **Percentage of a total** | part ÷ total × 100 | London: 21 ÷ 58 × 100 = 36.21% |
| **Market share** | the business's sales ÷ total market sales × 100 | £18m sales in a £240m market: 18 ÷ 240 × 100 = **7.50%** |
| **Mean (average)** | add up all the values ÷ the number of values | sales of 12, 15, 9 and 16 units: 52 ÷ 4 = **13 units** |
| **Change in percentage points** | new % − old % | market share from 7% to 9% is a rise of **2 percentage points** (but a 28.57% increase in share) |
| **Profitability ratios** | gross and net profit margin (2.4.1) | see 2.4.1 |

**The most common error** is dividing a percentage change by the **new** figure. Always divide by the **original** (older) figure.

**Interpreting, not just calculating**: a number on its own is not analysis. Say what it **shows** ("London generates over a third of sales, so…") and what the business should **do** about it ("…a new store there is lower risk than one in the South West").

### Using data to make decisions: a worked example

Fellside Outdoor is deciding where to open its next store. The chart **informs** (London and the North are the strongest regions). Market data could **support** a choice: if the outdoor-clothing market in Scotland is growing by 12% a year while London's is flat, Scotland may offer more future growth despite lower current sales. Financial data, such as the ARR on each possible store, would **justify** the final choice to the board. The best decisions combine **several sources** of data.

### The limitations of financial information

Financial information (income statements, margins, cash flow, ARR) is very useful, but it has **limitations** in **understanding performance** and in **making decisions**:

- **It is historical**: income statements show what **has** happened. Past results may not continue, especially if the market changes.
- **Forecasts are estimates**: cash-flow forecasts and ARR are based on **predicted** figures, which may be wrong.
- **It ignores qualitative factors**: financial data does not show **staff motivation**, **customer satisfaction**, **brand reputation**, product quality, ethical issues or the impact on the environment, yet these affect future performance.
- **It does not explain why**: a falling net margin shows a problem but not its cause (higher rent? price cuts? poor management?).
- **A single year can mislead**: one-off events, such as a big order or a large repair bill, distort the figures. Look at the **trend** over several years.
- **It needs comparison**: a 6% net margin is good in some industries and poor in others. Without **competitors' figures** or **market data**, it is hard to judge.
- **External factors**: figures may reflect the economy (2.1.3 interest rates, exchange rates, 1.5.2 the economy) rather than the business's own decisions.
- **Accuracy and presentation**: figures may be rounded, out of date, or presented in the most favourable way (for example, a graph with a vertical axis that does not start at zero).

**Questioning data** more generally: Who produced it, and are they **biased**? How **large** was the sample? How **recent** is it? Does it cover the whole market or one area? Is it **primary** or **secondary** (1.2.3)?

::: terms
- **Quantitative data**: information in the form of numbers, such as sales figures, costs, profits or market share.
- **Qualitative data**: information about opinions, feelings and reasons, which cannot easily be measured in numbers.
- **Financial data**: information about a business's money, such as revenue, costs, profit, margins and cash flow.
- **Marketing data**: information about customers and the effect of marketing, such as market research results and sales by product.
- **Market data**: information about the whole market, such as its size, growth, market share and competitors' prices.
- **Market share**: a business's sales as a percentage of total sales in the market.
- **Percentage change**: the change in a figure divided by the original figure, multiplied by 100.
- **Trend**: the general direction in which a figure moves over time.
- **Mean**: the average found by adding all the values and dividing by the number of values.
- **Historical data**: information about what has already happened, which may not show what will happen next.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify questions (1)**: read the **units** and the exact row or column asked for. Write the answer with its unit.
2. **Calculate (2)**: write the method in words, substitute, and give the unit. For percentage change, divide by the **original** figure.
3. **Interpret**: after any figure, add "this shows…" and "so the business should…".
4. **In explain questions about limitations**: name the limitation (historical, ignores qualitative factors, no comparison), then develop how it could lead to a poor decision.
5. **In justify and evaluate**: quote the figures, **do something with them** (a change, a percentage, a comparison), and use data on both sides.
6. **Question the data** in your evaluation: is it one year only? A forecast? Missing qualitative factors? This is strong AO3b.
7. **Conclude** with a "depends on" factor, often information the data does not give you.
:::

::: example Calculate - percentage change
A business's sales revenue rose from £4.2 million in 2025 to £4.83 million in 2026. Calculate the percentage change in sales revenue. (2)

(£4.83m − £4.2m) ÷ £4.2m × 100 = £0.63m ÷ £4.2m × 100 (1) = **15%** increase (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - market share
The UK market for garden furniture is worth £1.6 billion a year. Greenhaven plc's sales are £88 million. Calculate Greenhaven's market share. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. (2)

£88m ÷ £1,600m × 100 (1) = **5.50%** (1).
:::

::: example Explain - limitation of financial information
Explain **one** limitation of using financial information to make business decisions. (3)

Financial information such as an income statement is historical, showing what has already happened (1). Conditions such as customer tastes or competition may have changed since then (1), so a decision based only on last year's figures, such as expanding a product range, may fail if those conditions do not continue (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - using data
Stonegate Gyms plc has 50 gyms. Membership data shows:

| | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Members | 120,000 | 126,000 | 118,000 |
| Average monthly fee | £30 | £32 | £35 |
| Customer satisfaction (out of 10) | 7.8 | 7.1 | 6.2 |

Analyse what this data shows about Stonegate's performance. (6)

Membership rose by 5% from 120,000 to 126,000 in 2025 but fell to 118,000 in 2026, while the average fee rose from £30 to £35 (AO2). This suggests the fee rises are pushing some members to cheaper gyms. Monthly fee income is still higher in 2026 (118,000 × £35 = £4.13m against 126,000 × £32 = £4.03m in 2025), so revenue has held up for now, but losing members each year will eventually reduce it (AO3a).

Customer satisfaction has fallen every year, from 7.8 to 6.2 (AO2). Unhappy members are less likely to renew, and they tell friends, so the fall in members in 2026 may continue; combined with higher fees, members feel they get less value for more money, which could make price rises harder in future and damage Stonegate's reputation against rival gyms (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - using data for a decision
Tidepool Toys plc wants to launch one new product range. Its market research data shows:

| | Option 1: Wooden toys | Option 2: Electronic toys |
|---|---|---|
| UK market size | £180m | £950m |
| Market growth per year | 9% | 2% |
| Number of major competitors | 4 | 15 |
| Survey: "would buy" (sample 400 parents) | 32% | 41% |

Justify which **one** of these two options Tidepool Toys should choose. (9)

The wooden toys market is growing at 9% a year, over four times as fast as electronic toys, and has only 4 major competitors against 15 (AO2). Fast growth means demand is rising, so Tidepool can grow sales without having to take customers from rivals, and with fewer competitors it is more likely to gain a noticeable market share; for example 5% of £180m would be £9m of sales (AO3a).

However, the market is much smaller at £180m against £950m, and fewer surveyed parents said they would buy wooden toys (32% against 41%) (AO2). This suggests electronic toys have more immediate appeal, and a small market limits how large the range can eventually become (AO3a).

Tidepool should launch wooden toys, because a growing market with few rivals gives a better chance of building share than a crowded, slow-growing one where 15 competitors will compete hard on price. However, the survey of 400 parents may not represent the whole market, and the decision depends on whether Tidepool can make wooden toys at a cost that gives a good margin (AO3b).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** reason why a business should compare its financial data with that of its competitors. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: a figure on its own does not show whether performance is good or bad (1); a 5% net margin might look poor, but if rivals average 3% the business is doing well (1), so comparing helps managers set realistic targets and avoid unnecessary changes (1).

Discuss the limitations of financial information in understanding a business's performance. (6)
---

::: q
Lumen Lighting plc sells LED bulbs. Its sales revenue rose 10% last year, but the total UK market for LED bulbs grew by 25%.

Analyse what this data suggests about Lumen Lighting's performance. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: Lumen's sales up 10%; market up 25%.
- AO3a: although sales grew, the market grew much faster, so Lumen's market share has fallen; competitors are winning a larger share of new customers; Lumen may be losing on price, product range or promotion; without action, falling share may weaken its position with retailers; the 10% growth looks good on its own but is weak in context, showing why market data is needed alongside financial data.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Lumen throughout.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.
:::

::: watch
- **Percentage change divides by the original figure.** Using the new figure is the most common error, and it scores zero.
- Read the **units** on tables and graphs: £000 and £m change the answer completely.
- A percentage-point change is not the same as a percentage change.
- **Use the figures, then interpret them.** Copying numbers out of a table is not analysis: calculate a change or a share, then say what it means.
- Market data gives context: sales rising 10% is weak if the market grew 25%.
- In evaluation, **question the data**: one year, a forecast, a small sample, no qualitative information.
:::
