## What it is

A business can recruit well (2.5.2) and train well (2.5.3), but it only gets the best from its people if they **want** to work hard and stay. **Motivation** is the desire an employee has to do a job well and to put in effort. Edexcel asks for two things:
1. **The importance of motivation in the workplace**: **attracting employees**, **retaining employees** and **productivity**.
2. **How businesses motivate employees**:
   - **financial methods**: **remuneration**, **bonus**, **commission**, **promotion**, **fringe benefits**;
   - **non-financial methods**: **job rotation**, **job enrichment**, **autonomy**.

Motivation theorists (such as Maslow or Herzberg) are **not** on the 1BS0 specification: marks come from explaining these methods and applying them to the case.

### Why motivation matters

**1. Attracting employees**

A business known as a **good place to work**, with competitive pay, good perks, interesting jobs and chances to progress, finds recruitment easier:
- it receives **more applicants**, so it can choose the **best quality** candidates and fill vacancies **faster**;
- it may spend **less on advertising** jobs, because staff recommend it to friends.

When many businesses are short of staff, the one offering the best overall package wins the best people.

**2. Retaining employees**

**Retention** is keeping employees rather than having them leave. Motivated staff are **less likely to leave**, so **staff turnover** falls:
- the business spends less on **recruiting and training replacements** (advertising, interviews, induction, lost output while new staff learn);
- it keeps **experienced** staff who know its customers, products and systems.

Staff turnover (%) = number of staff leaving in a year ÷ average number of staff × 100.

**3. Productivity**

**Productivity** is output per worker in a given time (2.3.1). Motivated employees **work harder**, take **more care**, are **absent less** and give **better customer service**:
- more output from the same workforce means **lower cost per unit**, so the business can cut prices or earn higher profit margins;
- fewer mistakes mean less waste and better **quality** (2.3.3), and better service brings repeat customers.

**Worked figures**: 20 workers make 1,000 units a day, so productivity is 1,000 ÷ 20 = **50 units per worker**. If better motivation lifts output to 1,100 units, productivity becomes 1,100 ÷ 20 = **55 units per worker**, a 10% rise. With a daily wage bill of £2,000, labour cost per unit falls from £2,000 ÷ 1,000 = **£2.00** to £2,000 ÷ 1,100 = **£1.82** (to the nearest penny).

### Financial methods of motivation

Financial methods use **money**, or rewards worth money, to motivate.

**Remuneration**

**Remuneration** is the total payment an employee receives for their work: a **wage** (usually an **hourly** rate, so pay depends on hours worked) or a **salary** (a fixed **annual** amount paid monthly). It must be at least the legal minimum wage.
- **Benefits**: paying **above** the market rate helps a business **attract** good applicants and **retain** staff, because workers are less tempted by rivals; regular **pay rises** reward loyalty.
- **Drawbacks**: higher pay is a **permanent** rise in costs; a pay rise may motivate for only a short time before it is taken for granted; it does not reward the **best** workers more than the rest.

**Bonus**

A **bonus** is a **lump sum** paid **on top of** basic pay, usually for meeting a **target**. It can be:
- **individual**: for one person hitting their own target;
- **team**: shared by a team that meets a group target, which encourages co-operation;
- **company-wide**: paid to everyone if the business meets a profit target (a form of profit sharing), which makes staff care about the business's overall success.

- **Benefits**: rewards effort towards the targets the business needs, without a permanent rise in basic pay.
- **Drawbacks**: if targets are missed, no bonus is paid, which can **demotivate**, especially if the miss was outside staff's control; staff may **cut corners** on quality to hit output targets; a bonus once a year is a long way off for most staff, so its effect on day-to-day effort may be weak; staff may come to **expect** it.

**Commission**

**Commission** is payment based on a **percentage of the value of sales** an employee makes. It is common for **sales staff**, for example in car showrooms, estate agents and the sale of financial products. Staff are often paid a **basic salary plus commission**.

Commission (£) = commission rate (%) × value of sales.

- **Benefits**: pay is linked directly to revenue, so the business only pays when sales are made; the best sellers earn most, which attracts and keeps strong sales staff.
- **Drawbacks**: staff may use **pushy selling** or sell customers products they do not need, damaging reputation and leading to complaints or returns; staff compete instead of co-operating; **income is unpredictable**, which can be stressful; it suits only jobs where sales can be credited to one person.

**Promotion**

**Promotion** is moving an employee to a **higher-level job** with more **responsibility**, **status** and **pay**. It counts as a financial method because it brings higher pay.
- **Benefits**: it rewards good performance and shows staff there is a **career path**, which helps **retain** ambitious employees who might otherwise leave to progress elsewhere.
- **Drawbacks**: there are only a **limited number** of senior posts, so those **not** promoted may be demotivated; a good worker is not always a good manager; promoted staff may need **training** (2.5.3); in a flat structure (2.5.1) there are fewer levels to be promoted into.

**Fringe benefits**

**Fringe benefits** (perks) are **rewards in addition to pay** that are not paid as cash wages. Examples include **staff discount**, **company car**, **private health insurance**, gym membership, **pension contributions** above the legal minimum, free or subsidised **meals**, a work phone, childcare support or extra holiday.
- **Benefits**: they help a business stand out to applicants without raising pay, and staff feel looked after; a perk linked to the product (a staff discount) costs the business less than it is worth to staff.
- **Drawbacks**: they **cost money**; different employees value different perks, so some are wasted; they are soon **taken for granted**; perks such as a company car are expensive to provide.

### Non-financial methods of motivation

Non-financial methods change the **job itself** or the way staff are **managed**, rather than paying more.

**Job rotation**

**Job rotation** means moving employees **between different tasks or jobs** of a **similar level of difficulty**, at regular intervals. For example, a warehouse worker spends one week picking orders, the next packing and the next loading lorries.
- **Benefits**: **variety** reduces boredom from repetitive work; employees become **multi-skilled**, so the business can **cover absences** and move staff to where demand is highest; less risk of injury from repeating the same movement all day.
- **Drawbacks**: the tasks are **no more challenging**, so staff may simply swap one dull job for another; staff need **training** on each task; **productivity may fall** while they learn and as staff lose the speed that comes from specialising in one task.

**Job enrichment**

**Job enrichment** means giving employees **more challenging, responsible and interesting work**, using more of their skills. It adds **higher-level** tasks to a job, for example letting a production worker check quality and solve problems, letting an experienced shop assistant train new starters, or letting a care assistant plan residents' activities.
- **Benefits**: staff feel **trusted** and gain a sense of **achievement** and **recognition**; they use and develop their abilities, preparing them for **promotion**; problems are solved by the people closest to the work; managers have more time, and the business may need fewer supervisors.
- **Drawbacks**: staff need **training** to take on the extra responsibility; some employees do **not want** more responsibility and find it stressful; staff may expect **higher pay** for doing more; some jobs are hard to enrich.

**Job rotation vs job enrichment**: rotation gives **more** tasks at the **same** level (horizontal); enrichment gives **harder** tasks with **more responsibility** (vertical). Confusing the two is a common mistake.

**Autonomy**

**Autonomy** means giving employees the **freedom to make their own decisions** about how (and sometimes when or where) they do their work, without close supervision. For example, a team decides how to organise its own shifts, or a customer service adviser can offer a refund up to a set limit without asking a manager.
- **Benefits**: staff feel **trusted** and **valued**, raising motivation; decisions are made **faster** by the person dealing with the customer, improving service; staff may find **better, more creative** ways of working; fewer supervisors are needed. It links to **delegation** and flatter, **decentralised** structures (2.5.1).
- **Drawbacks**: employees may make **mistakes** or poor decisions; standards may become **inconsistent** across the business; it needs **skilled, trained and trustworthy** staff; managers lose some **control**; some employees prefer clear instructions and find autonomy stressful.

### Choosing methods

| Method | Works best when... | Less suitable when... |
|---|---|---|
| Higher remuneration | staff are leaving for better pay; vacancies are hard to fill | the business has tight costs or low profit margins |
| Bonus | targets are clear and measurable | targets depend on factors staff cannot control |
| Commission | individual sales can be measured | teamwork or honest advice matters most |
| Promotion | staff are ambitious; there are senior posts to fill | the structure is flat, with few senior posts |
| Fringe benefits | competing for staff; perks link to the product | staff value cash more than perks |
| Job rotation | work is repetitive; cover for absence is needed | specialist skill and speed matter most |
| Job enrichment | staff are skilled and want responsibility | staff do not want extra duties without extra pay |
| Autonomy | staff are experienced and trusted | mistakes are costly or safety-critical |

**Financial vs non-financial**:
- Financial methods give a **quick, visible** incentive and help most with **attracting** staff, but they **cost money** and the effect can fade.
- Non-financial methods are usually **cheaper** and can raise **long-term** motivation and retention, but they take time, need training and do not pay the bills. Staff who feel **underpaid** are unlikely to be won over by more interesting work alone.
- Most businesses use a **combination**: fair pay first, then non-financial methods on top.

::: terms
- **Motivation**: the desire an employee has to do a job well and to put effort into their work.
- **Retention**: a business's ability to keep its employees rather than having them leave.
- **Productivity**: the output produced per worker in a given period of time.
- **Remuneration**: the total payment an employee receives for their work, such as a wage or salary.
- **Bonus**: a lump sum paid on top of basic pay, usually for meeting a target.
- **Commission**: payment to an employee based on a percentage of the value of the sales they make.
- **Promotion**: moving an employee to a higher-level job with more responsibility, status and pay.
- **Fringe benefits**: rewards given in addition to pay that are not cash wages, such as a staff discount or company car.
- **Job rotation**: moving employees between different tasks of a similar level of difficulty at regular intervals.
- **Job enrichment**: giving employees more challenging, responsible and interesting work that uses more of their skills.
- **Autonomy**: giving employees the freedom to make their own decisions about how they do their work.
- **Staff turnover**: the rate at which employees leave a business and have to be replaced.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the method** exactly (remuneration, bonus, commission, promotion, fringe benefits, job rotation, job enrichment, autonomy) and say **how it works**.
2. **Explain (3)**: method → effect on the employee (feels rewarded, trusted, less bored, sees a career path) → effect on the business (**productivity**, **retention** and lower recruitment costs, **attracting** better applicants, quality, customer service).
3. **Never stop at "they feel happy"** or "money motivates": develop to what the business gains.
4. **Calculations**: commission = rate × sales; total pay = basic + commission or bonus; productivity = output ÷ workers; percentage change divides by the **original** figure.
5. **Use the case**: the type of work (repetitive? sales? skilled?), the size of the workforce, turnover figures, what staff say in surveys, and how much the business can afford.
6. **Weigh**: every method has a cost or risk (commission and pushy selling; bonus and corner-cutting; autonomy and mistakes; enrichment and training or pay demands).
7. **Justify (9) / evaluate (12)**: choose one option, give its benefits and drawbacks for this business using the figures, and conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as whether staff are skilled enough for autonomy or whether pay is already competitive.
:::

::: example Explain - why motivation matters
Explain **one** reason why it is important for a business to have motivated employees. (3)

Motivated employees are less likely to leave the business (1), so staff turnover is lower and the business spends less on advertising, interviewing and training replacements (1). It also keeps experienced staff who know its customers and systems, which helps maintain quality and customer service (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - commission
A car sales adviser earns a basic salary of £21,000 a year plus 2.5% commission on the value of cars she sells. Last year she sold cars worth £640,000. Calculate her total earnings for the year. (2)

Commission = 2.5% × £640,000 = £16,000 (1). Total earnings = £21,000 + £16,000 = **£37,000** (1). The correct answer scores 2.
:::

::: example Outline - job rotation
Ashgrove Foods Ltd makes ready meals in Grimsby. Its 60 production workers each spend the whole shift on one task, such as filling trays or sealing packs. Outline **one** benefit to Ashgrove of introducing job rotation. (2)

Ashgrove's workers would move between filling, sealing and packing, giving them variety and reducing the boredom of doing one task all shift (1), so they make fewer mistakes, and because they can do several tasks, Ashgrove can cover a worker who is absent without the production line stopping (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - job enrichment
Calder Care Ltd runs 12 care homes. Care assistants spend their shifts on routine tasks, and staff turnover is 35% a year. Calder plans to let experienced care assistants plan residents' activity programmes and train new starters.

Analyse the benefits to Calder Care of using job enrichment. (6)

Giving experienced assistants responsibility for planning activities and training new starters shows that Calder trusts them and values their experience (AO2). They gain a sense of achievement and a step towards senior roles, so they are less likely to leave; with turnover at 35%, even a small fall would cut Calder's spending on recruiting and inducting new care assistants and keep experienced carers whom residents know (AO3a).

New starters would be trained by experienced assistants who know how Calder's homes work (AO2). This means new staff learn good practice faster and settle in better, so quality of care improves across the 12 homes, which strengthens Calder's reputation with families choosing a care home and helps it fill its places (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - commission or a pay rise
Grantly Kitchens plc has 90 showrooms and 270 sales advisers, each paid a salary of £24,000 with no commission. Sales have been flat for two years.
Option 1: pay advisers 1% commission on the value of the kitchens they sell, on top of their salary.
Option 2: give every adviser a pay rise of £1,500 a year.

Justify which **one** of these two options Grantly should choose. (9)

Commission links advisers' pay directly to the kitchens they sell: an adviser who sells £480,000 of kitchens a year would earn an extra £4,800 (AO2). This gives every adviser a reason to follow up enquiries and close sales, which could lift Grantly's flat sales, and the business only pays more when it earns more revenue (AO3a).

However, advisers may push customers into more expensive kitchens or extras they do not need, leading to complaints and cancelled orders that damage Grantly's reputation; advisers may also compete for customers instead of helping each other in the showroom (AO3a).

Grantly should choose Option 1. The pay rise would cost a guaranteed 270 × £1,500 = £405,000 a year whether or not sales rise, and it rewards weak and strong advisers equally, so it is unlikely to fix the flat sales. This depends on Grantly monitoring customer satisfaction and cancellations alongside commission, so that advisers are rewarded for good sales, not just big ones (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Job rotation is not job enrichment.** Rotation moves staff between tasks of the **same** difficulty; enrichment gives **harder, more responsible** tasks. Examiners see this confusion often.
- **Develop to the business**: "staff feel valued" or "money motivates" gets little on its own. Take it on to productivity, retention (lower recruitment costs) or attracting better applicants.
- **Commission** is a percentage of the **value of sales**; a **bonus** is a lump sum for hitting a target. Do not mix them up.
- **Promotion** is on the spec as a **financial** method, because it brings higher pay.
- Every method has a **drawback**: commission and pushy selling; bonus and cutting corners; autonomy and mistakes; enrichment and training costs or pay demands. Use them when weighing.
- In calculations, percentage change divides by the **original** figure, and convert percentages correctly (2.5% = 0.025).
:::
