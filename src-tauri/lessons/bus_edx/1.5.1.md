## What it is

A **stakeholder** is any individual or group with an interest in a business, or who is affected by what it does. Stakeholders are not the same as **shareholders**: shareholders (owners) are just one stakeholder group. Edexcel asks you to know who the stakeholders are, what they want, how they are affected by the business, how they can affect it, and why their objectives can **conflict**.

### Stakeholders and their objectives

| Stakeholder | Who they are | Main objectives |
|---|---|---|
| **Shareholders (owners)** | The people who own the business: a sole trader, partners or company shareholders | Profit, a return on their investment, the business's survival and growth, a rising value of their share |
| **Employees** | People who work for the business | Fair pay, job security, good working conditions, training and promotion, being treated with respect |
| **Customers** | People and businesses who buy the products | Good quality at a fair price, choice, convenience, good customer service, safe products |
| **Managers** | People who run departments or the whole business on behalf of the owners | Business success (which reflects on them), good pay and bonuses, career progression, power and responsibility |
| **Suppliers** | Businesses that provide materials, stock or services | Regular, large orders, being paid on time, a long-term relationship |
| **Local community** | People living and working near the business | Local jobs, little noise, traffic or pollution, support for local causes, a safe and attractive area |
| **Pressure groups** | Organisations that campaign to change business behaviour on an issue, such as the environment, animal welfare or workers' rights | Ethical, environmentally friendly behaviour on their issue |
| **The government** | National and local government | Tax revenue, jobs, businesses following the law (consumer, employment, health and safety, environmental) |

In a small business the **owner** is often also the **manager**, and there may be few employees, but customers, suppliers, the community and the government are still stakeholders.

### How stakeholders are affected by business activity

Business decisions affect stakeholders in different ways:
- **Expanding** or opening a new site creates jobs and orders for suppliers, but may bring traffic and noise to the local community.
- **Raising prices** increases profit for owners if sales hold up, but customers pay more.
- **Cutting costs** may mean lower pay, fewer staff or cheaper suppliers, which helps owners but hurts employees or suppliers.
- **Closing** a business loses jobs, ends orders for suppliers and removes a service from the community.
- **Paying suppliers late** helps the business's cash flow but harms the supplier's.

### How stakeholders impact business activity

Influence works the other way too:
- **Customers** decide whether the business survives through what they buy, and through online reviews.
- **Employees** affect quality and service; unhappy staff may leave or work less well; staff may take industrial action in larger firms.
- **Owners and shareholders** make or approve the big decisions and provide finance.
- **Managers** make day-to-day decisions.
- **Suppliers** affect quality, cost and reliability, and can offer or withdraw trade credit (1.3.4).
- **The local community** can object to planning applications for new premises or extensions, and can boycott a business.
- **Pressure groups** run campaigns, protests and social media pressure that damage a business's reputation.
- **The government** passes laws (1.5.3), sets taxes and interest-rate conditions (1.5.4) and grants planning permission through local councils.

The more **power** a stakeholder has (customers in a competitive market, a key supplier, the government), the more the business must take its objectives into account.

### Conflicts between stakeholder groups

Because stakeholders want different things, a decision that pleases one group can harm another.

| Conflict | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| **Owners v employees** over pay | Higher pay raises costs and cuts profit; owners want profit, employees want better pay |
| **Owners v customers** over price | Higher prices raise profit per item; customers want lower prices |
| **Owners v local community** over expansion | Expansion raises profit and jobs, but brings traffic, noise or loss of green space |
| **Owners v suppliers** over payment terms | The business wants to pay later to help its cash flow; suppliers want paying quickly |
| **Owners v pressure groups** over costs | Ethical or green materials cost more and cut profit |
| **Employees v customers** over opening hours | Customers want longer hours; employees may not want evenings and weekends |

Not every decision causes conflict. Some actions benefit several groups: better training improves employees' skills and job satisfaction, gives customers better service and raises owners' profits. A business **resolves conflicts** by consulting stakeholders, compromising (a smaller pay rise with better conditions), or deciding which group's objectives matter most for its aims (1.3.1), and explaining why.

::: terms
- **Stakeholder**: any individual or group with an interest in a business or affected by its activities.
- **Shareholder**: an owner of a company, who holds part of it through shares; one type of stakeholder.
- **Employees**: people who work for a business in return for pay.
- **Managers**: people who organise and make decisions about part or all of a business on behalf of the owners.
- **Suppliers**: businesses that provide another business with materials, stock or services.
- **Local community**: the people who live and work near a business and are affected by its activities.
- **Pressure group**: an organisation that campaigns to change the behaviour of businesses or government on an issue.
- **Stakeholder conflict**: a situation where a business decision benefits one stakeholder group but harms another because their objectives differ.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the stakeholder** precisely: shareholders (owners), employees, customers, managers, suppliers, local community, pressure groups, government. Do not confuse stakeholder with shareholder.
2. **State their objective**, then the **effect** of the decision on them, or their **influence** on the business.
3. **Conflict questions**: name **two** groups, state each group's objective, and explain why the decision helps one and harms the other.
4. **Apply the case**: what the decision is, who is affected, how much power each group has.
5. **Justify and evaluate**: weigh which stakeholders' interests should come first, given the business's objectives and the power of each group; conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as whether customers would switch to rivals.
:::

::: example Explain - impact of a stakeholder
Explain **one** way customers can affect a business's activity. (3)

Customers decide whether to buy from the business (1), so if they are unhappy with its prices or service they will go to a competitor (1), which reduces sales and forces the business to change its product, price or service to win them back (1).
:::

::: example Explain - a conflict
Explain **one** possible conflict between the owners and employees of a business. (3)

Employees want higher pay, but owners want to keep costs down (1), because higher wages raise the business's total costs (1), so meeting employees' demands reduces the profit available to the owners (1).
:::

::: example Outline - stakeholder affected
Hill Farm Shop in Derbyshire plans to build a café and car park on a field next to the shop, opening seven days a week. Outline **one** way the local community could be affected. (2)

Local residents may face more traffic and noise (1), as visitors drive along the narrow lanes to the new car park every day of the week, which could make the area less peaceful and the roads less safe (1). Or new local jobs, developed.
:::

::: example Analyse - conflict in a case
Sam runs Sam's Supplies, a building merchant in Wigan. To improve cash flow, Sam plans to pay suppliers after 60 days instead of 30, and to extend opening hours to 8pm, which would mean staff working later shifts. Analyse the possible conflicts this could cause between Sam's stakeholders. (6)

Paying after 60 days helps Sam's cash flow, but suppliers want to be paid quickly to protect their own cash flow (AO2). A small supplier waiting an extra month for its money may struggle to pay its own bills, so it may refuse to give Sam credit, raise prices or prioritise other customers, which could leave Sam's Supplies short of stock (AO3a).

Opening until 8pm suits customers, such as tradespeople who want to collect materials after a day's work, but employees may not want evening shifts (AO2). If staff are unhappy they may leave or work less well, which means recruitment costs and worse customer service; Sam could reduce the conflict by paying more for late shifts or using volunteers, though that raises costs and cuts the profit the change was meant to protect (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - whose interests come first
Willow & Co is a small furniture maker in Shropshire. Its main supplier has offered cheaper timber from forests that are not certified as sustainable. Option 1: switch to the cheaper timber, cutting costs by £8,000 a year. Option 2: keep using certified sustainable timber. Justify which **one** of these options Willow & Co should choose. (9)

Option 2 protects Willow & Co's reputation with customers who buy hand-made furniture partly because of its quality and environmental values (AO2). These customers are prepared to pay higher prices, so keeping sustainable timber protects sales and avoids the risk of a pressure group or customers criticising the business online, which could damage its brand (AO3a).

However, the owners give up £8,000 a year of savings, which would raise profit or allow lower prices to compete with cheaper rivals. For a small business, £8,000 could fund new equipment or marketing (AO3a).

Willow & Co should keep the sustainable timber, because for a premium furniture maker, reputation with customers is worth more than the cost saving. This depends on what its customers value: if research showed they choose Willow & Co mainly on price rather than its green credentials, the cheaper timber might be justified, but for most premium buyers the risk to reputation outweighs £8,000 (AO3b).
:::

::: q
Which **one** of the following is the best definition of a **stakeholder**?

A A person who owns shares in a company

B An individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, a business

C A customer who buys regularly

D A manager responsible for a department

Select one answer. (1)
---
B (1). A defines a shareholder, who is only one type of stakeholder; C and D are examples of stakeholders, not definitions.

State **one** objective of suppliers. (1)
---

::: q
Explain **one** way the government can affect a business. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: it passes laws such as the minimum wage (1), which the business must follow (1), so its wage costs rise and profit may fall unless it raises prices (1). Or taxation, planning permission, developed.

::: q
Explain **one** way a pressure group could affect a business. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: it may run a social media campaign against the business (1), which damages its reputation with customers (1), so sales fall and the business may be forced to change its behaviour (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** reason why a business decision may benefit more than one stakeholder group. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: training staff (1) improves employees' skills and job satisfaction, and gives customers better service (1), so customers return and owners gain higher profit (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how the opening of a new 24-hour gym, Iron Works, on a residential street in Stockport could affect two of its stakeholder groups.
(6)
---
Indicative content: customers gain convenient access at any hour; local community may suffer late-night noise and parking problems, and may complain to the council; employees need to cover night shifts; owners gain more members and revenue; possible conflict between owners and community.
Level 3 (5–6): developed chains on two groups, applied to Iron Works. Level 2 (3–4): some development. Level 1 (1–2): isolated points. No context: maximum 3.
:::

::: watch
- **Stakeholder ≠ shareholder.** Shareholders (owners) are one of eight stakeholder groups in the spec.
- Learn the **eight groups** exactly: shareholders (owners), employees, customers, managers, suppliers, local community, pressure groups, the government.
- A **conflict** answer must name **two** groups and explain why their objectives clash. One group's unhappiness alone is not a conflict.
- Influence runs **both ways**: how stakeholders are affected, and how they affect the business. Read which the question asks.
- Use the case: which stakeholders are most affected by **this** decision, and which has the most power.
:::

::: test

---
Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):

- A decision that helps one group often harms another, such as cutting costs by cutting pay or switching to cheaper suppliers.

::: test
Rowan runs Rowan's Bakery in Hereford with eight staff. Profits have fallen. Option 1: cut costs by reducing staff hours by 10%. Option 2: keep staff hours and raise the price of bread and cakes by 8%.

Justify which **one** of these two options Rowan should choose. (9)
---
Indicative content:
- Option 1: protects prices for customers; immediate cost saving; but employees lose income, morale may fall, service may suffer and good staff may leave; conflict between owner and employees.
- Option 2: keeps staff happy and service level; raises revenue per sale; but customers pay more and may switch to supermarkets; conflict between owner and customers.
- Conclusion with a new factor, for example: depends on how loyal customers are to the bakery's quality, or whether staff would accept fewer hours temporarily.

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): one option weighed with benefits and drawbacks, applied to Rowan's stakeholders; a conclusion with a new "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (4–6): some developed reasoning and use of the case; a partly supported judgement.
- Level 1 (1–3): isolated points; little use of the case.
:::

::: test
Green Valley Camping is a family-run campsite in the Lake District owned by the Lowe family. They plan to double the number of pitches by building on a neighbouring field, adding 15 seasonal jobs. Local residents have complained about traffic on narrow roads, and an environmental pressure group says the field is home to rare wildflowers. The expansion could add £60,000 to annual profit. Planning permission from the local council is needed.

Evaluate whose interests Green Valley Camping should put first when deciding whether to expand. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
---
Indicative content:
- AO1b: stakeholder objectives; how stakeholders affect and are affected by business; conflicts.
- AO2: £60,000 extra profit; 15 seasonal jobs; resident complaints about traffic; pressure group and rare wildflowers; council planning permission.
- AO3a: Owners: expansion raises profit and secures the family business. Employees and community: 15 jobs help a rural area. Residents: traffic and congestion on narrow roads harm quality of life. Pressure group: could campaign and damage the campsite's reputation with nature-loving campers, its core customers. Government (council): can refuse permission, so ignoring objections risks the whole plan.
- AO3b: a supported judgement, for example the owners should weigh community and environmental concerns heavily, because the council and the campsite's customers both care about them; a compromise (a smaller expansion avoiding the wildflower area, traffic management) may achieve most of the profit; depends on how much power the council and pressure group have and how campers react.

Levels:
- Level 3 (9–12): accurate knowledge; detailed application to Green Valley; balanced, developed analysis; a supported judgement with a "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (5–8): sound knowledge and some application; some developed analysis; a judgement with limited support.
- Level 1 (1–4): isolated knowledge, little application; an assertion rather than a judgement.
:::
