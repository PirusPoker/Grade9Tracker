## What it is

Every business needs money to start and to keep going: for premises, equipment, stock and to cover the gap before customers pay. **Sources of finance** are where that money comes from. Edexcel splits them into **short-term** sources (to be repaid within about a year, for day-to-day cash needs) and **long-term** sources (for start-up costs and big purchases that last for years).

### Short-term sources

| Source | What it is | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overdraft** | The bank lets the business take its account below zero, up to an agreed limit | Flexible: use only what is needed, when needed; interest is paid only on the amount overdrawn; quick to arrange; ideal for a temporary cash-flow gap (1.3.3) | High interest rate; the bank can reduce the limit or ask for repayment at short notice; not suitable for long-term purchases |
| **Trade credit** | A supplier lets the business take goods now and pay later, typically 30 to 60 days | Usually no interest; the business can sell the stock before paying for it, which helps cash flow | New businesses with no track record may not be offered it; paying late damages the relationship; the business may lose discounts for paying promptly |

### Long-term sources

| Source | What it is | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal savings** | The owner's own money put into the business | No interest or repayments; the owner keeps full control; shows lenders the owner is committed | The owner may not have enough; the owner risks losing their savings (1.1.2) |
| **Loans** | A fixed sum borrowed from a bank, repaid in regular instalments with interest over an agreed period | Large sums available; repayments are predictable, which helps planning; the owner keeps control | Interest adds to costs (1.3.2a); repayments must be made even when sales are poor; banks may want security (an asset such as a house) and a business plan |
| **Share capital** | Money raised by selling shares (part-ownership) in a company; for a start-up this means a **private limited company** (1.4.1) | No repayments or interest; shareholders may bring skills and contacts | Only available to companies; owners give up part of the control and future profits; shareholders expect a return |
| **Venture capital** | Investment from specialist investors (venture capitalists) in a small business with high growth potential, in return for a share of the business | Large sums for risky ideas that banks would not lend to; investors bring expertise and contacts | The owner gives up a share of ownership and control; investors expect high returns and may push for fast growth; hard to obtain |
| **Retained profit** | Profit kept in the business from previous years rather than paid out to the owners | No interest or repayment; no loss of control | **Not available to a new start-up**, which has not yet made profit; using it means the owner takes less money out; may not be enough for big projects |
| **Crowdfunding** | Raising money from a large number of people, each contributing a small amount, usually through an online platform, in return for a reward, early product, a share or interest | Tests demand and builds publicity at the same time; can raise money for ideas banks reject; backers become loyal customers | If the target is not met the money may not be released; the idea is public, so rivals can copy it; platform fees; rewards or shares must be provided |

### Choosing a source: the questions to ask

1. **What is the money for, and for how long?** Match the source to the need. A short-term cash gap suits an overdraft or trade credit; a van or shop fit-out lasting years suits a loan, savings, share capital or crowdfunding. Using an overdraft to buy a van, or a five-year loan to cover one late-paying customer, is a mismatch.
2. **How much is needed?** Savings may cover a small start-up; venture capital and share capital suit larger sums.
3. **What will it cost?** Interest on loans and overdrafts; a share of future profit for share capital and venture capital; fees for crowdfunding.
4. **Does the owner want to keep control?** Loans, overdrafts and savings keep control; share capital and venture capital give some away.
5. **What risk can the owner bear?** Repayments are due whatever happens; equity investors share the risk.
6. **What is available?** A new business has no retained profit and may struggle to get trade credit or a loan without a track record or security. Share capital needs a company.

### Why start-ups find finance hard

A new business has no trading history, no profit record and often few assets, so lenders see it as risky. Banks will want a **business plan** with cash-flow and revenue forecasts (1.4.4), and often **security**. This is why many entrepreneurs rely on personal savings, family and friends, or crowdfunding.

::: terms
- **Source of finance**: where a business obtains the money it needs to start, operate or grow.
- **Short-term finance**: money needed for less than about a year, usually to cover day-to-day cash needs.
- **Long-term finance**: money needed for more than a year, usually for start-up costs or large purchases.
- **Overdraft**: an agreement allowing a business to take its bank account below zero, up to a limit, paying interest on the amount overdrawn.
- **Trade credit**: an arrangement where a supplier lets a business receive goods now and pay for them later.
- **Personal savings**: the owner's own money invested in the business.
- **Loan**: a fixed sum borrowed from a bank and repaid with interest in instalments over an agreed period.
- **Share capital**: money raised by selling shares, which are parts of the ownership of a company.
- **Venture capital**: investment from specialist investors in a small, high-growth business in return for a share of it.
- **Retained profit**: profit kept in the business rather than paid out to the owners.
- **Crowdfunding**: raising money from many people, each giving a small amount, usually through an online platform.
- **Security (collateral)**: an asset a lender can take if a loan is not repaid.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify** the source: short-term (overdraft, trade credit) or long-term (savings, venture capital, share capital, loans, retained profit, crowdfunding).
2. **Explain (3)**: name one advantage or disadvantage (1), then develop it with cost, control, risk or availability (2).
3. **Match source to need**: say what the money is for and for how long, and whether this source fits.
4. **Apply the case**: how much is needed, what the owner has, whether it is a company, whether the owner wants control, how risky the idea is.
5. **Justify (9) between two sources**: choose one; give its benefits for this business (cost, control, fit to purpose) and its drawback; conclude with a "depends on" factor such as how quickly sales will cover repayments, or whether the owner will accept losing some control.
:::

::: example Explain - disadvantage of retained profit
Explain **one** disadvantage to a small business of using retained profit as a source of finance. (3)

The business may not have enough retained profit (1), because a small business, and especially a new one, makes only small profits or none (1), so it may have to delay its investment or still find another source to cover the full cost (1).
:::

::: example Explain - advantage of an overdraft
Explain **one** advantage to a small business of using an overdraft. (3)

It is flexible (1): the business only borrows when its account goes below zero, up to the agreed limit (1), so it pays interest only on the amount and for the days it actually uses, which makes it a cheap way to cover a short cash-flow gap (1).
:::

::: example Outline - source in context
Luca runs Luca's Gelato in Brighton as a sole trader. He needs £2,000 to buy extra stock for a busy bank-holiday weekend. Outline **one** suitable source of finance for Luca. (2)

Trade credit (1): his ingredient supplier could let Luca take the stock now and pay in 30 days, by which time the bank-holiday sales will have brought in the cash to pay the £2,000 (1). Accept an overdraft developed with the short-term need.
:::

::: example Justify - loan or crowdfunding
Sophie wants to open The Board Room, a board-game café in Nottingham. She needs £20,000. Option 1: a five-year bank loan at 9% interest. Option 2: crowdfunding, offering backers free game sessions and membership. Justify which **one** of these options Sophie should choose. (9)

Crowdfunding would raise the £20,000 with no interest or repayments, so Sophie's monthly costs are lower while the café builds its customers (AO2). It also tests demand: if hundreds of local people back the café, she has evidence that it will work and a group of loyal first customers who have already paid for memberships, which brings publicity at no extra cost (AO3a).

However, if the campaign does not reach £20,000 she may receive nothing and lose weeks of time. Giving away free sessions and memberships also means some early revenue has already been spent, which could squeeze cash flow when the café opens (AO3a).

Sophie should choose crowdfunding, because a board-game café is a community idea that suits the publicity and support crowdfunding creates, and avoiding loan repayments reduces her risk in the first year. This depends on whether she already has an online following: if she has few contacts to share the campaign, it may fail, and the loan, with its predictable repayments, would be the more reliable way to raise the money (AO3b).
:::

::: example Discuss - venture capital
Discuss the advantages to a small business of using venture capital. (6)

Venture capitalists can provide large sums to a business with a risky but promising idea, which banks may refuse to lend to because there is no track record or security. Because the investment is in exchange for a share of the business rather than a loan, there are no interest payments or fixed repayments, so the business keeps more cash to spend on growth in its early years.

Venture capitalists also bring experience and contacts. They have often helped other businesses grow, so their advice on marketing, hiring and finding customers can help the business grow faster and avoid mistakes. However, the owner gives up part of the business and some control, and the investors will expect a high return, so the advantages are greatest for an owner who values rapid growth above keeping full control.
:::

::: watch
- **Match the source to the purpose.** Short-term needs (a cash gap, stock) suit overdrafts and trade credit; long-term assets suit loans, savings, share capital or crowdfunding.
- **Retained profit is not available to a start-up**: there is no profit yet.
- **Share capital needs a company.** A sole trader or partnership cannot sell shares (1.4.1).
- Venture capital and share capital mean **giving up some control and future profit**. Loans and overdrafts mean **interest and repayments**.
- An overdraft is not "free money": it has a **high interest rate** and can be withdrawn.
- In a justify, do not just list both sources' advantages. Pick one, weigh it, and conclude with what the choice depends on.
:::
