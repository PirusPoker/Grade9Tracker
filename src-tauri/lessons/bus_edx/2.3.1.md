## What it is

Theme 2 moves from starting a business to **growing** one, and growing a business means making more, or serving more people, without letting costs or quality slip. That is the job of **operations**. Edexcel asks you to know three things:
1. The **purpose of business operations**: to **produce goods** and to **provide services**.
2. The **production processes** (**job, batch and flow**) and their **impact**: keeping **productivity up** and **costs down**, and allowing **competitive prices**.
3. The **impact of technology on production**: how a business **balances cost, productivity, quality and flexibility**.

### The purpose of business operations

**Operations** (or production) is the part of a business that turns **inputs** (materials, labour, machinery, energy) into **outputs** that customers want.
- **Producing goods**: physical products, such as a car, a loaf of bread or a smartphone. Operations here means manufacturing, assembly and packing.
- **Providing services**: intangible products, such as a haircut, a train journey, a bank account or a hotel stay. Operations here means organising staff, premises and systems so the service is delivered quickly, reliably and to the right standard.

Many businesses do both: a restaurant **produces** meals (goods) and **provides** table service (a service). Whichever it is, operations decides **how much it costs** to make each unit, **how good** the product is, and **how quickly** customers get it, which feed straight into price, reputation and profit.

### Production processes: job, batch and flow

| Process | What it is | Typical examples | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Job production** | **One-off** products made individually, often to each customer's own specification | a made-to-measure suit, a wedding cake, a bespoke kitchen, a building, a portrait | high quality and **unique** products; meets each customer's exact needs, so a **premium price** is possible; flexible; skilled work can be motivating | **slow**; needs **skilled** (expensive) labour; **high cost per unit**; no economies of scale; hard to grow output |
| **Batch production** | A **group (batch)** of identical products is made together, then the equipment is changed to make a different batch | a bakery making 200 white loaves then 150 seeded loaves; clothing in different sizes; paint in different colours | some **variety**; lower unit costs than job production; equipment can be shared between products; can respond to demand for each line | **downtime** while machines are cleaned or reset between batches; stock of each batch must be stored; repetitive work; mistakes can affect a whole batch |
| **Flow production** | **Continuous** production of identical (standardised) products along a production line, often automated | cars, soft drinks, chocolate bars, newspapers, tins of beans | **high output**; **lowest cost per unit** because of **economies of scale**; consistent quality; machines can run around the clock | **very expensive** to set up; **inflexible**, as products are identical; if one part of the line breaks, the whole line stops; boring, repetitive work can lower motivation; needs high, steady demand |

**Quick test for which process a business uses**: is each item different (job)? Are identical items made in groups with changeovers (batch)? Or is the same product made non-stop (flow)?

### The impact of production processes

The spec names three impacts. They are linked in a chain:

**Productivity up → costs down → competitive prices**

**Productivity** is the **output per worker** (or per machine, or per hour) in a given period:

**Productivity = total output ÷ number of workers** (per period)

For example, a factory where 40 workers make 6,000 units a week has productivity of 6,000 ÷ 40 = **150 units per worker per week**. If output rises to 7,200 with the same 40 workers, productivity rises to 180, a 20% increase.

Productivity is not the same as total output. If a business takes on 10 more workers and output rises, productivity has only risen if output per worker rises too. Hiring 50 workers to make 7,500 units gives 7,500 ÷ 50 = 150, the same productivity as before.

- **Keeping productivity up**: flow production achieves very high output per worker because tasks are simplified, machines do much of the work, and there is no changeover time. Job production has low productivity because each item takes a skilled worker a long time.
- **Keeping costs down**: higher productivity means the **wage cost of each unit falls**, and bigger output spreads **fixed costs** (rent, machinery) over more units. Flow production benefits most from **economies of scale**, such as bulk-buying materials.
- **Allowing competitive prices**: a lower **cost per unit** lets the business **cut prices** and still make a profit, or keep its price and earn a **higher profit margin**. This is why mass-market goods such as chocolate bars or tins of beans are cheap.

Job production cannot compete on price, so it competes on **quality and uniqueness** instead, charging a premium. Batch sits in the middle.

**Choosing a process** depends on: the **level of demand** (flow needs high, steady demand); how much **variety** customers want; the **finance** available (flow needs large investment); the **skills** of the workforce; and whether the business competes on **price** or on **uniqueness**.

### The impact of technology on production

Technology in production includes **automation** (machines doing tasks without people), **robots** on production lines, **computer-aided design (CAD)**, **computer-aided manufacture (CAM)** and **3D printing**, plus software that tracks orders and stock. For services, technology includes self-service tills, online booking and apps.

The spec asks you to see technology as a **balance** between four things:

| Factor | How technology can help | The trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | lower **labour costs** in the long run; less waste; can run 24 hours | **high initial cost** to buy and install; maintenance and repairs; staff training; possible redundancy costs |
| **Productivity** | machines work faster than people, without breaks or tiredness, so output per worker rises | breakdowns stop production; staff take time to learn new systems, so productivity may **fall at first** |
| **Quality** | **precise, consistent** output with fewer human errors; CAD lets designs be tested before production | if a machine is set wrongly, every unit is faulty; some customers value **hand-made** quality, which machines cannot copy |
| **Flexibility** | **CAD/CAM** and programmable machines can switch designs quickly; 3D printing can make small numbers of custom parts | specialised machines may do only one task, so changing the product means expensive retooling; a line built for one product is **inflexible** |

**Why "balance"?** No business can maximise all four. A fully automated line gives high productivity and consistent quality at a low unit cost, but costs a lot up front and is inflexible. A small craft workshop is flexible and can offer unique quality, but its costs per unit are high. The right balance depends on the business's **size**, its **finance**, its **customers** (do they want low prices or uniqueness?) and its **competitors**.

**Impact on workers**: technology may replace some jobs, causing redundancies and worry among staff (lower motivation), while creating others that need new skills, which links to **retraining** in 2.5.3.

**Link to Theme 1**: investing in technology is a large **fixed cost** (1.3.2a), so it raises the **break-even** level of output in the short run (1.3.2b), but lower **variable costs** per unit can lower break even and raise profit once output is high.

::: terms
- **Operations**: the part of a business that turns inputs into goods or services for customers.
- **Job production**: making one-off, individual products, often to each customer's own specification.
- **Batch production**: making a group of identical products together before switching the equipment to a different product.
- **Flow production**: continuous production of identical, standardised products along a production line.
- **Productivity**: the output per worker (or per machine or per hour) in a given period of time.
- **Cost per unit**: the total cost of production divided by the number of units produced.
- **Economies of scale**: the fall in cost per unit that comes from producing on a larger scale.
- **Automation**: using machines and computer-controlled equipment to carry out tasks without people.
- **Downtime**: time when equipment is not producing, for example while it is reset between batches.
- **Flexibility**: the ability of a business to change what it produces, or how much, quickly and cheaply.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the case for the process**: one-offs (job), groups with changeovers (batch) or continuous identical output (flow). Name it.
2. **Explain questions (3)**: give one point (1), then two linked steps that develop it (1 + 1). For "explain one advantage of flow production", go point → why → effect on the business (costs, prices, sales or profit).
3. **Use the chain** productivity up → cost per unit down → competitive prices or higher margins. Most marks in this topic come from developing along it.
4. **Calculate productivity** as output ÷ workers (or hours). Give the unit: "units per worker per week".
5. **For technology**, always weigh the four factors: cost, productivity, quality and flexibility. A benefit in one usually costs something in another.
6. **Bring in the case**: the product, the size of orders, what customers value (price or uniqueness), the figures given.
7. **Justify (9)**: choose one option; give its benefits, then its drawbacks, applied to the case; conclude with a reason the other option is weaker and a "depends on" factor, such as demand staying high or the cost of finance.
:::

::: example Explain - flow production
Explain **one** advantage to a business of using flow production. (3)

Flow production makes very large numbers of identical products continuously (1), so the business benefits from economies of scale such as bulk-buying materials, and its cost per unit falls (1). This allows it to charge competitive prices and still make a profit, helping it win sales in a mass market (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - productivity
Ferncliff Foods Ltd employs 25 workers who produce 8,000 ready meals per day. After buying a new packing machine, 22 workers produce 8,800 meals per day. Calculate the percentage change in productivity. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. (2)

Before: 8,000 ÷ 25 = 320 meals per worker per day. After: 8,800 ÷ 22 = 400 meals per worker per day (1).
Change = (400 − 320) ÷ 320 × 100 = **25.00%** increase (1).
:::

::: example Outline - batch production
Crumb & Co Ltd is a bakery that supplies sandwich shops in Manchester. It makes white, wholemeal and seeded loaves in separate batches each night. Outline **one** drawback for Crumb & Co of using batch production. (2)

Each night the mixers and ovens must be cleaned and reset between the white, wholemeal and seeded batches, so no bread is made during this downtime (1), which lowers the bakery's productivity and raises its cost per loaf, squeezing the margin on its sales to sandwich shops (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - technology
Hartwell Furniture Ltd makes wooden dining tables in batches for UK retailers. It is considering buying a computer-controlled cutting machine for £180,000, which would cut the parts for each table in half the time.

Analyse the impact on Hartwell Furniture of introducing this technology. (6)

The machine cuts parts in half the time, so each worker can produce more tables per week, raising Hartwell's productivity (AO2). Because the wage cost and the fixed cost of the factory are spread over more tables, the cost per table falls, letting Hartwell offer retailers more competitive prices or earn a higher margin, which could win it bigger orders (AO3a).

However, the £180,000 cost is a large outlay for a batch furniture maker and may need a loan, adding interest to its costs (AO2). Staff will need training and output may dip while they learn the machine, so the benefit to productivity will take time to appear, and if retailers' orders fall, Hartwell may not produce enough tables to recover the cost (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - automation or more staff
Pemberton Drinks plc bottles fruit juice on a flow production line. Demand has risen by 30% and the line is at full capacity.
Option 1: install a robotic bottling line costing £2.4 million that doubles capacity and needs 10 fewer workers.
Option 2: run an extra night shift, hiring 35 more workers at a cost of £900,000 a year.

Justify which **one** of these two options Pemberton Drinks should choose. (9)

Installing the robotic line doubles capacity, which is more than enough to meet the 30% rise in demand and leaves room to grow (AO2). Robots work faster and more consistently than people, so productivity rises and fewer bottling errors occur, and with 10 fewer workers the wage cost per bottle falls, letting Pemberton offer supermarkets competitive prices while protecting its margin (AO3a).

However, £2.4 million is a large investment and must be financed, possibly with a loan adding interest, and if the line breaks down the whole of production stops (AO2). Cutting 10 jobs could worry remaining staff and lower motivation, and it will take time to install and train staff, so Pemberton may lose orders in the meantime (AO3a).

Pemberton should choose the robotic line. The night shift costs £900,000 every year, so after under three years it costs more than the robots, and it does not lower the cost per bottle. However, this depends on the 30% rise in demand lasting; if it is only a short-term trend, the night shift is less risky because staff can be let go when demand falls (AO3b).
:::

---
1 mark plus up to 2, developing how. ::: q
Discuss the impact on a business of moving from batch production to flow production. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):
- Productivity rises: no changeovers between batches, continuous output, more machines.
- Cost per unit falls through economies of scale, allowing competitive prices or higher margins.
- But very high set-up cost, needing finance; break-even output rises in the short run.
- Loss of variety: customers wanting different versions may go elsewhere; the line is inflexible.
- Demand must be high and steady; repetitive work may reduce motivation.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): accurate understanding; two developed lines of reasoning.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.

::: q
Brightwater Cosmetics plc makes hand cream in batches of different scents for UK chemists. Demand for its lavender cream has grown to 60% of total sales, and the company is considering a dedicated flow line for it.

Analyse the benefits to Brightwater of using flow production for its lavender hand cream. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: hand cream made in batches; lavender is 60% of sales; sells to UK chemists.
- AO3a: with high, steady demand for one product, a flow line avoids downtime from changing scents, so productivity rises; cost per tube falls through economies of scale such as bulk-buying lavender oil and tubes; Brightwater can offer chemists a lower price or keep a higher margin; consistent quality on a dedicated line; other scents can continue in batches, keeping variety.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Brightwater throughout.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.
:::

::: watch
- **Productivity is output per worker**, not total output. More output from more workers is not higher productivity unless output per worker rises.
- Name the process correctly: identical items in **groups** with changeovers is **batch**, not flow.
- Develop to the business: productivity → **cost per unit** → **price or margin** → sales or profit. Stopping at "it is faster" gets 1 mark.
- For technology, do not write only benefits. The spec says **balancing**: cost, productivity, quality and flexibility pull against each other.
- An "explain one way" question needs **how** it works, not why it is good.
- Percentage change divides by the **original** figure.
- In a justify, choose **one** option and weigh it; describing the benefits of both is not evaluation.
:::
