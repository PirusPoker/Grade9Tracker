## What it is

As a business grows it employs more people, and it has to decide how to **organise** them, how they will **communicate**, and on what terms they will **work**. Edexcel asks for three things:
1. **Different organisational structures and when each is appropriate**: **hierarchical** and **flat**; **centralised** and **decentralised**.
2. **The importance of effective communication**: the impact of **insufficient or excessive communication** on **efficiency** and **motivation**, and the **barriers to effective communication**.
3. **Different ways of working**: **part-time, full-time and flexible hours**; **permanent, temporary and freelance contracts**; and the **impact of technology on ways of working**: **efficiency** and **remote working**.

### Organisational structure: the basic terms

An **organisational structure** shows who is in charge of whom, how jobs are grouped, and how information and decisions pass through the business. It is often drawn as an **organisation chart**.

- **Hierarchy**: the levels of authority in a business, from the top (directors) to the bottom (operational staff).
- **Chain of command**: the line of authority through which instructions pass **down** and information passes **up**.
- **Span of control**: the number of employees **directly** supervised by one manager. A **narrow** span (say 3–4) means close supervision; a **wide** span (say 10+) means more freedom for staff.
- **Delayering**: removing one or more **levels** of management, making a structure flatter.

### Hierarchical and flat structures

A **hierarchical (tall)** structure has **many levels** of management, a **long chain of command** and **narrow spans of control**. A **flat** structure has **few levels**, a **short chain of command** and **wide spans of control**.

| | Hierarchical (tall) | Flat |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | close supervision, so fewer mistakes; clear roles and lines of responsibility; many levels give **promotion** opportunities, which can motivate; managers have time for each worker | **faster communication** (fewer layers for messages to pass through); **lower costs** (fewer managers' salaries); staff have more **responsibility and freedom**, which can motivate; managers are closer to customers and workers |
| **Disadvantages** | **slow communication and decisions**, as messages pass through many layers and may be distorted; **expensive**, with many managers; staff at the bottom may feel remote and closely controlled, lowering motivation | managers have wide spans, so they may be **overloaded** and unable to supervise or support everyone; fewer promotion opportunities; roles can be less clear; staff need to be skilled and self-motivated |
| **When appropriate** | large businesses with many staff; where close control matters (safety, finance, consistency across branches); staff who are less experienced | small or growing businesses; creative or fast-changing markets where quick decisions matter; skilled, experienced staff |

### Centralised and decentralised structures

This is about **where decisions are made**.
- **Centralised**: most important decisions are made by **senior managers at head office** and passed down. Branches follow head office's instructions.
- **Decentralised**: decision-making authority is **delegated** to managers lower down or in branches, regions or departments.

| | Centralised | Decentralised |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | **consistency**: every branch offers the same products, prices and standards, protecting the brand; decisions made by experienced senior managers; tight **control of costs**; bulk-buying for all branches lowers costs | local managers know **local customers and conditions**, so decisions suit the area; **faster** responses as there is no need to wait for head office; managers given responsibility may be more **motivated**; senior managers have more time for strategy |
| **Disadvantages** | slow to react to local needs; head office may not understand local markets; branch managers may feel powerless, lowering motivation; head office can be overloaded | **less consistency** between branches, which can confuse customers and weaken the brand; local managers may make poor decisions or decisions that do not fit the overall aims; harder to control costs; loses some bulk-buying benefits |
| **When appropriate** | chains where a consistent brand is the selling point; when tight control is needed (crisis, cost-cutting) | businesses in very different markets or countries; when quick local decisions matter; skilled local managers |

### Effective communication

**Communication** is passing information from a sender to a receiver, who understands it. It is **effective** when the right message reaches the right people at the right time, in a form they understand.

**Why it matters**: staff know what to do and why, so work is done correctly and efficiently; departments coordinate (sales tells production what to make); managers hear about problems early; staff feel informed and involved, which supports **motivation**.

**The impact of insufficient communication** (too little):
- On **efficiency**: staff do not know what to do or about changes, so they make **mistakes**, **duplicate** work or wait for instructions; departments are not coordinated; customers get wrong information.
- On **motivation**: staff feel **ignored and undervalued**; rumours spread; uncertainty about the future causes stress and can lead to higher **staff turnover**.

**The impact of excessive communication** (too much):
- On **efficiency**: **information overload**: staff spend so long reading emails and attending meetings that less real work gets done; important messages get **lost** among unimportant ones; decisions are slowed down.
- On **motivation**: staff feel **stressed** and micro-managed; constant messages outside working hours cause frustration and tiredness.

**Barriers to effective communication**:
- **Jargon** or technical language the receiver does not understand.
- **Too many layers** in the hierarchy, so messages are slow and get distorted as they pass down.
- **Distance and time zones**, especially with remote workers or overseas branches.
- **Language** differences.
- **The wrong method (medium)**: an important change sent in a long email instead of being explained face to face.
- **Information overload**: too many messages, so key ones are missed.
- **Attitudes**: the receiver does not trust the sender or is not interested; poor relationships between managers and staff.
- **Technology failures** and noise or interruptions.

**Reducing barriers**: plain language; fewer layers (delayering); choosing the right method; regular team briefings; two-way communication with chances to ask questions; training in communication skills.

### Different ways of working

**Hours**:
- **Full-time**: usually around 35–40 hours a week. Staff are committed and available, but are a bigger fixed cost.
- **Part-time**: fewer hours than full-time. Gives the business **flexibility** to cover busy periods (lunchtimes, weekends) and attracts people such as students or parents; but part-time staff may be less available for training or meetings, and handovers between staff can cause mistakes.
- **Flexible hours**: staff can vary when they work (for example start and finish times, or working longer days and fewer of them), within limits. It helps **attract and retain** staff with other commitments and can raise **motivation**, but makes it harder to arrange meetings and ensure cover.

**Contracts**:
- **Permanent**: no end date. Gives staff **security** and loyalty, so the business keeps skills and trained staff; but harder to reduce staff numbers if demand falls.
- **Temporary**: for a fixed period or task (seasonal staff at Christmas, cover for maternity leave). **Flexibility** to match staff to demand; but temporary staff may be less committed and need training each time.
- **Freelance**: self-employed people hired for a specific job (designers, IT specialists, writers). The business gets **specialist skills** when needed, without employment costs such as holiday pay; but freelancers may charge high rates, work for rivals too, and may not be available when needed.

### The impact of technology on ways of working

- **Efficiency**: software automates routine tasks (payroll, stock ordering, scheduling); cloud systems let staff share documents instantly; video calls replace travel; data is available immediately for decisions. This raises productivity but may need training and investment, and can reduce the number of jobs.
- **Remote working**: staff work from home or elsewhere, using laptops, the internet and video calls.

| Benefits of remote working | Drawbacks of remote working |
|---|---|
| lower **costs** of office space; can recruit from a **wider area**, finding better staff; no commuting, so staff may be more **motivated** and productive; helps retain staff who value flexibility | harder to **communicate** and **supervise**; staff may feel **isolated**, lowering motivation; harder to build team spirit and train new staff; IT and **data security** risks; some staff find it hard to separate work and home life |

Technology also makes communication possible **all the time**, which can lead to **excessive communication** if it is not managed.

::: terms
- **Organisational structure**: the way a business arranges its people, levels of authority and lines of communication.
- **Hierarchical structure**: a tall structure with many levels of management and narrow spans of control.
- **Flat structure**: a structure with few levels of management and wide spans of control.
- **Chain of command**: the line of authority through which instructions pass down and information passes up.
- **Span of control**: the number of employees directly supervised by one manager.
- **Centralised structure**: a structure in which most important decisions are made by senior managers at head office.
- **Decentralised structure**: a structure in which decision-making authority is delegated to managers in branches, regions or departments.
- **Delayering**: removing one or more levels of management from a structure.
- **Information overload**: receiving so much information that important messages are missed and work is slowed.
- **Freelance contract**: an agreement to pay a self-employed person to carry out a specific piece of work.
- **Flexible hours**: an arrangement allowing employees to vary when they work, within agreed limits.
- **Remote working**: carrying out work away from the business's premises, usually from home, using technology.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the structure** and use its features: levels, chain of command, span of control (hierarchical and flat); where decisions are made (centralised and decentralised).
2. **Explain (3)**: point → effect on communication, decisions, costs or motivation → effect on the business (efficiency, customers, profit, staff turnover).
3. **Communication questions**: say whether the problem is **too little** or **too much**, then develop its effect on **efficiency** and on **motivation** separately.
4. **Ways of working**: compare cost, flexibility and commitment. Temporary and part-time give flexibility; permanent gives commitment; freelance gives specialist skills.
5. **Use the case**: number of staff and branches, how different local markets are, how fast the market changes, the skills of staff.
6. **Justify (9)**: choose one option, weigh its benefits and drawbacks for this business, then conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as how different local markets are or how skilled branch managers are.
:::

::: example Explain - flat structure
Explain **one** disadvantage to a business of having a flat organisational structure. (3)

Each manager has a wide span of control, supervising many employees (1), so they have less time to support, train or check the work of each person (1), which can lead to mistakes and staff feeling unsupported, lowering productivity (1).
:::

::: example Explain - flexible hours
Explain **one** benefit to a business of offering employees flexible hours. (3)

Flexible hours let employees fit work around commitments such as childcare (1), so they feel trusted and valued and are less likely to leave (1), which reduces the business's staff turnover and the cost of recruiting and training replacements (1).
:::

::: example Outline - excessive communication
Wexford Insurance plc's 900 office staff receive an average of 120 internal emails a day. Outline **one** impact of this on Wexford Insurance. (2)

With 120 emails a day, staff spend a large part of their time reading messages instead of handling customers' insurance claims (1), so claims are processed more slowly and important messages may be missed, reducing efficiency (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - decentralisation
Corran Garden Centres plc has 45 garden centres in very different parts of the UK, from city edges to rural areas. All decisions on product ranges, prices and promotions are made at head office. Sales at many centres are falling.

Analyse the benefits to Corran Garden Centres of decentralising decision-making. (6)

Corran's 45 centres are in very different areas, so a single head-office range may not suit both city and rural customers (AO2). If centre managers can choose part of their product range and local promotions, they can stock what their customers actually want, such as small-space plants for city flats, which could reverse falling sales (AO3a).

Decentralising also means managers can react quickly to local conditions, for example running a promotion when the weather turns warm, without waiting for head office (AO2). Being trusted to make decisions can motivate centre managers, encouraging them to take ownership of their centre's results, which could raise sales and reduce manager turnover (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - centralise or decentralise
Lindqvist Burgers plc has 160 restaurants in the UK. At present each restaurant manager chooses suppliers, sets some prices and designs local menus. Customer complaints about inconsistent food quality have risen by 30%.
Option 1: centralise decisions on menus, suppliers and prices at head office.
Option 2: keep decentralised decisions, but train all 160 managers in quality standards.

Justify which **one** of these two options Lindqvist Burgers should choose. (9)

Centralising menus and suppliers means every restaurant uses the same ingredients and recipes, so customers get the same burger wherever they eat, tackling the 30% rise in complaints (AO2). Head office can also negotiate bulk deals with a few suppliers for all 160 restaurants, lowering the cost of ingredients and raising margins (AO3a).

However, managers who are used to making decisions may feel less trusted and less motivated, and some may leave (AO2). Head office will also be slower to respond to local tastes, so restaurants in areas with different customers may lose some sales (AO3a).

Lindqvist should centralise, because in a restaurant chain a consistent product is what customers expect, and training alone may not stop 160 managers choosing different suppliers. This depends on head office still asking managers for feedback, so that local knowledge is not lost and managers stay motivated (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Excessive communication is on the spec.** More communication is not always better: overload wastes time and lowers motivation.
- Separate the two impacts the spec names: **efficiency** and **motivation**.
- Hierarchical and flat is about **levels and spans of control**; centralised and decentralised is about **where decisions are made**. Do not mix them up.
- A flat structure has **wide** spans of control, not narrow ones.
- **Freelance** workers are self-employed specialists, not temporary employees.
- Always link ways of working to the business: costs, flexibility to match demand, motivation and retention.
- For "when appropriate", use the case: size, how different the local markets are, how fast decisions are needed.
:::
