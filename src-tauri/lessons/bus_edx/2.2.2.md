## What it is

**Price** is the amount customers pay for a product. It is the only element of the marketing mix that directly brings in **revenue**; the others all cost money. Setting the right price matters: too high and customers buy from rivals; too low and the business may not cover its costs, or customers may doubt the quality.

The spec asks for two things:
1. **Pricing strategies**: the approaches businesses use to set prices.
2. **Influences on pricing strategies**: technology, competition, market segments and the product life cycle.

Remember from 1.3.2a: **revenue = price × quantity**. A change in price changes revenue in two ways: through the price itself and through the number of units customers buy.

### Pricing strategies

The specification does not list named strategies, so the ones below are the standard set used at GCSE. Learn what each one is, when it suits, and its drawbacks.

**Penetration pricing**
- Setting a **low price** when a product is launched, to attract customers quickly and **gain market share**, then raising the price once customers are loyal.
- Suits: entering a market with strong competition; products where customers easily switch.
- Drawbacks: low margins at first; customers may leave when the price rises; a low price can suggest low quality.

**Price skimming**
- Setting a **high price** when a product is launched, while it is **new and has few or no competitors**, then lowering the price over time to reach more customers.
- Suits: innovative or technology products, where early adopters will pay more to be first.
- Drawbacks: attracts competitors to the market because of the high margins; may limit early sales; early buyers may feel cheated when the price drops.

**Competitive pricing**
- Setting a price **similar to competitors'** prices, slightly below, the same, or slightly above.
- Suits: markets with many similar products, where customers compare prices easily.
- Drawbacks: the business must compete on other parts of the mix; the price may not cover its own costs if they are higher than rivals'.

**Cost-plus pricing**
- Adding a **percentage mark-up** to the cost of making or buying the product.
- Selling price = cost per unit + (cost per unit × mark-up %).
- **Worked example**: a product costs £16 to make and the business adds a mark-up of 40%. Mark-up = 16 × 0.40 = £6.40. Selling price = 16 + 6.40 = **£22.40**.
- Suits: simple to calculate; ensures every sale covers its costs.
- Drawbacks: ignores what customers will pay and what competitors charge; inefficiency is passed on as a higher price.

**Premium pricing**
- Setting a **high price** to reflect and reinforce a **high-quality** or exclusive image.
- Suits: strong brands, luxury products, well-differentiated products (2.2.1).
- Drawbacks: limits the market to customers who can afford it; must be backed by genuine quality, service and promotion.

**Psychological pricing**
- Setting prices that **seem** lower than they are, such as **£9.99** instead of £10.
- Suits: everyday consumer products, especially in retail.
- Drawbacks: less suitable for premium brands, where round numbers can suggest quality.

**Promotional pricing and loss leaders** (in brief)
- **Promotional pricing**: temporary price cuts or special offers (for example "buy one get one free") to boost sales for a short time.
- **Loss leader**: selling a product **below cost** to attract customers who then buy other, profitable products.

| Strategy | Price level | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Penetration | Low at launch, then rises | New entrant in a competitive market |
| Skimming | High at launch, then falls | Innovative product with no rivals |
| Competitive | Around rivals' prices | Similar products, price-aware customers |
| Cost-plus | Cost + mark-up | Making sure every sale covers costs |
| Premium | Consistently high | Luxury or strongly differentiated brands |
| Psychological | Just below a round number | Everyday retail products |

### Influences on pricing strategies

**Technology**
- New technology in **production** can **lower costs** (automation, more efficient machines), allowing lower prices or higher margins.
- **E-commerce** lets customers **compare prices instantly** online, so prices must be competitive and price differences are harder to sustain.
- Technology allows **dynamic pricing**: online businesses can change prices quickly according to demand, time or customer.
- New technology products often start with **skimming** because they are innovative.

**Competition**
- With **many close competitors**, prices must be close to rivals' (competitive pricing), or a business risks losing customers.
- With **few competitors** or a strong USP, the business has more freedom to charge higher prices.
- A **new entrant** may use penetration pricing to win market share from established firms.

**Market segments**
- Different segments will pay different prices. A business may offer a **budget range** for price-sensitive segments and a **premium range** for higher-income segments.
- Prices may differ by age (student or senior discounts), location or time (peak and off-peak).
- The price must match what the **target segment** expects and can afford.

**The product life cycle (2.2.1)**
- **Introduction**: skimming (high) or penetration (low).
- **Growth**: price may be reduced as competitors enter and costs fall with higher output.
- **Maturity**: competitive pricing to defend market share; promotional pricing.
- **Decline**: price cuts to clear stock, or a higher price for a few loyal customers before withdrawal.

### Choosing a pricing strategy

A good choice fits:
- the **costs** of the product (the price must cover them in the long run);
- the **competition** in the market;
- the **target segment** and what it will pay;
- the **stage** of the product life cycle;
- the **rest of the marketing mix** (2.2.5): a premium product with premium promotion needs a premium price.

::: terms
- **Pricing strategy**: the approach a business uses to set the price of its product.
- **Penetration pricing**: setting a low price at launch to gain market share quickly, then raising it later.
- **Price skimming**: setting a high price at launch while a product is new and has few rivals, then lowering it over time.
- **Competitive pricing**: setting a price similar to the prices charged by competitors.
- **Cost-plus pricing**: setting a price by adding a percentage mark-up to the cost per unit.
- **Mark-up**: the amount or percentage added to the cost of a product to give its selling price.
- **Premium pricing**: setting a high price to reflect and reinforce a high-quality or exclusive image.
- **Psychological pricing**: setting a price just below a round number so that it seems lower, such as £9.99.
- **Loss leader**: a product sold below cost to attract customers who then buy other, profitable products.
- **Price-sensitive**: describes customers whose buying changes a lot when the price changes.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the strategy** and define it briefly using its key feature (low at launch, high at launch, near rivals, cost plus mark-up).
2. **Link it to an influence in the case**: technology, competition, market segment or product life cycle stage.
3. **Develop to money**: what the price does to the number of units sold, revenue, profit margin or market share.
4. **For cost-plus calculations**, find the mark-up (cost × percentage), then add it to the cost. Give the unit and check the answer is bigger than the cost.
5. **Apply the case**: the product's newness, rivals' prices, the target customers and the business's costs.
6. **Justify or evaluate**: choose one strategy, give its benefits and drawbacks, then conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as how quickly rivals will copy or how price-sensitive the segment is.
:::

::: example Calculate - cost-plus
A business makes garden benches at a cost of £85 each. It uses cost-plus pricing with a mark-up of 60%. Calculate the selling price of one bench. (2)

Mark-up £85 × 0.60 = £51 (1); selling price £85 + £51 = £136 (1). £136 scores 2 without working.
:::

::: example Calculate - average price from a table
A cinema chain sold the following tickets in one week:

| Child | 1,500 | £10,500 |

Calculate the average price per ticket across all tickets sold. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. (2)

::: example Explain - price skimming
Explain **one** advantage to a business of using price skimming. (3)

A high launch price targets early adopters who are willing to pay more to own a new product first (1), so each unit sold earns a large profit margin (1), which helps the business recover its research and development costs quickly (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - influence of competition
Brewhouse Coffee plc runs 400 coffee shops in a market with many large rival chains and independent cafés. Analyse how competition influences Brewhouse Coffee's pricing strategy. (6)

With many rival chains and cafés nearby, Brewhouse's customers can easily walk to another coffee shop if its prices are higher (AO2). This means it is likely to use competitive pricing, keeping its prices close to rivals', because a higher price would lose customers and sales across its 400 shops (AO3a).

Because it cannot easily charge more than rivals, Brewhouse must compete on other parts of the mix, such as the quality of its coffee, its loyalty app or shop locations (AO2). Competition also limits how far it can pass on rising costs, such as milk and coffee beans, so its profit margin may be squeezed unless it cuts costs elsewhere (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - choosing a strategy
Lumio Tech plc has developed a new smart bike light that automatically signals turns. No competitor offers anything similar yet. Option 1: price skimming at £120. Option 2: penetration pricing at £45. Justify which **one** of these options Lumio Tech should choose. (9)

Price skimming at £120 suits Lumio's light because it is innovative and has no competitors yet (AO2). Keen cyclists who want the latest safety technology will pay a high price, so Lumio earns a large margin on each unit and quickly recovers the cost of developing the light (AO3a).

However, the high margin will attract rivals, and similar lights could appear within a year or two at lower prices. At £120 many casual cyclists will not buy, so Lumio may sell fewer units and build less brand awareness before competitors arrive (AO3a).

Lumio should use price skimming, because the light is new and unique, and it can lower the price later as rivals enter, reaching more cyclists then. This depends on how easily the technology can be copied: if Lumio has a patent protecting its design, skimming can last longer, but if rivals can copy it quickly, penetration pricing would build market share before they arrive (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- Name the strategy **and tie it to the influence in the case**. A definition on its own scores little.
- **Skimming** is high then low, for new products with no rivals. **Penetration** is low then high, to win market share. Do not swap them.
- Cost-plus: the mark-up is a percentage **of cost**. Add it to the cost; do not take it off.
- The spec's influences are **technology, competition, market segments and the product life cycle**. Use these words.
- A price change affects **both** price and quantity, so revenue may rise or fall. Do not assume a price cut always raises revenue.
- Average price = total revenue ÷ total units. Check the question for "2 decimal places".
:::
