## What it is

**Legislation** is the body of laws passed by Parliament. Businesses must follow laws that protect **consumers** (the people who buy their products) and **employees** (the people who work for them). Edexcel examines the **principles** of these laws, not the details of particular Acts: what a business must do, what it costs, and what happens if it does or does not meet its obligations.

### The purpose of legislation

Without legislation, some businesses might sell unsafe or faulty products, mislead customers, pay unfairly low wages or put workers at risk to cut costs. Laws exist to:
- **protect consumers**, who usually know less about a product than the seller;
- **protect employees**, who have less power than their employer;
- create **fair competition**, so honest businesses are not undercut by those that cut corners.

### Principles of consumer law

Consumer law covers **quality** and **consumer rights**.

**Quality.** Goods sold to consumers must be:
- of **satisfactory quality**: not faulty or damaged, and lasting a reasonable time for the price paid;
- **fit for purpose**: able to do the job they are sold for, including any purpose the customer told the seller about;
- **as described**: matching the description, photos, sample or labelling given.

Services must be carried out with **reasonable care and skill**, within a reasonable time and at the price agreed.

**Consumer rights.** If goods are faulty, not fit for purpose or not as described, the customer is entitled to a **remedy**:
- a **refund** if the fault is reported shortly after purchase (30 days under current UK law);
- after that, a **repair or replacement**, and if that fails, a refund or a price reduction.

Consumers are also protected from **misleading** descriptions, prices or advertising, and products must be **safe**. When customers buy online they usually have a right to cancel within a short period after delivery.

The main Act in this area is the **Consumer Rights Act 2015**, but you do not need Act names in the exam.

### Principles of employment law

Employment law covers **recruitment, pay, discrimination and health and safety**.

| Area | What the business must do |
|---|---|
| **Recruitment** | Recruit fairly: job adverts, interviews and selection must not discriminate; employees must receive written terms of employment (pay, hours, holidays); businesses must check that a new employee has the right to work in the UK |
| **Pay** | Pay at least the **National Minimum Wage**, or the **National Living Wage** for older workers, which rise most years; give **equal pay** to men and women doing equal work; give paid holiday |
| **Discrimination** | Not treat anyone less favourably because of **protected characteristics** such as age, disability, race, religion or belief, sex, sexual orientation, pregnancy or gender reassignment, in recruitment, pay, training, promotion or dismissal; make reasonable adjustments for disabled employees |
| **Health and safety** | Provide a safe workplace: assess risks, provide training and protective equipment, maintain equipment, report serious accidents; employees must also follow safety rules |

Well-known Acts include the **Equality Act 2010** and the **Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974**. Again, principles matter more than names.

### The impact of legislation on businesses

**Cost.** Meeting the law costs money and time:
- higher **wage costs** when the minimum wage rises;
- **training** staff in safety, food hygiene or equality;
- **safety equipment**, risk assessments, guards on machinery, fire safety;
- **adjustments** for disabled employees or customers;
- **refunds, repairs and replacements** for faulty goods;
- **quality control** to make sure products meet standards;
- **administration**: contracts, records, checks, legal advice.

For a small business these costs can be a large share of revenue, which raises its prices or cuts its profit.

**Consequences of meeting obligations:**
- **Trust and reputation**: customers return and recommend a business that treats them fairly.
- **Motivated, loyal employees** who feel safe and fairly treated, so lower staff turnover and recruitment costs.
- **Fewer accidents**, less absence and fewer disruptions.
- Avoiding the costs of legal action.
- A wider pool of applicants when recruitment is fair, so the best person gets the job.

**Consequences of not meeting obligations:**
- **Fines** and **compensation** to customers or employees, plus **legal costs**; employees can take cases to an **employment tribunal**.
- **Bad publicity**, which damages reputation and sales, especially when stories spread on social media.
- **Lost customers** and difficulty recruiting staff.
- In serious cases, **prosecution**, **closure** by inspectors (for example on hygiene grounds) or a business being banned from operating.

The **cost of compliance** is usually much lower than the **cost of breaking the law**, but a business must still budget for it.

::: terms
- **Legislation**: laws passed by Parliament that businesses and individuals must follow.
- **Consumer law**: laws that protect customers, covering the quality of goods and services and their rights when things go wrong.
- **Satisfactory quality**: goods are free from faults and last a reasonable time for their price.
- **Fit for purpose**: goods can do the job they are sold for.
- **Consumer rights**: customers' legal entitlements to a refund, repair or replacement when goods are faulty or not as described.
- **Employment law**: laws that protect employees, covering recruitment, pay, discrimination and health and safety.
- **National Minimum Wage**: the lowest hourly rate that employers can legally pay workers, set by the government.
- **Discrimination**: treating someone less favourably because of a protected characteristic such as age, sex, race or disability.
- **Health and safety law**: laws requiring employers to protect the health, safety and welfare of employees at work.
- **Employment tribunal**: a legal body that hears disputes between employees and employers, such as unfair dismissal or discrimination claims.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the law area**: consumer law (quality, consumer rights) or employment law (recruitment, pay, discrimination, health and safety).
2. **Say what the business must do** in plain terms, such as pay at least the minimum wage, or provide a refund for faulty goods.
3. **Explain the impact**: the **cost** of meeting it (wages, training, equipment, refunds) and the effect on profit or prices.
4. **Consequences**: meeting it builds trust and motivation; not meeting it brings fines, compensation, tribunals and bad publicity.
5. **Apply the case**: what the business sells, how many staff it has, how a cost rise affects its margins.
6. **Justify and evaluate**: weigh the cost of a new legal requirement against ways to absorb or pass it on (prices, efficiency) and the benefits of compliance; conclude with a "depends on" factor such as how price-sensitive customers are.
:::

::: example Explain - impact of a minimum wage rise
Explain **one** disadvantage to a small business of a rise in the National Minimum Wage. (3)

Its wage costs rise (1), because it must pay all staff on the minimum rate at least the new, higher amount (1), so unless it raises prices or cuts staff hours, its profit falls (1).
:::

::: example State - meeting employment law
Lou runs Lou's Laundry in Barnsley and is recruiting two part-time staff. State **one** way Lou must meet employment law. (1)

She must not discriminate against applicants because of characteristics such as age, sex, race or disability (1). (Or: pay at least the minimum wage; give written terms; provide a safe workplace; check the right to work.)
:::

::: example Explain - consumer law
Explain **one** way consumer law affects a business that sells goods. (3)

Goods must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described (1), so if a customer returns a faulty item the business must give a refund, repair or replacement (1), which adds costs, so the business has an incentive to check quality before goods are sold (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - health and safety in context
Harry runs Harry's Joinery, a workshop in Swansea with five employees using power saws. Analyse the impact of health and safety law on Harry's Joinery. (6)

Harry must make the workshop safe, for example by fitting guards to saws, providing protective equipment such as goggles and ear defenders, and training staff to use machinery (AO2). This costs money and time away from production, which raises Harry's costs and could reduce profit in the short term, especially as a small business with only five employees (AO3a).

However, joinery is a high-risk activity, so meeting the law protects Harry's business as well as his staff (AO2). A serious accident could injure an employee, lead to compensation, a fine and possibly the workshop being closed by inspectors, and an experienced joiner off work would slow production. Meeting the law reduces these risks and shows staff that Harry cares, so they are more likely to stay, saving recruitment and training costs (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - responding to a wage rise
Cosy Corner is a café in Blackpool with six staff paid the minimum wage. The minimum wage is rising, adding £9,000 a year to wage costs. Option 1: raise menu prices by 5%. Option 2: cut staff hours, especially on quiet weekday afternoons. Justify which **one** of these options Cosy Corner should choose. (9)

Raising prices by 5% passes the extra cost on to customers, so the café keeps its staffing and level of service (AO2). Other cafés face the same wage rise and are likely to raise their prices too, so customers may accept a small increase without switching, protecting Cosy Corner's profit (AO3a).

However, in a seaside town with many cafés, some customers are price-sensitive and may go elsewhere, especially if a rival chooses not to raise prices. If sales fall, the higher price may not raise enough revenue to cover the £9,000 (AO3a).

Cosy Corner should raise prices, because cutting hours reduces staff income and could lead to slower service, harming the café's reputation for the sake of a small saving on quiet afternoons. This depends on how much revenue the café makes: if a 5% rise brings in much more than £9,000, a smaller rise would cover the cost and keep prices competitive (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- Edexcel tests **principles**, not Act names or dates. Say what the business must **do** and what happens if it does not.
- Keep **consumer law** (quality, refunds, repair, replacement) separate from **employment law** (recruitment, pay, discrimination, health and safety).
- The impact has two sides: the **cost** of meeting obligations and the **consequences** of meeting or not meeting them. Cover both.
- Develop consequences to **money**: fines, compensation, lost sales, recruitment costs.
- A minimum wage rise affects **costs** for the business and **income** for employees and customers. Read whose impact the question asks about.
:::
