## What it is

In Theme 1 (1.3.2a) you calculated **revenue**, **costs** and **profit** for a small business. Theme 2 takes this further. Edexcel asks for:
1. The **concept and calculation** of **gross profit** and **net profit**.
2. The **calculation and interpretation** of the **gross profit margin**, the **net profit margin** and the **average rate of return (ARR)**.

Every Paper 2 has calculation questions worth 2 marks each, and these formulae are some of the most common. **Formulae are not given in the exam**, so you must learn them word for word. A calculator is allowed.

### The formulae (learn these exactly)

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Gross profit** | gross profit = sales revenue − cost of sales |
| **Gross profit margin** | gross profit margin (%) = gross profit ÷ sales revenue × 100 |
| **Net profit** | net profit = gross profit − other operating expenses and interest |
| **Net profit margin** | net profit margin (%) = net profit ÷ sales revenue × 100 |
| **Average rate of return** | average rate of return (%) = average annual profit (total profit ÷ number of years) ÷ cost of investment × 100 |

### Gross profit

**Sales revenue** is the money from selling products: price × quantity sold (1.3.2a).

**Cost of sales** is the **direct cost** of making or buying the products that were actually **sold**: raw materials, components, or stock bought for resale, and (in manufacturing) the wages of the people making the product. For a shop it is what it paid its suppliers for the goods it sold.

**Gross profit** is the profit made from **trading** (buying or making and selling) **before** the other costs of running the business are taken off:

**gross profit = sales revenue − cost of sales**

### Net profit

**Other operating expenses** (overheads) are the costs of running the business that are not part of cost of sales: rent, salaries of office and management staff, marketing, insurance, utilities, and depreciation. **Interest** is the cost of borrowing on loans and overdrafts (1.3.2a, 1.3.4).

**Net profit** is what is left after **all** costs have been taken off:

**net profit = gross profit − other operating expenses and interest**

Net profit is the **"bottom line"**: it shows how profitable the whole business is. It can be kept as **retained profit** to fund growth (2.1.2) or paid to owners or shareholders.

### Profit margins

A profit figure on its own does not tell you much: £500,000 profit is excellent for a business with £2 million sales but poor for one with £200 million. **Margins** show profit as a **percentage of sales revenue**, which lets you **compare** businesses of different sizes, or one business over several years.

**gross profit margin (%) = gross profit ÷ sales revenue × 100**

**net profit margin (%) = net profit ÷ sales revenue × 100**

A gross profit margin of 40% means that for every £1 of sales, 40p is left after paying for the goods sold. A net profit margin of 8% means 8p of every £1 of sales is profit once **all** costs are paid.

### Worked example: income statement

Tarn Outdoor plc sells camping equipment. Its figures for the year:

| Item | £ |
|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 4,800,000 |
| Cost of sales | 2,880,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **1,920,000** |
| Other operating expenses | 1,150,000 |
| Interest | 130,000 |
| **Net profit** | **640,000** |

- Gross profit = £4,800,000 − £2,880,000 = **£1,920,000**
- Gross profit margin = £1,920,000 ÷ £4,800,000 × 100 = **40.00%**
- Net profit = £1,920,000 − £1,150,000 − £130,000 = **£640,000**
- Net profit margin = £640,000 ÷ £4,800,000 × 100 = 13.333…% = **13.33%** (to 2 decimal places)

**Two decimal places**: Edexcel often asks for this. Do not round until the very last step, and write both decimal places, even if they are zeros (40.00%).

### Interpreting margins

**A higher margin is better**, but always compare: with last year, with competitors, with the business's targets.

**Why the gross profit margin changes**:
- **Rises** if the business raises its **prices** or finds **cheaper suppliers** (or gets bulk discounts, 2.1.2 economies of scale).
- **Falls** if **supplier costs rise** (for example materials or energy), or the business **cuts prices** to compete, or sells more low-margin products.

**Why the net profit margin changes**: everything that affects the gross margin, **plus** changes in **other operating expenses and interest**. Higher rent, more staff, a big marketing campaign or a new loan all reduce the net profit margin.

**The key interpretation skill**: compare the two margins over time.
- **Gross margin steady but net margin falling** → the problem is **not** in buying or pricing; it is in **overheads or interest** (expenses are growing faster than sales).
- **Both margins falling** → the problem starts with **cost of sales or prices**, such as rising supplier costs or price cuts.

Example: next year Tarn Outdoor's sales rise to £5,200,000 and cost of sales to £3,120,000, so gross profit is £2,080,000 and the gross margin stays at **40.00%**. But other operating expenses jump to £1,600,000 (a new head office), with interest still £130,000. Net profit falls to £350,000, and the net margin falls from 13.33% to **6.73%**. Sales grew, but the business became less profitable because overheads rose faster than sales.

**How to improve margins**: raise prices (risk: lower sales if demand is price sensitive); cut cost of sales by negotiating with suppliers or switching (risk: lower quality); cut overheads (risk: poorer service, lower motivation); pay off loans to cut interest.

### Average rate of return (ARR)

When a business is deciding whether to **invest** (a new machine, a new shop, a new product), it wants to know whether the profit will justify the cost. The **average rate of return** shows the **average yearly profit as a percentage of the cost of the investment**:

**average rate of return (%) = average annual profit (total profit ÷ number of years) ÷ cost of investment × 100**

**Step by step**:
1. Find the **total profit** from the investment over its life. If the question gives total profit, use it. If it gives the total **returns** (money coming in from the investment), take the cost of the investment off first: total profit = total returns − cost of investment.
2. Divide by the **number of years** to get the **average annual profit**.
3. Divide by the **cost of investment** and multiply by 100.

**Example 1**: a machine costs £120,000 and is expected to make a total profit of £54,000 over 5 years. Average annual profit = £54,000 ÷ 5 = £10,800. ARR = £10,800 ÷ £120,000 × 100 = **9.00%**.

**Example 2**: a project costs £80,000 and brings in net returns of £25,000 a year for 4 years. Total returns = £100,000; total profit = £100,000 − £80,000 = £20,000; average annual profit = £20,000 ÷ 4 = £5,000; ARR = £5,000 ÷ £80,000 × 100 = **6.25%**.

**Interpreting ARR**:
- The **higher** the ARR, the better the return on the money invested.
- Compare it with the **interest rate** on a loan or savings: an ARR of 4% when a loan costs 7% means the investment does not cover the cost of borrowing; if the business used its own cash, it might earn more by saving it.
- Compare **projects**: the one with the higher ARR gives more profit per £1 invested.
- Compare with a **target** (criterion) rate set by the business.

**Limitations of ARR**: profits are **forecasts**, which may be wrong; it is an **average**, so it ignores **when** profit arrives (profit in year 1 is more useful than in year 5); it ignores **risk** and **non-financial factors**, such as the effect on staff, quality, customers or the environment.

::: terms
- **Sales revenue**: the money a business receives from selling its products, found by price × quantity sold.
- **Cost of sales**: the direct cost of making or buying the products that a business has sold.
- **Gross profit**: sales revenue minus cost of sales; the profit from trading before overheads.
- **Other operating expenses**: the costs of running the business that are not cost of sales, such as rent, salaries and marketing.
- **Net profit**: gross profit minus other operating expenses and interest; the profit after all costs.
- **Gross profit margin**: gross profit as a percentage of sales revenue.
- **Net profit margin**: net profit as a percentage of sales revenue.
- **Average rate of return**: the average annual profit from an investment as a percentage of its cost.
- **Interest**: the cost of borrowing money, paid to the lender on loans and overdrafts.
:::

::: method
1. **Write the formula in words**, then substitute the figures. A formula on its own earns no marks, but it guides you and earns a method mark if your answer is wrong.
2. **Margins always divide by sales revenue**, never by costs.
3. **Do not round until the end.** If asked for 2 decimal places, give exactly two (12.50%, not 12.5%).
4. **Include the unit**: £ for profit, % for margins and ARR.
5. **ARR**: total profit (subtract the cost first if you are given returns) → ÷ years → ÷ cost of investment → × 100.
6. **Interpret**: compare margins over time or with rivals. Gross margin steady but net falling means overheads or interest are rising. Then say what the business could do.
7. **Justify or evaluate**: use your calculated figures as evidence, add non-financial factors (risk, quality, staff, customers), and conclude with a "depends on" factor.
:::

::: example Calculate - gross profit margin
Holloway Kitchenware plc had sales revenue of £1,250,000 and cost of sales of £800,000. Calculate its gross profit margin. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. (2)

Gross profit = £1,250,000 − £800,000 = £450,000 (1).
Gross profit margin = £450,000 ÷ £1,250,000 × 100 = **36.00%** (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - average rate of return
A bakery chain is considering a new oven costing £45,000. It expects the oven to generate a total profit of £27,000 over 4 years. Calculate the average rate of return. (2)

Average annual profit = £27,000 ÷ 4 = £6,750 (1).
ARR = £6,750 ÷ £45,000 × 100 = **15%** (1).
:::

::: example Explain - net profit margin
Explain **one** reason why a business's net profit margin may fall even though its gross profit margin has stayed the same. (3)

The business's other operating expenses, such as rent or salaries, may have risen (1). Because the gross margin is unchanged, buying and pricing are not the problem, but higher overheads are taken off gross profit (1), so a smaller share of each £1 of sales is left as net profit (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - interpreting margins
Corvo Coffee plc runs 90 coffee shops. In 2025 its gross profit margin was 68% and net profit margin 11%. In 2026 its gross profit margin was 61% and net profit margin 5%. The price of coffee beans rose sharply during 2026, and Corvo kept its prices the same to stay competitive.

Analyse the reasons for the change in Corvo Coffee's profit margins. (6)

Corvo's gross profit margin fell from 68% to 61% because the sharp rise in coffee-bean prices increased its cost of sales, while it kept its prices the same (AO2). Because each cup now costs more to make but sells for the same price, less gross profit is made on each £1 of sales, reducing the margin (AO3a).

The net profit margin fell from 11% to 5%, so it has more than halved, a much bigger proportional fall than the gross margin's (AO2). The lower gross profit has to cover the same rent and staff costs across 90 shops, so the fall in gross profit passes straight through to net profit; Corvo could protect its margin by raising prices slightly, but this risks losing customers to rivals (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - comparing investments
Grayling Sports plc has £2 million to invest.
Option 1: open a new store costing £2 million, expected to make a total profit of £1.3 million over 5 years.
Option 2: build a new online shop costing £800,000, expected to make a total profit of £400,000 over 4 years.

Justify which **one** of these two options Grayling Sports should choose. (9)

The new store has an ARR of (£1.3m ÷ 5) ÷ £2m × 100 = 13%, slightly higher than the online shop's (£400,000 ÷ 4) ÷ £800,000 × 100 = 12.5% (AO2). It also produces a much larger average profit of £260,000 a year against £100,000, and a physical store lets customers try on footwear and get advice, which can raise sales of higher-value items (AO3a).

However, the store uses all £2 million, leaving nothing for other plans or emergencies, and store profits depend on footfall, which is falling in many town centres as shoppers move online. If the forecast is too optimistic, the store's fixed costs, such as rent, would cut profit sharply (AO3a).

Grayling should choose the online shop. Its ARR is almost as high, it costs £1.2 million less, so Grayling keeps money for other investments, and online sales are growing. This depends on Grayling having a strong enough brand to attract online shoppers who can easily compare prices with rivals (AO3b).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** way a business could increase its gross profit margin. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2, developing how. For example: negotiate lower prices with its suppliers (1), so the cost of sales for each product falls (1), and with the same selling price a larger share of each £1 of sales is left as gross profit (1).

Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):
- Shows the average yearly profit as a percentage of the cost, so projects of different sizes can be compared.
- Can be compared with the interest rate on borrowing or savings, or a target rate.
- Simple to calculate and understand.
- But based on forecast profits that may be wrong; ignores when the profit arrives; ignores risk.
- Ignores non-financial factors such as effects on staff, customers or reputation.

::: q
Pennine Pet Foods plc's sales revenue rose from £30 million to £36 million between 2025 and 2026. Its net profit rose from £2.4 million to £2.52 million.

Analyse what these figures show about Pennine Pet Foods' performance. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: revenue up 20% (£30m to £36m); net profit up 5% (£2.4m to £2.52m); net profit margin fell from 8.00% to 7.00%.
- AO3a: the business is growing and making more profit in total, which is positive for owners and for funding growth from retained profit; but profit has grown much more slowly than sales, so each £1 of sales now earns 7p rather than 8p; costs such as cost of sales or overheads have grown faster than sales, perhaps from price cuts to win sales, rising ingredient costs or extra spending on marketing; it should find which costs have risen and control them.

- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Pennine throughout, with figures used.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.
:::

::: watch
- **Learn the formulae exactly**: they are not given in the exam.
- **Divide by sales revenue** for both margins. Dividing by costs is the most common error.
- **Gross** profit takes off only cost of sales; **net** profit also takes off other operating expenses **and interest**. Forgetting the interest loses the mark.
- **Two decimal places means two**: 13.33%, 40.00%. Round only at the end.
- **ARR**: if you are given returns, subtract the cost of the investment to get total profit first. Then divide by years, then by the cost.
- Higher profit is not the same as a higher margin. Sales can rise faster than profit, so the margin falls.
- When interpreting, say **why** a margin changed (prices, supplier costs, overheads) and use the figures.
:::
