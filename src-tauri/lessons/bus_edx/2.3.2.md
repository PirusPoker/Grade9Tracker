## What it is

A business can only sell what it has, and it can only make products if materials arrive on time and in good condition. This topic is about **working with suppliers**. Edexcel asks three things:
1. **Managing stock**: interpreting **bar gate stock graphs**, and the use of **just in time (JIT)** stock control.
2. **The role of procurement**: **relationships with suppliers**, judged on **quality**, **delivery** (**cost, speed, reliability**), **availability**, **cost** and **trust**.
3. **The impact of logistics and supply decisions** on **costs**, **reputation** and **customer satisfaction**.

### Why stock matters

**Stock** (inventory) is the raw materials, components, work in progress and finished goods a business holds. Holding stock is a balancing act:
- **Too much stock**: cash is tied up that could be used elsewhere (a cash-flow problem, 1.3.3); storage, insurance and security costs rise; stock may go **out of date**, perish or go out of fashion, and be **wasted** or stolen.
- **Too little stock**: the business **runs out**, so production stops, workers stand idle and customers cannot be served. Sales are lost and customers may switch to rivals for good.

### Bar gate stock graphs

A **bar gate stock graph** shows how a business's stock level changes over time when it **reorders the same quantity each time**. The stock line slopes down as stock is used, then jumps straight up when a delivery arrives, giving a saw-tooth shape. The features you must be able to read:

| Feature | What it means | Where it is on the graph |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximum stock** | the most stock the business wants to hold (limited by space and cost) | the top of each "tooth" |
| **Buffer stock** (minimum stock) | a safety level kept in case of late deliveries or a rise in demand; stock should not fall below it | the lowest point each cycle, shown as a horizontal line |
| **Reorder level** | the stock level at which a new order is placed | a horizontal line between buffer and maximum |
| **Reorder quantity** | the amount ordered each time | the height of each vertical jump |
| **Lead time** | the time between **placing an order** and the **delivery arriving** | read along the **time axis**, from the reorder level to the jump |
| **Usage rate** | how fast stock is used | the slope of the line (units per day or week) |

Reading it:
- **Maximum stock** is 500 units, **buffer stock** 100 units, and the **reorder level** 250 units.
- Stock falls from 500 to 250 in 5 days, so the **usage rate** is 250 ÷ 5 = **50 units a day**.
- The first order is placed on **day 5** (stock hits the reorder level) and arrives on **day 8**, so the **lead time** is **3 days**.
- Stock first reaches the buffer level on **day 8**, just as the delivery arrives.
- The **reorder quantity** is 500 − 100 = **400 units** (the height of the jump).
- Each cycle lasts 8 days, so orders arrive on days 8, 16 and 24.

**Useful links between the numbers** (these let you calculate missing values):
- Reorder level = buffer stock + (usage rate × lead time): 100 + (50 × 3) = 250.
- Reorder quantity = maximum stock − buffer stock (if the delivery arrives exactly at buffer level): 500 − 100 = 400.
- Days for stock to fall = units used ÷ usage rate per day: from 500 to 100 is 400 ÷ 50 = 8 days.

**What can go wrong**: if **demand rises**, the line gets steeper and stock may fall below buffer before delivery; if a **delivery is late**, the business eats into its buffer stock; if both happen, it may **run out** (a stock-out). Graphs in the exam sometimes show this: a dip below the buffer line tells you something went wrong in that cycle.

### Just in time (JIT) stock control

**Just in time** means holding **little or no stock**: materials and components are delivered **exactly when they are needed** for production, and finished goods are sent out as soon as they are made. Car makers such as Toyota are well known for developing this approach.

| Advantages of JIT | Disadvantages of JIT |
|---|---|
| Less **cash tied up** in stock, so better cash flow | Relies completely on **reliable suppliers**: one late delivery stops production |
| Lower **storage costs**: less warehouse space, insurance and security | No buffer, so the business **cannot meet a sudden rise in demand** |
| Less **waste**: stock does not go out of date or out of fashion | Frequent small deliveries can cost more, and the business **loses bulk-buying discounts** |
| Space freed up can be used for production or selling | Needs very good **communication** and IT links with suppliers |
| Problems with materials are spotted quickly | Disruption (strikes, bad weather, transport problems) hits immediately |

JIT suits businesses with **reliable, nearby suppliers**, **predictable demand** and **perishable** or high-value stock (fresh food, fashion). It suits less well when supply is uncertain or demand jumps around.

### The role of procurement

**Procurement** means **finding, choosing and buying** the materials, components and services a business needs from suppliers, and managing those relationships. When choosing and working with suppliers, a business considers the factors the spec lists:

| Factor | What the business wants | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Quality** | materials of a consistent, suitable standard | poor inputs mean faulty products, waste, refunds and damage to the business's own reputation |
| **Delivery: cost** | low or no delivery charges | delivery costs add to the cost per unit and cut margins |
| **Delivery: speed** | short lead times | the business can hold less stock (or use JIT) and respond quickly to customers |
| **Delivery: reliability** | deliveries arrive on time, in full, every time | late deliveries cause stock-outs, idle workers and missed customer orders |
| **Availability** | the supplier can provide the items needed, in the quantities needed, when needed | if items are out of stock, production stops; useful if the business grows |
| **Cost** | a low price per unit, discounts for bulk orders, good **credit terms** (time to pay) | lower costs raise profit margins or allow lower prices; credit terms help cash flow |
| **Trust** | a supplier who is honest, keeps promises and treats the business fairly | trust allows long-term deals, flexibility in a crisis and sharing of plans |

**The cheapest supplier is not always the best.** A business must **balance** these factors. A supplier who is 5% cheaper but often late may cost more in lost sales than it saves.

**Benefits of a good relationship with suppliers**: better prices and discounts; priority when stock is short; flexibility with rush orders; longer credit periods that help cash flow; joint work on improving quality. Businesses build relationships by placing regular orders, paying on time and communicating clearly.

### Logistics and supply decisions

**Logistics** is the business of **getting goods to the right place at the right time**: moving and storing materials and products, from suppliers to the business and from the business to its customers. It includes transport, warehousing, distribution centres and delivery tracking.

The spec asks for the **impact** of logistics and supply decisions on three things:
- **Costs**: transport, fuel, warehousing and stock-holding costs; choosing a cheaper supplier or slower delivery cuts costs but may cause other problems; efficient routes and full lorries lower cost per delivery.
- **Reputation**: late or damaged deliveries, or products from a supplier with poor quality or poor working conditions, damage the brand and lead to bad reviews; reliable delivery builds a name for dependability.
- **Customer satisfaction**: customers expect products **in stock**, delivered **quickly** and **on time**, with tracking. Fast, reliable logistics brings repeat custom; stock-outs and late deliveries send customers to rivals.

For an online retailer, logistics is a big part of the service: fast, cheap, reliable delivery and easy returns can be a **competitive advantage** in itself.

::: terms
- **Stock**: the raw materials, components, work in progress and finished goods that a business holds.
- **Bar gate stock graph**: a graph showing how stock levels fall as stock is used and rise when deliveries arrive, over time.
- **Buffer stock**: the minimum level of stock a business keeps as a safety margin against late deliveries or extra demand.
- **Reorder level**: the stock level at which a business places a new order with its supplier.
- **Reorder quantity**: the amount of stock ordered from the supplier each time an order is placed.
- **Lead time**: the time between placing an order with a supplier and the delivery arriving.
- **Just in time (JIT)**: a stock control method where materials arrive exactly when needed, so little or no stock is held.
- **Procurement**: finding, choosing and buying the materials and services a business needs, and managing its suppliers.
- **Logistics**: moving and storing goods so they arrive in the right place at the right time.
- **Stock-out**: when a business runs out of stock and cannot produce or meet customer orders.
:::

::: method
1. **Bar gate graphs**: label what you read before you calculate. Maximum is the top, buffer is the floor, reorder level is the middle line, and lead time is along the **time** axis.
2. **Calculations**: usage rate = units used ÷ days; days to fall = units ÷ usage rate; reorder quantity = height of the jump. Give units ("days", "units").
3. **Explain JIT (3)**: point → what it does to stock → effect on costs, cash flow or production.
4. **Suppliers**: use the spec's factors by name (quality, delivery cost, speed, reliability, availability, cost, trust), and develop each to an effect on the business's costs, reputation or customers.
5. **Bring in the case**: perishable products, location of suppliers, size of orders, figures given for price, lead time or reliability.
6. **Justify (9)**: choose one option (one supplier, or JIT against holding stock). Weigh its benefits and drawbacks, then conclude with a new "depends on" factor, such as how important reliability is to this business's customers.
:::

Stock falls from 500 to 250 units, a fall of 250 units, at 50 units a day (1). 250 ÷ 50 = **5 days** (1).
:::

::: example Explain - JIT
Explain **one** advantage to a business of using just in time stock control. (3)

The business holds very little stock because materials arrive only when they are needed (1), so less cash is tied up in stock waiting to be used (1), which improves the business's cash flow and lets it use that money elsewhere, for example to pay suppliers or invest (1).
:::

::: example Outline - supplier relationship
Greenleaf Kitchens plc runs 140 restaurants across the UK and buys fresh vegetables daily from a farm cooperative. Outline **one** benefit to Greenleaf Kitchens of having a good relationship with its supplier. (2)

The cooperative is more likely to give Greenleaf priority on fresh vegetables when supply is short, such as after a poor harvest (1), so its 140 restaurants can keep serving their full menu and avoid disappointing customers (1).

::: example Analyse - logistics
Parcelwise Home Ltd sells furniture online across the UK. It uses a cheap courier, but 12% of deliveries arrive late and 4% are damaged. Online reviews have fallen from 4.5 to 3.6 stars.

Analyse the impact of Parcelwise's logistics decisions on the business. (6)

Using a cheap courier keeps Parcelwise's delivery costs low, which helps it offer competitive prices online (AO2). However, with 12% of deliveries late and 4% damaged, Parcelwise must pay for replacements, returns and refunds, so the savings on delivery are offset by extra costs and its profit margin may actually fall (AO3a).

The poor delivery record has already damaged its reputation, with reviews falling from 4.5 to 3.6 stars (AO2). Because online shoppers cannot see the furniture before buying, they rely on reviews, so many will choose a rival with better ratings; lower customer satisfaction means fewer repeat customers and lost sales (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - choosing a supplier
Northgate Bikes plc assembles e-bikes in Coventry and needs 20,000 battery packs a year.
Supplier A (overseas): £180 per pack, lead time 6 weeks, 90% of deliveries on time.
Supplier B (UK): £195 per pack, lead time 1 week, 99% of deliveries on time.

Justify which **one** of these two suppliers Northgate Bikes should choose. (9)

Supplier B's 99% on-time record and one-week lead time mean Northgate can hold far less stock, freeing cash and storage space, and it can react quickly if demand for e-bikes rises (AO2). Because batteries arrive reliably, the assembly line rarely stops for lack of parts, so workers are not left idle and customers receive their bikes on time, protecting Northgate's reputation (AO3a).

However, Supplier B costs £15 more per pack, which is £15 × 20,000 = £300,000 a year more than Supplier A, raising Northgate's cost per bike and squeezing its margin or forcing a higher price (AO2). If rivals buy cheaper batteries, Northgate may lose price-sensitive customers (AO3a).

Northgate should choose Supplier B. With Supplier A, one delivery in ten is late and a six-week lead time means holding large buffer stocks of expensive batteries, which ties up cash and could cost more in lost sales than the £300,000 saved. This depends on whether Northgate can pass some of the higher cost on to customers, which is more likely if its bikes are sold on quality rather than price (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Lead time is a time**, read along the time axis from when the order is placed to when it arrives. Students often give a stock level instead.
- The **buffer** is the floor, not the reorder level. "Earliest date stock reaches buffer level" means the first time the line touches the bottom line.
- Show your working for bar gate calculations: units used ÷ usage rate.
- **JIT is not "no stock problems"**: its big drawback is total reliance on suppliers.
- Use the spec's supplier factors **by name**: quality, delivery (cost, speed, reliability), availability, cost, trust.
- **Logistics** means moving and storing goods, not marketing. Link it to costs, reputation and customer satisfaction.
- The cheapest supplier is not automatically the best: weigh cost against reliability and quality.
