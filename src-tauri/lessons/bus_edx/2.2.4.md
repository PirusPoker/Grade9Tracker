## What it is

**Place** is the element of the marketing mix about **how the product reaches the customer**: the **method of distribution**, also called the **distribution channel**. It answers the question "Where and how can customers buy this product?", not "Where is the business located?" (that is 1.4.2).

The spec names two methods of distribution:
- **Retailers**: businesses that sell products to customers through **physical shops**.
- **E-tailers**: businesses that sell products to customers **online**, through **e-commerce**.

A manufacturer can sell through either or both, and it can sell online **directly** (through its own website) or through **another business's** website or shop.

### Distribution channels

A **distribution channel** is the route a product takes from the producer to the final customer.

| Channel | Route | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Through a retailer | Producer → retailer (shop) → customer | A biscuit maker sells to supermarkets, which sell to shoppers |
| Through an e-tailer | Producer → e-tailer (online seller or marketplace) → customer | A phone-case maker sells through a large online marketplace |
| Direct online | Producer → its own website → customer | A clothing brand sells from its own online shop and posts orders to customers |

Every business in the chain between producer and customer (an **intermediary**) takes a share of the final price as its profit margin.

### Retailers

A **retailer** buys products from producers (or from wholesalers) and sells them to the final customer, usually through **shops**. Retailers range from large supermarket and department-store chains to small independent shops.

**Benefits to a producer of selling through retailers**
- **Reach**: large chains have hundreds of shops across the country, putting the product in front of millions of shoppers.
- **Display and visibility**: shelf space, in-store promotion and point-of-sale displays encourage **impulse buying**.
- **Customers can see, touch and try** products before buying, which matters for clothes, furniture and food.
- **Trust**: being stocked by a well-known retailer gives a product credibility.
- The retailer handles **selling, staff and customer service**, so the producer can focus on making the product.
- Customers can take the product home **immediately**.

**Drawbacks**
- The **retailer's margin**: the retailer buys at a lower price and adds its own mark-up, so the producer receives much less than the shop price.
- **Loss of control**: the retailer decides on display, shelf position, the final price and promotions.
- **Power of large retailers**: big chains can demand low prices, long payment terms or contributions to promotion costs, squeezing the producer's profit.
- **Competition on the shelf**: the product sits beside rivals, including the retailer's own-brand products.
- **Limited shelf space**: retailers may refuse to stock new or small brands.

### E-tailers and e-commerce

An **e-tailer** is a business that sells products **online** through a website or app, with goods delivered to the customer or collected. **E-commerce** is buying and selling over the internet.

Producers can use e-commerce in two ways:
- **Selling direct online**: through their **own website**, keeping all the revenue and all the customer data.
- **Selling through third-party marketplaces**: large online platforms that list products from many sellers and charge a **fee** or **commission** on each sale. They offer huge reach and customer trust, but the producer pays fees, faces competition right next to its listing, and has less control.

**Benefits of e-tailing**
- **Wider reach**: customers anywhere in the country, or the world (2.1.3), can buy.
- **Open 24 hours**, every day.
- **Lower costs**: no rent, staff or running costs of shops; selling direct also cuts out the retailer's margin, so the business can charge less or keep more profit.
- **Customer data**: the business learns what customers buy and can send targeted promotions and e-newsletters (2.2.3).
- **Convenience** for customers, who can compare and order from home.

**Drawbacks**
- **Delivery costs** and the risk of late or damaged deliveries, which affect customer satisfaction.
- **Returns**: online customers return more items, especially clothing, adding cost.
- **No physical inspection**: customers cannot touch, try or test before buying, which puts some off.
- **Intense competition**: customers can compare prices instantly, pushing prices down (2.2.2).
- **Website costs**: building, maintaining and securing a website, plus online advertising to attract visitors.
- **Wait for delivery**: customers cannot take the product home straight away.

### Comparing the two

| | Retailers (shops) | E-tailers (online) |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | Limited to shoppers near the shops | National or global |
| Costs to the business | Retailer's margin (if indirect) or shop costs (if own shops) | Website, delivery, returns, marketplace fees |
| Customer experience | See, touch and try; take home immediately; staff advice | Convenient, 24/7; wide choice; easy price comparison; wait for delivery |
| Control | Retailer controls display and price | Direct online: full control; marketplace: limited control |
| Data | Little customer data | Detailed data on each customer |

### Multichannel distribution

Many businesses use **more than one channel**, known as **multichannel** distribution. For example, a brand might sell through supermarkets, through a large online marketplace and through its own website. Services such as **click-and-collect** (order online, collect in store) combine the convenience of online shopping with the immediacy of shops.

Multichannel distribution reaches more customers and spreads risk, but it is harder to manage: prices, stock and branding must be consistent across channels, and retailers may object if the producer undercuts them online.

### Choosing a method of distribution

The best method depends on:
- **The product**: products customers want to see or try (furniture, clothes, fresh food) suit shops; standard products customers know well (books, electronics, replacement parts) sell easily online.
- **The target market**: younger customers often shop online; some older customers prefer shops.
- **Costs and margins**: can the business afford a retailer's cut, or delivery and returns costs?
- **Control**: how important is it to control price, display and the customer experience?
- **The rest of the mix** (2.2.5): a premium brand needs a channel that supports its image.

::: terms
- **Place**: the element of the marketing mix concerned with how a product reaches the customer.
- **Method of distribution**: the way a business gets its products to its customers, such as through retailers or e-tailers.
- **Distribution channel**: the route a product takes from the producer to the final customer.
- **Retailer**: a business that sells products to the final customer, usually through physical shops.
- **E-tailer**: a business that sells products to customers online through a website or app.
- **E-commerce**: buying and selling goods and services over the internet.
- **Intermediary**: a business between the producer and the customer, such as a retailer, that takes a share of the price.
- **Online marketplace**: a website where many sellers list their products, usually paying a fee or commission on each sale.
- **Multichannel distribution**: selling through more than one channel, such as shops, a website and an online marketplace.
- **Click-and-collect**: a service where customers order online and collect the product from a shop.
:::

::: method
1. **Remember that place means the channel**, not the business's location. Name the method: retailer, e-tailer, direct online, marketplace or multichannel.
2. **For an explain**, make one point about the channel (reach, cost, control, customer experience), then develop it to sales, costs or profit.
3. **Use the right costs**: the retailer's margin for shops; delivery, returns, website and fees for online.
4. **Apply the case**: what the product is, who the customers are, whether they need to see or try it, and the business's size and margins.
5. **Link to the rest of the mix**: price, promotion and image must fit the channel.
6. **Justify or evaluate**: weigh one channel's benefits and drawbacks for this business, then conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as whether customers need to try the product or how high the retailer's margin is.
:::

::: example Explain - selling through retailers
Explain **one** advantage to a manufacturer of selling its products through retailers. (3)

Large retail chains have many shops visited by large numbers of customers (1), so the manufacturer's products are seen by far more people than it could reach on its own (1), which increases sales and builds brand awareness (1).
:::

::: example Explain - drawback of e-tailing
Explain **one** disadvantage to a business of selling its products online. (3)

Customers cannot see or try the product before buying (1), so more of them return items that do not fit or match the pictures (1), which adds return delivery and handling costs, reducing the business's profit (1).
:::

::: example Outline - direct online in context
Crafted Candles Ltd sells its candles through a chain of gift shops, which sells them at £20 and pays Crafted Candles £9 each. Outline **one** benefit to Crafted Candles of selling directly through its own website. (2)

Crafted Candles would receive the full price paid by the customer instead of £9 from the gift shop chain (1), so even after paying for delivery it could earn a much higher profit on each candle (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - moving online
Fernhill Books plc owns 120 bookshops in UK towns. Its shop sales have fallen as more customers buy books online. Analyse the benefits to Fernhill Books of increasing its online sales. (6)

Books are standard products that customers can buy confidently without seeing them, so they sell well online (AO2). By growing its own website, Fernhill can reach customers who no longer visit town centres, or who live far from its 120 shops, so it wins back sales it is currently losing to online sellers (AO3a).

Selling online also gives Fernhill data on what each customer reads (AO2). It can use this to send personalised recommendations and e-newsletters, encouraging repeat purchases, and it could offer click-and-collect so online orders also bring customers into its shops, where they may buy more. This helps Fernhill keep its shops busy while it grows online, reducing the risk of more shop sales being lost (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - supermarket or direct online
Golden Hive Ltd produces premium honey. It has been offered a contract with a national supermarket chain, which would sell each jar at £7 and pay Golden Hive £3.80. Option 1: accept the supermarket contract. Option 2: sell directly through its own website at £7.50 a jar, with delivery costs of about £2 a jar. Justify which **one** of these options Golden Hive should choose. (9)

Selling through the supermarket chain puts Golden Hive's honey on shelves in stores across the country, reaching millions of shoppers who would never find its website (AO2). Even at £3.80 a jar, the much larger volume of sales could earn more total profit, and being stocked by a national chain builds the brand's credibility (AO3a).

However, the supermarket keeps £3.20 of every £7 jar, so Golden Hive's margin is much lower than the roughly £5.50 it would receive selling online after delivery costs. The supermarket also controls display and promotions, and may demand price cuts later, and premium honey may get lost among cheaper brands and own-label honey on the shelf (AO3a).

Golden Hive should accept the supermarket contract, because reach is the biggest problem for a small producer and the volume of sales should outweigh the lower margin. This depends on whether Golden Hive can produce enough honey for a national chain: if its supply is limited, selling fewer jars online at a higher margin would be more profitable and protect its premium image (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Place is the distribution channel, not the location.** "It is near a busy high street" answers a location question, not a place question.
- The spec's methods are **retailers** and **e-tailers (e-commerce)**. Use these words.
- Selling online is **place**, not promotion. A website that sells is a channel; adverts on it are promotion.
- Always include the **costs** of the channel: the retailer's margin for shops; delivery, returns, fees and website costs online.
- The product matters: customers want to **see, touch or try** some products before buying, which favours shops.
- In a justify, compare **total profit**, not just the margin per unit: a lower margin through a retailer may sell many more units.
:::
