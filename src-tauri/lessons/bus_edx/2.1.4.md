## What it is

Businesses are increasingly judged not only on what they sell and how much profit they make, but on **how** they make it. Customers, employees, investors and pressure groups ask whether a business treats people fairly and whether it damages the planet. This topic covers three things:
1. How **ethical considerations** influence business activity, and the **trade-off between ethics and profit**.
2. How **environmental considerations** influence business activity, and the **trade-off between the environment, sustainability and profit**.
3. How **pressure group** activity can affect the **marketing mix**.

### Ethics

**Ethics** are moral principles about what is right and wrong. An **ethical** business decision is one that is morally right, not just legal. The difference matters:
- The **law** (1.5.3) sets the **minimum** a business **must** do, such as paying the minimum wage.
- **Ethics** is about doing what is **right**, which often goes **beyond** the law, such as paying a real living wage above the legal minimum, or refusing to buy from a supplier that uses child labour in a country where enforcement is weak.

Common ethical issues:
- **Treatment of employees**: fair pay, reasonable hours, safe conditions, no exploitation.
- **Sourcing**: buying from suppliers that treat their workers fairly; paying farmers a fair price (for example through **Fairtrade** certification).
- **Animal welfare**: free-range products, no animal testing.
- **Honesty with customers**: truthful advertising and labelling, fair prices, not targeting vulnerable people.
- **Treatment of suppliers**: paying on time and at fair prices.

### The trade-off between ethics and profit

A **trade-off** is when gaining one thing means giving up some of another. Acting ethically often **costs more in the short term**:
- Paying staff or suppliers more raises **costs**.
- Ethical materials (fair-trade cotton, free-range eggs) are dearer, so either **prices rise** or **margins fall**.
- Turning down cheap suppliers or profitable but unethical opportunities means **lost profit**.

But ethics can **raise profit in the long term**:
- A good **reputation** attracts customers willing to pay more; ethics can be a **unique selling point**.
- Fairly treated employees are more **motivated** and **loyal**, so productivity rises and staff turnover falls.
- Avoids **bad publicity**, boycotts and pressure-group campaigns that destroy sales.
- Attracts **investors** who only invest in responsible businesses.

| Acting ethically: costs | Acting ethically: benefits |
|---|---|
| Higher costs of wages, materials and suppliers | Stronger brand image and customer loyalty |
| Higher prices may lose price-sensitive customers | Can charge a premium price |
| Lower short-term profit | Motivated staff, easier recruitment |
| Time to check suppliers | Avoids scandals, boycotts and lost sales |

Whether ethics pays depends on the **customers**: some segments will pay more for ethical products, others mainly want the lowest price.

### The environment and sustainability

**Environmental considerations** are the effects of business activity on the natural world. Businesses can cause:
- **Pollution** of air, water and land.
- **Carbon emissions** that contribute to climate change. A business's **carbon footprint** is the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by its activities.
- **Waste**, especially packaging and single-use plastic going to landfill.
- **Using up finite resources** such as oil, minerals and forests.
- **Congestion** and noise from transport.

**Sustainability** means meeting today's needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs: using resources at a rate that can continue. Sustainable actions include:
- Using **renewable energy** and energy-efficient equipment.
- **Recyclable, reusable or reduced packaging**.
- Sourcing materials from **sustainable** sources (for example certified timber).
- **Cutting waste** and recycling.
- Reducing transport emissions: local suppliers, electric vehicles, fewer deliveries.

### The trade-off between the environment, sustainability and profit

In the **short term**, environmental action usually **costs money**:
- New equipment (solar panels, electric vans) needs large **investment**.
- Sustainable materials and packaging are often **dearer**.
- Changing processes takes time and training.

In the **long term**, it can **save money and raise sales**:
- **Lower energy and waste costs** once efficient equipment is installed.
- **Customers** increasingly prefer greener products and may pay a premium; it can **differentiate** the business.
- Prepares for future **laws** and taxes on emissions and plastic.
- Better reputation, easier to attract staff and investors.

The risk of "green" claims that are not true is called **greenwashing**. If customers or pressure groups expose it, the damage to reputation can be worse than doing nothing.

### Pressure groups and the marketing mix

A **pressure group** is an organisation that tries to influence businesses, governments or the public on a particular issue, such as the environment, animal welfare or workers' rights. Its tactics include:
- **Publicity campaigns** in the media and on **social media**, which can spread fast.
- **Boycotts**: urging customers to stop buying a business's products.
- **Protests** and demonstrations at shops, offices or events.
- **Lobbying** the government to change the law.

Pressure group activity can force a business to change each element of its **marketing mix**:

| Element | Possible impact |
|---|---|
| **Product** | Change ingredients, materials or packaging (for example removing plastic, palm oil or animal-tested ingredients); drop a controversial product |
| **Price** | Higher costs of ethical or sustainable inputs push prices up; or the business cuts prices to win back customers lost in a boycott |
| **Promotion** | Spend on advertising to repair its image or show its new ethical or green credentials; withdraw adverts criticised as misleading or harmful |
| **Place** | Retailers may stop stocking a product targeted by a campaign; the business may change suppliers or distribution to reduce its environmental impact |

The impact depends on how much **publicity** the campaign gets, how much customers **care** about the issue, and how easy it is for customers to **switch** to rivals.

::: terms
- **Ethics**: moral principles about what is right and wrong, guiding decisions beyond what the law requires.
- **Ethical behaviour**: business actions that are morally right, such as fair pay and responsible sourcing.
- **Trade-off**: giving up some of one thing, such as profit, in order to gain more of another, such as ethical standards.
- **Fairtrade**: a certification scheme that guarantees farmers and workers in developing countries a fair minimum price for their products.
- **Environmental considerations**: the effects of business activity on the natural world, such as pollution, emissions and waste.
- **Sustainability**: meeting present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- **Carbon footprint**: the total amount of greenhouse gases caused by a business's activities.
- **Pressure group**: an organisation that campaigns to influence businesses, the government or the public on a particular issue.
- **Boycott**: a refusal by customers to buy a business's products as a form of protest.
- **Greenwashing**: making misleading claims that a product or business is more environmentally friendly than it really is.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the issue**: ethical (people, animals, honesty) or environmental (pollution, carbon, waste, resources), or a pressure group campaign.
2. **Explain the influence on the business**: what it changes in sourcing, production, costs, pricing or marketing.
3. **Develop the trade-off**: the short-term cost (higher costs, lower margins, higher prices) against the long-term gain (reputation, loyalty, premium prices, lower energy costs, motivated staff).
4. **For pressure groups, name the element of the mix** that changes and how, then link to sales, costs or profit.
5. **Apply the case**: what the business sells, its customers (do they care, are they price-sensitive?), its competitors and its figures.
6. **Justify or evaluate**: choose and weigh one option, then conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as how much customers will pay for ethical products or how much publicity a campaign gets.
:::

::: example Explain - pressure group and the mix
Explain **one** impact a pressure group may have on a business's marketing mix. (3)

A pressure group may campaign against the plastic packaging used on a business's products (1), so the business may change its product by switching to recyclable or paper packaging (1), which raises its costs per unit but helps protect its sales from a boycott (1).
:::

::: example Explain - ethics and profit
Explain **one** reason why acting ethically may reduce a business's profit. (3)

Buying from suppliers that pay their workers fairly usually costs more than using the cheapest supplier (1), so the business's cost per unit rises (1), and unless it can charge customers a higher price, its profit margin falls (1).
:::

::: example Discuss - reducing environmental impact
Discuss the benefits to a business of reducing its environmental impact. (6)

Reducing its environmental impact can improve a business's reputation, as many customers prefer brands that cut waste and emissions (AO1b). This means customers may choose it over rivals and even pay a premium price, so its sales revenue and market share can rise, giving it a competitive advantage (AO3a).

It can also cut costs over time (AO1b). For example, energy-efficient machinery and lighting lower energy bills, and using less packaging reduces material and waste-disposal costs, so although the initial investment is high, the business's costs per unit fall in the long run and its profit can rise. It is also better prepared for stricter environmental laws, avoiding future fines (AO3a).
:::

::: example Analyse - trade-off in context
Fernleaf Coffee plc runs 300 coffee shops in the UK. It is considering buying all its coffee beans with Fairtrade certification, which would raise its cost per cup by 6p. Analyse the impact on Fernleaf Coffee of this decision. (6)

Across 300 shops selling many cups a day, an extra 6p per cup adds a large amount to Fernleaf's annual costs (AO2). Unless it raises prices, its profit margin falls; if it does raise prices, price-sensitive customers may switch to cheaper chains, reducing sales (AO3a).

However, Fairtrade beans would let Fernleaf promote its coffee as ethically sourced, which is important to many coffee drinkers (AO2). This could build customer loyalty and attract customers from rivals that are not Fairtrade, so its sales may rise enough to cover the extra 6p, and the ethical image protects it from criticism by pressure groups about low prices paid to farmers (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - ethical supplier
Rowan & Reed Ltd sells school uniforms through its website and 40 shops. Option 1: switch to an ethical supplier that pays factory workers a living wage, at £9 per uniform set. Option 2: stay with its current supplier at £6.50 per set, which has been accused of poor working conditions. Justify which **one** of these options Rowan & Reed should choose. (9)

Switching to the ethical supplier removes the risk of a scandal linking Rowan & Reed to poor working conditions (AO2). Parents choosing uniforms for their children may avoid a brand criticised on social media, so an ethical supplier protects sales across its 40 shops and website, and gives it a message it can promote to win customers from rivals (AO3a).

However, £9 is £2.50 more per set, an increase of about 38%. School uniforms are a necessity many families must buy on a tight budget, so if Rowan & Reed raises its prices, some parents may buy cheaper supermarket uniforms instead, and if it does not, its profit margin falls sharply (AO3a).

Rowan & Reed should switch to the ethical supplier, because an accusation of poor conditions is already public, and a campaign against it could cost far more in lost sales than £2.50 per set. This depends on how price-sensitive its customers are: if most buy on price alone, it could phase in the ethical supplier for part of the range first and promote it (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Ethical is not the same as legal.** Obeying the law is a minimum; ethics goes beyond it. Paying the minimum wage is legal, not especially ethical.
- The spec says **trade-off**: always give the **cost side** (higher costs, lower margins, higher prices) as well as the gain in reputation.
- Separate the **short term** (costs rise) from the **long term** (loyalty, lower energy bills, premium prices). This is the key to analysis.
- For pressure group questions, name the **element of the mix** that changes. "It damages the business" is not enough.
- Sustainability is about the **future**: using resources at a rate that can continue, not just "being green".
- Ethics pays only if customers **value** it. Use the case to judge how price-sensitive they are.
:::
