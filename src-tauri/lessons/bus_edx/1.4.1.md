## What it is

When an entrepreneur starts a business they must choose its **legal form**: who owns it, who makes decisions, who keeps the profit and, most importantly, **who is responsible for its debts**. Edexcel covers three types of ownership for start-ups (sole trader, partnership, private limited company) and the option of running a **franchise**. Public limited companies belong to Theme 2 (2.1.1), not here.

### Limited and unlimited liability

**Liability** is responsibility for the debts of the business.

| | **Unlimited liability** | **Limited liability** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | The owners are personally responsible for **all** the business's debts | The owners can lose only the money they have **invested** in the business |
| Legal identity | The owner and the business are legally the **same** | The business is a separate legal entity (it is **incorporated**): it owns its assets and owes its own debts |
| Applies to | Sole traders and ordinary partnerships | Private limited companies (Ltd) |
| If the business fails owing money | Creditors can claim the owner's **personal assets**: savings, car, even their home | Creditors can claim only the company's assets; the shareholders' personal possessions are safe |

**Implications for owners:**
- Unlimited liability means **greater personal risk** (1.1.2). Owners may be more cautious, avoid borrowing, and lose everything if the business fails.
- Limited liability **reduces personal risk**, which encourages people to start businesses and makes it easier to attract investors, who know they can lose only what they put in.
- Banks know limited liability protects owners, so they often ask the owners of a small company for a **personal guarantee** before lending, which removes part of that protection for that loan.

### Types of business ownership for start-ups

**Sole trader**: a business owned by one person, who may employ others.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Easy, quick and cheap to set up: register as self-employed with the tax authorities | **Unlimited liability**: personal assets at risk |
| The owner keeps **all the profit** | Limited finance: only the owner's savings and what they can borrow |
| Full **control**: quick decisions with no one to consult | Long hours; no one to share the workload or cover holidays and illness |
| Financial details stay **private** | Limited skills: the owner must do everything, from accounts to marketing |

**Partnership**: a business owned by two or more people, who share the running of it and its profits. Partners usually draw up a **deed of partnership** setting out how much each invests, how profits are shared and what happens if a partner leaves.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| More **capital**: each partner invests | **Unlimited liability** in an ordinary partnership; each partner can be responsible for debts run up by the others |
| Shared **skills and workload**: partners can specialise | **Profits are shared** between partners |
| Easy to set up; finances stay private | **Disagreements** between partners can slow decisions or end the business |
| Partners can cover for each other during holidays or illness | Decisions take longer than for a sole trader |

**Private limited company (Ltd)**: a business owned by **shareholders**, registered at Companies House, with **limited liability**. Shares cannot be sold to the general public on a stock exchange, and are usually held by the founders, family and friends.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Limited liability**: shareholders' personal assets are protected | More **paperwork and cost** to set up and run |
| Can raise finance by selling **shares** to chosen people (share capital, 1.3.4) | Must publish some **financial information**, so it is less private |
| Looks more established, which can help win customers, suppliers and lenders | Profits are shared among shareholders as dividends |
| The company continues if a shareholder leaves or dies | Original owners may lose some control as more shares are sold |

### Comparison

| Feature | Sole trader | Partnership | Private limited company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owners | One | Two or more | Shareholders |
| Liability | Unlimited | Unlimited (ordinary partnership) | Limited |
| Control | Owner alone | Shared among partners | Shareholders; directors run it |
| Raising finance | Owner's savings, loans | Partners' savings, loans | Plus sale of shares to chosen people |
| Privacy | Private | Private | Some accounts published |
| Set-up | Simple | Simple; deed advisable | More complex and costly |

### Franchising

A **franchise** is an arrangement where one business (the **franchisor**) sells another (the **franchisee**) the right to trade using its brand name, products and business model. Many fast-food outlets, coffee shops, estate agents, parcel shops and children's activity classes are franchises.

The franchisee usually pays:
- an **initial fee** to join;
- ongoing **royalties**, often a percentage of revenue, sometimes with a marketing contribution.

**Advantages to the franchisee:**
- A **proven business model** with existing demand, so a lower risk of failure than an independent start-up.
- An **established brand** that customers already recognise and trust, so sales start sooner.
- **Training and support** from the franchisor, including help choosing a site, setting up and running the business.
- **National advertising** paid for jointly, and often lower costs from the franchisor's **bulk buying**.
- Banks may be **more willing to lend** to a known franchise.

**Disadvantages to the franchisee:**
- The **initial fee** can be large, and **royalties** reduce profit every year, even when profits are low (royalties are often based on revenue, not profit).
- **Restrictions**: the franchisee must follow the franchisor's rules on products, suppliers, prices, decor and opening hours, so there is **less independence** (a reward of starting up, 1.1.2).
- The franchisee's **reputation depends on others**: a scandal at another branch or at the franchisor affects them too.
- The contract lasts a fixed time and may not be **renewed**, and selling the franchise may need the franchisor's approval.

The franchise can itself be run as a sole trader, a partnership or a private limited company.

::: terms
- **Liability**: legal responsibility for the debts of a business.
- **Unlimited liability**: owners are personally responsible for all the business's debts, so their personal assets can be taken to pay them.
- **Limited liability**: owners can lose only the money they have invested in the business, so their personal assets are protected.
- **Sole trader**: a business owned by one person, who has unlimited liability and keeps all the profit.
- **Partnership**: a business owned by two or more people who share its running, profits and, usually, unlimited liability.
- **Deed of partnership**: a legal agreement setting out each partner's investment, share of profit and responsibilities.
- **Private limited company**: a business owned by shareholders, with limited liability, whose shares cannot be sold to the public.
- **Shareholder**: a person who owns part of a company by holding its shares.
- **Franchise**: the right to trade using another business's brand name, products and business model, in return for fees.
- **Franchisor**: the business that sells the right to use its brand and business model.
- **Franchisee**: the business or person that buys the right to operate a franchise.
- **Royalty**: an ongoing payment from a franchisee to the franchisor, often a percentage of revenue.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the feature that belongs to the type of ownership** (unlimited liability, shared profits, limited liability, publishing accounts, royalties). Generic points such as "it is hard work" earn nothing.
2. **Explain (3)**: feature (1) → what it means in practice (1) → consequence for the owner (1), for example personal assets at risk, less control, less profit.
3. **Liability questions**: always say whose assets are at risk and what happens if the business fails.
4. **Franchise questions**: separate the franchisee's view from the franchisor's; link advantages to lower risk and disadvantages to fees, royalties and restrictions.
5. **Apply the case**: number of owners, how much they invest, how risky the idea is, how much control they want.
6. **Justify and evaluate**: weigh lower risk (limited liability, proven model) against cost and loss of control; conclude with a "depends on" factor such as the size of the debts involved or how much the owner values independence.
:::

::: example Explain - a disadvantage of being a sole trader
Explain **one** disadvantage to an entrepreneur of being a sole trader. (3)

The sole trader has unlimited liability (1), so they are personally responsible for all the debts of the business (1), which means that if it fails, their own possessions, such as their car or house, could be sold to pay what the business owes (1).
:::

::: example Explain - disadvantage of a partnership
Explain **one** disadvantage of operating as a partnership. (3)

Partners may disagree about how to run the business (1), for example over whether to borrow money to expand (1), which can slow down decisions or even lead to the partnership breaking up, harming the business (1).
:::

::: example Explain - advantage of a franchise
Explain **one** advantage to an entrepreneur of opening a franchise. (3)

The franchise has an established brand (1) that customers already know and trust (1), so the franchisee can attract customers from the day it opens, rather than spending months building a reputation, which reduces the risk of failure (1).
:::

::: example Outline - limited liability in context
Tariq and Megan plan to open a trampoline park in Doncaster, which will need a £180,000 loan. Outline **one** reason why they should set up as a private limited company. (2)

Limited liability (1): with a £180,000 loan, failure could leave huge debts, and as shareholders of a company they would lose only what they invested rather than their homes and savings (1).
:::

::: example Justify - franchise or independent
Zoe wants to open a children's coding club in Bath. Option 1: buy a franchise from a national coding-club brand for a £15,000 fee plus royalties of 8% of revenue. Option 2: start her own independent club, which would cost about £6,000 to set up. Justify which **one** of these options Zoe should choose. (9)

The franchise gives Zoe a brand that parents may already recognise, plus lesson plans, training and marketing support, so she can fill classes faster and with less risk than a new, unknown club (AO2). Parents are cautious about who teaches their children, so a trusted national name makes them more likely to sign up, bringing in revenue from the first term (AO3a).

However, the franchise costs £9,000 more to start, and 8% of every pound of revenue goes to the franchisor every year, even when her profits are low. She would also have to follow the franchisor's rules on courses and prices, limiting her independence (AO3a).

Zoe should choose the franchise, because the proven lesson plans and brand reduce the risk of a slow start, which matters when she will be paying rent for teaching space. This depends on how well known the brand is in Bath: if no local parents have heard of it, she would be paying a large fee and royalties for little benefit, and the independent route would let her keep all the profit (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Liability is about debts.** Unlimited = personal possessions at risk; limited = can lose only what was invested.
- **Sole traders and ordinary partnerships have unlimited liability; private limited companies have limited liability.**
- Disadvantages must be **specific to the type of ownership**. "Long hours" is weak for a sole trader; "unlimited liability" or "limited access to finance" is strong.
- A **private limited company cannot sell shares to the public**. That is a public limited company (2.1.1).
- Franchise questions: the **franchisee** buys the right; the **franchisor** sells it. Read which one the question asks about.
- Royalties are usually a share of **revenue**, so they are paid even when profit is low.
:::
