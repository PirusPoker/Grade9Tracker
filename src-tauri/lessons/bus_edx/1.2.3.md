## What it is

Not every customer wants the same thing. **Market segmentation** splits a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics or needs, so a business can choose which group to **target** and tailor its product, price, promotion and place to them. A **market map** then shows where competitors sit in the market, so the business can spot a **gap** and see the **competition**.

### Identifying market segments

Edexcel lists five ways to segment:

| Basis | How customers are grouped | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Where customers live or work: town, region, urban or rural, country | A Cornish ice-cream maker targeting tourists in coastal towns; a takeaway delivering within three miles |
| **Demographics** | Population characteristics such as gender, family size, ethnicity, occupation, religion | A baby-clothing shop targeting new parents; halal butchers serving Muslim customers |
| **Lifestyle** | How people choose to live: hobbies, values, activities | Vegan food for people avoiding animal products; outdoor kit for hikers; eco products for environmentally aware buyers |
| **Income** | How much customers earn and can spend | A budget hair salon for low-income customers; a luxury spa for high earners |
| **Age** | Customers' age group | Toys for under-5s; holidays for over-60s; gaming accessories for teenagers |

Businesses often combine bases: "families with young children on middle incomes living within ten miles" is a clearer target than any one basis alone.

### How segmentation helps a business target customers

- **Better-matched products**: knowing the segment's needs (1.2.1) lets the business design what they want, so more of them buy.
- **Efficient promotion**: advertising goes where that segment will see it (a teenage segment on social media, older customers in a local newspaper), so less money is wasted.
- **Right price**: a high-income segment may pay a premium; a student segment needs low prices.
- **Less head-on competition**: a small business can focus on a **niche** segment that larger firms ignore, such as gluten-free baking.
- **New sales**: spotting an under-served segment opens a new market.

**Drawbacks:** focusing on one segment means ignoring others, so if that segment's spending falls (for example tourists in a bad summer) sales drop sharply. Researching and serving several segments separately costs more.

### Market mapping

A **market map** (positioning map) is a diagram with two **axes**, each showing a feature customers care about. Competitors are plotted on it according to how customers see them.

Common pairs of axes:
- **Price**: low to high, and **quality**: low to high.
- **Traditional to modern**, and **cheap to expensive**.
- **Healthy to indulgent**, and **convenient to time-consuming**.
- **Young to old** customers, and **casual to formal** style.

**How to build one:** choose two features that matter to customers; research how customers see each competitor; plot them; look for **clusters** (many rivals in the same position, so heavy competition) and **gaps** (empty areas).

**Reading a map described as a table.** Exam questions may give positions as scores:

| Restaurant in a town | Price (1 low to 10 high) | Quality (1 low to 10 high) |
|---|---|---|
| Burger Bay | 2 | 3 |
| Pizza Point | 3 | 4 |
| Kebab King | 2 | 2 |
| The Grand Bistro | 9 | 9 |
| Le Jardin | 8 | 8 |

Low-price, low-quality outlets are clustered, and so are high-price, high-quality ones. There is a **gap** for **mid-price, good-quality** food (around 5 for price and 6 to 7 for quality), and nothing offers **low price with high quality**, although that position is hard to make profitable.

### What a market map is used for

- **Identifying a gap in the market**: a position no competitor fills.
- **Identifying the competition**: which businesses are closest to the planned position and so are direct rivals.
- **Positioning a new product**: deciding where to place it so it stands out.
- **Spotting threats**: a rival moving towards the business's position.

### Limits of market mapping

- **A gap may have no demand.** Nobody may want that combination (very high price and low quality is an obvious example), or there may be too few customers to make a profit. A gap needs **market research** to confirm demand before money is spent.
- **Positions are subjective.** They depend on how the owner or a small sample of customers sees each rival.
- **Only two features** are shown at a time, though customers judge on many.
- **Markets change**, so a map quickly goes out of date.

::: terms
- **Market segmentation**: dividing a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics or needs.
- **Market segment**: a group of customers within a market who share characteristics, such as age or lifestyle.
- **Target market**: the segment of customers a business chooses to aim its products and marketing at.
- **Demographics**: characteristics of a population, such as gender, family size, occupation or ethnicity.
- **Lifestyle segmentation**: grouping customers by how they live, such as their hobbies, values or activities.
- **Market map**: a diagram with two axes on which competitors are plotted to show their position in a market.
- **Gap in the market**: a position on a market map, or a customer need, that no competitor is currently meeting.
- **Niche market**: a small, specialised segment of a larger market.
- **Cluster**: a group of competitors positioned close together on a market map, showing intense competition.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the basis** using the spec words: location, demographics, lifestyle, income, age.
2. **Explain how it helps targeting**: what the business can tailor (product, price, promotion, place) for that segment, and the result (sales, less wasted promotion).
3. **Market map data**: read the two features, find the clusters and the empty positions; the gap is the combination no competitor offers.
4. **Never assume a gap is profitable.** Add that research must confirm demand, and that positions are subjective.
5. **Apply** to the case: which segment, which rivals are closest, what position the business should take.
6. **Justify and evaluate**: weigh the chance of new sales in a segment or gap against cost, risk and the lack of proven demand; conclude with a "depends on" factor.
:::

::: example Explain - segmentation by age
Explain **one** way a business could use age to segment its market. (3)

A leisure centre could split its customers into age groups such as children, adults and over-60s (1). Each group wants different activities and times, so it could offer swimming lessons for children after school and low-impact classes for over-60s on weekday mornings (1). By matching its timetable and promotion to each age group, more people in each group will sign up, increasing its sales (1).
:::

::: example Identify - a gap from a market map
A market map of coffee shops in a town uses price (1 low to 10 high) and atmosphere (1 basic to 10 luxurious). Positions: Bean Stop (2, 2), Café Express (3, 3), Corner Cup (2, 3), Velvet Lounge (8, 9).
Identify the gap in the market shown by this data. (1)

A mid-priced coffee shop with a relaxing, comfortable atmosphere (around 5 for price and 6 to 7 for atmosphere), since no competitor is in that position (1).
:::

::: example Outline - segmentation in context
Peak Kit is a small online shop in Sheffield selling walking boots and outdoor clothing.
Outline **one** way Peak Kit could segment its market. (2)

By lifestyle (1): Peak Kit could target people who go hill walking in the nearby Peak District, advertising in walking groups and on outdoor forums where these keen walkers will see its boots (1).
:::

::: example Discuss - benefits of a market map
Discuss the benefits to a new business of using market mapping. (6)

A market map shows where existing competitors are positioned on two features customers care about, such as price and quality. By seeing where rivals cluster, a new business can avoid launching into the most crowded position, where it would struggle to win customers from established firms, so it reduces the risk of low sales.

The map can also show a gap, a position no competitor fills. If the business positions itself there, it offers something different, so customers who want that combination have a reason to choose it, and it may be able to charge a suitable price without a price war. However, a gap is only useful if customers want it; a map cannot show demand, so the owner must still carry out research to check that enough customers would buy before investing.
:::

::: example Justify - market mapping or more advertising
Glow is a nail salon in Stockport. Sales have fallen since two new salons opened nearby. Option 1: spend £300 on research to build a market map of the eight salons in the area. Option 2: spend £300 on a month of local social media advertising. Justify which **one** of these options Glow should choose. (9)

Option 1 would show how the eight salons are positioned, for example on price and on the range of treatments, so the owner can see whether Glow is now competing head-on with the new salons (AO2). If the map reveals a gap, such as a premium salon offering bridal and special-occasion nails, Glow could reposition to target that segment, which gives a lasting reason for customers to choose it rather than a short-term boost (AO3a).

However, the map itself brings in no customers, and the positions are based on opinions, which may be inaccurate if the sample is small. The salon's sales are falling now, so it needs income soon (AO3a).

Glow should choose the market map, because advertising a salon that looks the same as two new rivals is unlikely to win back customers for long. This depends on Glow's cash position: if falling sales mean it may not be able to pay next month's rent, it needs the immediate customers that advertising brings first, and can map the market once its cash is secure (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- Use the **five spec bases**: location, demographics, lifestyle, income, age. Give a concrete group, not just the word.
- Segmentation is about **targeting**: say what the business would tailor for that segment and what it gains.
- **A gap is not automatically an opportunity.** Always add that research must confirm demand.
- When reading a market map, give the **position** of the gap (for example mid-price, high quality), not just "there is a gap".
- **Clusters** show heavy competition; a business entering a cluster needs another way to stand out.
- Positions on a map are **opinions**, so they may not match what all customers think.
:::
