## What it is

The **marketing mix** is the combination of decisions a business makes to sell its product to its target market. It is often called the **4Ps**: **price, product, promotion and place**. A start-up must get all four right, and they must **fit together**. Theme 2 (2.2) goes deeper into each P; here the focus is what each element is, why it matters, and how the elements work together.

### The four elements and why each matters

| Element | What it covers | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Product** | What is sold: the good or service, its features, quality, design, size, packaging, brand and range | If the product does not meet customer needs (1.2.1), no amount of promotion will make customers return. A strong product can carry a higher price |
| **Price** | What customers pay, and any discounts or offers | Price decides revenue per sale (1.3.2a) and must cover costs to make a profit. It also signals quality: too cheap can look poor, too dear can drive customers to rivals |
| **Promotion** | How the business tells customers about the product and persuades them to buy: advertising, social media, leaflets, special offers, sponsorship, public relations | Customers cannot buy what they do not know about. A start-up has no reputation, so it must build awareness |
| **Place** | How and where customers can buy: shop, market stall, website, marketplace, delivery app, through other retailers | The product must be available where and when target customers want to buy it, or sales are lost to rivals that are more convenient |

### How the elements work together

The 4Ps must be **consistent** with each other and with the target market. A mismatch confuses customers and wastes money.

- A **premium** product (hand-made chocolates in luxury packaging) needs a **high price**, promotion that stresses quality (attractive photos, gift-focused social media), and a **place** that suits it (a boutique shop or a well-designed website), not a discount market stall.
- A **budget** product needs a **low price**, promotion focused on value (offers, price comparisons) and a **place** that keeps costs down.

If one P changes, the others may need to change. Moving a product upmarket means raising the price and changing the promotion; cutting the price may mean simplifying the product to protect profit.

### Balancing the mix based on the competitive environment

What rivals do shapes the right mix (1.2.4):
- If competitors are **cheaper**, a small business may not win on price, so it puts more weight on **product** (quality, a USP, 1.1.3) and on service, and uses promotion to explain why it is worth paying more.
- If competitors **dominate promotion** with big advertising budgets, a small business may rely on low-cost promotion (social media, word of mouth, local events) and a convenient **place**.
- If the market is **crowded**, the business may target a **niche** segment (1.2.3) and tailor all four Ps to it.
- If there are **few competitors**, the business may be able to charge more and spend less on promotion.

### The impact of changing consumer needs on the mix

Customer needs change (1.1.1), and the mix must follow:
- **Product**: new ranges for new tastes, such as plant-based options, smaller portions, eco-friendly packaging.
- **Price**: if customers' incomes fall (1.5.4), they may want cheaper options or smaller pack sizes.
- **Promotion**: messages change to match what customers now care about, such as ethics or health; channels change as customers move to new platforms.
- **Place**: customers wanting convenience push businesses towards delivery, click-and-collect and longer hours.

A business that does not adapt its mix to changing needs loses customers to those that do.

### The impact of technology on the mix

**E-commerce** (selling online):
- **Place**: products can be sold 24 hours a day to customers anywhere, through the business's own website, marketplaces or social media shops. A small business can reach a national market without shops.
- **Price**: customers compare prices online in seconds, so prices are more competitive; but lower costs (no shop) can allow lower prices.
- **Product**: online businesses can offer a wider range (no shelf space limit) and personalised products.

**Digital communication** (social media, email, websites, messaging):
- **Promotion**: cheap, targeted promotion, aimed at the right age group, location or interests, with results that can be measured (clicks, likes, sales). Influencers and customer reviews spread the word.
- **Product**: customer feedback through comments and reviews helps improve the product quickly.
- **Two-way communication**: customers can ask questions and get quick replies, building relationships.

Remember: **e-commerce is a way of selling (place), not a form of promotion**. Advertising on social media is promotion; selling through an online shop is place.

### Why getting the mix right matters for a start-up

With limited money, a start-up cannot afford to waste spending on promotion for a product priced wrongly or sold in the wrong place. A well-balanced mix makes the most of a small budget, wins customers from rivals and builds the sales needed to survive.

::: terms
- **Marketing mix**: the combination of price, product, promotion and place that a business uses to sell its product to its target market.
- **Product (in the mix)**: the good or service sold, including its features, quality, design, packaging and range.
- **Price (in the mix)**: the amount customers pay for the product, including any discounts.
- **Promotion**: the ways a business tells customers about its product and persuades them to buy it.
- **Place**: how and where the product is made available to customers, such as shops, websites or delivery apps.
- **Balanced marketing mix**: a mix in which the four elements are consistent with each other and suit the target market and competition.
- **E-commerce**: buying and selling online, which affects the place element of the mix.
- **Digital communication**: communicating with customers through social media, email, websites and messaging.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the P** and say what it covers for this business.
2. **Explain (3)**: element (1) → how it affects customers (1) → effect on sales, revenue or profit (1).
3. **"Work together" questions**: show how changing one P requires the others to fit (premium product → higher price → quality-focused promotion → suitable place).
4. **Competition, changing needs, technology**: link each to a specific P change, then to sales or costs.
5. **Keep e-commerce under place.** Social media advertising is promotion.
6. **Justify (9) between two mix changes**: choose one; weigh its effect on sales and costs against how well it fits the other Ps and the target market; conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as what rivals charge or what research shows customers want.
:::

::: example Explain - importance of place
Explain **one** reason why place is an important element of the marketing mix. (3)

Customers must be able to buy the product where and when they want it (1); if it is not available in the shops or websites they use, they will buy a competitor's product instead (1), so choosing the right place increases sales and stops the business losing customers to more convenient rivals (1).
:::

::: example Explain - technology and the mix
Explain **one** way digital communication can affect the promotion of a small business. (3)

It allows targeted promotion on social media (1), so a business can show adverts only to people of the right age, location or interests (1), which means less money is wasted on people who will never buy, and more sales are made for each pound spent (1).
:::

::: example Outline - balancing the mix
Mesa is a new Mexican restaurant in Wolverhampton. Two large chain restaurants nearby offer cheaper meals and advertise on TV. Outline **one** way Mesa could balance its marketing mix to compete. (2)

Focus on product rather than price (1): Mesa could offer authentic, freshly made dishes that the chains do not, and promote this on local social media, so customers have a reason to pay slightly more than at the chains (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - changing consumer needs
Fresh Fold is a small business in Brighton that sells hand-made pasta at farmers' markets. More customers are asking for gluten-free products, and for home delivery. Analyse how these changing consumer needs could affect Fresh Fold's marketing mix. (6)

Product: Fresh Fold could add a gluten-free range to meet the growing demand (AO2). This brings in customers who currently cannot buy its pasta, increasing sales, although it may need separate equipment to avoid contamination, raising costs and possibly the price of the new range (AO3a).

Place: the requests for home delivery mean selling only at markets limits sales to customers who visit on market days (AO2). Adding an online shop with local delivery would let customers buy all week, increasing sales, but delivery adds costs and time, so Fresh Fold may need to set a delivery charge or a minimum order to protect its profit; it could promote the new range and delivery to its existing market customers to build online sales quickly (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - price cut or new product
Kerb is a coffee van in Plymouth. Sales have fallen since a café opened nearby. Option 1: cut the price of all coffees by 30p. Option 2: launch a range of iced drinks and smoothies for the summer. Justify which **one** of these options Kerb should choose. (9)

Option 2 gives Kerb a new product that the nearby café may not offer, so customers have a reason to choose the van, particularly in warm weather when hot coffee sales may dip (AO2). New products bring new customers rather than simply taking less money from existing ones, so revenue can rise without cutting the profit on each coffee (AO3a).

However, the new range needs ingredients, a blender and refrigeration, which cost money and space in a small van, and sales will be seasonal. If customers mainly choose the café for its seating, a new product will not win them back (AO3a).

Kerb should choose Option 2, because a 30p price cut reduces profit on every coffee and the café could match it, starting a price war a van cannot win. This depends on Kerb's location: if many of its customers are passing walkers or beach visitors in summer, cold drinks suit them well; if most are commuters wanting a quick hot coffee, a loyalty card might be better (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **E-commerce is place, not promotion.** Examiners give no credit when it is described as advertising.
- In "work together" questions, show the **link** between elements: changing one P means changing others.
- **Balance the mix to the competition**: a small business rarely wins on price against large rivals, so it leans on product, service and targeted promotion.
- When needs change, say **which P** changes and **how**, then the effect on sales or costs.
- Do not drift into Theme 2 detail (pricing strategies, product life cycle) unless it helps the answer. This topic is about the mix as a whole.
- Always use the case's product and customers when explaining a mix decision.
:::
