## What it is

Every business needs to know what it is trying to achieve. **Aims** and **objectives** give it direction, help the owner make decisions and give a way to measure success.

### Aims and objectives: the difference

| | Aim | Objective |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A **broad, long-term goal**: what the business wants to achieve overall | A **specific, measurable target** that helps achieve the aim |
| Example | "To become the most popular café in town" | "To serve 400 customers a week by the end of the first year" |
| How it is used | Gives overall direction and purpose | Breaks the aim into steps; progress can be checked |

Objectives are most useful when they are **SMART**: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic and Time-bound. "Increase sales" is vague; "increase monthly sales by 10% within six months" can be checked. SMART is good practice, but the exam focuses on the types of aims and objectives below.

**Why set them?** They give the owner a focus for decisions (for example, a survival objective means keeping costs low); they let the owner measure progress; they motivate the owner and any staff; and they show lenders and investors what the business plans to do (1.4.4).

### Financial aims and objectives

Financial aims and objectives are measured in **money or market figures**. Edexcel lists five:

| Objective | What it means for a start-up | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Survival** | Keep trading through the risky first months and years; cover costs and avoid running out of cash | "Break even within the first 12 months" |
| **Profit** | Make revenue greater than total costs, giving the owner an income and money to reinvest | "Make £15,000 profit in year two" |
| **Sales** | Increase the number of items sold or the revenue from sales | "Sell 2,000 units in the first year" |
| **Market share** | Increase the percentage of total market sales that the business holds | "Gain 10% of the town's takeaway market" |
| **Financial security** | Build enough income and reserves for the owner to feel financially safe, such as replacing a previous salary | "Pay myself £2,000 a month by the end of year two" |

**Key exam point:** on this spec, **sales and market share are financial** objectives. Multiple-choice questions test this split.

**Survival** is usually the first objective for a start-up, because many new businesses close in their early years (1.1.2). Profit often comes later.

### Non-financial aims and objectives

Non-financial aims and objectives are **not measured in money**. They reflect the owner's personal motives and values. Edexcel lists:

| Objective | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Social objectives** | Doing good for society or the environment, such as helping the community, providing jobs for disadvantaged people or reducing waste | A café that trains young people leaving care; a shop that uses only recycled packaging |
| **Personal satisfaction** | The pleasure of doing work you enjoy and are proud of | A furniture maker who loves crafting each piece |
| **Challenge** | The excitement of testing yourself and achieving something difficult | An engineer who wants to prove she can bring a product to market |
| **Independence and control** | Being your own boss and making your own decisions | A chef leaving a restaurant to run his own kitchen |

A **social enterprise** is a business whose main aim is social or environmental: it trades to make money, but uses its profit mainly for its social purpose rather than paying it to the owners. Non-financial objectives still need money: a business with a social aim must still survive financially.

### Why aims and objectives differ between businesses

| Factor | How it changes the objectives | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Size of the business** | A small start-up focuses on survival; a larger one may target market share or growth | A one-person bakery versus a regional chain |
| **Age (stage) of the business** | New businesses aim to survive and break even; established ones aim for profit and sales growth | Objectives change as the business evolves (covered further in 2.1.2) |
| **The owner's motives** | Some owners want wealth; others want independence, a challenge or to help others | A craftsperson may value satisfaction over profit |
| **Type of ownership** | A private limited company with outside shareholders may face pressure for profit; a sole trader can choose freely (1.4.1) | |
| **Level of competition** | Fierce competition may make survival or holding market share the main objective | A new takeaway on a street of takeaways |
| **Type of business** | A social enterprise puts social objectives first | |

### Conflicts between objectives

Objectives can pull against each other. A social objective, such as paying staff above the minimum wage or using ethical suppliers, raises costs and so may reduce **profit**. Growing **market share** by cutting prices may hurt **profit** in the short term. Independence may limit growth if the owner refuses outside investors. In justify questions, weigh which objective matters more for the business in the case.

::: terms
- **Aim**: a broad, long-term goal that gives a business its overall direction.
- **Objective**: a specific, measurable target that a business sets in order to achieve its aims.
- **Financial objective**: a target measured in money or market figures: survival, profit, sales, market share or financial security.
- **Non-financial objective**: a target not measured in money, such as social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, or independence and control.
- **Survival**: continuing to trade by covering costs and avoiding running out of cash, the main aim of most start-ups.
- **Market share**: the percentage of the total sales in a market made by one business.
- **Financial security**: having enough income and money in reserve for the owner to feel financially safe.
- **Social objective**: a goal of benefiting society or the environment rather than only making money.
- **Social enterprise**: a business whose main purpose is social or environmental and which uses most of its profit for that purpose.
- **SMART**: objectives that are specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify carefully.** Financial: survival, profit, sales, market share, financial security. Non-financial: social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence and control.
2. **Explain (3)**: name the objective (1), then say why it suits the business and what the owner would do to achieve it (2).
3. **"Why aims differ" questions**: pick one factor (size, age, owner's motives, ownership, competition) and develop how it changes the objective.
4. **Apply** in Sections B and C: use the owner's background and motives. Someone who left a job to "be their own boss" values independence.
5. **Justify (9) between objectives**: pick one; show what pursuing it does to costs, prices, customers and survival; give a drawback (the objective it sacrifices); conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as how secure the business's cash is.
:::

::: example Explain - why aims differ
Explain **one** reason why business aims and objectives differ between businesses. (3)

Businesses are at different stages (1). A new start-up has no customer base and limited cash, so its main objective is usually survival (1), while an established business that already covers its costs can aim for profit growth or a bigger market share (1).
:::

::: example Explain - a financial objective
Explain **one** reason why survival is an important objective for a new business. (3)

A new business has high risks in its first year (1), because it has no reputation and few regular customers while it still has to pay rent and other fixed costs (1), so its first target must be to cover its costs and avoid running out of cash; profit cannot be earned if the business closes (1).
:::

::: example Outline - non-financial objective in context
Ade worked as an accountant for 15 years before starting a pottery business in Whitstable. He says he wanted to "make things with my hands and answer to nobody".
Outline **one** non-financial objective Ade may have. (2)

Independence and control (1): after 15 years working for an employer, Ade wants to make his own decisions about what pottery to make and how to run his business (1). Accept personal satisfaction developed with his wish to make things by hand.
:::

::: example Justify - profit or social objective
Grounds for Good is a new coffee shop in Leicester run by Nia. Option 1: focus on profit, paying staff the minimum wage and buying the cheapest beans. Option 2: focus on a social objective, employing young people who have been unemployed for over a year, training them, and buying fair-trade beans, which raises costs by about £900 a month. Justify which **one** of these objectives Nia should focus on. (9)

Option 2 gives Grounds for Good a clear purpose that customers can identify with, especially in a city with many coffee chains (AO2). Customers who care about their community may choose it over rivals and pay slightly more, and local press coverage is free promotion, so sales may rise enough to cover some of the extra £900 a month (AO3a).

However, as a new business, survival matters most, and £900 a month of extra costs raises the sales Nia needs just to cover costs. Training inexperienced staff also takes time, which may slow service in the first months when the shop is trying to build a reputation (AO3a).

Nia should choose the social objective, because it gives a coffee shop in a crowded market a real point of difference that the chains cannot easily copy. This depends on her cash position: if she has enough savings or funding, such as a grant for employing young people, to cover the extra £900 a month while trade builds, the objective is sustainable; if not, she may need to start with the profit focus and add the social aim once the shop has survived its first year (AO3b).
:::

::: q
Define the term **business objective**. (1)
---
A specific, measurable target that a business sets to help it achieve its aims (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** benefit to a new business of setting objectives. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: they give the owner a clear focus (1), so decisions such as pricing or hiring can be checked against the target (1), and progress can be measured, so problems are spotted early and fixed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** reason why a social enterprise may find it harder to make a profit than other businesses. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: its social aims often raise its costs (1), such as paying for training or ethical materials (1), so for the same price it earns less profit per sale (1).
:::

::: q
State **two** non-financial objectives. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): social objectives; personal satisfaction; challenge; independence and control.
:::

::: q
Analyse why survival is likely to be the main objective for Pixel Repairs, a new phone-repair shop in Hull that has borrowed £12,000 and faces three established rivals in the same street.
(6)
---
Indicative content: £12,000 loan repayments must be paid whatever the sales; three established rivals already have customers, so building trade will be slow; rent and wages are fixed; running out of cash is the main risk; once regular customers are built, profit can become the focus.
Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Pixel Repairs. Level 2 (3–4): some development. Level 1 (1–2): isolated points. No context: maximum 3.
:::

::: watch
- **Sales and market share are financial objectives** on this spec. So are survival, profit and financial security.
- **Non-financial**: social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence and control. Learn the four exactly.
- An **aim** is broad; an **objective** is specific and measurable. Do not use them as if they mean the same.
- For "why objectives differ", develop **one** factor (size, stage, owner's motives, ownership, competition) rather than listing several.
- Social objectives still need the business to **survive financially**. Strong answers say how the extra costs will be covered.
- Use the owner's own words and background in the case to identify their objectives.
:::

::: test
Imran left his job as a sales manager to start Loop Bikes in Oxford, refurbishing old bikes and selling them at low prices. He wants the business to reduce waste, but he also has a mortgage and needs to replace his old salary within two years.

Analyse the possible conflict between Imran's objectives. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: social aim of reducing waste; low prices; mortgage; replacing his salary within two years (financial security).
- AO3a: refurbishing old bikes takes time and parts, so costs per bike are high, while low prices keep revenue per bike low; this squeezes the profit he needs to pay himself and his mortgage; pursuing financial security might mean raising prices or selling new bikes, which conflicts with his waste aim; but the waste-reducing message may attract customers willing to pay a little more, so the two aims need not conflict entirely.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Imran throughout.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.
:::

::: test
Explain **one** reason why a sole trader's objectives may differ from those of a private limited company with several shareholders. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: a sole trader answers only to themselves (1), so they can choose non-financial objectives such as independence or personal satisfaction (1), while shareholders who invested money expect a return, pushing a company towards profit objectives (1).

::: test
Rachel runs Rooted, a small plant shop and café in Sheffield. In its first year it broke even. She is deciding her main objective for year two.
Option 1: profit, by raising prices by 10% and cutting opening hours on quiet days.
Option 2: a social objective, running free weekly gardening workshops for local schools and older residents, costing about £150 a week.

Justify which **one** of these two objectives Rachel should choose. (9)
---
Indicative content:
- Option 1: having broken even, she can now aim to earn an income; higher prices increase profit per sale if customers stay; shorter hours cut wage costs; but customers may go elsewhere, and quieter hours may lose some sales.
- Option 2: builds goodwill and local reputation, bringing new customers; free promotion through schools and local press; but £150 a week (about £7,800 a year) adds to costs when she has only just broken even.
- Conclusion with a new factor, for example: depends on how loyal current customers are, or whether she can get local sponsorship or a grant for the workshops.

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): one option weighed with benefits and drawbacks, applied to Rooted; a conclusion with a new "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (4–6): some developed reasoning and use of the case; a partly supported judgement.
- Level 1 (1–3): isolated points; little use of the case.
:::

::: test
Ellis and Kemi are opening Two Forks, a restaurant in Bristol, as a partnership. Ellis wants the main objective to be gaining market share quickly by keeping prices low. Kemi wants to make a profit from the start so they can pay themselves. They have £20,000 of savings invested and a £15,000 loan. There are many restaurants nearby.

Evaluate which objective Two Forks should prioritise. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
---
Indicative content:
- AO1b: financial objectives (market share, profit, survival); why objectives differ; conflict between objectives.
- AO2: partnership with two owners who disagree; £35,000 invested and borrowed; many nearby rivals; Bristol.
- AO3a: Market share: low prices attract customers away from established rivals and build a base of regulars and reviews; but low prices mean low profit per meal, while loan repayments and their own living costs must be met. Profit: pays the partners and the loan, reducing financial risk; but higher prices in a crowded market may mean few customers at first, and a new restaurant without reviews may struggle. Survival may be the real priority, combining elements of both.
- AO3b: a supported judgement, for example market share for the first six months with a set date to review prices, because without customers there is no profit; depends on how long their savings can cover their personal costs, and how price-sensitive Bristol diners in that area are.

Levels:
- Level 3 (9–12): accurate knowledge; detailed application to Two Forks; balanced, developed analysis; a supported judgement with a "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (5–8): sound knowledge and some application; some developed analysis; a judgement with limited support.
- Level 1 (1–4): isolated knowledge, little application; an assertion rather than a judgement.
:::
