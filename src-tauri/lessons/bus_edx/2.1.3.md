## What it is

**Globalisation** is the process by which the world's economies become more closely connected, so that goods, services, money, people and ideas move between countries more easily. Cheaper transport, the internet and agreements to reduce trade barriers mean that a UK business can buy from a factory in Vietnam, sell to customers in Canada and compete with a rival in Germany, all in the same week.

For any business, globalisation brings both **opportunities** and **threats**. Edexcel asks about three areas:
1. The **impact** of globalisation: imports, exports, changing business locations and multinationals.
2. **Barriers** to international trade: tariffs and trade blocs.
3. How businesses **compete internationally**: the internet and e-commerce, and changing the marketing mix.

### Imports

**Imports** are goods and services bought from other countries. They affect UK businesses in two ways.

**Competition from overseas** (a threat)
- Foreign businesses sell into the UK market, often at **lower prices** because their wages or other costs are lower, or with products UK firms do not make.
- UK businesses must respond: cut costs, improve quality, **differentiate** their products (2.2.1) or target customers who value being British-made.
- Customers benefit from more **choice** and lower prices.

**Buying from overseas** (an opportunity)
- UK businesses can buy **raw materials, components or finished stock** from abroad, often more cheaply or of a type not available in the UK.
- Lower costs mean higher profit margins or lower prices for customers.
- Risks: longer **lead times** and delivery delays, harder **quality control**, **exchange-rate** changes (1.5.4), and ethical concerns about suppliers' working conditions (2.1.4).

### Exports

**Exports** are goods and services sold to customers in other countries.
- **Benefits**: a much larger market, so more sales; growth when the UK market is saturated; spreading risk across several countries; **economies of scale** from producing more.
- **Challenges**: different languages, tastes, laws and standards; transport and insurance costs; exchange-rate risk; tariffs; competition from local businesses that know their market.

### Changing business locations

Globalisation means a business can choose where to locate its production, offices or customer service **anywhere in the world**. Reasons to locate or **relocate** abroad include:
- **Lower costs**: cheaper labour, land or energy.
- **Proximity to markets**: producing close to overseas customers cuts transport costs and delivery times.
- **Avoiding tariffs**: making goods inside a trade bloc avoids the tariff that would be charged on goods brought in from outside.
- **Access to skills or raw materials** not available at home.

The drawbacks include the cost of moving, redundancies and bad publicity at home, communication problems across time zones and languages, quality concerns, and ethical questions about low pay. Some businesses have **reshored**, bringing production back to the UK for faster delivery and better quality control.

### Multinationals

A **multinational** is a business with operations (factories, offices or shops) in **more than one country**, not just one that sells abroad. Examples include car makers, fast-food chains, technology firms and supermarkets. Nissan, a Japanese company, builds cars in Sunderland.

| For the multinational | For the host country |
|---|---|
| **Benefits**: access to bigger markets; lower costs; avoiding tariffs; economies of scale; spreading risk | **Benefits**: jobs; training and new skills; investment; tax revenue; more choice for customers; business for local suppliers |
| **Drawbacks**: complex to manage; different laws and cultures; exchange-rate risk; reputation risk if overseas practices are criticised | **Drawbacks**: local firms may be forced out; profits may go back to the home country; the multinational may leave if costs rise elsewhere; possible environmental damage or low wages |

### Barriers to international trade

**Tariffs**
- A **tariff** is a **tax on imports**, charged by the government of the importing country.
- It raises the price of imported goods, making them **less competitive** against home-produced goods. It does not ban the import.
- Governments use tariffs to **protect home businesses** and jobs, and to raise tax revenue.
- **Worked example**: a UK shop imports jackets from outside the trade area at £40 each. A 12% tariff adds 40 × 0.12 = £4.80, so each jacket now costs **£44.80**. The shop must either raise its price or accept a lower margin.
- Effects: importers face **higher costs**; exporters face tariffs in **other** countries, making their goods dearer abroad; home producers competing with imports **gain**; customers pay higher prices.

**Trade blocs**
- A **trade bloc** is a group of countries that agree to **reduce or remove barriers to trade**, such as tariffs, between themselves.
- The **European Union (EU)** is the best-known example. Other trade agreements link countries in North America, South East Asia and elsewhere.
- **Inside the bloc**: businesses trade freely, with no tariffs and often shared rules and standards, so they have access to a large market at low cost.
- **Outside the bloc**: businesses usually face a **common tariff** on goods they sell into the bloc, and must meet the bloc's rules, so they are at a disadvantage against firms inside it. This is one reason multinationals build factories inside a bloc.
- Since leaving the EU, the UK trades with it under a separate agreement rather than as a member, which has added paperwork and checks for many UK businesses.

### How businesses compete internationally

**The internet and e-commerce**
- A website or online marketplace lets even a small business **sell to customers anywhere** without shops abroad.
- **Lower costs** of reaching overseas customers; open 24 hours across time zones.
- **Digital communication** (email, video calls) makes dealing with overseas suppliers and partners easier.
- Challenges: international delivery and returns costs, customs paperwork, website translation and local payment methods, and fierce global online competition.

**Changing the marketing mix to compete internationally**
- **Product**: adapt to local tastes, cultures, sizes, languages, laws and safety standards. For example, McDonald's adapts its menu to different countries.
- **Price**: set prices to suit local incomes and competitors, allowing for exchange rates, tariffs and transport costs.
- **Promotion**: translate and adapt advertising for local languages, culture and media habits; a slogan or image that works in the UK may offend or confuse elsewhere.
- **Place**: choose local retailers, distributors or online marketplaces popular in that country.

Adapting the mix wins more customers but **raises costs** and loses some economies of scale. Keeping one global mix is cheaper but may not suit local customers.

::: terms
- **Globalisation**: the growing connection of the world's economies through trade, investment, communication and the movement of people.
- **Imports**: goods and services bought from businesses in other countries.
- **Exports**: goods and services sold to customers in other countries.
- **Multinational**: a business with operations, such as factories or offices, in more than one country.
- **Relocation**: moving a business's operations from one place to another, which may be in another country.
- **Tariff**: a tax charged on imported goods, which makes them more expensive.
- **Trade bloc**: a group of countries that agree to reduce or remove trade barriers, such as tariffs, between themselves.
- **Barrier to trade**: anything that makes trading between countries more difficult or expensive, such as tariffs.
- **E-commerce**: buying and selling goods and services over the internet.
- **Host country**: the country in which a multinational sets up its operations.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the global factor** in the case: imports, exports, relocation, being a multinational, a tariff, a trade bloc, e-commerce or adapting the mix.
2. **Say whether it is a threat or an opportunity** for this business. Many factors are both.
3. **Open an explain answer with the point**, then develop two links to prices, costs, sales, market share or profit.
4. **For tariffs**, remember a tariff is a tax on imports, paid when goods enter a country. Calculate the extra cost as price × tariff rate.
5. **Apply the case**: where the business produces and sells, its costs, competitors and customers' tastes.
6. **For justify and evaluate**, weigh the cost savings or new sales against risks (quality, delays, reputation, exchange rates, cultural differences), then give a "depends on" conclusion.
:::

::: example Explain - competition from imports
Explain **one** impact on a UK business of increased competition from imports. (3)

Overseas rivals may sell similar products at lower prices because of lower labour costs (1), so UK customers may switch to the cheaper imports (1), which reduces the UK business's sales and forces it to cut prices or costs, squeezing its profit (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - tariff
A UK retailer imports 500 coffee machines from a country outside its trade area. Each machine costs £80. A tariff of 15% is charged on the machines. Calculate the total tariff paid on the 500 machines. (2)

Tariff per machine £80 × 15% = £12 (1); total £12 × 500 = £6,000 (1). An answer of £6,000 scores 2 without working.
:::

::: example Outline - globalisation in context
Brightwell Toys Ltd makes wooden toys in Cornwall and sells them in UK shops. Cheap plastic toys imported from overseas have become more popular. Outline **one** impact globalisation may have on Brightwell Toys. (2)

Cheaper imported plastic toys mean UK shops may stock fewer of Brightwell's wooden toys (1), so Brightwell's sales could fall unless it promotes the quality and British-made appeal of its wooden toys (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - factories in different countries
Vantor Appliances plc makes washing machines and has factories in the UK, Poland and Mexico. Analyse the benefits to Vantor of having factories in different countries. (6)

Having a factory in Poland lets Vantor produce inside the EU, so its washing machines sold to European customers avoid tariffs charged on goods from outside the bloc (AO2). This keeps its prices competitive against European rivals and protects its profit margin on a large market (AO3a).

A Mexican factory is close to customers in North and South America, cutting the cost and time of shipping heavy washing machines across oceans (AO2). Faster delivery to retailers there means fewer lost orders, and if one factory is hit by a problem, such as a strike or rising energy costs, Vantor can move production to the others, reducing the risk of being unable to supply customers (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - adapting the mix
Hearth & Home plc, a UK chain selling cookware, is opening its first 20 shops in Japan. Option 1: sell its existing UK range with Japanese labels. Option 2: develop a new range designed for Japanese kitchens and cooking styles. Justify which **one** of these options Hearth & Home should choose. (9)

Developing a range designed for Japanese kitchens means the products suit local cooking styles and the generally smaller kitchen space, so they meet customer needs better (AO2). This gives Hearth & Home a reason for Japanese customers to choose it over local rivals who already understand the market, increasing sales in its 20 new shops (AO3a).

However, a new range needs research, design and new suppliers, which is expensive and takes time. Hearth & Home also loses the economies of scale it gets from producing one range for all its shops, so its costs per unit will be higher (AO3a).

Hearth & Home should develop the new range, because selling UK products with new labels assumes Japanese customers want British cookware, and if they do not, all 20 shops could fail. This depends on how much market research it has done: if research shows strong demand for British-style products as something different, the cheaper option could work (AO3b).
:::

---

Discuss the impact of tariffs on businesses. (6)

Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):

::: q
Avalon Gin Ltd makes premium gin in Somerset. It wants to sell its gin to customers in the USA and Australia using its website.

Analyse the benefits and drawbacks to Avalon Gin of using e-commerce to compete internationally. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: premium gin; Somerset; USA and Australia; selling through its website.
- AO3a: Benefits: reaches overseas customers without opening shops or finding distributors, keeping costs low; the website is open across time zones; premium British gin may appeal as a distinctive product, supporting high prices. Drawbacks: shipping glass bottles is costly and risky; alcohol is subject to strict rules and taxes in other countries; customers cannot taste before buying; competition from established brands online.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Avalon Gin throughout.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.
:::

::: watch
- A **tariff is a tax on imports**. It makes imports dearer but does not ban them. A limit on quantity (a quota) is not in this spec, so do not confuse the two.
- A **trade bloc** removes barriers **inside** the bloc. Firms **outside** still face its tariffs. Say which side the business in the case is on.
- A **multinational** has operations in more than one country. A business that only exports from the UK is not a multinational.
- **Imports** have two sides in the spec: competition from overseas **and** buying from overseas. Read which one the question asks about.
- When changing the mix internationally, link the change to **local** tastes, laws, incomes or culture, and note the extra cost.
- Global factors link to **exchange rates** (1.5.4) and **ethics** (2.1.4). Use them for development and evaluation.
:::
