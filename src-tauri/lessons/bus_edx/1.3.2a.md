## What it is

To know whether an idea can work, an entrepreneur needs to calculate the money coming in (**revenue**), the money going out (**costs**) and the difference (**profit or loss**). Paper 1 always includes calculations from this topic, worth 2 marks each. **Formulae are not given in the exam**, so every formula below must be learned by heart. A calculator is allowed.

### Revenue

**Revenue** (sales revenue or turnover) is the money a business receives from selling its products.

**Revenue = price × quantity**

A candle maker sells 300 candles at £12: revenue = £12 × 300 = **£3,600**.

**Working backwards:** price = revenue ÷ quantity, and quantity = revenue ÷ price. If revenue was £3,600 from 300 candles, the **average selling price** = £3,600 ÷ 300 = £12. Average selling price is useful when a business sells at several prices: total revenue ÷ total units sold.

### Fixed and variable costs

| | **Fixed costs** | **Variable costs** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Costs that **do not change** with the level of output in the short run | Costs that **change directly** with the level of output |
| At zero output | Still have to be paid | Zero |
| Examples | Rent, business rates, salaries, insurance, loan interest, website subscription, advertising contract | Raw materials, ingredients, packaging, stock bought for resale, delivery per order, card-payment fees per sale |

Fixed costs are fixed **in total**, not forever: rent can rise when a lease is renewed. They simply do not change when output goes up or down.

**Total variable costs = variable cost per unit × quantity**

**Total costs (TC) = total fixed costs (TFC) + total variable costs (TVC)**

### Profit and loss

**Profit = revenue − total costs**

If total costs are greater than revenue, the answer is negative and the business has made a **loss**. Write a loss as, for example, "a loss of £400" or "−£400".

**Full worked example: Case Craft**, a business selling phone cases from a market stall.

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Selling price | £10 per case |
| Variable cost | £4 per case |
| Fixed costs (stall rent, insurance) | £1,500 per month |
| Cases sold in May | 400 |

1. Revenue = £10 × 400 = **£4,000**
2. Total variable costs = £4 × 400 = **£1,600**
3. Total costs = £1,500 + £1,600 = **£3,100**
4. Profit = £4,000 − £3,100 = **£900**

If only 200 cases were sold: revenue £2,000; TVC £800; TC £2,300; profit = £2,000 − £2,300 = **a loss of £300**. Fixed costs did not fall when sales fell, which is why low sales hurt so much.

### Interest

**Interest** is the extra amount paid back on top of the money borrowed: the cost of borrowing. It is a **fixed cost** for most small businesses with a loan.

**Interest (on loans) in % = (total repayment − borrowed amount) ÷ borrowed amount × 100**

Interest in pounds = total repayment − borrowed amount.

**Example.** A sole trader borrows £5,000 and repays £5,750 in total. Interest = £5,750 − £5,000 = £750. Interest % = £750 ÷ £5,000 × 100 = **15%**.

A common trap is dividing by the total repayment. Always divide by the **amount borrowed**.

### Percentage change

Edexcel also asks for the percentage change in costs, revenue or prices.

**Percentage change = (new figure − original figure) ÷ original figure × 100**

Total costs rise from £3,100 to £3,410: change = £310; £310 ÷ £3,100 × 100 = **10% increase**. Always divide by the **original** (older) figure. A negative answer is a decrease.

### How changes affect profit

| Change | Effect on revenue or costs | Effect on profit |
|---|---|---|
| Price rises (sales unchanged) | Revenue rises | Profit rises; but if customers buy fewer, revenue may fall |
| Sales volume rises | Revenue rises, variable costs rise | Profit rises as long as price is above variable cost per unit |
| Rent rises | Fixed costs rise | Profit falls by the full rise at every level of output |
| Ingredient prices rise | Variable cost per unit rises | Profit falls, more sharply the more units are sold |
| Interest rate on a loan rises | Interest (a fixed cost) rises | Profit falls (link to 1.5.4) |

### Showing your working

A correct answer with its unit scores **2 marks with no working**. If the answer is wrong, a correct method step can still earn 1 mark, so always set out the steps. Writing the formula on its own earns nothing. Round only at the end, and give 2 decimal places when the question asks for them.

::: terms
- **Revenue**: the money a business receives from selling its products, calculated as price × quantity.
- **Fixed costs**: costs that do not change with the level of output, such as rent, salaries and insurance.
- **Variable costs**: costs that change directly with the level of output, such as raw materials and packaging.
- **Total costs**: total fixed costs plus total variable costs.
- **Profit**: the amount by which revenue is greater than total costs.
- **Loss**: the amount by which total costs are greater than revenue.
- **Interest**: the extra amount repaid on top of the money borrowed, which is the cost of borrowing.
- **Average selling price**: total revenue divided by the number of units sold.
- **Percentage change**: the change in a figure divided by the original figure, multiplied by 100.
:::

::: method
1. **Write the formula you will use** (from memory, it is not given), then substitute the numbers. Writing the formula alone scores nothing, but it guides your method.
2. **Revenue** = price × quantity. **TVC** = variable cost per unit × quantity. **TC** = TFC + TVC. **Profit** = revenue − TC.
3. **Interest %** = (total repayment − borrowed) ÷ borrowed × 100.
4. **Percentage change** = (new − original) ÷ original × 100.
5. **Check units and rounding**: £ signs, % signs, 2 decimal places if asked; label a negative profit as a loss.
6. **Explain and justify questions**: link a change in price, output or costs to revenue or costs and then to profit. In a justify, use the case figures to show which option raises profit more, then weigh the risk to sales.
:::

::: example Calculate - total costs
A dog-treat maker has fixed costs of £1,800 a month. Each bag of treats has variable costs of £1.30. In June it made 1,100 bags. Calculate its total costs for June. (2)

TVC = £1.30 × 1,100 = £1,430 (1). TC = £1,800 + £1,430 = **£3,230** (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - profit from a table
| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Units sold | 650 |
| Selling price | £24 |
| Variable cost per unit | £9 |
| Fixed costs | £6,200 |
Calculate the profit. (2)

Revenue = £24 × 650 = £15,600; TC = £6,200 + (£9 × 650) = £6,200 + £5,850 = £12,050 (1). Profit = £15,600 − £12,050 = **£3,550** (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - interest percentage
Mo borrows £9,000 for a van and will repay £10,350 in total. Calculate the interest on the loan as a percentage. (2)

Interest = £10,350 − £9,000 = £1,350 (1). £1,350 ÷ £9,000 × 100 = **15%** (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - percentage change to 2 decimal places
A café's total costs rose from £8,400 in March to £9,150 in April. Calculate the percentage increase in total costs. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. (2)

Change = £9,150 − £8,400 = £750 (1). £750 ÷ £8,400 × 100 = **8.93%** (1).
:::

::: example Justify - cut variable costs or raise price
Sweet Spot makes cupcakes in Swindon. It sells 2,000 a month at £3.00, with variable costs of £1.20 each and fixed costs of £2,400 a month. Option 1: switch to a cheaper supplier, cutting variable cost to £0.95. Option 2: raise the price to £3.30. Justify which **one** of these options Sweet Spot should choose. (9)

Current profit = (£3.00 × 2,000) − (£2,400 + £1.20 × 2,000) = £6,000 − £4,800 = £1,200. Option 1 saves £0.25 on 2,000 cupcakes, so profit rises by £500 to £1,700 a month, and because the price stays at £3.00, customers have no reason to buy less (AO2, AO3a).

However, cheaper ingredients may lower the quality of the cupcakes. If regular customers notice a worse taste they may switch to rivals, and the £500 gain would be lost through lower sales; Sweet Spot's reputation may depend on quality (AO3a).

Sweet Spot should choose Option 1, because it raises profit by £500 without risking sales through a price rise, whereas Option 2 would add £600 only if all 2,000 customers kept buying at the higher price. This depends on whether the cheaper supplier's ingredients are of similar quality: Sweet Spot should test a batch with regular customers before switching (AO3b).
:::

---
C (1). Ingredients rise with the number of pizzas made. A, B and D are fixed costs.

---

::: q
A business borrowed £20,000 and repays £23,000 in total. Calculate the interest as a percentage. (2)
---
£23,000 − £20,000 = £3,000 (1); £3,000 ÷ £20,000 × 100 = 15% (1).

::: q
A business had revenue of £9,600 and total costs of £10,450. Calculate its profit or loss. (2)
---
£9,600 − £10,450 (1) = a loss of £850 (1). Accept −£850.

::: q
Explain **one** reason why a business's total costs might rise even if it sells the same amount. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: its fixed costs may rise, such as a rent increase when the lease is renewed (1), which adds the same amount to costs whatever the output (1), so total costs rise and profit falls even though sales are unchanged (1). Or suppliers raise prices, so variable cost per unit rises, developed.
:::

::: q
Analyse the impact on Rise & Shine, a small bakery in Leeds, of a 20% rise in the price of flour and butter.
(6)
---
Indicative content: flour and butter are variable costs, so variable cost per loaf rises; total costs rise at every level of output; if prices stay the same, profit per item and overall profit fall; raising prices could pass the cost on, but rival bakeries and supermarkets may keep customers if Rise & Shine is dearer; it could look for cheaper suppliers or cut waste.
Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to the bakery. Level 2 (3–4): some development. Level 1 (1–2): isolated points. No context: maximum 3.
:::

::: watch
- **Formulae are not given.** Learn revenue, TC, profit, interest % and percentage change by heart.
- **Interest %: divide by the amount borrowed**, not the total repayment.
- **Percentage change: divide by the original figure.** Students lost marks in 2025 by dividing by the new figure.
- Total variable costs = **variable cost per unit × quantity**. Do not add the per-unit figure to fixed costs.
- Write the **unit** (£ or %) and label a negative profit as a **loss**.
- Rent, salaries and insurance are **fixed**; materials, ingredients and packaging are **variable**.
- If the answer is wrong, a correct method step can still score 1 mark, so show your working.
:::

Explain **one** difference between fixed costs and variable costs. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: fixed costs do not change with output, but variable costs do (1); rent is paid in full whether a shop sells 10 items or 1,000 (1), whereas the cost of stock rises with every extra item sold, so it is zero if nothing is sold (1).

::: test

---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: reduce variable costs by finding a cheaper supplier (1), which lowers the cost of each item made (1), so if the price and sales stay the same, the gap between revenue and total costs widens and profit rises (1). Or raise price, sell more, cut fixed costs, developed.
