## What it is

**Cash** is the money a business holds in notes, coins and its bank account, ready to spend. It is not the same as profit. A business can be profitable on paper and still fail because it does not have the cash to pay its bills **when they are due**. A **cash-flow forecast** predicts the cash coming in and going out each month, so the owner can spot a shortage before it happens.

### Why cash matters

Edexcel lists these reasons:
- **To pay suppliers**: if suppliers are not paid on time they may stop delivering, refuse trade credit (1.3.4) or demand payment up front, so the business cannot make or sell its products.
- **To pay overheads**: rent, utilities, insurance and loan repayments are due whatever the sales. Unpaid rent can mean losing the premises.
- **To pay employees**: staff must be paid on time by law (1.5.3), and unpaid staff leave.
- **To prevent business failure (insolvency)**: a business is **insolvent** when it cannot pay its debts as they fall due. It may be forced to close even if its products sell well. Running out of cash is one of the most common reasons small businesses fail.

### The difference between cash and profit

| | **Cash** | **Profit** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Money actually held by the business now | Revenue minus total costs over a period |
| When counted | When money actually comes in or goes out | When a sale is made and costs are incurred, even if payment has not yet happened |
| Answers the question | "Can we pay our bills this month?" | "Is the business making money overall?" |

Why a **profitable business can run out of cash**:
- **Sales on credit**: a business sells goods in March but the customer pays in May. The sale counts towards profit in March, but no cash arrives until May.
- **Large one-off purchases**: buying a £6,000 oven uses a lot of cash in one month, though it will be used for years.
- **Paying for stock before it is sold**: a toy shop buys Christmas stock in October; cash goes out months before it comes back.
- **Loan repayments**: the repayment of the amount borrowed reduces cash.
- **Fast growth**: more orders need more stock and staff, paid for before customers pay.

Equally, a business can have plenty of cash and be **unprofitable**: for example, just after receiving a loan or the owner's savings.

### Cash-flow forecasts

A **cash-flow forecast** is a prediction of the cash inflows and outflows of a business over a future period, usually month by month.

- **Cash inflows**: money coming in, such as cash sales, payments from credit customers, a bank loan, the owner's capital, crowdfunding, or selling equipment.
- **Cash outflows**: money going out, such as rent, wages, stock and raw materials, utilities, loan repayments, interest, advertising and equipment.

**Formulae are not given in the exam**, so learn them:

**Net cash flow = cash inflows − cash outflows** (in a given period)

**Opening balance = closing balance of the previous period**

**Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow**

Negative figures are usually shown in **brackets**: (1,200) means −£1,200.

### Worked example: Bloom & Brew, a café opening in April

| | April (£) | May (£) | June (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 6,000 | 7,500 | 9,000 |
| Bank loan | 4,000 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total cash inflows** | **10,000** | **7,500** | **9,000** |
| Rent | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 |
| Wages | 2,800 | 2,800 | 2,800 |
| Stock | 2,400 | 2,900 | 3,300 |
| Equipment | 6,500 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total cash outflows** | **13,200** | **7,200** | **7,600** |
| **Net cash flow** | **(3,200)** | **300** | **1,400** |
| Opening balance | 2,000 | (1,200) | (900) |
| **Closing balance** | **(1,200)** | **(900)** | **500** |

How each figure was found:
- April net cash flow = £10,000 − £13,200 = −£3,200.
- April closing = £2,000 + (−£3,200) = −£1,200. This becomes May's opening balance.
- May net cash flow = £7,500 − £7,200 = £300; May closing = −£1,200 + £300 = −£900.
- June net cash flow = £9,000 − £7,600 = £1,400; June closing = −£900 + £1,400 = £500.

**Interpreting it.** The café will be **overdrawn** at the end of April and May, mainly because of the £6,500 equipment purchase. It is not failing: net cash flow is positive from May. The owner should arrange an **overdraft** of at least £1,200 in advance, or spread the equipment cost (lease it or buy some items later), or ask for a larger loan.

### What a cash-flow forecast is used for

- **Spotting shortfalls** in advance, so the owner can act before bills go unpaid.
- **Planning finance**: arranging an overdraft or loan of the right size at the right time, rather than an emergency loan on poor terms.
- **Timing spending**: delaying a purchase to a month with more cash.
- **Obtaining finance**: banks and investors expect a forecast in the business plan (1.4.4) to see whether a loan can be repaid.
- **Monitoring**: comparing actual cash flow with the forecast shows where the plan has gone wrong.

**What it is not.** A cash-flow forecast does **not** show profit and is not used to calculate break even. Examiners give no credit for saying it "works out the profit".

**Limitations.** It is only a **forecast**: sales may be lower than expected, customers may pay late, costs may rise. A new business has no past figures to base it on, so its forecast is especially uncertain.

### Ways to improve cash flow

| Increase inflows | Reduce or delay outflows |
|---|---|
| Ask customers to pay sooner, or offer a small discount for early payment | Ask suppliers for trade credit (pay later) |
| Sell for cash rather than on credit | Delay buying equipment, or lease it instead |
| Sell unused equipment or excess stock | Buy smaller amounts of stock more often |
| Inject owner's capital or arrange a loan or overdraft | Cut unnecessary overheads |

Each has a cost: discounts reduce revenue, overdrafts charge interest, and delaying purchases may slow growth.

::: terms
- **Cash**: the money a business holds in notes, coins and its bank account, available to spend.
- **Cash flow**: the movement of money into and out of a business over a period.
- **Cash inflow**: money coming into a business, such as sales receipts or a loan.
- **Cash outflow**: money leaving a business, such as payments for rent, wages or stock.
- **Net cash flow**: cash inflows minus cash outflows in a given period.
- **Opening balance**: the cash a business has at the start of a period, equal to the previous period's closing balance.
- **Closing balance**: the cash at the end of a period, equal to the opening balance plus net cash flow.
- **Cash-flow forecast**: a prediction of a business's future cash inflows and outflows, usually month by month.
- **Insolvency**: being unable to pay debts as they fall due, which can force a business to close.
- **Overheads**: regular costs of running a business that are not linked to output, such as rent and utilities.
:::

::: method
1. **Learn the three formulae**: net cash flow = inflows − outflows; opening balance = previous closing balance; closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.
2. **Complete-the-table questions**: work left to right, month by month. Closing balance of one month is the opening balance of the next. Watch the brackets for negatives.
3. **Check signs**: adding a negative net cash flow lowers the balance; a negative closing balance means the business is overdrawn.
4. **Explain (3)** on why cash matters: name who must be paid (suppliers, overheads, employees) and finish with the consequence (stopped deliveries, lost premises, insolvency).
5. **Cash v profit**: use a credit sale or a big purchase to show the timing difference.
6. **Justify (9) between fixes**: use the forecast's figures (size and timing of the shortfall) to choose a fix, weigh its cost (interest, lost sales, slower growth), and conclude with a "depends on" factor.
:::

::: example Calculate - closing balance
A business has an opening balance of £2,450. Its cash inflows for the month are £7,900 and its cash outflows are £9,000. Calculate its closing balance. (2)

Net cash flow = £7,900 − £9,000 = −£1,100 (1). Closing balance = £2,450 − £1,100 = **£1,350** (1).
:::

::: example Complete the table
| | July (£) | August (£) |
|---|---|---|
| Total cash inflows | 4,200 | 5,100 |
| Total cash outflows | 4,800 | 4,300 |
| Net cash flow | (600) | **X** |
| Opening balance | 1,000 | 400 |
| Closing balance | 400 | **Y** |
Complete the table by calculating the values of X and Y. (2)

X = £5,100 − £4,300 = **£800** (1). Y = £400 + £800 = **£1,200** (1).
:::

::: example Explain - advantage of a forecast
Explain **one** advantage to a small business of creating a cash-flow forecast. (3)

It shows in advance when the business may run short of cash (1), so the owner can arrange an overdraft or delay a purchase before the shortfall happens (1), which means bills such as rent and wages can still be paid on time and the business avoids becoming insolvent (1).
:::

::: example Explain - cash and profit
Explain **one** reason why a profitable business may run out of cash. (3)

It may sell to customers on credit (1), so the sales count towards its profit but the cash does not arrive for 30 or 60 days (1), while it still has to pay suppliers and staff now, so it can run out of cash even though it is making a profit on paper (1).
:::

::: example Justify - fixing a shortfall
Kerry runs Print Pal, a printing business in Hull. Her forecast shows a closing balance of −£2,800 in September, returning to +£1,500 in November. Option 1: arrange a £3,000 overdraft at 18% interest. Option 2: delay buying a £3,500 printer until December. Justify which **one** of these options Kerry should choose. (9)

An overdraft would cover the £2,800 shortfall with £200 to spare, so Kerry could still pay her suppliers and staff on time in September and October (AO2). Because the forecast shows the balance becoming positive by November, she would only use the overdraft for about two months, so the interest cost would be small, roughly £80 if she used all £2,800 for two months (AO3a).

However, the overdraft only covers the gap; it does not fix it. If sales are lower than forecast, the balance may not recover in November and she would be paying 18% interest on a growing debt. The bank can also ask for an overdraft to be repaid at short notice (AO3a).

Kerry should choose Option 1, because delaying the printer by three months could stop her taking on new orders, losing more profit than the overdraft interest costs. This depends on why she needs the printer: if it is a replacement for one that still works, delaying it costs her nothing and avoids interest entirely, so Option 2 would be better (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **A cash-flow forecast does not show profit.** Answers saying it is used to work out profit or break even scored zero in 2025.
- **Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.** The next month's opening balance is this month's closing balance.
- Watch **brackets**: (900) is −£900. Adding a negative net cash flow lowers the balance.
- **Cash ≠ profit.** Use credit sales or a big purchase to explain the difference.
- A loan received is a cash **inflow** but not revenue; repaying it is a cash **outflow**.
