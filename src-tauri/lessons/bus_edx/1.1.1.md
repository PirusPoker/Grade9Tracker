## What it is

Business is **dynamic**: markets never stand still. What customers want, the technology available and the products on offer keep changing, and every change opens an opportunity for someone and creates a threat for someone else. Edexcel's first topic asks two questions about this: **why** new business ideas come about, and **how** they come about.

### Why new business ideas come about

| Reason | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Changes in technology** | New technology makes a product or service possible, cheaper or more convenient than before | Smartphones and apps made app-booked taxis and food-delivery platforms possible; cheap cloud software lets a one-person firm run online bookings; 3D printing lets small firms make one-off parts |
| **Changes in what consumers want** | Tastes, lifestyles and values shift, so customers start wanting things they did not want before | Demand for plant-based food, gym classes and wellness, refillable and low-waste products, second-hand fashion, products that save time for busy households |
| **Products and services becoming obsolete** | A product is **obsolete** when it is no longer wanted or used because something better has replaced it. Its decline leaves a gap for the replacement, and often new services around it | DVD rental shops lost their customers to streaming; phone cameras made most compact cameras unnecessary; paper maps gave way to sat-navs and then to map apps |

The three reasons are linked. A new technology often **makes a product obsolete** and **changes what consumers expect** at the same time. When streaming arrived, customers quickly came to expect instant access to films without leaving home, so the rental shop was no longer convenient enough.

**How to use a reason in an answer.** It is not enough to say "the product became obsolete". Say **what replaced it**, **why customers switched** and **what opportunity** that opened. For example: "Phone cameras improved so much that most people stopped buying compact cameras, which left a gap for businesses selling phone accessories such as lenses and tripods."

### How new business ideas come about

| Route | What it means | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Original idea** | A genuinely new product, service or way of doing things that has not been offered before | Can be protected (for example by a patent) and has no direct competitors at first, so a higher price can be charged; the business can build a reputation as the first in the market | No proven demand, so it is high risk; research, development and testing are expensive and slow; customers may need educating about what it is, which costs money in promotion |
| **Adapting existing products, services or ideas** | Improving, changing or combining something that already exists, or taking an existing idea to a new place or new group of customers | Demand is already proven, so it is lower risk; it is cheaper and quicker to launch; customers already understand the product | Competitors already exist, so the business must find a way to stand out; it may have to compete on price, which squeezes profit; rivals can copy the adaptation |

Most small businesses start with an **adaptation**, not an invention. A café that adds vegan options, a dog-walking firm that adds GPS tracking so owners can see the walk on an app, or a takeaway that offers a cuisine new to its town are all adaptations. Each uses an existing, proven idea and changes one thing about it.

Ways of adapting include:
- **Improving** a product: better quality, a new feature, a more convenient size.
- **Combining** two ideas: a bookshop with a café; a launderette with a coffee bar.
- **Moving** an idea to a new location or market: a street-food style popular in big cities opened in a market town.
- **Changing the way it is delivered**: a mobile service that comes to the customer, or the same product sold online.

### The dynamic nature of business: why it matters

Because markets change, **standing still is risky**. A business that keeps selling yesterday's product will lose customers to those that adapt. The same change can be:
- an **opportunity** for a new entrepreneur with the right idea;
- a **threat** to an existing business whose product is becoming obsolete.

This is why the reasons for new ideas link to the rest of Theme 1. Spotting a change leads to **market research** (1.2.2) to check there is real demand, and the choice between original and adapted ideas is really a choice about **risk and reward** (1.1.2).

### Judging original versus adapted

In a justify or evaluate question the choice usually depends on **finance** (can the owner afford slow, costly development?), **evidence of demand** from research, how **crowded** the existing market is, whether the idea can be **protected** from copying, and the owner's **attitude to risk**.

::: terms
- **Dynamic**: constantly changing; markets keep shifting because of technology, tastes and new products.
- **Changes in technology**: new technology that makes a product or service possible, cheaper or more convenient, creating new business ideas.
- **Changes in what consumers want**: shifts in tastes, lifestyles and values that create demand for different products.
- **Obsolete**: no longer wanted or used because a better product or service has replaced it.
- **Original idea**: a genuinely new product, service or way of working that has not been offered before.
- **Adapting an existing idea**: improving, changing or combining a product, service or idea that already exists, or offering it to new customers.
- **Business opportunity**: a chance to meet a customer need that is not being met, or not being met well.
- **Proven demand**: evidence that customers already buy a type of product, which lowers the risk of launching it.
- **Patent**: legal protection that stops others copying an invention for a period of time.
:::

::: method
1. **Spot the command word and tariff.** Explain (3) = one reason plus two linked steps with no context; outline (2) = one point plus one development using the case; justify (9) and evaluate (12) need a judgement.
2. **Name the exact spec reason or route**: changes in technology, changes in what consumers want, products becoming obsolete; original idea or adapting an existing one.
3. **Build the chain**: the change happened, so customers now want or expect something different, so there is a gap a new business can fill, so it can make sales.
4. **Bring in the case** in Sections B and C: name the business, its product and the change that prompted it ("Priya noticed office workers wanting healthier lunches…").
5. **For original versus adapted**, weigh risk (proven demand or not) against reward (competition, price, protection).
6. **Justify conclusion**: choose one option, then add a "depends on" factor from the case that you have not used yet, such as the owner's savings or what the research showed.
:::

::: example Explain - a reason for new ideas
Explain **one** reason why new business ideas come about. (3)

Changes in technology create new ideas (1). For example, smartphones allow customers to order and pay through an app wherever they are (1), so entrepreneurs could set up businesses such as food-delivery and booking services that would not have been possible before (1).
:::

::: example Outline - from a mini-case
Tom ran a photo-printing shop for twenty years. As customers stopped printing photos, he converted the shop into a phone-repair business, which has been busy since opening.
Outline **one** reason why the idea for Tom's phone-repair business came about. (2)

Photo printing was becoming obsolete because customers now keep their photos on their phones (1), so Tom saw that the same customers needed their phones repaired, which gave him a new idea with steady demand from his existing high-street location (1).
:::

::: example Explain - adapting an idea
Explain **one** advantage to an entrepreneur of adapting an existing product rather than creating an original one. (3)

There is already proven demand for the product (1), because customers are already buying similar products (1), so the entrepreneur is less likely to fail through having no customers, which reduces the risk of losing the money invested (1).
:::

::: example Discuss - technology and new ideas
Discuss how changes in technology can lead to new business ideas. (6)

New technology can make a completely new service possible. Online booking and payment systems let a small business such as a personal trainer take bookings at any time without a receptionist, so ideas that once needed premises and staff can be started from home with very low fixed costs. This lowers the risk of starting up, so more people are able to turn an idea into a business.

Technology can also make existing products obsolete, which creates a gap for the replacement. As streaming spread, customers stopped renting DVDs, so demand moved to subscription services and to products that support them, such as smart TVs and streaming devices. However, the same change is a threat to firms that do not adapt, so whether technology creates an opportunity depends on whether the entrepreneur spots the change early, before larger rivals fill the gap.

(Level 3: two developed lines of reasoning, each with a clear cause and consequence, using accurate terms such as obsolete and fixed costs.)
:::

::: example Justify - original or adapted
Sana wants to start a business in Leeds. Option 1: launch an original product, a reusable lunch box with a built-in cooling pack she has designed, which would need £18,000 to develop. Option 2: open a salad bar, adapting the popular sandwich-shop idea, with start-up costs of £12,000. Justify which **one** of these options Sana should choose. (9)

Option 2, the salad bar, adapts an idea that already works, so there is proven demand from office workers wanting a quick, healthy lunch, and Sana needs £6,000 less to start (AO2). Because customers already understand what a salad bar sells, she will spend less on promotion and can make sales from the first week, which helps her survive the risky start-up period (AO3a).

However, there are likely to be sandwich shops and supermarkets nearby, so she must find a way to stand out, such as fresh ingredients made to order, and competition may stop her charging a high price, which keeps her profit per sale low (AO3a).

Overall Sana should choose the salad bar, because with no evidence yet that customers want her lunch box, spending £18,000 on development is a big risk for a first business. The decision depends on how crowded her chosen street is: if research shows there are already several healthy-food outlets near the offices, the original product, which has no direct competitors, may offer the better long-term reward (AO3b).

(Level 3: one option weighed with benefits and a drawback, applied to Sana throughout, and a conclusion that adds a new "depends on" factor.)
:::

::: q
Which **one** of the following is a reason why new business ideas come about?

A A business takes out a bank loan

B A product becomes obsolete

C A business pays its employees

D A business moves to larger premises

Select one answer. (1)
---
B (1). When a product becomes obsolete, a gap opens for its replacement. A, C and D are business activities, not reasons for new ideas.
:::

::: q
Define the term **obsolete**. (1)
---
No longer wanted or used because it has been replaced by something better (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** disadvantage to an entrepreneur of starting a business with an original idea. (3)
---
1 mark for a disadvantage, up to 2 for linked development. For example: there is no proven demand (1), because no one has sold the product before (1), so the entrepreneur may invest heavily and find too few customers buy it, losing the money put in (1). Or: development costs are high (1), as the product must be designed and tested (1), so it takes longer before the business makes a profit (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** way a change in what consumers want can create a business opportunity. (3)
---
1 mark for the way, up to 2 for development. For example: more consumers want to cut down on meat (1), so there is growing demand for plant-based meals that existing restaurants may not offer (1), so a new business specialising in them can attract customers whose needs are not being met (1).
:::

::: q
Gemma runs a mobile dog-grooming van. She noticed that many dog owners in her area work long hours and cannot take their dogs to a salon, so she visits customers' homes instead.

Outline **one** way Gemma's business idea came about. (2)
---
Gemma adapted an existing service (1): dog grooming already existed in salons, but she changed how it is delivered by bringing it to busy owners' homes, which suits customers who work long hours (1). No second mark without reference to Gemma's case.
:::

::: q
Analyse the impact on a high-street DVD rental shop of its service becoming obsolete. (6)
---
Indicative content: customers switch to streaming because it is cheaper per film and more convenient, so rentals and revenue fall; fixed costs such as rent and wages stay the same, so profit falls and losses follow; the shop may run short of cash to pay rent; it could adapt (sell retro games, become a café or repair shop) but that needs investment; without adapting it is likely to close.
Level 3 (5–6): developed chains linking obsolescence to revenue, costs and survival, applied to the rental shop throughout. Level 2 (3–4): some developed points with some application. Level 1 (1–2): isolated points. No reference to the rental shop: maximum 3.
:::

::: watch
- **"It became obsolete" is not an explanation.** Say what replaced it, why customers switched and what gap that left.
- Keep the three **why** reasons (technology, consumer wants, obsolescence) separate from the two **how** routes (original, adapting). Questions ask for one or the other.
- An **adaptation** is still a new business idea. Most successful start-ups are adaptations, so do not dismiss them as copying.
- An original idea is **high risk because demand is unproven**, not just because it is new. Link risk to money: development costs and possible failure.
- In an **outline** question, the second mark needs the case: name the business and what changed for it.
- In a **justify**, pick one option and weigh it. A conclusion that only repeats your points cannot reach the top level.
:::

::: test
Ruby wants to start a business in Norwich. She has £10,000 of savings.
Option 1: an original idea, a subscription box of craft kits designed by her, sold only online. She estimates development will cost £8,000.
Option 2: an adapted idea, a craft café where customers make items while having a drink, similar to cafés in nearby cities. Start-up costs would be £9,500.

Justify which **one** of these two options Ruby should choose. (9)
---
Indicative content:
- Option 1 benefits: no direct competitor with her designs; can sell across the UK, not just Norwich; lower start-up cost leaves a cash buffer. Drawbacks: demand unproven; online customers are hard to reach without spending on promotion; subscription income builds slowly.
- Option 2 benefits: proven idea in nearby cities, so lower risk; local customers can see it; sales from day one. Drawbacks: uses almost all her savings, leaving little cash; rent and staff costs are fixed; competition from existing cafés.
- Conclusion should add a new factor, for example: depends on what local research shows about demand, or whether she can find a lender for a buffer.

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): accurate knowledge applied to Ruby throughout; the chosen option weighed with benefits and drawbacks in developed chains; a conclusion that adds a new "depends on" factor from the case.
- Level 2 (4–6): some developed reasoning and use of the case; an attempt at a judgement that is only partly supported.
- Level 1 (1–3): isolated points, little use of the case, no real judgement.
Analysing only the benefits of both options, with no weighing, is not evaluation and cannot reach Level 3.
:::

::: test
Kiran is a product designer in Coventry. He has designed a folding baby buggy that also works as a car seat, which no one else makes. He estimates development and testing would cost £45,000 and take 18 months. Alternatively, he could set up a business selling a range of existing buggies online with his own brand name, which he could start for £8,000 within two months. Market research among 120 parents found that 38% would consider buying a buggy that is also a car seat.

Evaluate whether Kiran should launch his original product or adapt an existing one. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
---
Indicative content:
- AO1b knowledge: original ideas carry high risk and cost but no direct competition; adapted ideas have proven demand but face competition; risk and reward.
- AO2 application: £45,000 and 18 months versus £8,000 and two months; 38% of 120 parents is about 46 parents; the online branded buggies route.
- AO3a analysis: the original product could be patented and sold at a premium because nothing else does both jobs, so profit per unit is high; 38% interest suggests real demand. But 18 months with no sales means a long wait for any revenue, and he may need a loan or investor; safety testing for a car seat is demanding. The adapted route earns revenue quickly with low risk but faces many established online sellers, so prices and margins may be low.
- AO3b evaluation: a supported judgement, for example the original product because its unique feature gives it a clear selling point and the research shows interest, provided he can raise the £45,000; or the adapted route first to build income and a brand, then launch the original product later. The judgement should identify what it depends on (finance, sample size of only 120, whether a patent can be obtained).

Levels:
- Level 3 (9–12): accurate knowledge; developed application to Kiran's figures throughout; balanced analysis of both routes; a supported judgement with a "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (5–8): sound knowledge with some application; some developed analysis, possibly unbalanced; a judgement with limited support.
- Level 1 (1–4): isolated knowledge, little use of the case, an assertion rather than a judgement.
:::
