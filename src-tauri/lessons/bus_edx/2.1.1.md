## What it is

Theme 2 is about **building a business**: what happens once a start-up survives and wants to grow. Growth can bring **economies of scale** (lower costs per unit from buying and producing in bulk), a bigger **market share**, more power over suppliers and more profit. This topic covers three linked decisions:
1. **How** to grow: internally (organic) or externally (inorganic).
2. **What ownership** suits a large, growing business: the **public limited company (plc)**.
3. **How to pay** for growth: internal or external **sources of finance**.

### Internal (organic) growth

**Organic growth** means a business grows **from within**, by selling more of its own products or launching new ones, using its own resources. It is slower but lower-risk.

**1. New products**
- **Innovation**: turning a new idea into a product or process that customers want, such as a new flavour, feature or service.
- **Research and development (R&D)**: the scientific and technical work of investigating and testing new ideas before launch, for example testing new materials, recipes or software. R&D is **expensive and slow**, and many projects never reach the market, but a successful product can give the business a **unique selling point** and protect it from competitors.

**2. New markets**
- **Changing the marketing mix**: adapting price, product, promotion or place to attract a new market segment, for example a premium brand launching a budget range, or a children's product repackaged for adults.
- **Taking advantage of technology**: selling online through e-commerce to reach customers nationwide or worldwide, or using social media to reach new segments.
- **Expanding overseas**: exporting, or opening shops or factories in other countries (links to 2.1.3).

| Organic growth: advantages | Organic growth: disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Lower risk: the business grows in markets and products it understands | **Slow**: rivals may grow faster |
| The owners keep **control** and the business keeps its **culture** | Limited by the business's own profits and resources |
| Can often be financed from retained profit | New products may fail; R&D money may be lost |
| No problems of combining two different businesses | Overseas expansion brings unfamiliar laws, tastes and currencies |

### External (inorganic) growth

**External growth** means growing by **joining with or buying another business**. It is fast but more expensive and riskier.
- A **merger** is when two businesses **agree** to join together to form one new business, usually with the owners of both holding shares in the combined firm.
- A **takeover** is when one business **buys control** of another, usually by buying more than half of its shares. A takeover can be friendly or **hostile** (opposed by the target's directors).

| External growth: advantages | External growth: disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Fast**: instantly gains customers, products, staff, brands and market share | **Expensive**: buying a business can cost a huge sum, often needing loans or new shares |
| Removes or reduces a **competitor** | **Clash of cultures**: different ways of working cause conflict and demotivated staff |
| **Economies of scale**: bulk buying, shared costs, cutting duplicated jobs | **Job losses** and redundancy costs; bad publicity |
| Gains new skills, technology or access to new markets | Hard to manage a much larger firm; communication becomes slower |
| | The price paid may be too high; expected cost savings may not appear |

Well-known examples: Facebook (now Meta) bought Instagram in 2012, and Disney bought Pixar in 2006. Both were takeovers that gave the buyer products and skills it would have taken years to build itself.

### The public limited company (plc)

In Theme 1 you met the sole trader, partnership and private limited company (Ltd). A business that wants to raise large sums for growth can become a **public limited company (plc)**:
- Its shares can be **sold to the general public**, usually through the **stock market** (in the UK, the London Stock Exchange).
- The first sale of shares to the public is called a **stock market flotation**.
- Shareholders have **limited liability**, as with a Ltd.
- It must publish its **accounts** each year, so anyone, including competitors, can see its financial results.

| Becoming a plc: advantages | Becoming a plc: disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Can raise **very large sums** of share capital from the public to fund growth | **Flotation is expensive**: legal, banking and advertising costs |
| Shareholders have limited liability, so investors are willing to buy | **Loss of control**: the original owners may own a small share once new shares are sold |
| Being listed raises the business's **profile** and status, helping it borrow and attract customers | **Risk of takeover**: anyone can buy enough shares to gain control |
| Shares are easy to buy and sell, so investors can exit | **Accounts are public**, including to rivals |
| | Shareholders often want **short-term profits** and dividends, which may conflict with long-term plans |
| | The people who own the business (shareholders) are not the people who run it (directors), so their objectives may differ |

**Careful:** a plc is owned by its **shareholders**, not by "the public" in general, and it is not the same as a public-sector organisation run by the government.

### Sources of finance for growing and established businesses

Growth costs money: new premises, machinery, R&D, marketing campaigns or the price of a takeover. An established business has more options than a start-up (1.3.4).

**Internal sources** (from within the business):
- **Retained profit**: profit kept in the business rather than paid to the owners. **No interest** and no loss of control, but it may not be enough for big projects, and it means lower dividends for shareholders.
- **Selling assets**: raising cash by selling things the business owns but no longer needs, such as old machinery, spare land or a closed shop. No interest or loss of control, but the asset is gone, it may sell for less than it is worth, and a business only has so many spare assets.

**External sources** (from outside the business):
- **Loan capital**: borrowing a fixed sum from a bank or other lender, repaid with **interest** over an agreed time. The owners keep control, and repayments are predictable, but interest adds to costs, repayments must be made even if profits fall, and the lender may want **security** (assets it can take if the loan is not repaid).
- **Share capital**: money raised by selling **shares** to new or existing shareholders. It never has to be repaid and there is no interest, but new shareholders **own part of the business**, may expect **dividends** and gain a say in decisions. A Ltd can only sell shares privately (to people chosen by the existing owners); a **plc** can sell shares to the public through **stock market flotation**, raising far larger sums.

| | Retained profit | Selling assets | Loan capital | Share capital |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost | None (but lost dividends) | None | Interest | Dividends; flotation costs for a plc |
| Control | Kept | Kept | Kept (lender may impose conditions) | Shared with new shareholders |
| Repayment | None | None | Yes, with interest | None |
| Size available | Limited by past profits | Limited by spare assets | Depends on security and credit history | Potentially very large (plc) |

**Choosing finance for growth** depends on: how much is needed; whether the business has made enough profit; whether it has spare assets; how much debt it already has; current interest rates (1.5.4); and how much control the owners are willing to give up.

::: terms
- **Organic growth**: growth from within a business, by selling more of its existing products or developing new products and markets.
- **Innovation**: successfully turning a new idea into a product, service or process that customers want.
- **Research and development (R&D)**: investigating and testing new ideas, materials or technologies to create new products.
- **External (inorganic) growth**: growth by joining with or buying another business, through a merger or takeover.
- **Merger**: two businesses agreeing to join together to form one new business.
- **Takeover**: one business buying control of another, usually by buying more than half of its shares.
- **Public limited company (plc)**: a company with limited liability whose shares can be sold to the general public on the stock market.
- **Stock market flotation**: the first time a company's shares are offered for sale to the public on a stock exchange.
- **Retained profit**: profit kept and reinvested in the business instead of being paid out to the owners.
- **Selling assets**: raising cash by selling things the business owns, such as machinery, buildings or land.
- **Loan capital**: money borrowed from a bank or lender that must be repaid with interest over an agreed period.
- **Share capital**: money raised by selling shares in a company, giving the buyers part-ownership.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the method precisely**: organic (new products through innovation or R&D; new markets through the mix, technology or going overseas) or external (merger or takeover). Never mix up merger and takeover.
2. **Open an explain answer with what it is or does**, then develop two linked steps: speed, cost, risk, control, market share or economies of scale.
3. **Apply the case**: the business's size, cash, products, competitors and the figures given (profit, cost of the deal, interest rate).
4. **For finance**, say whether the source is internal or external, then weigh cost (interest, dividends), control, repayment and how much it can raise.
5. **For plc questions**, balance the large sums raised against loss of control, flotation cost, public accounts and takeover risk.
6. **Justify or evaluate**: take one option, give its benefits and drawbacks in context, then conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as how quickly rivals are growing or how much control the founders want.
:::

::: example Outline - internal growth
Hazelwood Foods Ltd makes ready meals sold in UK supermarkets. Outline **one** method of internal growth Hazelwood Foods could use. (2)

Hazelwood Foods could use research and development to create a new range of plant-based ready meals (1), giving supermarkets a new product to stock and attracting vegetarian customers, which increases its sales without buying another business (1).
:::

::: example Explain - disadvantage of a takeover
Explain **one** disadvantage to a business of growing through a takeover. (3)

The two businesses may have different cultures and ways of working (1), so employees from the business that has been taken over may resist changes and become demotivated (1), which reduces productivity and means the expected benefits of the takeover, such as lower costs, may not happen (1).
:::

::: example Explain - retained profit
Explain **one** advantage to a business of using retained profit to finance growth. (3)

Retained profit does not have to be repaid and carries no interest (1), so the business's costs do not rise when it uses the money (1), which means its profit is not reduced by interest payments and it avoids the risk of being unable to repay a loan if sales fall (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - becoming a plc
Arden Gyms Ltd owns 40 gyms in England. The founding family owns all the shares and wants to open 60 more gyms, costing about £90 million. Analyse the benefits to Arden Gyms of becoming a public limited company. (6)

As a plc, Arden Gyms could float on the stock market and sell shares to the public, raising far more than the family could raise privately (AO2). This could fund the £90 million expansion without taking on a huge loan, so the business would avoid large interest payments and the risk of being unable to repay if new gyms take time to fill (AO3a).

Becoming a plc would also raise Arden Gyms' profile, as its shares would be traded on the stock exchange (AO2). This could make banks and landlords more willing to deal with it when it signs leases for 60 new sites, and the publicity of the flotation could attract new members, increasing revenue across all its gyms (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - choosing a growth method
Riverside Coffee Ltd has 120 coffee shops in the north of England and wants to expand into the south. Option 1: open 30 new shops itself over three years. Option 2: take over a southern chain of 30 shops for £24 million. Justify which **one** of these options Riverside Coffee should choose. (9)

A takeover would give Riverside Coffee 30 shops with existing customers, staff and good locations immediately (AO2). This is much faster than finding and fitting out sites one by one, so Riverside would gain market share in the south before rival chains, and could cut costs by buying coffee and cups for 150 shops at once (AO3a).

However, £24 million is a large sum that would probably need a loan or new shares, adding interest costs or reducing the owners' control. The southern chain's staff may also resist Riverside's ways of working, and its shops may not suit Riverside's brand, so some may need expensive refits (AO3a).

Riverside Coffee should take over the southern chain, because in a crowded market the speed of gaining 30 established sites outweighs the cost and the risk. This depends on whether the chain's shops are profitable and in good locations: if many are loss-making, Riverside would be paying £24 million for problems, and organic growth would be safer (AO3b).
:::

---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: anyone can buy its shares on the stock market (1), so another business could buy enough shares to gain control (1), meaning the original owners could lose control of the business and its direction (1). Or: it must publish its accounts (1), so competitors can see its financial position (1) and use this to plan against it (1).

Explain **one** advantage to a business of growing organically. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: the business grows in products and markets it already understands (1), so it is less risky than buying an unfamiliar business (1), and the owners keep full control without a clash of cultures (1).

::: q
Discuss the impact on a business of using loan capital to finance growth. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):
- Owners keep control; the lender gains no ownership.
- Repayments are fixed and predictable, helping planning.
- Interest increases costs, reducing profit; variable rates may rise.
- Repayments are due even if the growth project fails, risking cash-flow problems; the lender may require security.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): accurate understanding; two developed lines of reasoning.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.

::: q
Tern Tech Ltd makes smart home thermostats sold in the UK. It plans to merge with Volta Ltd, a smaller firm that makes smart lighting and sells mainly in Germany.

Analyse the benefits to Tern Tech of this merger. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: thermostats and smart lighting; Volta's German market; merger agreed.
- AO3a: Tern gains a new product (lighting), so it can sell a wider smart-home range to its existing UK customers, increasing revenue per customer; it gains access to Volta's German customers and distribution, a quick way to expand overseas without building its own presence; shared R&D and bulk buying of electronic components lower costs per unit; but the merged firm must combine two cultures and languages.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Tern Tech throughout.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.
:::

::: watch
- A **merger** is agreed between two businesses to form one; a **takeover** is one business buying control of another. Mixing them up loses easy marks.
- A plc is owned by its **shareholders**. "Owned by the public" or "run by the government" is wrong.
- **Flotation** is only for a plc. A Ltd cannot sell shares on the stock market.
- Organic growth has two routes in the spec: **new products** (innovation, R&D) and **new markets** (the mix, technology, overseas). Name which one you mean.
- For finance, always say **who pays what**: interest for a loan, dividends and lost control for shares, nothing but the opportunity for retained profit.
- In a justify, the fast option is not automatically best. Weigh speed against cost, risk and control, using the case's figures.
:::
