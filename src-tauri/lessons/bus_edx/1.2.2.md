## What it is

**Market research** is gathering and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market to help a business make decisions. For a start-up with limited money, it is the main way to avoid spending savings on an idea nobody wants.

### The purpose of market research

| Purpose | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **To identify and understand customer needs** | Find out what customers want: price, quality, choice, convenience (1.2.1) | A gym asks members which class times suit them |
| **To identify gaps in the market** | Find a need that no business is meeting, or meeting well | A town has no late-opening café for shift workers |
| **To reduce risk** | Check there is real demand before money is spent, so the chance of failure falls | Testing a new sauce at a market stall before paying for a production run |
| **To inform business decisions** | Give evidence for choices on price, product, location and promotion | Finding that customers would pay £12 rather than £9 for a premium dog treat box |

Research **reduces** risk; it does not remove it. Customers may say one thing and do another, and markets change.

### Primary research

**Primary research** (field research) is collecting **new** information first-hand, for the business's own purpose.

| Method | What it is | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Survey** | Asking a sample of people the same questions, face to face, by phone or online | Reaches many people; online surveys are cheap; produces numbers that can be compared | Response rates can be low; people may not answer honestly; sample may not represent the market |
| **Questionnaire** | The written list of questions used in a survey, often with closed questions (tick boxes) and some open ones | Easy to analyse closed answers; same questions for everyone | Poorly worded or leading questions give misleading results; limited depth |
| **Focus group** | A small group of target customers discusses a product in depth, guided by a researcher | Detailed opinions and reasons why; can test samples and ideas; follow-up questions possible | Small group may not represent the market; one strong voice can sway others; time-consuming and may need payment or free samples |
| **Observation** | Watching and recording what people do, such as counting passers-by or watching how customers move around a shop | Shows real behaviour, not what people say they do; footfall counts help location decisions | Does not explain why people behave that way; time-consuming |

**Benefits of primary research:** it is up to date, specific to the business's own questions, and not available to competitors.
**Drawbacks:** it costs time and money to collect, and a small business may lack the skill to design a good questionnaire or choose a fair sample.

### Secondary research

**Secondary research** (desk research) uses information that **already exists**, collected by someone else for another purpose.

| Source | Examples | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Internet** | Competitors' websites and prices, online reviews, news articles, trade websites | Quick and often free; easy to check competitors | Quality varies; may be out of date or biased |
| **Market reports** | Reports by specialist research firms on the size and trends of a market | Detailed, professional analysis of a whole market | Expensive to buy; may cover the whole UK, not the business's local area |
| **Government reports** | Official statistics, such as population, incomes and spending patterns | Often free; large, reliable samples | General, not specific to the business; may be a few years old |

**Benefits of secondary research:** cheap or free, quick, and gives a broad picture of the market.
**Drawbacks:** not tailored to the business, possibly out of date, and also available to competitors.

Small businesses often start with **cheap secondary research** (local population figures, competitors' websites) and then use **low-cost primary methods** such as an online survey or observation.

### Qualitative and quantitative data

| Type | What it is | Example | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Quantitative** | Numerical data that can be counted and analysed statistically | "62% of 200 people would pay £5 or more" | Comparing options, spotting trends, calculations |
| **Qualitative** | Opinions, feelings and reasons, in words | "I'd buy it if the packaging was recyclable" | Understanding *why* customers behave as they do |

Closed questions and observation counts produce quantitative data; focus groups and open questions produce qualitative data. Good research usually uses both.

### The role of social media in collecting market research data

Social media gives small businesses cheap, fast research:
- **Polls and questions** in posts or stories ask followers to vote on new flavours, designs or prices.
- **Comments and reviews** show what customers like and dislike, in their own words (qualitative).
- **Analytics** (likes, shares, clicks, the age and location of followers) show which products and posts attract interest (quantitative).
- **Following competitors' pages** shows their prices, promotions and customers' complaints.

Limits: followers already like the business, so they are not a representative sample of the whole market; anyone can comment, including people who would never buy; polls can be answered on a whim.

### The reliability of market research data

**Reliable** data gives an accurate picture of the market, so decisions based on it are sound. Reliability depends on:
- **Sample size**: asking 10 people is far less reliable than asking 300.
- **Representative sample**: the sample should match the target market. Surveying only friends and family, or only one time of day, gives **bias**.
- **Question design**: leading questions ("Wouldn't you love a new café here?") push people towards an answer.
- **Honesty**: people may say they would buy to be polite.
- **Age of the data**: secondary data may be years old and the market may have changed.
- **Source**: who collected it, and could they be biased?

Unreliable data is dangerous because it can **increase** risk: an owner who trusts a biased survey may invest heavily in an idea with little real demand.

### Calculating with research data

Percentage of respondents = number giving that answer ÷ total respondents × 100.
Example: 84 of 240 people would buy weekly. 84 ÷ 240 × 100 = **35%**.

::: terms
- **Market research**: collecting and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market to inform business decisions.
- **Primary research**: collecting new information first-hand for the business's own purpose, also called field research.
- **Secondary research**: using information that already exists and was collected by someone else, also called desk research.
- **Survey**: asking a sample of people a set of questions to find out their views or behaviour.
- **Focus group**: a small group of target customers who discuss a product or idea in depth with a researcher.
- **Observation**: watching and recording how people behave, such as counting customers passing a site.
- **Qualitative data**: information about opinions, feelings and reasons, expressed in words.
- **Quantitative data**: numerical information that can be counted and analysed statistically.
- **Gap in the market**: a customer need that no business is currently meeting, or meeting well.
- **Reliability**: how far research data gives an accurate, trustworthy picture of the market.
- **Sample**: the group of people chosen to take part in research, meant to represent the target market.
- **Bias**: distortion in research results, for example from an unrepresentative sample or leading questions.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify first**: is the method primary (new data: survey, questionnaire, focus group, observation) or secondary (existing: internet, market reports, government reports)? Is the data qualitative or quantitative?
2. **Explain (3)**: one advantage or disadvantage of a method, then develop it to cost, time, accuracy or the decision it informs.
3. **Calculations**: part ÷ total × 100 for percentages; check whether 2 decimal places are needed.
4. **Reliability**: comment on sample size, who was asked, how questions were worded and how old the data is.
5. **Apply**: say what the research would tell **this** owner and what decision it would inform.
6. **Justify (9)** between methods: pick one, weigh what it reveals against its cost and reliability for the case, and finish with a "depends on" factor such as the owner's budget or how much detail is needed.
:::

::: example Explain - a disadvantage of primary research
Explain **one** disadvantage to a small business of using primary market research. (3)

It is expensive and time-consuming to carry out (1). The owner has to design a questionnaire, find a sample of people and analyse the results, or pay someone else to do it (1), which takes money and time away from running the business when a start-up has little of either (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - survey data
A smoothie bar surveyed 160 people. 52 said they would buy a smoothie at least once a week. Calculate the percentage of people who said they would buy at least once a week. (2)

52 ÷ 160 × 100 (1) = **32.5%** (1).
:::

::: example Outline - social media research
Coast Candles is a small business in Cornwall that sells candles through Instagram, where it has 4,000 followers.
Outline **one** way Coast Candles could use social media to collect market research data. (2)

It could post a poll asking followers to vote on three new scents (1), giving fast, free feedback from 4,000 existing customers about which scent to make in the largest quantity (1).
:::

::: example Discuss - reliability
Discuss the importance of the reliability of market research data to a new business. (6)

A new business uses research to decide whether to start up and how much to invest. If the data is reliable, because the sample is large and matches the target market, the owner can trust the estimate of demand, so stock, staffing and finance can be planned accurately and the risk of failure falls.

If the data is unreliable, for example a survey of 15 friends who all say they will buy, the owner may overestimate demand and invest savings in stock and premises that customers do not need. Unreliable research can therefore increase risk rather than reduce it, because it gives false confidence. However, even reliable research cannot guarantee sales, as people do not always do what they say, so the owner should test the idea on a small scale before committing large sums.
:::

::: example Justify - choosing a method
Rosie plans to open a children's soft-play centre in Wakefield. She has £600 for research. Option 1: a focus group of eight local parents, costing £300 including vouchers. Option 2: an online survey shared in local parents' Facebook groups, costing £50 in advertising. Justify which **one** of these methods Rosie should use. (9)

Option 2, the online survey, could reach several hundred local parents, the exact target market, for only £50 (AO2). A large sample gives quantitative data on how often parents would visit and what they would pay, which Rosie can use to forecast revenue in her business plan and show a lender, so it reduces risk (AO3a).

However, people who join Facebook groups may not represent all parents, and those who reply may be keen users who overstate how often they would visit. A survey also gives little detail on why parents choose one soft-play over another (AO3a).

Rosie should choose the online survey because she needs numbers to judge whether there is enough demand in Wakefield to cover the rent of a large building. It also leaves £550, which depends on how she uses it: if the survey shows strong demand, she could still afford a focus group afterwards to test her prices and layout, getting the reliability of a large sample and the depth of qualitative data (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Primary = new data collected first-hand. Secondary = data that already exists.** The internet is secondary even though it is online.
- A **questionnaire** is the list of questions; a **survey** is the process of asking a sample. Both are primary.
- **Qualitative** = words, opinions, reasons. **Quantitative** = numbers. Do not mix them up.
- Primary research is **not automatically reliable**: it is only as good as its sample and its questions, and it costs a small business time and money.
- Social media followers are **not a representative sample** of the whole market. Say so when evaluating.
- **Percentage = part ÷ total × 100.** Read which figure is the total.
- Research **reduces** risk; it never guarantees success.
:::
