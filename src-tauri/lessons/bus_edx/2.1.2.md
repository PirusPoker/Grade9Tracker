## What it is

In Theme 1 you learned that a new business sets **aims** (broad goals) and **objectives** (specific, measurable targets), and that a start-up usually focuses on **survival**. As a business evolves, its aims and objectives **change**. Edexcel asks two questions about this:
- **Why** do aims and objectives change? Five reasons are named in the spec.
- **How** do they change? Four kinds of change are named.

A strong answer always links the two: a **reason** (cause) leads to a **new objective** (effect), which then leads to **actions** and consequences for the business.

### Why aims and objectives change

| Reason | What it means | Example of the effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Market conditions** | Changes in demand, customer tastes, competition or the economy (1.5.4) | A new low-price rival forces a firm to shift from growth to protecting market share; a boom in demand encourages expansion |
| **Technology** | New technology creates new products, new ways of selling or new costs | The rise of online shopping leads a retailer to set an objective of growing online sales and closing some shops |
| **Performance** | How well the business is doing against its existing objectives | Strong profits and rising sales lead to more ambitious growth targets; falling sales or losses push the business back to survival |
| **Legislation** | New laws change what a business can sell or how it must operate | A ban on a type of product forces a business to exit that market; new environmental rules lead to sustainability targets |
| **Internal reasons** | Changes inside the business, such as new owners or managers, new shareholders, or the original owner's changing goals | A new chief executive sets an objective to cut costs; a founder nearing retirement focuses on stable profit rather than risky growth |

Two points to remember:
- **Performance works both ways.** Success leads to higher targets; failure leads to lower, survival-focused ones.
- **Internal reasons** are often about **people**: a takeover brings new owners with new priorities; shareholders in a plc (2.1.1) may push for higher profit and dividends.

### How aims and objectives change

**1. Focus on survival or growth**
- A business moves from **survival** to **growth** when it is secure and profitable: it now aims to increase sales, market share or the number of outlets.
- It moves from **growth** back to **survival** when it faces a crisis: a recession, a new competitor, falling sales, losses or cash-flow problems. Its objectives become cutting costs, protecting cash and keeping customers.

**2. Entering or exiting markets**
- **Entering** new markets: new regions, other countries, new customer segments or online. This happens when existing markets are saturated or a new opportunity appears.
- **Exiting** markets: withdrawing from areas, countries or segments that are unprofitable, too competitive or restricted by law, so resources can be used elsewhere.

**3. Growing or reducing the workforce**
- **Growing the workforce**: recruiting to support expansion, new shops or higher output.
- **Reducing the workforce**: making redundancies or not replacing staff who leave, to cut costs when sales fall or after technology replaces tasks.

**4. Increasing or decreasing the product range**
- **Increasing the range**: adding new products to attract new customers, sell more to existing customers, respond to new tastes or spread risk so the business does not depend on one product.
- **Decreasing the range**: cutting products that sell poorly or are in decline (link to the product life cycle, 2.2.1), to cut costs and focus on the best sellers.

### The business life cycle of objectives

A simple story shows the pattern:

| Stage | Situation | Typical objectives |
|---|---|---|
| Start-up | New, unknown, little cash | **Survival**; break even; build a customer base |
| Established | Profitable and known locally | **Growth**: more outlets, higher sales and market share, new products |
| Large and mature | Strong position, possibly a plc | **Profit and returns** for shareholders; market leadership; entering overseas markets; sometimes social or environmental targets |
| In difficulty | Falling sales, new rivals, recession | Back to **survival**: cut costs, exit weak markets, reduce the product range and workforce |

Many real businesses have gone through this cycle. A retailer can grow rapidly for years, then face competition from online sellers and shift to closing shops and cutting costs to survive.

### Why changing objectives matters

When objectives change, so do **decisions** across the business:
- **Marketing**: a new product range or a new target market changes the marketing mix.
- **Finance**: growth needs funding (2.1.1); survival needs cost control and cash.
- **Human resources**: growing means recruitment and training; survival may mean redundancies.
- **Operations**: new products may need new production methods or suppliers.

Changing objectives can also cause **conflict between stakeholders**: shareholders may welcome cost cuts that employees fear; a decision to exit a local market may upset that community.

::: terms
- **Aim**: a long-term, broad goal that a business wants to achieve.
- **Objective**: a specific, measurable target that helps a business achieve its aim.
- **Market conditions**: the state of a market, including demand, customer tastes, competition and the wider economy.
- **Performance**: how well a business is doing, measured by sales, profit, market share and other results.
- **Internal reasons**: changes within a business, such as new owners, managers or shareholders, that lead to new aims.
- **Survival**: an objective focused on continuing to trade, by covering costs and keeping enough cash.
- **Growth**: an objective focused on increasing the size of the business, such as sales, outlets or market share.
- **Product range**: the variety of different products a business offers for sale.
- **Exiting a market**: withdrawing from selling in a particular area, country or segment.
- **Redundancy**: dismissing employees because their jobs are no longer needed.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the reason** for the change in the case: market conditions, technology, performance, legislation or an internal reason.
2. **Name the new objective** precisely: survival, growth, entering or exiting a market, changing the workforce, changing the product range.
3. **Link cause to change** in an explain answer: "Because sales fell after a new rival opened (reason), the business focused on survival (change), so it cut costs (consequence)."
4. **Apply the case**: use the business's figures (sales, profit, number of outlets or staff) and what it sells.
5. **Analyse the consequences** for the rest of the business: finance, staff, marketing and stakeholders.
6. **Justify or evaluate**: weigh the chosen change's benefits and risks, then conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as how long the market change will last or how much finance the business has.
:::

::: example Explain - increasing the product range
Explain **one** reason why a business may increase its product range. (3)

Its existing products may be in the maturity or decline stage, with sales no longer growing (1), so adding new products gives customers a new reason to buy (1), which increases sales revenue and reduces the business's dependence on a single product (1).
:::

::: example Explain - why objectives change
Explain **one** reason why a business's objectives may change as a result of its performance. (3)

If the business's profits are much higher than its targets (1), its owners may feel confident enough to set more ambitious objectives such as opening new branches (1), because it now has the retained profit to finance growth rather than focusing on survival (1).
:::

::: example Outline - change in context
Marston Toys plc makes board games. Sales of its board games have fallen by 20% in two years as children spend more time on games consoles. Outline **one** way Marston Toys' objectives may change. (2)

Marston Toys may set an objective to enter the digital games market, developing app versions of its board games (1), because falling sales of board games caused by consoles mean it needs a new market to keep its revenue up (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - from growth to survival
Bluebell Bakeries Ltd has 200 bakery shops in the UK. Its objective was to open 30 new shops a year. Energy and ingredient costs have risen sharply and profit has fallen by 60%. Analyse why Bluebell Bakeries may change its objective from growth to survival. (6)

Bluebell's profit has fallen by 60% because energy and ingredient costs have risen sharply, and baking is energy-intensive (AO2). With much less profit, it has less retained profit to fund 30 new shops a year, and borrowing would add interest costs it can no longer easily afford, so continuing to grow could cause cash-flow problems (AO3a).

By focusing on survival, Bluebell could protect its cash, cut costs and keep its existing 200 shops profitable (AO2). It might close its least profitable shops and reduce its product range to the best-selling lines, cutting waste. This would let the business survive the period of high costs and return to growth once costs fall, rather than risking failure by expanding at the wrong time (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - workforce decision
Penrose Engineering Ltd makes car parts. A new automated production line will do the work of 40 of its 300 factory staff. Option 1: make the 40 workers redundant. Option 2: retrain the 40 workers to work in a new division making parts for electric vehicles. Justify which **one** of these options Penrose Engineering should choose. (9)

Retraining the 40 workers keeps experienced staff who already know Penrose's products and standards (AO2). A new electric-vehicle division lets Penrose enter a growing market, so the workers' skills support growth rather than being lost, and the other 260 staff see that the company values them, protecting motivation (AO3a).

However, retraining costs money and time, and the new division only makes sense if Penrose can win enough orders from electric-vehicle makers. If it cannot, Penrose will be paying 40 extra wages with little output, raising costs without matching revenue (AO3a).

Penrose should retrain the workers, because redundancy payments are also costly and the car industry is moving towards electric vehicles, so the new division protects its future. This depends on whether Penrose already has firm interest from electric-vehicle customers: without orders, redundancies may be the only way to keep costs under control (AO3b).
:::

::: q
Which **one** of the following is an internal reason for a change in a business's objectives?

A A new law bans a product the business sells

B A rival business cuts its prices

C A new chief executive is appointed

D New technology lets customers shop online

Select one answer. (1)
---
C (1). A is legislation, B is market conditions and D is technology, all of which are external.
:::

::: q
Which **two** of the following are ways in which a business's objectives might change as it evolves, as named in the specification?

A Entering or exiting markets

B Changing its name

C Growing or reducing the workforce

D Moving its head office

E Becoming a sole trader

Select two answers. (2)
---
A and C (1 mark each). B, D and E are not changes in aims and objectives named in the spec.
:::

::: q
Explain **one** reason why a business may decide to exit a market. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: the market may have become unprofitable because of strong competition (1), so the business is losing money selling there (1), and by leaving it can use its staff and finance in more profitable markets, raising overall profit (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** reason why a business may reduce its workforce. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: its sales have fallen in a recession (1), so it needs fewer staff to meet demand (1), and reducing the workforce cuts wage costs, helping it survive (1). Or: new technology does tasks previously done by staff (1), so fewer employees are needed for the same output (1), and cutting jobs lowers costs per unit (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss how a change in legislation could affect the objectives of a business. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):
- A ban or restriction on a product may force the business to exit that market or decrease its product range.
- New costs from compliance may shift the focus from growth to survival or cost control.
- New rules may create opportunities, such as demand for compliant or greener products, leading to new products or markets.
- Objectives such as sustainability targets may be added.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): accurate understanding; two developed lines of reasoning.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
:::

::: q
Hilltop Hotels plc owns 70 hotels in the UK. A new chief executive has been appointed after three years of falling profits. She has announced that the business will sell 15 of its smallest hotels.

Analyse the reasons why Hilltop Hotels' objectives have changed. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: 70 hotels; three years of falling profits; new chief executive; selling 15 smallest hotels.
- AO3a: poor performance (falling profits) means the growth objective was not working, so the focus shifts to profitability and survival; the new chief executive (an internal reason) brings new priorities and may be under pressure from shareholders for better returns; selling small hotels raises cash and cuts costs, letting the business concentrate on its most profitable hotels; market conditions such as competition from budget chains or holiday lets may lie behind the falling profits.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Hilltop Hotels throughout.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.
:::

::: watch
- Give the **reason with the change**. A list of possible objectives without saying why they changed scores little.
- Learn the five reasons by name: **market conditions, technology, performance, legislation, internal reasons**. Use the spec's words.
- **Performance** can push objectives up (success leads to growth) or down (failure leads to survival).
- Keep **why** and **how** separate: "a new competitor" is a reason; "reducing the product range" is a change.
- Develop changes into their **consequences** for finance, staff and marketing. That is where the analysis marks are.
:::

::: test

---

---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: its existing market is saturated, with few new customers left to win (1), so sales growth has slowed (1), and selling to a new region, country or segment gives it new customers, allowing revenue to keep growing (1).

Atlas Gym Group plc runs 150 gyms in the UK. Membership has fallen by 12% after low-cost rivals opened nearby. Its new objective is to protect market share rather than open new gyms.

- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.

::: test
Cartwright Kitchens Ltd makes fitted kitchens sold in the UK. Profit rose by 25% last year and the business has £3 million of retained profit. The directors want to grow.
Option 1: expand the product range by adding fitted bathrooms, sold to its existing UK customers.
Option 2: enter a new market by selling its kitchens in Ireland and France.

Justify which **one** of these two options Cartwright Kitchens should choose. (9)
---
Indicative content:
- Option 1: uses existing showrooms, fitters and customers; customers already trust the brand; but bathrooms need new suppliers, skills and designs, and the UK market may be competitive.
- Option 2: new customers, so sales can grow well beyond the UK; existing products already proven; but new laws, tastes and fitting standards; exchange-rate risk; needs local fitters or partners; higher costs to set up.
- Conclusion with a new factor, for example: depends on whether its fitters can be trained in bathrooms, or whether the UK kitchen market is saturated.

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): one option weighed with benefits and drawbacks, applied to Cartwright Kitchens; a conclusion with a new "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (4–6): some developed reasoning and use of the case; a partly supported judgement.
- Level 1 (1–3): isolated points; little use of the case.
:::

::: test
Greystone Retail plc owns 320 clothing shops in the UK and a website. Over five years:
- the share of its sales made online has risen from 15% to 40%;
- shop sales have fallen every year, and 60 shops now make a loss;
- profit has fallen from £48 million to £21 million;
- a new chief executive has just been appointed by the shareholders.

The new chief executive plans to close the 60 loss-making shops and reduce the workforce by 1,500.

Evaluate whether Greystone Retail should change its objectives in this way. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
---
Indicative content:
- AO1b: why objectives change (technology, market conditions, performance, internal reasons); how they change (exiting markets, reducing the workforce, survival rather than growth).
- AO2: online share up from 15% to 40%; 60 loss-making shops; profit fallen from £48 million to £21 million; new chief executive; 1,500 job cuts.
- AO3a: For: closing loss-making shops stops losses and raises profit; it follows customers' move online; savings could be invested in the website and delivery; shareholders appointed the new chief executive to improve performance. Against: redundancy payments and closure costs in the short term; job losses damage reputation and staff morale; some customers who shop in those towns may stop buying from Greystone at all, also reducing online sales; shops may support online sales through click-and-collect and returns.
- AO3b: a supported judgement, for example that the change is justified because the trend online is long-term and loss-making shops drain profit; depends on whether customers in those towns switch to the website or to rivals, and on whether some shops could be made profitable with lower rents.

Levels:
- Level 3 (9–12): accurate knowledge; detailed application to Greystone Retail; balanced, developed analysis; a supported judgement with a "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (5–8): sound knowledge and some application; some developed analysis; a judgement with limited support.
- Level 1 (1–4): isolated knowledge; little application; an assertion rather than a judgement.
:::
