## What it is

**Break even** is the level of output where a business's **total revenue equals its total costs**, so it makes neither a profit nor a loss. Selling more than this brings a profit; selling less means a loss. For a start-up, the break-even level of output answers the key question "how much do I need to sell just to cover my costs?" This builds directly on revenue, fixed costs and variable costs from 1.3.2a.

Edexcel examines both the **calculation** and the **interpretation of break-even diagrams**. **Formulae are not given in the exam**, so learn these three exactly.

### The formulae

**Break-even point in units = fixed costs ÷ (sales price − variable cost)**

Here the sales price and variable cost are **per unit**. The bracket (sales price − variable cost per unit) is the amount each unit sold contributes towards paying the fixed costs, sometimes called the **contribution per unit**.

**Break-even point in costs/revenue = break-even point in units × sales price**

**Margin of safety = actual or budgeted sales − break-even sales**

### Worked example: Bean Machine

Bean Machine is a coffee cart in Exeter.

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Fixed costs (pitch rent, insurance, loan repayment) | £3,000 a month |
| Selling price | £3.50 a cup |
| Variable cost (coffee, milk, cup, lid) | £1.00 a cup |
| Budgeted sales | 1,800 cups a month |

1. Break-even output = £3,000 ÷ (£3.50 − £1.00) = £3,000 ÷ £2.50 = **1,200 cups** a month.
2. Break-even revenue = 1,200 × £3.50 = **£4,200** a month.
3. Margin of safety = 1,800 − 1,200 = **600 cups** a month.

So sales could fall by 600 cups a month before Bean Machine starts making a loss. A large margin of safety means a lower risk of making a loss.

**Profit at the budgeted level**: revenue £6,300 − total costs (£3,000 + £1,800) = **£1,500**. A quick check: 600 cups above break even × £2.50 = £1,500.

Always round break-even output **up** to a whole unit if it is not a whole number: at 1,199.6 units the business has not quite broken even, so it needs 1,200.

### Working the formula backwards

Edexcel sometimes gives the break-even output and asks for another figure (in 2025 a question asked for fixed costs):
- **Fixed costs** = break-even output × (sales price − variable cost)
- **Sales price** = (fixed costs ÷ break-even output) + variable cost
- **Variable cost** = sales price − (fixed costs ÷ break-even output)

Example: break even is 400 units, price £45, variable cost £20. Fixed costs = 400 × (£45 − £20) = 400 × £25 = **£10,000**.

### Break-even diagrams

A **break-even diagram** (break-even chart) plots costs and revenue (£, vertical axis) against output or sales (units, horizontal axis). It has three lines:

| Line | Shape | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed costs** | Horizontal | Fixed costs are the same at every level of output |
| **Total costs** | Starts on the vertical axis at the fixed-cost level and slopes upwards | At zero output, total costs = fixed costs; each unit adds its variable cost |
| **Total revenue** | Starts at zero (the origin) and slopes upwards more steeply than total costs | No sales, no revenue; each unit adds the selling price |

Reading the diagram:
- **Break-even point**: where the total revenue and total costs lines **cross**. Read down to the horizontal axis for break-even output, and across to the vertical axis for break-even revenue.
- **Loss**: to the **left** of break even, total costs are above revenue. The gap between the lines is the loss at that output.
- **Profit**: to the **right** of break even, revenue is above total costs. The vertical gap between the lines is the profit.
- **Margin of safety**: the horizontal distance between the actual (or budgeted) output and the break-even output.

The same diagram as a table (Bean Machine):

| Cups per month | 0 | 600 | 1,200 | 1,800 | 2,400 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs (£) | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Total costs (£) | 3,000 | 3,600 | 4,200 | 4,800 | 5,400 |
| Total revenue (£) | 0 | 2,100 | 4,200 | 6,300 | 8,400 |
| Profit or loss (£) | −3,000 | −1,500 | 0 | 1,500 | 3,000 |

At 1,200 cups total revenue = total costs = £4,200, which is the break-even point.

### The impact of changes in revenue and costs

| Change | What happens on the diagram | Break-even output | Margin of safety (same sales) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Selling price rises** | Revenue line becomes steeper | **Falls** | Rises |
| **Selling price falls** | Revenue line becomes flatter | **Rises** | Falls |
| **Fixed costs rise** (such as rent) | Fixed and total cost lines shift **up** in parallel | **Rises** | Falls |
| **Fixed costs fall** | Fixed and total cost lines shift **down** | **Falls** | Rises |
| **Variable cost per unit rises** (such as dearer materials) | Total cost line becomes steeper | **Rises** | Falls |
| **Variable cost per unit falls** | Total cost line becomes flatter | **Falls** | Rises |

Bean Machine examples: if the price rises to £4.00, break even = £3,000 ÷ £3.00 = 1,000 cups. If pitch rent rises by £500, break even = £3,500 ÷ £2.50 = 1,400 cups.

A price rise lowers break even **only if customers keep buying**: if sales fall a lot, the margin of safety may shrink anyway.

### Uses and limitations of break-even analysis

**Uses**: shows a start-up how much it must sell before making a profit; tests "what if" changes in price or costs before deciding; supports a business plan and loan application (1.4.4); the margin of safety shows how risky a plan is.

**Limitations**:
- It assumes **all output is sold**, at the **same price**; in reality there are discounts, waste and unsold stock.
- It assumes costs stay the same; suppliers raise prices and fixed costs can change.
- It is only as accurate as the **forecast** figures; a new business is guessing at its sales.
- It is harder to use for a business selling many different products at different prices.
- It shows the quantity needed, not whether customers will actually buy that much; market research (1.2.2) is still needed.

::: terms
- **Break even**: the level of output where total revenue equals total costs, so there is neither profit nor loss.
- **Break-even output**: the number of units that must be sold to break even, found by fixed costs ÷ (sales price − variable cost).
- **Break-even revenue**: the revenue earned at the break-even point, found by break-even output × sales price.
- **Margin of safety**: the amount by which actual or budgeted sales are above break-even sales.
- **Break-even diagram**: a graph showing fixed costs, total costs and total revenue against output, with break even where revenue and total costs cross.
- **Contribution per unit**: the selling price minus the variable cost per unit, which goes towards paying fixed costs.
- **Profit area**: the part of a break-even diagram to the right of break even, where revenue is above total costs.
- **Loss area**: the part of a break-even diagram to the left of break even, where total costs are above revenue.
:::

::: method
1. **Write the formula from memory** and substitute: break even = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). Do the bracket first.
2. **Break-even revenue** = break-even units × price. **Margin of safety** = actual or budgeted sales − break-even sales, in units.
3. **Working backwards**: rearrange, for example fixed costs = break-even output × (price − variable cost).
4. **Units**: break-even output is in units (round up); break-even revenue is in £; margin of safety is in units unless asked otherwise.
5. **Diagram questions**: find where revenue and total costs cross; profit is the vertical gap to the right; margin of safety is the horizontal distance from break even to actual output.
6. **Explain the effect of a change**: say which line moves, which way break even moves, and what that means for risk and profit.
7. **Justify**: calculate break even and margin of safety under each option, then weigh the risk that sales change; conclude with a "depends on" factor.
:::

::: example Calculate - break-even output
A candle business has fixed costs of £2,700 a month. Each candle sells for £14 and has variable costs of £5. Calculate the break-even level of output. (2)

£2,700 ÷ (£14 − £5) = £2,700 ÷ £9 (1) = **300 candles** a month (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - fixed costs from break even
A driving instructor's break-even level of output is 160 lessons a month. Each lesson costs £38 and the variable cost per lesson is £8. Calculate the instructor's monthly fixed costs. (2)

Fixed costs = 160 × (£38 − £8) = 160 × £30 (1) = **£4,800** a month (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - margin of safety
A gym breaks even at 420 members. It has 515 members. Calculate its margin of safety. (2)

515 − 420 (1) = **95 members** (1).
:::

::: example Explain - a change in costs
Explain **one** effect on a business's break-even point of a rise in its rent. (3)

Rent is a fixed cost, so fixed costs rise (1). Each unit still contributes the same amount (price minus variable cost), so more units must be sold to cover the higher fixed costs (1). The break-even output rises, so the margin of safety falls and the business is more likely to make a loss if sales are lower than expected (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - a cost increase
Shine Bright is a window-cleaning business in Leeds. It charges £20 per house, with variable costs of £4 per house and fixed costs of £1,600 a month. It cleans 150 houses a month. Fuel and cleaning products have just risen, increasing variable costs to £6 per house.
Analyse the impact of the cost increase on Shine Bright's break-even point. (6)

Before the increase, Shine Bright broke even at £1,600 ÷ (£20 − £4) = 100 houses, giving a margin of safety of 50 houses (AO2). With variable costs at £6, each house contributes only £14, so break even rises to £1,600 ÷ £14 = 114.3, so 115 houses, and the margin of safety falls to 35 houses (AO2). The business is now more exposed: losing 36 regular customers would push it into a loss, where before it could lose 50 (AO3a).

Profit also falls, from £800 a month (50 houses above break even × £16) to £500 (150 × £14 = £2,100, minus £1,600 fixed costs). To restore its margin, Shine Bright could raise its price to £22, which would bring break even back to 100 houses, but customers may switch to cheaper window cleaners, so it may prefer to look for cheaper supplies or plan more efficient routes to save fuel (AO3a).
:::

::: q
A business has fixed costs of £8,400. Its product sells for £30 and has a variable cost of £16. Calculate the break-even level of output. (2)
---
£8,400 ÷ (£30 − £16) = £8,400 ÷ £14 (1) = 600 units (1).
:::

::: q
Using a break-even output of 600 units and a selling price of £30, calculate the break-even level of revenue. (2)
---
600 × £30 (1) = £18,000 (1).
:::

::: q
A business breaks even at 2,250 units. It expects to sell 3,000 units. Calculate the margin of safety. (2)
---
3,000 − 2,250 (1) = 750 units (1).
:::

::: q
On a break-even diagram, what is shown by the vertical distance between the total revenue line and the total costs line to the right of the break-even point? (1)
---
Profit (at that level of output) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** limitation of break-even analysis for a new business. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: it relies on forecast figures (1); a new business has no sales history, so its estimates of price, costs and sales may be wrong (1), meaning the break-even output may be inaccurate and the owner may be too confident about making a profit (1). Or assumes all output is sold at one price, developed.
:::

::: q
Explain **one** effect on break even of an increase in selling price. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: each unit contributes more towards fixed costs (1), because the gap between price and variable cost widens (1), so fewer units need to be sold to cover fixed costs and break-even output falls, provided customers keep buying (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Formulae are not given.** Break even = fixed costs ÷ (sales price − variable cost); break-even revenue = break-even units × price; margin of safety = actual or budgeted sales − break-even sales.
- Do the **bracket first**. £3,000 ÷ £3.50 − £1.00 is wrong; £3,000 ÷ £2.50 is right.
- Break-even output is in **units**, not pounds. Round **up** to a whole unit.
- Be ready to **rearrange**: fixed costs = break-even output × (price − variable cost). Most students could not do this in 2025.
- On a diagram, the **total costs** line starts at the fixed-cost level, not at zero; revenue starts at zero.
- A higher price lowers break even only **if sales hold up**. Say so in evaluation.
- Margin of safety is about **risk**: a small margin means a small fall in sales causes a loss.
:::

::: test
Fit Box is a small gym in Wigan. Its monthly fixed costs are £12,000 and the variable cost per member is £5 a month. It charges £35 a month and has 480 members.
Option 1: raise the membership fee to £40 a month.
Option 2: move to a cheaper unit, cutting fixed costs to £10,500 a month.

Justify which **one** of these two options Fit Box should choose. (9)
---
Indicative content:
- Current: break even = £12,000 ÷ £30 = 400 members; margin of safety 80.
- Option 1: break even = £12,000 ÷ £35 = 342.9, so 343 members; margin of safety 137 if all 480 stay; but members may leave for cheaper gyms, which would erode the margin.
- Option 2: break even = £10,500 ÷ £30 = 350 members; margin 130; no price rise so members stay; but moving costs, disruption and a possibly worse location may lose members.
- Conclusion with a new factor, for example: depends on how many members would cancel at £40, or how close the new unit is to current members.

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): one option weighed with benefits and drawbacks, using accurate break-even figures from the case; a conclusion with a new "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (4–6): some developed reasoning and use of the case; a partly supported judgement.
- Level 1 (1–3): isolated points; little use of the case.
:::

Their market research suggests average sales of 3,000 ice creams a month, but sales in summer could be three times higher than in winter. They will need a £20,000 bank loan to open.

Evaluate whether Sam and Ella should open Scoop, using break-even analysis. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
---
Indicative content:

- AO3a: For: average sales are a third above break even, so the plan is profitable on paper and the figures support a loan application. Against: the average hides seasonality: if summer sales are three times winter sales, winter months may be well below 2,250 and make losses, so cash for loan repayments must come from summer profits; break-even analysis assumes a constant price and costs; research forecasts for a new business may be optimistic.

- Level 3 (9–12): accurate knowledge; accurate break-even calculations from the case; balanced, developed analysis; a supported judgement with a "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (5–8): sound knowledge and some application; some developed analysis; a judgement with limited support.
- Level 1 (1–4): isolated knowledge, little application; an assertion rather than a judgement.
