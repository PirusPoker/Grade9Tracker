## What it is

**Product** is the first element of the marketing mix: the good or service a business offers to meet customer needs. In Theme 1 (1.4.3) you met the marketing mix at a simple level. Theme 2 goes deeper with three product ideas:
1. The **design mix**: balancing function, aesthetics and cost.
2. The **product life cycle (PLC)**: the phases a product goes through, and **extension strategies** to keep it selling.
3. **Differentiation**: why it matters that a product or service stands out from rivals.

### The design mix

The **design mix** is the combination of three elements that a business balances when designing a product:

| Element | What it means | Questions it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Function** | How well the product **works**: performance, reliability, durability, ease of use, features | Does it do the job? Is it safe? Does it last? |
| **Aesthetics** | How the product **looks, feels, sounds, smells or tastes**: its appearance, style, colour, shape and packaging | Is it attractive? Does it fit the brand image? |
| **Cost** | How much the product costs to **make**, which affects the price the business can charge and its profit | Can it be made at a cost that allows a profitable price? |

The three pull against each other, so the business must **balance** them for its **target market**:
- Better function (stronger materials, more features) and better aesthetics (premium finishes) usually **raise cost**.
- Cutting cost may reduce function or aesthetics.

Different products emphasise different elements:
- **Function** first: safety equipment, power tools, medical devices, hiking boots.
- **Aesthetics** first: designer fashion, perfume, jewellery, luxury furniture.
- **Cost** first: supermarket own-brand basics, budget furniture, disposable items.

The right balance depends on what the **target customers** value and the price they will pay. A budget brand that overspends on aesthetics will price itself out of its market; a luxury brand that cuts cost on aesthetics will lose its image.

### The product life cycle

The **product life cycle (PLC)** shows the stages a product goes through from its creation to its withdrawal. It is drawn as a graph with **sales on the vertical axis** and **time on the horizontal axis**. The curve starts at zero, rises slowly, then steeply, levels off, and finally falls.

| Phase | Sales | Profit | Typical price and promotion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Development** | None: the product is being researched, designed and tested | **Negative**: costs of R&D and testing with no revenue | No sales yet; market research and testing |
| **Introduction** | Low, rising slowly as customers learn about it | Usually **negative**: high launch costs and low sales | Heavy promotion to build awareness; either a high skimming price or a low penetration price (2.2.2) |
| **Growth** | Rising **rapidly** | Becomes **positive** and rises as costs are spread over more sales | Competitors start to enter; promotion builds brand loyalty; distribution widens |
| **Maturity** | **Peak** and level off; most potential customers have bought | **Highest**, then may start to fall as competition increases | Many competitors; prices may fall; promotion reminds customers and defends market share |
| **Decline** | **Falling**, as tastes change or newer products arrive | Falling; may become a loss | Promotion cut; price cuts to clear stock; eventually the product is **withdrawn** |

Key points:
- Not every product reaches every stage: many **fail at introduction**.
- The length of each stage varies hugely: a fashion item may last one season, while some food brands have been in maturity for decades.
- **Cash flow** is usually negative in development and introduction, so a business needs other products earning cash to fund new launches.

### Extension strategies

An **extension strategy** is an action taken to **extend the life** of a product, usually in **maturity** or early **decline**, to stop sales from falling. On the PLC graph it creates a new upward bump in sales before the decline. Common extension strategies:
- **New markets**: sell to a new segment or a new country.
- **New uses**: find new ways for customers to use the product.
- **Updated design** or new features: a new model, flavour, size or improvement.
- **New packaging**: a fresh look, new sizes or more convenient formats.
- **Promotion**: a new advertising campaign or rebranding to remind or attract customers.
- **Price changes**: cutting the price, or running special offers, to attract price-sensitive customers.

A well-known example is Lucozade, which was repositioned from a drink for people who were unwell to a sports and energy drink, reaching a new market.

Extension strategies **cost money**, and they only delay decline. A business must judge whether the extra sales will cover the cost, or whether it is better to invest in a **new product** instead.

### Differentiation

**Product differentiation** means making a product or service **different from competitors' products** in a way that customers value. A product can be differentiated by:
- **Design**: function (better performance, features) or aesthetics (distinctive look).
- **Quality** and reliability.
- **Branding** and image.
- **Unique selling point (USP)**: a feature no rival offers.
- **Customer service**, convenience or ethical and environmental features (2.1.4).

**Why differentiation matters**
- Customers have a **reason to choose** this product over rivals, so sales and market share rise.
- The business can charge a **higher price** without losing many customers, because customers see no exact substitute. This raises **profit margins**.
- It builds **brand loyalty** and repeat purchases.
- It reduces the need to compete on price alone, which would squeeze profit.

**Drawbacks**: differentiation can be **costly** (R&D, design, promotion), rivals may **copy** successful features, and the difference only matters if customers **value** it.

::: terms
- **Design mix**: the combination of function, aesthetics and cost that a business balances when designing a product.
- **Function**: how well a product works, including its performance, reliability and ease of use.
- **Aesthetics**: how a product looks, feels, tastes, sounds or smells, including its style and packaging.
- **Product life cycle**: the phases a product passes through over time, from development to decline.
- **Introduction**: the launch phase of the product life cycle, when sales are low and promotion costs are high.
- **Growth**: the phase when sales rise rapidly and the product becomes profitable.
- **Maturity**: the phase when sales peak and level off and competition is strongest.
- **Decline**: the phase when sales fall as customers switch to newer or different products.
- **Extension strategy**: an action taken to extend the life of a product and stop its sales from falling.
- **Differentiation**: making a product different from competitors' products in a way that customers value.
- **Unique selling point (USP)**: a feature that makes a product different from all its competitors.
:::

::: method
1. **Use the spec's terms precisely**: function, aesthetics, cost; development, introduction, growth, maturity, decline; extension strategy; differentiation.
2. **For the design mix**, say which element the business prioritises for its target market and what it gives up (usually cost or price).
3. **For the PLC**, identify the stage from the case (sales rising fast, levelling off, falling) and say what that means for sales, profit, price and promotion.
4. **For extension strategies**, name a specific strategy for this product, explain how it increases sales and note its cost.
5. **For differentiation**, link the difference to customer choice, then to price, margin or loyalty.
6. **Justify or evaluate**: weigh the chosen strategy's benefits against its costs and risks, then conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as which stage the product is at or what customers value.
:::

::: example Explain - the design mix
Explain **one** reason why a business may focus on the aesthetics of its product. (3)

Its target customers may choose products mainly on appearance and style, as in fashion or furniture (1), so an attractive design makes the product stand out from competitors (1), allowing the business to charge a higher price and increase its profit margin (1).
:::

::: example Explain - an extension strategy
Explain **one** way a business could extend the life of a product. (3)

It could launch the product in a new market, such as another country or a new segment (1), by adjusting its promotion and packaging to suit those customers (1), so the product gains new buyers and sales rise again instead of falling into decline (1).
:::

::: example Outline - PLC stage in context
Sales of Brightbox's smart speaker rose by 70% in its first year and a further 65% in its second year. Several rivals are now launching similar speakers. Outline the stage of the product life cycle the smart speaker is most likely to be in. (2)

The smart speaker is in the growth stage (1), because its sales are still rising rapidly by 65–70% a year and competitors are now being attracted into the market (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - importance of differentiation
Granary Bakes plc makes packaged bread sold in UK supermarkets, competing with large brands and supermarket own-label bread. Analyse the importance to Granary Bakes of differentiating its products. (6)

Supermarket shelves are full of similar loaves, so without differentiation customers have no reason to pick Granary's bread over cheaper own-label bread (AO2). By offering something distinctive, such as seeded, high-fibre or long-fermented recipes, Granary gives customers a reason to choose it, so it can win shelf space and protect its sales against the supermarkets' own products (AO3a).

Differentiation also allows Granary to charge a higher price than own-label bread (AO2). Customers who value the health benefits or taste of a distinctive loaf are less likely to switch when Granary charges more, so its profit margin per loaf is higher, giving it more profit to invest in new recipes. Without differentiation, it would have to compete on price with supermarkets, squeezing its margins (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - choosing an extension strategy
Seaview Snacks plc makes a range of crisps. Sales of its main brand have fallen by 5% a year for two years. Option 1: launch new flavours. Option 2: redesign the packaging and launch a large advertising campaign. Justify which **one** of these options Seaview Snacks should choose. (9)

Launching new flavours updates the product itself, giving existing customers something new to try and attracting customers who have become bored with the current range (AO2). New flavours can be promoted in-store and online, so sales could rise again and reverse the 5% annual fall, extending the brand's life (AO3a).

However, developing and testing new flavours costs money, and many new flavours fail. Supermarkets have limited shelf space, so new flavours may replace existing ones rather than adding sales, and rivals may quickly copy successful flavours (AO3a).

Seaview should launch new flavours, because a falling brand needs a reason for customers to buy, and new packaging and advertising for the same crisps may not change their minds. This depends on why sales are falling: if customers are switching to healthier snacks, new flavours of the same crisps may not help, and a baked or lower-fat version would be a better extension (AO3b).
:::

::: q

B There are no competitors

C Competition is at its strongest

D The product is still being tested

Select two answers. (2)
---
A and C (1 mark each). B describes introduction; D is development; E is growth.

::: q
Explain **one** reason why a business may focus on the cost element of the design mix. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: its target market is price-sensitive customers (1), so keeping production costs low allows a low selling price (1) while still making a profit on each unit sold, winning sales from more expensive rivals (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** drawback to a business of using an extension strategy. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: extension strategies such as new advertising campaigns cost money (1), and the product may still decline because customers have moved to newer products (1), so the money might be better spent developing a new product (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss the importance to a business of balancing the design mix. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):
- Function must meet customer needs, or the product will not sell and may be returned.
- Aesthetics attract customers and support the brand image and price.
- Cost determines the selling price and profit margin.
- Over-emphasising one element (for example costly aesthetics) can price the product out of its target market.
- The right balance depends on the target market.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): accurate understanding; two developed lines of reasoning.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.

::: q
Altura Outdoor plc makes waterproof jackets for serious hikers. A new range costs more to make because it uses stronger, lighter fabric.

Analyse why Altura Outdoor may prioritise function in its design mix. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: waterproof jackets; serious hikers; stronger, lighter fabric; higher cost.
- AO3a: serious hikers need jackets that keep them dry and safe in bad weather, so function matters most when they choose; a jacket that fails would damage Altura's reputation and lead to returns; better function differentiates Altura and allows a higher price, covering the higher cost; aesthetics matter less to this segment, so cost can be saved there.

- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Altura Outdoor throughout.

::: watch
- Learn the PLC phases **in order** and by the spec's names: **development, introduction, growth, maturity, decline**. Many students forget development.
- Draw or describe the PLC with **sales** on the vertical axis and **time** on the horizontal axis.
- The design mix is **function, aesthetics, cost**. Do not confuse it with the marketing mix (price, product, promotion, place).
- An extension strategy is used in **maturity or decline**, not at launch. Name a specific strategy and how it raises sales.
- Differentiation matters because it lets a business **charge more** and **keep customers**. Develop to price, margin or loyalty.
- Profit is usually **negative** in development and introduction, and **highest** in maturity.
:::
