## What it is

**External influences** are factors outside a business that it cannot control but that affect its sales, costs and profit. Theme 1 covers three of them: **technology** (1.5.2), **legislation** (1.5.3) and the **economic climate** (1.5.4). This topic asks two things:
- **Why external influences matter**: they can create opportunities or threats overnight, and a business that ignores them may lose customers, break the law or run out of cash.
- **How a business can respond** to a change in each one, and which response is most suitable.

You will rarely see a question that says "1.5.5". Instead, responses are tested inside **analyse, justify and evaluate** questions about a change in technology, the law or the economy: "Justify which option X should choose in response to rising costs."

### Why external influences are important

- **They change demand.** A recession, a tax change or a new app can raise or cut sales without the business doing anything differently.
- **They change costs.** A minimum wage rise, inflation or a weaker pound pushes costs up; a fall in interest rates or cheaper technology brings them down.
- **They change what the business is allowed or expected to do.** New laws must be followed, whatever they cost.
- **They change the competition.** New technology lets rivals, including online ones, reach the same customers.
- **Small businesses are especially exposed.** They have less cash to absorb shocks, often depend on one product or one local market, and have less time to research changes coming.

A business that **spots changes early** and **plans its response** can turn a threat into an opportunity; one that reacts late often loses out.

### Responding to changes in technology

| Change | Possible responses |
|---|---|
| Customers increasingly buy online | Open an online shop (e-commerce) or sell through an online marketplace; offer click-and-collect; use social media to reach customers |
| New payment systems (contactless, mobile wallets, apps) | Install card readers and accept app payments; offer online booking and prepayment |
| Digital communication becomes normal | Use email, messaging and video calls with customers and suppliers; send e-newsletters |
| New equipment or software that cuts costs | Invest in it (for example, booking software, stock systems, energy-efficient ovens); **train staff** to use it |
| Rivals use technology to undercut | Adapt the marketing mix: compete on personal service, quality or speed instead of price |

The drawbacks of responding: **set-up costs**, training time, ongoing fees, security risks and the need to keep updating. The risk of **not** responding: losing customers to rivals who are more convenient.

### Responding to changes in legislation

A business **must** comply with a new law, so the question is **how** it pays for and organises compliance:
- **Change processes**: new safety procedures, labelling, record-keeping, recruitment practices.
- **Train staff** in the new rules.
- **Absorb the cost**: accept a lower profit margin, which may suit a business with loyal customers and healthy profits.
- **Pass on the cost**: raise prices, which may lose price-sensitive customers.
- **Cut other costs** or improve efficiency to offset the extra cost, for example cutting staff hours, changing suppliers or reducing waste.
- **Use the law positively**: promote high standards ("all our products are allergy-labelled"), building trust.

### Responding to changes in the economic climate

| Change | Possible responses |
|---|---|
| Falling consumer income, rising unemployment | Cut prices or offer special offers; introduce a cheaper product range; focus on necessities; reduce staff hours; cut costs |
| Rising consumer income, falling unemployment | Introduce premium products; raise prices; expand; recruit (possibly with higher pay to attract staff) |
| Inflation | Raise prices; find cheaper suppliers; buy in bulk; cut waste; renegotiate contracts; keep wage rises in line |
| Rising interest rates | Repay loans or overdraft early; delay borrowing for expansion; offer cheaper products or payment plans to customers |
| Falling interest rates | Borrow to invest in equipment or expansion while borrowing is cheap |
| Tax rises | Cut costs; raise prices; reconsider expansion plans |
| Weaker pound | Importers: switch to UK suppliers, raise prices; exporters: sell more abroad, possibly raise prices in pounds |
| Stronger pound | Importers: widen margins or cut prices; exporters: cut prices in pounds to stay competitive, find new markets |

### How to judge a response

When a justify or evaluate question asks which response is best, weigh each option against:
- **Cost**: can the business afford it, and how long until it pays back?
- **Speed**: how quickly does it help? Some changes (a sudden cost rise) need a fast response.
- **Risk**: what could go wrong, for example customers reacting badly to a price rise?
- **Customers**: will it keep them? Does it fit what they value (price, quality, convenience)?
- **Competitors**: what are rivals doing? A price rise is safer if rivals face the same cost increase.
- **How long the change will last**: a temporary change may be worth absorbing; a permanent one needs a lasting response.

A good answer uses the **case**: the business's figures, products, customers and competitors decide which response is best.

::: terms
- **External influence**: a factor outside the business's control that affects its activity, such as technology, legislation or the economic climate.
- **Response**: an action a business takes to adapt to a change in its external environment.
- **Absorbing a cost**: accepting a cost increase without raising prices, so the profit margin falls.
- **Passing on a cost**: raising prices so that customers pay for a cost increase.
- **Cost cutting**: reducing spending, for example on staff hours, suppliers or waste, to protect profit.
- **Compliance**: following the requirements of the law.
- **Product range**: the set of different products a business sells, which it can widen or narrow in response to change.
- **Threat**: an external change that could reduce a business's sales or profit.
- **Opportunity**: an external change that a business could use to increase sales or profit.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the external change** in the case (technology, legislation or economic climate) and say whether it is a threat or an opportunity for this business.
2. **Name a realistic response** the owner could take, using the business's own products and situation.
3. **Explain how the response works** for an explain or outline: what it does to sales, costs or customers.
4. **Develop to money**: link the response to revenue, costs, profit or cash flow.
5. **Weigh it**: cost, speed, risk and customer reaction. For a justify, give the chosen option's benefits and drawbacks, not just the benefits of both.
6. **Conclude with a "depends on" factor** from the case, such as how long the change will last, how loyal customers are or how much cash the business has.
:::

::: example Explain - responding to a rise in costs
Explain **one** way a business could respond to rising costs caused by inflation. (3)

It could look for cheaper suppliers of its raw materials (1), comparing prices from several suppliers or buying in bulk to get a discount (1), so its cost per unit falls and it can protect its profit margin without raising prices (1).
:::

::: example Outline - responding to technology
Jas runs Jas's Jewels, a small jewellery shop in Wolverhampton. Sales in the shop have fallen as more customers buy jewellery online. Outline **one** way Jas could respond to this change in technology. (2)

Jas could set up an online shop selling her jewellery (1), so customers who no longer visit Wolverhampton high street can still buy from her and she wins back lost sales (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - responding to a new law
Clean Sweep is a cleaning company in Sheffield with 15 staff. A new law requires all cleaning staff to complete a certificated chemical-safety course. Analyse the possible responses of Clean Sweep to this new law. (6)

Clean Sweep must comply, so it could pay for all 15 staff to attend the course (AO2). This adds a one-off training cost and time away from paid jobs, which cuts revenue that week, but trained staff work more safely, reducing the risk of accidents and compensation claims (AO3a).

To cover the cost, Clean Sweep could raise its prices slightly (AO2). Because every cleaning company faces the same law, rivals are likely to raise prices too, so customers may accept a small rise. Clean Sweep could also advertise that all its staff are safety-certified, which may reassure business clients and help win contracts, turning the law into a selling point (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - choosing a response to falling incomes
The Hungry Fox is a restaurant in Reading. Customers' incomes have fallen and weekday bookings are down 25%. Option 1: introduce a cheaper two-course weekday set menu. Option 2: close on Mondays and Tuesdays to cut staff and energy costs. Justify which **one** of these options The Hungry Fox should choose. (9)

A cheaper set menu gives customers whose incomes have fallen a reason to keep eating out at The Hungry Fox rather than staying at home (AO2). Even with a lower price per meal, filling empty weekday tables adds revenue that covers some fixed costs such as rent, and customers who enjoy the set menu may return at weekends (AO3a).

However, the set menu lowers the profit on each meal, and some customers who would have paid full price may switch to it, reducing revenue from existing diners (AO3a).

The Hungry Fox should introduce the set menu, because closing two days cuts revenue completely and staff losing hours may leave. This depends on how long incomes stay low: if the fall is short-term, a set menu keeps customers' habits until things improve, but if bookings do not recover within a few months, reducing opening days may become necessary (AO3b).
:::

::: q
Which **one** of the following is a response to a change in legislation?

A Opening an online shop because customers prefer to buy online

B Training staff on a new food-labelling requirement

C Cutting prices because unemployment has risen

D Buying from UK suppliers because the pound has weakened

Select one answer. (1)
---
B (1). A responds to technology; C and D respond to the economic climate.
:::

::: q
State **one** way a business could respond to a rise in interest rates. (1)
---
Any one (1): repay loans or overdraft early; delay borrowing for expansion; offer cheaper products or payment plans; cut costs to cover higher interest.
:::

::: q
Explain **one** reason why it is important for a small business to respond to changes in technology. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: rivals who use new technology, such as online ordering, become more convenient for customers (1), so customers may switch to them (1), reducing the small business's sales and threatening its survival unless it adapts (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** drawback to a business of passing on a cost increase through higher prices. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: price-sensitive customers may buy from cheaper rivals (1), so the number of units sold falls (1), and revenue may fall by more than the cost saving, reducing profit (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss how a business could respond to a fall in consumer income. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):
- Cut prices or run special offers to keep customers; this keeps sales volume but cuts margins.
- Introduce a cheaper range or focus on essentials; this attracts customers trading down but may weaken a premium image.
- Cut costs (staff hours, suppliers, waste) to protect profit; this may harm service quality.
- Target customers less affected, for example wealthier segments.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): accurate understanding; two developed lines of reasoning.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
:::

::: q
Ivy runs Ivy Lane Prints, a printing business in Hull that imports its paper from Finland, priced in euros. The pound has weakened against the euro.

Analyse how Ivy Lane Prints could respond to the weaker pound. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: imported paper priced in euros; pound weaker, so paper costs more in pounds.
- AO3a: switch to a UK paper supplier, avoiding exchange-rate risk but possibly at a higher price or lower quality; raise print prices to pass on costs, risking customers going to online printers; buy a larger stock when the rate is favourable; cut waste to use less paper; the best response depends on how much paper costs as a share of total costs.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Ivy Lane Prints throughout.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.
:::

::: watch
- The question asks for the business's **response**. Describing the change (for example what inflation is) earns little on its own.
- Choose a response that a **small business could realistically afford**: an independent café will not build a national app.
- A business **must** comply with a new law. Responses are about how to comply and how to cover the cost, not whether to obey.
- An explain question about a "way" to respond needs development of **how** the response works, not just why it is good.
- In a justify, weigh **one** option's benefits and drawbacks. Listing the benefits of both options is not evaluation.
- Use **time**: is the change temporary or permanent? It is the most useful "depends on" factor here.
:::

::: test
A café sells 2,000 coffees a month at £3.00 each. Its milk costs rise by £0.15 per coffee. The owner responds by raising the price to £3.20 and sales fall to 1,900 coffees. Calculate the change in monthly revenue. (2)
---
Old revenue 2,000 × £3.00 = £6,000; new revenue 1,900 × £3.20 = £6,080 (1). Revenue rises by £80 (1). An answer of £80 increase scores 2.
:::

::: test
Explain **one** way a business could respond to rising unemployment in its area. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: it could introduce a cheaper product range (1), aimed at customers whose incomes have fallen or who are worried about their jobs (1), so it keeps their custom instead of losing them to discount rivals, protecting sales (1).
:::

::: test
Explain **one** benefit to a business of investing in new technology in response to a change in technology. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: accepting app and contactless payments (1) makes buying quicker and more convenient for customers (1), so fewer walk away from queues and sales increase (1).

::: test

---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: its competitors have not raised their prices (1), so a price rise could lead customers to switch to them (1), and losing sales could reduce profit by more than absorbing the cost would (1).

::: test
The Linen Basket is a laundry in Plymouth serving hotels and holiday lets. Its energy bills have risen by £800 a month because of inflation. Its monthly profit was £3,000.
Option 1: raise its prices by 8%.
Option 2: buy new energy-efficient machines for £15,000 using a bank loan, cutting energy costs by £500 a month.

Justify which **one** of these two options The Linen Basket should choose. (9)
---
Indicative content:
- Option 1: quick and needs no investment; hotels also face rising costs and may accept a rise; but hotels may switch to a cheaper laundry or do laundry in-house, losing large contracts.
- Option 2: cuts costs permanently by £500 a month, recovering the £15,000 in about 30 months; keeps prices competitive; but loan interest adds cost, and it only covers £500 of the £800 rise, so profit still falls by £300 a month plus interest.
- Conclusion with a new factor, for example: depends on how loyal its hotel customers are, or whether energy prices are expected to stay high (making the machines worthwhile).

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): one option weighed with benefits and drawbacks, applied to The Linen Basket; a conclusion with a new "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (4–6): some developed reasoning and use of the case; a partly supported judgement.
- Level 1 (1–3): isolated points; little use of the case.
:::

::: test
Hollow Tree Toys is a small toy shop in Bath run by Martha. Over the last two years:
- online sales of toys nationally have grown, and her shop's sales have fallen by 18%;
- consumer incomes in the area have fallen as prices of essentials have risen;
- her business rates have risen.

Martha is considering spending her £10,000 of savings on a website with an online shop.

Evaluate whether setting up an online shop is the best response for Hollow Tree Toys. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
---
Indicative content:
- AO1b: responses to changes in technology and the economic climate; e-commerce; costs and risks.
- AO2: shop sales down 18%; online toy sales growing; falling local incomes; rising business rates; £10,000 savings.
- AO3a: For: reaches customers beyond Bath who already buy online, reversing lost sales; open 24 hours; less dependent on falling local incomes. Against: £10,000 is a large share of her savings, with ongoing costs for delivery, returns and marketing; competing online with large retailers on price is hard; it does not solve rising business rates; local customers with lower incomes may want cheaper toys rather than online access. Alternatives: cheaper ways such as selling on an online marketplace or social media, or using the shop's personal service and events to bring customers in.
- AO3b: a supported judgement, for example that an online presence is needed but a cheaper start (a marketplace or simple web shop) is better first; depends on whether her toys are specialist or unique enough to compete online, and on how long the fall in local incomes lasts.

Levels:
- Level 3 (9–12): accurate knowledge; detailed application to Hollow Tree Toys; balanced, developed analysis; a supported judgement with a "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (5–8): sound knowledge and some application; some developed analysis; a judgement with limited support.
- Level 1 (1–4): isolated knowledge; little application; an assertion rather than a judgement.
:::
