## What it is

A business exists to meet **customer needs**: the things customers look for when they decide what to buy and where to buy it. Edexcel names four needs, and examines those words.

### The four customer needs

| Need | What customers look for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | A price they can afford and that seems fair for what they get (value for money) | Students and families on tight budgets choosing a discount supermarket |
| **Quality** | A product that works well, lasts and is made to a good standard; a service delivered well and reliably | A bride choosing a florist on reputation; a driver choosing a garage that fixes the fault first time |
| **Choice** | A range of options, such as sizes, flavours, styles or times, so they can find exactly what suits them | A pizza takeaway offering gluten-free bases and vegan toppings; a gym with classes all day |
| **Convenience** | Easy to get and use: a handy location, long opening hours, online ordering, quick service, delivery | Commuters buying coffee at the station; busy parents ordering a food box online |

### Different customers rank the needs differently

No business can be best at everything. Low prices usually mean lower quality or less choice; high quality usually means a higher price. A business must find out **which needs matter most to its own customers** and focus on them.

| Customer group | Likely top needs |
|---|---|
| Office workers buying lunch | Convenience and speed, then price |
| Couples booking a wedding venue | Quality and choice, price less important |
| Students buying food | Price, then convenience |
| Parents of young children choosing a nursery | Quality (safety, care), then convenience of location and hours |

In an exam case, choose the need that **fits the customers in the case**. A budget takeaway's customers want low prices and speed; claiming they mainly want premium quality ignores the context.

### Why identifying and understanding customers matters

Edexcel gives two reasons:

**1. Generating sales.** If a business offers what customers want, at a price they will pay, in a way that suits them, more customers buy and they come back. Satisfied customers recommend the business, which brings in new customers at no cost. Sales bring in **revenue**, which pays the costs.

**2. Business survival.** A new business has no reputation and limited money. If it misreads its customers, sales are low, revenue does not cover costs, and it runs out of cash. Understanding customers is how a small business survives against bigger, better-known rivals: it can meet a need they ignore, such as personal service or a local product.

The chain to learn: **identify needs → meet them better than rivals → more sales and repeat customers → revenue covers costs → survival and profit.**

### What happens when a business misreads needs

- It stocks products that do not sell, so money is tied up in unsold stock and may have to be sold at a loss.
- It sets the wrong price: too high and customers go elsewhere; too low and it loses profit it could have earned.
- It chooses the wrong opening hours or location, missing the times and places customers want to buy.
- Customers who are disappointed do not return and may post bad reviews online, which spreads quickly.

### Needs change over time

Customer needs are not fixed. Tastes change (1.1.1), technology raises expectations of convenience (people now expect to order online and pay by phone), and the economy changes what people can afford (1.5.4). A business must keep checking its customers' needs, which is the job of **market research** (1.2.2).

### Reading customer data in the exam

Section C often gives survey results and asks you to **identify** the most important need, or to calculate a percentage. Read the numbers carefully.

| Most important factor when choosing a hairdresser | Number of customers |
|---|---|
| Price | 34 |
| Quality of the haircut | 58 |
| Choice of styles and treatments | 12 |
| Convenience (location, opening hours) | 46 |
| **Total** | **150** |

Here the most important need is **quality** (58 of 150). As a percentage: 58 ÷ 150 × 100 = **38.67%**. Convenience is second (46, or 30.67%). A salon using this data might invest in training before cutting prices.

::: terms
- **Customer needs**: the things customers look for when deciding what to buy and where to buy it: price, quality, choice and convenience.
- **Price (as a need)**: customers want a price they can afford that gives value for money.
- **Quality (as a need)**: customers want a product or service that is reliable, well made and does its job well.
- **Choice (as a need)**: customers want a range of options so they can find what suits them.
- **Convenience (as a need)**: customers want products and services that are easy and quick to get and use.
- **Generating sales**: winning customers who buy, bringing revenue into the business.
- **Business survival**: the business continues to trade because its revenue and cash are enough to cover its costs.
- **Repeat customers**: customers who come back to buy again because their needs were met.
- **Value for money**: customers feel the benefits they get are worth the price they pay.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the need** using the spec word: price, quality, choice or convenience.
2. **Match it to the customers**: who buys from this business, and why would that need matter to them?
3. **Chain to the outcome**: need met → customers choose this business over rivals and return → more sales and revenue → survival or profit.
4. **Data questions**: identify the largest figure; for a percentage, divide the part by the total and multiply by 100, to 2 decimal places if asked.
5. **Analyse (6)**: use the case's customers and competitors in every paragraph. No context caps you at 3.
6. **Justify (9)**: pick the option that meets the case customers' **top** need, weigh its cost, and finish with a "depends on" factor such as what research shows.
:::

::: example Explain - importance of understanding customers
Explain **one** reason why it is important for a new business to identify its customers' needs. (3)

It helps the business generate sales (1). If it knows what customers want, such as low prices or a convenient location, it can offer exactly that (1), so customers choose it over rivals and return, bringing in the revenue it needs to cover its costs and survive (1).
:::

::: example Identify and calculate from data
A survey of 80 customers of a takeaway asked what mattered most to them. Price: 31; Quality: 18; Choice: 9; Convenience: 22.
(a) Identify the customer need that was most important to these customers. (1)
(b) Calculate the percentage of customers who chose convenience. (2)

(a) Price (1).
(b) 22 ÷ 80 × 100 (1) = **27.5%** (1).
:::

::: example Outline - a need in context
Mo's Motors is a car garage in a village in Suffolk. Most of its customers are farm workers who cannot be without their vehicles for long.
Outline **one** customer need that Mo's Motors should focus on. (2)

Convenience (1): customers need their vehicles back quickly to get to work on farms, so Mo could offer same-day repairs or a courtesy car so they are not left without transport (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - identifying needs
Leaf & Loaf is a new café in a market town in Devon. It is next to a busy bus station and a college, and opposite a large chain coffee shop.
Analyse the importance to Leaf & Loaf of identifying customer needs. (6)

Leaf & Loaf's customers are likely to include college students and bus passengers, two groups with different needs (AO2). Students may want low prices and somewhere to sit, while bus passengers want fast, take-away service before their bus leaves. If the owner identifies these needs, she can offer a student discount and a quick grab-and-go counter, so both groups choose Leaf & Loaf, generating more sales throughout the day (AO3a).

This matters even more because a large chain is directly opposite (AO2). The chain has a known brand, so customers have little reason to try a new café unless it meets a need better, such as fresher local food or cheaper student deals. Without this understanding, Leaf & Loaf would compete head-on and lose, revenue would not cover rent, and the business might not survive its first year (AO3a).

(Level 3: developed chains applied to Leaf & Loaf, its location and its rival throughout.)
:::

::: example Justify - two ways to meet needs
Chop Shop is a butcher's in a suburb of Nottingham. Sales have fallen as more local families shop at a supermarket with longer hours. The owner can afford one change. Option 1: open until 8pm on weekdays (currently 5.30pm), costing £400 a month in extra wages. Option 2: offer a local delivery service for online orders, costing £350 a month for a part-time driver plus £1,200 for a website. Justify which **one** of these options the owner should choose. (9)

Option 1 meets the need for convenience for families who work during the day and can only shop after 5.30pm, which is the time they currently go to the supermarket (AO2). Staying open until 8pm lets Chop Shop compete for these evening shoppers, so sales should rise, and there is no large one-off cost (AO3a).

However, the extra £400 a month must be covered by evening sales; if few customers come in, the wages are wasted. It also only matches the supermarket's hours rather than offering something better (AO3a).

The owner should choose Option 1 because it directly removes the reason families switched to the supermarket, and its cost is low and can be stopped quickly if it fails. This depends on how many families actually shop after work: a short survey of existing customers before the change would show whether the evening demand is there, and if most customers are busy parents who want time-saving above all, delivery might win (AO3b).
:::

1 mark for the way, up to 2 for development. For example: offer a wider range of products, such as gluten-free and vegan options in a bakery (1), so customers with different diets can find something they want (1), which brings in customers who would otherwise go elsewhere, increasing sales (1).

::: q
Discuss why understanding customer needs is important for the survival of a new business. (6)
---
Indicative content: a new business has no reputation, so must give customers a reason to switch; meeting needs brings sales and repeat customers, so revenue covers costs; word of mouth and good reviews cut promotion costs; misreading needs leads to unsold stock, wrong prices, low revenue and cash problems; needs change, so ongoing research is needed.
Level 3 (5–6): two developed lines of reasoning. Level 2 (3–4): some development. Level 1 (1–2): simple points.

::: watch
- Use the **four spec needs** by name: price, quality, choice, convenience. "Good service" should be linked to quality or convenience.
- **Match the need to the customers in the case.** Budget customers want price; time-poor customers want convenience.
- The two reasons it matters are **generating sales** and **business survival**. Finish your chain with revenue, costs or survival, not just "customers are happy".
- In data questions, check whether the question asks for a **number**, a **percentage** or the **name** of the need.
- A percentage of customers is **part ÷ total × 100**. Give 2 decimal places if the question asks.
- In an **analyse**, every paragraph needs the case. Without context the maximum is 3.
:::

::: test
Discuss how a small business can survive by meeting customer needs better than larger rivals. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):
- Large rivals often compete on price; a small business usually cannot, so it must meet another need better.
- Quality and personal service: knowing customers by name, giving advice, so customers return and recommend it.
- Convenience: local location, flexible hours, delivery, which large rivals may not offer locally.
- Choice in a niche: specialist ranges large rivals do not stock.

::: test
Pasta Fresca is a small Italian takeaway in Peterborough. Sales have been flat for a year. A survey of 200 customers found 44% rated convenience as their most important need and 31% rated choice. The owner can afford one change.

Evaluate whether meeting the customer need for price is the most important factor in the survival of Hartley's. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
