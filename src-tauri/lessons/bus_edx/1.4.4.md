## What it is

A **business plan** is a written document setting out what a new (or growing) business intends to do, how it will do it, and how it will be financed. It pulls together almost everything in Theme 1, which makes it a natural topic for synoptic questions. Edexcel examines two things: **what a plan identifies**, and **why planning matters**, in particular for **minimising risk** and **obtaining finance**.

### What a business plan identifies

| Section | What it shows | Links |
|---|---|---|
| **The business idea** | What the business will sell, to whom, and what makes it different (its USP) | 1.1.1, 1.1.3 |
| **Business aims and objectives** | What the owner wants to achieve, such as survival in year one, a profit target, or a social aim | 1.3.1 |
| **Target market (market research)** | Who the customers are, how many there are, what they want, and the evidence from research; the competitors and their strengths and weaknesses | 1.2.1–1.2.4 |
| **Forecast revenue, cost and profit** | Expected sales and prices, fixed and variable costs, expected profit or loss, often with break-even output | 1.3.2a, 1.3.2b |
| **Cash-flow forecast** | Month-by-month cash inflows and outflows and the closing balance, showing when cash may run short | 1.3.3 |
| **Sources of finance** | How much money is needed, what for, and where it will come from (savings, loan, crowdfunding and so on) | 1.3.4 |
| **Location** | Where the business will operate and why: premises, rent, proximity to the market, or online | 1.4.2 |
| **Marketing mix** | The product, price, promotion and place decisions | 1.4.3 |

A plan usually also describes the owner's experience and skills and the legal structure chosen (1.4.1).

### Who reads a business plan

- **The owner**: to think the idea through, set targets and check progress against them.
- **Banks and other lenders**: to judge whether a loan or overdraft will be repaid.
- **Investors** (venture capitalists, shareholders, crowdfunding backers): to judge whether the business could grow and give them a return.
- Sometimes **landlords**, **suppliers** offering trade credit, or **franchisors** considering a franchisee.

### The purpose of planning: minimising risk

Writing a plan **reduces** the risk of failure because it forces the owner to:
- **research the market** properly, so they find out whether customers want the product before spending money;
- **check the numbers**: if forecast revenue does not cover costs, or break even is far above likely sales, the owner can change the price, cut costs or rethink the idea before investing;
- **spot cash shortfalls** in advance with the cash-flow forecast, so an overdraft can be arranged or a purchase delayed;
- **think about competitors** and how to compete with them;
- **set objectives** against which progress can be measured, so problems are noticed early.

Planning does **not remove** risk. A business plan reduces risk; it does not guarantee success.

### The purpose of planning: obtaining finance

Lenders and investors rarely give money to a new business without a plan, because a start-up has no trading history. The plan shows:
- that the owner has researched the market and there is demand;
- that forecast revenue should cover costs and loan repayments;
- when cash will be tight and how it will be managed;
- how much is needed and what it will be used for;
- that the owner is committed and has thought things through.

A convincing plan makes a bank **more likely** to lend, and on better terms. But a lender trusts the plan only as far as its **forecasts look realistic**: over-optimistic figures can be spotted and may lead to a refusal.

### Limitations of a business plan

- **Forecasts can be wrong**: a new business has no past figures, so sales, costs and cash flow are estimates. If research was poor, the whole plan rests on weak evidence.
- **Circumstances change**: a new competitor, a rise in interest rates or a change in tastes can make a plan out of date quickly (1.5).
- **Time and cost**: writing a good plan takes time, and research and professional advice cost money that a small start-up may not have.
- **False confidence**: an owner may trust the plan too much and fail to react when reality differs.
- **Optimism bias**: owners keen on their idea may write over-optimistic forecasts, especially to persuade a bank.

To get the most from a plan, the owner should base it on reliable research, include cautious forecasts, and **review and update it regularly** against actual results.

::: terms
- **Business plan**: a written document setting out a business's idea, objectives, market, finances and marketing, and how it will achieve its aims.
- **Business idea**: what the business will sell, to whom, and what makes it different from rivals.
- **Target market**: the group of customers a business aims to sell to, identified through market research.
- **Forecast**: a prediction of future figures, such as revenue, costs, profit or cash flow.
- **Cash-flow forecast**: a prediction of a business's monthly cash inflows, outflows and balances.
- **Minimising risk**: reducing the chance of failure or loss by researching and planning before committing money.
- **Obtaining finance**: persuading lenders or investors to provide money, usually by showing them a credible business plan.
- **Lender**: a bank or other organisation that provides a loan or overdraft and expects repayment with interest.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the section or purpose** using the spec words: business idea, aims and objectives, target market, forecast revenue/cost/profit, cash-flow forecast, sources of finance, location, marketing mix; minimising risk, obtaining finance.
2. **Explain (3)**: benefit (1) → how the plan achieves it (1) → consequence for the owner or business (1).
3. **Say who uses the plan** in the question: the owner (planning, targets) or a lender or investor (finance).
4. **Always balance**: a plan reduces risk but does not remove it; forecasts can be wrong; plans go out of date.
5. **Apply the case**: what the owner needs money for, how much research they have done, how realistic their forecasts look.
6. **Justify and evaluate**: weigh the time and cost of planning against the risk it reduces and the finance it unlocks; conclude with a "depends on" factor, such as the quality of the research behind the forecasts.
:::

::: example Explain - minimising risk
Explain **one** way a business plan can help an entrepreneur minimise risk. (3)

It makes the entrepreneur produce a cash-flow forecast (1), which shows the months when the business is likely to run short of cash (1), so they can arrange an overdraft or delay spending in advance, reducing the risk of being unable to pay bills and becoming insolvent (1).
:::

::: example Outline - benefit to the owner
Kieran is starting Kieran's Kayaks, a kayak-hire business on a lake in Cumbria. He needs a £15,000 loan for kayaks and safety equipment. Outline **one** benefit to Kieran of producing a business plan. (2)

It helps him obtain the £15,000 loan (1): the bank can see his forecast bookings and cash flow, including the quiet winter months, and judge whether he can make the repayments (1).
:::

::: example Explain - limitation
Explain **one** limitation of a business plan for a new business. (3)

The forecasts in the plan may be inaccurate (1), because a new business has no past sales to base them on (1), so the owner may make decisions, such as how much stock to buy or how much to borrow, on figures that turn out to be too optimistic, leading to losses (1).
:::

::: example Discuss - obtaining finance
Discuss the role of a business plan in helping a start-up obtain finance. (6)

A start-up has no trading history, so a bank cannot judge it on past profits. The business plan fills this gap: it shows the market research behind the idea, forecast revenue and costs, and a cash-flow forecast. From these, the lender can see whether the business is likely to generate enough cash to make the loan repayments on time, which makes it more willing to lend.

The plan also shows how much is needed and what it will be spent on, and that the owner understands the business and its risks. This builds the lender's confidence in the owner and may lead to better terms. However, a plan does not guarantee finance: if the forecasts look unrealistic, or the research is thin, the lender may still refuse, so the plan helps only as far as its figures are credible.
:::

::: example Evaluate - does a plan guarantee success?
Amara has written a detailed business plan for Amara's Attic, a vintage furniture shop in Leicester, including research, a cash-flow forecast and a break-even calculation. Her bank has agreed a loan. Evaluate whether her business plan means the business will succeed. (12)

The plan reduces Amara's risk. Her market research shows there is demand, and the break-even calculation tells her how many pieces she must sell each month, so she can check whether that is realistic before opening (AO1b, AO2). The cash-flow forecast warns her of months when cash will be short, so she can manage her stock buying and avoid being unable to pay her rent or loan repayments, which is a common cause of failure (AO3a).

However, the plan is built on forecasts, and Amara has never run a shop. If customers buy less than her research suggested, or vintage furniture becomes harder to source at a good price, her revenue and costs will differ from the plan (AO2). New competitors, such as online marketplaces for second-hand furniture, or a fall in consumer incomes could reduce sales, making the plan out of date (AO3a).

Overall, the plan makes success more likely but does not guarantee it. It depends on how reliable her research was and whether she uses the plan actively: if she compares actual sales with the forecast each month and adjusts her prices, stock and promotion when they differ, the plan will keep reducing her risk; if she treats it as finished once the loan is agreed, it will soon stop helping (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- A business plan **reduces** risk; it does **not** remove it or guarantee success or a loan.
- The two purposes in the spec are **minimising risk** and **obtaining finance**. Use those words.
- Develop **how** the plan helps: research shows demand; the cash-flow forecast shows shortfalls; forecasts show whether repayments can be made.
- Learn the **sections** the spec lists: idea, aims and objectives, target market, forecast revenue/cost/profit, cash-flow forecast, sources of finance, location, marketing mix.
- Limitations: forecasts may be wrong, circumstances change, plans take time. Use one in every evaluation.
- In context questions, say what this owner needs the plan for (a loan, a franchise, a lease).
:::
