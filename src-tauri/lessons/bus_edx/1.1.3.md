## What it is

**Enterprise** is the activity of setting up and running a business, and spotting and acting on opportunities. This topic covers what businesses are for, and what the entrepreneur does.

### The purpose of business activity

Edexcel lists three purposes:

1. **To produce goods or services.**
   - **Goods** are physical products you can touch and keep: a loaf of bread, a bike, a phone case.
   - **Services** are tasks done for a customer, with nothing physical to keep: a haircut, a driving lesson, a delivery, insurance.
   - Many businesses offer both. A bike shop sells bikes (goods) and repairs them (a service).
2. **To meet customer needs.** A business survives only if people want what it offers at a price they will pay (customer needs are covered in 1.2.1).
3. **To add value.** A business turns inputs into something customers value more than the inputs cost.

### Added value

**Added value** is the difference between the **selling price** of a product and the **cost of the bought-in materials and components** (the inputs bought from other businesses) used to make it.

**Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs**

**Worked example.** A florist buys flowers, ribbon and wrapping for £8 and sells the finished bouquet for £30. Added value = £30 − £8 = **£22** per bouquet. This £22 is not all profit: it must still cover rent, wages and other costs. But the more value a business adds, the more room it has to cover those costs and make a profit.

A business can increase added value by **raising the price customers will pay** (by making the product more desirable), by **cutting the cost of inputs**, or both. The spec lists five ways to make customers willing to pay more:

| Way | How it adds value | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Convenience** | Saves the customer time or effort, so they will pay extra | A sandwich shop at a station; ready-chopped vegetables; home delivery; a mobile mechanic who comes to you |
| **Branding** | A recognised name and image that customers trust and want to be seen with | A well-known trainer brand charges far more than an unbranded pair made in a similar way |
| **Quality** | Better materials, better workmanship or a more reliable product or service | Hand-made furniture in solid oak; a hairdresser with highly trained stylists |
| **Design** | A product that looks better or works better | A kettle with an attractive shape and a fast-boil feature; a clear, easy-to-use app |
| **Unique selling point (USP)** | A feature that no competitor offers, which makes the product stand out | A café that is also a cat sanctuary; a gym open 24 hours in a town where others close at 9pm |

**The key exam link.** In each case, the feature makes customers willing to pay a **higher price relative to the cost of the inputs**, so the business adds more value per item. Examiners give the final mark of a "way to add value" answer only when it reaches this financial point. Ending at "more customers will buy it" is not enough.

Adding value can also **cost money**. Better ingredients raise input costs; branding needs promotion; design needs development. Adding value only makes sense if the extra price customers will pay is greater than the extra cost.

### The role of the entrepreneur

An **entrepreneur** is someone who sets up a business, taking the risk in the hope of reward. Edexcel lists three roles:

| Role | What it involves | Example: Maya opening a bakery |
|---|---|---|
| **Organises resources** | Brings together the things a business needs: premises, equipment, materials, staff and money (these are the factors of production: land, labour, capital and enterprise) | Finds a unit, buys ovens, sources flour, hires an assistant, arranges a loan |
| **Makes business decisions** | Decides what to sell, at what price, where, and how to promote it | Chooses to specialise in sourdough, sets prices, decides on opening hours |
| **Takes risks** | Puts their own money, time and income at risk (1.1.2) | Invests her savings and leaves her job |

The entrepreneur is different from a manager employed by a business. The manager also organises and decides, but the entrepreneur **bears the risk** and keeps the reward.

::: terms
- **Enterprise**: the activity of spotting a business opportunity and taking the initiative and risk to act on it.
- **Entrepreneur**: a person who organises resources, makes business decisions and takes risks to start and run a business.
- **Goods**: physical products that can be touched and kept, such as food, clothes or furniture.
- **Services**: tasks carried out for a customer, with nothing physical to keep, such as a haircut or a repair.
- **Added value**: the difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials and components used to make it.
- **Branding**: giving a product a recognisable name, logo and image that customers trust and identify with.
- **Unique selling point (USP)**: a feature of a product or service that no competitor offers, making it stand out.
- **Convenience**: making a product or service quicker or easier for the customer to get or use.
- **Resources**: the inputs a business needs, such as premises, equipment, materials, workers and finance.
:::

::: method
1. **Calculate added value**: selling price − cost of bought-in inputs. Show units (£) and say per item if relevant.
2. **"Explain one way" (3)**: name the way (1); say how it changes the product for the customer (1); finish with the money point, that customers will pay a higher price relative to the input costs, so more value is added (1).
3. **Discuss (6)**: two lines of reasoning about one named way, each ending in price, cost, revenue or profit, with a limitation such as the cost of adding the value.
4. **Entrepreneur questions**: use the three spec roles by name, organises resources, makes business decisions, takes risks, and give a concrete action.
5. **Justify (9) between two ways of adding value**: pick one; set what customers in the case would pay extra for against the extra cost; conclude with a "depends on" factor from the case.
:::

::: example Calculate - added value
A furniture maker buys timber, screws and varnish costing £85 to make one table. She sells the table for £320. Calculate the added value per table. (2)

£320 − £85 (1) = **£235** per table (1). (2 marks for the correct answer without working.)
:::

::: example Explain - a USP
Explain **one** way a unique selling point could add value to a product. (3)

A USP gives the product a feature that no competitor offers, such as a gym being open 24 hours (1). Customers who want that feature cannot get it elsewhere, so they see the product as better than rivals' (1). This means the business can charge a higher price than competitors without raising its input costs much, increasing the value added per sale (1).
:::

::: example Explain - the entrepreneur
Explain **one** role of an entrepreneur. (3)

An entrepreneur organises resources (1). They find and bring together the premises, equipment, materials, staff and finance a business needs (1), so the business can produce goods or services to meet customer needs; without this, an idea would never become a working business (1).
:::

::: example Discuss - branding
Discuss the way a small business could use branding to add value. (6)

A small business can build a distinctive name, logo and packaging that customers recognise, and link it to a clear image such as "local, hand-made and natural". Customers who identify with that image see the product as different from unbranded alternatives, so they are willing to pay a higher price, while the cost of the ingredients stays the same. The gap between price and input cost widens, so more value is added on each item.

A strong brand also builds trust and loyalty, so customers come back and recommend it, which makes demand less sensitive to price. However, building a brand takes time and money for a small business, such as a professional logo, quality packaging and social media promotion. If the business cannot afford to promote it widely, few people will recognise the brand, so the extra cost may not be covered by a higher price.

(Level 3: two developed lines of reasoning ending in the financial idea of added value, with a balancing point.)
:::

::: example Justify - two ways to add value
Kofi runs Kofi's Coffee, a café near a business park in Reading. He can spend £6,000 on one of two options. Option 1: a pre-order app so office workers can collect drinks without queuing (convenience). Option 2: switching to premium, ethically sourced beans and training staff as baristas (quality). Justify which **one** of these options Kofi should choose. (9)

Option 1 suits Kofi's customers, office workers with short breaks who value time (AO2). If they can order ahead and skip the queue, they are more likely to choose Kofi's over a rival, and some will pay a small premium for the time saved, so revenue rises without much increase in the cost of each drink (AO3a).

However, the app has running costs and fees, and customers must download it; if few do, the £6,000 is wasted. Rival chains already offer similar apps, so it may not make Kofi's stand out for long (AO3a).

Kofi should choose the app, because on a business park the main need is speed, and convenience lets him add value without raising the cost of his ingredients, which premium beans would. This depends on how many regular customers he has: if most of his trade is from the same office workers each day, uptake of the app will be high; if trade is mostly passing customers, quality and taste may matter more (AO3b).
:::

::: q
A bakery buys ingredients and packaging for £1.40 to make one cake. It sells the cake for £4.50. Calculate the added value per cake. (2)
---
£4.50 − £1.40 (1) = £3.10 per cake (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** way convenience could add value to a service. (3)
---
1 mark for the way, up to 2 for development. For example: a mobile car-valeting service cleans cars at the customer's workplace (1), saving the customer the time of driving to a car wash (1), so customers will pay a higher price than at a fixed car wash, increasing added value (1).
:::

::: q
State **one** role of an entrepreneur. (1)
---
Any one (1): organises resources; makes business decisions; takes risks.
:::

::: q
Explain **one** reason why adding value may not increase a business's profit. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: adding value often raises costs, such as better materials for quality (1); if customers will not pay a high enough price to cover the extra cost (1), the gap between price and total cost does not rise, so profit may stay the same or fall (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how design could add value for Pip & Pot, a small business in Bath selling plant pots online that it buys from a supplier for £6 each and sells for £14.
(6)
---
Indicative content: a distinctive, attractive design or practical feature (self-watering, recycled material) makes the pots stand out from cheaper online rivals; customers pay a higher price, say £20, while bought-in cost rises only a little, so added value rises above £8 per pot; good design photographs well online, helping sales; but redesign may need a new supplier or higher input costs, and online rivals can copy designs quickly.
Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Pip & Pot. Level 2 (3–4): some development and use of the case. Level 1 (1–2): isolated points. No context: maximum 3.
:::

::: watch
- **Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs.** It is not the same as profit, which also takes away rent, wages and other costs.
- In "explain one way to add value", the **third mark** needs the financial idea: a higher price relative to the input costs. "Customers will buy more" alone stalls at 2.
- Name **one** way and develop it. Listing convenience, branding and quality scores 1 mark at most.
- A USP must be something **no competitor offers**. "Good quality" is not a USP unless rivals cannot match it.
- The entrepreneur's roles are **organises resources, makes business decisions, takes risks**. Use these words.
- Adding value **costs money**. Strong discuss and justify answers weigh the extra cost against the higher price.
:::

::: test
Hollie runs Hollie's Hampers in Shropshire, selling gift hampers online. Each hamper uses bought-in food and a wicker basket costing £22, and sells for £45. Rival online hampers sell for between £35 and £50. Hollie plans to add hand-written gift cards and next-day delivery.

Analyse how these changes could add value for Hollie's Hampers. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- AO2: current added value £45 − £22 = £23; rivals priced £35–£50; online gift buyers; hand-written cards and next-day delivery.
- AO3a: next-day delivery adds convenience for last-minute gift buyers, who will pay towards the top of the £35–£50 range or above; hand-written cards add a personal, quality touch that sets the hampers apart; a higher price for little extra input cost raises added value above £23; but next-day courier costs and Hollie's time raise costs, so the price rise must exceed them.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): developed chains applied to Hollie's Hampers throughout.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning with some use of the case.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
An answer with no reference to the context is capped at 3 marks.

---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: it gains more than one source of revenue (1): a bike shop that also repairs bikes earns money when bike sales are slow, such as in winter (1), so its income is more stable and it is less likely to run short of cash (1).

::: test
Arjun owns Arjun's Ales, a small brewery in Kent. Each bottle of beer costs £0.90 in bought-in ingredients and bottles, and sells to pubs for £2.10. He has £8,000 to spend.
Option 1: improve quality by using locally grown hops and a longer brewing time, raising input costs to £1.20 a bottle, but pubs say they would pay £2.80.
Option 2: invest in branding: a new name, label design and a stand at local food festivals, keeping input costs at £0.90, with pubs expected to pay £2.40.

Justify which **one** of these two options Arjun should choose. (9)
---
Indicative content:
- Option 1: added value rises from £1.20 to £1.60 a bottle (£2.80 − £1.20); local hops are a selling point for Kent pubs; but costs rise per bottle, and a longer brew time cuts output.
- Option 2: added value rises to £1.50 a bottle (£2.40 − £0.90); recognition can grow sales to new pubs and shops; but branding takes time to work, and festival stands are a cost each year.
- Conclusion adds a new factor, for example: depends on whether pubs actually pay £2.80, or on how much output he loses with the longer brew.

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): one option weighed with benefits and drawbacks, using Arjun's figures; a conclusion with a new "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (4–6): some developed reasoning and use of the case; a partly supported judgement.
- Level 1 (1–3): isolated points; little use of the case.
:::

::: test
Fern is opening a dog-grooming salon in Taunton. Two salons already operate in the town, both charging about £40 per groom. Fern's bought-in costs (shampoo, products) are about £4 per groom. She is deciding whether to offer a premium service with organic products and a free pick-up and drop-off service, charging £55, or to match rivals at £40.

Evaluate whether Fern should offer the premium service. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
---
Indicative content:
- AO1b: added value; convenience and quality as ways to add value; a USP.
- AO2: £40 rivals; £4 bought-in cost; premium price £55; pick-up service; Taunton market with two rivals.
- AO3a: Premium: pick-up is convenient for busy owners and is a USP in Taunton, organic products signal quality, so added value could rise from £36 to about £50 per groom less the extra product cost; it avoids a price war with established rivals. But pick-up needs a van, fuel and time, so fewer grooms a day; organic products cost more; customers may not pay £15 more for a new salon with no reputation.
- AO3b: a supported judgement, for example premium, because a new entrant matching two established rivals on price has no reason for customers to switch; depends on market research showing enough local owners will pay £55, and on whether she can fit enough pick-ups into a day.

Levels:
- Level 3 (9–12): accurate knowledge; detailed application to Fern; balanced, developed analysis; a supported judgement with a "depends on" factor.
- Level 2 (5–8): sound knowledge and some application; some developed analysis; a judgement with limited support.
- Level 1 (1–4): isolated knowledge, little application; an assertion rather than a judgement.
:::
