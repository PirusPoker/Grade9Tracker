## What it is

The **economic climate** is the general state of the economy: how many people have jobs, how much money they have to spend, how fast prices are rising, what it costs to borrow, how much tax is taken and what the pound is worth against other currencies. A small business cannot control any of these, but every one of them changes its **sales**, its **costs** or both. Edexcel names six factors, and you need to be able to trace each one through to a business's profit.

The skill in this topic is the **chain**: *change in the economy → effect on customers or on costs → effect on sales, costs and profit*. The size of the effect then **depends on the business**: what it sells (a necessity or a luxury), who its customers are, whether it borrows, and whether it imports or exports.

### 1. Unemployment

**Unemployment** is the number of people who want to work and are able to work but cannot find a job.

When unemployment **rises**:
- unemployed people have less income, and people still in work worry about their jobs and save more, so **spending falls**;
- businesses selling **luxuries** or non-essential services (restaurants, gyms, holidays, beauty treatments) see the biggest drop in sales;
- businesses selling **necessities** or cheap alternatives (discount supermarkets, budget brands, repair services) may hold up or even gain customers who **trade down**;
- for the business as an employer there is an upside: more people are looking for work, so it is **easier and often cheaper to recruit**, and staff are less likely to leave.

When unemployment **falls**, the reverse happens: spending rises, but recruiting becomes harder and businesses may have to offer higher pay to attract and keep staff.

### 2. Changing levels of consumer income

**Consumer income** is the money people have to spend. What matters most is **disposable income**: income left after tax and essential bills. It changes when wages rise or fall, when taxes or benefits change, or when the prices of essentials such as energy and food rise.

- **Higher consumer income**: customers buy more, buy more often and buy better-quality or premium versions, so sales and profit tend to rise. Luxury and premium businesses benefit most.
- **Lower consumer income**: customers cut back on non-essentials and switch to cheaper brands. A premium bakery may lose sales while a budget supermarket gains them.

### 3. Inflation

**Inflation** is a general rise in the level of prices over time, usually measured as a percentage per year. A 4% rate of inflation means that, on average, prices are 4% higher than a year ago.

Inflation affects a business in three ways:
- **Costs rise**: raw materials, energy, rent and other supplies cost more, and employees ask for **pay rises** to keep up with the cost of living.
- **Prices**: the business may raise its own prices to protect its profit margin, but if rivals do not, it may lose customers.
- **Customers' spending power falls** if prices rise faster than wages. Each pound buys less, so **real income** falls and customers cut back, especially on non-essentials.

A business that cannot pass its higher costs on to customers sees its **profit margin squeezed**. A business with a strong brand or loyal customers finds it easier to raise prices.

### 4. Changes in interest rates

The **interest rate** is the cost of borrowing money and the reward for saving it. In the UK the Bank of England sets the base rate, and banks' rates on loans, overdrafts, mortgages and savings follow it.

Interest rates have a **double effect** on a business:

| | Interest rates **rise** | Interest rates **fall** |
|---|---|---|
| **Effect on the business's costs** | Repayments on loans and overdrafts with variable rates rise, increasing costs and reducing profit and cash; new borrowing for expansion becomes more expensive, so investment may be delayed | Borrowing is cheaper, so costs fall and investment in new equipment or premises becomes more attractive |
| **Effect on customers** | Customers with mortgages, loans or credit cards have higher repayments, so less disposable income; saving becomes more attractive than spending; demand for big purchases bought on credit (cars, furniture, kitchens) falls | Customers' repayments fall and saving pays less, so they spend more; sales of goods bought on credit rise |

A business that has **borrowed heavily** and sells **expensive or credit-bought products** is hit hardest by a rise. A business with no debt selling cheap everyday items is hardly affected.

### 5. Government taxation

**Taxation** is money collected by the government to pay for public services. Taxes that matter to a small business include:
- **income tax** and **National Insurance** on people's earnings, which affect consumers' disposable income (and the employer pays National Insurance on its staff's wages);
- **VAT** (value added tax), added to the price of most goods and services;
- **corporation tax** on the profits of companies, or income tax on a sole trader's profit;
- **business rates** on premises.

A **rise in taxation** reduces customers' disposable income (so sales fall), raises the business's costs (for example higher employer contributions) or takes a bigger share of its profit, leaving less **retained profit** to reinvest. A **cut in taxation** has the opposite effects: customers have more to spend, and the business keeps more of its profit to invest in growth.

### 6. Changes in exchange rates

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another, for example £1 = €1.20. It matters to any business that **imports** (buys goods or materials from abroad) or **exports** (sells to customers abroad), and to UK businesses competing with imported goods.

Use the memory aid **SPICED**: a **S**trong **P**ound makes **I**mports **C**heaper and **E**xports **D**earer.

**Worked example: importing.** Lena runs a homeware shop in Leeds and buys lamps from a supplier in Spain for €60 each.
- At £1 = €1.20, each lamp costs her 60 ÷ 1.20 = **£50**.
- If the pound strengthens to £1 = €1.50, each lamp costs 60 ÷ 1.50 = **£40**. Her costs fall by £10 a lamp, so she can widen her margin or cut her price.
- If the pound weakens to £1 = €1.00, each lamp costs **£60**. Her costs rise and her profit is squeezed.

**Worked example: exporting.** A pottery in Stoke sells mugs to a shop in France at £12 each.
- At £1 = €1.20, the French shop pays 12 × 1.20 = **€14.40**.
- If the pound strengthens to £1 = €1.50, the same mug costs the French shop 12 × 1.50 = **€18.00**. Exports are dearer, so French orders may fall unless the pottery cuts its sterling price.

To convert pounds into a foreign currency, **multiply** by the rate. To convert a foreign currency into pounds, **divide** by the rate.

| | **Strong** (rising) pound | **Weak** (falling) pound |
|---|---|---|
| Importer of materials or stock | Cheaper imports, lower costs, higher margins | Dearer imports, higher costs, lower margins |
| Exporter | Dearer to overseas customers, sales may fall | Cheaper to overseas customers, sales may rise |
| UK firm competing with imports | Foreign rivals' goods become cheaper in the UK, more competition | Imported rivals become dearer, UK firm more competitive |
| Tourism business in the UK | Fewer overseas visitors, as the UK is dearer for them | More overseas visitors |

### Putting it together: it depends

No economic change affects every business the same way. When judging the impact, ask:
- **Necessity or luxury?** Luxuries are hit harder in a downturn and gain more in a boom.
- **Who are the customers?** Wealthy customers are less affected by small changes in income than customers on low wages.
- **Does the business borrow?** Interest rate changes matter far more to a firm with a large loan or overdraft.
- **Does it import or export?** Exchange rates can matter more than anything else to these firms.
- **How big is the change, and how long will it last?** A small, short-term change may be absorbed; a large or lasting one forces a response (see 1.5.5).
- **Does it have competition?** A business with close rivals finds it harder to pass rising costs on in its prices.

Several changes often arrive together. For example, an improving economy usually brings falling unemployment and rising incomes (good for sales) but may also bring higher inflation and harder recruitment (bad for costs).

::: terms
- **Economic climate**: the overall state of the economy, including employment, incomes, prices, interest rates, taxes and exchange rates.
- **Unemployment**: people who are willing and able to work but do not have a job.
- **Consumer income**: the money people have available to spend, which depends on wages, taxes and benefits.
- **Disposable income**: income left after tax and essential bills, available to spend or save.
- **Inflation**: a general and sustained rise in prices over time, which reduces what each pound can buy.
- **Interest rate**: the cost of borrowing money, and the reward for saving, shown as a percentage per year.
- **Taxation**: money the government collects from individuals and businesses, such as income tax, VAT and corporation tax.
- **Exchange rate**: the price of one currency in terms of another, such as £1 = €1.20.
- **Imports**: goods and services bought from businesses in other countries.
- **Exports**: goods and services sold to customers in other countries.
- **Necessity**: a product people keep buying even when their income falls, such as basic food.
- **Luxury**: a non-essential product whose sales fall sharply when incomes fall and rise when incomes rise.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the economic change** and its direction precisely: "interest rates rise", "the pound weakens", "unemployment falls".
2. **Open an explain answer with the first link**: the effect on customers (income, spending) **or** on the business's costs (loans, imports, wages, materials).
3. **Develop two linked steps** to the business: sales, revenue, costs, profit or cash flow. Use "so" and "which means".
4. **For exchange rates, write SPICED out** and do any conversion carefully: pounds to foreign currency multiply, foreign currency to pounds divide. Give the unit.
5. **Bring in the case**: what the business sells (necessity or luxury), whether it borrows, imports or exports, its customers and its figures.
6. **Show the other side** for 6, 9 and 12 marks: a rise in unemployment also makes recruiting easier; a weak pound hurts importers but helps exporters.
7. **Justify or evaluate**: decide how serious the impact is and finish with a "depends on" factor from the case, such as how long the change lasts or how loyal the customers are.
:::

::: example Explain - rising unemployment
Explain **one** disadvantage to a business of rising unemployment. (3)

Fewer people have a regular income, and those in work worry about losing their jobs (1), so customers cut back on spending, especially on non-essential products (1), which reduces the business's sales revenue and profit (1).
:::

::: example Calculate - exchange rate conversion
Kit runs Kit's Kitchenware in Bristol. He buys knives from a German supplier for €84 each. The exchange rate is £1 = €1.40. Calculate the cost of one knife in pounds. (2)

84 ÷ 1.40 (1) = £60 (1). A correct answer of £60 scores 2 marks without working.
:::

::: example Outline - interest rates in context
Amira owns The Pine Room, a furniture shop in Norwich. Many customers buy sofas using credit. The Bank of England has raised interest rates. Outline **one** impact on The Pine Room of the rise in interest rates. (2)

Customers who would buy a sofa on credit will face higher repayments, so fewer may buy (1), which reduces The Pine Room's sales of expensive furniture and its revenue (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - higher inflation
Jonah runs Crumbs, a bakery in Exeter selling bread and celebration cakes. Inflation has risen to 6%, and the price of flour, butter and energy has increased. Analyse the impact of higher inflation on Crumbs. (6)

Inflation has pushed up the price of flour, butter and energy, which are Crumbs' main costs (AO2). Unless Jonah raises his prices by the same amount, the cost of each loaf and cake rises faster than its price, so his profit margin is squeezed and the bakery's profit falls (AO3a).

At the same time, customers' wages may not keep up with 6% inflation, so their real income falls (AO2). Bread is a necessity, so sales of loaves are likely to hold up, but celebration cakes are a treat that customers may cut back on or buy from a cheaper supermarket instead. This means Crumbs could lose sales on its highest-value products just when its costs are rising, a double hit to profit (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - responding to a weaker pound
Rosa runs Bloom & Vase, a florist in Cheltenham that imports most of its flowers from the Netherlands. The pound has weakened from £1 = €1.25 to £1 = €1.10, so her flowers now cost more. Option 1: raise the price of bouquets by 10%. Option 2: buy more flowers from British growers, which are seasonal and offer a smaller range. Justify which **one** of these options Bloom & Vase should choose. (9)

Raising prices by 10% passes on the higher cost of Dutch flowers, which have become about 14% dearer in pounds (AO2). Customers buying flowers for weddings and special occasions are often less sensitive to price, so most will still buy, and Rosa protects her profit margin without changing her range (AO3a).

However, other florists who also import from the Netherlands face the same cost rise, but supermarkets selling cheaper bouquets may take some of Rosa's everyday customers if her prices rise (AO3a). The pound could also weaken further, so a single price rise may not be enough.

Bloom & Vase should raise prices, because the wide range of Dutch flowers is what attracts wedding customers, and British flowers would shrink that range for much of the year. This depends on how much of her trade is occasion flowers rather than everyday bunches: if most sales are cheap bunches, a 10% rise could lose more revenue than it gains, and switching some lines to British flowers in summer would be wiser (AO3b).
:::

B Its raw materials become dearer in pounds

::: q
Explain **one** advantage to a business of a decrease in interest rates. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: the cost of repaying its loans and overdraft falls (1), so its costs are lower (1), which increases profit or frees cash to invest in growth (1). Or: customers' loan and mortgage repayments fall (1), so they have more disposable income (1), which increases demand and sales (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** advantage to a business of a decrease in taxation. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: a cut in income tax leaves customers with more disposable income (1), so they spend more on the business's products (1), increasing revenue and profit (1). Or: lower tax on profit means the business keeps more of its profit (1), so it has more retained profit (1) to invest in new equipment or expansion without borrowing (1).
:::

::: q
Explain **one** way a rise in consumer income could affect a business selling luxury products. (3)
---
1 mark plus up to 2. For example: customers have more money left after essentials (1), so they are more willing to buy non-essential, premium items (1), which increases the business's sales and allows it to charge higher prices (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss the impact of rising unemployment on businesses. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO1b, AO3a):
- Less income and job insecurity, so spending falls; luxury and non-essential businesses lose sales most.
- Necessity and budget businesses may gain as customers trade down.
- Easier to recruit; more applicants; less pressure to raise wages; lower staff turnover.
- Falling sales may force cost cuts, which may add further to unemployment.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): accurate understanding; two developed lines of reasoning, for example falling sales and easier recruitment.
- Level 2 (3–4): some developed reasoning.
- Level 1 (1–2): isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **SPICED**: a Strong Pound makes Imports Cheaper and Exports Dearer. Write it out before you answer, and do not reverse it.
- **Conversions**: pounds to foreign currency, multiply; foreign currency to pounds, divide. Check the answer makes sense: if £1 buys more than €1, the euro price is the bigger number.
- **Interest rates hit twice**: on the business's own borrowing and on its customers' spending. Use whichever fits the case, and the other for development.
- **Inflation** means prices in general rise. It is not the same as a single business raising its price.
- Do not stop at "the economy is bad". Name the factor and push the chain to **sales, costs or profit**.
- In an evaluate, the answer **depends on the business**: a necessity seller, a debt-free firm or an exporter may gain from a change that harms others.
:::
