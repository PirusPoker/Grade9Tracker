## What it is

**Quality** is about meeting customers' expectations, every time. A product does not have to be luxurious to be high quality: a £1 pen that writes smoothly until the ink runs out meets its customers' expectations. Edexcel asks for:
1. **The concept of quality** and its importance in **the production of goods** and **the provision of services**, through **quality control** and **quality assurance**.
2. How managing quality allows a business to **control costs** and **gain a competitive advantage**.

### What quality means

A product or service is of good quality when it is **fit for purpose** and **meets or exceeds customer expectations**, consistently. What customers expect depends on the price and the market: diners at a fast-food outlet expect speed, low prices and food that tastes the same every visit; diners at an expensive restaurant expect exceptional food, attentive service and a special atmosphere. Both businesses can be "high quality" for their customers.

**Quality in goods** includes: reliability (it works and keeps working), durability, safety, appearance and design, accurate size and finish, and **consistency** (every unit the same).

**Quality in services** includes: speed and punctuality, staff knowledge and politeness, accuracy (the right order, the right bill), a clean and pleasant environment, and **consistency** between visits and between branches. Services are harder to control than goods because they are **produced and consumed at the same time** by people: a rude member of staff or a slow day cannot be "inspected out" before the customer sees it. This is why service businesses rely heavily on **staff training**, clear standards and customer feedback.

### Quality control

**Quality control (QC)** means **checking (inspecting) products** to find faults, usually **at the end of production** (or at set points), and removing faulty items before they reach customers.
- Carried out by **inspectors** or quality-control staff, often by testing a **sample** from each batch.
- Faulty products are **scrapped** or **reworked** (fixed).
- In services: a manager checking a hotel room before a guest arrives, or a mystery shopper visiting a branch.

| Advantages of quality control | Disadvantages of quality control |
|---|---|
| Faulty products are stopped before they reach customers, protecting reputation | Finds faults **after** they happen, so materials and time have already been **wasted** |
| Simple to set up; responsibility is clear (the inspectors) | Inspectors are an extra **cost**, adding no value themselves |
| Checks can be targeted at the riskiest stages | Sampling may **miss** faults in unchecked items |
| | Workers may not take responsibility for quality ("the inspector will catch it") |

### Quality assurance

**Quality assurance (QA)** means building quality into **every stage** of production so that faults are **prevented** rather than found. The aim is to get it right first time.
- Standards are set for **every stage**: materials from suppliers, each process, packaging, delivery and after-sales.
- **Every worker is responsible** for checking their own work and the work passed to them, often in teams.
- Systems and processes are checked and improved, and staff are **trained** to the standard.
- In services: a restaurant chain with set recipes, portion sizes, cleaning schedules and staff training so every branch delivers the same experience.

| Advantages of quality assurance | Disadvantages of quality assurance |
|---|---|
| Faults are **prevented**, so less **waste** and reworking, lowering costs | **Training** all staff and setting up systems is **expensive** and takes time |
| Problems are spotted at the stage where they happen, so causes are fixed | Relies on **motivated**, skilled staff taking responsibility |
| Staff given responsibility for quality may be more **motivated** | Paperwork and checks at every stage can slow production |
| Consistent quality builds a reputation customers trust | Benefits take time to appear |

**The key difference**: quality **control** = **inspect and detect** at the end, done by inspectors. Quality **assurance** = **prevent** at every stage, done by everyone. Many businesses use both: QA systems, plus final checks.

### Why quality matters: controlling costs

Poor quality is expensive. The **costs of poor quality** include:
- **Waste**: scrapped materials, and the labour and energy used to make faulty products.
- **Reworking**: paying staff to fix faults.
- **Returns, refunds and replacements**, plus delivery costs to collect and re-send.
- **Product recalls**: if a safety fault is found after sale, the business must recall products, which can cost very large sums and attract media attention.
- **Lost customers and sales**: dissatisfied customers do not return and tell others, through reviews and social media.
- **Legal costs and fines** if faulty goods break consumer law or cause harm (consumer law, 1.5.1).

Managing quality **controls costs** because getting things right first time means less waste, fewer refunds and fewer recalls. Spending on training and better systems is a cost now, but it can save more than it costs later.

### Why quality matters: competitive advantage

Consistently good quality can give a **competitive advantage** (2.2.5):
- It **differentiates** the business from rivals, giving customers a reason to choose it.
- Customers will often **pay a higher price** for proven quality, raising profit margins.
- It builds **brand reputation** and **customer loyalty**, leading to repeat custom and positive **word of mouth** and reviews, which is cheaper than winning new customers through promotion.
- It makes it easier to win contracts from other businesses (for example, a component maker supplying car manufacturers, who demand strict standards).
- Lower costs from less waste can also allow **more competitive prices**.

**Link to 2.3.1**: technology such as computer-controlled machines can improve consistency, but a wrongly set machine can produce faults in large numbers, so quality systems still matter.

**Ways to improve quality** (useful for "state one way" questions): introduce quality assurance; train staff; use better or more reliable suppliers (2.3.2); invest in more precise technology; inspect more products; use customer feedback (2.3.4) to find and fix problems; set clear standards for every task.

::: terms
- **Quality**: meeting or exceeding customer expectations so that a product or service is fit for purpose, consistently.
- **Quality control**: checking products, usually at the end of production, to find and remove faulty items.
- **Quality assurance**: building quality checks into every stage of production so that faults are prevented.
- **Inspection**: examining products or a sample of them to see whether they meet the required standard.
- **Reworking**: fixing faulty products so that they reach the required standard.
- **Product recall**: when a business asks customers to return products found to be faulty or unsafe after sale.
- **Consistency**: delivering the same standard of product or service every time.
- **Competitive advantage**: something that makes a business's offering more attractive than its rivals', so customers choose it.
- **Waste**: materials, time and money lost when products are faulty or not used.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the system correctly**: quality control = inspect and detect, at the end, by inspectors; quality assurance = prevent, at every stage, by every worker.
2. **Explain (3)**: point → how it works → effect on the business. For "explain one method to improve quality", develop **how** the method finds or prevents faults, then its effect.
3. **Link quality to money**: fewer faults → less waste, fewer refunds → lower costs; consistent quality → loyalty and reputation → higher sales or a higher price.
4. **Goods or service?** For a service, stress staff training, standards and consistency, because faults happen in front of the customer.
5. **Use the case**: the product, the figures for faults or returns, what customers value, reviews.
6. **Justify (9)**: pick one option (for example QA or more inspectors), weigh its benefits and drawbacks for this business, then conclude with a new "depends on" factor, such as the cost of training against the cost of current faults.
:::

::: example Explain - quality assurance
Explain **one** benefit to a business of using quality assurance. (3)

Quality assurance checks quality at every stage of production, so faults are prevented rather than found at the end (1). This means fewer products are scrapped or reworked, so less material and labour is wasted (1), which lowers the business's costs and raises its profit margin (1).
:::

::: example Explain - a method to improve quality
Explain **one** method a business could use to improve the quality of its service. (3)

The business could train its staff to a set standard, for example on how to greet customers and deal with complaints (1). Trained staff know exactly what is expected and have the product knowledge to answer questions (1), so customers receive the same high standard of service on every visit, whichever member of staff serves them (1).
:::

::: example Outline - cost of poor quality
Brightspark Electrics plc makes kettles sold in UK supermarkets. Last year 5% of its kettles were returned as faulty. Outline **one** impact on Brightspark of this level of faults. (2)

With 5% of kettles returned, Brightspark must pay for refunds or replacements and for collecting faulty kettles (1), raising its costs and reducing the profit made on each kettle sold through supermarkets (1).
:::

::: example Analyse - quality and competitive advantage
Harrow Hotels plc runs 60 mid-priced hotels. A mystery-shopper survey found that room cleanliness and check-in times vary widely between hotels. Its average online review score is 3.4 out of 5, below its main rival's 4.2.

Analyse how improving quality could give Harrow Hotels a competitive advantage. (6)

At present Harrow's quality is inconsistent between its 60 hotels, which explains its 3.4 rating against the rival's 4.2 (AO2). Travellers compare review scores when booking, so if Harrow sets clear standards for cleaning and check-in in every hotel, its score should rise and more travellers will choose it over the rival, increasing room bookings and revenue (AO3a).

Consistent quality would also build loyalty (AO2). A guest who has a good stay in one Harrow hotel will trust the brand in other cities, so business travellers may book Harrow repeatedly, and higher review scores could let Harrow raise its room prices without losing customers, improving its margins (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - QA or more inspectors
Tidewell Foods plc makes ready meals for supermarkets. 3% of meals are rejected at the final inspection, and a supermarket has complained about a mislabelled batch.
Option 1: introduce quality assurance, training all 300 production staff to check their own stage, at a cost of £250,000.
Option 2: employ 6 more quality-control inspectors at £28,000 each per year.

Justify which **one** of these two options Tidewell Foods should choose. (9)

Quality assurance would make each of the 300 staff responsible for checking their own stage, so problems such as a wrong label are caught where they happen, not at the end (AO2). This prevents faults, cutting the 3% of meals now rejected, saving ingredients and labour, and reducing the risk of a mislabelled batch reaching the supermarket and damaging Tidewell's reputation with a key customer (AO3a).

However, training 300 staff costs £250,000 and takes time, during which output may slow, and QA only works if staff are motivated to take responsibility; if they are not, faults may continue (AO3a).

Tidewell should choose quality assurance. Six inspectors would cost £168,000 every year, so within two years they cost more than the training, and they only find faults after the food is made, so the waste continues. This depends on management supporting staff with time and resources to check their work, and on keeping some final checks while QA is introduced so supermarket contracts are not put at risk (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Do not swap the two.** Quality control **inspects** finished output; quality assurance **prevents** faults at every stage. This is the commonest error in this topic.
- Quality does not mean "luxury". It means **meeting customer expectations consistently**.
- Develop quality to **costs** (waste, refunds, recalls) or to **competitive advantage** (loyalty, reputation, a higher price).
- For a "method to improve quality" question, explain **how** the method works, not just that quality improves.
- In services, quality depends on **staff**: training and consistency are the key points.
- In a justify, include the drawbacks of your chosen option, such as the cost and time of training.
:::
