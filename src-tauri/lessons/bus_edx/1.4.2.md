## What it is

Choosing where to base a business affects how many customers it reaches, what it pays for premises and staff, and how easily it gets its supplies. For a start-up, a poor location can mean too few customers to survive; a good one may cost more than the owner can afford. Edexcel names the factors precisely, so use its words.

### Factors influencing location

**Proximity to the market**: being close to customers.
- Essential when customers must **visit** the business: shops, cafés, hairdressers, gyms, dentists. These need **footfall** (the number of people passing) and easy access, parking or public transport.
- Important when the product is **perishable** or **bulky** and must be delivered quickly or cheaply: bakeries, florists, builders' merchants.
- Important for a **service** carried out at the customer's home within a travel radius: plumbers, mobile dog groomers.
- The cost: sites near lots of customers usually have **higher rent**.

**Proximity to labour**: being close to workers with the right skills at a wage the business can afford.
- Matters most when the business needs **skilled** staff (software firms near universities, engineering firms in areas with a manufacturing tradition) or **many** staff.
- Lower-wage areas reduce costs for labour-intensive work.
- A start-up with no employees may care little about this factor at first.

**Proximity to materials**: being close to raw materials or suppliers.
- Matters most when materials are **heavy, bulky or perishable**, so transport is expensive or slow: a sawmill near forests, a dairy near farms, a quarry where the stone is.
- Less important for a business whose inputs are light or delivered cheaply.

**Proximity to competitors**: being near (or away from) rivals.
- **Near**: customers often go where they can compare (restaurant streets, car showrooms, antique shops), so being in a cluster brings footfall. Businesses can also benefit from shared suppliers and skilled workers in the area.
- **Away**: a small shop next to a large rival selling the same things may lose customers on price, so it may choose a location where it faces less direct competition.

**The nature of the business activity**: what the business does decides which factor dominates.

| Type of activity | Factor that usually dominates | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Retail and consumer services | Proximity to the market (footfall, access) | A coffee shop by a station |
| Manufacturing with bulky inputs | Proximity to materials, plus transport links and cheap land | A brick maker near clay pits |
| Skilled, knowledge-based services | Proximity to labour | A design agency in a city with art colleges |
| Trades and mobile services | Proximity to the market, within travel range | An electrician working within 20 miles |
| Online-only businesses | Cost of premises and courier access | A clothing e-tailer in a cheap warehouse unit |

### The impact of the internet on location decisions

**E-commerce** is selling products online, through the business's own website, a marketplace or social media shops. It has changed location decisions:

- **Freedom from footfall**: an online business does not need a high-street site, so it can locate where **rent is cheapest**, even at the owner's home. This lowers fixed costs and break even (1.3.2b), and so lowers the risk of starting up.
- **A wider market**: customers anywhere in the UK, or abroad, can buy, so the business is no longer limited to people who pass its door.
- **New location needs**: an online business needs good broadband, storage space and easy access for **couriers**, rather than footfall.

But many businesses still need **fixed premises**:
- Services delivered face to face (hairdressers, cafés, gyms, dentists) cannot be delivered online.
- Some customers want to see, touch or try products before buying, and value advice.
- A shop builds trust and a local reputation.

So the choice is **e-commerce and/or fixed premises**:

| Model | Location implication | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed premises only | Location chosen for footfall and access | A local barber |
| E-commerce only | Location chosen for cost, storage and couriers | A print-on-demand T-shirt seller working from a unit |
| Both (hybrid) | A smaller or cheaper shop, with a website extending its reach; the shop may act as a showroom and a collection point | A bike shop with an online store and click-and-collect |

### Making the decision

Most location decisions are a **trade-off** between being close to customers or inputs and the **cost** of the site. The owner must ask which factor matters most for **this** business, and whether the extra sales or savings from a better site outweigh its higher rent.

::: terms
- **Location**: the place where a business is based or operates from.
- **Proximity to the market**: being close to the customers who buy the business's products.
- **Proximity to labour**: being close to a supply of workers with the right skills at an affordable wage.
- **Proximity to materials**: being close to the raw materials or suppliers a business needs, especially when they are heavy, bulky or perishable.
- **Proximity to competitors**: being near rival businesses, which can attract customers who come to compare or can take customers away.
- **Nature of the business activity**: what the business does, which decides which location factor matters most.
- **Footfall**: the number of potential customers who pass a location.
- **E-commerce**: buying and selling goods and services online.
- **Fixed premises**: a physical building such as a shop, office or workshop from which a business trades.
:::

::: method
1. **If the question names one factor, write only about that factor.** In 2025, students who drifted from proximity to the market onto other factors had part of their answer ignored.
2. **Explain (3)**: factor (1) → why it matters for this kind of business (1) → effect on sales or costs (1).
3. **Match the factor to the nature of the activity**: retail and services, the market; heavy manufacturing, materials; skilled services, labour; online, cost and couriers.
4. **Internet questions**: say whether the business needs fixed premises, e-commerce or both, and develop the effect on rent, reach and customers.
5. **Apply the case**: what the business sells, who its customers are, what the sites cost.
6. **Justify (9) between two sites or between shop and online**: choose one; set the extra sales or savings against the extra cost and risk; conclude with a "depends on" factor such as how much of the trade is passing or planned.
:::

::: example Explain - proximity to materials
Explain **one** reason why proximity to materials may be important for a business. (3)

If the raw materials are heavy or bulky (1), such as timber for a sawmill, they are expensive to transport long distances (1), so locating close to them keeps transport costs down, which lowers total costs and allows lower prices or higher profit (1).
:::

::: example Outline - location factor from a case
Lily runs Crust, a bakery in a busy street next to Bedford railway station. Most of her customers buy breakfast on the way to their train. Outline **one** factor that could have influenced Crust's location. (2)

Proximity to the market (1): Lily's customers are commuters buying breakfast on their way to the train, so a site next to the station puts Crust where hundreds of potential customers pass every morning (1).
:::

::: example Discuss - proximity to market
Discuss how proximity to the market could influence the location of a small business. (6)

For a business that customers must visit, such as a café or hairdresser, being close to the market is essential. A site with high footfall puts the business in front of many potential customers, so it gains passing trade and impulse purchases without spending as much on promotion, increasing sales.

However, sites close to the market, such as a busy high street, usually charge higher rent, which raises fixed costs and the level of sales needed to break even. So a small business must judge whether the extra customers will bring in more revenue than the extra rent costs. For a business that delivers or sells online, proximity to the market matters less, as customers do not need to visit, so it can choose a cheaper site further away.

(Level 3: stays on proximity to the market throughout, with two developed lines of reasoning.)
:::

::: example Analyse - impact of the internet
Hattie makes handmade leather bags in a workshop in rural Herefordshire. Her nearest town has few shoppers, so she sells online through her own website and a marketplace. Analyse the impact of the internet on Hattie's location decision. (6)

Because Hattie sells through e-commerce, she does not need to be close to her market, so she can work from a rural workshop with low rent instead of paying for a shop in a town (AO2). This keeps her fixed costs low, so she needs to sell fewer bags to break even, which reduces the risk of running a small craft business (AO3a).

The internet also gives her customers across the UK and abroad, rather than the few shoppers in her nearest town (AO2). Her market is far larger than a local shop could reach, so she can sell more bags at a premium price; but she depends on good broadband and courier collections from a rural address, and without a shop customers cannot feel the leather before buying, so strong photos and reviews matter (AO3a).
:::

::: example Justify - shop or online only
Jamal is starting Gear Up, selling refurbished gaming consoles in Coventry. Option 1: rent a small shop in the city centre for £1,400 a month. Option 2: sell online only, from a storage unit costing £250 a month. Justify which **one** of these options Jamal should choose. (9)

Selling online only saves Jamal £1,150 a month in rent, which lowers his fixed costs and break-even point, so the business is less likely to make a loss while it builds a reputation (AO2). Online, he can sell to gamers across the UK, not just in Coventry, so his potential market is much bigger than a city-centre shop could reach (AO3a).

However, customers buying refurbished electronics may worry about quality and want to see and test a console before paying. Without a shop, Jamal must build trust through warranties, reviews and clear photos, and he faces competition from many other online sellers (AO3a).

Jamal should choose online only, because the rent saving is large compared with the likely sales of a small specialist shop, and gamers are used to buying online. This depends on whether he can build trust quickly: offering a 12-month warranty and easy returns would make online buyers as confident as shop buyers, but if research shows local customers strongly prefer to test before buying, a shop could justify its rent (AO3b).
:::

::: watch
- **Write only about the factor the question names.** Discussing other factors gains nothing.
- Use the spec phrases: **proximity to the market, labour, materials and competitors**; **nature of the business activity**; **e-commerce and/or fixed premises**.
- Being near **competitors** can be a benefit (customers come to compare) or a drawback (rivals take sales). Say which and why.
- **E-commerce is a way of selling**, not a way of promoting. It affects location because customers do not need to visit.
- Good locations **cost more**. Always weigh the extra sales against the extra rent.
- In an outline or analyse, use the case: what the business does and who its customers are.
:::
