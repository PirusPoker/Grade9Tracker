## What it is

**A3** is the first 10-mark question in Component 1. It covers a section of roughly fifteen lines in the middle of the extract and asks **how the writer shows** something about a character or the scene, using **language**. It tests **AO2**. The live papers and specimen use a small family of question types:

| Question type | Example shape (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| Thoughts and feelings | How does the writer show X's thoughts and feelings about [something] in these lines? |
| What sort of person | How does the writer show what kind of person X is? |
| Change | How does the writer show X's feelings changing in these lines? |
| Atmosphere or tension | How does the writer create tension / a sense of danger in these lines? |

The question usually gives two bullets: **what the writer says** about the character (the content), and **the writer's use of language** to show it (the method). The best answers fuse the two in every paragraph: *what* X feels, *which words* show it, and *how* those words make the reader understand or share the feeling.

**How it is banded.** A3 is marked in five bands. In plain terms:

| Band | What the examiner sees |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Thorough, **perceptive** exploration; **detailed analysis** of how language works on the reader; **subtleties** of technique explored; precise terminology |
| 7–8 | Detail and **insight**; beginning to **analyse** language effects; terminology used accurately |
| 5–6 | Some detail; **some understanding** of how language achieves effects; some accurate terminology |
| 3–4 | **Straightforward** comments on some feelings or traits; terms may just be named |
| 1–2 | Identifies and begins to comment on one or two points |

The jump from 5–6 to 7–8 is the jump from *explaining* to *analysing*: not just "the word 'trembled' shows she is scared" but "'trembled' is involuntary, so her fear is something her body admits even while her words insist she is fine". The jump to 9–10 comes from **subtlety** (what lies under the surface), **development** (how the feeling moves through the lines) and **precision**.

**What to analyse.**
- **Word classes.** Verbs carry action and attitude ("slammed", "drifted"); adjectives and adverbs colour judgement; nouns can be loaded ("prison" for a house).
- **Imagery.** Similes, metaphors and personification transfer feelings onto objects and settings: a "sullen" sky reflects a sullen mind.
- **Sentence forms.** A short sentence after long ones can mark shock or decision; a long, breathless sentence can show panic; fragments can show a mind breaking apart; questions show doubt.
- **Repetition and patterning.** Repeated words or structures can show obsession, rising emotion or a mind going round in circles.
- **Narrative perspective.** Free indirect thought puts us inside the character; a first-person narrator may reveal more than they intend.
- **Dialogue and action.** What a character says, fails to say or does with their hands often reveals more than description.

**Tracking development.** Fifteen lines rarely hold one static feeling. Look for the **turn**: anxiety becoming relief, pride collapsing into shame, calm giving way to dread. Organise your answer through the lines in order and use connectives that show movement ("at first… but by the time…", "this hope is then crushed when…"). Tracking is one of the easiest ways to show "thorough" coverage.

**Paragraph shape.** For each point:
1. **Point**: what the character feels or is like (or what the atmosphere is).
2. **Short embedded quotation**: two to six words.
3. **Close analysis**: zoom in on one word or image; give its connotations; name the technique only if it helps.
4. **Effect**: what the reader feels, understands or expects as a result.
5. **Link**: how this connects to or develops into the next point.

Four or five such paragraphs, or one continuous answer that does the same, in **12–13 minutes**, will cover the section thoroughly.

::: terms
- **Thoughts and feelings**: what a character thinks and feels, which the question asks you to explore and link to language.
- **Analysis**: explaining how specific words, images or sentence forms create an effect, not just naming them.
- **Word class**: the grammatical type of a word, such as noun, verb, adjective or adverb.
- **Imagery**: language that creates a picture, especially simile, metaphor and personification.
- **Pathetic fallacy**: describing weather or surroundings so that they reflect a character's feelings.
- **Sentence form**: the length and type of a sentence (simple, compound, complex, minor) used for effect.
- **Repetition**: using a word, phrase or structure more than once for emphasis.
- **Tension**: a feeling of anxious expectation that something will happen.
- **Development**: the way a feeling or impression changes across a section of text.
- **Subtlety**: a meaning that is implied or layered rather than obvious.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **focus** (whose feelings? about what?) and bracket the line range.
2. Read the lines and mark the **turn**: where does the feeling change?
3. Pick four to six moments in order through the lines, each with a short quotation.
4. For each: point, embedded quotation, zoom on one word or image, effect on the reader, link onward.
5. Include at least one comment on a **sentence form** or **perspective** as well as word choice.
6. Make one point about what is **not said**: the feeling beneath the surface. That is where 9–10 lives.
7. No introduction needed. A one-line closing sentence on the overall movement of the feeling is enough.
:::

::: example A 9–10 paragraph on thoughts and feelings
Lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel): *"Ellen read the letter twice, folded it into four, and put it behind the clock. Then she took it out and read it again. Outside the hens were complaining about something; she would have to see to them. She would have to see to everything now."*

Answer extract: Ellen's first reaction is control rather than grief: the precise sequence of verbs, "read… folded… put", suggests she is managing the news like a household task, folding it "into four" as if making it smaller. That control cracks immediately: "Then she took it out and read it again" shows she cannot leave it alone, and the short, separate sentence makes the relapse feel involuntary. The writer then turns outward to the hens "complaining", a mild, almost comic personification that keeps ordinary life noisily going on. The final sentence is the turn. "She would have to see to everything now" repeats "see to" but widens it from hens to "everything", so we realise, without being told, that the letter has left her alone with every responsibility. The free indirect thought lets her face it in her own plain words, and the understatement makes the loss heavier.
(Perceptive, tracks development, analyses repetition, perspective and understatement: 9–10 level.)
:::

::: example From 5–6 to 7–8
5–6: "The writer shows Tom is nervous by saying his hands 'shook'. This shows he is scared. The writer also uses a short sentence 'He waited.' to build tension."

7–8: "Tom's hands 'shook' as he signed, an involuntary verb that betrays fear his calm words deny. The writer then isolates 'He waited.' as a sentence of its own: the full stop holds the reader in the same suspended pause as Tom, so the tension is felt rather than described." The difference is analysis of *how* each choice works.
:::

::: example Creating tension
Lines (original, in the style of a 1940s novel): *"The footsteps on the gravel stopped. For a long moment there was nothing: no wind, no bird, only the tick of the hall clock, which had never seemed so loud. Then, very gently, someone tried the handle."*

Analysis: The writer creates tension through **silence**. "Stopped" removes the one sound we were tracking, and the list "no wind, no bird" empties the scene. The clock's tick becomes "so loud" because everything else is gone, so time itself is made audible and slow. The adverbs "very gently" are the most frightening touch: a violent knock would be less sinister than a careful, testing hand, because gentleness implies patience and intent. The sentence beginning "Then" releases the held breath only to tighten it again.
:::

::: example Covering "what sort of person"
Lines (original, in the style of a 1930s novel): *"Cousin Albert lent money to everybody and forgot nothing. He kept the debts in a small black book, and on Sunday afternoons he would read it through, moving his lips."*

Analysis: The opening clause seems generous, but the balancing clause "and forgot nothing" turns kindness into calculation. The "small black book" has sinister connotations (black, secret, a record of sins). The detail "moving his lips" makes his Sunday ritual almost a prayer: he worships what he is owed. The writer builds a miser through a few precise actions, without any judging adjective.
:::

::: q
What are the two bullet points usually given in A3, and how should you use them? (2)
---
What the writer says about the character, and the writer's use of language (1); combine them in every paragraph: the feeling, the words that show it and their effect (1).
:::

::: q
What separates a 7–8 answer from a 5–6 answer? (2)
---
5–6 explains with some understanding of effects (1); 7–8 begins to analyse how specific words, images and sentence forms work on the reader, with insight (1).
:::

::: q
Give three language features you might analyse in A3. (3)
---
Any three: verb choice; adjectives or adverbs; simile, metaphor or personification; pathetic fallacy; sentence forms (short, long, fragments); repetition; free indirect thought / perspective; dialogue.
:::

::: q
Why is tracking development useful in an A3 answer? (2)
---
Feelings usually change across the lines (1); following the change in order shows thorough coverage and insight into the character, both features of higher bands (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the effect of the verb in: "Her hope curdled when she saw his face." (2)
---
"Curdled" suggests milk going sour (1): her hope turns suddenly unpleasant and spoiled, something good becoming sickening, so the change feels physical and irreversible (1).
:::

::: q
What does "subtleties of technique" mean in practice? (2)
---
Exploring implied or layered effects (1), such as irony, understatement, what a character avoids saying, or how a choice works on two levels at once (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Labels are not analysis.** "There is a metaphor" earns almost nothing; explain what it makes us feel about the character.
- **Stay on the focus.** If the question asks about feelings about growing older, analyse those feelings, not her clothes.
- **Use the whole range.** Answers that only analyse the first five lines miss development.
- **Quote briefly.** Long quotations hide which word you mean.
- **Don't retell.** Every sentence should be about how the writer shows, not what happens next.
- **Twelve to thirteen minutes.** Leave time for A4 and A5.
:::

::: test
Analyse how the writer shows the character's feelings in: "Jack laughed too loudly at the joke, and went on laughing after everyone else had stopped." (2)
---
"Too loudly" and laughing after others have stopped suggest the laugh is forced (1); he seems nervous, desperate to fit in, or hiding hurt, so the reader senses discomfort beneath the noise (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the effect of the sentence form in: "She ran. Past the church, past the bakery, past the house where the dog barked, past everything." (3)
---
The short opening sentence "She ran." is abrupt and urgent (1); the long list built on repeated "past" mimics breathless, unstoppable movement (1); ending on "past everything" suggests she is escaping her whole life, not just the street, so the feeling is panic or release (1).
:::

::: test
Identify an example of pathetic fallacy in this sentence and explain its effect: "The fog pressed against the windows as Mr Hale read the doctor's letter for the third time." (2)
---
"The fog pressed against the windows" (1); the heavy, closing-in fog mirrors his oppressive fear or sense of being trapped by the bad news (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how free indirect thought shows the character's feelings: "It did not matter. Of course it did not matter. Who cared about a silly dance anyway?" (3)
---
The narration takes on the character's own voice without speech marks (1); the repetition "did not matter… did not matter" reveals she is trying to convince herself (1); the dismissive rhetorical question shows hurt pride disguised as indifference, so the reader sees the opposite of what she says (1).
:::

::: test
Analyse the word "shrank" in: "Under his father's gaze, Peter seemed to shrink." (2)
---
"Shrink" suggests becoming physically smaller (1): Peter feels diminished, powerless and childlike before his father, who seems to dominate him with a look alone (1).
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel).

> Owen had been told to wait in the corridor, and so he waited. The chair was too low for him and his knees stuck up awkwardly, like a grasshopper's. Through the frosted glass of the door he could see the shapes of his mother and the headmaster, and hear their voices rising and falling without words. Once his mother laughed, a short bright sound, and he felt a sudden warmth, because perhaps it was going to be all right after all. Then the voices dropped. He studied the floor. There was a crack in the linoleum that ran from his left shoe to the skirting board, and he followed it with his eyes, there and back, there and back, until he knew every turn of it. A clock somewhere struck the half hour. He thought of the window he had broken, how it had seemed to hang in the air for a moment before it fell, glittering, as if it were deciding. He had not meant it. He had told them he had not meant it. When the door opened at last his mother came out first and did not look at him, and she was holding her handbag very tightly in both hands.

How does the writer show Owen's thoughts and feelings in these lines? You should consider what the writer says about Owen and the writer's use of language to show his thoughts and feelings. You must refer to the text to support your answer, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: thorough, perceptive exploration of Owen's feelings and how they change; detailed analysis of language, sentence forms and perspective; subtleties explored (e.g. what the mother's final gesture tells him); precise terminology.
- 7–8: detailed exploration with insight; begins to analyse how language achieves effects; accurate terminology.
- 5–6: explores feelings in some detail; some understanding of how language is used; some accurate terminology.
- 3–4: straightforward comments on some feelings, with some reference; terms may be named.
- 1–2: identifies and begins to comment on one or two feelings. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content: obedient, subdued ("told to wait… and so he waited", the repetition stresses passivity); awkward and out of place (chair too low; simile "like a grasshopper's" makes him gangly and childlike); excluded and anxious (voices "without words", seen through frosted glass, so he must guess); hope ("a sudden warmth", the mother's "short bright sound"; free indirect "perhaps it was going to be all right after all"); hope crushed ("Then the voices dropped.", short sentence as a turn); avoidance and anxious fixation (studying the crack, repetition "there and back, there and back" shows a mind looping to avoid thinking); guilt and memory (the window "seemed to hang… as if it were deciding", slow-motion personification shows the moment replaying, and suggests he felt it was out of his hands); defensiveness and hurt ("He had not meant it. He had told them…", repetition, short sentences, he feels disbelieved); final dread (mother "did not look at him", handbag held "very tightly": he reads her silence as anger or shame; the writer withholds the outcome).
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1930s novel).

> Miss Lacey had taught the piano in the front room of Number 9 for forty years, and this was the last lesson. The pupil, a stout child named Brenda, played her scales with the usual mistakes, and Miss Lacey corrected them with the usual words. Nobody would have guessed. The removal men were coming on Thursday; the piano was sold to a public house in Shrewsbury. She found that she could not look at it directly, so she looked at Brenda's hands instead, small and pink and careless on the yellowed keys. How many hands had there been? Hundreds. A thousand, perhaps. She could remember almost none of them. When the clock struck five Brenda jumped up at once, as children always did, and Miss Lacey heard herself say, "Same time next week, dear," and then stood quite still in the middle of the room, with the music still open on the stand, while the child's footsteps went away down the street.

How does the writer show Miss Lacey's thoughts and feelings in these lines? You should consider what the writer says about Miss Lacey and the writer's use of language to show her thoughts and feelings. You must refer to the text to support your answer, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands as for all A3 questions:
- 9–10: thorough, perceptive; detailed analysis; subtleties (restraint, irony of the habitual phrase) explored; precise terms.
- 7–8: detail and insight; beginning to analyse; accurate terms.
- 5–6: some detail; some understanding of language; some accurate terms.
- 3–4: straightforward comments; terms may be named.
- 1–2: begins to comment. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content: a sense of an ending ("forty years", "the last lesson"); she hides her feelings behind routine (repetition of "the usual mistakes… the usual words"); the minor sentence "Nobody would have guessed." emphasises her concealment and dignity; loss expressed through practical facts (removal men, piano "sold to a public house", the plainness makes it bleak, and a public house is an undignified fate for it); grief she cannot face ("could not look at it directly", the piano almost personified like a person she is leaving); "yellowed keys" suggest age and long use, contrasting with Brenda's "small and pink and careless" hands (youth, indifference, list of adjectives); rhetorical question and the answer "Hundreds. A thousand, perhaps." show her mind spiralling over a lifetime; "She could remember almost none of them" reveals the sadness that her life's work has left little trace; "heard herself say" shows habit taking over, as if she is outside herself; the irony of "Same time next week" when there is no next week is the most poignant detail; the final stillness, "music still open", and the fading footsteps leave her isolated; perspective: third person close to Miss Lacey, so understatement carries the feeling.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1940s novel).

> The lane narrowed between high hedges until the car could go no further, and Harriet got out and walked. It was later than she had meant. The sky over the hill had turned the colour of a bruise, and the hedges on either side leaned in, black and dripping, so that she seemed to be walking down a throat. Somewhere a gate banged, and banged again, though there was no wind. She told herself it was a farm. Of course it was a farm; there were farms all over this valley. She walked faster. Her shoes, chosen for a London office, slid on the wet stones, and twice she nearly fell. When at last she saw a light ahead, a single square window low down in the dark, she stopped, and found that she did not want to go any nearer to it at all.

How does the writer create tension in these lines? You must refer to the text to support your answer, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands as for A3 (9–10 perceptive, detailed analysis of how language builds tension, subtleties explored; 7–8 insight, beginning to analyse; 5–6 some understanding; 3–4 straightforward; 1–2 begins to comment).
Indicative content: setting closing in ("narrowed", "could go no further", she must continue alone on foot); time pressure ("later than she had meant", short sentence); threatening imagery ("colour of a bruise", violence and injury; hedges "leaned in", personification; metaphor of "walking down a throat", being swallowed); unexplained sound (gate "banged, and banged again, though there was no wind", repetition plus the impossible detail); self-reassurance in free indirect thought ("Of course it was a farm"), which shows fear while denying it; short sentence "She walked faster." shows rising panic; vulnerability (city shoes, slipping, "nearly fell"); the light, normally a comfort, becomes sinister ("single square window low down in the dark"); the ending reverses expectation: she stops and does not want to go on, leaving the threat undefined, which increases tension.
:::

::: test
Explain why "The writer uses a lot of description to show she is sad" would be placed in the lowest band. (2)
---
It is vague and general, with no quotation (1) and no analysis of any specific word or technique, so it only begins to comment (1).
:::

::: test
Rewrite this 3–4 band comment as a 7–8 band comment: "'Grey' shows the town is boring." (3)
---
For example: "The repeated adjective 'grey' (1) drains the town of colour and life, suggesting monotony (1), and since it also describes the people's faces, the dullness seems to have seeped into them, reflecting the narrator's own hopelessness (1)."
:::

::: test
What should a one-line closing sentence in an A3 answer do? (1)
---
Sum up how the feeling or impression develops across the lines (the overall movement), rather than repeat earlier points.
:::

::: test
In "Every tick of the clock was a small hammer," explain the effect of the metaphor on the reader's sense of the character's feelings. (2)
---
Turning the tick into a "hammer" makes ordinary time feel painful and violent (1); it suggests the character is waiting in agony, each second hitting her, so the reader feels her anxiety or dread (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how dialogue reveals feeling in: "'I'm perfectly all right,' said Joan, to the teapot." (2)
---
The words claim she is fine (1), but saying them "to the teapot" shows she cannot look anyone in the eye, so the dialogue reveals the hurt or embarrassment it denies (1).
:::

::: test
Read this line (original, in the style of a 1960s novel): "He smiled at the guests, poured the wine, told the story about the fishing trip, and all the while the telegram lay folded in his breast pocket like a blade." What does the simile suggest about his thoughts and feelings? (2)
---
The telegram is "like a blade": dangerous and painful, close to his heart (1); he is outwardly sociable but inwardly wounded or dreading the news, and the list of hosting actions shows the effort of hiding it (1).
:::
