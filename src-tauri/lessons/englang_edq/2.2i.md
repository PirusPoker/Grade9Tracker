## What it is

Section B of Component 2 gives you **one hour for two compulsory 20-mark tasks**, B1 and B2. The paper advises **30 minutes on each: about 5 minutes planning and 25 minutes writing**, aiming at **300–400 words** each. The two tasks almost always differ in **form, audience and register**: a formal letter to the council and a lively magazine article; a talk to your class and a review. This topic is about doing both well under time pressure: **planning fast**, **organising** clearly (AO5), **switching register**, and **writing accurately** (AO6).

**Why the clock matters.** Each task is worth exactly the same: 20 marks, a quarter of the paper. A brilliant B1 of 550 words followed by a rushed 120-word B2 loses far more than it gains, because a short, undeveloped second piece cannot reach the higher bands for organisation or development. **Two good pieces beat one great one and a fragment.**

**The hour, minute by minute.**

| Minutes | B1 | Minutes | B2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Plan: purpose, audience, form, register; 4–5 paragraph points; opening and ending | 30–35 | Plan B2 the same way, deliberately **resetting register** |
| 5–27 | Write about 350 words (about 15 words a minute) | 35–57 | Write about 350 words |
| 27–30 | Check AO6: spelling, punctuation, paragraphing | 57–60 | Check AO6 |

If B1 overruns, **stop at 32 minutes** whatever happens: write a one- or two-sentence conclusion and move on. You can return at the end if time allows.

**The five-minute plan.** Write it in the margin or the planning space. Keep it to decisions, not sentences:
1. **P-A-F-R**: purpose, audience, form, register. (e.g. *persuade / council / letter / formal, courteous, firm*)
2. **Viewpoint** in one line, if the task asks for views.
3. **4–5 paragraph points**, each with one specific detail or example.
4. **Opening**: the hook or first sentence.
5. **Ending**: the final idea or line (call to action, verdict, circular return).
6. **Two or three devices or ambitious words** to use.

**Paragraphing and cohesion (AO5 organisation).** 300–400 words is about **four or five paragraphs** of 60–90 words. Each needs a clear **topic sentence**. Paragraphs should **link**: the end of one leads into the start of the next. **Discourse markers** guide the reader:

| Function | Discourse markers |
|---|---|
| Sequence | First, To begin with, Next, Finally, Above all |
| Adding | Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, What is more |
| Contrast | However, On the other hand, Nevertheless, Yet, By contrast |
| Cause and effect | Therefore, As a result, Consequently, This means that |
| Example | For instance, Take, for example, Consider |
| Concession | Admittedly, Of course, It is true that… but |
| Conclusion | Ultimately, In short, Above all, So, |

Use them for **logic**, not decoration. Vary them; "Firstly… Secondly… Thirdly…" in every piece is mechanical. In a talk or lively article, spoken signposts work better ("Here's the thing.", "But that's not all.").

**Switching register between tasks.** Plan B2 as if you had never written B1. Before writing, say to yourself: *Who is reading this one?* Then check your first line: does it sound like a councillor's letter, or a teenage magazine? The commonest slip is carrying B1's register into B2: a stiff, formal magazine article, or a chatty letter to a headteacher.

**AO6 under time pressure: the final three minutes.** You cannot rewrite, but you can fix the errors that cost most:
- **Sentence boundaries**: comma splices ("The park is closing, this is wrong.") and run-ons. Replace the comma with a full stop, a semicolon or a conjunction.
- **Capital letters** for names, places, "I", and sentence starts.
- **Apostrophes**: *it's* (it is) vs *its* (belonging to it); *students'* (plural possession) vs *student's*; no apostrophes in plain plurals ("the computer's" ✗).
- **Homophones**: there/their/they're; your/you're; where/were/wear; affect/effect; to/too.
- **Commonly misspelt words**: necessary, definitely, separate, environment, government, recommend, embarrass, beginning, argument, achieve, sincerely, accommodation, conscience, business, believe.
- **Tense and agreement**: "The number of students *is* rising" (not "are").

**What earns the top AO6 marks.** Not long words for their own sake, but **precise** vocabulary, **varied** sentences (a short one for emphasis, a complex one for a developed idea, a question for engagement) and **a range of punctuation used accurately**: a semicolon, a colon, a pair of dashes or brackets, as well as commas and full stops. One accurate semicolon is worth more than five shaky ones.

::: terms
- **Discourse marker**: a word or phrase that signals how ideas connect, such as however, therefore or for instance.
- **Topic sentence**: the opening sentence of a paragraph that states its main point.
- **Cohesion**: the way sentences and paragraphs link so that writing flows logically.
- **Comma splice**: an error where two complete sentences are joined by only a comma.
- **Homophones**: words that sound the same but differ in spelling and meaning, such as there and their.
- **P-A-F-R**: a planning check of purpose, audience, form and register.
- **Register shift**: changing formality and tone between two tasks with different audiences.
- **Proofreading**: checking writing for errors in spelling, punctuation and grammar after drafting.
- **Agreement**: making a verb match its subject in number, such as "the team is" or "the players are".
:::

::: method
1. Read **both** tasks first (one minute), so ideas for B2 can collect while you write B1.
2. Plan B1 in **five minutes**: P-A-F-R, viewpoint, 4–5 points, opening, ending.
3. Write B1 for about **22 minutes**: four or five linked paragraphs, 300–400 words.
4. Check B1 for **three minutes**: sentence boundaries, apostrophes, homophones, capitals.
5. At **30 minutes**, move to B2, even if B1 needs a quick final sentence.
6. Plan B2 afresh, **resetting register** for the new audience.
7. Write and check B2 the same way. Never leave it unfinished.
:::

::: example Two five-minute plans, side by side
B1 (invented for practice): *Your local council plans to replace the town library with a "community hub" with fewer books but more computers and meeting rooms. Write a letter to the council giving your views.*
- P-A-F-R: argue and persuade / councillors / formal letter / respectful, firm.
- View: support a hub only if the book collection and quiet study space are protected.
- Points: (1) the library as a free quiet space (my revision; elderly readers); (2) computers are needed: job applications, homework; (3) books are not outdated: children's reading, people without devices; (4) counter: costs; rebut: phased plan, volunteers; (5) proposal: keep a reading room plus new hub.
- Opening: "Dear Councillor Price, I am writing as one of the many students who revise in Hartfield Library every weekend…"
- Ending: "Build the hub, by all means; just don't build it on the books." (antithesis)

B2 (invented for practice): *Write an article for a teenage magazine with the title "The Best Advice I Was Ever Given".*
- P-A-F-R: entertain and reflect / teenagers / magazine article / lively, warm, personal.
- Points: (1) anecdote: the moment I heard it (grandad, failed swimming test); (2) what it meant ("Do it badly first"); (3) how I used it (guitar, a speech); (4) why it matters for readers (perfectionism, fear); (5) return to the swimming pool.
- Opening: "I was nine, soaking wet and furious."
- Ending: circular: passing the swimming test, badly.
Notice the complete **register reset** between the two plans.
:::

::: example Editing for AO6 in three minutes
Draft: *"The council say's the library is to expensive, I dont agree. Their are many people who's only acess to computers is the library and it's opening hours are already short."*

Corrected: *"The council says the library is too expensive. I do not agree: there are many people whose only access to computers is the library, and its opening hours are already short."*

Fixes: "say's" → "says" (no apostrophe in a verb); "to" → "too"; comma splice → full stop; "dont" → "do not" (or "don't"); "Their" → "there"; "who's" → "whose"; "acess" → "access"; "it's" → "its" (possession); a colon introduces the reason. Six quick fixes lift the AO6 band.
:::

::: example Using discourse markers for cohesion
Without markers: *"Sports day is popular. Some students hate it. It could include more events. Everyone could take part."*
With markers: *"Sports day is undoubtedly popular with many students. However, for those who dread the hundred metres, it can be the worst day of the year. Therefore, rather than scrapping it, the school could add events such as a quiz relay or a tug of war, so that, ultimately, everyone has a way to take part."*
The markers show contrast, consequence and conclusion, turning a list into an argument.
:::

::: example Keeping the time
At 27 minutes a student is still on paragraph three of B1. Right decision: write one strong concluding sentence that returns to the main view ("For all these reasons, I urge you to keep the library's reading room open."), check the first lines quickly for errors, and start planning B2 by minute 30. A shorter, complete B1 and a full B2 will outscore a long B1 and a hurried 100-word B2.
:::

::: q
How long should you spend on each Section B task, and how should that time be divided? (2)
---
About 30 minutes each (1): about 5 minutes planning and 25 minutes writing, leaving a few minutes to check (1).
:::

::: q
Why is it a mistake to spend 45 minutes on B1? (2)
---
B1 and B2 are worth 20 marks each (1); a rushed, short B2 cannot reach the higher bands, losing more marks than the extra time on B1 gains (1).
:::

::: q
What does P-A-F-R stand for, and why is it useful in planning? (2)
---
Purpose, audience, form, register (1); it makes you decide how to match the writing to the task before you start, which is central to AO5 (1).
:::

::: q
Correct the comma splice: "The trip was cancelled, nobody was told." (1)
---
"The trip was cancelled. Nobody was told." / "The trip was cancelled; nobody was told." / "The trip was cancelled, but nobody was told." (any correct version).
:::

::: q
Give one discourse marker for each function: adding, contrast, cause and effect. (3)
---
Adding: furthermore / moreover / in addition (1); contrast: however / nevertheless / on the other hand (1); cause and effect: therefore / consequently / as a result (1).
:::

::: q
Why should you read both Section B tasks before starting B1? (1)
---
So that ideas for B2 can develop in the back of your mind while you write B1, making the second plan quicker.
:::

::: watch
- **Thirty minutes each.** The second task is worth exactly as much as the first.
- **Plan both.** An unplanned second piece drifts and repeats.
- **Reset register.** Re-read B2's audience before writing its first line.
- **Paragraph visibly.** Four or five clear paragraphs, each with a topic sentence.
- **Fix the costly errors.** Comma splices, its/it's, there/their, missing capitals.
- **Never leave B2 unfinished.** A short, complete ending beats none.
:::

::: test
Write a five-point plan for this task: "Write an article for your school/college magazine with the title 'Why Every Student Should Learn to Swim'." (5)
---
One mark per developed, relevant plan point, e.g. (1) P-A-F-R: persuade and inform / students / magazine article / lively. (2) Opening anecdote: a friend who panicked at a lake. (3) Safety: rivers, holidays, plausible figure about people who cannot swim. (4) Health and confidence; counter-argument (cost, embarrassment) with rebuttal (free lessons, adult-sized beginner groups). (5) Ending: call to action, e.g. join the Thursday lessons. Credit any plan with a clear purpose, distinct points with detail, an opening and an ending.
:::

Rewrite this opening of a magazine article, which has been written in the register of a formal letter: "Dear Readers, I am writing to inform you of my views regarding the merits of learning to cook." (2)
---
For example: "Last year I burned toast so badly the smoke alarm still flinches when I walk past. This year I cooked Sunday lunch for eight. Here's why everyone should learn to cook." (1 for removing letter conventions and formal register; 1 for a lively, engaging opening suited to a magazine.)
:::

::: test
Add three discourse markers to improve the cohesion of this paragraph: "Phones can be distracting in lessons. They are useful for research. Schools should set clear rules rather than banning them." (3)
---
For example: "Admittedly, phones can be distracting in lessons. However, they are also useful for research. Therefore, schools should set clear rules rather than banning them." (1 per appropriate marker that signals concession, contrast and consequence.)
:::

::: test
It is 28 minutes into Section B and you are halfway through B1. Explain what you should do. (2)
---
Write a brief, strong conclusion to finish B1 within the next two minutes (1), then move on to plan B2 at about 30 minutes, returning to B1 only if time remains at the end (1).
:::

::: test
Identify and correct the agreement error: "The number of students who walk to school have fallen this year." (2)
---
The subject is "the number" (singular), so the verb should be "has" (1): "The number of students who walk to school has fallen this year." (1)
:::

::: test
Correct the spelling of these commonly misspelt words: "definately, seperate, enviroment, goverment". (2)
---
definitely, separate, environment, government (2 for all four; 1 for two or three).
:::

::: test
Section B, set 1, task B1.

Your headteacher is considering replacing the traditional sports day with a "charity fun day", with stalls, games and sponsored challenges, but no races. Write a letter to your headteacher giving your views on this idea.

12 marks are awarded for communication and organisation; 8 marks are awarded for vocabulary, sentence structure, punctuation and spelling. You should aim to write about 300–400 words. (20)
---
Award AO5 out of 12 and AO6 out of 8.
AO5 (12): 11–12 fully matched to a formal letter to the headteacher; respectful, assured register; a clear, consistent view convincingly developed with reasons and specific examples; counter-argument addressed; cohesive paragraphs with purposeful discourse markers; effective close. 8–10 clearly suited, consistent register and view, developed points, well organised. 5–7 generally appropriate, some development, paragraphs. 3–4 some awareness, limited development, simple organisation. 1–2 basic.
AO6 (8): 8 wide, precise vocabulary, varied sentences, confident range of punctuation, virtually all accurate; 6–7 varied, mostly accurate, a range of punctuation; 4–5 some variety, generally accurate; 2–3 limited variety, basic punctuation, errors; 1 little control.
Indicative: "Dear Mr/Mrs…"; purpose and view in the opening. For the change: inclusion of non-sporty students, money raised for good causes, community involvement. Against: loss of a tradition, the value of competition and celebrating athletic talent, house spirit. A compromise (races in the morning, fun day in the afternoon) is a strong option. Reward developed reasons with examples, a counter-argument with rebuttal, a polite closing paragraph and "Yours sincerely" (named headteacher). Penalise chatty register.
:::

Section B, set 1, task B2.

Write an article for a teenage magazine with the title "The Best Advice I Was Ever Given".

12 marks are awarded for communication and organisation; 8 marks are awarded for vocabulary, sentence structure, punctuation and spelling. You should aim to write about 300–400 words. (20)
---
Award AO5 out of 12 and AO6 out of 8.
AO5 (12): 11–12 fully matched to a lively, reflective magazine article for teenagers; the given title used; engaging personal voice; ideas convincingly developed through anecdote and reflection, with clear relevance to readers; a well-crafted structure (e.g. circular); memorable ending. 8–10 clearly suited, engaging, developed with detail, well organised. 5–7 generally appropriate, some development. 3–4 some awareness, limited development. 1–2 basic.
AO6 (8): bands as above.
Indicative: the title exactly as given; an anecdotal opening (who gave the advice and when); the advice itself and what it meant; examples of applying it; why it matters to readers of the same age (direct address, inclusive humour); a circular or reflective ending. Reward a register clearly different from a formal letter (lively, personal, but standard English), varied sentences and controlled paragraphing.
:::

::: test
Explain one way to show a range of punctuation accurately in a Section B response. (2)
---
For example, use a colon to introduce a list or explanation, a semicolon to join two closely related sentences, or a pair of dashes or brackets for parenthesis (1), using each correctly and only where it suits the meaning rather than forcing it in (1).
:::

Any two, e.g.: use a lively, personal voice with direct address instead of formal courtesy (1); include humour and vivid description where the letter used reasoned argument (1); replace the salutation and sign-off with a headline and a clear verdict and recommendation (1); use shorter, punchier sentences for magazine readers (1).
