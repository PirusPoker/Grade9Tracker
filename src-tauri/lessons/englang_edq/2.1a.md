## What it is

Section A of **Component 1** (20th Century Literature Reading and Creative Prose Writing) is 40 marks on **one unseen extract of 20th-century literary prose**, about **60–100 lines**, printed with line numbers in a separate resource booklet. It is usually the opening or a self-contained section of a 20th-century novel or short story. You are not expected to know the writer; you are tested on how well you **read**: finding, inferring, explaining the writer's choices and judging the whole.

**The shape of Section A.** Five questions walk through the extract **in order**, each with its own line range, so the paper does the dividing up for you.

| Question | Marks | What it asks | Skill (AO) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | 5 | List five things you learn about a person or place in the first few lines | AO1: find and infer |
| A2 | 5 | What impressions does the writer create of someone or something in a short section? (language) | AO2: language |
| A3 | 10 | How does the writer show a character's thoughts and feelings / what sort of person they are / a change? (language) | AO2: language |
| A4 | 10 | What impressions does the writer create, and how? (**language and structure**) | AO2: language and structure |
| A5 | 10 | A reader's statement about the final section and **the passage as a whole**: to what extent do you agree? | AO4: evaluate |

Notice the build: two short warm-ups, then three 10-mark questions. Thirty of the forty marks sit in A3–A5, so time must follow the marks.

**Timing.** The paper advises about **10 minutes reading** and **50 minutes answering** Section A. A sensible split:

| Stage | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Read the extract twice | 10 |
| A1 | 5 |
| A2 | 6–7 |
| A3 | 12–13 |
| A4 | 12–13 |
| A5 | 12–13 |

A student who spends twelve minutes on A1 and fourteen on A2 leaves 24 minutes for thirty marks. Five points for A1, a compact paragraph for A2, then move on.

**A ten-minute reading routine.**
1. **First read, for gist (about 4 minutes).** Who, where, roughly when, and what happens? Read to the end without annotating, because 20th-century stories often **turn** near the close, and A5 will ask about exactly that.
2. **Second read, with the questions open (about 6 minutes).** Bracket each question's line range and annotate it for that question's focus. Mark where the **perspective** shifts, dialogue starts, time jumps or the tone changes.

**Narrative perspective** tells you whose mind you are inside, and it affects every answer.

| Perspective | How you spot it | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| **First person** | "I", "me", "my" | Intimate and immediate, but possibly **unreliable**: the narrator may misread others or themselves, and the reader can see more than they do |
| **Third person limited** | "he", "she", but we stay with one character's thoughts | We share one viewpoint closely; other people are seen only from outside, so their motives stay hidden |
| **Third person omniscient** | The narrator knows several minds and can comment | Wider view, irony, a sense of judgement from above |
| **Free indirect thought** | Third-person narration slips into a character's own words: "Well, she would not cry. Not here." | We hear the character's voice without speech marks, often with irony |

**How 20th-century literary fiction tends to work.** It is rarely as direct as a Victorian narrator. Expect:
- **Character shown through action and dialogue** rather than summary. A man who straightens the same fork three times is anxious; the writer may never say so.
- **Understatement and subtext.** People say less than they mean. A conversation about the weather may really be about a broken marriage.
- **Free indirect thought** and interior detail, so that feelings come through the rhythm of the sentences.
- **Symbolic or telling objects**: a stopped clock, an unworn wedding dress, a letter left unopened.
- **A turn or revelation near the end** that reframes the extract, often quietly.

Your job is to **infer** and **justify**: say what the text implies, then point to the words that imply it.

**Staying inside the line ranges.** Each question names its lines. Material from outside them earns nothing for that question, however brilliant. The one exception is **A5**, which names the final lines and also asks you to "consider the passage as a whole", so there you may range across the extract.

**Terminology.** Use it where it sharpens a point: simile, metaphor, personification, verb choice, adjective, short sentence, repetition, dialogue, juxtaposition, foreshadowing, contrast, first-person narration. A correct term attached to a clear effect helps. A term without an effect earns almost nothing, and a wrong one damages your credibility.

::: terms
- **Unseen extract**: a passage you have not studied before; here about 60–100 lines of 20th-century literary prose.
- **Line range**: the lines a question tells you to use; material from outside it gains no credit.
- **Narrative perspective**: the point of view the story is told from, such as first person or third person limited.
- **Third person limited**: narration using "he" or "she" that stays close to one character's thoughts.
- **Omniscient narrator**: a narrator who can see into several characters' minds and comment on them.
- **Free indirect thought**: third-person narration that takes on a character's own words and attitude without speech marks.
- **Unreliable narrator**: a narrator whose account the reader has reason to doubt or see beyond.
- **Inference**: a conclusion the reader draws from evidence that the text implies but does not state.
- **Subtext**: meaning that lies beneath what characters actually say or do.
- **Understatement**: presenting something as smaller or calmer than it is, often to suggest strong feeling held back.
- **Turning point**: the moment where a situation, feeling or understanding changes.
- **Foreshadowing**: a hint early in a text of something that happens later.
:::

::: method
1. Open the paper and read all five questions first (one minute). Note each line range and focus.
2. **First read of the extract** for gist: who, where, when, what happens, and how it ends. Do not stop to annotate.
3. **Second read** with the questions beside you: bracket each line range, underline the three or four most useful words for each question's focus, and note shifts in perspective, time or mood.
4. Decide the narrative perspective and whose mind you share, because it shapes your comments in A3–A5.
5. Answer **in order**, keeping strictly to each line range (A5 alone may use the whole passage).
6. Time by marks: about 5 minutes for A1, 6–7 for A2, 12–13 for each of A3, A4 and A5.
7. In every analytical answer, infer first (what the text suggests) and then justify it (which words, how they work).
:::

::: example A two-read annotation
Extract (original passage written in the style of a 1950s short story): *"Mr Hollis had polished the brass plate by the door every morning for thirty-one years. On the Monday after the funeral he polished it again, though the name on it was no longer quite his."*

First read: an older man, a business or house with a brass name plate, a recent funeral. Second read, annotating: "every morning for thirty-one years" (habit, routine, loyalty); "polished it again" (he carries on as if nothing has changed, a way of coping); "no longer quite his" (the plate perhaps names a partnership or a family firm, "Hollis and Son"? The funeral may be the son's or partner's). The final clause is the **turn**: a sentence about cleaning becomes a sentence about grief. That is what A3 or A5 will want you to have noticed.
:::

::: example Working out perspective
*"She would not look at the clock. If she looked at the clock it would be twenty past, and if it was twenty past he was not coming, and she had told everyone he was coming."*

Perspective: third person ("she", "he") but this is **free indirect thought**. The circular logic ("if… it would be… and if it was…") is her own anxious reasoning, not a narrator's. Effect: the reader is placed inside her panic and also sees what she will not admit, that he is not coming. The last clause adds a second fear, public humiliation, revealing pride as well as hurt. A strong A3 answer names the method and explains how the looping sentence structure creates the feeling. (2 marks if asked "Identify the perspective and explain one effect".)
:::

::: example Line-range discipline
Question: *"Read lines 7–13. What impressions does the writer create of the landlady?"* A candidate writes a fine paragraph about her cruelty to the lodger, drawn from line 40. It earns nothing, because it is outside lines 7–13. The same detail could be gold in A4 if line 40 falls in A4's range. Bracket each range on the extract before you write, and check every quotation against the bracket.
:::

---
20th-century stories often turn or reveal something near the end (1), which changes how earlier details read and is the focus of A5 (1).

::: watch
- **Line ranges are strict.** Bracket each one before writing and check every quotation against it. Only A5 may use the whole passage.
- **Read to the end before answering.** The final turn often changes what earlier details mean.
- **Don't over-write A1 and A2.** Five points and one compact paragraph respectively; the marks are in A3–A5.
- **Identify the perspective.** Saying "the writer tells us she is angry" when the anger comes through free indirect thought misses the method.
- **Infer, then justify.** A comment with no quotation, or a quotation with no comment, stays in the lower bands.
- **Terminology only with an effect.** A label alone is not analysis.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1930s novel).

> I had always supposed that Uncle Edgar liked me. He sent a postal order every birthday, signed in his careful green ink, and once he had let me hold his watch. It was only later, sorting his papers, that I found he had sent exactly the same postal order to every child in the parish.

Identify the narrative perspective and explain one effect it has here. (2)
---
First person (1). Effect, any one: the narrator's childhood belief is shown and then undercut, so we share their disappointment; the older narrator looking back ("only later") creates gentle irony about a child's misreading (1).
:::

::: test
Using the same Uncle Edgar passage, what can you infer about Uncle Edgar from the detail "every child in the parish"? (1)
---
Any one: his generosity was dutiful or impersonal rather than special to the narrator; he was orderly and methodical; he saw his giving as an obligation to the community.
:::

::: test
A question says: "Read lines 14–30." A student quotes a strong image from line 34. How much credit does the quotation receive for this question, and why? (2)
---
None (1); it lies outside the named line range, and only material within the range is credited for that question (1).
:::

::: test
Read this sentence (original, written in the style of a 1950s novel).

> Well, she was not going to cry, not in front of Mrs Archer, who would only tell the whole street by teatime.

Name the method used to present the character's thoughts and explain its effect. (2)
---
Free indirect thought (1). Effect: we hear the character's own voice ("Well", "not in front of") without speech marks, so we feel her pride and her fear of gossip from the inside (1).
:::

::: test
Give two features you would expect in a 20th-century literary extract that make inference necessary. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): character revealed through action and dialogue rather than stated; understatement or subtext; free indirect thought; symbolic objects; an ending that turns or reveals something.
:::

::: test
How long does the paper advise for reading the extract, and what should the second read focus on? (2)
---
About 10 minutes (1); annotating each question's line range for its focus, noting shifts in perspective, time or mood (1).

::: test
Read the passage below (original passage written in the style of a 1950s short story).

> The house at the end of Mill Lane had been empty since the war. Its gate hung from one hinge and the garden had grown up to the windowsills, so that nettles looked in where people had once looked out. A milk bottle, green with age, still stood on the step. Children in the lane said a woman in a grey coat could be seen at the upstairs window on wet evenings, though none of them had seen her personally.

List five things you learn about the house in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, up to five. Accept, for example: it is at the end of Mill Lane; it has been empty since the war / for many years; the gate hangs from one hinge / is broken; the garden is overgrown, up to the windowsills; nettles grow against the windows; it is neglected or uncared for; a milk bottle, green with age, is still on the step; it was abandoned suddenly (the bottle left behind); local children think it is haunted / tell stories of a woman at the upstairs window; it has an upstairs. Do not credit whole sentences copied unselectively.
:::

::: test
Read the passage below (original passage written in the style of a 1940s novel).

> Dr Mallory came into the ward as if it belonged to him, which in a sense it did. He did not look at the patients. He looked at the charts, tapping each one with a pencil as a man might tap a barometer, and spoke to the sister over their heads in a voice pitched for a lecture hall. When old Mr Pike tried to tell him about the pain in his side, the doctor smiled, said "Quite so," and moved on.

What impressions does the writer create of Dr Mallory in these lines? You must refer to the language used in the text, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (5)
---
Award a single mark out of 5 for the accuracy and perceptiveness of impressions and the range of supported comment on language.
- 5: accurate, perceptive impressions supported by a well-chosen range of evidence with precise comment on language.
- 4: accurate impressions with a thorough range of evidence and some comment on language.
- 3: some valid impressions with a range of evidence.
- 2: straightforward impressions, little support.
- 1: very limited response. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative: arrogant and proprietorial ("as if it belonged to him", simile/comparison); cold, detached ("did not look at the patients"); sees patients as data (charts, "tap a barometer" simile reduces people to readings); self-important ("voice pitched for a lecture hall"); dismissive, condescending ("over their heads", literal and figurative); superficially polite but uncaring ("Quite so" then "moved on"; the smile is empty).
:::

::: test
Read the passage below (original passage written in the style of a 1930s novel).

> All through the long service Agnes kept her eyes on the hymn book, though she knew every word. Beside her, her mother sang loudly and a little flat, as she always did, and every so often pressed Agnes's arm as though to make sure she was still there. Across the aisle sat Tom Pritchard in his brother's suit, which was too big at the shoulders. He had not looked at her once. Agnes counted the pages of the hymn book, then the panes in the east window, then the brass rings on the curtain behind the font. Twenty-two. She had known it would be twenty-two. When the vicar read the banns for Tom Pritchard and Edith Morgan, her mother's hand closed hard on her arm and stayed there, and Agnes sang the next hymn so steadily that a woman in the pew in front turned round to see who it was.

How does the writer show Agnes's thoughts and feelings in these lines? You should consider what the writer says about Agnes and the writer's use of language to show her thoughts and feelings. Refer to the text, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: thorough, perceptive exploration of Agnes's feelings, including what is held back; detailed analysis of how word choice, sentence forms and narrative perspective shape the reader's response; subtle effects explored; precise terminology.
- 7–8: detailed exploration with some insight; begins to analyse how language creates effects; accurate terminology.
- 5–6: explores feelings in some detail; some understanding of how language is used; some accurate terminology.
- 3–4: straightforward comments on some feelings; terminology may simply be named.
- 1–2: identifies and begins to comment on one or two feelings. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content: she avoids looking up ("kept her eyes on the hymn book, though she knew every word"), so she is hiding her face or avoiding Tom; the hurt of being ignored ("He had not looked at her once", short, blunt sentence); the counting sequence (hymn pages, panes, brass rings) shows a desperate attempt to control her mind and not feel; "Twenty-two. She had known it would be twenty-two." minor sentence and repetition reveal numbness and a need for certainty when everything else is uncertain; she seems to have expected the banns, which makes her composure sadder; her mother's touch ("to make sure she was still there", then "closed hard… and stayed there") shows the mother knows and fears for her; the climax is understated: she sings "so steadily" that a stranger turns, suggesting the effort is so great it becomes unnatural; free indirect style and third person limited keep us close but the narrator never names her heartbreak, so subtext carries the feeling; Tom's "brother's suit, which was too big" may suggest he is not ready for marriage or is a boy pretending to be a man, viewed through Agnes's critical, wounded eyes.
:::

::: test
Read the passage below (original passage written in the style of a 1950s short story). It is the end of a longer story in which Mrs Vaughan, a widow, has spent weeks preparing for a visit from her son, who now lives in London and has not been home for six years.

> At four o'clock the car drew up and David got out, and after him a young woman Mrs Vaughan had not been told about. "This is Claire," said David, "we thought we'd surprise you." Claire shook her hand and said what a sweet little house it was.
>
> They stayed an hour. Claire did not take sugar and did not eat cake, and David ate one slice quickly, looking at his watch over the rim of his cup. He talked about the flat, and his work, and a holiday they meant to take in Italy. Mrs Vaughan said that sounded lovely. She said it several times.
>
> When the car had gone she cleared the table. There was most of a Victoria sponge left, and the best cups, and the little dish of lemon curd she had made because he had liked it as a boy. She stood for some time with the dish in her hand. Then she put a cover over it, and set it on the top shelf of the larder, where it would keep.

"In this ending, the writer makes us feel sorry for Mrs Vaughan but also shows that she is stronger than she seems." To what extent do you agree with this view? You should write about your thoughts and feelings about how Mrs Vaughan is presented and how the writer has created these thoughts and feelings. Refer to the language used in the text. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: persuasive, sustained evaluation that weighs both parts of the statement; perceptive overview of the ending's effect; convincing, purposeful references; insight into the writer's methods.
- 7–8: critical evaluation with well-selected references; clear engagement; explores how methods create thoughts and feelings.
- 5–6: an evaluation with appropriate references; some awareness of the whole and of the writer's methods.
- 3–4: a personal opinion supported by straightforward references; limited sense of method.
- 1–2: a simple opinion with basic reference. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content, sympathy: the unannounced girlfriend ("had not been told about") and the surprise that excludes her; Claire's patronising "sweet little house"; the son's hurry ("ate one slice quickly", "looking at his watch"), after six years away; the list of their plans (flat, work, Italy) shows a life she is not part of; "She said it several times" exposes her strained politeness and loneliness; the leftover food and "best cups" show the scale of her preparation against an hour's visit; the lemon curd made "because he had liked it as a boy" shows she still sees a child who no longer exists. Strength: she is gracious and does not complain; she "stood for some time", then acts; the final action of covering and storing the dish is controlled, practical, dignified; "where it would keep" can suggest endurance, or that her love is preserved and patient; the understated, plain style refuses melodrama, which matches her restraint. Counter-view: the ending could suggest resignation or emotional shutting-away rather than strength. Best answers take a clear, qualified stance and tie each judgement to a method.
:::

::: test
Explain what is meant by subtext, using this example (original line in the style of a 1960s novel): "'Lovely weather,' said her sister-in-law, looking at the dust on the mantelpiece." (2)
---
Subtext is meaning beneath what is said (1). Here the polite remark about the weather hides criticism: the glance at the dust implies she is judging the housekeeping (1).
:::

::: test
Read this sentence (original, written in the style of a 1940s novel).

> For a long time afterwards Frank would tell people it had been the happiest summer of his life, and for a long time nobody contradicted him.

Explain how the writer creates a sense that something will go wrong. (2)
---
"For a long time afterwards" shows the narrator looking back from the future, so the summer is already over and judged (1); "nobody contradicted him" hints that others knew it was not happy, foreshadowing trouble or self-deception (1).
:::

::: test
A first-person narrator says (original line in the style of a 1960s novel): "Nobody at the office minded me leaving early; I am sure of that." Explain why a reader might treat this narrator as unreliable. (2)
---
The insistence "I am sure of that" sounds defensive, as if he is persuading himself (1); a first-person narrator can only report his own view, so the reader suspects the colleagues did mind (1).
:::
