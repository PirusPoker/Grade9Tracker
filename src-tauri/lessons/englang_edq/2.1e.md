## What it is

**A4** covers the longest section of the extract, often around thirty lines, and asks a two-part question: **"What impressions does the writer create of [a character / a relationship / a place]? How does the writer create these impressions?"** It is worth **10 marks** and tests **AO2**, but with one crucial addition: you must refer to the **language and structure** used. In the mark scheme's terms, the top bands want analysis of how language **and the organisation of events** achieve effects. An answer that never mentions structure cannot reach the top.

**What structure means in a thirty-line section.** Structure is about **order, placing and proportion**: what the writer shows first, what comes next, where the focus moves and why.

| Structural feature | What to look for | Example comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening focus** | Where the section begins: an action, a detail, a line of dialogue | "Opening on her hands rather than her face makes us read her through what she does" |
| **Shift in focus** | From one character to another, from inside to outside, from wide to close | "The focus cuts from the noisy party to the child alone on the stairs, isolating her" |
| **Turning point** | A moment where mood, power or understanding changes | "Her reply is the hinge of the section: after it he never speaks again" |
| **Shift in time** | Flashback, memory, a jump forward, a summary of years | "The flashback to their wedding sits between two silences, so the past mocks the present" |
| **Contrast and juxtaposition** | Two things placed side by side | "His long speech is followed by her one-word answer" |
| **Placing of dialogue** | When speech starts and stops; who gets the last word | "She speaks only at the end, so her words land with full weight" |
| **Paragraph and sentence length** | Pace: long paragraphs slow us down, a one-line paragraph stops us | "The single-sentence paragraph isolates the shock" |
| **Withholding** | Information delayed or never given | "We are not told what the letter says, so we read her reaction for clues" |
| **Endings that echo or reverse** | The section closes on something that mirrors or overturns its opening | "It ends where it began, at the gate, but now she is leaving" |
| **Repetition across the section** | A motif that returns | "The clock is mentioned three times, each time louder" |

**Weave, don't bolt on.** The commonest weak answer analyses language for three paragraphs and then adds: "The writer also uses structure. There is a short paragraph." That shows awareness but no understanding. Instead, make structure part of your argument about the **impressions**:

> *"Nora's indifference to Gerald is first shown structurally: the section opens with his elaborate plans for their meeting, so when the single short sentence 'She did not look up' follows, his expectations collapse in five words."*

That sentence makes a point about character, uses structure (the order of events and a short sentence) and analyses language, all at once. Two or three such moves across the answer are enough to show structure is understood.

**Relationship questions.** A4 often asks about a **relationship** between two characters. Track **power and distance**: who speaks more, who interrupts, who moves towards whom, who has the final word, and how that changes across the section. Structure is a natural tool here: the order in which characters speak and act is the relationship.

**Answering both parts of the stem.** "What impressions?" asks for **judgements** (adjectives: possessive, tender, strained, unequal). "How does the writer create them?" asks for **methods** (language and structure). Every paragraph should hold one impression and the methods that create it.

**Band shape.** The bands match A3's (1–2 up to 9–10) with "language **and the organisation of events**" in each descriptor. In practice: 5–6 shows some understanding of how language *and* structure work; 7–8 analyses both with insight; 9–10 gives accurate, perceptive impressions with detailed analysis of both and explores subtleties of technique.

**Timing:** about **12–13 minutes**. Read the section with three questions in mind: what is my overall impression, where does it change, and how is the section ordered?

::: terms
- **Structure**: the way a text is organised, including the order of events, shifts in focus and the shape of paragraphs and sentences.
- **Organisation of events**: the order and placing of what happens in a section, as the mark scheme describes structure.
- **Shift in focus**: a move of attention from one character, place, time or perspective to another.
- **Juxtaposition**: placing two contrasting things side by side to highlight the difference.
- **Flashback**: a move back in time to an earlier event within the narrative.
- **Pace**: the speed at which a story seems to move, controlled by sentence and paragraph length and by detail.
- **Withholding**: delaying or never giving information, so the reader must infer or wait.
- **Cyclical structure**: an ending that returns to the opening, often with a change of meaning.
- **Motif**: an image, object or phrase that recurs and gathers meaning.
- **Hinge**: a single moment or line on which a section turns.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the focus (who or what, or which relationship) and bracket the line range.
2. Read the section and write three or four **impression adjectives**.
3. Map the section's **structure** in a few words: how it opens, where it turns, how it ends, and any shifts of focus, time or pace.
4. Plan four or five paragraphs in **line order**, each with one impression.
5. In each paragraph: impression, short quotation, language analysis, and where it fits, a **structural** comment (why it is placed here, what it is set against, how it changes the pace).
6. Make sure at least two paragraphs contain clear structural analysis, and comment on how the section **ends**.
7. Finish with a sentence on how the section's shape leaves the reader seeing the character or relationship.
:::

::: example Weaving structure into an impression
Lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel), summary: a long paragraph describes Frank arriving home, calling out cheerfully, telling his wife about his promotion as he takes off his coat; then a one-line paragraph: *"Joyce went on peeling potatoes."*

Answer extract: The relationship seems **one-sided and strained**. Frank's speech fills a long paragraph, full of exclamations and plans, so the structure gives him all the space and noise. The writer then **juxtaposes** it with a single, flat line, "Joyce went on peeling potatoes." Placed as its own paragraph, it lands like a closed door: the verb phrase "went on" shows she has not even paused for his news. The contrast in paragraph length mirrors the imbalance of the marriage: his enthusiasm is met by a silence that says more than speech.
:::

::: example Commenting on an ending
A section about a girl preparing for her first dance ends: *"In the mirror, for a moment, she saw her mother's face."* Structural comment: the writer saves this detail for the end, where it **reframes** the whole excitement. The preparations we have watched with her now seem to repeat her mother's life, perhaps her mother's disappointments. The final position gives the line the weight of a revelation; a reader looks back over the section with a new, sadder understanding.
:::

::: example A relationship tracked through the order of events
Lines summary (original, in the style of a 1930s novel): at the start the father questions his son across the breakfast table; the son answers in single words; mid-section the son mentions he has joined up; the father puts down his cup; the section ends with the father asking, quietly, whether he has had enough to eat.

Answer extract: The relationship moves from **conflict** to a **buried tenderness**. The writer structures the opening as an interrogation, the father's long questions met by clipped replies, to show distance and resentment. The son's announcement is the **hinge**, placed mid-section and delivered without warning, which mirrors the shock. After it, the father's dialogue shrinks: "put down his cup" is the only reaction we see. The writer ends not on argument but on the small question about breakfast, a domestic concern that reveals, through understatement, the father's fear and love. The structure takes us from the father's control to his helplessness.
:::

::: q
What does A4 require that A3 does not? (1)
---
Analysis of structure (the organisation of events) as well as language.
:::

::: q
Give four structural features you could comment on in a thirty-line section. (2)
---
Any four (2 for four, 1 for two or three): opening focus; shift in focus; turning point; flashback or time shift; contrast or juxtaposition; placing of dialogue; paragraph or sentence length and pace; withholding; ending that echoes or reverses; recurring motif.
:::

::: q
What is meant by "weaving" structure into your answer? (2)
---
Making structural comments part of each argument about an impression (1) rather than adding a separate, general paragraph about structure at the end (1).
:::

::: q
What two parts does the A4 question stem have, and what does each require? (2)
---
"What impressions…?" requires judgements about the character, relationship or place (1); "How does the writer create these impressions?" requires analysis of methods, both language and structure (1).
:::

::: q
In a relationship question, what can structure show about power? (2)
---
Who speaks more, who interrupts, who acts first or has the final word (1); changes in this order across the section show power shifting between the characters (1).
:::

::: q
Why is the end of a section often worth a structural comment? (2)
---
Its final position gives a detail extra weight (1), and an ending that echoes or reverses the opening can reframe how we see the character or relationship (1).
:::

::: watch
- **No structure, no top band.** The question says language and structure; both must appear.
- **Feature-spotting structure** ("there are short paragraphs") earns little. Say why it is placed there and what it does to the impression.
- **Answer the impressions part.** Analysing methods with no clear judgement about the character loses focus.
- **Use the whole section.** Thirty lines: cover beginning, middle and end, and especially the turn.
- **Don't confuse structure with language.** A simile is language; where the simile is placed, and what it is set against, is structure.
- **Keep to time.** 12–13 minutes, then A5.
:::

::: test
Explain the structural effect of placing a one-sentence paragraph, "Nobody answered.", after a long paragraph in which a girl knocks and calls at her friend's door. (2)
---
The short paragraph contrasts sharply with the long one, stopping the pace (1); isolating the silence makes it feel heavy and ominous, emphasising the girl's rejection or worry (1).
:::

::: test
A section begins with a mother laughing with her daughter at a fair and ends with the mother alone, counting coins at the kitchen table. Explain one effect of this structure. (2)
---
The movement from shared joy to solitary worry is a contrast that reveals what the cheerfulness hides (1); ending on the coins leaves the reader with the mother's hidden poverty or sacrifice, making her more sympathetic (1).
:::

::: test
Why might a writer withhold what is written in a letter a character receives? (2)
---
It creates suspense or mystery (1) and makes the reader focus on the character's reaction, inferring the news from it, which reveals character (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a flashback placed in the middle of a quarrel between two old friends might affect the reader's impression of their relationship. (3)
---
The flashback interrupts the present, slowing the pace (1); a memory of earlier closeness contrasts with the current anger (1), so the reader sees what has been lost, making the quarrel sadder and the relationship more complex (1).
:::

::: test
Turn this bolt-on comment into an integrated one: "The writer also uses structure because the dialogue is at the end." (3)
---
For example: "The writer holds back the dialogue until the end of the section (1), so after a long, silent description of his waiting her first words, 'I'm leaving,' strike with full force (1), which shows how little power he has in the relationship (1)."
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel).

> Every Sunday Grandad Pugh walked to the chapel at a quarter to ten, in his good black suit, with Dilys holding his hand. He walked slowly and stopped to speak to everyone, and everyone stopped to speak to him. Mrs Jenkins at the shop gave Dilys a barley sugar. The minister shook his hand at the door as if it were a privilege. Inside, Grandad sang the bass line in a voice that seemed to come up out of the floor, and Dilys, small beside him, felt it through the pew and was proud.
>
> It was in the summer that she was eleven that he began to forget the words. At first he hummed through the gaps, and nobody noticed but Dilys. Then he stopped singing altogether, and stood with the hymn book open at the wrong page, looking at it as if it were written in another language. Once, outside, he called her by her mother's name.
>
> The next Sunday it rained. Dilys put on her coat and waited in the hall at a quarter to ten, and Grandad came out of the kitchen in his slippers and his cardigan and looked at her, puzzled and polite, as if she were a visitor who had called at an awkward time.
>
> "Not today, bach," he said. "I don't think we'll bother today."
>
> She took off her coat and hung it on its peg. Through the window she could hear the chapel bell ringing down in the village, and it went on ringing for a long time.

What impressions does the writer create of Grandad Pugh and of his relationship with Dilys in these lines? How does the writer create these impressions? You must refer to the language and structure used in the text, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: accurate, perceptive impressions; detailed analysis of how both language and the organisation of events create them; subtleties explored (e.g. the reversal of roles, the ending's understatement); precise terminology.
- 7–8: impressions explored with detail and insight; begins to analyse both language and structure; accurate terminology.
- 5–6: impressions explored in some detail; some understanding of how language and structure are used; some accurate terminology.
- 3–4: straightforward comments on some impressions; terms may be named.
- 1–2: identifies and begins to comment on one or two features. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content, impressions: Grandad as respected, dignified, a pillar of the community (everyone stops, minister acts "as if it were a privilege"); his strength and presence (bass voice "up out of the floor", hyperbole); a loving, proud relationship (holding hands, Dilys "felt it through the pew and was proud"); his decline into confusion or dementia (forgetting words, wrong page, "another language" simile); the painful reversal: she now notices and protects him ("nobody noticed but Dilys"); final distance and loss (he looks at her "as if she were a visitor", polite but not recognising; slippers and cardigan replace the "good black suit").
Structure: the first paragraph establishes ritual and repetition ("Every Sunday", habitual past) so the change is felt as a break; the time marker "in the summer that she was eleven" is a turning point; decline is ordered in stages ("At first… Then… Once…"), building gradually; the third paragraph echoes the first (a quarter to ten, the hall, the clothes) to show what has gone; the dialogue is held back until near the end, and is brief and gentle ("bach"), making his withdrawal quiet rather than dramatic; the final paragraph reverses the first (coat taken off, not put on); ending on the bell "ringing for a long time" without them: the community continues, and the sound becomes an image of loss and of time passing.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1930s novel).

> Mrs Thorne interviewed the new maid in the drawing room, standing by the fireplace, while the girl stood on the carpet in the middle of the room where there was nothing to hold on to. Mrs Thorne asked her age, her religion, her previous place and the reason she had left it, and wrote down each answer in a small leather book without looking up. The girl's answers grew shorter and shorter. When she was asked whether she had a young man, she went red to the roots of her hair and said, "No, madam," and Mrs Thorne wrote that down too.
>
> Then Mrs Thorne explained the house. The girl would rise at six. She would black the grates before breakfast, and on no account would she be seen on the front stairs. Visitors were not permitted. Followers were not permitted. Singing was not permitted, except on Sundays, and then only hymns.
>
> "Have you any questions?" said Mrs Thorne, closing the book.
>
> The girl looked at the window, where a thrush was hopping along the sill outside, quite free, and then back at the carpet.
>
> "No, madam," she said.

What impressions does the writer create of the relationship between Mrs Thorne and the new maid in these lines? How does the writer create these impressions? You must refer to the language and structure used in the text, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands as for all A4 questions (9–10 perceptive impressions and detailed analysis of language and the organisation of events, subtleties explored; 7–8 detail and insight, beginning to analyse both; 5–6 some understanding of both; 3–4 straightforward; 1–2 begins to comment).
Indicative content, impressions: an unequal, cold, controlling relationship; Mrs Thorne is powerful, impersonal, intrusive; the maid is vulnerable, intimidated, powerless, losing her voice.
Language: positioning ("standing by the fireplace" versus "the middle of the room where there was nothing to hold on to", vulnerability made physical); the list of questions shows interrogation; "without looking up" shows indifference; "wrote that down too" treats even her private life as a record; the blush shows embarrassment and shame; the rules are delivered in short, repetitive sentences ("not permitted… not permitted… not permitted"), a relentless list stripping away freedoms, with the final exception ("only hymns") almost comic in its strictness; "madam" stresses hierarchy.
Structure: the section is organised as an interview in which Mrs Thorne holds all the speech; "The girl's answers grew shorter and shorter" tracks the maid shrinking; the second paragraph moves from questions to commands, power increasing; "Have you any questions?" offers a voice only after every freedom has gone, so it is hollow; the shift of focus to the thrush "quite free" juxtaposes the bird's freedom with her confinement (symbolism placed just before her answer); the ending repeats "No, madam", a cyclical echo that shows nothing has changed and that she has accepted her silence; short final paragraph leaves the reader with her resignation.
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between a language comment and a structural comment on this detail: a section ends with "The door clicked shut." (2)
---
Language: the quiet onomatopoeic "clicked" suggests finality without drama (1). Structure: placing it as the last line of the section closes the scene and leaves the character shut out, ending on separation (1).
:::

::: test
In a section, a son speaks only once, at the very end, after his parents have argued about him for twenty lines. What impression might this structure create? (2)
---
He seems excluded, powerless or ignored while others decide for him (1); holding his single line back to the end gives it great weight, so it may reveal his true feelings or overturn their argument (1).
:::

::: test
What is a cyclical ending, and what effect can it have on a reader's impression of a character? (2)
---
An ending that returns to the opening's image or situation (1); it can show that nothing has changed (entrapment), or that the same situation now means something different because the character has changed (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how sentence length can control pace in a narrative section. (2)
---
Long, flowing sentences slow the pace, allowing detail or calm (1); short sentences speed it up or stop it abruptly, creating tension, shock or emphasis (1).
:::

::: test
Read this outline (original, in the style of a 1960s short story): a section opens with a wide view of a busy station concourse, narrows to one bench, and ends on a close-up of an unclaimed suitcase. Explain how the structure shapes the reader's impression. (3)
---
The movement from wide to close narrows the focus like a camera (1), isolating the bench from the crowd and creating a sense of loneliness or abandonment (1); ending on the unclaimed suitcase leaves an unanswered question, creating mystery or unease about its owner (1).
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1940s novel).

> The two brothers had not spoken for eleven years, and now they sat on either side of their mother's bed. The ward was quiet. A nurse came and went. Robert, the elder, read the newspaper from the first page to the last, folding it with exaggerated care at each turn. Alun looked out of the window at the hospital car park and counted the cars. Between them their mother slept, her hand open on the blanket.
>
> At three o'clock she woke, and looked from one to the other, and smiled as if nothing had happened in eleven years at all. "There you are, boys," she said. "Have you had your tea?"
>
> Neither of them answered. Then Robert put down the paper. Alun turned from the window. And because she was looking at them, and because there was nothing else to do, they each took one of her hands.

What impressions does the writer create of the brothers' relationship in these lines? How does the writer create these impressions? You must refer to the language and structure used in the text, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands as for A4 (1–2 to 9–10; top bands need detailed analysis of both language and the organisation of events).
Indicative content: estrangement, stubbornness and awkwardness ("not spoken for eleven years", "on either side", physical division); avoidance shown through displacement activities (Robert's newspaper folded "with exaggerated care", a performance of indifference; Alun counting cars); short sentences "The ward was quiet. A nurse came and went." create tense stillness; the mother placed "between them", physically and emotionally the link; her "hand open" an image of welcome. Structure: the first paragraph is static and divided, alternating between the brothers; the mother's waking at "three o'clock" is the turning point; her dialogue, treating them as "boys", reverses time and ignores the quarrel, creating poignancy and gentle irony; "Neither of them answered." stalls the moment; then a sequence of short parallel sentences ("Robert put down the paper. Alun turned from the window.") mirrors them moving together for the first time; the final sentence's repetition "because… because…" shows reluctance and necessity; the ending, each holding one hand, joins them through her, suggesting a fragile, unspoken reconciliation rather than a resolved one.
:::

::: test
Why can an A4 answer that analyses language well but never mentions structure not reach the top band? (2)
---
The question requires language and structure, and the top bands describe analysis of both (1); without comment on the organisation of events the answer misses half the task, however good the language analysis (1).
:::
