## What it is

**A5** is the last reading question in Component 1, worth **10 marks**, and the only one that tests **AO4**: evaluating texts critically and supporting your judgement with textual references. It always asks you to read the **final section** of the extract and **consider the passage as a whole**. It gives you a **statement**, presented as a reader's view, and asks **"To what extent do you agree?"** or **"How far do you agree with this view?"** Two bullets usually follow: write about **your thoughts and feelings** about how a character (or situation) is presented, and **how the writer has created** those thoughts and feelings.

**What a statement looks like.** Statements are deliberately arguable, often with **two parts** or a strong word you can push against:
- "In this ending, Jim is presented as a selfish man who deserves no sympathy."
- "The writer makes the ending both surprising and satisfying."
- "Elsie is a weak character who is controlled by everyone around her."

Look for the **strong words** ("no sympathy", "both", "everyone") and the **two halves**. Most good answers agree with some of it and challenge some of it.

**Evaluation is not analysis, and not retelling.** A3 and A4 ask *how* the writer creates effects. A5 asks *how well* and *how far*: you judge the statement, the character and the ending, and you **justify** that judgement by pointing to the writer's methods. The commonest weak answer simply retells the ending ("Then Jim goes home and finds the letter…"). Every paragraph needs:

> **Judgement** (how far I agree, and why) + **evidence** (short quotation or precise reference) + **method** (how the writer makes me think or feel this) + **effect on me as a reader**.

**Taking a stance.** Decide your position in the first sentence and sustain it. The safest strong stance is **qualified**: *"I largely agree that Jim is presented as selfish, but by the ending the writer complicates our response so that some sympathy is unavoidable."* A one-sided answer can score well if it is persuasive, but considering the other side and then reaching a judgement shows the "critical" thinking the top bands reward. Avoid sitting on the fence for the whole answer: "some people might agree, others might not" is not a stance.

**Using the whole passage.** The question names the final lines, but it also says *consider the passage as a whole*. Use the ending as your centre and reach back: how does the ending **change** our view of earlier events? What did earlier sections set up that the ending **pays off** or **reverses**? This is where your two reads at the start of the exam pay off.

**Recognising different responses.** The spec asks you to recognise that readers may respond differently. Phrases like *"A reader might at first feel…, but…"*, *"It could be argued that…; however, the writer's choice of… suggests…"* and *"On a first reading… but by the end…"* show this naturally.

**What the bands reward.** In plain terms:

| Band | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | A **persuasive**, sustained evaluation with an **overview** of the whole text; convincing, well-selected references; perceptive comments on how the writer creates thoughts and feelings, explored with insight |
| 7–8 | A **critical** evaluation with well-selected references; clear engagement; explores how the writer creates thoughts and feelings |
| 5–6 | An evaluation with appropriate references; **some** awareness of the whole text and of the writer's methods |
| 3–4 | A **personal opinion** with straightforward references; limited sense of the whole or of method |
| 1–2 | A simple opinion with basic reference |

The key words to aim at: **sustained**, **coherent**, **persuasive**, **overview**.

**A twelve-minute plan.**
- 1 minute: underline the strong words and two halves; decide your stance.
- 1 minute: pick four or five references (at least two from the final lines, one or two from earlier).
- 10 minutes: write an opening stance sentence, three or four evaluative paragraphs, and a one-sentence conclusion that answers "to what extent" directly.

::: terms
- **Evaluation**: judging how far a view is valid, or how effective a text is, and justifying the judgement.
- **Stance**: the position you take on the statement, such as "I largely agree".
- **Qualified judgement**: a stance that agrees in part and limits or challenges another part.
- **Overview**: a sense of the passage as a whole, including how the ending relates to what came before.
- **Sustained**: kept consistent and developed throughout the answer.
- **Critical**: questioning and weighing, rather than simply accepting or describing.
- **Retelling**: summarising events without judgement; it earns little credit in A5.
- **Reader response**: the thoughts and feelings a text creates in a reader, which may differ between readers.
- **Ambiguity**: openness to more than one interpretation.
- **Pay-off**: a moment late in a story that fulfils something set up earlier.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **strong words** and any **two halves** in the statement.
2. Decide a clear, preferably **qualified** stance and write it as your first sentence.
3. Select four or five references: at least two from the named final lines and one or two from earlier in the passage.
4. Write three or four paragraphs. Each one opens with a judgement, gives evidence, names the writer's method, and explains its effect on you as a reader.
5. Include at least one "however" paragraph that weighs the other side, then show why your stance still holds.
6. Show overview: say how the ending changes or confirms your response to the whole passage.
7. End with one sentence that answers "to what extent" directly.
:::

::: example A strong opening stance
Statement: *"In this ending, Mr Price is presented as a cruel man who deserves no sympathy."*

Opening: "I agree that the writer presents Mr Price's treatment of his daughter as cruel, but I disagree that he deserves *no* sympathy: the ending reveals a loneliness that makes him pitiable as well as harsh." This takes a position on both parts ("cruel", "no sympathy"), signals a qualified judgement and sets up the structure of the answer.
:::

::: example Rewriting a retelling as evaluation
Retelling: "At the end Mr Price sits alone in the kitchen and looks at the photograph of his wife."

Evaluation: "The ending makes it hard to withhold sympathy completely. The writer leaves Mr Price alone in the kitchen with the photograph of his wife, and the shift to a quiet, slow description after the shouting of the earlier scene invites us to see his anger as grief that has nowhere to go. This does not excuse his cruelty, but it complicates it." The same detail now carries a judgement, a method (shift in pace and focus), an effect and a link to the whole.
:::

::: example Showing overview
"The final line, in which Doris finally laughs, is surprising only on a first reading. Looking back, the writer has prepared for it: the two moments earlier where she 'almost smiled' at her brother's jokes show that her coldness was a defence. The ending therefore feels earned rather than sudden, which is why I find it satisfying." This links the ending to earlier details and evaluates its effectiveness: overview in action.
:::

::: example Weighing both sides in one paragraph
"It could be argued that Elsie is weak, since she agrees to every demand her mother makes and her replies are reduced to 'Yes, Mother'. However, the writer's choice to end with Elsie quietly posting the application form, an action given its own paragraph, suggests a hidden strength. Her compliance is a mask, and the final image persuades me that the statement underestimates her."
:::

::: q
Which assessment objective does A5 test, and what does it mean? (2)
---
AO4 (1): evaluating texts critically and supporting judgements with appropriate textual references (1).
:::

::: q
What part of the extract does A5 ask you to use? (2)
---
The named final lines (1), while also considering the passage as a whole (1).
:::

::: q
Why does retelling score poorly in A5? (2)
---
It describes what happens without judging the statement (1) or explaining how the writer creates the reader's thoughts and feelings (1).
:::

::: q
What is a qualified stance? Give an example opening. (2)
---
Agreeing with part of a statement while limiting or challenging another part (1). E.g. "I largely agree that she is presented as selfish, but by the end the writer makes us understand why." (1)
:::

::: q
Give two phrases that show you recognise different responses to a text. (2)
---
Any two, e.g. "A reader might at first feel…, but…"; "It could be argued that…; however…"; "On a first reading… but by the end…".
:::

::: q
What four things should each A5 paragraph contain? (2)
---
A judgement on the statement; evidence (quotation or reference) (1); the writer's method; its effect on you as a reader (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Retelling is the biggest mark-loser.** Every paragraph must open with a judgement.
- **Answer "to what extent".** Say how far you agree, at the start and at the end.
- **Use the ending and the whole.** Ignoring earlier sections loses overview; ignoring the ending misses the focus.
- **Method matters.** "I feel sorry for her" needs "because the writer…".
- **Don't sit on the fence.** Weigh both sides, then decide.
- **Watch the strong words.** "No sympathy", "completely", "both" are where to push back.
:::

::: test
Statement: "Arthur is presented as a completely selfish character." Which word in the statement offers the clearest opportunity to disagree, and why? (2)
---
"completely" (1): it is absolute, so any moment of kindness, regret or vulnerability in the text lets you qualify the statement (1).
:::

::: test
Rewrite this retelling as an evaluative sentence: "At the end, Lily tears up the letter and goes out into the garden." (3)
---
For example: "The ending persuades me that Lily is stronger than the statement suggests (1): the decisive verb 'tears' shows her rejecting the letter's demands (1), and the move out into the open garden suggests release, so I am left admiring rather than pitying her (1)."
:::

::: test
Statement: "The writer makes the reader feel sorry for Mrs Dyer." Read this ending (original, in the style of a 1950s short story): "Mrs Dyer waved until the bus had turned the corner, then lowered her hand slowly, as if it had become very heavy." Give a stance and one piece of evidence with its effect. (3)
---
Stance, e.g. "I agree" (1); evidence "lowered her hand slowly, as if it had become very heavy" (1); the simile makes her sadness physical, suggesting grief or loneliness after the departure, so the reader feels pity (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what "overview" means in an A5 answer and why it matters. (2)
---
Showing awareness of the passage as a whole, e.g. how the ending changes or confirms earlier impressions (1); it is a feature of the top band, showing perceptive, critical evaluation rather than comment on isolated details (1).
:::

::: test
A student writes: "Some people might agree with the statement and some might not." Why will this not gain much credit, and how could it be improved? (2)
---
It avoids taking a stance and gives no evidence or method (1); improve by stating a clear, qualified judgement with reasons, e.g. "I largely agree because…, although…" (1).
:::

::: test
Read the passage below. It is the end of a longer story (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel). Earlier, Stan, a proud allotment-keeper, has spent the summer competing with his neighbour Ivor to grow the biggest marrow for the village show, sabotaging Ivor's water butt one night.

> The judges came round at eleven. Stan stood beside his marrow with his arms folded, and Ivor stood beside his, two tables away, and neither looked at the other. Stan's was the bigger. Anyone could see it was the bigger. The judges weighed it, and wrote something down, and moved on to Ivor's, and weighed that, and wrote something down, and conferred in low voices with their backs turned.
>
> The red card went on Ivor's marrow.
>
> Stan did not hear what the judge said about "uniformity" and "the condition of the skin". He was looking at Ivor, who had turned quite pale and was holding the card as if it might break. Ivor's wife had died at Easter. Stan had forgotten that; or not forgotten it, exactly, but put it somewhere he did not have to look at it.
>
> Afterwards, in the beer tent, people came up to shake Ivor's hand. Stan waited until they had gone. Then he went over, and stood for a while, and said, "Well. It was a good marrow." It was the hardest sentence he had ever spoken. Ivor nodded, and after a moment pushed his pint across the table, and Stan sat down.
>
> Neither of them mentioned the water butt. Neither of them, as far as anyone knows, ever did.

"In this ending, the writer shows that Stan gets exactly what he deserves." To what extent do you agree with this view? You should write about your thoughts and feelings about how Stan is presented in this ending and in the story as a whole, and how the writer has created these thoughts and feelings. You must refer to the language used in the text to support your answer. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: persuasive, sustained evaluation with a clear overview; convincing, well-selected references; perceptive exploration of how the writer creates thoughts and feelings.
- 7–8: critical evaluation with well-selected references; clear engagement; explores methods.
- 5–6: an evaluation with appropriate references; some awareness of the whole and of methods.
- 3–4: a personal opinion with straightforward references; limited interaction with the whole or with method.
- 1–2: a simple opinion with basic reference. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content, agree: his sabotage earlier makes losing feel just; his pride ("arms folded", "Anyone could see it was the bigger", repetition shows arrogance) is punished; the one-line paragraph "The red card went on Ivor's marrow." delivers the reversal bluntly, a satisfying comeuppance; the judges' criteria ("uniformity", "condition of the skin") suggest size is not everything, an irony aimed at Stan.
Disagree / qualify: the ending gives him more than punishment: it offers moral growth; his realisation about Ivor's grief ("put it somewhere he did not have to look at it") shows guilt and self-knowledge; "the hardest sentence he had ever spoken" shows humility and courage; Ivor's pint "pushed across the table" brings forgiveness and friendship, more than he deserves, perhaps; the silence about the water butt is ambiguous: an unpunished secret, or a shared understanding; the gentle, understated tone and comic opening (the rivalry described like a duel) mean the reader warms to him. A persuasive answer might argue he gets what he needs rather than what he deserves, or that he loses the prize but wins something better.
:::

::: test
Read the passage below. It is the end of a longer story (original passage written in the style of a 1930s novel). Earlier, Clara, a shy typist, has been invited by her glamorous employer, Mrs Fane, to a grand dinner party, where she has been ignored all evening.

> When the ladies withdrew, Clara found herself alone at the end of the long drawing room, holding a coffee cup she did not want. Mrs Fane's laugh rose and fell somewhere among the cushions. Nobody looked her way. She studied a painting of a dead pheasant until she knew every feather.
>
> It was then that the old man came in from the terrace, the one who had sat at the very bottom of the table and had not been introduced. He came straight across the room to her, as if there were no one else in it, and said, "You're the only person here who has looked at that picture properly. My wife painted it." They talked about the pheasant, and then about his wife, who had died, and then about a great many things, and Clara forgot to hold her cup properly and spilt a little coffee on the carpet, and neither of them minded.
>
> At eleven Mrs Fane swept over to say the car was waiting. "I hope you weren't too dreadfully bored, Miss Gray," she said, and did not wait for an answer.
>
> In the car Clara looked out at the wet black streets and smiled, and did not explain to anyone why.

"The ending of this story is too neat and unconvincing: Clara's lonely evening is simply rescued by luck." To what extent do you agree with this view? You should write about your thoughts and feelings about how the ending is presented and how the writer has created these thoughts and feelings. You must refer to the language used in the text to support your answer. (10)
---
Bands as for all A5 questions:
- 9–10: persuasive, sustained evaluation with overview; convincing references; perceptive, insightful exploration of methods.
- 7–8: critical evaluation; well-selected references; explores methods.
- 5–6: evaluation with appropriate references; some awareness of whole and methods.
- 3–4: personal opinion with straightforward references.
- 1–2: simple opinion, basic reference. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content, agree: the rescue arrives suddenly and by chance ("It was then"); the old man's attention is idealised ("as if there were no one else in it"); the coincidence of his wife painting the picture Clara happened to study may feel contrived; the final smile is a tidy resolution.
Disagree: the ending is prepared for: Clara's careful attention to the painting ("until she knew every feather") is a character trait (observant, sincere) that earns the connection, so it is not pure luck; the old man is also an outsider ("very bottom of the table", "had not been introduced"), so their meeting pairs two overlooked people, which feels true; the spilt coffee and "neither of them minded" is a small, realistic detail of relaxed happiness; the writer avoids neatness in places: Mrs Fane remains thoughtless ("did not wait for an answer"), nothing changes socially, and Clara "did not explain to anyone why", a private, understated ending rather than a fairy-tale one; contrast between the "wet black streets" and her smile keeps the world bleak while her feeling changes. Strong answers may judge that the coincidence is slightly convenient but the restraint of the ending makes it convincing.
:::

::: test
Read the passage below. It is the end of a longer story (original passage written in the style of a 1960s short story). Earlier, fifteen-year-old Kevin has been told by his father that the family is leaving their village for a new town, and has spent the story angrily refusing to pack.

> On the last morning the van came at seven. Kevin watched from his bedroom window as the men carried out the kitchen table, the wardrobe, his mother's sewing machine, and the dresser that had been his grandmother's, which took four of them and a great deal of swearing. The house looked smaller with every load.
>
> His father came up and stood in the doorway. He did not tell Kevin to hurry. He looked round the empty room, at the pale square on the wallpaper where the football poster had been, and said, "I was born in the back bedroom. Did you know that?"
>
> Kevin had not known that.
>
> They went downstairs together. At the gate his father stopped and put his hand flat against the gatepost, just for a second, the way you might touch a horse. Then he got into the van without looking back, and Kevin, who had meant to sulk all the way to the new town, found that he had taken the seat beside him.

"In this ending, the writer shows that Kevin's father understands his son's feelings better than Kevin realised." How far do you agree with this view? You should write about your thoughts and feelings about how the father and son are presented, and how the writer has created these thoughts and feelings. You must refer to the language used in the text to support your answer. (10)
---
Bands as for A5 (9–10 persuasive, sustained, overview, insight into method; 7–8 critical, well-selected references, explores method; 5–6 evaluation, some awareness; 3–4 personal opinion, straightforward references; 1–2 simple opinion).
Indicative content, agree: the father's restraint ("did not tell Kevin to hurry") shows sensitivity; his question about being born in the back bedroom reveals he too is losing a home, a shared grief; the short paragraph "Kevin had not known that." marks Kevin's realisation; the gatepost touched "the way you might touch a horse", a tender simile, shows the father's private love for the place; the final sentence shows Kevin drawn to sit beside him, suggesting understanding has passed between them.
Qualify or challenge: the father does not mention Kevin's feelings directly; perhaps he is revealing his own feelings rather than understanding Kevin's; he gets into the van "without looking back", which could seem hard or determined; the reconciliation is wordless and unspoken, so it could be argued the understanding is mutual rather than one-sided, or that it is Kevin who learns to understand his father. Methods: the list of furniture leaving and "The house looked smaller with every load" build loss; "pale square on the wallpaper" is a trace of the past; dialogue held back to one line gives it weight; understatement throughout. Best answers sustain a judgement, e.g. the father understands because he shares the loss, and the ending shows Kevin discovering this.
:::
