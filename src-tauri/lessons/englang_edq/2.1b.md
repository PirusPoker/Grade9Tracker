## What it is

**A1** is the first question in Component 1 Section A and the quickest five marks on the paper. It always takes the same shape: **"Read lines 1–[about 6]. List five things you learn about [a character / a place / a situation] in these lines."** It tests **AO1**: identifying and interpreting **explicit** and **implicit** information.

**How it is marked.** One mark for each valid point or inference, up to **five**. There are no levels and no marks for style. The mark scheme lists a dozen or so acceptable points, and anything else that is true and drawn from the lines is credited too. What scores nothing:
- a point from **outside the line range**;
- a point that is **not about the focus** (the question asks about the father, you list something about the kitchen);
- **unselective copying**: writing out whole sentences and leaving the examiner to find the point;
- the **same point twice** in different words;
- **speculation** the text does not support.

**Explicit and implicit.** An explicit point is stated: "she was tall". An implicit point is inferred from evidence: "she wore a silk scarf" suggests she is wealthy or takes care over her appearance. Both are credited equally, so you do not need to hunt for clever inferences, but inferences are often how you find five distinct points in a short section.

**What counts as a separate point?** Each point should tell the examiner something **different** about the focus.

| Lines say | Separate points? |
|---|---|
| "He wore a black coat and black gloves." | One point (dressed in black) or two (black coat; black gloves). Two is usually accepted as they are separate details, but safer to use a different detail for your fifth point |
| "She was tall and thin." | Two points: tall; thin |
| "The cottage was small, damp and cold." | Three points: small; damp; cold |
| "He was rich. He had plenty of money." | One point said twice |

**Fair inference versus speculation.** An inference must be what the detail **reasonably suggests**.

| Detail | Fair inference | Speculation (no mark) |
|---|---|---|
| "her hands were red and rough" | she does manual work / housework | she is a washerwoman in a mill |
| "he checked his watch twice" | he is in a hurry or anxious | he is late for a secret meeting |
| "the curtains had not been opened for weeks" | the house is neglected / someone is ill or withdrawn | the owner is dead |

**Short quotation or own words?** Either works. A short quotation ("a warm smile") or a brief paraphrase ("she seems friendly") both score. What fails is the whole sentence copied out. A good habit: **numbered list, one short line each**, five to six points.

**Should you write six?** One extra point is a sensible insurance policy in case one is judged repeated or off-focus. Writing ten is a waste of time.

**Timing.** About **five minutes**. Do not analyse language here; that begins in A2.

::: terms
- **Explicit information**: information stated directly in the text.
- **Implicit information**: information suggested by details rather than stated; it must be inferred.
- **Inference**: a reasonable conclusion drawn from evidence in the text.
- **Speculation**: a guess that goes beyond what the text supports; it gains no credit.
- **Unselective copying**: writing out whole sentences instead of picking out the relevant detail; it gains no credit.
- **Focus**: the person, place or thing the question asks about, such as "the old man" or "the town".
- **Line range**: the lines named in the question, here usually the first five or six lines.
- **Paraphrase**: putting a detail into your own words without changing its meaning.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **focus** in the question (who or what?) and bracket the **line range** on the extract.
2. Read the lines once, then go through them again, underlining every detail about the focus. Aim for seven or eight.
3. Choose five or six **distinct** points: mix explicit details with fair inferences.
4. Write a numbered list, one short line each, as a brief quotation or your own words.
5. Check: every point inside the range, every point about the focus, no point repeated, no whole sentences copied.
6. Stop at five minutes and move on to A2.
:::

::: example Five points from six lines
Lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel): *"Mrs Lewis had kept the post office in Pengarth for twenty years and knew everybody's business. She was a small, quick woman with spectacles on a chain, and she wrote every parcel's weight in a ledger with a pencil she sharpened twice a day. She did not approve of the new bus service."*

Question: List five things you learn about Mrs Lewis.

1. She has run the village post office for twenty years. (1)
2. She knows everyone's business / is nosy. (1)
3. She is small and quick. (1)
4. She wears spectacles on a chain. (1)
5. She is precise and orderly: she records every parcel and sharpens her pencil twice a day. (1)
6. She dislikes change (disapproves of the new bus service). (insurance point)

Point 5 is an inference from two details. Point 3 might be split into two, but using a different detail for the next point is safer.
:::

::: example Turning copied sentences into points
Weak: "1. Mrs Lewis had kept the post office in Pengarth for twenty years and knew everybody's business." This is a whole sentence. An examiner may credit nothing, because the candidate has not selected.

Strong: "1. She has kept the post office for twenty years. 2. She knows everyone's business." The same material, selected, now earns two marks instead of possibly none.
:::

::: example Inference from a place
Lines (original, in the style of a 1930s novel): *"The station at Coldharbour consisted of a single platform, a bench with one slat missing and a lamp that nobody lit. The timetable in its glass case was four years out of date."*

Question: List five things you learn about the station.
1. It has only one platform (it is small). (1)
2. It has a broken bench / a slat is missing. (1)
3. The lamp is never lit, so it is dark at night. (1)
4. The timetable is four years out of date. (1)
5. It is neglected / few trains or passengers use it (inferred from the broken bench, unlit lamp and old timetable). (1)
:::

::: example Staying on focus
Question: List five things you learn about **Arthur**. Lines: *"Arthur's mother kept a spotless kitchen. Arthur himself left boot-prints on it every evening, whistling, and was forgiven."* "His mother kept a spotless kitchen" is about the **mother**, so it scores nothing for Arthur. Points about Arthur: he wears boots / does outdoor work; he is careless or untidy; he whistles, so seems cheerful or carefree; he is forgiven, so he is loved or indulged.
:::

::: q
How many marks is A1 worth, and how are they awarded? (2)
---
Five marks (1); one mark for each valid point or inference about the focus, to a maximum of five (1).
:::

::: q
What is the difference between an explicit and an implicit point? (2)
---
An explicit point is stated directly (1); an implicit point is inferred from details that suggest it (1).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons a point might receive no mark in A1. (2)
---
Any two: outside the line range; not about the focus; a whole sentence copied unselectively; a repeat of an earlier point; unsupported speculation.
:::

::: q
"He kept his collar turned up although it was not raining." Give a fair inference and an example of speculation. (2)
---
Fair inference: he wants to hide his face or not be recognised / he is cold or self-conscious (1). Speculation: e.g. he is a wanted criminal on the run (1).
:::

::: q
How long should you spend on A1? (1)
---
About five minutes.
:::

::: q
Why might you write six points rather than five? (1)
---
As insurance, in case one point is judged repeated, off-focus or outside the line range.
:::

::: watch
- **Copying whole sentences** scores nothing: select and shorten.
- **Check the focus.** A detail about someone else in the same lines does not count.
- **Check the range.** Points from line 8 when the question says lines 1–6 are wasted.
- **Do not repeat a point** in different words ("rich", "had lots of money").
- **Infer fairly.** Say what the detail suggests, not a story you have invented around it.
- **No language analysis.** It earns nothing extra here and costs time you need for A3–A5.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel).

> Gwen Rowlands was nineteen and worked on the cosmetics counter at Hopkins's, where she wore a pink overall and an expression of patient boredom. She saved half her wages in a tin under the bed. On Saturdays she went to the pictures alone, because none of her friends would sit through anything with subtitles. She meant to live in Paris one day, and was learning French from a book borrowed from the library.

List five things you learn about Gwen in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, maximum five. Accept, for example: she is nineteen; she works on the cosmetics counter at Hopkins's (a shop); she wears a pink overall; she is bored by her job; she is patient; she saves half her wages / is careful with money; she keeps her savings in a tin under her bed; she goes to the pictures on Saturdays; she goes alone; she likes foreign films with subtitles; her tastes differ from her friends'; she is independent; she wants to live in Paris / is ambitious; she is learning French from a library book; she is determined or self-improving. No credit for whole sentences copied.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1930s novel).

> The farmhouse stood a mile from the road at the end of a rutted track. Its walls were whitewashed, though not recently, and a pair of geese patrolled the yard with the air of policemen. Smoke rose from only one of the four chimneys. In the porch hung a row of oilskins and a shotgun with a rag tied round the barrel.

List five things you learn about the farmhouse in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, maximum five. Accept: it is a mile from the road / isolated; it is reached by a rutted (rough, poorly kept) track; its walls are whitewashed; the whitewash is old / it has not been maintained recently; there are geese in the yard; the geese act as guards; it has four chimneys / is a fair size; only one fire is lit, so few people live there or rooms are unused / money is short; oilskins hang in the porch, so people work outdoors in bad weather; there is a shotgun in the porch; the gun suggests shooting or protection / farming life. Do not credit whole sentences.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1960s short story).

> My father came home from the docks at six exactly, every night of my childhood. He hung his cap on the third hook, never the second, and washed his hands for a long time at the kitchen sink. He did not talk about his work. He read the evening paper from the back page forwards, and if anyone spoke to him before he reached the front, he did not hear them.

List five things you learn about the narrator's father in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, maximum five. Accept: he works at the docks; he comes home at six exactly / is punctual; he is a creature of habit (third hook, never the second); he washes his hands for a long time (his work is dirty / he wants to wash it off); he does not talk about his work / is private or reserved; he reads the evening paper every night; he reads it back to front (sport first, perhaps); he ignores or shuts out his family while reading; he needs time alone after work; he is set in his ways. No credit for copying whole sentences.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1940s novel).

> The town had done its best for the victory parade. Bunting crossed the high street from the bank to the Co-op, though some of it was faded from the Coronation. The band was short of a cornet and two drums. Children waved paper flags their teachers had cut out that morning, and the mayor's chain of office caught the sun so that he seemed, from a distance, to be on fire.

List five things you learn about the town's parade in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, maximum five. Accept: it is a victory parade / a celebration; the town made an effort ("done its best"); bunting is hung across the high street; it stretches from the bank to the Co-op; some bunting is old / reused from an earlier celebration; the town is short of money or materials; there is a band; the band is incomplete (missing a cornet and two drums), perhaps because men are away; children wave paper flags; the flags were homemade by teachers that morning / last-minute; the mayor attends in his chain of office; it is sunny. No credit for whole sentences.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1920s novel).

> Lady Harcourt received her visitors in the morning room, sitting very upright on a chair that nobody else was permitted to use. She was past seventy and had buried two husbands, both of whom were said to have been grateful for the rest. Her rings were too large for her fingers now, and she turned them constantly as she talked.

List five things you learn about Lady Harcourt in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, maximum five. Accept: she has a title / is upper class; she receives visitors formally in a morning room; she sits very upright / has rigid posture; she has her own chair that no one else may use / is controlling or possessive; she is over seventy; she has been widowed twice; she was a difficult or domineering wife (the husbands were "grateful for the rest"); people gossip about her; she wears large rings / is wealthy; she has grown thinner (rings too large); she fidgets with the rings / is restless or anxious; she talks a lot. Do not credit copied whole sentences.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel).

> The classroom smelled of chalk and wet coats. Its windows were too high to see out of, and the radiator under them knocked all morning without giving any heat. On the wall a map of the world still showed half of it coloured pink. Thirty-eight desks stood in rows, each with an inkwell, and each with initials carved into its lid by boys long since grown up and gone.

List five things you learn about the classroom in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, maximum five. Accept: it smells of chalk; it smells of wet coats (it is a rainy season / pupils come in wet); the windows are too high to see out of; the radiator knocks / is noisy; the radiator gives no heat so the room is cold; there is an old (out-of-date) map of the world; there are thirty-eight desks / the class is large; desks are in rows (formal teaching); each desk has an inkwell; the desks are old and carved with initials; generations of boys have used it / it is a boys' school. No credit for whole sentences.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1930s novel).

> Uncle Leonard arrived with three suitcases, a parrot in a covered cage and no warning at all. He was browner than anyone in our street and wore a straw hat in March. He kissed my mother on both cheeks, which made her blush, and gave me a coin from a country I had never heard of.

List five things you learn about Uncle Leonard in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, maximum five. Accept: he is the narrator's uncle; he arrives unexpectedly / is impulsive or thoughtless; he brings three suitcases, so means to stay a while; he owns a parrot; he is suntanned / has been somewhere hot; he wears a straw hat out of season / is unconventional; he is affectionate or flamboyant (kisses on both cheeks); his manners are foreign or unusual to the family; he embarrasses the mother; he is generous to the narrator; he has travelled abroad (a foreign coin). No credit for whole sentences.
:::

::: test
"Her shoes had been mended so often that the cobbler no longer charged her." Is the point "she is poor" explicit or implicit? Explain. (2)
---
Implicit (1): the text never states that she is poor; it is inferred from the repeatedly mended shoes (and perhaps the cobbler's kindness) (1).
:::

::: test
Read this line (original, written in the style of a 1950s novel): "The vicar's wife arranged the flowers every Friday and rearranged anybody else's on Saturday." Which detail suggests she is controlling, and why? (2)
---
"rearranged anybody else's" (1): she cannot leave other people's work alone and must impose her own way (1).
:::

::: test
A student answers an A1 question about "the old sailor" with: "The old sailor sat on the harbour wall every morning, watching the boats come in and out and smoking a clay pipe that had belonged to his father." How many marks will this earn, and how should it be improved? (2)
---
Possibly none, as it is one whole sentence copied unselectively (1). Improve by splitting it into short separate points: he sits on the harbour wall every morning; he watches the boats; he smokes a clay pipe; the pipe was his father's / he is sentimental (1).
:::

::: test
A question asks about "the narrator's grandmother". The lines include: "Grandmother's cottage was the last in the row and the only one with a red door." Can "the cottage has a red door" be credited? Explain. (2)
---
Probably not as it stands, because it is about the cottage rather than the grandmother (1); it could be credited if turned into a point about her, e.g. she lives in the last cottage in the row / she has chosen a door unlike her neighbours' (1).
:::

::: test
Give one fair inference from: "He paid for the drinks with a five-pound note and did not wait for his change." (1)
---
Any one: he is wealthy or careless with money; he wants to impress; he is in a hurry.
:::

::: test
Explain why language analysis is unnecessary in an A1 answer. (2)
---
A1 tests AO1, finding and inferring information (1); marks are one per point, so analysis of word choice gains nothing and wastes time needed for the 10-mark questions (1).
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1960s novel).

> The flat was on the fourteenth floor, and the lift worked on alternate days. From the kitchen window you could see the whole city and, on clear evenings, the hills beyond it. Mrs Okafor had covered the table with a cloth she had brought from home, and the only picture on the wall was a calendar from the corner shop, still turned to March although it was June.

List five things you learn about the flat in these lines. (5)
---
One mark per valid point, maximum five. Accept: it is on the fourteenth floor / in a tower block; the lift is unreliable / works only on alternate days; it has a kitchen window; it has a wide view of the city; the hills are visible on clear evenings; Mrs Okafor lives there; the table has a cloth from her former home / she has brought something of home with her; it is sparsely decorated (one picture); the picture is a free calendar from the corner shop; the calendar has not been turned since March, suggesting she is busy, distracted, unsettled or not yet at home there. No credit for whole sentences.
:::
