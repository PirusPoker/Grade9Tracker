## What it is

**A6** is the final reading question in Component 2 and the last 10 marks of Section A. It tests **AO3**: comparing **writers' ideas and perspectives, and how these are conveyed**, across two texts. The pattern (paraphrased) is:

> Both of these texts are about [a shared topic].
> Compare:
> - [the writers' ideas / experiences / feelings / the challenges they faced];
> - how the writers [convey / make these clear to readers].
> You must use the text to support your comments and make it clear which text you are referring to.

So there are always **two strands**:
1. **What**: the ideas, attitudes, experiences or feelings each writer presents on the focus.
2. **How**: the methods each writer uses to convey them: form, tone, language, structure, and the era and audience that shape them.

**Compare, don't describe.** The commonest weakness is two mini-essays: a paragraph on Text 1, then a paragraph on Text 2, with "both texts are different" at the end. That is **description** and is held in the lower bands. A comparison puts the texts **side by side in every paragraph**, with **paired evidence** and **comparative connectives**.

**What to compare.**

| Area | Questions to ask of both texts |
|---|---|
| **Ideas and attitudes** | What does each writer think or feel about the focus? Admiring, frightened, amused, critical? |
| **Experiences** | What happens to each? What challenges, rewards, surprises? |
| **Tone** | Breezy or solemn? Dramatic or understated? Does it shift? |
| **Language** | Imagery, emotive or technical vocabulary, humour, hyperbole, understatement |
| **Structure** | Chronological or not? Where is the climax? How does each end? |
| **Form and audience** | A newspaper feature for the public versus a private diary or letter; who was the writer addressing? |
| **Era** | What has changed (technology, safety, attitudes), and what is the same in human terms? |

**The two centuries are a gift.** Many differences come from the period: a 21st-century article quotes experts, uses statistics and short paragraphs for a broad audience; a 19th-century diary or letter uses long, formal sentences and understatement for a private reader. Explain **how** the era shapes the method, but keep the focus on the question: the writers' ideas and how they convey them.

**Integrated comparison.** Each paragraph should follow a pattern such as:
1. **Comparative point**: "Both writers are awed by the mountain, but they express it very differently."
2. **Text 1 evidence and method**: short quotation and brief analysis.
3. **Comparative connective**: "whereas", "in contrast", "similarly".
4. **Text 2 evidence and method**: short quotation and brief analysis.
5. **Judgement**: what the difference or similarity shows.

**Comparative connectives.** Similarity: *both, similarly, likewise, in the same way, also*. Difference: *whereas, while, however, in contrast, unlike, on the other hand, conversely*. Use them at the **start** of points, not only as the final word of a paragraph.

**How it is banded.** In plain terms:

| Band | What the examiner sees |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | **Perceptive, detailed** comparison of ideas and methods; well-chosen evidence from both texts; **sustained** integrated comparison; insight into how writers convey their perspectives |
| 7–8 | **Detailed** comparison of a range of ideas and how they are conveyed; well-chosen evidence; mostly integrated |
| 5–6 | **Some** comparison of ideas and some methods; relevant evidence from both; may be partly separate |
| 3–4 | **Simple** comparisons, mostly of content; some reference |
| 1–2 | Identifies basic similarities or differences |

**A 12-minute plan.** Two minutes jotting three or four comparison points (each with a short quotation from each text); ten minutes writing four integrated paragraphs. Start with the biggest difference or similarity in **ideas**; move to **methods**; end with a short judgement about how the era or form shapes each perspective.

::: terms
- **Comparison**: discussing two texts side by side, showing similarities and differences.
- **Perspective**: a writer's point of view on a subject, shaped by experience, era and purpose.
- **Integrated comparison**: comparing both texts within each paragraph rather than writing about them separately.
- **Paired evidence**: a quotation from each text placed together to support one comparative point.
- **Comparative connective**: a linking word such as whereas, similarly or in contrast that signals comparison.
- **Methods**: the ways a writer conveys ideas, such as language, tone, structure and form.
- **Form**: the type of text, such as diary, letter or newspaper feature, which shapes its style.
- **Era**: the period a text comes from, which affects attitudes, vocabulary and conventions.
- **Sustained**: kept up throughout the answer, as comparison must be for the top band.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **shared topic** and the **two strands** (what; how).
2. Spend two minutes jotting three or four comparison points, each with a short quotation from **each** text.
3. Begin with the most important similarity or difference in **ideas or experiences**.
4. In every paragraph: comparative point, Text 1 evidence and method, connective, Text 2 evidence and method, judgement.
5. Compare **methods** too: tone, language, structure, form and audience.
6. Use the **era** to explain differences, briefly.
7. End with a one- or two-sentence overall judgement on how the two perspectives differ.
:::

::: example A 9–10 paragraph
Shared topic: two writers' first sight of the sea.

Answer extract: Both writers are overwhelmed by the sea, but the modern blogger treats the moment as comic and personal, whereas the Victorian diarist treats it as almost religious. In Text 1 the writer, aged twenty, admits she "squealed like a toddler", a self-mocking simile that invites readers to laugh with her, and the short paragraphs keep the tone breezy. In contrast, the diarist in Text 2 writes of "the vast and solemn waters, which seemed to rebuke all littleness", where the abstract vocabulary and long, balanced sentence create reverence rather than fun. The difference reflects form and era: a blog performs feelings for an audience, while a private Victorian diary records an experience of awe, perhaps shaped by a religious sense of nature's power.
(Integrated, paired evidence, methods analysed, perceptive comment on form and era: 9–10 level.)
:::

::: example Description versus comparison
Description (3–4): "Text 1 is about a girl going on a train and she is excited. She says 'buzzing'. Text 2 is about a man on a train and he is scared. He says 'alarming'. They are different."
Comparison (7–8): "While the modern teenager in Text 1 is 'buzzing' with excitement on her first solo train trip, the Victorian gentleman in Text 2 finds the speed 'alarming'. The slang 'buzzing' makes the experience energetic and casual, whereas his formal adjective, from a time when rail travel was new, shows genuine unease at a technology not yet trusted." The second paragraph compares in every sentence and explains methods.
:::

::: example Comparing structure
Text 1 (a 21st-century article about a hill race) opens with the finish line and then flashes back through the race; Text 2 (an 1880s account of a mountain walk) is strictly chronological from dawn to dusk.

Comparison: Both writers build towards the hardest moment, but the journalist in Text 1 grabs attention by beginning with the triumph and then reveals the pain that led to it, so we read the struggle knowing it will be rewarded. In contrast, the Victorian writer's chronological structure makes us share the uncertainty of the day hour by hour, so the summit feels genuinely earned. The modern article is shaped for impatient readers; the older account for those who expected a full, orderly narrative.
:::

---
AO3: comparing writers' ideas and perspectives, and how they are conveyed, across texts.

::: q
Why does writing about Text 1 and then Text 2 separately score in the lower bands? (2)
---
It describes each text rather than comparing them (1); the higher bands need sustained, integrated comparison with paired evidence (1).
:::

::: q
Give four comparative connectives, two for similarity and two for difference. (2)
---
Similarity: both, similarly, likewise, in the same way, also (1 for two). Difference: whereas, while, however, in contrast, unlike, conversely (1 for two).
:::

::: q
Explain one way the era of each text might shape the writers' methods. (2)
---
For example, a 21st-century article may use statistics, expert quotations and short paragraphs for a broad public audience, whereas a 19th-century diary may use long, formal sentences and understatement for a private reader (1 for the difference, 1 for linking it to era or audience).
:::

::: q
How long should you spend on A6, and how should you divide the time? (2)
---
About 12 minutes (1): two minutes planning paired points and about ten minutes writing (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Compare in every paragraph.** Two separate essays are description.
- **Both strands.** What the writers think, and how they show it.
- **Paired evidence.** A quotation from each text for each point.
- **Methods, not just content.** Tone, language, structure and form lift the band.
- **Era as explanation, not history.** One sentence on why the methods differ is enough.
- **Make it clear which text.** Name the text or writer each time.
:::

::: test
Explain one similarity in the ideas of these two extracts. Text 1 (21st-century article): "Nobody tells you how lonely night shifts are. The hospital is full, and yet you feel like the last person awake on earth." Text 2 (1850s letter): "The night watch is a solitary business; one hears the whole ship breathing and is yet entirely alone." (2)

::: test
Explain one difference in method in these two extracts. Text 1 (21st-century newspaper): "The storm brought 90mph winds, toppled 300 trees and left 12,000 homes without power." Text 2 (1880s diary): "The wind screamed all night like a creature in torment, and we lay awake expecting the roof to go." (3)
---
Text 1 uses facts and statistics to show the storm's scale objectively (1); Text 2 uses a simile and personification ("screamed… like a creature in torment") to convey fear subjectively (1); this reflects form: a news report informs the public, while a diary records personal experience (1).

---
For example: "Both writers stress how hard the climb was, but while Text 1's blunt, modern 'brutal' makes the effort sound violent, Text 2's formal 'exceedingly arduous' conveys the same hardship with Victorian restraint." (1 for linking both; 1 for a comment on how the methods differ.)

::: test
Read these two extracts.

Text 1 (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century newspaper feature):
> Sixteen-year-old Jade Kowalski had never been further than Leeds when she stepped off the coach at Victoria last month. "Honestly? I nearly got straight back on," she laughs. "It was so loud. Everyone was walking like they were late for something." By the second day, though, she was hooked. She rode the top deck of a bus "just to look", ate dumplings in Chinatown and sat on the steps at Trafalgar Square watching a man juggle fire. "Back home you know everyone," she says. "Here nobody knows you, and that's kind of amazing. You can be anyone." She is already saving to come back, and has a list on her phone headed simply: NEXT TIME.

Text 2 (original passage written in the style of an 1840s letter). *In the 1840s a young man from the country visited London for the first time. This is part of a letter he wrote to his sister.*
> My dear Martha, I have been in the great city four days, and I do not know whether I am delighted or terrified; I think both at once. The noise never ceases. From before light until long after midnight the carts and cabs roar past my window, and the crowds in the Strand press so close that one is carried along like a leaf on a mill-race, whether one will or no. I have seen the river, black with ships; I have seen a lord's carriage, all gold, and not ten yards from it a child with no shoes asking for bread. Nobody looks at anybody. At home I could not walk to the church without being stopped six times; here I might die upon the pavement and be stepped over. Yet I cannot deny it is a wonderful place, and I shall be sorry to leave it.

Both of these texts are about a first visit to London. Compare:
- the writers' feelings about the city;
- how they convey these feelings.

You must use the text to support your comments and make it clear which text you are referring to. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: perceptive, detailed, sustained integrated comparison of feelings and methods; well-chosen paired evidence; insight into how form and era shape each perspective.
- 7–8: detailed comparison of a range of feelings and methods; well-chosen evidence; mostly integrated.
- 5–6: some comparison of feelings and some methods, with relevant evidence from both; may be partly separate.
- 3–4: simple comparisons, mostly of content, some reference.
- 1–2: identifies basic similarities or differences. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content. Similarities: both are first overwhelmed by noise and pace (Jade: "so loud", people "walking like they were late"; the letter-writer: noise that "never ceases", the simile "like a leaf on a mill-race" shows helplessness); both end positively (Jade "hooked", saving, list "NEXT TIME"; the letter-writer "a wonderful place", "sorry to leave"); both contrast city anonymity with home where "you know everyone" / being "stopped six times". Differences: Jade sees anonymity as freedom ("You can be anyone"), whereas the letter-writer finds it chilling (hyperbole "I might die upon the pavement and be stepped over"); the letter notices extreme inequality (gold carriage juxtaposed with a shoeless child), while Jade's account focuses on fun experiences (bus, dumplings, fire-juggler); the letter states mixed feelings openly ("delighted or terrified… both at once"), whereas Jade's feelings change across the article from wanting to leave to loving it. Methods: Text 1 is a feature with direct quotation, colloquial voice ("Honestly?", "kind of amazing"), a journalist's structure from doubt to delight, a list of activities and a punchy capitalised ending; Text 2 is a private letter with long, piled-up sentences mimicking the crowd, semicolons, a formal address to his sister and a balanced final sentence ("Yet I cannot deny…"); era and audience explain the different registers.
:::

::: test
Read these two extracts.

Text 1 (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century blog post):
> **Summit day (or: why I will never complain about stairs again)**
>
> We left the hut at 2am, head-torches bobbing in a line like a slow, sad disco. For five hours I saw nothing but the boots in front of me and the circle of light on the snow. My thighs burned. My water froze. At one point I seriously considered lying down and letting the mountain have me. Then, at 7.12am, the slope just… stopped. The sun came up over a sea of cloud, pink and gold, and the whole Alps lay out below us like a crumpled map. I cried. Our guide, Luca, who has done this climb ninety times, shook my hand and said, "Every time, it is the first time." Best. Morning. Ever.

Text 2 (original passage written in the style of an 1860s journal). *In the 1860s an English clergyman climbed a high peak in the Alps with two local guides. This is part of the account he wrote.*
> We quitted the hut at half past two, the stars still shining with extraordinary brilliance, and for some hours ascended by lantern-light over snow so hard that each step must be cut with the axe. The cold was severe; my beard was soon a mass of ice. I confess that near the top, where the ridge narrowed to a mere knife-edge with a fall of some thousands of feet on either hand, I was obliged to fix my eyes upon the heels of the guide and think of nothing else. At length we stood upon the summit. I shall not attempt to describe the prospect; words are poor things. It is enough to say that we uncovered our heads, and that none of us spoke for some time.

Both of these texts are about climbing a mountain. Compare:
- the writers' experiences of the climb;
- how the writers make these experiences clear to readers.

You must use the text to support your comments and make it clear which text you are referring to. (10)
---
Bands as for all A6 questions:
- 9–10: perceptive, detailed, sustained integrated comparison of experiences and methods; well-chosen paired evidence; insight.
- 7–8: detailed comparison of a range; well-chosen evidence; mostly integrated.
- 5–6: some comparison of experiences and methods; relevant evidence.
- 3–4: simple comparisons, mostly content.
- 1–2: basic similarities or differences. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content. Similarities: both start in the dark before dawn from a hut (2am; half past two) and climb by artificial light (head-torches; lantern); both suffer cold (water froze; beard "a mass of ice"); both cope with fear or exhaustion by focusing on the person ahead (boots in front; "the heels of the guide"); both are deeply moved at the summit (she cried; they uncovered their heads and were silent). Differences: Text 1 stresses physical suffering and humour ("thighs burned", wanting to let the mountain "have me", self-mocking subtitle about stairs), whereas Text 2 stresses danger and skill (steps cut with the axe; the "knife-edge" ridge with a vast drop); Text 1 describes the view in vivid imagery ("sea of cloud", "like a crumpled map"), whereas Text 2 refuses to describe it ("words are poor things"), conveying awe through silence and reverence, fitting a clergyman. Methods: Text 1's short sentences, ellipsis ("just… stopped") and the fragment "Best. Morning. Ever." create a breathless, comic, conversational voice for blog readers, plus the guide's quotation for wisdom; Text 2's long, formal sentences, understatement ("I confess… I was obliged") and restrained ending create dignity; both are chronological and build to the summit as a climax, but Text 1 gives an exact time (7.12am) while Text 2 says only "At length".
:::
