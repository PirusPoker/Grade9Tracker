## What it is

**AO6** covers **vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation**: using a range of vocabulary and sentence structures for clarity, purpose and effect, with accurate spelling and punctuation. In Component 1 Section B it is worth **16 of the 40 marks**; in each Component 2 Section B task it is worth **8 of the 20**. Across the qualification, AO6 is **20%** of your grade. These are the most learnable marks in English Language: a story with a good idea and careless accuracy can lose a whole band, while a careful writer protects them every time.

**What the top AO6 band looks like.** Roughly: sentence structures **varied appropriately and for effect**; **virtually all** sentence construction **controlled and accurate**; a **range of punctuation** used **confidently** and accurately; **virtually all spelling** correct, including complex and irregular words; **tense and agreement** totally secure; a **wide, well-judged vocabulary**. Notice "virtually all": an occasional slip is tolerated, a pattern of errors is not.

**Sentence types and when they help.**

| Type | Example | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** (one main clause) | *The door opened.* | Shock, emphasis, a decision, a turning point |
| **Compound** (clauses joined by and/but/or/so) | *She knocked, but nobody came.* | Balance, contrast, steady narration |
| **Complex** (main clause + subordinate clause) | *Although the lights were on, nobody came.* | Show cause, time, condition, contrast; add depth |
| **Minor** (no main verb) | *Silence. Then footsteps.* | Fragments of thought, atmosphere, pace (use sparingly) |

Variety is not random. Use a short sentence **after** long ones for impact; a long, flowing sentence for a rush of thought or movement; a complex sentence that **delays** its main clause to build suspense ("When at last the letter arrived, three weeks late and stained with rain, I…").

**Sentence openings.** Vary them: start with a **subordinate clause** (*As the bus pulled away,*), an **adverb** (*Slowly,*), a **prepositional phrase** (*Beneath the bridge,*), a **participle** (*Turning the key,*), a **noun** or a line of **dialogue**. A page where every sentence begins "I" or "Then" signals a limited range.

**Punctuation that lifts the band.**
- **Semicolon (;)**: joins two closely related main clauses: *The house was silent; even the clock had stopped.* Also separates items in a complex list.
- **Colon (:)**: introduces an explanation, list or reveal: *There was only one problem: the key was gone.*
- **Dash (–)**: adds an afterthought or a sharp turn: *He smiled – but only with his mouth.* A pair of dashes works like brackets.
- **Brackets ( )**: add a quieter aside: *My aunt (who never laughed) was laughing.*
- **Speech punctuation**: *"I'm leaving," she said. "Don't wait up."* New speaker, new line; punctuation inside the speech marks; a comma before the closing mark when the sentence continues with "she said".
- **Apostrophes**: **omission** (*can't, it's = it is*) and **possession** (*the girl's bag*, one girl; *the girls' bags*, more than one; *the children's coats*, irregular plural).

**The commonest errors.**
- **Comma splice**: two sentences joined by a comma. *I opened the door, it was dark.* Fix with a full stop, a semicolon, or a conjunction (*I opened the door and it was dark*; *When I opened the door, it was dark*).
- **Run-on**: sentences fused with no punctuation at all.
- **Its / it's**: *it's* = it is or it has; *its* = belonging to it (*The dog wagged its tail*).
- **Misplaced apostrophes**: *apple's for sale* (plural, no apostrophe needed).
- **Their / there / they're**, **your / you're**, **to / too**, **where / were / we're**, **affect / effect**, **practice / practise**, **of** instead of **have** (*could of* is always wrong).
- **Tense drift**: past tense narration slipping into present.
- **Agreement**: *The group of boys was waiting* (the group is singular); *Neither of them was ready*.

**Commonly misspelt words to learn.** separate, definitely, necessary, embarrass, disappear, beginning, believe, receive, occasion, accommodation, conscience, conscious, rhythm, immediately, especially, environment, government, argument, surprise, tomorrow, until, writing, business, cemetery.

**Precise, not showy, vocabulary.** The top band rewards words that are **well judged**, not merely long. *She ambulated precipitously* is worse than *She hurried*. A precise verb (*shuffled*, *lurched*, *strode*) often replaces a weak verb plus adverb (*walked slowly*). Ambition means the **exact** word, including the less obvious one, spelt correctly.

**A five-minute proofreading routine.** At the end of Section B:
1. **Read for sentence boundaries**: hunt for comma splices and run-ons.
2. **Check every apostrophe and every "its/it's"**.
3. **Check speech punctuation** and new lines for new speakers.
4. **Check tense**: does any verb slip into the present?
5. **Check homophones** (there/their/they're, your/you're) and words you know you misspell.
Correct neatly with a single line through the error.

::: terms
- **AO6**: the assessment objective for vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation; 16 of the 40 marks in Component 1 Section B.
- **Simple sentence**: a sentence with one main clause.
- **Compound sentence**: two main clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction such as and, but or so.
- **Complex sentence**: a main clause with at least one subordinate clause.
- **Minor sentence**: a sentence without a main verb, used for effect.
- **Subordinate clause**: a clause that cannot stand alone, often starting with although, when, because or if.
- **Comma splice**: the error of joining two complete sentences with only a comma.
- **Semicolon**: a punctuation mark that joins two closely related main clauses.
- **Colon**: a punctuation mark that introduces an explanation, list or reveal.
- **Apostrophe of possession**: an apostrophe showing ownership, before the s for one owner and after it for plural owners ending in s.
- **Apostrophe of omission**: an apostrophe showing where letters are missing, as in can't.
- **Agreement**: matching a verb to its subject in number, such as "the group was" and "they were".
:::

::: method
1. Plan two or three **deliberate effects**: one short sentence at the turning point, one complex sentence that delays its main clause, one semicolon or colon.
2. While writing, vary **sentence openings**; avoid starting consecutive sentences with "I" or "Then".
3. Choose **precise verbs and nouns** rather than adjective and adverb pile-ups.
4. Punctuate dialogue correctly as you go; new speaker, new line.
5. Use ambitious punctuation **only where you are sure** it is correct.
6. Leave **five minutes** to proofread: sentence boundaries, apostrophes, speech, tense, homophones.
:::

::: example Varying sentences for effect
Flat: "I walked into the hall. It was dark. I saw a light. I went towards it. I was scared."

Varied: "Feeling along the wall for a switch that was not there, I edged into the hall. Darkness. Then, at the far end, a thin line of light appeared beneath a door; it flickered, as if someone on the other side had walked past it. I stopped."
Effects: a participle opening; a minor sentence for atmosphere; a semicolon joining two linked ideas; a simple sentence at the end for the moment of fear. The emotion is not named but the structure enacts it.
:::

::: example Fixing comma splices
Error: "The bus was late, I decided to walk, it started to rain."

Fixes: "The bus was late, so I decided to walk. It started to rain." / "The bus was late; I decided to walk. Then it started to rain." / "Because the bus was late, I decided to walk – and it started to rain."
Each version shows control of sentence boundaries, and the last uses a complex sentence and a dash for a wry turn.
:::

::: example Punctuating dialogue
Unpunctuated: where have you been asked mum nowhere I said you're soaked she said

Correct:
"Where have you been?" asked Mum.
"Nowhere," I said.
"You're soaked," she said.
Rules shown: question mark inside the speech marks; comma before the closing mark when "I said" follows; a new line for each speaker; an apostrophe of omission in "You're".
:::

::: example Precise vocabulary
Showy: "The extraordinarily magnificent sunset was incredibly beautiful and very colourful."
Precise: "The sunset bruised the sky purple, then drained away behind the gasworks."
One exact verb ("bruised") and one unexpected detail ("the gasworks") outclass four intensifiers. Ambition is about precision.
:::

::: q
How many marks is AO6 worth in Component 1 Section B, and in each Component 2 Section B task? (2)
---
16 of 40 in Component 1 (1); 8 of 20 in each Component 2 task (1).
:::

::: q
What is a comma splice? Give three ways to fix one. (3)
---
Two complete sentences joined with only a comma (1). Fixes, any two: a full stop; a semicolon; a conjunction; rewriting as a complex sentence (1 each, max 2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between "its" and "it's". (2)
---
"It's" means it is or it has (apostrophe of omission) (1); "its" means belonging to it, with no apostrophe (1).
:::

::: q
Give two uses of a colon. (2)
---
Any two: to introduce an explanation; to introduce a list; to introduce a reveal or a quotation (1 each).
:::

::: q
Why might "She strode" be better than "She walked very quickly and confidently"? (2)
---
A precise verb carries the meaning in one word (1), so the writing is sharper and shows well-judged vocabulary rather than piled-up adverbs (1).
:::

::: q
List the five checks in the proofreading routine. (2)
---
Sentence boundaries (comma splices, run-ons); apostrophes and its/it's; speech punctuation; tense; homophones and personal misspellings (2 for all five, 1 for three or four).
:::

::: watch
- **Comma splices** are the single biggest AO6 mark-loser. Check every comma between two complete sentences.
- **Tense drift** from past to present in the middle of a story.
- **Speech punctuation**: missing commas, punctuation outside the speech marks, no new line for a new speaker.
- **Apostrophes in plurals** ("the boy's ran") and its/it's confusion.
- **Showy vocabulary misused or misspelt** is worse than a plain word used precisely.
- **Monotonous openings**: "I… I… Then… Then…".
- **No proofreading time**: five minutes at the end protects several marks.
:::

::: test
Correct the errors in this sentence: "The dog wagged it's tail, it was pleased to see us." (2)
---
"The dog wagged its tail; it was pleased to see us." (or a full stop / "because it was pleased") (1 for its; 1 for fixing the comma splice).
:::

::: test
Punctuate this dialogue correctly, setting it out on separate lines: are you coming asked Ffion in a minute I said hurry up she said the film starts at eight (4)
---
"Are you coming?" asked Ffion.
"In a minute," I said.
"Hurry up," she said. "The film starts at eight."
(1 for speech marks around all spoken words; 1 for the question mark and commas inside the speech marks; 1 for a new line for each speaker; 1 for correct capitals, including the new sentence "The film…").
:::

::: test
Combine these two sentences using a semicolon: "The shop was closed. A handwritten sign hung in the window." (1)
---
"The shop was closed; a handwritten sign hung in the window."
:::

::: test
Rewrite this sentence as a complex sentence that begins with a subordinate clause: "I heard the news. I sat down on the stairs." (2)
---
For example: "When I heard the news, I sat down on the stairs." (1 for a subordinate clause opening; 1 for correct comma after the subordinate clause).
:::

::: test
Vary the openings of these sentences so that no two begin the same way: "I opened the gate. I walked up the path. I knocked on the door. I waited." (3)
---
For example: "Opening the gate, I walked up the path. At the door I knocked twice. Nobody came; I waited." (1 for each of three different, correct openings, e.g. participle, prepositional phrase, noun/subject other than "I").
:::

::: test
Correct the apostrophes: "The childrens coats were hanging on the teachers pegs, and the girls lunchboxes were on the shelf. Its nearly lunch time." (4)
---
"The children's coats were hanging on the teachers' pegs (or teacher's, if one teacher), and the girls' lunchboxes (or girl's) were on the shelf. It's nearly lunch time." (1 each: children's; teachers'/teacher's; girls'/girl's; It's).
:::

::: test
Choose the most precise word in brackets and explain why: "The old man (walked / shuffled / moved) towards the bus stop." (2)
---
"Shuffled" (1): it suggests short, dragging steps, conveying age or frailty that the general verbs do not (1).
:::

::: test
Identify and correct the agreement error: "The team of players were waiting for their coach, and neither of them were ready." (2)
---
"The team of players was waiting…" (the team is singular) (1); "neither of them was ready" (neither is singular) (1). (Accept "were" for team only if the candidate explains collective nouns can take plural in British usage; "neither… was" is required.)
:::

::: test
Correct the spelling errors: "I definately beleived it was neccessary to seperate the two dogs immediatly." (4)
---
definitely; believed; necessary; separate; immediately (1 each, max 4).
:::

::: test
Write one sentence about a storm that uses a dash for a sudden turn. (2)
---
For example: "The wind dropped, the rain softened, and for a moment the whole street seemed to breathe again – then the lights went out." (1 for a correctly used dash; 1 for a genuine turn or afterthought it introduces).
:::

::: test
Explain how a short simple sentence placed after two long sentences can create an effect in a story. (2)
---
The contrast in length makes it stand out and stops the reader (1), creating emphasis, shock, tension or finality at a key moment (1).
:::

::: test
Read this extract from a student's story and rewrite it with all errors corrected and the sentences improved: "me and my brother was walking home, it was raining, we seen a cat stuck up a tree so we tryed to get it down." (4)
---
For example: "My brother and I were walking home in the rain when we saw a cat stuck up a tree, so we tried to get it down." (1 for "My brother and I were"; 1 for "saw"; 1 for "tried"; 1 for removing the comma splices with a complex sentence or correct punctuation).
:::
