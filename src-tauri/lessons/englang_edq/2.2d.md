## What it is

**A4** is the 10-mark question on **Text 2**, the 19th-century text. It gives you a **statement** about the text, usually in quotation marks as if from a reader or critic, and asks **"To what extent do you agree?"** (or "How far do you agree?"). It tests **AO4**: **evaluating texts critically** and supporting the judgement with well-chosen references. The pattern (paraphrased) is:

> "[A statement about how the text presents something, or what it suggests.]"
> To what extent do you agree with this view?
> You should comment on:
> - what is said
> - how it is said.
> You must refer to the text to support your comments.

Statements in live papers have been about how a diary suggests a growing sense of failure; whether an account keeps the reader in suspense; whether a newspaper report paints a vivid picture of difficult conditions; how a writer feels about what he saw. The **specimen paper** used a different form: **"What do you think and feel about [the writer's] views on…?"** Both are evaluation. Be ready for either.

**What evaluation is.** Evaluation means **judging how well and how far** the text does something, and **why**, with evidence. It is not:
- **retelling** the text in order;
- **agreeing** and then listing quotations;
- **language analysis** with no judgement (that is A2's job).

A good A4 answer keeps asking: *Does this detail support the statement? How strongly? Does anything qualify or contradict it? What is the overall effect on me as a reader?*

**The two bullets.**
- **What is said**: the content, events, opinions and details that bear on the statement.
- **How it is said**: the methods that create the effect the statement describes (word choice, imagery, tone, sentence forms, structure, the diary or letter form, understatement).

The best answers **fuse** them: "The statement is most convincing in the third letter, where the writer's short, flat sentences ('The house is very quiet. I do not go out.') make her loneliness feel complete."

**Taking a stance.** Decide your overall answer before writing, and make it **qualified**:
- **Largely agree, but…**: the statement is true for most of the text, with a significant exception.
- **Partly agree**: true in one way or one section, not in another.
- **Largely disagree**: the text suggests something more complex or nearly the opposite.

A sustained, qualified stance is the clearest sign of the top band. Answers that agree completely usually miss the nuance the statement was written to test.

**Dealing with 19th-century attitudes.** Older writers may hold views on class, gender, race, empire, children or animals that seem wrong today. Two steps:
1. **On its own terms**: what does the writer believe, and how do they present it (confidently, humorously, with sympathy)?
2. **Your response**: in "what do you think and feel" questions especially, you may judge the view, but do it with reasons and evidence ("the writer's pity for the workers is sincere, but he never questions why children are in the mill at all").

**How it is banded.** In plain terms:

| Band | What the examiner sees |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | **Persuasive, sustained** evaluation; perceptive judgement on what is said and how; **well-selected** references from across the text; a clear, qualified stance throughout |
| 7–8 | **Thoughtful, developed** evaluation; clear judgement with well-chosen references to content and method |
| 5–6 | **Some** evaluation; relevant references; some comment on how ideas are presented |
| 3–4 | **Simple** opinion with straightforward reference; may drift into retelling |
| 1–2 | Basic comment with little reference |

**Moving from 5–6 to 9–10.** Statement: *"The governess's letters show her becoming increasingly homesick."*
- 5–6: "I agree because she says 'I think of you all every hour', which shows she misses home."
- 7–8: "I largely agree: 'every hour' is a plain, insistent phrase, and coming after a letter full of cheerful news about her pupils it shows her composure slipping."
- 9–10: "The statement is persuasive for the later letters, but 'increasingly' is the key word: the letters' structure, each shorter and with less news of the household, charts a gradual withdrawal rather than a sudden crisis, so 'I think of you all every hour' reads as the end of a long erosion that her polite manner has been hiding."

**Timing.** About **12–13 minutes**, after a careful second reading of the 19th-century text.

::: terms
- **Evaluation**: judging how far and how well a text achieves an effect, with reasons and evidence.
- **Statement**: the view quoted in the question, which you must test against the text.
- **Stance**: your overall position on the statement, such as largely agree, partly agree or largely disagree.
- **Qualified judgement**: a view that accepts the statement in part while noting limits or exceptions.
- **Sustained**: kept consistent and developed across the whole answer, not just stated at the start.
- **What is said**: the content of the text, its events, details and the writer's opinions.
- **How it is said**: the writer's methods, such as language, tone, structure and form.
- **Understatement**: presenting something as less serious than it is, often to show feeling through restraint.
- **Context**: the time and situation in which a text was written, used to understand the writer's views.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **key words** in the statement (e.g. "increasingly", "vivid", "only", "dangerous").
2. Reread the 19th-century text and mark evidence **for** and **against** in two colours.
3. Decide a **qualified stance** and write it in your first sentence.
4. Work through the text in order, judging each section: how far does it support the statement, and **how** is that effect created?
5. Include at least one point that **qualifies** the statement.
6. Weave **what is said** and **how it is said** together in every paragraph.
7. End with a one-sentence overall judgement that answers "to what extent".
:::

::: example A 9–10 opening and paragraph
Statement: *"The writer presents the voyage as nothing but a misery."*

Answer extract: I largely agree that the letter dwells on misery, but "nothing but" goes too far, because the writer's humour and her final glimpse of land suggest a resilience that the statement ignores. The first half certainly supports it: the list of discomforts ("the cold, the damp, the ceaseless motion") is relentless, and the long sentences, piling clause on clause, make the days feel endless. Yet the tone is never self-pitying. Her wry remark that the ship's biscuit "would serve to mend the road" turns suffering into a joke for her reader, which suggests she is coping, and perhaps protecting her family from worry.
(Clear qualified stance from the first sentence; judges the statement's key words; fuses what and how: 9–10 level.)
:::

::: example "What do you think and feel about the writer's views?"
Text (original passage written in the style of an 1860s magazine): *"The street boys of the city are a lively race, impudent, ragged and very quick. I confess I have rarely been so well entertained as by their antics at the station; though one cannot but wish that some society would take them up and teach them a trade before they learn a worse one."*

Answer extract: The writer's view is a mixture of amusement and concern, and I find the amusement uncomfortable. He describes the boys as entertainment ("well entertained", "antics"), the way one might describe a show, and the triple "impudent, ragged and very quick" makes their poverty sound colourful rather than hard. His concern is real but distant: "one cannot but wish" passes the duty to "some society", not to himself. I think he means well, and the hint that they may "learn a worse" trade shows he understands the danger, but his tone keeps the boys at arm's length.
:::

::: example Weighing evidence against the statement
Statement: *"The writer shows nothing but admiration for the new factory."*
Evidence for: "a temple of industry"; the precise praise of the engine's "perfect regularity"; exclamations of wonder.
Evidence against: the brief, quieter sentence about the children "who did not look up as we passed"; the noise that "left us unable to speak".
Judgement: largely agree about the machines, but the writer's admiration stops at the people. The short, unexplained remark about the children is placed between two paragraphs of praise, as if he notices them and moves on. A careful reader evaluates that silence.
:::

::: example From retelling to evaluation
Retelling (3–4): "First he gets to the inn and it is dirty. Then he goes to bed and cannot sleep. Then in the morning he leaves."
Evaluation (7–8): "The statement that the writer finds the journey 'unbearable' is supported by the inn scene, where the precise, disgusted detail of the 'greasy' cloth makes us share his distaste; but his comic exaggeration of the snoring guest suggests he is enjoying the telling, so 'unbearable' is too strong." Evaluation judges the statement; retelling only summarises.
:::

What does A4 ask you to do, and which assessment objective does it test? (2)
---
Evaluate a statement about the 19th-century text and decide how far you agree (1); AO4, evaluating texts critically with references (1).
:::

::: q
What are the two bullet points in A4? (2)
---
What is said (1); how it is said (1).
:::

::: q
Give the specimen paper's alternative form of the A4 question. (1)
---
"What do you think and feel about [the writer's] views on…?"
:::

::: q
Why is "I completely agree" usually a weaker stance than a qualified one? (2)
---
Statements are often written to contain an overstatement or key word to test (1); a qualified stance shows you have weighed evidence on both sides, which is what persuasive, sustained evaluation requires (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between evaluation and retelling. (2)
---
Retelling summarises what happens in order (1); evaluation judges how far the text supports a view and how its methods create that effect, with evidence (1).
:::

::: q
How should you respond to a 19th-century view that seems prejudiced today? (2)
---
Understand and explain the view in its context and how the writer presents it (1), then give your own reasoned judgement of it, supported by evidence (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Answer the statement.** Every paragraph should say how far this part of the text supports it.
- **Key words matter.** "Nothing but", "increasingly", "only" and "vivid" are the hinges of the question.
- **Qualify.** Total agreement rarely reaches the top band.
- **Fuse what and how.** Content without method, or method without judgement, is half an answer.
- **Don't retell.** Order your answer through the text, but judge as you go.
- **Don't just condemn old attitudes.** Explain them, then evaluate them.
:::

No, it contradicts it (1): "most horribly" shows fear, and closing her eyes until they were across shows she could not bear to look (1).

"A good deal affected" is a restrained, polite phrase for what must be deep shock and grief (1); the understatement suggests strong feeling held back by Victorian manners, so the reader senses more emotion, not less (1).

::: test
Read this extract (original passage written in the style of an 1860s magazine account). *In the 1860s a writer for a popular magazine visited a large cotton mill in the north of England. This is part of the account he wrote.*

> Nothing that I had read had prepared me for the first sight of the great spinning room. It is a quarter of a mile in length, and filled from end to end with machines of such size and intricacy that the eye is lost among them; and every one of these thousand iron servants performs its task with a regularity that no human hand could equal. The noise is beyond description. My companion shouted into my ear, and I saw his lips move, but heard nothing.
>
> Among the machines move the operatives, chiefly women and girls, some of them, I should judge, not above ten years old. They are quick and silent, and did not look up as we passed. The air is warm and thick with a fine white dust which settles on the hair and clothing, so that the youngest seemed like little old women.
>
> I came away with the liveliest admiration for the genius of those who have built such a place. It is, in every sense, a wonder of the age.

"The writer is far more impressed by the machines than concerned about the workers." To what extent do you agree with this view? You should comment on:
- what is said
- how it is said.

You must refer to the text to support your comments. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: persuasive, sustained evaluation with a clear, qualified stance; perceptive comment on what is said and how; well-selected references across the account.
- 7–8: thoughtful, developed evaluation; clear judgement; well-chosen references to content and method.
- 5–6: some evaluation with relevant references; some comment on how it is said.
- 3–4: simple opinion, straightforward references; may retell.
- 1–2: basic comment. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content, supporting: the account opens and closes with admiration for the machines, a framing structure that buries the workers in the middle; hyperbole and awe ("Nothing that I had read had prepared me", "the eye is lost among them", "beyond description"); the metaphor "thousand iron servants" humanises the machines, giving them a dignity and loyalty that the people are not given; "no human hand could equal" ranks machine above human; the ending "liveliest admiration for the genius" praises the builders, not the workers; "a wonder of the age" is a confident final verdict. Qualifying: he does notice the workers and records disturbing details plainly (age "not above ten", "did not look up", the dust); the simile "like little old women" is a striking, even pitying image of childhood lost; the deafening noise is presented as overwhelming, which may imply the harshness of the conditions. Judgement: largely agree; his concern, if it is concern, is unspoken, and the matter-of-fact tone ("I should judge") contrasts sharply with his emotional praise of the machines; a top answer may argue the girls' description is more powerful precisely because he does not comment on it, though the writer seems not to realise it.
:::

::: test
Read this extract (original passage written in the style of an 1850s letter). *In 1853 a young man left England to search for gold in Australia. This is part of a letter he wrote to his parents.*

> Dear Father and Mother,
>
> You will be glad to hear that I am in good health and spirits, and have not yet been eaten by anything, though the flies here would do it if they could. We arrived at the diggings on the ninth after twelve days' walking, carrying all we own upon our backs, and have pitched our tent among some thousands of others, so that the whole valley looks like a fair that has lost its way.
>
> I will not pretend the work is easy. We dig from sunrise until we can no longer see, and stand all day in water to the knees; and in five weeks we have found gold to the value of perhaps six pounds, which is not a fortune. Two men from our ship have given up and gone back to the town. But there is a feeling here that tomorrow will be the day, and I confess I share it. Every man you meet has a story of a lump as big as a hen's egg found by somebody he knows.
>
> Do not fret for me. I am learning a great deal, and I would not have missed it for the world.
>
> Your loving son, William

"William's letter presents life at the gold diggings as a great adventure." To what extent do you agree with this view? You should comment on:
- what is said
- how it is said.

You must refer to the text to support your comments. (10)
---
Bands as for all A4 questions:
- 9–10: persuasive, sustained, qualified evaluation; perceptive on what and how; well-selected references.
- 7–8: thoughtful, developed evaluation with well-chosen references.
- 5–6: some evaluation with relevant references.
- 3–4: simple opinion, may retell.
- 1–2: basic comment. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content, supporting: cheerful opening ("good health and spirits") and comic exaggeration about the flies; the simile "like a fair that has lost its way" makes the camp lively and strange; the feeling "tomorrow will be the day", which he "confess[es]" he shares, conveys excitement and hope; the rumour of a lump "as big as a hen's egg" is vivid and tempting; the ending "would not have missed it for the world" is an emphatic, positive verdict. Qualifying: "I will not pretend the work is easy" marks a turn to honesty; the long sentence of hardship ("from sunrise until we can no longer see", water "to the knees") stresses exhaustion; facts undercut the hope (six pounds in five weeks, "which is not a fortune", dry understatement; two men have given up); the rumours are second-hand ("somebody he knows"), so the adventure may be partly illusion. Context and audience: a letter to parents, so "Do not fret for me" suggests he may be softening the truth; the humour may be a way of reassuring them. Judgement: largely agree that he presents it as an adventure, but a hard, unrewarding one, and his optimism may be partly for his parents' sake.
:::

::: test
Read this extract (original passage written in the style of an 1890s magazine article).

> It has lately become the fashion for young ladies to be seen upon bicycles, and I am bound to say that I cannot approve of it. A lady upon a bicycle, her face flushed and her hair escaping its pins, is not an elegant spectacle; and I am informed by a medical friend that violent exercise of this kind can only weaken the constitution of the gentler sex. It is said in its favour that the bicycle gives a girl a freedom she has never before enjoyed. I do not doubt it. That is precisely my objection. A young woman who may ride ten miles in an afternoon, without escort and without leave, will very soon wish to do a great many other things without leave also; and where, I ask, will that end?

What do you think and feel about the writer's views on women riding bicycles? You should comment on:
- what is said
- how it is said.

You must refer to the text to support your comments. (10)
---
Bands as for all A4 questions:
- 9–10: persuasive, sustained evaluation of the writer's views; perceptive personal response grounded in what is said and how; well-selected references; understands the views in context while judging them.
- 7–8: thoughtful, developed response with clear judgement and well-chosen references.
- 5–6: some evaluation with relevant references; some comment on methods.
- 3–4: simple opinion with straightforward reference.
- 1–2: basic comment. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content: the writer's disapproval is clear from the start ("I cannot approve"), presented as polite but firm; objections based on appearance ("not an elegant spectacle", the mocking image of a flushed face and loose hair) suggest women are valued for looks; the appeal to an unnamed "medical friend" is unsupported evidence and reflects period assumptions about "the gentler sex"; the turn "I do not doubt it. That is precisely my objection." is the most revealing moment: short, emphatic sentences admit that the real fear is women's freedom; the repetition of "without leave" and the rhetorical question "where, I ask, will that end?" show anxiety about women's independence, framed as a slippery slope. Personal response: many readers will find the views patronising and controlling, and may find the writer unintentionally comic; a strong answer recognises the context (a time of debate about women's independence) and that his honesty about his real objection undermines his argument, since the "freedom" he fears is what makes the bicycle valuable. Reward a sustained, reasoned judgement, whether critical, amused or sympathetic to his period.
:::

::: test
Explain why "the writer's tone is dramatic" is not yet evaluation, and how to make it evaluative. (2)
---
It identifies a feature but makes no judgement about the statement or the effect (1); it becomes evaluation when it says how far the dramatic tone supports or challenges the statement, e.g. "the dramatic tone makes the danger feel real, which strongly supports the view that the journey was perilous" (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the letter form might affect your judgement of a statement about a 19th-century writer's feelings. (2)
---
A letter is written to a known reader, often family (1), so the writer may hide or soften feelings to avoid causing worry; a strong answer considers that the cheerful surface may not show the whole truth (1).
:::

::: test
Give two ways you could signal a qualified stance in the first sentence of an A4 answer. (2)
---
Any two: "I largely agree, but…"; "This is true of the opening, though not of the ending"; "The statement is partly convincing…"; "'Nothing but' goes too far, because…"; "I agree up to a point…" (1 each).
:::

The exclamation and the admission that she "could not speak" show she is overcome with awe (1); the comic detail that even her talkative aunt fell silent emphasises how extraordinary the sight was, strongly supporting the statement (1).

The statement usually concerns the text as a whole, and evidence that supports or qualifies it may appear in different places, especially the ending (1); judging across the whole text allows a sustained, balanced evaluation, which the top band rewards (1).
