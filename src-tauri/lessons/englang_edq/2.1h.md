## What it is

This topic is about the **AO5** marks in Component 1 Section B: 24 of the 40. AO5 asks you to communicate **clearly, effectively and imaginatively**, and to **organise** ideas with structural and grammatical features so the writing is coherent and cohesive. The spec's subject content adds **using language imaginatively and creatively** and **creating emotional impact**. In the top band, examiners look for **plot and characterisation developed with detail, originality and imagination**, and a narrative that is **sophisticated** and **fully engages** the reader. That is a description of craft, and craft can be learned.

**1. Narrative voice.** Decide who is telling the story and keep it consistent.

| Choice | Strengths | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| **First person, past tense** ("I waited") | Intimate; thoughts come naturally; suits "write about a time when" | Can slide into "I felt… I felt…"; the narrator's personality must come through |
| **Third person, past tense** ("She waited") | Control; you can describe the main character from outside and inside | Can feel distant unless you stay close to one character |
| **Present tense** ("I wait") | Immediacy, tension | Very easy to slip back into past tense: a costly AO6 and AO5 error |

A **voice** is more than a pronoun. It is the attitude in the sentences: wry, anxious, plain, precise. "I was nervous about the interview" has no voice. "I had ironed my shirt twice and was considering a third attempt" has one, and it shows nerves at the same time.

**2. Show, don't tell: characterisation.** Reveal people through:
- **Action and gesture**: "He rolled the ring round and round his finger" (anxiety, perhaps a marriage under strain).
- **Objects**: what a character keeps, carries or throws away. A mother who keeps every school report in a biscuit tin tells us more than "she was proud".
- **Dialogue, sparingly**: a few lines that reveal character or tension. What people avoid saying matters as much as what they say.
- **Other characters' reactions**: the room going quiet when someone enters.

Name an emotion at most once or twice in the whole story; let the reader infer the rest.

**3. Setting and atmosphere inside the action.** Description should **serve the story**, not stop it. Weave setting into movement and feeling: not a paragraph of weather followed by a paragraph of plot, but *"She walked out into rain so fine it seemed to be hanging in the air, waiting for her to make up her mind."* The setting reflects her state, and the story keeps moving. Choose two or three **precise, unexpected details** rather than a catalogue of senses.

**4. Pace.** Control how fast time passes on the page.
- **Slow the key moment**: spend a full paragraph on five seconds at the turning point, with physical detail, thought and sound.
- **Compress the rest**: "The next three days passed in a blur of packing" covers time in a line.
- **Use time shifts sparingly**: one brief flashback (two or three sentences, clearly signalled) can deepen a character; more than that breaks the shape.
- **Vary sentence and paragraph length**: a one-line paragraph at the turning point stops the reader.

**5. Dialogue: punctuation and restraint.** New speaker, new line. Punctuation inside the speech marks. Use "said" mostly; let the words carry the tone. Three or four exchanges are usually plenty in 600 words. Dialogue that only passes on information ("Hello." "Hello, how are you?") wastes words.

**6. Endings.** The strongest endings **echo or reverse** the opening, end on an **image or action**, and leave something for the reader to feel. Avoid: "it was all a dream", sudden death, "and then I woke up", a spelt-out moral, and the rushed summary ending ("and we all lived happily").

**7. Originality.** Originality rarely means a strange plot. It usually means an **unexpected angle on an ordinary situation**: the wedding told by the waiter; the race told by the girl who came last but beat her own time; the storm seen only through a dog's behaviour. Ordinary material, seen precisely, feels original.

::: terms
- **Narrative voice**: the personality and attitude of the storyteller, shown through how the sentences sound.
- **Show, don't tell**: revealing feelings and character through action, detail and dialogue instead of naming them.
- **Characterisation**: the methods a writer uses to create and develop a character.
- **Pace**: how quickly the story seems to move, controlled by detail, sentence length and time shifts.
- **Flashback**: a brief scene from earlier in time, set inside the main story.
- **Atmosphere**: the mood a setting creates, such as tense, peaceful or eerie.
- **Pathetic fallacy**: setting or weather that reflects a character's feelings.
- **Dialogue**: the words characters speak, set out with speech punctuation.
- **Cliché**: an overused phrase or idea that has lost its force, such as "my heart was pounding".
- **Emotional impact**: the feeling a piece of writing creates in its reader.
:::

::: method
1. Choose narrator and tense, and keep them steady. Give the narrator an attitude.
2. Open inside the situation with an action, image or line of speech; ground the reader in who and where within three sentences.
3. Introduce the main character through a gesture, an object or a few words of dialogue, not a list of adjectives.
4. Weave setting into the action, using two or three precise details that reflect mood.
5. Compress the build-up; **slow down** at the turning point with detail, thought and short sentences.
6. Use dialogue for tension or character, three or four exchanges at most, correctly punctuated.
7. End on an image or action that echoes or reverses the opening. Do not explain the moral.
:::

::: example A grade-9 extract: "The Photograph" (about 330 words of a 550-word story)
The photograph had been on the hall table for nine years, and I had stopped seeing it. That is what happens to photographs. They start as a window and end up as wallpaper.

It was only because Mum asked me to dust that I picked it up at all. Two people on a beach, squinting: a young man with too much hair, and a girl in a yellow raincoat, laughing at something outside the frame. I had always assumed the girl was Mum. I turned it over. On the back, in handwriting I did not recognise, someone had written: *Pen-y-Cwm, 1989. Before.*

Before what?

I carried it into the kitchen. Mum was peeling potatoes at the sink with the radio on, and she did not turn round when I came in, so I said nothing for a while and watched the curls of peel drop into the bowl. Then I put the photograph on the draining board beside her.

She stopped peeling. She did not pick it up. The radio went on cheerfully about traffic on the M4.

"That's your Auntie Bethan," she said eventually. "Not me. People always thought we were the same."

"I didn't know you had a sister."

"No," she said. "Well." And she began peeling again, faster, so that a long spiral of skin broke and fell in two.

*Commentary:* The **voice** is reflective and wry ("start as a window and end up as wallpaper"), giving the narrator a mind of her own. The **opening** is inside the situation, and the photograph becomes a **symbol** at once. **Withholding** ("Before.") and the one-line paragraph "Before what?" create mystery and control pace. Characterisation is all **shown**: the mother not turning round, not picking it up, peeling "faster". The cheerful radio is an **ironic contrast** with the tension. Dialogue is sparse and loaded ("No. Well."). The broken spiral of peel is a precise **image** that carries the mother's feeling without naming it. This is top-band AO5 control: original angle, developed character, sophisticated pacing.
:::

::: example Telling rewritten as showing
Telling: "My grandfather was a proud man who found it hard to accept help."

Showing: "When the nurse offered him her arm, Grandad looked at it as if she had offered him a used tissue, and walked the length of the corridor on his own, very slowly, stopping twice to examine noticeboards he could not possibly read without his glasses."
The second version shows pride (the disdainful look), stubbornness (walking alone), and frailty concealed by dignity (the pretence of reading). The reader infers all three.
:::

::: example Slowing the key moment
Rushed: "I opened the envelope and I had got in. I was so happy."

Slowed: "The envelope was thinner than I had expected, which I decided was a bad sign. I opened it at the kitchen table with a butter knife, carefully, as if the letter might be damaged by bad news. *We are pleased.* I read the three words four times before they arranged themselves into a sentence. Somewhere behind me the kettle clicked off, and I realised I had been holding my breath."
Five seconds of story now take a paragraph: physical detail, thought, a sound, a held breath. The emotion is never named, yet it is unmistakable.
:::

::: example Fixing a clichéd ending
Cliché: "Suddenly I woke up. It had all been a dream. I promised myself I would never go near that house again."

Better: "I left the key where I had found it, under the flowerpot, and walked home the long way, past the house, without looking up at the window." The ending stays inside the story, echoes an earlier object, and leaves a feeling (unease, respect, loss) for the reader to hold.
:::

::: q
Give one advantage and one risk of writing in the present tense. (2)
---
Advantage: immediacy and tension (1). Risk: easy to slip into the past tense, which damages control and accuracy (1).
:::

::: q
List three ways to show character without naming traits. (3)
---
Any three: action or gesture; objects a character keeps or uses; dialogue; other characters' reactions; what a character avoids saying.
:::

::: q
How should description be used in a Section B narrative? (2)
---
Woven into the action and feelings of the story (1), using a few precise details that create mood without stopping the plot (1).
:::

::: q
How do you control pace at the turning point? (2)
---
Slow down: spend a paragraph on a few seconds, with physical detail, thought and sound (1); use short sentences or a one-line paragraph to stop the reader (1).
:::

::: q
What makes a story "original" in the AO5 sense? (2)
---
An unexpected angle or precise, fresh detail on an ordinary situation (1), rather than a bizarre plot; e.g. a wedding told by the waiter (1).
:::

::: q
Give two rules for dialogue in a Section B story. (2)
---
Any two: new speaker, new line; punctuation inside the speech marks; use it sparingly; make it reveal character or tension; mostly use "said".
:::

::: watch
- **Over-plotting** is the classic failure: no room for detail or character.
- **Tense slips** (past to present and back) cost AO5 control and AO6 accuracy.
- **Naming emotions** repeatedly ("I was scared… I was terrified") flattens the writing; show them instead.
- **Description that stops the story** reads as a descriptive piece and caps the mark.
- **Too much dialogue**, or dialogue that only passes information, wastes words.
- **Clichés** ("heart pounding", "blood ran cold", "it was all a dream") signal a lack of imagination.
- **Rushed endings**: start the final paragraph on time.
:::

::: test
Rewrite this sentence to show, not tell: "Megan was very nervous before her driving test." (2)
---
For example: "Megan adjusted the mirror, then adjusted it back, then checked her seatbelt for the third time while the examiner was still writing her name." (1 for physical behaviour replacing the label; 1 for precise, controlled detail).
:::

::: test
Add one detail to this sentence that reveals something about the character: "Mr Davies opened the shop at eight o'clock." (2)
---
For example: "Mr Davies opened the shop at eight o'clock exactly, by the church clock, and turned the sign so that it hung perfectly straight." (1 for the added detail; 1 because it reveals a trait, here precision or pride).
:::

---
Weak because it introduces rather than starts the story, tells the reader what to feel, and uses a cliché (1). Rewrite, e.g.: "The coach had already left by the time I reached the school gates, and my PE kit was on it." (1 for starting inside the situation; 1 for a voice and a clear problem).

::: test
Cut and sharpen this paragraph to under 40 words, keeping the key moment: "It was a really really dark and scary night and I was very very scared. I walked down the road. It was very cold and very dark. Then I heard a noise behind me and I was terrified and my heart was pounding." (4)

For example: "The road was unlit. Frost had stiffened the hedges, and my breath hung in front of me like a question. Then, behind me, a twig snapped, and I stopped walking." (1 for removing repetition and intensifiers; 1 for removing clichés; 1 for precise, original detail; 1 for controlled sentence variety and a tense ending).

::: test
Continue this story in about 100 words, in the same first-person voice: "The note was taped to my locker, folded into a perfect square. Nobody folds things that carefully unless they mean it." (6)
---
Award up to 6: 5–6 consistent, distinctive first-person voice; plot advanced with a clear development or tension; precise detail and controlled sentences; accurate punctuation. 3–4 voice mostly consistent; some development; some precise detail. 1–2 voice lost or tense inconsistent; little development; errors. Credit continuations that show character through action (the narrator's reluctance to open it, glancing round the corridor), withholding, and a short sentence for impact.

::: test
Explain how pathetic fallacy can create atmosphere without stopping the story, using an example of your own. (2)
---
The setting reflects the character's feelings while the action continues (1); e.g. "She signed the papers as the first heavy drops of rain began to tap at the office window." (1).
:::

::: test
Identify and correct the dialogue errors: "I'm not going she said. You can't make me." (2)
---
"I'm not going," she said. "You can't make me." (1 for speech marks around both spoken parts; 1 for the comma inside the speech marks and the capital for the new sentence.)
:::

::: test
Rewrite this ending to avoid the cliché: "Then I woke up and realised it had all been a dream." (2)
---
For example, an ending that stays in the story and echoes an earlier detail: "I hung the key back on its hook by the door, where it swung for a moment and then was still." (1 for removing the dream device; 1 for ending on a meaningful image or action).
:::

::: test
Write a short flashback of two or three sentences that deepens a character, to be placed inside a story about a man selling his late father's boat. (3)
---
For example: "When Owen was seven, his father had let him steer the boat across the bay, his big hands hovering an inch above Owen's on the wheel, never quite touching. 'You've got it,' he'd said. Owen had not had it at all." (1 for a clear signal of a time shift; 1 for brevity; 1 for detail that deepens the relationship and links to the present story).
:::

::: test
Explain why a story told from an unexpected angle might score more highly for AO5. Give an example. (2)
---
It shows originality and imagination, features of the top band (1); e.g. a school prize-giving told from the point of view of the caretaker setting out the chairs (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between compressing time and slowing time in a narrative, with an example of each. (2)
---
Compressing covers a long period in a sentence, e.g. "The rest of the term passed in a grey blur of revision." (1). Slowing spends many words on a short moment, e.g. a paragraph on the seconds before a door opens (1).
:::
