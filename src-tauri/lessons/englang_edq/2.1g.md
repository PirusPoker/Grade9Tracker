## What it is

**Section B** of Component 1 is a single piece of **creative prose writing** worth **40 marks**, half the paper. You choose **one of four titles** and write about **450–600 words**. The paper advises **10 minutes planning and 35 minutes writing**. It is marked for:
- **AO5, communication and organisation: 24 marks.** Is the writing clear, controlled, imaginative and well organised? Are plot and characterisation developed?
- **AO6, vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation: 16 marks.** Is it accurate, varied and precise? (See topic 2.1i.)

**It must be a story.** The spec says the response should be a **narrative or recount**. A purely **descriptive** piece, a poem, a play script or a diary of random thoughts cannot reach the full mark range, however beautifully written. Description belongs **inside** your story, not instead of it.

**The four title types.** Every paper so far has offered a mix of these:

| Type | How it appears | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| **A single title** | A short noun phrase, e.g. *The Spare Key* | Free to interpret: literal, metaphorical, first or third person. The title must be clearly relevant to the story you tell, ideally in more than one sense |
| **"Write a story which begins:"** | A given first sentence, e.g. *I should never have opened the gate.* | Copy the sentence **exactly** as your first line. The story must grow from it, so the sentence should matter, not be dropped and forgotten |
| **"Write a story which ends:"** | A given last sentence, e.g. *...and the house was quiet again.* | Copy the sentence **exactly** as your last line. Plan **backwards**: what situation makes that line land with meaning? |
| **"Write about a time when…"** | A recount prompt, e.g. *Write about a time when you had to apologise.* | A first-person recount, real or imagined. You can invent freely; examiners mark the writing, not the truth. It still needs a shape: a situation, a turning point, a reflective ending |

**Choosing in under two minutes.** Read all four, then ask of each: *Can I see one specific situation, one place, one or two characters and an ending?* Choose the title where you can see the **ending** fastest. Do not choose the "exciting" one if you cannot see how it ends; choose the one you can control. A quiet, personal recount handled with precision regularly outscores an action thriller.

**Why one focused incident beats an epic.** 450–600 words is about **four to six paragraphs**. That is room for one scene told well, perhaps with a brief look back. Stories that cover a whole holiday, a war, or a kidnapping and rescue end up as lists of events ("then… then…"), with no space for the **detail, characterisation and control** that AO5's top band asks for. The rule of thumb is: **one situation, two characters at most, a short span of time, one turning point**.

**The ten-minute plan.** Keep it to a few lines, but make each line a decision:
1. **Title choice and interpretation** (one line): what the story is *really* about, e.g. *"The Spare Key": a girl lets herself into her grandfather's house after his move to a care home.*
2. **Narrator and tense**: first or third person, past tense (the safest for control).
3. **Five beats**, one per paragraph:
   - **Opening**: start inside the situation, with an image, action or line of speech.
   - **Build**: establish character and what they want or fear.
   - **Complication**: something gets in the way, or something is discovered.
   - **Turning point**: the moment that changes things, written slowly.
   - **Ending**: an image or action that echoes the opening, ideally with a shift in meaning.
4. **Three precise details** you will use: an object, a sound, a gesture.
5. **Two or three ambitious words or effects** to deploy (and spell correctly).

**Making the ending deliberate.** Plan the ending first. A strong ending usually **echoes** the opening (a **cyclical** structure) or **reverses** it, and it trusts the reader rather than explaining a moral. Avoid "it was all a dream", sudden deaths and "I will never forget that day" as the final line.

**Planning for "begins" and "ends" titles.** For a given opening, write it down first, then ask: *Who says this, and why should they not have done it?* For a given ending, ask: *What situation makes this sentence surprising, ironic or moving?* The best responses make the given sentence carry **more meaning** than it seems to on the paper.

**Timing in the exam.** 10 minutes planning; about 30 minutes writing; the last 5 minutes proofreading for AO6. Start your final paragraph by the 30-minute mark of writing, whatever happens.

::: terms
- **Narrative**: a piece of writing that tells a story, with characters, events and a shape.
- **Recount**: a first-person account of an experience, real or imagined, told as a story.
- **Title interpretation**: the way a writer reads a title, literally or metaphorically, to shape a story.
- **Given sentence**: an opening or closing line supplied by the task, which must be used exactly.
- **Turning point**: the moment in a story where the situation, a character or the reader's understanding changes.
- **Cyclical structure**: an ending that returns to an image or idea from the opening.
- **Beat**: one stage or step in the plan of a story, usually one paragraph.
- **AO5**: communication and organisation, worth 24 of the 40 marks in Section B.
- **AO6**: vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation, worth 16 of the 40 marks.
- **Focus**: the narrowing of a story to one situation, place and short period of time.
:::

::: method
1. Read all four titles. For each, try to see **one situation and an ending** in under thirty seconds.
2. Choose the title you can **control**, not the most dramatic one.
3. Write a one-line interpretation: what the story is really about.
4. Decide narrator and tense.
5. Plan five beats: opening, build, complication, turning point, ending. Write the **ending first**.
6. If the title is "begins" or "ends", copy the given sentence exactly into the plan and build the story around it.
7. Note three precise details and two or three ambitious words or effects.
8. Write for about 30 minutes, start the ending on time, then proofread for 5 minutes.
:::

::: example Choosing between four titles
Titles: (a) *The Spare Key*; (b) *Write a story which begins: I should never have opened the gate.*; (c) *Write about a time when you had to apologise.*; (d) *Write a story which ends: ...and the house was quiet again.*

Thinking aloud: (b) suggests danger or a dog, but the obvious stories are chases and I cannot see an ending. (c) is a recount: I can see a scene with my younger brother and a broken model, but the ending might be preachy. (a) gives me an instant situation and ending: a girl uses her grandfather's spare key to clear his house after he moves into a home, finds the key's hiding place has a note in it, and leaves the key there. One place, two characters (one absent), one afternoon, one turning point. **Choose (a).** Decision made in under two minutes.
:::

::: example A full plan for "The Spare Key"
Interpretation: the key is literal and also stands for trust between a girl and her grandfather.
Narrator: first person, past tense.
1. Opening: "The key was where it had always been, under the third flowerpot, wrapped in a sandwich bag." I let myself into Grandad's house the week after he moved to the care home.
2. Build: the house without him; his chair, the radio set to his station; I am here to fetch his photographs.
3. Complication: the photographs are not where Mum said; I search and find a shoebox labelled with my name.
4. Turning point (slow): inside, every birthday card I ever made him, and a note: "For when you come to fetch things. Keep the key."
5. Ending: I lock up, then put the key back under the third flowerpot, "where it had always been". Echoes the opening; the meaning has changed: I am not ready to let the house go.
Details: the sandwich bag, the radio's crackle, the smell of pipe tobacco. Ambitious effects: a one-line paragraph at the turning point; a semicolon in the ending.
:::

::: example Planning backwards from a given ending
Title: *Write a story which ends: ...and the house was quiet again.*
Weak idea: a burglary, a fight, police arrive, "and the house was quiet again" (the line is merely the end of noise).
Strong idea: a family's last chaotic Christmas before the eldest daughter emigrates; the story is all noise, crackers, arguments, laughter; it ends after she has left for the airport, with the narrator, her younger brother, alone in the kitchen: "...and the house was quiet again." The given sentence now carries loss: quiet is no longer peace but absence. Planning backwards turned a flat line into the story's emotional pay-off.
:::

::: example Turning a "time when" prompt into a shaped recount
Prompt: *Write about a time when you had to apologise.*
Shape: (1) Open at the door of a neighbour's house, holding a cake I did not bake. (2) Flashback in two sentences: the football through her greenhouse a week ago, and my denial. (3) She opens the door; she is older and frailer than I remembered. (4) Turning point: she already knew it was me, and has kept the ball for me. (5) Ending: walking home with the ball, the apology still feeling unfinished; next Saturday I go back to help mend the glass. A recount still needs a turning point and an ending that means something.
:::

::: q
How many marks is Component 1 Section B worth, and how are they divided? (2)
---
40 marks (1): 24 for AO5 (communication and organisation) and 16 for AO6 (vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation) (1).
:::

::: q
Why will a purely descriptive piece not reach the full mark range? (1)
---
The task requires a narrative or recount; description should be part of a story, not a substitute for one.
:::

::: q
What must you do with a given opening or closing sentence? (2)
---
Use it exactly as written (1), as the first or last line, and make it meaningful to the story rather than an afterthought (1).
:::

::: q
How many words should you aim for, and how long should you plan? (2)
---
About 450–600 words (1); about 10 minutes planning (1).
:::

::: q
Give four limits that keep a Section B story focused. (2)
---
One situation; one or two characters (1); a short span of time; one turning point (1).
:::

::: q
Why should you plan the ending first? (2)
---
A deliberate ending shapes everything before it (1), and knowing it prevents a rushed or clichéd conclusion such as "it was all a dream" (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Narrative, not description.** A beautiful description with no story cannot reach the top.
- **Copy given sentences exactly.** Changing "the" to "my" in a given ending is a slip examiners notice.
- **Choose control over drama.** The title you can finish well beats the one with the most exciting premise.
- **One incident.** Covering weeks of events produces summary, not storytelling.
- **Plan briefly, but plan.** Ten minutes of decisions saves the story from drifting.
- **No clichéd endings.** Dreams, sudden deaths and morals spelt out all lose AO5 marks.
- **Keep five minutes** at the end for AO6 checks.
:::

---
For example: "A girl spends one summer afternoon with an old man who mends bicycles at the end of her street; she means to return his spanner the next day, but by morning his shop is shut and the sign gone." (1 for a situation that gives the line emotional weight; 1 for a clear, focused plan).
:::

::: test
Plan five beats for the title "The Last Bus" (one line each). (5)
---
One mark per coherent beat in a logical order, e.g. (1) Opening: a girl runs for the last bus home and misses it. (2) Build: she is alone at a dark stop, phone dead, having lied to her parents about where she was. (3) Complication: a car pulls up; it is her father. (4) Turning point: in the silent car, he says he has known about the lie for weeks. (5) Ending: they stop at the chip shop they used to visit when she was small, echoing an earlier detail. Award marks for focus, a turning point and a deliberate ending.
:::

::: test
Write an opening sentence for a story that must end: "...and that was the last time I trusted the weather forecast." (2)
---
For example: "The sun was so bright on the morning of Dad's fortieth birthday picnic that Mum let us leave our coats in the car." (1 for a sentence that sets up the ending; 1 for a controlled, vivid start with a hint of irony).
:::

::: test
Explain how to approach "Write about a time when you were proud of someone." (3)
---
It invites a first-person recount, real or imagined (1); focus on one specific moment rather than a general account of the person (1); give it a story shape with a turning point and an ending that reveals why the pride mattered (1).
:::

::: test
Give two endings to avoid in Section B and explain why. (2)
---
Any two with reason, e.g. "it was all a dream" undoes the story and seems lazy; a sudden death to end the plot is melodramatic; spelling out a moral ("I learned my lesson") is heavy-handed and distrusts the reader (1 each).
:::

::: test
What should you do in the last five minutes of the Section B time, and why? (2)
---
Proofread (1): check spelling, punctuation, tense and paragraphing, because AO6 is 16 of the 40 marks (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what is meant by interpreting a single title "in more than one sense", using "The Spare Key" as an example. (2)
---
Using the title literally and figuratively (1): e.g. a real spare key hidden for a family member, which also stands for trust or belonging between them (1).
:::

::: test
Choose one of the following titles for your writing. You should aim to write about 450–600 words. 24 marks are awarded for communication and organisation; 16 marks are awarded for vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation.

(a) The Spare Key.
(b) Write a story which begins: I should never have opened the gate.
(c) Write about a time when you had to apologise.
(d) Write a story which ends: ...and the house was quiet again.
(40)
---
Award two marks: AO5 out of 24 and AO6 out of 16. The response must be a narrative or recount; purely descriptive or other forms cannot reach the full range. Given sentences must be used exactly.
AO5 (communication and organisation, 24):
- 20–24: fully coherent and controlled; plot and characters developed with detail, originality and imagination; sophisticated organisation that engages the reader throughout; structural and grammatical features used ambitiously; precise, ambitious communication.
- 15–19: clearly controlled and coherent; plot and characters developed with some detail; clearly organised, engaging, with some ambition in structure.
- 10–14: mostly controlled; some development of plot and character; organised in paragraphs with some structural features; clear communication.
- 5–9: some control; limited development; simple organisation; communication sometimes unclear.
- 1–4: basic, with little control or organisation.
AO6 (vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation, 16):
- 14–16: appropriate, effective variation of sentences; virtually all constructions accurate; a range of punctuation used confidently; virtually all spelling correct, including complex words; tense and agreement secure; wide, well-judged vocabulary.
- 11–13: varied sentences; mostly accurate; a range of punctuation mostly accurate; most spelling correct; tense secure; vocabulary chosen for effect.
- 7–10: some variety; generally accurate sentences; some range of punctuation; spelling of common words accurate; tense mostly consistent.
- 4–6: limited variety; some control; basic punctuation; simple words spelt correctly.
- 1–3: little control; limited punctuation; frequent errors.
Indicative: (a) a key used literally and as a symbol of trust, home or loss; (b) the sentence used first and exactly, with a consequence that develops character rather than a chase; (c) a first-person recount with a clear incident, turning point and reflection shown rather than preached; (d) a story planned towards the ending so that "quiet" carries meaning (absence, peace, relief, grief). Reward one focused situation, a deliberate turning point, an ending that echoes or reverses the opening, and detail that develops character.
:::
