## What it is

**A2** is the 10-mark question on **Text 1**, the 21st-century text. It asks **how the writer tries to** achieve an aim: show something about a person or experience, persuade the reader, or make an account vivid. It tests **AO2**: how writers use **language and structure** to achieve effects and influence readers. The pattern (paraphrased) is:

> [Context sentence, e.g. "X is described as very determined."] How does the writer try to show this?
> You should comment on:
> - what is said
> - the use of language, tone and structure
> - other ways the writer tries to show [the aim].

Typical aims in live papers: how hard or dangerous an experience was; that someone loves their work; that a rescue was both dangerous and dramatic; that a person is inspiring; how an account is made vivid and interesting. **The aim is your thesis.** Every point must explain how a detail serves *that* aim, not language in general.

**The three bullets are the mark scheme.** Cover all three across the answer:

| Bullet | What it means | Examples of what to discuss |
|---|---|---|
| **What is said** | the content the writer chooses to include | events, facts, the person's actions and words, difficulties overcome |
| **Language, tone and structure** | how it is written | word choice, imagery, emotive language, tone and how it is created, the opening and ending, shifts, sentence lengths, order of information |
| **Other ways** | the non-fiction methods particular to journalism | quotations from the subject or witnesses, expert opinion, facts and figures, anecdote, headline and standfirst, subheadings, time markers, the writer's own presence or reaction |

**Tone.** A2 names tone explicitly, so say what it is and **how it is created**. Common tones: admiring, affectionate, humorous, urgent, dramatic, sceptical, awed, matter-of-fact. Tone is built from word choice ("remarkable", "simply"), sentence forms (short, punchy statements), and the balance of material (whose quotations, how much detail on difficulty). Tone can **shift**: light at the start, serious in the middle, uplifting at the end.

**Structure in non-fiction.** Think about the whole article as a design:
- **Headline and standfirst**: the angle in a few words, often with wordplay.
- **The opening**: an anecdote, a dramatic moment, a striking fact, a scene-setting image. Why start here?
- **Order of information**: chronological, or a dramatic moment first then a flashback; problems before triumph.
- **Placing of quotations**: the subject's own words often come at emotional peaks.
- **Paragraph and sentence length**: one-line paragraphs for impact.
- **The ending**: a quotation, a look ahead, a circular return to the opening image.

**Terminology for non-fiction.** Use terms where they sharpen the point: **direct address**, **emotive language**, **statistics**, **expert opinion**, **rhetorical question**, **triple (list of three)**, **juxtaposition**, **hyperbole**, **anecdote**, **superlative**, **colloquial language**, **metaphor**, **semantic field**.

**How it is banded.** In plain terms:

| Band | What the examiner sees |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | **Subtle, perceptive** analysis; a wide range of well-chosen details from across the text; precise comments on language, tone and structure; terminology fluent and accurate |
| 7–8 | **Detailed** analysis of a range of methods; clear understanding of how they serve the aim; tone and structure included |
| 5–6 | **Some** explanation of methods with relevant references; some comment on language or tone |
| 3–4 | **Simple** comments, often describing what is said; some reference |
| 1–2 | Identifies one or two basic points |

**The range rule.** A2 rewards **range across the whole text**. An answer that analyses three words in the first paragraph brilliantly will still be capped, because it ignores the structure and the "other ways". Plan six to eight points spread across the beginning, middle and end.

**Timing.** About **12–13 minutes**. Write continuously; no introduction needed beyond a sentence naming the tone and overall approach.

::: terms
- **Aim**: what the question says the writer is trying to show or do, which every point must serve.
- **Tone**: the attitude or voice created by the writer's choices, such as admiring, urgent or humorous.
- **Standfirst**: the short introductory paragraph under a headline that sums up the story's angle.
- **Anecdote**: a short personal story used to engage the reader or illustrate a point.
- **Expert opinion**: the view of a qualified person, quoted to give authority to a point.
- **Emotive language**: words chosen to stir the reader's feelings, such as pity, anger or admiration.
- **Statistic**: a number or figure used as evidence, often to show scale or achievement.
- **Juxtaposition**: placing two contrasting ideas or details close together for effect.
- **Direct quotation**: the subject's or a witness's own words, which add authenticity and voice.
- **Circular structure**: an ending that returns to an image or idea from the opening.
- **Semantic field**: a group of words linked by meaning, such as words to do with battle or danger.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **aim** in the question. It is the thesis of your answer.
2. Reread the text and mark **six to eight details** from the start, middle and end.
3. For each detail, decide which bullet it serves: what is said, language/tone/structure, other ways.
4. Open with one sentence naming the **tone** and the overall approach.
5. Work through the text in order: point, short quotation, analysis of the word or method, how it serves the aim.
6. Include at least one comment on **structure** (opening, shift, ending) and two on **other ways** (quotations, figures, headline).
7. Finish with a sentence on how the ending leaves the reader feeling about the aim.
:::

::: example A 9–10 opening and two points
Text (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century newspaper feature): *"Sixty miles from the nearest town, with the generator coughing and the wind tearing at the tent, Dr Leah Marsh is stitching a cut on a shepherd's hand by torchlight. 'This,' she says, without looking up, 'is the best job in the world.'"*

Question: How does the writer try to show that Dr Marsh loves her work?

Answer extract: The writer's tone is quietly admiring, and the article builds its picture of her love for the job by setting it against hardship. The opening drops us into a scene of isolation and discomfort: "sixty miles from the nearest town" is a fact that establishes remoteness, while the personified generator "coughing" and wind "tearing" make the setting hostile and almost violent. Placing her **direct quotation** straight after this list of difficulties creates a deliberate juxtaposition: "the best job in the world" is a superlative that would sound like a cliché anywhere else, but here it reveals genuine devotion. The detail "without looking up" is small but telling: she is too absorbed in her patient to perform for the journalist, so her love is shown in action rather than claimed.
(Perceptive, analyses structure, other ways and language precisely in service of the aim: 9–10 level.)
:::

::: example Explaining tone
Text (original, in the style of a 21st-century magazine column): *"Nobody warns you about the goats. You plan for the hills, the rain and the blisters. You do not plan for the goat that eats your map."*

Tone and how it is created: the tone is **comic and self-mocking**. The writer uses a **triple** of predictable hardships ("hills, the rain and the blisters") and then undercuts it with the absurd goat; the repetition of "You plan… You do not plan" sets up the anticlimax; the short, deadpan sentences let the joke land without fuss. The humour makes the walker likeable and makes the account entertaining, which serves an aim such as "make the account interesting for readers".
:::

::: example Commenting on "other ways"
Text (original, in the style of a 21st-century news report): *"'In twenty years I've never seen water rise that fast,' said station manager Ian Pryce. The crew had eleven minutes to reach the car before it was swept away."*

Other ways: the **expert quotation** from someone with "twenty years" of experience adds authority: if a veteran was shocked, the danger was exceptional. The **figure** "eleven minutes" creates a countdown that makes the rescue feel urgent and precise. Together they show the rescue was dangerous without the journalist having to say so, which makes the account more convincing.
:::

::: example From 5–6 to 7–8
5–6: "The writer uses statistics like '400 miles' to show it was hard. The writer also uses quotes to make it interesting."
7–8: "The figure '400 miles', placed in the first line, establishes the sheer scale of the challenge before we meet the walker, so every later detail about blisters and rain is read against that distance. Her own words, 'I cried most mornings', are given a paragraph to themselves; the plain, honest admission makes the hardship real and makes her persistence more admirable." The difference: precise explanation of **how** each method affects the reader and serves the aim.
:::

::: q
What are the three bullet points in A2? (3)
---
What is said (1); the use of language, tone and structure (1); other ways the writer tries to achieve the aim (1).
:::

::: q
Give three "other ways" a journalist might use to show that an experience was dangerous. (3)
---
Any three: quotations from people involved or witnesses; expert opinion; facts and figures (times, distances, numbers); anecdote; a dramatic headline or standfirst; subheadings; time markers that create a countdown; the writer's own reaction.
:::

::: q
Why does A2 reward range across the whole text? (2)
---
The band descriptors look for a wide range of well-chosen details and methods (1); an answer that only covers one part of the text cannot show how the whole text, including its structure, works towards the aim (1).
:::

::: q
Identify the tone of this sentence and explain how it is created: "And then, against every prediction, every expert and every sensible bet, she won." (3)
---
Triumphant, admiring or dramatic tone (1); the triple "every prediction, every expert and every sensible bet" builds up the odds against her (1); delaying the short main clause "she won" to the end gives it climactic impact (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one effect of starting an article with an anecdote. (2)
---
It engages the reader immediately with a specific, human moment (1), making the topic personal and vivid before facts or arguments are given (1).
:::

::: q
What should the first sentence of an A2 answer do? (1)
---
Name the overall tone and approach the writer uses to achieve the aim, so the answer has a clear thesis.
:::

::: watch
- **Serve the aim.** Every point should explain how a detail shows what the question names.
- **All three bullets.** Answers on language alone rarely pass 6 out of 10.
- **Say what the tone is, and how it is made.** Naming it without evidence earns little.
- **Range.** Beginning, middle and end, including the headline and the final line.
- **Not feature-spotting.** "There is a statistic" earns nothing until you explain its effect.
- **Twelve to thirteen minutes.** Leave time for A3 and A4.
:::

::: test
Explain why a journalist might include this quotation in an article about a mountain rescue: "'I've done this for thirty years, and that was the worst night of my life,' said team leader Gwen Price." (2)
---
The speaker's long experience gives the comment authority (1), so calling it "the worst night of my life" convinces the reader that the rescue was exceptionally dangerous (1).
:::

::: test
Identify the tone of this extract and explain one way it is created: "Was it wise to swim the length of Lake Windle in February? Absolutely not. Would I do it again? Try and stop me." (Original, in the style of a 21st-century blog.) (2)
---
Humorous, self-mocking or exuberant tone (1); created by the question-and-answer pattern with blunt, short replies ("Absolutely not", "Try and stop me") (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the effect of the statistics in this sentence: "Each year the charity's eleven volunteers answer more than 40,000 calls." (2)
---
The contrast between the small number of volunteers and the huge number of calls (1) emphasises how hard they work and how much they are needed, making the reader admire them (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the headline tries to interest the reader: "From Bin Lorry to Ballet Shoes: the binman who became a dancer at 40." (3)
---
The alliteration "Bin… Ballet" is catchy (1); the juxtaposition of an ordinary, unglamorous job with ballet surprises the reader (1); the detail "at 40" suggests an unusual, inspiring late change, which makes the reader want to know how (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the effect of placing this one-sentence paragraph at the end of an article about a lifeboat crew: "Then the pager went off again." (2)
---
The short, isolated paragraph ends on suspense and a sense of routine danger (1): the crew's work never stops, which reinforces their dedication and the reader's admiration (1).
:::

::: test
Rewrite this 3–4 band comment as a 7–8 band comment: "The writer uses emotive language like 'heartbreaking' to make us feel sad." (3)
---
For example: "The adjective 'heartbreaking' (1) appeals directly to the reader's sympathy, and because it is used to describe the empty shelves rather than the people, it makes us imagine the families' disappointment for ourselves (1), which serves the writer's aim of showing why the food bank matters so much (1)."
:::

::: test
Read this extract (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century newspaper report).

> **Trapped on the ledge: the night the cliffs came down**
>
> At 9.40pm on Tuesday, a section of cliff the size of a house collapsed onto the beach at Selcombe, cutting off three teenagers who had gone looking for fossils. By the time the coastguard arrived, the tide was already lapping at the rocks beneath their feet.
>
> "You could hear the cliff groaning," said volunteer Dan Fry, 26. "Every few minutes another chunk would crack off and smash on the shingle. We had to work in total darkness so the lights didn't loosen anything else."
>
> For three hours the team inched along a ledge barely wider than a boot. Below them, the sea was rising at a metre an hour. Above them, the cliff was still moving.
>
> One by one, the teenagers were roped and lowered into a waiting inshore boat. The last, a fifteen-year-old girl, had been clinging to the rock so long that her fingers had to be eased free.
>
> At 12.52am the boat reached the slipway. The girl's mother, waiting in a borrowed coat, could not speak. She simply held on.
>
> "That's why we do it," said Fry. "Not for the medals. For that."

How does the writer try to show that the rescue was both dangerous and dramatic? You should comment on:
- what is said
- the use of language, tone and structure
- other ways the writer tries to show that the rescue was dangerous and dramatic.

You must refer to the text to support your comments, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: subtle, perceptive analysis of how the writer shows danger and drama; a wide range of well-chosen details across the whole text; language, tone and structure analysed precisely; fluent terminology.
- 7–8: detailed analysis of a range of methods with clear understanding of how they create danger and drama; well-chosen references.
- 5–6: some explanation of methods; some relevant references; some comment on language or tone.
- 3–4: simple comments with some reference; may retell.
- 1–2: identifies one or two basic points. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content: dramatic headline (colon, "Trapped", "the cliffs came down" sounds catastrophic); precise times (9.40pm, 12.52am) frame the account and create a ticking-clock structure; simile "the size of a house" shows scale; tide "lapping" adds a second threat; expert/witness quotation from Dan Fry, with personification "the cliff groaning" and violent verbs "crack… smash" (onomatopoeic); darkness as an added danger and a sign of professionalism; "inched along a ledge barely wider than a boot" (hyperbole/precise image shows peril); balanced sentences "Below them… Above them…" trap them between two threats; statistic "a metre an hour"; short sentence "the cliff was still moving" maintains tension; the girl's fingers "eased free" is an emotive human detail; time-lapse to the slipway; the mother's silence and short sentences ("She simply held on.") give an emotional climax through understatement; circular ending on Fry's words, antithesis "Not for the medals. For that." turns drama into admiration; tone urgent then moving.
:::

::: test
Read this extract (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century magazine profile).

> **The woman who taught a village to read**
>
> Margaret Oyelaran is 83, walks with a stick, and has no intention of retiring. Twice a week, in the back room of the Fox and Hounds in Little Hamworth, she sits down with whoever turns up and a pile of donated books.
>
> She started the reading club eleven years ago, after a neighbour admitted, in tears, that he had never been able to read his grandchildren's birthday cards. "He'd been hiding it for sixty years," she says. "Sixty years! Can you imagine the effort that takes?"
>
> Since then, more than ninety adults have passed through her room. Some come for a month; some have come every Tuesday for a decade. One, a retired lorry driver, read his first whole novel at seventy-one, and celebrated with a pint and a round of applause from the bar.
>
> She is fierce about one thing. "Nobody here is stupid. Nobody. They were let down, that's all." Her voice softens. "And it's never, ever too late."
>
> As I leave, a man in work boots is sounding out the title of a thriller, his finger moving under each letter. Margaret is beaming as though he has won the lottery.

Margaret Oyelaran is described as a very inspiring person. How does the writer try to show this? You should comment on:
- what is said
- the use of language, tone and structure
- other ways the writer tries to show that Margaret is a very inspiring person.

You must refer to the text to support your comments, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands as for all A2 questions:
- 9–10: subtle, perceptive analysis across the whole text; language, tone, structure and other ways analysed precisely; fluent terminology.
- 7–8: detailed analysis of a range of methods; well-chosen references.
- 5–6: some explanation with relevant references.
- 3–4: simple comments, may retell.
- 1–2: one or two basic points. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content: headline presents her as the cause of a whole village's change (hyperbolic, admiring); the opening triple "is 83, walks with a stick, and has no intention of retiring" builds to a determined punchline; the humble setting (back room of a pub, donated books) makes her generosity unshowy; the origin anecdote, the neighbour "in tears" with the birthday cards, is emotive and explains her motive; her direct speech with repetition and exclamation ("Sixty years!") and a rhetorical question shows empathy; figures (eleven years, ninety adults, "every Tuesday for a decade") prove impact and commitment; the lorry driver anecdote at seventy-one, with the pint and applause, is warm and comic; "She is fierce" and repetition "Nobody… Nobody" reveal conviction; the shift "Her voice softens" adds tenderness; "never, ever too late" works as an inspiring message for readers; the ending uses the writer's presence and a present-tense scene of a new learner, so her work continues; simile "as though he has won the lottery" shows her joy in others' success; tone affectionate, admiring, gently humorous.
:::

::: test
Explain how the writer creates a sense of urgency in: "Four minutes. That was all the crew had before the car went under." (2)
---
The minor sentence "Four minutes." isolates the figure and creates a countdown (1); the short follow-up stresses how little time there was, so the reader feels the pressure (1).
:::

::: test
An A2 answer analyses only the language of the first paragraph in detail. Explain why it is unlikely to reach the top band. (2)
---
It lacks range across the whole text (1) and ignores tone, structure and other ways such as quotations and figures, which the bullets and bands require (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the writer uses juxtaposition to make the subject seem admirable: "By day Sam stacks shelves in a supermarket; by night he is translating Homer." (2)
---
The balanced sentence sets an ordinary, low-paid job against a demanding scholarly passion (1), which surprises the reader and makes Sam seem quietly remarkable and dedicated (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the effect of the writer's own presence in a feature: "I tried to lift one of the hay bales she carries all day. I managed about three steps." (2)
---
The writer's first-person, self-deprecating test makes the account vivid and relatable (1), and it proves how strong and hardworking the subject is by contrast (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the effect of the rhetorical question that opens this article: "Who would choose to spend Christmas Day in a hospital kitchen, peeling four hundred potatoes?" (2)
---
It engages the reader directly and invites the answer "nobody" (1), so the reader is surprised and curious about the volunteers who do choose it, which makes their dedication seem remarkable (1).
:::

::: test
Identify the semantic field in this sentence and explain its effect: "The crew battled the blaze, attacked it from three sides and finally defeated it at dawn." (2)
---
A semantic field of war or combat ("battled", "attacked", "defeated") (1); it presents the fire as an enemy and the firefighters as brave soldiers, making the event dramatic and the crew heroic (1).
:::
