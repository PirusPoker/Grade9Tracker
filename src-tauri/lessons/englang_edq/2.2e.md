## What it is

**A5** is the first question on **both texts**. It is worth **4 marks** and tests **AO1**: selecting and **synthesising** evidence from different texts. The pattern (paraphrased) is:

> Using information from both texts, explain briefly [what / how / the ways in which…].
> You should make it clear which text you are referring to.

The focus is always concrete and factual: what happened in two rescues, the conditions two people faced, what food two expeditions ate, how two groups of performers lived. It is **not** a language question. "Explain briefly" means **gather the relevant information from both texts and bring it together**, in your own words where possible, in about **five minutes**.

**What synthesis means.** To synthesise is to **combine details from two sources into one overview**, showing how they connect. It is more than listing Text 1, then Text 2. A synthesised answer:
- selects **only** details relevant to the focus;
- covers **both** texts;
- **links** them: both…, whereas…, similarly…, unlike…;
- makes it **clear which text** each detail comes from.

**How it is marked.** A5 uses a simple four-point scale. In plain terms:

| Mark | What the examiner sees |
|---|---|
| **4** | **Clear synthesis** of a **range** of relevant details from both texts, with links made throughout |
| **3** | **Some synthesis**: relevant details from both texts and some connection made between them |
| **2** | **Some relevant detail from each** text, but presented separately with little or no linking |
| **1** | **Limited** detail, or detail from **one text only** |

The two rules that decide the mark: **one text only caps you at 1**, and **no linking caps you at 2**.

**Information, not analysis.** Do not comment on word choice, tone or technique. "The writer uses the adjective 'freezing' to make us feel…" scores nothing extra and costs time. Say what the conditions *were*: "both men endured freezing water".

**Own words or short quotations?** Your own words show understanding and keep the answer brief. Short quotations are fine where the detail is specific ("a metre an hour"). Avoid copying whole sentences.

**A three-sentence model.** For most A5 questions, three or four well-built sentences reach 4 marks:
1. **A shared point, linked**: "Both writers… (Text 1: detail; Text 2: detail)."
2. **A difference, linked**: "However, whereas in Text 1…, in Text 2…."
3. **A further detail or second difference**, linked again.

Example (on how two travellers crossed a river): *Both travellers crossed by boat, the modern kayaker in her own inflatable canoe (Text 1) and the Victorian party in a hired ferry pulled along a rope (Text 2). However, while the kayaker crossed alone in about ten minutes, the ferry took the whole party, their luggage and two horses, and needed several trips. Both crossings were risky: the kayaker was swept downstream by the current, and the ferryman in Text 2 warned that the rope had broken the week before.*

**Finding the details fast.** You have already read both texts for A1–A4. Reread with the A5 focus in mind and underline every relevant detail: usually three to five in each text. Then look for pairs: same, different, more or less.

**Timing.** Five minutes. It is worth only 4 marks, and A6 (10 marks) follows.

::: terms
- **Synthesis**: bringing details from two texts together into one overview, showing how they connect.
- **Select**: choose only the details that are relevant to the focus named in the question.
- **Focus**: the specific topic of the question, such as the food eaten or the conditions faced.
- **Linking**: using words such as both, whereas, similarly and unlike to connect details from the two texts.
- **Overview**: a summary point that covers both texts at once, such as "both rescues took place at night".
- **Explicit detail**: information stated directly in a text, which A5 mainly uses.
- **Paraphrase**: putting information into your own words, which keeps A5 answers brief.
- **Range**: several relevant details rather than one, needed for the top mark.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **focus** in the question (what exactly must be explained).
2. Reread both texts and underline **three to five relevant details in each**.
3. Pair them: what is **similar**, what is **different**?
4. Write three or four sentences, each **linking** both texts.
5. Label each detail's source: "(Text 1)", "in the 19th-century account".
6. Use your own words or very short quotations; **no language analysis**.
7. Stop at about five minutes.
:::

::: example A 4-mark answer
Question: Using information from both texts, explain briefly how the two schools were heated.
Text 1 (21st century, summary): a new school with solar panels and underfloor heating; "too warm by ten o'clock"; windows that open automatically.
Text 2 (19th century, summary): a single stove at one end of the room; children near it "roasted", those at the far end "could see their breath"; pupils bring a lump of coal each in winter.

Answer: Both schools had a heating system, but they were very different in how well they worked. The modern school in Text 1 is heated evenly by solar power and underfloor heating, so much so that it becomes too warm and windows have to open themselves, whereas the Victorian schoolroom in Text 2 relied on one stove, which left some children far too hot and others freezing. Unlike in Text 1, where heating is automatic, in Text 2 the pupils had to supply the fuel themselves by bringing coal in winter.
(Clear synthesis of a range of details from both texts, linked throughout: 4 marks.)
:::

::: example The same question at 2 marks
Answer: "In Text 1 the school has solar panels and underfloor heating and it gets too warm. In Text 2 there is a stove and the children bring coal."
Why 2: relevant detail from each text, but no linking word and no overview; the texts are presented separately. Adding "Both… but whereas…" and one more detail would lift it to 3 or 4.
:::

::: example Cutting language analysis
Weak: "Text 2 uses the hyperbole 'roasted' to show how hot the children were, which makes the reader feel sorry for them."
Better: "Children near the stove in Text 2 were far too hot."
The first sentence analyses language, which A5 does not reward, and takes three times as long. The second gives the information and moves on.
:::

::: q
How many marks is A5 worth, and which assessment objective does it test? (2)
---
4 marks (1); AO1, selecting and synthesising evidence from different texts (1).
:::

::: q
What is the maximum mark for an A5 answer that uses only one text? (1)
---
1 mark.
:::

::: q
What does "synthesise" mean in A5? (2)
---
To bring together relevant details from both texts (1) into a connected overview, showing similarities or differences (1).
:::

::: q
Give three linking words or phrases useful in A5. (3)
---
Any three: both; similarly; likewise; whereas; while; unlike; in contrast; however; also; on the other hand; but (1 each).
:::

::: q
Why should you avoid language analysis in A5? (2)
---
A5 rewards selection and synthesis of information, not analysis of methods (1), so analysis earns no extra marks and wastes time needed for A6 (1).
:::

::: q
How long should you spend on A5? (1)
---
About five minutes.
:::

::: watch
- **Both texts, or 1 mark.** Check you have used each text at least twice.
- **Link, don't list.** Two separate paragraphs on Text 1 and Text 2 cap the answer at 2.
- **Information only.** No analysis of words or techniques.
- **Stay on the focus.** Details about something else, however interesting, earn nothing.
- **Label your sources.** Make it clear which text each detail comes from.
- **Five minutes.** Then go straight to A6.
:::

Text 1 (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century blog post):
> Saturday mornings at Hallam Street market start early. By seven the stalls are up: fresh pasta, Ghanaian spices, vintage vinyl, second-hand bikes. My favourite is the woman who sells sourdough from the back of a converted ambulance. You pay by tapping your phone, and if it rains, everyone just squeezes under the railway arches until it stops.

Text 2 (original passage written in the style of an 1850s magazine account):
> The Saturday-night market in the poorer streets is lit by a hundred flaring lamps and loud with the cries of the sellers. Bargains are struck by the halfpenny, and the trade goes on until near midnight.

Using information from both texts, explain briefly what could be bought at the markets and how. You should make it clear which text you are referring to. (4)
---
1 mark: limited detail or one text only. 2 marks: some relevant detail from each text. 3 marks: some synthesis with links. 4 marks: clear synthesis of a range of details.
Indicative: Text 1, fresh pasta, spices, vintage records, second-hand bikes, sourdough from a converted ambulance; paid for by tapping a phone. Text 2, basic weekly food (potatoes, herrings, cheap meat, day-old bread at half price); paid in small coins with bargaining "by the halfpenny". Synthesis: both sell food, but Text 1 offers specialist and luxury goods as well as non-food items, whereas Text 2 sells cheap essentials for poorer families; payment in Text 1 is contactless at a fixed price, while in Text 2 prices are bargained in tiny sums; both markets are busy social places (morning in Text 1, until near midnight in Text 2).
