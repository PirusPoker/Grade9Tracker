## What it is

**Section B** of Component 2 is worth **40 marks**: **two compulsory tasks, B1 and B2, of 20 marks each**. There is no choice. Each task gives a **real-life context** and a **form**, and asks you to write about **300–400 words**. The paper advises **30 minutes per task: about 5 minutes planning and 25 minutes writing**. Each task is marked for:
- **AO5, communication and organisation: 12 marks.** Is the writing matched to purpose, audience and form? Are ideas developed and well organised?
- **AO6, vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation: 8 marks.**

The spec calls this **transactional, persuasive and/or discursive writing**: writing that does a job in the real world, for "a range of audiences and purposes, adapting style to form". This topic covers the **forms** and their **register**; 2.2h covers persuasive technique; 2.2i covers planning and accuracy under time pressure.

**The forms the paper sets.** Live papers and the specimen have used letters (to a headteacher, a newspaper, the council), articles (for a school or college magazine, a community magazine, a travel magazine), reviews, a talk to your class, and a report for the headteacher. Guides and speeches are also fair game. Show each form's conventions **briefly**, then spend your time on content.

| Form | Essential conventions | Register and tone |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal letter** | "Dear Mrs Patel," / "Dear Sir or Madam,"; first paragraph states **why you are writing**; clear paragraphs; a polite closing paragraph; "Yours sincerely" (named) or "Yours faithfully" (unnamed), then your name. **No addresses or dates needed.** | Formal, courteous, firm; persuasive but respectful of the reader |
| **Letter to a newspaper** | "Dear Editor," / "Sir,"; refer to the issue or earlier letter that prompted you; your view stated early | Formal or semi-formal; can be passionate, but argued |
| **Article** | A **headline** (use the given title if there is one); an optional **standfirst**; an engaging opening (anecdote, question, striking fact); subheadings optional; a memorable ending | Depends on the magazine: lively and witty for a school magazine; polished and evocative for a travel magazine; community-minded for a local one |
| **Review** | Name what is reviewed; **specific details** (what it is like, what stands out); balanced judgement; a clear **verdict** and **recommendation**, often with who it suits | Personal, opinionated and lively, but informed; aimed at the stated readers |
| **Talk or speech** | Greet the audience ("Good morning, everyone"); **signpost** the structure ("First…, Finally…"); direct address and inclusive "we"; rhetorical questions; a strong close ("Thank you for listening.") | Spoken: conversational, rhythmic, warm; still standard English for a class talk |
| **Report** | A **title**; often "To / From / Subject" or an introduction stating purpose; **sections with subheadings**; findings then **recommendations**; a conclusion | Formal, impersonal, objective; third person or a measured "I"; no rhetoric |
| **Guide** | A title; short introduction; clear **sections** or numbered steps; second person ("you"); practical tips | Friendly, clear, advisory; imperatives ("Pack…", "Don't forget…") |

**Register: matching the audience.** Register is the level of formality and the kind of language you choose. It is half of AO5. Ask: **Who exactly is reading this, and what do I want them to do or feel?**
- **Headteacher, governors, council**: formal, respectful, reasoned; no slang; concrete proposals.
- **Newspaper readers**: formal but forceful; a clear view; evidence and examples.
- **School/college magazine**: lively, witty, direct; you can be personal and humorous, but stay in **standard English** (no "u", "gonna", text speak).
- **Your class**: spoken warmth and humour; inclusive "we"; signposting.
- **Travel or community magazine**: vivid, descriptive, informative, aimed at adults.

**Purpose in every paragraph.** Each task has a job: persuade the council, inform new students, recommend a day out, argue a view. Make every paragraph serve it. If a paragraph does not move the reader towards the purpose, cut it in the plan.

**"Using information provided by others."** Some tasks give a quoted letter or bullet points ("You could include…"). Use them as a springboard: respond to the quoted view directly, and develop each bullet with your own ideas. Do not copy the prompt's wording at length.

**What the examiner does not want.** Address blocks, dates, columns, drawn pictures, invented web links. Elaborate layout earns nothing; the conventions only need to be **signalled**.

**How AO5 is banded (12 marks), in plain terms:**

| Band | What the examiner sees |
|---|---|
| 11–12 | Writing **fully** matched to purpose, audience and form; register confident and sustained; ideas **convincingly developed** with detail; sophisticated, cohesive structure |
| 8–10 | **Clearly** matched; register consistent; ideas developed with relevant detail; well paragraphed with clear links |
| 5–7 | **Generally** appropriate; some development; paragraphs and some cohesion |
| 3–4 | **Some awareness** of purpose and audience; limited development; simple organisation |
| 1–2 | **Basic** awareness; little organisation |

**How AO6 is banded (8 marks):** 8 = wide, precise vocabulary, varied sentences, confident range of punctuation, virtually no errors; 6–7 = varied and mostly accurate, range of punctuation, most spelling correct; 4–5 = some variety, generally accurate, some range of punctuation; 2–3 = limited variety, basic punctuation, some errors that hinder; 1 = little control.

::: terms
- **Transactional writing**: writing for a real-world purpose and audience, such as a letter, report or article.
- **Form**: the type of text you are asked to write, with its own conventions.
- **Register**: the level of formality and type of language chosen to suit the audience and purpose.
- **Convention**: a typical feature of a form, such as a salutation in a letter or subheadings in a report.
- **Salutation**: the greeting that opens a letter, such as "Dear Sir or Madam".
- **Standfirst**: a short introductory line under an article's headline that sums up its angle.
- **Signposting**: words that guide a listener or reader through a structure, such as "firstly" or "finally".
- **Recommendation**: a specific proposed action, a key feature of reports and some letters.
- **Verdict**: the overall judgement given in a review.
- **AO5**: communication and organisation, worth 12 of the 20 marks in each Section B task.
- **AO6**: vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation, worth 8 of the 20 marks in each task.
:::

::: method
1. Underline **form, audience and purpose** in the task (e.g. *letter / council / persuade*).
2. Decide the **register**: formal, semi-formal or lively, and one or two words for the tone.
3. Plan four or five paragraph points that serve the purpose, with one specific example or detail each.
4. Signal the **form** in the first lines: salutation, headline, greeting or title.
5. Write an opening that states the purpose or hooks the reader, in the right register.
6. Develop each point with reasons, examples and detail; link paragraphs smoothly.
7. End with the form's natural close: sign-off, verdict, call to action, recommendations or thanks.
8. Leave two or three minutes to check accuracy.
:::

::: example Openings in four forms
Task contexts are invented for practice.

**Formal letter to the council** (on a plan to switch off streetlights after midnight): *"Dear Councillor Hughes, I am writing as a resident of Elm Park, and as a sixteen-year-old who walks home from a part-time job most evenings, to urge you to reconsider the proposal to switch off streetlights after midnight."*

**Article for a school magazine** (title: *Why Every Teenager Should Learn to Cook*): *"Last year I set fire to a tea towel making beans on toast. This year I made a three-course meal for my grandparents' anniversary, and nobody needed a fire extinguisher. The difference? Six Tuesday evenings and a lot of chopped onions."*

**Talk to your class** (about a skill you think everyone should learn): *"Good morning, everyone. Hands up if you've ever pretended to know how to change a bike tyre. Keep them up. That's most of you, isn't it? Today I'm going to convince you that twenty minutes of learning could save you hours of walking."*

**Report for the headteacher** (on how students use the school library): *"Report on student use of the school library. Introduction: This report, requested by the headteacher, summarises how students currently use the library, identifies three problems, and makes recommendations for improvement."*

Each opening signals the form, sets the register and makes the purpose clear in one or two sentences.
:::

::: example A full model: review (about 330 words)
Task: *A local website is publishing reviews of places to spend a day out. Write a review of a museum, gallery, park or attraction you have visited.*

**Stormhaven Lighthouse: worth every one of its 142 steps**

If your idea of a museum is glass cases and a gift shop, Stormhaven Lighthouse will change your mind. Perched on the cliffs two miles from the town, this restored Victorian lighthouse manages to be a history lesson, a workout and the best view on the coast, all for £6.

The visit begins in the keeper's cottage, where the rooms have been left almost exactly as the last keeper's family lived in them. A kettle sits on the range; a child's slate lies on the table with half a sum on it. It is unexpectedly moving. Short recordings, voiced by local actors, tell you about storms, shipwrecks and the monotony of polishing brass at three in the morning.

Then comes the climb. There are 142 steps, and the staff will tell you the exact number as a warning rather than a boast. The spiral is narrow, so if you are nervous of heights, take it slowly; there are small windows every twenty steps where you can stop and pretend to admire the view while you get your breath back.

The lamp room is the reason to come. The great lens, taller than a person, throws rainbows across the walls when the sun hits it, and the gallery outside gives you the whole bay: fishing boats, the town, the headland and, on a clear day, the mountains beyond.

It is not perfect. The café is tiny and was out of sandwiches by one o'clock, and there is no lift, so visitors with limited mobility can only see the cottage. But the staff are enthusiastic, the price is fair and the experience is genuinely memorable.

**Verdict:** four stars out of five. Ideal for families with children over seven, history fans and anyone who fancies earning their ice cream. Go early, take a picnic, and count the steps yourself.

Why it scores highly: the headline and verdict signal the form; precise details (142 steps, the slate, the lens) make the judgement credible; balanced criticism; register lively but standard; varied sentences and accurate punctuation. (AO5 11–12; AO6 8.)
:::

::: example Fixing register
Task: a letter to the headteacher about the canteen.
Wrong register: *"Hiya Mr Brooks, the canteen food is honestly well grim and everyone's sick of it lol."*
Right register: *"Dear Mr Brooks, I am writing on behalf of the Year 10 student council to share our concerns about the quality and choice of food in the canteen, and to propose three practical improvements."*
The second keeps the honesty but uses formal vocabulary, a clear purpose and the tone of someone the headteacher will take seriously.
:::

::: example Turning notes into a report section
Notes: *library open 8.30–4; busiest lunchtime; only 12 computers; queue; students leave.*

Report section: **Findings: computer access.** The library is open from 8.30am to 4pm, but demand is concentrated at lunchtime, when it is busiest. With only twelve computers available, queues form quickly, and many students leave without completing their work. This suggests that the number of computers, rather than the opening hours, is the main barrier to use.
(Subheading, impersonal formal register, findings interpreted for the reader: the core of report style.)
:::

::: q
How many marks is each Section B task worth, and how are they divided? (2)
---
20 marks each (1): 12 for AO5 (communication and organisation) and 8 for AO6 (vocabulary, sentence structure, punctuation and spelling) (1).
:::

::: q
How many words should each Section B response be, and how long should you spend on each? (2)
---
About 300–400 words (1); about 30 minutes each, roughly 5 planning and 25 writing (1).
:::

::: q
Give two conventions of a formal letter and say what you should leave out. (3)
---
Any two: a salutation ("Dear…"); the purpose in the first paragraph; clear paragraphs; a polite closing paragraph; "Yours sincerely/faithfully" and your name (1 each); leave out addresses and dates, which earn nothing (1).
:::

::: q
How does the register of a report differ from that of a school-magazine article? (2)
---
A report is formal, impersonal and objective, with sections, findings and recommendations (1); a school-magazine article is lively, personal and engaging, with humour and direct address, though still in standard English (1).
:::

::: q
What are the essential features of a review? (3)
---
Any three: names what is reviewed; specific details about it; balanced judgement (strengths and weaknesses); a clear verdict; a recommendation, saying who it suits; an engaging, opinionated voice (1 each).
:::

::: q
Give two features that show you are writing a talk rather than an article. (2)
---
Any two: a greeting to the audience; signposting of structure; inclusive "we" and direct address; rhetorical questions inviting response; spoken rhythm and asides; a closing "Thank you for listening" (1 each).
:::

::: watch
- **Signal the form, then write.** Addresses, dates and columns earn nothing.
- **Register is half of AO5.** Slang to a headteacher or a dry report style in a lively article loses marks.
- **Every paragraph serves the purpose.** Drifting into anecdote with no point weakens development.
- **Standard English in every form.** Lively is not the same as text speak.
- **Use the given title exactly** if the task supplies one.
- **Develop ideas.** Four developed points beat eight listed ones.
- **300–400 words in 25 minutes.** Do not let the first task eat the second's time.
:::

::: test
Your school/college is reviewing how students use the library and study spaces. Write a report for the governors explaining how these spaces are used at the moment and recommending improvements.

You could include:
- how and when students use the library and study spaces now;
- problems you have noticed;
- your recommendations.

12 marks are awarded for communication and organisation; 8 marks are awarded for vocabulary, sentence structure, punctuation and spelling. You should aim to write about 300–400 words. (20)
---
Award AO5 out of 12 and AO6 out of 8.
AO5 (12): 11–12 fully matched to a governors' report: formal, impersonal register sustained; clear sections with subheadings; findings developed with specific detail and convincing, practical recommendations; sophisticated organisation. 8–10 clearly suited, consistent register, well-organised sections, developed findings and recommendations. 5–7 generally appropriate, some sections or paragraphs, some development. 3–4 some awareness of form and audience, limited development, simple organisation. 1–2 basic.
AO6 (8): 8 wide, precise vocabulary, varied sentences, confident range of punctuation, virtually all accurate; 6–7 varied, mostly accurate, a range of punctuation, most spelling correct; 4–5 some variety, generally accurate; 2–3 limited variety, basic punctuation, errors; 1 little control.
Indicative: a title and introduction stating the report's purpose; subheadings such as "Current use", "Problems", "Recommendations"; specific findings (busy at lunchtime, too few computers or seats, noise, limited opening hours, out-of-date books, lack of quiet space for revision); recommendations that are realistic and justified (extended hours before exams, a silent study zone, more charging points, student librarians, a book-request system); a conclusion summarising benefits. Reward impersonal register and logical organisation; penalise chatty or persuasive rhetoric unsuited to a report.
:::

::: test
Your local council is considering a plan to switch off streetlights in residential areas between midnight and 5am to save money. Write a letter to the council giving your views on this plan.

12 marks are awarded for communication and organisation; 8 marks are awarded for vocabulary, sentence structure, punctuation and spelling. You should aim to write about 300–400 words. (20)
---
Award AO5 out of 12 and AO6 out of 8.
AO5 (12): 11–12 fully matched to a formal letter to the council; courteous but assured register; a clear view sustained and convincingly developed with reasons, examples and evidence; counter-arguments addressed; cohesive structure with an effective close. 8–10 clearly suited, consistent register, view developed with detail, well paragraphed. 5–7 generally appropriate, some development, paragraphs. 3–4 some awareness, limited development. 1–2 basic.
AO6 (8): bands as above (8 wide and precise, virtually all accurate; 6–7 varied and mostly accurate; 4–5 some variety, generally accurate; 2–3 limited; 1 little control).
Indicative: salutation and opening stating purpose and the writer's position; either side is acceptable. Against: safety of shift workers, students and young people returning late; fear of crime; road safety; vulnerable residents. For: cost savings redirected to services; reduced light pollution and energy use; benefits for wildlife; evidence that crime may not rise if done carefully. Developed proposals (a trial period, keeping lights on main routes, motion-sensor lighting). Reward formal politeness with conviction, specific examples, a counter-argument acknowledged and answered, and a proper sign-off ("Yours faithfully" for an unnamed council officer).
:::

You have been asked to write a guide for students joining your school/college next September, called "Surviving Your First Week". Write your guide.

---
