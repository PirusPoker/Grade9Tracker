## What it is

**Component 2** is the longer English Language paper: **2 hours, 80 marks, 60% of the GCSE**. Section A (40 marks) tests reading of **two unseen non-fiction texts**, one from the **21st century** and one from the **19th century**, about **900–1200 words** between them. Section B (40 marks) is two writing tasks (topics 2.2g–2.2i). This topic is the reading toolkit that every Section A question depends on.

**The layout of Section A.** In the specimen paper and every live paper so far the order has been the same:

| Where | Text | Questions | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separate resource booklet | **Text 1**: 21st-century (usually a newspaper or magazine article) | A1 (a)(b)(c) retrieval; A2 how the writer tries to… | 3 + 10 |
| Printed on the question paper | **Text 2**: 19th-century (diary, letter, magazine account, report) with a short **italic introduction** | A3 (a)(b)(c) retrieval; A4 evaluate a statement | 3 + 10 |
| Both texts | — | A5 synthesis; A6 comparison | 4 + 10 |

The paper advises **10 minutes reading** and **50 minutes answering**. Read the **italic introduction** to the 19th-century text with care: it gives the writer, the date, the situation and often the outcome ("…but was forced to return"). It is the fastest context you will ever be given, and it frequently unlocks what the statement in A4 is about.

**Forms you may meet.** The spec lists letters, autobiography and biography, diaries, reports, articles, and digital and multi-modal texts. Each form has habits worth knowing because they explain the writer's choices:

| Form | Typical features | What to notice |
|---|---|---|
| **Newspaper / magazine article** | headline, standfirst, quotations from people, facts and figures, short paragraphs | the journalist's angle; whose voices are included and whose are not |
| **Feature or profile** | an anecdotal opening, a named subject, a mix of narrative and interview | how the subject is made admirable, ordinary or surprising |
| **Blog or online post** | first person, informal, direct address, opinions, humour | persona and relationship with readers |
| **Diary or journal** | dated entries, first person, present-tense immediacy, private feeling | how mood changes from day to day |
| **Letter** | address to a known reader, conventions of greeting and closing | what the writer wants from the recipient; politeness hiding feeling |
| **Autobiography / memoir** | retrospective first person, reflection, selected episodes | the gap between the younger self and the older narrator |
| **Report or account** | third person, factual tone, chronological order, official or eyewitness voice | where opinion slips into apparently neutral reporting |
| **Speech** | rhetoric, direct address, repetition | the audience the speaker must win over |

**Purpose, audience, viewpoint, tone (PAVT).** Before any analysis, settle four things for each text:
- **Purpose**: to inform, entertain, persuade, argue, describe, record or advise. Most texts have more than one.
- **Audience**: who was meant to read it (a sister, a magazine's middle-class readers, a newspaper's general public).
- **Viewpoint**: the writer's attitude to the subject (admiring, critical, horrified, amused, ambivalent).
- **Tone**: the "voice" this produces on the page (enthusiastic, wry, indignant, solemn, conversational).

**Decoding 19th-century prose.** The older text is not harder to analyse, only slower to read. Expect:
- **Long periodic sentences**, often built with semicolons and several clauses, where the main point arrives late. Read to the full stop, then ask "what is the main statement?"
- **Formal and Latinate vocabulary**: *commence* (begin), *endeavour* (try), *apprehension* (fear), *conveyance* (vehicle), *countenance* (face), *perceive* (see).
- **Words whose meaning has shifted**: *awful* (awe-inspiring), *nice* (precise), *intelligence* (news), *presently* (soon), *situation* (a job), *sensible of* (aware of), *want* (lack).
- **Understatement and politeness**: "I confess I was not entirely easy" can mean "I was terrified". Victorian writers often show strong feeling through restraint.
- **Conventions of address**: "Dear Madam", "I remain, your affectionate brother", "the reader will forgive me". The writer's sense of their audience is built into the manners.
- **Moral and social attitudes** of the time (on class, gender, empire, work, religion). Understand them in context; you can still judge them in A4.

**A little context, used to understand.** Common 19th-century subjects include railway and steamship travel, exploration, factories and mines, workhouses and poverty, schools, public health, new science and inventions, and seaside or city life. Use context only to understand what the writer means or why it was surprising then. You are not writing history, and no marks are given for facts the text does not contain.

**Facts, opinions, bias and evidence.** The spec asks you to identify **bias and misuse of evidence** and to distinguish **supported from unsupported statements**:
- A **fact** can be checked ("the bridge is 400 yards long"); an **opinion** is a judgement ("the finest bridge in England").
- **Bias** is one-sided selection: only the evidence that suits the writer's view.
- A **supported** statement comes with evidence (a figure, an example, an eyewitness); an **unsupported** one asks to be taken on trust.
- **Misuse of evidence**: a single anecdote presented as typical, a statistic without its context, an "expert" who is not one.

**A ten-minute reading routine.**
1. **Skim the questions first** (one minute) so you know what to look for in each text.
2. **Text 1** (three or four minutes): headline, standfirst and first paragraph for the angle; then read for PAVT, marking a few strong details for A2.
3. **Text 2** (four or five minutes): italic introduction first; then read to every full stop; jot the date, form and a one-line summary of what happens or what the writer thinks.
4. Note the **shared topic** of both texts, since A5 and A6 will be about it.

::: terms
- **Non-fiction**: writing about real people, events or ideas, such as articles, letters, diaries and reports.
- **Form**: the type of text, such as a letter, diary, article, report or speech, with its own conventions.
- **Purpose**: what a text is trying to do, such as inform, persuade, entertain, record or advise.
- **Audience**: the readers a text was written for, which shapes its content and register.
- **Viewpoint**: the writer's attitude or position on the subject they are writing about.
- **Tone**: the voice or mood created by the writer's choices, such as indignant, wry or admiring.
- **Periodic sentence**: a long sentence that delays its main point until the end, common in 19th-century prose.
- **Archaic vocabulary**: words, or senses of words, that are no longer in everyday use.
- **Bias**: a one-sided presentation that selects or slants evidence to favour one view.
- **Supported statement**: a claim backed by evidence such as a fact, figure, example or eyewitness account.
- **Italic introduction**: the short context note printed above the 19th-century text on the paper.
:::

::: method
1. Spend one minute reading the **questions** so you know what each text must give you.
2. Read **Text 1** (21st century): headline, standfirst, opening, then PAVT. Mark strong details.
3. Read the **italic introduction** to Text 2 slowly. Note who, when, what form and what happened.
4. Read **Text 2** to each full stop. Translate difficult sentences in your head into a plain modern version.
5. Ask of each text: **fact or opinion? supported or not?** Where is the writer's bias showing?
6. Note the **shared topic** and one similarity and one difference for A5 and A6.
7. Keep to **ten minutes**; you will reread the relevant section for each question anyway.
:::

::: example Decoding a 19th-century sentence
Sentence (original passage written in the style of an 1850s travel letter): *"Having been assured by the landlord that the conveyance would be at the door by seven, and having in consequence denied myself the comfort of a second cup, I was not a little vexed to find, at a quarter before nine, that no vehicle of any description had presented itself."*

Plain version: The landlord promised the coach would come at seven, so I skipped my second cup of tea, and I was very annoyed when it still had not arrived at a quarter to nine.

What the original adds: the long periodic sentence makes the reader wait, just as the writer waited; "not a little vexed" is understatement, so the anger is comic and polite; "no vehicle of any description" is mock-formal exaggeration. The tone is **wry irritation**, a gentleman making a joke of his own discomfort. (Decoding the sense first, then noticing how the manner shapes the tone, is exactly what A3 and A4 require.)
:::

::: example Purpose, audience, viewpoint, tone
Text (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century local newspaper report): *"Villagers in Carrowby have hit out at plans to close the last bus route to the town, branding the decision 'a death sentence for anyone who can't drive'. The council says the route carries an average of nine passengers a day."*

PAVT: **Purpose**: to inform about a local dispute, but the angle also invites sympathy. **Audience**: local readers, many of whom may use the bus. **Viewpoint**: leans towards the villagers: their emotive quotation comes first and is vivid ("a death sentence"), while the council's reply is reduced to one dry figure. **Tone**: concerned, slightly indignant. Note the **bias by placing**: whoever speaks first and most colourfully shapes the reader's view.
:::

::: example Fact, opinion and supported claims
Text (original passage written in the style of an 1880s magazine article): *"The new public baths in the town were opened last month and have been visited by upwards of two thousand persons. It is the general opinion that no benefit to the labouring classes has ever been so keenly felt; and there can be no doubt that the improvement in their habits will soon be visible to all."*

Analysis: "opened last month" and "upwards of two thousand persons" are **facts** (checkable figures). "It is the general opinion" claims a consensus but gives no source, so it is an **unsupported** statement presented as common knowledge. "There can be no doubt" is a confident **opinion** about the future, and "the improvement in their habits" shows a **condescending** class attitude typical of the period: the writer assumes the poor needed improving. A strong reader separates the evidence from the persuasion.
:::

::: example Reading an italic introduction
Introduction (original, in the style of the paper's context notes): *"In 1862 a young governess travelled alone from Liverpool to take up a position with a family in Canada. This is part of a letter she wrote to her mother during the voyage."*

What it tells you before you read: the **form** (a private letter, so personal feelings, but also a daughter perhaps protecting her mother from worry); the **situation** (a long sea voyage, alone, to work abroad); a **likely tension** (homesickness versus courage); the **audience** (her mother). If A4's statement says "the writer tries to hide how frightened she is", the introduction has already told you why she might.
:::

::: q
Why should you read the italic introduction to the 19th-century text carefully? (2)
---
It gives context such as the writer, date, form and situation (1), which helps you understand the text and the focus of the questions, especially A4 (1).

::: q
Give the modern meaning of "intelligence" in: "We received intelligence of the ship's arrival on Tuesday." (1)
---
News or information.
:::

::: q
What is the difference between a supported and an unsupported statement? (2)
---
A supported statement is backed by evidence such as a figure, example or eyewitness account (1); an unsupported one asks the reader to accept it without evidence (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how understatement can show strong feeling in a 19th-century text. (2)
---
Victorian writers often describe strong feelings in restrained, polite terms, such as "I was not entirely easy" (1); the gap between the mild words and the situation lets the reader sense how intense the feeling really was (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Read the italic introduction.** Skipping it throws away the context that explains the 19th-century text.
- **Read to the full stop.** Long Victorian sentences put the main point last; stopping halfway leads to misreading.
- **Don't assume old words mean what they mean now.** "Awful", "nice" and "presently" have shifted.
- **Context is for understanding, not display.** No marks for history the text does not contain.
- **Separate fact from opinion.** Confident tone is not evidence.
- **Keep to ten minutes.** You will reread each section as you answer.
:::

---

::: test
Give two conventions you would expect in a 19th-century letter. (2)
---
Any two: a formal greeting ("My dear sister", "Dear Sir"); a formal sign-off ("I remain, your affectionate…"); a date or place at the top; polite, formal phrasing; references to a shared past or family; addressing the reader directly (1 each).
:::

::: test
Explain how this sentence shows bias: "One mother, Kerry, told us the new school uniform rules have 'ruined her family's finances', proving the policy is a disaster for every parent." (2)
---
It uses a single anecdote from one parent (1) and presents it as proof about "every parent", which is a misuse of evidence and a one-sided generalisation (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what the long sentence suggests about the writer's experience: "We walked on through the mud and the dark and the rain, losing the path and finding it and losing it again, until we no longer knew how long we had walked or why." (Original passage written in the style of an 1870s travel account.) (2)
---
The long sentence, with repeated "and" and "losing… finding… losing", imitates the endless, exhausting walk (1); the ending "or why" suggests confusion and despair, as if the writer has lost all sense of purpose (1).
:::

::: test
Read these two short extracts.

Text A (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century newspaper feature):
> Volunteer Maya Osei, 19, spends every Saturday morning on Brackley beach with a litter-picker and a bin bag. "Last week we filled thirty bags in two hours," she says. "Most of it was plastic bottles, but we also found a shopping trolley and half a sofa." The group now has more than sixty members and has been thanked by the local council.

Text B (original passage written in the style of an 1860s magazine account):
> On the morning following the storm the shore was found to be strewn for a mile with the wreckage of the vessel: spars, casks, a quantity of oranges, and the remains of the ship's boat. The fishermen of the place set to work at first light to gather what might be saved, and by evening the greater part had been carried above the tide line.

Using information from both texts, explain briefly what was found on the shore and who cleared it. You should make it clear which text you are referring to. (4)
---
1 mark: limited detail, or one text only. 2 marks: some relevant detail from both texts. 3 marks: some synthesis, linking the two. 4 marks: clear synthesis of a range of details from both.
Indicative: Text A, mainly plastic bottles, plus a shopping trolley and half a sofa; cleared by volunteers led by Maya Osei, a group of over sixty, filling thirty bags in two hours. Text B, wreckage of a ship after a storm (spars, casks, oranges, the ship's boat) spread over a mile; cleared by local fishermen working from first light to evening. Synthesis: both shores were littered and cleared by local people working together, but in A the rubbish is modern waste dropped by people, while in B it comes from a single wreck; A's clearing is regular volunteering, B's a one-off salvage.

Read this extract (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century newspaper feature).

> **Up before the larks: the baker who never sleeps in**
>
> At 3am, when the rest of Ludford is asleep, a single window glows on the High Street. Inside, Tom Ashby, 58, is already elbow-deep in dough. He has been doing this, six days a week, for thirty-one years.
>
> "People think I'm mad," he laughs, dusting flour from his forearms. "Maybe I am. But there's nothing like it: the town silent, the ovens ticking, and you're the only one awake who knows what tomorrow smells like."
>
> His shop sells around 400 loaves a day, and the queue on a Saturday stretches past the chemist's. Supermarkets have opened two branches within a mile. Tom shrugs. "They sell bread. I make it. People can taste the difference."
>
> Not everything is easy. Rising flour and energy prices have doubled his costs in five years, and his back, he admits, "isn't what it was". But when a little girl presses her nose to the window at 7am, waiting for the first doughnuts, the tiredness, he says, simply disappears.

How does the writer try to show that Tom Ashby loves his work? You should comment on:
- what is said
- the use of language, tone and structure
- other ways the writer tries to show that Tom loves his work.

You must refer to the text to support your comments, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: subtle, perceptive analysis of how the writer shows Tom's love of his work; a wide range of well-chosen details across the whole text; tone and structure analysed precisely; terminology used accurately.
- 7–8: detailed analysis with clear understanding of a range of methods, including tone or structure; well-chosen references; accurate terminology.
- 5–6: some explanation of how the writer shows this; some relevant references; some comment on language or tone.
- 3–4: simple comments with some reference; may describe what is said rather than how.
- 1–2: identifies one or two basic points. 0: nothing creditworthy.

I make it." reveals craftsman's pride, short sentences give confidence; structure turns to difficulties ("Not everything is easy"), rising costs and his back, so the love is shown as strong enough to survive hardship; the ending anecdote of a child at the window is emotive and circular (night to morning), and "simply disappears" ends on warmth; tone affectionate and admiring throughout.

::: test
Read this extract (original passage written in the style of an 1850s letter). *In 1854 a young woman went to work as a teacher in a new village school in the north of England. This is part of a letter she wrote to her brother.*

> My dear Robert,
>
> You asked me to tell you honestly how I find my situation, and so I shall, though you must not repeat it to Mother. The schoolroom is a barn, lately whitewashed, with a stove that smokes whenever the wind is in the east, which is to say always. I have forty-three scholars between the ages of five and thirteen, and not above a dozen of them can read their letters. Their parents keep them at home whenever there is work in the fields, and send them back to me in winter, when there is none, so that I must begin again every November as though nothing had been learned at all.
>
> And yet I would not have you think me unhappy. There is a boy named Joseph, the son of a carter, who walks four miles each way and has never once been late; and on Friday last, having read a whole page aloud without stumbling, he looked up at me with such a face that I was obliged to turn to the window. I think I could bear a great deal of smoke for such a moment.
>
> Your affectionate sister, Ann

"Ann's letter shows that she finds her work hopeless and wishes she had never taken it." To what extent do you agree with this view? You should comment on:
- what is said
- how it is said.

You must refer to the text to support your comments. (10)
---
Bands:
- 9–10: persuasive, sustained evaluation of the statement; perceptive judgement of what is said and how; well-selected references across the whole letter; a clear, qualified stance.
- 7–8: thoughtful, developed evaluation; clear judgement supported by well-chosen references to content and method.
- 5–6: some evaluation with relevant references; some comment on how ideas are presented.
- 3–4: simple, straightforward opinion with some reference.
- 1–2: basic comment, little reference. 0: nothing creditworthy.
Indicative content, for the statement: honest admission of hardship ("tell you honestly", "you must not repeat it to Mother", so worse than she admits publicly); poor conditions (a "barn", a smoking stove, the dry joke "which is to say always" shows weariness); the scale of the task (forty-three pupils, "not above a dozen" can read); the frustrating cycle of attendance, emphasised by the long sentence and "begin again every November as though nothing had been learned at all". Against the statement: "And yet I would not have you think me unhappy" is the turn of the whole letter, placed at the start of the second paragraph; the anecdote of Joseph (four miles "each way", "never once been late") is detailed and admiring; his reading triggers feeling she conceals with restraint, "obliged to turn to the window", understatement for tears; the final sentence balances "smoke" against "such a moment", so the hardship is outweighed. Best answers: largely disagree; she finds the work exhausting and at times dispiriting but not hopeless, and nothing suggests regret; the structure (hardship then hope) and the polite, wry tone show resilience.
