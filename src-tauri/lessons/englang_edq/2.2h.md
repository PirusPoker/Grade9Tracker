## What it is

Most Section B tasks ask you to **give your views**, **persuade** someone to act, or **advise** a reader. The spec calls this **writing for impact**: selecting, organising and emphasising facts, ideas and key points; citing evidence and quotation effectively to support views; creating emotional impact; and using language persuasively, **including rhetorical devices such as rhetorical questions, antithesis and parenthesis**. It also asks you to **maintain a consistent point of view**. This topic is the toolkit for that writing.

**Argue, persuade, advise: what's the difference?**

| Purpose | Aim | Typical tone | Example task context |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Argue** | Present a reasoned case for a view, dealing with the other side | measured, logical, confident | a letter to a newspaper giving your views |
| **Persuade** | Move the reader to agree **and act**, using reason and emotion | passionate, direct, urgent | a talk urging your class to join a campaign |
| **Advise** | Help the reader make a good decision or solve a problem | supportive, clear, authoritative | a guide or article offering tips |

Most tasks blend them. A letter to the council argues and persuades; a guide advises and sometimes persuades.

**The ideas come first.** The top AO5 band rewards ideas that are **convincingly developed and supported**. A response stuffed with devices but thin on ideas sounds hollow. Every paragraph needs:
1. **A clear point** (the topic sentence).
2. **A reason**: why it matters.
3. **Support**: an example, anecdote, plausible fact or expert view.
4. **Development**: the consequence, the bigger picture, or a vivid detail.
5. **A link** to the next point or back to your main view.

**The named devices, used with purpose.**

| Device | What it is | Example (on a later school start) | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rhetorical question** | a question asked for effect, not an answer | "Who honestly learns quadratic equations at 8.40am?" | invites agreement; makes the reader think |
| **Antithesis** | balanced contrast of opposites in parallel phrases | "We don't need longer days; we need better ones." | memorable, makes a choice seem clear |
| **Parenthesis** | an extra comment inserted with brackets, dashes or commas | "Teenagers – as every parent knows – are not morning people." | adds an aside, a shared joke or evidence, creating a confiding voice |
| **Triple (list of three)** | three items or phrases in a row | "tired, irritable and unable to concentrate" | rhythm and a sense of completeness |
| **Direct address** | speaking to the reader as "you" | "You know the feeling." | involves the reader personally |
| **Inclusive pronouns** | "we", "us", "our" | "Our school can lead the way." | builds a sense of shared purpose |
| **Anecdote** | a brief personal story | "Last Tuesday, my friend fell asleep in a science test." | makes the issue human and real |
| **Statistics and facts** | figures, used plausibly | "Nearly half of students in my year say they sleep under seven hours." | gives authority; **keep them believable** |
| **Expert opinion** | a view from a qualified person | "Sleep scientists have long argued that teenage body clocks run later." | adds credibility |
| **Emotive language** | words chosen to stir feeling | "exhausted children dragging themselves through the gates" | engages sympathy or anger |
| **Counter-argument and rebuttal** | state the opposing view fairly, then answer it | "Some say a later start means a later finish. True, but…" | shows fairness and strengthens your case |
| **Hyperbole** | deliberate exaggeration | "the longest hour in human history" | humour or emphasis, used sparingly |

**Plausible evidence.** Invented statistics are allowed in an exam, but they must be **believable** and **modest**. "A recent survey found 99.9% of students hate homework" undermines your credibility; "In a survey of my year group, more than half said…" sounds real. Expert views should be general ("doctors recommend…") rather than invented named professors with absurd claims.

**A consistent point of view.** Decide your stance before you write and **keep it**. Acknowledging the other side is a strength, but your position must never wobble. "On the other hand, maybe they're right" in the conclusion destroys a persuasive piece. Use **"Some argue…; however…"** to keep control.

**Structure of a persuasive piece (about 300–400 words).**
1. **Opening**: hook (anecdote, question, striking fact) and **clear view**.
2. **Strongest point**, fully developed.
3. **Second point**, a different angle (practical, emotional, financial, moral).
4. **Counter-argument and rebuttal**.
5. **Conclusion**: return to the opening, a call to action, a memorable final line (antithesis works well).

**Ending well.** The last line is what the reader remembers. Avoid "So that's why I think…". Try a **call to action** ("Sign the petition this week."), a **circular return** ("Let's give Sam his first lesson back."), or an **antithesis** ("Start later; finish stronger.").

::: terms
- **Argue**: to present a reasoned case for a view, supported by evidence and answering the other side.
- **Persuade**: to move a reader to agree and act, using both reasoning and emotional appeal.
- **Advise**: to help a reader decide or act well, with clear, supportive guidance.
- **Rhetorical question**: a question asked for effect, to make the reader think or agree, not to get an answer.
- **Antithesis**: a balanced contrast of opposite ideas in parallel phrases, such as "not words, but actions".
- **Parenthesis**: an extra word, phrase or clause inserted into a sentence with brackets, dashes or commas.
- **Counter-argument**: an opposing view that the writer states in order to answer it.
- **Rebuttal**: the writer's answer to a counter-argument, showing why it is wrong or less important.
- **Anecdote**: a brief personal story used to illustrate a point and engage the reader.
- **Call to action**: a closing instruction or appeal that tells the reader what to do next.
- **Consistent viewpoint**: keeping one clear stance throughout a piece of writing.
:::

::: method
1. Decide your **stance** in one sentence and keep it.
2. Plan **three or four developed points**, each from a different angle, plus one counter-argument.
3. For each point, note a **reason** and a **support** (anecdote, plausible figure, expert view).
4. Choose **three or four devices** that suit the audience and place them where they help most (opening, a key point, the ending).
5. Open with a hook and your view; end with a call to action or memorable line.
6. Keep register matched to the form and audience (see 2.2g).
7. Check that every paragraph moves the reader towards your view.
:::

::: example A full model: letter to a newspaper (about 360 words)
Task (invented for practice): *A letter in your local newspaper says: "Teenagers should not have part-time jobs during term time. School must come first." Write a letter to the newspaper giving your views.*

Dear Editor,

I read last week's letter claiming that teenagers should not work during term time with interest, and – as a sixteen-year-old who works eight hours every weekend at a garden centre – with some alarm. I agree that school must come first. I simply disagree that a part-time job pushes it aside.

Consider what a Saturday job actually teaches. In the last year I have learned to deal with an angry customer, to handle money accurately and to turn up on time on a frosty February morning when every part of me wanted to stay in bed. No textbook taught me that. Employers say again and again that young people arrive at work lacking exactly these skills; surely we should welcome the chance to learn them early?

There is also the matter of money. Not every family can afford to fund a teenager's bus pass, football boots and school trips. For many of my friends, a job is not a luxury but a necessity, and it gives them a quiet pride in paying their own way.

Your correspondent fears that work will harm exam results. It is a fair concern, and nobody should be stacking shelves until midnight before a maths exam. But the answer is balance, not a ban. A few hours at the weekend, with a sensible employer, leaves plenty of time for revision; in my experience it actually sharpens it, because when time is limited you stop wasting it.

Banning term-time work would not make teenagers study harder. It would make them poorer, less confident and less ready for the world that is waiting for them. Let us trust young people to manage their time, and support them while they learn how.

Yours faithfully,
Jordan Ellis

Why it scores highly: a clear, consistent view stated early and sustained; points from different angles (skills, money, counter-argument) each developed with personal evidence; devices used with purpose (parenthesis, rhetorical question, antithesis "not a luxury but a necessity" and "balance, not a ban"); formal, courteous register; strong closing triple and call. (AO5 11–12; AO6 8.)
:::

::: example Devices in a talk
Task context (invented): a talk to your class persuading them to learn first aid.

*"Imagine it's lunchtime. Your best friend – the one who always eats too fast – starts choking. Everyone freezes. Would you know what to do? Most of us wouldn't. And that's the problem: it isn't that we don't care; it's that we don't know. Two hours. That's all a basic course takes. Two hours that could give someone fifty more years."*

Devices: an imagined scenario (anecdote) with direct address; parenthesis adding humour and familiarity; a rhetorical question; an honest, inclusive admission ("Most of us"); antithesis ("isn't that we don't care; it's that we don't know"); a minor sentence and repetition of "Two hours" contrasted with "fifty more years". Each device serves the persuasive aim, and the spoken rhythm suits a talk.
:::

::: example Counter-argument and rebuttal
Weak: *"Some people think school uniform is good. I don't agree."* (States the other side, does not answer it.)
Strong: *"Supporters of uniform argue that it hides differences in wealth. It is a generous aim, but anyone who has seen the gap between a £12 supermarket blazer and a £60 branded one knows that uniform does not hide wealth; it simply moves the competition to shoes, bags and coats."*
The strong version states the view fairly, concedes something, then answers it with a concrete, believable detail.
:::

::: example Developing a point fully
Undeveloped: *"Libraries are important because people can read books."*
Developed: *"Libraries are one of the last places where you can stay all afternoon without being expected to buy anything. For a teenager with a noisy home, a library is a desk, a quiet room and free Wi-Fi; for an elderly person living alone, it may be the only conversation they have all day. Close it, and you do not just lose books: you lose a refuge."*
The developed version gives a reason, two specific examples for different people, and a consequence ending in a strong line.
:::

::: q
What is the difference between arguing and persuading? (2)
---
Arguing presents a reasoned case for a view, dealing with the other side logically (1); persuading aims to move the reader to agree and act, using emotional appeal as well as reasons (1).
:::

::: q
Name the three rhetorical devices the spec gives as examples. (3)
---
Rhetorical questions (1); antithesis (1); parenthesis (1).
:::

::: q
Why should invented statistics be modest and plausible? (2)
---
Absurd figures (e.g. "99.9%") make the writer seem unreliable and weaken the argument (1); believable figures add authority and convince the reader (1).
:::

::: q
What does the top AO5 band reward more than a range of devices? (1)
---
Ideas that are convincingly developed and supported (with reasons, examples and detail), matched to purpose and audience.
:::

::: q
Why should you include a counter-argument, and what must follow it? (2)
---
It shows fairness and awareness of the other side, which makes the argument more convincing (1); it must be followed by a rebuttal that answers it, so your viewpoint stays consistent (1).
:::

::: q
Give two effective ways to end a persuasive piece. (2)
---
Any two: a call to action; a circular return to the opening anecdote or image; an antithesis or memorable short line; a final rhetorical question that leaves one obvious answer (1 each).
:::

::: watch
- **Ideas before devices.** Devices decorate an argument; they cannot replace one.
- **Plausible evidence.** Silly statistics and invented "world experts" lose credibility.
- **Keep your viewpoint.** Never end by agreeing with the other side.
- **Answer the counter-argument.** Stating it without a rebuttal weakens your case.
- **Don't shout.** Endless exclamation marks and capital letters are not persuasion.
- **Fit the audience.** Emotive hyperbole may suit a talk to your class, not a letter to the council.
:::

Write an antithesis for a speech arguing that your town needs a youth centre. (2)
---
For example: "Give us somewhere to go, and we'll stop being told we have nowhere to be." or "This town doesn't have a youth problem; it has a youth-centre problem." (1 for balanced contrasting halves; 1 for relevance and persuasive force.)
:::

::: test
Add a parenthesis to this sentence to make it more persuasive or personal: "Homework clubs help students who have nowhere quiet to work." (2)
---
For example: "Homework clubs help students who – like me, with three younger brothers – have nowhere quiet to work." (1 for correctly punctuated parenthesis with brackets, dashes or commas; 1 for content that adds a persuasive or personal aside.)
:::

::: test
Write a rebuttal to this counter-argument in an article supporting a later school start: "Critics say a later start would make it harder for parents to get to work." (3)
---
For example: "It is a fair concern (1), but schools already open early for breakfast clubs, which could easily continue for families who need them (1); a supervised hour of reading or homework is surely better than a classroom of exhausted students trying to learn (1)." (1 for acknowledging; 1 for a practical answer; 1 for turning it back to the writer's view.)
:::

::: test
Identify the device and explain its effect: "We have the money. We have the space. What we don't have is the will." (2)
---
Repetition / triple building to a contrast (accept antithesis or tricolon) (1); the parallel "We have…" sentences build up the resources, so the final turn exposes the lack of will as the only obstacle, making inaction seem inexcusable (1).
:::

::: test
Rewrite this weak opening of a letter to the council so that it hooks the reader and states the view clearly: "I am writing about the park. I think it should not be closed." (3)
---
For example: "Dear Councillor Shaw, every evening at six, a dozen children from Riverside Estate play football on the only patch of green they can walk to. I am writing to urge you not to sell Riverside Park, because closing it would take away far more than grass." (1 for an engaging hook; 1 for a clear viewpoint; 1 for formal register suited to the council.)
:::

::: test
Explain why this sentence would weaken a persuasive conclusion: "But then again, maybe the people who disagree are right." (2)
---
It abandons the writer's viewpoint at the end (1), leaving the reader unconvinced; a persuasive piece must maintain a consistent viewpoint and finish strongly (1).
:::

::: test
Write a plausible statistic and an expert view that could support an argument for more cycle lanes. (2)
---
For example: Statistic: "In our town, nearly a third of car journeys are under two miles." (1). Expert view: "Road-safety experts point out that separated lanes make cyclists far less likely to be hit." (1). (Credit believable, modest, relevant content.)
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between using emotive language in a talk to your class and in a report for governors. (2)
---
In a talk to your class emotive language suits a persuasive, spoken purpose and engages listeners (1); a report for governors should be formal and objective, so emotive language would seem biased and unprofessional (1).
:::

::: test
Develop this point into three or four sentences for a persuasive article: "School trips are valuable." (4)
---
For example: "School trips are valuable because they turn a page in a textbook into something you can touch. When my class visited a working Victorian mill, the noise of a single loom taught us more about child labour than a week of notes. Trips also give quieter students a chance to shine away from the classroom. Cut them to save money, and we save pennies while losing the lessons students actually remember." (1 for a reason; 1 for a specific example; 1 for further development; 1 for a strong link back to the view.)
:::

::: test
A letter has appeared in your local newspaper saying: "Teenagers spend far too much time on their phones. Schools should ban phones completely, from the moment students arrive to the moment they leave."

Write a letter to the newspaper giving your views on this suggestion.

12 marks are awarded for communication and organisation; 8 marks are awarded for vocabulary, sentence structure, punctuation and spelling. You should aim to write about 300–400 words. (20)
---
Award AO5 out of 12 and AO6 out of 8.
AO5 (12): 11–12 fully matched to a letter to a newspaper; formal, assured register; a clear, consistent viewpoint convincingly developed with reasons, examples and plausible evidence; counter-argument fairly stated and answered; rhetorical devices used purposefully; cohesive structure with a strong ending. 8–10 clearly suited, consistent viewpoint, developed points, some effective devices, well paragraphed. 5–7 generally appropriate, some development, some devices, paragraphs. 3–4 some awareness, limited development, simple organisation. 1–2 basic.
AO6 (8): 8 wide, precise vocabulary, varied sentences, confident range of punctuation, virtually all accurate; 6–7 varied, mostly accurate, a range of punctuation; 4–5 some variety, generally accurate; 2–3 limited variety, basic punctuation, errors; 1 little control.
Indicative: "Dear Editor"; response to the quoted letter in the opening; either view or a qualified position. For a ban: distraction in lessons, social media pressure, cyberbullying, better concentration and conversation at break. Against: safety and contact with parents, phones as learning tools, teaching self-control rather than banning, students with medical or caring needs. A middle way: phones off and away in lessons, allowed at lunch. Reward developed reasons with anecdote or plausible evidence, a rebuttal, devices such as rhetorical question, antithesis or parenthesis used with purpose, and "Yours faithfully" sign-off.
:::

---
Keeping one clear stance from the opening to the conclusion, so that acknowledging the other side never turns into agreeing with it.
