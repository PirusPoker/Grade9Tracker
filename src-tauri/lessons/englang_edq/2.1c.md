## What it is

**A2** follows straight on from A1 in the extract. It asks: **"Read lines [about 7–13]. What impressions does the writer create of [a character or place] in these lines?"**, and adds that you must refer to the **language** used, with relevant subject terminology where appropriate. It is worth **5 marks** and tests **AO2**: explaining, commenting on and analysing how writers use language to achieve effects.

**What an impression is.** An impression is the **judgement a reader forms** from the writing, best expressed as an adjective or short phrase: *arrogant*, *lonely*, *fussy but kind*, *threatening*, *a place that has given up*. It is not a summary of what happens ("he goes into the shop"). Every A2 answer is built from impressions, each tied to the words that create it.

**How it is marked.** Unlike A3–A5, A2 is not banded; it is marked on a **single-mark scale from 0 to 5**, roughly:

| Mark | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 5 | Accurate and **perceptive** impressions; a **well-chosen** range of evidence; language choices commented on precisely; terminology used well |
| 4 | Accurate impressions with a **thorough range** of evidence and comment on language |
| 3 | Some valid impressions supported by a range of evidence, with some comment on word choice |
| 2 | Straightforward impressions with limited support |
| 1 | A very limited response |

Three things push the mark up: **accuracy** (the impression is really there in the text), **range** (three or four distinct impressions, not one repeated), and **comment on language** (why *these words* create *that impression*). A list of adjectives with quotations but no comment usually stops at 3.

**The compact answer.** Five marks in about **six or seven minutes** means one tight paragraph, or three or four short ones, of this shape:

> **Impression** + short embedded **quotation** + brief **comment on the word choice** (+ term if it helps).

For example: *"He seems vain: he 'admired' himself in the shop window, and the verb suggests he enjoys what he sees rather than simply checking his appearance."* That one sentence does all three jobs. Three or four sentences like it, with a link or a contrast between them, is a 5.

**Getting to perceptive.** The difference between 4 and 5 is usually one of these:
- **Layered impressions**: "she is generous, but the generosity seems designed to be noticed."
- **Contradiction or tension** within the character or place: "the room is grand yet neglected".
- **Irony**: the narrator calls someone "a great comfort" while showing the opposite.
- **Precision** on a single word: why "perched" rather than "sat"? It suggests a bird, someone light, wary, ready to leave.
- **Development across the lines**: the impression shifts from first sight to the end of the section.

**Using terminology lightly.** Name a technique when it adds precision: verb, adjective, adverb, simile, metaphor, personification, list, short sentence, contrast, dialogue. Never let the term replace the comment. "This is a simile" earns nothing; "the simile 'like a hen guarding eggs' makes her protectiveness comic and fussy" earns credit.

**Characters and places.** Questions are as likely to ask about a place (a town, a room, a beach) as a person. For a place, impressions are about **atmosphere and feeling**: oppressive, cheerful, decayed, hostile, welcoming, lifeless. The same method applies.

::: terms
- **Impression**: a judgement the reader forms about a character or place, usually expressed as an adjective.
- **Perceptive**: noticing subtle or layered meanings, not only the obvious ones.
- **Embedded quotation**: a short quotation built into your own sentence rather than set apart.
- **Connotation**: an idea or feeling that a word suggests beyond its literal meaning.
- **Verb choice**: the writer's selection of an action word, which often carries attitude or tone.
- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as".
- **Metaphor**: a comparison that describes one thing as if it were another.
- **Irony**: a gap between what is said and what is meant, or between what a character believes and what the reader sees.
- **Single-mark scale**: a scheme where the whole answer is judged and given one mark from 0 to 5, rather than banded.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **focus** and bracket the **line range** (usually about lines 7–13).
2. Read the lines and jot three or four **adjectives** for your impressions, including at least one that is less obvious (a contradiction, an irony, a hidden feeling).
3. For each impression, choose the **shortest quotation** that proves it, ideally one or two words.
4. Write: impression, embedded quotation, a comment on what the word or image suggests (connotation, effect).
5. Add one link or contrast between impressions, or show how the impression develops across the lines.
6. Stop at about seven minutes. A2 does not need an introduction or conclusion.
:::

::: example A five-mark answer on a character
Lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel): *"Mr Bevan perched on the edge of the sofa as though it might be taken from him at any moment. He agreed with everything his sister-in-law said, nodding before she had finished speaking, and when the tea came he held the saucer in both hands like an offering."*

Answer: Mr Bevan seems **nervous and insecure**: he "perched" on the sofa, a verb that suggests a bird ready to fly off, and the simile "as though it might be taken from him" implies he feels he has no right to be there. He also appears **submissive**, "nodding before she had finished speaking", which shows he agrees out of fear rather than conviction. The final simile, holding the saucer "like an offering", makes him almost **worshipful** towards his sister-in-law, so the reader senses she dominates him, and perhaps feels sorry for him. (5: accurate, layered impressions, well-chosen words, precise comments.)
:::

::: example A five-mark answer on a place
Lines (original, in the style of a 1930s novel): *"The seaside town in November had the look of a party after the guests have gone. Shutters were down along the front; a single deckchair, forgotten by the council, flapped on the empty beach. The pier lights still came on at dusk, for nobody."*

Answer: The town feels **abandoned and melancholy**. The simile of "a party after the guests have gone" suggests that its purpose has gone with the summer visitors, leaving something faintly embarrassing behind. The "single deckchair… forgotten" makes the town seem **neglected**, and "flapped" gives the chair a restless, pointless movement. The short final clause "for nobody" is the most poignant detail: the lights carry on performing for an audience that will not come, so the place seems **lonely**, almost pitiful. (5)
:::

::: example Why a list stops at 3
"He is rude ('snapped'). He is impatient ('drummed his fingers'). He is rich ('gold watch'). He is old ('grey hair')." Four accurate impressions with evidence, but no comment on language: the mark is likely to be 3. Adding one clause each lifts it: "'snapped', a sharp, sudden verb, as if his patience breaks like a twig…".
:::

::: example Finding the less obvious impression
Lines (original, in the style of a 1960s short story): *"Aunt Rose was famously generous. She told you so herself, and she kept a list."* The obvious impression is generosity. The perceptive one is that the generosity is **self-regarding**: "She told you so herself" undercuts "famously", and "she kept a list" suggests she expects repayment. The short sentences deliver the irony with a deadpan tone. Naming that irony is what moves an answer from 4 to 5.
:::

::: q
How many marks is A2 worth and how is it marked? (2)
---
Five marks (1); on a single-mark scale from 0 to 5 according to the accuracy of impressions, range of evidence and comment on language (1).
:::

::: q
What is an impression, and how is it best expressed? (2)
---
A judgement the reader forms about a character or place (1), best expressed as an adjective or short phrase, e.g. "arrogant" (1).
:::

::: q
Why does a list of impressions with quotations but no comment usually score only 3? (2)
---
It shows range and evidence (1), but A2 tests language, so without comment on how the words create the impression it cannot reach 4 or 5 (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways of making an A2 answer perceptive. (2)
---
Any two: layered impressions; contradiction or tension in the character or place; irony; precise comment on a single word's connotations; showing how the impression develops across the lines.
:::

::: q
How long should A2 take and how long should the answer be? (2)
---
About six or seven minutes (1); one compact paragraph or three or four short ones, no introduction or conclusion (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the impression created by the verb in: "She marched into the kitchen." (2)
---
She seems determined, angry or in charge (1); "marched" has military connotations of purpose and discipline, so her entry feels like an attack or inspection (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Impressions, not story.** "She goes to the door" is not an impression; "she is reluctant" is.
- **Comment on the words.** Quotation without comment caps you at about 3.
- **Range.** Three or four distinct impressions, not one impression said three ways.
- **Short quotations.** One to five words, embedded; long quotations hide what you are analysing.
- **Stay in the lines.** A brilliant impression from line 20 does not count in a lines 7–13 question.
- **Do not overrun.** Five marks, about seven minutes.
:::

::: test
Read these lines (original passage written in the style of a 1950s novel).

> The new headmaster, Mr Crane, stood at the front of the hall with his hands behind his back and let the silence go on for a long time. He was younger than anyone had expected, thin, with a voice that never rose. When a boy at the back coughed, Mr Crane did not look at him; he simply waited until the boy had stopped and then waited a little longer. By the end of assembly nobody coughed at all.

What impressions does the writer create of Mr Crane in these lines? You must refer to the language used in the text, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (5)
---
Single mark 0–5: 5 accurate, perceptive impressions with a well-chosen range of evidence and precise comment on language; 4 accurate impressions with a thorough range of evidence and comment; 3 some impressions with a range of evidence; 2 straightforward impressions; 1 very limited; 0 nothing creditworthy.
Indicative: controlled, calm, confident ("hands behind his back", "let the silence go on"); uses silence as power; quietly intimidating ("a voice that never rose"; menace through restraint); patient but steely ("waited… and then waited a little longer", repetition shows deliberate pressure); authority established without force (the final short sentence "nobody coughed at all" shows his effect); surprising (younger than expected), so his power seems more unsettling; perhaps cold or unfriendly.
:::

"crept" (it suggests moving quietly so as not to be heard, afraid of discovery) or "pressed flat against her mouth" (stifling a sound, fear of making noise) (1 for the word, 1 for the explanation).

::: test
Explain what the phrase "a smile that stopped at her teeth" implies about the character. (2)
---
The smile is not genuine; it does not reach her eyes (1), so she seems insincere, cold or hostile behind polite behaviour (1).

::: test
Read this line (original, in the style of a 1930s novel): "The colonel's moustache bristled, and he declared the matter closed." Give one impression of the colonel and explain how one word creates it. (2)
---
Impression, e.g. bad-tempered, authoritarian or proud (1); "bristled" suggests an animal's fur rising in anger, or "declared… closed" shows he refuses discussion (1).
:::

::: test
What is the main difference between a 4-mark and a 5-mark A2 answer? (2)
---
A 5 is perceptive (1): it notices layered or subtle impressions, such as contradiction or irony, and comments precisely on well-chosen words, rather than giving accurate but straightforward impressions (1).
:::

::: test
Read this line (original, in the style of a 1960s novel): "The estate was new and very clean, and every house had the same tree in the same place in its front garden." Give an impression of the estate and explain how the language creates it. (2)
---
Impression, e.g. uniform, soulless, lacking character (1); repetition of "same… same" emphasises sameness, and "very clean" may hint at sterility rather than praise (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why "He is kind because he is nice to people" would earn little or no credit in A2. (2)
---
It is a vague, circular impression with no quotation (1) and no comment on the writer's language, so it shows neither evidence nor analysis (1).
:::

::: test
Read this line (original, in the style of a 1950s novel): "Mrs Kemp's front room was kept for best, which meant that nobody had sat in it since the funeral." Explain one impression of the room and one of Mrs Kemp. (2)
---
The room is unused, cold or lifeless, preserved like a museum (1); Mrs Kemp values respectability or appearances over comfort, or is stuck in grief since the funeral (1).
:::
