## What it is

**A1** and **A3** are the two sets of short retrieval questions in Component 2. Each is worth **3 marks**, usually split into **(a), (b) and (c) at 1 mark each**. They test **AO1**: identifying and interpreting explicit information.

| Question | Text | Typical shapes (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|
| **A1 (a)(b)(c)** | **Text 1**, the 21st-century text in the separate resource booklet | How long did…? Name one… Who…? How will… be used? Where were…? |
| **A3 (a)(b)(c)** | **Text 2**, the 19th-century text printed on the question paper | What is X compared to? What detail shows…? How did he manage to…? What does the writer mean by…? |

In the specimen paper the second set was split **1 + 2**, with a 2-mark item asking what the writer means by a phrase. Be ready for either pattern: **look at the mark in brackets** and give one clear point per mark.

**These are the easiest marks on the paper**, but they are lost every year in three ways:
1. **Answering from the wrong text.** A1 is always on the 21st-century text and A3 on the 19th-century one (in the specimen and every paper since). An answer taken from the other text scores nothing, however true.
2. **Not answering the exact question.** "How long" wants a time; "where" wants a place; "name one" wants one name. An answer about something nearby in the text does not count.
3. **Writing too much.** A long answer that contains a wrong detail alongside the right one can be treated as a guess. Give the answer, not a paragraph.

**Read the key word.** Before you look at the text, underline the question word and decide what kind of answer it needs:

| Key word | Answer type | Example answer |
|---|---|---|
| **How long / how many / how far** | a number with its unit | "eighteen months"; "four miles" |
| **Who / name one** | a person or a named thing | "her brother"; "the London Marathon" |
| **Where / when** | a place or time | "to the hospital"; "at first light" |
| **How did… / what did… do to…** | a short action | "the dogs pulled him out" |
| **What is X compared to** | the thing in the comparison | "a fish" |
| **What detail shows…** | a specific detail, briefly quoted or paraphrased | "they worked through the night" |
| **What does the writer mean by…** | an explanation in your own words | "she was very frightened but hid it" |

**Short quotation or own words?** Either is fine for straightforward retrieval: "a short quotation or a paraphrase". For "what does the writer mean by", you **must use your own words**, since repeating the phrase explains nothing.

**Finding details in 19th-century wording.** The answer to A3 is in the text, but it may be wrapped in older phrasing. "We had not proceeded above a furlong" means they had gone less than about a furlong (an eighth of a mile). "Our fare consisted of a little salt beef and biscuit" answers "What did they eat?" Read the sentence, translate it in your head, then answer in modern words or with the shortest exact quotation.

**Interpret, don't infer wildly.** A1 and A3 are mostly explicit. If the question asks how someone felt, the text will say or clearly show it. Do not invent reasons or feelings the text does not support.

**Timing.** These six marks should take **three or four minutes per set**. The ten-mark questions that follow each set (A2 and A4) need the time far more.

::: terms
- **Retrieval**: finding and giving information that is stated in a text.
- **Explicit information**: information stated directly in the text, not left for the reader to work out.
- **Key word**: the question word (how long, who, where, name one) that decides what kind of answer is needed.
- **Paraphrase**: putting a detail from the text into your own words while keeping its meaning.
- **Short quotation**: the exact words from the text, kept to the few words that answer the question.
- **Resource booklet**: the separate booklet that holds Text 1, the 21st-century text, for A1 and A2.
- **Interpret**: to explain what a phrase means, often required by a "what does the writer mean by" question.
- **Text 2**: the 19th-century text printed on the question paper, used for A3 and A4.
:::

::: method
1. Check **which text** the question is on: A1 is the 21st-century text, A3 the 19th-century text.
2. Underline the **key word** and decide what type of answer it needs (time, place, person, action).
3. Check the **mark**: one point per mark.
4. Scan the text in order; the (a)(b)(c) answers usually follow the order of the text.
5. Answer in a **short phrase or sentence**: a brief quotation or your own words.
6. For "what does the writer mean by", **explain in your own words**, one idea per mark.
7. Move on within three or four minutes.
:::

::: example A1 set on a 21st-century text
Text (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century newspaper report): *"Seventeen-year-old Priya Nair has become the youngest person to row solo across the Irish Sea, completing the crossing from Wales in 41 hours. Priya, who trained for two years at her local rowing club, said the worst moment came on the first night, when a container ship passed 'close enough to read the name'. She plans to use the £3,000 she raised for a new boat for the club."*

(a) How long did the crossing take? **41 hours.** (1)
(b) Where did Priya train? **At her local rowing club.** (1)
(c) How will the money she raised be used? **To buy a new boat for the club.** (1)
Note: (a) asks how long the crossing took, not how long she trained; "two years" would score nothing.
:::

::: example A3 set on a 19th-century text
Text (original passage written in the style of an 1840s journal): *"We quitted the inn at six, the morning being fine though very cold, and made for the pass. The guide, an old man of few words, went before us with a lantern, and every hundred yards or so stopped to strike the snow with his staff, as a man might knock upon a door to learn if any one is within. By noon the path had vanished entirely, and Mrs Hale, who had borne all with perfect good humour, was at last obliged to be carried."*

(a) What was the weather like when they left the inn? **Fine but very cold.** (1)
(b) What is the guide's action with his staff compared to? **A man knocking on a door to see if anyone is inside.** (1)
(c) What happened to Mrs Hale by noon? **She had to be carried.** (1)
:::

::: example A 2-mark "what does the writer mean by" item
From the same text: *What does the writer mean by "Mrs Hale, who had borne all with perfect good humour"?* (2)

Answer: Mrs Hale had put up with all the hardships of the journey (1) cheerfully, without complaining (1).
Two marks need two parts of meaning: what she endured and how she endured it. Copying "she bore all with good humour" scores nothing, because it does not explain.
:::

::: example Wrong text, wrong key word
Question A3 (a): *"How far did the travellers walk on the first day?"*
Weak answer: "Priya rowed across the Irish Sea." (Wrong text: zero.)
Weak answer: "They walked from morning until night." (Wrong key word: the question is how far, not how long; zero.)
Correct form: "About twelve miles." A number with a unit, taken from the 19th-century text.
:::

::: q
Which text are A1 and A3 each based on? (2)
---
A1 on Text 1, the 21st-century text in the resource booklet (1); A3 on Text 2, the 19th-century text on the question paper (1).
:::

::: q
How many marks are A1 and A3 worth in total, and how are they usually divided? (2)
---
3 marks each, 6 in total (1), usually as (a), (b) and (c) at 1 mark each (1).
:::

::: q
Why might a correct detail from the wrong text score nothing? (1)
---
Each question tells you which text to use, and only information from that text is credited.
:::

::: q
What kind of answer does "How long" require? Give an example. (2)
---
A length of time (1), e.g. "three weeks" or "41 hours" (1).
:::

::: q
How does a "what does the writer mean by" question differ from a standard retrieval question? (2)
---
It asks you to interpret or explain the meaning of a phrase (1), in your own words, rather than simply locate a detail (1).
:::

::: q
How long should you spend on each set of three retrieval questions? (1)
---
About three or four minutes.
:::

::: watch
- **Right text, every time.** A1 is the 21st-century text; A3 is the 19th-century text.
- **Answer the key word.** "How long" is a time; "where" is a place; "name one" is one name.
- **Don't write a paragraph.** A right answer buried among wrong details can lose the mark.
- **Own words for meaning questions.** Repeating the phrase does not explain it.
- **Don't over-infer.** These questions are mostly explicit.
- **Keep it quick.** Three or four minutes, then on to the 10-mark question.
:::

Read this extract (original passage written in the style of a 21st-century magazine article).

> When Grace Okafor opened her community fridge in a converted phone box in Hartley last spring, she expected a few neighbours to drop in. Within a month it was being restocked three times a day. Local cafés now donate unsold sandwiches every evening, and a team of fourteen volunteers checks the dates each morning. "The only rule," says Grace, "is take what you need and leave what you can."

(a) Where is the community fridge kept?
(b) How often was it being restocked within a month?
(c) What do local cafés donate?
(3)
---
(a) In a converted phone box (in Hartley) (1). (b) Three times a day (1). (c) Unsold sandwiches (1).

::: test
Read this sentence (original, in the style of an 1850s letter): "I own I was not a little cast down when the coach went off without me, my box still upon its roof." What does the writer mean by "I own I was not a little cast down"? (2)
---
She admits (1) that she was very upset or disappointed (1). (Accept "I confess I was quite depressed/unhappy"; "not a little" means a lot.)

::: test
Read this sentence (original, in the style of an 1880s report): "The children employed in the mill are for the most part under the age of twelve, and are seldom allowed above half an hour for their dinner." What does the writer mean by "seldom allowed above half an hour for their dinner"? (2)

They are rarely given (1) more than half an hour for their midday meal (1).

::: test
Explain why this answer scores 0. Question: "How did the rescuers reach the trapped walkers?" Answer: "The walkers had been trapped for two days." (1)
---
It does not answer the key word "how": it gives how long they were trapped, not the method the rescuers used.
