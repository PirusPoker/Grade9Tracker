## What it is

New technology brings **impacts** — good and bad — on different groups of people called **stakeholders** (users, workers, businesses, the wider public, and the environment). OCR asks you to **discuss** these impacts and to know the **laws** that regulate technology. A "discuss" answer is marked on giving **both sides** and reaching a **conclusion**, not on listing facts.

**The impact types** — learn the words, because the exam wants you to label each point:

- **Ethical** — what is morally right or wrong, even when it is legal. Is it right to collect and sell users' data, to replace workers with automation, or to use AI that is biased against some groups?
- **Legal** — what the law allows or forbids. Hacking, misusing personal data and piracy are covered by the Acts below.
- **Cultural** — how technology affects groups in society and the way we live: the **digital divide** between those who can afford and access technology and those who cannot, the effect of social media on relationships, and changes to how we shop and work.
- **Environmental** — effects on the natural world: the **energy** used by devices and data centres, **e-waste** (discarded electronics containing toxic materials), and the scarce raw materials mined to build hardware — set against savings such as less travel and less paper.
- **Privacy** — how much control people keep over their personal data, given tracking, CCTV, and data collection by apps and websites.

**The legislation** — know each Act, what it covers, and match it to a scenario:

- **Data Protection Act 2018** — controls how organisations **collect, store and use personal data**. Data must be handled fairly and lawfully, kept secure and accurate, used only for its stated purpose, and kept no longer than needed; individuals can ask to see the data held about them. Use it for any scenario about holding customer or user data.
- **Computer Misuse Act 1990** — makes it illegal to gain **unauthorised access** to a computer (hacking), to do so intending to commit a further crime, and to make **unauthorised changes** to data such as spreading malware or deleting files. Use it for hacking and malware.
- **Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988** — protects the **intellectual property** of creators — software, music, films, books and images — so copying, sharing or using someone's work without permission is illegal. Use it for piracy and copying software or media.

**Software licences:**

- **Open source** — the **source code is published**, and users are free to view, modify and share it (for example Linux and Firefox). Often free, and the community can fix and improve it, but official support may be limited.
- **Proprietary (closed source)** — the source code is **kept secret** and you buy a **licence** to use the finished program under set terms (for example Windows and Photoshop). You cannot legally modify or share it, but you usually get official support and updates.

::: terms
- **Ethical issues**: questions about whether a use of technology is right or wrong.
- **Legal issues**: questions about whether a use of technology breaks the law.
- **Cultural issues**: effects of technology on society and the way people live, such as the digital divide.
- **Digital divide**: the gap between people who have access to computers and the Internet and those who do not.
- **Environmental issues**: effects of technology on the environment — energy use, e-waste, mining for raw materials.
- **Privacy issues**: concerns about how personal data is collected, stored and shared.
- **Data Protection Act 2018**: the law that controls how organisations collect, store and use personal data, and gives people rights over their data.
- **Computer Misuse Act 1990**: the law that makes it illegal to access or modify computer material without permission.
- **Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988**: the law that protects creators' work, including software, from being copied or used without permission.
- **Open-source software**: software whose source code is freely available for users to view, modify and share.
- **Proprietary software**: software whose source code is kept secret; users buy a licence to use it but cannot modify it.
- **Software licence**: a legal agreement that sets out how software may be used and shared.
:::

::: method
**How to structure a "discuss the impact" answer:**
1. **Identify the stakeholders** — who is affected? Users, employees, the business, the public, the environment.
2. **Give both sides** — at least one **positive** and one **negative** impact, each explained rather than just named.
3. **Label each point** with the right category — ethical, legal, cultural, environmental or privacy — so the examiner sees the range.
4. **Reach a justified conclusion** — say which way the balance falls and *why*. A discussion with no conclusion is capped below full marks.
:::

::: example Discussing an environmental impact
Discuss the environmental impact of people replacing their smartphone every year. [6]

**Negatives:** frequent replacement creates large amounts of **e-waste**, and discarded phones contain toxic materials such as lithium and lead that can leak out if they are not recycled; each new phone also needs **scarce raw materials** to be mined and **energy** to manufacture and ship, adding to carbon emissions. Those affected include the **public** living near landfill and mining sites, and future generations. **Positives:** newer phones are often more **energy-efficient** in use, and old phones can be **recycled or reused**, recovering materials and reducing waste if people are encouraged to do so. **Conclusion:** on balance the yearly upgrade cycle is environmentally harmful, because manufacturing and e-waste outweigh the efficiency gains, so the impact would be reduced most by keeping phones longer and recycling them properly.
:::

::: example Matching the law to the crime
An employee copies a company database of customers onto a USB stick and takes it home without permission. State which two laws may have been broken, and why. [2]

The **Computer Misuse Act 1990**, because the data was accessed and copied without authorisation, and the **Data Protection Act 2018**, because personal customer data has not been kept secure and used only for its proper purpose.
:::

::: example Discussing automation
Discuss the impact of a supermarket replacing staffed checkouts with self-service machines. [8]

**Customers:** shorter queues and faster service for small shops (positive), but some, especially older or less confident users, find the machines difficult and impersonal, which widens the **digital divide** (negative). **Employees:** fewer checkout jobs are needed, which is an **ethical** concern as people lose work, although some staff are retrained to help at the machines. **The business:** lower wage costs and more tills in the same floor space (positive), set against the cost of buying and maintaining the machines and higher losses from theft (negative). **Conclusion:** the change benefits the business financially and speeds up service for confident customers, but the ethical cost to employees and the exclusion of some customers are real, so a fair approach keeps some staffed tills alongside the machines.
:::

::: example Open source vs proprietary
Compare open-source and proprietary software licences. [4]

With **open-source** software the source code is made freely available, so users can view, modify and share it, and it is often free of charge, but official support may be limited. With **proprietary** software the source code is kept secret and users buy a licence to run the finished program under set terms; they cannot modify or share it, but they usually receive official support, updates and a warranty.
:::

::: example The Data Protection Act [4]
A gym stores its members' names, addresses, bank details and health information. Describe two ways the gym must follow the Data Protection Act 2018. [4]

Keep the data secure (1): it must protect the data from hackers and loss — for example with passwords, encryption and backups — especially the bank and health details (1).

Use it only for the stated purpose and keep it no longer than needed (1): it cannot sell members' details to other companies without consent, and must delete the data of people who have left once it is no longer required (1).
:::

::: example A cultural impact: the digital divide [4]
Discuss one cultural impact of banks closing branches and moving services online. [4]

It widens the digital divide (1): people without a computer, smartphone or Internet access, or without the skills to use them — often older or poorer people — find it harder to manage their money (1).

People in rural areas with poor broadband are also disadvantaged (1), while those who are online gain convenient 24-hour banking — so the change benefits some groups and excludes others (1).
:::

::: q
Name the four main impact types, besides privacy, that OCR asks you to discuss.
---
Ethical, legal, cultural and environmental impacts.
:::

::: q
Which law applies when a company holds and processes its customers' personal data?
---
The Data Protection Act 2018.
:::

::: q
A person guesses someone else's password and logs into their email account. Which law has been broken?
---
The Computer Misuse Act 1990, because they gained unauthorised access to a computer system.
:::

::: q
Someone downloads and shares films they have not paid for. Which law has been broken?
---
The Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988.
:::

::: q
What is the digital divide, and which type of impact is it an example of?
---
It is the gap between those who can afford and access technology and those who cannot; it is a cultural impact.
:::

::: q
Give two things the Data Protection Act 2018 requires an organisation to do with personal data.
---
Any two of: keep it secure, keep it accurate, use it only for the stated purpose, keep it no longer than needed, and let individuals see the data held about them.
:::

::: q
Why does a "discuss" question lose marks if you only give the advantages of a technology?
---
Because "discuss" is marked on presenting both sides and reaching a conclusion, so a one-sided answer cannot reach the top band.
:::

::: q
State one ethical and one environmental concern about large data centres.
---
Ethical: the way the data they hold is collected and used. Environmental: the large amount of energy and cooling they consume.
:::

::: watch
- **Match the law to the scenario.** Hacking and malware are the **Computer Misuse Act 1990**; holding or misusing personal data is the **Data Protection Act 2018**; copying software, music or films is the **Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988**. Naming the wrong Act scores nothing.
- **Get the full names and dates right** — they are part of the answer, and examiners expect the standard name of each Act.
- **"Discuss" means both sides plus a conclusion.** A list of only good points, or only bad points, is capped below full marks.
- **Name the stakeholders.** Say *who* is affected — users, employees, the business, the public, the environment — and how each is affected differently.
- **Label the impact type.** Use the words ethical, legal, cultural, environmental and privacy so the examiner can see you are covering the range.
- **Open source means the code is shared, not merely free.** Proprietary means the code is secret and licensed — do not reduce the difference to "free versus paid".
:::

::: test
A company wants to introduce facial recognition cameras in a shopping centre. Discuss one ethical, one legal and one privacy issue. (3)
---
Ethical: people are tracked without meaningful consent; possible bias in recognition (1). Legal: the Data Protection Act requires the data to be collected lawfully, kept secure and not held longer than needed (1). Privacy: movements and identities are recorded and could be leaked or misused (1).
:::

:::

::: test

Open source: the source code is freely available and can be modified and shared (1); proprietary: code is owned and hidden, used under licence for a fee (1). Open source is free and adaptable; proprietary usually has professional support and guaranteed updates (1).

::: test
A supermarket plans to replace all its staffed checkouts with self-service machines. Discuss the ethical, legal, cultural and environmental issues this raises. (8)
---
Ethical: staff lose jobs; is it right to replace people with machines to increase profit? Customers who need help may be disadvantaged (2).
Legal: machines must be accessible (Equality Act); data from payment cards must be protected under the Data Protection Act (1–2).
Cultural: less human contact; older people or those less confident with technology may struggle — digital divide (2).
Environmental: machines use electricity and create e-waste when replaced, though fewer staff journeys (1–2).
Conclusion weighing benefits (cost, speed) against the impacts (1).
Levels: 7–8 — thorough, detailed points covering several aspects, applied to the context, with a clear conclusion; 4–6 — some detailed points with some application; 1–3 — basic points, little use of the context.
:::
