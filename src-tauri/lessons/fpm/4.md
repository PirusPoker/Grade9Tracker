## What it is

You need to sketch two families of graph and read things off them.

**Polynomials** — quadratics, cubics and the like. A sketch shows the shape, where the curve crosses the axes, and (for cubics) how it behaves at the ends. The roots come from factorising; the $y$-intercept is the constant term; the shape comes from the sign of the leading term.

**Rational functions with a linear denominator** — things like $y = \dfrac{2x + 1}{x - 3}$. These have **asymptotes**: lines the curve gets ever closer to but never touches. A vertical asymptote where the denominator is zero, and a horizontal one at the value $y$ heads to as $x$ gets huge.

Then you use the sketches: the solutions of $f(x) = g(x)$ are the $x$-coordinates where the two graphs cross, and $f(x) > g(x)$ holds where one graph is above the other. That includes equations you cannot solve by algebra, like $2^x = 3 - x$, which is why graphical methods are on the spec at all.

::: method
1. **Sketching a polynomial:** factorise to find the roots (where it crosses the $x$-axis); a repeated factor like $(x - 2)^2$ means the curve **touches** the axis there rather than crossing. Put $x = 0$ for the $y$-intercept. Look at the leading term: $+x^2$ is a U, $-x^2$ an upside-down U; $+x^3$ goes from bottom-left to top-right, $-x^3$ from top-left to bottom-right.
2. **Sketching $y = \dfrac{ax + b}{cx + d}$:** vertical asymptote where $cx + d = 0$. Horizontal asymptote $y = \dfrac{a}{c}$ (the ratio of the $x$ coefficients — what you get when $x$ is enormous and the constants stop mattering). Find where it crosses the axes ($x = 0$ and $y = 0$). Then the curve fills in: two pieces, one in each "corner" pair the asymptotes create, passing through the intercepts you found.
3. **Draw asymptotes as dashed lines** and write their equations on the sketch. Examiners mark them.
4. **Solving from graphs:** $f(x) = g(x)$ where the curves meet; count the meetings to state the number of roots. $f(x) > g(x)$ where the graph of $f$ is above the graph of $g$; give the answer as a range of $x$.
5. **Rearranging to use a given graph:** if you have drawn $y = x^3 - 3x$ and are asked to solve $x^3 - 3x + 2 = 0$, rewrite it as $x^3 - 3x = -2$ and draw the line $y = -2$. Adding a suitable straight line to a curve already drawn is the standard trick.
:::

::: example A cubic
Sketch $y = (x + 1)(x - 2)^2$.

Roots: $x = -1$ (crosses) and $x = 2$ (repeated — touches).

$y$-intercept: $x = 0$ gives $1 \times 4 = 4$, so $(0, 4)$.

Leading term $+x^3$: starts bottom-left, rises through $(-1, 0)$, passes $(0, 4)$, comes down to touch the axis at $(2, 0)$, then rises to top-right.
:::

::: example A rational function
Sketch $y = \dfrac{2x - 1}{x + 2}$, stating the equations of the asymptotes.

Vertical asymptote: $x + 2 = 0$, so $x = -2$.

Horizontal asymptote: $y = \dfrac{2}{1} = 2$.

Crosses the $y$-axis at $x = 0$: $y = -\dfrac12$. Crosses the $x$-axis where $2x - 1 = 0$: $x = \dfrac12$.

For large positive $x$, $y$ is a little below 2 (e.g. $x = 100$: $\frac{199}{102} < 2$); just to the right of $x = -2$, the top is negative and the bottom is a small positive, so $y \to -\infty$. That fixes the bottom-right branch through $(0, -\frac12)$ and $(\frac12, 0)$, and the other branch sits top-left, above $y = 2$.
:::

::: example Number of solutions from a sketch
On the same axes sketch $y = x^2 - 4$ and $y = \dfrac{1}{x}$, and state the number of real solutions of $x^3 - 4x - 1 = 0$.

$x^2 - 4 = \dfrac1x$ becomes, on multiplying by $x$, $x^3 - 4x = 1$, i.e. $x^3 - 4x - 1 = 0$. So the solutions are where the two graphs meet.

The parabola has roots $\pm 2$ and vertex $(0, -4)$. The reciprocal graph has branches in the first and third quadrants with both axes as asymptotes.

They meet once for $x < -2$ (the parabola is above the axis there, the reciprocal branch just below — they cross), once between $-2$ and $0$ (parabola negative, reciprocal branch plunging), and once for $x > 2$. Three real solutions.
:::

::: example Using a graph already drawn
The graph of $y = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x$ is drawn. Explain how to use it to solve $x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x = 4 - x$ and $x^3 - 6x^2 + 8x = 0$.

First: draw the line $y = 4 - x$ on the same axes; the solutions are the $x$-coordinates where the line meets the curve.

Second: $x^3 - 6x^2 + 8x = 0$ is $x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x = x$, so draw the line $y = x$ and read off the crossings.
:::

::: example An inequality from graphs
Sketch $y = x^2$ and $y = x + 2$ on the same axes and hence solve $x^2 > x + 2$.

They meet where $x^2 - x - 2 = 0$, $(x - 2)(x + 1) = 0$: at $x = -1$ and $x = 2$.

The parabola is above the line to the left of $x = -1$ and to the right of $x = 2$. So $x < -1$ or $x > 2$.
:::

::: q
Sketch $y = x(x - 3)(x + 2)$, marking where it meets the axes.
---
Roots $x = 0, 3, -2$, all simple (crosses at each). $y$-intercept $0$. Leading term $+x^3$: from bottom-left, up through $(-2, 0)$, down through $(0, 0)$, up through $(3, 0)$ to top-right.
:::

::: q
Sketch $y = (x - 1)^2(3 - x)$.
---
Touches the axis at $x = 1$, crosses at $x = 3$. $y$-intercept: $1 \times 3 = 3$. Leading term $-x^3$: the curve comes from top-left, passes down through $(0, 3)$, touches the axis at $(1, 0)$, rises to a small hump, then falls through $(3, 0)$ to bottom-right.
:::

::: q
Write down the equations of the asymptotes of $y = \dfrac{3x + 2}{x - 1}$ and the coordinates of the points where it meets the axes.
---
Vertical $x = 1$; horizontal $y = 3$. Meets $y$-axis at $(0, -2)$; meets $x$-axis at $\left(-\dfrac23, 0\right)$.
:::

::: q
Sketch $y = \dfrac{x}{x + 3}$, showing the asymptotes.
---
Vertical asymptote $x = -3$, horizontal $y = 1$. Passes through the origin (the only axis crossing). Right branch: through $(0, 0)$, rising towards $y = 1$ from below as $x \to \infty$, falling to $-\infty$ as $x \to -3^+$. Left branch: above $y = 1$, rising to $+\infty$ as $x \to -3^-$.
:::

::: q
Find the equations of the asymptotes of $y = \dfrac{4 - 2x}{2x + 5}$.
---
Vertical: $2x + 5 = 0$, $x = -\dfrac52$. Horizontal: $y = \dfrac{-2}{2} = -1$.
:::

::: q
The graph of $y = x^3 - 3x$ has been drawn. State the equation of the line you would add to solve $x^3 - 5x + 2 = 0$.
---
$x^3 - 5x + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow x^3 - 3x = 2x - 2$. Draw $y = 2x - 2$.
:::

::: q
The graph of $y = \dfrac{1}{x}$ has been drawn. Which straight line would you add to solve $x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0$?
---
Divide by $x$: $x - 3 + \dfrac1x = 0$, so $\dfrac1x = 3 - x$. Draw $y = 3 - x$.
:::

::: q
By sketching $y = 2^x$ and $y = 6 - x$ on the same axes, state the number of solutions of $2^x + x = 6$ and find the solution.
---
$2^x$ rises from near zero through $(0, 1)$; $6 - x$ falls through $(0, 6)$ and $(6, 0)$. An increasing curve and a decreasing line cross exactly once. At $x = 2$ both equal $4$, so the single solution is $x = 2$ exactly.
:::

::: q
Sketch $y = x^2 - 2x$ and $y = 3$ on the same axes and hence solve $x^2 - 2x \le 3$.
---
Meet where $x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0$, $(x - 3)(x + 1) = 0$: $x = -1, 3$. The parabola is at or below the line between them: $-1 \le x \le 3$.
:::

::: q
Sketch $y = \dfrac{1}{x - 2}$ and $y = x - 2$ on the same axes and hence solve $\dfrac{1}{x - 2} = x - 2$.
---
The reciprocal graph has asymptotes $x = 2$ and $y = 0$; the line passes through $(2, 0)$ with gradient 1. They meet where $(x - 2)^2 = 1$, so $x - 2 = \pm 1$: $x = 1$ and $x = 3$. Two crossings, one on each branch.
:::

::: watch
- **Write the asymptote equations on the sketch**, as $x = \dots$ and $y = \dots$, and draw them dashed. Sketch marks are usually: shape, asymptotes, intercepts — three separate marks.
- The horizontal asymptote of $\dfrac{ax + b}{cx + d}$ is $y = \dfrac{a}{c}$, **not** $y = 0$ (that is only for $\dfrac{1}{x}$-type graphs where there is no $x$ on top).
- A repeated root **touches**; a single root **crosses**. Get this the wrong way and the sketch is wrong everywhere else too.
- Check the end behaviour from the leading term. A cubic with $-x^3$ ends bottom-right.
- When rearranging to use a drawn graph, keep the drawn curve on one side **exactly as drawn**, and put everything else on the other side — that is the line to add.
- "Hence" means use the graphs: state the crossings, do not start solving algebraically from scratch (though algebra is fine for checking).
:::
