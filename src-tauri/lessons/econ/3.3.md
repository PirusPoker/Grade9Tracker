## What it is

Workers sell their labour in the **labour market**: firms are the buyers, workers the sellers, and the price is the **wage**.

**Choosing an occupation** — two kinds of factor:

- **Wage factors**: the basic pay, overtime rates, bonuses, commission, pensions. (Note **piece rates** — paid per unit produced — versus **time rates** — paid per hour; and a **salary**, a fixed annual amount.)
- **Non-wage factors**: job satisfaction, working conditions, hours, job security, holidays, promotion chances, location, status, fringe benefits (company car, health insurance), training.

People take lower-paid jobs when non-wage factors are attractive — nurses, teachers, charity workers — and demand higher pay for dangerous, unpleasant or antisocial-hours work.

**How wages are set**: by **demand for labour** and **supply of labour**.

- **Demand for labour** slopes down: at a lower wage, firms hire more. It is a **derived demand** — firms want workers only because consumers want the product. Demand for labour rises when demand for the product rises, when labour becomes more productive, or when capital gets dearer.
- **Supply of labour** slopes up: a higher wage attracts more people into the occupation. Supply rises with population, migration, more training places, or the job becoming more attractive.
- **Equilibrium wage** is where they cross.

A **minimum wage** set *above* the equilibrium raises the wage of those still employed but, on the diagram, demand contracts to Qd and supply extends to Qs — a **surplus of labour** (unemployment). Whether that happens in practice depends on how responsive firms are: if demand for labour is inelastic, few jobs are lost.

**Trade unions** — organisations of workers that bargain collectively over pay and conditions. They can raise wages by restricting supply (closed shops, long apprenticeships) or by threatening strikes. They succeed most when demand for labour is inelastic, the union covers most of the workforce, and the firm is profitable. The risk is fewer jobs if the wage rises above what firms will pay.

**Why wages differ:**

| Cause | Example |
|---|---|
| **Skill and qualifications** — supply of skilled workers is small | surgeons earn more than porters |
| **Training time** and its cost | pilots, lawyers |
| **Demand for the product** — derived demand | footballers, software developers |
| **Danger, unpleasantness, antisocial hours** — compensating wage | oil-rig workers, night shifts |
| **Bargaining power** — unions, professional bodies | doctors versus cleaners |
| **Public vs private sector** | public-sector pay set by government, often lower at the top |
| **Region** — cost of living, local demand | London pay above the national average |
| **Discrimination** by gender, age or ethnicity | the gender pay gap |
| **Experience and age** | senior workers earn more |

**Mobility of labour**: **occupational mobility** — the ease of moving between jobs (limited by skills, qualifications, training time); **geographical mobility** — the ease of moving between places (limited by housing costs, family ties, language, information). Immobility keeps wage differences alive: if everyone could become a surgeon tomorrow, surgeons' pay would fall.

**Division of labour / specialisation**: splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker (the pin factory; a car assembly line).

- *Advantages*: workers become skilled and fast at one task; no time lost switching tasks; training is quicker and cheaper; machinery can be designed for each task; output per worker (productivity) rises, cutting unit costs; workers can be matched to their best skill.
- *Disadvantages*: boring, repetitive work lowers motivation and raises absenteeism and staff turnover; workers become dependent on each other, so one absence or strike stops the line; narrow skills make workers occupationally immobile if the job disappears; loss of craftsmanship and variety.

::: terms
- **Wage rate**: the payment to labour per unit of time.
- **Wage factors**: the pay received for a job — basic wage, overtime, bonuses, commission.
- **Non-wage factors**: the other features of a job that affect its attractiveness — job security, promotion prospects, working conditions, holidays, training, fringe benefits.
- **Derived demand**: demand for a factor of production that arises from demand for the product it makes.
- **Trade union**: an organisation of workers that negotiates collectively with employers over pay and conditions.
- **Collective bargaining**: negotiation between a trade union and employers on behalf of a group of workers.
- **Minimum wage**: the lowest wage an employer may legally pay, set by the government.
- **Occupational mobility**: the ability of workers to move from one type of job to another.
- **Geographical mobility**: the ability of workers to move from one area to another for work.
- **Division of labour**: splitting the production process into separate tasks, each carried out by a different worker.
:::

::: method
1. **Wage and non-wage factors**: for "why choose this job", give one of each, applied to the job in the question.
2. **Wage differences**: think *supply* (how many can do it? training, skills, danger) and *demand* (how much is the product wanted? how productive are they?). Two developed reasons for 4 marks; use a diagram with S far to the left for skilled work.
3. **Draw the labour market** like any market: axes "wage rate" and "quantity of labour", D and S, We and Qe. Shifts work the same way — more demand for the product shifts D right → higher wage.
4. **Minimum wage**: horizontal line above We; read Qd and Qs; state the surplus; evaluate — depends on elasticity of demand for labour and the level set.
5. **Unions**: raise wages by shifting supply left or bargaining; evaluate with "depends on elasticity, union membership, firm's profits, laws".
6. **Division of labour**: advantages for the firm (productivity, costs) *and* for the worker; disadvantages likewise. Always mention the boredom → motivation chain.
:::

::: example Define
Define "derived demand". (2)

Demand for a factor of production, such as labour (1), that comes from the demand for the goods and services it produces (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why wages differ
Explain why a doctor is paid more than a shop assistant. (4)

The supply of doctors is small: becoming one requires high qualifications and many years of costly training that few people can complete, so supply is far to the left and the equilibrium wage high (2). Demand for doctors is strong and inelastic because healthcare is essential and their productivity is high; shop assistants need little training, so supply is large relative to demand and the wage is low (2).
:::

::: example Explain — the minimum wage
Explain, using a diagram, the effect of a national minimum wage set above the equilibrium wage. (4)

At the minimum wage more people want to work (supply extends to Qs) but firms hire fewer (demand contracts to Qd), so there is a surplus of labour — unemployment of Qs − Qd (2). Workers who keep their jobs receive a higher wage and higher living standards, but some lose their jobs and firms' costs rise, possibly raising prices (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — a trade union
Analyse how a trade union might raise the wages of its members. (6)

By bargaining collectively the union negotiates with the employer on behalf of all workers, backed by the threat of a strike that would stop production, which is more powerful than individual workers asking for a rise (2). The union may also restrict the supply of labour — insisting on long apprenticeships or membership requirements — which shifts the supply curve left and raises the equilibrium wage (2). Its success depends on how inelastic the demand for labour is: if the firm can easily replace workers with machines or has low profits, a higher wage will cause job losses, so the union may accept a smaller rise to protect employment (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — division of labour
Discuss whether a furniture firm should introduce division of labour. (8)

For: each worker specialises in one task — cutting, assembling, finishing — and becomes faster and more skilled at it, no time is lost switching between jobs, training is quicker and machines can be designed for each task; productivity rises and unit costs fall, so the firm can lower prices or raise profit (4). Against: repetitive work is boring, so motivation falls, absenteeism and staff turnover rise and quality may suffer; workers depend on each other, so one absence halts the line; and craftsmanship is lost, which matters if customers pay for hand-made furniture (3). Judgement: for mass-produced flat-pack furniture the cost savings clearly outweigh the drawbacks; for a small firm selling bespoke pieces, the loss of craftsmanship and motivation may cost more than it saves (1).
:::

::: example Explain — non-wage factors
A nurse is offered two jobs: a hospital post paying 38,000 dollars a year, and an agency post paying 44,000 dollars. Explain two non-wage factors that might lead her to choose the hospital job. (4)

Job security (1): a permanent hospital post gives a steady contract, while agency work can stop at short notice when shifts are not needed (1).

Training and promotion prospects (1): the hospital may pay for her to specialise and offers a clear route to becoming a senior nurse, which could mean higher pay in the long run (1).
:::

::: example Explain — mobility of labour
Explain why a coal miner made redundant in a remote area might find it hard to get another job. (4)

Low occupational mobility (1): mining skills do not transfer easily to growing industries such as IT or health care, and retraining takes time and money (1).

Low geographical mobility (1): jobs may exist in cities far away, but moving means leaving family, selling a house in an area where prices are low and paying much higher housing costs where the jobs are (1).
:::

::: q
State two wage factors and two non-wage factors that influence the choice of occupation. (4)
---
Wage: basic pay, bonuses (or overtime, pension). Non-wage: job satisfaction, working conditions (or hours, security, promotion, location).
:::

::: q
Explain why the demand curve for labour slopes downwards. (2)
---
At a lower wage it is cheaper to employ workers (1), so firms hire more of them and substitute labour for machinery (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why nurses may accept lower pay than workers with similar qualifications in private firms. (4)
---
Non-wage factors are attractive: job satisfaction from helping people, job security and a pension (2). These benefits compensate for lower pay, so the supply of nurses stays high enough to hold wages down (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between occupational and geographical mobility of labour. (4)
---
Occupational mobility is the ability to move between types of job; it is limited by lack of skills or qualifications (2). Geographical mobility is the ability to move between areas for work; it is limited by housing costs, family ties and language (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why wages are higher in cities than in rural areas. (4)
---
Demand for labour is higher in cities because more firms and consumers are there, shifting demand right (2). The cost of living, especially housing, is higher, so workers need — and firms must pay — higher wages to attract them (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two disadvantages of division of labour for workers. (4)
---
Repetitive tasks are boring, reducing job satisfaction and motivation (2). Workers gain only narrow skills, so if their job disappears they find it hard to move to other work — low occupational mobility (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how an increase in the demand for a product affects the wages of the workers who make it. (4)
---
Demand for labour is derived from demand for the product (1), so higher demand for the product shifts the demand for labour to the right (1). With supply unchanged, the equilibrium wage rises and more workers are employed (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why piece rates might raise productivity. (2)
---
Workers are paid per unit produced (1), so they have an incentive to work faster and produce more (1) — though quality may suffer.
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should raise the national minimum wage. (8)
---
For: higher pay for the lowest earners reduces poverty and inequality, raises their spending and so demand in the economy, may motivate workers and cut staff turnover, and reduces the need for benefits (3). Against: if set above equilibrium, firms hire fewer workers, causing unemployment, especially among the young and unskilled; costs rise, which may be passed on as higher prices or make firms less competitive abroad; some employers may move to the informal economy (3). Judgement: a modest rise, where demand for labour is inelastic and firms are profitable, raises incomes with few job losses; a large rise in a weak economy risks unemployment — the outcome depends on the level chosen (2).
:::

::: watch
- The labour market diagram has **wage rate** and **quantity of labour** on the axes — not price and quantity.
- Demand for labour is **derived** — link it to demand for the product.
- A **minimum wage above equilibrium** creates a **surplus** of labour; evaluate with elasticity.
- Wage differences: argue from **supply** (skill, training, danger) *and* **demand** (product demand, productivity).
- Division of labour: advantages for **the firm** and disadvantages for **the worker**, then the effect on cost and quality.
:::

---
