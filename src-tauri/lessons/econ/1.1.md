## What it is

Economics starts from one fact: **wants are infinite, resources are finite.** There is never enough land, labour, capital and enterprise to make everything everyone would like. That gap is **scarcity**, and it is faced by every decision-maker — a consumer with a fixed income, a worker with 24 hours in a day, a firm with a budget, a government with tax revenue.

Because of scarcity, every economy — however it is organised — has to answer **three fundamental questions**:

1. **What** to produce? (More hospitals or more roads? Food or fighter jets?)
2. **How** to produce it? (With many workers or with machines? Which technology?)
3. **For whom** to produce? (Who gets the output — the rich, those in need, everyone equally?)

A **market economy** answers them through prices; a **planned economy** through government decisions; a **mixed economy** through both.

One more distinction: an **economic good** uses scarce resources, so it has an opportunity cost and a price (bread, cars, haircuts). A **free good** is so abundant that using it costs nothing — air, sunlight, seawater. Almost everything is an economic good; "free" gifts and free healthcare are *not* free goods, because resources were used to produce them.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Scarcity**: the situation in which there are not enough resources to satisfy all wants.
- **Wants**: desires for goods and services, which are unlimited.
- **Needs**: things essential for survival (food, water, shelter).
- **Economic good**: a good that is scarce, so has an opportunity cost.
- **Free good**: a good that is not scarce, so has zero opportunity cost.

::: method
1. **Define precisely.** A 2-mark "define" needs the two key ideas: for scarcity, *limited resources* **and** *unlimited wants*. One idea gets one mark.
2. **Explain with a link.** "Explain" questions (4 marks) want a chain: point → because → so. "Air is a free good *because* it is not scarce, *so* using it has no opportunity cost."
3. **Apply to the decision-maker named.** If the question says "a government", give a government example (tax revenue spent on defence rather than schools), not a shopper.
4. **The three questions**: always state all three, then show how the economy in the question answers each.
5. **Free vs economic good**: the test is scarcity, not price. Ask: does producing or using it use up a scarce resource?
6. **Read the command word** on Paper 2: *define* (2), *explain* (4), *analyse* (6), *discuss* (8). The number of marks tells you how many developed points to make.
:::

::: example Define
Define "scarcity". (2)

Scarcity is the situation where resources are limited (1) relative to the unlimited wants of people (1), so not all wants can be satisfied.
:::

::: example Explain — a free good
Explain why sunlight is a free good but electricity is not. (4)

Sunlight is a free good because it is not scarce: there is enough for everyone to use as much as they want without reducing what is available to others, so it has no opportunity cost (2). Electricity is an economic good because producing it uses scarce resources — fuel, power stations, workers — that could have been used for something else, so it has an opportunity cost and a price (2).
:::

::: example Explain — scarcity for a decision-maker
Explain how a government faces the basic economic problem. (4)

A government has limited resources — its tax revenue and what it can borrow — but faces unlimited demands from the public for healthcare, education, defence, roads and benefits (2). It cannot fund everything, so it must choose what to provide; spending more on hospitals, for example, means less for schools, which is the basic economic problem of scarcity forcing choice (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — the three questions
Analyse how a market economy answers the three basic economic questions. (6)

*What to produce*: firms produce the goods consumers are willing to pay for; if demand for electric cars rises, their price rises, profits rise and more are made (2). *How to produce*: firms choose the cheapest method to maximise profit — using machines where labour is expensive, workers where it is cheap (2). *For whom*: goods go to those able and willing to pay; income determines who gets the output, so a rich consumer can buy more than a poor one (2).
:::

::: q
Define "economic good". (2)
---
A good that is scarce (1) and so has an opportunity cost when it is produced or consumed (1).
:::

::: q
Define "wants" and "needs", and give one example of each. (4)
---
Wants are desires for goods and services which are unlimited (1), e.g. a new phone (1). Needs are the essentials for survival (1), e.g. water (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a free bus service is not a free good. (4)
---
A free good has no opportunity cost because it is not scarce (1). A bus service uses scarce resources — buses, fuel, drivers' time — which could have produced other goods (2), so it has an opportunity cost even though passengers do not pay; it is an economic good that happens to have a zero price (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why scarcity is a problem faced by rich countries as well as poor ones. (4)
---
Scarcity means resources are limited relative to wants, and wants are unlimited everywhere (1). Even a rich country cannot satisfy every want — its citizens want more healthcare, faster transport, more leisure — so it still has to choose between uses of its resources (2). The difference is the level of wants that can be met, not the existence of scarcity (1).
:::

::: q
State the three basic economic questions. (3)
---
What to produce; how to produce it; for whom to produce it.
:::

::: q
Explain how a consumer faces the basic economic problem. (4)
---
A consumer has a limited income (1) but unlimited wants (1). Spending on one item means giving up another — buying concert tickets means not buying new trainers — so they must choose how to allocate their income between wants (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse how the three basic economic questions might be answered in a planned economy. (6)
---
The government decides *what* to produce through a central plan, setting targets for output of steel, food and housing rather than responding to consumer demand (2). It decides *how* by allocating labour and capital to state-owned firms and choosing technology centrally (2). It decides *for whom* by setting wages and prices and rationing goods, aiming at more equal distribution than a market would give (2).
:::

::: q
Give two examples of free goods and two of economic goods. (4)
---
Free: air, sunlight (or rainwater, seawater). Economic: bread, a car (anything that uses scarce resources).
:::

::: watch
- **Scarcity needs both halves**: limited resources *and* unlimited wants. "Not enough resources" alone is half the definition.
- Free good means **not scarce**, not "no charge". Free healthcare and free apps are economic goods.
- Answer for the **decision-maker named** in the question — consumer, worker, firm or government — with an example that fits.
- The three questions are what/how/for whom. "Where" and "when" are not among them.
- Match the length to the marks: a 2-mark define is two lines; a 6-mark analyse is three developed points.
:::
