## What it is

A **firm** is a business that produces goods or services. Firms are classified three ways:

**1. By sector** (stage of production):

- **Primary** — extracting natural resources: farming, fishing, mining, forestry.
- **Secondary** — manufacturing and construction: turning raw materials into goods.
- **Tertiary** — services: retail, banking, transport, education, tourism.

As countries develop, employment shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary.

**2. By ownership**: **private sector** (owned by individuals or shareholders, aiming for profit: sole traders, partnerships, private and public limited companies) and **public sector** (owned by the government: state schools, nationalised industries, aiming at public service).

**3. By size** — measured by number of employees, value of output or sales, capital employed, or market share. Most firms are small. Small firms survive because: some markets are small or local; some customers want personal service; some products cannot be mass-produced (bespoke tailoring); owners prefer to stay in control; and setting up is easy in services. Large firms exist because of **economies of scale**.

**Growth of firms**: **internal (organic)** growth — expanding output, opening branches, from retained profit; **external** growth — **merger** (two firms agree to join) or **takeover** (one buys another). Three kinds of merger:

| Type | Definition | Example | Main motive |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal** | firms at the *same stage* of the *same industry* | two supermarkets | economies of scale, larger market share, less competition |
| **Vertical** | firms at *different stages* of the *same industry* — **forward** (towards the consumer, e.g. a brewer buying pubs) or **backward** (towards the raw material, e.g. a car maker buying a steel plant) | | secure supplies or outlets, control quality, cut suppliers' profit margins |
| **Conglomerate** | firms in *different industries* | a tobacco firm buying a food firm | spreading risk (diversification) |

**Economies of scale** — falls in **average cost** (cost per unit) as a firm grows. **Internal** economies come from the firm's own size:

- **Purchasing (bulk-buying)** — discounts for large orders.
- **Technical** — large, specialised machinery is only worth buying at high output; large lorries and ships carry more per trip.
- **Financial** — big firms borrow more cheaply, being lower risk, and can sell shares.
- **Managerial** — specialist managers (finance, marketing, HR) whose salaries are spread over more output.
- **Marketing** — an advert costs the same whether the firm sells a thousand or a million units.
- **Risk-bearing** — a range of products and markets, so one failure does not sink the firm.

**External** economies come from the *industry* growing in an area, benefiting every firm in it: a pool of skilled labour, specialist suppliers and colleges nearby, better roads and infrastructure, a shared reputation (Silicon Valley, the City of London).

**Diseconomies of scale** — average cost *rises* when a firm grows too large:

- **Management** becomes harder — too many layers; slow decisions.
- **Communication** breaks down between departments and sites.
- **Motivation** falls — workers feel like a number; industrial relations worsen.
- **Coordination** and control problems; waste.

The **average total cost (ATC) curve** falls as economies of scale are gained, reaches a minimum at the **optimum output**, then rises as diseconomies set in.

::: terms
- **Primary sector**: firms that extract raw materials — farming, fishing, mining.
- **Secondary sector**: firms that manufacture goods and build.
- **Tertiary sector**: firms that provide services.
- **Private sector firm**: a firm owned by individuals or shareholders.
- **Public sector firm (public corporation)**: a firm owned and run by the government.
- **Small firm**: a firm with few employees, a small market share and low output; small firms survive by serving niche or local markets and giving personal service.
- **Merger**: the joining of two or more firms into one.
- **Horizontal merger**: a merger between firms at the same stage of production in the same industry.
- **Vertical merger**: a merger between firms at different stages of production in the same industry — backwards towards suppliers or forwards towards customers.
- **Conglomerate merger**: a merger between firms in different industries.
- **Economies of scale**: the fall in long-run average cost that results from an increase in the scale of production.
- **Internal economies of scale**: cost savings from a firm's own growth — buying, marketing, financial, managerial, technical, risk-bearing.
- **External economies of scale**: cost savings from the growth of the whole industry in an area — skilled labour, specialist suppliers.
- **Diseconomies of scale**: the rise in long-run average cost that results from a firm growing too large.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify a merger** by asking: same industry? Same stage? Yes/yes → horizontal; yes/no → vertical (then forward or backward); no → conglomerate. Give the motive that fits.
2. **Economies of scale are about average cost**, not total cost. Total cost always rises with output; the point is that cost *per unit* falls. Say "average cost falls" every time.
3. **Name the type** and explain the mechanism: "purchasing economies — buying in bulk earns discounts, so the cost per unit of materials falls."
4. **Internal vs external**: internal comes from the firm's own growth; external from the industry's growth in a region.
5. **Diseconomies**: management, communication, motivation. Link each to *why* cost per unit rises.
6. **Small-firm questions**: "why do small firms survive?" — small market, personal service, non-standard products, owner control, low capital needs.
:::

::: example Define
Define "economies of scale". (2)

The reduction in average cost per unit (1) that a firm gains as it increases its scale of production (1).
:::

::: example Classify
A chocolate manufacturer buys a cocoa plantation, then a rival chocolate maker, then a chain of clothing shops. Identify each type of merger. (3)

Cocoa plantation: backward vertical — an earlier stage of the same industry (1). Rival chocolate maker: horizontal — same stage, same industry (1). Clothing shops: conglomerate — a different industry (1).
:::

::: example Explain — two economies of scale
Explain two internal economies of scale a supermarket chain might gain. (4)

Purchasing economies: buying goods in huge quantities earns large discounts from suppliers, so the cost per item sold falls (2). Marketing economies: a national advertising campaign costs the same however many stores it covers, so the advertising cost per unit sold is lower for a large chain (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — diseconomies
Analyse why a firm that grows very large may experience diseconomies of scale. (6)

As a firm grows it needs more layers of management; decisions take longer and information is distorted as it passes up and down, so mistakes and delays raise cost per unit (2). Communication between many departments and sites becomes harder, leading to duplication of work and poor coordination — one factory over-producing while another is short of parts (2). Workers in a large organisation may feel unimportant and lose motivation, so productivity falls and absenteeism and staff turnover rise; the firm ends up paying more per unit of output than at its optimum size (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — a horizontal merger
Discuss whether a merger between two large airlines would benefit consumers. (8)

Benefits: the merged airline gains economies of scale — bulk purchase of aircraft and fuel, shared marketing and maintenance — so average costs fall and fares may be cut; it can offer a wider network of routes and more frequent flights (3). Costs: with one fewer competitor the airline has more market power and may raise fares and reduce service quality, since consumers have fewer alternatives; routes may be cut where the two airlines overlapped; and a very large airline may suffer diseconomies of scale, so costs might not fall at all (3). Judgement: consumers benefit only if cost savings are passed on and competition remains from other airlines; on routes where the merged firm becomes a monopoly they are likely to lose, so regulators often require the airline to give up some routes (2).
:::

::: example Explain — why small firms survive
Explain two reasons why small firms continue to exist alongside very large firms. (4)

They serve small or niche markets (1): demand for, say, handmade furniture or a local plumber is too small for large firms to find it worth supplying (1).

They offer personal service and flexibility (1): a small firm can adapt a product to one customer's wishes and give personal attention, which many customers value and will pay for (1).
:::

::: q
Give one example each of a primary, secondary and tertiary sector firm. (3)
---
Primary: a farm or mine; secondary: a car factory or builder; tertiary: a bank, shop or school.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a horizontal and a vertical merger. (4)
---
Horizontal: firms at the same stage of the same industry join, e.g. two banks, usually to gain economies of scale and market share (2). Vertical: firms at different stages of the same industry join, e.g. a retailer buying a manufacturer, to secure supplies or outlets (2).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason a firm might undertake a conglomerate merger. (2)
---
To spread risk (1): if demand falls in one industry, profits from another keep the firm going (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why many firms remain small. (4)
---
The market may be small or local — a village shop or hairdresser cannot sell nationally (2). Some customers want personal service or bespoke products that cannot be mass-produced, so a small firm serves them better (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between internal and external economies of scale. (4)
---
Internal economies come from the growth of the firm itself, such as bulk-buying discounts or specialised machinery (2). External economies come from the growth of the whole industry in an area, such as a pool of skilled workers or specialist suppliers, which benefit all firms there (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how technical economies of scale reduce average cost. (2)
---
Large firms can afford specialised machinery that produces at high speed (1); its cost is spread over a large output, so cost per unit falls (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one financial economy of scale. (2)
---
Large firms are seen as lower risk (1), so banks lend to them at lower interest rates, reducing their cost of borrowing per unit (1).
:::

::: q
Draw and explain the shape of an average total cost curve as a firm grows. (4)
---
A U-shape: average cost falls at first as economies of scale are gained (1), reaches a minimum at the optimum output (1), then rises as diseconomies of scale — management and communication problems — set in (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse the possible effects on a firm's costs of a backward vertical merger. (6)
---
Buying its supplier guarantees the firm's raw materials and lets it control their quality and delivery, avoiding costly production stoppages (2). The supplier's profit margin is no longer paid, so input costs fall, and the firm may gain purchasing economies by buying raw materials for both businesses together (2). However, running a business at a different stage needs different skills; management may be stretched, causing diseconomies of scale, and the firm loses the option of switching to cheaper suppliers if its own becomes inefficient (2).
:::

::: watch
- Economies of scale = **lower average cost**. Never say "lower costs" without "average" or "per unit".
- **Name the type** (purchasing, technical, financial, managerial, marketing, risk-bearing) and explain **why** cost per unit falls.
- Vertical mergers need **forward** or **backward**; horizontal means **same stage**.
- Diseconomies are about **management, communication and motivation** — not "costs rise because the firm buys more".
- Sectors: **primary, secondary, tertiary** — classify by what the firm *does*, not how big it is.
:::
