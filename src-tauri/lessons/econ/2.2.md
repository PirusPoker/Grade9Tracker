## What it is

**Demand** is the quantity of a good that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price in a given period. **Individual demand** is one consumer's; **market demand** adds up every consumer's demand at each price.

The **law of demand**: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, and vice versa — so the demand curve slopes **downwards**. Two reasons: at a higher price fewer people can afford the good, and those who can afford it switch to cheaper substitutes.

**Movement along the curve** — caused only by a change in the good's **own price**:

- Price falls → **extension** of demand (more bought, moving down the curve).
- Price rises → **contraction** of demand (less bought, moving up the curve).

**Shift of the curve** — caused by anything *other* than the good's own price (the **conditions of demand**). The whole curve moves, so more or less is demanded **at every price**:

- **Increase in demand** (shift right, D1 → D2): more demanded at every price.
- **Decrease in demand** (shift left): less at every price.

What shifts demand:

| Condition | Increases demand when… |
|---|---|
| **Income** | income rises (for *normal* goods); for *inferior* goods — cheap own-brand food, bus travel — demand falls as income rises |
| **Price of substitutes** | a substitute (a rival product, e.g. tea for coffee) gets dearer |
| **Price of complements** | a complement (used together, e.g. petrol for cars) gets cheaper |
| **Tastes and fashion** | advertising, health reports, trends make it more popular |
| **Population** | population grows, or its age structure changes (more older people → more demand for care) |
| **Other** | expectations of future price rises; interest rates fall (for goods bought on credit); weather; laws |

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Demand**: the willingness and ability of consumers to buy a product at a given price in a given period.
- **Market demand**: the total demand for a product from all consumers, found by adding individual demands at each price.
- **Extension of demand**: a rise in quantity demanded caused by a fall in price.
- **Contraction of demand**: a fall in quantity demanded caused by a rise in price.
- **Substitute**: a good that can be used in place of another.
- **Complement**: a good used together with another.

::: method
1. **Ask: did the good's own price change?** If yes → *movement along* the curve (extension or contraction). If no → *shift* of the curve (increase or decrease). This single question is worth more marks than anything else in this topic.
2. **Draw it properly.** Axes labelled Price and Quantity; downward-sloping line labelled D; for a shift, draw D2 parallel to D1 and show the change in quantity at the *same* price with dotted lines.
3. **Use the right words.** Extension/contraction for movements; increase/decrease for shifts. "Demand rises because price fell" is wrong — *quantity demanded* rises.
4. **For a shift, name the condition and the direction.** "Income rises → demand for restaurant meals (a normal good) increases → curve shifts right."
5. **Substitutes and complements**: substitute's price *up* → demand for this good *up*. Complement's price *up* → demand for this good *down*.
6. **Normal vs inferior**: check whether the good is one people buy *less* of as they get richer.
:::

::: example Define
Define "extension of demand". (2)

A rise in the quantity demanded of a good (1) caused by a fall in its price, shown as a movement down along the demand curve (1).
:::

::: example Movement or shift?
For each event, state whether it causes a movement along or a shift of the demand curve for coffee, and in which direction. (a) The price of coffee rises. (b) The price of tea rises. (c) A report says coffee is good for health. (4)

(a) Movement along: a contraction of demand, up the curve (1). (b) Shift: tea is a substitute, so coffee demand increases — the curve shifts right (2). (c) Shift right: tastes change in coffee's favour (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a complement
Explain, with the help of a diagram, how a fall in the price of games consoles affects the demand for video games. (4)

Consoles and games are complements — used together (1). Cheaper consoles mean more people buy one, so more games are bought at every price (1). The demand curve for games shifts to the right from D1 to D2, so at the unchanged price of games the quantity demanded rises from Q1 to Q2 (2). *(Diagram as the second one above, labelled for games.)*
:::

::: example Analyse — rising incomes
Analyse how a rise in incomes affects the demand for (a) foreign holidays and (b) bus travel. (6)

Foreign holidays are a normal good: with more income, consumers can afford holidays they previously could not, so demand increases and the curve shifts right (2). Bus travel is for many people an inferior good: as incomes rise they switch to owning cars or flying, so demand for bus travel decreases and its curve shifts left (2). The size of each shift depends on how much income rises and on the good — luxuries such as holidays respond strongly, while some people who cannot drive keep using buses regardless of income (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a demand curve slopes downwards. (2)
---
At a higher price fewer consumers can afford the good (1) and some switch to cheaper substitutes, so less is demanded (1).
:::

::: q
State two factors, other than price, that could increase the demand for cars. (2)
---
A rise in incomes; a fall in the price of petrol (a complement); a rise in the price of public transport (a substitute); advertising.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between an increase in demand and an extension of demand. (4)
---
An increase in demand is a shift of the whole curve to the right, caused by a change in a condition of demand such as income, so more is bought at every price (2). An extension is a movement along the existing curve caused by a fall in the good's own price (2).
:::

::: q
Define "inferior good" and give an example. (2)
---
A good whose demand falls as income rises (1), e.g. own-brand supermarket food or bus travel (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how market demand is found from individual demand. (2)
---
By adding up the quantity demanded by every consumer (1) at each price (1).
:::

::: q
Explain, using a diagram, the effect on the demand for margarine of a rise in the price of butter. (4)
---
Butter and margarine are substitutes (1). Dearer butter makes some consumers switch to margarine, so more margarine is demanded at every price (1). The demand curve for margarine shifts right from D1 to D2; at the same price quantity rises from Q1 to Q2 (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse the effect on the demand for houses of a fall in interest rates. (6)
---
Most houses are bought with mortgages, so lower interest rates reduce monthly repayments and make borrowing cheaper (2). More households can now afford to buy, so demand for houses increases and the demand curve shifts to the right (2). The effect depends on confidence and on banks' willingness to lend; if buyers expect prices to fall or cannot get a mortgage, the shift will be small (2).
:::

::: q
A government launches a campaign warning of the dangers of sugary drinks. Explain the likely effect on the demand curve for sugary drinks. (4)
---
The campaign changes tastes against sugary drinks (1), so at every price fewer are demanded (1): the demand curve shifts to the left, a decrease in demand (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Own price → movement along; anything else → shift.** Confusing the two is the most costly error in the whole course.
- Words matter: **extension/contraction** (movement) versus **increase/decrease** (shift).
- **Label diagrams**: axes, D1 and D2, the price and both quantities, and an arrow for the direction.
- Substitute dearer → demand **up**; complement dearer → demand **down**.
- **Inferior goods** shift left when income rises. Do not assume every good is normal.
:::
