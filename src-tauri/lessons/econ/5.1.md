## What it is

**Living standards** are the level of material comfort and well-being of a population — how much people can consume, and the quality of their lives. Two indicators dominate:

**Real GDP per head (per capita)** $= \dfrac{\text{real GDP}}{\text{population}}$. It shows the average output — and so roughly the average income — per person, adjusted for inflation. A rise means the economy is producing more per person. It is simple, available for every country and comparable over time.

Its **limitations** as a measure of living standards:

- It is an **average** — it says nothing about **distribution**. A country with a few billionaires and many poor can have the same GDP per head as one where income is spread evenly.
- It ignores **what is produced** — spending on weapons or prisons counts the same as spending on hospitals.
- It ignores **the informal economy** — unrecorded work, subsistence farming, home production, cash-in-hand jobs — which is large in poorer countries.
- It ignores **leisure and working conditions** — a country with higher output from 60-hour weeks is not necessarily better off.
- It ignores **the environment** — pollution and resource depletion are not deducted; cleaning up an oil spill *adds* to GDP.
- Comparing countries needs **exchange rates**, which distort — prices of local goods differ (purchasing power).
- It says nothing about **health, education, freedom, safety** or happiness.

**The Human Development Index (HDI)** — published by the United Nations — combines three dimensions into one score from 0 to 1:

1. **Health**: life expectancy at birth.
2. **Education**: mean years of schooling of adults and expected years of schooling for children.
3. **Living standards**: GNI (gross national income) per head, adjusted for purchasing power.

Countries above about 0.8 are "very high" human development. The HDI is broader than GDP and rewards a country that turns income into health and education; but it still ignores distribution, the environment, political freedom and human rights, and its three parts are weighted equally by choice, not by evidence.

**Why living standards differ between countries**: quantity and quality of factors of production (capital, skills, natural resources); productivity and technology; investment; political stability and the rule of law; trade and openness; population growth; government spending on health and education; climate and disease; history (colonialism, war).

**Why income distribution differs** (within a country): the ownership of wealth (land, capital, property, inheritance); education and skills; the mix of high- and low-paid jobs; the strength of unions and minimum wages; how progressive the tax system is and how generous benefits are; discrimination; regional differences; the size of the informal economy.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Living standards**: the level of material well-being of a population, indicated by the goods and services people consume.
- **Real GDP per head**: real GDP divided by the population; the average output per person.
- **Human Development Index**: a composite measure of development combining life expectancy, years of schooling and GNI per head.
- **Income distribution**: how a country's total income is shared among its population.

::: method
1. **Calculate GDP per head** by dividing; give units ("dollars per person"). If two countries are compared, compute both and state which is higher.
2. **Limitations of GDP per head**: distribution, what is produced, informal economy, leisure, environment, exchange rates. Two or three developed for 4–6 marks.
3. **HDI: three components** — health (life expectancy), education (schooling), income (GNI per head). Name all three; then its advantage over GDP and one limitation.
4. **Comparing countries**: use both measures — a country can rank higher on HDI than on GDP if it spends on health and education (Cuba, Sri Lanka) or lower if income is concentrated (oil states).
5. **Distribution questions**: reasons incomes differ (ownership of assets, skills, taxes and benefits) and why it matters for living standards.
:::

::: example Define
Define "real GDP per head". (2)

The total value of a country's output, adjusted for inflation (1), divided by its population, giving the average output or income per person (1).
:::

::: example Calculate
Country A has real GDP of 900 billion and a population of 30 million; country B has real GDP of 400 billion and a population of 8 million. Calculate real GDP per head for each and state which has the higher average income. (3)

A: $900{,}000 \div 30 = 30{,}000$ per person (1). B: $400{,}000 \div 8 = 50{,}000$ per person (1). B has the higher GDP per head even though A's total GDP is larger (1).
:::

::: example Explain — limitations of GDP per head
Explain two reasons why real GDP per head may not accurately measure living standards. (4)

It is an average, so it ignores distribution: if most income goes to a small elite, the typical person's standard of living is far below the figure suggests (2). It excludes the informal economy — subsistence farming, unpaid housework, cash jobs — which provides a large part of real consumption in developing countries, so their living standards are understated (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — the HDI
Analyse why the HDI may be a better indicator of living standards than GDP per head. (6)

The HDI combines income with life expectancy and years of schooling, so it captures whether a country's income is actually turned into health and education — two things that directly affect people's quality of life (2). A country with high oil revenues but poor schools and hospitals ranks lower on HDI than on GDP, while a poorer country that invests in health and education ranks higher, giving a fuller picture (2). However the HDI still ignores income distribution, the environment and political freedom, and it uses equal weights that are a matter of judgement, so it improves on GDP rather than replacing the need for other indicators (2).
:::

::: q
State the three components of the HDI. (3)
---
Life expectancy at birth; years of schooling (mean and expected); GNI per head adjusted for purchasing power.
:::

::: q
A country's real GDP is 60 billion and its population is 12 million. Calculate real GDP per head. (1)
---
$60{,}000{,}000{,}000 \div 12{,}000{,}000 = 5{,}000$ per person.
:::

::: q
Explain why a rise in GDP per head might not improve living standards. (4)
---
The extra output might be capital goods or weapons rather than consumer goods, so people have no more to consume (2). The extra income may go to a small group, or be accompanied by longer hours and more pollution that lower the quality of life (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why GDP figures understate living standards in many developing countries. (2)
---
Much production is informal — subsistence farming, home-made goods, cash work (1) — and is not recorded in GDP, though people consume it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of the HDI. (4)
---
Advantage: it includes health and education, not just income, giving a broader view of well-being (2). Disadvantage: it ignores income distribution and the environment, so two countries with the same HDI may differ greatly in inequality (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why income is distributed unequally within a country. (4)
---
Wealth such as land, property and shares is owned by a few and earns them rent, interest and profit, and can be inherited (2). Differences in education and skills mean some workers earn far more than others (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why living standards are generally higher in countries with more capital per worker. (4)
---
More capital raises productivity, so each worker produces more output (2). Higher output per head means higher incomes and more goods and services to consume (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should use GDP per head or the HDI to judge whether its policies are improving living standards. (8)
---
GDP per head: simple, published frequently, comparable over time, and income is the basis of most consumption; a rise usually means more resources for health and education too (3). HDI: broader — it shows whether income is translated into longer lives and more schooling, which are what people ultimately value; it exposes countries where growth benefits few; but it is published less often, changes slowly, ignores distribution and the environment, and its weights are arbitrary (3). Judgement: use both — GDP per head to track the economy's short-run performance and the HDI to check that growth is improving lives; neither alone is adequate, and measures of inequality and the environment should be added (2).
:::

::: watch
- GDP per head is an **average** — "ignores distribution" is the first limitation to give.
- The HDI has exactly **three** components: **health, education, income**. Name them.
- Both measures ignore **distribution and the environment**; the HDI is broader, not perfect.
- Show the division for **per head** calculations and give units.
- Do not confuse **living standards** (material well-being) with **economic growth** (rising real GDP).
:::
