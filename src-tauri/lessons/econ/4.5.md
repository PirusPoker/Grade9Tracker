## What it is

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a year. **Economic growth** is an increase in **real GDP** — GDP adjusted for inflation, so that it measures a rise in actual output, not just in prices.

- **Nominal (money) GDP** is measured at current prices. If prices rise 5% and output is unchanged, nominal GDP rises 5% but nothing more has been produced.
- **Real GDP** removes the price effect: $\text{real GDP} = \dfrac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{price index}} \times 100$. Roughly, real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation.
- **Growth rate** $= \dfrac{\text{real GDP this year} - \text{real GDP last year}}{\text{real GDP last year}} \times 100$.
- **GDP per head (per capita)** $=$ GDP $\div$ population — the usual measure of living standards (5.1).

**Causes of growth** — the economy's capacity rises (PPC shifts out) or spare capacity is used (a move towards the PPC):

- More or better **capital** — investment, new technology.
- More or better **labour** — population growth, immigration, education, training, health.
- Discovery of **natural resources**.
- **Enterprise** and innovation.
- Higher **demand** — consumer spending, investment, government spending, exports — using unemployed resources.
- **Trade** and specialisation.

**Consequences of growth:**

- *Benefits*: higher incomes and living standards; more jobs; more tax revenue for public services and less need for benefits; less poverty; firms earn more profit and invest; the country's international standing rises.
- *Costs*: pollution, congestion, depletion of resources and damage to the environment; inflation if demand grows faster than capacity; more imports, worsening the current account; longer hours and stress; benefits may be unequally shared, widening inequality; growth built on capital goods means fewer consumer goods now.

**Recession** — a fall in real GDP for two consecutive quarters (six months). **Causes**: a fall in demand — lower consumer confidence, higher interest rates, a fall in exports as other countries slow, a financial crisis cutting lending, a cut in government spending, a sharp rise in oil prices raising costs. **Consequences**: rising unemployment; falling incomes and spending (which deepens the recession); business failures; lower investment; falling tax revenue and rising benefit spending, so a bigger budget deficit; possibly falling prices; poverty and social problems — though inflation and imports usually fall.

**Policies for growth and against recession:**

- **Expansionary fiscal policy** — tax cuts, more government spending → higher demand. *Works if* there is spare capacity; *but* raises the deficit and takes time.
- **Expansionary monetary policy** — lower interest rates → more borrowing and investment. *Works if* confidence is there; *but* lags, and rates cannot fall below zero.
- **Supply-side policy** — education, infrastructure, tax incentives for investment → higher capacity. *Works for* long-run growth; *but* slow and costly.
- **Exchange-rate depreciation** — boosts exports; *but* raises import prices.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Gross domestic product**: the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
- **Real GDP**: GDP adjusted for inflation, measured at constant prices.
- **Economic growth**: an increase in real GDP over time.
- **Recession**: a fall in real GDP for two successive quarters.
- **GDP per head**: GDP divided by the population.

::: method
1. **Real vs nominal**: any question with prices rising needs real GDP. Show $\dfrac{\text{nominal}}{\text{index}} \times 100$ or the subtraction of inflation.
2. **Growth rate calculation**: change ÷ original × 100. Two years of figures → one growth rate.
3. **Causes**: split into *capacity* (factors of production, investment, technology) and *demand* (using spare resources). Two developed points.
4. **Consequences**: benefits *and* costs, then a judgement about sustainability and how the gains are shared.
5. **Recession**: definition (two quarters), a demand-side cause, then the chain of consequences — unemployment → lower income → less spending → deeper recession → bigger budget deficit.
6. **Policies**: name it, give the chain to higher demand or capacity, then "how well it works" — spare capacity, confidence, lags, side effects.
:::

::: example Calculate — real GDP
A country's nominal GDP rises from 800 billion to 848 billion in a year. Inflation over the year was 4%. Calculate nominal growth and estimate real growth. (3)

Nominal growth $= \dfrac{848 - 800}{800} \times 100 = 6\%$ (1). Real growth ≈ 6% − 4% = 2% (1). Output has actually risen only about 2%; the rest of the rise in nominal GDP is higher prices (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — with a price index
Nominal GDP is 600 billion and the price index is 120 (base year = 100). Calculate real GDP. (2)

Real GDP $= \dfrac{600}{120} \times 100 = 500$ billion at base-year prices (2).
:::

::: example Explain — causes of growth
Explain two causes of economic growth. (4)

Investment in capital: new machinery and technology raise output per worker, so the economy can produce more with the same labour — its capacity rises (2). Improved education: a better-skilled workforce is more productive and can adopt new techniques, increasing the quality of labour and total output (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — consequences of recession
Analyse the consequences of a recession for an economy. (6)

Falling output means firms need fewer workers, so unemployment rises; those who lose jobs have lower incomes and cut spending, reducing demand further and deepening the recession (2). Firms earn lower profits, cut investment and some go bankrupt, reducing the economy's future capacity; the government collects less tax and pays more benefits, so its budget deficit grows (2). Not every effect is negative: lower demand reduces inflation and spending on imports, improving the current account; but the overall cost in lost output, poverty and lower living standards is large, especially if the recession lasts (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — policies for growth
Discuss whether cutting interest rates is the best way to promote economic growth. (8)

For: lower rates make borrowing cheaper, so consumers spend more on credit and firms invest more; mortgage holders have more to spend; a lower exchange rate may boost exports; the central bank can act quickly and the effect is broad (3). Against: the effect takes up to two years; if confidence is low nobody borrows; rates cannot go below zero; cheap credit can cause household debt, asset price bubbles and inflation; and rate cuts do nothing to raise long-run capacity (3). Judgement: cutting rates is effective for a demand shortfall with spare capacity, but for sustained growth supply-side policies — education, infrastructure, investment incentives — are more important; the best approach combines the two (2).
:::

::: q
Define "real GDP". (2)
---
The total value of a country's output (1) adjusted for inflation, measured at constant prices (1).
:::

::: q
Real GDP falls from 500 billion to 490 billion. Calculate the growth rate. (2)
---
$\dfrac{490 - 500}{500} \times 100 = -2\%$; the economy has shrunk by 2%.
:::

::: q
Explain why nominal GDP is a poor measure of economic growth. (2)
---
Nominal GDP rises when prices rise even if output is unchanged (1); only real GDP shows whether more has actually been produced (1).
:::

::: q
Define "recession". (2)
---
A fall in real GDP (1) for two consecutive quarters (six months) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two possible causes of a recession. (4)
---
A fall in consumer confidence leads households to save rather than spend, cutting demand and output (2). A rise in interest rates raises borrowing costs, reducing consumer spending and business investment (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two benefits of economic growth. (4)
---
Higher incomes let people buy more goods and services, raising living standards (2). Higher tax revenue lets the government spend more on health and education without raising tax rates (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two costs of economic growth. (4)
---
More production increases pollution and uses up non-renewable resources, harming the environment (2). If demand grows faster than capacity, inflation rises and more imports are bought, worsening the current account (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a government could use fiscal policy to end a recession, and one limitation. (4)
---
It could raise spending on infrastructure and cut taxes, increasing total demand so firms produce more and hire workers (2). The limitation is that it raises the budget deficit and takes time to work, and households may save the tax cut (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether economic growth always improves the living standards of a country's population. (8)
---
For: growth raises average incomes, so people can afford more and better goods, housing and healthcare; it creates jobs and cuts poverty; higher tax revenue funds schools and hospitals (3). Against: the gains may go mostly to the rich, leaving many no better off; growth may bring pollution, congestion and stress that lower the quality of life; if it comes from producing capital goods or exports, consumption may not rise at first; and GDP counts output, not welfare (3). Judgement: growth is usually necessary for higher living standards but not sufficient — the outcome depends on how the gains are distributed and whether growth is environmentally sustainable, which is why indicators such as the HDI are used alongside GDP (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Growth = rise in real GDP.** Nominal GDP alone is never enough; adjust for inflation.
- Growth rate = **change ÷ original × 100**. A negative answer is a contraction.
- Recession = **two consecutive quarters** of falling real GDP — the definition mark is exact.
- Consequences: give **both** benefits and costs; recession has one or two silver linings (lower inflation, fewer imports).
- Policies: the effectiveness depends on **spare capacity, confidence and time lags** — say which.
:::
