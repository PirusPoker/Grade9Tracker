## What it is

**Supply** is the quantity of a good that producers are willing and able to sell at each price in a given period. **Individual supply** is one firm's; **market supply** adds up every firm's supply at each price.

The **law of supply**: as price rises, quantity supplied rises — the supply curve slopes **upwards**. A higher price makes production more profitable, so existing firms produce more (paying overtime, running machines longer) and new firms enter the market.

**Movement along the curve** — caused only by a change in the good's **own price**:

- Price rises → **extension** of supply (more offered for sale, up the curve).
- Price falls → **contraction** of supply.

**Shift of the curve** — caused by a change in the **conditions of supply**. More or less is supplied **at every price**:

- **Increase in supply** (shift right, S1 → S2): more at every price.
- **Decrease in supply** (shift left): less at every price.

What shifts supply:

| Condition | Increases supply when… |
|---|---|
| **Costs of production** | wages, raw materials, energy or rent get cheaper |
| **Technology** | better methods lower the cost per unit |
| **Indirect taxes** | a tax on the good is cut (a tax raises costs → shift left) |
| **Subsidies** | the government pays producers a grant per unit |
| **Weather / natural events** | good harvests; disasters shift supply left |
| **Number of firms** | new firms enter the market |
| **Prices of other goods the firm could make** | a farmer's alternative crop gets cheaper, so land switches back |
| **Expectations** | firms expect prices to fall, so sell now |

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Supply**: the willingness and ability of producers to sell a product at a given price in a given period.
- **Extension of supply**: a rise in quantity supplied caused by a rise in price.
- **Contraction of supply**: a fall in quantity supplied caused by a fall in price.
- **Subsidy**: a payment by the government to producers to encourage production of a good.

::: method
1. **Same test as demand: did the good's own price change?** Yes → movement along (extension/contraction). No → shift (increase/decrease).
2. **Costs are the key shifter.** Anything that raises the cost of making each unit (wages, materials, tax) shifts supply **left**; anything that lowers it (technology, subsidy) shifts supply **right**.
3. **Draw it**: upward-sloping S; for a shift, S2 parallel to S1, dotted lines showing the change in quantity at the same price.
4. **Weather and disasters** are the usual cause for agricultural and fishing questions — a bad harvest is a decrease in supply, curve shifts left.
5. **Watch the direction of a tax.** A tax *increase* is a *decrease* in supply. A subsidy *increase* is an *increase* in supply.
6. **Explain the chain**: cause → change in cost/profitability → firms supply more or less at every price → curve shifts.
:::

::: example Define
Define "supply". (2)

The willingness and ability of producers to sell a good (1) at a given price in a given period (1).
:::

::: example Movement or shift?
For each event, state whether it causes a movement along or a shift of the supply curve for bread, and in which direction. (a) The price of bread rises. (b) The price of flour rises. (c) New ovens bake bread faster. (4)

(a) Movement along: an extension of supply (1). (b) Shift left: flour is a raw material, so costs rise and less is supplied at every price (2). (c) Shift right: better technology lowers cost per loaf (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a subsidy
Explain, with the help of a diagram, the effect of a government subsidy on the supply of solar panels. (4)

A subsidy is a payment to producers per unit made, which lowers their cost of production (1). At any given price producing solar panels is now more profitable, so firms are willing to supply more (1). The supply curve shifts to the right from S1 to S2 and, at the unchanged price, quantity supplied rises from Q1 to Q2 (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — a rise in wages
Analyse how a rise in wages in the car industry affects the supply of cars. (6)

Wages are a cost of production, so higher wages raise the cost of making each car (2). At every price car-making is now less profitable, so firms cut output; supply decreases and the curve shifts to the left (2). The size of the shift depends on how large a share of costs wages are — in a highly automated factory the effect is small — and firms may respond over time by replacing workers with robots, which would limit the fall in supply (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a supply curve slopes upwards. (2)
---
A higher price makes production more profitable (1), so existing firms produce more and new firms enter the market (1).
:::

::: q
State two factors, other than price, that could decrease the supply of wheat. (2)
---
Bad weather ruining harvests; a rise in the price of fertiliser or fuel (or a tax on wheat, or farmers switching land to a more profitable crop).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between an increase in supply and an extension of supply. (4)
---
An increase in supply is a shift of the whole curve to the right, caused by a change in a condition of supply such as lower costs, so more is offered at every price (2). An extension is a movement along the curve caused by a rise in the good's own price (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the effect of an indirect tax on the supply curve of cigarettes. (2)
---
The tax raises the cost of selling each packet (1), so supply decreases and the curve shifts to the left (or upwards by the amount of the tax) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how market supply is found from individual supply. (2)
---
By adding the quantity each firm is willing to supply (1) at each price (1).
:::

::: q
Explain, using a diagram, the effect of improved technology on the supply of smartphones. (4)
---
Better technology lowers the cost of making each phone (1), so at every price producing them is more profitable and firms supply more (1). The supply curve shifts right from S1 to S2; at the same price quantity rises from Q1 to Q2 (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse the effect on the supply of fish of a storm that destroys part of a country's fishing fleet. (6)
---
The storm reduces the capital available — fewer boats — so the industry can catch less fish at any price (2). Supply decreases and the curve shifts left, so at the existing price a shortage appears and price will rise (2). The effect lasts until boats are repaired or replaced; if insurance pays out quickly the shift is temporary, but if fishermen leave the industry it may be long-lasting (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a fall in the price of barley might increase the supply of wheat. (2)
---
Farmers can grow either crop on their land (1); barley is now less profitable, so land is switched to wheat, raising wheat supply at every price (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Own price → movement along; anything else → shift.** Same rule as demand.
- **Higher costs shift supply left**; lower costs, technology and subsidies shift it right.
- A **tax decreases** supply; a **subsidy increases** it. Get the direction right before drawing.
- **Label**: S1, S2, P, Q1, Q2, axes. An arrow showing the direction of the shift helps.
- Supply is about **producers** — do not slip into talking about consumers wanting more.
:::
