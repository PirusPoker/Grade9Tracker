## What it is

**Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures how responsive the quantity supplied is to a change in price.

$$PES = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity supplied}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

Price and quantity supplied move the same way, so PES is **positive**.

| Value | Name | Meaning | Curve |
|---|---|---|---|
| greater than 1 | **elastic** | firms can raise output by a bigger % than the price rise | flat-ish |
| less than 1 | **inelastic** | output responds by a smaller % | steep |
| 0 | perfectly inelastic | output cannot change (a concert hall's seats tonight) | vertical |
| infinity | perfectly elastic | any amount supplied at the going price | horizontal |

**What makes supply elastic or inelastic:**

- **Time** — the biggest factor. Immediately after a price rise, firms can only sell what they have (inelastic). Over weeks they add shifts and overtime; over years they build factories and new firms enter (elastic).
- **Spare capacity** — idle machines and workers → output can rise quickly → elastic. Running flat out → inelastic.
- **Stocks** — goods that can be stored (tinned food) → elastic; perishables (fresh fish) → inelastic.
- **Ease of switching production** — a factory that can switch between products → elastic.
- **Factor mobility** — if skilled workers and specialised capital are easy to obtain → elastic.
- **Length of the production process** — crops take a season, ships take years → inelastic. Manufactured goods with short production runs → elastic.

Agricultural goods and housing are the classic **inelastic** supplies; mass-produced manufactured goods are the classic **elastic** ones.

::: terms
- **Price elasticity of supply (PES)**: a measure of the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.
- **PES formula**: percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price.
- **Price elastic supply**: quantity supplied changes by a greater percentage than price (PES greater than 1).
- **Price inelastic supply**: quantity supplied changes by a smaller percentage than price (PES less than 1).
- **Unit elasticity of supply**: quantity supplied changes by the same percentage as price (PES equal to 1).
- **Perfectly inelastic supply**: quantity supplied does not change when price changes (PES = 0).
- **Perfectly elastic supply**: producers will supply any quantity at the current price but none below it (PES is infinite).
- **Spare capacity**: the ability to raise output using resources that are not fully used.
:::

::: method
1. **Calculate** exactly as for PED: percentage change in quantity supplied over percentage change in price, both from original values. The answer is positive.
2. **Interpret**: bigger than 1 elastic, smaller than 1 inelastic. State it.
3. **Explaining why supply is inelastic**: reach first for *time* and *production period* (crops, housing, mining), then spare capacity and stocks.
4. **Explaining why supply is elastic**: spare capacity, stocks, easy-to-source inputs, short production runs.
5. **Linking to price changes**: when demand rises, an inelastic supply means a large price rise and a small quantity rise; elastic supply means the opposite. Use this in analyse questions about shortages and price spikes.
:::

::: example Calculate
The price of wheat rises from 200 to 240 per tonne and the quantity supplied rises from 50,000 to 52,000 tonnes. Calculate the PES and interpret it. (4)

% change in quantity $= \frac{2000}{50000} \times 100 = 4\%$ (1). % change in price $= \frac{40}{200} \times 100 = 20\%$ (1). PES $= \frac{4}{20} = 0.2$ (1). Less than 1, so supply is inelastic — as expected for a crop whose output is fixed until the next harvest (1).
:::

::: example Explain — inelastic supply
Explain two reasons why the supply of new houses is price inelastic. (4)

Building a house takes many months, from obtaining planning permission to finishing construction, so when prices rise builders cannot quickly increase the number completed (2). Suitable land and skilled construction workers are limited, so even with more time it is hard to expand output by a large percentage (2).
:::

::: example Explain — elastic supply
Explain why the supply of printed T-shirts is likely to be price elastic. (4)

Printing firms usually have spare capacity — machines and staff that can run extra hours — so output can rise quickly when price rises (2). Blank T-shirts can be held in stock and the production process takes minutes, so quantity supplied can respond by a large percentage to a small price change (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — time
Analyse how the PES of oil changes over time after a rise in its price. (6)

Immediately after the price rise, producers can only pump from existing wells, which are already near capacity, so quantity supplied barely changes — supply is highly inelastic (2). Over months, producers can reopen high-cost wells and run equipment harder, so supply becomes somewhat more elastic (2). Over years, the high price makes it profitable to explore and drill new fields, and new producers enter, so quantity supplied can rise substantially — supply is far more elastic in the long run than the short run (2).
:::

::: q
Define "price elasticity of supply". (2)
---
A measure of the responsiveness (1) of quantity supplied to a change in price (1).
:::

::: q
Price rises by 10% and quantity supplied rises by 25%. Calculate the PES and interpret it. (3)
---
PES $= \frac{25}{10} = 2.5$ (2); elastic (1).
:::

::: q
Price falls from 50 to 40 and quantity supplied falls from 1,000 to 900. Calculate the PES. (3)
---
% change in Q $= -10\%$; % change in P $= -20\%$; PES $= \frac{-10}{-20} = 0.5$, inelastic.
:::

::: q
Explain why PES is positive while PED is negative. (2)
---
Quantity supplied rises when price rises — same direction, so positive (1); quantity demanded falls when price rises — opposite directions, so negative (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the supply of fresh strawberries is price inelastic. (4)
---
Strawberries take a season to grow, so quantity cannot be increased until the next harvest whatever the price (2). They are perishable and cannot be stored, so growers cannot release stocks when price rises (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how spare capacity affects a firm's PES. (2)
---
With idle machines and workers a firm can raise output quickly when price rises, so supply is elastic (1); at full capacity it cannot, so supply is inelastic (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a supply curve that is perfectly inelastic, and give an example. (2)
---
Quantity supplied does not change at all when price changes — a vertical line (1); e.g. tickets for a stadium on match day, or land (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse why an increase in demand for a good with inelastic supply causes a large rise in its price. (6)
---
The increase in demand creates a shortage at the existing price, so price starts to rise (2). Because supply is inelastic, the higher price brings only a small extension of supply — firms cannot quickly raise output — so the shortage is not eliminated by extra quantity (2). Price therefore has to rise a long way, rationing the limited quantity among buyers, before quantity demanded falls back to match the little extra supplied; with elastic supply the extra output would have kept the price rise small (2).
:::

::: watch
- PES is **positive**. A negative answer means you mixed up the direction of a change.
- **Time** is the determinant examiners most want to see — short run inelastic, long run elastic.
- Give reasons **specific to the good**: growing season for crops, planning permission for housing, spare capacity for factories.
- Inelastic supply + rising demand = **big price rise, small quantity rise**. Use it to explain price spikes.
- Do not confuse PES with PED in the formula — the top line is quantity **supplied**.
:::

::: test
Price rises from 40 to 48 and quantity supplied rises from 500 to 600. Calculate the PES. (2)
---
20% ÷ 20% (1) = 1 — unitary (1).
:::

::: test
PES is 0.4. Price rises by 15%. Calculate the change in quantity supplied. (2)
---
0.4 × 15% (1) = 6% rise (1).
:::

::: test
Explain two factors that make supply price inelastic. (4)
---
Long production time, e.g. crops or ships — output cannot respond quickly (2); no spare capacity or stocks, so firms cannot raise output without investing (2).
:::

::: test
Explain why the supply of manufactured goods is usually more elastic than the supply of agricultural products. (2)
---
Factories can increase output quickly by adding shifts or using stocks (1); crops take a growing season and depend on land and weather (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the elasticity of supply affects the price rise caused by an increase in demand. (3)
---
If supply is inelastic, output cannot expand much (1), so the shortage pushes price up a lot (1); if supply is elastic, output rises and the price increase is small (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why PES rises in the long run. (2)
---
Firms can build capacity, hire and train workers, and new firms can enter (1); so a given price rise brings a larger increase in quantity (1).
:::

::: test
A firm holds large stocks of finished goods. Explain the effect on its PES. (2)
---
It can release stock as soon as price rises (1); supply is more elastic (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what a perfectly inelastic supply curve looks like and give an example. (2)
---
A vertical line — quantity does not change whatever the price (1); e.g. seats at a stadium on match day or an original painting (1).
:::
