## What it is

The **balance of payments** is a record of all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a year. Its most important part is the **current account**, which has **four components**:

| Component | Credits (money in, +) | Debits (money out, −) |
|---|---|---|
| **Trade in goods** (visible trade) | exports of goods — cars, wheat, oil | imports of goods |
| **Trade in services** (invisible trade) | exports of services — tourism, banking, insurance, transport, education for foreign students | imports of services — holidays abroad, foreign shipping |
| **Primary income** | interest, profits and dividends earned on assets abroad; wages of residents working abroad | the same paid out to foreigners |
| **Secondary income** (current transfers) | foreign aid received, remittances sent home by emigrants, payments from international bodies | aid given, remittances sent out, contributions to international organisations |

The **balance of trade (in goods)** $=$ exports of goods $-$ imports of goods. The **current account balance** $=$ the sum of all four balances. A **deficit** means the country pays out more than it receives; a **surplus** the reverse. A deficit on the current account is financed by inflows on the financial account (foreigners buying the country's assets or lending to it) — which is why a persistent deficit means growing debt or growing foreign ownership.

**Causes of a current account deficit:**

- **Low competitiveness** — high inflation, low productivity, poor quality → exports lose sales, imports win them.
- **A high exchange rate** — exports dear, imports cheap.
- **Fast domestic growth** — rising incomes suck in imports.
- **Slow growth abroad** — export markets shrink.
- **Structural change** — a country losing its manufacturing base; dependence on imported energy or food.
- **High interest rates** attracting hot money and raising the currency.
- **Profits repatriated** by foreign multinationals; large aid payments.

Causes of a **surplus** are the mirror image: low inflation, high productivity, a low currency, weak domestic demand, oil or commodity wealth, large earnings on assets abroad (Germany, China, Norway).

**Consequences of a deficit:**

- Lower demand for domestic output → lower **GDP** and **employment** (spending leaks abroad).
- **Debt** to foreigners and interest on it; sale of assets to foreigners.
- Downward pressure on the **exchange rate** (residents sell the currency to buy imports) → import prices rise → **inflation**; but a lower currency then helps correct the deficit.
- Loss of confidence, making borrowing dearer.
- *But*: consumers enjoy cheap imports; a deficit from importing capital goods may raise future growth; and a small deficit financed easily is not a problem.

**Consequences of a surplus**: higher GDP and employment from export demand; foreign assets accumulate; upward pressure on the currency; inflation from higher demand; other countries may retaliate with protection; consumers could have enjoyed more imports.

**Policies for balance of payments stability:**

| Policy | How it works | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Contractionary fiscal or monetary policy** (higher taxes, interest rates) | cuts incomes and spending, so imports fall | slows growth, raises unemployment; higher interest rates raise the currency, which works the other way |
| **Depreciation of the currency** | exports cheaper, imports dearer | inflation; depends on elasticity; retaliation |
| **Protection** (tariffs, quotas) | cuts imports directly | higher prices, retaliation, inefficiency; may break trade rules |
| **Supply-side policy** (training, investment, innovation) | raises productivity and quality → competitiveness → more exports, fewer imports | slow, costly |
| **Export promotion** — subsidies, trade missions, marketing | raises exports | cost; may be counted as unfair |

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Current account of the balance of payments**: a record of a country's trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income with the rest of the world.
- **Balance of trade**: the value of exports of goods minus the value of imports of goods.
- **Current account deficit**: a situation where the value of money leaving the country on the current account exceeds the value coming in.
- **Current account surplus**: a situation where the value of money coming into the country on the current account exceeds the value leaving.

::: method
1. **Calculating balances**: exports minus imports for each component, keeping signs; then add the four balances. Show each line.
2. **Classify items**: physical goods → trade in goods; tourism, transport, finance, education → services; interest, profit, dividends, wages → primary income; aid, remittances, transfers → secondary income.
3. **Causes**: split into *competitiveness* (inflation, productivity, quality), *exchange rate* and *incomes* (domestic vs foreign growth). Two developed chains.
4. **Consequences**: GDP and employment, debt, exchange rate, inflation — each as a chain; then the "not necessarily a problem" evaluation.
5. **Policies**: name it, chain to fewer imports or more exports, then drawback; distinguish quick demand-side fixes from slow supply-side cures. Note conflicts with other aims.
:::

::: example Calculate
A country has: exports of goods 300 billion, imports of goods 380 billion; exports of services 150 billion, imports of services 100 billion; primary income balance +20 billion; secondary income balance −15 billion. Calculate the balance of trade in goods and the current account balance. (4)

Trade in goods $= 300 - 380 = -80$ billion, a deficit (1). Trade in services $= 150 - 100 = +50$ billion (1). Current account $= -80 + 50 + 20 - 15 = -25$ billion — a deficit of 25 billion (2).
:::

::: example Classify
Classify each of the following in the current account of country X: (a) a tourist from abroad spending money in X; (b) X importing oil; (c) a firm in X receiving profit from its factory abroad; (d) a worker in X sending money to family abroad. (4)

(a) Export of services — a credit (1). (b) Import of goods — a debit (1). (c) Primary income — a credit (1). (d) Secondary income — a debit (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a cause
Explain how high inflation can cause a current account deficit. (4)

If prices rise faster at home than abroad, exports become dearer in foreign markets and sales fall (2). Imports become relatively cheaper than domestic goods, so residents buy more of them; with exports down and imports up the current account moves into deficit (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — consequences
Analyse the consequences of a large and persistent current account deficit. (6)

Spending on imports leaks out of the economy, so demand for domestic output is lower than it would be; firms produce less and employ fewer workers, reducing GDP and raising unemployment (2). To pay for the deficit the country must borrow from abroad or sell assets to foreigners, building up debt on which interest must be paid — a burden on future generations — and foreign ownership of the economy grows (2). Residents selling their currency to buy imports push the exchange rate down, which raises import prices and inflation; a falling currency does eventually make exports more competitive, but a large deficit can also cause a loss of confidence and a sudden currency crisis (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — policy
Discuss whether a government should use import tariffs to correct a current account deficit. (8)

For: tariffs raise the price of imports directly, so demand for them contracts and the deficit narrows quickly; domestic producers gain sales and jobs; the government raises revenue; they can be targeted at the goods causing the deficit (3). Against: consumers pay higher prices and have less choice; protected industries have less incentive to become efficient, so competitiveness worsens in the long run; trading partners retaliate, cutting the country's exports, which may leave the deficit no better; tariffs may break trade agreements; and they do nothing about the underlying causes such as low productivity (4). Judgement: tariffs are at best a short-term patch with serious side effects; supply-side measures to raise competitiveness, or a depreciation, tackle the cause more effectively, so tariffs should be a last resort (1).
:::

::: q
State the four components of the current account. (4)
---
Trade in goods; trade in services; primary income; secondary income.
:::

::: q
Exports of goods are 450 billion and imports of goods 520 billion. Calculate the balance of trade. (1)
---
$450 - 520 = -70$ billion: a deficit of 70 billion.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between the balance of trade and the current account balance. (2)
---
The balance of trade covers only goods (1); the current account adds services, primary income and secondary income (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two causes of a current account surplus. (4)
---
High productivity and low inflation make the country's goods cheap and competitive, so exports are high (2). Weak domestic demand or a low exchange rate limits imports while encouraging exports (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a rise in domestic incomes affects the current account. (4)
---
Households spend part of their higher income on imported goods and foreign holidays, so imports rise (2). Firms may also sell more at home rather than exporting, so the current account worsens (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a current account deficit could affect the exchange rate. (4)
---
Residents supply more of the currency to buy imports than foreigners demand to buy exports (2). The excess supply pushes the exchange rate down — a depreciation (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a current account deficit may not be a problem. (4)
---
If the deficit is small or temporary and easily financed by inflows of investment, it costs little (2). If the imports are capital goods, they raise future productive capacity and exports, so the deficit is an investment (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how supply-side policies could improve a country's current account. (4)
---
Training and investment raise productivity, cutting unit costs, and encourage innovation and quality (2). Domestic goods become more competitive, so exports rise and consumers buy fewer imports (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether raising interest rates is an effective way to reduce a current account deficit. (8)
---
For: higher rates cut borrowing and spending, so households buy fewer imports; lower demand also reduces inflation, making exports more competitive over time; the policy is quick to implement (3). Against: higher rates attract hot money, raising the exchange rate, which makes exports dearer and imports cheaper — working against the aim; lower spending slows growth and raises unemployment; firms cut investment, harming future competitiveness; and the deficit's causes — low productivity, poor quality — are untouched (4). Judgement: interest rates are a blunt and partly self-defeating tool for the current account; they are better reserved for inflation, while the deficit is tackled through supply-side improvements and, if needed, a lower exchange rate (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Four components** — goods, services, primary income, secondary income. Classify carefully: tourism is a **service**; profits from abroad are **primary income**; remittances and aid are **secondary income**.
- Balance of trade = **goods only**. Current account = **all four**.
- Keep **signs**: exports +, imports −; show every line of a calculation.
- Consequences and causes must be **chains**, not lists; a deficit is not automatically bad.
- Higher interest rates **raise the currency**, which partly undoes their effect on the deficit — the classic evaluation point.
:::
