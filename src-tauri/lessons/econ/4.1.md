## What it is

**Macroeconomics** looks at the whole economy rather than a single market. Governments pursue a set of **macroeconomic aims**, and the exam expects six:

| Aim | What it means | How success is judged |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic growth** | a rise in the real output of the economy | growth in **real GDP** of about 2–3% a year, steady rather than boom and bust |
| **Full employment (low unemployment)** | everyone willing and able to work can find a job | the **unemployment rate** low — around 3–5% (some frictional unemployment always exists) |
| **Price stability (low inflation)** | a low and stable rise in the general price level | inflation on target — typically **2%** measured by the CPI |
| **Balance of payments stability** | the value of exports and imports of goods, services and income roughly in balance | the **current account** near balance; no large persistent deficit |
| **Redistribution of income** | a fairer distribution of income and wealth | a smaller gap between rich and poor; less poverty; measured by income shares or the Gini coefficient |
| **Sustainable development** (sometimes listed) | growth that does not exhaust resources or damage the environment for future generations | pollution and resource use falling relative to output |

Why they matter: growth raises living standards; employment gives incomes and self-respect and cuts benefit spending; low inflation protects savings and keeps exports competitive; balance of payments stability avoids debt to foreigners and a falling currency; redistribution reduces poverty and social tension.

**Conflicts between the aims** — the reason policy is hard. Achieving one aim often worsens another:

- **Growth / full employment vs low inflation** — the classic trade-off. Boosting demand (lower interest rates, tax cuts) raises output and jobs, but as the economy nears full capacity firms raise prices: **demand-pull inflation**. Curbing inflation with higher interest rates slows growth and raises unemployment.
- **Growth vs balance of payments** — faster growth raises incomes, and consumers spend some of the extra on **imports**, pushing the current account into deficit.
- **Growth vs sustainability / the environment** — more output means more energy, resources, pollution and congestion.
- **Full employment vs balance of payments** — the same import leakage as above.
- **Low inflation vs balance of payments** — raising interest rates to cut inflation attracts foreign money, raising the exchange rate, so exports become dearer and the current account worsens.
- **Redistribution vs growth** — higher taxes on the rich and higher benefits may reduce incentives to work, invest and take risks, slowing growth.
- **Growth vs redistribution** in the other direction: growth may benefit the rich first, widening inequality.

Some aims support each other: growth creates jobs; low inflation helps exports; supply-side policies (4.4) can raise growth and cut inflation together.

::: terms
- **Macroeconomic aims**: the six goals of government policy — economic growth, full employment (low unemployment), price stability (low inflation), balance of payments stability, redistribution of income, and protection of the environment.
- **Economic growth**: an increase in the real output (real GDP) of an economy over time.
- **Full employment**: a situation where everyone willing and able to work at the going wage can find a job.
- **Price stability**: a low and stable rate of inflation.
- **Balance of payments stability**: avoiding a large or persistent deficit or surplus on the current account.
- **Redistribution of income**: reducing inequality by transferring income from richer to poorer groups.
- **Conflict of aims**: when a policy that helps achieve one aim makes another harder to achieve.
:::

::: method
1. **State the aim and its measure.** "Low inflation, measured by the CPI, with a target of about 2%." Aims without criteria are half answers.
2. **Give the reason it matters** for "explain why governments aim for…" — link to living standards, incomes or stability.
3. **Conflicts: show the mechanism.** Not "growth conflicts with inflation" but "policies that raise demand to boost growth push the economy towards full capacity, so firms raise prices — demand-pull inflation."
4. **Use the import chain** for growth/employment vs balance of payments: higher incomes → more spending → some on imports → current account worsens.
5. **For "discuss whether the aims always conflict"**: give two conflicts, then a case where aims support each other (growth creates jobs; supply-side policies help several aims), and a judgement — conflicts are strongest when demand is increased quickly near full capacity.
:::

::: example Define
Define "economic growth". (2)

An increase in the real output of an economy (1), measured by the rise in real GDP over a year (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why an aim matters
Explain why a government aims for price stability. (4)

Low inflation protects the value of money, so savers are not penalised and people on fixed incomes such as pensioners can keep their living standards (2). Stable prices keep the country's exports competitive against foreign goods and let firms plan and invest with confidence, supporting growth and employment (2).
:::

::: example Explain — a conflict
Explain how the aim of economic growth may conflict with the aim of low inflation. (4)

To raise growth a government may cut interest rates or taxes, increasing total demand in the economy (1). As output rises towards full capacity, firms cannot easily produce more, shortages of workers and materials appear and firms raise prices (2), so faster growth brings demand-pull inflation, conflicting with price stability (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — growth and the balance of payments
Analyse how a policy to reduce unemployment might affect the balance of payments. (6)

To cut unemployment the government increases spending or cuts taxes, raising incomes as more people find work (2). Households spend part of their higher income on imported goods — cars, electronics, foreign holidays — so imports rise while exports are unchanged, moving the current account towards deficit (2). If the extra demand also causes inflation, exports become less competitive, worsening the deficit further; the conflict is smaller if the new jobs are in export industries, which would raise exports as well (2).
:::

::: example Explain — protecting the environment
Explain how the aim of economic growth may conflict with the aim of protecting the environment. (4)

Economic growth means producing more goods and services (1), which uses more energy, raw materials and transport (1), so pollution, carbon emissions and the using up of non-renewable resources increase (1); a government that pushes for faster growth may therefore make it harder to meet environmental targets, unless the growth comes from cleaner technology (1).
:::

::: q
State the four main macroeconomic aims of a government. (4)
---
Economic growth; full employment (low unemployment); price stability (low inflation); balance of payments stability (also redistribution of income).
:::

::: q
Explain how each of economic growth and unemployment is measured. (2)
---
Growth by the percentage change in real GDP (1); unemployment by the percentage of the labour force without a job but looking for one (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why governments aim for full employment. (4)
---
Employed people earn incomes, raising living standards and reducing poverty (2). The government collects more tax and pays less in benefits, and using all workers means the economy produces its maximum output (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a government aims for a stable balance of payments. (4)
---
A persistent current account deficit means the country is spending more abroad than it earns, which must be financed by borrowing or selling assets to foreigners (2). It can also push down the exchange rate, raising import prices and inflation (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how the aim of redistributing income may conflict with economic growth. (4)
---
Redistribution requires higher taxes on the better-off and higher benefits for the poor (1). High taxes may reduce the incentive to work harder, invest and start businesses (2), and generous benefits may reduce the incentive to seek work, lowering output and growth (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how economic growth may conflict with protecting the environment. (4)
---
Growth means more output, requiring more energy, raw materials and transport (2). This increases pollution, congestion and the use of non-renewable resources, damaging the environment and future generations (2).
:::

::: q
Give one example of two macroeconomic aims that support each other, and explain why. (2)
---
Growth and full employment (1): rising output requires more workers, so unemployment falls as the economy grows (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government can achieve all its macroeconomic aims at the same time. (8)
---
Against: the aims conflict — raising demand for growth and jobs causes demand-pull inflation and sucks in imports, worsening the current account; cutting inflation with higher interest rates slows growth and raises unemployment; redistribution through high taxes may weaken incentives and growth (4). For: some aims reinforce each other — growth creates jobs and tax revenue for redistribution; low inflation keeps exports competitive, helping the balance of payments; supply-side policies such as education and investment can raise growth, cut unemployment and reduce inflation together, if slowly (3). Judgement: in the short run, especially near full capacity, trade-offs are unavoidable and governments must prioritise; over the long run, supply-side improvements make it possible to move all the aims in the right direction at once (1).
:::

::: watch
- Each aim needs its **measure** — real GDP, unemployment rate, CPI inflation, current account.
- Conflicts need a **mechanism**: demand → capacity → prices; income → imports; taxes → incentives.
- The biggest conflict: **growth/employment versus inflation**. Learn it perfectly.
- Do not confuse **the balance of payments** with **the government budget** — one is trade with abroad, the other tax and spending.
- Balance: some aims **complement** each other; say so in a discuss answer.
:::

::: test
Explain why a government aims to reduce unemployment. (4)
---
Unemployed resources mean lower output — the economy is inside its PPC (1). The government loses tax revenue and pays more in benefits (1). Unemployed people suffer lower incomes and living standards (1), and long-term unemployment can cause social problems and a loss of skills (1).
:::

::: test
Analyse how a policy to cut income tax could help achieve one macroeconomic aim but conflict with another. (6)
---
Lower income tax raises disposable income, so consumer spending increases and firms produce more, boosting economic growth and employment (2). Lower tax may also increase incentives to work, raising output (2). But higher spending may increase demand faster than supply, causing inflation, and more spending on imports may worsen the current account; it may also widen inequality, conflicting with redistribution (2).
Marking: up to 6 marks for a developed, logical chain of analysis; 1–2 for simple points without explanation.
:::

::: test
Discuss whether economic growth should be a government's most important macroeconomic aim. (8)
---
Yes: growth raises incomes and living standards; creates jobs; increases tax revenue for public services; makes it easier to redistribute income (4).
No: growth can cause inflation, a current account deficit, pollution and resource depletion; the benefits may be unequally shared; price stability or employment may matter more to people's wellbeing (4).
Conclusion: growth is important but should be sustainable and balanced with other aims; its priority depends on the country's situation.
Marking: up to 4 marks for developed analysis of both sides; up to 4 for evaluation — weighing the arguments and reaching a justified conclusion.
:::
