## What it is

**Production** is the process of turning inputs (the factors of production) into goods and services. A firm's **demand for factors** — how much land, labour and capital it wants — depends on:

- **Demand for the product** — factor demand is *derived* demand; more orders mean more workers and machines wanted.
- **The price of the factor** — dearer labour → fewer workers, more machines.
- **The price of other factors** — if capital becomes cheaper (new technology), firms substitute it for labour.
- **Productivity of the factor** — a more productive worker is worth hiring at a higher wage.
- **Availability** — a shortage of skilled labour pushes firms towards capital.

**Labour-intensive** production uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital: hairdressing, teaching, hand-made goods, farming in poor countries. **Capital-intensive** production uses a high proportion of capital: car assembly, oil refining, chemicals, electricity generation.

A firm chooses between them by comparing:

| Choose labour-intensive when… | Choose capital-intensive when… |
|---|---|
| labour is cheap and plentiful | labour is expensive or scarce |
| output is small — machines would sit idle | output is large — machines run flat out (technical economies of scale) |
| the product is varied or personal | the product is standardised |
| capital is expensive to buy or borrow for | interest rates are low; the firm has funds |
| flexibility matters — workers can be laid off | consistency matters — machines make no mistakes |

**Production vs productivity** — do not confuse:

- **Production** is the *total* output produced (10,000 cars a month).
- **Productivity** is output *per unit of input* — usually **labour productivity** = output ÷ number of workers (or per hour worked). Ten workers making 200 chairs a week have a productivity of 20 chairs per worker per week.

Production can rise without productivity rising (hire more workers). Productivity rising means the *same* inputs make *more* — that is what cuts average cost.

**What raises productivity:**

- **Investment in capital** — more and better machines per worker.
- **Technology** — faster, more accurate processes.
- **Training and education** — skilled workers produce more.
- **Motivation** — pay incentives, better conditions, good management.
- **Division of labour** and specialisation.
- **Better organisation** of the workplace.

**Effect of investment**: new capital raises the capital-to-labour ratio → output per worker rises → average cost falls → the firm can cut prices or raise profit, and the economy can produce more (the PPC shifts out). The cost: capital goods are expensive, they take time to install, workers must be trained, and some jobs may be lost.

::: terms
- **Demand for factors of production**: how much of each factor a firm wants to employ, which depends on demand for its product, the price and productivity of the factor, and the price of other factors.
- **Production**: the total output of goods and services produced by a firm or economy.
- **Productivity**: output per unit of input, e.g. output per worker per hour.
- **Labour productivity**: total output divided by the number of workers (or hours worked).
- **Labour-intensive**: production that uses a large proportion of labour relative to capital.
- **Capital-intensive**: production that uses a large proportion of capital relative to labour.
- **Investment**: spending on capital goods.
:::

::: method
1. **Productivity = output ÷ input.** Show the division: "5,000 units ÷ 50 workers = 100 units per worker." If given hours, divide by total hours.
2. **Production vs productivity**: read the question carefully. "Output rose" is production; "output per worker rose" is productivity.
3. **Labour- or capital-intensive?** Decide from the relative cost of labour and capital, the scale of output and the nature of the product — then justify with two reasons applied to the firm in the question.
4. **Investment chain**: investment → more/better capital per worker → higher productivity → lower average cost → lower price or higher profit / higher output. Then the drawbacks: cost, training, redundancies.
5. **Factor demand questions**: derived demand first, then factor prices and productivity.
:::

::: example Define
Define "productivity". (2)

The amount of output produced (1) per unit of input, such as output per worker per hour (1).
:::

::: example Calculate
A factory with 40 workers produces 6,000 units a week. After investing in new machines, 32 workers produce 6,400 units. Calculate productivity before and after. (3)

Before: 6,000 ÷ 40 = 150 units per worker (1). After: 6,400 ÷ 32 = 200 units per worker (1). Productivity has risen by 50 units per worker, a third higher, even though fewer workers are employed (1).
:::

::: example Explain — choosing capital-intensive production
Explain why a car manufacturer is likely to use capital-intensive production. (4)

Cars are standardised products made in very large numbers, so expensive robots and assembly lines run continuously and their cost is spread over a huge output — technical economies of scale (2). Labour in car-producing countries is relatively expensive, and machines weld and paint more accurately and consistently than workers, so capital gives lower cost per car and higher quality (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — investment
Analyse the effects on a firm of investing in new technology. (6)

New technology raises labour productivity: each worker, using better equipment, produces more per hour, so the firm's output rises or the same output needs fewer workers (2). Average cost falls, letting the firm cut prices to win sales or keep prices and raise profit; quality and consistency may also improve, strengthening its competitiveness (2). Against this, the investment is expensive and may require borrowing at interest, workers must be trained or made redundant — which can damage morale — and if demand does not rise the firm may have spare capacity, so the benefit depends on the cost of capital and the state of demand (2).
:::

::: example Explain — the demand for labour
Explain two factors that could increase a construction firm's demand for bricklayers. (4)

Higher demand for new houses (1): demand for labour is derived from demand for the product, so if more homes are wanted, the firm needs more bricklayers to build them (1).

A rise in the price of machinery that could replace bricklayers (1): if capital becomes more expensive relative to labour, the firm uses more labour-intensive methods and employs more bricklayers (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between production and productivity. (4)
---
Production is the total quantity of output produced (2). Productivity is output per unit of input, such as per worker or per hour; production can rise by adding workers without productivity changing (2).
:::

::: q
A firm's 25 workers produce 2,000 units a day. Calculate labour productivity. (1)
---
2,000 ÷ 25 = 80 units per worker per day.
:::

::: q
Explain two factors that influence a firm's demand for labour. (4)
---
Demand for the product: labour is a derived demand, so more sales mean more workers are wanted (2). The wage rate relative to the cost of capital: if wages rise, firms substitute machines for workers (2).
:::

::: q
Give two examples of labour-intensive industries and explain why they are labour-intensive. (4)
---
Hairdressing and hotel service (1). Each customer wants personal, varied attention that machines cannot provide (1); output is small-scale so expensive capital could not be used fully (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways a firm could raise the productivity of its workers. (4)
---
Training improves skills so tasks are done faster and with fewer errors (2). Investing in better machinery gives each worker more capital to work with, raising output per hour (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a firm in a country with low wages might choose labour-intensive production. (4)
---
Cheap labour makes hiring workers less costly per unit of output than buying machines (2). Capital may be expensive to import and borrowing costly, so the return on investment is lower than on employing workers (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how higher productivity can lead to lower prices. (4)
---
Higher productivity means more output from the same inputs, so the cost per unit falls (2). In a competitive market firms pass lower costs on as lower prices to win customers (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a rise in wages might change a firm's method of production. (6)
---
Higher wages raise the cost of labour relative to capital, so labour-intensive methods become more expensive per unit of output (2). The firm has an incentive to substitute capital for labour — buying machines, automating tasks — becoming more capital-intensive and cutting its demand for workers (2). This is only possible if suitable technology exists and the firm can afford the investment; for personal services that cannot be automated, the firm may instead raise prices or accept lower profit (2).
:::

::: q
Explain one possible disadvantage for workers of a firm investing in new capital. (2)
---
Machines may replace workers (1), causing redundancies or requiring retraining (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Productivity is per unit of input** — always show the division. Rising output alone is production, not productivity.
- Labour- vs capital-intensive is about the **proportion** of each factor, not whether any machines are used.
- Justify the choice of method with **relative factor costs, scale and the product** — applied to the firm in the question.
- Investment: give the chain **investment → productivity → average cost → price/profit**, plus a drawback.
- Factor demand is **derived** — start there.
:::
