## What it is

**Economic development** is broader than growth: it is the process of improving living standards, health, education and economic freedom over time. Countries are grouped as **developed** (high income — the USA, Germany, Japan), **emerging** or newly industrialised (China, India, Brazil) and **developing** (low income — much of sub-Saharan Africa). The differences show up in a set of **characteristics**:

| Characteristic | Developed | Developing |
|---|---|---|
| GDP per head | high | low |
| Structure of employment | tertiary (services) dominant | primary (agriculture) dominant, often subsistence |
| Population growth | low or negative; ageing | high; young |
| Education | universal, long | low literacy, especially for girls |
| Health | high life expectancy, low infant mortality | low life expectancy, high infant mortality |
| Savings and investment | high | low — the poverty cycle |
| Capital and technology | modern | old or scarce |
| Exports | manufactured goods and services | primary commodities |
| Urbanisation | high | rising fast; slums |
| Public services | extensive | limited |

**Why countries differ in development:**

- **The poverty cycle (vicious circle)**: low income → low savings → low investment → low capital per worker → low productivity → low income. Breaking it needs outside capital (aid, foreign investment) or a jump in productivity.
- **Population growth** outpacing output → GDP per head stagnates; resources go to feeding children rather than investment.
- **Low levels of education and health** → low productivity.
- **Dependence on primary products** — prices are volatile and tend to fall relative to manufactured goods; bad harvests wreck income; little value added.
- **Lack of infrastructure** — roads, power, water, communications.
- **Debt** — interest payments swallow export earnings.
- **Political instability, war, corruption, weak legal systems** — deter investment.
- **Trade barriers** in rich countries against developing-country goods, especially farm products.
- **Climate, disease, geography** — landlocked, tropical, drought-prone.
- **Historical factors** — colonial economies built to export raw materials.

**Consequences of the differences:** low living standards and absolute poverty; migration from poor to rich countries; dependence on aid; environmental damage from desperate use of resources; political instability and conflict; a global market for rich countries' goods that is smaller than it could be. For developed countries: cheap imports and labour, but also pressure to give aid, manage migration and cope with instability.

**Strategies for development** (linking to policies elsewhere in the course): investment in education and health; infrastructure; encouraging foreign direct investment by multinationals (6.2); export diversification into manufacturing; microfinance and land reform; population policies; foreign aid and debt relief; fairer trade access; good governance.

::: terms
- **Economic development**: the process of improving the living standards, health, education and economic freedom of a population over time.
- **Developed country**: a country with high income per head, high living standards and most employment in the secondary and tertiary sectors.
- **Developing country**: a country with low income per head, low living standards and a large share of employment in agriculture.
- **Poverty cycle**: the situation in which low income leads to low saving and investment, low productivity and so continued low income.
- **Primary product dependence**: reliance on exporting raw materials and agricultural goods for most export earnings.
- **Foreign aid**: help given by one country or organisation to another — grants, loans, food, expertise.
:::

::: method
1. **Characteristics**: give the *contrast* — "developing countries have a high proportion of workers in agriculture, whereas developed countries are dominated by services". Three or four for a 4–6 mark question.
2. **Causes**: lead with the poverty cycle (draw the circle in words), then population, education/health, primary dependence, infrastructure, governance. Develop each into a chain ending in low income or low growth.
3. **Consequences**: for the poor country and for the rest of the world.
4. **Strategies**: match to the cause — poverty cycle → foreign investment or aid; primary dependence → diversify; low skills → education.
5. **Evaluate**: aid and investment can help but may be misused or create dependence; growth must be shared; development takes decades.
:::

::: example Define
Define "economic development". (2)

The process by which a country improves the living standards of its population (1) — including health, education and economic freedom, not just income (1).
:::

::: example Explain — characteristics
Explain two ways a developing economy differs from a developed one. (4)

Structure of employment: in a developing economy most people work in agriculture, often subsistence farming, whereas in a developed economy most work in services (2). Population: developing countries have high birth rates and young, fast-growing populations, while developed countries have low birth rates and ageing populations (2).
:::

::: example Explain — the poverty cycle
Explain how the poverty cycle keeps a country poor. (4)

Low incomes mean households spend everything on necessities and save little, so banks have little to lend and investment in capital is low (2). With little capital per worker, productivity stays low, so output and incomes stay low — and the cycle begins again unless capital comes from outside (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — primary product dependence
Analyse why dependence on primary products may hold back a country's development. (6)

Prices of commodities such as coffee, copper and cocoa swing widely on world markets, so export earnings are unstable; a price collapse cuts government revenue and makes planning investment impossible (2). Over the long run primary product prices tend to fall relative to manufactured goods, so the country must export more and more to buy the same imports of machinery — its terms of trade worsen (2). Little value is added before export, so the profitable processing and manufacturing jobs are elsewhere; diversifying into manufacturing or processing is the usual way out, but requires the capital and skills the country lacks (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — foreign aid
Discuss whether foreign aid is an effective way to promote development. (8)

For: aid provides the capital a poor country cannot save itself, breaking the poverty cycle; spent on health, education and infrastructure it raises productivity for the long term; emergency aid saves lives after disasters; debt relief frees export earnings for investment (4). Against: aid may be wasted or stolen by corrupt governments; tied aid must be spent on the donor's goods; it can create dependence and reduce the incentive to reform; food aid can undercut local farmers; and the amounts are small compared with trade or private investment (3). Judgement: well-targeted aid for health, education and infrastructure in countries with good governance is effective, but aid alone does not develop a country — access to trade, private investment and sound policies matter more (1).
:::

::: q
State three characteristics of a developing country. (3)
---
Low GDP per head; high proportion of employment in agriculture; high birth rate (or low life expectancy, low literacy, reliance on primary exports).
:::

::: q
Explain why a high rate of population growth can slow development. (4)
---
If population grows as fast as output, GDP per head does not rise (2). Resources must go to feeding, housing and schooling children rather than to investment in capital, and the dependency ratio is high (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how poor infrastructure limits development. (4)
---
Without reliable roads, ports and electricity firms face high transport and production costs and cannot reach markets (2). Foreign firms are discouraged from investing, and farmers cannot get produce to towns before it spoils, so output and incomes stay low (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a lack of education is both a cause and a consequence of underdevelopment. (4)
---
Cause: uneducated workers have low productivity, so output and incomes are low and new technology cannot be used (2). Consequence: poor countries cannot afford schools and poor families need children to work, so few are educated (2).
:::

::: q
Explain one consequence for developed countries of large differences in development. (2)
---
Migration from poor to rich countries (1), creating a larger workforce but pressure on services and political tension (1) — or cheaper imports and labour.
:::

::: q
Explain how debt can hold back a developing country. (4)
---
Interest and repayments to foreign lenders take a large share of export earnings and tax revenue (2). Less is left for investment in health, education and infrastructure, so productivity and growth stay low (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how encouraging multinational companies to invest could help a developing country. (4)
---
Multinationals bring capital, technology and management skills the country lacks, breaking the poverty cycle (2). They create jobs, train workers, pay taxes and generate exports, raising incomes and foreign currency (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether developing countries should concentrate on producing manufactured goods rather than primary products. (8)
---
For manufacturing: it adds more value and creates skilled jobs, its prices are more stable and rising relative to commodities, it reduces vulnerability to bad harvests and price collapses, and it builds skills and technology that spread through the economy — the path taken by the East Asian economies (3). Against: many developing countries lack the capital, skills, infrastructure and energy to compete with established producers; they may have a genuine advantage in agriculture or minerals; protecting new industries costs consumers and may breed inefficiency; and neglecting farming risks food shortages (3). Judgement: diversifying gradually into processing and manufacturing while continuing to earn from primary products, with investment in education and infrastructure, is more realistic than an abrupt switch (2).
:::

::: watch
- Development is **broader than growth** — health, education, freedom, distribution.
- The **poverty cycle** is the single most useful explanation: income → saving → investment → productivity → income.
- **Contrast** developed and developing on each characteristic; do not just describe one.
- Causes need **chains to low income**; strategies need to be **matched to the cause**.
- Aid and multinationals: give **both sides** and judge by governance and how the money is used.
:::

::: test
Explain two reasons why countries differ in their level of development. (4)
---
Differences in natural resources and climate (2); differences in education, healthcare, infrastructure, political stability and investment (2).
:::

::: test
Explain why a developing country's economy is often dominated by the primary sector. (3)
---
Lack of capital and skills for manufacturing (1); low incomes mean demand is mainly for food (1); exports rely on raw materials, which have low and unstable prices (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a lack of clean water and healthcare holds back economic development. (3)
---
Illness reduces the productivity of workers (1); children miss school (1); resources are spent treating preventable disease rather than investing (1).
:::

::: test
Explain two ways developed countries can help developing countries. (4)
---
Aid — grants or loans for infrastructure, health and education (2); reducing trade barriers so developing countries can export, or debt relief (2).
:::

::: test
Explain one possible disadvantage of foreign aid. (2)
---
It may create dependency or be spent poorly through corruption (1); tied aid may benefit the donor more than the recipient (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why developing countries often have a high rate of population growth. (2)
---
High birth rates because children provide labour and support in old age, and contraception is limited (1); falling death rates thanks to basic healthcare (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how investment by multinational companies can promote development. (3)
---
Brings capital and jobs (1); transfers technology and skills to local workers (1); increases exports and tax revenue (1).
:::

::: test
Analyse whether a developing country should borrow to fund development. (4)
---
Borrowing allows immediate investment in infrastructure and education (1); this can raise growth and future tax revenue to repay the loan (1); but interest payments drain resources, especially in foreign currency (1); if growth fails, debt becomes a burden that limits future spending (1).
:::
