## What it is

The **money supply** is the total amount of money in an economy — notes and coins plus bank deposits. **Monetary policy** is the use of **interest rates**, the **money supply** and the **exchange rate** by the central bank (or government) to influence the economy. Its usual target is **low inflation**, but it also affects growth, employment and the balance of payments.

**The three measures:**

**1. The interest rate** — the main tool. The central bank sets a base rate; commercial banks follow. The interest rate is both the *cost of borrowing* and the *reward for saving*.

- **Raising** the rate (contractionary): borrowing dearer → households cut spending on credit-bought goods and firms cut investment; saving more attractive; mortgage repayments rise, leaving less to spend → total demand falls → inflation falls. Also attracts foreign money → exchange rate rises → imports cheaper (lower inflation) but exports dearer.
- **Cutting** the rate (expansionary): the reverse — more borrowing, spending and investment → higher demand, output and jobs.

**2. The money supply** — the central bank can create money to buy government bonds from banks (**quantitative easing**), giving banks more to lend and pushing interest rates down; or restrict lending through reserve requirements. More money → more spending → higher output, but too much money chasing too few goods → inflation.

**3. The exchange rate** — the central bank can buy or sell its own currency, or change interest rates, to move the exchange rate. A *lower* rate makes exports cheaper and imports dearer → helps the balance of payments and growth, but raises import prices → inflation. A *higher* rate does the opposite.

**How monetary policy serves the aims:**

| Aim | Policy | Chain |
|---|---|---|
| Low inflation | raise interest rates | borrowing dearer, saving more attractive → spending and investment fall → demand-pull inflation falls |
| Growth and employment | cut interest rates | cheaper credit → more consumption and investment → output rises → firms hire |
| Balance of payments | raise rates / lower exchange rate | less spending on imports; a lower currency boosts exports |
| Stability | adjust rates gradually | smooth the ups and downs of demand |

**Limitations**: **time lags** — a rate change takes 18 months or more to have its full effect; **confidence** — in a deep recession firms will not invest and households will not borrow however cheap credit is; rates cannot fall much below zero; **conflicts** — higher rates hurt growth, jobs and exporters and raise mortgage costs; banks may not pass on rate changes; **cost-push inflation** (from oil prices) is not cured by higher rates, which merely add to the squeeze.

::: terms
- **Money supply**: the total amount of money in an economy at a given time.
- **Monetary policy**: the use of interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate to influence the economy.
- **Interest rate**: the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving, expressed as a percentage.
- **Expansionary monetary policy**: cutting interest rates or increasing the money supply to raise total demand.
- **Contractionary monetary policy**: raising interest rates or reducing the money supply to lower total demand.
- **Total (aggregate) demand**: the total spending on a country's goods and services — by households, firms, the government and foreigners.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the tool and the direction**: "raise the interest rate (contractionary monetary policy)".
2. **Trace the chain through spending**: borrowing/saving → consumer spending and investment → total demand → output/jobs/prices. Two or three links per point.
3. **Add the exchange-rate route** for higher marks: higher rates → hot money inflow → currency appreciates → imports cheaper, exports dearer.
4. **Match the aim**: inflation → raise rates; recession/unemployment → cut; current account deficit → raise rates or lower the exchange rate.
5. **Always evaluate**: time lags, confidence, conflicts with other aims, whether the inflation is demand-pull or cost-push.
6. **Monetary vs fiscal**: monetary = interest rates and money (central bank); fiscal = tax and spending (government). Do not mix them in one answer unless comparing.
:::

::: example Define
Define "monetary policy". (2)

The use of interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate (1) by the central bank or government to influence total demand and achieve macroeconomic aims (1).
:::

::: example Explain — reducing inflation
Explain how a rise in interest rates can reduce inflation. (4)

Higher interest rates raise the cost of borrowing and mortgage repayments, so households cut spending, and firms reduce investment because loans are dearer (2). Saving becomes more rewarding, so more income is saved rather than spent; total demand falls, easing the pressure that was pushing prices up — demand-pull inflation falls (2).
:::

::: example Explain — the exchange rate route
Explain how a rise in interest rates might affect a country's exchange rate and its inflation. (4)

Higher rates attract foreign savers seeking a better return, so demand for the currency rises and the exchange rate appreciates (2). A stronger currency makes imports cheaper, lowering the price of imported goods and raw materials and so reducing inflation — though exports become dearer abroad (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — cutting rates in a recession
Analyse how a cut in interest rates might help an economy in recession. (6)

Cheaper borrowing encourages households to buy houses, cars and other goods on credit, while lower mortgage repayments leave those with variable-rate loans more to spend; consumer spending rises (2). Firms find investment projects more profitable when loans are cheaper, so they buy new capital, raising demand for machinery and creating jobs; the lower rate may also depress the exchange rate, boosting exports (2). However, if confidence is very low households may save rather than borrow and firms may delay investment regardless of the rate; banks may not pass the cut on; and the effects take many months, so the policy may need to be combined with expansionary fiscal policy (2).
:::

::: example Explain — increasing the money supply
Explain how an increase in the money supply could reduce unemployment. (4)

With more money in the economy, banks have more to lend, so borrowing becomes easier and cheaper (1). Households borrow and spend more, and firms borrow to invest (1), so total demand rises (1); firms need more workers to meet the higher demand, so cyclical unemployment falls — though if the economy is near full capacity, the extra demand may instead raise inflation (1).
:::

::: q
State the three tools of monetary policy. (3)
---
Interest rates; the money supply; the exchange rate.
:::

::: q
Explain why a rise in interest rates might reduce consumer spending. (4)
---
Borrowing on credit cards and loans costs more, so consumers buy fewer big-ticket items (2). Mortgage repayments rise, leaving less disposable income, and higher returns make saving more attractive than spending (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a cut in interest rates affects investment by firms. (2)
---
Loans to buy capital are cheaper (1), so more projects earn a return above the cost of borrowing and investment rises (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how an increase in the money supply might cause inflation. (4)
---
More money in the economy means banks lend more and households and firms spend more (2). If the economy is near capacity output cannot rise to match, so too much money chases too few goods and prices are bid up (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two limitations of monetary policy. (4)
---
Time lags: interest rate changes take up to two years to affect spending fully, by which time conditions may have changed (2). Low confidence: in a deep recession households and firms will not borrow even at very low rates, so cutting rates has little effect (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between monetary and fiscal policy. (2)
---
Monetary policy uses interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate, usually via the central bank (1); fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why raising interest rates to cut inflation may conflict with other aims. (4)
---
Lower spending and investment reduce output and cost jobs, harming growth and employment (2). A higher exchange rate makes exports less competitive, worsening the current account, and higher mortgage costs hit indebted households (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether monetary policy is the best way to reduce inflation. (8)
---
For: interest rates can be changed quickly and frequently by an independent central bank without political delay; higher rates cut borrowing, spending and investment, lowering demand-pull inflation, and a stronger currency reduces import prices; the policy is reversible (3). Against: the full effect takes up to two years; it is ineffective against cost-push inflation from oil or wage rises, where higher rates just deepen the squeeze; it harms growth, jobs, exporters and borrowers; and fiscal policy — higher taxes or lower spending — or supply-side measures to raise capacity may tackle the cause more directly (3). Judgement: monetary policy is the most practical tool against demand-pull inflation, but works best alongside fiscal restraint and long-term supply-side policies; whether it is "best" depends on the cause of the inflation (2).
:::

::: watch
- Interest rate = **cost of borrowing and reward for saving**. Use both halves in the chain.
- Chains, not assertions: rate ↑ → borrowing ↓, saving ↑ → spending and investment ↓ → demand ↓ → inflation ↓.
- Add the **exchange rate** route — it is the mark most students miss.
- **Time lags and confidence** are the standard limitations; also whether inflation is **cost-push**.
- Monetary policy is run by the **central bank**; do not credit it to "the government cutting taxes".
:::

---
