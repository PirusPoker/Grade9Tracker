## What it is

Markets differ in how many sellers there are and how much power each has over price. Two extremes are examined:

**A competitive market** has **many** buyers and sellers, each too small to influence price; **similar products**; **easy entry and exit** (no barriers to new firms starting up); and **good information** for buyers. Examples: street food, fruit and vegetables, hairdressers, taxi services.

Effects of competition:

- **Price** — firms are **price takers**: charge more than rivals and you lose your customers. Prices stay close to average cost.
- **Profit** — only normal profit in the long run. If profits rise, new firms enter, supply rises and price falls back.
- **Efficiency** — firms must cut costs to survive; wasteful firms close.
- **Quality and innovation** — firms compete on quality and new products to attract customers.
- **Choice** — many sellers, lots of variety.
- Downsides: small firms cannot gain economies of scale; low profit means little money for research; duplication of effort.

**A monopoly** is a market with a **single seller** (in law, often a firm with more than 25% of the market). It exists because of **barriers to entry**: legal barriers (patents, licences), ownership of a key resource, very high start-up costs, economies of scale so large that one firm can supply the whole market cheaper than two could (a **natural monopoly** — water, electricity grids, railways), brand loyalty, and aggressive pricing to drive rivals out.

Effects of monopoly:

- **Price** — the monopolist is a **price maker**: with no rivals it restricts output and sets price above the competitive level.
- **Profit** — high profits persist because barriers keep new firms out.
- **Choice** — one supplier, so little choice; quality may fall since customers cannot go elsewhere.
- **Efficiency** — with no competition there is less pressure to cut costs (X-inefficiency).
- **But**: large profits can fund **research and innovation**; a monopoly can exploit **economies of scale**, so its average cost — and possibly price — may be lower than many small firms could achieve; a natural monopoly avoids wasteful duplication (two sets of water pipes).

Governments regulate monopolies with **competition policy** — investigating mergers, banning anti-competitive practices, capping prices of utilities, or nationalising them.

::: terms
- **Competitive market**: a market with many firms, none large enough to influence the market price.
- **Monopoly**: a market with a single seller of a product with no close substitutes.
- **Market power**: a firm's ability to influence the price and output in its market.
- **Barriers to entry**: obstacles that prevent new firms from entering a market — high set-up costs, legal protection, control of resources, brand loyalty.
- **Price taker**: a firm that must accept the market price.
- **Price maker**: a firm with the power to set its own price.
:::

::: method
1. **Describe the market with its features**: number of firms, product similarity, ease of entry. Then derive the effects on price, profit, choice, efficiency.
2. **Competition chain**: many firms → each is a price taker → must match rivals' prices → must cut costs → only normal profit; high profit attracts entrants.
3. **Monopoly chain**: one firm + barriers to entry → price maker → restricts output, raises price → high profit persists → less incentive to be efficient.
4. **Balance monopoly**: always give the possible benefits — economies of scale, funds for innovation, no duplication — before a judgement. The exam rewards "it depends on whether the monopoly passes on lower costs and whether it is regulated".
5. **Barriers to entry** explain *why* monopoly power lasts. Name two specific ones for a given industry.
:::

::: example Define
Define "monopoly". (2)

A market in which there is only one seller (1) of a product that has no close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry (1).
:::

::: example Explain — competition and price
Explain why prices tend to be low in a competitive market. (4)

With many firms selling similar products, any firm charging more than its rivals loses its customers, so each firm must accept the market price — it is a price taker (2). If firms earn high profits, new firms enter easily, increasing supply and pushing price down towards average cost, so prices stay low (2).
:::

::: example Explain — barriers to entry
Explain two barriers to entry that might protect a monopoly water company. (4)

Enormous start-up costs: a rival would have to lay its own network of pipes and reservoirs, costing billions before earning any revenue (2). Economies of scale: the existing firm spreads its fixed costs over every customer, so a new entrant with fewer customers would have far higher average costs and could not compete on price — a natural monopoly (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — effects of monopoly on consumers
Analyse the effects of a monopoly on consumers. (6)

With no competitors the monopolist can restrict output and raise price above the competitive level, so consumers pay more and buy less than they would in a competitive market (2). Choice is limited to one supplier, and because customers cannot switch, the firm has less incentive to maintain quality or customer service and may become inefficient, keeping costs and prices high (2). However, the monopolist's size may bring economies of scale that lower average cost, and its high profits can fund research into new products; if it passes savings on or is regulated by the government, consumers may benefit — so the effect depends on the firm's behaviour and regulation (2).
:::

::: example Explain — a monopoly's possible benefits
Explain two ways in which a monopoly might benefit consumers. (4)

Economies of scale (1): as the only supplier, a monopoly produces on a very large scale, so its average costs may be low enough to charge a lower price than many small competing firms could (1).

Research and development (1): the high profits a monopoly earns, protected by barriers to entry, can pay for research into new products and better technology, which consumers benefit from (1).
:::

::: q
State three features of a competitive market. (3)
---
Many buyers and sellers; similar products; easy entry and exit (or good information).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a "price taker". (2)
---
A firm that has to accept the price set by the market (1) because it is too small to influence it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why firms in a competitive market are likely to be efficient. (4)
---
They cannot raise price above rivals, so the only way to increase profit is to cut costs (2). Firms with higher costs than rivals make losses and are driven out, leaving only efficient producers (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons a monopoly might charge a higher price than a competitive firm. (4)
---
It has no rivals to undercut it, so consumers must pay its price or go without (2). Barriers to entry stop new firms entering when profits are high, so the high price is not competed away (2).
:::

::: q
Give two examples of barriers to entry. (2)
---
Patents; high start-up costs (or economies of scale, legal licences, control of a raw material, brand loyalty).
:::

::: q
Explain one possible advantage of a monopoly. (2)
---
Large economies of scale lower average cost (1), so a single firm may supply the market more cheaply than several small ones could (1) — or its profits fund innovation.
:::

::: q
Explain why a competitive market may offer more choice than a monopoly. (2)
---
Many firms each try to attract customers with different products (1), whereas a monopolist offers only its own range with no rival alternatives (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a monopoly may affect the efficiency of an industry. (6)
---
Without competition the monopolist faces no pressure to keep costs down; managers may allow waste or high salaries because profits are protected by barriers to entry (2). It may restrict output below the competitive level to hold prices up, so resources are under-used and consumers get less than they would in a competitive market (2). On the other hand a single large producer can exploit economies of scale and avoid the duplication of two firms building the same infrastructure, so for natural monopolies one firm may actually be the most efficient structure, provided a regulator controls its prices (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should break up a monopoly into several smaller firms. (8)
---
For: competition between the new firms would push prices down towards average cost, raise quality and choice, and force efficiency, ending the monopolist's excess profits; consumers gain (3). Against: smaller firms lose economies of scale, so average costs — and possibly prices — could rise; in a natural monopoly such as water, duplication of networks would be wasteful; the monopolist's research funding would fall; and breaking up a firm is costly and may take years (3). Judgement: breaking up works where the monopoly holds no significant economies of scale, as with a retail chain, but for natural monopolies regulating prices or nationalising is better than splitting the firm (2).
:::

::: watch
- Competitive: **price taker, normal profit, efficient, choice.** Monopoly: **price maker, high profit, barriers to entry, less choice.**
- Always explain **why** — the chain through entry of new firms (competition) or barriers (monopoly).
- Monopoly is not all bad: **economies of scale, innovation, natural monopoly**. Give both sides before judging.
- Name **specific barriers** for the industry in the question.
- "Monopoly" in law can mean 25%+ market share, but define it as a **single seller** for the 2-mark definition.
:::

::: test
Explain two characteristics of a monopoly. (4)
---
A single seller with a large share of the market — no close substitutes (2); high barriers to entry keep competitors out, so the firm is a price maker (2).
:::

::: test
Explain two ways a monopoly might harm consumers. (4)
---
Higher prices because there is no competition (2); less choice, lower quality or less innovation because there is no pressure to improve (2).
:::

::: test
Explain two reasons a monopoly might benefit consumers. (4)
---
Economies of scale can lower costs and, potentially, prices (2); high profits can fund research and development into new products (2).
:::

::: test
Explain how barriers to entry protect a monopoly. (3)
---
Legal barriers such as patents stop others copying products (1); high start-up costs or economies of scale make it too expensive to enter (1); brand loyalty or control of raw materials keeps customers or inputs away from rivals (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why firms in a competitive market tend to be efficient. (3)
---
They must keep costs low or lose customers to cheaper rivals (1); inefficient firms make losses and leave (1); competition rewards innovation and quality (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why prices tend to be lower in competitive markets. (2)
---
Many sellers compete for customers (1); any firm charging more than the market price loses sales, so price is driven down to close to cost (1).
:::

::: test
Explain two ways a government could regulate a monopoly. (4)
---
Set a maximum price or limit price rises (2); set quality standards, fine anti-competitive behaviour, or break the firm up (2).
:::

::: test
Analyse whether a monopoly is always bad for the economy. (4)
---
It can raise prices and restrict output, reducing consumer welfare (1); but natural monopolies such as water avoid wasteful duplication (1); economies of scale and R&D can benefit consumers (1); the outcome depends on regulation and the firm's behaviour (1).
:::
