## What it is

**Globalisation** is the process by which the world's economies become more **integrated** — more trade in goods and services, more movement of capital, labour, technology and ideas across borders, so that events in one country quickly affect others.

**Causes of the rise in globalisation:**

- **Cheaper transport** — containerisation, larger ships, air freight.
- **Cheaper, faster communication** — the internet, mobile phones — letting firms manage production anywhere and sell online everywhere.
- **Trade liberalisation** — the removal of tariffs and quotas through the World Trade Organization and trade blocs (the EU, ASEAN, USMCA).
- **Growth of multinational companies** locating production where costs are lowest.
- **Deregulation of financial markets** — capital moves freely.
- **Rising incomes in emerging economies** creating new markets.

Recent years have also seen globalisation **slowing or reversing** in places — trade wars, tariffs, Brexit, pandemic supply-chain disruption, "reshoring" of production.

**Effects of globalisation:** for consumers — lower prices, more choice; for firms — bigger markets, economies of scale, cheaper inputs, but more competition; for workers — jobs in export industries, but job losses where industries move abroad and pressure on low-skilled wages; for developing countries — investment, jobs and technology, but dependence and exploitation risks; for governments — more tax from growth but less control of the economy; for the environment — more transport emissions, though also spreading clean technology. Inequality between and within countries can rise; economic shocks spread faster.

**Multinational companies (MNCs)** produce in more than one country (Toyota, Shell, Nestlé, Apple). They locate abroad to: cut costs (cheap labour, land, raw materials); get inside tariff walls; be near growing markets; access skills or resources; benefit from grants and low taxes; spread risk.

| | Advantages to the **host** country | Disadvantages to the **host** country |
|---|---|---|
| Jobs | employment, often at higher wages than local firms; training | jobs may be low-skilled; senior posts go to foreigners; local firms driven out |
| Investment | capital, technology and management skills the country lacks; higher output and exports | profits sent home (repatriated); may leave when grants end or costs rise |
| Government | tax revenue; infrastructure | tax avoidance via transfer pricing; pressure to cut taxes and regulations; political influence |
| Society | new products, consumer choice | exploitation of workers; pollution; depletion of resources; loss of culture |

For the **home** country: profits and dividends flow back, exports of components, access to markets; but jobs move abroad and the balance of payments loses the exports the firm once made at home.

**Trade restrictions (protection)** — the tools:

- **Tariff**: a tax on imports. Raises the import price, so domestic supply extends and demand contracts; imports fall; the government gains revenue.
- **Quota**: a physical limit on the quantity imported. Cuts supply, raises price; no revenue (the gain goes to whoever holds the import licence).
- **Subsidy** to domestic producers: lowers their costs so they can undercut imports; costs the taxpayer.
- **Embargo**: a complete ban on trade with a country or in a product (arms, for political reasons).
- Also: exchange controls, red tape and standards that make importing hard, voluntary export restraints.

**Reasons for protection**: protect **infant industries** until they can compete; protect **declining (sunset) industries** so unemployment falls gradually; protect **strategic industries** (food, energy, defence); prevent **dumping**; protect jobs and correct a **current account deficit**; raise **tariff revenue**; **retaliate** against other countries' barriers; block goods made with child labour or to low standards.

**Consequences of protection**: consumers pay higher prices with less choice; protected firms become inefficient; other countries retaliate, so exports fall — a trade war; resources are misallocated away from what the country does best; world trade and output fall. Protection helps the protected industry and its workers in the short run at the expense of everyone else.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Globalisation**: the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, migration and technology.
- **Multinational company**: a firm that produces in more than one country.
- **Tariff**: a tax on imported goods.
- **Quota**: a limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported.
- **Embargo**: a complete ban on trade in a good or with a country.
- **Dumping**: selling exports at a price below their cost of production.

::: method
1. **Define globalisation through integration** — trade, capital, labour, technology across borders — then give a cause and an effect.
2. **MNC questions**: state which country (host or home) and which stakeholder (workers, government, local firms, consumers); then two advantages, two disadvantages, and a judgement that "depends on the MNC's behaviour and the government's rules".
3. **Tariff mechanics**: import price rises → domestic supply extends, demand contracts → imports fall → government revenue. Draw the world-price diagram if asked.
4. **Quota vs tariff**: both raise price and cut imports; a tariff gives revenue, a quota gives certainty about quantity.
5. **Reasons for protection**: name three and explain each; then the consequences — higher prices, inefficiency, retaliation.
6. **Discuss protection**: strongest case for infant industries, dumping and strategic goods; strongest case against is consumers and retaliation; judge by whether it is temporary and targeted.
:::

::: example Define
Define "multinational company". (2)

A firm that has its headquarters in one country (1) but owns and operates production facilities in one or more other countries (1).
:::

::: example Explain — causes of globalisation
Explain two causes of the growth of globalisation. (4)

Improvements in transport, especially containerisation, have cut the cost of shipping goods, so it is profitable to produce far from the market and trade over long distances (2). The internet and cheap communication allow firms to coordinate production and sell across borders instantly, and let consumers buy from anywhere (2).
:::

::: example Explain — a tariff
Explain, with the help of a diagram, the effect of a tariff on imported steel. (4)

The tariff raises the price of imported steel from the world price to the world price plus the tariff (1). At the higher price domestic producers extend their supply and consumers contract their demand, so the quantity imported falls to the gap between them (2). The government collects tariff revenue on the remaining imports, but domestic buyers of steel pay more (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — MNCs and a host country
Analyse the effects on a developing country of a multinational setting up a factory there. (6)

The MNC brings investment, technology and management skills the country lacks, creating jobs — often at higher wages than local firms — and training workers; output, exports and tax revenue rise, helping to break the poverty cycle (2). Against this, profits may be sent home rather than reinvested, the best jobs may go to foreign staff, and local firms may be driven out by the competition; the MNC may pollute or exploit workers if regulations are weak (2). The net effect depends on the government's bargaining power and regulation: if it insists on local hiring, training, environmental standards and fair taxes, the gains are large, but if the MNC uses transfer pricing and leaves when grants end, the country gains little (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — protection
Discuss whether a government should protect its domestic industries from foreign competition. (8)

For: protecting infant industries gives them time to reach the scale needed to compete; shielding declining industries slows job losses; strategic industries such as food and defence should not depend on foreign supply; protection prevents dumping and can improve a current account deficit; tariffs raise revenue (4). Against: consumers pay higher prices and have less choice; protected firms have no incentive to become efficient; other countries retaliate, so the country's exporters lose sales; resources are kept in industries where the country is inefficient, lowering total output; and infant industries often never grow up (3). Judgement: protection is justified when temporary and targeted — infant industries, dumping, security — but permanent, general protection harms the economy; for most industries the better response is supply-side help to become competitive (1).
:::

::: q
Define "globalisation". (2)
---
The increasing integration of the world's economies (1) through trade, movement of capital and labour, and shared technology (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a tariff and a quota. (4)
---
A tariff is a tax on imports, raising their price and giving the government revenue (2). A quota is a limit on the quantity that may be imported; it raises price by restricting supply but gives the government no revenue (2).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why a multinational might locate production in a developing country. (2)
---
Cheaper labour and land; access to raw materials (or a growing market, tax incentives, avoiding tariffs).
:::

::: q
Explain two disadvantages of multinationals for a host country. (4)
---
Profits are sent back to the home country, so much of the benefit leaves (2). MNCs may exploit workers or the environment where laws are weak, and may leave suddenly when costs rise, causing unemployment (2).
:::

::: q
Explain what dumping is and why countries restrict it. (4)
---
Selling exports below their cost of production, often to destroy foreign competitors (2). Domestic firms cannot compete and close; once they are gone the dumping firm can raise prices, so governments impose tariffs to block it (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the infant industry argument for protection. (4)
---
A new industry has high average costs because it has not yet achieved economies of scale (2). A temporary tariff protects it from established foreign rivals until it grows large enough to compete without protection (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two consequences of protection for consumers. (4)
---
Higher prices, since imports are taxed or restricted and domestic firms face less competition (2). Less choice and possibly lower quality, as protected firms have less incentive to innovate (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how an embargo differs from a tariff. (2)
---
An embargo bans trade in a good or with a country completely, usually for political reasons (1); a tariff allows trade but taxes it (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether globalisation benefits developing countries. (8)
---
For: access to world markets lets them export and grow; multinationals bring investment, jobs and technology; cheaper imports raise living standards; migration brings remittances; and countries such as China and Vietnam have cut poverty dramatically through trade (3). Against: developing countries may be stuck exporting low-value primary products while rich countries protect their own farmers; MNCs may exploit workers and repatriate profits; local firms and cultures are swamped; economic shocks spread quickly; inequality within countries rises and governments lose control over their economies (3). Judgement: globalisation offers developing countries their fastest route to growth, but the benefits depend on good governance, investment in skills and fair trade rules; countries that manage it well gain, those that do not may lose (2).
:::
