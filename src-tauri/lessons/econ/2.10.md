## What it is

A **mixed economic system** has both a **private sector** (firms and individuals allocating resources through markets) and a **public sector** (the government owning resources and providing goods and services). Every real economy is mixed; they differ only in the balance — the government's share of spending is larger in Sweden than in Singapore.

**Why mix?** Markets are efficient at producing most goods, but they fail (2.9). So the government steps in to:

- provide **public goods** (defence, street lighting) that the market will not;
- provide or subsidise **merit goods** (education, healthcare);
- tax or ban **demerit goods**;
- correct **externalities**;
- control **monopolies**;
- reduce **inequality** through taxes and benefits;
- stabilise the economy.

**Advantages of a mixed economy**: gets the efficiency, choice and incentives of markets *and* corrects their failures; public goods provided; merit goods available to all; the poor protected. **Disadvantages**: taxes needed to fund the public sector reduce incentives; government provision can be inefficient and bureaucratic, since there is no profit motive or competition; governments may make poor decisions or be influenced by political pressure; the balance is hard to get right.

**The government's toolkit** — the methods of intervention:

| Method | What it is | Used for | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Maximum price** (price ceiling) | a legal upper limit set *below* equilibrium | keeping essentials affordable — rent controls, bread | creates a **shortage**; queues, black markets |
| **Minimum price** (price floor) | a legal lower limit set *above* equilibrium | protecting producers' incomes — farm prices; minimum wage | creates a **surplus**; government may have to buy it up |
| **Indirect tax** | tax on a good, raising its price (shifts S left) | reducing demerit goods and external costs; raising revenue | regressive; little effect if demand is inelastic |
| **Subsidy** | payment to producers, lowering price (shifts S right) | encouraging merit goods and external benefits | costs taxpayers; may keep inefficient firms alive |
| **Regulation** | laws and rules — bans, age limits, emission standards, quality rules | demerit goods, pollution, consumer protection | enforcement costs; may be ignored |
| **Nationalisation / state ownership** | the government owns and runs an industry | natural monopolies (water, rail), strategic industries | inefficiency without competition |
| **Direct provision** | the government provides the good free at the point of use | public goods, merit goods (state schools, NHS) | funded by taxes; possible over-use |
| **Quotas** | limits on the quantity that can be produced or imported | protecting resources (fishing), protecting industries | shortages and higher prices |
| **Competition policy** | breaking up or regulating monopolies | protecting consumers | hard to judge when a firm is too powerful |

A **maximum price below equilibrium**: at the ceiling, quantity demanded (Qd) exceeds quantity supplied (Qs) — a shortage. A **minimum price above equilibrium** is the mirror image: Qs exceeds Qd, a surplus.

::: terms
- **Mixed economic system**: an economy in which resources are allocated partly by the price mechanism and partly by the government.
- **Public sector**: the part of the economy owned and controlled by the government.
- **Private sector**: the part of the economy owned and run by individuals and firms.
- **Maximum price**: a legally imposed price above which a good cannot be sold, set below equilibrium to make it affordable.
- **Minimum price**: a legally imposed price below which a good cannot be sold, set above equilibrium to raise producers' incomes or cut consumption.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending, used to reduce consumption of demerit goods and goods with external costs.
- **Subsidy**: a payment by the government to producers to lower the cost of production and encourage supply.
- **Regulation**: rules set by the government to control the behaviour of firms or consumers — bans, limits, standards.
- **Nationalisation**: the transfer of an industry from private to government ownership.
- **Privatisation**: the transfer of an industry from government to private ownership.
- **Direct provision**: the government producing a good or service itself, such as state schools or hospitals.
- **Quota**: a limit on the quantity of a good that can be produced, sold or imported.
- **Government failure**: when government intervention makes the allocation of resources worse rather than better.
:::

::: method
1. **Match the failure to the tool.** Public good → direct provision. Merit good → subsidy or free provision. Demerit good → tax, regulation or ban. External cost → tax or regulation. Monopoly → competition policy or nationalisation. Inequality → taxes and benefits, minimum wage.
2. **Price controls: draw them.** A maximum price is a horizontal line *below* Pe → read off Qd and Qs → shortage. A minimum price is *above* Pe → surplus. If drawn on the wrong side of Pe it has no effect — say so.
3. **Tax and subsidy: shift supply.** A tax shifts S left (price up, quantity down); a subsidy shifts S right (price down, quantity up). Draw the shift and the new equilibrium.
4. **Every tool has a drawback**: mention it for evaluation. Shortage/black market for max prices; surplus for min prices; regressive tax; taxpayer cost of subsidies; inefficiency of state ownership.
5. **"Discuss" structure**: what the intervention does → its benefits → its costs/drawbacks → whether it works depends on (elasticity, size of the tax, enforcement) → judgement.
:::

::: example Define
Define "mixed economic system". (2)

An economy in which some resources are allocated by the price mechanism through the private sector (1) and some by the government through the public sector (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a maximum price
Explain, using a diagram, the effect of a government setting a maximum price for bread below the equilibrium price. (4)

At the maximum price, which is below Pe, quantity demanded rises (demand extends) while quantity supplied falls (supply contracts) (2). The result is a shortage equal to Qd − Qs; some consumers cannot buy bread at the legal price, so queues form and a black market may appear where bread is sold illegally above the maximum (2).
:::

::: example Explain — a subsidy for a merit good
Explain how a subsidy could correct the under-consumption of vaccinations. (4)

A subsidy paid to clinics lowers their cost of providing each vaccination, so supply increases and the supply curve shifts right (2). The price to consumers falls, so demand extends and more people are vaccinated, moving consumption closer to the socially optimal level where the external benefits are enjoyed (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — a tax on sugary drinks
Discuss whether a tax is the best way to reduce consumption of sugary drinks. (8)

A tax raises producers' costs, shifting supply left and raising the price, so demand contracts and consumption falls; it also raises revenue that can fund health education, and it leaves consumers free to choose (3). However, if demand is inelastic — sugary drinks are cheap and habit-forming — the fall in consumption is small; the tax is regressive, hitting poorer families hardest; and firms may absorb the tax rather than raise prices (3). Alternatives such as regulation (limits on sugar content, advertising bans) or education campaigns change behaviour more directly but cost money and take time; the most effective approach probably combines a tax with regulation and information, so a tax alone is not the best way but is a useful part (2).
:::

::: example Explain — a minimum price
Explain, using a diagram, the effect of a government setting a minimum price for milk above the equilibrium price. (4)

Diagram: demand and supply curves with a horizontal minimum-price line above the equilibrium price; quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at that price (2).

Farmers receive a higher price, so quantity supplied extends, while consumers pay more, so quantity demanded contracts (1). The result is a surplus of milk, which the government may have to buy and store or destroy, at a cost to taxpayers (1).
:::

::: example Explain — regulation
Explain how the government could use regulation to reduce pollution from factories. (4)

It could set a legal limit on the amount of smoke or chemicals each factory may release, and fine firms that go over it (1). Firms then have to install filters or cleaner processes (1), so pollution — an external cost — falls without the government having to spend money (1); the limits must be enforced by inspections, and firms may pass the extra costs on to consumers as higher prices (1).
:::

::: example Explain — nationalisation
Explain one reason why a government might nationalise its railways. (4)

To provide services that are not profitable but benefit society (1). A private rail firm would cut unprofitable rural routes, leaving some communities with no public transport (1).

In public ownership the government can keep those routes running and set fares that more people can afford (1), taking account of external benefits such as fewer cars on the roads and less pollution, which a private firm would ignore (1).
:::

::: q
State two advantages of a mixed economy over a market economy. (2)
---
Public goods are provided; merit goods are available to those who cannot pay (or: externalities corrected, inequality reduced).
:::

::: q
Define "maximum price". (2)
---
A legal upper limit on the price of a good (1), set below the equilibrium price to make it affordable (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a minimum price set above equilibrium creates a surplus. (4)
---
At the higher price supply extends as producers are encouraged to sell more (1), while demand contracts as some consumers stop buying (1). Quantity supplied therefore exceeds quantity demanded, leaving unsold output (2) which the government may have to buy.
:::

::: q
Explain how an indirect tax reduces the consumption of a demerit good. (4)
---
The tax raises the cost of supplying each unit, so supply shifts left and the price rises (2). The higher price contracts demand, so less is consumed; the effect is larger the more elastic demand is (2).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of nationalising an industry. (4)
---
Advantage: the government can run it in the public interest — keeping prices low, serving unprofitable areas — rather than for profit (2). Disadvantage: without competition or the profit motive it may be inefficient, with high costs paid by taxpayers (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a government provides education free of charge. (4)
---
Education is a merit good with external benefits — a skilled workforce, lower crime — so the market under-provides it (2). Free provision means the poor can access it too, raising consumption to the socially desirable level and reducing inequality (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two disadvantages of a mixed economy. (4)
---
The public sector must be funded by taxes, which reduce incentives to work and invest (2). Government-provided services may be inefficient because they lack competition and the profit motive (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse the effects of a government imposing a maximum rent on housing. (6)
---
The maximum rent is set below equilibrium, so more tenants want to rent (demand extends) while landlords offer fewer properties (supply contracts) — a shortage of rented housing (2). Those who find a property pay less, which helps low-income tenants, but others cannot find anywhere and may be pushed into overcrowding or illegal lettings above the legal rent (2). In the long run landlords may sell up or stop maintaining properties, so the quality and quantity of rented housing falls; the policy helps existing tenants at the expense of future ones (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether governments should subsidise public transport. (8)
---
For: public transport has external benefits — less congestion and pollution, access to jobs for those without cars — so the market under-provides it; a subsidy lowers fares, extends demand and shifts people out of cars, and helps low-income groups (3). Against: subsidies cost taxpayers money with an opportunity cost in other spending; if demand for public transport is inelastic the extra passengers are few; subsidised operators may become inefficient; and the benefit goes partly to people who would have used buses anyway (3). Judgement: subsidies are worthwhile in congested cities where the external benefits are large and alternatives exist, less so in rural areas with few passengers; they work best alongside measures that raise the cost of driving (2).
:::

::: watch
- A **maximum price works only if below Pe**; a **minimum price only if above**. Draw the line on the right side, and read Qd and Qs from it.
- Max price → **shortage**; min price → **surplus**. Never the other way round.
- **Tax shifts supply left; subsidy shifts supply right.** Both change quantity via a price change — say which way.
- Every intervention gets an **evaluation point** (drawback) — that is where the top marks are.
- Mixed economy = **both** sectors; do not describe it as "mostly government".
:::

::: test
Discuss whether a mixed economic system is better than a market economic system. (8)
---
Mixed is better: government provides public and merit goods, corrects external costs, reduces inequality through taxes and benefits, and can stabilise the economy (4).
Market may be better: more competition, efficiency and innovation; lower taxes; fewer risks of government failure, such as poor information or inefficient state firms; consumers decide what is produced (4).
Conclusion: a mixed economy usually gives a better balance of efficiency and fairness, but depends on how well the government intervenes.
Marking: up to 4 marks for developed analysis of both sides; up to 4 for evaluation — weighing the arguments and reaching a justified conclusion.
