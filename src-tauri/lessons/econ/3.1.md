## What it is

Before money, people **bartered** — swapped goods directly. Barter needs a **double coincidence of wants** (you must want what I have *and* I must want what you have), which makes trade slow and rare. Money solves this.

**Money** is anything generally accepted as payment for goods and services. Its **forms** today: notes and coins (cash), and **bank deposits** — money in current accounts moved by cards, transfers and apps, which is by far the largest part.

**The four functions of money:**

1. **Medium of exchange** — accepted in payment, so no double coincidence of wants is needed.
2. **Store of value** — it keeps its worth over time, so you can save it and spend later (inflation weakens this).
3. **Unit of account** (measure of value) — prices are stated in it, so different goods can be compared.
4. **Standard of deferred payment** — debts and future payments can be agreed in it.

**The characteristics of good money** — what something needs to *be* money:

- **Acceptable** — everyone takes it.
- **Durable** — does not wear out or rot.
- **Portable** — easy to carry.
- **Divisible** — splits into small units for small purchases.
- **Scarce / limited in supply** — otherwise it loses value.
- **Uniform** — every unit is the same.
- **Hard to forge**.

**Commercial banks** (high-street banks such as HSBC or Barclays) are private firms that aim for profit. Their functions:

- **Accept deposits** from customers, paying interest on savings accounts.
- **Make loans** and overdrafts to households and firms, charging interest — the gap between the interest charged and paid is their main profit.
- **Provide payment services** — cards, transfers, cheques, apps.
- Other services: foreign currency, safe deposit, mortgages, advice.

**The central bank** (the Bank of England, the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank) is the government's bank and the bankers' bank. It does *not* serve the public directly. Its functions:

- **Issues notes and coins** — the only body allowed to.
- **Operates monetary policy** — sets the base interest rate and controls the money supply to keep inflation on target.
- **Banker to the government** — holds its accounts, manages its borrowing (issuing government bonds) and the national debt.
- **Banker to the commercial banks** — holds their deposits and is the **lender of last resort**, lending to banks in trouble so the system does not collapse.
- **Regulates the banking system** — rules on how much banks must hold in reserve.
- **Manages the foreign exchange reserves** and may intervene in the currency market.

::: terms
- **Money**: anything generally accepted as a means of payment for goods and services.
- **Barter**: the direct exchange of goods and services for other goods and services without money.
- **Double coincidence of wants**: the need, under barter, for each person to want what the other is offering.
- **Medium of exchange**: the function of money as something accepted in payment for goods and services.
- **Unit of account (measure of value)**: the function of money as a way of measuring and comparing the value of goods.
- **Store of value**: the function of money as a way of keeping wealth to spend in the future.
- **Standard of deferred payment**: the function of money as a way of settling debts in the future.
- **Characteristics of money**: acceptable, durable, portable, divisible, scarce (limited in supply) and difficult to forge.
- **Commercial bank**: a financial institution that accepts deposits from and makes loans to the public and firms, aiming for profit.
- **Central bank**: the institution that issues a country's currency, operates monetary policy and acts as banker to the government and to commercial banks.
- **Lender of last resort**: the central bank's role of lending to commercial banks that are short of money, to prevent the banking system collapsing.
:::

::: method
1. **Functions are what money does; characteristics are what money is.** Do not mix the lists. "Medium of exchange" is a function; "durable" is a characteristic.
2. **Explain a function with the problem it solves.** Medium of exchange → no double coincidence of wants. Unit of account → prices comparable. Store of value → saving possible. Deferred payment → borrowing and lending possible.
3. **Link characteristics to functions** for analyse marks: money must be *scarce* to be a *store of value*; *divisible* to be a *medium of exchange* for small items.
4. **Commercial vs central bank**: commercial banks serve the public for profit; the central bank serves the government and the banks, and controls the currency. If a question mentions interest rates for the whole economy or notes and coins, it is the central bank.
5. **Bank profit**: they borrow (deposits) cheap and lend dear. State this when asked how banks make money.
:::

::: example Define
Define "money". (2)

Anything that is generally accepted (1) as a means of payment for goods and services (1).
:::

::: example Explain — functions
Explain two functions of money. (4)

Medium of exchange: money is accepted in payment for any good, so a seller does not need to want what the buyer has — this removes the double coincidence of wants required by barter (2). Store of value: money keeps its value over time, so people can save now and buy later, which barter goods such as fish cannot do (2).
:::

::: example Explain — characteristics
Explain why a good form of money needs to be scarce and divisible. (4)

If money were not scarce — if anyone could produce more — its value would fall and it could not act as a store of value or be accepted in exchange (2). Divisibility lets money be split into small units, so it can be used for cheap purchases as well as expensive ones and exact change can be given (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — central bank as lender of last resort
Analyse why a central bank acts as lender of last resort. (6)

Commercial banks lend out most of the money deposited with them and keep only a fraction in reserve, so if many depositors withdraw at once a bank can run out of cash even though it is not insolvent (2). If a bank failed to pay depositors, panic could spread and customers of other banks would rush to withdraw, threatening the whole banking system and the economy (2). The central bank therefore lends to banks that cannot borrow elsewhere, restoring confidence; this stabilises the system, though it may encourage banks to take more risks knowing they will be rescued (2).
:::

::: example Explain — commercial banks
Explain two functions of a commercial bank. (4)

Accepting deposits (1): households and firms keep their money safely in accounts and can make payments from them by card or transfer, and savers earn interest (1).

Lending (1): the bank lends to households and firms as loans, overdrafts and mortgages, charging interest, which lets people buy houses and firms invest in capital before they have saved the full amount (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why barter is inefficient. (2)
---
It needs a double coincidence of wants — each party must want what the other has (1) — so finding a trading partner is slow and much trade never happens (1).
:::

::: q
State the four functions of money. (4)
---
Medium of exchange; store of value; unit of account (measure of value); standard of deferred payment.
:::

::: q
State four characteristics of a good form of money. (4)
---
Acceptable, durable, portable, divisible (also scarce, uniform, hard to forge).
:::

::: q
Explain why high inflation weakens money's function as a store of value. (2)
---
Inflation reduces what a unit of money can buy (1), so money saved loses purchasing power over time (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two functions of a commercial bank. (4)
---
Accepting deposits: customers place money in accounts for safety and interest (2). Making loans: the bank lends to households and firms, charging interest, which is its main source of profit (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a commercial bank makes a profit. (2)
---
It pays a lower rate of interest on deposits than it charges on loans (1); the difference, plus fees for services, is its profit (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two functions of a central bank. (4)
---
It issues the country's notes and coins, being the only institution permitted to do so (2). It operates monetary policy, setting the base interest rate to control inflation and influence spending (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a central bank and a commercial bank. (4)
---
A commercial bank is a profit-making firm that takes deposits from and lends to the public (2). The central bank is owned by the state, does not serve the public directly, and instead controls the currency and monetary policy and acts as banker to the government and the commercial banks (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a rise in the central bank's interest rate might affect commercial banks and their customers. (6)
---
Commercial banks borrow from the central bank at the base rate, so when it rises they raise the rates they charge on loans and mortgages (2). Borrowing becomes dearer, so households take fewer loans and firms invest less; existing borrowers on variable rates have less money to spend after repayments (2). Banks also raise the interest paid on deposits, encouraging saving; overall the demand for loans falls, which may reduce banks' lending and profits even though the margin per loan may rise (2).
:::

- Explain functions through the **problem of barter** — the double coincidence of wants.
- Central bank: **issues currency, sets interest rates, banker to government and banks, lender of last resort.** It does not take deposits from the public.
- Commercial bank: **deposits, loans, payments** — for **profit**.
- "Money" is not just cash: **bank deposits** are the main form.

::: test
Discuss whether a central bank should act as lender of last resort to commercial banks that are in difficulty. (8)
---
For: prevents a bank collapse spreading panic and a run on other banks; protects savers' deposits; keeps lending to firms and households going, protecting jobs and output (4).
Against: encourages banks to take excessive risks because they expect to be rescued (moral hazard); may cost taxpayers; may keep inefficient banks in business (4).
Conclusion: usually justified to protect the financial system, but with strict conditions and regulation to limit risk-taking.
Marking: up to 4 marks for developed analysis of both sides; up to 4 for evaluation — weighing the arguments and reaching a justified conclusion.
:::
