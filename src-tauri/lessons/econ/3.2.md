## What it is

A **household** is a person or group living together who share their income. Every household does three things with its **disposable income** (income after tax and benefits): it **spends**, it **saves**, or it **borrows** to spend more than it earns.

**Spending (consumption)** — what drives it:

- **Income** — the main factor. As income rises, spending rises, but by a smaller proportion: the rich save a larger share. Poor households spend almost everything on necessities.
- **Wealth** — owning a house or shares that have risen in value makes people feel richer and spend more.
- **Interest rates** — high rates make borrowing dear and saving attractive, so spending falls; low rates do the opposite.
- **Confidence** — people who expect a secure job and rising income spend more; fear of unemployment cuts spending.
- **Inflation expectations** — if prices are expected to rise, people buy now.
- **Age** — the young and the old spend most of their income; the middle-aged save most.
- **Availability of credit** — easy credit raises spending.
- **Advertising, fashion and social pressure.**

**Pattern of spending**: poor households spend a high share on food, housing and fuel; as income rises, the share spent on these necessities falls and the share on leisure, travel, cars and services rises. The *amount* spent on food rises, but the *percentage* falls.

**Saving** — income not spent. Reasons: for a house deposit or a car (target saving), for retirement, for emergencies (precautionary), to earn interest. What raises saving:

- **Higher income** — the ability to save rises faster than income.
- **Higher interest rates** — the reward for saving rises.
- **Low confidence** — people save for fear of losing their job.
- **Age** — the middle-aged save for retirement.
- **Tax incentives** — tax-free savings accounts.
- **Culture and habits** — some countries save far more than others.
- **Government provision** — a generous state pension reduces the need to save.

**Borrowing** — spending future income now: mortgages, car loans, credit cards, overdrafts. What raises borrowing:

- **Low interest rates** — repayments are cheaper.
- **Confidence** in future income.
- **Wealth** — banks lend against it.
- **Easy access to credit** — banks willing to lend, credit cards widely available.
- **Age** — the young borrow for houses and cars.
- **Big purchases** and emergencies.

The young typically **borrow**, the middle-aged **save**, the retired **spend** their savings — the life cycle.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Disposable income**: income remaining after direct taxes have been paid and benefits received.
- **Saving**: the part of disposable income that is not spent.
- **Borrowing**: obtaining money from a lender, to be repaid with interest.
- **Consumer expenditure**: spending by households on goods and services.

::: method
1. **Income first, then interest rates, then confidence.** These three explain most questions on spending, saving and borrowing. Develop each with a "because → so" chain.
2. **Interest rates cut both ways**: a rise raises saving *and* cuts borrowing *and* cuts spending. Say all three if the question is about interest rates.
3. **Distinguish amount from proportion.** As income rises, the amount spent rises but the *proportion* spent falls; the proportion saved rises.
4. **Use the life cycle** for age questions: young borrow, middle-aged save, old dis-save.
5. **For "how would X affect a household"** questions, pick the decision-maker's situation: a low-income household has little room to save; a wealthy one responds to interest rates and confidence.
:::

::: example Define
Define "disposable income". (2)

The income a household has left to spend or save (1) after direct taxes have been deducted and any state benefits added (1).
:::

::: example Explain — income and spending
Explain how a rise in income affects the pattern of a household's spending. (4)

Total spending rises because the household can afford more, but by less than the rise in income, since some of the extra is saved (2). The share of spending on necessities such as food and fuel falls while the share on luxuries — holidays, eating out, cars — rises, as the household's basic needs are already met (2).
:::

::: example Explain — interest rates
Explain how a rise in interest rates might affect household saving and borrowing. (4)

Saving rises because the reward for saving — the interest earned — is higher, so households give up spending in return for a larger future sum (2). Borrowing falls because loan and mortgage repayments become more expensive, so households are less willing to take on debt and some postpone big purchases (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — confidence
Analyse how a fall in consumer confidence affects household spending, saving and borrowing. (6)

If households fear unemployment or falling incomes they cut spending, especially on luxuries and items that can be postponed such as cars and furniture (2). They increase precautionary saving to build a cushion against losing their job, so the proportion of income saved rises (2). They also avoid new borrowing, since debt repayments would be hard to meet on a lower income, and may repay existing debt; the combined effect reduces demand in the economy, which can itself cause the job losses they feared (2).
:::

::: q
State three factors that influence how much a household spends. (3)
---
Income; interest rates; confidence (also wealth, age, availability of credit, expected inflation).
:::

::: q
Explain why poorer households spend a larger proportion of their income than richer ones. (4)
---
Poor households must spend almost all their income on necessities — food, housing, fuel — leaving little to save (2). Richer households have their needs met with a smaller share of income, so a larger proportion can be saved (2).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why a household might save. (2)
---
For a house deposit or large purchase; for retirement; as a precaution against emergencies; to earn interest.
:::

::: q
Explain why young adults tend to borrow more than middle-aged people. (4)
---
Young adults have low current incomes but large needs — a house, a car, furniture — so they borrow against expected future income (2). The middle-aged usually earn more and have bought these items, so they repay debt and save for retirement instead (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a rise in house prices could affect household spending. (4)
---
Homeowners feel wealthier, so they spend more of their current income and may borrow against the higher value of their house (2). Those saving for a deposit must save more and spend less, so the effect is not the same for every household (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a generous state pension might affect the amount households save. (2)
---
Households need to save less for retirement (1) because the state will provide income in old age, so saving falls (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why households might increase spending when they expect prices to rise. (2)
---
Goods will cost more later (1), so buying now before the increase is cheaper — spending is brought forward (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a cut in interest rates might affect household spending, saving and borrowing. (6)
---
Lower interest rates reduce the reward for saving, so households save less and spend more of their income (2). Loans and mortgages become cheaper, encouraging borrowing for cars, houses and other large purchases; those with existing variable-rate mortgages have lower repayments and more to spend (2). The overall effect is higher consumer spending, though it is weaker if confidence is low and people fear debt, or if banks are unwilling to lend (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Income** is always the first factor — then interest rates, confidence, wealth, age.
- Interest rate changes affect **all three**: spending, saving and borrowing. Give the direction of each.
- **Proportion vs amount**: richer households spend more in total but a **smaller proportion**.
- The **life cycle**: young borrow, middle-aged save, old spend savings — a quick 2-mark explanation.
- Saving is *not spending*; it is not the same as investment (which firms do on capital goods).
:::
