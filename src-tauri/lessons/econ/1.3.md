## What it is

Because resources are scarce, every choice means giving something up. **Opportunity cost** is the value of the **next best alternative** given up when a choice is made. Not *everything* you give up — only the best thing you could have had instead.

If you have 20 dollars and choose a cinema ticket over a pizza or a book, and pizza was your second choice, the opportunity cost of the film is the pizza. The book does not count; it was never going to be chosen.

Opportunity cost is the idea that ties economics together. It is why:

- **Free goods** have no opportunity cost (nothing is given up to use air), while **economic goods** always do.
- The **PPC** (1.4) slopes downwards — producing more of one good means producing less of the other.
- Every decision-maker faces it:

| Decision-maker | The choice | Opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumer** | spend limited income on trainers or a phone | the phone given up |
| **Worker** | work overtime or have the evening off | the leisure lost (or the wages lost) |
| **Firm** | spend profit on a new machine or on a marketing campaign | the sales the campaign would have brought |
| **Government** | spend tax revenue on a new hospital or a new motorway | the motorway not built |

Opportunity cost is measured in **what is given up**, not in money — money is only the means. A government spending 5 billion dollars on defence has an opportunity cost of the schools or hospitals that 5 billion could have built.

::: terms
- **Opportunity cost**: the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
- **Choice**: selecting between alternatives, which scarcity makes necessary.
- **Economic good**: a good that has an opportunity cost because it uses scarce resources.
- **Free good**: a good with no opportunity cost because it is not scarce.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the choice made and the alternatives.** Then pick the *next best* one — the single alternative that would have been chosen otherwise. That is the opportunity cost.
2. **Express it as the alternative, not as money.** "The opportunity cost of the car is the holiday", not "20,000 dollars". If the question gives money, convert it into what the money would otherwise have bought.
3. **Match the decision-maker.** A consumer gives up other goods; a worker gives up leisure or another job's wages; a firm gives up another investment or other output; a government gives up other public spending or lower taxes.
4. **Link to scarcity in "explain" answers.** "Because resources are scarce, choosing X means Y cannot also be produced, so the opportunity cost of X is Y."
5. **Use it to judge decisions.** In "discuss" questions, a good decision is one where the benefit exceeds the opportunity cost. Show both sides: what is gained *and* what is given up.
:::

::: example Define
Define "opportunity cost". (2)

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative (1) that is given up when a choice is made (1).
:::

::: example Identify — a consumer
A student has 30 dollars. She ranks her options: (1) a concert ticket, (2) a jumper, (3) a book. She buys the ticket. State the opportunity cost. (2)

The jumper — it was the next best alternative (1). The book is not the opportunity cost because it would not have been chosen even without the ticket (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a government
Explain the opportunity cost to a government of building a new airport. (4)

The government has limited tax revenue, so money spent on the airport cannot be spent elsewhere (1). The opportunity cost is the next best use of that money — for example new schools or hospitals that could have been built instead (2). Alternatively, if the money was raised by borrowing, the opportunity cost is the future spending lost when the debt is repaid with interest (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a firm
Analyse the opportunity cost to a firm of buying a new machine. (6)

Money spent on the machine cannot be used for other purposes, so the opportunity cost is the next best use of the funds — perhaps an advertising campaign or paying higher dividends to shareholders (2). If the firm borrowed to buy the machine, it also gives up the interest it could have earned by saving the money, and takes on repayments that reduce future profit (2). The decision is worthwhile if the extra output and revenue from the machine are worth more than the alternative given up; if demand for the product falls, the machine may earn less than the campaign would have, making the opportunity cost greater than the benefit (2).
:::

::: example Explain — a worker's opportunity cost
A 16-year-old can either leave school for a full-time job paying 250 dollars a week or stay at school for two more years. Explain the opportunity cost of staying at school. (4)

The opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up — the full-time job (1): about 250 dollars a week of income, over 25,000 dollars across two years, and two years of work experience (1).

She accepts this cost because staying at school should lead to qualifications (1) and a higher-paid job later, so the income she gives up now may be more than repaid over her working life (1).
:::

::: q
A worker is offered 50 dollars for four hours' overtime on Saturday, when he would otherwise play football. Identify the opportunity cost of accepting. (2)
---
The four hours of football (leisure) given up (1); the alternative — refusing — would have an opportunity cost of the 50 dollars in wages (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why free goods have no opportunity cost. (2)
---
A free good is not scarce (1), so using it does not take resources away from producing anything else — nothing is given up (1).
:::

::: q
A farmer can grow wheat or barley on a field. Explain the opportunity cost of growing wheat. (2)
---
The barley that could have been grown on the same field instead (1); the land is scarce, so it cannot produce both at once (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a consumer's decision to save part of their income involves an opportunity cost. (4)
---
Income is limited, so money saved cannot be spent now (1). The opportunity cost of saving is the goods and services the consumer gives up buying today, such as a meal out or new clothes (2). Conversely, spending has the opportunity cost of the interest and future spending that saving would have provided (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the opportunity cost of a choice is usually not the same as its money cost. (4)
---
Money cost is the price paid; opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up (1). The two differ because the alternative may be non-monetary — the leisure lost by working — or the money could have bought something of different value to the person (2). Two people paying the same price can face different opportunity costs because their next best alternatives differ (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the opportunity cost to a student of staying in education until 18 rather than taking a job at 16. (4)
---
The wages they could have earned in the two years of work (2). They also give up the work experience gained, though the benefit of education — higher future earnings — may outweigh this (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse the opportunity cost to an economy of a government increasing spending on defence. (6)
---
Resources — land, labour, capital — used to produce weapons and pay soldiers cannot produce other goods, so the opportunity cost is the civilian output given up, such as hospitals, housing and schools (2). If the spending is financed by higher taxes, households and firms have less to spend, so consumption and investment elsewhere fall (2). If it is financed by borrowing, future taxpayers give up spending when the debt is repaid with interest; the opportunity cost is spread into the future but still exists (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a firm should always choose the option with the lowest opportunity cost. (8)
---
In favour: the option with the lowest opportunity cost gives up the least, so if the benefits of the options are similar, it leaves the firm best off; choosing a cheaper machine that gives up only a small advertising budget keeps other plans intact (3). Against: opportunity cost is only one side — the *benefit* of each option matters too; an option with a higher opportunity cost may bring far greater revenue, so a firm should compare the benefit of each option with what it gives up, not simply minimise the sacrifice (3). Also, opportunity costs are uncertain and depend on future demand, so a firm must estimate them; overall, the firm should choose where benefit exceeds opportunity cost by the most, which is not always the lowest opportunity cost (2).
:::

::: watch
- Opportunity cost is the **next best** alternative — one thing, not a list of everything not chosen.
- Answer in terms of the **thing given up**, not a sum of money.
- Match the example to the **decision-maker** the question names.
- Free goods: no opportunity cost. Economic goods: always an opportunity cost, even when the price is zero.
- In evaluation, weigh **benefit against opportunity cost** — never just one side.
:::

::: test
Analyse how a rise in interest rates changes the opportunity cost of spending. (4)
---
Higher interest means saving now earns more (1); the return given up by spending — the opportunity cost — rises (1); consumers are likely to save more and spend less (1); firms also face a higher opportunity cost of investing, so investment falls (1).
:::

::: test

Money cost ignores alternatives that are not paid for in money, e.g. time (1); opportunity cost captures the real sacrifice made — what else those resources could have produced (1).
:::

::: test
Analyse the opportunity cost to a government of cutting taxes. (6)
---
Lower taxes mean less tax revenue for the government (2). With less revenue it must spend less on services such as health, education or infrastructure, or borrow more (2). The opportunity cost is the public services that are not provided, or the future interest and debt; households gain more disposable income, so the government must judge whether the benefit of the tax cut is greater than the services given up (2).

::: test
Discuss whether a young person should go to university or start work at 18, using the idea of opportunity cost. (8)
---
University: the opportunity cost is three or more years of wages and work experience, plus tuition fees and debt; but graduates usually earn more over their lifetime and have more career choice (4).
Work at 18: the opportunity cost is the higher lifetime earnings and qualifications of a degree; but the worker earns immediately, avoids debt and gains experience, which may be worth more in some careers (4).
Conclusion: depends on the career chosen, expected earnings, costs and the individual's preferences — the choice should weigh what is given up in each case.
Marking: up to 4 marks for developed analysis of both sides; up to 4 for evaluation — weighing the arguments and reaching a justified conclusion.
