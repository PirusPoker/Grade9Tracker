## What it is

Put the demand curve and the supply curve on the same diagram and they cross once. That crossing is **market equilibrium**: the price at which the quantity consumers want to buy exactly equals the quantity producers want to sell. There is no shortage and no surplus, so the price has no reason to change.

- **Equilibrium price (Pe)**: where demand = supply. Also called the market-clearing price.
- **Equilibrium quantity (Qe)**: the amount bought and sold at that price.

**Disequilibrium** is any price other than Pe:

- Price **above** Pe → quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded: a **surplus** (excess supply). Unsold stock builds up, so sellers cut the price. As price falls, demand extends and supply contracts until the surplus disappears.
- Price **below** Pe → quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: a **shortage** (excess demand). Queues form, sellers raise the price. As price rises, demand contracts and supply extends until the shortage disappears.

The market therefore moves *itself* back to equilibrium: this is the **price mechanism** at work. Through it the three questions are answered — what to produce (whatever clears the market at a profitable price), how (the least-cost method, since that is what survives competition), and for whom (those willing and able to pay Pe).

From a **schedule** (table) the equilibrium is the row where the two quantities are equal:

| Price | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 500 | 100 | shortage of 400 |
| 4 | 400 | 200 | shortage of 200 |
| 6 | 300 | 300 | **equilibrium** |
| 8 | 200 | 400 | surplus of 200 |
| 10 | 100 | 500 | surplus of 400 |

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Equilibrium price**: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- **Shortage (excess demand)**: a situation where quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price.
- **Surplus (excess supply)**: a situation where quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the current price.

::: method
1. **From a table**: find the row where Qd = Qs. Equilibrium price is that row's price; equilibrium quantity is that quantity. Shortage or surplus at any other price = the difference between the two columns.
2. **From a diagram**: draw D downward, S upward, mark where they cross, drop dotted lines to both axes and label Pe and Qe.
3. **Surplus or shortage?** Above Pe → surplus (supply is the longer line). Below Pe → shortage (demand is the longer line). Draw a horizontal line at the given price and read the gap between the curves.
4. **Explain the return to equilibrium in steps**: surplus → sellers cut price → demand extends, supply contracts → gap closes. Use the words extension and contraction: this is a *movement along* both curves, not a shift.
5. **Link to the price mechanism** for analyse answers: price signals, incentivises and rations until the market clears.
:::

::: example Define
Define "equilibrium price". (2)

The price at which the quantity demanded (1) equals the quantity supplied, so there is no shortage or surplus (1).
:::

::: example From a schedule
Using the table in the notes, state the equilibrium price and quantity, and calculate the shortage or surplus at a price of 4. (3)

Equilibrium price 6, quantity 300, because that is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (2). At a price of 4, demand is 400 and supply 200, so there is a shortage of 200 units (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a surplus
Explain, with the help of a diagram, what happens if the price of a good is above its equilibrium. (4)

At a price above Pe the quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded, so there is a surplus (excess supply) shown by the horizontal gap between the curves above the equilibrium (2). Firms are left with unsold stock, so they lower the price; as price falls demand extends and supply contracts until the market reaches Pe and the surplus is removed (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — the three questions
Analyse how the price mechanism resolves a shortage and answers "for whom to produce". (6)

At a price below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied and there is a shortage; consumers compete for the limited goods and bid the price up (2). The rising price rations the good: consumers who are unwilling or unable to pay the higher price drop out, so demand contracts, while higher profits give firms an incentive to supply more, so supply extends (2). Price rises until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied; the good goes to those able and willing to pay the equilibrium price, which is how the market decides *for whom* (2).
:::

::: q
Define "shortage". (2)
---
A situation where quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied (1) at the current price, i.e. price is below equilibrium (1).
:::

::: q
At a price of 5, 800 units are demanded and 1,100 supplied. Is there a shortage or a surplus, and of how much? (2)
---
A surplus (excess supply) (1) of 300 units (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what happens to price and quantity when a market is in disequilibrium with a shortage. (4)
---
Consumers compete for the limited goods, so price rises (1). As price rises demand contracts and supply extends (2) until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at a new, higher price with a higher quantity sold than before (1).
:::

::: q
Price 1: Qd 90, Qs 30. Price 2: Qd 70, Qs 50. Price 3: Qd 60, Qs 60. Price 4: Qd 40, Qs 80. State the equilibrium price and quantity. (2)
---
Price 3 (1), quantity 60 (1).
:::

::: q
Using the schedule above, calculate the surplus at a price of 4. (1)
---
80 − 40 = 40 units.
:::

::: q
Explain why a market cannot stay at a price below equilibrium in a free market. (4)
---
Below equilibrium there is a shortage — buyers want more than sellers offer (1). Some buyers are willing to pay more, so sellers raise the price (1); this contracts demand and extends supply until the shortage is gone at the equilibrium price (2). Only a government price control could keep price below Pe.
:::

::: q
Draw and explain a diagram showing the equilibrium in a market. (4)
---
Downward-sloping D, upward-sloping S, both labelled; the intersection marked with dotted lines to Pe on the price axis and Qe on the quantity axis (2). At Pe quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no pressure on price to change (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a market for hotel rooms in a seaside town returns to equilibrium after the summer holidays end. (6)
---
When the holidays end demand for rooms decreases, so at the summer price there is a surplus of empty rooms (2). Hotels cut their prices to fill rooms; the lower price extends demand — some visitors are attracted by cheap deals — and contracts supply as some rooms are closed for the season (2). Price falls until the number of rooms wanted equals the number offered, giving a new lower equilibrium price and quantity; the adjustment is quick because prices can be changed daily online (2).
:::

::: watch
- Equilibrium = **Qd equals Qs**. Give both the price *and* the quantity when asked for the equilibrium.
- Above Pe = **surplus**; below Pe = **shortage**. Say which and, from a table, give the size.
- The return to equilibrium is a **movement along** both curves (extension/contraction), not a shift.
- On the diagram: **label** D, S, Pe, Qe and the axes. Dotted lines to the axes get the reading marks.
- Do not say "demand equals supply" carelessly — it is *quantity* demanded that equals *quantity* supplied at the equilibrium price.
:::
