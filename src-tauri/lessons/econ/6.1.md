## What it is

Just as workers specialise (3.3), **countries specialise**: each concentrates on producing the goods and services it is relatively best at, and **trades** for the rest. Saudi Arabia produces oil, Bangladesh clothing, Switzerland pharmaceuticals and banking, New Zealand dairy.

**The basis for specialisation** — countries specialise where they have an advantage from:

- **Natural resources and climate** — oil, minerals, fertile land, sunshine for tourism.
- **Labour** — a large supply of cheap labour (textiles) or highly skilled labour (software, finance).
- **Capital and technology** — advanced machinery, research.
- **Historical expertise, reputation and infrastructure** — French wine, German engineering, ports and financial centres.

The principle: a country should specialise in what it can produce at the **lowest opportunity cost** compared with other countries (its *comparative advantage*). Then total world output is higher than if every country tried to make everything.

**Advantages of specialisation for a country:**

- Higher **output** and **efficiency** — resources go to their best use; economies of scale from producing for the world market.
- **Higher incomes and living standards** — exports earn foreign currency; imports give goods the country cannot make cheaply.
- **Lower prices and more choice** for consumers.
- **Skills and technology** develop in the specialised industry.
- **Employment** in the export industries.

**Disadvantages:**

- **Over-dependence** on a narrow range of exports — a fall in world price or demand (oil, coffee) devastates the economy.
- **Structural unemployment** if the specialised industry declines or is out-competed; workers may lack other skills.
- **Dependence on imports** for essentials — food, energy — risky in a war or crisis.
- **Depletion** of natural resources; environmental damage.
- **Primary product traps** — volatile prices, low value added (5.4).
- Loss of self-sufficiency and of variety in the economy.

**Free trade** is international trade with **no barriers** — no tariffs, quotas or other restrictions. **Advantages**: countries can specialise, so world output and incomes rise; consumers get lower prices, wider choice and better quality; firms get bigger markets and economies of scale; competition forces efficiency and innovation; technology and ideas spread; countries that trade with each other are less likely to fight. **Disadvantages**: domestic industries may be destroyed by cheaper imports, causing structural unemployment; **infant industries** in developing countries cannot grow against established foreign firms; countries become dependent on others; **dumping** (selling exports below cost) can wipe out local producers; loss of tariff revenue for governments; environmental and labour standards may be undercut; the gains are unequally shared.

::: terms
- **Specialisation**: concentrating on the production of a limited range of goods or services.
- **International trade**: the exchange of goods and services between countries.
- **Free trade**: international trade without restrictions such as tariffs and quotas.
- **Exports**: goods and services sold to other countries.
- **Imports**: goods and services bought from other countries.
- **Over-specialisation**: relying on too narrow a range of products, leaving a country exposed if demand or prices for them fall.
:::

::: method
1. **Basis of specialisation**: name the resource or factor advantage *and* link it to low cost or opportunity cost — "abundant cheap labour makes clothing cheap to produce".
2. **Advantages of specialising**: efficiency and economies of scale → higher output → higher income; plus cheaper imports for consumers.
3. **Disadvantages**: over-dependence and vulnerability first, then structural unemployment and resource depletion. Use an example (an oil economy when prices crash).
4. **Free trade vs protection**: advantages are consumers, efficiency, growth; disadvantages are jobs, infant industries, dependence, dumping. Balance and judge — usually "gains overall, but losers need help".
5. **Link to 6.2** for the tools of protection and to 5.4 for primary product dependence.
:::

::: example Define
Define "free trade". (2)

The exchange of goods and services between countries (1) without government restrictions such as tariffs, quotas or embargoes (1).
:::

::: example Explain — basis of specialisation
Explain two reasons why a country might specialise in producing clothing. (4)

It may have a large supply of low-wage labour, so labour-intensive clothing production costs less than in high-wage countries (2). It may have built up expertise, factories and a reputation with foreign buyers over time, giving it lower costs and established markets that newcomers lack (2).
:::

::: example Explain — a disadvantage
Explain why over-specialisation can harm an economy. (4)

If most exports come from one product, such as oil or coffee, a fall in its world price or in demand sharply cuts export earnings, incomes and tax revenue (2). Workers and capital are concentrated in that industry, so when it declines there is structural unemployment and few alternative industries to absorb workers (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — free trade and consumers
Analyse how free trade benefits consumers. (6)

Without tariffs, imported goods are cheaper, so consumers pay lower prices and their real incomes rise; they can buy goods that are not produced at home at all, such as tropical fruit in cold countries (2). Wider choice — many countries' products compete in the same market — means better quality and variety, and domestic firms must match foreign quality and prices to survive, so efficiency and innovation improve (2). Consumers may lose, however, if imports destroy the industry they work in; the gains are spread widely while the losses are concentrated, which is why free trade is popular with consumers but resisted by affected workers (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — should a country specialise?
Discuss whether a developing country should specialise in producing a small number of primary products. (8)

For: it can exploit its natural resources and cheap labour where it has the lowest opportunity cost, gaining efficiency and economies of scale; export earnings pay for imported capital goods; specialising is quicker and cheaper than building industries from scratch (3). Against: primary product prices are volatile and tend to fall over time, so earnings are unstable and the country must export ever more; over-dependence leaves it exposed to a single market; the industries create few skilled jobs and little value added; resources may run out (3). Judgement: specialising is a sensible starting point but the country should diversify gradually into processing and manufacturing to reduce risk and add value — the successful developing economies did exactly that (2).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why countries trade with each other. (2)
---
To obtain goods they cannot produce (e.g. lacking the climate or resources); to buy goods more cheaply than they can make them.
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a country having a "comparative advantage" in a product. (2)
---
It can produce the product at a lower opportunity cost than other countries (1), giving up less of other goods to make it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two advantages to a country of specialising in the products it is best at. (4)
---
Resources are used where they are most productive, so output and incomes rise (2). Producing for the world market allows economies of scale, lowering average cost and prices (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why free trade can cause unemployment in a country. (4)
---
Cheaper imports undercut domestic producers, who lose sales and cut output (2). Workers in those industries lose their jobs and may lack the skills for jobs in expanding export industries — structural unemployment (2).
:::

::: q
Explain what an "infant industry" is and why free trade may harm it. (4)
---
A new industry that has not yet grown large enough to achieve economies of scale (2). Under free trade it must compete with established foreign firms that have lower average costs, so it may be driven out before it can grow (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how free trade can increase competition and efficiency. (4)
---
Domestic firms face rivals from around the world rather than a protected home market (2). To keep customers they must cut costs, improve quality and innovate, raising efficiency (2).
:::

::: q
Explain one risk of a country depending on imports for its food. (2)
---
In a war, crisis or crop failure abroad supplies may be cut off or prices rise sharply (1), threatening food security (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether free trade benefits all countries. (8)
---
For: specialisation raises world output; every country gains access to cheaper goods, wider choice and larger markets; competition drives efficiency; technology spreads; developing countries can export their way to growth as East Asian economies did (3). Against: gains are unequal — developed countries with capital and technology capture most of the value, while developing countries stuck in primary products see falling prices; infant industries cannot survive; workers in uncompetitive industries lose jobs; dumping and rich countries' own protection of agriculture make trade unfair; dependence brings vulnerability (3). Judgement: free trade raises total income, but within and between countries there are losers; it benefits all countries only if the losers are compensated, developing countries are allowed to protect infant industries for a time, and rules are applied fairly (2).
:::

::: watch
- Specialisation is based on **lower cost or opportunity cost** — name the factor that gives it (resources, labour, capital, expertise).
- The key disadvantage is **over-dependence / vulnerability** — always with an example.
- Free trade: benefits go to **consumers and efficient producers**; costs fall on **uncompetitive industries and their workers**.
- **Infant industry** and **dumping** are the two arguments against free trade examiners most expect.
- Define free trade as trade **without barriers** — tariffs, quotas, embargoes.
:::
