## What it is

**Employment** means having a job. **Unemployment** means being **willing and able to work, actively looking, but without a job**. **Full employment** does not mean zero unemployment — some people are always between jobs — but the lowest rate the economy can sustain, typically 3–5%.

The **labour force** (working population) is everyone of working age who is either employed or unemployed. Those not in the labour force — students, the retired, full-time carers, the long-term sick, those who have given up looking — are **economically inactive**. The **participation rate** is the share of the working-age population in the labour force.

**Measuring unemployment:**

- **The labour force survey** — a large sample survey (the international standard) asking whether people are without work, available to start within two weeks and have looked in the last four weeks.
- **The claimant count** — those registered for unemployment benefit; cheaper, but misses people not eligible or not claiming.

$$\text{unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{number unemployed}}{\text{labour force}} \times 100$$

The denominator is the **labour force**, not the whole population.

**Types (causes) of unemployment:**

| Type | Cause | Example | Policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frictional** | people between jobs; time taken to search | a graduate looking for a first job; a worker who quit to find something better | better job information, job centres |
| **Structural** | a long-term decline of an industry or region, or a skills mismatch with the jobs available | coal miners after pits close; workers replaced by automation | retraining, help with relocation, regional aid |
| **Cyclical (demand-deficient)** | a fall in total demand during a recession; firms lay off workers across the whole economy | job losses in 2009 | expansionary fiscal and monetary policy |
| **Seasonal** | demand for some labour varies with the season | ski instructors in summer; farm workers in winter | diversifying local economies |

Also: **technological** (a form of structural), and **voluntary** unemployment when benefits are close to wages.

**Consequences of unemployment:**

- *For the unemployed*: lost income and living standards; loss of skills the longer it lasts; stress, ill health, family breakdown.
- *For firms*: lower demand for their goods; but a larger pool of labour and less wage pressure.
- *For the government*: lower income tax and VAT revenue, higher benefit spending → bigger budget deficit; more spending on health and policing.
- *For the economy*: output below capacity (inside the PPC) — a waste of resources; lower growth; higher poverty and inequality; social problems, crime.

**Policies against unemployment** — match the policy to the type:

- **Expansionary fiscal policy** (lower taxes, more spending) and **expansionary monetary policy** (lower interest rates) raise demand → firms hire. *Works for* cyclical unemployment; *not* structural; risks inflation and a deficit.
- **Supply-side policies**: education and training (skills mismatch), improving mobility (relocation grants, job centres), cutting income tax and benefits to raise work incentives, regional subsidies to attract firms, deregulation and lower employment costs so firms hire more. *Works for* frictional and structural unemployment; slow and costly.
- A **lower exchange rate** boosts exports and export jobs.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Unemployment**: the situation of people who are willing and able to work and actively seeking a job, but cannot find one.
- **Labour force**: all those of working age who are employed or unemployed.
- **Unemployment rate**: the number unemployed as a percentage of the labour force.
- **Full employment**: the lowest sustainable rate of unemployment, where everyone willing and able to work at the going wage can find a job.
- **Cyclical unemployment**: unemployment caused by a lack of total demand in the economy.
- **Structural unemployment**: unemployment caused by long-term changes in the structure of the economy, leaving workers' skills mismatched with jobs.

::: method
1. **Define with "willing, able, seeking"**. Someone who is not looking is inactive, not unemployed.
2. **Calculate the rate** with the labour force as the denominator. If given employed and unemployed, add them to get the labour force first.
3. **Identify the type** from the cause: between jobs → frictional; industry decline / skills → structural; recession → cyclical; time of year → seasonal.
4. **Match policy to type.** Demand-side for cyclical; supply-side for structural and frictional. Say why the other kind would not work.
5. **Consequences** by stakeholder: the unemployed, firms, the government, the economy. Then evaluate — the effect depends on duration and the level of benefits.
6. **"How well do policies work"**: spare capacity, time lags, cost, inflation risk, whether the skills gap is addressed.
:::

::: example Define
Define "unemployment". (2)

The situation where people who are willing and able to work and are actively looking for a job (1) cannot find one (1).
:::

::: example Calculate
A country has 30 million people employed and 2 million unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate. (2)

Labour force $= 30 + 2 = 32$ million (1). Unemployment rate $= \dfrac{2}{32} \times 100 = 6.25\%$ (1).
:::

::: example Identify the type
A shipyard closes because ships are now built more cheaply in Asia, and its 3,000 welders cannot find work in the town. Identify the type of unemployment and explain a suitable policy. (4)

Structural unemployment: a long-term decline of an industry has left workers with skills no longer demanded in the area (2). A supply-side policy such as government-funded retraining in skills local growing firms need, or grants to help workers move to regions with vacancies, would address the mismatch; raising total demand would not, since the jobs they had are gone (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — consequences
Analyse the consequences of a rise in unemployment for a government. (6)

Tax revenue falls: the unemployed pay no income tax and spend less, so VAT receipts also fall (2). Spending rises: more unemployment benefit is paid, and long-term unemployment raises spending on health services and policing as poverty and crime increase, so the budget deficit widens (2). The government may then need to borrow more or cut other services, and it faces political pressure to act; it also loses the output the unemployed could have produced, lowering growth and future tax revenue (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — cutting benefits
Discuss whether a government should cut unemployment benefits to reduce unemployment. (8)

For: lower benefits widen the gap between benefit income and wages, increasing the incentive to take a job, especially low-paid work; this reduces voluntary and frictional unemployment; it also cuts government spending and can fund training instead (3). Against: most unemployment in a recession is cyclical — there are no jobs to take, so lower benefits simply increase poverty; the unemployed spend less, reducing demand and possibly causing more job losses; hardship raises health and crime costs; and structural unemployment needs retraining, not incentives (3). Judgement: cutting benefits may reduce unemployment slightly when jobs are plentiful, but in a downturn or where skills are the problem it causes harm without creating jobs; better to combine modest benefits with training and job-search support (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between someone who is unemployed and someone who is economically inactive. (2)
---
The unemployed are without a job but seeking work (1); the inactive are not looking — students, retired people, carers — so are not in the labour force (1).
:::

::: q
A country has a labour force of 50 million and 3.5 million unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate. (1)
---
$\dfrac{3.5}{50} \times 100 = 7\%$.
:::

::: q
Explain what the labour force survey measures and one advantage over the claimant count. (4)
---
It surveys a sample of households, counting those without work who are available and have looked recently (2). It includes people not entitled to or not claiming benefits, so it is more complete and comparable between countries (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between frictional and structural unemployment. (4)
---
Frictional is short-term unemployment while workers move between jobs, caused by the time it takes to search (2). Structural is longer-term, caused by the decline of industries or a mismatch between workers' skills and available jobs (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why cyclical unemployment rises in a recession. (2)
---
Total demand falls, so firms sell less and cut output (1); they need fewer workers and lay them off across the whole economy (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two consequences of unemployment for the unemployed individual. (4)
---
Lower income, so a lower standard of living and possibly poverty (2). Loss of skills and confidence the longer it lasts, making it harder to find work, plus stress and ill health (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a cut in income tax could reduce unemployment. (4)
---
Households keep more of their pay, so they spend more, raising demand and prompting firms to hire (2). The higher take-home pay also increases the incentive for the unemployed to accept jobs rather than stay on benefits (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why expansionary fiscal policy may not reduce structural unemployment. (2)
---
Extra demand creates jobs in growing industries (1), but structurally unemployed workers lack the skills or live in the wrong region to fill them (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should be concerned about a low rate of unemployment. (8)
---
Not concerned: low unemployment means high incomes and output, less poverty, higher tax revenue and lower benefit spending, and social benefits such as lower crime — it is a main macroeconomic aim (3). Concerned: very low unemployment can signal an overheating economy — firms compete for scarce workers, pushing up wages and causing inflation; higher incomes raise imports, worsening the current account; a shortage of workers limits growth; and low headline unemployment may hide low-paid, part-time or inactive workers (3). Judgement: low unemployment is desirable, but a government should watch for wage inflation and skill shortages and use supply-side policies to raise capacity rather than let demand overheat (2).
:::

::: watch
- The unemployment rate's denominator is the **labour force**, not the population.
- Unemployed = **seeking work**. Retired and students are **inactive**.
- Name the **type** from the cause, and **match the policy** — demand-side for cyclical, supply-side for structural/frictional.
- Consequences: cover **individual, firms, government, economy**.
- Full employment is **not zero** unemployment — frictional always exists.
:::
