## What it is

The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another — for example, £1 = 1.25 US dollars. It is set in the **foreign exchange market**, where currencies are bought and sold. A **floating** exchange rate is determined by demand and supply with no government intervention; a **fixed** rate is held at a set level by the central bank buying and selling currency.

**Why a currency is demanded** (bought) — foreigners need pounds to:

- buy the country's **exports** of goods and services (including **tourists** visiting);
- **invest** in the country — buying its firms, factories, shares or property (foreign direct investment);
- **save** there — deposits and bonds attracting **hot money** when its interest rates are high;
- **speculate**, expecting the rate to rise.

**Why a currency is supplied** (sold) — residents sell pounds to:

- buy **imports** and holidays abroad;
- invest or save abroad;
- speculate, expecting the rate to fall.

The rate rises when demand rises (more exports, higher interest rates, inward investment, confidence) or supply falls; it falls when imports rise, interest rates fall, or speculators sell.

**Appreciation**: the currency rises in value — £1 buys *more* dollars (1.25 → 1.40). **Depreciation**: it falls — £1 buys *fewer* dollars (1.25 → 1.10). (For fixed rates the words are **revaluation** and **devaluation**.) Floating rates **fluctuate** constantly because trade flows, interest rates, speculation and confidence change every day.

**Effects of a change** — the core skill. Remember: **exports are priced in the home currency, imports in the foreign one**.

| | Appreciation (£ stronger) | Depreciation (£ weaker) |
|---|---|---|
| Export prices abroad | rise → exports fall | fall → exports rise |
| Import prices at home | fall → imports rise | rise → imports fall |
| Current account | worsens | improves |
| Inflation | falls (cheaper imports, less demand) | rises (dearer imports, more demand) |
| Growth and jobs | slow (exporters suffer) | boosted (exporters gain) |
| Tourism | fewer visitors, cheaper foreign holidays | more visitors, dearer holidays abroad |
| Firms importing materials | lower costs | higher costs |

**Evaluation**: the size of the effect depends on the **price elasticity of demand** for exports and imports — if demand is inelastic, a depreciation raises import spending in the short run and does little for export volumes; it takes time for buyers to switch (the effect improves after a year or so); firms may keep foreign prices unchanged and take the gain in profit margins; and a depreciation caused by loss of confidence may bring inflation without much export benefit.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Exchange rate**: the price of one currency in terms of another currency.
- **Floating exchange rate**: an exchange rate determined by the demand for and supply of the currency in the foreign exchange market.
- **Appreciation**: a rise in the value of a currency in terms of other currencies.
- **Depreciation**: a fall in the value of a currency in terms of other currencies.
- **Hot money**: funds moved between countries to take advantage of differences in interest rates or expected exchange-rate changes.

::: method
1. **Conversions**: multiply by the rate to go from pounds to dollars; divide to come back. Show it both before and after the change.
2. **Demand and supply of the currency**: exports and inward investment = demand; imports and outward investment = supply. Interest rates move hot money.
3. **Direction words**: more dollars per pound = appreciation. Check with a number before writing.
4. **Effects chain**: depreciation → export prices fall abroad, import prices rise at home → exports rise, imports fall → current account improves, growth rises, inflation rises. Reverse it for appreciation.
5. **Always evaluate with elasticity and time**: inelastic demand → small volume effects, short-run worsening; importers' costs; whether the cause was a loss of confidence.
6. **Link to policy**: raising interest rates to cut inflation appreciates the currency (4.3); a government may want depreciation to fix a current account deficit (6.4).
:::

::: example Calculate
The exchange rate changes from £1 = 1.25 dollars to £1 = 1.40 dollars. (a) State whether the pound has appreciated or depreciated. (b) Calculate the dollar price of a British export priced at £100 before and after. (c) Calculate the pound cost of an American import priced at 200 dollars before and after. (5)

(a) Appreciated — £1 now buys more dollars (1). (b) Before: $100 \times 1.25 = 125$ dollars; after: $100 \times 1.40 = 140$ dollars — the export is dearer abroad (2). (c) Before: $200 \div 1.25 = £160$; after: $200 \div 1.40 = £142.86$ — the import is cheaper at home (2).
:::

::: example Explain — a cause
Explain how a rise in a country's interest rates affects its exchange rate. (4)

Higher interest rates give foreign savers a better return, so they buy the currency to deposit money there — hot money inflows raise demand for the currency (2). Residents are also less inclined to move savings abroad, reducing supply; the currency appreciates (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — a depreciation
Analyse the effects of a depreciation of a country's currency on its economy. (6)

Exports become cheaper in foreign currency, so foreign buyers purchase more, while imports become dearer at home, so residents switch to domestic goods; export industries expand and the current account improves (2). Higher demand for domestic output raises growth and employment, and tourism rises as the country becomes cheaper to visit (2). But import prices rise, so the cost of imported food, fuel and raw materials increases — cost-push inflation — and firms relying on imported components face higher costs; the size of the export gain depends on elasticity of demand and takes time, so in the short run the current account may even worsen (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — a strong currency
Discuss whether a strong (appreciating) currency is good for an economy. (8)

Good: imports are cheaper, so consumers' real incomes rise and firms' imported materials cost less, lowering inflation; foreign holidays are cheaper; a strong currency often reflects confidence and attracts investment; it forces exporters to become more efficient (3). Bad: exports are dearer abroad, so export industries lose sales, cut output and jobs; imports rise as they undercut domestic firms, so the current account worsens and growth slows; tourism falls; manufacturing regions may suffer structural unemployment (4). Judgement: a strong currency benefits consumers and helps control inflation, but harms exporters and growth; whether it is good depends on the country's priorities — an economy with high inflation may welcome it, one with unemployment and a trade deficit will not (1).
:::

::: q
Define "depreciation" of a currency. (2)
---
A fall in the value of a currency (1) in terms of other currencies under a floating exchange rate, so it buys fewer units of foreign currency (1).
:::

::: q
£1 = 1.30 dollars. Calculate the price in dollars of a £40 book, and the price in pounds of a 65-dollar T-shirt. (2)
---
$40 \times 1.30 = 52$ dollars (1); $65 \div 1.30 = £50$ (1).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why the demand for a country's currency might rise. (2)
---
Higher exports; higher interest rates attracting hot money (or inward investment, speculation).
:::

::: q
Explain how an increase in imports affects the exchange rate. (4)
---
To buy imports residents must sell their own currency to obtain foreign currency, increasing its supply on the foreign exchange market (2). With demand unchanged, the currency's price falls — it depreciates (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how an appreciation of the currency affects a firm that exports. (4)
---
Its goods cost more in foreign currency, so foreign customers buy less and sales fall (2). To keep sales it may cut its foreign price, reducing its profit margin; either way revenue is likely to fall (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how a depreciation could increase inflation. (4)
---
Imported goods and raw materials cost more in domestic currency, raising prices directly and firms' costs — cost-push inflation (2). Higher exports and reduced imports raise demand for domestic output, adding demand-pull pressure (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a depreciation may not improve the current account in the short run. (4)
---
Demand for imports is inelastic in the short run — contracts and habits take time to change — so the higher import prices raise total import spending (2). Export orders also take time to respond, so the deficit may widen before it narrows (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why floating exchange rates fluctuate. (2)
---
Demand and supply of the currency change constantly (1) as trade flows, interest rates, speculation and confidence change (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should try to lower the value of its currency to reduce a current account deficit. (8)
---
For: a lower currency makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, raising export sales and cutting imports, improving the current account and boosting output and jobs in export industries; it can be achieved by cutting interest rates or selling the currency (3). Against: it raises import prices, causing inflation that erodes the competitiveness gained; if demand for exports and imports is inelastic the deficit may worsen at first; firms importing components face higher costs; other countries may retaliate by depreciating too; and lower interest rates may conflict with the inflation aim (3). Judgement: depreciation can help when demand for traded goods is elastic and the economy has spare capacity, but it treats the symptom; improving productivity and quality through supply-side policy is a more durable cure (2).
:::
