## What it is

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the **general price level** — prices *on average* across the economy, not one product. It means each unit of money buys less: the **purchasing power** of money falls. **Deflation** is a sustained *fall* in the general price level. (**Disinflation** is a fall in the *rate* of inflation — prices still rise, but more slowly.)

**Measuring it — the consumer price index (CPI):**

1. A **survey** finds what a typical household buys — the **basket** of goods and services.
2. Each item is given a **weight** according to its share of spending (food weighs more than cinema tickets).
3. Prices are collected each month and each item's price change is multiplied by its weight.
4. The result is an **index**: base year = 100. Inflation is the percentage change in the index over a year.

$$\text{inflation rate} = \frac{\text{CPI this year} - \text{CPI last year}}{\text{CPI last year}} \times 100$$

Problems: the basket must be updated as tastes change; quality improvements make like-for-like comparison hard; different households buy different baskets, so the CPI is nobody's exact cost of living; sampling errors.

**Causes of inflation:**

- **Demand-pull** — total demand grows faster than the economy can produce; "too much money chasing too few goods". Causes: rising consumer spending (tax cuts, cheap credit, confidence), government spending, exports, a growing money supply. Firms respond to shortages by raising prices.
- **Cost-push** — firms' costs rise and they pass them on: higher wages (especially if unions push wages above productivity), dearer raw materials and energy (oil), higher import prices after a fall in the exchange rate, higher indirect taxes, higher rents.
- Also **imported inflation** (a form of cost-push) and **monetary inflation** (excess money supply).

**Consequences of inflation:**

- **Savers lose** — money saved buys less; if the interest rate is below inflation, the *real* interest rate is negative.
- **Lenders lose, borrowers gain** — loans are repaid in money worth less; a mortgage of fixed size shrinks in real terms.
- **Fixed incomes fall in real terms** — pensioners and those on fixed wages.
- **Menu costs** (changing prices) and **shoe-leather costs** (searching for the best deals).
- **Uncertainty** cuts investment — firms cannot plan.
- **Exports** become less competitive, imports cheaper → current account deficit.
- **Wage-price spiral** — workers demand pay rises to keep up, raising costs and prices again.
- **Redistribution**: from savers to borrowers, from fixed to flexible incomes; the poor are hit hardest by rising food and fuel prices.
- Mild inflation (about 2%) is considered healthy: it lets firms raise prices and profits, encourages spending rather than hoarding, and avoids deflation.

**Deflation** sounds pleasant but is dangerous: consumers **postpone purchases** waiting for lower prices, so demand and output fall; the **real value of debt rises**, squeezing borrowers; firms' revenues fall while wages are hard to cut, so profits fall and jobs go; a spiral of falling prices and demand.

**Policies against inflation:**

- **Contractionary monetary policy** — higher interest rates reduce borrowing and spending. Effective against demand-pull; lags; hurts growth and jobs.
- **Contractionary fiscal policy** — higher taxes, lower government spending. Cuts demand; unpopular; deepens a slowdown.
- **Supply-side policy** — raise productivity and capacity to cut costs. Best against cost-push; slow.
- **Exchange-rate appreciation** — cheaper imports; hurts exporters.
- Direct controls on prices or wages — short-term only, cause shortages.

::: terms
- **Inflation**: a sustained rise in the general price level over time.
- **Deflation**: a sustained fall in the general price level.
- **Disinflation**: a fall in the rate of inflation — prices still rise, but more slowly.
- **Consumer price index (CPI)**: a weighted index of the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by a typical household.
- **Base year**: the year a price index is compared with, given the value 100.
- **Weights**: the importance of each item in the CPI, based on the share of household spending on it.
- **Demand-pull inflation**: inflation caused by total demand rising faster than the economy's ability to supply.
- **Cost-push inflation**: inflation caused by rising costs of production being passed on as higher prices.
- **Real interest rate**: the nominal interest rate minus the rate of inflation.
:::

::: method
1. **Define "general price level"** — the average of prices — and "sustained". One product getting dearer is not inflation.
2. **CPI calculations**: index change as a percentage; weighted index = Σ(weight × price index) ÷ Σ weights. Show the multiplication.
3. **Classify the cause**: does the story start with *spending* (demand-pull) or with *costs* (cost-push)? Name it, explain the chain.
4. **Consequences by group**: savers, borrowers, lenders, fixed incomes, firms, exporters, the government. Use *real* values — "the real value of savings falls".
5. **Real interest rate** = nominal − inflation. A saver with 2% interest and 5% inflation loses 3% a year.
6. **Policy answers**: match the tool to the cause (demand-pull → monetary/fiscal; cost-push → supply-side), then evaluate with lags and conflicts.
:::

::: example Calculate — inflation from the CPI
The CPI was 105 last year and is 108.15 this year. Calculate the inflation rate. (2)

$\dfrac{108.15 - 105}{105} \times 100 = 3\%$ (2).
:::

::: example Calculate — a weighted index
A simple basket has three groups: food (weight 40, price index 110), housing (weight 30, index 105), other (weight 30, index 120). Calculate the overall price index. (3)

$40 \times 110 = 4{,}400$; $30 \times 105 = 3{,}150$; $30 \times 120 = 3{,}600$; total $11{,}150$ (2). Divide by the total weight of 100: index $= 111.5$, so prices have risen 11.5% since the base year (1).
:::

::: example Explain — demand-pull
Explain how a cut in interest rates could cause demand-pull inflation. (4)

Cheaper borrowing raises consumer spending on credit and firms' investment, so total demand rises (2). If the economy is near full capacity, firms cannot raise output quickly enough to meet the extra demand; shortages develop and firms raise prices, so the general price level rises (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — savers, lenders and borrowers
Analyse how inflation affects savers, lenders and borrowers. (6)

Savers lose: the money they have put aside buys less each year, and if the interest they earn is below the inflation rate the real value of their savings falls — 3% interest with 5% inflation is a real loss of 2% (2). Lenders also lose, because loans are repaid in money that is worth less than when it was lent, unless they charged an interest rate high enough to cover inflation (2). Borrowers gain: the real value of their debt falls, and if their wages rise with prices the repayments take a smaller share of income — so inflation redistributes from savers and lenders to borrowers, discouraging saving and possibly investment (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — deflation
Discuss whether falling prices are good for an economy. (8)

Good: consumers can buy more with the same income, raising living standards for those on fixed incomes; savers' money gains value; if prices fall because productivity has risen, firms remain profitable and exports become more competitive (3). Bad: if prices are expected to keep falling, households postpone purchases, so demand and output fall and firms cut jobs; the real value of debt rises, squeezing borrowers and causing defaults; firms' revenues fall but wages are hard to cut, so profits and investment collapse; the central bank cannot cut interest rates below zero to respond (4). Judgement: a small fall in prices caused by higher productivity is harmless, but deflation caused by weak demand is more damaging than mild inflation — which is why central banks target about 2% rather than zero (1).
:::

::: example Explain — cost-push inflation
Explain how a rise in the world price of oil could cause cost-push inflation. (4)

Oil is used to make fuel, plastics and electricity, so its price is a cost for almost every firm (1). When oil becomes dearer, transport and energy costs rise across the economy (1); firms pass the higher costs on to consumers as higher prices to protect their profits (1), so the general price level rises — inflation caused by costs, not by higher demand (1).
:::

Define "inflation". (2)
---

::: q
The CPI rises from 120 to 126. Calculate the inflation rate. (2)
---
$\dfrac{6}{120} \times 100 = 5\%$.
:::

::: q
Explain why items in the CPI basket are weighted. (2)
---
Households spend more on some items than others (1); weighting means a price rise in a major item like food affects the index more than one in a minor item (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two causes of cost-push inflation. (4)
---
A rise in the price of oil raises energy and transport costs for most firms, which pass them on in higher prices (2). Wage rises greater than productivity growth raise labour cost per unit, so firms increase prices to protect profit (2).
:::

::: q
A saver receives 2% interest while inflation is 6%. Calculate the real interest rate and explain what it means. (2)
---
$2 - 6 = -4\%$ (1): the purchasing power of the savings falls by 4% a year (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how inflation can affect a country's exports. (4)
---
Domestic prices rise faster than those abroad, so exports become dearer in foreign markets (2). Foreign buyers switch to cheaper suppliers, so export sales fall and the current account worsens (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a government might prefer 2% inflation to zero. (4)
---
Mild inflation lets firms raise prices and profits, encouraging investment, and lets real wages adjust without cutting money wages (2). It keeps the economy away from deflation, where postponed spending and rising real debt cause recession (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why higher interest rates may not cure cost-push inflation. (4)
---
Cost-push inflation comes from higher costs, such as oil or wages, not excess demand (2). Higher rates cut demand and output but do not reduce those costs, so prices stay high while unemployment rises (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should always try to reduce inflation. (8)
---
For reducing it: inflation erodes savings and fixed incomes, hurts exports, creates uncertainty that cuts investment, redistributes income arbitrarily and can spiral if expectations take hold; price stability is a key aim (3). Against: mild inflation is healthy and its cure — higher interest rates or taxes — slows growth and raises unemployment; if inflation is cost-push from a temporary oil shock, the pain of policy may exceed the benefit; and if the economy is in recession, reducing inflation further risks deflation (3). Judgement: governments should act against high or accelerating inflation, especially demand-pull, but tolerate low inflation of around 2% and be cautious about attacking temporary cost-push inflation (2).
:::

::: watch
- Inflation is a rise in the **general** price level, **sustained**. A single price rise is not inflation.
- CPI: **basket, weights, index, base year 100**. Inflation = % change in the index.
- **Demand-pull** starts with spending; **cost-push** starts with costs. Say which and why.
- Consequences need **real** language: real value of savings, real interest rate, real debt.
- **Deflation is harmful** — postponed spending, rising real debt. Do not describe it as simply "prices fall, good for consumers".
:::
