## What it is

A **production possibility curve (PPC)** shows the **maximum combinations of two goods** an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently used. It turns scarcity, choice and opportunity cost into one diagram.

Reading the diagram:

- **On the curve (A, B)**: all resources fully employed and used efficiently. The economy cannot make more of one good without making less of the other.
- **Inside the curve (C)**: resources are **unemployed or used inefficiently** — a recession, or workers and machines standing idle. The economy could make more of both goods.
- **Beyond the curve (D)**: **unattainable** with current resources and technology.
- **Moving along the curve** (A → B): more consumer goods, fewer capital goods. The capital goods given up are the **opportunity cost** of the extra consumer goods.
- **Shifting outwards** (the dashed curve): **economic growth** — the economy can now produce more of both. Caused by more or better factors of production: investment in capital, education, new technology, discovery of resources, immigration.
- **Shifting inwards**: fewer or worse resources — war, natural disaster, emigration of skilled workers, a disease that reduces the workforce.

Why is the curve **bowed outwards**? Resources are not equally good at making both goods. As an economy makes more and more consumer goods it has to switch resources that were well suited to capital goods, so each extra unit of consumer goods costs more capital goods — **increasing opportunity cost**. A straight-line PPC would mean constant opportunity cost.

A special shift: if only one industry improves (say, new technology in farming but not in manufacturing), the curve **pivots** — it moves out along one axis only.

::: terms
- **Production possibility curve (PPC)**: a curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods that can be produced with existing resources and technology when they are fully employed.
- **Productive efficiency**: producing on the PPC, using all resources to get the maximum possible output.
- **Unemployed resources**: resources that are not being used, shown by a point inside the PPC.
- **Unattainable point**: a combination of goods outside the PPC that cannot be produced with current resources.
- **Movement along the PPC**: reallocating resources from one good to the other, which has an opportunity cost.
- **Economic growth**: an increase in the productive capacity of an economy, shown by an outward shift of the PPC.
- **Capital goods**: goods used to produce other goods — machinery, equipment.
- **Consumer goods**: goods bought by households for their own satisfaction.
:::

::: method
1. **Label the axes with the two goods** and draw a curve bowed outwards from the vertical axis to the horizontal axis. Label it PPC. Unlabelled diagrams score nothing.
2. **Place the point the question describes.** Full employment → on the curve. Unemployment → inside. "Cannot be reached" → beyond.
3. **Movement along = opportunity cost.** Mark the two points, drop dotted lines to both axes and read off the change in each good. "Moving from A to B gains 40 consumer goods and gives up 20 capital goods, so the opportunity cost of 40 consumer goods is 20 capital goods."
4. **Shift = change in resources or technology.** Outward shift for growth (draw a second curve further out); inward shift for a loss of resources. Say what caused it.
5. **Explain the bow.** Resources are not equally suited to both goods, so opportunity cost increases as more of one good is produced.
6. **Capital vs consumer goods choice.** Choosing more capital goods now (fewer consumer goods) means more growth later — a bigger outward shift. This is the classic 6-mark analyse.
:::

::: example Define
Define "production possibility curve". (2)

A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods (1) that an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed (1).
:::

::: example Read — opportunity cost along the curve
An economy can produce either 100 units of food and 0 clothing, or 80 food and 30 clothing, or 50 food and 50 clothing (all points on its PPC). Calculate the opportunity cost of increasing clothing from 30 to 50 units. (2)

Food falls from 80 to 50, so 30 units of food are given up (1). The opportunity cost of 20 extra units of clothing is 30 units of food, i.e. 1.5 units of food per unit of clothing (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a point inside the curve
Explain what a point inside the PPC shows and give one reason an economy might be there. (4)

A point inside the curve shows that the economy is not producing its maximum possible output: some resources are unemployed or being used inefficiently (2). This could happen in a recession, when demand falls and firms lay off workers and leave machinery idle, so the economy produces less of both goods than it could (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — capital versus consumer goods
Analyse, using a PPC, the effect on an economy of producing more capital goods and fewer consumer goods. (6)

In the short run the economy moves along its PPC towards the capital goods axis: it produces more machinery and factories, and the opportunity cost is the consumer goods given up, so living standards may fall for a time (2). The extra capital goods raise the quantity and quality of the economy's capital, increasing its productive capacity (2). Over time this shifts the whole PPC outwards, so the economy can eventually produce more of *both* capital and consumer goods than before — a short-term sacrifice for long-term growth (2).
:::

::: example Explain — an inward shift of the PPC
A major earthquake destroys many factories and roads in a country. Explain, with the help of a PPC diagram, the effect on the economy. (4)

Diagram: a PPC with capital goods on one axis and consumer goods on the other; the curve shifts inwards, towards the origin (2).

The earthquake destroys capital, reducing the quantity of the country's resources (1), so the maximum combinations of goods it can produce fall — its productive capacity has shrunk, the opposite of economic growth (1).
:::

::: q
State what is shown by a point on a PPC. (2)
---
A combination of the two goods that the economy can produce (1) when all resources are fully and efficiently employed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what a point outside the PPC shows. (2)
---
A combination of goods the economy cannot currently produce (1) because it does not have enough resources or the technology to reach it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a PPC is usually drawn bowed outwards. (4)
---
Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods (1). As more of one good is produced, resources better suited to the other good must be moved across, so each extra unit costs more of the other good (2) — opportunity cost increases, which gives the bowed shape (1).
:::

::: q
Give two causes of an outward shift of a country's PPC. (2)
---
Investment in new capital; improved technology (or education raising labour quality, discovery of resources, immigration).
:::

::: q
Give two causes of an inward shift of a PPC. (2)
---
A natural disaster destroying capital; war; emigration of workers; a disease reducing the labour force.
:::

::: q
An economy is at a point on its PPC producing 60 cars and 200 computers. It moves to another point on the curve producing 80 cars and 150 computers. Calculate the opportunity cost of the extra 20 cars. (2)
---
50 computers (200 − 150) (1); 2.5 computers per car (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a movement along a PPC and a shift of the PPC. (4)
---
A movement along the curve is a change in the combination of the two goods produced with the same resources, showing opportunity cost (2). A shift of the curve is a change in the economy's total productive capacity caused by a change in the quantity or quality of its resources or technology (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse how an improvement in education could affect a country's PPC. (6)
---
Education raises the quality of labour, so each worker can produce more output per hour (2). With more productive workers the economy's maximum output rises, shifting the PPC outwards so more of both goods can be produced (2). The effect takes time, since students must finish their education and enter the workforce, and depends on whether the skills learned match the jobs available; if they do not, the shift will be small (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Label everything**: both axes with the goods, the curve, and every point. Unlabelled points earn nothing.
- Inside = **unemployment or inefficiency**; on = **full employment**; beyond = **unattainable**. Do not confuse "beyond" with "growth" — growth is a *shift* of the whole curve.
- A movement along the curve is **not** growth. Growth means the curve moves.
- Read opportunity cost as a **change in the other good**, and give the number if the question gives numbers.
- The bow means **increasing** opportunity cost; a straight line means constant.
:::

::: test
Analyse how an increase in investment in new technology affects a country's production possibility curve. (6)
---
New technology increases the productivity of existing resources, so more can be produced with the same land, labour and capital (2). The PPC shifts outwards, showing economic growth — the economy can now produce more of both goods (2). If the technology applies to only one good, the curve shifts outwards more along that axis; the country can reach combinations that were previously unattainable, raising living standards (2).
Marking: up to 6 marks for a developed, logical chain of analysis; 1–2 for simple points without explanation.
:::

::: test
Discuss whether a country should produce more capital goods and fewer consumer goods. (8)
---
For: more capital goods now means more machinery and factories, increasing future productive capacity — the PPC shifts outwards further in future, allowing more of both goods later (4).
Against: fewer consumer goods now means lower living standards today; people may be unwilling to sacrifice current consumption, especially in poor countries where basic needs are unmet (4).
Conclusion: a balance is needed; the right choice depends on the country's current living standards and how quickly the investment raises future output.
Marking: up to 4 marks for developed analysis of both sides; up to 4 for evaluation — weighing the arguments and reaching a justified conclusion.
:::
