## What it is

**The budget** is the government's annual statement of its planned **spending** and **tax revenue**.

- A **budget deficit**: spending exceeds revenue. The gap is borrowed (by selling government bonds), adding to the **national debt**.
- A **budget surplus**: revenue exceeds spending; debt can be repaid.
- A **balanced budget**: the two are equal.

Deficits grow in recessions (tax revenue falls, benefit spending rises) and shrink in booms. A large deficit means rising debt, interest payments that crowd out other spending, and possibly higher taxes later; but running a deficit in a recession supports demand and jobs.

**Why governments spend** (and on what): public goods (defence, police, roads); merit goods (education, health); welfare benefits to redistribute income (pensions, unemployment and child benefits); interest on the national debt; subsidies to firms; investment in infrastructure; managing the economy — raising spending to boost demand in a slump. Effects: higher demand, output and jobs; better skills and infrastructure raising growth; less poverty; but higher taxes or borrowing, and the risk of inflation if the economy is at capacity.

**Why governments tax**: to raise revenue for spending; to redistribute income (progressive taxes); to discourage demerit goods and pollution (taxes on tobacco, fuel); to protect domestic industry (tariffs); to manage demand (raising taxes cools a boom).

**Classifying taxes:**

| Classification | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct tax** | paid directly by the person or firm on income or wealth; cannot be passed on | income tax, corporation tax (on firms' profits), inheritance tax, capital gains tax |
| **Indirect tax** | on spending; collected by the seller and passed on in the price | VAT / sales tax, excise duties on fuel, alcohol, tobacco, import tariffs |
| **Progressive** | takes a *larger percentage* of income as income rises | income tax with rising bands |
| **Proportional** | the same percentage at every income | a flat-rate income tax |
| **Regressive** | takes a *smaller percentage* of income as income rises (though the poor may pay less in money) | VAT, excise duties, a poll tax |

Who taxes affect: **income tax** — households' disposable income and the incentive to work; **corporation tax** — firms' profit, investment and where multinationals locate; **VAT and excise duties** — consumers through higher prices, and firms through lower sales; regressive taxes fall hardest on the poor; taxes on imports raise prices for consumers but protect domestic producers.

A **good tax** (the principles of taxation): equitable (fair), certain, convenient to pay, economical to collect, flexible, and efficient (does not distort behaviour much).

**Fiscal policy** is the use of **government spending and taxation** to influence the economy.

- **Expansionary fiscal policy** — cut taxes and/or raise spending → a bigger deficit → higher total demand → more output and jobs. Used against recession and unemployment.
- **Contractionary fiscal policy** — raise taxes and/or cut spending → lower demand → less inflation and fewer imports. Used against inflation or a current account deficit.

**How fiscal policy serves the aims:**

- *Growth and employment*: expansionary policy raises demand; spending on infrastructure and education also raises capacity (a supply-side effect).
- *Inflation*: contractionary policy reduces demand-pull inflation.
- *Balance of payments*: higher taxes cut spending on imports.
- *Redistribution*: progressive taxes and benefits transfer income to the poor.

**Limitations**: time lags (budgets are annual; projects take years); if demand rises at full capacity the result is inflation, not output; tax cuts may be saved, not spent; borrowing raises debt and interest costs and may **crowd out** private investment by raising interest rates; higher taxes weaken incentives; political pressure makes cutting spending hard.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Budget deficit**: a situation where government spending exceeds tax revenue in a year.
- **National debt**: the total amount the government owes, accumulated from past deficits.
- **Direct tax**: a tax on income or wealth paid directly to the government by the person or firm on whom it is levied.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending, collected by sellers and passed on in prices.
- **Progressive tax**: a tax that takes a larger percentage of income as income rises.
- **Regressive tax**: a tax that takes a smaller percentage of income as income rises.
- **Fiscal policy**: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy.

::: method
1. **Deficit vs surplus**: spending minus revenue. Positive → deficit; show the subtraction. Link deficits to borrowing and the national debt.
2. **Classify a tax twice**: direct or indirect, *and* progressive, proportional or regressive. VAT is indirect *and* regressive; income tax is direct *and* progressive.
3. **Progressive/regressive is about the percentage**, not the amount. A rich person pays more VAT in money but a smaller share of income.
4. **Fiscal policy answers**: name the direction (expansionary/contractionary), the measures (which tax, which spending), the effect on total demand, then on the aim — and then the limitation or side effect.
5. **Effects on decision-makers**: a change in income tax → households' disposable income and work incentives; corporation tax → firms' investment; VAT → prices and sales.
6. **Discuss questions**: benefits vs costs; "it depends on" the state of the economy (spare capacity or not), the size of the change, and how households respond.
:::

::: example Calculate
A government raises 620 billion in tax and spends 680 billion. State whether it has a deficit or surplus and calculate its size. Explain one consequence. (4)

Spending exceeds revenue, so a budget deficit (1) of 680 − 620 = 60 billion (1). The government must borrow the 60 billion, adding to the national debt; future taxpayers will pay interest on it, which reduces the money available for other spending (2).
:::

::: example Classify
A country has: (a) income tax at 20% on the first 30,000 and 40% above; (b) VAT at 20%; (c) a tax of 15% on all company profits. Classify each as direct or indirect and as progressive, proportional or regressive. (6)

(a) Direct; progressive — higher incomes pay a larger percentage (2). (b) Indirect; regressive — the poor spend a larger share of their income, so VAT takes a larger percentage from them (2). (c) Direct (paid by the firm on its profit); proportional — the same 15% whatever the profit (2).
:::

::: example Explain — expansionary fiscal policy
Explain how a government could use fiscal policy to reduce unemployment. (4)

It could cut income tax, raising households' disposable income so they spend more, increasing demand for goods and services; firms then need more workers (2). It could also increase its own spending — on roads, schools or hospitals — which directly employs workers and raises demand for the output of construction and supply firms, creating further jobs (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — raising income tax
Analyse the effects of a rise in income tax on the economy. (6)

Households have less disposable income, so consumer spending falls, reducing total demand; firms sell less, cut output and may lay off workers, so unemployment may rise and growth slow (2). Lower demand reduces demand-pull inflation and cuts spending on imports, improving the current account, and the extra revenue reduces the budget deficit or funds public services (2). Higher tax rates may reduce the incentive to work overtime or seek promotion and could encourage tax avoidance or emigration of skilled workers; the overall effect depends on whether the economy was overheating — if it was, the policy is stabilising; if it was in recession, it deepens the downturn (2).
:::

::: q
Define "budget surplus". (2)
---
A situation where government tax revenue exceeds government spending (1) in a given year (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a direct tax and an indirect tax, giving an example of each. (4)
---
A direct tax is levied on income or wealth and paid straight to the government by the taxpayer, e.g. income tax (2). An indirect tax is levied on spending, collected by the seller and included in the price, e.g. VAT (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why VAT is regressive. (2)
---
Poorer households spend a larger proportion of their income (1), so VAT takes a larger percentage of their income than of a rich household's, even though the rich pay more in total (1).
:::

::: q
Give three reasons why governments tax. (3)
---
To raise revenue for public spending; to redistribute income; to discourage consumption of demerit goods (or to manage demand, protect domestic industry).
:::

::: q
Explain how a rise in corporation tax might affect firms. (4)
---
Firms keep less of their profit, so they have less to reinvest in new capital and less incentive to expand (2). Multinationals may move production to countries with lower rates, reducing investment and jobs in the country (2).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by contractionary fiscal policy and when it might be used. (4)
---
Raising taxes and/or cutting government spending to reduce total demand (2). Used when inflation is rising because demand exceeds the economy's capacity, or to reduce a large budget deficit or current account deficit (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways government spending can promote economic growth. (4)
---
Spending on infrastructure — roads, ports, broadband — lowers firms' costs and raises the economy's productive capacity (2). Spending on education and training improves the quality of labour, raising productivity and output (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two limitations of using fiscal policy to end a recession. (4)
---
Time lags: it takes time to plan and pass a budget and for spending projects to start, by which time the economy may have recovered by itself (2). Households may save a tax cut rather than spend it, especially if confidence is low, so demand rises less than intended (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should always avoid a budget deficit. (8)
---
For avoiding: deficits add to the national debt, so interest payments grow and crowd out spending on services; borrowing may raise interest rates and reduce private investment; a persistent deficit can lead to loss of confidence, a falling currency and higher inflation; future taxpayers bear the burden (3). Against: in a recession a deficit is unavoidable and useful — tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises automatically, supporting demand and employment; cutting spending or raising taxes in a slump would deepen it; borrowing to invest in infrastructure and education can raise growth and future tax revenue that more than repays the debt (3). Judgement: a deficit is acceptable, even desirable, in a recession or to fund investment, but should be reduced when the economy grows so that debt stays sustainable — the aim is a balanced budget over the cycle, not every year (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Budget** (tax vs spending) is not the **balance of payments** (exports vs imports). Keep the words apart.
- Classify taxes on **both** scales: direct/indirect **and** progressive/proportional/regressive.
- Regressive means a smaller **percentage**, not a smaller amount.
- Fiscal policy answers: **direction → measure → total demand → aim → limitation.**
- Government spending on infrastructure and education has a **supply-side** effect too — mention it for growth questions.
:::

Progressive: the proportion of income paid rises as income rises, e.g. income tax with higher bands (2); regressive: the proportion falls as income rises, e.g. VAT or a duty on cigarettes (2).
:::

::: test
Explain what is meant by expansionary fiscal policy and when it would be used. (3)

Increasing government spending and/or cutting taxes (1) to raise total demand (1); used in a recession or when unemployment is high (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a cut in income tax could reduce unemployment. (4)
---
Households keep more of their income (1); consumer spending rises (1); firms sell more and produce more (1); they hire more workers to meet demand (1).
:::

::: test
Explain two reasons why a government might run a budget deficit. (4)
---
To boost demand during a recession, spending more than it collects (2); a recession itself cuts tax revenue and raises benefit spending automatically (2).
:::

::: test
Explain two disadvantages of a large national debt. (4)
---
Interest payments take a growing share of tax revenue, leaving less for services (2); future taxes may have to rise or spending fall; lenders may demand higher interest rates (2).
:::

::: test
Explain how a rise in indirect taxes could affect inflation. (2)
---
Firms pass the tax on in higher prices (1); the price level rises — cost-push inflation (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why fiscal policy may take time to work. (2)
---
Decisions must be planned, approved and implemented, e.g. building projects take years (1); households and firms take time to change their spending (1).
:::

::: test
Analyse the effects of an increase in government spending on infrastructure. (4)
---
Creates jobs in construction, raising incomes and demand (1); better roads and communications lower firms' costs and raise productivity (1); long-run growth improves (1); but it must be financed by higher taxes or borrowing, and may cause inflation if the economy is near capacity (1).
:::
