## What it is

**Market failure** is when the free market, left to itself, allocates resources **inefficiently** — producing too much of some things, too little of others, or none at all. It is the main justification for government intervention.

**The vocabulary** (all must be learned exactly):

- **Private cost**: the cost of an activity to the person or firm carrying it out (the factory's wages and materials).
- **External cost**: a cost imposed on third parties not involved in the activity (the pollution breathed by neighbours).
- **Social cost** = private cost + external cost.
- **Private benefit**: the benefit to the person consuming or firm producing (the enjoyment of a good).
- **External benefit**: a benefit to third parties (a vaccinated person protects others).
- **Social benefit** = private benefit + external benefit.
- **Externality**: an effect of an activity on third parties (an external cost or external benefit).

Markets only count **private** costs and benefits, so wherever external ones exist, the market gets the quantity wrong.

**The four types of good:**

| Type | Definition | Examples | Market outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public good** | non-rival (one person's use does not reduce another's) and non-excludable (non-payers cannot be stopped from benefiting) | street lighting, defence, lighthouses, flood defences | **not provided at all** — the free-rider problem: nobody pays if they can benefit without paying |
| **Private good** | rival and excludable | food, phones | provided by the market |
| **Merit good** | a good the government judges people under-consume because they underestimate its benefits; it also has external benefits | education, healthcare, vaccinations | **under-consumed / under-produced** |
| **Demerit good** | a good people over-consume because they underestimate its harm; it also has external costs | tobacco, alcohol, gambling, illegal drugs | **over-consumed / over-produced** |

**Causes of market failure:**

1. **Externalities** — external costs mean over-production (factories pollute because it costs them nothing); external benefits mean under-production.
2. **Public goods** — free-rider problem, so no private provision.
3. **Merit and demerit goods** — information failure: people do not know the true benefits or harms.
4. **Monopoly** — a single dominant seller restricts output and raises price above the competitive level (see 3.7).
5. **Factor immobility** — workers cannot easily change occupation or region, leaving unemployment alongside vacancies.
6. **Inequality** — market incomes leave some unable to afford necessities.

**Consequences of market failure:**

- **Over-production** of goods with external costs → pollution, congestion, ill health.
- **Under-production** of goods with external benefits → lower skills, more disease.
- **No public goods** → without government there would be no defence or street lights.
- **Higher prices and less choice** under monopoly.
- **Inefficient allocation of resources** overall — and this is what justifies the interventions in 2.10.

::: method
1. **Define with both halves.** Public good: non-rival *and* non-excludable. Merit good: under-consumed *because* benefits underestimated (and has external benefits). Demerit: over-consumed *because* harm underestimated.
2. **Social = private + external.** If numbers are given, add them. If "social cost exceeds private cost", there is an external cost and the market over-produces.
3. **Identify the third party** when explaining an externality. "Pollution from the factory is an external cost because *nearby residents* suffer ill health without being compensated."
4. **Link cause to consequence.** Externality → market ignores it → wrong quantity (over or under) → harm to society → resources misallocated.
5. **For "why does the market fail to provide X"**: public good → free-rider; merit good → information failure and external benefits ignored.
6. **Analyse and discuss** questions want the chain: what the market does → why → the effect on society → (for discuss) whether intervention would do better.
:::

::: example Define
Define "public good" and give an example. (2)

A good that is non-rival — one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others — and non-excludable — non-payers cannot be prevented from benefiting (1), such as street lighting or national defence (1).
:::

::: example Calculate
A chemical plant's private cost of production is 2 million dollars a year. Pollution from the plant costs local residents 500,000 dollars in health care and cleaning. Calculate the social cost and explain why this is a market failure. (4)

Social cost = private cost + external cost = 2,000,000 + 500,000 = 2.5 million dollars (2). The firm bases its output only on the 2 million it pays itself, ignoring the 500,000 borne by residents; so it produces more chemicals than is best for society — the market over-produces because the external cost is not included in price (2).
:::

::: example Explain — merit good
Explain why education is a merit good and why the market would under-provide it. (4)

Education is a merit good because individuals underestimate its long-term benefits — higher earnings and better health — and it has external benefits: an educated workforce raises productivity and reduces crime for everyone (2). A free market charges fees, so poorer families cannot afford it, and consumers who undervalue it buy too little; the external benefits are not paid for, so less is provided than society would want (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — external costs
Analyse the consequences of a market ignoring the external costs of car use. (6)

Drivers pay only their private costs — fuel, insurance, the car — and do not pay for the congestion, air pollution and accidents they impose on others, so the private cost of driving is below the social cost (2). Because driving seems cheaper than it really is, more journeys are made than is socially efficient: roads are over-used, pollution damages health and journey times lengthen for everyone (2). Resources are misallocated — too much land and spending go to roads and cars, too little to public transport — and third parties bear costs without compensation, which the government may try to correct with fuel taxes or congestion charges (2).
:::

::: q
Define "market failure". (2)
---
A situation where the free market allocates resources inefficiently (1), producing too much or too little of a good, or none at all (1).
:::

::: q
Define "external cost" and give an example. (2)
---
A cost imposed on third parties who are not part of the activity (1), e.g. noise from an airport suffered by residents (1).
:::

::: q
The private benefit of a vaccination is worth 80 dollars to the person; it also prevents infections worth 40 dollars to others. Calculate the social benefit and identify the external benefit. (2)
---
Social benefit = 80 + 40 = 120 dollars (1); the external benefit is the 40 dollars to others (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the free-rider problem. (2)
---
Because a public good is non-excludable, people can benefit without paying (1); so nobody pays and no firm will provide it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why tobacco is classed as a demerit good. (4)
---
Smokers underestimate the harm to their own health, so they consume more than they would with full information (2). Smoking also creates external costs — passive smoking and healthcare costs for others — which the market ignores, so it is over-consumed relative to what is best for society (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a public good and a merit good. (4)
---
A public good is non-rival and non-excludable, so the market provides none of it because of free-riders (2). A merit good can be provided by the market — it is rival and excludable, like healthcare — but is under-consumed because people undervalue its benefits and ignore its external benefits (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how monopoly can cause market failure. (4)
---
A monopolist faces no competition, so it can restrict output to push price above the competitive level (2). Consumers pay more and buy less than they would in a competitive market, and with no rivals the firm has less incentive to be efficient or innovate, so resources are misallocated (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse how external benefits lead to under-production of a good. (6)
---
When a good has external benefits, third parties gain but do not pay — for example, everyone benefits from a neighbour's well-kept garden or a worker's training (2). Consumers base demand only on their private benefit, so demand is lower than the social benefit would justify and the equilibrium quantity is below the socially optimal level (2). Society loses the extra benefit the missing output would have brought; the government may respond with subsidies or direct provision to raise consumption towards the optimum (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether the existence of external costs always means a government should intervene. (8)
---
For intervention: external costs mean the market over-produces the good, harming third parties and misallocating resources; a tax equal to the external cost or a regulation would reduce output towards the social optimum and compensate victims through the revenue raised (3). Against: measuring an external cost is difficult, so a tax may be set too high or low; intervention has costs of its own — administration, enforcement, possible job losses in the industry; and if the external cost is small the cost of intervening may exceed the benefit (3). Judgement: intervention is justified when the external cost is large and can be estimated reasonably, as with major pollution, but for minor externalities the costs of government action may outweigh the gains, so not always (2).
:::

::: watch
- Public good needs **both** non-rival *and* non-excludable. One alone is half marks.
- Merit goods are **under**-consumed; demerit goods **over**-consumed. Both involve **information failure** and **externalities** — mention both for full marks.
- **Social = private + external.** Show the addition if numbers are given.
- Name the **third party** when explaining an externality.
- Do not call healthcare a public good — it is a **merit** good (rival and excludable). The public/merit mix-up is the commonest error in this topic.
:::
