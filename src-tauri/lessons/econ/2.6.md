## What it is

The law of demand says quantity falls when price rises. **Price elasticity of demand (PED)** says *by how much*: it measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price.

$$PED = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

where a percentage change is $\dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100$.

Because price and quantity move in opposite directions, PED is **negative** — but the exam is interested in the size, so ignore the sign and read the number:

| Value (ignoring sign) | Name | Meaning | Curve |
|---|---|---|---|
| greater than 1 | **elastic** | quantity changes by a bigger % than price | flat-ish |
| less than 1 | **inelastic** | quantity changes by a smaller % than price | steep |
| exactly 1 | unit elastic | same % change | |
| 0 | perfectly inelastic | quantity does not change at all | vertical |
| infinity | perfectly elastic | any price rise loses all demand | horizontal |

**What makes demand elastic or inelastic:**

- **Substitutes** — many close substitutes → elastic (one brand of cola). Few → inelastic (petrol, tap water).
- **Necessity or luxury** — necessities → inelastic (bread, medicine); luxuries → elastic (cruises).
- **Share of income** — a small share (salt, matches) → inelastic; a large share (cars) → elastic.
- **Addictiveness / habit** — cigarettes → inelastic.
- **Time** — in the short run demand is inelastic (no time to find alternatives); it becomes more elastic over time.
- **Brand loyalty** and how narrowly the good is defined (all food is inelastic; one supermarket's bread is elastic).

**PED and revenue.** Total revenue $= P \times Q$. When price changes, revenue moves in the direction of whichever changes by the bigger percentage:

| Demand is… | Raise price → revenue | Cut price → revenue |
|---|---|---|
| **inelastic** | **rises** (quantity falls by less than price rose) | falls |
| **elastic** | falls | **rises** (quantity rises by more than price fell) |
| unit elastic | unchanged | unchanged |

Total consumer spending on the good is the same number as the firm's revenue, so the table also gives the effect on consumer spending.

**Why PED matters:**

- **Firms**: pricing decisions — cut price only if demand is elastic; raise it if inelastic.
- **Governments**: indirect taxes raise the most revenue on inelastic goods (petrol, tobacco, alcohol); a tax to *cut* consumption works poorly if demand is inelastic; subsidies for elastic goods change behaviour more.
- **Consumers**: whether a price rise will hit their spending badly (inelastic necessities) or can be avoided by switching.
- **Workers/unions**: a wage rise is safer for jobs if demand for the product is inelastic — costs can be passed on without losing many sales.

::: terms
- **Price elasticity of demand (PED)**: a measure of the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.
- **PED formula**: percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price.
- **Price elastic demand**: quantity demanded changes by a greater percentage than price (PED greater than 1, ignoring the sign).
- **Price inelastic demand**: quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than price (PED less than 1).
- **Unit elasticity**: quantity demanded changes by the same percentage as price (PED equal to 1).
- **Perfectly inelastic demand**: quantity demanded does not change at all when price changes (PED = 0).
- **Perfectly elastic demand**: any rise in price causes quantity demanded to fall to zero (PED is infinite).
- **Total revenue**: price multiplied by quantity sold — the same as total consumer spending on the product.
:::

::: method
1. **Calculate both percentage changes** from the *original* values: $\frac{\text{change}}{\text{original}} \times 100$. Quantity on top, price on the bottom.
2. **Divide** and state the value with its sign, then interpret: "PED = −0.5, which is inelastic because it is less than 1 (ignoring the sign)."
3. **Rearranging**: if PED and one percentage are given, % change in quantity $=$ PED $\times$ % change in price.
4. **Revenue**: work out revenue before and after ($P \times Q$) if numbers are given; otherwise use the rule — inelastic: revenue follows price; elastic: revenue follows quantity.
5. **Explaining elasticity of a good**: pick the two strongest determinants (usually substitutes and necessity) and apply them to *that* good.
6. **Decision-maker questions**: state what the decision-maker wants (more revenue, more tax, fewer sales of a harmful good), then say which elasticity makes that work.
:::

::: example Calculate
The price of a magazine rises from 4 to 5 and sales fall from 2,000 to 1,800 a week. Calculate the PED and say whether demand is elastic or inelastic. (4)

% change in quantity $= \frac{1800 - 2000}{2000} \times 100 = -10\%$ (1). % change in price $= \frac{5 - 4}{4} \times 100 = 25\%$ (1). PED $= \frac{-10}{25} = -0.4$ (1). Ignoring the sign, 0.4 is less than 1, so demand is inelastic (1).
:::

::: example Revenue
Using the magazine figures, calculate the change in total revenue and explain the result. (4)

Before: $4 \times 2000 = 8{,}000$. After: $5 \times 1800 = 9{,}000$ (2). Revenue rises by 1,000 because demand is inelastic: the 25% rise in price outweighs the 10% fall in sales (2).
:::

::: example Explain — why a good is inelastic
Explain two reasons why the demand for petrol is price inelastic. (4)

Petrol has few close substitutes — most drivers cannot switch to another fuel quickly — so a price rise causes only a small fall in quantity demanded (2). It is also a necessity for people who must drive to work, and the habit of driving is hard to change in the short run, so buyers keep purchasing almost as much when price rises (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — a firm's pricing decision
A cinema is considering cutting its ticket prices. Analyse how PED should affect its decision. (6)

If demand for its tickets is elastic — likely, since streaming and other cinemas are close substitutes — a price cut increases quantity demanded by a larger percentage than the price fell, so total revenue rises (2). If demand is inelastic, the extra customers would not make up for the lower price per ticket and revenue would fall, so the cinema should keep or even raise prices (2). Elasticity may differ by time: demand is more elastic for weekday showings when people have alternatives, so the cinema might cut weekday prices and hold weekend prices — and it must also consider that extra customers raise costs and buy snacks, affecting profit as well as revenue (2).
:::

::: example Explain — PED and the government
Explain why a government seeking tax revenue is likely to put indirect taxes on products with price inelastic demand. (4)

A tax raises the price of the product (1). When demand is price inelastic — petrol, cigarettes, alcohol — quantity demanded falls by a smaller percentage than the price rises (1), so most people keep buying and the tax is collected on almost the same number of units (1). On a product with elastic demand, sales would fall sharply and the tax would raise little revenue; the drawback is that taxes on inelastic necessities hit poorer households hardest (1).
:::

::: q
Define "price elasticity of demand". (2)
---
A measure of the responsiveness (1) of quantity demanded to a change in price (1).
:::

::: q
Price rises by 20% and quantity demanded falls by 50%. Calculate PED and interpret it. (3)
---
PED $= \frac{-50}{20} = -2.5$ (2); elastic, since 2.5 is greater than 1 (1).
:::

::: q
Price falls from 10 to 8 and quantity demanded rises from 500 to 550. Calculate PED. (3)
---
% change in Q $= \frac{50}{500} \times 100 = 10\%$; % change in P $= \frac{-2}{10} \times 100 = -20\%$; PED $= \frac{10}{-20} = -0.5$ (inelastic).
:::

::: q
Demand for a good has PED of −2. Price rises by 5%. Calculate the change in quantity demanded. (2)
---
$-2 \times 5\% = -10\%$: quantity demanded falls by 10%.
:::

::: q
Explain why the demand for a particular brand of trainers is likely to be more elastic than the demand for trainers in general. (4)
---
A single brand has many close substitutes — other brands — so a price rise makes buyers switch, giving a large fall in quantity (2). Trainers in general have few substitutes, so a rise in the price of all trainers causes a much smaller fall in quantity (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the effect on a firm's revenue of raising its price when demand is elastic. (4)
---
With elastic demand the percentage fall in quantity is bigger than the percentage rise in price (2). Since revenue is price multiplied by quantity, the loss of sales outweighs the higher price per unit, so total revenue falls (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why governments tend to place high taxes on goods with inelastic demand. (4)
---
When demand is inelastic, a tax that raises the price causes only a small fall in quantity sold (2), so the government collects the tax on almost as many units as before and total tax revenue is high; with elastic demand sales would collapse and little tax would be raised (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how the time period affects the PED of a good. (4)
---
In the short run consumers have little time to find substitutes or change habits, so demand is inelastic (2). Over time they can switch — buying a smaller car after petrol prices rise — so demand becomes more elastic in the long run (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should raise the tax on cigarettes to reduce smoking. (8)
---
For: a higher tax raises the price, and even with inelastic demand some smokers cut down or quit — especially young people and those on low incomes, who are more price-sensitive — so consumption falls and health improves; the tax also raises considerable revenue that can fund healthcare (4). Against: because cigarettes are addictive, demand is highly inelastic, so most smokers keep buying and the fall in consumption is small; the tax is regressive, taking a larger share of poor smokers' income, and very high taxes encourage smuggling and illegal sales which raise no revenue (3). Overall the tax reduces smoking only modestly on its own and works best alongside education campaigns and bans on advertising; its main certain effect is revenue rather than a large fall in smoking (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Percentage changes from the original values**, quantity on top. Then divide — do not multiply.
- Keep the **negative sign** in the working but interpret the **size**: bigger than 1 elastic, smaller than 1 inelastic.
- **Inelastic: revenue follows price.** Elastic: revenue follows quantity. Learn both lines.
- Steep does not automatically mean inelastic on every point, but at IGCSE: **steep = inelastic, flat = elastic** is accepted.
- Explain elasticity with determinants **applied to the good** — "few substitutes because…", not just the list.
:::

::: test
The price of a concert ticket falls from 50 dollars to 40 dollars and sales rise from 2,000 to 2,600. Calculate the PED and explain what happens to total revenue. (4)
---
Percentage change in price = −10 ÷ 50 × 100 = −20%; percentage change in quantity = 600 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = +30% (1). PED = 30 ÷ −20 = −1.5, so demand is price elastic (1). Revenue before = 50 × 2,000 = 100,000 dollars; after = 40 × 2,600 = 104,000 dollars (1), so revenue rises by 4,000 dollars, as expected when price falls and demand is elastic (1).
:::

::: test
Analyse why the demand for cigarettes is price inelastic and what this means for a government that taxes them. (6)
---
Cigarettes are addictive, have no close substitutes and are a habit, so smokers keep buying when the price rises — demand is price inelastic (2). A tax raises the price, but quantity demanded falls by a smaller percentage, so most smokers continue to buy and pay the tax (2). This makes the tax a reliable source of revenue for the government, but a less effective way of reducing smoking; it also takes a larger share of income from poorer smokers (2).
Marking: up to 6 marks for a developed, logical chain of analysis; 1–2 for simple points without explanation.
:::

::: test
Discuss whether a bus company should raise its fares to increase its revenue. (8)
---
Raise: if demand for bus travel is price inelastic — commuters and those without cars have few alternatives — a fare rise increases revenue, since passenger numbers fall by a smaller percentage (4).
Do not raise: if demand is elastic — people can walk, cycle, drive or car-share — passengers fall by a larger percentage and revenue falls; fewer passengers may lead to route cuts, more congestion and loss of goodwill; demand may be more elastic in the long run (4).
Conclusion: depends on the PED of its passengers, which may differ by route and time of day; the company could raise peak fares (inelastic) and cut off-peak fares (elastic).
Marking: up to 4 marks for developed analysis of both sides; up to 4 for evaluation — weighing the arguments and reaching a justified conclusion.
:::
