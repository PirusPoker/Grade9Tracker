## What it is

A **market** is any arrangement that brings **buyers and sellers** together so they can exchange a good or service. It does not have to be a place: a fruit stall is a market, and so is a website selling second-hand phones, the foreign exchange market where currencies are traded, and the labour market where workers sell their time to employers.

| Market | Buyers | Sellers | What is exchanged |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product market (e.g. cars) | consumers | firms | goods and services |
| Labour market | firms | workers | labour, for wages |
| Housing market | households | builders and owners | houses |
| Foreign exchange market | importers, tourists, investors | exporters, banks | currencies |
| Stock market | investors | firms and other investors | shares |

In a market, buyers form **demand** and sellers form **supply**. The two meet at a **price**. That price does three jobs — it is the **price mechanism**:

1. **Signals**: a rising price tells producers that consumers want more of this good.
2. **Incentivises**: higher prices raise profits, so firms produce more; lower prices push them to produce less.
3. **Rations**: when a good is scarce, its price rises until only those willing and able to pay get it.

Through these three jobs the market economy answers the three questions from 1.1 — *what* to produce (what sells at a profit), *how* (the cheapest method), *for whom* (those who can pay). This is what is meant by markets **allocating resources**: moving land, labour and capital towards the goods people want and away from those they do not.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Market**: an arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to exchange goods and services.
- **Demand**: the willingness and ability of consumers to buy a product at a given price.
- **Supply**: the willingness and ability of producers to sell a product at a given price.
- **Price mechanism**: the way prices in a market signal, incentivise and ration to allocate resources.

::: method
1. **Define "market" with both halves**: buyers and sellers *and* the purpose (to exchange). Then give an example that fits the question — a physical market and an online one both count.
2. **Identify the buyers and sellers** for any market you are given. In the labour market, note that the roles reverse: firms buy, households sell.
3. **Explain allocation with the three jobs of price.** Signal → incentive → ration. Use a change (demand for electric cars rises) and trace it through: price up → profit up → more resources move into electric cars.
4. **Remember "willing and able"** in demand and supply definitions. Wanting a Ferrari is not demand unless you can pay for it.
:::

::: example Define
Define "market". (2)

A market is any arrangement — a place, a website or a network — that brings together buyers and sellers (1) so that they can exchange goods or services (1).
:::

::: example Identify
Identify the buyers and sellers in the market for accountants. (2)

Buyers are the firms that hire accountants (1); sellers are the accountants themselves, offering their labour for wages (1).
:::

::: example Explain — the price mechanism
Explain how the price mechanism allocates resources when the demand for a good rises. (4)

Higher demand raises the price, which signals to firms that consumers want more of the good (1). The higher price increases profits, giving firms an incentive to expand output and attracting new firms into the market (2). Land, labour and capital are therefore moved into producing this good and away from others — resources have been reallocated without any central direction (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a fall in demand
Analyse how a fall in demand for newspapers affects the allocation of resources. (6)

Lower demand causes the price of newspapers (and advertising space) to fall, signalling to producers that consumers want fewer newspapers (2). Profits fall, so firms cut output, close titles and make journalists and printers redundant; some firms leave the industry altogether (2). The labour and capital released move into industries where prices and profits are higher — for example online news — so resources are reallocated towards what consumers now want (2).
:::

::: q
Give two examples of markets that are not physical places. (2)
---
An online marketplace such as an auction website; the foreign exchange market (or the stock market).
:::

::: q
Define "demand". (2)
---
The willingness (1) and ability (1) of consumers to buy a product at a given price.
:::

::: q
Identify the buyers and sellers in the housing market. (2)
---
Buyers are households wanting a home (1); sellers are property developers and existing owners (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the three functions of price in a market. (3)
---
Signalling — price changes tell producers and consumers about scarcity; incentive — higher prices encourage more production; rationing — price rises until demand matches the limited supply.
:::

::: q
Explain why wanting a product is not the same as demanding it. (2)
---
Demand requires both willingness and *ability* to pay (1); wanting something you cannot afford does not create demand in the market (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the price mechanism answers the question "for whom to produce". (4)
---
Goods go to those willing and able to pay the market price (1). When a good is scarce, its price rises and rations it to those who value it most and can afford it (2); those with higher incomes can therefore buy more (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how the price mechanism would respond to a shortage of a good. (6)
---
A shortage means demand exceeds supply at the current price, so buyers compete and the price rises (2). The higher price rations the good — some buyers drop out — and signals to producers that more is wanted (2). Higher profits give existing firms an incentive to raise output and attract new firms, so supply rises until the shortage disappears (2).
:::

::: watch
- A market is an **arrangement**, not necessarily a place. Say "brings buyers and sellers together to exchange".
- In the **labour market**, firms are the buyers and workers the sellers.
- Demand and supply are both "**willing and able**" at a given price.
- The three functions of price — **signal, incentive, ration** — are the backbone of any "how do markets allocate resources" answer.
:::
