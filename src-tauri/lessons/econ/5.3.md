## What it is

A country's **population** changes through three things:

- **Birth rate**: the number of live births per 1,000 people per year.
- **Death rate**: the number of deaths per 1,000 people per year.
- **Net migration**: immigration (people moving in) minus emigration (people moving out).

**Natural increase** $=$ birth rate $-$ death rate. **Population growth** $=$ natural increase $+$ net migration. A birth rate of 20 and a death rate of 8 per 1,000 gives natural increase of 12 per 1,000, i.e. 1.2% a year.

**Why birth rates vary:**

- **Living standards and income** — richer countries have lower birth rates: children are costly, not workers.
- **Education, especially of women** — later marriage, careers, knowledge of contraception.
- **Availability and acceptance of contraception**; religion and culture.
- **Infant mortality** — where many children die, families have more.
- **Pensions and welfare** — where the state supports the old, children are not needed as security.
- **Government policy** — China's one-child policy; pro-natal incentives in some European countries.
- **Age structure** — a young population has more people of child-bearing age.

**Why death rates vary:**

- **Healthcare** — doctors, hospitals, vaccination.
- **Nutrition, clean water and sanitation.**
- **Living and working conditions**, housing, safety.
- **Wars, famine, natural disasters, epidemics.**
- **Age structure** — an old population has a *higher* death rate even with excellent healthcare (Japan's death rate exceeds Nigeria's).
- **Lifestyle** — smoking, diet, exercise.

**Why people migrate:** *push* factors — unemployment, low wages, war, persecution, disaster; *pull* factors — jobs, higher wages, better public services, safety, family, education. Barriers: immigration laws, cost, language.

**Optimum population** — the size that, with the country's resources and technology, gives the **highest output (GDP) per head**. Below it the country is **under-populated** (resources under-used; Canada, Australia); above it **over-populated** (too many people for the resources; output per head would rise if population fell). "Over-population" is about the ratio of people to resources, not absolute numbers — the Netherlands is dense but not over-populated.

**Effects of population size changing:**

- *Growth*: a larger workforce and market → more output and economies of scale; but pressure on housing, schools, hospitals, food and the environment; unemployment if jobs do not grow; if growth is from births, the **dependency ratio** rises at first (more children per worker).
- *Decline*: a shrinking workforce and market; falling demand; but less pressure on resources.

**Effects of the age structure changing:**

- **An ageing population** (falling birth rate, rising life expectancy — Japan, Italy, Germany): more retired people per worker → higher **dependency ratio** → more spending on pensions and healthcare, higher taxes on a smaller workforce; labour shortages; slower growth; changing demand (care homes, not schools); but older workers are experienced, and firms serving the elderly grow.
- **A young population** (high birth rate — much of Africa): high dependency ratio of children; spending needed on schools; later a large workforce if jobs and education are provided (a "demographic dividend"), or high youth unemployment if not.
- **Gender balance** and **geographical distribution** (urbanisation) also matter.

::: terms
- **Birth rate**: the number of live births per 1,000 of the population per year.
- **Death rate**: the number of deaths per 1,000 of the population per year.
- **Natural increase**: birth rate minus death rate.
- **Immigration**: people coming into a country to live.
- **Emigration**: people leaving a country to live elsewhere.
- **Net migration**: the difference between immigration and emigration.
- **Optimum population**: the population size that makes the best use of a country's resources, giving the highest output per head.
- **Under-population**: too few people to make the best use of a country's resources.
- **Over-population**: too many people for a country's resources, so output per head is lower than it could be.
- **Ageing population**: a rise in the average age of the population, with a growing share of older people.
- **Dependency ratio**: the number of people not of working age (children and the retired) relative to the working population.
:::

::: method
1. **Rates are per 1,000.** Natural increase = birth rate − death rate; add net migration for total growth; divide by 10 to get a percentage.
2. **Causes of birth/death rate differences**: give the factor and the mechanism ("female education → later marriage and smaller families").
3. **Age structure trap**: a *high* death rate can mean an *old* population, not bad healthcare. Check the context.
4. **Optimum population** is about **output per head**, not the number of people. Over-populated = more people would lower output per head.
5. **Ageing population effects**: dependency ratio → pensions and health spending → taxes; workforce shrinks → growth; demand pattern changes. Then a positive or two for balance.
6. **Migration**: for the receiving country — bigger workforce, skills, fills vacancies, demand; but pressure on services and possibly lower wages for the low-skilled. For the sending country — remittances but "brain drain".
:::

::: example Calculate
A country of 50 million has a birth rate of 18 per 1,000, a death rate of 7 per 1,000 and net migration of +3 per 1,000. Calculate the rate of natural increase and the total population growth in people. (4)

Natural increase $= 18 - 7 = 11$ per 1,000, i.e. 1.1% (2). Total growth $= 11 + 3 = 14$ per 1,000 $= 1.4\%$ of 50 million $= 700{,}000$ people (2).
:::

::: example Explain — birth rate
Explain two reasons why birth rates are lower in developed countries. (4)

Women are more educated and have careers, so they marry later and choose smaller families, and contraception is widely available and accepted (2). Children are expensive rather than a source of income or old-age support, since the state provides pensions and schooling is compulsory, so families choose to have fewer (2).
:::

::: example Explain — death rate
Explain why a developed country might have a higher death rate than a developing country. (4)

The death rate is deaths per 1,000 of the whole population, so it depends on age structure as well as health (1). A developed country with a low birth rate and high life expectancy has a large proportion of elderly people, who die in greater numbers each year (2). A developing country with a young population has few old people, so its death rate can be lower despite poorer healthcare (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — an ageing population
Analyse the effects on an economy of an ageing population. (6)

With more retired people per worker the dependency ratio rises, so spending on state pensions and healthcare increases while the number of taxpayers falls; taxes must rise or other spending be cut (2). A shrinking workforce causes labour shortages and slower growth, and firms may have to raise wages or invest in automation; the retirement age may be raised or immigration encouraged (2). Demand shifts towards healthcare, care homes and leisure for the elderly and away from schools and children's goods, so some industries grow while others shrink; older workers also bring experience, so the effects are not all negative (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — immigration
Discuss whether an increase in immigration benefits an economy. (8)

Benefits: immigrants enlarge the workforce, filling vacancies and skill shortages — doctors, builders, fruit pickers — raising output and growth; they are often young, lowering the dependency ratio and paying taxes that fund pensions; they bring enterprise and new ideas and increase demand for goods and housing (4). Costs: pressure on housing, schools and hospitals in the short run; competition may lower wages for low-skilled domestic workers or raise unemployment among them if jobs are scarce; and social tension may arise (3). Judgement: immigration of working-age, skilled people into an economy with vacancies is a clear gain; the effects depend on the immigrants' skills, the state of the labour market and whether public services expand to meet the larger population (1).
:::

::: example Explain — optimum population
Explain why a country with a small population and large natural resources might be under-populated. (4)

The optimum population is the size that gives the highest output per head with the country's resources (1). If there are too few workers, much of the land, minerals and capital cannot be used (1), and firms cannot achieve economies of scale because the domestic market is small (1); so a larger population — through immigration, for example — could raise output per head (1).
:::

::: q
Define "birth rate". (2)
---
The number of live births (1) per 1,000 of the population per year (1).
:::

::: q
Birth rate 12 per 1,000, death rate 10 per 1,000, net migration −4 per 1,000. Calculate the population growth rate. (2)
---
$12 - 10 - 4 = -2$ per 1,000: the population falls by 0.2% a year.
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why death rates have fallen in developing countries. (4)
---
Vaccination and better healthcare have cut deaths from disease, especially among infants (2). Clean water, sanitation and better nutrition have reduced deaths from waterborne illness and hunger (2).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by an "over-populated" country. (2)
---
A country whose population is above its optimum (1), so that output per head would rise if the population were smaller relative to its resources (1).
:::

::: q
Give two push and two pull factors for migration. (4)
---
Push: unemployment, war (or persecution, disaster, low wages). Pull: higher wages, better public services (or safety, family, education).
:::

::: q
Explain how a high birth rate affects a country's dependency ratio and government spending. (4)
---
More children per worker raises the dependency ratio (1). The government must spend more on schools, child health and family support (2), funded from a workforce that is small relative to the population (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit and one cost to a developing country of emigration of its workers. (4)
---
Benefit: emigrants send remittances home, raising incomes and foreign currency, and unemployment falls (2). Cost: a "brain drain" of doctors, engineers and the educated reduces skills and the return on the country's education spending (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two effects of population growth on an economy. (4)
---
A larger workforce and market can raise output and allow economies of scale (2). But pressure on housing, food, public services and the environment rises, and unemployment may increase if jobs do not grow as fast (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether a government should try to reduce its country's birth rate. (8)
---
For: in an over-populated country a lower birth rate cuts the child dependency ratio, reduces pressure on schools, food and housing, raises output per head and frees women to work, increasing the labour force; China's policy accompanied rapid growth (3). Against: in the long run a low birth rate causes an ageing population with a high old-age dependency ratio, labour shortages and heavy pension costs; coercive policies breach individual freedom and can distort the gender balance; and a young population is a future workforce if educated and employed (3). Judgement: reducing a very high birth rate through education, healthcare and voluntary family planning benefits a poor, fast-growing country, but the aim should be a moderate, stable rate rather than the lowest possible (2).
:::

::: watch
- Rates are **per 1,000**; convert to a percentage by dividing by 10.
- Population growth = **natural increase + net migration**. Do not forget migration.
- A high death rate may reflect an **old age structure**, not poor healthcare.
- Optimum population is about **output per head**, not size or density.
- Ageing population: **dependency ratio → pensions and healthcare → taxes → workforce → growth**, plus a positive for balance.
:::
