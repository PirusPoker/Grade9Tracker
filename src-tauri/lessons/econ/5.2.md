## What it is

**Poverty** is examined in two forms:

- **Absolute poverty**: living below the minimum income needed to meet basic needs — food, clean water, shelter, clothing, basic healthcare. The World Bank line is about 2.15 US dollars a day (at purchasing power). It is measured by the number or percentage of people below that line. Absolute poverty can, in principle, be eliminated by growth.
- **Relative poverty**: being poor *compared with others in the same society* — usually defined as a household income below 60% of the country's median income. Someone in relative poverty in a rich country may have far more than a person above the absolute line in a poor one. Relative poverty is about inequality, so it persists even as a country gets richer unless the distribution changes.

**Causes of poverty:**

- **Unemployment** — no wage; benefits (where they exist) are below wages.
- **Low pay** — unskilled, part-time or informal work; no minimum wage.
- **Lack of education and skills** — low productivity, low wages, and it passes to the next generation.
- **Poor health and disability** — cannot work, or medical costs eat income.
- **Old age** — no or low pension.
- **Large families / single-parent households** — income spread thinly; childcare limits work.
- **Discrimination** by gender, ethnicity, caste.
- **Location** — rural areas, regions of industrial decline, slums.
- **Debt**, high inflation eroding fixed incomes, natural disasters, war and corruption.
- **Lack of assets** — no land or capital to earn rent, interest or profit.

**Policies to reduce poverty and redistribute income:**

| Policy | How it helps | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive taxation** | the rich pay a larger share; funds benefits and services | may reduce incentives to work and invest; tax avoidance |
| **Welfare benefits / transfer payments** | unemployment benefit, pensions, child benefit, food subsidies raise the incomes of the poor directly | cost; may reduce the incentive to work; must be targeted |
| **National minimum wage** | raises pay of the lowest earners | may cost jobs if set too high |
| **Free or subsidised education and healthcare** | raises skills and health, so earning power rises — attacks the *causes* | costly; takes years to work |
| **Job creation / economic growth** | more jobs and higher wages; the most powerful long-run cure for absolute poverty | growth may bypass the poorest; environmental cost |
| **Training and job-search help** | move people into work | cost; jobs must exist |
| **Subsidised housing, food, fuel** | reduce the cost of essentials | cost; can benefit the non-poor too |
| **Regional policy** | attract firms to poor areas | firms may leave when grants end |
| **Microfinance, land reform** (developing countries) | give the poor assets to earn from | small scale |
| **Foreign aid and debt relief** | resources for health, education, infrastructure | can create dependence; corruption |

Redistribution means taking from the richer (progressive taxes) and giving to the poorer (benefits, free services) — it reduces *relative* poverty; growth and jobs reduce *absolute* poverty.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Absolute poverty**: a situation where income is too low to meet basic needs such as food, shelter and clothing.
- **Relative poverty**: a situation where income is low compared with others in the same society, usually below 60% of median income.
- **Transfer payments**: payments by the government, such as benefits and pensions, made without any good or service in return.
- **Progressive tax**: a tax that takes a larger percentage of income as income rises.

::: method
1. **Define both types** with their key phrase: absolute → *basic needs*; relative → *compared with others / below 60% of median*.
2. **Distinguish** in explanations: growth can end absolute poverty; relative poverty needs redistribution.
3. **Causes**: pick those relevant to the country in the question — unemployment and low pay for a developed country; lack of education, health, assets and rural location for a developing one.
4. **Policies**: name it, give the chain to higher income for the poor, then the drawback. Distinguish policies that *treat symptoms* (benefits) from those that *tackle causes* (education, jobs).
5. **Discuss questions**: usually "is growth enough?" or "benefits vs education" — argue both, then judge by time horizon and type of poverty.
:::

::: example Define
Define "relative poverty". (2)

A situation in which a person's income is low compared with the rest of the population (1), usually defined as below 60% of median income, so they cannot afford the normal living standard of their society (1).
:::

::: example Explain — absolute vs relative
Explain why relative poverty can rise even when absolute poverty is falling. (4)

Absolute poverty falls when growth raises the incomes of the poorest above the basic-needs line (1). Relative poverty depends on the gap between the poor and the rest: if the incomes of the rich rise faster than those of the poor, more people fall below 60% of the median even though everyone is better off in absolute terms (2). So rapid but unequal growth reduces absolute poverty while increasing relative poverty (1).
:::

::: example Explain — causes
Explain two causes of poverty in a developed country. (4)

Unemployment: without a wage, a household must rely on benefits that are usually well below average earnings, pushing it below the relative poverty line (2). Low pay: unskilled or part-time workers earn too little to reach a normal living standard, especially in high-cost cities, and lack the qualifications to move to better jobs (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — education as a policy
Analyse how increased spending on education could reduce poverty. (6)

Education raises skills, so workers are more productive and can earn higher wages in better jobs rather than being trapped in low-paid or informal work (2). Educated parents earn more and value their children's schooling, breaking the cycle in which poverty passes from one generation to the next; they also have better health knowledge, reducing illness that keeps people out of work (2). The effect takes many years to appear and depends on jobs being available for the newly skilled; in the short run benefits or a minimum wage are still needed, so education is a long-term solution rather than an immediate one (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — benefits
Discuss whether increasing welfare benefits is the best way to reduce poverty. (8)

For: benefits raise the incomes of the poor immediately and directly, lifting families above the poverty line; they support those who cannot work — the old, sick and disabled — and the extra spending boosts demand and jobs locally (3). Against: benefits are expensive and require higher taxes that may reduce incentives; generous benefits can reduce the incentive to take low-paid work, creating dependency; they treat the symptom rather than the cause, so poverty returns if they are cut; and in developing countries governments may lack the revenue and administration to pay them (3). Judgement: benefits are essential for those unable to work and as a short-term safety net, but reducing poverty permanently needs education, healthcare and job creation; the best approach combines targeted benefits with policies that raise earning power (2).
:::

::: q
Define "absolute poverty". (2)
---
A situation where income is too low (1) to afford the basic needs of life — food, water, shelter, clothing (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why absolute poverty is more common in developing countries. (2)
---
Average incomes are far lower and many people work in subsistence farming or the informal economy (1), so a large share cannot afford basic needs (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a minimum wage might reduce poverty, and one limitation. (4)
---
It raises the pay of the lowest-paid workers, lifting working families above the poverty line (2). But if set above what firms will pay, some workers lose their jobs, and it does not help those without work (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how progressive taxation redistributes income. (4)
---
Higher earners pay a larger percentage of their income in tax, reducing the gap in after-tax income (2). The revenue funds benefits and free services used mostly by the poor, raising their real income (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two causes of poverty in a developing country. (4)
---
Lack of education means low skills and low productivity, so wages are low and many work informally (2). Poor healthcare means illness prevents work and medical costs consume income (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why poverty can pass from one generation to the next. (4)
---
Poor families cannot afford education or good nutrition for their children, and children may work instead of studying (2). The children grow up with low skills and poor health, so they earn low incomes and their own children face the same situation (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how economic growth can reduce absolute poverty. (4)
---
Growth creates jobs and raises wages, giving the poor more income (2). It also raises tax revenue, letting the government spend more on health, education and benefits (2).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether economic growth is sufficient to end poverty in a country. (8)
---
For: growth raises incomes and creates jobs, which is how most countries have cut absolute poverty; more tax revenue funds schools, clinics and benefits; a growing economy makes redistribution easier because nobody has to be made worse off (3). Against: the gains may go mainly to those who already own capital, land or skills, leaving the poorest behind — relative poverty can rise; growth may be in capital-intensive industries creating few jobs; people who cannot work — the old, sick, disabled — gain nothing without transfers; and growth can bring inflation that hits the poor hardest (3). Judgement: growth is necessary for ending absolute poverty but not sufficient; it must be combined with redistribution and investment in education and health so the poor can share in it (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Absolute** = cannot meet basic needs; **relative** = below 60% of median. Give the key phrase for each.
- Growth attacks **absolute** poverty; redistribution attacks **relative** poverty. Use this in every discuss.
- Policies: **chain plus drawback**; separate symptom-treating (benefits) from cause-treating (education, jobs).
- Match causes to the **type of country** in the question.
- Progressive tax is about the **percentage** rising with income, not the amount.
:::
