## What it is

Fiscal and monetary policy work on **demand**. **Supply-side policy** works on the economy's **capacity to produce** — it aims to increase the quantity and quality of the factors of production and make markets work better, so that the economy can produce more *without* inflation. On a PPC it is an outward shift; on an aggregate view it is more output at every price level.

**Supply-side measures** — learn a list with a mechanism for each:

| Measure | How it raises capacity |
|---|---|
| **Education and training** | better skills → higher productivity → more output per worker; fewer structurally unemployed |
| **Cutting income tax** | greater reward for working → more people join the workforce, work longer hours, seek promotion |
| **Cutting corporation tax** | firms keep more profit → more investment and enterprise; attracts multinationals |
| **Cutting welfare benefits / tightening rules** | stronger incentive for the unemployed to take jobs (harsh, but on the list) |
| **Privatisation** | selling state firms to the private sector → profit motive and competition → efficiency |
| **Deregulation** | removing rules that raise firms' costs or block new entrants → more competition, lower costs |
| **Competition policy** | breaking up monopolies → lower prices, more innovation |
| **Trade union reform** | limits on strikes → fewer days lost, more flexible wages |
| **Infrastructure spending** | roads, rail, ports, broadband → lower transport and communication costs, higher productivity |
| **Subsidies and grants for research, new firms, investment** | more innovation and capital |
| **Improving labour mobility** | job centres, retraining, help with moving → vacancies filled faster |
| **Immigration of skilled workers** | more and better labour |

**How supply-side policy serves the aims** — it is the one policy that can help *all* of them at once, which is its great attraction:

- **Growth**: more and better factors → higher productive capacity → sustainable long-run growth.
- **Employment**: training and mobility reduce structural unemployment; lower taxes and benefit reform raise the incentive to work; more competitive firms create jobs.
- **Inflation**: higher productivity lowers costs per unit; more capacity means demand can rise without shortages → less demand-pull *and* cost-push pressure.
- **Balance of payments**: more productive, lower-cost firms are more competitive abroad → exports rise, imports are replaced.

**Limitations**: **time** — education and infrastructure take years to affect output; **cost** — spending on training and roads needs tax revenue or borrowing; **uncertainty** — tax cuts may not change behaviour much; **side effects** — cutting benefits increases poverty and inequality, deregulation can harm workers' safety or the environment, privatised monopolies may raise prices; and supply-side policy does nothing for a recession caused by *lack of demand* — extra capacity is useless if nobody is buying.

Key definitions to learn word for word:

- **Supply-side policy**: government measures designed to increase the productive capacity of the economy by improving the quantity and quality of the factors of production and the efficiency of markets.
- **Productive capacity**: the maximum output an economy can produce with its resources.
- **Privatisation**: the transfer of ownership of a firm or industry from the public sector to the private sector.
- **Deregulation**: the removal of government rules and controls on firms.

::: method
1. **Define supply-side policy through capacity**: "increase the productive capacity / potential output of the economy". Contrast with demand-side (fiscal, monetary).
2. **For each measure, give the mechanism**: measure → which factor improves or which incentive changes → productivity or capacity rises → output.
3. **Link to the aim asked**: growth (capacity), employment (skills, incentives), inflation (costs, capacity), balance of payments (competitiveness). One chain per aim.
4. **Evaluate**: time lags, cost, uncertain effect on behaviour, side effects on equality or the environment, useless against a demand shortfall.
5. **Compare with demand-side policies** when asked which is better: demand-side is quick but risks inflation; supply-side is slow but raises the long-run limit.
:::

::: example Define
Define "supply-side policy". (2)

Government measures that aim to increase the productive capacity of the economy (1) by improving the quantity and quality of factors of production and the efficiency of markets (1).
:::

::: example Explain — two measures
Explain two supply-side measures a government could use to reduce unemployment. (4)

Increasing spending on training gives unemployed workers the skills firms are looking for, so structural unemployment falls as they fill vacancies in growing industries (2). Cutting income tax raises the take-home pay from working, increasing the incentive for the unemployed to accept jobs and for others to join the labour force (2).
:::

::: example Explain — inflation
Explain how supply-side policy can help to reduce inflation. (4)

Measures such as training and investment in infrastructure raise productivity, so firms' cost per unit falls and there is less pressure to raise prices — reducing cost-push inflation (2). By raising the economy's capacity, supply-side policy lets demand grow without shortages of goods or workers, so demand-pull inflation is also reduced (2).
:::

::: example Discuss — privatisation
Discuss whether privatising a state-owned electricity company would benefit the economy. (8)

For: private owners seek profit, so they cut waste and costs and invest in new technology; if competition is introduced prices may fall and service improve; the government gains revenue from the sale and stops subsidising losses, freeing money for other spending (3). Against: electricity supply is a natural monopoly, so the private firm may simply raise prices and cut unprofitable rural services; profit-seeking may reduce safety and long-term investment; jobs may be cut; and the government loses future profits and control of a strategic industry (3). Judgement: privatisation raises efficiency where real competition can be created, but for a natural monopoly the gains depend on strong regulation of prices and standards; without it consumers may lose (2).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between supply-side policy and fiscal policy. (4)
---
Supply-side policy aims to raise the economy's productive capacity — its ability to produce — through measures like training and deregulation (2). Fiscal policy uses tax and government spending mainly to change the level of demand in the economy (2).
:::

::: q
State three supply-side measures. (3)
---
Education and training; cutting income or corporation tax; privatisation (or deregulation, infrastructure spending, trade union reform).
:::

::: q
Explain how cutting corporation tax could increase economic growth. (4)
---
Firms keep more of their profits, so they have more funds and a bigger incentive to invest in new capital (2). Investment raises the quantity and quality of capital, increasing productivity and the economy's capacity, so output grows (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how better infrastructure raises productive capacity. (4)
---
Better roads, ports and broadband reduce the time and cost of moving goods and information, lowering firms' costs (2). Resources are used more efficiently and new areas become viable for business, so more output can be produced from the same factors (2).
:::

::: q
Explain how supply-side policy could improve the current account of the balance of payments. (4)
---
Higher productivity and lower unit costs make domestic goods cheaper and better quality, so exports rise (2). Domestic products can also compete with imports, so import spending falls; the current account improves (2).
:::

::: q
Explain two disadvantages of supply-side policies. (4)
---
They take a long time — education and infrastructure affect output only after years (2). They can be expensive or unfair: training and roads cost tax revenue, and cutting benefits to boost work incentives increases poverty (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why supply-side policy may not help an economy in a recession. (2)
---
A recession is caused by a lack of demand (1); increasing capacity does nothing if firms cannot sell what they already produce (1).
:::

::: q
Discuss whether supply-side policies are more effective than demand-side policies in achieving economic growth. (8)
---
Supply-side: they raise the economy's capacity, so growth can continue in the long run without inflation and with a stronger current account; training and investment raise productivity permanently; they can address several aims at once (3). Demand-side: fiscal and monetary policies act quickly and are essential when the economy has spare capacity — cutting interest rates or taxes puts idle workers and machines back to work within months, which supply-side measures cannot do; but near full capacity they cause inflation and imports rather than growth (3). Judgement: the two are complements — demand-side policy for short-run recovery, supply-side for long-run growth; supply-side is more effective for sustained growth but is slow and costly, so the best choice depends on whether the problem is too little demand or too little capacity (2).
:::

::: watch
- Supply-side is about **capacity** — the PPC shifting out — not about spending more.
- Each measure needs its **mechanism**; a list of measures with no explanation earns identification marks only.
- Supply-side can help **all four aims**; say so, with one chain each.
- The standard limitations: **time, cost, uncertain incentives, side effects, no help against a demand slump.**
- **Privatisation** and **deregulation** are supply-side; **cutting interest rates** is monetary, not supply-side.
:::
