## What it is

**Quality** means a product or service that meets — or beats — what the customer expects. It is not the same as "expensive": a budget airline delivers quality if flights are on time and the price is low as promised.

**What customers expect:**

- **Goods** — fit for purpose, reliable and durable, safe, well designed and finished, consistent, good value, as described.
- **Services** — done correctly, on time, by friendly, knowledgeable staff; easy to contact; problems fixed quickly; a clean, pleasant environment.

**Spotting and measuring quality problems**: **inspection** of finished goods or samples; **customer complaints, returns and reviews**; **customer surveys and mystery shoppers**; counting **defect rates**, **rework** and **scrap**; warranty claims; staff reporting faults; comparing with competitors (benchmarking).

**What poor quality costs** — the "cost of getting it wrong":

- **Scrap and rework** — materials and labour wasted on faulty items.
- **Refunds, replacements and repairs** under consumer law.
- **Product recalls** — hugely expensive and public.
- **Lost customers** and **damaged reputation** — bad reviews and word of mouth spread fast; customers switch to rivals.
- **Compensation and legal costs** if a faulty product causes harm.
- **Lower prices** — a business known for poor quality cannot charge a premium.
- **Demotivated staff** working with faulty processes.

**Total quality management (TQM)** — an approach in which **every employee** is responsible for quality at **every stage**, aiming to get things **"right first time"** rather than inspecting faults out at the end. Features: each worker checks their own work; the next stage is treated as an internal customer; continuous improvement (kaizen) with staff suggestions; quality circles (groups meeting to solve problems); a culture where quality comes first. *Advantages*: fewer defects, so less waste and rework; lower costs of inspection; higher, more consistent quality and customer satisfaction; motivated staff who take ownership; supports a premium reputation. *Disadvantages*: training costs; takes time to change culture; needs everyone's commitment — resistance from staff or managers undermines it; can slow production initially.

**Costs of maintaining quality** — training, better materials, inspection and testing, quality systems, more time per unit, possibly slower output. **Benefits** — higher **sales** from satisfied, loyal customers and word of mouth; a stronger **reputation** and **brand**; ability to charge a **higher price**; **lower costs** from less waste and fewer returns; **avoiding recalls**, compensation and legal action; easier to attract retailers and staff. In most cases the cost of quality is lower than the cost of getting it wrong.

**Quality and growth**: as a business grows — more sites, franchisees, outsourced production — keeping quality consistent gets harder, because the owner can no longer see everything. Standard procedures, training, monitoring and TQM become essential; a single poor franchise damages the whole brand.

Key terms to learn:

- **Quality**: meeting customers' expectations of a product or service.
- **Quality control**: checking products, usually by inspection at the end of production, to find faults.
- **Total quality management (TQM)**: an approach where all employees are responsible for quality throughout the production process, aiming to get it right first time.
- **Product recall**: withdrawing a faulty or unsafe product from customers and shops.
- **Rework**: correcting faulty products so they can be sold.

::: method
1. **Define quality through customer expectations**, then say what those expectations are for the business in the case (a service vs a good).
2. **Costs of poor quality**: pick two or three from the list and chain them to profit and reputation for the case.
3. **TQM answers**: explain "right first time / everyone responsible", then an advantage and a drawback applied to the business.
4. **Weighing quality**: cost now (training, materials, time) against benefit later (sales, price, reputation, avoided recalls). Conclude that for most businesses the benefit wins — but note when it might not (a discount business whose customers only care about price).
5. **Growth link**: when a case involves expansion, franchising or outsourcing, raise the quality-control problem and how to solve it.
:::

::: example Explain — what customers expect
Explain what customers might expect from a quality hairdressing service. (2)

A good haircut done skilfully as requested (1), by friendly staff who are on time and in a clean salon (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a cost of poor quality
Explain one cost to a toy manufacturer of a quality problem. (3)

A product recall (1). If a toy is found to be unsafe the manufacturer must collect and replace or refund every unit sold (1), costing money directly and generating publicity that makes parents distrust the brand, cutting future sales (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — TQM
Analyse how introducing total quality management could benefit a car-parts manufacturer. (6)

Under TQM each worker checks their own work and treats the next stage as a customer, so faults are caught and fixed at once rather than found by inspectors at the end; fewer faulty parts reach customers, so returns, warranty claims and rework fall, cutting costs (2). Staff are involved in continuous improvement, suggesting ways to reduce defects and waste; this raises motivation and often finds savings managers would miss (2). Car makers demand near-zero defects from suppliers, so consistent quality wins and keeps contracts and may allow higher prices; the manufacturer must invest in training and accept slower output at first, and TQM only works if managers and staff genuinely commit to it (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — is quality worth it?
A budget clothing retailer is considering spending more on quality control and better fabrics, raising costs by 8%. Evaluate whether the benefits would outweigh the costs. (9)

Costs: 8% higher costs cut already thin margins, and its price-focused customers may not notice or pay for better quality; competitors could undercut it; better fabrics and inspection slow the supply chain in a fast-fashion market (3). Benefits: fewer returns and complaints cut handling costs and refunds; clothes that last generate repeat purchases and good reviews, and social media makes poor quality visible quickly; a reputation for "cheap but decent" is a strong position against pure discounters; avoiding a safety recall protects the brand (3). Judgement: a targeted improvement — quality control on high-return lines and safety-critical items, better fabrics only where customers complain — would likely pay for itself through lower returns and loyalty, while an 8% across-the-board rise might not; it depends on the current return rate and whether customers are choosing rivals because of quality (3).
:::

::: q
State two ways a business could measure the quality of its products. (2)
---
Inspecting samples; counting customer returns or complaints (or surveys, defect rates, reviews).
:::

::: q
Explain one difference between quality control and total quality management. (2)
---
Quality control inspects finished products to find faults (1); TQM makes every worker responsible for preventing faults at every stage (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one cost to a restaurant of poor quality. (3)
---
Lost customers (1): diners who receive a bad meal do not return and leave negative reviews online (1), so future bookings and revenue fall (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit to a business of a reputation for high quality. (3)
---
Higher prices (1): customers are willing to pay more for a product they trust to work well and last (1), raising the profit margin on each sale (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of introducing TQM. (2)
---
Training all staff costs money and time (1), and if some employees or managers do not commit to it, the approach fails (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why maintaining quality becomes harder as a business franchises its outlets. (3)
---
Franchisees are independent owners the franchisor does not directly control (1); each may cut corners on ingredients or service (1), so quality varies between outlets and one poor franchise damages the whole brand's reputation (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how getting quality "right first time" reduces costs. (2)
---
Faulty items do not have to be scrapped or reworked (1), saving the materials and labour wasted on them and the cost of inspection (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the effects on a smartphone maker of a fault that causes some phones to overheat. (6)
---
The maker must recall affected phones, paying for collection, repair or replacement and losing the revenue from stock withdrawn from shops — a direct cost in the millions (2). News of the fault spreads worldwide within hours; customers fear the brand's phones are unsafe, so sales of other models fall and retailers may reduce shelf space, damaging reputation for years (2). Compensation claims and regulatory investigations add legal costs; to recover, the maker must invest in better testing and TQM to prove quality has improved — showing that the cost of preventing the fault would have been far lower than the cost of getting it wrong (2).
:::

::: watch
- Quality = **meeting customer expectations**, which differ between goods and services — define it that way.
- Costs of poor quality: **waste, refunds, recalls, reputation, lost sales** — chain to profit.
- TQM = **everyone, every stage, right first time**. Distinguish it from end-of-line inspection.
- Weigh **cost now vs benefit later**; the benefit usually wins, but say when it might not.
- Raise the **growth / franchising / outsourcing** quality problem whenever the case expands.
:::
