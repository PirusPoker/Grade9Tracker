## What it is

The **legal structure** (ownership) of a business decides who owns it, who controls it, where its finance comes from, who gets the profit and — the big one — who pays if it goes wrong.

**Liability** is the key idea. **Unlimited liability**: the owner is personally responsible for all the business's debts — the business and the owner are legally the same, so a failed business can cost the owner their house and savings. **Limited liability**: the company is a separate legal entity from its owners, so shareholders can lose only the money they invested in shares — their personal possessions are safe.

| Structure | Owners | Liability | Finance | Control | Profit | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole trader** | one person | unlimited | owner's savings, loans — hard to raise much | total — quick decisions | all to the owner | plumbers, hairdressers, market stalls, freelancers |
| **Partnership** | 2–20 partners (a deed of partnership sets the terms) | unlimited (usually) | partners' capital — more than one person | shared, so disagreements possible | shared as agreed | solicitors, dentists, accountants, small family firms |
| **Private limited company (Ltd)** | shareholders — shares sold privately, often family and friends | limited | share capital from chosen investors, easier bank loans | shareholders vote in proportion to shares; owners usually keep control | dividends to shareholders | growing family businesses, most medium-sized firms |
| **Public limited company (plc)** | shareholders — shares traded on the stock exchange, anyone can buy | limited | huge sums from selling shares to the public; cheaper borrowing | founders can lose control; directors run it; shareholders want dividends; takeover risk | dividends; shareholders expect returns every year | large businesses — supermarkets, banks, airlines |
| **Not-for-profit** (charities, social enterprises, community groups) | trustees or members | usually limited | donations, grants, trading | trustees; must follow charity rules | surplus reinvested in the cause, not paid out | social or environmental aims |

**Sole trader**: *advantages* — easy and cheap to set up, keeps all the profit, full control, privacy (accounts not published). *Disadvantages* — unlimited liability, hard to raise finance, long hours and no cover when ill, all the skills must come from one person, business ends if the owner stops.

**Partnership**: *advantages* — more capital and skills, shared workload and decisions, still easy to set up. *Disadvantages* — unlimited liability, profits shared, disagreements, each partner is liable for the others' decisions.

**Ltd**: *advantages* — limited liability, easier to raise finance by selling shares, more credible with banks and suppliers, continues if an owner dies. *Disadvantages* — more paperwork and legal requirements, accounts must be filed publicly at Companies House, profits shared with shareholders, shares cannot be sold to the public.

**plc**: *advantages* — can raise very large amounts, higher profile, economies of scale. *Disadvantages* — expensive to set up and run, must publish detailed accounts, loss of control by original owners, pressure for short-term dividends, risk of takeover if shares are bought up.

**Not-for-profit / social enterprise**: run like a business but the surplus goes to the aim; can attract grants, donations and customers who share the values; may struggle for finance and is judged on its aim, not on profit.

**Choosing a structure** depends on: how much finance is needed; how much risk the owner will accept; whether they want to keep control; how big the business is or wants to be; whether privacy matters; the cost and paperwork they can cope with. As businesses grow they typically move sole trader → partnership or Ltd → plc.

Key terms to learn:

- **Sole trader**: a business owned and run by one person, with unlimited liability.
- **Partnership**: a business owned by two or more people who share the profits and the unlimited liability.
- **Private limited company**: a company whose shares are sold privately and whose owners have limited liability.
- **Public limited company**: a company whose shares can be bought by the public on the stock exchange.
- **Limited liability**: the owners' financial responsibility is limited to the amount they invested.
- **Unlimited liability**: the owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business.
- **Shareholder**: a part-owner of a company who receives dividends from its profit.

::: method
1. **Liability first.** In any comparison, the liability difference is usually the top-scoring point: unlimited (sole trader, partnership) vs limited (Ltd, plc). Explain what it means for the owner's personal money.
2. **Then finance, control, profit** — the four columns of the table. For each, say which structure wins and why.
3. **"Recommend a structure"** (6–9 marks): state the business's needs (finance? risk? control?), match each to a structure, weigh the drawbacks, then recommend one **for this business** with a justified condition ("Ltd, provided the owners are willing to file public accounts").
4. **Ltd vs plc**: both have limited liability; the difference is **who can buy shares** and the consequences — scale of finance vs loss of control and takeover risk.
5. **Not-for-profit questions**: judge success by the aim, not profit; note that a surplus is still needed to keep going.
6. **Growth questions**: the structure changes as the business needs more capital — track the reasons for each step.
:::

::: example Define
Define "limited liability". (2)

The owners of a company are responsible for its debts only up to the amount they have invested (1); their personal possessions cannot be taken to pay business debts (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a disadvantage
Explain one disadvantage to Leo of running his window-cleaning business as a sole trader. (3)

Unlimited liability (1). If the business runs up debts it cannot pay, Leo is personally responsible (1), so he could lose his savings or even his home to pay creditors (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — becoming an Ltd
Maya and her brother run a successful bakery as a partnership and want to open three more shops. Analyse the benefits of becoming a private limited company. (6)

As an Ltd the bakery could sell shares to family, friends or chosen investors, raising the capital needed to fit out three new shops, which two partners could not fund from savings alone (2). Limited liability would protect Maya's and her brother's personal assets: with more shops the debts — leases, equipment loans — are far larger, so the risk of unlimited liability would otherwise be much greater (2). Banks and landlords also treat a company as more established and credible, making loans and leases easier to obtain; against this, they would face more paperwork and must publish accounts, but for a business expanding this fast the access to finance and protection outweigh the cost (2).
:::

::: example Recommend
Sunita is starting a mobile dog-grooming business with 3,000 of savings. She wants to keep things simple and work alone. Recommend whether she should set up as a sole trader or a private limited company. Justify your answer. (9)

Sole trader suits her: setting up is free and immediate, she keeps all the profit and every decision, and her accounts stay private; with a van and clippers her costs and debts are small, so the risk of unlimited liability is modest (3). An Ltd would give limited liability — useful if a dog were injured and she were sued — and could help if she later wanted to raise finance for a second van, but it means registering at Companies House, filing public accounts and more administration, which she has said she wants to avoid (3). Recommendation: sole trader now, because her capital needs are tiny, she works alone and simplicity is her stated priority; she should take out public liability insurance to cover the main risk, and revisit becoming an Ltd only if she expands or takes on debt (3).
:::

::: q
State two features of a sole trader. (2)
---
Owned by one person; unlimited liability (or keeps all the profit, easy to set up).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of a partnership over a sole trader. (3)
---
More capital (1): each partner contributes savings, so the business can afford more equipment or stock (1), letting it start bigger or grow faster than one person could fund (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a private and a public limited company. (2)
---
An Ltd's shares are sold privately to chosen people (1); a plc's shares are traded on the stock exchange, where anyone can buy them (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of becoming a public limited company. (3)
---
Loss of control (1): anyone can buy shares, so the original owners may end up with a minority (1), and another company could buy enough shares to take the business over (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a not-for-profit organisation still needs to make a surplus. (2)
---
Income must cover its costs or it cannot continue operating (1); any surplus is reinvested in its aims rather than paid to owners (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason a growing sole trader might become a private limited company. (3)
---
To raise finance (1): an Ltd can sell shares to investors (1), providing money for expansion that a sole trader could only borrow, if a bank would lend at all (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the impact of unlimited liability on a partner in a small building firm. (6)
---
Building projects involve large costs for materials and subcontractors, so if a client fails to pay or a job goes wrong the firm can quickly owe more than it has (2). With unlimited liability each partner is personally responsible for the whole debt — including debts run up by the other partner — so their house and savings are at risk (2). This makes partners cautious about taking on big contracts and may lead them to convert to an Ltd as the firm grows, accepting more paperwork in exchange for protecting their personal assets (2).
:::

::: q
Recommend whether a family-owned Ltd with 40 staff and plans to expand across Europe should become a plc. Justify your answer. (9)
---
For: floating on the stock exchange could raise the millions needed for European expansion without borrowing, raise the company's profile and make it easier to attract managers and partners abroad (3). Against: the family would lose control as outside shareholders vote and could be taken over; the cost of floating and the ongoing reporting requirements are heavy for a 40-person firm; shareholders would press for dividends, which could conflict with reinvesting in expansion (3). Recommendation: not yet — a firm this size is small for a plc; it should first seek a bank loan or sell a minority stake to a private investor, keeping family control, and consider a flotation only when it is much larger and the family is prepared to give up control (3).
:::

::: watch
- **Limited liability protects the owners' personal money**, not the company — and it belongs to Ltd and plc, not sole traders or partnerships.
- Ltd vs plc: the difference is **who can buy the shares**, and everything else follows.
- Recommendations must be **for this business** — its size, risk, finance needs and the owner's wishes — with a condition attached.
- A sole trader can have **employees**; "owned and run by one person" refers to ownership.
- Not-for-profits are judged on their **aim**; profit is the wrong measure.
:::
