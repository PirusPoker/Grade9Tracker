## What it is

An **organisational structure** is the way a business arranges its people into roles, levels and departments, and shows who reports to whom. It is drawn as an **organisation chart**.

**Why have a structure?** So everyone knows their role and responsibilities and who their manager is; so communication has clear routes; so work is coordinated and not duplicated; so managers know how many people they supervise; and so decisions are made at the right level. Without one, a growing business becomes chaotic.

**The main job roles**, top to bottom:

- **Directors** — set the overall strategy and objectives; appointed by shareholders; the board is led by the chief executive.
- **Senior managers** — run departments or divisions (finance, marketing, operations, HR) and turn strategy into plans.
- **Supervisors / team leaders** — manage day-to-day work of a team of operatives.
- **Operatives** — the workers who make the product or deliver the service.
- **Support staff** — administration, IT, maintenance.

**The key terms:**

- **Chain of command** — the line of authority from the top of the business to the bottom, along which instructions pass down and information passes up.
- **Span of control** — the number of employees a manager is directly responsible for. **Wide** span: many subordinates — less supervision each, more independence, cheaper; **narrow** span: few — close supervision, more managers, more expensive.
- **Delegation** — passing authority for a task down to a subordinate. Frees managers' time, develops and motivates staff, speeds decisions — but the manager keeps responsibility, and it needs trust and training.
- **Delayering** — removing one or more layers of management to make the structure flatter. Cuts salary costs and speeds communication, but widens spans of control and can overload the remaining managers and demotivate those left.

| | **Tall structure** | **Flat structure** |
|---|---|---|
| Layers | many | few |
| Span of control | narrow | wide |
| Chain of command | long | short |
| Supervision | close — good for inexperienced staff and quality control | light — staff need to be capable and trusted |
| Communication | slow, messages distorted through layers | fast, direct |
| Cost | many managers — expensive | fewer managers — cheaper |
| Promotion | many steps to climb — motivating for the ambitious | few promotion opportunities |
| Decisions | slow, but checked | quick; managers may be overloaded |
| Suits | large, traditional organisations; the armed forces; where errors are costly | small businesses; creative and tech firms; businesses that have delayered |

**Centralisation** — decisions made at the top / head office. *For*: consistency across the business, senior expertise, economies of scale in buying, strong control. *Against*: slow, ignores local knowledge, demotivates local managers. **Decentralisation** — decision-making authority passed to branches, regions or departments. *For*: quicker decisions, adapted to local customers, motivates managers, frees head office. *Against*: inconsistency, duplication, loss of control, local managers may lack skill. Many businesses centralise finance and buying but decentralise pricing, stock and staffing.

**Structure and communication**: a tall structure means messages pass through many layers — slow, and each layer can distort or delay them; a flat structure gives direct, fast communication but managers with wide spans may not have time to talk to everyone. Centralised businesses communicate top-down; decentralised ones need good systems to share information between sites.

Key terms to learn:

- **Organisational structure**: the way a business arranges its employees into levels and roles, showing who reports to whom.
- **Chain of command**: the line of authority running from the top of the business to the bottom.
- **Span of control**: the number of employees a manager is directly responsible for.
- **Delegation**: giving a subordinate the authority to carry out a task, while the manager keeps responsibility.
- **Delayering**: removing layers of management from a structure.
- **Centralisation**: keeping decision-making at the top of the business. **Decentralisation**: passing it to lower levels or local sites.

::: method
1. **Define with the exact phrase**: span of control = number of subordinates; chain of command = line of authority; delayering = removing layers; delegation = passing authority.
2. **Tall vs flat**: give both halves of each effect — "a wide span means fewer managers and faster communication, *but* less supervision of each worker".
3. **Delayering**: cost savings and faster communication vs wider spans, overload and lower morale — and the one-off redundancy cost.
4. **Centralisation**: consistency and control vs speed and local knowledge; recommend a mix for a multi-site business.
5. **Communication**: always link the structure to how fast and how accurately messages travel.
6. **Recommend a structure** by matching it to size, the skill of staff, how much consistency matters, and the speed of decisions the market needs.
:::

::: example Define
Define "span of control". (2)

The number of employees (1) that a manager is directly responsible for (1).
:::

::: example Explain — wide span
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of a wide span of control. (4)

Advantage: fewer managers are needed, so salary costs are lower and communication is faster because there are fewer layers (2). Disadvantage: each manager has many staff to oversee, so workers get less supervision and support, which can lower quality or leave problems unnoticed (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — delayering
A hotel chain plans to delayer by removing its regional-manager level. Analyse the possible effects. (6)

Removing a layer cuts the salaries of the regional managers, reducing costs, and messages between head office and hotel managers no longer pass through an extra step, so decisions and information travel faster (2). Hotel managers gain more authority and responsibility, which can motivate them and let them respond quickly to local customers (2). However, senior managers at head office now have a much wider span of control, so they may be overloaded and give each hotel less support; redundancy payments are an immediate cost, morale may fall among staff who fear further cuts, and standards could drift without regional oversight — the gains depend on hotel managers being capable of the extra responsibility (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — centralisation
A coffee chain with 80 branches currently lets each branch manager set prices, menus and opening hours. Evaluate whether it should centralise these decisions. (9)

For centralising: customers expect the same menu and prices in every branch, so consistency protects the brand; head office can negotiate bulk supplies for a standard menu, gaining economies of scale; senior managers have more expertise and data; control over quality and costs is tighter (3). Against: local managers know their customers — a branch near a university needs different hours and products from one in a business district; centralised decisions are slower to respond to local events; managers who lose authority may be demotivated and leave (3). Judgement: centralise the core — the main menu, brand pricing and quality standards — but leave local managers discretion over opening hours and a few local products within limits; full centralisation would sacrifice the local responsiveness that keeps branches profitable, so a mixed approach suits a chain of this size (3).
:::

::: q
State two reasons why a business has an organisational structure. (2)
---
So employees know their roles and who they report to; so communication has clear channels (or coordination, accountability, control).
:::

::: q
Define "chain of command". (2)
---
The line of authority in a business (1) from the top managers down to the operatives, along which instructions pass (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit to a manager of delegation. (3)
---
Frees time (1): passing routine tasks to a capable subordinate lets the manager focus on planning and important decisions (1); it also develops the subordinate's skills and motivation (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of a tall organisational structure. (3)
---
Slow communication (1): messages pass through many layers between directors and operatives (1), so decisions take longer to reach staff and may be distorted on the way (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of decentralisation for a supermarket chain. (3)
---
Local responsiveness (1): store managers can stock products local customers want and react to a nearby rival's promotion at once (1), raising sales compared with waiting for head-office decisions (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what "delayering" means and one reason a business might do it. (3)
---
Removing one or more layers of management from the structure (1); to cut management salary costs (1) and speed up communication between the top and the bottom (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a flat structure may offer fewer promotion opportunities. (2)
---
With few layers there are few management positions to move up into (1), so ambitious staff may leave to progress elsewhere (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how the organisational structure of a business affects communication. (6)
---
In a tall structure with a long chain of command, information from operatives passes through several managers before reaching directors, and instructions come back the same way, so communication is slow and each layer can misunderstand or filter the message (2). In a flat structure with wide spans, messages travel directly and quickly, and staff can raise problems straight with senior managers; but a manager responsible for many people has less time for each, so some communication may be rushed or missed (2). Centralised businesses communicate mainly top-down, which gives consistent messages but little feedback; decentralised ones need good IT and meetings to share information between sites — the best structure for communication depends on the size of the business and how much staff need close guidance (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Both halves** of every span/structure point: wide span = fewer managers and faster messages **and** less supervision.
- Definitions must use the exact idea: span = **number of subordinates**; chain = **line of authority**; delegation passes **authority**, the manager keeps **responsibility**.
- Delayering has a **one-off redundancy cost** and **morale** effects, not just savings.
- Centralisation questions usually end with a **mix** — central standards, local discretion.
- Always connect the structure to **communication speed and accuracy**.
:::
