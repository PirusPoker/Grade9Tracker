## What it is

**Market segmentation** is dividing a market into groups of customers — **segments** — who share similar characteristics and needs, so that a business can **target** each group with a product and marketing mix that fits it. Nobody sells one product to "everyone"; a business chooses which segments to serve.

**The ways to segment** examined by AQA:

| Basis | Segments | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Gender** | male / female (and products aimed at either) | razors, fragrances, clothing ranges |
| **Age** | children, teenagers, young adults, families, over-60s | toys, gaming, holidays, retirement products |
| **Location** | country, region, urban / rural, climate | ski wear in mountain areas, regional food tastes, local delivery zones |
| **Income** | low, middle, high; budget vs premium | discount supermarkets vs luxury stores, economy vs first class |

Also used: lifestyle and interests (fitness, gaming), family status, occupation, buying behaviour (loyal, price-sensitive).

**How a business uses segmentation**: identify the segments in its market → research each one's needs → choose which to **target** (one niche, several, or the mass market) → design the **marketing mix** for that segment: the product's features and design, a price the segment will pay, promotion through media the segment uses, and distribution where the segment shops.

**Why segment** — the benefits:

- **Meets customer needs more precisely**, so more sales and satisfied, loyal customers.
- **Marketing spend is targeted** where it works — an advert for retirement homes in a teenagers' app is wasted money.
- **Finds gaps in the market** — a segment nobody serves well.
- **Allows premium pricing** for a segment that values the product.
- **Product range decisions** — different versions for different segments (a family car and a sports version).
- **Competitive advantage** — a small business can dominate a niche a large rival ignores.

**The risks:**

- **Targeting a segment badly** — misjudging what it wants, or promoting through the wrong channel, wastes money and damages the brand; stereotyping a group can cause offence.
- **Aiming at too broad a market** — trying to please everyone means the product suits nobody particularly well, the marketing message is bland, and the business is outcompeted by specialists.
- **Too narrow a segment** — the niche may be too small to be profitable, or it may shrink or change.
- **Costs** — research and separate marketing mixes for each segment cost more than one mass campaign.
- Segments overlap and change; data on customers may be poor.

::: terms
- **Market segmentation**: dividing a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics and needs, so each group can be targeted.
- **Market segment**: a group of customers within a market who share similar characteristics — such as age, gender, location or income.
- **Target market**: the segment or segments a business chooses to aim its product and marketing at.
- **Niche market**: a small, specialised segment of a larger market.
- **Mass market**: a very large market where products are aimed at most customers.
- **Gap in the market**: a customer need that no business is currently meeting well.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the segment, then the marketing decision that follows.** "Segmenting by age — targeting over-60s — so the phone has large buttons, a simple menu, is priced modestly and is advertised in daytime TV." A list of ways to segment with no decision attached scores little.
2. **Why segment?** — chain: know the segment's needs → tailor the mix → more sales and less wasted promotion.
3. **Risks**: too broad (suits nobody), too narrow (too small), misjudged (wasted money, offence). Apply to the case.
4. **Recommend a target segment** using the case data (ages, incomes, location) and say how each P would change for it.
5. **Evaluate**: segmentation works when segments are distinct, reachable and large enough to be profitable — otherwise a single mix is cheaper.
:::

::: example Define
Define "market segmentation". (2)

Dividing a market into groups of customers (1) who share similar characteristics or needs, such as age or income, so each can be targeted differently (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a basis and a decision
Explain how a holiday company might use segmentation by age. (3)

It could identify a segment of 18–25 year olds (1) and design holidays for them — lively resorts, shared apartments, low prices (1) — promoted through social media and booked on an app, rather than the brochures and cruises aimed at older customers (1).
:::

::: example Explain — segmenting by location
Explain how a brewery could benefit from segmenting its market by location. (3)

Tastes differ by region — for example, drinkers in one area prefer darker, traditional ales while city bars want lighter craft lagers (1). By identifying these location segments, the brewery can sell a different range in each area and promote it through local pubs, festivals and regional media (1), so its products match what drinkers there want, raising sales without wasting money pushing beers into areas that do not buy them (1).
:::

::: example Explain — targeting a segment badly
A sportswear brand launches a range aimed at over-60s, advertised only on TikTok with teenage influencers. Explain one problem with this approach. (3)

The promotion does not reach the target segment (1): few over-60s use TikTok, and those who see teenage influencers are unlikely to feel the range is for them (1), so the advertising budget is largely wasted and the range may fail — the brand should have researched which media this segment uses, such as newspapers, Facebook or GP-surgery magazines (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — benefits
Analyse how segmentation by income could benefit a car manufacturer. (6)

By identifying a high-income segment, the manufacturer can design a luxury model with premium materials and technology and charge a high price that this segment is willing to pay, earning a large profit per car (2). For the middle-income segment it can offer a cheaper family model with fewer features, promoted on reliability and running costs, so it sells to both groups without either feeling the product is wrong for them (2). Promotion is targeted — the luxury model in business magazines and airport lounges, the family car on television — so advertising money reaches the right buyers; the risk is that the same brand across both segments may dilute the luxury image, which is why some manufacturers use separate brand names (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — niche or broad
A new bakery in a town of 40,000 people is deciding whether to specialise in gluten-free products or sell a full range to everyone. Evaluate which it should choose. (9)

Specialising: the gluten-free segment is underserved, so the bakery could become the obvious choice for those customers, charge premium prices and attract people from further away; its marketing message is clear and it avoids competing head-on with supermarkets and existing bakeries (3). But in a town of 40,000 the segment is small — perhaps a few hundred regular customers — and may not generate enough sales to cover rent and wages; if a supermarket adds a gluten-free range, its advantage disappears (3). Judgement: sell a full range but with a strong gluten-free speciality as its point of difference — this keeps the mass market for daily revenue while owning the niche; a pure niche would be viable only in a larger town or with online sales, so the decision depends on how big the gluten-free segment is locally and whether the bakery can deliver beyond the town (3).
:::

::: q
Explain how a supermarket might use segmentation by location. (2)
---
Stores in coastal areas could stock more seafood and picnic goods (1), matching local tastes and tourist demand so sales rise (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit of segmentation for a small business. (3)
---
Finding a niche (1): a small business cannot compete with large rivals across the whole market (1), but can serve one segment better than anyone — such as vegan cakes — and build loyal customers there (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one risk of aiming a product at too broad a market. (3)
---
The product suits nobody particularly well (1); customers choose specialist rivals that meet their exact needs (1), so sales are lower than expected despite the large potential market (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one risk of targeting a segment badly. (2)
---
If the business misjudges what the segment wants, the product and promotion miss (1), wasting the money spent and possibly offending customers with stereotypes (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how segmentation helps a business spend its promotion budget effectively. (2)
---
It advertises only through media the target segment uses (1), so money is not wasted reaching people who would never buy (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a business might target several segments with different products. (2)
---
Each segment has different needs and budgets (1), so separate products let the business sell to more of the market without one product compromising for everyone (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a sportswear brand could use segmentation to increase sales. (6)
---
Segmenting by age and gender lets the brand design ranges for teenage boys, women runners and older gym-goers, each with the fit, style and features that group wants, so each product sells better than a single generic range (2). Segmenting by income lets it offer a premium performance line at high prices alongside a budget range in supermarkets, capturing both groups (2). Promotion can be targeted — TikTok influencers for teenagers, running magazines for serious athletes — so marketing spend reaches buyers; the cost is more product lines and campaigns to manage, so the brand must make sure each segment is large enough to justify its own range (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Segment → decision.** Always attach the marketing action to the segment; listing ways to segment earns identification marks only.
- The spec's four bases are **gender, age, location, income** — use those names.
- Risks come in two directions: **too broad** and **too narrow / misjudged**. Give both in an evaluation.
- Segmentation **costs more** than one mass campaign — worth it only if segments are distinct and large enough.
- Niche marketing is how a **small business** beats large rivals — a strong application point.
:::
