## What it is

A **stakeholder** is any individual or group with an **interest in** — affected by, or able to affect — a business. **Internal** stakeholders are inside it (owners, managers, employees); **external** ones are outside (customers, suppliers, the local community, the government, lenders).

| Stakeholder | What they want from the business | How the business affects them | How they influence the business |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Owners / shareholders** | profit, dividends, a rising value; for a sole trader also independence and satisfaction | their income and wealth depend on it | decide objectives; vote at meetings; can sell up or remove directors |
| **Employees** | fair pay, job security, good conditions, training, promotion | their livelihood, skills, wellbeing | work harder or less; leave; strike or complain through unions; their skills decide quality |
| **Customers** | quality products, fair prices, good service, safety | what they can buy, how well they are treated | buy elsewhere; reviews and word of mouth; complaints |
| **Suppliers** | regular orders, prompt payment, fair prices, long contracts | their sales and cash flow depend on orders | can raise prices, offer worse terms, refuse to supply, go elsewhere |
| **Local community** | jobs, no pollution, noise or congestion, local spending, sponsorship | employment, environment, traffic | protests, planning objections, petitions, support or boycott |
| **Government** | taxes paid, laws obeyed, jobs created | tax revenue, employment | laws, taxes, grants, planning permission |
| **Lenders (banks)** | interest paid, loan repaid | risk on their money | can refuse or call in loans |

**Stakeholder conflict**: the same pound of profit can go only one way, so what one group wants often comes at another's expense:

- **Employees vs owners** — higher wages mean lower profit and dividends.
- **Customers vs owners** — lower prices mean lower profit.
- **Suppliers vs owners** — paying suppliers promptly and fairly cuts the business's cash and margin.
- **Community vs owners/customers** — a new factory brings jobs and cheaper goods but noise, traffic and pollution.
- **Employees vs customers** — shorter hours for staff mean less convenience for customers; automation lowers prices but cuts jobs.
- **Growth vs everyone** — a merger brings shareholders returns but redundancies for staff.

Businesses **resolve** conflicts by deciding which stakeholder matters most for the decision (usually owners and customers, because without them there is no business), by compromising (a modest pay rise, phased over a year), by consulting affected groups, and by recognising that long-term success needs most stakeholders reasonably satisfied — unhappy staff or customers eventually cost the owners money too.

::: terms
- **Stakeholder**: any individual or group with an interest in a business — affected by its activities or able to affect them.
- **Internal stakeholder**: a stakeholder inside the business — owners, managers and employees.
- **External stakeholder**: a stakeholder outside the business — customers, suppliers, the local community, the government, lenders.
- **Supplier**: a business that provides another business with the materials, goods or services it needs.
- **Local community**: the people who live and work near a business and are affected by its jobs, traffic, noise and pollution.
- **Lender**: a bank or other organisation that has lent the business money and wants interest paid and the loan repaid.
- **Stakeholder conflict**: a situation where the objectives of different stakeholders cannot all be met at once, so meeting one group's objective harms another's.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the stakeholder and what they want** — the objective — before anything else. Two marks are usually available just for that.
2. **Effect of a decision**: for each stakeholder named, say whether the decision helps or harms them and *why*, using the case.
3. **Conflict answers**: show the **same resource going two ways** — "the £50,000 profit can either fund a pay rise or be paid as dividends, not both".
4. **Influence**: explain the *mechanism* — customers influence by buying elsewhere; employees by leaving or striking; the community by objecting to planning permission.
5. **Evaluate**: which stakeholder should the business prioritise *in this case*? Usually the one whose loss would threaten survival — then note the long-term cost of ignoring the others.
:::

::: example Define
What is meant by a "stakeholder"? Give one example of an external stakeholder. (2)

Any person or group with an interest in, or affected by, a business (1); for example a supplier (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a stakeholder's objective
Explain one objective that the employees of a supermarket might have. (3)

Job security (1). Employees rely on their wages to pay rent and bills (1), so they want the supermarket to stay open and not replace them with self-checkouts or cut hours (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — conflict
A car factory plans to install robots that will cut costs by 20% but make 300 workers redundant. Analyse how this creates conflict between stakeholders. (6)

Owners and shareholders want the robots: lower costs raise profit and dividends and make the factory more competitive, so they support the decision (2). The 300 employees lose their jobs and income, and the local community loses spending in shops and may see unemployment rise, so they oppose it — the same cost saving that benefits owners is the wages of the workers (2). Customers may gain from lower prices, so they side with the owners; the conflict is that the business cannot both cut costs by 20% and keep all 300 jobs, so management must choose whose objective to prioritise and may try to soften the conflict with redundancy pay and retraining (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — which stakeholder matters most
A café's landlord has raised the rent. The owner can raise prices, cut staff hours, or accept lower profit. Evaluate which stakeholder group the owner should prioritise. (9)

Prioritising customers (no price rise) keeps them loyal in a competitive high street, protecting revenue, but the owner must then cut staff hours or accept less profit (2). Prioritising employees (no cuts) keeps service quality and morale, avoiding the cost of replacing staff who leave, but the owner's income falls (2). Prioritising the owner (raise prices and cut hours) protects profit now but risks losing customers and staff, which could cut revenue by more than the rent rise (2). Judgement: customers should come first because without them there is no revenue at all; a small price rise spread across the menu is likely to be tolerated if service stays good, so protecting staff hours as well is the best balance — the owner takes a modest cut in profit for a year and reviews; it depends on how price-sensitive the café's customers are (3).
:::

::: example Explain — how a stakeholder influences a business
A supermarket chain applies for planning permission to open a 24-hour store next to a housing estate. Explain how the local community could influence this decision. (3)

Residents worried about night-time noise, lorries and traffic can object to the planning application at the council (1). If enough residents object, the council may refuse permission or add conditions, such as no deliveries between 11pm and 7am (1), so the supermarket may have to change its plans — shorter opening hours or a different site — or it will not be able to open the store at all (1).
:::

::: example Explain — the effect of a decision on a stakeholder
A clothing retailer decides to pay its suppliers after 90 days instead of 30 days. Explain the effect of this decision on its suppliers. (3)

The suppliers wait three times as long to be paid for goods they have already made and delivered (1), so they have less cash to pay their own workers and buy materials (1); a small supplier could run short of cash and struggle to survive, or may raise its prices or stop supplying the retailer — which shows the decision could hurt the retailer too (1).
:::

::: q
State two internal stakeholders. (2)
---
Owners; employees (or managers).
:::

::: q
State one objective of a supplier and one of a customer. (2)
---
Supplier: regular orders / prompt payment (1). Customer: good quality at a fair price (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way the local community might be affected by a new distribution warehouse. (3)
---
More jobs (1): the warehouse employs local people as pickers and drivers (1), raising local incomes and spending in nearby shops — though traffic and noise may increase (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how customers can influence a business. (2)
---
If unhappy they buy from competitors and leave poor reviews (1), cutting the business's sales and forcing it to improve quality, price or service (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one conflict between the objectives of owners and employees. (3)
---
Employees want higher wages (1) while owners want higher profit (1); wages are a cost, so every pay rise reduces the profit available for dividends or reinvestment (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way a business could reduce conflict with its suppliers. (2)
---
Pay invoices on time and agree longer contracts (1), giving suppliers the reliable cash flow they want so they keep prices and quality stable (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the effect on stakeholders of a supermarket chain deciding to pay all staff the real living wage. (6)
---
Employees gain higher income and feel valued, so motivation rises, staff turnover falls and service improves (2). Owners face higher wage costs, reducing profit and dividends unless productivity rises or prices increase; shareholders may object in the short term (2). Customers may pay slightly higher prices but receive better service, and the community benefits from higher local incomes; the business may also gain reputation and attract ethically minded shoppers, so the long-term effect on owners may be positive even though the immediate effect is a cost (2).
:::

::: q
Evaluate whether a business should always put its shareholders first. (9)
---
For: shareholders own the business and provided the capital; without profit and dividends they sell shares, the share price falls and the business struggles to raise finance; directors have a duty to them (3). Against: ignoring customers, employees and suppliers damages the business — poor service loses sales, low pay loses skilled staff, late payment loses suppliers — so shareholder returns depend on satisfying other stakeholders; the community and government can block expansion or impose fines (3). Judgement: shareholders' interests should usually be prioritised in the long run, but that is best achieved by treating other stakeholders fairly; the balance depends on the decision — a pricing choice weighs customers heavily, a redundancy programme weighs employees — so "always first" is too simple (3).
:::

::: watch
- Identify **who** and **what they want** before discussing effects — that is where the first marks sit.
- Conflict = **the same money or resource going two ways**. Show it explicitly.
- Influence needs a **mechanism** — how the stakeholder can actually affect the business.
- Owners and shareholders are stakeholders too — do not treat "stakeholder" as meaning "everyone except the owner".
- Evaluations: prioritise the stakeholder **critical to survival in this case**, then note the long-term cost of ignoring the rest.
:::
