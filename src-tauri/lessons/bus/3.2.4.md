## What it is

**Globalisation** is the process by which businesses and countries become more connected — trading, investing and operating across borders — so that the world works increasingly as one market. Driven by cheaper transport, the internet, fewer trade barriers and multinational companies.

**Benefits for a UK business**: sell to a **larger market** abroad, so more sales and economies of scale; **cheaper supplies** and manufacturing from lower-cost countries; access to skilled labour and new ideas; spread risk across countries. **Drawbacks**: **more competition** at home from foreign firms with lower costs; pressure to cut prices and wages; long supply chains that can be disrupted; different languages, laws, cultures and currencies to manage; ethical scrutiny of overseas suppliers; jobs moved abroad damage the business's reputation at home.

**Competing internationally** — a UK business competes with foreign rivals on three things:

- **Price** — hard to win when rivals have lower labour costs; requires efficiency, economies of scale or automation.
- **Quality** — reliability, durability, service; a premium reputation ("British-made") lets a business charge more.
- **Design** — innovation, style, features, a USP; design is where a high-cost country can beat a low-cost one (Dyson, Burberry, Aston Martin).

Most UK businesses compete on quality and design rather than price.

**Exchange rates** — the price of one currency in terms of another (£1 = 1.25 US dollars). When the pound **rises** (appreciates, gets stronger) £1 buys more foreign currency; when it **falls** (depreciates, weakens) it buys less. You will *not* be asked to convert currency — only to explain the effect of a change.

The two rules — remember **SPICED**: **S**trong **P**ound, **I**mports **C**heaper, **E**xports **D**earer.

| | Pound rises (strong) | Pound falls (weak) |
|---|---|---|
| **UK exporter** (sells abroad) | its goods cost more in foreign currency → sales fall, or it cuts its price → lower profit margin | its goods are cheaper abroad → sales rise, or it keeps the foreign price and earns more pounds → higher margin |
| **UK importer** (buys from abroad) | foreign goods and materials cost fewer pounds → **costs fall** → higher profit or lower prices | imports cost more pounds → costs rise → lower profit or higher prices |
| **Tourism to the UK** | dearer for foreign visitors → fewer tourists | cheaper → more tourists |

A business that both imports materials and exports products is affected both ways — work out which side is bigger.

::: terms
- **Globalisation**: the process by which businesses and economies around the world become more connected through trade, investment and communication.
- **Multinational**: a business that has operations — factories, offices or shops — in more than one country.
- **Export**: a good or service produced in one country and sold to customers in another.
- **Import**: a good or service bought from another country.
- **Exchange rate**: the value of one currency expressed in terms of another, e.g. £1 = 1.25 US dollars.
- **Appreciation**: a rise in the value of a currency — the pound buys more foreign currency.
- **Depreciation**: a fall in the value of a currency — the pound buys less foreign currency.
- **SPICED**: a way to remember the effect of a strong pound: Strong Pound, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer.
:::

::: method
1. **Globalisation questions**: benefit → chain → applied to the business; then a drawback, usually competition or supply-chain risk.
2. **Competing internationally**: say which of price, quality or design the business should compete on and *why* — a UK firm rarely wins on price.
3. **Exchange rates — three steps.** (a) Is the business an importer, an exporter, or both? (b) Has the pound risen or fallen? (c) Apply SPICED: what happens to its costs or its foreign price → sales → profit.
4. **Always give the business a choice** in the chain: an exporter facing a strong pound can *either* keep its foreign price and accept lower margins *or* raise its foreign price and lose sales.
5. **Evaluate**: effects depend on how much the business trades abroad, whether it can switch suppliers, and whether rivals face the same change.
:::

::: example Explain — a benefit of globalisation
Explain one benefit of globalisation to a UK toy manufacturer. (3)

Cheaper production (1). The manufacturer can have toys made in a country with lower labour costs (1), reducing its cost per toy so it can lower prices or increase its profit margin (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a drawback of globalisation
Explain one drawback of globalisation for a UK company that makes kitchen knives. (3)

More competition (1): knife makers in countries with lower wages can now sell into the UK cheaply, online and through supermarkets (1), so the UK company must cut its prices — squeezing its profit margin — or lose customers, unless it can persuade them its knives are worth more because of their quality or design (1).
:::

::: example Explain — importing and exporting at once
A UK bicycle manufacturer imports its frames from Taiwan and exports half of its finished bikes to Europe. Explain how a fall in the value of the pound would affect it. (4)

Imports: the frames cost more pounds to buy, so its costs rise (1) and its profit margin on each bike falls unless it raises prices (1).

Exports: its bikes become cheaper in euros, so European customers buy more (1), raising sales and revenue. The overall effect depends on which is bigger — the rise in the cost of frames or the extra export sales; since half its output is exported, the gain in sales could outweigh the higher cost of frames (1).
:::

::: example Exchange rate — an exporter
A Scottish whisky distiller exports most of its output to the USA. Explain the effect on the distiller of a rise in the value of the pound. (3)

A stronger pound makes each bottle cost more in dollars (1), so American customers buy less or switch to cheaper rivals, cutting sales (1); if the distiller lowers its dollar price to keep sales, it receives fewer pounds per bottle, so profit falls either way (1).
:::

::: example Exchange rate — an importer
A UK bicycle shop buys its bikes from a manufacturer in Taiwan. Analyse the effect of a fall in the value of the pound. (6)

A weaker pound means each bike costs more pounds to buy — the same price in Taiwanese currency now needs more pounds (2). The shop's costs rise; it must either raise its prices, risking lost sales to competitors or to second-hand bikes, or keep prices and accept a lower profit margin on each bike (2). It might respond by seeking a cheaper supplier, switching to UK-made bikes, or ordering ahead before further falls; the effect is serious because almost all its stock is imported, though rivals importing from the same country face the same rise, which limits the loss of competitiveness (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — competing internationally
A UK furniture maker faces cheaper imports from Eastern Europe. Evaluate whether it should compete on price or on design and quality. (9)

Competing on price would mean cutting costs to match lower foreign wages — automating, sourcing cheaper wood, reducing staff — which is hard for a UK firm and could damage the quality its customers expect; margins would be thin and rivals can usually go lower still (3). Competing on design and quality lets it charge premium prices for distinctive, durable furniture, target customers who value craftsmanship and a UK-made label, and avoid a price war it cannot win; but it needs investment in designers and marketing, and the premium market is smaller (3). Judgement: design and quality, because a high-cost country's advantage lies in skill and innovation rather than cheap labour; it should accept lower volumes at higher margins and use e-commerce to reach premium customers abroad — though if its current customers are price-driven, it must also trim costs enough to stay within reach (3).
:::

::: q
A UK cheese maker exports to France. The pound falls in value. Explain the effect on its sales. (3)
---
Its cheese becomes cheaper in euros (1), so French customers buy more (1); alternatively it can keep the euro price and receive more pounds per sale, raising profit (1).

::: q
A UK electronics retailer imports from Japan. The pound rises. Explain the effect on its costs. (2)
---
Each product costs fewer pounds to buy (1), so its costs fall and it can lower prices or raise its profit margin (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why exchange rate changes create uncertainty for a business that trades abroad. (2)
---
Its costs or foreign prices can change without it doing anything (1), making profits hard to forecast and plans harder to make (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a UK hotel might be affected by a fall in the pound. (3)
---
The UK becomes cheaper for foreign tourists (1), so more visit and bookings rise (1); UK residents also find holidays abroad dearer and may holiday at home, adding further demand (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a UK car manufacturer that imports parts and exports finished cars is affected by a rise in the pound. (6)
---
Imported parts cost fewer pounds, so its production costs fall, which helps its profit margin on every car (2). But its exported cars cost more in foreign currency, so overseas sales fall unless it cuts its foreign price and accepts a lower margin; since most of its revenue comes from exports, this effect is likely to be larger than the saving on parts (2). The net effect is probably negative, though smaller than for an exporter that buys everything in the UK; the manufacturer could respond by sourcing more parts abroad while the pound is strong or by emphasising quality and design so buyers accept higher prices (2).
:::

::: watch
- **SPICED**: Strong Pound, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer. Work out importer or exporter **first**.
- No currency calculations are set — the marks are for the **chain of effects** on costs, prices, sales and profit.
- Give the business its **choice**: change the price or absorb the change in margin.
- UK firms compete on **quality and design**, rarely on price — a strong evaluation line.
- Globalisation's key drawback is **competition** — cheaper foreign rivals in the home market.
:::

::: test
Highland Knitwear sells jumpers made in Scotland to customers in the USA. The pound has risen from £1 = 1.20 US dollars to £1 = 1.40 US dollars. Analyse the effects on Highland Knitwear. (6)
---
A stronger pound makes its jumpers dearer in dollars: a £60 jumper rises from 72 dollars to 84 dollars, so US customers buy fewer or switch to cheaper rivals, reducing sales (2). If it cuts its dollar price to keep sales, it receives fewer pounds for each jumper, reducing its profit margin (2). It could respond by stressing quality and its Scottish heritage so customers accept the higher price, or by finding new markets; any materials it imports, such as wool, become cheaper, which partly offsets the loss (2).

::: test
A UK toy manufacturer faces cheap imports from abroad. It could cut costs and prices, or focus on high-quality, well-designed toys at higher prices. Evaluate which strategy it should choose. (9)
---
Compete on price: keeps customers who buy on price; may need automation, cheaper materials or moving production abroad; but it is hard to beat countries with lower wages, margins become very thin, and quality or jobs may suffer (3).
Compete on design and quality: justifies higher prices and margins; builds a brand (safety, durability, educational value) valued by parents; avoids a price war; but needs investment in design and marketing, and the premium market is smaller (3).
Judgement: justified — usually design and quality, because a UK firm cannot easily match low-wage countries on cost; depends on its current customers, finance available for design, and how strong its brand is (3).
Levels: 7–9 — both sides developed and applied to the case, with a justified judgement; 4–6 — developed points with some application, a judgement that is not fully supported; 1–3 — simple points, little use of the case.
:::
