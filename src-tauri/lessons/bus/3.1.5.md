## What it is

Where a business is based affects its costs, its sales and its access to workers — so **location** is one of the first big decisions. The factors that drive it:

| Factor | What it means | Matters most for |
|---|---|---|
| **Proximity to the market** (customers) | being near the people who buy, or where they pass by | shops, cafés, hairdressers, restaurants — anything customers visit; perishable or bulky products delivered to customers |
| **Proximity to raw materials** | being near the inputs, especially if they are heavy, bulky or perishable | sawmills, food processing, quarrying, breweries near water |
| **Proximity to labour** | a supply of workers with the right skills at the right cost | tech firms near universities; call centres where wages are lower; factories near skilled trades |
| **Proximity to competitors** | being near rivals — sometimes a *plus* (customers come to compare: car showrooms, restaurant quarters, jewellers), sometimes a *minus* (a small shop next to a supermarket) | retail, restaurants, professional services |
| **Costs** | rent, land prices, business rates, wages, transport | everything; low-cost sites out of town suit warehouses and factories |
| Also: **transport links** (motorways, ports, airports), **government grants** for locating in areas of high unemployment, **the owner's home**, **image** of the area (a luxury brand on a prestige street), **infrastructure** (broadband, power) | | |

**Which factor matters most** depends on the business. A **manufacturer** of heavy goods weighs raw materials, transport and cheap land. A **retailer** weighs footfall — the number of customers walking past — and will pay high rent for it. An **online business** needs cheap space, good broadband and courier links, and can be anywhere. A **service** needing skilled staff goes where those staff live.

**Relocation** — moving from one site to another — happens when a business outgrows its premises, wants lower costs, its customers or workers have moved, its lease ends, or it is offered a grant. **Costs of relocating**: moving expenses, lost trading while moving, losing local customers and staff who will not move, redundancy payments, new signage and marketing, disruption to suppliers and deliveries, and the risk that the new site does not work.

::: terms
- **Location**: the place where a business is based.
- **Proximity**: nearness to something — the market, raw materials, labour or competitors.
- **Proximity to the market**: being close to the customers who buy the product, or to where they pass by.
- **Proximity to raw materials**: being close to the inputs a business uses, which matters most when they are heavy, bulky or perishable.
- **Proximity to labour**: being close to a supply of workers with the right skills at a wage the business can afford.
- **Proximity to competitors**: being close to rival businesses — a benefit when customers come to compare, a drawback when rivals take sales.
- **Footfall**: the number of potential customers passing a location.
- **Infrastructure**: the basic services a location offers a business — roads, rail, ports, power, broadband.
- **Relocation**: moving a business from one location to another.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the factor, then apply it to the business.** "Proximity to the market matters most for the café because customers must walk in, so a high-footfall street is worth the higher rent."
2. **Rank the factors for this business** — retailer: market first, cost second; factory: raw materials/transport and cost first; online: cost and broadband.
3. **Weigh the trade-off**: a good location usually costs more. Say what the higher cost buys and whether the business can afford it.
4. **Relocation questions**: benefits (lower cost, bigger site, better market) against the one-off costs and risks — lost customers, staff, disruption.
5. **Recommend** a location with a justification and a condition: "the retail park, provided the rent is within the budget, because…".
:::

::: example Explain — a factor
Explain why proximity to the market is important to a florist. (3)

Flowers are bought on impulse and for occasions, so the florist needs customers walking past the shop (1). A site on a busy high street or near a station gives high footfall and more sales (1); flowers are also perishable, so being near customers means less waste from unsold stock (1).
:::

::: example Compare — two businesses
Explain why a sawmill and an accountancy firm would choose their locations for different reasons. (4)

A sawmill processes heavy, bulky timber, so it locates near forests to cut the cost of transporting logs, on cheap land with good road access for lorries (2). An accountancy firm's inputs are skilled staff and its customers are businesses, so it locates in a town centre near clients and near where qualified accountants live, accepting higher rent because it needs little space (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — relocation
Ravi's clothing factory in the city centre is offered a site on an out-of-town industrial estate with rent 40% lower. Analyse the possible drawbacks of relocating. (6)

Some of his skilled machinists may not be able to travel to the new site, so he could lose experienced staff and face the cost of recruiting and training replacements, lowering productivity for months (2). The move itself costs money — transporting machinery, refitting the new building, redundancy pay for staff who leave — and production stops during the move, so orders may be late and customers lost (2). The city-centre site may have had advantages he loses: closeness to fabric suppliers and buyers who visit, and the reputation of a known address; if these were important, the 40% rent saving could be outweighed by lower sales, so he should compare the total effect rather than the rent alone (2).
:::

::: example Explain — proximity to labour
Explain why a video-games developer might choose to locate near a university. (3)

A games developer depends on skilled programmers and artists — its product is only as good as its staff (1). A university produces graduates in computing and design each year, so the developer can recruit skilled workers without paying them to relocate (1); it can also offer placements and work with lecturers on new technology, keeping a steady supply of talent that would be hard to find in an area with no university (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — choosing a site
Leila is opening a sandwich bar. Site A is on the high street opposite the station, with rent of £36,000 a year and about 5,000 people passing each day. Site B is two streets away, with rent of £15,000 a year and about 800 people passing each day. Evaluate which site she should choose. (9)

Site A: a sandwich bar depends on passing trade — commuters buy on impulse on the way to the train — so six times the footfall should bring far more customers; if even a small share of the 5,000 buy lunch or breakfast, the extra revenue could easily cover the £21,000 difference in rent (3). Site B: the rent is £21,000 a year lower, cutting fixed costs and the risk of making a loss in the first year when she has few regulars; she could build trade through delivery apps and office lunch orders, which do not need footfall (3). Judgement: Site A, because proximity to the market is the key location factor for a food outlet selling to people on the move, and the station guarantees a steady flow of customers; but only if her forecast shows she can sell enough — about 160 more sandwiches a week, each leaving £2.50 after ingredients, would pay the extra rent (160 × £2.50 × 52 = £20,800) — and she has the cash to survive the first few months. If her budget cannot cover the higher rent, Site B with strong delivery sales is the safer start (3).
:::

::: q
State two factors that influence where a business locates. (2)
---
Proximity to the market; cost of premises (or raw materials, labour, competitors, transport links).
:::

::: q
Explain why a jeweller might choose to locate near other jewellers. (2)
---
Customers visit an area with several jewellers to compare (1), so the concentration attracts more shoppers than a single shop would on its own (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a frozen-food manufacturer might locate near a port. (3)
---
Its raw materials, such as fish, arrive by ship (1); a nearby factory cuts transport time and cost (1), and the fish can be processed quickly before it spoils (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason an online retailer does not need a town-centre location. (2)
---
Customers buy through the website rather than visiting (1), so the business can use cheap out-of-town warehouse space as long as couriers can reach it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a government grant might affect a location decision. (2)
---
A grant for setting up in an area of high unemployment lowers start-up costs (1), which may persuade a factory to locate there rather than nearer its market (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one cost to a business of relocating. (3)
---
Lost customers (1): regular customers of a shop may not follow it to a new area (1), so revenue falls until new customers are found (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse which location factor is most important for a new coffee shop. (6)
---
Proximity to the market is most important: a coffee shop depends on passing trade, so a site near offices, a station or a busy shopping street brings the footfall it needs, and customers will not travel far for a coffee (2). Cost is the main constraint — high-footfall sites carry high rent, so the owner must check that expected sales cover it; a cheaper side-street site saves rent but may not attract enough customers to survive (2). Competitors matter too: a street with several cafés can be a destination, but a chain next door with lower prices could take most of the custom — overall footfall matters most, provided the rent leaves room for profit (2).
:::

::: q
Recommend whether a growing furniture maker should move from its cramped town-centre workshop to a larger, cheaper unit 20 miles away. Justify your answer. (9)
---
For moving: more space allows higher output and new machinery, rent per square metre is lower, and lorries can deliver raw timber and collect finished furniture more easily than in a town centre (3). Against: skilled craftspeople may not travel 20 miles and are hard to replace; customers who visited the workshop showroom may be lost; moving heavy machinery is costly and halts production for weeks (3). Recommendation: move, because a business that has outgrown its site cannot grow without more space, and furniture is bulky so transport access matters more than a town-centre address; but keep a small town-centre showroom for customers, offer travel help to key staff, and time the move for the quietest trading period (3).
:::

::: watch
- Answer for **this business** — a factory and a café weigh the factors completely differently.
- **Footfall** is the word for retail; **raw materials and transport** for manufacturing; **skilled labour** for professional services.
- Good locations **cost more** — always weigh the trade-off.
- Relocation has **one-off costs and risks** (customers, staff, disruption), not just the rent saving.
- Being near competitors can be an **advantage** — say when.
:::

::: test

:::
