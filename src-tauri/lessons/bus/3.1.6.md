## What it is

A **business plan** is a written document setting out what a business will do, how, and what money it needs. It is written **when starting up** (to test the idea and plan the launch), **to raise finance** (banks and investors will not lend without one), **to set objectives** (targets for the first years) and **to organise the business** — deciding who does what across marketing, operations, finance and HR.

**The main sections of a business plan:**

1. **The business idea** — the product or service, and its USP (what makes it different).
2. **Aims and objectives** — what the business is trying to achieve, with targets.
3. **Market research** — who the customers are, how big the market is, who the competitors are.
4. **Marketing plan** — the product, price, promotion and place (the marketing mix).
5. **Operations** — how the product will be made or the service delivered; premises, equipment, suppliers.
6. **Human resources** — who will run it; staff needed; skills.
7. **Finance** — start-up costs, sources of finance, a cash-flow forecast, forecast sales, costs and profit, break-even.
8. **The owners' details and experience.**

**Benefits of planning**: forces the entrepreneur to research the market and think through problems *before* spending money; identifies the finance needed and helps obtain it; sets targets that progress can be measured against; reduces (but cannot remove) risk; keeps the business focused. **Drawbacks**: takes time and possibly money to produce; forecasts are guesses and may be badly wrong; the plan can become out of date as conditions change; a plan is not a guarantee — following it rigidly may stop the business adapting; some entrepreneurs succeed without one.

**Basic financial terms** — the finance section runs on these:

- **Fixed costs** do **not** change with output: rent, insurance, salaries, loan repayments, advertising. Paid even if nothing is sold.
- **Variable costs** change **directly** with output: raw materials, packaging, piece-rate wages. Variable cost per unit × units made.
- **Total costs** $=$ fixed costs $+$ total variable costs.
- **Revenue** (sales revenue, turnover) $=$ selling price $\times$ quantity sold. Money coming in from sales.
- **Profit** $=$ revenue $-$ total costs. If costs exceed revenue, it is a **loss**.

::: terms
- **Business plan**: a document describing a business, its objectives, its market and how it will operate and be financed.
- **Fixed costs**: costs that do not change with the level of output — rent, insurance, salaries — and must be paid even if nothing is sold.
- **Variable costs**: costs that change directly with the level of output — raw materials, packaging.
- **Total costs**: fixed costs plus total variable costs.
- **Revenue**: the income from sales: selling price × quantity sold. Also called sales revenue or turnover.
- **Profit**: revenue minus total costs, when revenue is greater than costs.
- **Loss**: the amount by which total costs are greater than revenue.
:::

::: method
1. **Why plan?** Give the reason and its consequence: "to raise finance — a bank will only lend if it can see forecast sales and costs showing the loan can be repaid".
2. **Benefits vs drawbacks** for evaluation: planning reduces risk *but* forecasts are estimates; the judgement usually depends on how new and how large the business is.
3. **Fixed or variable?** Ask "does it rise if we make one more unit?" No → fixed. Yes → variable.
4. **Calculations, in this order**: total variable cost = VC per unit × output; total cost = FC + TVC; revenue = price × quantity; profit = revenue − total cost. Write each line and show units. No formulae are given in the exam.
5. **Interpret**: a loss means costs exceed revenue — say what the business could change (price, costs, volume).
:::

::: example State
State two sections you would expect to find in a business plan. (2)

Market research (1); a cash-flow forecast (or marketing plan, objectives, finance) (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a reason to plan
Explain one reason why Hana should write a business plan before opening her nail salon. (3)

To raise finance (1). Hana needs a loan for equipment and the first months' rent, and a bank will want to see forecast sales and costs before lending (1); a plan showing the salon can afford the repayments makes the bank more likely to agree (1).
:::

::: example Calculate
A candle maker has fixed costs of £2,000 a month. Each candle costs £3 in wax, wick and jar and sells for £8. Last month she sold 800 candles. Calculate her total costs, revenue and profit. (4)

Total variable cost = £3 × 800 = £2,400; total costs = £2,000 + £2,400 = £4,400 (2).

Revenue = £8 × 800 = £6,400 (1).

Profit = £6,400 − £4,400 = £2,000 (1).
:::

::: example Classify — fixed or variable
A pizza takeaway pays: rent on its shop; flour, cheese and toppings; the manager's salary; the boxes each pizza is sold in. Classify each as a fixed or a variable cost. (4)

Ask of each: "does it rise if one more pizza is made?"

Rent — fixed: the same every month whether it sells 10 pizzas or 1,000 (1). Flour, cheese and toppings — variable: every extra pizza uses more (1).

Manager's salary — fixed: paid the same however busy the shop is (1). Boxes — variable: one box per pizza sold (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — a loss, and what to change
In January the candle maker above sells only 300 candles. Calculate her profit or loss, and explain one way she could return to profit. (4)

Total costs = £2,000 + (£3 × 300) = £2,000 + £900 = £2,900 (1). Revenue = £8 × 300 = £2,400 (1).

Profit = £2,400 − £2,900 = −£500, a loss of £500 (1).

Her fixed costs stay at £2,000 however few she sells, so low sales months cause losses; she could promote the candles for Valentine's Day to raise sales above the 400 she needs to cover her costs (each candle leaves £5 towards the £2,000 fixed costs, and £2,000 ÷ £5 = 400) (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — is a plan worth it?
Marcus wants to open a food truck and says a business plan is a waste of time because "you can't predict the future". To what extent do you agree? (9)

Marcus has a point: his forecasts of sales are guesses, the plan takes weeks to write while he could be trading, and a rigid plan could stop him reacting to what customers actually want once he opens (3). But a food truck involves buying a vehicle and equipment — a large sum he probably needs to borrow, and no bank will lend without a plan; planning forces him to research where to pitch, what licences he needs and what his costs are, which could reveal that his idea is unprofitable *before* he spends the money; and targets let him judge whether the truck is working (3). Judgement: mostly disagree — the plan's value is in the thinking and the finance it unlocks, not in perfect prediction; he should write a short plan focused on costs, cash flow and location, and treat it as a living document to update as he learns; it depends mainly on whether he needs to borrow (3).
:::

Explain one drawback of business planning. (2)

Classify each as fixed or variable: shop rent, flour for a bakery, the manager's salary, delivery boxes. (4)

::: q
A gym has fixed costs of £15,000 a month, variable costs of £5 per member and 1,200 members paying £30 a month. Calculate monthly profit. (3)
---
Total costs = £15,000 + (£5 × 1,200) = £21,000 (1); revenue = £30 × 1,200 = £36,000 (1); profit = £36,000 − £21,000 = £15,000 (1).
:::

::: q
A business has revenue of £48,000 and total costs of £52,000. Calculate the profit or loss and suggest one action. (3)
---
A loss of £4,000 (1). It could raise prices or cut variable costs by finding a cheaper supplier (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why fixed costs are a problem for a new business with few customers. (3)
---
Fixed costs such as rent must be paid whatever the sales (1); with few customers revenue is low (1), so the business may make a loss until sales grow enough to cover them (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a business plan helps a business set and monitor objectives. (3)
---
The plan states targets such as sales in year one (1); actual results can be compared with them each month (1), showing whether the business is on track or needs to change course (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a cash-flow forecast in a business plan could help a start-up avoid failure. (6)
---
The forecast shows expected money in and out each month, revealing months where outflows exceed inflows — for example paying for stock before customers pay (2). Knowing this in advance, the owner can arrange an overdraft or delay a purchase, rather than running out of cash unexpectedly, which is the most common reason new businesses close (2). Lenders also see that the owner understands the cash position, making them more willing to provide the overdraft that bridges the gap; the forecast only helps, though, if its estimates are realistic — over-optimistic sales figures would hide the problem (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Business plan questions ask why and what is in it** — never to write one.
- **Fixed costs are paid even at zero output.** Variable costs are per unit × output.
- **Revenue is not profit.** Revenue is money in from sales; profit is what is left after all costs.
- Show every calculation line: TVC, TC, revenue, profit — **no formulae are given** in the exam.
- Evaluation of planning: benefits are research, finance and targets; drawbacks are time and unreliable forecasts — judge by the business's size and need for finance.
:::

::: test
Sam plans to open a mobile coffee van. Analyse how a business plan could help Sam's business succeed. (6)
---
Planning forces Sam to research the market — where commuters and events are, what competitors charge — reducing the risk of picking poor locations or prices (2). The finance section shows how much he needs to buy the van and equipment and helps him obtain a loan, as a bank will want to see forecast sales and costs (2). Setting objectives such as sales targets lets him measure progress each month and spot problems early, e.g. if he is not covering his fixed costs (2).
Levels: 5–6 — a developed chain of reasoning applied to the case; 3–4 — some developed points with some application; 1–2 — simple points.
:::

::: test
A new gym has monthly fixed costs of £18,000. Each member pays £30 a month and costs the gym £6 a month in variable costs. It has 700 members. The owner says "we are making a healthy profit". To what extent do you agree? (9)
---
Calculation: revenue = 700 × £30 = £21,000; total costs = £18,000 + (700 × £6) = £22,200; so a loss of £1,200 a month (3).
Against the owner: the gym is making a loss, not a profit — costs exceed revenue; each member contributes £24 towards fixed costs, so it needs 750 members (£18,000 ÷ £24) to break even (3).
Judgement: disagree — the gym is loss-making, though close to breaking even; if it can recruit 50+ more members or cut fixed costs it could become profitable; a new gym often makes early losses while building membership (3).
Levels: 7–9 — both sides developed and applied to the case, with a justified judgement; 4–6 — developed points with some application, a judgement that is not fully supported; 1–3 — simple points, little use of the case.
:::
