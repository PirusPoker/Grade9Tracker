## What it is

**Cash** is the money a business actually has available — in the bank and in the till — to pay its bills. **Cash flow** is the movement of money **into** the business (**inflows**: sales receipts, loans, capital from owners, grants) and **out** of it (**outflows**: wages, rent, suppliers, loan repayments, equipment, tax).

**Cash is not profit.** Profit is revenue minus costs *for a period*, recorded when a sale is made — even if the customer has not yet paid. Cash is what is actually in the bank *now*. A business can be **profitable but run out of cash** because:

- customers buy **on credit** and pay weeks later, while wages and suppliers must be paid now;
- it has spent cash on **stock** or **equipment** that will only earn revenue later;
- it is **growing fast** — more sales mean more stock and wages to fund before the money comes in (overtrading);
- a big customer pays late or not at all (bad debt);
- a loan repayment or tax bill falls due.

Equally, a business can have cash but make a loss (it borrowed, or sold an asset). **More businesses fail from running out of cash than from making losses** — a loss can be survived for a while, but unpaid wages and suppliers stop a business immediately.

**Consequences of cash-flow problems**: cannot pay suppliers → deliveries stop → nothing to sell; cannot pay wages → staff leave; missed loan repayments → the bank calls in the loan; lost early-payment discounts; forced to sell assets or take expensive emergency borrowing; damaged reputation with suppliers and lenders; ultimately **insolvency** and closure. **Value of positive cash flow**: bills paid on time, discounts taken, the ability to invest and expand, less need to borrow, confidence from lenders and suppliers, a cushion against shocks.

**The cash-flow forecast** — a table predicting inflows and outflows month by month:

| | Jan | Feb | Mar |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Total inflows** | 12,000 | 14,000 | 18,000 |
| **Total outflows** | 15,000 | 13,000 | 12,500 |
| **Net cash flow** (inflows − outflows) | −3,000 | 1,000 | 5,500 |
| **Opening balance** (last month's closing) | 5,000 | 2,000 | 3,000 |
| **Closing balance** (opening + net) | 2,000 | 3,000 | 8,500 |

Three rules: **net cash flow = inflows − outflows**; **closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow**; **this month's opening balance = last month's closing balance**. A **negative closing balance** means the business will run out of cash that month and must arrange an overdraft or change its plans. You will be asked to fill in missing figures and interpret them, not to build a whole forecast.

**Why forecast**: to spot shortfalls in advance and arrange an overdraft before they happen; to show lenders the business can repay; to plan large purchases for months with spare cash; to set targets and monitor. **Limits**: based on estimates — sales may be lower or costs higher; unexpected events; only as good as the assumptions.

**Fixing cash-flow problems:**

| Fix | How it helps | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| **Rescheduling payments** — ask suppliers for longer to pay; spread a loan over longer | delays outflows | suppliers may refuse or lose trust; longer loans cost more interest |
| **Overdraft** | covers a short-term gap | expensive; can be withdrawn; not a cure for a long-term problem |
| **Cutting outflows** — reduce stock, delay buying equipment, cut staff hours, find cheaper suppliers | less cash going out | may hurt sales, quality or morale; delays growth |
| **Raising inflows** — chase late payers, offer discounts for prompt payment, sell for cash not credit, run a sales promotion, sell unused assets | more cash in, sooner | discounts cut profit; promotions may cheapen the brand; assets may be needed |
| **New finance** — a loan, share issue or the owner's money | injects cash | interest, repayments or loss of control; lenders may refuse a business already in trouble |
| Also: leasing instead of buying, debt factoring (selling invoices to a factor for immediate cash minus a fee) | | |

Key terms to learn:

- **Cash flow**: the movement of money into and out of a business over a period.
- **Cash inflow**: money coming into the business, e.g. from sales. **Cash outflow**: money leaving it, e.g. wages.
- **Net cash flow**: cash inflows minus cash outflows for a period.
- **Cash-flow forecast**: a prediction of the expected inflows, outflows and balances over future months.
- **Opening balance**: the cash a business has at the start of a period. **Closing balance**: the cash at the end, which becomes the next period's opening balance.
- **Insolvency**: being unable to pay debts when they fall due.

::: method
1. **Cash vs profit** — define both, then give the reason they differ in this case (credit sales, stock, equipment). The exam loves "explain why a profitable business can fail".
2. **Forecast arithmetic**: net = in − out; closing = opening + net; carry closing across to next month's opening. Show each step; a wrong figure carried correctly still earns method marks.
3. **Interpret**: find the month with the lowest or negative closing balance — that is the problem; say what caused it (a big outflow, low inflows) and what the business should do *before* that month.
4. **Fixes**: name the fix, explain how it changes inflows or outflows, and give its drawback. Match short-term fixes (overdraft, chase debts) to short-term problems and finance/restructuring to long-term ones.
5. **Evaluate**: forecasts are estimates; the best response depends on the cause — timing (overdraft) versus fundamental (costs too high, sales too low).
:::

::: example Calculate
Complete the missing figures: April — inflows 20,000, outflows 26,000, opening balance 4,000. May — inflows 30,000, outflows 22,000. (4)

April: net cash flow $= 20{,}000 - 26{,}000 = -6{,}000$ (1); closing balance $= 4{,}000 + (-6{,}000) = -2{,}000$ (1). May: opening balance $= -2{,}000$; net $= 30{,}000 - 22{,}000 = 8{,}000$ (1); closing $= -2{,}000 + 8{,}000 = 6{,}000$ (1). April's negative closing balance means the business needs an overdraft of at least 2,000 for that month.
:::

::: example Explain — cash vs profit
Explain why a profitable furniture maker might still run out of cash. (3)

It sells to shops on 60 days' credit, so a sale is recorded as profit at once but the cash arrives two months later (1). Meanwhile it must pay for timber and wages now (1); if several large orders are in progress at once, more cash is going out than coming in, and the bank balance can hit zero even though every order is profitable (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — consequences
Analyse the consequences for a restaurant of a cash-flow problem. (6)

If the restaurant cannot pay its food suppliers on time, they may refuse further deliveries or demand cash up front, so it cannot serve a full menu and loses customers (2). Wages paid late lead staff to leave, damaging service, and missed rent or loan payments risk eviction or the bank calling in the loan, which could close the business even though it is popular and profitable (2). To survive it may take an expensive emergency overdraft, sell equipment or cut opening hours, each of which raises costs or reduces revenue; a good cash-flow forecast would have shown the shortfall early enough to arrange finance cheaply, so the deeper consequence is lost control over decisions (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — a fix
A wholesaler's forecast shows a negative closing balance for three months because customers pay late. Evaluate whether it should offer a 5% discount for payment within 10 days. (9)

For: the discount brings cash in weeks earlier, turning the negative balances positive without borrowing; it is quick to implement, encourages customers to keep paying promptly, and avoids overdraft interest and the risk of the bank withdrawing facilities (3). Against: 5% off every early-paid invoice is a direct cut in profit — on thin wholesale margins it could remove most of the profit on those sales; some customers would have paid on time anyway and now pay less; and it does not address customers who ignore the discount and still pay late (3). Judgement: worth doing as a targeted, temporary measure for the largest slow payers, combined with firmer credit control — chasing invoices and refusing further credit to persistent late payers — rather than a blanket discount; if the shortfall is only timing, a short overdraft might be cheaper than 5% of revenue, so it depends on the size of the gap and the margins involved (3).
:::

State two cash inflows and two cash outflows for a shop. (4)
---
Inflows: cash sales; a bank loan (or owner's capital, grant). Outflows: rent; wages (or stock purchases, loan repayments).

::: q
Explain the difference between cash and profit. (2)
---
Profit is revenue minus costs over a period, recorded when sales are made (1); cash is the money actually available in the business, which depends on when payments are received and made (1).
:::

::: q
Inflows 9,000; outflows 11,500; opening balance 3,000. Calculate the net cash flow and closing balance. (2)
---
Net $= -2{,}500$ (1); closing $= 3{,}000 - 2{,}500 = 500$ (1).
:::

::: q
A forecast shows a closing balance of −4,000 in July. Explain what this means and one action the business could take. (3)
---
The business will be 4,000 short of cash at the end of July and cannot pay all its bills (1); it could arrange an overdraft of at least 4,000 before July (1) or delay a large purchase into a later month (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason a fast-growing business may face cash-flow problems. (3)
---
Growth means buying more stock and hiring more staff before the extra sales are paid for (1); cash goes out to fund the growth (1) while customers on credit pay weeks later, so the business can run short despite rising profit (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit of positive cash flow. (2)
---
Bills can be paid on time (1), keeping suppliers and lenders confident and allowing early-payment discounts (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one drawback of using an overdraft to solve a cash-flow problem. (2)
---
Interest is high and the bank can withdraw the overdraft at short notice (1), so it is expensive and does not cure a long-term shortage (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a cash-flow forecast helps a business obtain a bank loan. (2)
---
It shows the bank when money will come in and go out (1), demonstrating that the business can afford the repayments, which makes the bank more willing to lend (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a cash-flow forecast may prove inaccurate. (2)
---
It is based on estimates of sales and costs (1); customers may buy less or pay later, or costs may rise unexpectedly (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a manufacturer could improve its cash flow without borrowing. (6)
---
It could chase late-paying customers, offer small discounts for prompt payment and stop giving credit to poor payers, so cash from sales arrives sooner (2). It could cut outflows by holding less stock — buying materials just in time — negotiating longer credit from suppliers, and delaying purchases of new equipment or leasing it instead of buying (2). Selling unused machinery raises a lump sum. Each measure has a cost — discounts cut profit, lower stock risks shortages, suppliers may refuse longer terms — so the manufacturer should combine the cheapest ones first and use the forecast to check whether they close the gap (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Cash is not profit.** Be able to explain why a profitable business can fail — credit sales, stock, equipment, growth.
- Forecast rules: **net = in − out; closing = opening + net; closing carries to next opening.** Show the working.
- A **negative closing balance** = the problem month; act **before** it, not in it.
- Every fix has a **drawback**; match short-term fixes to timing problems and finance or cost changes to fundamental ones.
- Forecasts are **estimates** — a standard evaluation point.
:::
