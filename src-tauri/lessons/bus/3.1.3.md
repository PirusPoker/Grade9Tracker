## What it is

An **aim** is the overall goal a business is working towards ("to be the leading coffee chain in the north"). An **objective** is a specific, measurable target that helps achieve it ("open 10 new shops by 2028"). Good objectives are **SMART** — specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-bound.

**The main objectives:**

| Objective | What it means | Who typically has it |
|---|---|---|
| **Survival** | staying in business — covering costs, keeping cash coming in | new businesses; any business in a recession or facing a new competitor |
| **Profit maximisation** | making as much profit as possible | established private businesses; plcs under shareholder pressure |
| **Growth** | increasing sales, outlets, staff or market share | businesses that have survived and want economies of scale |
| **Market share** | a bigger percentage of the market's total sales | businesses in competitive markets — supermarkets, phone makers |
| **Customer satisfaction** | meeting customers' needs so they return and recommend | service businesses; anyone wanting repeat sales |
| **Social and ethical objectives** | fair treatment of workers and suppliers, sustainability, helping the community | social enterprises, charities, increasingly all brands |
| **Shareholder value** | rising share price and dividends | plcs |

**Why set objectives?** They give the business a **direction** and everyone a target to work towards; they let managers **measure success** (was the target hit?); they help **decision-making** (does this choice move us towards the objective?); they **motivate** staff; and they are needed to **get finance** — a bank wants to see the plan.

**Why objectives differ** between businesses:

- **Size** — a start-up needs survival; a large plc pursues growth and shareholder value.
- **Competition** — in a crowded market the objective may be market share or customer satisfaction; a monopoly can chase profit.
- **Type of business** — a charity's objective is social; a family firm may value independence over growth; a plc must satisfy shareholders.
- **The owners' personal goals** — some want a comfortable living, not an empire.
- **Sector and product** — a luxury brand protects image; a discount retailer chases volume.

**Why objectives change** over time: a business that has *survived* its first years moves to *growth* and *profit*; a *recession* sends even large firms back to survival; new *competition* shifts the focus to market share; reaching one target (10 shops) leads to the next (50); a change of ownership or a new CEO; changing public expectations push ethical objectives up the list.

**Measuring success** — not only profit. Success can be judged by revenue and sales growth, market share, customer satisfaction scores and reviews, staff turnover, meeting the social aim (meals provided, carbon cut), survival itself, or the owner's own satisfaction. The right measure is the one that matches the objective the business set.

::: terms
- **Aim**: the long-term goal a business is working towards.
- **Objective**: a specific, measurable target that helps a business achieve its aim.
- **SMART objective**: an objective that is specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound.
- **Survival**: the objective of continuing to trade — covering costs and keeping enough cash to pay the bills — especially in the early years or hard times.
- **Profit maximisation**: making the largest possible profit — the biggest gap between revenue and costs.
- **Growth**: increasing the size of the business — its sales, number of outlets, staff or market share.
- **Market share**: a business's sales as a percentage of the total sales in its market.
- **Customer satisfaction**: meeting or exceeding customers' expectations so they buy again and recommend the business.
- **Social and ethical objectives**: targets about treating people and the environment fairly — paying suppliers fairly, cutting pollution, helping the community.
- **Shareholder value**: the return to shareholders from dividends and a rising share price.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the objective, then explain what it looks like in practice for this business** — "survival: covering the rent and wages each month while the café builds regular customers".
2. **"Why might X have objective Y?"** — link to size, age, competition, type of ownership. New = survival; plc = shareholder value; charity = social.
3. **"Why might objectives change?"** — give the trigger (survived, recession, new rival, new owner) and the shift (from what to what).
4. **Measuring success**: pick the measure that matches the objective; a 9-marker on "is the business successful?" should judge against *its* objectives, not profit alone.
5. **Objectives can conflict**: growth may cut short-term profit; ethical sourcing raises costs. Use this for evaluation.
:::

::: example Define
What is meant by a "business objective"? (2)

A specific, measurable target (1) that a business sets to help it achieve its overall aim, such as increasing sales by 10% this year (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why survival
Explain why survival is likely to be the main objective of a newly opened restaurant. (3)

A new restaurant has few regular customers and high start-up costs — fitting out the kitchen, stock, staff wages (1). Until customers know it exists, revenue may not cover costs, so it may make a loss at first (1); the priority is keeping enough cash coming in to pay bills and stay open until it becomes established (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — objectives changing
Fern's Cakes has grown from a market stall to three shops in five years. Analyse how its objectives are likely to have changed. (6)

At the market stall the objective was survival: Fern needed to sell enough each week to cover the stall fee and ingredients, with profit a secondary concern (2). Once regular customers were established and the business made a reliable profit, the objective shifted to growth — opening shops to reach more customers and gain economies of scale in buying ingredients (2). With three shops the objectives will be broader: profit to fund further expansion, customer satisfaction to keep the reputation consistent across all three, and possibly market share against local competitors; each stage was triggered by achieving the previous objective (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — measuring success
A community bicycle-repair social enterprise made a small loss last year but repaired 2,000 bikes for people on low incomes and trained 15 unemployed people as mechanics. Its manager says it has been a success. To what extent do you agree? (9)

Against: a loss cannot continue indefinitely — the enterprise must at least cover its costs to survive, and a loss suggests its pricing or costs need attention; from a financial viewpoint it has not succeeded (3). For: its objectives are social — affordable repairs and training the unemployed — and on those measures it clearly succeeded: 2,000 repairs and 15 people with new skills, some of whom may now be employed; judging a social enterprise on profit is measuring the wrong thing (3). Judgement: largely a success, because it achieved the objectives it set, but only sustainable if the loss is small and temporary; the fair verdict depends on whether it can reach break-even next year while keeping its social output — success should be measured against both its social aims and its survival (3).
:::

::: example Explain — why objectives differ
Explain why the objectives of a large supermarket plc are likely to differ from those of a sole trader who runs a village café. (4)

The supermarket is a plc owned by thousands of shareholders who expect a return, so its objectives are likely to be shareholder value and growth in market share against rivals such as Aldi and Lidl (2). The café owner owns the business alone and is small and new to the market, so her objectives are more likely to be survival and a comfortable personal income, with non-financial aims such as independence or being part of the village — she has no shareholders to satisfy and no realistic chance of competing for market share nationally (2).
:::

::: example Apply — writing a SMART objective
A gym's owner says his aim is "to get more members". Rewrite this as a SMART objective and explain one benefit of doing so. (3)

"Increase membership from 400 to 480 — a 20% rise — by 31 December next year" (1): it is specific (members), measurable (480), and time-bound (a date), and 20% is achievable for an established gym. Benefit: progress can be checked month by month (1), so if the gym is behind by, say, June, the owner can act — a promotion or a referral scheme — rather than finding out too late (1).
:::

::: q
State two objectives a business might have. (2)
---
Survival; growth (or profit maximisation, market share, customer satisfaction, social/ethical, shareholder value).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by "market share" as an objective. (2)
---
Aiming to increase the percentage of the market's total sales that the business makes (1), e.g. from 10% to 15% of UK crisp sales (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why a business sets objectives. (3)
---
To measure success (1): a target such as 500 sales a month can be compared with what actually happened (1), so managers know whether their decisions are working and what to change (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a plc is more likely than a sole trader to have shareholder value as an objective. (3)
---
A plc's shares are owned by the public, who expect dividends and a rising share price (1); if returns are poor they sell, the share price falls and directors risk losing their jobs (1). A sole trader has no shareholders and can pursue personal goals such as a comfortable income (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a recession might change a business's objectives. (3)
---
Falling consumer spending cuts sales and profit (1), so a business aiming for growth may switch to survival (1), cutting costs and protecting cash rather than opening new outlets (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way, other than profit, that success could be measured for a hotel. (2)
---
Customer satisfaction, e.g. review scores or the percentage of repeat bookings (1), because satisfied guests return and recommend, which shows the service is working (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse why a small independent bookshop and a national bookshop chain might have different objectives. (6)
---
The independent is a small business whose owner may value personal satisfaction and a steady income; with limited finance its realistic objectives are survival and customer satisfaction — loyal local readers who keep it going against online rivals (2). The chain is likely a plc with shareholders expecting returns, so its objectives are profit, market share and growth, using its size to negotiate discounts and open stores (2). Size, ownership and competition therefore push them in different directions: the independent cannot compete on price so competes on service, while the chain competes on scale — each sets the objectives its situation makes achievable (2).
:::

::: q
Evaluate whether profit maximisation should be the main objective of a family-run garden centre. (9)
---
For: profit funds reinvestment, pays the family a living and provides a cushion against bad years; without profit the business cannot survive, so pursuing it keeps costs controlled and prices sensible (3). Against: maximising profit might mean cutting staff, raising prices or reducing quality, which could drive away the loyal local customers a garden centre depends on; the family may value independence, reputation and enjoyment of the work more than the last pound of profit; ethical and community objectives may matter to them (3). Judgement: profit should be an important objective but not the overriding one — a satisfactory profit combined with customer satisfaction and the family's own goals is more likely to keep the business healthy long term; it depends on the family's finances and how competitive the local market is (3).
:::

::: watch
- **Aim = broad goal; objective = measurable target.** Do not use them interchangeably in a definition.
- Link every objective to the **business's situation** — age, size, ownership, competition.
- Objectives **change** — give the trigger and the direction of the change.
- Judge success against the **objective actually set**; profit is not the only measure and is the wrong one for not-for-profits.
- SMART is useful vocabulary for "why is this a good objective?".
:::

So the business can check whether it has achieved them (1); e.g. 'increase sales by 10% this year' can be measured, 'do better' cannot (1).

A charity aims to help its cause, e.g. reduce homelessness (1); a supermarket aims for profit, growth and market share (1).
:::

Explain one reason why a business might change its objectives over time. (2)
---
Once it has survived, a business may switch to growth or profit (1); or external changes such as a recession force it to focus on survival again (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between an aim and an objective. (2)
---
An aim is the overall long-term goal, e.g. to be the market leader (1); objectives are specific measurable targets that help achieve the aim, e.g. open five new stores this year (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how objectives help a business measure its success. (2)
---
Actual results can be compared with the targets set (1); the business can see where it is falling short and take action (1).
:::

Explain one way a business might benefit from having a social objective. (2)
---
Customers who value ethical behaviour are attracted (1); staff are motivated and the business builds a good reputation (1).
:::

Analyse how the objectives of a business might change as it grows from a sole trader to a plc. (4)
---
A sole trader focuses on survival and a comfortable income (1); as it grows, growth and market share become priorities (1); as a plc, shareholders demand profit and dividends (1); objectives become more formal and financial, possibly at the expense of the founder's personal goals (1).
:::
