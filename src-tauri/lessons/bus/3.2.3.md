## What it is

The **economic climate** is the state of the economy a business operates in. Three parts are examined: **interest rates**, **employment levels** and **consumer spending (and income)**. The exam does not ask *why* these change — only what happens to a business when they do.

**Interest rates** — the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving, expressed as a percentage.

*Effect on a business that has borrowed*: a rise raises loan and overdraft repayments → costs rise, profit falls, less cash for investment; a business with a large variable-rate loan can be pushed into loss. A fall does the opposite. A business *planning* to borrow (to expand, buy machinery) finds investment cheaper when rates are low, so investment rises when rates fall.

*Effect on consumers*: higher rates raise mortgage and credit repayments, so households have **less to spend**; saving becomes more attractive; buying on credit (cars, furniture, kitchens) falls sharply. Businesses selling expensive items bought on credit suffer most; those selling cheap essentials are hardly affected. Lower rates boost spending, especially on big-ticket goods.

*Effect on other businesses*: customers who are businesses cut orders when rates rise, so suppliers of machinery and business services see demand fall too.

**Employment levels** — the share of people in work.

*High employment / low unemployment*: consumers have incomes → **more spending** → more sales; but workers are scarce → **wages rise**, recruitment is harder, staff turnover rises (they can move for better pay). *High unemployment*: sales fall, especially for non-essentials; but labour is plentiful and cheaper to hire; government may raise taxes or cut spending, hitting demand further. A business's own decisions affect employment — expanding creates jobs, automation removes them.

**Consumer spending and income** — the total spent by households, driven by income, taxes, confidence, interest rates and employment.

*As incomes rise*: spending shifts **from cheaper to better** — from own-brand to branded, from bus to car, from a UK holiday to abroad; demand for luxuries rises fastest. *As incomes fall* (recession): consumers **trade down** — discount supermarkets, own brands, repairs instead of replacements, staying in instead of going out. Businesses selling **necessities** or cheap substitutes (Aldi, Primark, fast food) can do *better* in a downturn; those selling **luxuries** (restaurants, holidays, new cars, designer goods) do worse. A business can respond by cutting costs, launching a value range, or moving upmarket when incomes rise.

Key terms to learn:

- **Interest rate**: the cost of borrowing money, or the reward for saving it, expressed as a percentage.
- **Level of employment**: the proportion of the working population that has a job.
- **Consumer spending**: the total amount households spend on goods and services.
- **Consumer income**: the money households have available to spend.
- **Recession**: a period of falling output and spending in the economy.

::: method
1. **Follow the effect through to *this* business.** Interest rate up → *its* loan repayments up → *its* profit down; or → *its* customers spend less → *its* sales down. Say which route applies and why.
2. **Ask: has the business borrowed? What does it sell? Who buys it?** A luxury seller with a big loan is hit twice by a rate rise; a debt-free discounter may gain.
3. **Employment**: two sides — demand for the business's products *and* the supply and cost of its workers.
4. **Income changes**: use "trade up / trade down" and name the product type — necessity, luxury, cheap substitute.
5. **Evaluate**: the effect depends on the type of product, the level of borrowing, and how the business responds (cost cuts, value ranges, new markets).
:::

::: example Explain — interest rates and a borrower
Explain one effect of a rise in interest rates on a hotel with a large bank loan. (3)

Higher repayments (1): the interest on the loan increases, so the hotel's monthly costs rise (1), reducing its profit and leaving less money for refurbishment or marketing (1).
:::

::: example Explain — interest rates and consumers
Explain how a fall in interest rates might affect a furniture retailer. (3)

Customers' mortgage repayments fall and credit becomes cheaper (1), so they have more to spend and are more willing to buy expensive items such as sofas on finance (1); the retailer's sales and revenue rise (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — unemployment
Analyse the effect of rising unemployment on a chain of restaurants. (6)

More people out of work means household incomes fall, and eating out is one of the first non-essentials cut, so customer numbers and revenue fall — particularly at the chain's higher-priced restaurants (2). With lower revenue and unchanged fixed costs such as rent, profit margins shrink; the chain may cut opening hours, reduce staff or close weaker branches (2). On the other side, a larger pool of job seekers makes it easier to recruit waiting and kitchen staff at lower wages, cutting labour costs; the chain could also respond by launching cheaper set menus to attract customers who are trading down, so the overall effect depends on how well it adapts its offer (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — rising incomes
Consumer incomes in a region are rising strongly. Evaluate whether a discount clothing retailer there should move upmarket. (9)

For moving upmarket: as incomes rise, customers trade up to better-quality, branded clothes, so the discount range may lose sales; higher-priced lines carry bigger margins; a broader range keeps existing customers as their tastes change (3). Against: the retailer's brand and reputation are built on low prices, and confusing that image could lose its core customers without convincing wealthier shoppers who prefer established brands; upmarket stock costs more and sells more slowly; incomes can fall again in the next downturn, when discounters do well (3). Judgement: it should not abandon its position but could add a slightly higher-quality range within its stores to capture trading-up customers while keeping value lines; the safest response depends on how long income growth lasts and on whether its stores and staff can present a higher-quality range credibly (3).
:::

::: q
State two parts of the economic climate that affect businesses. (2)
---
Interest rates; the level of employment (or consumer spending/income).
:::

::: q
Explain one effect of a rise in interest rates on consumer spending. (2)
---
Mortgage and loan repayments rise, leaving households less disposable income (1), so spending, especially on items bought on credit, falls (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a business might delay expansion when interest rates rise. (2)
---
Borrowing to fund the expansion becomes more expensive (1), so the expected profit may no longer cover the interest, making the investment less worthwhile (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one effect of high employment on a business's costs. (3)
---
Wages rise (1): with few unemployed workers, businesses compete for staff (1), so they must offer higher pay to recruit and keep employees, raising costs (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a discount supermarket might see sales rise during a recession. (3)
---
Falling incomes make consumers trade down (1): they switch from premium supermarkets to cheaper stores and own brands (1), so the discounter gains customers and sales even as total spending falls (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one effect of rising consumer incomes on a luxury car dealer. (2)
---
Customers can afford expensive purchases and trade up from cheaper cars (1), so the dealer's sales and profit rise (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a business could respond to falling consumer spending. (3)
---
Launch a value range (1): cheaper versions of its products keep customers who are trading down (1), protecting sales volume even if the profit per item is lower (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a rise in interest rates could affect a house-building company. (6)
---
Builders borrow heavily to buy land and fund construction before houses are sold, so higher rates raise their interest costs directly, cutting profit on each development (2). Customers need mortgages, and higher rates make monthly repayments unaffordable for some, so demand for new houses falls and the builder may have to cut prices or leave homes unsold, tying up cash (2). The company might respond by slowing new projects, building smaller, cheaper homes, or offering incentives to buyers; the overall effect is strongly negative because it is hit from both the cost and the demand side, more so than a business selling cheap essentials (2).
:::

::: watch
- You are asked **what happens to the business**, never why rates or employment change.
- Interest rates work through **two routes**: the business's own borrowing and its customers' spending. Say which applies.
- Employment affects **sales and the labour supply** — give both sides.
- Income changes: **trade up / trade down** — and identify whether the product is a **necessity, luxury or cheap substitute**.
- Evaluations turn on **what the business sells, how much it has borrowed, and how it responds**.
:::
