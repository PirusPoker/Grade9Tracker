## What it is

**Recruitment** is finding and attracting people to fill a vacancy; **selection** is choosing the best of them. Getting it right matters: a good hire raises productivity, quality and morale; a bad one costs money, disrupts the team and has to be repeated.

**Internal recruitment** — filling the post from existing employees (promotion or transfer). *Advantages*: cheaper and quicker — no advertising or agency fees; the candidate is known, so less risk; they already understand the business, so little induction; motivates staff who see a path to promotion. *Disadvantages*: a smaller pool of candidates; no new ideas or skills from outside; leaves another vacancy to fill; can cause resentment among colleagues who were not chosen.

**External recruitment** — attracting candidates from outside, through job websites, social media, newspapers, recruitment agencies, job centres, universities, or headhunting. *Advantages*: a wider choice of candidates; brings **new skills, experience and ideas**; no gap left behind; can find specialists the business lacks. *Disadvantages*: **expensive** (adverts, agencies, interview time) and **slow**; the candidate is unknown, so higher risk of a poor fit; longer induction; can demotivate insiders passed over.

**The stages of recruitment and selection:**

1. **Job analysis** — deciding whether the vacancy needs filling and what the job actually involves.
2. **Job description** — a document describing the **job**: title, duties and responsibilities, hours, pay, who it reports to.
3. **Person specification** — a document describing the **person** needed: qualifications, skills, experience and personal qualities, usually split into *essential* and *desirable*.
4. **Advertising** the vacancy, internally and/or externally.
5. **Selection** — application forms and CVs shortlisted against the person specification; then **interviews**, **tests** (skills, aptitude, psychometric), **assessment centres** and **references**. Selection must be fair and non-discriminatory under the Equality Act.
6. **Appointment** and the **contract of employment**.

**What effective recruitment gives the business**: the right skills, so higher productivity and quality; lower staff turnover, because well-matched people stay; lower costs of re-recruiting and training; better customer service; a motivated team; new ideas from outside hires; legal compliance.

**Contracts of employment** — a legal agreement setting out pay, hours, holidays, duties and notice. The main types:

| Contract | Meaning | Benefits for the business | Drawbacks / issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Full-time** | usually 35–40 hours a week | committed, available staff; easier to train and manage | high fixed wage cost; less flexible |
| **Part-time** | fewer hours, e.g. 16–25 a week | covers peaks (weekends, evenings), lower cost, attracts parents and students, flexibility | more staff to manage and train; communication harder; less continuity |
| **Job share** | two people split one full-time job | keeps skilled staff who cannot work full time; cover for holidays | coordination and handover between the two; double training |
| **Zero-hour** | no guaranteed hours; the business offers work when needed and the worker may accept or refuse | pay only for hours worked; maximum flexibility for seasonal or unpredictable demand | workers have insecure income, so low motivation and loyalty; may be unavailable when needed; reputational and legal criticism |
| Also: **permanent** vs **temporary / fixed-term** (a set period — a project, maternity cover) and **freelance / self-employed** contractors | | | |

::: terms
- **Recruitment**: the process of attracting suitable people to apply for a vacancy.
- **Selection**: choosing the most suitable candidate from the applicants.
- **Internal recruitment**: filling a vacancy with someone who already works for the business, by promotion or transfer.
- **External recruitment**: filling a vacancy with someone from outside the business.
- **Job description**: a document that describes the job — its title, duties, responsibilities, hours and pay.
- **Person specification**: a document that describes the person needed — the qualifications, skills, experience and qualities of the ideal candidate.
- **Shortlisting**: choosing which applicants to interview by comparing their applications with the person specification.
- **Reference**: a statement from a previous employer or teacher about a candidate's reliability and ability.
- **Contract of employment**: a legal agreement between employer and employee setting out pay, hours, holidays, duties and notice.
- **Full-time**: working a full working week, usually 35–40 hours.
- **Part-time**: working fewer hours than a full working week.
- **Job share**: an arrangement where two part-time employees share the duties of one full-time job.
- **Zero-hour contract**: a contract with no guaranteed hours; the employee works only when the business offers work.
- **Staff turnover**: the rate at which employees leave a business and have to be replaced.
:::

::: method
1. **Internal vs external**: cost, speed, risk (known vs unknown), new ideas, effect on other staff. Recommend based on whether the skill exists inside and how much the business needs fresh thinking.
2. **Job description = the job; person specification = the person.** Do not mix them; the exam loves this distinction.
3. **Stages in order**, and say what each achieves — the person specification lets applicants be shortlisted fairly, for example.
4. **Contracts**: match the type to the demand pattern — steady → full-time; peaks → part-time; unpredictable or seasonal → zero-hour; project → temporary. Always give the worker's side of zero-hour contracts.
5. **"Why does effective recruitment matter?"** — chain to productivity, turnover, cost and service.
:::

::: example Explain — the two documents
Explain the difference between a job description and a person specification. (2)

A job description sets out the duties, responsibilities and conditions of the job itself (1); a person specification sets out the qualifications, skills and qualities the ideal candidate should have (1).
:::

::: example Explain — internal recruitment
Explain one advantage of internal recruitment for a supermarket promoting a supervisor to store manager. (3)

Lower risk (1): the supermarket already knows the supervisor's abilities and attitude from their work record (1), so it is less likely to appoint someone who turns out unsuitable, and they need little induction since they know the store's systems (1).
:::

::: example Describe — the recruitment process
A dental practice needs a new receptionist. Describe the stages it should go through to recruit and select one. (6)

Job analysis and job description: the practice decides what the receptionist will do — booking appointments, taking payments, greeting patients — and writes it down with the hours and pay (1).

Person specification: it sets out the person needed — essential: good communication and IT skills; desirable: experience on a medical reception desk (1).

Advertising: it advertises on a job website and in the local area, since receptionists are usually recruited locally (1).

Shortlisting: applications are compared with the person specification and the best four or five are invited to interview (1).

Interview and tests: candidates answer the same questions and might role-play booking a nervous patient, so they are judged fairly against each other (1).

References and appointment: the practice checks references from previous employers, offers the job and issues a contract of employment (1).
:::

::: example Explain — part-time staff
Explain one benefit to a cinema of employing most of its staff part-time. (3)

Flexibility to match busy times (1): a cinema is busiest in the evenings, at weekends and in school holidays (1), so part-time staff can be rostered for those peaks only, keeping enough people on duty when customers arrive without paying for staff standing idle on quiet weekday mornings (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — zero-hour contracts
Analyse the effects on a seaside café of employing its staff on zero-hour contracts. (6)

Demand depends on weather and season, so zero-hour contracts let the owner call in staff on busy sunny days and pay nothing on wet ones, keeping wage costs in line with sales and protecting profit in a business with unpredictable trade (2). But workers with no guaranteed income may take other jobs and be unavailable when called, or leave for secure work, so the café faces high turnover, constant retraining and inconsistent service (2). Motivation and loyalty are likely to be low, which shows in customer service; the café might combine a small core of part-time staff on guaranteed hours with zero-hour extras for peaks, balancing flexibility against reliability (2).
:::

::: example Recommend — internal or external
A software company needs a new head of marketing to lead a push into the US market. Its current marketing manager is capable but has only worked in the UK. Recommend whether it should recruit internally or externally. Justify your answer. (9)

Internal: promoting the current manager is quick and cheap, rewards loyalty, motivates the team and keeps knowledge of the company's products and culture; the risk is known (3). External: the US push needs experience the current manager lacks — knowledge of US customers, media and contacts — which an external hire could bring, along with fresh ideas; but it costs more, takes months, the candidate is unknown, and the current manager may leave if passed over (3). Recommendation: recruit externally, because the objective is a new market where specific experience is essential and the cost of a failed US launch far exceeds recruitment costs; the company should promote the current manager to a senior UK role to retain them, and could reconsider if a candidate with genuine US experience cannot be found (3).
:::

It gives criteria to shortlist applicants against (1), so selection is fair, consistent and based on what the job needs (1).

Explain one advantage of part-time contracts for a restaurant. (3)
---

::: q
Explain one drawback of a job share for the business. (2)
---
The two employees must hand over work and coordinate (1), which takes time and can cause inconsistency or things being missed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one drawback of a zero-hour contract for the employee. (2)
---
No guaranteed income (1), making it hard to budget or get a mortgage, and hours may vanish without notice (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse why a business should follow all the stages of the recruitment process rather than simply interviewing people it knows. (6)
---
Job analysis and a job description make the business decide exactly what the role involves, avoiding hiring for a vacancy that no longer exists or has changed (2). A person specification gives objective criteria, so applicants are shortlisted on skills rather than personal connections; this widens the pool, finds better candidates and keeps the business within equality law, avoiding discrimination claims (2). Structured selection — interviews, tests, references — reduces the chance of appointing someone who cannot do the job; the process costs time, but a poor appointment costs far more in lost productivity, disruption and repeating the recruitment (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Job description describes the job; person specification describes the person.** Never swap them.
- Internal vs external: **cost, speed, risk, new ideas, effect on other staff** — apply to the vacancy in question.
- Contracts: **match the type to the demand pattern** and give both sides of zero-hour contracts (flexibility vs insecurity).
- Effective recruitment matters because of **productivity, turnover and cost** — chain them.
- Selection must be **fair under the Equality Act** — a free mark in many answers.
:::

---
The supermarket pays staff only for the hours it needs, so labour costs fall and it can easily cover busy periods such as Christmas (2). But staff have insecure incomes, so motivation and loyalty may be low, leading to poor customer service and high staff turnover, raising recruitment and training costs (2). Workers may be unavailable when needed because they have other jobs, and the supermarket may attract criticism and damage its reputation for treating staff unfairly (2).

::: test
A large hotel needs a new head chef. It could promote its current sous-chef or advertise the role externally. Evaluate which method of recruitment the hotel should use. (9)
---
Internal: cheaper and quicker; the sous-chef knows the kitchen, menu and staff, so little induction; lower risk as the hotel knows their work; motivates other staff by showing a promotion path (3).
External: wider pool of candidates; could bring new ideas, cooking styles and experience of running a kitchen; the sous-chef may lack leadership experience; but it is costly, slower and riskier, and could demotivate the sous-chef if passed over (3).
Judgement: justified — e.g. advertise externally but invite the sous-chef to apply, so the best candidate is chosen fairly; depends on the sous-chef's experience and whether the hotel wants to change its menu (3).
Levels: 7–9 — both sides developed and applied to the case, with a justified judgement; 4–6 — developed points with some application, a judgement that is not fully supported; 1–3 — simple points, little use of the case.
:::
