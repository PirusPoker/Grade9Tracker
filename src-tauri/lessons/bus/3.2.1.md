## What it is

Technology changes how businesses sell, talk to customers and run themselves. Two areas are examined: **e-commerce** and **digital communication**.

**E-commerce** is buying and selling online — through a website, an app or a marketplace (Amazon, Etsy, eBay). **M-commerce** is the same on a mobile.

*How it helps a business*: reaches a **wider market** — national and global customers, 24 hours a day, without opening shops; **lower costs** — no high-street rent, fewer sales staff, automated ordering; **data** on what customers browse and buy, so marketing can be targeted; easy to **compare and change prices**; small businesses can compete with large ones. *Costs and drawbacks*: building and maintaining a website or app; delivery costs and returns; **more competition**, since customers can compare prices instantly; security and fraud risks; customers cannot touch or try the product; technical failures stop all sales; needs staff with digital skills.

**Digital communication** — email, websites, social media (Instagram, TikTok, X), messaging and live chat, video calls, cloud documents, mobile apps.

*How it changes dealings with stakeholders*:

- **Customers** — instant enquiries and complaints via social media and chat; targeted advertising; reviews visible to all; loyalty apps and personalised offers.
- **Employees** — remote and flexible working; instant messaging and video meetings replace travel; but always-on pressure and less face-to-face contact.
- **Suppliers** — electronic ordering and payment; real-time stock data shared, enabling just-in-time; quicker responses.
- **Owners and managers** — live sales and cost data for faster decisions; cheaper communication across sites.

*Benefits*: faster, cheaper, reaches more people, measurable (clicks, opens, followers), two-way. *Drawbacks*: negative publicity spreads fast; staff time to manage accounts; security and data-protection responsibilities; can be impersonal; some customers prefer phone or face-to-face.

**Real examples the spec expects you to name**: online ordering apps (Domino's, supermarket delivery), click-and-collect, contactless and mobile payment, social-media marketing (a bakery on Instagram), customer-service chatbots, cloud accounting software, video conferencing, online marketplaces, CRM databases, barcode and RFID stock systems.

**Weighing the decision to adopt technology**: benefits (more sales, lower costs, better service, data) against costs (initial investment, training, maintenance, security, staff resistance, redundancies) — and the risk of *not* adopting when competitors do.

::: terms
- **E-commerce**: buying and selling goods and services over the internet.
- **M-commerce**: e-commerce carried out on mobile devices such as phones and tablets.
- **Online marketplace**: a website where many different sellers list their products for customers to buy — Amazon, eBay, Etsy.
- **Click-and-collect**: ordering online and collecting the goods from a shop or collection point.
- **Digital communication**: exchanging information electronically — email, websites, social media, messaging, video calls.
- **Social media**: online platforms where users share content and interact, used by businesses for marketing and customer service.
:::

::: method
1. **Name a specific technology and a real use** — "an ordering app like Domino's", not "the internet".
2. **E-commerce chain**: technology → wider market / lower cost → more sales or profit → applied to the case. Then a drawback: competition, delivery, set-up cost.
3. **Stakeholder questions**: pick the stakeholder, name the channel, say what changes for them — and give the two-way effect (faster service for customers, but complaints are public).
4. **Benefits vs costs for adopting**: initial cost and training against long-term savings and sales; conclude with "depends on" the size of the business, its customers' habits and what competitors are doing.
5. **Small business angle**: e-commerce lets a tiny firm sell nationally, but exposes it to global competition.
:::

::: example Explain — e-commerce
Explain one benefit to a small pottery business of selling through its own website. (3)

Wider market (1). Customers anywhere in the country — or abroad — can buy without visiting the studio (1), so sales are no longer limited to local passing trade and revenue can grow without opening a shop (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a drawback
Explain one drawback of e-commerce for a bookshop. (3)

More competition (1): online, customers can compare its prices instantly with large retailers such as Amazon (1), so it may have to cut prices or lose sales, reducing profit margins (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — digital communication and customers
Analyse how using social media might affect a restaurant's relationship with its customers. (6)

Posting photos of dishes and offers on Instagram reaches customers cheaply and instantly, and customers can message to book or ask questions, so engagement rises and new customers are attracted by shares and tags (2). Customers also post reviews and complaints publicly; a quick, polite response can turn a complaint into loyalty and shows other users the restaurant cares, strengthening its reputation (2). But a bad review or a mishandled reply can spread widely within hours, and managing accounts takes staff time every day — so the relationship becomes closer and more public, which is a benefit only if the restaurant responds well (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — adopting technology
A family-run hardware shop is considering spending £15,000 on a website with online ordering. Evaluate whether it should. (9)

For: online ordering reaches customers beyond the town, lets regulars order outside opening hours, and click-and-collect brings them into the shop; competitors already sell online, so without it the shop risks losing customers who expect to order digitally; the site also gives data on what sells (3). Against: £15,000 is a large sum for a small shop and the site will need updating and stock-keeping every day; deliveries of heavy hardware are costly; online it competes directly with national chains on price, where it cannot win; its older customers may prefer to visit and ask advice (3). Judgement: it should go online, but modestly — a simpler site with click-and-collect rather than full delivery, keeping the cost down and playing to its strength of personal advice; the decision depends on whether its customers actually shop online and whether the £15,000 could be cut by using an off-the-shelf platform (3).
:::

::: example Explain — digital communication with suppliers and staff
A chain of 12 florists has started using a shared online system: shops record their stock, the wholesaler sees it and delivers automatically, and managers talk by video call instead of meeting at head office. Explain one benefit for the business's dealings with its suppliers and one with its employees. (4)

Suppliers: the wholesaler sees each shop's stock in real time, so it can deliver flowers before they run out without being asked (1); the shops hold less stock and throw away fewer wilted flowers, cutting costs (1).

Employees: managers no longer lose half a day travelling to meetings (1), so they spend more time in their shops serving customers and the chain saves on travel costs (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how m-commerce has changed the way a clothing retailer sells to customers. (6)
---
Customers can browse and buy from their phones anywhere, so sales happen at any time and impulse purchases from social-media adverts rise; the retailer can send push notifications with offers straight to the customer (2). The app collects data on browsing and purchases, letting the retailer personalise recommendations and target promotions, raising conversion rates and repeat purchases (2). The retailer must, however, invest in an app that works smoothly, handle high return rates for clothes bought unseen, and compete on screen with every other brand a customer can swipe to — so m-commerce raises both reach and competitive pressure (2).
:::

::: test
Pure Soap is a small business selling handmade soap at local craft fairs. It is considering selling through Etsy, an online marketplace. Analyse the effects of this decision on Pure Soap. (6)
---
Etsy gives access to customers across the UK and abroad, far beyond local fairs, so sales and revenue could grow quickly without the cost of building its own website (2). But Etsy charges fees on each sale and Pure Soap must pay for packaging and postage, reducing its profit margin per bar; it must also produce more soap to keep up with orders (2). On Etsy, customers can compare it instantly with many other soap sellers, so it must compete on photos, reviews and price; good reviews build a reputation, bad ones spread quickly (2).
Levels: 5–6 — a developed chain of reasoning applied to the case; 3–4 — some developed points with some application; 1–2 — simple points.

::: test
A family-run cinema is deciding whether to spend £20,000 on an app for booking tickets and ordering snacks to the seat. Evaluate whether it should make this investment. (9)
---
For: convenience brings customers who expect to book online; the app gives data on customers' preferences for targeted offers; ordering snacks from the seat could increase spending per visit; it reduces queues and staff needed at the ticket desk; rival multiplex cinemas already offer apps (3).
Against: £20,000 is a large sum for a small cinema; ongoing maintenance, updates and security; some older customers may not use it; a cheaper option is an off-the-shelf booking website; the extra revenue may not repay the cost if audiences are small (3).
Judgement: justified decision — e.g. use a cheaper existing booking platform first and add seat ordering only if it raises spending per customer; depends on audience size, age of customers and competition (3).
Levels: 7–9 — both sides developed and applied to the case, with a justified judgement; 4–6 — developed points with some application, a judgement that is not fully supported; 1–3 — simple points, little use of the case.
:::
