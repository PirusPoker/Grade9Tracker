## What it is

**Market research** is collecting and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market, so that decisions are based on evidence rather than guesswork.

**Why businesses research:**

- **To identify and understand customers** — who they are, what they want, what they will pay, where they shop.
- **To spot opportunities** — a gap in the market, a growing segment, a new trend.
- **To understand competitors** — their prices, products, strengths and weaknesses.
- **To reduce risk** — test an idea before spending on production and promotion.
- **To measure success** — customer satisfaction, brand awareness, market share.
- **To inform the marketing mix** — the right product, price, promotion and place.

**Primary research** (field research) — **new** information collected **first-hand** for the business's own purpose:

| Method | What it is | Good for | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaires** | a set list of questions, on paper, online or in the street | large numbers, cheap online, quantitative data | badly worded questions mislead; low response rates |
| **Surveys** | asking a sample of people — by phone, online, face to face | measuring opinions across a market | the sample must represent the market or results are biased |
| **Interviews** | one-to-one, in depth | detailed reasons and opinions (qualitative) | slow, expensive, small numbers |
| **Focus groups** | a small group discussing a product with a moderator | reactions to ideas, prototypes, adverts | small, may be swayed by strong personalities; expensive |
| Also: observation, test marketing, trial launches | | | |

*Advantages*: up to date, specific to the business's question, not available to competitors. *Disadvantages*: expensive, time-consuming, needs skill to design and analyse; small samples can mislead.

**Secondary research** (desk research) — information that **already exists**, collected by someone else for another purpose:

- **Internal**: the business's own sales records, customer data, loyalty cards, website analytics.
- **External**: **the internet** (competitors' websites, reviews, social media, industry sites), **the printed press** (newspapers, trade magazines), government statistics (census, ONS), market research reports (Mintel), trade associations.

*Advantages*: cheap or free, fast, gives the big picture and market size. *Disadvantages*: may be out of date, not specific to the business's question, of unknown reliability, and available to competitors too.

**Qualitative vs quantitative:**

- **Qualitative** data is about **opinions, feelings and reasons** — "why do you prefer this brand?" — from interviews and focus groups. Rich and detailed, but hard to summarise and based on few people.
- **Quantitative** data is **numbers** — "how many people bought it?", "what percentage would pay £10?" — from questionnaires, surveys and sales data. Easy to analyse and compare, but does not explain *why*.

**Choosing a method**: depends on the question (why → qualitative; how many → quantitative), the budget (secondary and online questionnaires are cheap), the time available, the size and type of business (a start-up uses free secondary sources and a street questionnaire; a plc commissions focus groups), and how much accuracy the decision needs.

**Handling data** — you are expected to read tables and charts and calculate:

- **Market share** $= \dfrac{\text{business's sales}}{\text{total market sales}} \times 100$ (by value or by volume — check which).
- **Percentage change** $= \dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100$.
- Reading trends off a line graph, comparing bars, finding the largest segment in a pie chart, and saying **what the numbers mean** for the business.

::: terms
- **Market research**: the process of gathering and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market.
- **Primary research**: collecting new, first-hand information for a specific purpose (field research).
- **Secondary research**: using information that already exists, collected by someone else for another purpose (desk research).
- **Questionnaire**: a set list of questions answered by a number of people, on paper or online.
- **Survey**: asking a sample of people for their views, by questionnaire, phone or face to face.
- **Focus group**: a small group of people who discuss a product, idea or advert in depth, guided by a researcher.
- **Qualitative data**: information about opinions, feelings and reasons — why people think or act as they do.
- **Quantitative data**: information in the form of numbers that can be measured, compared and analysed.
- **Sample**: the group of people selected to take part in research, meant to represent the whole market.
- **Bias**: when research results do not reflect the whole market, for example because the sample is unrepresentative or questions are leading.
- **Market share**: a business's sales as a percentage of the total sales in the market.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify**: primary (new, first-hand) or secondary (already exists); qualitative (why) or quantitative (how many). Most questions want both classifications.
2. **Recommend a method for the business's question and budget**: "a small café wanting to know why customers stopped coming should interview some of them — cheap, qualitative, gives reasons".
3. **Advantages and disadvantages** of any method: cost, time, accuracy, relevance, whether competitors can see it.
4. **Data questions**: show the calculation; then **interpret** — "market share fell from 20% to 15%, so despite higher sales the business is losing ground to rivals".
5. **Evaluate research**: it reduces risk but does not remove it — samples can be unrepresentative, people say one thing and do another, and markets change after the research.
:::

::: example Classify
Classify each as primary or secondary and as qualitative or quantitative: (a) a focus group discussing a new logo; (b) government population statistics; (c) an online questionnaire asking how often people buy coffee. (3)

(a) Primary, qualitative (1). (b) Secondary, quantitative (1). (c) Primary, quantitative (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — market share
Total sales of bottled water in a region were 2,500,000 last year. Aqua Ltd sold 375,000 bottles. Calculate Aqua's market share. (2)

$\dfrac{375{,}000}{2{,}500{,}000} \times 100 = 15\%$ (2).
:::

::: example Interpret — a table
A bakery's monthly sales: January £8,000; February £8,400; March £9,240. The whole local market grew 20% over the same period. Calculate the bakery's percentage growth from January to March and comment. (4)

$\dfrac{9{,}240 - 8{,}000}{8{,}000} \times 100 = 15.5\%$ (2). Sales rose, but the market grew faster (20%), so the bakery's market share fell — it is gaining customers more slowly than its rivals and should investigate why (2).
:::

::: example Interpret — using findings to decide
A café surveyed 200 customers about a new breakfast menu. Results: 120 would buy breakfast at least once a week; of these, 90 want it served before 8am; the most popular price was £4.50. The café currently opens at 9am. Using the data, explain one decision the café should make. (4)

60% of those surveyed would buy breakfast weekly (120 ÷ 200 × 100), which suggests enough demand to launch the menu (1).

Of the 120, 75% want breakfast before 8am (90 ÷ 120 × 100), which is when commuters pass, but the café does not open until 9am (1).

Decision: open at 7am on weekdays and launch the breakfast menu at around £4.50 (1), because opening at 9am would miss most of the customers the research found (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a limitation of a sample
A gaming café surveys its own customers on a Saturday afternoon to find out whether people in the town would pay £10 for an evening gaming tournament. Explain one limitation of this research. (3)

The sample is not representative (1): only people who already use the café, and who are free on a Saturday afternoon, are asked (1), so the results are biased towards keen gamers and will probably overestimate how many people in the town would pay £10 — the café could run the tournament and find demand much lower than expected (1).
:::

::: example Recommend — a method
A start-up wants to know whether teenagers in its town would buy a new energy drink and what flavours they prefer. Recommend a research method. Justify your answer. (6)

An online questionnaire shared through local school social media would reach many teenagers cheaply and quickly, giving quantitative data on how many would buy and at what price — important for a start-up with little money (2). Because flavour preference is about taste and opinion, a small focus group of teenagers tasting samples would add qualitative reasons the questionnaire cannot capture, and costs little if held in a school or café (2). Secondary research — industry reports on the energy-drink market and competitors' websites — would show market size and prices for free. This combination fits the budget and answers both the "how many" and the "which flavour" questions; the main limit is that teenagers who answer a school survey may not represent all local teenagers (2).
:::

State two reasons a business carries out market research. (2)
---
To understand customer needs; to find out about competitors (or to spot opportunities, reduce risk).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between primary and secondary research. (2)
---
Primary research collects new information first-hand, e.g. a survey (1); secondary research uses information that already exists, e.g. government statistics (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of secondary research for a small business. (3)
---
Low cost (1): reports, statistics and competitors' websites are free or cheap to access (1), so a business with little money can learn about market size and trends without paying for its own survey (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of using a focus group. (2)
---
Only a few people take part (1), so their views may not represent the whole market, and one confident member can sway the others (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between qualitative and quantitative data, with an example of each. (4)
---
Qualitative data is about opinions and reasons, e.g. why customers like a brand's design (2); quantitative data is numerical, e.g. 60% of customers buy weekly (2).
:::

::: q
A firm sold 45,000 units in a market of 300,000. Calculate its market share. (2)
---
$\dfrac{45{,}000}{300{,}000} \times 100 = 15\%$.
:::

::: q
A business's sales fell from 120,000 to 102,000. Calculate the percentage change. (2)
---
$\dfrac{102{,}000 - 120{,}000}{120{,}000} \times 100 = -15\%$.
:::

::: q
Explain why a questionnaire might give misleading results. (3)
---
If the questions are leading or unclear people give inaccurate answers (1); if only a certain type of person responds the sample is biased (1); and people may say they would buy something but not actually do so (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how market research could help a clothing retailer decide whether to open a second shop in a nearby town. (6)
---
Secondary research — population statistics, income levels and a list of existing clothing shops in the town — would show whether the market is large enough and how strong competition is, cheaply and quickly (2). Primary research, such as a street questionnaire in the town, would reveal whether residents currently travel elsewhere for clothes, what styles and prices they want and whether they know the brand (2). Together these reduce the risk of spending on a lease and fit-out for a shop that fails; but the research cannot guarantee success — respondents may not become customers, and a rival could open at the same time — so it informs the decision rather than making it (2).
:::

- **Classify on both axes**: primary/secondary **and** qualitative/quantitative.
- Recommend a method by matching **the question** (why or how many) and **the budget** — and say its limitation.
- **Show calculations** for market share and percentage change, then **interpret** what the number means.
- Secondary research is **cheap but may be out of date or not specific**; primary is **specific but expensive**.
- Research **reduces** risk; it never removes it — samples, honesty and changing markets.
:::

::: test
A restaurant owner used an online survey of 50 followers of the restaurant's social-media page before launching a new menu. Analyse the limitations of this research. (6)
---
The sample is small and made up only of existing followers, who already like the restaurant, so it is not representative of all potential customers and the results are biased (2). An online survey gives quantitative data but little explanation of why people like or dislike dishes; tasting sessions or a focus group would give better qualitative data (2). Customers may say they would order new dishes but not actually do so, so the owner should test the menu on a small scale before a full launch (2).
Levels: 5–6 — a developed chain of reasoning applied to the case; 3–4 — some developed points with some application; 1–2 — simple points.

::: test
A toy company plans to launch a new board game. It has £5,000 to spend on market research. Evaluate whether it should spend it on primary or secondary research. (9)
---
Primary: focus groups where families play the game give detailed feedback on rules, fun and price; questionnaires show how many would buy; the data is specific and not available to competitors; but it is costly and time-consuming (3).
Secondary: market reports and online data show the size of the board-game market, trends and competitors' prices cheaply; but they are not specific to this game and may be out of date (3).
Judgement: justified — mostly primary research (play-testing with families) because the key question is whether this particular game appeals, using free secondary sources first for market size and prices; £5,000 can cover both (3).
Levels: 7–9 — both sides developed and applied to the case, with a justified judgement; 4–6 — developed points with some application, a judgement that is not fully supported; 1–3 — simple points, little use of the case.
:::
