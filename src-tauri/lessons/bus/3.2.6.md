## What it is

A **market** is where buyers and sellers meet to exchange goods and services — a physical place, a website, or simply all the customers for a product ("the UK coffee market"). **Competition** is rivalry between businesses in the same market for the same customers.

**How competition affects a business:**

- **Price** — rivals force prices down; a business cannot charge much more than competitors for the same thing. Profit margins are squeezed.
- **Quality and service** — must at least match rivals or customers switch; drives improvement.
- **Innovation** — new products and features to stand out; a USP.
- **Marketing** — more spending on promotion to be noticed.
- **Efficiency** — costs must be controlled to survive on lower prices.
- **Customer focus** — market research and customer service matter more.
- **Risk** — a new entrant or a price war can wipe out profit; weaker businesses close.

A business faces **little or no competition** when it has a **unique product** (a patent, a secret recipe), a **strong brand**, it is the only supplier in an area (the one village shop, a rural bus route), it dominates the market (a **monopoly**), or barriers such as huge start-up costs keep rivals out (water companies). It can then charge higher prices, but may become complacent, and regulators may step in.

**Competitive markets**, by contrast, have many similar businesses — cafés, hairdressers, taxi firms, online clothing — where the customer can switch easily.

**Risk and uncertainty.** **Risk** is the chance that something goes wrong and the business loses money — it can be identified and often measured or insured against. **Uncertainty** is not knowing what will happen: future events cannot be predicted or controlled. Every business faces both:

- **Financial risks** — the business fails and the owner loses their investment (and personal assets with unlimited liability); running out of cash; bad debts.
- **Market risks** — customers do not buy; a competitor launches something better; tastes change.
- **Operational risks** — suppliers fail, equipment breaks, key staff leave, fire, theft, cyber-attack.
- **External uncertainty** — recession, interest rates, exchange rates, new laws, pandemics, weather: the business cannot control these and often cannot predict them.

Uncertainty cannot be removed, because the future is unknowable and outside events are beyond the business's control — the best it can do is prepare.

**Why entrepreneurs take the risk anyway**: the potential **rewards** — profit, independence, satisfaction, building something, a challenge; they believe in their idea; they may have spotted a gap; they weigh risk against reward and judge it worth it.

**How businesses reduce risk**: **market research** before launching; a **business plan** and cash-flow forecast; **insurance** against fire, theft, liability; **limited liability** to protect personal assets; keeping a **cash reserve** or overdraft facility; **diversifying** products, customers and suppliers; **contingency plans**; starting small and testing; training staff; monitoring competitors.

::: terms
- **Market**: the buyers and sellers of a particular product, or the place where they exchange.
- **Competition**: rivalry between businesses selling similar products to the same customers.
- **Monopoly**: a market dominated by a single supplier.
- **Barriers to entry**: things that make it hard for new businesses to enter a market — high start-up costs, patents, strong brands.
- **Price war**: rival businesses repeatedly cutting prices to win customers from each other, squeezing everyone's profit.
- **Risk**: the possibility that a business decision leads to a loss; it can be identified, assessed and reduced.
- **Uncertainty**: not knowing what will happen in the future, because events outside the business cannot be predicted or controlled.
- **Diversification**: spreading risk by selling a wider range of products, or to more markets or customers.
- **Contingency plan**: a plan for what the business will do if something goes wrong.
:::

::: method
1. **Competition answers**: name the effect (price, quality, innovation, marketing) and chain it to sales, costs or profit for the business in the case.
2. **Little competition**: explain *why* (unique product, location, brand, barriers) and the consequence (pricing power, but complacency and possible regulation).
3. **Risk vs uncertainty**: risk can be identified and managed; uncertainty cannot be predicted. Give one of each for the business.
4. **"Why start a business despite the risk?"** — reward vs risk, and the belief/opportunity behind it.
5. **Managing risk**: name the method and say *which risk it reduces* — insurance for fire and theft, market research for market risk, cash reserves for cash-flow risk, limited liability for personal loss.
:::

::: example Define
Explain what is meant by "competition" in a market. (2)

Rivalry between businesses selling similar products (1) to attract the same customers, for example by lowering prices or improving quality (1).
:::

::: example Explain — the effect of competition
Explain one effect on a taxi firm of a ride-hailing app entering its town. (3)

Lower prices (1): customers can compare fares instantly and choose the cheapest (1), so the firm must cut its fares to keep passengers, reducing its profit per journey (1).
:::

::: example Explain — little or no competition
The only shop in a remote Scottish village sells groceries at prices about 15% higher than a supermarket 30 miles away. Explain why it can do this, and one risk of relying on it. (4)

It faces little competition (1): for villagers the nearest alternative is a 60-mile round trip, so for everyday items the time and fuel cost more than the higher prices, and they keep buying locally (1).

Risk: if a supermarket starts home deliveries to the village, customers gain an easy alternative (1), so the shop could lose much of its trade unless it keeps prices reasonable and offers what a delivery cannot — local produce, a post office, friendly service (1).
:::

::: example Explain — risk vs uncertainty
Using an example of each, explain the difference between risk and uncertainty for a new ice-cream parlour. (4)

Risk is a known possibility of loss that can be assessed and reduced — for example, that a freezer breaks and stock is lost, which insurance and a service contract can cover (2). Uncertainty is not knowing what the future holds and being unable to control it — for example, whether the summer will be hot or wet, which decides sales and cannot be predicted or insured (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — reducing risk
Analyse how market research could reduce the risk of Jamal's new vegan restaurant failing. (6)

Research into the number of vegans and vegetarians in the area and their spending would tell Jamal whether there are enough customers to cover his costs, before he signs a lease — reducing the risk of opening where demand is too small (2). Asking potential customers about dishes and prices helps him design a menu people actually want and set prices they will pay, reducing the market risk that his product misses the target (2). Studying competitors shows him whether the market is already served and how to differentiate; research cannot remove uncertainty — a recession or a rival opening later is still possible — but it turns guesses into evidence and lets him plan for the risks he can see (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — starting up
Priya has been offered a secure job but wants to start her own graphic design business. Evaluate whether the risk is worth taking. (9)

Against: she gives up a guaranteed salary for uncertain income; most new businesses earn little at first; she may lose savings, and clients, competitors and the economy are beyond her control; the stress and hours are heavy (3). For: the rewards are independence, keeping all the profit, satisfaction and potential earnings above a salary; graphic design needs little capital — a computer and software — so the financial risk is small, and she can reduce it further by starting part-time, researching demand, keeping a cash reserve and setting up as an Ltd (3). Judgement: the risk is worth taking because the potential loss is low relative to the reward and much of it can be managed; she should keep costs minimal and perhaps freelance alongside the job first — the decision depends on her savings and whether she has clients lined up (3).
:::

::: q
State two ways a business might respond to increased competition. (2)
---
Cut prices; improve quality or service (or advertise more, innovate, cut costs).
:::

::: q
Explain why a business with a unique product faces little competition. (2)
---
Customers cannot buy the same thing elsewhere (1), so the business can set higher prices without losing them to rivals (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit of competition for customers. (2)
---
Lower prices (1), because rivals undercut each other to win sales (1) — or better quality and more choice.
:::

::: q
Give two risks a new business faces. (2)
---
Not attracting enough customers; running out of cash (or a competitor, supplier failure, the owner losing their investment).
:::

::: q
Explain why uncertainty cannot be removed from business. (2)
---
Outside events such as recessions, new laws or changing tastes cannot be predicted or controlled by the business (1), so there is always a chance of something unexpected (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason an entrepreneur is willing to take the risk of starting a business. (3)
---
The potential reward (1): if the business succeeds they keep all the profit and could earn far more than as an employee (1), and gain the independence of being their own boss, which they judge worth the risk of loss (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how insurance helps a business manage risk. (2)
---
For a fixed premium the insurer pays for losses from events such as fire or theft (1), so a disaster does not wipe out the business's cash (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the impact on a small independent bookshop of a large chain opening nearby. (6)
---
The chain buys in bulk and can sell popular titles cheaper, so customers may switch, cutting the bookshop's sales and revenue while its rent and wages stay fixed, so profit falls (2). To respond, the shop must find a USP — specialist ranges, author events, expert recommendations, a café — so customers have reasons to visit beyond price; it may also improve service and use social media to build a loyal community (2). If it competes on the chain's terms it will lose; by focusing on what a chain cannot easily copy it can survive, but the increased competition permanently squeezes its margins and raises the risk of closure (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Risk can be managed; uncertainty cannot be predicted.** Do not use the words interchangeably.
- Competition answers must show the **chain to price, sales, costs or profit** for the business in the case.
- Explain **why** a business faces little competition — unique product, location, brand, barriers — not just that it does.
- Reducing risk: **name the method and the risk it targets**.
- Entrepreneurs take risk for **reward**; a good evaluation weighs the size of the potential loss against it.
:::
