## What it is

**Procurement** is the process of obtaining the goods and services a business needs — finding suppliers, agreeing prices and terms, ordering, and managing deliveries. **Logistics** is moving and storing those goods — transport, warehousing, stock control — from supplier to business to customer. Together they form the **supply chain**: every business and step involved in getting a product from raw material to the final customer.

**Stock management** — two approaches:

| | **Just-in-time (JIT)** | **Just-in-case (JIC)** |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | stock arrives only when needed; little or none held | **buffer stock** held in case of late deliveries or a surge in demand |
| Stock costs | low — no warehouse, insurance, handling, waste | high — storage space, insurance, cash tied up, risk of stock going out of date or fashion |
| Deliveries | frequent, small — higher delivery costs, **no bulk discounts** | fewer, larger — cheaper per unit, **bulk discounts** |
| Risk | a late delivery stops production or empties shelves; cannot meet sudden demand | can keep selling and producing through delays; meets surges |
| Suits | reliable suppliers nearby; predictable demand; perishable or expensive stock; manufacturers with good IT | unreliable or distant suppliers; unpredictable or seasonal demand; cheap, non-perishable stock; where a stock-out is very costly |

The trade-off is the whole topic: **holding stock costs money; running out costs sales.** (Stock control charts are *not* examined — you will not be asked to draw or read one.)

**Choosing a supplier** — three factors:

- **Price** — lower prices mean lower costs and higher margins or lower selling prices; but the cheapest may be poor on the other two.
- **Quality** — the supplier's inputs decide the quality of the business's own product; poor materials mean returns, waste and reputation damage.
- **Reliability** — delivering on time, every time, in the right quantity; essential for JIT; an unreliable supplier causes stock-outs and lost sales.

Also considered: delivery speed and flexibility, credit terms (paying 30–60 days later helps cash flow), location, ethical standards, capacity to grow with the business, and how many suppliers to use (one — bigger discounts and a closer relationship; several — spreads the risk).

**Effective supply chain management** — coordinating suppliers, transport, stock and information so that the right products reach the right place at the right time at the lowest cost. What it achieves: **lower costs** (less stock, efficient transport, bulk deals), **better quality** (trusted suppliers), **faster delivery** to customers, **fewer stock-outs**, **flexibility** to respond to demand, and a **competitive advantage** — a business that can restock a shelf overnight beats one that takes a week. Modern supply chains rely on IT: electronic ordering, barcode and RFID tracking, and sharing sales data with suppliers.

::: terms
- **Procurement**: finding and buying the goods and services a business needs from suppliers, including agreeing prices and delivery terms.
- **Logistics**: managing the transport and storage of goods — from supplier to business to customer.
- **Supply chain**: all the businesses and activities involved in producing a product and delivering it to the final customer.
- **Supply chain management**: coordinating suppliers, transport, stock and information so the right products reach the right place at the right time at the lowest cost.
- **Buffer stock**: extra stock held to cover unexpected demand or late deliveries.
- **Just-in-case (JIC)**: holding buffer stock so the business does not run out.
- **Stock-out**: running out of stock, so production stops or customers cannot buy.
- **Bulk discount**: a lower price per unit for buying in large quantities.
- **Credit terms**: the time a supplier allows before its invoice must be paid, e.g. 30 days.
:::

::: method
1. **JIT vs JIC**: always weigh **lower stock costs** against **more deliveries, lost bulk discounts and stock-out risk** — then apply to the business's product (perishable? expensive?), suppliers (reliable? near?) and demand (steady? seasonal?).
2. **Supplier choice**: rank price, quality and reliability *for this business* — a restaurant needs reliability and quality; a discount retailer weights price. Say why.
3. **Chain every factor to a consequence**: unreliable supplier → stock-out → lost sales and reputation; cheap supplier → poor quality → returns and waste.
4. **Supply chain management**: explain what good coordination *achieves* — cost, speed, availability — and give a concrete practice (sharing sales data, tracking deliveries).
5. **Recommend** JIT or JIC, or a supplier, with a justified condition ("JIT, provided the supplier is within a day's delivery and demand stays steady").
:::

The process of finding and buying the goods and services a business needs (1), including choosing suppliers and agreeing prices and delivery terms (1).

::: example Explain — a factor in supplier choice
Explain why reliability is important when a restaurant chooses a food supplier. (3)

A restaurant needs fresh ingredients delivered on time every day (1); if a delivery is late or short, dishes cannot be made and customers are turned away or served a reduced menu (1), losing sales and damaging its reputation with diners who may not return (1).
:::

::: example Explain — logistics and unit costs
An online retailer of pet food used to send each order from whichever of its three small warehouses had the stock. It has moved everything into one large warehouse and uses software to plan delivery routes. Explain how this could lower its unit costs. (3)

One warehouse means one set of rent, staff and systems instead of three, so storage costs are spread over far more orders (1). Route-planning software groups deliveries by area, so each van covers fewer miles per parcel, cutting fuel and driver costs (1); with storage and delivery cost per order both lower, the average cost of each order falls — and faster, more reliable deliveries may also win more customers (1).
:::

::: example Explain — effective supply chain management
A sandwich chain shares its daily sales data from every shop with its bread supplier. Explain one benefit of this for the chain. (3)

The supplier can see what each shop sold and bake the right amount for the next day without waiting for an order (1), so shops receive fresh bread in the quantity they need — fewer stock-outs at lunchtime and less stale bread thrown away (1); this cuts waste costs and lost sales, giving the chain a cost and service advantage over rivals whose ordering is slower (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — JIT vs JIC
A garden centre sells plants, compost and garden furniture, with demand peaking in spring and depending on the weather. Analyse whether it should use just-in-time or just-in-case stock management. (6)

Demand is seasonal and unpredictable — a sunny bank holiday can sell a month's furniture in a weekend — so just-in-case buffer stock of compost and furniture lets it meet surges and avoid empty shelves when customers are most willing to buy, and buying in bulk before spring earns discounts (2). But plants are perishable and furniture takes space; holding large stocks ties up cash over winter, needs storage and risks plants dying unsold, all of which favour just-in-time for living stock (2). The best approach mixes the two: JIC for durable, discounted lines like compost and furniture before the season, and JIT weekly deliveries for plants from reliable local nurseries — the choice depends on how perishable each product is and how quickly suppliers can respond (2).
:::

::: example Recommend — choosing a supplier
A clothing retailer must choose between Supplier A (cheapest, but 20% of last year's deliveries were late and 5% of garments faulty) and Supplier B (15% dearer, on time, under 1% faulty). Recommend which it should choose. Justify your answer. (9)

Supplier A's lower price would cut costs and raise margins or allow lower prices, important in competitive clothing retail; if the retailer holds buffer stock, late deliveries might be tolerated (3). But 20% late deliveries mean new ranges miss the season and shelves stand empty, losing sales that matter more than the saving; 5% faulty garments mean refunds, returns handling and customers who lose trust in the brand — costs that could exceed the 15% price gap (3). Recommendation: Supplier B, because reliability and quality protect revenue and reputation, which are worth more than the price difference for a fashion business where timing is everything; the retailer might use its order volume to negotiate B's price down, and keep A only for basic, non-seasonal lines where lateness matters less (3).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of just-in-case stock management. (3)

::: q
Explain one cost of holding large amounts of stock. (2)
---
Cash is tied up in stock that could be used elsewhere (1), and the stock needs storage space, insurance and may go out of date (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a business using JIT may pay more per unit for its supplies. (2)
---
It orders small quantities frequently (1), so it loses the bulk discounts available for large orders and pays more delivery charges (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit of effective supply chain management for a supermarket. (3)
---
Fewer stock-outs (1): sales data shared with suppliers lets them deliver what is selling before shelves empty (1), so customers find what they want and do not switch to a rival store (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a business might use more than one supplier for the same material. (2)
---
If one supplier fails or is late the other can deliver (1), reducing the risk of production stopping (1) — though it may lose some bulk discount.
:::

::: q
Explain how trade credit from a supplier helps a business. (2)
---
The business receives goods now but pays 30 or 60 days later (1), so it can sell them before paying, improving cash flow (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how switching to just-in-time could affect a furniture manufacturer's costs and risks. (6)
---
The manufacturer would no longer hold weeks of timber and fittings, freeing cash and cutting warehouse, insurance and handling costs; less material would be damaged or wasted in storage (2). Against this, ordering small quantities frequently raises delivery costs and forfeits bulk discounts, and the business must invest in IT and planning to schedule deliveries precisely (2). The main risk is a late delivery halting production and delaying customer orders; JIT therefore lowers costs only if suppliers are reliable and nearby — otherwise the cost of stoppages could exceed the stock savings, so the decision depends on the quality of its supplier relationships (2).
:::

::: watch
- The marks are in the **trade-off**: stock costs vs stock-outs; price vs quality and reliability.
- **JIT loses bulk discounts and needs reliable suppliers** — the two points examiners look for.
- **Stock control charts are not examined**; do not draw one.
- Supplier choice must be **ranked for this business** with reasons, not a list of three factors.
- Supply chain management is about **coordination and information** — say what it achieves for cost, speed and availability.
:::
