## What it is

**Legislation** is law made by Parliament. Businesses must obey it, and it affects their costs, how they recruit and train, what they can sell and how. Only brief knowledge of each law is needed; the marks are for the **effect on the business**.

**Employment law** — protects workers:

- **National Minimum Wage (NMW)** — the lowest hourly rate an employer may legally pay, with lower rates for younger workers and apprentices. The **National Living Wage (NLW)** is the higher legal minimum for workers aged 21 and over. (The *real* living wage is a voluntary, higher figure set by a charity.)
- **The Equality Act 2010** — makes it illegal to discriminate against workers or job applicants because of **protected characteristics**: age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage, pregnancy, race, religion, sex, sexual orientation. Covers recruitment, pay, promotion, training and dismissal; requires reasonable adjustments for disabled workers.
- Also: rights to a written contract, paid holiday, rest breaks, maximum working hours, maternity and paternity leave, protection from unfair dismissal.

**Health and safety law** — **the Health and Safety at Work Act 1974**: employers must provide a safe workplace and safe equipment, carry out risk assessments, provide protective clothing and training, and report accidents; employees must take reasonable care of themselves and others and follow safety rules. Enforced by the Health and Safety Executive, which can inspect, fine and prosecute.

**Consumer law** — protects customers:

- **Trade descriptions** — it is illegal to describe goods or services falsely (a "leather" sofa that is plastic; "hand-made" when machine-made; misleading prices or "was" prices).
- **The Consumer Rights Act 2015** — goods must be of **satisfactory quality**, **fit for purpose** and **as described**; services must be carried out with reasonable care and skill; customers have rights to a refund, repair or replacement.
- Also: the Sale of Goods Act (older law), consumer credit rules, data protection.

**How legislation affects a business:**

| Cost or effect | Example |
|---|---|
| **Higher wage costs** | the NLW rising each April forces pay rises for the lowest paid, which may push up other wages too |
| **Training costs** | health and safety training, equality training for managers |
| **Equipment and premises** | guards on machinery, protective clothing, ramps and adjustments, fire systems |
| **Administration** | risk assessments, contracts, records, checking product descriptions |
| **Recruitment** | fair, documented processes; cannot ask about age or plans for children |
| **Product changes** | withdrawing unsafe or misdescribed products; honest labelling |
| **Pricing** | higher costs may be passed on in prices, or absorbed |

**Benefits of complying**: safer, fairer, more motivated staff who stay longer; fewer accidents and sick days; a better reputation with customers; avoiding the costs below. **Consequences of failing to comply**: fines and prosecution; compensation claims; being forced to stop trading; bad publicity and lost customers; difficulty recruiting; imprisonment of directors in serious cases.

Key terms to learn:

- **Legislation**: laws passed by Parliament that businesses must obey.
- **National Minimum Wage**: the lowest hourly pay an employer can legally give.
- **National Living Wage**: the legal minimum hourly rate for workers aged 21 and over.
- **Discrimination**: treating someone unfairly because of a protected characteristic.
- **Trade description**: a statement about a product that must be accurate by law.

::: method
1. **One line on the law, then the effect.** "The Equality Act bans discrimination in recruitment — so the café must write a fair job advert and interview on skills, which takes management time."
2. **Costs come in types**: wages, training, equipment, administration, prices. Pick the ones that apply to the case.
3. **Balance**: complying costs money, but non-compliance costs more — fines, compensation, reputation, staff. Use that for "explain the impact" and evaluation.
4. **A change in the law** (NLW rises): follow it through — costs up → prices, profit, staffing, automation; then who it affects most (labour-intensive businesses with low-paid staff).
5. **Do not recite the Act.** Names and one purpose each are enough; the marks are on the business.
:::

::: example Identify and explain
State one law that protects employees and explain one way it affects a business. (3)

The Health and Safety at Work Act 1974 (1). The business must carry out risk assessments and provide safety equipment and training (1), which costs money and time but reduces accidents and the sick leave and compensation claims they cause (1).
:::

::: example Explain — consumer law
Explain how consumer law affects an online electronics retailer. (3)

Products must be as described and of satisfactory quality (1), so the retailer must check its listings are accurate and handle refunds or replacements when goods are faulty (1); this raises costs of returns and administration, but avoids fines and builds customer trust (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a rise in the National Living Wage
Analyse the effect of a rise in the National Living Wage on a chain of care homes. (6)

Care homes employ many staff on or near the minimum, so a rise raises the wage bill directly and immediately, and staff just above the minimum may expect rises too to keep the gap (2). Fees are often fixed by local councils, so the chain may not be able to pass the cost on; profit falls, and it may respond by cutting staff hours, using more agency workers, or reducing spending on activities and maintenance (2). On the other hand, better pay reduces the high staff turnover typical of care work, cutting recruitment and training costs and improving quality of care — so part of the cost is recovered, and the chain's competitors face the same rise, limiting any loss of competitiveness (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — the cost of legislation
"Legislation only adds cost to a small business." To what extent do you agree? (9)

Agree: for a small business the costs are real and proportionally heavy — minimum wage rises, safety equipment, training, and hours of administration spread over few sales; the owner may lack an HR department to keep up with changing rules, and one mistake can mean a fine it cannot afford (3). Disagree: legislation also brings benefits — safe workplaces mean fewer accidents and less absence; fair treatment keeps staff and attracts good applicants; consumer law builds trust so customers buy; and the same rules bind competitors, so no rival gains by cutting corners legally; non-compliance would cost far more in fines, claims and reputation (3). Judgement: legislation raises costs in the short term but most of it protects the business from larger costs later, so "only" is wrong; the burden depends on how labour-intensive and low-paid the business is — a café with many minimum-wage staff feels it far more than a one-person consultancy (3).
:::

::: q
State the purpose of the Equality Act 2010. (1)
---
To prevent discrimination against people because of protected characteristics such as age, sex, race, disability or religion.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between the National Minimum Wage and the National Living Wage. (2)
---
Both are legal minimum hourly rates (1); the National Living Wage is the higher rate that applies to workers aged 21 and over, while the NMW applies to younger workers (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way the Equality Act affects how a business recruits. (3)
---
It must not discriminate (1), so job adverts and interviews must focus on skills, not age or gender (1); managers may need training and records to show the process was fair, adding cost but widening the pool of applicants (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one consequence for a business of breaking health and safety law. (2)
---
It can be fined or prosecuted by the Health and Safety Executive (1), and an injured worker may claim compensation, while the publicity damages its reputation (1).
:::

::: q
Give one example of a false trade description. (1)
---
Labelling a jumper "100% wool" when it contains polyester (or advertising a "half-price" sale when the higher price was never charged).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit to a business of complying with health and safety law. (3)
---
Fewer accidents (1): staff are less likely to be injured, so there is less absence and disruption (1), and workers feel valued, improving motivation and retention (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how consumer law might affect a car dealer's costs. (3)
---
Cars must be of satisfactory quality and as described (1), so the dealer must inspect vehicles before sale and describe them honestly (1), and must pay for repairs or refunds if a car proves faulty, raising its costs (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the impact of employment legislation on a fast-food restaurant. (6)
---
Minimum wage law sets the pay of its largely young, part-time workforce, and each rise increases the wage bill — its largest cost after food — squeezing thin profit margins unless prices rise (2). The Equality Act requires fair recruitment and treatment of a diverse workforce, and health and safety rules mean training on fryers and knives and providing protective equipment, all of which cost management time and money (2). Complying does bring benefits: lower staff turnover, fewer accidents, and no fines or bad publicity; because all rivals face the same laws, the restaurant is not disadvantaged, but its labour-intensive model means it feels legislation more than most (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Brief knowledge of the law; full marks for the effect on the business.** Do not write paragraphs about the Act.
- Name the law correctly: **Equality Act 2010**, **Health and Safety at Work Act 1974**, **National Minimum / Living Wage**, **trade descriptions / Consumer Rights Act**.
- Costs of complying **and** costs of not complying — both sides in any evaluation.
- The **National Living Wage is legal**; the "real living wage" is voluntary.
- Legislation hits **labour-intensive, low-paid** businesses hardest — say so when judging impact.
:::
