## What it is

The finance vocabulary, used precisely — **no formulae are given in the exam**:

- **Fixed costs** — do not change with output (rent, salaries, insurance).
- **Variable costs** — change directly with output (materials, packaging); variable cost per unit × output.
- **Total costs** $=$ fixed $+$ variable.
- **Revenue** $=$ price $\times$ quantity sold.
- **Profit** $=$ revenue $-$ total costs; negative is a **loss**.

**Average rate of return (ARR)** — judges whether an **investment** (a new machine, a shop, a project) is worth it, by expressing the average yearly profit as a percentage of the amount invested:

$$ARR = \frac{\text{average annual profit}}{\text{cost of investment}} \times 100$$

where average annual profit $= \dfrac{\text{total return over the project} - \text{cost of investment}}{\text{number of years}}$.

Compare the ARR with the interest rate the money could earn in a bank, with the business's target rate, and with alternative projects: higher is better. It ignores *when* the money comes in and how risky the forecast is.

**Break-even** — the level of output at which **total revenue equals total costs**: no profit, no loss. Below it the business makes a loss; above it, every extra unit adds profit. AQA does **not** ask you to draw the chart or use the break-even formula — but it does ask you to **read** a chart, **identify** the break-even point and **margin of safety**, and **evaluate** the technique.

Reading the chart: the **fixed cost line** is horizontal; the **total cost line** starts at fixed costs and rises with output; the **revenue line** starts at zero and rises more steeply (if price exceeds variable cost per unit). Where **revenue crosses total costs** is the **break-even point (BEP)** — read the output on the horizontal axis (3,000 units here) and the revenue on the vertical (21,000). Left of it the gap is a loss; right of it the gap is profit.

**Margin of safety** $=$ actual (or planned) output $-$ break-even output. It shows how far sales can fall before the business makes a loss. In the chart, with sales of 4,500 units, the margin of safety is $4{,}500 - 3{,}000 = 1{,}500$ units.

For interest (not required): break-even output $= \dfrac{\text{fixed costs}}{\text{price} - \text{variable cost per unit}}$; the chart above uses fixed costs 12,000, price 7, variable cost 3, so $12{,}000 \div 4 = 3{,}000$.

**How useful is break-even analysis?** *Useful*: simple and quick; shows the minimum sales needed before starting; helps set prices and judge whether targets are realistic; the margin of safety shows risk; lenders like to see it; "what if" — price up, costs down — can be tested. *Limits*: assumes every unit is sold at the same price and all output is sold; assumes costs stay fixed or exactly variable — in reality bulk discounts and overtime bend the lines; ignores changes in the market; based on forecasts that may be wrong; only works for one product at a time; a static picture of a moving business.

Key terms to learn:

- **Average rate of return**: the average annual profit from an investment as a percentage of its cost.
- **Break-even point**: the level of output at which total revenue equals total costs.
- **Break-even chart**: a graph showing fixed costs, total costs and revenue against output, used to find the break-even point.
- **Margin of safety**: the amount by which actual or planned output exceeds break-even output.

::: method
1. **Costs and revenue**: classify, then calculate in order — TVC, TC, revenue, profit — showing each line.
2. **ARR in three steps**: total profit = total return − cost; average annual profit = total profit ÷ years; ARR = average annual profit ÷ cost × 100. Then **compare** with the alternative (a bank rate or another project) to judge.
3. **Reading a chart**: find where the revenue and total cost lines cross; drop to the output axis for the BEP; read revenue/costs at that point if asked. Margin of safety = current output − BEP, in **units**.
4. **"How would X change the chart?"** — price rise → revenue line steeper → BEP falls; fixed costs rise → both cost lines shift up → BEP rises; variable cost rise → total cost line steeper → BEP rises.
5. **Evaluate break-even**: simple and useful for planning and lenders, but built on assumptions — one price, all sold, linear costs, static — so it guides rather than decides.
:::

::: example Calculate — ARR
A café is considering a 50,000 refit expected to bring extra profit of 15,000, 18,000, 18,000 and 19,000 over four years. Calculate the average rate of return and comment. (5)

Total return $= 15{,}000 + 18{,}000 + 18{,}000 + 19{,}000 = 70{,}000$; total profit $= 70{,}000 - 50{,}000 = 20{,}000$ (1). Average annual profit $= 20{,}000 \div 4 = 5{,}000$ (1). ARR $= \dfrac{5{,}000}{50{,}000} \times 100 = 10\%$ (2). At 10% a year the refit beats leaving the money in a bank at 4%, so it looks worthwhile — provided the forecast profits are realistic (1).
:::

::: example Read — the chart
Using the chart above, identify the break-even output and the margin of safety if the business sells 4,500 units. (2)

Break-even is where revenue equals total costs: 3,000 units (1). Margin of safety $= 4{,}500 - 3{,}000 = 1{,}500$ units (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a change
Explain how a rise in rent would affect a business's break-even chart and break-even point. (3)

Rent is a fixed cost, so the fixed cost line moves up and the total cost line moves up by the same amount (1). The revenue line is unchanged, so it now crosses total costs further to the right (1); the break-even output rises, meaning more units must be sold before profit is made, and the margin of safety shrinks (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — usefulness
Sara is deciding whether to open a second hair salon and has produced a break-even chart. Evaluate how useful it is to her decision. (9)

Useful: the chart shows the minimum number of clients a month needed to cover the salon's rent, wages and other costs, so Sara can compare that with what the first salon achieved in its early months and judge whether the target is realistic; the margin of safety shows how much room she has if custom is slower than hoped; and the chart will help persuade the bank to lend (3). Limits: it assumes one average price per client and that every appointment is filled, ignores that costs change — she may need extra staff at busy times — and says nothing about whether customers exist in the new area; the figures are her forecasts, which may be optimistic; it is a snapshot that cannot show the months of loss while the salon builds custom (3). Judgement: the chart is useful as a first test and to structure her thinking, but the decision should rest on market research about demand in the new area and a cash-flow forecast for the first year; break-even tells her how many clients she needs, not whether she will get them (3).
:::

::: q
A business has fixed costs of 30,000, variable costs of 12 per unit, and sells 5,000 units at 20. Calculate its profit. (3)
---
TC $= 30{,}000 + 12 \times 5{,}000 = 90{,}000$ (1); revenue $= 100{,}000$ (1); profit $= 10{,}000$ (1).
:::

::: q
A machine costing 80,000 is expected to earn 22,000 a year for five years. Calculate the ARR. (3)
---
Total return $110{,}000$; profit $30{,}000$; average annual $6{,}000$ (2); ARR $= \dfrac{6{,}000}{80{,}000} \times 100 = 7.5\%$ (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one limitation of using ARR to compare investments. (2)
---
It ignores when the returns arrive (1), so a project paying most of its profit in year 5 looks as good as one paying early, though the early money is worth more and less risky (1).
:::

::: q
Define "break-even point". (2)
---
The level of output or sales (1) at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither profit nor loss (1).
:::

::: q
A business breaks even at 8,000 units and currently sells 11,000. Calculate its margin of safety and explain what it shows. (3)
---
$11{,}000 - 8{,}000 = 3{,}000$ units (1). Sales could fall by 3,000 units (1) before the business starts making a loss, so it has a reasonable cushion (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a price increase would affect the break-even point, assuming sales are unchanged. (2)
---
The revenue line becomes steeper (1), so it crosses total costs at a lower output — the break-even point falls (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason a break-even chart may be inaccurate. (2)
---
It assumes all output is sold at one price (1), but in reality a business may discount to sell stock or fail to sell everything it makes (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a lender might want to see a break-even analysis. (2)
---
It shows the sales needed to cover costs (1), so the lender can judge whether the business is likely to earn enough to repay a loan (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how break-even analysis could help a new business set its price. (6)
---
By drawing revenue lines for different prices, the owner can see how the break-even output changes: a higher price makes the revenue line steeper and lowers the number of sales needed, while a lower price raises it (2). Comparing each break-even output with the sales that market research suggests are achievable shows which prices are realistic — a price that needs more customers than exist is ruled out (2). The analysis cannot say how many customers each price will attract, and it assumes costs behave simply, so it should be combined with research on what customers will pay; it narrows the choice rather than making it (2).
:::

::: watch
- **No formulae are given** — recall ARR and the cost/revenue/profit relationships.
- ARR: **total profit = return − cost; ÷ years; ÷ cost × 100.** Then **compare** with an alternative to judge.
- AQA asks you to **read and interpret** break-even charts, **not draw** them or use the break-even formula.
- **Margin of safety is in units** and equals output − break-even output.
- Evaluate break-even by its **assumptions** — one price, all sold, linear costs, forecasts.
:::
