## What it is

**Ethics** are moral principles about what is right and wrong. A business behaves **ethically** when it does the right thing even where the law does not require it — it goes beyond the legal minimum.

**Examples of ethical behaviour**: paying suppliers in developing countries a fair price (Fairtrade); paying staff the real living wage and giving good conditions; refusing to use child labour or sweatshops; honest advertising; not testing on animals; protecting customer data; sourcing sustainably; donating to charity; treating all staff equally. **Unethical behaviour**: exploiting workers, misleading customers, aggressive tax avoidance, bribery, selling harmful products to children, dumping waste.

**Benefits of behaving ethically**: attracts customers who care (and can charge a premium — Fairtrade coffee, organic food); builds **reputation and brand loyalty**; attracts and keeps motivated staff; avoids bad publicity, fines and boycotts; attracts ethical investors; suppliers stay loyal. **Drawbacks**: **higher costs** — fair wages, better materials, ethical suppliers all cost more; higher prices may lose price-sensitive customers; competitors who ignore ethics may undercut; some customers do not care; the business may be accused of "greenwashing" if it overclaims.

**Environmental considerations** — how business activity damages the environment, and the pressure to reduce it:

| Issue | What it is | Ways to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| **Traffic congestion** | deliveries, staff and customers add to traffic | consolidated deliveries, electric vehicles, home working, local sourcing |
| **Recycling** | using materials again rather than sending them to landfill | recycled packaging, take-back schemes, separating waste |
| **Disposal of waste** | rubbish and by-products, some hazardous | reduce, reuse, recycle; safe disposal; less packaging |
| **Noise and air pollution** | factories, lorries, aircraft | quieter or cleaner equipment, filters, limits on operating hours |
| **Global warming** | carbon emissions from energy and transport | renewable energy, energy efficiency, carbon offsetting |
| **Scarce resources** | using up non-renewables such as oil, timber, minerals | sustainable sourcing, renewable materials, efficiency |

**Sustainability** means meeting today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.

**Why businesses go green**: customer demand and reputation; laws and taxes on pollution; **cost savings** in the long run (less energy, less packaging, less waste); attracting staff; pressure from the community and investors; ethical values of the owners. **Costs of going green**: investment in new equipment and vehicles; more expensive sustainable materials; time to change processes; possible loss of competitiveness against cheaper, dirtier rivals.

**The trade-off** — the heart of every question: ethical and environmental choices usually **cost money now** and **pay back later** in reputation, loyalty, lower risk and sometimes lower running costs. Whether it is worth it depends on the business's customers (do they care and will they pay?), its competitors, its finances and the size of the cost.

Key terms to learn:

- **Ethics**: moral principles about what is right and wrong.
- **Ethical behaviour**: acting in a way that is morally right, beyond what the law requires.
- **Environmental responsibility**: a business acting to reduce the damage its activities cause to the environment.
- **Sustainability**: using resources in a way that does not harm future generations.
- **Fairtrade**: a scheme guaranteeing producers in developing countries a fair price.

::: method
1. **Define ethics as going beyond the law**, and give an example applied to the business.
2. **Benefits are mostly long-term and about reputation; costs are mostly short-term and about money.** Build every answer around that.
3. **Environmental questions**: name the specific issue (congestion, waste, emissions), the effect on stakeholders, then what the business could do and what it would cost.
4. **Trade-off**: show the two sides in numbers or consequences — "paying growers 20% more raises costs, but Fairtrade branding lets it charge 30% more and win customers".
5. **Judgement**: it depends on whether *this* business's customers care and will pay, how much the change costs, and what competitors do. Note that some green measures save money, so there is not always a trade-off.
:::

::: example Define
What is meant by "ethical behaviour" in business? (2)

Acting in a way that is morally right (1), such as paying fair wages or sourcing responsibly, even when the law does not require it (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a benefit
Explain one benefit to a chocolate maker of using Fairtrade cocoa. (3)

Attracts customers (1). Many consumers prefer to buy products that treat farmers fairly, and the Fairtrade logo signals this on the packaging (1), so the maker gains sales and can charge a higher price, which may cover the extra cost of the cocoa (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — an environmental measure
A parcel delivery firm is considering replacing its diesel vans with electric ones. Analyse the effects on the business. (6)

The new vans cost far more to buy, so the firm must borrow or use retained profit, raising costs in the short term and reducing profit for the first years (2). Once bought, electric vans are cheaper to run — lower fuel and maintenance costs — and avoid clean-air-zone charges in cities, so running costs per delivery fall and the investment pays back over time (2). The firm also cuts air pollution and carbon emissions, which improves its reputation with customers and businesses who want green deliveries and may win contracts from them; overall a cost now for lower costs and more sales later, provided it can fund the purchase (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — the trade-off
A fast-fashion retailer is considering switching to sustainable fabrics that would raise its costs by 15%. Evaluate whether it should. (9)

For: young customers increasingly care about the environment and criticise fast fashion; sustainable fabrics protect the brand from boycotts and bad publicity, attract ethically minded shoppers, and prepare the business for tougher environmental laws; staff and investors may also prefer a responsible employer (3). Against: fast fashion competes on very low prices, and a 15% cost rise must either be passed on — losing price-sensitive customers to rivals — or absorbed, cutting profit margins that are already thin; switching suppliers disrupts the supply chain; some customers say they care but still buy the cheapest (3). Judgement: it should switch gradually, starting with a sustainable range priced slightly higher to test whether its customers will pay, rather than moving all products at once; the right answer depends on how price-sensitive its customers are and whether competitors are moving the same way — being left as the only unsustainable brand could cost more than 15% (3).
:::

::: q
State two ways a business could behave ethically. (2)
---
Pay a fair wage; source materials from Fairtrade suppliers (or honest advertising, no child labour, charity donations).
:::

::: q
Explain one drawback of behaving ethically. (2)
---
Higher costs (1): fair wages or ethically sourced materials cost more, reducing profit unless prices rise (1).
:::

::: q
State three environmental issues caused by business activity. (3)
---
Traffic congestion; waste disposal; air pollution (or noise, global warming, use of scarce resources).
:::

::: q
Explain one way a supermarket could reduce its waste. (2)
---
Sell products close to their date at a discount or donate them to food banks (1), reducing food sent to landfill and the cost of disposal (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a business might go beyond what environmental law requires. (3)
---
To improve its reputation (1): customers increasingly choose brands seen as green (1), so cutting emissions or packaging can raise sales and loyalty, outweighing the cost (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way behaving sustainably could reduce a business's costs. (3)
---
Energy efficiency (1): installing LED lighting or insulation cuts electricity and heating bills (1), so after the initial cost, running costs are lower every year (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one effect of a factory's air pollution on its local community. (2)
---
Residents suffer poorer health and a worse environment (1), leading to complaints and objections that could restrict the factory's expansion (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how behaving unethically might damage a clothing brand. (6)
---
If the brand is found to use sweatshop labour, the story spreads quickly through social media and the press, so customers who dislike exploitation stop buying and may organise boycotts, cutting sales (2). Retailers may drop the brand to protect their own reputation, and skilled staff may leave or refuse to join a company seen as unethical, raising recruitment costs (2). Regaining trust is slow and expensive — audits, higher supplier payments, advertising — so the short-term saving from cheap labour is outweighed by lost revenue and long-term damage to the brand's value (2).
:::

::: q
Evaluate whether a small café can afford to behave ethically. (9)
---
Against: a small café has thin margins, so paying the real living wage, buying Fairtrade coffee and using compostable cups all raise costs it may struggle to pass on when chains nearby are cheaper; it lacks the scale to negotiate discounts on ethical supplies (3). For: ethical choices are part of a small café's appeal — customers who choose an independent over a chain often value fair treatment of staff and suppliers and will pay a little more; motivated, fairly paid staff give better service and stay longer, cutting recruitment costs; some measures, such as reducing waste and energy use, save money (3). Judgement: it can afford selected ethical behaviour that its customers value and that costs little — fair pay, local sourcing, waste reduction — and should build them into its brand; adopting every ethical measure regardless of cost could sink it, so it depends on choosing the ones customers will pay for (3).
:::

::: watch
- Ethical = **beyond the law**. Obeying the law is not ethical behaviour, just legal.
- Benefits: **reputation, loyalty, staff, avoiding scandal** (long-term). Costs: **money now** (short-term). Say which is which.
- Name the **specific** environmental issue from the spec list — congestion, recycling, waste, noise, air pollution, global warming, scarce resources.
- Some green measures **save money** — a strong evaluation point that not every choice is a trade-off.
- Judge by **whether this business's customers care and will pay**, and what competitors do.
:::
