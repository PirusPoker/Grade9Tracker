## What it is

The **marketing mix** is the combination of the **4Ps** a business uses to sell to its target market: **Product**, **Price**, **Promotion** and **Place**. The four have to fit each other and the customer — a luxury product at a bargain price sold in a discount store makes no sense.

### Price

| Method | How it works | When used | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Price skimming** | launch at a **high** price, then lower it over time | new, innovative products with no rivals yet (games consoles, new phones); recovers development costs from early adopters | high price attracts competitors; may deter customers |
| **Penetration pricing** | launch at a **low** price to win customers quickly, then raise it | entering a competitive market; building market share and habit (a new streaming service) | low margins at first; customers may leave when price rises |
| **Competitive pricing** | set at or near what rivals charge | markets with similar products where price is easily compared (petrol, supermarkets) | no price advantage; must compete on other Ps |
| **Loss leader** | sell a product **below cost** to draw customers in, who then buy other, profitable items | supermarkets (bread, milk), printers (sold cheap, ink dear), games consoles | loses money unless customers buy more; may be copied |
| **Cost-plus pricing** | cost per unit **plus a mark-up** (percentage) | simple; guarantees a profit per unit if it sells; small businesses, manufacturers | ignores what customers will pay and what rivals charge |

*What influences a pricing decision*: **costs** (must be covered in the long run); **the market and customers** (what they will pay, their incomes, how price-sensitive they are); **competition** (many rivals → prices close to theirs; a unique product → freedom); **the stage of the product life cycle** (high at launch to skim, cut at maturity to compete, cut in decline to clear stock); the business's objectives and image. The basic rule: as price rises, demand usually falls — so a higher price raises revenue per unit but may cut the number sold.

### Product

The product must satisfy the customer's need better than the alternatives. Key ideas: **design** (function, appearance, cost to make — the design mix); **image**; a **unique selling point (USP)** — the feature that makes it different from rivals (a taste, a guarantee, speed, ethics); **brand image** — the personality and reputation attached to the name, built through consistent quality and promotion, which allows premium prices and loyalty; **product range** and **new product development**. *Risks of new products*: high development cost, most launches fail, competitors copy, tastes change, damage to the brand if it flops.

- **Research and development** — before launch: costs, no sales.
- **Introduction (launch)** — sales low and rising slowly; heavy promotion; usually a loss.
- **Growth** — sales rise fast; profit appears; competitors enter; wider distribution.
- **Maturity** — sales peak and level off; market saturated; heavy competition; profits highest but price pressure; most products spend longest here.
- **Decline** — sales fall as tastes and technology move on; price cuts, promotion stopped, product withdrawn.

**Extension strategies** at maturity delay decline: update or improve the product (new flavour, new model); new packaging; **new markets** (export, a new segment); **new uses**; price cuts or promotion campaigns; **rebranding**. They cost money and may only delay the inevitable.

### Promotion

Methods: **advertising** (TV, radio, press, online, social media, billboards — reaches many; costly for TV, cheap and targeted online), **sales promotion** (discounts, BOGOF, coupons, loyalty cards, free samples, competitions — quick boost but may cheapen the brand), **personal selling** (sales staff — persuasive for expensive or complex products; costly), **public relations** (press releases, events, sponsorship — builds image), **direct marketing** (email, mail, texts to named customers), **social media and influencers**, **sponsorship**, **product placement**. *What decides the promotional mix*: the **budget**, the **target market** and which media reach it, the **product** (complex → personal selling; mass-market → TV), the **stage of the life cycle** (awareness at launch; reminders at maturity), **competitors'** activity, and the **objective** (awareness, sales, image).

### Place (distribution)

How the product reaches the customer:

- **Retailers** — shops sell to the public; wide reach and customers can see the product; the retailer takes a margin and controls display.
- **Wholesalers** — buy in bulk from producers and sell smaller quantities to retailers; a middleman (producer → wholesaler → retailer → customer).
- **Direct selling** — from producer to customer: **telesales** (phone — personal, but intrusive and costly), **e-commerce** (websites — 24/7, global, low cost, but delivery and competition), **m-commerce** (apps and mobile — convenience, impulse buying, notifications), factory shops, catalogues.

The choice depends on the product (fresh, bulky, expensive?), the customers (where and how they shop), cost and control, and the image wanted (exclusive brands limit outlets).

### Integrating the mix

The 4Ps must be **consistent** — a premium product needs a premium price, quality promotion and exclusive outlets; a budget product needs low price, mass promotion and wide availability. The mix **changes over time**: at launch, high promotion and skimming or penetration pricing; at maturity, competitive pricing and reminder advertising; in decline, price cuts and fewer outlets. It also changes with technology (e-commerce, social media), competition and customer tastes.

::: terms
- **Marketing mix**: the combination of product, price, promotion and place (the 4Ps) used to market a product.
- **Price skimming**: setting a high price for a new product, then lowering it later as competitors arrive.
- **Penetration pricing**: setting a low price when a product is launched to win customers and market share quickly, then raising it.
- **Competitive pricing**: setting a price at or close to the prices rivals charge.
- **Loss leader**: a product sold below cost to attract customers who then buy other, profitable products.
- **Cost-plus pricing**: adding a percentage mark-up to the cost of making each unit.
- **Mark-up**: the amount, usually a percentage of cost, added to the unit cost to set the price.
- **Unique selling point (USP)**: a feature that makes a product different from, and better than, competitors' products.
- **Brand image**: the personality and reputation customers associate with a brand name.
- **Product differentiation**: making a product distinct from competitors' — through design, quality, features or branding — so customers prefer it.
- **Product life cycle**: the stages a product passes through — development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline.
- **Extension strategy**: action taken to prolong the life of a product at maturity, such as a new version, new packaging or a new market.
- **Product portfolio**: the whole range of products a business sells.
- **Boston matrix**: a model that classifies products by market share and market growth — stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs — to manage the product portfolio.
- **Promotion**: communicating with customers to inform and persuade them to buy — advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, public relations.
- **Sales promotion**: short-term incentives to buy — discounts, buy-one-get-one-free, coupons, free samples, competitions.
- **Promotional mix**: the combination of promotional methods a business uses.
- **Distribution channel**: the route a product takes from producer to customer — directly, through a retailer, or through a wholesaler and a retailer.
- **Retailer**: a business that sells goods to the final customer, usually in small quantities.
- **Wholesaler**: a business that buys in bulk from producers and sells in smaller quantities to retailers.
- **Telesales**: selling directly to customers by telephone.
:::

::: method
1. **Pricing**: name the method, explain how it works, then *why it suits this product's situation* (new and unique → skimming; competitive → penetration or competitive; supermarket → loss leader). Cost-plus: unit cost × (1 + mark-up); show it.
2. **Life cycle**: identify the stage from the sales trend in the case, then say what the mix should be at that stage; at maturity propose an **extension strategy** with a reason and a cost.
3. **Boston matrix**: classify each product (share high/low, growth high/low), name the category, and say what to do with it; then judge the balance of the portfolio.
4. **Promotion**: choose methods for the target market and budget; give a reason and a drawback.
5. **Place**: choose the channel for the product and customer; weigh reach and cost against control and margin.
6. **Integration**: for any "evaluate the mix" question, check the four Ps agree with each other and with the target segment; say how the mix should change as the product ages.
:::

::: example Calculate — cost-plus
A furniture maker's unit cost for a chair is £40. It uses cost-plus pricing with a 60% mark-up. Calculate the selling price. (2)

Mark-up = 60% × £40 = £24 (1).

Price = £40 + £24 = £64 (1). (Or in one step: £40 × 1.6 = £64.)
:::

::: example Explain — a pricing method
Explain why a technology company might use price skimming for a new virtual-reality headset. (3)

Skimming sets a high launch price (1). The headset is new with few rivals, so enthusiasts will pay a premium to have it first (1), letting the company recover its development costs quickly before cutting the price to reach a wider market when competitors appear (1).
:::

::: example Explain — penetration pricing
A new streaming service launches at £2.99 a month when established rivals charge about £10. Explain why it might use penetration pricing. (3)

The market is crowded and customers already subscribe to rivals (1), so a very low price gives them a reason to try it and builds a large subscriber base quickly (1); once people are used to the service and its shows, it can raise the price towards rivals' levels, betting that most will stay rather than go to the trouble of cancelling (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a loss leader
Explain how a supermarket might benefit from selling bread below cost as a loss leader. (3)

Cheap bread attracts shoppers into the store, because it is bought often and its price is easy to compare (1). Once inside, most customers also buy other items — butter, meat, household goods — on which the supermarket makes a profit (1), so the profit on the rest of the basket more than covers the loss on the bread, and shoppers may switch their whole weekly shop from a rival (1).
:::

::: example Explain — USP and brand image
A small brewery sells its beer at £1 a can more than supermarket brands. Explain how a USP and brand image could let it charge this higher price. (4)

USP: every beer is brewed with local, organic hops in small batches (1), so it tastes different from mass-produced beer and customers who value this cannot get the same thing elsewhere (1).

Brand image: distinctive can designs, a story about the brewery and its town, and a reputation for quality make customers see it as a premium, authentic product (1), so they are willing to pay more for it and stay loyal rather than switching to a cheaper beer (1).
:::

::: example Explain — benefits and risks of a new product
A crisp manufacturer is launching a range of lentil-based snacks. Explain one benefit and one risk of developing this new product. (4)

Benefit: it reaches a new, growing segment of health-conscious snackers (1), adding sales and spreading the business's risk if sales of traditional crisps fall (1).

Risk: development, new machinery and launch advertising cost a lot before any sales are made (1), and if shoppers do not like the taste the product will fail and that money is lost — most new food products are withdrawn within a few years (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — the life cycle
Sales of a soft-drink brand have been flat for three years after rapid growth. Analyse how an extension strategy could help the business. (6)

Flat sales after growth show the brand is at maturity: the market is saturated and competitors are fighting for the same customers, so without action sales will start to decline (2). Launching new flavours or a sugar-free version gives existing customers a reason to buy again and attracts new segments, while new packaging and a promotion campaign renew interest; entering export markets finds customers where the product is still new (2). Extension strategies cost money — development, marketing — and may only delay decline; the business should compare the cost with the profit the brand still earns and consider whether the money would be better spent on a new product that becomes the next star (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — choosing the promotional mix
Priya is opening a small independent gift shop in a market town with a promotion budget of £2,000. Analyse how she should promote the shop. (6)

With a small budget, national advertising is out of the question; local, targeted methods reach the people who can actually visit — a launch event with the local newspaper covering it (public relations) costs little and creates word of mouth (2). Social media posts showing new stock, run by Priya herself, cost only time, reach local shoppers repeatedly and let customers share what they like; a small paid local Facebook advert could target people within ten miles (2). A sales promotion such as 10% off for the first month or a loyalty card would turn first visits into regular custom, though it reduces her margin; a mix of free PR and social media with one short promotion suits her budget best, and she should track which method brings customers in (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — choosing a distribution channel
A craft gin distillery sells through a few local shops. It is deciding whether to sell mainly through its own website instead. Analyse the effects of this change. (6)

Selling direct online, the distillery keeps the whole price instead of giving the shops their margin — often a third or more — so its profit per bottle rises (2). A website also reaches gin drinkers across the UK, 24 hours a day, rather than only local shoppers, so sales could grow well beyond what a few shops achieve (2). But it must pay for the website, packaging and courier delivery of heavy, fragile bottles, and handle age checks and breakages; without shops, customers cannot see or taste the gin, so it must invest in online promotion to be found — the change pays only if online sales are large enough to cover these costs (2).
:::

::: example Explain — how the mix changes over the life cycle
A new games console is launched at a high price with heavy advertising. Explain how its marketing mix is likely to change when it reaches maturity. (4)

Price: at maturity rival consoles compete directly, so the price is cut or bundles with free games are offered to keep it competitive (1), reaching more price-sensitive buyers who waited (1).

Product and promotion: a slimmer or updated version is released as an extension strategy (1), with advertising switched from launch hype to reminding customers of the games library and special offers (1).
:::

::: example Boston matrix
A games company has: Game A — 40% share of a fast-growing market; Game B — 35% share of a market that has stopped growing; Game C — 3% share of a fast-growing market; Game D — 2% share of a shrinking market. Classify each product and recommend what the company should do. (6)

A is a star (high share, high growth) — invest in updates and marketing to hold the lead as the market grows (1). B is a cash cow (high share, low growth) — spend little, keep it selling and use its profit to fund the others (1). C is a question mark (low share, high growth) — decide whether investment could turn it into a star, or drop it (1). D is a dog (low share, low growth) — withdraw it or sell it off unless it costs nothing to keep (1). The portfolio is reasonably balanced: B's cash should fund A and a decisive test of C, while D is cut; the risk is that when A's market matures the company needs a new question mark ready to develop (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — the mix
A new premium ice-cream brand plans: luxury ingredients; a high price; promotion through Instagram influencers; sold only through its own website. Evaluate whether this marketing mix is likely to succeed. (9)

Product, price and promotion are consistent — a premium product justifies a high price, and influencers reach the young, affluent segment likely to buy it, building brand image cheaply compared with TV (3). Place is the weak link: ice cream is an impulse purchase bought when people want it now, and it is expensive and difficult to deliver frozen by post, so selling only online limits sales severely and adds delivery costs that push the price higher; premium ice cream normally sells through upmarket supermarkets and delis where shoppers can see it (3). Judgement: the mix will not succeed as planned because place contradicts how the product is bought; adding selected retailers — while keeping the website for gift packs — would make the four Ps consistent; success then depends on securing shelf space and whether the price is acceptable next to established premium brands (3).
:::

Against: limited distribution means low sales volume and may be hard to find; influencers may not reach older, wealthier buyers; a high price may deter customers in a crowded premium market; competition from established brands (3).
Judgement: justified — likely to succeed in a niche if the product quality justifies the price, but growth may need wider distribution such as premium supermarkets; depends on product quality and the size of the target market (3).
Levels: 7–9 — both sides developed and applied to the case, with a justified judgement; 4–6 — developed points with some application, a judgement that is not fully supported; 1–3 — simple points, little use of the case.
