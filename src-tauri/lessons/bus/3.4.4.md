## What it is

**Training** is teaching employees the skills and knowledge they need to do their job well. It costs time and money, so a business must be clear about what it gains.

**Benefits of training:**

- **Higher productivity** — trained staff work faster with fewer mistakes, so unit costs fall.
- **Coping with new technology** — new machines, software and systems are useless until staff can use them.
- **Motivation** — training shows the business values the employee and opens up promotion; staff feel more confident.
- **Retention** — motivated, developing staff stay, cutting turnover and recruitment costs (though trained staff are also more attractive to rivals).
- **Quality** — fewer defects and complaints; consistent standards across sites.
- **Customer service** — staff who know the products and how to deal with people give better service.
- Also: **health and safety** compliance, fewer accidents; flexibility (multi-skilled staff can cover each other); a better reputation as an employer.

**Costs of training**: course fees and trainers' pay; lost output while staff are away; the risk that trained staff leave for competitors; the time of managers who deliver it.

**Types of training:**

**Induction training** — for **new employees** when they start. Covers: the business's history, aims and culture; the site, facilities and health and safety procedures; who's who and the structure; the employee's role, systems and equipment; policies (holidays, sickness, conduct). *What it achieves*: new starters become productive quickly, feel welcome and confident (so they are more likely to stay past the first months, when turnover is highest), make fewer early mistakes, understand safety rules (reducing accidents) and know the standards expected. *Cost*: staff time; new starters are not productive during it.

**On-the-job training** — learning **while doing the job**, at the workplace: shadowing an experienced worker, being shown by a supervisor, mentoring, coaching, learning by doing.

*Advantages*: cheap — no course fees or travel; the employee keeps producing while learning; it is specific to the business's own methods and equipment; easy to organise; the trainee sees the real situation. *Disadvantages*: the trainer's own output falls while they teach; bad habits and shortcuts get passed on; the trainer may not be a good teacher; no outside expertise or qualification; mistakes are made on real products or customers.

**Off-the-job training** — learning **away from the workplace**: courses at a college or training centre, online courses, conferences, qualifications, or a separate training room in the business.

*Advantages*: taught by specialist trainers; brings in new ideas and best practice from outside; can lead to recognised qualifications (motivating); no disruption to the workplace during learning; suits complex or theoretical skills (accountancy, management, new software). *Disadvantages*: expensive — fees, travel, accommodation; lost output while the employee is away; may be too general and not match the business's own methods; qualified staff may leave; hard to check what was learned.

**Choosing**: on-the-job for practical, business-specific skills and where cost matters (a café, a warehouse); off-the-job for specialist, technical or management skills, qualifications, and when in-house expertise does not exist (a new accounting system, a professional qualification). Many businesses use both.

::: terms
- **Training**: teaching employees the skills and knowledge they need to do their job well.
- **Induction training**: training for new employees when they join, introducing the business, its procedures, health and safety and their role.
- **On-the-job training**: training carried out in the workplace while the employee does the job, often by watching and working with an experienced colleague.
- **Off-the-job training**: training carried out away from the normal workplace — a college course, a training centre, an online course.
- **Productivity**: the output per worker in a given period of time.
- **Multi-skilled**: able to do several different jobs, so staff can cover for each other.
- **Staff retention**: keeping employees in the business rather than losing them to other employers.
:::

::: method
1. **Benefit chains**: training → skills → productivity/quality/service → lower costs or higher sales → profit; or training → feels valued → motivation → retention → lower recruitment costs.
2. **Induction**: say what it covers *and* what it achieves — productive quickly, safe, stays.
3. **On vs off the job**: cost, disruption, relevance, quality of teaching, qualifications, risk of leaving. Two developed points each side.
4. **Recommend for this business**: match the skill needed (practical vs specialist), the size and finances, and whether expertise exists in-house. Give a condition ("off-the-job for the new software, provided the supplier's course covers their version").
5. **Always mention the cost side** — and the risk that trained staff leave — for evaluation.
:::

::: example Explain — induction
Explain one benefit to a supermarket of giving new employees induction training. (3)

New staff become productive quickly (1). Learning the till system, stock procedures and store layout on day one means they can serve customers without constant help (1), so colleagues are not distracted and customers are not kept waiting during the starter's first weeks (1).
:::

::: example Explain — on-the-job
Explain one disadvantage of on-the-job training for a garage. (3)

Bad habits passed on (1). If the experienced mechanic doing the training takes shortcuts on safety checks, the trainee learns to do the same (1), so poor practice spreads and could lead to faulty repairs and complaints (1).
:::

::: example Explain — off-the-job training
Explain one advantage of sending an electrician's apprentices to college one day a week for off-the-job training. (3)

Expert teaching and qualifications (1): college lecturers teach the theory and regulations in depth, with equipment for practising safely (1), so the apprentices gain recognised qualifications and a broader understanding than they would pick up on customers' jobs — and the business's own electricians are not slowed down by teaching (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — training and technology
A hotel is installing a new booking and payment system. Analyse how training staff to use it could benefit the hotel. (6)

Without training, staff would make errors — double bookings, wrong charges — and check-in would slow down, frustrating guests; training lets them use the system confidently from the start, so the investment in technology actually delivers faster service (2). Trained staff can use the system's features — upselling rooms, tracking guest preferences — raising revenue per guest and improving service and reviews (2). Training also reassures staff who fear technology will make their jobs harder or redundant, maintaining motivation and reducing the chance that experienced receptionists leave; the cost is a few days of lost time, small against the cost of a system nobody can use properly (2).
:::

::: example Recommend — on or off the job
A small marketing agency wants its five junior designers to learn advanced video-editing software that none of the senior staff know well. Recommend on-the-job or off-the-job training. Justify your answer. (9)

On-the-job training would be cheap and let the designers keep working on client projects while they learn, and the skills would be applied directly to the agency's own work; but nobody in the agency knows the software well, so there is no one to teach it, and learning by trial and error on client work risks poor results and wasted time (3). Off-the-job training — a course run by the software provider or a specialist — would teach best practice properly and quickly, possibly with a certificate that motivates the juniors and impresses clients; but five people on a course means fees, and days of lost billable work, and the content may go beyond what the agency needs (3). Recommendation: off-the-job, because the expertise does not exist in-house, and the cost of five juniors fumbling with software on client projects is higher than the course fees; the agency could send two designers, who then train the other three on the job, combining the strengths of both methods and cutting the cost (3).
:::

::: q
State two benefits of training to a business. (2)
---
Higher productivity; better quality (or motivation, retention, customer service, coping with new technology).
:::

::: q
State two things covered in induction training. (2)
---
Health and safety procedures; the employee's role and the business's systems (or the layout, colleagues, policies, aims).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of on-the-job training for a small café. (3)
---
Low cost (1): a new barista learns from an experienced colleague during shifts (1), so the café pays no course fees and the trainee is serving customers while learning (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of off-the-job training. (3)
---
Specialist teaching (1): an outside trainer knows the latest techniques and can explain them properly (1), giving the employee skills the business could not teach itself (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one cost of training to a business. (2)
---
Lost output (1): while staff are on a course they are not producing or serving customers, so revenue is lower that day (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how training can improve staff retention. (3)
---
Training shows employees the business is investing in them (1), so they feel valued and see a path to promotion (1), making them less likely to leave for another employer (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one risk to a business of paying for employees to gain qualifications. (2)
---
Qualified staff become more attractive to competitors (1) and may leave for higher pay, so the business loses its investment (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how training could improve the quality of a restaurant's food and service. (6)
---
Training kitchen staff in recipes, portion sizes and hygiene means dishes are prepared consistently and safely, so customers get the same quality every visit and food is not wasted through mistakes (2). Training waiting staff in product knowledge and handling complaints improves service — they can recommend dishes, spot problems early and deal with unhappy diners calmly, raising satisfaction and reviews (2). Better quality and service bring repeat customers and word-of-mouth recommendations, increasing revenue; the restaurant must find time to train during quiet periods and accept the cost, but poor quality costs far more in lost customers (2).
:::

::: watch
- Every benefit needs its **chain to the business** — productivity, quality, service, retention → cost or sales.
- **Induction is for new starters**; say what it covers and what it achieves.
- On-the-job: **cheap, relevant, but bad habits and trainer's lost time.** Off-the-job: **expert, qualifications, but expensive and disruptive.**
- The **recommendation is the mark** — choose for this business and skill, with a condition; a combination is often best.
- Mention the **cost of training** and the **risk trained staff leave** in evaluations.
:::
