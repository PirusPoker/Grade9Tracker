## What it is

Once a business has survived, it usually wants to **grow** — for more profit, economies of scale, market share and security. Two routes:

**Organic (internal) growth** — growing from within, using the business's own resources:

- **Opening new stores or branches** — more locations, more customers.
- **Franchising** — the business (franchisor) sells the right to use its name, products and systems to independent operators (franchisees), who pay a fee and a share of sales. Fast, low-cost growth for the franchisor; the franchisee gets a proven brand but pays for it and loses independence. Franchisees are motivated as owners, but quality is harder to control.
- **E-commerce** — selling online reaches national or global customers without new premises.
- **Outsourcing** — paying another firm to do part of the work (deliveries, IT, manufacturing) so the business can grow without buying capacity; risks losing control of quality.
- Also: launching new products, entering new markets, expanding the premises.

*Advantages*: lower risk, keeps control, growth at a manageable pace, financed from profit. *Disadvantages*: slow; limited by the business's own finance and management; competitors may grow faster.

**External growth** — joining with another business:

- **Merger** — two businesses agree to combine into one.
- **Takeover** — one business buys control of another (over 50% of its shares), with or without agreement.

*Advantages*: fast; instantly gains the other firm's customers, products, staff, skills and premises; removes a competitor; economies of scale; access to new markets. *Disadvantages*: expensive; clashes of culture and systems; redundancies and low morale; diseconomies of scale from sudden size; may be blocked by the competition authority; the acquired business may have hidden problems.

**Economies of scale** — as a business grows, its **average unit cost** falls:

$$\text{average unit cost} = \frac{\text{total cost}}{\text{output}}$$

- **Purchasing economies** — buying materials in bulk earns discounts, so the cost per unit of materials falls.
- **Technical economies** — a large business can afford specialised machinery and production lines that produce more per hour; expensive equipment is used fully, spreading its cost over many units.
- Also (for completeness): managerial (specialist managers), marketing (advertising cost spread over more sales), financial (cheaper loans).

**Diseconomies of scale** — grow *too* big and average unit cost **rises**:

- **Poor communication** — messages pass through more layers and sites; they are delayed, distorted or lost.
- **Coordination problems** — managers cannot oversee everything; departments duplicate work or pull in different directions; decisions slow.
- **Falling motivation** — workers feel like a number in a huge organisation, so productivity falls and absenteeism rises.

::: terms
- **Organic growth**: growth from within the business, using its own resources — new branches, new products, franchising, selling online.
- **External growth**: growth by joining with another business through a merger or a takeover.
- **Merger**: two businesses agreeing to join together to form one business.
- **Takeover**: one business buying control of another by buying more than half of its shares.
- **Franchise**: an agreement that lets one business trade under the name, products and systems of another in return for a fee and a share of sales.
- **Franchisor**: the business that sells the right to use its brand and system.
- **Franchisee**: the independent operator who buys the right to trade under the franchisor's brand.
- **Outsourcing**: paying another business to carry out work that was previously done in-house.
- **Economies of scale**: the fall in average unit cost as a business increases its output.
- **Purchasing economies**: lower unit costs from buying materials in bulk at a discount.
- **Technical economies**: lower unit costs from using larger, more advanced or more fully used machinery and equipment.
- **Diseconomies of scale**: the rise in average unit cost when a business becomes too large — from poor communication, coordination and motivation.
- **Average unit cost**: total cost divided by the number of units produced.
:::

::: method
1. **Organic or external?** Identify which route the case describes, then give advantages and disadvantages *of that route for that business*.
2. **Franchising**: answer from the right side — franchisor (fast growth, fees, less control) or franchisee (proven brand, fees, no independence).
3. **Economies of scale are about average unit cost.** Total cost rises with output; the point is cost *per unit* falls. Name the type (purchasing, technical) and give the mechanism.
4. **Calculate** average unit cost = total cost ÷ output, before and after growth; interpret the fall (or rise).
5. **Diseconomies**: communication, coordination, motivation — link each to *why* cost per unit rises.
6. **Evaluate a growth decision**: speed and gains vs cost, risk and control; conclude with "it depends on" finance, the state of the market, and management capacity.
:::

::: example Calculate
A bakery produced 10,000 loaves last year at a total cost of £12,000. This year it produced 25,000 loaves at a total cost of £25,000. Calculate the average unit cost each year and explain the change. (4)

Last year: £12,000 ÷ 10,000 = £1.20 per loaf (1).

This year: £25,000 ÷ 25,000 = £1.00 per loaf (1).

Unit cost fell by 20p because the bakery gained economies of scale — bulk-buying flour more cheaply and using its ovens more fully — even though total cost more than doubled (2).
:::

::: example Explain — purchasing economies
A supermarket chain grows from 50 to 200 stores. Explain how purchasing economies of scale could benefit it. (3)

It now buys four times as much from each supplier — tins, milk, bread (1). Suppliers want to keep such a large order, so they offer bulk discounts and the chain can negotiate lower prices per item (1). Its cost per unit falls, so it can either keep its prices and earn a higher profit margin, or cut prices to win customers from smaller rivals who pay more for the same goods (1).
:::

::: example Explain — technical economies
Explain how a growing soft-drinks manufacturer could benefit from technical economies of scale. (3)

With higher output it can afford an automated bottling line that fills thousands of bottles an hour, which a small firm could not justify (1). The machine's large cost is spread across millions of bottles, and it needs fewer workers per bottle than filling by smaller machines (1), so the cost of producing each bottle falls, making the firm more competitive on price (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a diseconomy of scale
A software company has grown from 40 to 900 employees across five countries in four years. Explain one diseconomy of scale it might suffer. (3)

Poor communication (1): instructions from head office now pass through several layers of managers and time zones, so they arrive late or get changed on the way (1). Teams may work on the wrong version of a product or duplicate each other's work, wasting staff time that is paid for — so the cost of producing each piece of software rises instead of falling (1).
:::

::: example Explain — franchising
Explain one benefit to a coffee chain of growing through franchising. (3)

Fast growth at low cost (1). Franchisees pay for their own premises and equipment, so the chain can open many outlets without borrowing (1), while collecting fees and a share of sales from each one (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a takeover
A national gym chain takes over a rival with 30 gyms. Analyse the possible disadvantages. (6)

The takeover is expensive — the chain must pay for the rival's shares, probably by borrowing, so interest payments rise and profit falls until the gyms pay back (2). The two businesses have different systems, pricing and cultures; merging memberships, IT and staff causes confusion, and redundancies among duplicated head-office roles lower morale across both firms (2). Managing 30 extra sites at once risks diseconomies of scale — slower decisions, poorer communication with gym managers, and falling service standards that could drive members away, so the expected economies of scale may not appear for some time (2).
:::

::: example Evaluate — organic vs external
A profitable regional pizza chain with 12 restaurants wants to double in size within three years. Evaluate whether it should grow organically or by taking over another chain. (9)

Organic growth — opening its own restaurants — keeps control of quality and culture, is financed from profit with less debt, and each new site can be tested before the next; but opening 12 restaurants in three years is slow, needs many new managers, and rivals may take the best sites first (3). A takeover doubles the business at once, brings trained staff and existing customers, removes a competitor and delivers purchasing economies immediately; but it costs far more up front, the acquired restaurants may differ in menu and standards, and integrating them risks diseconomies and staff losses (3). Judgement: with a three-year target, a takeover is the only realistic way to double, provided a suitable chain is available at a sensible price and the business can finance it; if not, franchising offers a middle route — faster than opening its own sites, cheaper than a takeover. The choice depends on the finance available and how much control over quality it is willing to give up (3).
:::

::: q
State two methods of organic growth. (2)
---
Opening new stores; franchising (or e-commerce, outsourcing, new products).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a merger and a takeover. (2)
---
A merger is two businesses agreeing to combine as equals (1); a takeover is one business buying control of another, which may not want to be bought (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of external growth over organic growth. (3)
---
Speed (1): a takeover gives the business the other firm's outlets and customers immediately (1), whereas opening new sites one by one could take years (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one purchasing economy of scale a supermarket chain gains. (3)
---
Buying in bulk (1): ordering thousands of units from a supplier earns a large discount (1), so the cost of each item on the shelf is lower than a small shop pays (1).
:::

::: q
A factory's total costs are £400,000 for 50,000 units. Calculate the average unit cost. (2)
---
£400,000 ÷ 50,000 = £8 per unit.
:::

::: q
Explain why economies of scale are measured by average unit cost, not total cost. (2)
---
Total cost always rises when more is produced (1); only a fall in the cost per unit shows the business has become more efficient as it grew (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one diseconomy of scale a business might face after rapid growth. (3)
---
Poor communication (1): with more layers and sites, messages take longer and may be misunderstood (1), causing mistakes and delays that raise the cost of each unit (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one drawback to a franchisee of buying a franchise. (3)
---
Loss of independence (1): the franchisee must follow the franchisor's menu, prices and rules (1), so they cannot adapt to local customers and must pay ongoing fees from their profit (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how outsourcing its deliveries could help an online furniture retailer grow. (6)
---
Using a specialist courier means the retailer does not have to buy vans, employ drivers or manage routes, so it can take orders from the whole country without a large investment in capital (2). The courier already has the scale to deliver cheaply and reliably, giving the retailer a lower delivery cost per item than it could achieve itself while it is small — an economy of scale borrowed from the supplier (2). The risk is loss of control: late or damaged deliveries reflect on the retailer, not the courier, so the retailer must choose a reliable partner and monitor performance; done well, outsourcing lets it grow quickly and focus on selling furniture (2).
:::
