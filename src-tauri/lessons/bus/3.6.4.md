## What it is

**Financial statements** record how a business has performed and what it is worth. They matter because owners and managers use them to **judge performance** (is profit rising? are costs under control?), to **make decisions** (expand, cut costs, change prices), and because outsiders — **lenders, investors, suppliers, employees, the tax authority** — rely on them to decide whether to lend, invest, supply or work for the business. Companies must publish them by law.

**The income statement** (profit and loss account) — shows revenue, costs and profit **over a period**, usually a year:

| Line | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue** (sales, turnover) | income from sales | 200,000 |
| **Cost of sales** | the direct cost of the goods sold — materials, stock bought for resale, production wages | 120,000 |
| **Gross profit** | revenue − cost of sales | 80,000 |
| **Expenses** (overheads) | the other running costs — rent, salaries, marketing, utilities, interest | 50,000 |
| **Net profit** (operating profit, profit before tax) | gross profit − expenses | 30,000 |

Gross profit shows how profitable the products themselves are; net profit shows what is left after running the whole business.

**The statement of financial position** (balance sheet) — a **snapshot on one day** (the last day of the financial year) of what the business **owns** and **owes**:

- **Assets** — what the business owns or is owed. **Non-current (fixed) assets** last more than a year: premises, machinery, vehicles. **Current assets** turn into cash within a year: stock (inventory), trade receivables (money owed by customers), cash.
- **Liabilities** — what the business owes. **Current liabilities** due within a year: trade payables (money owed to suppliers), overdraft, tax due. **Non-current liabilities** due after a year: bank loans, mortgages.
- **Net assets** $=$ total assets $-$ total liabilities, which equals the **capital (equity)** — the owners' investment plus retained profit.

It is a **snapshot** because the figures are true only on that day: a big customer paying the next day changes cash and receivables at once. So it can be flattered by timing, and it shows book values (what was paid), not what assets would fetch today.

**The profit margins** — the two ratios AQA requires (not ROCE, not the current ratio):

$$\text{gross profit margin} = \frac{\text{gross profit}}{\text{revenue}} \times 100 \qquad \text{net profit margin} = \frac{\text{net profit}}{\text{revenue}} \times 100$$

Both are **percentages of revenue** — how many pence of profit each pound of sales produces. From the table: GPM $= 80{,}000 \div 200{,}000 \times 100 = 40\%$; NPM $= 30{,}000 \div 200{,}000 \times 100 = 15\%$.

- A **falling gross margin** means cost of sales is rising faster than prices (dearer materials, discounting, waste) — fix by raising prices, finding cheaper suppliers, cutting waste.
- A **falling net margin with a steady gross margin** means expenses are the problem — rent, salaries, marketing have grown — fix by controlling overheads.
- Margins differ by industry: supermarkets run on low margins and huge volumes; jewellers on high margins and low volumes. Compare like with like.

**Judging performance** — a single figure means little. Compare:

- **With previous years** — is the trend up or down? Why?
- **With competitors and the industry average** — a 5% net margin is good for a supermarket, poor for a software firm.
- **With the business's objectives** — did it hit its targets?
- **From different stakeholders' viewpoints**: owners/shareholders want profit and dividends; **lenders** look at profit and liabilities to judge whether loans can be repaid; **employees** look at profit for job security and pay claims; **suppliers** at whether they will be paid; **customers** at whether high margins mean they are overcharged; **the government** at taxable profit; **managers** at margins to find where to act.

Key terms to learn:

- **Income statement**: a financial statement showing a business's revenue, costs and profit over a period.
- **Statement of financial position**: a snapshot of a business's assets, liabilities and capital on a particular date.
- **Gross profit**: revenue minus cost of sales.
- **Net profit**: gross profit minus expenses.
- **Assets**: items a business owns or is owed. **Liabilities**: amounts a business owes.
- **Gross profit margin**: gross profit as a percentage of revenue.
- **Net profit margin**: net profit as a percentage of revenue.

::: method
1. **Know the two statements apart**: income statement = performance over a **period**; statement of financial position = position on a **day**.
2. **Calculate in order**: gross profit = revenue − cost of sales; net profit = gross profit − expenses; then each margin = profit ÷ revenue × 100. Show every line; give margins to one decimal place with a % sign.
3. **Interpret the margins**, not just state them: compare with last year or a rival, say which costs moved, and suggest the action.
4. **Assets vs liabilities**: classify items given; explain "snapshot" with a timing example.
5. **Stakeholder judgement**: for each stakeholder named, say which figure they look at and why; an "evaluate performance" question needs comparisons (years, competitors, objectives) and a conclusion that depends on the viewpoint.
:::

::: example Calculate — the margins
A shop had revenue of 200,000, cost of sales of 120,000 and expenses of 50,000. Calculate its gross and net profit margins. (4)

Gross profit $= 200{,}000 - 120{,}000 = 80{,}000$; GPM $= \dfrac{80{,}000}{200{,}000} \times 100 = 40\%$ (2). Net profit $= 80{,}000 - 50{,}000 = 30{,}000$; NPM $= \dfrac{30{,}000}{200{,}000} \times 100 = 15\%$ (2).
:::

::: example Interpret
Last year the same shop's gross profit margin was 45% and its net profit margin 12%. Analyse what the change in the two margins suggests. (6)

The gross margin has fallen from 45% to 40%, so each pound of sales now earns 5p less gross profit; cost of sales has risen relative to revenue — the shop may be paying suppliers more, discounting to sell, or losing stock to waste or theft (2). Yet the net margin has risen from 12% to 15%, so expenses have fallen as a share of revenue — the shop has cut overheads such as staff costs or rent, or spread them over higher sales, by more than the gross margin fell (2). Overall performance has improved at the bottom line, but the falling gross margin is a warning: if it continues, cost savings will run out; the owner should investigate supplier prices and pricing before the trend erodes profit (2).
:::

::: example Classify
Classify each as a non-current asset, current asset, current liability or non-current liability: a delivery van; stock; money owed to a supplier; a five-year bank loan; cash in the bank. (5)

Van — non-current asset (1); stock — current asset (1); money owed to a supplier — current liability (1); five-year loan — non-current liability (1); cash — current asset (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — performance from different viewpoints
A bakery chain's net profit rose from 400,000 to 450,000 while revenue rose from 5 million to 7.5 million. Evaluate whether the chain has performed well. (9)

Profit rose by 50,000 (12.5%), so in absolute terms the chain earned more, and revenue grew by 50%, showing strong sales growth — shareholders receive more profit and employees may feel more secure (3). But the net profit margin fell from 8% ($400{,}000 \div 5{,}000{,}000$) to 6% ($450{,}000 \div 7{,}500{,}000$): each pound of sales earns less, suggesting the growth came with heavy discounting, higher costs or new shops not yet efficient; lenders may worry that costs are rising faster than sales, and managers should find where margins were lost (3). Judgement: performance is mixed — good on growth and total profit, weaker on efficiency; whether it counts as "well" depends on the objective (if the aim was market share, it succeeded; if profit margin, it did not) and on whether the fall in margin is a temporary cost of expansion or a lasting problem, which the next year's figures will show (3).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between gross profit and net profit. (2)
---
Gross profit is revenue minus the cost of sales (1); net profit is gross profit minus all other expenses such as rent and salaries (1).
:::

::: q
Revenue 90,000; cost of sales 54,000. Calculate the gross profit margin. (2)
---
Gross profit $36{,}000$; margin $= \dfrac{36{,}000}{90{,}000} \times 100 = 40\%$.
:::

::: q
Net profit 12,000 on revenue of 150,000. Calculate the net profit margin. (2)
---
$\dfrac{12{,}000}{150{,}000} \times 100 = 8\%$.
:::

::: q
Explain why a statement of financial position is described as a snapshot. (2)
---
It shows assets and liabilities on one particular day (1); the figures change the next day as cash is received and bills paid, so it does not show the position over time (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason a bank would look at a business's financial statements before lending. (3)
---
To judge whether the loan can be repaid (1): the income statement shows whether profit is enough to cover repayments (1), and the statement of financial position shows existing debts and assets that could act as security (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one action a business could take if its gross profit margin is falling. (3)
---
Find a cheaper supplier (1): this reduces cost of sales for the same revenue (1), so gross profit per pound of sales rises and the margin recovers (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why comparing a business's net profit margin with a competitor's is useful. (2)
---
It shows whether the business converts sales into profit as efficiently as its rival (1), highlighting whether its costs or prices are out of line (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how employees might use a business's financial statements. (2)
---
Rising profit suggests secure jobs and grounds for a pay rise (1); falling profit or high debts warn of possible redundancies (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse why a rise in net profit does not necessarily mean a business has performed better. (6)
---
Profit may have risen only because revenue grew — if costs grew faster, the net profit margin fell and the business is less efficient per pound of sales than before (2). The rise may come from one-off events — selling a property, a single large contract — that will not repeat, or from cutting spending on training and maintenance that will hurt future performance (2). Judged against competitors who grew profit faster, or against the business's own targets, the rise may be poor; and other stakeholders may see it differently — customers may have faced price rises, employees wage freezes — so performance must be judged on margins, trends, comparisons and objectives, not one figure (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Income statement = period; statement of financial position = one day (snapshot).**
- Only **two ratios**: gross and net profit margin, both **÷ revenue × 100**. Not ROCE, not the current ratio.
- **No formulae given** — recall them. Show gross/net profit before the margin.
- **Interpret**: a falling gross margin points at cost of sales or prices; a falling net margin with a steady gross margin points at expenses.
- Judge performance by **comparison** (years, competitors, objectives) and **stakeholder viewpoint**, never a single number.
:::
