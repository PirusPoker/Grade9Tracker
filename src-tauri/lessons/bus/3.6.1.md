## What it is

Every business needs money — to start up, to buy equipment and stock, to cover the gap before customers pay, to expand. **Sources of finance** are where that money comes from. **Internal** sources come from inside the business; **external** sources come from outside.

**Internal sources:**

| Source | What it is | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retained profit** | profit from previous years kept in the business rather than paid out | free — no interest or repayment; no loss of control; immediately available | only if the business has made profit; owners get lower dividends; may not be enough |
| **Selling assets** | selling equipment, vehicles or property the business no longer needs (or selling and leasing back) | raises cash without debt; disposes of unused assets | one-off; the asset may be needed later; may sell below value in a hurry |
| Also: **owner's savings** (for a sole trader, the owner's own money) | | | |

**External sources:**

| Source | What it is | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Family and friends** | money lent or given by people the owner knows | cheap or interest-free; flexible repayment; quick | can damage relationships; limited amounts; may expect a say |
| **Bank loan** | a fixed sum borrowed and repaid with interest over a set period (1–10 years) | large sums; fixed repayments help planning; no loss of ownership | interest adds to costs; must be repaid whatever the sales; may need security (collateral); new businesses may be refused |
| **Mortgage** | a long-term loan (up to 25 years) secured on property | very large sums at lower interest; spreads cost over many years | property lost if repayments fail; long commitment |
| **Overdraft** | permission to spend more than is in the current account, up to a limit | flexible — used only when needed; quick; interest only on the amount used | high interest rate; bank can withdraw it at any time; for short-term needs only |
| **Trade credit** | buying stock or materials now and paying the supplier later (30–90 days) | free short-term finance; sell the goods before paying | short term only; lose early-payment discounts; supplier may refuse if paid late |
| **Hire purchase** | paying for an asset in instalments over time; the business owns it after the last payment | spreads the cost of expensive equipment; use it immediately | more expensive overall (interest); the asset can be repossessed if payments stop |
| **Share issue** | selling shares in a limited company to new or existing shareholders (an Ltd privately; a plc on the stock exchange) | large sums; no repayment or interest; investors share the risk | dilutes ownership and control; dividends expected; only for companies; costly to arrange |
| **Government grants** | money from central government or local councils for specific purposes — start-ups, jobs in deprived areas, green investment, training | does not have to be repaid; no interest | conditions attached; competitive and slow to obtain; limited amounts |
| Also: **crowdfunding**, **leasing**, **venture capital**, **debt factoring** | | | |

**Choosing a source** depends on:

- **Amount** — small (overdraft, trade credit, friends) or large (loan, mortgage, shares).
- **Purpose and time** — short-term needs (cash-flow gap, stock) → overdraft or trade credit; long-term assets (premises, machinery) → loan, mortgage, hire purchase, retained profit. Match the length of the finance to the life of what it buys.
- **Cost** — interest rates and fees; retained profit and grants are free.
- **Control** — shares dilute ownership; loans do not.
- **Risk** — debt must be repaid whatever happens; loans may need personal guarantees.
- **The age and type of business** — a **new business** has no retained profit and no track record, so it relies on the owner's savings, family and friends, grants, a small loan if a bank will lend, and trade credit once suppliers trust it; an **established business** can use retained profit, larger loans on its track record, and — if a company — share issues.

::: terms
- **Internal source of finance**: money from within the business — retained profit, selling assets, the owner's savings.
- **External source of finance**: money from outside the business — loans, overdrafts, trade credit, share issues, grants.
- **Retained profit**: profit kept in the business, after tax and dividends, to be reinvested.
- **Selling assets**: raising money by selling equipment, vehicles or property the business no longer needs.
- **Bank loan**: a fixed sum borrowed from a bank and repaid with interest in instalments over a set period.
- **Mortgage**: a long-term loan to buy property, secured on that property.
- **Overdraft**: an arrangement allowing a business to take more out of its bank account than it has in it, up to an agreed limit.
- **Trade credit**: buying goods or materials now and paying the supplier at an agreed later date, e.g. 30 days.
- **Hire purchase**: buying an asset by paying in instalments, owning it only after the final payment.
- **Share issue**: raising finance by selling new shares in a limited company.
- **Government grant**: money given by the government for a specific purpose, which does not have to be repaid.
- **Crowdfunding**: raising money from a large number of people, each contributing a small amount, usually online.
- **Collateral (security)**: an asset the lender can take and sell if a loan is not repaid.
:::

::: method
1. **Match the source to the need**: how much, for how long, for what. Short-term gap → overdraft or trade credit; long-term asset → loan, HP, mortgage, retained profit; big expansion → shares (if a company) or a large loan.
2. **Then match it to the business**: new or established? Sole trader or company? Profitable? A start-up cannot use retained profit or a public share issue.
3. **Advantage and disadvantage for each source in the case** — cost, repayment, control, risk, speed, availability.
4. **Recommend** one (or a combination) with a justified condition: "a bank loan for the van, provided the repayments fit the cash-flow forecast; trade credit for stock".
5. **Common evaluation lines**: debt adds fixed costs and risk; shares lose control but share the risk; internal sources are cheapest but limited.
:::

::: example Classify
Classify each as internal or external: retained profit; a bank overdraft; selling a spare van; trade credit. (4)

Retained profit — internal (1). Overdraft — external (1). Selling a van — internal (1). Trade credit — external (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a source
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using retained profit to expand a business. (4)

Advantage: it costs nothing — no interest or repayments — and the owners keep full control (2). Disadvantage: the profit is no longer available to pay dividends, so shareholders may object, and the amount is limited to what the business has earned (2).
:::

::: example Explain — trade credit
A new bicycle shop's main supplier offers 60 days' trade credit. Explain one benefit of this for the shop. (3)

The shop receives the bikes now but pays for them two months later (1), so it can sell many of them — and receive the customers' money — before the supplier's bill is due (1). This means it needs less cash or borrowing to stock the shop, which eases cash flow in its early months and saves the interest it would pay on an overdraft (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — overdraft vs loan
A garden-centre owner needs £20,000 to buy stock for the spring season, which she will sell over three months. Analyse whether an overdraft or a bank loan is more suitable. (6)

The need is short-term — the stock is sold within three months and the money comes back — so an overdraft fits: she draws only what she needs, pays interest only on the amount used, and repays as sales come in, without a long commitment (2). A bank loan would give a fixed £20,000 with fixed monthly repayments over a year or more; that is dearer overall because interest is charged on the full sum for the full term, and she would still be repaying long after the stock is sold (2). The overdraft's interest rate is higher and the bank can withdraw it, so if spring sales fail she could be in difficulty; but for a seasonal, self-liquidating need the overdraft is the better match, with trade credit from suppliers reducing how much she needs to borrow at all (2).
:::

::: example Analyse — finance for an established company
A family-owned private limited company with ten years of profits needs £2 million to build a second factory. Analyse whether it should use a bank loan or issue new shares. (6)

As an established, profitable business it has a track record and a factory to offer as security, so a bank is likely to lend; a loan keeps ownership within the family, and interest on it is a known, fixed cost that can be planned for (2). But £2 million of debt means large repayments for years whatever happens to sales; if demand falls, the company still has to pay, which could cause cash-flow problems (2). Selling shares to new investors raises the money without any repayments or interest, so it is less risky — but as an Ltd it can only sell privately, and new shareholders would take part of the future profits as dividends and have a say in decisions; the choice depends on how strongly the family wants to keep control compared with how confident it is that the second factory's profits will cover the loan repayments (2).
:::

::: example Recommend — a start-up
Tom is starting a mobile coffee van and needs £25,000 for the van and equipment. He has £5,000 savings. Recommend how he should raise the remaining £20,000. Justify your answer. (9)

As a new sole trader with no trading record, Tom cannot use retained profit or issue shares, and banks are wary of lending to start-ups; family and friends might lend part of the sum cheaply, and a start-up grant or loan scheme for new businesses could contribute without high interest (3). Hire purchase on the van itself would spread its cost over several years while letting him trade immediately, with the van as security so the lender is more willing; a bank loan for the equipment would give fixed repayments he can plan for, if he presents a business plan with a cash-flow forecast (3). Recommendation: hire purchase for the van, a small bank or start-up loan for the equipment, and family for any shortfall — matching long-term assets to long-term finance and keeping repayments spread; he should avoid an overdraft for the van because it is short-term and expensive, and the plan depends on his forecast showing the repayments are affordable (3).
:::

::: q
State two internal sources of finance. (2)
---
Retained profit; selling assets (or the owner's savings).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of a bank loan. (2)
---
Interest must be paid, raising costs (1), and repayments are due whether or not the business is making sales (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between an overdraft and a loan. (2)
---
An overdraft is flexible short-term borrowing on the current account, with interest only on what is used (1); a loan is a fixed sum repaid in set instalments over an agreed period (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of trade credit for a small shop. (3)
---
Free short-term finance (1): the shop receives stock now and pays the supplier 30 days later (1), so it can sell the goods and use the revenue to pay, improving cash flow (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of a share issue for the owners of a private limited company. (3)
---
Loss of control (1): new shareholders own part of the business and can vote on decisions (1), and expect dividends from profit that the original owners would otherwise have kept (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a new business finds it harder than an established one to raise finance. (3)
---
It has no retained profit or trading record (1), so banks see it as high risk and may refuse or charge high interest (1), leaving it reliant on the owner's savings, family and grants (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of hire purchase. (4)
---
Advantage: the business can use an expensive asset immediately while spreading the cost over instalments (2). Disadvantage: the total paid is higher than the cash price because of interest, and the asset can be repossessed if payments are missed (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a business should not use an overdraft to buy premises. (2)
---
An overdraft is short-term, expensive and can be withdrawn at any time (1); premises are a long-term asset and need long-term finance such as a mortgage (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the advantages to an established plc of raising finance through a share issue rather than a loan. (6)
---
A share issue can raise very large sums, and the money never has to be repaid, so the plc takes on no debt and no fixed interest costs that would burden it in a bad year (2). Investors share the risk: if the expansion fails, shareholders lose value but the company is not left with unaffordable repayments, whereas a loan must be repaid regardless (2). The costs are that new shares dilute existing shareholders' ownership and dividends will be expected, and arranging an issue is expensive and slow; but for a large, long-term investment with uncertain returns, equity is safer than debt, especially if interest rates are high (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Match the length of finance to the need**: short-term gaps → overdraft/trade credit; long-term assets → loan/HP/mortgage/retained profit.
- **Start-ups cannot use retained profit or public share issues** — a common error.
- Every source needs **cost, repayment, control and risk** considered for the case.
- Overdraft: **flexible but expensive and withdrawable**. Loan: **fixed, plannable, must be repaid.**
- Recommendations should usually be a **combination**, with a condition tied to the cash-flow forecast.
:::
