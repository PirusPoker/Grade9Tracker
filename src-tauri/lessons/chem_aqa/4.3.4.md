## What it is

**Separate science only (Higher tier):** this whole subsection (4.3.4, chemistry only, HT only) is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. Trilogy students still need concentration in **g/dm³** (topic 4.3.2), but not concentration in mol/dm³ or titration calculations.

A **solution** is a **solute** dissolved in a **solvent** (usually water). Its **concentration** tells you how much solute is packed into each unit of volume. In topic 4.3.2 you met concentration in **grams per dm³**. Chemists usually prefer **moles per dm³ (mol/dm³)**, because moles tell you how many particles are present, and reactions happen between particles in fixed ratios.

### The key equation

$$\text{concentration (mol/dm}^3) = \frac{\text{amount of solute (mol)}}{\text{volume of solution (dm}^3)}$$

| Quantity | Unit | How to get it |
|---|---|---|
| amount of solute, $n$ | mol | $n = \dfrac{\text{mass (g)}}{M_r}$ |
| volume of solution, $V$ | dm³ | cm³ ÷ 1000 (1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ = 1 litre) |
| concentration, $c$ | mol/dm³ | $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ |

Rearranging (MS 3b, change the subject):

- $n = c \times V$ — amount in moles from concentration and volume.
- $V = \dfrac{n}{c}$ — volume needed to supply a given number of moles.
- mass of solute $= n \times M_r = c \times V \times M_r$.

**Always convert cm³ to dm³ first.** 25.0 cm³ = 0.0250 dm³. Forgetting this makes your answer 1000 times too big — the single most common error in this topic.

### How concentration depends on mass and volume

The spec asks you to **explain** how concentration (mol/dm³) is related to the mass of solute and the volume of solution:

- **More mass of solute** in the same volume → more moles → **higher** concentration. Concentration is **directly proportional** to the mass dissolved (at constant volume): double the mass, double the concentration.
- **Same mass in a larger volume** → the same moles are spread out → **lower** concentration. Concentration is **inversely proportional** to volume: double the volume, halve the concentration.
- The **$M_r$** links the two units: a solute with a bigger $M_r$ gives fewer moles per gram, so 10 g of a heavy solute gives a lower mol/dm³ than 10 g of a light one.

### Converting between mol/dm³ and g/dm³

$$\text{concentration (g/dm}^3) = \text{concentration (mol/dm}^3) \times M_r$$

$$\text{concentration (mol/dm}^3) = \frac{\text{concentration (g/dm}^3)}{M_r}$$

Mini-example: sodium hydroxide, NaOH, $M_r = 40$. A 0.500 mol/dm³ solution contains $0.500 \times 40 = 20.0$ g/dm³.

### Worked mini-examples

**1. Concentration from mass.** 4.00 g of NaOH ($M_r$ 40) is dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm³.
- $n = 4.00 \div 40 = 0.100$ mol
- $V = 250 \div 1000 = 0.250$ dm³
- $c = 0.100 \div 0.250 = $ **0.400 mol/dm³** (which is $0.400 \times 40 = 16.0$ g/dm³)

**2. Mass needed to make a solution.** What mass of copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄ ($M_r$ 159.5), is needed to make 500 cm³ of a 0.200 mol/dm³ solution?
- $n = c \times V = 0.200 \times 0.500 = 0.100$ mol
- mass $= 0.100 \times 159.5 = 15.95$ g = **16.0 g** (3 s.f.)

**3. Moles in a sample.** How many moles of HCl are in 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid?
- $n = 0.100 \times 0.0250 = $ **0.00250 mol** ($2.50 \times 10^{-3}$ mol)

### Reacting volumes: finding an unknown concentration

If two solutions react completely and you know **both volumes** and the **concentration of one**, you can calculate the concentration of the other. This is what a **titration** does (see topic 4.4.2, **Required practical 2: (chemistry only) determination of the reacting volumes of solutions of a strong acid and a strong alkali by titration** — there is no Trilogy equivalent).

The four-step method:

1. **Moles of the known solution:** $n = c \times V$ (V in dm³).
2. **Mole ratio** from the balanced equation → moles of the unknown.
3. **Concentration of the unknown:** $c = n \div V$.
4. If asked, **convert to g/dm³**: multiply by $M_r$.

**Example A (1 : 1 ratio).** 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is exactly neutralised by 21.50 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid.

$$\text{HCl} + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

- moles HCl $= 0.100 \times 0.02150 = 0.00215$ mol
- ratio 1 : 1, so moles NaOH $= 0.00215$ mol
- $c(\text{NaOH}) = 0.00215 \div 0.0250 = $ **0.0860 mol/dm³**
- in g/dm³: $0.0860 \times 40 = $ **3.44 g/dm³**

**Example B (1 : 2 ratio).** 25.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol/dm³ NaOH is neutralised by 18.75 cm³ of sulfuric acid.

$$\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

- moles NaOH $= 0.150 \times 0.0250 = 0.00375$ mol
- ratio H₂SO₄ : NaOH = 1 : 2, so moles H₂SO₄ $= 0.00375 \div 2 = 0.001875$ mol
- $c(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4) = 0.001875 \div 0.01875 = $ **0.100 mol/dm³**
- in g/dm³ ($M_r$ of H₂SO₄ = 98): $0.100 \times 98 = $ **9.80 g/dm³**

Sulfuric acid is **diprotic** — each H₂SO₄ releases two H⁺ ions — so it needs **twice** as many moles of NaOH. Missing the ratio is the second most common error.

### Titration results (the data you are given)

A titration gives a table of burette readings. The **titre** = final reading − initial reading. The first run is a **rough** titration and is usually ignored. Use only **concordant** titres — those within **0.10 cm³** of each other — and take their **mean**.

| Run | Rough | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Titre / cm³ | 22.00 | 21.45 | 21.95 | 21.55 |

Concordant: 21.45 and 21.55 (0.10 cm³ apart). Mean $= (21.45 + 21.55) \div 2 = $ **21.50 cm³**. Run 2 is an anomaly and is left out.

### Skills you are tested on

- **MS 1a:** working with decimals and standard form ($2.15 \times 10^{-3}$ mol).
- **MS 1c:** ratios from the balanced equation.
- **MS 3b, 3c:** rearranging $c = n/V$ and substituting with the right units.
- **WS 4.2, 4.3, 4.6:** using SI-style units correctly (dm³, mol), converting cm³ ↔ dm³, and giving answers to a sensible number of **significant figures** (match the data — usually 3 s.f.).
- **AT 1, 3, 8:** using a pipette and burette accurately to find the concentration of strong acids and alkalis.

::: terms
- **Solute**: the substance that dissolves in a solvent to make a solution, such as sodium chloride in salt water.
- **Solvent**: the liquid in which a solute dissolves; in GCSE solutions this is usually water.
- **Concentration (mol/dm³)**: the amount of solute in moles dissolved in each cubic decimetre of solution.
- **Cubic decimetre (dm³)**: a unit of volume equal to 1000 cm³ or one litre; divide cm³ by 1000 to get dm³.
- **Titration**: a technique that measures the exact volumes of an acid and an alkali that react completely, using an indicator.
- **Titre**: the volume of solution added from the burette to reach the end point (final reading minus initial reading).
- **Concordant results**: titres that agree with each other to within 0.10 cm³; only these are used to calculate the mean.
- **End point**: the point in a titration where the indicator changes colour permanently because the reaction is just complete.
:::

::: method
**Calculations — the AQA routine.** Write the **equation** you are using, **substitute** with units, give the **answer with a unit** and to **3 significant figures** (or the number the data uses). Show every step: if the final answer is wrong you still get method marks for the moles, the ratio and the division.

1. Convert every volume to **dm³** (÷ 1000) before you do anything else.
2. Moles of the known solution: $n = c \times V$.
3. Use the **balanced equation** to get the mole ratio. Write it down: "ratio H₂SO₄ : NaOH = 1 : 2".
4. Concentration of the unknown: $c = n \div V$.
5. If the question asks for **g/dm³**, multiply by $M_r$ — and say which $M_r$ you used.

**"Explain" questions** about concentration: link **mass → moles → concentration** using $c = n/V$. Use "more moles in the same volume" or "the same moles in a larger volume". Do not just say "it is stronger" — strength is about ionisation (topic 4.4.2), not concentration.

**Choosing titres:** ignore the rough titre, pick the titres within **0.10 cm³** of each other, and average only those. State which ones you used.

**Required-practical questions** (Required practical 2, chemistry only): know why each piece of equipment is used — **pipette** for an accurate fixed volume of one solution, **burette** to add the other and measure the volume to 0.05 cm³. Errors that make the titre **too big**: overshooting the end point, an air bubble in the burette tip. Improvement answers: add the solution **dropwise** near the end point, **swirl**, use a **white tile**, repeat until **concordant**.

**6-mark extended responses** usually combine method and calculation: "Describe how you would find the concentration of a sodium hydroxide solution." Give the method in a logical order (pipette alkali, indicator, burette acid, add until colour change, read volume, repeat), then explain how the volumes and the known concentration give the unknown concentration. Linking the steps in order is what lifts an answer to Level 3.
:::

::: example Concentration from mass
5.85 g of sodium chloride (NaCl, $M_r$ 58.5) is dissolved in water to make 500 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration in mol/dm³. (3)

moles $= 5.85 \div 58.5 = 0.100$ mol (1)
volume $= 500 \div 1000 = 0.500$ dm³ (1)
$c = 0.100 \div 0.500 = $ **0.200 mol/dm³** (1)
:::

::: example Mass needed
Calculate the mass of potassium hydroxide (KOH, $M_r$ 56) in 200 cm³ of a 0.150 mol/dm³ solution. (3)

moles $= c \times V = 0.150 \times 0.200 = 0.0300$ mol (1)(1)
mass $= 0.0300 \times 56 = $ **1.68 g** (1)
:::

::: example Titration with a 1 : 2 ratio
25.0 cm³ of potassium hydroxide solution is neutralised by a mean titre of 22.40 cm³ of 0.0500 mol/dm³ sulfuric acid.
H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
Calculate the concentration of the potassium hydroxide in mol/dm³ and in g/dm³ ($M_r$ of KOH = 56). (5)

moles H₂SO₄ $= 0.0500 \times 0.02240 = 0.00112$ mol (1)
ratio 1 : 2, so moles KOH $= 0.00112 \times 2 = 0.00224$ mol (1)
$c(\text{KOH}) = 0.00224 \div 0.0250 = $ **0.0896 mol/dm³** (1)
in g/dm³: $0.0896 \times 56 = 5.0176$ = **5.02 g/dm³** (1)(1)
:::

::: example Explaining concentration
Student A dissolves 10 g of sodium hydroxide in 250 cm³ of solution. Student B dissolves 10 g of sodium hydroxide in 500 cm³. Explain which solution has the higher concentration in mol/dm³. (2)

Both contain the same amount of NaOH (10 ÷ 40 = 0.25 mol) (1). Student A's moles are in half the volume, so A's solution is twice as concentrated (1.0 mol/dm³ compared with 0.50 mol/dm³) (1).
:::

::: example Choosing concordant titres
Titres: rough 23.10 cm³; run 1 22.65 cm³; run 2 22.55 cm³; run 3 22.90 cm³. Calculate the mean titre. (2)

Concordant results are runs 1 and 2 (within 0.10 cm³); the rough and run 3 are excluded (1).
Mean $= (22.65 + 22.55) \div 2 = $ **22.60 cm³** (1)
:::

Why is the rough titration not included when calculating the mean titre? (1)
---
It is done quickly to find the approximate end point, so it usually overshoots and is less accurate than the careful runs.

::: watch
- **cm³ not converted to dm³.** 25.0 cm³ is 0.0250 dm³. Leaving it as 25.0 makes the answer 1000 times wrong and loses the mark for the answer.
- **Ignoring the mole ratio.** With sulfuric acid and sodium hydroxide the ratio is 1 : 2; with sodium carbonate and hydrochloric acid it is 1 : 2. Always write the ratio out.
- **Dividing by the wrong volume.** In the last step divide by the volume of the **unknown** solution, not the burette volume.
- **Averaging every titre.** Use only concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³); never include the rough.
- **No unit, or the wrong unit.** mol/dm³ and g/dm³ are different; give the one asked for, and multiply by $M_r$ to change mol/dm³ into g/dm³.
- **Over-rounding mid-calculation.** Keep all the figures until the end, then round to 3 s.f.
:::

::: test
A student forgot to rinse the burette with the acid before filling it, so there was some water left inside. Explain the effect on the calculated concentration of the alkali in the flask. (3)
---
The acid in the burette is diluted by the water, so its concentration is lower than the stated value (1). A larger titre is needed to neutralise the alkali (1). Because the calculation uses the stated (higher) acid concentration with this larger volume, the calculated moles of acid, and so the calculated concentration of the alkali, are **too high** (1).

Sodium carbonate reacts with hydrochloric acid: Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂. 25.0 cm³ of a sodium carbonate solution reacted exactly with 23.20 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid. ::: test
Describe how a student could use a titration with 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid to find the concentration of a sodium hydroxide solution in mol/dm³. Include how the results would be used in the calculation. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear, logically ordered method that would give accurate, repeatable results, linked to a correct outline of the calculation. **Level 2 (3–4):** a method with the main steps but some detail or order missing, or a correct method with no calculation. **Level 1 (1–2):** some relevant steps, e.g. use a burette and indicator, without a workable sequence.

Indicative content: measure 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide into a conical flask with a pipette (and pipette filler); add a few drops of indicator, e.g. phenolphthalein or methyl orange; place the flask on a white tile; fill a burette with the hydrochloric acid and record the initial reading at eye level from the bottom of the meniscus; add acid while swirling; add dropwise near the end point; stop at the first permanent colour change (phenolphthalein pink → colourless); record the final reading; titre = final − initial; do a rough titration first, then repeat until titres are concordant (within 0.10 cm³); calculate the mean of concordant titres. Calculation: moles HCl = 0.100 × mean titre in dm³; ratio HCl : NaOH = 1 : 1 so moles NaOH are the same; concentration NaOH = moles ÷ 0.0250 dm³.
:::
