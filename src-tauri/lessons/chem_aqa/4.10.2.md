## What it is

This subsection is in **both** GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 5.10.2). There is no Higher-tier-only content. It covers **life cycle assessments (LCAs)** — how the environmental impact of a product is judged from "cradle to grave" — and the ways we can **reduce, reuse and recycle** materials.

### Life cycle assessment: the four stages

A **life cycle assessment (LCA)** is carried out to assess the **environmental impact** of a product in each of these stages:

| Stage | What is assessed | Examples of impacts |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Extracting and processing raw materials** | getting the raw materials out of the ground or growing them, and purifying them | mining and quarrying damage habitats and use energy; crude oil extraction; trees felled; energy for processing |
| 2. **Manufacturing and packaging** | turning materials into the product and packing it | energy and water used; waste and pollution produced; chemicals used |
| 3. **Use and operation during its lifetime** | what happens while the product is used | fuel burned by a car; electricity used by a fridge; how many times it can be reused |
| 4. **Disposal at the end of its useful life** | landfill, incineration or recycling | landfill space; toxic substances leaching; gases from burning; energy saved by recycling |

**Transport and distribution at each stage** are also included — moving raw materials, products and waste uses fuel and releases carbon dioxide.

### What can and cannot be easily measured

- **Use of water, resources, energy sources and production of some wastes can be fairly easily quantified** — for example litres of water, megajoules of energy, kilograms of solid waste, kilograms of CO₂ released.
- **Allocating numerical values to pollutant effects is less straightforward and requires value judgements.** How do you give a number to the harm caused by litter to wildlife, or the visual impact of a quarry? Different people will judge these differently.
- So **LCA is not a purely objective process.**

### Selective (abbreviated) LCAs can be misused

**Selective or abbreviated LCAs** can be devised to evaluate a product, but these **can be misused to reach pre-determined conclusions**, for example **in support of claims for advertising purposes**. A company might include only the stages where its product looks good — for example a paper bag maker stressing that paper is **biodegradable** and made from a **renewable** resource, while leaving out the large amounts of **energy and water** used to make it. Always ask: **who carried out the LCA, which stages were included, and was any data left out?**

### Comparative LCA: plastic and paper shopping bags

You must be able to carry out a **simple comparative LCA** for shopping bags made from **plastic** (poly(ethene)) and **paper**.

| Stage | Plastic (poly(ethene)) bag | Paper bag |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials | **crude oil** — a **finite** resource; extracted, fractionally distilled and cracked to give ethene | **timber** — a **renewable** resource (if forests are replanted); trees felled and pulped |
| Manufacturing | ethene is polymerised; uses **less energy and water** than making paper; lighter, so less material per bag | pulping and paper making use **large amounts of energy and water** and chemicals |
| Transport | **light and thin**, so less fuel to transport | heavier and bulkier, so **more fuel** to transport |
| Use | strong, waterproof, **can be reused many times** | weaker, tears when wet, **usually used only once or a few times** |
| Disposal | **not biodegradable** — lasts for many years in landfill; **litter** harms wildlife (animals swallow or get tangled in it); can be recycled, but often is not; burning releases CO₂ and possibly toxic gases | **biodegradable** — rots in landfill (releasing carbon dioxide or methane); easily **recycled** or composted; not toxic |

**Typical data (per bag, illustrative):**

| | Plastic bag | Paper bag |
|---|---|---|
| Energy to make / MJ | 0.6 | 1.7 |
| Water used / dm³ | 0.3 | 1.0 |
| CO₂ produced / g | 60 | 140 |
| Mass / g | 8 | 55 |
| Typical number of uses | 4 | 1 |

**Using the data (MS 1a, 1c, 1d, 2a):**
- Ratio of energy to make a paper bag to a plastic bag $= \frac{1.7}{0.6} = 2.8$, so a paper bag takes nearly **3 times** as much energy.
- Energy **per use**: plastic $= \frac{0.6}{4} = 0.15$ MJ; paper $= \frac{1.7}{1} = 1.7$ MJ. Per use, the paper bag needs about **11 times** as much energy.
- Percentage less CO₂ for plastic $= \frac{140 - 60}{140} \times 100 = 57\%$ (2 s.f.).
- **Estimate** before calculating (MS 1d): 1.7 ÷ 0.6 is a bit less than 3 — a quick check that your answer is sensible.

**Conclusion:** on **energy, water, CO₂ and transport**, plastic bags usually have the **lower impact**, especially if reused. Paper bags score better on **raw materials** (renewable) and **disposal** (biodegradable, less harm to wildlife). Which is "better" depends on how the litter and wildlife effects are weighted — a **value judgement**. Reusable cotton or thick plastic "bags for life" are better still **if** they are reused many times.

### Ways of reducing the use of resources

**The reduction in use, reuse and recycling of materials by end users reduces**:
- the **use of limited resources**
- the **use of energy sources**
- **waste**
- **environmental impacts**.

**Metals, glass, building materials, clay ceramics and most plastics are produced from limited raw materials.** **Much of the energy** for the processes comes from **limited resources** (fossil fuels). **Obtaining raw materials from the Earth by quarrying and mining causes environmental impacts** — loss of habitats, noise, dust, traffic, scars on the landscape and waste rock.

| Strategy | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce** | use less material in the first place | thinner cans, less packaging |
| **Reuse** | use the product again for the same purpose | **glass bottles** washed and refilled (milk bottles) |
| **Recycle** | process the material to make a new product | glass bottles **crushed and melted** to make different glass products; metals melted and recast |

**Glass.** Some products, such as **glass bottles, can be reused**. Glass bottles can also be **crushed and melted to make different glass products**. Recycled glass (cullet) melts at a lower temperature than the raw materials (sand, sodium carbonate and limestone), which saves energy. **Other products cannot be reused and so are recycled for a different use.**

**Metals.** **Metals can be recycled by melting and recasting or reforming into different products.** **The amount of separation required for recycling depends on the material and the properties required of the final product.** For example, **some scrap steel can be added to iron from a blast furnace to reduce the amount of iron that needs to be extracted from iron ore** — the steel does not need to be fully separated from other metals because the mixture will be adjusted anyway. For uses that need very pure metal (e.g. copper for wiring), more separation and purification are needed.

**Benefits of recycling metals:** less ore has to be mined (conserves finite ores, less landscape damage); **much less energy** than extracting from ore (recycling aluminium uses only about 5% of the energy needed to extract it by electrolysis); less waste sent to landfill; fewer greenhouse gas emissions.

**Limits of recycling:** collecting, **transporting** and **sorting** the waste costs energy and money; mixtures (e.g. different plastics, or alloys) are hard to **separate**; quality may fall each time (e.g. paper fibres shorten; some plastics degrade); not all materials can be recycled economically.

**Evaluating ways of reducing the use of limited resources:** use the information given to compare energy saved, raw materials conserved, cost, transport, and environmental impacts, then reach a **justified conclusion**.

::: terms
- **Life cycle assessment**: an assessment of the environmental impact of a product at every stage from raw materials to disposal.
- **Raw materials**: the substances extracted from the Earth or grown, such as crude oil or timber, that a product is made from.
- **Value judgement**: a decision based on opinion about how important something is, such as how harmful litter is to wildlife.
- **Selective LCA**: an abbreviated life cycle assessment that leaves out some stages or data and can be used to mislead.
- **Reuse**: using a product again for the same purpose, such as washing and refilling a glass bottle.
- **Recycling**: processing a used material, for example by melting it, to make a new product.
- **Biodegradable**: able to be broken down by microorganisms, so that it rots away when disposed of.
- **Finite raw material**: a raw material, such as a metal ore or crude oil, that will eventually run out.
:::

::: method
**Name the four LCA stages in order:** extracting and processing raw materials → manufacturing and packaging → use and operation → disposal — and remember **transport** at each stage.

**Interpret an LCA table:** quote the data. "The paper bag uses 1.7 MJ compared with 0.6 MJ for the plastic bag — almost three times as much." Compare **per use** if numbers of uses are given. Use percentages and ratios (MS 1c), and give sensible significant figures (MS 2a).

**Evaluate / compare (e.g. plastic vs paper bags):** structure your answer **stage by stage**, giving an advantage or disadvantage of **each** product at each stage, then a **justified conclusion** ("Overall the plastic bag has a lower environmental impact because... although...").

**Explain why LCA is not objective:** some things (water, energy, waste, CO₂) can be measured, but **pollutant effects** need **value judgements**. Selective LCAs can be **misused** — e.g. in **advertising** — by leaving out stages.

**Reduce/reuse/recycle questions:** say what is **saved** — limited raw materials, energy (often from fossil fuels), landfill space, environmental damage from **mining and quarrying** — and give limits (collection, transport, sorting, separation, cost).

**6-mark questions:** "Compare the environmental impact of X and Y using the data." Level 3 answers use **the data given**, cover **all stages**, include **calculations or quantitative comparisons**, and finish with a **reasoned conclusion**.
:::

::: example Not purely objective
Explain why a life cycle assessment is not a purely objective process. (2)

Some values, such as water use, energy use and mass of waste, can be measured easily (1), but giving numerical values to the effects of pollutants requires value judgements, which depend on opinion (1).
:::

::: example Per-use comparison
A reusable cotton bag takes 7.5 MJ to make and is used 150 times. A plastic bag takes 0.6 MJ to make and is used 3 times. Which has the lower energy cost per use? Show your working. (3)

Cotton: 7.5 ÷ 150 = 0.05 MJ per use (1). Plastic: 0.6 ÷ 3 = 0.2 MJ per use (1). The cotton bag has the lower energy cost per use — a quarter of the plastic bag's (1).
:::

::: example Misuse of LCAs
A paper bag company advertises that its bags are "better for the environment because they are made from a renewable resource and are biodegradable". Suggest why this claim might be misleading. (2)

It is a selective LCA — it only mentions the raw material and disposal stages (1). It leaves out the larger amounts of energy and water used to manufacture paper bags and the extra transport fuel because they are heavier (1).
:::

::: example Scrap steel
Explain why scrap steel is added to iron from the blast furnace. (2)

It reduces the amount of iron that has to be extracted from iron ore, conserving the finite ore (1) and saving the energy (from fossil fuels) needed for extraction (1).
:::

::: q
Give one environmental advantage of a plastic bag over a paper bag. (1)
---
Any one: uses less energy/water to make; lighter so less fuel to transport; stronger so can be reused more times; produces less CO₂ during manufacture.

::: q
Describe two ways in which glass bottles can be used again. (2)
---
They can be washed and reused/refilled (1). They can be crushed and melted to make different glass products (1).

Give two environmental impacts of obtaining raw materials by quarrying and mining. (2)
---
Any two: destroys habitats; scars the landscape; noise; dust; increased traffic; large amounts of waste rock; uses energy (1 each).

::: q
Making a can from recycled aluminium uses 5% of the energy needed to make it from ore. If making a can from ore takes 3.4 MJ, calculate the energy saved by recycling. (2)
---
Energy for recycled can = 0.05 × 3.4 = 0.17 MJ (1). Energy saved = 3.4 − 0.17 = **3.23 MJ** (accept 3.2 MJ) (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Missing a stage.** Use all four stages, and remember transport.
- **Not using the data.** In "evaluate" or "compare" questions with a table, quote figures and do a comparison — "uses more energy" alone is weak.
- **No conclusion.** "Evaluate" needs a **justified judgement** at the end.
- **Calling LCAs objective.** Pollutant effects need **value judgements**; selective LCAs can be **misused** in advertising.
- **Confusing reuse and recycle.** Reuse = same product used again; recycle = processed into a new product.
- **Recycling is not free.** Collection, transport and sorting use energy — mention this when evaluating.
:::

::: test
The table shows LCA data for two drinks containers. Glass bottle: energy to make 7.0 MJ, CO₂ released 380 g, number of uses 20. Plastic bottle: energy to make 2.3 MJ, CO₂ released 110 g, number of uses 1. (a) Calculate the energy per use for each container. (b) Calculate the percentage of the plastic bottle's CO₂ that the glass bottle releases per use. (4)
---
(a) Glass: 7.0 ÷ 20 = 0.35 MJ per use (1); plastic: 2.3 ÷ 1 = 2.3 MJ per use (1). (b) Glass CO₂ per use = 380 ÷ 20 = 19 g (1); 19 ÷ 110 × 100 = **17%** (17.3%) (1).

It depends on the material and the properties needed in the final product (1). E.g. scrap steel can be added straight to blast furnace iron with little separation, but copper for electrical wiring must be very pure so needs more separation/purification (1).

Suggest two reasons why not all plastic waste is recycled. (2)

Any two: different plastics are hard to separate/sort; collecting and transporting waste costs energy and money; recycled plastic may be lower quality; some plastics (e.g. thermosetting) cannot be melted and reshaped; recycling may be more expensive than making new plastic (1 each).

::: test
A supermarket replaces its plastic bags with paper bags and advertises that it is "helping the environment". Use your knowledge of life cycle assessments to evaluate this claim. (4)
---
Paper is from a renewable resource and is biodegradable, so less long-lasting litter and less harm to wildlife (1). But paper bags need more energy and water to make and are heavier, so more fuel to transport and more CO₂ (1). Paper bags are usually used only once, whereas plastic bags can be reused several times (1). Conclusion: the claim is only partly true / may be based on a selective LCA; overall impact depends on how many times bags are reused and how litter effects are weighted — a value judgement (1).
:::

::: test
Recycling aluminium uses about 5% of the energy needed to extract it from its ore. A town recycles 2.0 × 10⁵ kg of aluminium a year. Extracting 1 kg from ore uses 200 MJ. Calculate the energy saved per year. Give your answer in standard form. (3)
---
Energy to extract from ore = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 200 = 4.0 × 10⁷ MJ (1). Energy to recycle = 0.05 × 4.0 × 10⁷ = 2.0 × 10⁶ MJ (1). Saved = 4.0 × 10⁷ − 2.0 × 10⁶ = **3.8 × 10⁷ MJ** (1).
:::

::: test
Carry out a comparative life cycle assessment of plastic (poly(ethene)) and paper shopping bags. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** compares both bags at all (or most) stages of the LCA with specific points, includes transport and reuse, recognises value judgements, and reaches a justified conclusion. **Level 2 (3–4):** compares the bags at two or three stages with some correct points; a simple conclusion. **Level 1 (1–2):** a few relevant points about one or both bags with little comparison.

Indicative content: raw materials — plastic from crude oil (finite), extracted and processed by fractional distillation and cracking; paper from timber (renewable if replanted), felling and pulping. Manufacture — plastic needs less energy and water; paper making uses large amounts of energy, water and chemicals. Transport — plastic is lighter so less fuel; paper heavier and bulkier. Use — plastic is stronger, waterproof and can be reused many times; paper usually used once, tears when wet. Disposal — plastic is not biodegradable, lasts many years in landfill, litter harms wildlife, can be recycled but often is not, burning releases CO₂; paper is biodegradable, easily recycled or composted. Pollutant effects such as harm to wildlife need value judgements, so the LCA is not fully objective. Conclusion: plastic bags have lower energy, water and CO₂ impacts, especially if reused; paper bags are better at disposal; overall the best choice depends on reuse and how impacts are weighted.
:::
