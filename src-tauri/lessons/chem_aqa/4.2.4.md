## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection (4.2.4) is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. Trilogy students can skip this lesson.

The properties of a substance do not only depend on what it is made of — they also depend on **how big the pieces are**. When particles get extremely small, a much bigger share of their atoms sit on the **surface**, and the material can behave very differently from the same material "in bulk". This is the science of **nanoparticles**.

### Sizes of particles

**Nanoscience** is the study of structures **1–100 nm** in size, of the order of **a few hundred atoms**. 1 nanometre (nm) = $1 \times 10^{-9}$ m — one billionth of a metre.

| Particle type | Diameter in nm | Diameter in m | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atom | about 0.1–0.5 nm | about $1 \times 10^{-10}$ m | atomic radius about 0.1 nm |
| Small molecule (e.g. H₂O, CO₂) | under 1 nm | about $10^{-10}$ to $10^{-9}$ m | a few atoms |
| **Nanoparticle** | **1–100 nm** | $1 \times 10^{-9}$ to $1 \times 10^{-7}$ m | a few hundred atoms |
| **Fine particles (PM2.5)** | **100–2500 nm** | $1 \times 10^{-7}$ to $2.5 \times 10^{-6}$ m | e.g. soot from diesel engines |
| **Coarse particles (PM10)** | **2500–10 000 nm** | $2.5 \times 10^{-6}$ to $1 \times 10^{-5}$ m | often called **dust** |

"PM" stands for **particulate matter**; the number is the maximum diameter in micrometres (PM2.5 = up to 2.5 μm, PM10 = up to 10 μm). **Nanoparticles are smaller than fine particles.**

**Comparing "nano" dimensions with atoms (MS 1b, 2h):** an atom is about $1 \times 10^{-10}$ m across. A nanoparticle of 10 nm is $1 \times 10^{-8}$ m, so it is $\frac{10^{-8}}{10^{-10}} = 10^{2}$ = about **100 atoms across** — two **orders of magnitude** bigger than an atom. A 100 nm nanoparticle is about $10^{3}$ times the diameter of an atom.

> An **order of magnitude** is a factor of 10. To compare sizes, divide the larger by the smaller; the power of 10 in the answer is the number of orders of magnitude.

### Surface area to volume ratio

Nanoparticles have a very **high surface area to volume ratio** (SA : V). For a **cube** of side $L$:

- surface area = $6 \times L^2$ (six square faces)
- volume = $L^3$
- SA : V = $\frac{6L^2}{L^3} = \frac{6}{L}$

| Side of cube | Surface area | Volume | SA : V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 cm | 6 cm² | 1 cm³ | 6 : 1 (6 cm⁻¹) |
| 0.1 cm | 0.06 cm² | 0.001 cm³ | 60 : 1 (60 cm⁻¹) |
| 0.01 cm | 0.0006 cm² | 0.000 001 cm³ | 600 : 1 (600 cm⁻¹) |

**As the side of a cube decreases by a factor of 10, the surface area to volume ratio increases by a factor of 10.** A 10 nm cube has an SA : V a million times larger than a 1 cm cube (1 cm = $10^{7}$ nm, so the side has shrunk by $10^{6}$).

**Worked mini-example (MS 5c).** A cube-shaped particle has sides of 20 nm.
- Surface area = $6 \times 20^2 = 6 \times 400 = 2400$ nm²
- Volume = $20^3 = 8000$ nm³
- SA : V = $\frac{2400}{8000} = 0.3$ nm⁻¹ (check: $\frac{6}{20} = 0.3$ ✓)

### Why nanoparticles behave differently

Because of their **high surface area to volume ratio**:
- **Nanoparticles may have properties different from those of the same material in bulk.** A much larger fraction of the atoms are on the surface, exposed and able to react. (For example, bulk gold is unreactive, but gold nanoparticles can act as catalysts; bulk titanium dioxide is a white powder, but nanoparticles of it are transparent on skin.)
- **Smaller quantities may be needed to be effective** than for materials with normal particle sizes — so less material is used, saving cost and resources.

### Uses of nanoparticles

Nanoparticles have many applications, and **new applications are an important area of research**:

| Area | Example of use | Property used |
|---|---|---|
| **Medicine** | carrying drugs to targeted cells; antibacterial coatings (silver nanoparticles) on wound dressings | tiny size gets into cells; huge surface area |
| **Electronics** | very small, fast components and conductive inks | tiny size; good conductors (e.g. nanotubes) |
| **Cosmetics and sun creams** | titanium dioxide or zinc oxide nanoparticles block UV light | absorb/reflect UV but are transparent, so no white film; spread well |
| **Deodorants** | silver nanoparticles kill bacteria that cause body odour | antibacterial; large surface area |
| **Catalysts** | catalytic converters, industrial catalysts | high SA : V gives more surface for reactions, so less catalyst metal needed |

You do **not** need to learn specific examples or properties beyond these — exam questions give you the information and ask you to **evaluate** a use.

### Risks of nanoparticles

There are **possible risks** associated with nanoparticles, and because they are new, their long-term effects are **not fully known**:
- They are so small they may be **breathed in**, or **absorbed through the skin**, and could pass into cells, the bloodstream or organs and damage them.
- Their high surface area may make them **more reactive** (e.g. able to catalyse harmful reactions in the body).
- They can be **washed into the environment** (e.g. from sun cream or clothing) and may harm wildlife or kill useful bacteria (e.g. in sewage treatment).
- They are hard to detect and remove; **toxicity is difficult to test** and effects may take years to appear.

**Evaluating (WS 1.3, 1.4, 1.5):** weigh benefits (effective, less material needed, new properties) against risks (unknown long-term effects, possible toxicity, environmental release), and reach a **justified conclusion**. Good answers also mention that more research and testing is needed, and that particle size and use (e.g. inhaled powder vs. bound in a solid) affect the level of risk.

::: terms
- **Nanoscience**: the study of structures 1 to 100 nm in size, of the order of a few hundred atoms.
- **Nanoparticle**: a particle between 1 and 100 nm across, smaller than fine particles.
- **Nanometre**: one billionth of a metre, written 1 nm or 1 x 10^-9 m.
- **Fine particles (PM2.5)**: particles with diameters between 100 and 2500 nm.
- **Coarse particles (PM10)**: particles with diameters between 2500 nm and 10 000 nm, often called dust.
- **Surface area to volume ratio**: the surface area of a particle divided by its volume; it increases as particles get smaller.
- **Bulk material**: a material made of normal-sized particles, whose properties can differ from nanoparticles of the same substance.
- **Order of magnitude**: a factor of ten, used to compare sizes quickly.
:::

::: method
**Know the size bands cold:** atom about 0.1 nm; nanoparticle 1–100 nm; fine (PM2.5) 100–2500 nm; coarse (PM10) 2500–10 000 nm. Convert with 1 nm = $1 \times 10^{-9}$ m and 1 μm = 1000 nm.

**Calculations**
- **Surface area to volume of a cube:** write SA = $6L^2$, V = $L^3$, substitute, divide, give the unit (e.g. nm⁻¹) or write it as a ratio "x : 1". Show both working lines — method marks are given for each.
- **Scaling:** side ÷ 10 → SA : V × 10. Side ÷ 1000 → SA : V × 1000.
- **Orders of magnitude / how many times bigger:** convert both to metres in standard form, then divide the larger by the smaller. "$10^{-7} \div 10^{-10} = 10^{3}$" → 1000 times, 3 orders of magnitude.
- **Estimates (MS 1d):** "about how many atoms across is a 50 nm particle?" → $50 \div 0.1$ ≈ 500 (accept anything sensible, with working).

**Command words**
- **Explain why nanoparticles have different properties** → "very high **surface area to volume ratio**, so a much larger proportion of atoms are on the surface" — the phrase *surface area to volume ratio* is the mark.
- **Explain why less is needed** → high SA : V, so the same mass has much more surface available (e.g. more catalyst surface for reactions).
- **Evaluate the use of nanoparticles for a purpose** → use the information given: at least one **advantage**, at least one **risk**, then a **conclusion** that weighs them ("on balance… because…"). One-sided answers cap at about half marks.
- **Explain possible risks** → small size lets them enter the body (lungs, skin, cells); unknown long-term effects; may be toxic or reactive; may enter the environment.

**Extended response (4–6 marks):** AQA gives data, e.g. about a sun cream. Level 3 needs advantages and risks both linked to nanoparticle size / SA : V, plus a justified judgement.
:::

::: example Surface area to volume
A cube-shaped nanoparticle has sides of 5 nm. (a) Calculate its surface area to volume ratio. (b) How would the ratio change for a cube of side 50 nm? (4)

(a) Surface area = $6 \times 5^2 = 150$ nm² (1). Volume = $5^3 = 125$ nm³ (1). SA : V = $150 \div 125 = 1.2$ nm⁻¹ (1.2 : 1) (1). (b) The side is 10 times larger, so the ratio is 10 times smaller: 0.12 nm⁻¹ (1).
:::

::: example Comparing sizes
A nanoparticle has a diameter of 40 nm. A typical atom has a diameter of $2 \times 10^{-10}$ m. How many times bigger is the nanoparticle? (2)

40 nm = $40 \times 10^{-9}$ = $4 \times 10^{-8}$ m (1). $\frac{4 \times 10^{-8}}{2 \times 10^{-10}} = 200$ times (1).
:::

::: example Classifying particles
Particles of diameter (a) $5 \times 10^{-8}$ m, (b) $1.5 \times 10^{-6}$ m, (c) $8 \times 10^{-6}$ m are found in air. Classify each. (3)

(a) $5 \times 10^{-8}$ m = 50 nm → nanoparticle (1). (b) $1.5 \times 10^{-6}$ m = 1500 nm → fine particle, PM2.5 (1). (c) $8 \times 10^{-6}$ m = 8000 nm → coarse particle, PM10 / dust (1).
:::

::: example Why less catalyst
Platinum is an expensive catalyst. Explain why using platinum nanoparticles can reduce the cost of a catalytic converter. (3)

Nanoparticles have a very high surface area to volume ratio (1), so a greater proportion of platinum atoms are on the surface where reactions happen (1). This means a smaller mass of platinum is needed to give the same catalytic effect, so less expensive metal is used (1).
:::

::: example Evaluating a use
Nanoparticles of titanium dioxide are used in sun creams. They block UV light and are transparent on the skin. Some scientists worry they may pass through the skin. Evaluate their use. (4)

Advantages: they protect skin from UV, reducing sunburn and skin cancer risk (1); they are transparent so do not leave a white film, so people are more likely to use the cream (1). Disadvantages: they are so small they may be absorbed through skin into cells or the blood, and long-term effects are not known; they may wash into water and harm living things (1). Conclusion: the benefit of UV protection is well established, so their use is reasonable, but more research is needed into long-term risks (1).
:::

::: watch
- Writing "nanoparticles have a large surface area" — the mark is for **high surface area to volume ratio**. A nanoparticle's actual surface area is tiny.
- Getting the size bands in the wrong order or units: nanoparticle 1–100 nm < fine 100–2500 nm < coarse 2500–10 000 nm. Watch the powers: nm is $10^{-9}$ m.
- In SA : V questions, forgetting there are **six** faces, or using $L^2$ for volume.
- Saying the ratio "increases by 10" when it increases **by a factor of 10** (multiplies by 10).
- One-sided evaluations. Give an advantage, a risk, **and** a conclusion.
- Claiming nanoparticles are "dangerous" as fact. Say there are **possible** risks and effects are **not yet fully known**.
