## What it is

**Separate science only (Higher tier):** this whole subsection (4.3.5, chemistry only, HT only) is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. Trilogy students can skip it.

### Equal moles, equal volumes

In a gas the particles are **very far apart** compared with their own size, so almost all of a gas's volume is empty space. That means the **size** of the particles hardly matters: what sets the volume is **how many** particles there are, plus the temperature and pressure. This gives a simple rule (Avogadro's law):

> **Equal amounts in moles of gases occupy the same volume under the same conditions of temperature and pressure.**

So one mole of hydrogen (2 g) takes up the same volume as one mole of carbon dioxide (44 g), even though the carbon dioxide molecules are much heavier. This is a good example of a **model** (WS 1.2): treating gas particles as tiny and far apart explains why the rule works for every gas.

### The molar volume: 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure

At **room temperature and pressure (RTP)** — defined by AQA as **20 °C and 1 atmosphere** — **one mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³** (24 000 cm³).

$$\text{volume of gas (dm}^3) = \text{amount (mol)} \times 24$$

$$\text{amount (mol)} = \frac{\text{volume of gas (dm}^3)}{24} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{\text{volume (cm}^3)}{24\,000}$$

| Gas | $M_r$ | Mass of 1 mol | Volume of 1 mol at RTP |
|---|---|---|---|
| hydrogen, H₂ | 2 | 2 g | 24 dm³ |
| oxygen, O₂ | 32 | 32 g | 24 dm³ |
| carbon dioxide, CO₂ | 44 | 44 g | 24 dm³ |
| methane, CH₄ | 16 | 16 g | 24 dm³ |

The value 24 dm³ is only true at RTP. At a higher temperature the gas expands (larger volume per mole); at a higher pressure it is squashed (smaller volume per mole). It **does not** apply to solids, liquids or solutions — water at 20 °C is a liquid, so 1 mol of water is only 18 cm³.

### Calculation type 1: mass ↔ volume of a gas

Route: **mass → moles (÷ $M_r$) → volume (× 24)**, or backwards.

**Mini-example.** Calculate the volume of 8.8 g of carbon dioxide at RTP ($M_r$ 44).
- moles $= 8.8 \div 44 = 0.200$ mol
- volume $= 0.200 \times 24 = $ **4.8 dm³** (4800 cm³)

**Backwards.** Calculate the mass of 600 cm³ of oxygen at RTP ($M_r$ 32).
- moles $= 600 \div 24\,000 = 0.0250$ mol
- mass $= 0.0250 \times 32 = $ **0.800 g**

**Finding $M_r$.** 0.32 g of a gas occupies 240 cm³ at RTP. moles $= 240 \div 24\,000 = 0.0100$ mol, so $M_r = 0.32 \div 0.0100 = $ **32** (the gas could be oxygen).

### Calculation type 2: gas volumes straight from the equation

Because equal moles of gases have equal volumes, **the volume ratio of gases equals the mole ratio** in the balanced equation. You do not need to convert to moles at all if every substance you are dealing with is a gas at the same temperature and pressure.

$$\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{NH}_3(g)$$

1 volume of nitrogen reacts with 3 volumes of hydrogen to make 2 volumes of ammonia. So 30 dm³ of hydrogen reacts with **10 dm³** of nitrogen and makes **20 dm³** of ammonia.

$$\text{CH}_4(g) + 2\text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

50 cm³ of methane needs **100 cm³** of oxygen and makes **50 cm³** of carbon dioxide. The water is a **liquid** at RTP, so it has (almost) no volume and is left out of the gas total. Check the state symbols.

**Excess gas.** 20 cm³ of carbon monoxide is mixed with 20 cm³ of oxygen and ignited: 2CO(g) + O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g). 20 cm³ of CO uses only 10 cm³ of O₂ and makes 20 cm³ of CO₂, so 10 cm³ of O₂ is **left over**. Final gas volume $= 20 + 10 = $ **30 cm³** (down from 40 cm³).

### Calculation type 3: from a solid (or solution) to a gas volume

If one substance is **not** a gas, go through moles:

1. moles of the given substance ($n$ = mass ÷ $M_r$, or $c \times V$ for a solution)
2. mole ratio from the equation → moles of the gas
3. volume of gas $= n \times 24$ dm³

**Mini-example.** What volume of hydrogen is made when 0.120 g of magnesium ($A_r$ 24) reacts with excess hydrochloric acid?

$$\text{Mg} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{MgCl}_2 + \text{H}_2$$

- moles Mg $= 0.120 \div 24 = 0.00500$ mol
- ratio Mg : H₂ = 1 : 1, so 0.00500 mol H₂
- volume $= 0.00500 \times 24 = 0.120$ dm³ = **120 cm³**

**Mini-example.** 5.00 g of calcium carbonate ($M_r$ 100) reacts with excess acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. moles CaCO₃ $= 0.0500$ mol → 0.0500 mol CO₂ → $0.0500 \times 24 = $ **1.20 dm³**.

### Changing the subject (MS 3b, 3c)

| You want | Use |
|---|---|
| volume | $V = n \times 24$ |
| moles | $n = V \div 24$ |
| mass | $m = n \times M_r = \dfrac{V}{24} \times M_r$ |
| $M_r$ | $M_r = m \div n = \dfrac{m \times 24}{V}$ |

Always state whether the volume is in dm³ or cm³ — divide cm³ by **24 000**, not 24.

### Why measured volumes are often lower

In practice (e.g. collecting hydrogen in a **gas syringe**) the volume collected is often a little less than predicted: some gas escapes before the bung is inserted, some dissolves in the solution, the room may not be exactly 20 °C, or the solid may be impure. These are the "suggest a reason" points in data questions (WS 4.1–4.3).

::: terms
- **Avogadro's law**: equal amounts in moles of different gases occupy the same volume at the same temperature and pressure.
- **Molar volume**: the volume occupied by one mole of any gas; it is 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure.
- **Room temperature and pressure (RTP)**: the standard conditions of 20 °C and 1 atmosphere pressure used for gas volume calculations.
- **Volume ratio**: for reacting gases at the same conditions, the ratio of their volumes is the same as the mole ratio in the equation.
- **Excess**: a reactant present in more than the amount needed, so some is left over when the reaction stops.
- **Gas syringe**: a graduated syringe used to collect and measure the volume of a gas produced in a reaction.
:::

::: method
**Calculations.** Follow equation → substitute → answer with unit. Write "moles = volume ÷ 24" or "volume = moles × 24" before you use it — the equation itself can earn a mark.

**Decide which route you need:**
- Gas to gas (both gases, same conditions): use the **volume ratio** directly from the equation — no moles, no 24.
- Solid or solution to gas (or back): go **through moles** and use the mole ratio, then × 24.
- Mass of a gas ↔ volume of the same gas: mass ÷ $M_r$ × 24.

**Units:** dm³ uses 24; cm³ uses 24 000. If the answer asks for cm³, convert at the end (× 1000). Give 2 or 3 significant figures to match the data.

**"Explain" questions** ("Explain why 1 mol of hydrogen and 1 mol of carbon dioxide have the same volume"): gas particles are **far apart** compared with their size, so the volume depends on the **number of particles**, not their size or mass; equal moles means equal numbers of particles.

**State symbols matter.** Water at RTP is a liquid, so it does not count in gas volume totals. Read the (g) and (l) labels before adding volumes.

**"Suggest" / evaluate questions** on gas collection experiments: give a specific reason the measured volume differs from the calculated one (gas lost before the bung went in, gas dissolving in water, temperature not 20 °C, impure reactant, reaction not complete), and suggest an improvement (gas syringe instead of collecting over water, repeat and take a mean, add the solid in a separate tube and tip it over after sealing).

**Extended responses (4–6 marks):** combine a practical method (gas syringe, measuring mass, excess acid, record volume until no more gas) with the calculation of the expected volume and a comparison.
:::

::: example Mass to volume
Calculate the volume, in dm³, occupied by 3.2 g of oxygen gas (O₂) at room temperature and pressure. ($A_r$ O = 16) (3)

$M_r$ O₂ $= 32$; moles $= 3.2 \div 32 = 0.10$ mol (1)
volume $= 0.10 \times 24$ (1) $= $ **2.4 dm³** (1)
:::

::: example Gas volumes from an equation
Ammonia is made by N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). Calculate the volume of hydrogen needed to react with 15 dm³ of nitrogen, and the volume of ammonia made. All volumes are at the same temperature and pressure. (2)

Ratio N₂ : H₂ : NH₃ = 1 : 3 : 2, so hydrogen $= 15 \times 3 = $ **45 dm³** (1); ammonia $= 15 \times 2 = $ **30 dm³** (1).
:::

::: example Solid to gas volume
Hydrogen peroxide decomposes: 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g). Calculate the volume of oxygen, in cm³ at RTP, made from 0.68 g of hydrogen peroxide ($M_r$ 34). (4)

moles H₂O₂ $= 0.68 \div 34 = 0.020$ mol (1)
ratio H₂O₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1, so moles O₂ $= 0.010$ mol (1)
volume $= 0.010 \times 24 = 0.24$ dm³ (1) $= $ **240 cm³** (1)
:::

::: example Finding the relative formula mass
A sample of an unknown gas has a mass of 1.32 g and occupies 720 cm³ at RTP. Calculate its relative formula mass. (3)

moles $= 720 \div 24\,000 = 0.0300$ mol (1)
$M_r = $ mass ÷ moles $= 1.32 \div 0.0300$ (1) $= $ **44** (1) (consistent with carbon dioxide or propane)
:::

::: example Excess gas
50 cm³ of hydrogen is mixed with 40 cm³ of oxygen and exploded. The products are cooled back to RTP. 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l). Calculate the final volume of gas. (3)

50 cm³ of H₂ reacts with $50 \div 2 = 25$ cm³ of O₂ (1). Oxygen left over $= 40 - 25 = 15$ cm³ (1). The water is a liquid at RTP, so the final gas volume is **15 cm³** of oxygen (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Dividing cm³ by 24.** cm³ must be divided by **24 000**; only dm³ is divided by 24.
- **Using the volume ratio when one substance is not a gas.** For a solid or a solution you must go through moles and $M_r$ first.
- **Counting water as a gas at RTP.** It is a liquid, so it is left out of gas volume totals.
- **Forgetting the mole ratio.** In 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ the oxygen is **half** the moles of hydrogen peroxide.
- **Using 24 dm³ at other conditions.** It only applies at 20 °C and 1 atm.
- **Leaving the excess out.** When one gas is in excess, the leftover gas is part of the final volume.
:::
