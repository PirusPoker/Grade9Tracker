## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 only (chemistry only) — it is **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy students still need reversible reactions and equilibrium from topic 4.6.2, but not the Haber process or fertilisers). Applying equilibrium and rate ideas to the Haber process, and explaining the choice of conditions, is **Higher tier** only.

### The Haber process

**The Haber process is used to manufacture ammonia, which can be used to produce nitrogen-based fertilisers.**

**Raw materials** — **nitrogen** and **hydrogen**:

| Raw material | Source you must recall |
|---|---|
| **Nitrogen** | the **air** (about 78% nitrogen) — separated by fractional distillation of liquefied air |
| **Hydrogen** | **natural gas** (methane), reacted with steam; it can also come from cracking crude oil fractions or electrolysis of water |

**The process:**

1. The **purified gases** (nitrogen and hydrogen in a 1 : 3 ratio by volume) are compressed and passed over a **catalyst of iron**.
2. Conditions: a **high temperature (about 450 °C)** and a **high pressure (about 200 atmospheres)**.
3. **Some** of the hydrogen and nitrogen react to form ammonia. The reaction is **reversible**, so some of the ammonia produced breaks down into nitrogen and hydrogen:

$$\text{nitrogen} + \text{hydrogen} \rightleftharpoons \text{ammonia}$$

$$\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$$

4. **On cooling, the ammonia liquefies and is removed.** (Ammonia has a much higher boiling point, −33 °C, than nitrogen or hydrogen.)
5. **The remaining hydrogen and nitrogen are recycled** back into the reactor, so very little is wasted.

Only about 15% of the gases are converted to ammonia on each pass, but recycling means almost all the nitrogen and hydrogen eventually becomes ammonia.

The forward reaction is **exothermic** (about 92 kJ released per mole of N₂ reacting); the reverse reaction is endothermic.

### Choosing the conditions (Higher tier)

Use the principles of **dynamic equilibrium** (topic 4.6.2) and **rates** (topic 4.6.1). There is a **trade-off** between the **rate of production** and the **position of equilibrium** (yield).

**Temperature (Higher tier):**
- The forward reaction is **exothermic**, so a **lower temperature** shifts the equilibrium to the **right**, giving a **higher yield** of ammonia.
- But a **lower temperature** makes the reaction **too slow** (fewer particles have the activation energy; the iron catalyst also works less well).
- **450 °C is a compromise**: a **reasonable yield** produced at a **fast enough rate**.

**Pressure (Higher tier):**
- There are **4 molecules of gas on the left** (1 N₂ + 3 H₂) and **2 on the right** (2 NH₃).
- **Higher pressure** shifts the equilibrium to the side with **fewer molecules of gas** — the **right** — so the **yield increases**. Higher pressure also **increases the rate** (gas particles are closer together, more frequent collisions).
- But very high pressures need **expensive, thick-walled equipment** and **lots of energy** to run compressors, and there is a greater **safety risk** (explosions).
- **200 atmospheres is a compromise** between yield, rate and cost.

**Catalyst (Higher tier):**
- The **iron catalyst increases the rate** of both forward and reverse reactions equally (it lowers the activation energy).
- It **does not change the position of equilibrium** or the yield — but it means equilibrium is approached faster, so a **lower temperature** (better yield) can be used while keeping a useful rate. This saves energy.

**Summary of the trade-offs:**

| Condition | Effect on yield | Effect on rate | Cost / practical issue | Chosen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low temperature | higher | slower | — | |
| High temperature | lower | faster | more energy for heating | **450 °C** compromise |
| High pressure | higher | faster | expensive equipment, energy for compressors, safety | **200 atm** compromise |
| Iron catalyst | no effect | faster | catalyst is cheap and lasts a long time | **used** |
| Remove ammonia and recycle gases | pulls equilibrium to the right; no waste | — | cooling needs energy | **used** |

**Availability and cost of raw materials and energy (Higher tier):** air is free and plentiful; hydrogen comes from natural gas, a **finite** and increasingly expensive resource. Energy for heating and compression is a major cost, so the **exothermic** reaction's heat is used to warm the incoming gases. Conditions are chosen to give the **most ammonia per day for the lowest cost**, not the highest possible yield.

### Interpreting graphs of conditions (Higher tier)

You may be given a graph of **% yield of ammonia at equilibrium** against **pressure**, with lines for different temperatures.

| Pressure / atm | Yield at 350 °C / % | Yield at 450 °C / % | Yield at 550 °C / % |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 37 | 16 | 7 |
| 200 | 52 | 28 | 13 |
| 400 | 68 | 44 | 25 |

Reading the pattern: **yield increases as pressure increases** (fewer gas molecules on the right) and **decreases as temperature increases** (forward reaction exothermic). At the chosen 450 °C and 200 atm the equilibrium yield is about **28%**. A rate graph would show the opposite trend for temperature: **rate increases as temperature increases**.

**Using the data (MS 1a, 1c):** going from 200 atm to 400 atm at 450 °C increases the yield from 28% to 44% — an increase of 16 percentage points, which is $\frac{16}{28} \times 100 = 57\%$ more ammonia, but it would double the pressure and greatly increase the equipment and energy costs.

### NPK fertilisers

**Compounds of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are used as fertilisers to improve agricultural productivity.** They replace the elements that crops remove from the soil, so plants grow faster and give bigger yields (nitrogen for proteins and leaf growth, phosphorus for roots, potassium for flowers and fruit).

**NPK fertilisers contain compounds of all three elements.** They are **formulations** — carefully designed mixtures — **of various salts containing appropriate percentages of the elements**.

**Industrial production of NPK fertilisers can be achieved using a variety of raw materials in several integrated processes.**

**Nitrogen compounds — from ammonia.** **Ammonia can be used to manufacture ammonium salts and nitric acid** (ammonia is oxidised to make nitric acid). Ammonia is then neutralised by acids:

| Salt | Reaction |
|---|---|
| **ammonium nitrate** | ammonia + nitric acid: $\text{NH}_3 + \text{HNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$ |
| **ammonium sulfate** | ammonia + sulfuric acid: $2\text{NH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow (\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4$ |
| **ammonium phosphate** | ammonia + phosphoric acid |

**Potassium compounds** — **potassium chloride** and **potassium sulfate** are **obtained by mining** and can be used directly (they are soluble).

**Phosphorus compounds** — **phosphate rock** is **obtained by mining**, but it **cannot be used directly as a fertiliser** because it is **insoluble**, so plants cannot absorb it. **Phosphate rock is treated with nitric acid or sulfuric acid to produce soluble salts** that can be used as fertilisers.

**Salts produced when phosphate rock is treated with acids (recall):**

| Acid added to phosphate rock | Products |
|---|---|
| **nitric acid** | **phosphoric acid and calcium nitrate** |
| **sulfuric acid** | **single superphosphate** (a mixture of **calcium phosphate and calcium sulfate**) |
| **phosphoric acid** | **triple superphosphate** (**calcium phosphate**) |

The phosphoric acid made with nitric acid can be neutralised with ammonia to make ammonium phosphate.

**Percentage of an element in a fertiliser (MS 1c)** — uses relative formula mass (topic 4.3.1):

$$\% \text{ of element} = \frac{A_r \times \text{number of atoms of that element}}{M_r} \times 100$$

Ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃: $M_r = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80$; % N $= \frac{2 \times 14}{80} \times 100 = 35\%$.
Ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄: $M_r = 2(14 + 4) + 32 + 64 = 132$; % N $= \frac{28}{132} \times 100 = 21.2\%$.
So ammonium nitrate supplies more nitrogen per kilogram.

### Preparing an ammonium salt in the laboratory (AT 4)

**Making ammonium sulfate in the lab:**
1. Put 25.0 cm³ of **dilute ammonia solution** in a conical flask with a few drops of **methyl orange** indicator.
2. **Titrate** with **dilute sulfuric acid** from a burette until the indicator just changes colour (yellow to red). Record the volume.
3. **Repeat without the indicator**, using the same volumes, so the product is not contaminated.
4. **Evaporate** some of the water by heating gently, then leave to **crystallise**; filter and dry the crystals.

**Comparing laboratory and industrial production:**

| | Laboratory | Industrial |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | small — grams | very large — thousands of tonnes |
| Process | **batch** | **continuous** |
| Starting materials | ammonia solution and dilute acid bought in | made on site from raw materials (air, natural gas, sulfur, phosphate rock) in **several integrated stages** |
| Concentration | **dilute** solutions | **concentrated** reactants (e.g. ammonia gas into concentrated acid) |
| Method | titration, then crystallisation | reactants mixed in large reactors; the heat released evaporates water; product dried and made into granules |
| Energy | heating needed for crystallisation | heat from the exothermic reaction is used |
| Time | slow; hours to crystallise | fast, continuous output |

::: terms
- **Haber process**: the industrial process that makes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen over an iron catalyst at about 450 °C and 200 atmospheres.
- **Reversible reaction**: a reaction in which the products can react to re-form the reactants, shown with the double arrow.
- **Dynamic equilibrium**: the state in a closed system where the forward and reverse reactions happen at the same rate, so amounts stay constant.
- **Compromise conditions**: conditions chosen to balance a reasonable yield against a fast enough rate and an acceptable cost.
- **NPK fertiliser**: a formulation of salts containing nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium in appropriate percentages.
- **Phosphate rock**: a mined, insoluble mineral that must be treated with acid to make soluble phosphate fertilisers.
- **Single superphosphate**: the mixture of calcium phosphate and calcium sulfate made by treating phosphate rock with sulfuric acid.
- **Triple superphosphate**: calcium phosphate made by treating phosphate rock with phosphoric acid.
:::

::: method
**Describe the Haber process:** raw materials and sources (nitrogen from **air**, hydrogen from **natural gas**), **iron catalyst**, **450 °C**, **200 atmospheres**, **reversible** reaction, **cool to liquefy ammonia**, **recycle unreacted gases**. Write the balanced equation with the reversible arrow.

**(Higher tier) Explain the conditions:** for each condition give **effect on yield** (using equilibrium: exothermic forward reaction; 4 gas molecules → 2) **and** effect on **rate**, **and** the **cost/safety** issue, then say it is a **compromise**. A full answer to "explain why 450 °C is used" needs all three: lower temperature → higher yield; but too slow; so 450 °C gives an acceptable yield at an acceptable rate.

**(Higher tier) Catalyst:** increases rate, **does not change yield** or equilibrium position. Never write "the catalyst increases the yield".

**(Higher tier) Graphs:** describe the trend with values ("as pressure increases from 100 to 400 atm at 450 °C, yield rises from 16% to 44%"), then explain it using equilibrium.

**Fertiliser questions:** recall that phosphate rock is **insoluble** and the products with each acid — nitric → phosphoric acid + calcium nitrate; sulfuric → single superphosphate; phosphoric → triple superphosphate. Ammonia + nitric acid → ammonium nitrate.

**Percentage of an element:** (A_r × number of atoms) ÷ M_r × 100. Show the M_r working.

**Compare lab and industrial production:** use a table-style comparison — **scale, batch vs continuous, starting materials, concentration, number of stages, energy** — and refer to the information given.

**6-mark questions:** e.g. "Explain how the conditions in the Haber process are chosen." Level 3 covers temperature, pressure, catalyst and recycling, each linked to **both** equilibrium position and rate, plus cost.
:::

::: example Describing the process
Describe how ammonia is made in the Haber process. (5)

Nitrogen (from the air) and hydrogen (from natural gas) are purified (1). They are passed over an iron catalyst (1) at about 450 °C and 200 atmospheres (1). Some of the nitrogen and hydrogen react to form ammonia in a reversible reaction (1). The mixture is cooled so the ammonia liquefies and is removed, and the unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled (1).
:::

::: example Choice of temperature (Higher tier)
The forward reaction in the Haber process is exothermic. Explain why a temperature of 450 °C is used. (3)

A lower temperature would shift the equilibrium to the right / give a higher yield of ammonia, because the forward reaction is exothermic (1). But at a lower temperature the rate of reaction would be too slow (1). 450 °C is a compromise that gives a reasonable yield at a fast enough rate (1).
:::

::: example Percentage of nitrogen
Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium phosphate, (NH₄)₃PO₄. (Aᵣ: H = 1, N = 14, O = 16, P = 31) (3)

Mᵣ = 3 × (14 + 4) + 31 + 4 × 16 = 54 + 31 + 64 = 149 (1). Mass of nitrogen = 3 × 14 = 42 (1). % N = 42 ÷ 149 × 100 = **28.2%** (1).
:::

::: example Phosphate rock
Explain why phosphate rock is treated with sulfuric acid before being used as a fertiliser, and name the product. (3)

Phosphate rock is insoluble (1), so plants cannot absorb the phosphorus from it. Treating it with sulfuric acid produces a soluble salt (1): single superphosphate, a mixture of calcium phosphate and calcium sulfate (1).

::: watch
- **"The catalyst increases the yield."** It does not — it increases the **rate** only.
- **Half an answer on conditions (Higher tier).** For temperature or pressure you need the **yield** effect **and** the **rate** (or **cost**) effect, then "**compromise**".
- **Wrong direction for temperature.** The forward reaction is **exothermic**, so **lower** temperature gives **higher** yield (but slower rate).
- **Pressure explanation.** Count gas molecules: **4 → 2**. Saying "pressure pushes the reaction forward" scores nothing.
- **Phosphate rock products.** Nitric → phosphoric acid + calcium nitrate; sulfuric → single superphosphate; phosphoric → triple superphosphate. Students often mix the last two.
- **Sources.** Hydrogen comes from **natural gas**, not from the air or water (at GCSE).
:::

(Higher tier) Explain how the conditions used in the Haber process are chosen. In your answer refer to the position of equilibrium, rate of reaction and the cost of the process. (6)
---
