## What it is

Atoms are held together by **strong chemical bonds**. There are three types — **ionic, covalent and metallic** — and which one forms depends on whether the elements are metals or non-metals. All of this is common to GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464.

| Bond type | Between | Particles | How it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ionic** | a **metal** and a **non-metal** (compounds) | **oppositely charged ions** | electrons are **transferred** from metal to non-metal; the ions attract |
| **Covalent** | **non-metals** (most non-metal elements and compounds of non-metals) | **atoms** | atoms **share pairs of electrons** |
| **Metallic** | atoms of **metallic elements and alloys** | **atoms** (positive metal ions) | atoms share **delocalised electrons** |

In every case the bonding is explained by **electrostatic forces** (attraction between opposite charges) and the **transfer or sharing of electrons**.

### Ionic bonding

When a metal atom reacts with a non-metal atom, **electrons in the outer shell of the metal atom are transferred** to the non-metal atom.
- **Metal atoms lose electrons → positively charged ions.**
- **Non-metal atoms gain electrons → negatively charged ions.**
- Ions from metals in **Groups 1 and 2** and non-metals in **Groups 6 and 7** have the **electronic structure of a noble gas** (Group 0) — a full outer shell.

**Charge from the group number:**

| Group | Electrons lost or gained | Ion charge | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | loses 1 | **1+** | Na → Na⁺ (2,8,1 → 2,8) |
| 2 | loses 2 | **2+** | Mg → Mg²⁺ (2,8,2 → 2,8) |
| 6 | gains 2 | **2−** | O → O²⁻ (2,6 → 2,8) |
| 7 | gains 1 | **1−** | Cl → Cl⁻ (2,8,7 → 2,8,8) |

**Dot and cross diagrams** show the electron transfer. Draw the metal's electrons as dots and the non-metal's as crosses (or the other way round), show the electron(s) moving, then draw the ions in **square brackets** with the **charge at the top right**. Usually only the outer shell is drawn. For sodium chloride:

You must be able to draw these for compounds of **Group 1 or 2 metals with Group 6 or 7 non-metals**. Get the **ratio** right so the charges cancel:
- **Magnesium oxide, MgO:** Mg gives **2** electrons to one O → [Mg]²⁺ [O]²⁻.
- **Calcium chloride, CaCl₂:** Ca gives **one electron to each of two** Cl atoms → one [Ca]²⁺ and two [Cl]⁻.
- **Sodium oxide, Na₂O:** **two** Na atoms each give one electron to one O → two [Na]⁺ and one [O]²⁻.

### Ionic compounds

An ionic compound is a **giant structure of ions** (a **lattice**). It is held together by **strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions**. These forces act **in all directions** in the lattice — this is **ionic bonding**.

In **sodium chloride** each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺ ions, in a regular cubic arrangement. You only need to know the structure of sodium chloride.

NaCl can be represented by a **dot and cross** diagram, a **ball and stick** model, a **2D** diagram (a grid of alternating + and − ions) or a **3D** (space-filling) model. If a diagram shows a regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions, the substance is **ionic**.

**Limitations of each representation:**

| Representation | Shows | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Dot and cross | how electrons are transferred and the ions' charges | does **not** show the **giant structure**, the arrangement of ions or their **relative sizes** |
| Ball and stick | the 3D arrangement of ions in the lattice | shows **gaps** between ions that are not really there; the **sticks** suggest bonds are physical lines between pairs of ions, but the forces act in **all directions**; ions not to scale |
| 2D diagram | the alternating pattern and ratio of ions | only **one layer**; does not show the 3D structure |
| 3D space-filling | the arrangement and relative sizes of the ions | only the **outer** ions can be seen; hard to see the pattern inside |

All of them show only a **small part** of a lattice that really contains billions of ions.

**Empirical formula from a diagram:** count the ions of each type in the repeating pattern and give the **simplest whole-number ratio**. A diagram showing 4 Mg²⁺ and 8 F⁻ ions → ratio 1 : 2 → **MgF₂**. Check the charges cancel: 2+ and 2 × 1− ✓.

### Covalent bonding

When atoms **share pairs of electrons** they form **covalent bonds**. **These bonds are strong.** Each shared pair is one covalent bond; atoms usually share enough to fill their outer shells.

Covalently bonded substances come in three forms:
1. **Small molecules** — a few atoms joined together: H₂, Cl₂, O₂, N₂, HCl, H₂O, NH₃, CH₄, CO₂. You should recognise these from their formula.
2. **Very large molecules — polymers**, e.g. poly(ethene).
3. **Giant covalent structures** — billions of atoms all joined by covalent bonds, e.g. **diamond** and **silicon dioxide** (SiO₂, silica: each Si bonded to four O, each O to two Si).

**Dot and cross diagrams you must be able to draw:**

| Molecule | Shared pairs | Line (displayed) form | Lone pairs on central atom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen, H₂ | 1 | H–H | — |
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | 1 | Cl–Cl | 3 on each Cl |
| Oxygen, O₂ | 2 (**double bond**) | O=O | 2 on each O |
| Nitrogen, N₂ | 3 (**triple bond**) | N≡N | 1 on each N |
| Hydrogen chloride, HCl | 1 | H–Cl | 3 on Cl |
| Water, H₂O | 2 | H–O–H | 2 on O |
| Ammonia, NH₃ | 3 | N bonded to three H | 1 on N |
| Methane, CH₄ | 4 | C bonded to four H | none |

**Representing covalent bonds with lines:** a single line is a single bond. You must use lines for small molecules (H–Cl), for the **repeating unit of a polymer**, and for **part of a giant covalent structure**. Poly(ethene) is drawn as the repeating unit in brackets with a large number $n$: —(CH₂—CH₂)ₙ—, where each carbon has two H atoms drawn above and below and the bonds extend through the brackets.

**Limitations of covalent representations:**
- **Dot and cross** — shows which atom the electrons came from, but **not the shape** of the molecule or the **bond angles**; electrons are all identical really.
- **Displayed (line) formula** — shows which atoms are bonded, but **not the 3D shape** or the lone pairs, and not which electrons came from which atom.
- **Ball and stick** — shows the **3D shape**, but bonds are not really sticks, atoms are not to scale, and it shows big gaps between atoms.
- **3D space-filling** — shows relative sizes and shape, but makes it **hard to see the bonds**.
- For giant structures, every diagram shows only a **small part** of the structure.

**Molecular formula from a model:** count every atom of each element in the molecule. A ball and stick model with 2 carbons, 6 hydrogens and 1 oxygen → **C₂H₆O**. (For a giant covalent structure give the ratio instead, e.g. SiO₂.)

### Metallic bonding

Metals are **giant structures of atoms arranged in a regular pattern** (layers). The **outer-shell electrons** of the metal atoms are **delocalised** — free to move through the whole structure — leaving **positive metal ions**. The **sharing of delocalised electrons** gives **strong metallic bonds**: the **electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons**.

Metallic bonding occurs in **metallic elements and alloys**. You should recognise a metal from a diagram showing a regular arrangement of positive ions surrounded by free electrons.

::: terms
- **Ionic bonding**: the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, acting in all directions in a lattice.
- **Ion**: an atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost electrons and so carries an electrical charge.
- **Covalent bond**: a shared pair of electrons between two atoms, usually non-metals; the bond is strong.
- **Metallic bonding**: the attraction between positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons in a metal.
- **Delocalised electrons**: outer-shell electrons in a metal that are free to move through the whole structure.
- **Giant ionic lattice**: a regular three-dimensional arrangement of a huge number of oppositely charged ions.
- **Dot and cross diagram**: a diagram showing electrons from different atoms as dots or crosses to show how they are transferred or shared.
- **Empirical formula**: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms or ions of each element in a compound.
- **Molecular formula**: the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.
- **Giant covalent structure**: a huge network of atoms all joined by strong covalent bonds, such as diamond or silicon dioxide.
:::

::: method
**Identify the bond type first:** metal + non-metal → ionic; non-metals only → covalent; metal element or alloy → metallic. Then use the matching words: **ions** and **electrostatic attraction** (ionic), **shared pairs of electrons** (covalent), **positive ions** and **delocalised electrons** (metallic). Never say "molecules" for an ionic compound or "ions" for a covalent molecule.

**Command words**
- **Describe** what happens when X bonds → state the electron movement, the ions or shared pairs formed, and their charges/electronic structures.
- **Explain** the bonding → add the **force**: "the oppositely charged ions are held by strong electrostatic forces of attraction in all directions".
- **Draw / complete** a dot and cross diagram → outer shells only is fine; correct number of electrons on each atom; for ions, **square brackets and charges**; for molecules, shared pairs **in the overlap** and **all lone pairs** shown.
- **Give a limitation** → say what the model **fails to show** or **shows wrongly** (gaps, sticks, no 3D shape, not to scale, only a small part).
- **Deduce** a formula from a diagram → count, then simplify to a ratio for giant structures; give the full count for molecules.

**Ion charges:** Group 1 → 1+, Group 2 → 2+, Group 6 → 2−, Group 7 → 1−. Write formulae so the total charge is zero: K⁺ with O²⁻ needs two K⁺ → K₂O.

**Extended response (4–6 marks), e.g. "Describe how magnesium and oxygen form magnesium oxide":** electronic structures before (2,8,2 and 2,6) → two electrons transferred from Mg to O → Mg²⁺ (2,8) and O²⁻ (2,8), both noble-gas structures → giant lattice held by strong electrostatic forces in all directions. Level 3 answers use precise terms and link each step.
:::

::: example Ionic dot and cross in words
Describe the dot and cross diagram for magnesium oxide and explain how the bonding forms. (4)

A magnesium atom (2,8,2) transfers its **two** outer electrons to an oxygen atom (2,6) (1). Magnesium becomes a Mg²⁺ ion with structure 2,8, drawn in square brackets with 2+ and an empty or full 2,8 shell (1). Oxygen becomes an O²⁻ ion with 2,8 — eight outer electrons, six crosses and two dots — in square brackets with 2− (1). The oppositely charged ions attract by strong electrostatic forces in all directions, forming a giant ionic lattice (1).
:::

::: example Charges from group numbers
Potassium is in Group 1 and sulfur is in Group 6. Work out the formula of potassium sulfide and explain. (2)

Potassium forms K⁺ (loses 1 electron) and sulfur forms S²⁻ (gains 2 electrons) (1). Two K⁺ are needed to balance one S²⁻, so the formula is **K₂S** (1).
:::

::: example Covalent dot and cross
Describe the bonding in ammonia, NH₃, as a dot and cross diagram would show it. (3)

Nitrogen (2,5) has five outer electrons and needs three more; each hydrogen has one and needs one more (1). Nitrogen shares one pair of electrons with each of three hydrogen atoms, giving three single covalent bonds, each a dot and a cross in the overlap (1). One lone pair (two electrons) remains on the nitrogen, so nitrogen has eight outer electrons and each hydrogen two (1).
:::

::: example Limitations
Give two limitations of using a ball and stick model to represent sodium chloride. (2)

It shows gaps between the ions, but in reality the ions are touching / closely packed (1). The sticks suggest bonds between particular pairs of ions, but ionic bonding is electrostatic attraction acting in all directions (1). (Also accept: ions not shown to scale; only a small part of the lattice is shown.)
:::

::: example Formula from a diagram
A 2D diagram of an ionic compound shows 6 Ca²⁺ ions and 12 F⁻ ions. Give its empirical formula. (2)

Ratio Ca²⁺ : F⁻ = 6 : 12 = 1 : 2 (1). Formula **CaF₂**, and the charges cancel: 2+ + 2(1−) = 0 (1).
:::

::: example Metallic bonding
Describe the structure and bonding in a metal. (3)

A giant structure of atoms arranged in a regular pattern / layers (1). The outer-shell electrons are delocalised, free to move through the whole structure, leaving positive metal ions (1). Strong metallic bonds result from the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons (1).
:::

::: watch
- Ionic bonding is the **electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions** — not "sharing" and not the electron transfer itself. The transfer **forms** the ions; the attraction **is** the bond.
- In ionic dot and cross diagrams, put **square brackets and charges** on every ion and draw the **right number** of each ion (CaCl₂ needs **two** chloride ions).
- Covalent dot and cross: shared pairs go **in the overlap**, and **lone pairs** must be drawn (two on O in water, one on N in ammonia, three on each Cl).
- Metallic bonding: **positive ions** + **delocalised electrons**. Do not call them "free atoms" or say the metal contains negative ions.
- Never call an ionic compound a molecule. Sodium chloride is a **giant ionic lattice**.
- For ionic or giant covalent structures from a diagram give the **simplest ratio** (empirical formula); for a molecule give the **actual count**.
:::

Covalent — between non-metal atoms; atoms share pairs of electrons; each shared pair is a strong bond; forms small molecules, polymers or giant covalent structures; the bond is the attraction of the two nuclei for the shared pair. Metallic — in metals and alloys; outer electrons become delocalised and free to move; regular layers of positive metal ions; strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons. All three are strong bonds based on electrostatic attraction.
