## What it is

This subsection is mostly in **both** GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 5.4.2). **Titrations (4.4.2.5) are separate science only.** The redox explanation of metal–acid reactions and strong and weak acids are **Higher tier**.

### Reactions of acids with metals

Acids react with some metals to produce a **salt and hydrogen**:

$$\text{metal} + \text{acid} \rightarrow \text{salt} + \text{hydrogen}$$

You need the reactions of **magnesium, zinc and iron** with **hydrochloric** and **sulfuric** acids:

| Metal | with hydrochloric acid | with sulfuric acid |
|---|---|---|
| magnesium | Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ | Mg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂ |
| zinc | Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ | Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂ |
| iron | Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂ | Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂ |

Magnesium fizzes fastest, then zinc, then iron (the reactivity series, topic 4.4.1). Iron forms **iron(II)** salts with these acids. Test for hydrogen: a lit splint gives a **squeaky pop**.

**Redox in metal–acid reactions (Higher tier).** These are **redox** reactions. The ionic equation (chloride or sulfate ions are spectators) is:

$$\text{Mg}(s) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+}(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)$$

- Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ : magnesium **loses electrons**, so it is **oxidised**.
- 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ : hydrogen ions **gain electrons**, so they are **reduced**.

The same pattern applies to zinc (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻) and iron (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻).

### Neutralisation and salts

A **base** is a substance that neutralises an acid. An **alkali** is a base that **dissolves in water** (a soluble metal hydroxide). Acids are neutralised by:

| Reactant | General equation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **alkali** (soluble hydroxide, e.g. NaOH, KOH) | acid + alkali → salt + water | HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O |
| **base** (insoluble hydroxide or metal oxide, e.g. CuO, Mg(OH)₂) | acid + base → salt + water | H₂SO₄ + CuO → CuSO₄ + H₂O |
| **metal carbonate** (e.g. CaCO₃, Na₂CO₃) | acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide | 2HNO₃ + CaCO₃ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + H₂O + CO₂ |

The **salt** depends on:
1. **The acid** — **hydrochloric** acid makes **chlorides**, **nitric** acid makes **nitrates**, **sulfuric** acid makes **sulfates**.
2. **The positive ion** in the base, alkali or carbonate (e.g. copper oxide gives a **copper** salt, potassium hydroxide gives a **potassium** salt).

**Deducing the formula of a salt** — balance the charges using the common ions:

| Positive ions | | Negative ions | |
|---|---|---|---|
| sodium Na⁺ | potassium K⁺ | chloride Cl⁻ | nitrate NO₃⁻ |
| ammonium NH₄⁺ | magnesium Mg²⁺ | hydroxide OH⁻ | oxide O²⁻ |
| calcium Ca²⁺ | zinc Zn²⁺ | sulfate SO₄²⁻ | carbonate CO₃²⁻ |
| copper(II) Cu²⁺ | iron(II) Fe²⁺ | | |
| iron(III) Fe³⁺ | aluminium Al³⁺ | | |

Examples: Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → **MgCl₂**; Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ → **Na₂SO₄**; Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ → **Al₂(SO₄)₃** (2 × 3+ = 6+ balances 3 × 2− = 6−); Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻ → **Ca(NO₃)₂**. Put brackets round a group ion when there is more than one of it.

### Making soluble salts

Soluble salts can be made by reacting an acid with a **solid insoluble** substance — a **metal, metal oxide, hydroxide or carbonate**. Add the solid **until no more reacts** (it is in **excess**), **filter off** the excess solid, and **crystallise** the salt from the solution.

**Required practical 1 (Trilogy: Required practical 8): preparation of a pure, dry sample of a soluble salt from an insoluble oxide or carbonate using a Bunsen burner to heat dilute acid and a water bath or electric heater to evaporate the solution.**

Making copper(II) sulfate crystals from copper(II) oxide and dilute sulfuric acid:

1. Measure about 40 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker with a measuring cylinder.
2. **Warm** the acid gently with a Bunsen burner (on a tripod and gauze) — **do not boil**. Warming speeds up the reaction.
3. Add copper(II) oxide (black powder) a spatula at a time, **stirring**, until **some solid remains** unreacted — the oxide is now in **excess**, so all the acid has been used up. The solution turns **blue**.
4. **Filter** the warm mixture into an evaporating basin to remove the excess copper(II) oxide. The filtrate is copper(II) sulfate solution.
5. Heat the solution in the evaporating basin over a **water bath** (or on an electric heater) to evaporate some of the water, until about half remains or crystals start to form at the edge (the **crystallisation point**).
6. Leave the solution to **cool and crystallise** (slowly, for larger crystals).
7. Filter off the crystals and **pat them dry** between filter papers (or leave in a warm place).

$$\text{CuO}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) \rightarrow \text{CuSO}_4(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

With a **carbonate** (e.g. copper(II) carbonate), excess is shown when the **fizzing stops** and solid remains: CuCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O + CO₂.

| Step | Why |
|---|---|
| excess solid | to make sure **all the acid** reacts, so the salt is not contaminated with acid |
| filter | to remove the unreacted solid so the salt is **pure** |
| water bath / electric heater, not direct heating to dryness | gentle heating avoids **spitting** and stops the crystals being decomposed (losing their water of crystallisation) |
| cool slowly | gives larger, purer crystals |

**Risks:** wear **eye protection** (acid and hot spitting solution); copper(II) oxide and copper(II) sulfate are **harmful** — avoid skin contact and do not ingest; take care with the Bunsen flame and hot glassware (let it cool before moving); do not heat the evaporating basin to dryness. **Typical result:** bright **blue crystals** of hydrated copper(II) sulfate. A **low yield** can come from solution left on the filter paper, crystals lost on transfer, or not leaving long enough to crystallise.

### The pH scale and neutralisation

- **Acids** produce **hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq)**, in aqueous solution.
- **Alkalis** in aqueous solution contain **hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq)**.
- The **pH scale**, from **0 to 14**, measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is. **pH 7 is neutral**; acids have **pH < 7**; alkalis have **pH > 7**.

pH can be measured with **universal indicator** (or another **wide range indicator**) or a **pH probe** (pH meter):

| pH | 0–2 | 3–4 | 5–6 | 7 | 8–10 | 11–14 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Universal indicator colour | red | orange | yellow | green | blue | purple |
| Meaning | strongly acidic | acidic | weakly acidic | neutral | weakly alkaline | strongly alkaline |

To use universal indicator: add a few drops to the solution (or dip in a strip of paper), and **compare the colour with a pH colour chart** to give an approximate whole-number pH. A **pH probe** gives a more precise numerical reading (to 0.1 or 0.01) and is not affected by the solution's own colour.

**In every acid–alkali neutralisation**, hydrogen ions react with hydroxide ions to make water:

$$\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

As alkali is added to acid, the pH rises slowly, then jumps rapidly around pH 7 at the point of neutralisation, then levels off at a high pH.

### Titrations (separate science only)

**Separate science only:** this section is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Trilogy 8464.

A **titration** measures the **volumes of acid and alkali** that react exactly, using an **indicator** to show the end point. You must be able to describe titrations using **strong acids** (hydrochloric, sulfuric, nitric) and **strong alkalis**.

**Required practical 2 (chemistry only; no Trilogy equivalent): determination of the reacting volumes of solutions of a strong acid and a strong alkali by titration. (Higher tier) Determination of the concentration of one of the solutions in mol/dm³ and g/dm³ from the reacting volumes and the known concentration of the other solution.**

1. Rinse a **pipette** with the alkali, then use it (with a **pipette filler**) to transfer exactly **25.0 cm³** of alkali into a **conical flask**.
2. Add a few drops of a suitable **indicator** — **phenolphthalein** (pink in alkali → colourless at the end point) or **methyl orange** (yellow in alkali → red in acid). Place the flask on a **white tile** so the colour change is easy to see.
3. Rinse a **burette** with the acid, fill it using a funnel (then remove the funnel), and read the **initial volume** at **eye level** from the **bottom of the meniscus**, to the nearest **0.05 cm³**. Make sure the tip has no air bubble.
4. Add acid to the flask while **swirling**. Near the end point add it **drop by drop**.
5. Stop at the **first permanent colour change**. Record the **final volume**. **Titre = final − initial**.
6. Do a **rough** titration first, then repeat accurately until you get **concordant** titres (within **0.10 cm³**), and calculate the **mean**.

**Universal indicator is not used** in titrations because it changes colour **gradually** through many colours, so there is no sharp end point.

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| independent / dependent | this is a measurement, not a variable investigation; the measured quantity is the titre |
| control | same volume of alkali (pipette), same indicator and amount of indicator, same concentration of acid |
| accuracy | pipette and burette are much more accurate than a measuring cylinder; rinse each with the solution it will hold |
| errors | overshooting the end point, air bubble in burette tip, funnel left in burette, reading the top of the meniscus, water left in burette diluting the acid (all give titres that are wrong) |
| risks | eye protection; acids and alkalis are irritant/corrosive — dilute, wipe spills; never pipette by mouth |

**Titration calculations (Higher tier)** use $n = c \times V$ and the mole ratio (full method in topic 4.3.4). Example: 25.0 cm³ of NaOH is neutralised by 20.00 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ HCl. moles HCl $= 0.100 \times 0.02000 = 0.00200$ mol = moles NaOH; $c = 0.00200 \div 0.0250 = $ **0.0800 mol/dm³**; in g/dm³ $= 0.0800 \times 40 = $ **3.20 g/dm³**.

### Strong and weak acids (Higher tier)

| | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong acid** | **completely ionised** in aqueous solution | hydrochloric, nitric, sulfuric |
| **Weak acid** | only **partially ionised** in aqueous solution | ethanoic, citric, carbonic |

$$\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}^+(aq) + \text{Cl}^-(aq) \qquad \text{(all molecules ionise)}$$

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+(aq) + \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-(aq) \qquad \text{(only a small fraction ionise)}$$

**Strong/weak** is about the **degree of ionisation**. **Concentrated/dilute** is about the **amount of acid** (in moles) in a given volume. They are independent: you can have a dilute strong acid (0.01 mol/dm³ HCl) or a concentrated weak acid (5 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid).

- **For a given concentration, the stronger the acid, the lower the pH** — because more H⁺ ions are released. 0.1 mol/dm³ HCl has pH 1; 0.1 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid has a pH of about 3.
- **As pH decreases by one unit, the hydrogen ion concentration increases by a factor of 10.**

| pH | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| H⁺ concentration relative to pH 5 | 10 000 × | 1000 × | 100 × | 10 × | 1 × |

Order-of-magnitude calculation (MS 2h): going from pH 6 to pH 3 is 3 units, so [H⁺] increases by $10^3 = 1000$ times. A **neutral** solution (pH 7) has equal concentrations of H⁺ and OH⁻; the lower the pH below 7, the higher the H⁺ concentration and the more acidic the solution.

::: terms
- **Salt**: a compound formed when the hydrogen in an acid is replaced by a metal (or ammonium) ion.
- **Base**: a substance that neutralises an acid to form a salt and water, such as a metal oxide or insoluble hydroxide.
- **Alkali**: a base that dissolves in water, forming a solution that contains hydroxide ions, OH⁻.
- **Neutralisation**: the reaction between an acid and a base, in which H⁺ and OH⁻ ions react to make water.
- **pH scale**: a scale from 0 to 14 measuring acidity or alkalinity; below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is alkaline.
- **Crystallisation**: evaporating some solvent from a solution and leaving it to cool so that crystals of the solute form.
- **Strong acid**: an acid that is completely ionised in aqueous solution, such as hydrochloric acid.
- **Weak acid**: an acid that is only partially ionised in aqueous solution, such as ethanoic acid.
- **Concentrated**: describes a solution with a large amount of solute in moles per unit volume.
:::

::: method
**Predict the products** questions: identify the type of reaction (metal, alkali/base, carbonate), write the general equation, then **name the salt** from the acid (chloride, nitrate, sulfate) and the metal. Iron with these acids gives **iron(II)** salts.

**Salt formulae:** write the ions with charges, then balance so the total charge is zero. Use brackets for group ions: Ca(NO₃)₂, (NH₄)₂SO₄.

**Required practical 1 (Trilogy RP 8) questions:** the method must be in **order** and complete: warm acid → add solid until **excess** → **filter** → **evaporate** (water bath) → **crystallise** → dry. Always give the **reason** for each step if asked "explain". Common 2-markers: "Why add excess?" (to use up all the acid); "How do you know it is in excess?" (solid remains / fizzing stops); "Why not heat to dryness?" (crystals decompose / spitting).

**Required practical 2 questions (chemistry only):** name the apparatus and why (pipette for a fixed accurate volume; burette for measuring the added volume to 0.05 cm³); state a suitable indicator and its colour change; explain **concordant** results; identify errors and their effect on the titre.

**pH questions:** give the **colour** and the approximate pH; use "acidic", "neutral", "alkaline". When an alkali is added to an acid, **pH increases**.

**Strong vs concentrated (Higher tier):** these words are not interchangeable. Strong = **completely ionised**; concentrated = **lots of acid per dm³**. In "explain" answers mention **ionisation** and **H⁺ ions**.

**pH calculations (Higher tier):** each pH unit is a **factor of 10** in H⁺ concentration. Count the units difference, then 10 to that power; say which solution has the higher concentration.

**6-mark extended responses** — usually "Describe how to make a pure, dry sample of …" or "Describe how to carry out a titration …". Use numbered steps with reasons and safety. Name real reagents (e.g. zinc oxide + sulfuric acid → zinc sulfate).
:::

::: example Salt formula

::: example Required practical 1 — making zinc sulfate
Describe how to make pure, dry crystals of zinc sulfate from zinc oxide and dilute sulfuric acid. (6)

Warm about 50 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker (do not boil) (1). Add zinc oxide a spatula at a time, stirring, until some solid remains unreacted, so the oxide is in excess and all the acid has reacted (1). Filter the mixture to remove the excess zinc oxide, collecting the zinc sulfate solution (1). Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin on a water bath until crystals start to form at the edge / about half the water has evaporated (1). Leave the solution to cool so that crystals form (1). Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers (1). (Safety: eye protection; do not heat to dryness.)
:::

::: example Redox in a metal–acid reaction (Higher tier)
Zinc reacts with sulfuric acid: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂. Explain, in terms of electrons, why this is a redox reaction. (3)

Zinc atoms lose electrons to form zinc ions (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻), so zinc is oxidised (1). Hydrogen ions gain electrons to form hydrogen molecules (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂), so hydrogen ions are reduced (1). Oxidation and reduction occur in the same reaction, so it is redox (1).
:::

::: example Strong and weak acids (Higher tier)
A student measures the pH of 0.1 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid as 1 and 0.1 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid as 3. (a) Explain the difference. (b) How many times greater is the hydrogen ion concentration in the hydrochloric acid? (4)

(a) Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid: it is completely ionised (1). Ethanoic acid is weak, only partially ionised (1), so at the same concentration it releases fewer H⁺ ions and has a higher pH (1).
(b) 2 pH units difference, so $10^2 = $ **100 times** greater (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Wrong salt name.** Hydrochloric → chloride, sulfuric → sulfate, nitric → nitrate. Iron in dilute acids gives **iron(II)** salts.
- **Forgetting carbon dioxide** when an acid reacts with a carbonate.
- **Required practical order and reasons.** Excess solid → filter → evaporate → crystallise → dry. "Excess" is there to use up **all the acid**; "filter" removes the **excess solid**, not the salt.
- **Heating to dryness** instead of to the crystallisation point.
- **Confusing strong with concentrated** (Higher tier). Strong = fully **ionised**; concentrated = lots of acid **per dm³**.
- **pH factor of 10.** One pH unit is ten times the H⁺ concentration, not "one more" or "double". Lower pH = **more** H⁺.
- **Titration:** using universal indicator, reading the top of the meniscus, or averaging non-concordant titres.
:::

::: test
(Separate science only, Higher tier) 25.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid is neutralised by 18.20 cm³ of 0.150 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide solution. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Calculate the concentration of the hydrochloric acid in g/dm³. ($M_r$ of HCl = 36.5) Give your answer to 3 significant figures. (5)
---
moles NaOH $= 0.150 \times 0.01820 = 0.00273$ mol (1); ratio 1 : 1 so moles HCl $= 0.00273$ mol (1); $c = 0.00273 \div 0.0250 = 0.1092$ mol/dm³ (1); $0.1092 \times 36.5 = 3.9858$ (1) $= $ **3.99 g/dm³** (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) Lemon juice contains citric acid. A student says: "Citric acid in lemon juice has a pH of 2, so it must be a strong acid." Evaluate this statement. (3)
---
Citric acid is a weak acid; it is only partially ionised in water (1). pH depends on both strength and concentration, so a low pH does not on its own show the acid is strong — a concentrated weak acid can have a low pH (1). To compare strength, the pH of solutions of the **same concentration** must be compared; the statement is therefore not justified (1).
:::
