## What it is

**Separate science only:** the whole of AQA 4.8.3 (identification of ions by chemical and spectroscopic means) is **(chemistry only)**. It is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464, and so **Required practical 7** (identifying ions) has **no Trilogy equivalent**. There is no Higher-tier-only content in this subsection.

An **ionic compound** contains a positive ion (**cation**, usually a metal ion) and a negative ion (**anion**). To identify an unknown single ionic compound, you do **one test for the cation** (flame test or sodium hydroxide) and **one test for the anion** (carbonate, halide or sulfate test), then put the names together. You must be able to **identify species from the results** of all of these tests.

### Flame tests (metal ions)

Flame tests identify some metal ions (cations). The compound is heated in a flame, and the metal ions give out light of a **distinctive colour**.

| Metal ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| Lithium, Li⁺ | **crimson** |
| Sodium, Na⁺ | **yellow** |
| Potassium, K⁺ | **lilac** |
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | **orange-red** |
| Copper(II), Cu²⁺ | **green** |

Other flame colours are not required. "Red" alone is ambiguous: lithium is **crimson** and calcium is **orange-red**.

**Method.** Clean a **nichrome (or platinum) wire loop** by dipping it in dilute hydrochloric acid and holding it in a hot, **blue (roaring) Bunsen flame** until it gives no colour. Dip the clean loop in the solid sample (or moisten it with acid first so the solid sticks) and hold it in the **edge of the blue flame**. Observe the colour. Clean the loop between samples so one sample does not contaminate the next. A blue flame is used because a yellow (safety) flame would hide the colours.

**Masking.** If a sample contains a **mixture of ions**, some flame colours can be **masked**: the bright yellow of sodium, for example, hides the lilac of potassium. Even a trace of sodium (from fingers or dirty loops) can give a misleading yellow. This is one big advantage of flame emission spectroscopy (below).

### Metal hydroxides (sodium hydroxide test)

**Sodium hydroxide solution** can be used to identify some metal ions. Add a few drops of dilute sodium hydroxide solution to a solution of the compound. Many metal hydroxides are **insoluble**, so a **precipitate** forms.

| Metal ion in solution | Precipitate with a few drops of NaOH | With excess NaOH |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminium, Al³⁺ | **white** | **dissolves** (colourless solution) |
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | **white** | stays (does not dissolve) |
| Magnesium, Mg²⁺ | **white** | stays (does not dissolve) |
| Copper(II), Cu²⁺ | **blue** | stays |
| Iron(II), Fe²⁺ | **green** | stays (slowly turns brown at the surface as it is oxidised) |
| Iron(III), Fe³⁺ | **brown** (orange-brown) | stays |

**Telling the white precipitates apart:** add **excess** sodium hydroxide: only **aluminium hydroxide dissolves**. Calcium and magnesium both stay white, so use a **flame test**: calcium gives **orange-red**; magnesium gives no characteristic colour.

**Balanced equations for the precipitates.** You must be able to write these. The general ionic equation is metal ion + hydroxide ions → metal hydroxide, with the number of OH⁻ equal to the charge on the metal ion:

- Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s)
- Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)
- Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)
- Al³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Al(OH)₃(s)
- Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Mg(OH)₂(s); Ca²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Ca(OH)₂(s)

Full equations work too: CuSO₄ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂ + Na₂SO₄, and FeCl₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃ + 3NaCl. You do **not** need the equation for aluminium hydroxide dissolving in excess (which forms sodium aluminate).

### Carbonates

Carbonate ions (CO₃²⁻) react with **dilute acids** to form **carbon dioxide** gas. Add dilute hydrochloric acid: the mixture **fizzes**. Bubble the gas through **limewater**, which turns **milky**, proving it is carbon dioxide (4.8.2).

Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ (ionic: CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂)

Fizzing on its own is not enough; the limewater result is what identifies the gas as CO₂.

### Halides

Halide ions in solution produce **precipitates with silver nitrate solution** in the presence of **dilute nitric acid**. Add a few drops of dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution.

| Halide ion | Precipitate | Ionic equation |
|---|---|---|
| Chloride, Cl⁻ | **white** (silver chloride) | Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl |
| Bromide, Br⁻ | **cream** (silver bromide) | Ag⁺ + Br⁻ → AgBr |
| Iodide, I⁻ | **yellow** (silver iodide) | Ag⁺ + I⁻ → AgI |

**Why dilute nitric acid first?** It reacts with any **carbonate ions**, which would otherwise form a precipitate with silver ions and give a false positive. **Hydrochloric acid must not be used**, because it adds chloride ions, which would always give a white precipitate.

### Sulfates

Sulfate ions (SO₄²⁻) in solution produce a **white precipitate** with **barium chloride solution** in the presence of **dilute hydrochloric acid**. The precipitate is **barium sulfate**: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄.

**Why dilute hydrochloric acid first?** It removes **carbonate ions**, which would also form a white precipitate (barium carbonate) with barium ions. **Sulfuric acid must not be used**, because it adds sulfate ions.

### Required practical 7: identifying ions

**Required practical 7: use of chemical tests to identify the ions in unknown single ionic compounds covering the ions from sections Flame tests to Sulfates.** (Chemistry 8462 only; not in Trilogy.)

**Plan for one unknown solid:**

1. **Flame test** on a little of the solid: crimson, yellow, lilac, orange-red or green identifies Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ or Cu²⁺.
2. Dissolve some solid in distilled water. Add **sodium hydroxide** dropwise, then in excess: identifies Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺.
3. To a fresh portion of the solution add **dilute hydrochloric acid**: fizzing that turns limewater milky shows **carbonate**.
4. To a fresh portion add **dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate**: white, cream or yellow precipitate shows chloride, bromide or iodide.
5. To a fresh portion add **dilute hydrochloric acid then barium chloride**: white precipitate shows **sulfate**.
6. Combine: for example, green precipitate with NaOH and white precipitate with barium chloride gives **iron(II) sulfate**.

Use a **fresh sample** for each test so reagents from one test do not affect another. Use distilled water, not tap water, which contains ions.

**Risks:** sodium hydroxide is corrosive/irritant (eye protection); silver nitrate stains skin and is an irritant; barium chloride is toxic (small volumes, wash hands); keep hair and sleeves away from the Bunsen flame; dilute acids are irritants.

**Typical errors:** a dirty flame-test loop (a sodium yellow masks other colours); adding too much NaOH at once so you miss that a white precipitate dissolves in excess; adding the wrong acid before silver nitrate or barium chloride.

### Instrumental methods

Elements and compounds can be detected and identified using **instrumental methods** (machines rather than test-tube reactions). Instrumental methods are **accurate**, **sensitive** and **rapid**.

**Advantages compared with the chemical tests in this specification:**

- **Sensitive**: detect very **small amounts** of a substance (useful in forensic science and drug testing, where samples are tiny).
- **Accurate**: results are reliable and less affected by human judgement of colours.
- **Rapid**: fast, often automated, so many samples can be analysed quickly.
- They can analyse **mixtures** (no masking) and can measure **concentration**, not just identify the ion.

Disadvantages: the equipment is expensive, and needs trained operators and reference data for comparison.

### Flame emission spectroscopy

**Flame emission spectroscopy** is an instrumental method used to analyse **metal ions in solutions**. The sample is put into a **flame** and the light given out is passed through a **spectroscope**. The output is a **line spectrum**: a set of bright lines at particular wavelengths. Every metal ion has its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint.

- **Identify** the ions: compare the lines in the sample's spectrum with **reference spectra** of known metal ions. If all the lines of a reference appear in the sample (at the same wavelengths), that ion is present.
- **Measure concentration**: the **intensity** (brightness) of the lines increases with concentration; comparing with standards of known concentration (a calibration graph) gives the concentration.
- A **mixture** of ions just gives the lines of each metal ion added together, so the ions can all be identified: no masking.

**Interpreting results (the skill AQA tests):** you will be given the sample's result and a **reference set in the same form** (a chart or table of wavelengths). Match **every** line of a reference metal to lines in the sample before concluding it is present; a metal is absent if any of its lines is missing. Explain your answer by quoting the matching wavelengths.

::: terms
- **Cation**: a positively charged ion, such as a metal ion; identified by flame tests or sodium hydroxide.
- **Anion**: a negatively charged ion, such as carbonate, halide or sulfate; identified by tests with acids and precipitating reagents.
- **Flame test**: heating a compound on a clean nichrome loop in a blue Bunsen flame to see the colour its metal ions give.
- **Precipitate**: an insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed, such as a metal hydroxide or silver halide.
- **Masking**: when one flame colour hides another, as the yellow of sodium hides the lilac of potassium.
- **Instrumental method**: analysis using a machine; accurate, sensitive and rapid compared with chemical tests.
- **Flame emission spectroscopy**: an instrumental method in which a sample's flame light is passed through a spectroscope to give a line spectrum.
- **Line spectrum**: a pattern of bright lines at particular wavelengths that is characteristic of a metal ion.
:::

::: method
**Every ion test answer needs reagent + result.** Name the **reagent(s)** (including the acid added first) and the **observation** (colour of flame or precipitate, fizzing plus limewater milky). Then name the ion.

- Metal ion, flame: crimson Li⁺, yellow Na⁺, lilac K⁺, orange-red Ca²⁺, green Cu²⁺.
- Metal ion, NaOH: white (Al³⁺ dissolves in excess; Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ do not), blue Cu²⁺, green Fe²⁺, brown Fe³⁺.
- Carbonate: dilute acid, fizzes, gas turns limewater milky.
- Halide: dilute **nitric** acid then **silver nitrate**: white Cl⁻, cream Br⁻, yellow I⁻.
- Sulfate: dilute **hydrochloric** acid then **barium chloride**: white precipitate.

**Command words.**
- **Identify** a compound from results: work out the cation and the anion separately, then name the compound (e.g. "iron(III) chloride"). Use Roman numerals for copper and iron.
- **Explain** why an acid is added first: to **remove carbonate ions**, which would also form a precipitate; and say why the other acid would not do (it adds the ion being tested for).
- **Suggest** how to distinguish two similar ions: pick a test where the results differ (Ca²⁺ vs Mg²⁺: flame test; Al³⁺ vs Mg²⁺: excess NaOH).
- **State advantages** of instrumental methods: **accurate, sensitive, rapid**, works on tiny samples and mixtures, measures concentration.
- **Interpret** a spectrum: match **all** lines of a reference metal to the sample's lines; quote wavelengths.

**Equations (2 marks).** Write the precipitate equation with the right number of OH⁻ (equal to the metal ion's charge) and state symbols: Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s). In full equations, balance the sodium salt too.

**Required practical 7 questions.** Expect: the order of tests, using a fresh sample each time, cleaning the flame loop, adding NaOH dropwise then in excess, why nitric (not hydrochloric) acid with silver nitrate, why hydrochloric (not sulfuric) acid with barium chloride, risks. **6-mark questions** often ask you to describe tests to identify the ions in two or three unknown compounds; Level 3 needs reagents, results and conclusions for both ions in each compound, in a logical order.
:::

::: example Identifying a compound
A solid gives a lilac flame. Its solution gives a cream precipitate with dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate. Name the compound. (2)

Lilac flame: **potassium** ion (1). Cream precipitate: **bromide** ion. Compound: **potassium bromide** (1).
:::

::: example Writing a precipitate equation
Write a balanced ionic equation, with state symbols, for the reaction of iron(II) ions with sodium hydroxide solution, and give the colour of the precipitate. (3)

Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s) (1 for formulae, 1 for balancing with state symbols). **Green** precipitate (1).
:::

::: example Distinguishing white precipitates
Solutions of aluminium sulfate, calcium chloride and magnesium chloride each give a white precipitate with a few drops of sodium hydroxide. Describe how to tell which is which. (3)

Add **excess** sodium hydroxide: the precipitate that **dissolves** is aluminium hydroxide, so that is aluminium sulfate (1). Flame test the other two solids: **orange-red** flame is calcium chloride (1); no characteristic colour is magnesium chloride (1).
:::

::: example Why the acid?
In the sulfate test, explain why dilute hydrochloric acid is added before barium chloride, and why sulfuric acid is not used. (2)

Hydrochloric acid reacts with/removes any **carbonate ions**, which would also give a white precipitate with barium ions (1). Sulfuric acid contains **sulfate ions**, so it would give a white precipitate whatever the sample (1).
:::

::: example Interpreting a flame emission spectrum
A sample's spectrum has lines at 405, 589 and 766 nm. Reference lines: Li 610 and 671 nm; Na 589 nm; K 405 and 766 nm; Ca 423, 445 and 616 nm. Which metal ions are present? (2)

**Sodium** (589 nm matches) and **potassium** (both 405 and 766 nm match) (1). Not lithium or calcium, because none of their lines appear (1). (A flame test would show only yellow, masking the potassium.)
:::

Why can a flame test fail to identify the metal ions in a mixture? (1)
---

::: watch
- **Mixing up the reds.** Lithium is **crimson**; calcium is **orange-red**. "Red" alone does not score.
- **Missing the acid** in halide and sulfate tests, or using the wrong one: **nitric** acid with silver nitrate, **hydrochloric** acid with barium chloride.
- **Precipitate colours swapped**: chloride **white**, bromide **cream**, iodide **yellow**; iron(II) **green**, iron(III) **brown**.
- **Forgetting "in excess"** when telling aluminium from calcium and magnesium.
- **Carbonate test without limewater.** Fizzing alone does not prove carbon dioxide.
- **Wrong number of OH⁻.** Fe³⁺ needs 3OH⁻ to make Fe(OH)₃; Cu²⁺ needs 2OH⁻.
- **Spectra conclusions from one line.** Every line of the reference metal must appear in the sample.
:::

::: test
Solution X gives a white precipitate with a few drops of sodium hydroxide that dissolves in excess. With dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate it gives a white precipitate. Identify X and explain each result. (4)
---
White precipitate dissolving in excess NaOH: **aluminium** ions (only aluminium hydroxide dissolves in excess) (1)(1). White precipitate with acidified silver nitrate: **chloride** ions (silver chloride) (1). X is **aluminium chloride** (1).

::: test
A student adds dilute hydrochloric acid to a sample of sodium carbonate before testing it for sulfate with barium chloride. Explain what would happen without the acid and why the acid prevents this. (3)
---
Without acid, carbonate ions react with barium ions to form a **white precipitate of barium carbonate** (1), a false positive for sulfate (1). The acid reacts with the carbonate ions (forming carbon dioxide and water), removing them (1).
:::

::: test
Flame emission spectroscopy can measure concentration. Standard solutions of sodium ions gave these readings: 0 mg/dm³, 0 units; 10 mg/dm³, 24 units; 20 mg/dm³, 48 units; 30 mg/dm³, 72 units. A sample of river water gives 54 units. Calculate the concentration of sodium ions in the sample, assuming intensity is proportional to concentration. (3)
---
Intensity per mg/dm³ $= 24 \div 10 = 2.4$ units (1). Concentration $= 54 \div 2.4$ (1) $=$ **22.5 mg/dm³** (accept 23 mg/dm³) (1).
:::

::: test
A forensic scientist has a tiny sample of a powder containing a mixture of metal salts. Evaluate whether flame tests or flame emission spectroscopy should be used to identify the metal ions. (4)
---
Flame tests are cheap, quick and simple (1), but need a visible amount of sample and colours in a mixture can be **masked**, and colour judgement is subjective (1). Flame emission spectroscopy is **sensitive** (works on a tiny sample), **accurate**, and gives a line spectrum for each ion so **every ion in the mixture** can be identified by comparison with reference spectra; it can also measure concentrations (1). Judgement: flame emission spectroscopy, because the sample is tiny and a mixture, even though the equipment is expensive (1).
:::

::: test
You are given three unlabelled white solids: sodium carbonate, potassium chloride and magnesium sulfate. Describe tests you would carry out to identify each solid, giving the expected results. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a logical plan that identifies all three solids, with correct reagents (including acidification) and correct results for cation and anion tests. **Level 2 (3–4):** correct tests and results that would identify at least two solids, or all three with some errors or missing acids. **Level 1 (1–2):** one or two correct tests or results, not a workable plan.

Indicative content: flame test each solid with a clean nichrome loop in a blue flame: **yellow** = sodium carbonate, **lilac** = potassium chloride, no colour = magnesium sulfate. Dissolve each in distilled water and use fresh portions. Add dilute hydrochloric acid: **fizzing**, gas turns **limewater milky** = carbonate (sodium carbonate). Add dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate: **white precipitate** = chloride (potassium chloride). Add dilute hydrochloric acid then barium chloride: **white precipitate** = sulfate (magnesium sulfate). Sodium hydroxide added to the magnesium sulfate solution gives a **white precipitate that does not dissolve in excess** (confirms Mg²⁺ rather than Al³⁺; Ca²⁺ ruled out by no flame colour). Safety: eye protection; barium chloride is toxic.
:::
