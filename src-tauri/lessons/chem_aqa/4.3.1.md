## What it is

Quantitative chemistry is about **how much**: how much product a reaction makes, what a compound is made of, and how sure we are about a measurement. This lesson covers the foundations — **conservation of mass**, **balanced equations**, **relative formula mass ($M_r$)**, **percentage by mass**, **apparent mass changes when gases are involved**, and **uncertainty in measurements**. Everything here is common to GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 5.3.1), at both tiers.

### Conservation of mass

**The law of conservation of mass:** no atoms are lost or made during a chemical reaction, so **the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants**. Atoms are only **rearranged** into new substances.

This is why we can write **balanced symbol equations**: there must be the **same number of atoms of each element on both sides**.

### Multipliers in equations: big numbers and subscripts

| Where the number is | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Subscript**, inside a formula | number of atoms of the element just before it (or of the group in brackets) | H₂O: 2 H, 1 O. Ca(OH)₂: 1 Ca, 2 O, 2 H |
| **Normal script, in front of** a formula | number of **whole units** (molecules / formula units) | 3H₂O: 3 molecules → 6 H, 3 O |

- **Never change a subscript** to balance — that changes the substance (H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, not water). Only change the **big numbers in front**.
- A big number multiplies **everything** in the formula after it: **2Ca(OH)₂** contains 2 Ca, 4 O and 4 H.

**Balancing, step by step** — methane burning:

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (unbalanced)

1. C: 1 on each side ✓.
2. H: 4 on the left, 2 on the right → put **2** in front of H₂O.
3. O: now 2 + 2 = 4 on the right → put **2** in front of O₂.

**CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O** — check: C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 4 ✓.

More to practise: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂; Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂; 2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃.

### Relative formula mass, $M_r$

The **relative formula mass ($M_r$)** of a compound is the **sum of the relative atomic masses ($A_r$) of the atoms in the numbers shown in the formula**. It has **no units** (it is relative to carbon-12). $A_r$ values come from the periodic table.

Useful $A_r$ values: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Na = 23, Mg = 24, Al = 27, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, K = 39, Ca = 40, Fe = 56, Cu = 63.5.

| Formula | Working | $M_r$ |
|---|---|---|
| CO₂ | 12 + (2 × 16) | **44** |
| CaCO₃ | 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) | **100** |
| Ca(OH)₂ | 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) | **74** |
| Mg(NO₃)₂ | 24 + 2 × (14 + 3 × 16) | **148** |
| (NH₄)₂SO₄ | 2 × (14 + 4 × 1) + 32 + (4 × 16) | **132** |

**Masses in a balanced equation add up.** The sum of the $M_r$ of the reactants **in the quantities shown** equals the sum for the products:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO: (2 × 24) + (2 × 16) = 48 + 32 = **80**; 2 × (24 + 16) = **80** ✓

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂: 100 → 56 + 44 = **100** ✓

This is a quick check that an equation is balanced, and it is conservation of mass in numbers.

### Percentage by mass of an element in a compound

$$\text{\% by mass of element} = \frac{A_r \times \text{number of atoms of that element}}{M_r \text{ of the compound}} \times 100$$

**Worked mini-example:** percentage of iron in iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃.
- $M_r$ = (2 × 56) + (3 × 16) = 112 + 48 = 160
- % Fe = $\frac{112}{160} \times 100$ = **70%**

**Another:** percentage of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (a fertiliser). $M_r$ = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80. There are **two** N atoms: % N = $\frac{28}{80} \times 100$ = **35%**.

### Mass changes when a reactant or product is a gas

Some reactions **seem** to break the law of conservation of mass. This is almost always because a **gas** is involved and its mass has not been measured. In a **non-enclosed system** (open container), gas can enter or leave.

| Reaction (open container) | What the balance shows | Why (particle model) |
|---|---|---|
| **Metal + oxygen**, e.g. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO | mass **increases** | oxygen atoms from the **air** (a gas, not weighed at the start) combine with the metal atoms; the solid oxide contains both |
| **Thermal decomposition of a metal carbonate**, e.g. CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂ | mass **decreases** | CO₂ molecules form and **escape into the air**, leaving the metal oxide as the only solid product |
| **Acid + carbonate**, e.g. CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ | mass **decreases** | CO₂ gas escapes from the flask |
| **Acid + metal**, e.g. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ | mass decreases (slightly) | hydrogen gas escapes (very light, so a small change) |
| Precipitation in solution, e.g. making BaSO₄ | **no change** | no gas; all atoms stay in the container |

In a **closed (sealed) system**, the mass stays **exactly the same**, because no particles can get in or out.

**Worked mini-example:** 2.4 g of magnesium is burned and forms 4.0 g of magnesium oxide. Mass of oxygen that reacted = 4.0 − 2.4 = **1.6 g**. The mass "gained" is the mass of oxygen atoms from the air.

**Practical opportunity (AT 1, 2, 6):** heating a weighed metal in a crucible with a lid lifted occasionally (to let air in but keep oxide in), or heating a carbonate and weighing before and after. Heat until **constant mass** to make sure the reaction is complete.

### Chemical measurements and uncertainty

**Whenever a measurement is made there is always some uncertainty** about the result. Repeat readings are rarely identical, because of random errors and the resolution of the equipment.

- **Mean** = add up the valid results and divide by how many there are (leave out **anomalous** results).
- **Range** = highest − lowest value.
- **Uncertainty** = **half the range** about the mean. Write the result as **mean ± uncertainty**.

**Worked mini-example:** volumes of gas collected in four repeats: 24.5, 25.1, 24.8, 25.0 cm³.
- Mean = $\frac{24.5 + 25.1 + 24.8 + 25.0}{4} = \frac{99.4}{4}$ = 24.85 ≈ **24.9 cm³**
- Range = 25.1 − 24.5 = 0.6 cm³
- Uncertainty = $\frac{0.6}{2}$ = ± 0.3 cm³ → result **24.9 ± 0.3 cm³**

**Representing the distribution of results:** plot repeats as points or a bar with **range bars** (error bars from lowest to highest). Overlapping range bars mean two results may **not** be significantly different. A **smaller range** means more **precise** results (results close together); **accurate** results are close to the **true value**.

::: terms
- **Law of conservation of mass**: no atoms are lost or made in a chemical reaction, so the mass of products equals the mass of reactants.
- **Balanced symbol equation**: an equation with the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.
- **Relative atomic mass (Ar)**: the average mass of an atom of an element compared with one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
- **Relative formula mass (Mr)**: the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms shown in a formula; it has no units.
- **Non-enclosed system**: an open container in which gases can enter from or escape to the surroundings.
- **Closed system**: a sealed container in which no substances can enter or leave, so total mass stays constant.
- **Uncertainty**: the amount by which a result may differ from the mean, estimated as half the range of repeat results.
- **Range**: the difference between the highest and lowest values in a set of repeat measurements.
- **Anomalous result**: a result that does not fit the pattern of the others and is left out when calculating a mean.
:::

::: method
**Calculations — always show working.** Write the formula or equation → substitute numbers → answer with a unit (or "no unit" for $M_r$) to a sensible number of significant figures. Method marks are given even if the final answer is wrong.

- **$M_r$:** list each element × number of atoms; multiply brackets out carefully: Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2(16 + 1). Never give $M_r$ a unit.
- **% by mass:** multiply $A_r$ by the **number of atoms** of that element in the formula **before** dividing by $M_r$ (two N in NH₄NO₃).
- **Mass checks:** for "show that the equation obeys conservation of mass", add $M_r$ values **times the big numbers** on each side and show both totals are equal.
- **Mass from a mass change:** mass of gas = difference in balance readings (e.g. oxygen gained, CO₂ lost).

**Balancing:** change only the **big numbers in front**, never subscripts. Balance elements that appear in one substance on each side first, leave O₂ or H₂ until last, then **re-count every atom**.

**Explaining mass changes (2–4 marks):** (1) name the **gas**, (2) say whether it **joins from the air** or **escapes into the air**, (3) say this is a **non-enclosed system**, (4) conclude that **mass is still conserved** — no atoms are made or destroyed; they just moved in or out of the container. Use the balanced equation given to name the gas.

**Uncertainty questions:** mean (ignore anomalous results) → range → uncertainty = range ÷ 2 → write "mean ± uncertainty" with the unit. "Suggest how to reduce uncertainty" → repeat more times, use equipment with a higher resolution, remove a source of random error.

**Command words:** *calculate* (number + unit), *explain* (reason using particles/atoms), *suggest* (apply to a new situation), *describe* (what you would see on the balance: increases / decreases / stays the same).
:::

::: example Counting atoms
How many atoms of each element are shown by 3Al₂(SO₄)₃? (3)

Al: 3 × 2 = 6 (1). S: 3 × 3 = 9 (1). O: 3 × 3 × 4 = 36 (1).
:::

::: example Balancing
Balance: Fe₂O₃ + CO → Fe + CO₂ (2)

Fe: put 2 in front of Fe. O: Fe₂O₃ gives 3 O to remove, each CO takes one O to make CO₂, so 3CO and 3CO₂ (1). **Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂**. Check: Fe 2 = 2, C 3 = 3, O 3 + 3 = 6 and 3 × 2 = 6 ✓ (1).
:::

::: example Relative formula mass and percentage
Calculate (a) the $M_r$ of ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄, and (b) the percentage by mass of nitrogen in it. Give (b) to 3 significant figures. (4)

(a) $M_r$ = 2 × (14 + 4 × 1) + 32 + 4 × 16 (1) = 36 + 32 + 64 = **132** (1). (b) Mass of N = 2 × 14 = 28 (1). % N = $\frac{28}{132} \times 100$ = **21.2%** (1).
:::

::: example Conservation of mass in an equation
Show that the equation 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO obeys the law of conservation of mass. ($A_r$: Cu = 63.5, O = 16) (2)

Reactants: (2 × 63.5) + (2 × 16) = 127 + 32 = 159 (1). Products: 2 × (63.5 + 16) = 2 × 79.5 = 159 — the totals are equal (1).
:::

::: example Explaining a mass decrease
A student heats 5.00 g of copper carbonate in an open boiling tube. The solid left has a mass of 3.22 g. The equation is CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂. Explain the change in mass. (3)

The mass has decreased by 1.78 g (1). Carbon dioxide gas is produced and escapes from the open tube into the air (1). Mass is still conserved: no atoms are lost; the total mass of copper oxide plus carbon dioxide equals 5.00 g, but the CO₂ was not weighed (1).
:::

::: example Uncertainty
A student measures the temperature rise of a reaction five times: 6.8, 7.1, 9.6, 6.9, 7.2 °C. Calculate the mean and its uncertainty. (3)

9.6 °C is anomalous and is left out (1). Mean = (6.8 + 7.1 + 6.9 + 7.2) ÷ 4 = 28.0 ÷ 4 = 7.0 °C (1). Range = 7.2 − 6.8 = 0.4, so uncertainty = ± 0.2 °C; result 7.0 ± 0.2 °C (1).
:::

::: watch
- Changing **subscripts** to balance an equation instead of the numbers in front.
- Forgetting that a number in front multiplies **every** atom in the formula, including inside brackets: 2Ca(OH)₂ has **4** H.
- Giving $M_r$ a unit such as "g" or "g/mol". $M_r$ has **no units**.
- In % by mass, using one atom when the formula has two: N in NH₄NO₃ is **2 × 14**.
- Saying "mass was lost" or "atoms were destroyed". Say a **gas escaped** (or was gained from the air) in a **non-enclosed system** — mass is **still conserved**.
- Uncertainty is **half** the range, not the whole range — and leave out anomalous results first.
:::

::: test
A student heats a crucible containing magnesium. Four repeat experiments give the following mass of oxide: 1.52 g, 1.46 g, 1.50 g, 1.48 g. (a) Calculate the mean. (b) Calculate the uncertainty and write the result in the form mean ± uncertainty. (c) Suggest one way to reduce the uncertainty. (4)
---
(a) (1.52 + 1.46 + 1.50 + 1.48) ÷ 4 = 5.96 ÷ 4 = 1.49 g (1). (b) Range = 1.52 − 1.46 = 0.06 g (1); 1.49 ± 0.03 g (1). (c) Use a balance with higher resolution / do more repeats / heat to constant mass each time (1).
:::

::: test
Hydrated copper sulfate is heated in an open evaporating basin: CuSO₄·5H₂O → CuSO₄ + 5H₂O. A student says the decrease in mass proves that mass is not conserved. Use the equation and the particle model to explain why the student is wrong. (3)
---
Water is produced as water vapour (a gas) (1). The water molecules escape from the open basin into the air, so they are not weighed (1). No atoms are destroyed: the mass of copper sulfate plus the water released equals the mass of the hydrated copper sulfate, so mass is conserved (1).
:::

::: test
A student carries out three reactions, each in an open container on a balance. Reaction 1: magnesium ribbon is heated in a crucible: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Reaction 2: zinc carbonate is heated strongly: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂. Reaction 3: sodium sulfate solution is added to barium chloride solution: Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄ + 2NaCl. Predict and explain the change in mass shown by the balance in each reaction. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** correct predictions for all three reactions, each explained in terms of a gas entering or leaving (or not), linked to particles/atoms and conservation of mass. **Level 2 (3–4):** correct predictions for at least two reactions with some explanation, or all three with limited explanation. **Level 1 (1–2):** one or two correct predictions with little or no explanation.

Indicative content: Reaction 1 — mass increases; oxygen from the air (a gas, not weighed at the start) reacts with magnesium; oxygen atoms become part of the solid magnesium oxide. Reaction 2 — mass decreases; carbon dioxide gas forms and escapes into the air; only zinc oxide is left as the solid product. Reaction 3 — mass stays the same; no gas is involved; all products (solid precipitate and dissolved salt) stay in the container. In every case mass is conserved — no atoms are made or destroyed; the changes happen because the containers are non-enclosed systems; in a sealed container reactions 1 and 2 would show no overall change (as long as enough oxygen was sealed in for reaction 1).
:::
