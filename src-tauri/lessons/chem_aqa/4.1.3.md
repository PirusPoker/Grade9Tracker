## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection (4.1.3, chemistry only) is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. Trilogy students can skip it.

The **transition elements** (transition metals) are the block of metals in the middle of the periodic table, between Group 2 and Group 3. They are metals with **similar properties to each other**, which are **different from those of the Group 1 metals**. You must be able to illustrate their properties using six of them from the first row: **chromium (Cr), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), cobalt (Co), nickel (Ni) and copper (Cu)**.

### Comparison with Group 1

| Property | Group 1 (Li, Na, K) | Transition metals (Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu) |
|---|---|---|
| Melting point | **low** (Na 98 °C, K 63 °C) | **high** (Fe 1538 °C, Cu 1085 °C, Cr 1907 °C) — mercury is the one exception |
| Density | **low** — Li, Na and K float on water (Na 0.97 g/cm³) | **high** (Fe 7.9 g/cm³, Cu 9.0 g/cm³, Ni 8.9 g/cm³) |
| Strength | weak | **strong** — used for structures (iron and steel), pipes and cables |
| Hardness | **soft** — can be cut with a knife | **hard** |
| Reaction with oxygen | tarnish within seconds in air; burn readily | react **slowly** or only when heated: iron rusts slowly (it needs water as well), copper slowly forms a dull layer |
| Reaction with water | react **vigorously with cold water**, giving hydrogen and an alkaline hydroxide | **little or no reaction** with cold water: copper does not react; iron reacts only very slowly (rusting with oxygen) |
| Reaction with halogens | react **vigorously**, e.g. sodium burns in chlorine | react **less vigorously**, usually only when **heated**, e.g. hot iron wool glows in chlorine to form iron(III) chloride |

**Summary:** transition metals are **less reactive**, **denser**, **stronger and harder** and have **higher melting points** than Group 1 metals.

Why they are useful as a result: their strength, hardness and high melting points make them good **structural** metals (iron in steel), and their low reactivity means they **do not react with water**, so copper is used for **water pipes**.

### Typical properties of transition metals

**1. Ions with different charges.** Many transition elements form **more than one ion**. The charge is shown by a **Roman numeral** in the name.

| Metal | Common ions | Example compounds |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ | iron(II) chloride FeCl₂; iron(III) chloride FeCl₃; iron(III) oxide Fe₂O₃ (rust) |
| Copper | Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺ | copper(I) oxide Cu₂O; copper(II) oxide CuO; copper(II) sulfate CuSO₄ |
| Chromium | Cr²⁺ and Cr³⁺ | chromium(III) oxide Cr₂O₃ |
| Manganese | Mn²⁺ (and higher states, e.g. +4 in MnO₂, +7 in manganate(VII)) | manganese(II) sulfate; manganese(IV) oxide MnO₂ |
| Cobalt | Co²⁺ and Co³⁺ | cobalt(II) chloride CoCl₂ |
| Nickel | Ni²⁺ (mainly) | nickel(II) sulfate NiSO₄ |

Group 1 metals only ever form **1+** ions. To find the charge on a transition metal ion from a formula, balance the charges: in FeCl₃ there are three Cl⁻ (total 3−), so the iron must be **Fe³⁺** → iron(III) chloride.

**2. Coloured compounds.** Transition metal compounds are usually **coloured**, whereas Group 1 compounds are **white** and give **colourless** solutions.

| Compound / ion | Colour |
|---|---|
| Copper(II) sulfate (hydrated) / Cu²⁺(aq) | **blue** |
| Iron(II) compounds, Fe²⁺(aq) | **pale green** |
| Iron(III) compounds, Fe³⁺(aq) | **yellow-brown / orange-brown** |
| Potassium manganate(VII) | **purple** |
| Potassium dichromate(VI) | **orange** |
| Chromium(III) compounds | **green** |
| Cobalt(II) chloride | **pink** (hydrated), **blue** (anhydrous) |
| Nickel(II) compounds | **green** |

These colours are used in analysis: for example the hydroxide precipitates of Cu²⁺ (blue), Fe²⁺ (green) and Fe³⁺ (brown) identify metal ions (topic 4.8.3), and blue cobalt chloride paper turns pink with water. Pottery glazes and stained glass are coloured with transition metal compounds.

**3. Useful as catalysts.** A **catalyst** speeds up a reaction without being used up. Transition metals and their compounds are common catalysts:

| Catalyst | Reaction |
|---|---|
| **Iron** | the **Haber process**: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (making ammonia for fertilisers) |
| **Nickel** | **hydrogenation** of alkenes, e.g. hardening vegetable oils to make margarine |
| **Manganese(IV) oxide**, MnO₂ | decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ |
| **Copper(II) ions** (copper sulfate) | speed up the reaction between zinc and dilute sulfuric acid |

Catalysts reduce costs because reactions happen faster or at lower temperatures, saving energy. Group 1 metals are not used as catalysts.

### Putting it together

A question may give you a mystery metal and its properties. **High melting point, high density, forms coloured compounds, more than one ion, catalytic** → transition metal. **Low melting point, low density, soft, reacts vigorously with water, white compounds, only a 1+ ion** → Group 1.

::: terms
- **Transition metals**: the block of metals between Group 2 and Group 3, including chromium, manganese, iron, cobalt, nickel and copper.
- **Density**: the mass of a substance per unit volume, measured in g/cm³; transition metals have much higher densities than Group 1.
- **Catalyst**: a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up itself.
- **Roman numeral**: the number in brackets in a name, such as iron(III), that shows the charge on the metal ion.
- **Hydrated**: describes a compound containing water within its crystals, such as blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate.
- **Haber process**: the industrial manufacture of ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen using an iron catalyst.
- **Hydrogenation**: adding hydrogen to an alkene using a nickel catalyst, for example to harden vegetable oils.
:::

::: method
**Comparison questions** ("Compare the properties of iron and sodium"): AQA wants **both sides** in each point, using comparatives. Write "iron has a **higher** melting point **than** sodium", not "iron has a high melting point". Cover the spec list: **melting point, density, strength, hardness, reactivity with oxygen, water and halogens**.

**Explain-a-use questions:** match the property to the use. Copper water pipes → does not react with water. Iron girders → strong, hard. Iron in the Haber process → catalyst.

**Typical-property questions:** the three are **ions with different charges**, **coloured compounds**, **catalysts**. Always give a **named example** from Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni or Cu — "iron forms Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺", "copper(II) sulfate is blue", "iron is the catalyst in the Haber process".

**Data questions:** you may be given a table of melting points, densities or reactions for unfamiliar metals and asked which is a transition metal. Look for **high melting point + high density + coloured compounds + several ion charges**. Show which data you used.

**Working out ion charges:** total negative charge of the anions ÷ number of metal ions. Fe₂O₃: three O²⁻ = 6−, shared by two Fe, so each is **3+**.

**Extended responses (4–6 marks):** organise point by point (physical properties, then chemical reactivity, then typical properties), giving a comparison and an example for each.
:::

::: example Comparing with Group 1
Compare the physical properties of iron and sodium. (4)

Iron has a much higher melting point than sodium (1538 °C compared with 98 °C) (1). Iron is much denser than sodium; sodium floats on water but iron sinks (1). Iron is stronger than sodium (1). Iron is harder than sodium, which is soft enough to cut with a knife (1).
:::

::: example Ion charges
Name the compounds FeCl₂ and CuO and give the charge on the metal ion in each. (4)

FeCl₂: two Cl⁻ ions (2−), so iron is Fe²⁺ (1) — iron(II) chloride (1).
CuO: one O²⁻ ion (2−), so copper is Cu²⁺ (1) — copper(II) oxide (1).
:::

::: example Identify the metal
Metal Q has a melting point of 1455 °C and a density of 8.9 g/cm³, and its compounds form green solutions. Metal R has a melting point of 39 °C and a density of 1.5 g/cm³, and its compounds are white. Identify which is a transition metal and explain. (3)

Q is the transition metal (1). It has a high melting point and a high density (1), and it forms coloured compounds, whereas R has a low melting point and density and white compounds, which are typical of a Group 1 metal (1). (Q is nickel; R is rubidium.)
:::

::: example Catalysts
Give two examples of transition metals or their compounds used as catalysts, and state the reaction each catalyses. (2)

Iron catalyses the Haber process, making ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen (1). Nickel catalyses the hydrogenation of vegetable oils (alkenes) to make margarine (1). (Also accept manganese(IV) oxide for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.)
:::

::: example Reactivity with water
Describe how the reactions of copper and potassium with cold water differ. (2)

Potassium reacts violently: it fizzes, melts and the hydrogen produced ignites with a lilac flame, forming an alkaline solution (1). Copper does not react with cold water at all (1).
:::

::: q
Give three typical properties of transition metals that Group 1 metals do not have. (3)
---
Form ions with different charges (1); form coloured compounds (1); useful as catalysts (1). (Accept high melting point / high density / hard and strong.)
:::

::: q
Which is denser, cobalt or lithium? (1)
---
Cobalt.
:::

::: q
State the formulae of the two common ions of iron. (2)
---
Fe²⁺ (1) and Fe³⁺ (1).
:::

::: q
What colour is a solution of copper(II) sulfate? (1)
---
Blue (from the Cu²⁺ ions).
:::

::: q
Name the metal used as the catalyst in the Haber process. (1)
---
Iron (a transition metal).
:::

::: q
Explain why copper, not sodium, is used to make water pipes. (2)
---
Copper does not react with water, whereas sodium reacts vigorously with water (1); copper is also strong and hard but can be shaped, while sodium is soft (1).
:::

::: q
Work out the charge on the manganese ion in MnO₂. (1)
---
4+ (two O²⁻ give 4−, so manganese is Mn⁴⁺ — manganese(IV) oxide).
:::

::: q
Describe how the reaction of iron with chlorine differs from that of sodium with chlorine. (2)
---
Sodium reacts vigorously with chlorine, burning with an orange-yellow flame, even without much heating (1); iron reacts less vigorously and has to be heated (hot iron wool glows) to form iron(III) chloride (1).
:::

::: q
Anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride paper is blue. Suggest what happens when a drop of water is added, and how this property can be used. (2)
---
It turns pink (hydrated cobalt(II) chloride forms) (1); so it can be used to test for the presence of water (1).
:::

::: watch
- In comparisons use **comparative words** — higher, denser, harder, less reactive — and name **both** metals. One-sided statements ("iron is strong") often score zero.
- Transition metals are **less** reactive than Group 1, not more. They react with oxygen, water and halogens **slowly** or only **when heated**.
- Group 1 metals have **low** densities (some float); transition metals have **high** densities. Do not mix the two up.
- For "typical properties" give an **example with each**: Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, blue copper(II) sulfate, iron in the Haber process.
- The Roman numeral is the **charge on the metal ion**, not the number of metal atoms: iron(III) oxide is Fe₂O₃.
:::

::: test
Which of these is a transition metal? A — calcium; B — potassium; C — nickel; D — aluminium. (1)
---
C — nickel.
:::

::: test
Give two differences between the chemical reactions of iron and of sodium. (2)
---
Any two: sodium reacts vigorously with cold water but iron reacts only very slowly (rusting) (1); sodium tarnishes rapidly in air but iron reacts slowly with oxygen (1); sodium reacts vigorously with chlorine but iron needs heating (1).
:::

::: test
Potassium manganate(VII) is purple and potassium chloride is white. Explain this difference. (2)
---
Potassium manganate(VII) contains a transition metal (manganese) (1); transition metal compounds are typically coloured, whereas Group 1 compounds such as potassium chloride are white (1).
:::

::: test
A 4.0 cm³ block of copper has a mass of 35.8 g. A 4.0 cm³ piece of sodium has a mass of 3.88 g. Calculate the density of each metal using density = mass ÷ volume, and use your answers to explain which one would float on water (density 1.0 g/cm³). (4)
---
Copper: $35.8 \div 4.0 = 8.95$ g/cm³ (1). Sodium: $3.88 \div 4.0 = 0.97$ g/cm³ (1). Sodium would float (1) because its density is less than that of water, while copper's is much greater (1).
:::

::: test
Name the compounds Fe₂O₃ and CoCl₂, showing how you worked out the charge on each metal ion. (4)
---
Fe₂O₃: three O²⁻ = 6− shared between two iron ions, so each is Fe³⁺ (1) — iron(III) oxide (1). CoCl₂: two Cl⁻ = 2−, so cobalt is Co²⁺ (1) — cobalt(II) chloride (1).
:::

::: test
Hydrogen peroxide decomposes very slowly. When a small amount of manganese(IV) oxide is added, oxygen is given off rapidly and the manganese(IV) oxide can be recovered unchanged at the end. (a) What is the role of the manganese(IV) oxide? (b) Write a balanced equation for the decomposition. (3)
---
(a) It is a catalyst — it speeds up the reaction without being used up (1). (b) 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ (1 for correct formulae, 1 for balancing).
:::

::: test
Suggest why iron, rather than a Group 1 metal, is used to make bridges. (2)
---
Iron is much stronger and harder than Group 1 metals (1); Group 1 metals would react with water and oxygen in the air/rain, whereas iron reacts only slowly (and has a high melting point) (1).
:::

::: test
Compare the properties of the transition metals with those of the Group 1 metals. Refer to named examples in your answer. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a detailed comparison covering physical properties, reactivity and typical properties, each with a named example and comparative language. **Level 2 (3–4):** several correct comparisons across at least two areas, with some examples. **Level 1 (1–2):** a few simple statements about one type of metal, or comparisons without examples.

Indicative content: transition metals have higher melting points (iron 1538 °C vs sodium 98 °C); higher densities (Group 1 metals such as lithium, sodium and potassium float on water); stronger; harder (Group 1 can be cut with a knife); less reactive with oxygen (iron rusts slowly, sodium tarnishes in seconds); less reactive with water (copper does not react, potassium reacts violently); less reactive with halogens (iron must be heated in chlorine); transition metals form ions with different charges (Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺) whereas Group 1 form only 1+ ions; transition metal compounds are coloured (copper(II) sulfate blue, iron(II) green) whereas Group 1 compounds are white; transition metals are catalysts (iron in the Haber process, nickel in hydrogenation, manganese(IV) oxide for hydrogen peroxide).
:::
