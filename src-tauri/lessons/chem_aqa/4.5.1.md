## What it is

Every chemical reaction involves an **energy change**, because bonds are broken and new bonds are made. This topic (AQA 4.5.1) is in both GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (where it is numbered 5.5.1). The bond energy calculations are **(Higher tier)** only.

### Energy is conserved

**Energy is conserved in chemical reactions.** The amount of energy in the universe at the end of a reaction is the same as before it. Energy is only **transferred** between the chemicals (the **system**) and everything around them (the **surroundings**: the water the chemicals are dissolved in, the beaker, the thermometer, the air).

So if a reaction transfers energy **to** the surroundings, the **products must have less energy than the reactants**, by exactly the amount transferred.

### Exothermic and endothermic reactions

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Energy transfer | **to** the surroundings | **from** the surroundings |
| Temperature of the surroundings | **increases** (thermometer reading goes up) | **decreases** (thermometer reading goes down) |
| Energy of products compared with reactants | lower | higher |
| Overall energy change | negative | positive |
| Examples | combustion, many oxidation reactions, neutralisation (acid + alkali), metals with acids, displacement of metals | thermal decomposition (e.g. calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide), citric acid + sodium hydrogencarbonate |
| Everyday uses | **self-heating cans**, **hand warmers** | some **sports injury (instant cold) packs** |

You decide which type a reaction is **from the temperature change of the surroundings**: if the reading on the thermometer in the reaction mixture rises, the reaction is exothermic; if it falls, it is endothermic. At GCSE you only **measure** the temperature change here; you do not calculate energy changes from it.

### Evaluating uses

AQA asks you to **evaluate** uses and applications "given appropriate information". Pick out points from the data and weigh them up:

- **Self-heating cans**: a sealed compartment holds a reactant (for example calcium oxide) that reacts exothermically with water when a seal is pierced, heating the food or drink. *For*: hot food with no stove or electricity. *Against*: single use, adds bulk and cost, the heating chemicals must be disposed of, and the temperature reached and its duration are limited.
- **Hand warmers**: one type uses the slow exothermic oxidation of iron powder in air (single use, lasts hours); a reusable type uses crystallisation of a supersaturated salt solution, reset by boiling the pack. *Compare*: maximum temperature, how long the heat lasts, whether it can be reused, cost per use.
- **Cold packs**: an endothermic process (for example, ammonium nitrate dissolving in water) takes in energy from the injury, cooling it. *For*: no freezer needed, instant, portable. *Against*: single use only, and the chemicals may be harmful if the pack splits.

A good evaluation ends with a **judgement** that is supported by the information ("pack B is better for a long walk because it stays above 40 °C for longest, even though it costs more").

### Required practical 4 (Trilogy: required practical 10): temperature changes

**Required practical 4: investigate the variables that affect temperature changes in reacting solutions such as, eg acid plus metals, acid plus carbonates, neutralisations, displacement of metals.** In Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 this is **Required practical activity 10**.

A typical version (neutralisation):

1. Use a measuring cylinder to put 30 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid into a **polystyrene cup**, standing in a beaker so it does not tip over.
2. Record the starting temperature with a thermometer.
3. Add 5 cm³ of dilute sodium hydroxide solution, put a **lid** on, stir, and record the **highest temperature** reached.
4. Keep adding 5 cm³ portions, recording the highest temperature each time, up to about 40 cm³.
5. Repeat the whole experiment and calculate mean temperatures.
6. Plot **highest temperature (y-axis)** against **volume of sodium hydroxide added (x-axis)**. Draw two straight lines of best fit (one through the rising points, one through the falling points). Where they cross shows the volume of alkali that exactly neutralised the acid, and the maximum temperature.

**Why the temperature rises then falls:** while acid is still in excess, each portion of alkali reacts and releases more energy, so the temperature rises. Once all the acid has reacted, more (cooler) alkali does not react but still absorbs energy, so the mixture cools.

| Variable type | In this practical |
|---|---|
| Independent | volume of alkali added (other versions: type of metal, mass of metal powder, concentration of acid) |
| Dependent | temperature change (highest temperature minus starting temperature) |
| Control | volume and concentration of acid, starting temperature, same type of cup, lid on, stirring, same thermometer |

**Errors and improvements.** The main error is **energy lost to the surroundings**, which makes the temperature change too small. Reduce it with a polystyrene cup (a good insulator), a **lid**, and by reading the highest temperature promptly. A thermometer reading to 0.1 °C (digital) has better resolution than one reading to 1 °C. Repeating and calculating a mean improves repeatability; anomalies are left out of the mean.

**Risks.** Acids and alkalis are irritants (corrosive when concentrated): wear eye protection, wipe up spills. In acid + metal versions, hydrogen is flammable: no naked flames.

Other versions: add a measured mass of a powdered metal (magnesium, zinc, iron) to copper sulfate solution and compare the temperature rise: the **more reactive** the metal compared with copper, the **bigger** the rise. Use excess powder of the same particle size.

### Reaction profiles

Chemical reactions happen only when particles **collide with sufficient energy**. The minimum energy that particles must have to react is the **activation energy**. It is the energy needed to start breaking bonds, so it is the energy needed for a reaction to occur.

A **reaction profile** (energy level diagram) has **energy** on the y-axis and **progress of reaction** on the x-axis. It shows:

- the energy of the **reactants** (a horizontal line on the left) and of the **products** (a horizontal line on the right);
- a **curved line** that rises from the reactants to a peak and falls to the products;
- the **activation energy**: an arrow from the **reactants' level up to the peak**;
- the **overall energy change**: an arrow from the **reactants' level to the products' level**.

- **Exothermic:** products are **lower** than reactants; the overall change arrow points **down**.

- The activation energy is always measured **from the reactants**, not from the products and not from zero.

### Bond energies and the energy change (Higher tier)

During a chemical reaction:

- energy must be **supplied to break bonds** in the reactants (bond breaking is **endothermic**);
- energy is **released when bonds form** in the products (bond making is **exothermic**).

A **bond energy** is the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond (in kJ/mol); the same amount is released when one mole of that bond forms. The overall energy change is:

$$\text{energy change} = \text{energy to break bonds in reactants} - \text{energy released forming bonds in products}$$

- If **more energy is released forming bonds than is needed to break bonds**, the answer is **negative**: the reaction is **exothermic**.
- If **more energy is needed to break bonds than is released forming them**, the answer is **positive**: the reaction is **endothermic**.

**Worked mini-example: burning methane.** CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Bond energies (kJ/mol): C–H 412, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464.

1. Draw or list every bond. Reactants: 4 × C–H and 2 × O=O. Products: 2 × C=O (in O=C=O) and 4 × O–H (two in each H–O–H).
2. Bonds broken: $4 \times 412 + 2 \times 498 = 1648 + 996 = 2644$ kJ
3. Bonds formed: $2 \times 805 + 4 \times 464 = 1610 + 1856 = 3466$ kJ
4. Energy change $= 2644 - 3466 = -822$ kJ/mol. Negative, so **exothermic**.

**Worked mini-example: an endothermic one.** 2HI → H₂ + I₂. H–I 298, H–H 436, I–I 151.
Broken: $2 \times 298 = 596$ kJ. Formed: $436 + 151 = 587$ kJ. Change $= 596 - 587 = +9$ kJ/mol: **endothermic**.

Key skill points (MS 1a): count bonds using the **balancing numbers** (2O₂ means two O=O bonds); remember a **double bond** has its own bond energy (C=O is not two C–O bonds); bonds that appear unchanged on both sides can be counted on both sides (they cancel), and you still get the right answer.

::: terms
- **Exothermic reaction**: a reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings increases.
- **Endothermic reaction**: a reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings decreases.
- **Conservation of energy**: energy is not created or destroyed in a reaction; the total energy before and after is the same.
- **Activation energy**: the minimum amount of energy that colliding particles must have to react.
- **Reaction profile**: a diagram of energy against progress of reaction showing reactants, products, activation energy and overall energy change.
- **Bond energy**: the energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond, released again when that bond forms (kJ/mol).
- **Overall energy change**: energy needed to break the bonds in the reactants minus energy released forming bonds in the products.
- **Surroundings**: everything other than the reacting chemicals, such as the water, container, thermometer and air.
:::

::: method
**Command words.**
- **Describe** an observation: say what the thermometer does ("the temperature increases from 19 °C to 27 °C").
- **Explain** whether a reaction is exo- or endothermic: link the temperature change to the **direction of energy transfer** ("the temperature of the surroundings rose, so energy was transferred **to** the surroundings: exothermic").
- **Evaluate** a use (hand warmer, cold pack, self-heating can): give points **for** and **against** taken from the data (temperature reached, how long it lasts, cost, reusable or not, safety), then a **justified conclusion**.
- **Compare** profiles: say which has the higher activation energy and which releases more energy, quoting the diagram.

**Drawing a reaction profile (2–3 marks).** Label axes (energy; progress of reaction), put reactants and products at the right relative heights, draw a **curved** line through a peak, and draw the arrows with the **correct start and end**: activation energy from **reactants to peak**; overall energy change from **reactants to products**. Arrowheads matter.

**Bond energy calculations (Higher tier).** Same routine every time:
1. Write out (or sketch) every bond in every molecule, multiplied by the **balancing numbers**.
2. Total energy to **break** (reactants). Total energy **released** forming (products).
3. Energy change = broken − formed. **Keep the sign** and give units (kJ/mol).
4. State **exothermic** if negative, **endothermic** if positive, and explain: "more energy is released forming bonds than is needed to break bonds".
To find an **unknown bond energy**, set up the same expression with $x$ and solve.

**Required practical 4 (Trilogy 10) questions.** Know the independent, dependent and control variables; why a **polystyrene cup and lid** are used (insulation, reduces energy transfer to the surroundings); why you record the **highest** temperature; how to read the crossing point of two lines of best fit; and the main error (energy loss makes the temperature rise too small).

**6-mark extended response.** Usually "Plan an investigation into..." or "Explain, in terms of bonds, why..." Level 3 needs a **logical sequence** of steps that would produce valid results (equipment, measured quantities, what is kept the same, how the temperature change is found, repeats, how results are processed), or a full chain of reasoning linking bonds broken and made to the sign of the energy change.
:::

::: example Identifying the type of reaction
A student adds sodium hydrogencarbonate to citric acid solution. The temperature falls from 21.0 °C to 14.5 °C. Explain whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. (2)

The temperature of the surroundings (the solution) decreased (1), so energy was taken in from the surroundings: the reaction is **endothermic** (1).
:::

::: example Bond energy calculation (Higher tier)
Hydrogen burns in oxygen: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–H 436, O=O 498, O–H 464. Calculate the overall energy change and say whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. (4)

Bonds broken: $2 \times 436 + 498 = 1370$ kJ (1)
Bonds formed: 2 H₂O molecules each have 2 O–H bonds, so $4 \times 464 = 1856$ kJ (1)
Energy change $= 1370 - 1856 = $ **−486 kJ/mol** (1)
Negative, so **exothermic**: more energy is released making bonds than is used breaking bonds (1).
:::

::: example Finding an unknown bond energy (Higher tier)
H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. The overall energy change is −185 kJ/mol. H–H = 436 kJ/mol and H–Cl = 432 kJ/mol. Calculate the Cl–Cl bond energy. (3)

broken − formed = change: $(436 + x) - (2 \times 432) = -185$ (1)
$436 + x - 864 = -185$, so $x = -185 + 864 - 436$ (1)
$x = $ **243 kJ/mol** (1)
:::

::: example Reading a reaction profile
On a reaction profile the reactants are at 50 kJ, the peak is at 180 kJ and the products are at 120 kJ. (a) Find the activation energy. (b) Find the overall energy change and name the type of reaction. (3)

(a) Activation energy = peak − reactants $= 180 - 50 = $ **130 kJ** (1)
(b) Overall change = products − reactants $= 120 - 50 = $ **+70 kJ** (1); products are higher than reactants, so the reaction is **endothermic** (1).
:::

::: example Evaluating hand warmers
Warmer A (iron oxidation) reaches 52 °C, stays warm for 8 hours and is single use. Warmer B (crystallisation) reaches 54 °C, stays warm for 1 hour and can be reused about 100 times. Evaluate which is better for a day of outdoor work. (4)

A lasts 8 hours compared with only 1 hour for B (1). B is reusable, so cheaper per use and less waste (1). Both reach a similar maximum temperature, about 52–54 °C (1). For a full day outdoors, A is better because it keeps working for most of the day, while B would need re-boiling every hour, which is not possible outdoors (1).
:::

Define activation energy. (1)

The **minimum** energy that particles must have (when they collide) to react.

::: q
In Required practical 4 (Trilogy 10), why is the reaction done in a polystyrene cup with a lid? (2)
---
Polystyrene is a good thermal insulator and the lid stops energy escaping from the surface (1), so less energy is transferred to the surroundings and the measured temperature change is more accurate (1).
:::

::: q
On an exothermic reaction profile, where should the arrow for the overall energy change start and finish? (2)
---
It starts at the energy level of the **reactants** (1) and points **down** to the energy level of the **products** (1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) Explain, in terms of bonds, why a reaction is endothermic. (2)
---
The energy needed to **break** bonds in the reactants (1) is **greater than** the energy released when bonds **form** in the products (1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. Bond energies (kJ/mol): N≡N 945, H–H 436, N–H 391. Calculate the overall energy change. (3)
---
Broken: $945 + 3 \times 436 = 2253$ kJ (1). Formed: 2 NH₃ have 6 N–H, $6 \times 391 = 2346$ kJ (1). Change $= 2253 - 2346 = $ **−93 kJ/mol** (exothermic) (1).
:::

::: q
A student adds the same mass of zinc, iron and magnesium powder to copper sulfate solution. Predict which gives the largest temperature rise and explain why. (2)
---
**Magnesium** (1): it is the most reactive of the three, furthest above copper, so the displacement reaction releases the most energy (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Mixing up system and surroundings.** In an exothermic reaction the **surroundings** get hotter because energy leaves the chemicals. Saying "the reaction takes in heat so it gets hot" scores nothing.
- **Activation energy drawn from the wrong place.** It goes from the **reactants** up to the peak, not from the x-axis and not from the products.
- **Missing arrowheads or a straight line.** The profile must be curved and the arrows must point the right way.
- **(Higher tier) Not using balancing numbers.** 2O₂ is **two** O=O bonds; 2H₂O is **four** O–H bonds.
- **(Higher tier) Wrong subtraction or lost sign.** Always do broken − formed; a negative answer means exothermic. Write the sign and kJ/mol.
- **Treating C=O as two C–O bonds.** Double bonds have their own bond energy.
- **Saying bond breaking releases energy.** Breaking bonds always **needs** energy; making bonds **releases** it.
:::

::: test
Results: volume of NaOH added (cm³) and highest temperature (°C): 0, 19.0; 5, 22.1; 10, 25.0; 15, 27.9; 20, 30.0; 25, 29.3; 30, 28.4; 35, 27.6. Explain why the temperature rises and then falls, and suggest the volume that exactly neutralised the acid. (4)
---

After neutralisation is complete, extra alkali does not react; it is cooler and absorbs energy, and energy is lost to the surroundings, so the temperature falls (1). Neutralisation volume about **20 cm³** (accept 19–21 cm³ from where the two lines of best fit cross) (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) Hydrogen peroxide decomposes: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. The structure of H₂O₂ is H–O–O–H. Bond energies (kJ/mol): O–H 464, O–O 146, O=O 498. Calculate the energy change and state whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. (4)
---
Broken: each H₂O₂ has 2 O–H and 1 O–O, so $2 \times (2 \times 464 + 146) = 2148$ kJ (1). Formed: $4 \times 464 + 498 = 2354$ kJ (1). Change $= 2148 - 2354 = $ **−206 kJ/mol** (1). **Exothermic**, because the energy released forming bonds is greater than the energy needed to break bonds (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Propane burns: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Propane has 2 C–C bonds and 8 C–H bonds. Bond energies (kJ/mol): C–C 348, C–H 412, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464. Calculate the energy change for the reaction. (4)
---
Broken: $2 \times 348 + 8 \times 412 + 5 \times 498 = 696 + 3296 + 2490 = 6482$ kJ (1)(1). Formed: $6 \times 805 + 8 \times 464 = 4830 + 3712 = 8542$ kJ (1). Change $= 6482 - 8542 = $ **−2060 kJ/mol** (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A student says: "Bond energy calculations give a different answer from the value measured in an experiment, so the experiment must be wrong." Suggest two reasons why the two values differ. (2)
---
Any two: bond energies are **average** values taken from many different compounds, so they are not exact for these molecules (1); in an experiment energy is **lost to the surroundings** (heat loss), so the measured value is too small (1); the reaction may be incomplete or the substances may not be in the states (gases) the bond energies assume (1).
:::

::: test
Plan an investigation to find out which of three metals (magnesium, zinc and iron) gives the biggest temperature rise when added to copper sulfate solution. Include how you would make it a fair test and make the results accurate. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a coherent, logically ordered method that would produce valid results, with control variables, repeats and how the temperature change is worked out. **Level 2 (3–4):** a workable method with the main steps but some detail missing or out of order. **Level 1 (1–2):** some relevant points, e.g. add metal to copper sulfate and use a thermometer, without a workable sequence.

Conclusion: largest mean temperature rise shows the most reactive metal (expected order magnesium > zinc > iron).
