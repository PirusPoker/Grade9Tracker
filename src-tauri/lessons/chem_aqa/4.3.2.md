## What it is

Chemists cannot count atoms one by one, so they count them in huge, fixed-size bundles called **moles**. Moles let you turn a balanced equation into real masses: how much product a given mass of reactant makes, which reactant runs out first, and what the balancing numbers must be. This subsection is common to GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 5.3.2). **Everything except concentration in g/dm³ is Higher tier only**, and is flagged below.

### Moles and the Avogadro constant (Higher tier)

- Chemical amounts are measured in **moles**. The symbol for the unit mole is **mol**.
- **The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is numerically equal to its relative formula mass ($M_r$)** (or $A_r$ for an element made of atoms). One mole of carbon is 12 g; one mole of CO₂ is 44 g; one mole of water is 18 g.
- **One mole of any substance contains the same number of the stated particles** — atoms, molecules, ions, electrons or formula units — as one mole of any other substance.
- That number is the **Avogadro constant: $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ per mole**.

So the number of **atoms** in 1 mol of carbon (C) is the **same** as the number of **molecules** in 1 mol of carbon dioxide (CO₂): $6.02 \times 10^{23}$. But 1 mol of CO₂ contains **3 mol of atoms** ($3 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}$ atoms), because each molecule has three atoms. Always check **which particle** the question asks about.

**The key equation** (you must recall it and rearrange it — MS 3b):

$$\text{number of moles} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{M_r} \qquad \text{mass (g)} = \text{moles} \times M_r \qquad M_r = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{moles}}$$

**Number of particles** = moles × $6.02 \times 10^{23}$.

**Worked mini-examples**
- Moles in 9.0 g of water ($M_r$ = 18): $\frac{9.0}{18}$ = **0.50 mol**.
- Mass of 0.25 mol of calcium carbonate ($M_r$ = 100): 0.25 × 100 = **25 g**.
- Molecules in 9.0 g of water: 0.50 × $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ = **$3.01 \times 10^{23}$** molecules; atoms = 3 × that = **$9.03 \times 10^{23}$** atoms.
- Mass of one carbon atom: $\frac{12}{6.02 \times 10^{23}}$ = **$1.99 \times 10^{-23}$ g** (3 s.f.).
- $M_r$ of a substance if 0.20 mol has a mass of 8.8 g: $\frac{8.8}{0.20}$ = **44** (could be CO₂).

**Significant figures (MS 2a):** give answers to the same number of significant figures as the **least precise** data in the question (usually 2 or 3). Standard form (MS 1b) is needed for numbers of particles.

### Amounts of substances in equations (Higher tier)

A balanced equation tells you the **ratio of moles**. For example:

**Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂**

shows that **1 mole of magnesium reacts with 2 moles of hydrochloric acid to produce 1 mole of magnesium chloride and 1 mole of hydrogen gas**.

**Masses of substances shown in an equation:** multiply each $M_r$ by its balancing number.

| | Mg | 2HCl | MgCl₂ | H₂ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Moles | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Mass (g) | 24 | 2 × 36.5 = 73 | 95 | 2 |

Check: 24 + 73 = 97 = 95 + 2 ✓ (conservation of mass).

**Reacting-mass calculations — the three-step method**

1. **Moles of the known substance** = mass ÷ $M_r$.
2. **Use the ratio** from the balanced equation to find moles of the unknown.
3. **Mass of the unknown** = moles × $M_r$.

**Example:** what mass of magnesium oxide forms when 6.0 g of magnesium burns? 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO ($A_r$: Mg = 24, O = 16)
1. Moles Mg = 6.0 ÷ 24 = 0.25 mol
2. Ratio Mg : MgO = 2 : 2 = 1 : 1 → 0.25 mol MgO
3. Mass MgO = 0.25 × 40 = **10 g**

**Example:** what mass of iron can be made from 80 g of iron(III) oxide? Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ ($A_r$: Fe = 56, O = 16)
1. $M_r$ Fe₂O₃ = 160; moles = 80 ÷ 160 = 0.50 mol
2. Ratio Fe₂O₃ : Fe = 1 : 2 → 1.0 mol Fe
3. Mass Fe = 1.0 × 56 = **56 g**

### Using moles to balance equations (Higher tier)

If you know the **masses** of reactants and products, you can work out the **balancing numbers**:

1. Convert each mass to **moles** (mass ÷ $M_r$).
2. **Divide every value by the smallest** number of moles.
3. If any value is not a whole number, multiply all of them to get the **simplest whole-number ratio**.
4. Write these as the balancing numbers.

**Example:** 5.4 g of aluminium reacts with 21.3 g of chlorine, Cl₂, to form 26.7 g of aluminium chloride, AlCl₃. ($A_r$: Al = 27, Cl = 35.5)

| | Al | Cl₂ | AlCl₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass (g) | 5.4 | 21.3 | 26.7 |
| $M_r$ | 27 | 71 | 133.5 |
| Moles | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.20 |
| ÷ smallest (0.20) | 1 | 1.5 | 1 |
| × 2 | **2** | **3** | **2** |

**2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃** ✓ (Notice the masses also obey conservation of mass: 5.4 + 21.3 = 26.7.)

Rearranging equations (MS 3b, 3c) runs through all of this: if you are given moles and mass, rearrange to find $M_r$; if you know moles and $M_r$, find mass.

### Limiting reactants (Higher tier)

In a reaction with two reactants, it is common to use an **excess** of one reactant to make sure **all of the other reactant is used up**. The reactant that is **completely used up** is the **limiting reactant**, because it **limits the amount of products** that can form. The other reactant is **in excess** — some is left over.

- The amount of product depends **only on the limiting reactant**. Doubling the excess reactant makes **no more** product; doubling the limiting reactant (while the other is still in excess) **doubles** the product.

**How to find the limiting reactant**
1. Work out the **moles** of each reactant.
2. Use the equation ratio to see how many moles of the other reactant each would need.
3. The one that **runs out first** is limiting. Use **its** moles to calculate the product.

**Example:** 4.8 g of magnesium is added to 7.3 g of hydrogen chloride (as hydrochloric acid). Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ ($A_r$: Mg = 24, H = 1, Cl = 35.5)
- Moles Mg = 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.20 mol; moles HCl = 7.3 ÷ 36.5 = 0.20 mol
- 0.20 mol Mg would need 0.40 mol HCl, but only 0.20 mol is present → **HCl is limiting**; magnesium is in excess.
- Moles H₂ = 0.20 ÷ 2 = 0.10 mol → mass H₂ = 0.10 × 2 = **0.20 g**. (Mass of MgCl₂ = 0.10 × 95 = 9.5 g.)

**Example in masses:** iron and sulfur react, Fe + S → FeS. 5.6 g Fe (0.10 mol) is heated with 4.0 g S (0.125 mol). Iron is limiting (1 : 1 ratio and fewer moles), so 0.10 mol FeS = **8.8 g** forms, and 0.025 mol (0.80 g) of sulfur is left over.

### Concentration of solutions (both tiers)

Many reactions take place in **solutions**. Concentration can be measured as **mass per given volume of solution**, e.g. **grams per dm³ (g/dm³)**.

$$\text{concentration (g/dm}^3\text{)} = \frac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (dm}^3\text{)}}$$

**1 dm³ = 1000 cm³**, so to convert cm³ to dm³, **divide by 1000**.

**Worked mini-examples**
- 5.0 g of salt dissolved to make 250 cm³ of solution: 250 cm³ = 0.250 dm³; concentration = 5.0 ÷ 0.250 = **20 g/dm³**.
- Mass of solute in 50 cm³ of a 40 g/dm³ solution: mass = concentration × volume = 40 × 0.050 = **2.0 g**.

**How mass, volume and concentration are related (Higher tier):**
- If the **mass of solute is increased** in the same volume of solution, the **concentration increases** — concentration is **directly proportional** to mass (double the mass → double the concentration).
- If the **same mass** is dissolved in a **larger volume** of solution, the **concentration decreases** — concentration is **inversely proportional** to volume (double the volume → half the concentration).
- A more concentrated solution has **more solute particles in the same volume**.

**Separate science only:** concentrations in **mol/dm³** and titration calculations are in the next topic (4.3.4) and are not in Trilogy.

::: terms
- **Mole**: the unit for amount of substance (symbol mol); one mole has a mass in grams equal to the Mr of the substance.
- **Avogadro constant**: the number of particles in one mole of any substance, 6.02 x 10^23 per mole.
- **Molar ratio**: the ratio of moles of substances shown by the balancing numbers in an equation.
- **Limiting reactant**: the reactant that is completely used up in a reaction, so it limits the amount of product.
- **Excess**: describes a reactant present in more than the amount needed, so some is left over at the end.
- **Concentration**: the amount of solute in a given volume of solution, for example measured in grams per dm3.
- **Solute**: the substance that dissolves in a solvent to make a solution.
- **Cubic decimetre (dm3)**: a unit of volume equal to 1000 cm3 or one litre.
:::

::: method
**Recall and rearrange:** moles = mass ÷ $M_r$. Write the equation, substitute, answer with unit and sensible significant figures. Rearranging (mass = moles × $M_r$) is a common 1-mark step.

**Reacting masses (Higher tier) — the moles triangle route:** mass → **moles** (÷ $M_r$) → **ratio** from the equation → moles of the other substance → **mass** (× $M_r$). Lay it out in three labelled lines; examiners award a mark per line. A common short cut — scaling masses directly ("100 g CaCO₃ gives 44 g CO₂, so 25 g gives 11 g") — also earns full marks if clearly shown.

**Balancing from masses (Higher tier):** mass → moles for **every** substance → divide by the smallest → whole-number ratio → write the balanced equation. Show the table.

**Limiting reactant (Higher tier):** moles of **both** reactants → compare with the equation ratio → the one that runs out first is limiting → calculate product from the limiting reactant only. In "explain" questions, say "**the limiting reactant is completely used up**, so no more product can form; the other reactant is **in excess**".

**Avogadro questions:** number = moles × $6.02 \times 10^{23}$. Read the particle carefully — molecules or atoms? 1 mol H₂O = $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ molecules but $3 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}$ atoms. Give the answer in standard form to 3 s.f.

**Concentration (both tiers):** convert cm³ to dm³ (÷ 1000) **first**, then concentration = mass ÷ volume or mass = concentration × volume. HT "explain how concentration is related to mass and volume" → more mass in the same volume → higher concentration; same mass in more volume → lower concentration (more/fewer solute particles per dm³).

**Command words:** *calculate* (show working, unit), *explain* (use "limiting", "excess", "used up", "ratio"), *determine* (use the data given), *show that* (work to more significant figures than the value given, then compare).
:::

::: example Moles and particles (Higher tier)
Calculate (a) the number of moles in 11 g of carbon dioxide, (b) the number of CO₂ molecules, (c) the number of oxygen atoms. ($A_r$: C = 12, O = 16; Avogadro constant = $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ per mole) (4)

(a) $M_r$ = 44 (1); moles = 11 ÷ 44 = 0.25 mol (1). (b) 0.25 × $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ = $1.505 \times 10^{23}$ ≈ $1.51 \times 10^{23}$ molecules (1). (c) Two O atoms per molecule: 2 × $1.505 \times 10^{23}$ = $3.01 \times 10^{23}$ atoms (1).
:::

::: example Reacting mass (Higher tier)
Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced when 25 g of calcium carbonate is heated. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ ($A_r$: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16) (3)

Moles CaCO₃ = 25 ÷ 100 = 0.25 mol (1). Ratio 1 : 1, so 0.25 mol CO₂ (1). Mass CO₂ = 0.25 × 44 = **11 g** (1).
:::

::: example Balancing from masses (Higher tier)
9.2 g of sodium reacts with 3.2 g of oxygen, O₂, to form 12.4 g of sodium oxide, Na₂O. Use these masses to write the balanced equation. ($A_r$: Na = 23, O = 16) (4)

Moles: Na = 9.2 ÷ 23 = 0.40; O₂ = 3.2 ÷ 32 = 0.10; Na₂O = 12.4 ÷ 62 = 0.20 (2 marks for all three; 1 for two). Divide by 0.10: 4 : 1 : 2 (1). **4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O** (1).
:::

::: example Limiting reactant (Higher tier)
4.0 g of hydrogen is reacted with 16 g of oxygen. 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. (a) Which reactant is limiting? (b) Calculate the maximum mass of water. ($A_r$: H = 1, O = 16) (4)

(a) Moles H₂ = 4.0 ÷ 2 = 2.0 mol; moles O₂ = 16 ÷ 32 = 0.50 mol (1). 2.0 mol H₂ would need 1.0 mol O₂, but only 0.50 mol is present, so **oxygen is limiting** (1). (b) Moles H₂O = 2 × 0.50 = 1.0 mol (1). Mass = 1.0 × 18 = **18 g** (1).
:::

::: example Concentration (both tiers)
A student dissolves 12.5 g of copper sulfate in water and makes the solution up to 500 cm³. (a) Calculate the concentration in g/dm³. (b) What mass would be in 20 cm³ of this solution? (3)

(a) 500 cm³ = 0.500 dm³ (1); 12.5 ÷ 0.500 = **25 g/dm³** (1). (b) 25 × 0.020 = **0.50 g** (1).
:::

::: example Explaining concentration (Higher tier)
Solution A contains 10 g of solute in 200 cm³. Solution B contains 10 g of the same solute in 400 cm³. Explain which is more concentrated. (2)

Solution A: it has the same mass of solute in half the volume, so its concentration is twice as high (50 g/dm³ compared with 25 g/dm³) (1); there are more solute particles in each dm³ of A (1).
:::

(Higher tier) Calculate the number of moles in 20 g of sodium hydroxide, NaOH. ($A_r$: Na = 23, O = 16, H = 1) (2)

$M_r$ = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40 (1). Moles = 20 ÷ 40 = 0.50 mol (1).
:::

(Higher tier) Calculate the mass of 0.10 mol of sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄ ($M_r$ = 98). (1)
---
0.10 × 98 = 9.8 g.
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) How many atoms are in 2.0 mol of magnesium? (Avogadro constant = $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ per mole) (1)
---
2.0 × $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ = $1.20 \times 10^{24}$ atoms.
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) One mole of carbon and one mole of carbon dioxide have different masses. Explain why they contain the same number of particles. (2)
---
One mole of any substance contains the same number of particles, the Avogadro constant, $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ (1). The masses differ because each CO₂ molecule has a greater mass than a carbon atom ($M_r$ 44 vs $A_r$ 12), so one mole of CO₂ has mass 44 g and one mole of C has mass 12 g (1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) Calculate the mass of magnesium chloride formed when 2.4 g of magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ ($A_r$: Mg = 24, Cl = 35.5) (3)
---
Moles Mg = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.10 mol (1). Ratio 1 : 1 → 0.10 mol MgCl₂ (1). Mass = 0.10 × 95 = 9.5 g (1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) 3.2 g of methane, CH₄, reacts with 12.8 g of oxygen, O₂, to make 8.8 g of carbon dioxide and 7.2 g of water. Use the masses to write a balanced equation. ($A_r$: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16) (3)
---
Moles: CH₄ 0.20, O₂ 0.40, CO₂ 0.20, H₂O 0.40 (1). Ratio 1 : 2 : 1 : 2 (1). CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) Explain why a chemist uses an excess of one reactant. (2)
---
To make sure all of the other (limiting) reactant reacts / is used up (1), so the maximum amount of product is made from it (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the concentration in g/dm³ of a solution made by dissolving 8.0 g of solute in water to make 200 cm³ of solution. (2)
---
200 cm³ = 0.200 dm³ (1). 8.0 ÷ 0.200 = 40 g/dm³ (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the mass of solute in 25 cm³ of a solution of concentration 80 g/dm³. (2)
---
25 cm³ = 0.025 dm³ (1). 80 × 0.025 = 2.0 g (1).
:::

::: watch
- Forgetting to convert **cm³ to dm³** (÷ 1000) before using the concentration equation — answers come out 1000 times too big or small.
- Ignoring the **balancing numbers**: in 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, the ratio Mg : O₂ is **2 : 1**, not 1 : 1. Read the ratio from the equation every time.
- Multiplying $M_r$ by the balancing number **and** using the mole ratio — double-counting. Use one route only: moles then ratio.
- Working out the product from the reactant in **excess** instead of the **limiting** reactant.
- Confusing **molecules** and **atoms** in Avogadro questions: 1 mol H₂O has $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ molecules but three times as many atoms.
- Rounding too early — keep full values in the calculator and round only the final answer to 2 or 3 significant figures.
:::

Moles Al = 54 ÷ 27 = 2.0 mol (1). Ratio Al₂O₃ : Al = 2 : 4 = 1 : 2, so 1.0 mol Al₂O₃ (1). $M_r$ Al₂O₃ = 102, so mass = 1.0 × 102 = 102 g (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) 2.8 g of nitrogen, N₂, reacts with 0.60 g of hydrogen, H₂, to form 3.4 g of ammonia, NH₃. Use these masses to balance the equation. ($A_r$: N = 14, H = 1) (3)
---
Moles: N₂ = 2.8 ÷ 28 = 0.10; H₂ = 0.60 ÷ 2 = 0.30; NH₃ = 3.4 ÷ 17 = 0.20 (1). Divide by 0.10 → 1 : 3 : 2 (1). N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) Show that 1.0 kg of methane, CH₄, contains about $3.8 \times 10^{25}$ molecules. ($A_r$: C = 12, H = 1; Avogadro constant = $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ per mole) (3)
---
1.0 kg = 1000 g; $M_r$ = 16 (1). Moles = 1000 ÷ 16 = 62.5 mol (1). 62.5 × $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ = $3.76 \times 10^{25}$, which is about $3.8 \times 10^{25}$ (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) 7.95 g of copper oxide, CuO, is added to a solution containing 4.9 g of sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄. CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O. (a) Show which reactant is limiting. (b) Calculate the maximum mass of copper sulfate that can form, to 3 significant figures. (c) Explain why adding more copper oxide would not increase the mass of copper sulfate. ($A_r$: Cu = 63.5, O = 16, H = 1, S = 32) (5)
---
(a) Moles CuO = 7.95 ÷ 79.5 = 0.10 mol; moles H₂SO₄ = 4.9 ÷ 98 = 0.050 mol (1). Ratio 1 : 1, so sulfuric acid is used up first — it is limiting (1). (b) 0.050 mol CuSO₄ × 159.5 (1) = 7.98 g (1). (c) Copper oxide is already in excess; the amount of product is fixed by the limiting reactant (sulfuric acid), which is all used up (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A student adds different masses of magnesium ribbon to separate 50 cm³ portions of the same dilute hydrochloric acid. Each portion contains 0.010 mol of HCl. She measures the total volume of hydrogen produced each time. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. ($A_r$ Mg = 24.) Predict and explain how the volume of hydrogen changes as the mass of magnesium is increased from 0.02 g to 0.30 g. Include a calculation in your answer. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a correct prediction of the shape (rises in proportion, then levels off), clearly explained using limiting reactant / excess for both parts, with a correct calculation of the mass of magnesium at which the change happens. **Level 2 (3–4):** correct prediction with some explanation using limiting reactant ideas; calculation may be missing or contain an error. **Level 1 (1–2):** simple prediction (e.g. "volume goes up then stays the same") with little explanation.

Indicative content: 0.010 mol HCl reacts with 0.010 ÷ 2 = 0.0050 mol Mg; mass of Mg = 0.0050 × 24 = 0.12 g. Below 0.12 g, magnesium is the limiting reactant and the acid is in excess, so the volume of hydrogen is directly proportional to the mass of magnesium — doubling the mass doubles the volume. Above 0.12 g, hydrochloric acid is the limiting reactant and is all used up, magnesium is in excess (some ribbon is left over), so the volume of hydrogen stays constant at its maximum (0.0050 mol H₂). A graph of volume against mass is a straight line through the origin that levels off at 0.12 g. Adding more magnesium after this point cannot make more hydrogen because no acid is left to react.
:::
