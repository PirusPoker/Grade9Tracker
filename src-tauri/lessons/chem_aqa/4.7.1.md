## What it is

Crude oil is the main source of fuels and of the starting materials (**feedstock**) for making plastics and many other chemicals. This topic (AQA 4.7.1, carbon compounds as fuels and feedstock) is in both GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (numbered 5.7.1 there). One detail is **separate science only**: the names and formulae of individual alkenes (flagged below).

### Crude oil

- **Crude oil is a finite resource** found in rocks: it is being used up far faster than it forms, so it will run out.
- It is the remains of an **ancient biomass** consisting mainly of **plankton** that was buried in **mud** and changed over millions of years by heat and pressure.
- It is a **mixture** of a **very large number of compounds**. Most of them are **hydrocarbons**: molecules made up of **hydrogen and carbon atoms only**.

### Alkanes

Most of the hydrocarbons in crude oil are **alkanes**. The alkanes are a **homologous series**: a family of compounds with the same **general formula**, similar chemical properties, and a gradual trend in physical properties. Each member differs from the next by CH₂.

$$\text{General formula of alkanes: } \text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$$

Alkanes are **saturated**: every carbon–carbon bond is a **single** bond, so they contain as many hydrogen atoms as possible.

| Name | Molecular formula | Number of C atoms |
|---|---|---|
| methane | CH₄ | 1 |
| ethane | C₂H₆ | 2 |
| propane | C₃H₈ | 3 |
| butane | C₄H₁₀ | 4 |

**Recognising an alkane:** check the formula fits C$_n$H$_{2n+2}$ (hydrogens = 2 × carbons + 2) and that there are only single bonds. C₆H₁₄ is an alkane ($2 \times 6 + 2 = 14$); C₅H₁₀ is not ($2 \times 5 + 2 = 12$).

### Fractional distillation

The many hydrocarbons in crude oil are separated into **fractions** by **fractional distillation**. Each fraction contains molecules with a **similar number of carbon atoms**, so they have similar boiling points.

How it works, in terms of **evaporation and condensation**:

1. Crude oil is **heated** strongly so that most of it **evaporates** (turns into vapour).
2. The vapour enters the bottom of a **fractionating column**, which is **hot at the bottom and cooler towards the top** (a temperature gradient).
3. The vapours **rise** up the column and cool down.
4. Each hydrocarbon **condenses** (turns back to liquid) when it reaches a level in the column where the temperature is **below its boiling point**. The liquid is collected on trays at that level and piped off.
5. Hydrocarbons with **small molecules** have **low boiling points**, so they rise high and condense near the **top** (or leave as gases). Hydrocarbons with **large molecules** have **high boiling points**, so they condense near the **bottom**. The very largest do not evaporate and leave at the bottom as a residue.

| Position in column | Fraction (example) | Molecule size | Boiling point |
|---|---|---|---|
| top (coolest) | refinery gases, e.g. **liquefied petroleum gases (LPG)** | smallest (1–4 C) | lowest |
| | **petrol** | | |
| | **kerosene** (jet fuel) | | |
| | **diesel oil** | | |
| | **heavy fuel oil** | | |
| bottom (hottest) | bitumen (residue) | largest | highest |

You need to know these fuels exist (petrol, diesel oil, kerosene, heavy fuel oil, LPG); you do not need to learn the names of other fractions or exact temperatures.

The fractions can be processed to produce **fuels** and **feedstock for the petrochemical industry**. Many useful materials on which modern life depends come from the petrochemical industry: **solvents, lubricants, polymers, detergents**. The huge variety of natural and synthetic carbon compounds exists because **carbon atoms can form families of similar compounds** (chains and rings of different lengths, joined by strong C–C bonds).

### Properties of hydrocarbons

Some properties depend on the **size of the molecules**:

| As molecular size (chain length) increases... | Trend | Effect on use as a fuel |
|---|---|---|
| **boiling point** | increases | large molecules are liquids or solids at room temperature, harder to vaporise |
| **viscosity** (how thick it is; resistance to flow) | increases | large molecules flow less easily |
| **flammability** (how easily it ignites) | decreases | small molecules ignite easily and burn cleanly, so make better fuels |

Reason: larger molecules have stronger **intermolecular forces** between them, so more energy is needed to separate them. Short-chain hydrocarbons (petrol, LPG) are therefore the most useful fuels, and the most in demand.

### Combustion

The combustion of hydrocarbon fuels **releases energy** (it is exothermic). During combustion, the carbon and hydrogen in the fuel are **oxidised**. **Complete combustion** (plenty of oxygen) produces **carbon dioxide and water**:

$$\text{hydrocarbon} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water}$$

**Balancing method** (do C, then H, then O):

1. C atoms: one CO₂ for each carbon.
2. H atoms: one H₂O for every two hydrogens.
3. O atoms: count O in the products and divide by 2 to get O₂. If you get a half, **double everything**.

| Alkane | Balanced equation for complete combustion |
|---|---|
| methane | CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O |
| propane | C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O |
| butane | 2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O |
| octane | 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O |

Worked: butane C₄H₁₀ gives 4CO₂ and 5H₂O; oxygen atoms $= 8 + 5 = 13$, so 6½ O₂; double: 2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O.

(With too little oxygen, incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide and soot; this is covered in topic 4.9.3.)

### Cracking

**Cracking** breaks down large hydrocarbon molecules into **smaller, more useful** molecules. It is a **thermal decomposition** reaction. There are two main methods; you need the conditions in general terms:

| Method | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Catalytic cracking** | the long-chain hydrocarbon is **heated to vaporise** it and the vapour is passed over a **hot catalyst** (powdered aluminium oxide or zeolite, about 550 °C) |
| **Steam cracking** | the hydrocarbon is **vaporised**, **mixed with steam** and heated to a **very high temperature** (about 800–900 °C), without a catalyst |

The products of cracking include **alkanes** and another type of hydrocarbon called **alkenes**. Alkenes contain a **carbon–carbon double bond** (C=C), so they are **unsaturated**.

**Balancing cracking equations:** the total numbers of C and H atoms on the left must equal the totals on the right. Example:

$$\text{C}_{10}\text{H}_{22} \rightarrow \text{C}_8\text{H}_{18} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_4$$

Check: C: $10 = 8 + 2$; H: $22 = 18 + 4$. To find a missing product, subtract: C₁₁H₂₄ → C₇H₁₆ + ? gives C₄H₈.

**Testing for alkenes with bromine water.** Alkenes are **more reactive** than alkanes and react with **bromine water**: shake the hydrocarbon with orange bromine water. An **alkene** turns it from **orange to colourless**. An alkane leaves it **orange** (no change).

**Why cracking is useful:**
- There is a **high demand for fuels with small molecules** (petrol, LPG), more than fractional distillation supplies, but too much of the long-chain fractions. Cracking converts the surplus long chains into petrol-sized molecules, matching **supply to demand**.
- **Alkenes** are used to **produce polymers** (such as poly(ethene)) and as **starting materials** for many other chemicals.

Modern life depends on hydrocarbons: fuels for transport, heating and electricity generation; plastics; solvents; lubricants; detergents; medicines.

**Separate science only:** GCSE Chemistry 8462 students should know that the first alkenes are **ethene, C₂H₄**, and **propene, C₃H₆** (the full alkene topic is 4.7.2). Combined Science: Trilogy students do **not** need to know the formulae or names of individual alkenes; in a Trilogy question the formulae will be given.

::: terms
- **Crude oil**: a finite resource found in rocks, formed from ancient plankton buried in mud; a mixture of very many compounds.
- **Hydrocarbon**: a compound made of molecules containing hydrogen and carbon atoms only.
- **Alkane**: a saturated hydrocarbon with only single C–C bonds and the general formula CnH2n+2.
- **Homologous series**: a family of compounds with the same general formula and similar chemical properties.
- **Fraction**: a mixture of hydrocarbons with a similar number of carbon atoms and similar boiling points.
- **Fractional distillation**: separating crude oil into fractions by evaporation and then condensation at different heights of a column.
- **Viscosity**: how thick a liquid is, or how much it resists flowing.
- **Cracking**: thermal decomposition of large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more useful alkanes and alkenes.
- **Alkene**: an unsaturated hydrocarbon containing a carbon–carbon double bond, which decolourises bromine water.
:::

::: method
**Command words.**
- **Describe** how fractional distillation works: heat → evaporate → vapour rises → column cooler at the top → each hydrocarbon **condenses** at the level where the temperature falls below its **boiling point** → fractions collected. Use **evaporate** and **condense**, as the specification says.
- **Explain** a trend in properties: link **molecule size** to **intermolecular forces** to the property ("longer chains have stronger intermolecular forces, so more energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point is higher").
- **Give / state** the conditions for cracking: catalytic = **vaporise, hot catalyst**; steam = **vaporise, mix with steam, very high temperature**.
- **Explain why cracking is needed**: use **supply and demand** data; say that the short-chain fractions are in higher demand than supply, and the long-chain fractions in surplus; and that alkenes make **polymers**.
- **Evaluate** a fuel from data: compare boiling point (state at room temperature), flammability, viscosity, energy released, products.

**Equations.** For complete combustion, balance C, then H, then O; double everything if you need half an O₂. For cracking, check C and H totals are equal on both sides; to find a missing product, subtract the formulae.

**Practical-style questions.** The bromine water test: state the reagent, the starting colour (**orange**) and the result for an alkene (**colourless**). For comparing flammability or viscosity of hydrocarbons, control the volume and temperature of each sample, and note fire risks (use small volumes, no naked flame near the bottles, eye protection).

**6-mark extended response.** Usually "Describe how crude oil is separated and how the fractions are made more useful" or "Explain why cracking is carried out". Level 3 needs a **logical sequence** (heating, evaporation, temperature gradient, condensing at boiling points, fractions with similar chain lengths) linked to **why** (molecule size → boiling point → where it condenses), and for cracking a link to **supply and demand** and **alkenes for polymers**.
:::

::: example Recognising alkanes
Which of these are alkanes: C₇H₁₆, C₆H₁₂, C₁₂H₂₆, C₄H₈? Explain. (2)

Alkanes fit CnH2n+2 (H = 2C + 2). C₇H₁₆ ($2 \times 7 + 2 = 16$) and C₁₂H₂₆ ($2 \times 12 + 2 = 26$) are alkanes (1). C₆H₁₂ and C₄H₈ have two fewer hydrogens than an alkane would, so they are not alkanes (they are alkenes) (1).
:::

::: example Balancing a combustion equation
Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of hexane, C₆H₁₄. (2)

C₆H₁₄ gives 6CO₂ and 7H₂O; oxygen atoms $= 12 + 7 = 19$, so 9½ O₂ (1). Double: **2C₆H₁₄ + 19O₂ → 12CO₂ + 14H₂O** (1).
:::

::: example Cracking equation
Complete the cracking equation: C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄ + ? (1)

Carbon: $16 - 8 - 2 = 6$; hydrogen: $34 - 18 - 4 = 12$. Missing product **C₆H₁₂**.
:::

::: example Explaining the column
Explain why petrol is collected higher up the fractionating column than diesel oil. (3)

Petrol molecules are **smaller** (shorter chains) than diesel molecules, with weaker intermolecular forces, so petrol has a **lower boiling point** (1). The column is **cooler higher up** (1). Petrol vapour rises further before the temperature falls below its boiling point and it **condenses** (1).
:::

::: example Supply and demand
Data (percentage of crude oil): LPG supply 4%, demand 10%; petrol supply 12%, demand 26%; heavy fuel oil supply 34%, demand 12%. Use the data to explain why oil companies crack heavy fuel oil. (3)

The supply of heavy fuel oil (34%) is much greater than demand (12%), so there is a surplus (1). Petrol and LPG demand is higher than supply (26% vs 12%; 10% vs 4%) (1). Cracking converts the surplus long-chain molecules into smaller, more useful molecules such as petrol, plus alkenes for making polymers (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how crude oil is separated into fractions by fractional distillation, and explain why the fractions contain molecules of different sizes. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear, logically sequenced description of the process, using evaporation and condensation, linked to an explanation in terms of molecule size and boiling point. **Level 2 (3–4):** most steps described, with some link to boiling point. **Level 1 (1–2):** basic statements, e.g. oil is heated and fractions are collected.

Indicative content: crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons with different chain lengths; it is heated so most of it **evaporates**; the vapour enters near the bottom of a fractionating column; the column has a temperature gradient, **hot at the bottom and cooler at the top**; vapours rise and cool; each hydrocarbon **condenses** when it reaches a level where the temperature is below its boiling point; the liquid is collected on trays/taken off at that height; larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces and higher boiling points, so condense low down (e.g. heavy fuel oil, diesel), and the largest leave as a residue (bitumen); smaller molecules have lower boiling points, so rise higher before condensing (petrol, kerosene), and the smallest leave the top as gases (LPG); so each fraction contains molecules with a similar number of carbon atoms and similar boiling points.
