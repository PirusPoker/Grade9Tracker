## What it is

This topic (AQA 4.8.2) is in both GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464, where it is numbered **5.8.2**. There is no Higher-tier-only content. You must know **four gas tests**: for **hydrogen**, **oxygen**, **carbon dioxide** and **chlorine**. They are short, but they turn up all over both papers: in electrolysis, rates, acids and the ion tests in 4.8.3.

Each test relies on a property that only that gas shows: a **sound**, a **relit splint**, a **precipitate** or **bleaching**.

### The four tests

| Gas | Test | Positive result | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydrogen**, H₂ | hold a **burning (lit) splint** at the **open end** of a test tube of the gas | burns rapidly with a **(squeaky) pop** | hydrogen reacts explosively with oxygen in the air: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O |
| **Oxygen**, O₂ | insert a **glowing splint** into a test tube of the gas | the splint **relights** | oxygen supports combustion; there is far more of it than in air (about 21% O₂) |
| **Carbon dioxide**, CO₂ | **shake** the gas with, or **bubble** it through, **limewater** (calcium hydroxide solution) | limewater turns **milky (cloudy)** | CO₂ reacts to form insoluble calcium carbonate, a white precipitate: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O |
| **Chlorine**, Cl₂ | put **damp litmus paper** into the gas | the litmus paper is **bleached** and turns **white** | chlorine dissolves in the water on the paper and forms a bleaching agent |

### Details that earn the marks

**Hydrogen.** The splint is **burning** (has a flame), not glowing. Hold it at the **mouth** of the tube; you do not push it deep inside. The pop is a small explosion as hydrogen and oxygen from the air react. Common sources: a metal reacting with an acid (magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen), a reactive metal with water, and the cathode in electrolysis of many solutions.

**Oxygen.** The splint is **glowing** (blown out so it is just smouldering, with no flame). It **relights** (bursts into flame) in oxygen. "Glowing splint glows brighter" is not enough; say it **relights**. Sources: decomposition of hydrogen peroxide with a catalyst (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂), the anode in electrolysis of some solutions (such as sodium sulfate or dilute sulfuric acid), photosynthesis.

**Carbon dioxide.** Limewater is an aqueous solution of **calcium hydroxide**. The gas must be **bubbled through** or **shaken with** it. The milkiness is a fine white **precipitate of calcium carbonate**, which is insoluble. A burning splint going out is **not** a test for CO₂ (many gases put out a flame, such as nitrogen). Sources: an acid reacting with a carbonate (4.8.3 uses this to identify carbonate ions), combustion of carbon fuels, respiration, fermentation, thermal decomposition of carbonates.

**Chlorine.** The litmus paper must be **damp**: dry chlorine does not bleach dry paper, because chlorine has to dissolve in water to form the acidic bleaching substances. With **blue** litmus you may see it turn **red first** (the solution is acidic) and then **white** (bleached). The mark is for **bleached / turns white**. Chlorine is a **toxic** pale green gas with a sharp smell, so this test is done in a **fume cupboard**. Source: the anode in electrolysis of a solution containing chloride ions, such as brine (sodium chloride solution) or hydrochloric acid.

### Collecting gases for testing

| Method | Use for | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Over water (upturned test tube full of water) | hydrogen, oxygen | they are only slightly soluble in water |
| Delivery tube into limewater | carbon dioxide | the gas goes straight into the test reagent |
| Upward delivery (gas syringe or test tube upside down) | hydrogen | it is less dense than air, so it stays in an upside-down tube |
| Fume cupboard, downward delivery | chlorine | toxic; denser than air |

A gas syringe collects any of them and also measures volume, as in the rates practical.

### Using the tests to identify a gas

Run through the tests in a logical order, changing only one thing at a time:

1. Is it pale green with a sharp smell, and does it bleach damp litmus? → **chlorine**.
2. Does it turn limewater milky? → **carbon dioxide**.
3. Does a glowing splint relight? → **oxygen**.
4. Does a lit splint give a pop? → **hydrogen**.

In electrolysis questions, these tests identify the products at the electrodes: hydrogen at the cathode (when the metal is more reactive than hydrogen), and oxygen or chlorine (or another halogen) at the anode.

### Risks

- **Hydrogen** is flammable and forms explosive mixtures with air: test only small volumes (a test tube), keep away from other flames.
- **Chlorine** is toxic: small amounts only, in a **fume cupboard**.
- **Oxygen** makes things burn much more fiercely: keep the tube away from clothing and other flammable materials.
- **Limewater** (calcium hydroxide) is an irritant: wear **eye protection**.

::: terms
- **Burning splint**: a wooden splint with a flame, held at the open end of a test tube to test for hydrogen.
- **Glowing splint**: a splint that has been lit and blown out so it smoulders without a flame, used to test for oxygen.
- **Limewater**: an aqueous solution of calcium hydroxide that turns milky when carbon dioxide is bubbled through it.
- **Precipitate**: an insoluble solid formed when two solutions react, such as calcium carbonate in limewater.
- **Bleaching**: removing colour; chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper so it turns white.
- **Damp litmus paper**: indicator paper moistened with water so that a gas such as chlorine can dissolve on it.
:::

::: method
**Every gas-test answer needs two parts: the test and the result.** One mark is for the **test** (what you use and how), one is for the **result** (what you see). Write them as a pair:

- Hydrogen: **lit/burning splint** at the mouth of the tube → **squeaky pop**.
- Oxygen: **glowing splint** inserted → **relights**.
- Carbon dioxide: **bubble through limewater** → **turns milky/cloudy**.
- Chlorine: **damp litmus paper** → **bleached/turns white**.

**Command words.**
- **Describe** a test: give what you do and what you observe. "Use limewater" alone is half the answer.
- **Identify** a gas from results: name it and quote the result that shows it.
- **Explain** a result: give the chemistry behind it (hydrogen reacts with oxygen explosively; CO₂ forms insoluble calcium carbonate; chlorine dissolves in the water on damp paper and bleaches it).
- **Suggest** a safety precaution: match it to the hazard (fume cupboard for chlorine because it is toxic; small volumes of hydrogen because it is flammable).

**Precise words matter.** Use **burning** for hydrogen and **glowing** for oxygen; write **relights**, not "glows brighter"; write **milky** or **cloudy**, not "changes colour"; write **damp**, not just "litmus paper"; write **bleached** or **white**, not "turns red".

**Linking questions.** Gas tests are often the final mark in electrolysis, rates or acid questions: "Describe a test for the gas produced at the anode" in brine electrolysis means the chlorine test; "Identify the gas made when zinc reacts with sulfuric acid" means hydrogen with its test.
:::

::: example Describing a test
Describe the test for oxygen and give the result. (2)

Put a **glowing splint** into a test tube of the gas (1). The splint **relights** (1).
:::

::: example Identifying gases from electrolysis
Concentrated sodium chloride solution is electrolysed. Name the gases at each electrode and describe a test for each. (4)

Cathode: **hydrogen** (1); a **lit splint** at the mouth of the tube gives a **squeaky pop** (1). Anode: **chlorine** (1); **damp litmus paper** is **bleached / turns white** (1).
:::

::: example Explaining the limewater test
Explain why limewater turns milky when carbon dioxide is bubbled through it. Include a word equation. (3)

Carbon dioxide reacts with calcium hydroxide in the limewater (1) to form **calcium carbonate**, which is **insoluble**, so it forms a white **precipitate** that makes the solution look milky (1). Calcium hydroxide + carbon dioxide → calcium carbonate + water (1).
:::

::: example Choosing a test from unknowns
Three test tubes contain hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide, but the labels are missing. Describe how to identify each gas using a splint and limewater. (3)

Bubble some of each gas through **limewater**: the one that turns it **milky** is **carbon dioxide** (1). Hold a **burning splint** at the mouth of each remaining tube: a **pop** shows **hydrogen** (1). Put a **glowing splint** into the last tube: it **relights**, so it is **oxygen** (1).
:::

::: q
A student tests a gas with a burning splint and the splint goes out. The student concludes the gas is carbon dioxide. Explain why this is not a valid conclusion. (2)
---
Many gases (e.g. nitrogen) put out a burning splint, so it is not a test specific to CO₂ (1). The student should bubble the gas through **limewater**; only CO₂ turns it milky (1).

::: watch
- **Burning versus glowing.** Hydrogen: **burning** splint, **pop**. Oxygen: **glowing** splint, **relights**. Swapping these loses both marks.
- **"Glows brighter" or "burns more"** for oxygen does not score. Write **relights**.
- **Missing "damp"** in the chlorine test, or saying the litmus "turns red". The mark is for **bleached / white**.
- **"Limewater changes colour"** or "turns clear". It turns **milky** or **cloudy**.
- **Giving the test without the result** (or the result without the test). Each test is usually 2 marks: one for each.
- **A splint going out is not a test for carbon dioxide.**
:::

::: test
Dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed using inert electrodes. A gas at the cathode pops with a lit splint. The gas at the anode relights a glowing splint. Identify both gases and explain how the test results show this. (4)
---
Cathode: **hydrogen** (1), because only hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop (1). Anode: **oxygen** (1), because oxygen supports combustion strongly enough to relight a glowing splint (1).
:::

::: test
A student has four unlabelled test tubes, each containing a different gas: hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide or chlorine. Explain the order you would test them in, starting with the safest tests, and why chlorine should be identified first. (3)
---
Identify chlorine first using **damp litmus paper** (bleached) in a fume cupboard, because it is **toxic** and should not be released (1); also its pale green colour may already show it. Then test the rest with **limewater** (CO₂ goes milky) (1). Then use a glowing splint (oxygen relights) and a burning splint (hydrogen pops); hydrogen with a lit splint is a small explosion, so use small volumes (1).
:::

::: test
Plan how to show that the gas made when a carbonate reacts with an acid is carbon dioxide, and explain the chemistry of the positive result. Include safety. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear, logically ordered method using limewater correctly, a full explanation of the result with the reaction forming insoluble calcium carbonate, and relevant safety linked to hazards. **Level 2 (3–4):** a workable method with the correct test and result; explanation or safety incomplete. **Level 1 (1–2):** names limewater or the result, but no workable method.

Indicative content: put a carbonate (e.g. marble chips, calcium carbonate) in a test tube or conical flask; add dilute hydrochloric acid; fit a bung with a delivery tube; bubble the gas through limewater in a second test tube (or shake collected gas with limewater); positive result: limewater turns milky/cloudy; chemistry: CO₂ reacts with calcium hydroxide; calcium carbonate forms; it is insoluble so it forms a white precipitate; Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O; a burning splint going out is not enough because other gases do that too; safety: eye protection (acid and limewater are irritants); remove the delivery tube from the limewater before stopping (avoid suck-back); do not seal the apparatus so pressure cannot build up.
:::
