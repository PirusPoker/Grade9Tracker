## What it is

This subsection is in **both** GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 5.4.3). Writing and balancing **half equations** at the electrodes is **Higher tier**.

### The process of electrolysis

An ionic compound is made of positive and negative ions held in a giant lattice. As a **solid** the ions cannot move, so it does not conduct. When it is **melted** or **dissolved in water**, the ions are **free to move** about within the liquid or solution. These liquids and solutions conduct electricity and are called **electrolytes**.

Passing a direct current through an electrolyte makes the ions move to the electrodes:

| Electrode | Charge | Attracts | What happens there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **cathode** | **negative** | **positive ions** (cations, e.g. Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺, H⁺) | ions **gain electrons** → **reduction** |
| **anode** | **positive** | **negative ions** (anions, e.g. Br⁻, Cl⁻, OH⁻) | ions **lose electrons** → **oxidation** |

The ions are **discharged** at the electrodes, producing **elements**. Breaking down a compound using electricity in this way is **electrolysis**. Memory aids: **PANIC** — **P**ositive **A**node, **N**egative **I**s **C**athode; and **OIL RIG** with **AN OX** (anode oxidation) and **RED CAT** (reduction at cathode).

The electrodes are usually **inert** (unreactive) — **graphite** or **platinum** — so they carry the current without reacting.

### Electrolysis of molten ionic compounds

When a simple (binary) ionic compound is electrolysed **molten** with inert electrodes:
- the **metal** is produced at the **cathode** (negative);
- the **non-metal** is produced at the **anode** (positive).

**Lead bromide, PbBr₂:** lead ions, Pb²⁺, move to the cathode and become **molten lead** (silvery liquid collecting at the bottom); bromide ions, Br⁻, move to the anode and become **bromine** (**brown/orange vapour**).

| Molten compound | At the cathode (−) | At the anode (+) |
|---|---|---|
| lead bromide, PbBr₂ | lead | bromine |
| zinc chloride, ZnCl₂ | zinc | chlorine |
| sodium chloride, NaCl | sodium | chlorine |
| potassium iodide, KI | potassium | iodine |
| aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃ | aluminium | oxygen |

Bromine and lead vapour are toxic, so lead bromide is only electrolysed in a **fume cupboard**. **Anhydrous zinc chloride** is a **safer alternative** for practical work.

### Using electrolysis to extract metals

Electrolysis is used to extract a metal if it is **too reactive to be extracted by reduction with carbon** (it is above carbon in the reactivity series) **or if the metal reacts with carbon**. It is **expensive**: **large amounts of energy** are needed to **melt** the compound and to produce the **electric current**.

**Aluminium** is made by electrolysing a molten mixture of **aluminium oxide and cryolite**, using **carbon (graphite)** electrodes:

- **Why a mixture?** Aluminium oxide has a very high melting point (over 2000 °C). Dissolving it in molten **cryolite** gives an electrolyte that is liquid at a much **lower temperature** (about 950 °C), so **less energy** is needed, which **reduces costs**.
- At the **cathode**: aluminium ions gain electrons and **molten aluminium** forms, collecting at the bottom of the cell.
- At the **anode**: oxide ions lose electrons and **oxygen** forms.
- **Why must the positive electrode be continually replaced?** The anode is made of **carbon**. At the high temperature, the **oxygen** produced **reacts with the carbon** to form **carbon dioxide**: C + O₂ → CO₂. The anode gradually **burns away**, so it must be replaced regularly.

Overall: $2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \rightarrow 4\text{Al} + 3\text{O}_2$.

### Electrolysis of aqueous solutions

In an **aqueous** solution there are **two** sources of ions: the dissolved compound **and the water**. A small number of water molecules break down into **hydrogen ions** and **hydroxide ions**:

$$\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^-$$

So each electrode has a "choice". Which ion is discharged depends on the **relative reactivity** of the elements:

**At the cathode (negative):**
- **hydrogen** is produced if the metal is **more reactive than hydrogen** (e.g. Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺… stay in solution);
- the **metal** is produced if it is **less reactive than hydrogen** (e.g. copper, silver).

**At the anode (positive):**
- **oxygen** is produced (from OH⁻) **unless** the solution contains **halide ions** (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻), in which case the **halogen** is produced.
- Sulfate, nitrate and carbonate ions are not discharged — oxygen forms instead.

| Aqueous solution (inert electrodes) | Cathode (−) | Anode (+) | Left in solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| copper(II) chloride, CuCl₂ | **copper** | **chlorine** | (water) |
| copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄ | **copper** | **oxygen** | sulfuric acid |
| sodium chloride, NaCl | **hydrogen** | **chlorine** | sodium hydroxide |
| sodium sulfate, Na₂SO₄ | **hydrogen** | **oxygen** | sodium sulfate (water is electrolysed) |
| potassium bromide, KBr | **hydrogen** | **bromine** | potassium hydroxide |
| dilute sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄ | **hydrogen** | **oxygen** | sulfuric acid (more concentrated) |
| silver nitrate, AgNO₃ | **silver** | **oxygen** | nitric acid |

Shortcut: look at the **metal** (above or below hydrogen in the reactivity series) to decide the cathode product, and look for a **halide** to decide the anode product.

**Identifying the products:**

| Product | Observation / test |
|---|---|
| hydrogen | bubbles at the cathode; lit splint → **squeaky pop** |
| oxygen | bubbles at the anode; **relights a glowing splint** |
| chlorine | pale green gas, choking smell; **bleaches damp blue litmus paper** (turns red then white) |
| bromine | orange/brown colour around the anode |
| iodine | brown colour around the anode |
| copper | **pink-brown coating** on the cathode; blue colour of Cu²⁺ fades |

### Required practical 3 (Trilogy: Required practical 9): investigate what happens when aqueous solutions are electrolysed using inert electrodes

This should be an **investigation involving developing a hypothesis** — for example: *"If the metal in the salt is less reactive than hydrogen, the metal will be deposited at the cathode; otherwise hydrogen will be given off."* or *"If the solution contains halide ions, a halogen will form at the anode."*

**Method (e.g. copper(II) chloride, then copper(II) sulfate, sodium chloride and so on):**

1. Pour about 50 cm³ of the solution into a small beaker.
2. Place two **inert graphite electrodes** in the solution, held apart by a lid or clamp so they do not touch.
3. Connect them to a **low-voltage DC** power supply (about 4–6 V).
4. For gases, place small test tubes **filled with the solution** upside down over each electrode to collect the gas.
5. Switch on and **observe** each electrode for a few minutes: bubbles, coatings, colour changes.
6. **Test** any gases (glowing splint, lit splint, damp litmus paper) and record products at each electrode.
7. Clean or replace the electrodes and repeat with the next solution.

| Variable | In this practical |
|---|---|
| **independent** | the solution (the ions it contains) |
| **dependent** | the product at each electrode |
| **control** | voltage, electrode material (inert graphite), concentration and volume of solution, time |

**Risks:** **chlorine is toxic** — use low voltage, run for a short time only, keep the room well ventilated (or use a fume cupboard), and switch off once the gas is identified; wear **eye protection**; copper salts are harmful; do not touch the electrodes while the current is on; keep water away from the mains plug of the power pack.

**Typical results:** CuCl₂ — pink copper on the cathode, bubbles of chlorine (bleaches litmus) at the anode. CuSO₄ — copper at the cathode, oxygen at the anode. NaCl — hydrogen at the cathode, chlorine at the anode. Na₂SO₄ — hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode, roughly **2 : 1 by volume**.

### Half equations (Higher tier)

At the **cathode**, positive ions **gain electrons** — **reduction**. At the **anode**, negative ions **lose electrons** — **oxidation**. You must be able to write half equations and to **complete and balance** supplied ones. Electrons are written as $e^-$; the **charges must balance** on each side.

| Electrode | Half equation | Type |
|---|---|---|
| cathode | Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb | reduction |
| cathode | Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu | reduction |
| cathode | Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al | reduction |
| cathode | Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (molten NaCl) | reduction |
| cathode | 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | reduction |
| anode | 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ | oxidation |
| anode | 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ | oxidation |
| anode | 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (molten Al₂O₃) | oxidation |
| anode | 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ | oxidation |

The anode reactions can also be written with electrons **subtracted** on the left, for example $4\text{OH}^- - 4e^- \rightarrow \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or $2\text{Cl}^- - 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2$. Both forms are accepted by AQA.

**Balancing a half equation:** 1) balance the atoms (2Cl⁻ for Cl₂); 2) add electrons to the side that is more positive until the total charges are equal. For 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O: left charge = 4−, right = 0, so add **4e⁻** on the right.

::: terms
- **Electrolysis**: the breaking down of an ionic compound, molten or in solution, using an electric current.
- **Electrolyte**: a molten ionic compound or a solution of ions that conducts electricity because its ions can move.
- **Cathode**: the negative electrode, where positive ions gain electrons and are reduced.
- **Anode**: the positive electrode, where negative ions lose electrons and are oxidised.
- **Inert electrode**: an electrode, usually graphite or platinum, that conducts but does not react with the electrolyte or products.
- **Discharged**: describes an ion that gains or loses electrons at an electrode and becomes an uncharged atom or molecule.
- **Cryolite**: an aluminium compound used to dissolve aluminium oxide, lowering the operating temperature of the electrolysis.
- **Half equation**: an equation showing the electrons gained or lost by one species at a single electrode.
:::

::: method
**Predict the products** — say which electrode, and give a reason:
- Molten binary compound: **metal at the cathode**, **non-metal at the anode**.
- Aqueous solution: cathode — **metal if less reactive than hydrogen, otherwise hydrogen**; anode — **halogen if halide ions present, otherwise oxygen**.
State the ion that moves there: "Cu²⁺ ions are attracted to the negative cathode."

**Explain why electrolytes conduct:** the ions are **free to move** when molten or dissolved; in the solid they are fixed in a lattice. Do not say "electrons move through the solution" — it is the **ions** that move.

**Aluminium questions** — two classic 2-markers:
- "Why is cryolite used?" → lowers the **melting point / operating temperature**, so **less energy** is needed and costs are lower.
- "Why must the anode be replaced?" → **oxygen** forms at the anode and **reacts with the carbon** anode to make **carbon dioxide**, so the anode **wears away**.

**Required practical 3 (Trilogy RP 9) questions:** state a **hypothesis** using the reactivity rules; identify independent, dependent and control variables; give the **gas tests**; give risks (chlorine is toxic — low voltage, short time, ventilation). Describe observations, e.g. "bubbles at the anode", "pink-brown solid on the cathode".

**Half equations (Higher tier):** balance the **atoms**, then add **electrons** so the charges balance. Electrons are on the **left** at the cathode (gained) and on the **right** at the anode (lost). Then say **oxidation** or **reduction** with a reason in terms of electrons.

**Extended responses (6 marks):** e.g. "Describe and explain what happens when copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed." Cover: ions present (Cu²⁺, H⁺, SO₄²⁻, OH⁻), where each moves, which are discharged and why (copper less reactive than hydrogen; no halide so oxygen), observations, half equations (Higher), and what is left (sulfuric acid; blue colour fades).
:::

::: example Molten electrolyte
Molten lead bromide is electrolysed using graphite electrodes. Name the products at each electrode and explain why solid lead bromide does not conduct electricity. (4)

Lead forms at the cathode (negative electrode) (1); bromine forms at the anode (positive electrode) (1). In the solid the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice (1), so they cannot move to carry charge; when molten the ions are free to move (1).
:::

::: example Aqueous solution
Predict the products at each electrode when aqueous sodium chloride is electrolysed with inert electrodes. Explain your answer. (4)

Cathode: hydrogen (1), because sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so H⁺ ions (from water) are discharged instead of Na⁺ (1). Anode: chlorine (1), because the solution contains halide (chloride) ions, which are discharged in preference to OH⁻ (1). Sodium hydroxide solution is left.
:::

::: example Aluminium extraction
Explain why aluminium is extracted by electrolysis rather than by heating with carbon, and why the anodes need replacing. (4)

Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot reduce aluminium oxide (1). At the anode, oxide ions lose electrons and form oxygen (1). The anode is made of carbon, which reacts with the oxygen at high temperature (1) to form carbon dioxide, so the anode burns away and must be replaced (1).
:::

::: example Half equations (Higher tier)
Complete and balance these half equations, and say whether each is oxidation or reduction: (a) Al³⁺ + __ → Al; (b) Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + __; (c) OH⁻ → O₂ + H₂O + __. (5)

(a) Al³⁺ + **3e⁻** → Al — reduction (gain of electrons) (1)(1).
(b) **2**Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + **2e⁻** — oxidation (loss of electrons) (1)(1).
(c) **4**OH⁻ → O₂ + **2**H₂O + **4e⁻** (1) — oxidation.
:::

::: example Required practical 3 hypothesis
A student writes the hypothesis: "In aqueous solutions, the metal is produced at the cathode only if it is less reactive than hydrogen." Suggest two solutions she could electrolyse to test it and the expected results. (4)

Sodium sulfate (or potassium nitrate) solution (1): sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so bubbles of hydrogen should form at the cathode, giving a squeaky pop with a lit splint (1).

::: watch
- **Mixing up the electrodes.** Cathode = **negative**, attracts **positive** ions; anode = **positive**, attracts **negative** ions.
- **"Sodium forms at the cathode" in aqueous NaCl.** In solution, a metal more reactive than hydrogen stays as ions — **hydrogen** forms instead.
- **"Sulfur" or "sulfate" at the anode.** Sulfate, nitrate and carbonate ions are not discharged; **oxygen** forms.
- **Saying electrons flow through the electrolyte.** Current in the electrolyte is carried by **moving ions**.
- **Aluminium anode:** it is replaced because it **reacts with oxygen** to form carbon dioxide — not because it "dissolves" or "melts".
- **Half equations (Higher tier):** electrons on the wrong side, or atoms balanced but charges not. Check both.
:::

(Higher tier) Write half equations for the reactions at each electrode during the electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide, and state which is oxidation. (3)
---

:::

::: test
(Higher tier) During electrolysis of sodium sulfate solution, the volume of gas at the cathode is twice the volume at the anode. Use half equations to explain this. (4)
---
Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ (1). Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ (1). The same number of electrons flows through each electrode; 4 electrons make 2 molecules of H₂ but only 1 molecule of O₂ (1). Equal moles of gas have equal volumes, so the hydrogen volume is twice the oxygen volume (1).
:::
