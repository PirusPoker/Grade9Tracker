## What it is

Carbon is unusual: its atoms can bond to each other in several different ways, giving substances with completely different properties. AQA expects you to know four forms — **diamond**, **graphite**, **graphene** and **fullerenes** (including **carbon nanotubes**) — and to **explain their properties from their structure and bonding**. This whole subsection is common to GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 5.2.3).

The key idea in every answer: **covalent bonds are strong and need a lot of energy to break**. If a substance is a **giant covalent structure**, melting it means breaking **many** covalent bonds. If it is made of **molecules**, melting it only means overcoming the **weak intermolecular forces** between molecules.

### Diamond

In diamond **each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds** with four other carbon atoms, in a **giant covalent structure** (a rigid 3D tetrahedral network that goes on in every direction).

| Property | Explanation from structure and bonding |
|---|---|
| **Very hard** | every atom is held in place by four strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D network, so it is very hard to move atoms out of place |
| **Very high melting point** | a huge number of strong covalent bonds must be broken, which needs a lot of energy |
| **Does not conduct electricity** | all four outer electrons of each carbon atom are used in covalent bonds, so there are **no delocalised electrons** (and no ions) free to move and carry charge |

Diamond is used in cutting tools and drill tips because it is so hard.

### Graphite

In graphite **each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds** with three other carbon atoms, making **layers of hexagonal rings**. There are **no covalent bonds between the layers** — only weak forces. Because each carbon uses only three of its four outer electrons in bonding, **one electron from each carbon atom is delocalised**.

| Property | Explanation from structure and bonding |
|---|---|
| **Soft and slippery** | there are no covalent bonds between the layers, only weak forces, so the **layers can slide over each other** |
| **Very high melting point** (like diamond) | to melt it you must break the **many strong covalent bonds** within the layers |
| **Conducts electricity** | the **delocalised electrons** can move through the structure and **carry charge** |
| **Conducts thermal energy** | the delocalised electrons transfer energy through the structure |

So **graphite is similar to metals** in that it has **delocalised electrons** — that is why both conduct. Uses: pencil "lead" (layers rub off onto paper), lubricants, and **electrodes** in electrolysis (conducts and has a high melting point).

> Graphite is soft but still has a high melting point. "Soft" is about layers sliding (weak forces between layers); "high melting point" is about breaking strong covalent bonds within layers. Do not mix the two up.

### Graphene

**Graphene is a single layer of graphite** — a sheet of carbon atoms in hexagonal rings, **one atom thick**. Each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds and has one delocalised electron.

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Very strong** for its mass | the atoms are held by strong covalent bonds in a continuous hexagonal network |
| **Conducts electricity very well** | delocalised electrons move freely across the sheet |
| **Very light, thin and almost transparent** | it is only one atom thick |

These properties make graphene **useful in electronics** (e.g. fast, flexible circuits and touchscreens) and **composites** (adding a little graphene to a material makes it much stronger without adding much mass).

### Fullerenes

**Fullerenes are molecules of carbon atoms with hollow shapes.** Their structure is based on **hexagonal rings** of carbon atoms, but they may also contain rings of **five or seven** carbon atoms (the five-membered rings let the sheet curve round into a ball).

- The **first fullerene discovered was Buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀**, which has a **spherical** shape (like a football: 20 hexagons and 12 pentagons).
- C₆₀ is a **simple molecule**. Within each molecule the covalent bonds are strong, but **between molecules there are only weak intermolecular forces**. So, unlike diamond and graphite, C₆₀ has a relatively **low melting (sublimation) point** and is **soft and slippery** — the molecules roll over each other.

**Carbon nanotubes** are **cylindrical fullerenes** with **very high length to diameter ratios** — they can be millions of times longer than they are wide (a tube about 1 nm across can be several millimetres long).
- They have very **high tensile strength** (hard to snap when stretched) because of the strong covalent bonds along the tube.
- They **conduct electricity and thermal energy** (delocalised electrons).
- Their properties make them useful for **nanotechnology, electronics and materials** — e.g. reinforcing tennis rackets and bike frames, in tiny electrical circuits.

**Uses of fullerenes you can give:**

| Use | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Delivering drugs** into the body | the hollow cage can carry a drug molecule inside it |
| **Lubricants** | spherical C₆₀ molecules roll over one another |
| **Catalysts** (or catalyst supports) | very large surface area for their mass; catalyst atoms can be attached |
| **Reinforcing materials** (nanotubes) | very high tensile strength and low mass |
| **Electronics** (nanotubes) | conduct electricity; tiny size |

### Recognising the forms from diagrams (MS 5b)

You may be shown a 2D drawing of a 3D structure and asked to name it. Use the bonding:

| Form | Bonds per carbon | Shape | Giant or molecule? | Delocalised electrons? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diamond | 4 | 3D tetrahedral network | giant covalent | no |
| Graphite | 3 | stacked flat layers of hexagons | giant covalent (layers) | yes, 1 per C |
| Graphene | 3 | **one** flat layer of hexagons | giant covalent (sheet) | yes, 1 per C |
| C₆₀ fullerene | 3 | hollow sphere, hexagons and pentagons | **molecule** | yes |
| Nanotube | 3 | hollow cylinder, very long and thin | molecule (very large) | yes |

**Working scientifically (WS 1.2, 1.4):** these structures are **models**. A 2D drawing cannot show every atom in a 3D network, and ball and stick models show gaps and "sticks" that do not really exist. New carbon forms (graphene was first isolated in 2004) show how scientific understanding develops and leads to new technologies.

::: terms
- **Giant covalent structure**: a huge network of atoms all joined to each other by strong covalent bonds, such as diamond or graphite.
- **Delocalised electron**: an electron not tied to one atom or bond, free to move through the structure and carry charge.
- **Diamond**: a form of carbon in which each atom forms four covalent bonds in a rigid 3D giant covalent structure.
- **Graphite**: a form of carbon in which each atom forms three covalent bonds, making layers of hexagonal rings with no covalent bonds between layers.
- **Graphene**: a single layer of graphite, one atom thick, which is very strong and conducts electricity.
- **Fullerene**: a molecule of carbon atoms with a hollow shape, based on hexagonal rings that may include five- or seven-membered rings.
- **Buckminsterfullerene**: the first fullerene discovered, C60, a spherical molecule of sixty carbon atoms.
- **Carbon nanotube**: a cylindrical fullerene with a very high length to diameter ratio, used in nanotechnology, electronics and materials.
- **Intermolecular forces**: weak forces of attraction between separate molecules, much weaker than covalent bonds.
:::

::: method
**Every answer is structure → bonding → property.** State the structure (giant covalent / layers / molecule), state the bonds (number per carbon, strong covalent, what is or is not between layers or molecules), then link to the property with "so" or "because".

**Command words**
- **Explain why diamond is hard / has a high melting point** → "giant covalent structure; each carbon forms **four** strong covalent bonds; **many** bonds must be broken; needs **a lot of energy**". "Strong bonds" alone is not enough — say *many* and *energy*.
- **Explain why graphite conducts** → "each carbon forms three bonds, so **one electron per carbon is delocalised**; these electrons **move through the structure and carry charge**". Never say ions move in graphite.
- **Explain why graphite is soft / slippery** → "layers of carbon atoms; **no covalent bonds between layers** / only weak forces; layers **slide** over each other".
- **Compare** (e.g. diamond and graphite) → make **matched pairs** of points: 4 bonds vs 3 bonds; no delocalised electrons vs one per atom; rigid network vs layers. Use "whereas".
- **Suggest a use** → link to one property: cutting tools (hard), electrodes (conducts + high melting point), drug delivery (hollow cage), reinforcing (high tensile strength).
- **Identify from a diagram** → count bonds per carbon and look at the shape: 4 bonds = diamond; flat stacked layers = graphite; a single layer = graphene; closed ball = C₆₀; long tube = nanotube.

**Molecule vs giant structure trap:** C₆₀ is a **molecule**. Its low melting point is because of **weak intermolecular forces**, not because covalent bonds break. When a molecular substance melts, the covalent bonds stay intact.

**Extended response (4–6 marks):** a typical question asks you to compare two or three forms of carbon. Level 3 answers cover structure, bonding and **at least two properties for each**, each property explained, with clear comparisons. Plan with a quick table (bonds per C / delocalised electrons / between layers or molecules) before writing.
:::

::: example Diamond's properties
Explain why diamond has a very high melting point and does not conduct electricity. (4)

Diamond has a giant covalent structure in which each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds (1). Melting needs many strong covalent bonds to be broken, which requires a lot of energy (1). All four outer electrons of each carbon are used in bonding (1), so there are no delocalised electrons (or ions) free to move and carry charge (1).
:::

::: example Graphite as a lubricant and electrode
Graphite is used both as a lubricant and as electrodes in electrolysis. Explain these uses. (4)

Lubricant: graphite is made of layers of carbon atoms with no covalent bonds between the layers, only weak forces (1), so the layers can slide over each other (1). Electrode: one electron from each carbon atom is delocalised, so the electrons can move through the structure and carry charge (1); it also has a very high melting point because many strong covalent bonds in the layers must be broken, so it does not melt in hot molten electrolytes (1).
:::

::: example Comparing C₆₀ with diamond
Diamond and buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀, are both made only of carbon atoms. Explain why C₆₀ has a much lower melting point. (3)

Diamond is a giant covalent structure, so melting breaks many strong covalent bonds (1). C₆₀ is made of separate (simple) molecules (1). Only the weak intermolecular forces between the molecules need to be overcome, which needs much less energy; the covalent bonds within each molecule do not break (1).
:::

::: example Identifying from a description
A form of carbon is described as "a sheet one atom thick, in which each atom is bonded to three others in hexagonal rings". (a) Name it. (b) Give one property and a use that depends on it. (3)

(a) Graphene (1). (b) It conducts electricity because it has delocalised electrons (1), so it is used in electronics (1). (Or: very strong and light, so used in composites.)
:::

::: example Nanotube use
Carbon nanotubes are added to the frames of racing bicycles. Suggest why. (2)

Nanotubes have very high tensile strength because of the strong covalent bonds along the tube (1), and they have very low mass / are very light, so the frame is strong without being heavy (1).
:::

::: watch
- Saying graphite conducts because of "free ions" or "free electrons" with no explanation — say **one electron per carbon is delocalised and moves through the structure carrying charge**.
- Explaining graphite's softness with "weak covalent bonds between layers". There are **no** covalent bonds between the layers — only weak forces.
- Explaining high melting points with "strong bonds" only. Write **many strong covalent bonds** must be broken, needing **a lot of energy**.
- Saying C₆₀ melts by breaking covalent bonds. It is a **molecule**: melting overcomes **weak intermolecular forces**.
- Mixing up graphene (one layer, flat) with fullerenes (hollow, closed shapes).
- Writing "diamond has 4 bonds so it is strong" without saying the structure is **giant** / a 3D network.
:::

::: test
A student says: "Graphene and graphite conduct electricity for the same reason that a metal does." Evaluate this statement. (3)
---
Partly correct: graphene and graphite have delocalised electrons that move through the structure and carry charge (1), and metals also conduct because of delocalised electrons (1). However, the structures are different — graphite/graphene have covalent bonds between carbon atoms, while metals are positive ions held by metallic bonding — so the statement is right about the charge carriers but not the bonding (1).
:::

::: test
Buckminsterfullerene has the formula C₆₀. (a) Describe its shape. (b) Explain why it is slippery and can be used as a lubricant. (c) Explain why it has a much lower melting point than graphite. (5)
---
(a) A hollow sphere / ball made of hexagonal and pentagonal rings of carbon atoms (1). (b) The molecules are spherical (1) and are held by only weak intermolecular forces, so they can roll / slide over each other (1). (c) C₆₀ is made of separate molecules, so only weak intermolecular forces need overcoming (1), whereas graphite is a giant covalent structure in which many strong covalent bonds must be broken, needing much more energy (1).
:::

::: test
Carbon nanotubes are being tested as a way of carrying medicines to cancer cells, and as wires in very small electronic circuits. Use your knowledge of their structure to explain why they are suitable for each use. (4)
---
Medicine: nanotubes are hollow (1), so drug molecules can be carried inside them and they are small enough to reach / enter cells (1). Wires: each carbon forms three bonds so there are delocalised electrons (1) that can move along the tube and carry charge, and the tube is tiny in diameter (1).
:::

Graphite — each carbon forms three covalent bonds in layers of hexagonal rings; no covalent bonds between layers, only weak forces, so layers slide and graphite is soft and slippery; one electron per carbon is delocalised, so graphite conducts electricity and thermal energy (like a metal); used for electrodes and lubricants.
