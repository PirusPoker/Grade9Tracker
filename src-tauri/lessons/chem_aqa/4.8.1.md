## What it is

This topic (AQA 4.8.1) is in both GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464, where it is numbered **5.8.1**. It is about telling **pure substances** from mixtures, recognising **formulations**, and using **paper chromatography**, including **Required practical 6** (Trilogy: **Required practical 12**). There is no Higher-tier-only content here, but the Rf calculation and significant figures are tested at both tiers.

Chemical analysis starts with simple questions: is this substance pure? What is in this mixture? Analysts use **melting and boiling points** and **chromatography** to answer them.

### Pure substances

In chemistry, a **pure substance** is a **single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance**. Pure water contains only H₂O molecules; pure copper contains only copper atoms.

In everyday language "pure" means something different: a substance that has had **nothing added** to it, so it is unadulterated and in its natural state. "Pure milk" or "pure orange juice" are **mixtures** in the chemical sense (milk contains water, fats, proteins, lactose and more), even though nothing has been added.

**Melting and boiling points identify purity.** Pure elements and compounds **melt and boil at specific (fixed) temperatures**. Mixtures do not:

| | Pure substance | Impure substance (mixture) |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | at one **sharp, fixed** temperature | over a **range** of temperatures, usually **lower** than the pure substance |
| Boiling | at one **fixed** temperature | over a **range**, usually **higher** than the pure substance |
| Heating curve | a flat line (plateau) while it melts or boils | a sloping section instead of a flat one |

Examples: pure water melts at exactly 0 °C and boils at exactly 100 °C (at normal atmospheric pressure). Salty water freezes below 0 °C (which is why salt is spread on icy roads) and boils above 100 °C.

**Using the data (the skill AQA tests):** compare the measured value with the known value for the pure substance (from a data book). If a sample of a compound melts sharply **at** the data-book value, it is pure. If it melts **below** the expected value **over a range** (for example 128–133 °C when the pure substance melts at 136 °C), it is impure. The closer the melting point is to the true value, and the narrower the range, the purer the sample.

### Formulations

A **formulation** is a **mixture that has been designed as a useful product**. Each chemical in it has a **particular purpose**, and the components are mixed in **carefully measured quantities** to make sure the product has the **required properties**.

| Formulation | Typical components and their jobs |
|---|---|
| **Paints** | pigment (colour), binder (sticks the pigment to the surface), solvent (makes it spread, then evaporates), additives (e.g. to speed drying) |
| **Medicines** (tablets) | active drug, fillers (bulk to make a sensible size), binders (hold the tablet together), coatings and flavourings |
| **Fertilisers** | compounds providing nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium in set proportions (NPK) |
| **Fuels** | the fuel itself plus additives (anti-knock agents, detergents to keep engines clean) |
| **Alloys** | a metal mixed with set amounts of other elements to make it harder or resist corrosion |
| **Cleaning agents** | detergents, water softeners, enzymes, fragrances |
| **Foods** | ingredients plus preservatives, emulsifiers, colourings in exact amounts |

**Identifying a formulation from information:** look for (1) a **mixture** of several substances, (2) **specific, measured amounts** (percentages on the label), and (3) each component having a **function** in a **useful product**. A substance found naturally, or a single compound, is not a formulation. You do not need to know the names of ingredients in branded (proprietary) products.

### Chromatography

**Chromatography** can be used to **separate mixtures** and can give information to help **identify substances**. Every type of chromatography has two phases:

- a **stationary phase**, which does not move: in paper chromatography, the **paper** (strictly, the water held in the paper fibres);
- a **mobile phase**, which moves through the stationary phase: the **solvent** (often water or ethanol).

**How paper chromatography separates a mixture.** Separation depends on how each substance **distributes itself between the two phases**. As the solvent rises up the paper, each substance in the spot is constantly moving between dissolving in the solvent and sticking to the paper.

- A substance that is **more soluble in the solvent** (more attracted to the mobile phase) spends more time in the solvent, so it **travels further** up the paper.
- A substance that is **more strongly attracted to the paper** (the stationary phase) spends more time stuck to the paper, so it **moves a shorter distance**.

Different substances therefore end up as separate spots at different heights. A substance that does not dissolve in the solvent at all stays on the start line.

In this chromatogram ink A is a mixture of two dyes, one of which is dye R (same height in the same solvent); ink B is also a mixture and does not contain R.

**Rf values.** The ratio of the distance moved by a compound (measured to the **centre of the spot** from the **start line**) to the distance moved by the solvent (from the **start line** to the **solvent front**) is the **Rf value**:

$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by substance}}{\text{distance moved by solvent}}$$

- Rf has **no units** (a ratio of two distances) and is always **less than 1**, because a substance cannot travel further than the solvent.
- Give your answer as a **decimal**, usually to **2 significant figures**, or to the same number of significant figures as the measurements (MS 2a).
- **Estimate first** (MS 1d): a spot just under halfway up the solvent distance must have an Rf a bit under 0.5. If your calculation disagrees, you have divided the wrong way round.

**Worked mini-example.** The solvent front is 8.0 cm above the start line. A red spot's centre is 3.2 cm above the start line. $Rf = 3.2 \div 8.0 = 0.40$.

**Identifying substances with Rf values.** Different compounds have **different Rf values in different solvents**. A compound always has the **same Rf in the same solvent** (on the same type of paper at the same temperature), so comparing Rf values with those of known substances, run in the same solvent, helps identify the compounds in a mixture. If an unknown spot matches a known substance in **more than one** solvent, the identification is more reliable.

**Pure or impure?** The compounds in a **mixture may separate into different spots** depending on the solvent, but a **pure compound produces a single spot in all solvents**. So: one spot in one solvent does not prove purity (two substances might have the same Rf in that solvent); one spot in **several different solvents** is good evidence the substance is pure. Two or more spots in any solvent means it is a mixture.

### Required practical 6 (Trilogy: required practical 12): paper chromatography

**Required practical 6: investigate how paper chromatography can be used to separate and tell the difference between coloured substances. Students should calculate Rf values.** In Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 this is **Required practical activity 12**.

**Method:**

1. Draw a **start line in pencil** about 2 cm from the bottom of a piece of chromatography paper. (Pencil is graphite, which is insoluble, so it will not move with the solvent; ink would dissolve and run.)
2. Use a capillary tube or fine glass rod to put a **small, concentrated spot** of each coloured substance (inks, food colourings) on the line, well spaced out. Let each spot dry and re-spot to make it concentrated but still small.
3. Pour solvent (water, or ethanol for inks that do not dissolve in water) into a beaker to a depth of about 1 cm, **below the start line**, so the spots do not dissolve straight into the solvent in the beaker.
4. Hang the paper in the beaker so the bottom edge dips into the solvent and the paper does not touch the sides. Put a **lid** on, so the air inside becomes saturated with solvent vapour and the solvent does not evaporate from the paper.
5. Leave until the solvent has risen most of the way up the paper. Remove it and **immediately mark the solvent front in pencil** (it evaporates and becomes invisible).
6. Let the paper dry. Measure from the start line to the solvent front and to the **centre** of each spot, then calculate the Rf values.

| Variable | In this practical |
|---|---|
| Independent | the coloured substance (ink or dye) tested |
| Dependent | distance moved by each spot, or its Rf value |
| Control | the solvent, type of paper, height of start line, temperature, lid on, time left |

**Typical results:** food colourings often split into two or three spots (mixtures); a single dye gives one spot; a spot that stays on the line is insoluble in that solvent, so the experiment should be repeated with a different solvent.

**Risks:** ethanol and other organic solvents are **flammable** (no naked flames) and give off vapour (good ventilation, lid on); some inks and dyes are irritants (avoid skin contact, eye protection).

**Errors and improvements:** spots too large, which smear and overlap (use a fine capillary); solvent above the start line; start line in ink; measuring to the top of a spot rather than its centre; taking the paper out without marking the solvent front. Repeat with a second solvent to confirm identifications.

::: terms
- **Pure substance (chemistry)**: a single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance.
- **Formulation**: a mixture designed as a useful product, made by mixing components in carefully measured quantities.
- **Chromatography**: a method of separating mixtures that uses a stationary phase and a mobile phase.
- **Stationary phase**: the phase that does not move in chromatography; in paper chromatography it is the paper.
- **Mobile phase**: the phase that moves through the stationary phase, carrying substances; in paper chromatography it is the solvent.
- **Rf value**: distance moved by a substance divided by distance moved by the solvent, both measured from the start line.
- **Solvent front**: the furthest point the solvent reaches up the paper, marked in pencil as soon as the paper is removed.
- **Chromatogram**: the paper (or record) showing the separated spots after chromatography.
:::

::: method
**Purity from data.** Compare the sample's melting or boiling point with the pure value. Say whether it is **sharp/fixed** or **over a range**, and whether it is **at**, **below** (melting) or **above** (boiling) the pure value. Conclude "pure" or "impure" and quote the numbers.

**Command words.**
- **Explain** how paper chromatography separates: name both phases, then say substances that are **more soluble in the solvent** travel **further**, those **more attracted to the paper** travel less, so they separate. A list of steps is not an explanation.
- **Suggest** how to show a substance is pure: run it in **several different solvents**; a pure substance gives **one spot in every solvent**.
- **Identify** from a chromatogram: match spots at the **same height** (same Rf) **in the same solvent** to known substances. Count spots to say how many substances there are (at least).
- **Identify a formulation**: point to a mixture, measured quantities and components with particular purposes in a useful product.

**Rf calculations (2–3 marks).**
1. Write the equation: Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent.
2. Measure both distances **from the start line**; the substance distance to the **centre** of the spot.
3. Divide and give the answer as a decimal with **no units**, usually 2 significant figures.
4. Check it is **less than 1**. To find a distance from an Rf, rearrange: distance moved by substance = Rf × distance moved by solvent.

**Required practical 6 (Trilogy 12) questions.** Expect "why pencil?", "why must the start line be above the solvent?", "why a lid?", "suggest why a spot stayed on the start line", and Rf calculations from measured values. Always link the step to its **reason**.

**6-mark questions** usually ask you to **describe** the chromatography method (logical sequence with reasons) or **plan** how to identify dyes in an unknown ink. Level 3 needs the full sequence including reference substances run on the same paper, and how results are analysed (Rf, comparing heights).
:::

::: example Calculating an Rf value
In a chromatogram the solvent front is 7.5 cm from the start line. The centre of a yellow spot is 5.1 cm from the start line. Calculate the Rf value of the yellow dye. (2)

$R_f = \dfrac{5.1}{7.5}$ (1) $= 0.68$ (1). No units; 2 significant figures like the data.
:::

::: example Working backwards from Rf
A dye has an Rf of 0.35 in water. The solvent front is 9.0 cm above the start line. How far above the start line will the centre of the dye spot be? (2)

Distance $= R_f \times$ solvent distance $= 0.35 \times 9.0$ (1) $=$ **3.2 cm** (the exact answer 3.15 cm rounds to 3.2 cm to 2 s.f.; 3.15 cm is also accepted) (1).
:::

::: example Using melting point data
Pure aspirin melts at 136 °C. Sample P melts at 136 °C; sample Q melts between 128 °C and 133 °C. Which sample is pure? Explain. (2)

**P** is pure (1): it melts at the **specific** temperature of pure aspirin, while Q melts **over a range** and **below** 136 °C, which shows it contains impurities (1).
:::

::: example Interpreting a chromatogram
A brown food colouring gives three spots. Two match the heights of known dyes E102 and E133 in the same solvent; one matches nothing. What can you conclude? (3)

The colouring is a **mixture** of at least three dyes (1). It contains **E102 and E133**, because these spots have the same Rf values as the known dyes in the same solvent (1). The third dye is **not** any of the reference dyes tested (1).
:::

::: example Identifying a formulation
A weedkiller label says: glyphosate 36%, surfactant 10% (helps it spread on leaves), water 54%. Explain why the weedkiller is a formulation. (2)

It is a **mixture** of several substances in **measured, fixed proportions** (1), each with a **specific purpose** (active chemical, spreading agent, solvent) designed to make a useful product (1).
:::

What is a formulation? Give two examples. (3)

A **mixture** designed as a **useful product**, made with components in **carefully measured quantities** (1). Any two of paints, medicines, fuels, cleaning agents, alloys, fertilisers, foods (1)(1).

::: q
Name the stationary phase and the mobile phase in paper chromatography. (2)
---
Stationary phase: the **paper** (1). Mobile phase: the **solvent** (1).
:::

::: q
Why is the start line drawn in pencil rather than ink? (1)
---
Pencil (graphite) is insoluble in the solvent, so it will not move up the paper; ink would dissolve and separate, confusing the results.
:::

::: q
Why must the solvent level be below the start line? (1)
---
Otherwise the substances in the spots would dissolve into the solvent in the beaker instead of moving up the paper.
:::

::: q
The solvent moved 6.4 cm and a spot moved 1.6 cm. Calculate the Rf value. (2)
---
$R_f = 1.6 \div 6.4$ (1) $= 0.25$ (1).
:::

::: q
A sample gives one spot in water. A student says this proves it is pure. Explain why the student may be wrong and suggest what they should do. (2)
---
Two (or more) substances could have the **same Rf** in water and so appear as one spot (1). Run it again in **several different solvents**; if it still gives a single spot in all of them it is pure (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Dividing the wrong way round.** Rf is substance ÷ solvent, so it must be **less than 1**. An answer of 2.5 is a giveaway.
- **Measuring from the bottom of the paper** or to the top of the spot. Measure from the **start line** to the **centre** of the spot.
- **Giving Rf units.** It is a ratio; no units.
- **"Impurities raise the melting point."** They **lower** it and spread it over a **range**. (Impurities raise the boiling point.)
- **Describing instead of explaining separation.** You must refer to solubility in the **solvent** (mobile phase) versus attraction to the **paper** (stationary phase).
- **Everyday "pure".** In chemistry, "pure" means one substance only, not "natural" or "nothing added".
- **Trilogy students:** the practical is numbered **12** in your specification, but the content is the same.
:::

::: test
Explain how paper chromatography separates the dyes in an ink. (3)
---
The paper is the stationary phase and the solvent is the mobile phase (1). Each dye is distributed between the two phases differently / has a different solubility in the solvent and attraction to the paper (1). Dyes that are more soluble in the solvent (less attracted to the paper) travel further up the paper, so the dyes separate into spots at different heights (1).

::: test
In Required practical 6 (Trilogy 12), a student's chromatogram shows the solvent front at 8.4 cm. Spots: dye A 2.1 cm, dye B 5.9 cm. (a) Calculate the Rf value of each dye to 2 significant figures. (b) A reference dye has Rf 0.70 in the same solvent. Which dye might it be? (3)
---
(a) A: $2.1 \div 8.4 = 0.25$ (1). B: $5.9 \div 8.4 = 0.702 = 0.70$ (1). (b) **Dye B** (same Rf in the same solvent) (1).
:::

::: test
A student's chromatogram is spoiled: all the colours have run together near the bottom, and one spot never left the start line. Suggest two mistakes in the method and one reason for the spot on the start line. (3)
---
Mistakes, any two: spots too large or not dried/concentrated; solvent level above the start line; start line drawn in ink; paper touching the side of the beaker; paper left in too short a time (1)(1). Spot on start line: that substance is **insoluble** in the solvent used (try another solvent) (1).
:::

::: test
An unknown ink, U, is spotted alongside four pure dyes (P, Q, R, S) and run in water. U gives spots at the same heights as P and S. It is then run in ethanol, where it again matches P and S. (a) What does U contain? (b) Explain why running it in a second solvent makes the conclusion more reliable. (3)
---
(a) Dyes **P and S** (1). (b) Two different dyes could have the same Rf in one solvent by chance (1); dyes have different Rf values in different solvents, so matching in **two** solvents makes it much less likely the match is a coincidence (1).
:::

::: test
A dye has Rf 0.42 in a solvent. The paper is 15.0 cm long, the start line is 2.0 cm from the bottom, and the solvent front stops 1.0 cm from the top. Calculate the distance between the start line and the centre of the dye spot. (3)
---
Solvent distance $= 15.0 - 2.0 - 1.0 = 12.0$ cm (1). Distance moved by dye $= 0.42 \times 12.0$ (1) $=$ **5.0 cm** (5.04 cm) (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how a student could use paper chromatography to find out whether a black felt-tip pen contains any of three named food dyes, and how they would analyse the results. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a coherent method in a logical order that would give valid results, with reasons for key steps, and a clear account of how the results identify the dyes (Rf / same height). **Level 2 (3–4):** a workable method with most steps, some analysis; reasons or detail missing. **Level 1 (1–2):** some relevant steps, not a workable sequence; little or no analysis.

Indicative content: draw a start line in **pencil** about 2 cm from the bottom of chromatography paper (pencil does not dissolve); put a small concentrated spot of the black ink and of each of the three dyes on the line, spaced apart, using a capillary tube; put solvent (e.g. water) in a beaker to below the start line so the spots do not dissolve into it; hang the paper in, not touching the sides; cover with a lid to stop evaporation; leave until the solvent nearly reaches the top; remove and mark the solvent front in pencil; dry. Analysis: count the spots from the black ink (number of dyes in it); compare heights of spots with the reference dyes on the same paper, or measure distances from the start line and calculate Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent; matching Rf values show which dyes are present; repeat in a different solvent to confirm; a spot on the start line means insoluble in that solvent.
:::
