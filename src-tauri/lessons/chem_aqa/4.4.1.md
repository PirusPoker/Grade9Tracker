## What it is

This subsection is in **both** GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 5.4.1). The part on oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons is **Higher tier** only.

### Metal oxides: oxidation and reduction in terms of oxygen

Metals react with oxygen to form **metal oxides**:

$$2\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{MgO} \qquad 4\text{Fe} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \qquad 2\text{Cu} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CuO}$$

The metal **gains oxygen**, so it is **oxidised**. These are **oxidation** reactions.

| Term | Definition (in terms of oxygen) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxidation** | **gain of oxygen** | Mg → MgO |
| **Reduction** | **loss of oxygen** | CuO → Cu |

Oxidation and reduction often happen together. In 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂, copper(II) oxide **loses** oxygen (reduced) while carbon **gains** oxygen (oxidised).

### The reactivity series

When metals react, their atoms **lose electrons** to form **positive ions** (e.g. Mg → Mg²⁺). The **reactivity of a metal is related to its tendency to form positive ions**: the more easily a metal's atoms lose their outer electrons, the more reactive the metal. Arranging metals by reactivity gives the **reactivity series**. Carbon and hydrogen are non-metals but are often added as reference points.

| Metal | Reaction with cold water | Reaction with dilute acid (HCl or H₂SO₄) |
|---|---|---|
| **Potassium** (most reactive) | very vigorous: floats, melts, whizzes about, hydrogen ignites with a **lilac flame**; forms KOH | dangerously violent — never done in school |
| **Sodium** | vigorous: floats, melts into a ball, fizzes, moves about; forms NaOH | dangerously violent |
| **Lithium** | steady fizzing, floats, does not melt; forms LiOH | violent |
| **Calcium** | fizzes steadily, sinks; solution goes cloudy (Ca(OH)₂ is only slightly soluble) | very vigorous fizzing |
| **Magnesium** | very slow — a few bubbles over several days | vigorous fizzing; ribbon dissolves quickly and the tube gets warm |
| *carbon* | — | — |
| **Zinc** | no visible reaction | steady fizzing |
| **Iron** | no visible reaction (rusts very slowly with water and air) | slow fizzing |
| *hydrogen* | — | — |
| **Copper** (least reactive) | no reaction | **no reaction** |

The order you must know: **K > Na > Li > Ca > Mg > (C) > Zn > Fe > (H) > Cu**. (Aluminium sits between magnesium and carbon in the full series; gold is below copper.) The spec covers reactions with water and acids at **room temperature only** — not steam.

General equations:
- metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen, e.g. $2\text{Na} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2$
- metal + acid → salt + hydrogen, e.g. $\text{Zn} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2 + \text{H}_2$

**Explaining reactivity:** potassium is more reactive than sodium because a potassium atom loses its outer electron **more easily** — it has a **greater tendency to form a positive ion** (K⁺). Copper has very little tendency to form Cu²⁺, so it does not react with water or dilute acid.

### Deducing an order of reactivity from results

You may be given observations or data (e.g. bubbles per minute, temperature rise, time for a piece of metal to disappear) for metals with acid, and asked to put them in order. **More bubbles, a faster rate of fizzing or a bigger temperature rise = more reactive.** For a fair comparison: same mass/size and surface area of metal, same acid, same concentration and volume, same starting temperature.

| Metal (0.1 g in 20 cm³ of 1 mol/dm³ HCl) | Temperature rise / °C |
|---|---|
| W | 0 |
| X | 18 |
| Y | 4 |
| Z | 9 |

Order of reactivity: **X > Z > Y > W** (W could be copper).

### Displacement reactions

A **more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a compound** (in solution, or from its oxide).

$$\text{Mg} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{MgSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$$

Observations: the grey magnesium becomes coated in **pink-brown copper**, the **blue** colour of the copper(II) sulfate fades, and the mixture gets warm. If the added metal is **less** reactive (e.g. copper in magnesium sulfate) there is **no reaction**. Displacement results let you deduce an order: a metal that displaces the most others is the most reactive.

| Metal added ↓ / solution → | magnesium sulfate | zinc sulfate | copper(II) sulfate |
|---|---|---|---|
| magnesium | — | reaction | reaction |
| zinc | no reaction | — | reaction |
| copper | no reaction | no reaction | — |

Order: Mg > Zn > Cu.

### Extracting metals and reduction with carbon

**Unreactive metals** such as **gold** are found in the Earth **as the metal itself** (native). Most metals are found as **compounds** (e.g. oxides in ores) and must be extracted by chemical reactions. **How** a metal is extracted depends on where it sits relative to carbon:

| Position | Method | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| more reactive than carbon | **electrolysis** of the molten compound (topic 4.4.3) | potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium |
| less reactive than carbon | **reduction with carbon** (heat the oxide with carbon) | zinc, iron, copper (and lead, tin) |
| very unreactive | found **native** — just separated physically | gold (and platinum) |

Carbon can only take oxygen from a metal that is **less reactive** than carbon:

$$2\text{ZnO} + \text{C} \rightarrow 2\text{Zn} + \text{CO}_2 \qquad 2\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{C} \rightarrow 4\text{Fe} + 3\text{CO}_2 \qquad 2\text{CuO} + \text{C} \rightarrow 2\text{Cu} + \text{CO}_2$$

In each, the **metal oxide is reduced** (loses oxygen) and the **carbon is oxidised** (gains oxygen). Heating magnesium oxide with carbon does nothing, because magnesium is more reactive than carbon. You do **not** need the details of any industrial process, but you must be able to **interpret or evaluate** an extraction method from information given — for example, comparing energy use, cost, purity, the availability of the ore, or the pollution (CO₂) produced.

### Oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons (Higher tier)

At Higher tier, oxidation and reduction are defined by **electrons**:

- **Oxidation Is Loss** of electrons; **Reduction Is Gain** of electrons — **OIL RIG**.

In the displacement of copper by magnesium, the **sulfate ion is a spectator** (it is unchanged), so the **ionic equation** leaves it out:

$$\text{Mg}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)$$

Split into **half equations**:

$$\text{Mg} \rightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+} + 2e^- \quad \text{(magnesium loses electrons: oxidised)}$$

$$\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu} \quad \text{(copper ions gain electrons: reduced)}$$

A reaction in which oxidation and reduction both happen is a **redox** reaction.

**Writing an ionic equation for a displacement (Higher tier):**
1. Write the full balanced equation: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
2. Split the soluble ionic compounds into ions: Zn + Cu²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → Zn²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + Cu
3. Cross out the spectator ions (SO₄²⁻): **Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu**
4. Check charges balance on both sides (2+ and 2+).

More examples:
- Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Cu (iron is oxidised, copper ions reduced)
- Cu + 2Ag⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2Ag (copper wire in silver nitrate: silver crystals grow; copper oxidised, silver ions reduced)
- Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂ (halogen displacement: chlorine is reduced, bromide ions are oxidised)

**Identifying the species:** whichever species **gains electrons** (its charge becomes more negative, e.g. Cu²⁺ → Cu) is **reduced**; whichever **loses electrons** (charge becomes more positive, e.g. Fe → Fe²⁺) is **oxidised**. The same idea applies in electrolysis half equations (topic 4.4.3) and metal–acid reactions (topic 4.4.2).

::: terms
- **Oxidation**: the gain of oxygen by a substance, or (Higher tier) the loss of electrons.
- **Reduction**: the loss of oxygen by a substance, or (Higher tier) the gain of electrons.
- **Reactivity series**: a list of metals in order of reactivity, with carbon and hydrogen included as reference points.
- **Displacement reaction**: a reaction in which a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive one in a compound.
- **Ore**: a rock containing enough of a metal compound to make extracting the metal worthwhile.
- **Native metal**: an unreactive metal, such as gold, that is found in the Earth as the uncombined element.
- **Spectator ion**: an ion that is present in a reaction mixture but is unchanged, so it is left out of the ionic equation.
- **Redox reaction**: a reaction in which one substance is oxidised and another is reduced at the same time.
:::

::: method
**Describe** observations: give what you would **see** — fizzing/bubbles, metal getting smaller, floating, melting, lilac flame, solution colour fading, a pink-brown coating — not just "it reacts". Name the products (metal hydroxide or salt + hydrogen).

**Explain** reactivity: link it to the **tendency to form a positive ion** (lose electrons). "Potassium is more reactive than lithium because its atoms lose their outer electron more easily to form K⁺ ions."

**Deduce an order** from results: say which data you used ("most bubbles", "largest temperature rise", "displaces the most metals") and give the full order. Comment on fair testing if asked: same mass and surface area, same acid concentration and volume.

**Extraction questions:** compare the metal's position with **carbon**. Below carbon → reduction with carbon. Above carbon → electrolysis (more expensive, uses lots of electricity). Gold → native. For "evaluate" questions use the data given: energy cost, cost of carbon vs electricity, purity of product, CO₂ emissions, scarcity of ore — and finish with a **justified conclusion**.

**Identify oxidised/reduced:**
- Foundation and Higher: by **oxygen** — gains oxygen = oxidised; loses oxygen = reduced.
- Higher: by **electrons** — loses electrons = oxidised; gains electrons = reduced (OIL RIG). Look at the charges: Fe → Fe²⁺ (charge goes up) is oxidation; Cu²⁺ → Cu (charge goes down) is reduction.

**Ionic equations (Higher tier):** write the full equation, split soluble ionic compounds into ions, cancel spectators, then check that **atoms and charges both balance**. Metals and their solid oxides are **not** split into ions in a displacement equation.

**Extended responses (6 marks):** e.g. "Plan an investigation to place four metals in order of reactivity." Give a clear sequence: same volume and concentration of acid, same mass of each metal in powder form, measure temperature rise (or rate of bubbles), repeat, put in order — plus controls and safety (eye protection, no potassium/sodium with acid).
:::

::: example Observations with water
Describe what you would see when a small piece of sodium is added to water, and name the products. (4)

The sodium floats (1), melts into a silvery ball and moves around on the surface (1), fizzing as a gas is given off (1). The products are sodium hydroxide and hydrogen (1).
:::

::: example Extraction method
Explain why iron can be extracted by heating iron(III) oxide with carbon, but aluminium cannot be extracted from aluminium oxide this way. (3)

Iron is less reactive than carbon, so carbon can remove the oxygen from iron(III) oxide (reduce it) (1). Aluminium is more reactive than carbon (1), so carbon cannot remove the oxygen from aluminium oxide; aluminium must be extracted by electrolysis instead (1).
:::

::: example Oxidised and reduced (oxygen)
In the reaction ZnO + C → Zn + CO, identify the substance oxidised and the substance reduced. Explain your answer. (2)

Zinc oxide is reduced because it loses oxygen (1). Carbon is oxidised because it gains oxygen (1).
:::

::: example Ionic equation (Higher tier)
Iron filings are added to copper(II) sulfate solution. Write the ionic equation and explain, in terms of electrons, which species is oxidised and which is reduced. (4)

Fe(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) (1)
Iron atoms lose two electrons each (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻), so iron is oxidised (1)(1).
Copper(II) ions gain two electrons each (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu), so copper ions are reduced (1).
:::

::: example Deducing an order
A student adds strips of three metals, P, Q and R, to solutions of each other's sulfates. P displaces both Q and R. R displaces nothing. Put the metals in order of reactivity and explain. (2)

P > Q > R (1). A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one: P displaces both so it is the most reactive; R displaces neither so it is the least reactive (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Saying "it reacts" without observations.** Describe what you see: fizzing, floating, melting, colour change, coating of metal.
- **Mixing up the definitions.** By oxygen: oxidation = **gain** of oxygen. By electrons: oxidation = **loss** of electrons. OIL RIG is about electrons only.
- **Naming the wrong thing as reduced.** In CuO + C, it is the **copper oxide** (or copper ions) that is reduced, not "copper".
- **Extraction direction.** Carbon reduces oxides of metals **below** it in the series. Metals **above** carbon need electrolysis.
- **Ionic equations with spectators left in, or unbalanced charges.** Cancel the sulfate (or nitrate); check charges add up the same on both sides.
- **Using steam reactions.** The spec only covers water and acids at room temperature.
:::

Copper is extracted by heating copper(II) oxide with carbon: 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂. Identify the substance that is reduced and the substance that is oxidised. (2)

Copper(II) oxide is reduced — it loses oxygen (1). Carbon is oxidised — it gains oxygen (1).

::: test
A student adds equal masses of four metal powders to 25 cm³ portions of the same dilute hydrochloric acid and records the temperature rise. Results: metal A 2 °C, metal B 0 °C, metal C 15 °C, metal D 7 °C. (a) Put the metals in order of reactivity. (b) Suggest the identity of metal B. (c) Give two variables the student kept constant. (4)
---
(a) C > D > A > B (1). (b) Copper (accept any metal less reactive than hydrogen, e.g. silver or gold) (1). (c) Any two: volume of acid; concentration of acid; mass of metal; particle size of metal (powder); starting temperature of acid (2).
:::

::: test
Titanium is more reactive than carbon. It is extracted by first converting titanium oxide to titanium chloride, then reacting the chloride with molten sodium in an argon atmosphere. Suggest why titanium is expensive, compared with iron. (3)
---
Titanium cannot be extracted by reduction with carbon because it is more reactive than carbon (1). The process needs several steps and sodium, which itself has to be extracted by electrolysis, using a lot of energy/electricity (1). The argon atmosphere and high temperatures add cost, whereas iron is made in one step by heating its oxide with cheap carbon (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Zinc reacts with silver nitrate solution: Zn + 2AgNO₃ → Zn(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag. (a) Write the ionic equation. (b) Write the two half equations. (c) State which species is reduced. (4)
---
(a) Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag (1). (b) Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (1); Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag (accept 2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag) (1). (c) Silver ions (Ag⁺) are reduced because they gain electrons (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Chlorine reacts with potassium iodide solution: Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂. Explain why this is described as a redox reaction. (3)
---
Chlorine gains electrons (Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻), so chlorine is reduced (1). Iodide ions lose electrons (2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻), so iodide ions are oxidised (1). Oxidation and reduction happen at the same time, so it is a redox reaction (1).
:::

::: test
Describe the reactions of potassium, magnesium, iron and copper with water and with dilute acid, and explain how these reactions show the order of reactivity of the metals. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** detailed observations for all four metals with both water and acid, products named, a correct order and an explanation linked to the tendency to form positive ions. **Level 2 (3–4):** observations for most metals with some products, and a correct order, or a partial explanation. **Level 1 (1–2):** a few simple statements about some of the metals or the order.

Indicative content: potassium reacts violently with cold water — floats, melts, fizzes, lilac flame — forming potassium hydroxide and hydrogen; potassium is too dangerous to add to acid. Magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water but fizzes vigorously in dilute acid, forming a salt (e.g. magnesium chloride) and hydrogen. Iron does not react noticeably with cold water and fizzes slowly with acid, forming iron(II) chloride/sulfate and hydrogen. Copper does not react with water or dilute acid. The more vigorous the reaction, the more reactive the metal: potassium > magnesium > iron > copper. Reactivity depends on the tendency of the metal atoms to lose electrons and form positive ions; potassium forms K⁺ most easily, copper hardly forms Cu²⁺ at all; copper is below hydrogen in the series so it cannot displace hydrogen from acid.
:::
