## What it is

This topic explains **why** substances behave as they do — why salt melts at 801 °C but methane boils at −162 °C, why copper conducts and sulfur does not — by linking properties to **bonding and structure**. All of it is common to GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. The structures of the forms of carbon (diamond, graphite, graphene, fullerenes) are covered in detail in the next topic.

### The three states of matter

The three states are **solid, liquid and gas**. **Melting** and **freezing** happen at the **melting point**; **boiling** and **condensing** happen at the **boiling point**.

In the **simple particle model**, particles are represented as **small solid spheres**:

| State | Arrangement | Movement | Spacing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Solid** | regular pattern | vibrate about fixed positions | very close together |
| **Liquid** | random | move around each other | close together |
| **Gas** | random | move quickly in all directions | far apart |

Changes of state:
- **Melting** (solid → liquid) and **boiling** (liquid → gas): energy is **transferred to** the substance (it is absorbed) to overcome the **forces between the particles**.
- **Freezing** (liquid → solid) and **condensing** (gas → liquid): energy is **transferred from** the substance (released to the surroundings) as forces form between the particles.

**The key idea:** the amount of energy needed to change state depends on the **strength of the forces between the particles**. The nature of the particles depends on the **type of bonding and structure**. The **stronger the forces between the particles, the higher the melting point and boiling point**.

| Structure | Particles | Forces that must be overcome to melt or boil | Melting/boiling points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | ions | strong ionic bonds (electrostatic attraction) | high |
| Small molecules | molecules | **weak intermolecular forces** | low |
| Giant covalent | atoms | strong covalent bonds | very high |
| Metallic | positive ions + delocalised electrons | strong metallic bonds | mostly high |

### Predicting states from data

Compare the temperature with the melting point (mp) and boiling point (bp):
- temperature **below the mp** → **solid**
- temperature **between the mp and the bp** → **liquid**
- temperature **above the bp** → **gas**

Worked: bromine has mp −7 °C and bp 59 °C. At 25 °C, 25 is above −7 but below 59 → **liquid**. At 100 °C → **gas**. At −20 °C → **solid**. The range over which bromine is a liquid is 59 − (−7) = **66 °C**.

### Limitations of the particle model (Higher tier)

The simple model is useful but limited. In the model:
- there are **no forces** between the particles — yet it is exactly the forces that decide the melting and boiling point;
- **all particles are spheres** — real particles are atoms, ions or molecules of many different shapes and sizes;
- the spheres are **solid** (and inelastic) — real atoms are mostly empty space, with a tiny nucleus and electrons.

So the model **cannot explain** why different substances change state at different temperatures, or how much energy a change of state needs.

### Bulk properties

**Atoms themselves do not have the bulk properties of materials.** A single copper atom is not shiny, does not conduct and has no melting point; a single gold atom is not yellow. Properties such as melting point, conductivity, colour and hardness belong to the **material** — a huge number of particles and the forces and bonds between them.

### State symbols

In equations: **(s)** solid, **(l)** liquid, **(g)** gas, **(aq)** aqueous — dissolved in water.

- 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)
- CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
- Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Water itself is **(l)**, not (aq). A salt solution or an acid is **(aq)**. A precipitate is **(s)**.

### Properties of ionic compounds

Ionic compounds have regular **giant ionic lattices** with **strong electrostatic forces of attraction in all directions** between oppositely charged ions.

- **High melting and boiling points** — **large amounts of energy** are needed to break the **many strong bonds** (sodium chloride melts at 801 °C).
- **Do not conduct when solid** — the ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move.
- **Conduct when melted or dissolved in water** — the **ions are free to move**, so **charge can flow**.

Only the sodium chloride structure is needed.

### Properties of small molecules

Substances made of small molecules (H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, O₂, Cl₂) are usually **gases or liquids** with **relatively low melting and boiling points**.
- The **covalent bonds within** each molecule are **strong**, but the **forces between the molecules (intermolecular forces)** are **weak**.
- When the substance melts or boils, it is the **intermolecular forces that are overcome, not the covalent bonds**. Boiling water gives steam (H₂O molecules), not hydrogen and oxygen.
- **Intermolecular forces increase with the size of the molecules**, so **larger molecules have higher melting and boiling points**.
- They **do not conduct electricity** because the molecules have **no overall electric charge** (and there are no free electrons or ions).

| Alkane | Formula | Boiling point (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | −162 |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | −89 |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | −42 |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | −1 |
| Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 36 |

Bigger molecules → stronger intermolecular forces → more energy to separate them → higher boiling point.

### Polymers

Polymers have **very large molecules**. The atoms in a polymer molecule are linked by **strong covalent bonds**. The **intermolecular forces between polymer molecules are relatively strong** (because the molecules are so large), so polymers are **solids at room temperature**. You should recognise a polymer from a diagram: a **repeating unit in brackets with $n$**, or long chains.

### Giant covalent structures

Substances with giant covalent structures are **solids with very high melting points**. **All the atoms are linked to other atoms by strong covalent bonds**, and these **bonds must be overcome** to melt or boil them — a huge amount of energy. Examples: **diamond** and **graphite** (forms of carbon) and **silicon dioxide** (silica, melts at about 1700 °C). Recognise them from diagrams: a continuing network of atoms with no separate molecules, drawn with bonds running off the edge.

**Do not confuse** small molecules and giant covalent structures: both contain covalent bonds, but melting a small-molecule substance breaks only weak intermolecular forces, whereas melting a giant covalent substance breaks strong covalent bonds.

### Properties of metals and alloys

- Metals have **giant structures of atoms with strong metallic bonding**, so **most metals have high melting and boiling points**.
- In **pure metals** the atoms are arranged in **layers** that can **slide over each other**, so metals can be **bent and shaped** (malleable). Pure metals are often **too soft** for many uses.
- An **alloy** is a **mixture of a metal with other elements** (usually other metals). Alloys are **harder** than pure metals: the **different-sized atoms distort the layers**, making it **more difficult for the layers to slide** over each other. Examples: steel (iron + carbon and other elements), brass (copper + zinc), bronze (copper + tin).

### Metals as conductors

- **Good electrical conductors** — the **delocalised electrons** carry **electrical charge** through the metal.
- **Good thermal conductors** — **energy is transferred by the delocalised electrons**.

### Summary of the structure types

| Property | Giant ionic | Small molecules | Polymers | Giant covalent | Metallic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| State at room temperature | solid | usually gas or liquid | solid | solid | solid (except mercury) |
| Melting point | high | low | moderate | very high | mostly high |
| Conducts when solid? | no | no | no | no (graphite is the exception) | yes |
| Conducts when molten or dissolved? | yes | no | no | — | yes (molten) |
| Reason | ions free to move when molten/dissolved | molecules uncharged | molecules uncharged | no free charged particles | delocalised electrons |

::: terms
- **Melting point**: the temperature at which a solid melts and a liquid freezes.
- **Boiling point**: the temperature at which a liquid boils and a gas condenses.
- **Intermolecular forces**: the weak forces of attraction between separate molecules, overcome when a molecular substance melts or boils.
- **Aqueous**: dissolved in water, shown by the state symbol (aq).
- **Alloy**: a mixture of a metal with other elements, usually other metals, which is harder than the pure metal.
- **Particle model**: a model representing particles as small solid spheres to explain the three states of matter.
- **Bulk properties**: properties such as melting point, colour and conductivity that belong to a material made of many particles, not to single atoms.
- **Polymer**: a substance made of very large molecules of repeating units joined by covalent bonds.
:::

::: method
**The golden rule:** before explaining any property, **name the structure and the particles**, then say **which forces or bonds** are involved, how **strong** they are, and link that to **energy**.

**Explaining melting/boiling points** (the most common 3–4 mark question):
1. Name the structure: "giant ionic lattice" / "small molecules" / "giant covalent" / "giant metallic".
2. Name what must be overcome: "strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions" / "weak intermolecular forces (not covalent bonds)" / "many strong covalent bonds" / "strong metallic bonds".
3. Link to energy: "a lot of energy is needed" or "little energy is needed".
4. Conclude: high or low melting point.

**Explaining conductivity:** say what carries the charge and whether it can move. Ionic: "ions are free to move when molten or dissolved, so charge can flow". Metals: "delocalised electrons carry charge through the metal". Small molecules: "molecules have no overall charge".

**Comparing** (e.g. "Compare the melting points of sodium chloride and chlorine"): explain **both** substances and use comparatives (stronger/weaker, more/less energy).

**Predicting states:** compare temperature with mp and bp and state the answer clearly; show values.

**State symbols:** water and other pure liquids (l); solutions (aq); precipitates and metals (s).

**Alloys (2–3 marks):** different sized atoms → distort the layers → layers cannot slide as easily → harder.

**Higher tier limitations:** state the three — no forces, all spheres, solid spheres — and say why each matters.

**6-mark extended response** (e.g. "Explain the differences in properties of sodium chloride, methane and diamond"): Level 3 explains melting point **and** conductivity for each, linked to structure and bonding, with correct use of "intermolecular forces" for methane. Level 2 covers most properties with some explanation. Level 1 states properties with little explanation.
:::

::: example Melting point — ionic
Explain why magnesium oxide has a high melting point. (3)

Magnesium oxide has a giant ionic lattice (1). There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions (Mg²⁺ and O²⁻) in all directions (1). A large amount of energy is needed to overcome these many strong bonds, so the melting point is high (1).
:::

::: example Melting point — small molecules
Explain why methane has a low boiling point. Refer to the bonds and forces in methane. (3)

Methane is made of small molecules (1). There are only weak intermolecular forces between the molecules (1). Little energy is needed to overcome these forces; the strong covalent bonds within the molecules are not broken when it boils (1).
:::

::: example Conductivity of ionic compounds
Explain why sodium chloride conducts electricity when molten but not when solid. (3)

In the solid the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice and cannot move (1). When molten the ions are free to move (1), so they can carry charge through the liquid (1).
:::

::: example Alloys
Explain why steel is harder than pure iron. (3)

In pure iron the atoms are arranged in layers that can slide over each other (1). In steel, atoms of other elements have different sizes, which distort the layers (1). This makes it more difficult for the layers to slide, so the alloy is harder (1).
:::

::: example Predicting states
Substance A has mp −218 °C and bp −183 °C. Substance B has mp 44 °C and bp 280 °C. State the state of each at 20 °C and suggest the structure of A. (3)

A: 20 °C is above its boiling point → gas (1). B: 20 °C is below its melting point → solid (1). A has very low melting and boiling points, so it is made of small molecules (it is oxygen) (1).
:::

::: watch
- For small molecules, say **intermolecular forces** are overcome. Saying "covalent bonds break" when a molecular substance melts or boils is the single most common lost mark.
- "Ionic compounds have strong **intermolecular** forces" is wrong — they have **no molecules**. Say **strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions**.
- Ionic compounds conduct when molten or dissolved because **ions** move — not electrons.
- Always link to **energy**: "a lot of energy is needed to overcome…".
- Alloys: **different-sized atoms distort the layers** so they **cannot slide** as easily. Just "alloys are mixtures" scores nothing.
- Giant covalent: **many strong covalent bonds must be broken** — do not mention intermolecular forces.
- Water in an equation is (l), not (aq).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) The particle model represents particles as solid spheres with no forces between them. Explain why this model cannot explain why sodium chloride melts at 801 °C but chlorine melts at −101 °C. (3)
---
The model has no forces between the particles, but the melting point depends on the strength of the forces between particles (1). It represents all particles as identical spheres, so it does not distinguish between ions in a lattice (NaCl) and molecules (Cl₂) (1). So it cannot show that NaCl has strong ionic bonds while chlorine has only weak intermolecular forces (1).

Indicative content: sodium chloride — giant ionic lattice; strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions in all directions; large amount of energy to overcome, so high melting point; does not conduct when solid because ions are fixed; conducts when molten or dissolved because ions are free to move and carry charge.
