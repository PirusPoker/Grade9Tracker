## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection (AQA 4.5.2, chemistry only) is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. Trilogy students can skip it. The fuel cell half equations are **(Higher tier)**.

A **cell** contains chemicals that react to produce **electricity**. The chemical energy store of the reactants is transferred electrically to a circuit. A **battery** is two or more cells joined together.

### A simple cell

Make a simple cell by putting **two different metals** into an **electrolyte** (a solution containing ions, such as salt water or dilute acid, or even a lemon) and connecting them with a wire through a voltmeter.

- The **more reactive** metal loses electrons more easily, so it becomes the **negative** electrode. Electrons flow from it, through the external wire, to the **less reactive** metal (the **positive** electrode).
- The **voltage** (potential difference) depends on several factors, including the **type of electrodes** (which metals) and the **type of electrolyte** (and its concentration).
- The **bigger the difference in reactivity** between the two metals, the **bigger the voltage**.
- Two electrodes of the **same metal** give **0 V**: there is no difference in reactivity, so no tendency for electrons to flow one way.

**Interpreting data (relative reactivity).** Typical voltages for cells with a copper electrode and a second metal (same electrolyte; values for illustration):

| Second metal | Voltage with copper (V) |
|---|---|
| magnesium | 2.7 |
| zinc | 1.1 |
| iron | 0.8 |
| lead | 0.5 |
| copper | 0.0 |

The larger the voltage, the further the metal is above copper in the reactivity series, so the order is magnesium > zinc > iron > lead > copper. You can also **combine** values: a magnesium–zinc cell gives about $2.7 - 1.1 = 1.6$ V, because both are measured against copper.

If a metal is **less** reactive than copper (for example silver), the voltage reading is **negative** when connected the same way round, because the electrons now flow the other way: copper becomes the negative electrode.

### Batteries

**Batteries consist of two or more cells connected in series** to provide a greater voltage. The voltages add up: four 1.5 V cells in series give $4 \times 1.5 = 6.0$ V.

### Non-rechargeable and rechargeable cells

| | Non-rechargeable | Rechargeable |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | the chemical reactions **stop when one of the reactants has been used up** | the chemical reactions are **reversed when an external electrical current is supplied** |
| Example | **alkaline batteries** | the cells in mobile phones, laptops and electric cars (for example lithium-ion) |
| After use | thrown away (should be recycled) | recharged hundreds of times, then eventually replaced |

You do not need to know the chemistry inside particular cells beyond this.

**Evaluating the use of cells:** consider voltage needed, cost, how long it lasts, whether it is rechargeable, mass/size, and the **environmental impact** (mining the metals, toxic or flammable chemicals, disposal and recycling). Non-rechargeable cells are cheap and convenient but create a lot of waste; rechargeable cells cost more at first but are cheaper over their lifetime and produce less waste.

### Fuel cells

A **fuel cell** is supplied with a **fuel from an external source** (for example **hydrogen**) and **oxygen or air**. The fuel is **oxidised electrochemically** inside the cell to produce a **potential difference**. Unlike an ordinary cell, it does not run flat while the fuel keeps flowing.

In a **hydrogen fuel cell** the overall reaction is the **oxidation of hydrogen to produce water**:

$$2\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$

This is the same overall reaction as burning hydrogen, but the energy is transferred **electrically** rather than mainly by heating. The only product is water.

How it works: hydrogen goes in at the negative electrode, oxygen at the positive electrode, with an electrolyte (often an acid, or a polymer membrane that lets H⁺ ions through) between them. Electrons released from hydrogen flow round the external circuit, powering a motor or device, and are used up at the oxygen electrode.

### Half equations in the hydrogen fuel cell (Higher tier)

With an **acidic** electrolyte:

| Electrode | Half equation | Type |
|---|---|---|
| **negative** electrode (hydrogen side) | $2\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^-$ | **oxidation** (loss of electrons) |
| **positive** electrode (oxygen side) | $\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | **reduction** (gain of electrons) |

Adding the two half equations, the 4H⁺ and 4e⁻ cancel, leaving $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$. You may also see the hydrogen half equation written as $\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-$, which is equally correct.

If the electrolyte is **alkaline** (contains OH⁻ ions), the half equations are $2\text{H}_2 + 4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 4\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^-$ and $\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^-$. Either version is accepted as long as it is balanced for atoms **and** charge.

Remember **OIL RIG**: Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons), Reduction Is Gain. Hydrogen is oxidised; oxygen is reduced.

### Hydrogen fuel cells compared with rechargeable batteries

Hydrogen fuel cells offer a **potential alternative** to rechargeable cells and batteries (for example in cars, buses and as backup power).

| Hydrogen fuel cells | Rechargeable batteries |
|---|---|
| **Only product is water**: no carbon dioxide or pollutants **at the point of use** | no pollutants at the point of use either |
| refuelled in minutes; give a continuous supply while fuel is supplied | recharging takes a long time (often hours) |
| do not lose capacity with repeated use in the same way | can only be recharged a limited number of times; capacity falls with age |
| fewer toxic metal compounds to dispose of | contain toxic or rare metal compounds; disposal and recycling are difficult |
| hydrogen is a **flammable gas**, hard to store: needs high pressure tanks and takes up a large volume | stored electricity is easy and safe to handle; charging points are widespread |
| hydrogen is usually made from **fossil fuels** (natural gas) or by **electrolysis** of water, which uses electricity that may come from fossil fuels | the electricity for charging may also come from fossil fuels |
| fuel cells are expensive (they use precious metal catalysts); few hydrogen filling stations | batteries are cheaper and mass-produced; can store electricity from renewables |

An **evaluation** weighs these up and decides: for example, "fuel cells are better for long-distance lorries because refuelling is fast and they give a long range, but only if the hydrogen is made using renewable electricity; for most cars, batteries are currently more practical because charging points already exist."

::: terms
- **Cell**: a device containing chemicals that react to produce electricity (a potential difference).
- **Battery**: two or more cells connected together in series to give a greater voltage.
- **Electrolyte**: a liquid or solution containing ions that can move, placed between the electrodes of a cell.
- **Non-rechargeable cell**: a cell whose reactions stop when one reactant is used up, such as an alkaline battery.
- **Rechargeable cell**: a cell whose chemical reactions are reversed when an external electrical current is supplied.
- **Fuel cell**: a cell supplied with fuel (such as hydrogen) and oxygen from outside, in which the fuel is oxidised electrochemically.
- **Oxidation**: loss of electrons (or gain of oxygen); hydrogen is oxidised in a hydrogen fuel cell.
- **Half equation**: an equation showing the electrons lost or gained at one electrode, balanced for atoms and charge.
:::

::: method
**Command words.**
- **State / give**: short facts ("batteries are cells connected in series").
- **Explain** why a cell gives a voltage, or why a bigger voltage: link to **difference in reactivity** of the two metals ("zinc and copper are further apart in the reactivity series than iron and copper, so the voltage is bigger").
- **Suggest** why one reading is 0 V: same metal for both electrodes, or no electrolyte, or a broken circuit.
- **Use the data**: put metals in reactivity order from voltages, or predict a missing voltage by subtracting two readings measured against the same metal. Show the subtraction.
- **Evaluate** fuel cells vs rechargeable batteries: you must give **advantages and disadvantages of both** and then a **conclusion with a reason**. Use the information given; quote figures (range, refuelling time, cost).

**Half equations (Higher tier).** Hydrogen at the negative electrode **loses** electrons: $2\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^-$. Oxygen at the positive electrode **gains** electrons: $\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Check: atoms balance, total charge the same on both sides, electrons on the right for oxidation and on the left for reduction.

**Required practicals.** There is no required practical in this subsection. Questions may still describe a simple-cell experiment: the **independent variable** is the metal pair (or the electrolyte), the **dependent variable** is the voltage, and **control variables** include the electrolyte and its concentration, the size of the electrodes and the distance between them, and the temperature.

**6-mark extended response.** Typically "Evaluate the use of hydrogen fuel cells compared with rechargeable batteries for powering cars." Level 3 needs **both sides** covered with linked reasoning (e.g. "only water is produced, **but** hydrogen is often made from natural gas, **so** carbon dioxide is still released overall") and a **justified conclusion**. A one-sided list stays in Level 2 at best.
:::

::: example Reading a voltage table
Voltages for cells made with copper and another metal: tin 0.48 V, nickel 0.59 V, zinc 1.10 V, magnesium 2.71 V. (a) Put the metals, including copper, in order of reactivity. (b) Predict the voltage of a zinc–nickel cell. (3)

(a) magnesium > zinc > nickel > tin > copper (1) (the bigger the voltage with copper, the more reactive the metal).
(b) Both are measured against copper, so voltage $= 1.10 - 0.59 = $ **0.51 V** (1)(1).
:::

::: example Batteries
A torch needs 4.5 V. Each cell gives 1.5 V. How many cells are needed, and how should they be connected? (2)

$4.5 \div 1.5 = $ **3 cells** (1), connected **in series** so their voltages add up (1).
:::

::: example Why rechargeable cells can be recharged
Explain why an alkaline battery stops working and cannot be recharged, but a lithium-ion battery can be recharged. (3)

In the alkaline battery the chemical reaction stops when one of the reactants has been **used up** (1). In a rechargeable cell, supplying an **external electric current** (1) **reverses** the chemical reactions, re-forming the reactants (1).
:::

::: example Half equations (Higher tier)
Write the half equations for the reactions at the two electrodes of a hydrogen fuel cell with an acidic electrolyte, and identify which is oxidation. (3)

Negative electrode: $2\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^-$ (1). This is **oxidation**, because hydrogen loses electrons (1).
Positive electrode: $\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (1).
:::

::: example Evaluating a fuel-cell bus
A city replaces diesel buses with hydrogen fuel cell buses. The hydrogen is made by electrolysis of water using electricity from wind turbines. Evaluate this decision. (4)

For: the only product at the point of use is water, so there is no carbon dioxide or local air pollution in the city (1); the hydrogen is made using renewable electricity, so the whole process releases very little carbon dioxide (1). Against: hydrogen is a flammable gas that must be stored in high-pressure tanks, and the buses and refuelling station are expensive (1). Conclusion: a good decision, because using renewable electricity removes the main environmental drawback of hydrogen, and buses return to one depot where refuelling can be managed safely (1).
:::

What is needed to make a simple chemical cell? (2)
---
Two **different** metals (electrodes) (1) in contact with an **electrolyte** (1).
:::

::: q
Give two factors that affect the voltage produced by a cell. (2)
---
Any two: the type of metals used as electrodes (difference in reactivity); the type of electrolyte; the concentration of the electrolyte; temperature (1)(1).
:::

::: q
What is a battery? (1)
---
Two or more cells connected together **in series** to provide a greater voltage.

::: q
A student makes a cell with two copper electrodes in salt water. The voltmeter reads 0 V. Explain why. (2)
---
Both electrodes are the **same metal** (1), so there is no difference in reactivity and no tendency for electrons to flow from one to the other (1).
:::

::: q
Why does a non-rechargeable cell eventually stop producing electricity? (1)
---
The chemical reaction stops when one of the **reactants has been used up**.
:::

::: q
Name the product of the overall reaction in a hydrogen fuel cell and write the balanced equation. (2)
---
**Water** (1). $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (1).
:::

::: q
Give two advantages and two disadvantages of hydrogen fuel cells compared with rechargeable batteries. (4)
---
Advantages (any two): only water produced; quick to refuel; continuous supply while fuel is supplied; not limited by a number of recharge cycles; fewer toxic metals to dispose of (1)(1). Disadvantages (any two): hydrogen is flammable/explosive; hydrogen is hard to store (high pressure, large volume); hydrogen is usually made from fossil fuels or with electricity from fossil fuels; fuel cells are expensive; few refuelling stations (1)(1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) Complete and balance the half equation at the positive electrode of an acidic hydrogen fuel cell: O₂ + ... H⁺ + ... e⁻ → ... H₂O. (2)
---
$\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (1 for 4H⁺ and 4e⁻, 1 for 2H₂O).
:::

::: q
A magnesium–copper cell gives 2.7 V and an iron–copper cell gives 0.8 V. Estimate the voltage of a magnesium–iron cell and say which electrode is negative. (3)
---
$2.7 - 0.8 = $ **1.9 V** (1)(1). **Magnesium** is the negative electrode because it is more reactive (it loses electrons more easily) (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"Two metals" without "different".** A cell needs two **different** metals; identical electrodes give 0 V.
- **Parallel instead of series.** Cells in a battery are connected **in series**; that is why the voltages add.
- **One-sided evaluations.** "Fuel cells only make water" alone is not an evaluation. Add how the hydrogen is made and stored, compare with batteries, and finish with a judgement.
- **Saying fuel cells produce no pollution at all.** No pollution **at the point of use**; making hydrogen from natural gas or with fossil-fuel electricity still releases carbon dioxide.
- **(Higher tier) Unbalanced charge in half equations.** Count charges: $\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^-$ has total charge 0, matching 2H₂O.
- **(Higher tier) Electrons on the wrong side.** Oxidation (hydrogen) has electrons on the **right**; reduction (oxygen) has them on the **left**.
:::

::: test
Voltages measured with metal X as one electrode: X–zinc 0.30 V, X–copper 1.40 V, X–magnesium 1.30 V. In every cell, X was the negative electrode except with magnesium. Use the data to place X, zinc, copper and magnesium in order of reactivity, and explain your reasoning. (4)
---
X is negative with zinc and copper, so X is **more reactive** than both (1). Magnesium is negative with X, so magnesium is **more reactive than X** (1). The X–copper voltage is bigger than the X–zinc voltage, so copper is further below X than zinc, so zinc is more reactive than copper (1). Order: **magnesium > X > zinc > copper** (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Write the half equation for the reaction at the negative electrode of a hydrogen fuel cell, and explain why it is described as oxidation. (2)
---
$\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-$ (or $2\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^-$) (1). Hydrogen **loses electrons**, and loss of electrons is oxidation (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) In an alkaline hydrogen fuel cell, the reaction at the positive electrode is O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. The hydroxide ions react with hydrogen at the negative electrode to form water. Write a balanced half equation for the negative electrode, and show that the two half equations add up to the overall reaction. (3)
---
$2\text{H}_2 + 4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 4\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^-$ (1). Adding: $2\text{H}_2 + 4\text{OH}^- + \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^- \rightarrow 4\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^- + 4\text{OH}^-$ (1). Cancelling 4OH⁻, 4e⁻ and 2H₂O from both sides leaves $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (1).
:::

::: test
A delivery company is choosing between battery-powered and hydrogen fuel cell vans. Battery van: range 250 km, recharge time 6 hours, running cost 4p per km. Fuel cell van: range 600 km, refuel time 5 minutes, running cost 9p per km. Evaluate the use of hydrogen fuel cells compared with rechargeable batteries for these vans. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a balanced evaluation covering both technologies, using the data and chemical knowledge, with a justified conclusion. **Level 2 (3–4):** advantages and disadvantages of both, with some use of data, but the conclusion is missing or unsupported. **Level 1 (1–2):** simple statements about one technology.

Indicative content. Fuel cell: range more than twice as long (600 km vs 250 km); refuels in 5 minutes instead of 6 hours, so vans can work longer shifts; only water is produced at the point of use; not limited by a number of recharge cycles. But: more than twice the running cost per km; hydrogen is flammable and must be stored under high pressure; few refuelling stations; hydrogen is often made from natural gas, releasing carbon dioxide, unless made by electrolysis using renewable electricity. Battery: cheaper to run; charging points are widespread and vans can charge overnight at the depot; no emissions at the point of use; electricity can come from renewables. But: long recharge time, shorter range, batteries degrade after many charges and contain metals that are hard to recycle. Conclusion with reason: e.g. battery vans for short urban rounds where 250 km is enough and overnight charging is possible; fuel cell vans for long-distance routes where range and quick refuelling matter more than cost.
:::
