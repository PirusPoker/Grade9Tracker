## What it is

The **rate of reaction** is how quickly reactants are used up or products are made. This topic (AQA 4.6.1) is in both GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (numbered 5.6.1 there). Rates in **mol/s** and the **gradient of a tangent** are **(Higher tier)**.

### Calculating the mean rate

$$\text{mean rate of reaction} = \frac{\text{quantity of reactant used}}{\text{time taken}} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \text{mean rate of reaction} = \frac{\text{quantity of product formed}}{\text{time taken}}$$

| Quantity measured | How | Unit of rate |
|---|---|---|
| mass (g) | mass lost on a balance as a gas escapes | **g/s** |
| volume of gas (cm³) | gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder | **cm³/s** |
| amount in moles (mol) **(Higher tier)** | convert mass using $n = \text{mass} \div M_r$ | **mol/s** |

**Mini-examples.**
- 48 cm³ of hydrogen is collected in 2 minutes. Rate $= 48 \div 120 = $ **0.40 cm³/s** (convert minutes to seconds first).
- A flask loses 1.32 g in 60 s. Rate $= 1.32 \div 60 = $ **0.022 g/s**.
- **(Higher tier)** 0.88 g of carbon dioxide ($M_r$ 44) escapes in 40 s. Moles $= 0.88 \div 44 = 0.020$ mol; rate $= 0.020 \div 40 = $ **5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/s**.

### Graphs of rate

Plot **quantity of product formed** (or reactant used up) on the y-axis against **time** on the x-axis (MS 4a, 4c).

- The curve is **steepest at the start**, when the reactants are most concentrated, so the rate is highest.
- It gets **less steep** as reactants are used up: the rate falls.
- It becomes **flat (horizontal)** when the reaction has **stopped** because one reactant has run out.
- A **faster** reaction gives a steeper curve that levels off **sooner**. If the same amounts of reactants are used, it levels off at the **same final height** (same amount of product). More reactant gives a **higher** final level.

**The rate at a particular time** is the **slope of a tangent** to the curve at that time. Draw a straight line that just touches the curve at that point, with the same steepness as the curve there.

**Gradient of a tangent (Higher tier).** Make a big right-angled triangle on the tangent and calculate:

$$\text{rate} = \text{gradient} = \frac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}$$

Example: a tangent drawn at 20 s passes through (5 s, 16 cm³) and (35 s, 52 cm³). Gradient $= (52 - 16) \div (35 - 5) = 36 \div 30 = $ **1.2 cm³/s**. Use points far apart on the tangent (not points on the curve) to reduce reading error.

### Factors that affect the rate

| Increase this... | ...and the rate | Collision theory explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **concentration** of a solution | increases | more reactant particles in the same volume, so **more frequent collisions** |
| **pressure** of a gas | increases | particles closer together (more per unit volume), so **more frequent collisions** |
| **surface area** of a solid (smaller pieces, powder) | increases | more particles exposed on the surface, so **more frequent collisions** |
| **temperature** | increases | particles move faster, so **more frequent collisions** **and** collisions are **more energetic**: a greater proportion have at least the activation energy |
| **catalyst** present | increases | provides a different pathway with a **lower activation energy** |

### Collision theory and activation energy

Reactions can only happen when reacting particles **collide** with each other **with sufficient energy**. The minimum energy needed is the **activation energy**. Most collisions do not have enough energy and the particles just bounce apart. So the rate depends on the **frequency of successful collisions** (collisions per second with energy at least equal to the activation energy).

- Concentration, pressure and surface area change only **how often** particles collide.
- Temperature changes **both** how often particles collide **and** how energetic the collisions are. The energy effect is the bigger of the two, so a small rise in temperature can increase the rate a lot.

**Write "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per second"**, not just "more collisions": a bigger amount of reactant gives more collisions in total but not necessarily faster.

**Proportionality (MS 1c).** In simple cases, if you double the concentration of a reactant in solution, there are twice as many particles in the same volume, so the frequency of collisions doubles, and the rate **doubles**: rate is **directly proportional** to concentration. On a graph of rate against concentration this gives a **straight line through the origin**.

**Surface area to volume ratio (MS 5c).** Cutting a solid into smaller pieces increases the surface area to volume ratio, exposing more particles.

| Pieces | Total surface area (cm²) | Total volume (cm³) | SA : V |
|---|---|---|---|
| one 2 cm cube | $6 \times 2 \times 2 = 24$ | 8 | 3 : 1 |
| eight 1 cm cubes | $8 \times 6 \times 1 \times 1 = 48$ | 8 | 6 : 1 |

Halving the side length doubles the surface area to volume ratio, so the frequency of collisions at the surface (and the rate) roughly doubles. Powders react fastest; this is why flour dust and coal dust can explode.

### Catalysts

A **catalyst** changes (increases) the rate of a chemical reaction but is **not used up** during the reaction. It is chemically unchanged at the end, so only small amounts are needed and it can be reused. Different reactions need **different catalysts**. **Enzymes** are catalysts in biological systems.

A catalyst works by providing a **different pathway** (route) for the reaction that has a **lower activation energy**. More collisions now have enough energy to react, so the frequency of successful collisions increases. A catalyst does **not** change the overall energy change of the reaction.

**Identifying a catalyst.** A substance is a catalyst if it **speeds up the reaction** but is **not included in the chemical equation** (and its mass is the same at the end). For example, adding manganese(IV) oxide to hydrogen peroxide makes oxygen bubble off much faster, but the equation is just $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2$. You only need the names of catalysts that the specification mentions elsewhere; any others will be given in the question. A useful investigation (AT 5) compares different metal salts or oxides as catalysts for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide by measuring the volume of oxygen given off.

### Required practical 5 (Trilogy: required practical 11): rate and concentration

**Required practical 5: investigate how changes in concentration affect the rates of reactions by a method involving measuring the volume of a gas produced and a method involving a change in colour or turbidity.** In Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 this is **Required practical activity 11**. It should be an investigation that **develops a hypothesis**, for example: "As the concentration of hydrochloric acid increases, the rate increases, because there are more acid particles in the same volume, so collisions are more frequent."

**Method 1: volume of gas.** Magnesium ribbon (or marble chips) with hydrochloric acid of different concentrations.
1. Measure 50 cm³ of acid into a conical flask with a measuring cylinder.
2. Connect a bung and delivery tube to a **gas syringe** (or an upturned measuring cylinder full of water in a trough).
3. Add a fixed length of magnesium ribbon (e.g. 3 cm), replace the bung quickly and start the stopwatch.
4. Record the volume of hydrogen every 10 s until no more gas is produced.
5. Repeat with different concentrations of acid; plot volume against time for each. Compare initial gradients or the time to collect a fixed volume.

**Method 2: change in turbidity (cloudiness).** Sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid: $\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl} + \text{SO}_2 + \text{S} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. The sulfur forms a pale yellow precipitate that makes the mixture cloudy.
1. Draw a black cross on paper and put a conical flask on it.
2. Add a measured volume of sodium thiosulfate solution (diluted with water to vary concentration, keeping the total volume the same).
3. Add a measured volume of hydrochloric acid, swirl and start the stopwatch.
4. Looking down from above, stop the clock when the **cross can no longer be seen**.
5. Repeat for each concentration. Calculate **rate = 1 ÷ time** (unit s⁻¹ or /s).

Example results: times 100 s, 50 s, 33 s and 25 s for concentrations 0.04, 0.08, 0.12 and 0.16 mol/dm³ give rates 0.010, 0.020, 0.030 and 0.040 /s: doubling the concentration doubles the rate.

| Variable type | Gas method | Cloudiness method |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | concentration of acid | concentration of sodium thiosulfate |
| Dependent | volume of gas in a given time (or time for a fixed volume) | time for the cross to disappear |
| Control | volume of acid, length/mass of magnesium, temperature, size of flask | total volume, volume and concentration of acid, temperature, same cross and flask, same observer |

**Errors and improvements.**
- Gas escapes before the bung is replaced, giving low readings: use a divided flask or put the magnesium in a small tube that is tipped over after the bung is in.
- A gas syringe is more precise than counting bubbles or a measuring cylinder.
- Judging when the cross disappears is **subjective** (different people decide at different moments): use the same person, or use a **light sensor/colorimeter** and data logger.
- Temperature changes alter the rate: use a water bath.
- Repeat each concentration and take a mean to improve repeatability.

**Risks.** Hydrochloric acid is an irritant: wear eye protection. Hydrogen is flammable: no naked flames. The thiosulfate reaction produces **sulfur dioxide**, a toxic gas that can trigger asthma: use a well-ventilated room, small volumes, and dispose of the mixture promptly.

::: terms
- **Rate of reaction**: how quickly a reactant is used up or a product is formed, measured per unit time.
- **Mean rate**: the quantity of reactant used or product formed divided by the total time taken.
- **Collision theory**: the idea that particles react only when they collide with each other with sufficient energy.
- **Activation energy**: the minimum amount of energy that particles must have when they collide in order to react.
- **Successful collision**: a collision between reactant particles that has at least the activation energy and so leads to reaction.
- **Catalyst**: a substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being used up, by providing a pathway with a lower activation energy.
- **Enzyme**: a protein that acts as a catalyst in biological systems.
- **Tangent**: a straight line that touches a curve at one point with the same gradient; its slope gives the rate at that time.
- **Turbidity**: cloudiness of a liquid caused by a precipitate, used to follow the sodium thiosulfate reaction.
:::

::: method
**Command words.**
- **Describe** a graph or trend: say what happens, with data ("the volume increases quickly at first, then more slowly, and stays at 58 cm³ after 70 s").
- **Explain** a rate change: use the full collision theory chain, **factor → particles → frequency (and energy) of collisions → rate**. For temperature, give **both** effects: particles move faster so collide more often, **and** more collisions have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy.
- **Predict** a new curve: same final height if the same amount of limiting reactant; steeper if faster; higher final level if more of the limiting reactant.
- **Suggest** improvements to a practical: gas syringe, light sensor, water bath, repeats, same observer.

**Calculations.** Equation → substitute (with **seconds**, not minutes) → answer with **unit** (g/s, cm³/s, mol/s) and sensible significant figures (match the data, usually 2 or 3 s.f.). For mol/s **(Higher tier)**, convert mass to moles with $n = m \div M_r$ first. For a **tangent (Higher tier)**, draw it, make a large triangle, read two points **on the tangent**, and divide change in y by change in x. For the cloudiness method, rate $= 1 \div$ time.

**Required practical 5 (Trilogy 11) questions.** Know both methods, the variables, why the thiosulfate method is subjective, why total volume is kept constant (so only concentration changes), what the hypothesis is, and the safety point about sulfur dioxide. Be ready to plot a graph (rate or 1/time against concentration) and describe the relationship (directly proportional if a straight line through the origin).

**6-mark extended response.** Often "Plan an investigation into the effect of concentration on rate" or "Explain the effect of temperature, surface area and a catalyst on rate." Level 3 needs **linked** reasoning in a logical order or a complete workable method (equipment, quantities, control variables, measurements, repeats, processing the results). Lists of disconnected facts stay in Level 1–2.
:::

::: example Mean rate
Marble chips react with acid. In the first 50 s, 0.65 g of carbon dioxide is lost. Calculate the mean rate in g/s. (2)

rate $= 0.65 \div 50$ (1) $= $ **0.013 g/s** (1)
:::

::: example Rate in mol/s (Higher tier)
0.12 g of magnesium ($A_r$ 24) reacts completely with excess acid in 60 s. Calculate the mean rate of reaction in mol/s. (3)

moles Mg $= 0.12 \div 24 = 0.0050$ mol (1)
rate $= 0.0050 \div 60$ (1) $= $ **8.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol/s** (1)
:::

::: example Gradient of a tangent (Higher tier)
A tangent drawn to a volume–time curve at 30 s passes through (10 s, 18 cm³) and (50 s, 46 cm³). Calculate the rate at 30 s. (2)

gradient $= (46 - 18) \div (50 - 10) = 28 \div 40$ (1) $= $ **0.70 cm³/s** (1)
:::

::: example Explaining temperature
Explain why increasing the temperature increases the rate of reaction between magnesium and hydrochloric acid. (3)

Particles have more energy and **move faster**, so they **collide more frequently** (1). A **greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy** (1). So there are more successful collisions per second, and the rate increases (1).
:::

::: example Surface area to volume ratio
A 1 cm cube of calcium carbonate is cut into 1000 cubes, each with side 0.1 cm. Calculate the surface area to volume ratio before and after, and explain the effect on rate. (4)

Before: SA $= 6 \times 1 \times 1 = 6$ cm², V $= 1$ cm³, ratio **6 : 1** (1).
After: SA $= 1000 \times 6 \times 0.1 \times 0.1 = 60$ cm², V still 1 cm³, ratio **60 : 1** (1).
The ratio is 10 times bigger, so more particles are exposed to the acid (1), collisions are more frequent and the rate increases (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"More collisions" instead of "more frequent collisions".** The mark is for the **frequency** (collisions per second).
- **Temperature with only one effect.** You need both: more frequent collisions **and** more collisions with at least the activation energy (more energetic collisions).
- **Saying catalysts "give particles more energy".** They **lower the activation energy** by providing a different pathway; they do not add energy.
- **Minutes left in the rate.** Convert to seconds before dividing when the unit is per second.
- **(Higher tier) Gradient from points on the curve.** Read the two points from the **tangent line**, as far apart as possible.
- **Changing the final height.** Faster reactions do **not** make more product. The curve levels off at the same height unless the amount of limiting reactant changes.
- **Sulfur dioxide safety.** In the thiosulfate practical, the risk is toxic SO₂ gas: say "ventilated room", not just "wear goggles".
:::

::: test
A student says: "Raising the temperature by 10 °C doubles the rate because the particles collide twice as often." Evaluate this statement. (3)
---
The rate often roughly doubles for a 10 °C rise, so the first part can be correct (1). But a 10 °C rise only increases collision frequency slightly; it is not doubled (1). The main reason is that particles have more energy, so a much greater proportion of collisions have energy at least equal to the activation energy, so the number of successful collisions per second increases greatly (1).
