### Using the Earth's resources and sustainable development

Humans use the Earth's resources to provide **warmth, shelter, food and transport**. **Natural resources, supplemented by agriculture**, provide **food, timber, clothing and fuels**. **Finite resources** from the Earth, oceans and atmosphere are **processed to provide energy and materials**.

| Type of resource | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Finite** (non-renewable) | will run out; not replaced at a useful rate, or there is a fixed amount | fossil fuels (coal, crude oil, natural gas), metal ores, limestone, sand for glass, phosphate rock |
| **Renewable** | replaced as fast as we use it (if managed properly) | timber from managed forests, crops, wool, cotton, fresh water from rain, biofuels |

To decide if a resource is finite or renewable from given information, ask: **can it be replaced within a human lifetime at the rate we use it?** Crude oil took millions of years to form, so it is finite; wood from a forest where trees are replanted is renewable.

**Natural products supplemented or replaced by agricultural and synthetic products:**

| Natural product | Agricultural or synthetic replacement |
|---|---|
| wool, silk, cotton (clothing) | synthetic fibres such as polyester and nylon (made from crude oil) |
| natural rubber (from rubber trees) | synthetic rubber |
| timber for building or furniture | plastics, composites (e.g. MDF), metals |
| wild-caught food, wild plants | farmed crops and livestock |
| animal manure | synthetic NPK fertilisers (topic 4.10.4) |
| natural dyes (e.g. indigo from plants) | synthetic dyes |
| fossil fuels | biofuels such as ethanol from sugar cane, biodiesel |

**Chemistry and sustainable development.** Chemistry improves agricultural and industrial processes to make new products and plays an important role in **sustainable development**: **development that meets the needs of current generations without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.** In practice this means using less energy and fewer finite resources, producing less waste, recycling, and using renewable raw materials and catalysts.

### Reading resource data and orders of magnitude (MS 2c, 2h, 4a, WS 3.2)

You may be asked to extract information from charts, graphs and tables, or use **orders of magnitude** (powers of ten) to judge whether a number is significant.

**Mini-example:** world reserves of copper are about $1 \times 10^9$ tonnes and annual use is about $2 \times 10^7$ tonnes. Years remaining $\approx \frac{1 \times 10^9}{2 \times 10^7} = 50$ years — an order of magnitude of $10^1$ to $10^2$ years, so copper is a **finite resource that could run low within a lifetime**, which is why recycling and new extraction methods matter.

### Potable water

**Water of appropriate quality is essential for life.** For humans, drinking water should have **sufficiently low levels of dissolved salts and microbes**. Water that is safe to drink is called **potable water**.

**Potable water is not pure water** in the chemical sense, because it **contains dissolved substances** (salts such as calcium and magnesium compounds, and small amounts of chlorine). **Pure water** contains **only water molecules** — it boils at exactly 100 °C and leaves no residue when evaporated.

| | Pure water | Potable water |
|---|---|---|
| Contains | only H₂O | water plus low levels of dissolved salts; very few microbes |
| Safe to drink? | yes (but tasteless) | yes |
| Boiling point | exactly 100 °C | slightly above 100 °C |
| Residue after evaporating | none | a small amount of solid |

**The methods used depend on available supplies of water and local conditions.**

**In the UK**, **rain** provides water with low levels of dissolved substances (**fresh water**) that collects **in the ground** (aquifers) and in **lakes and rivers**. Most potable water is produced by:

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| 1. **Choose an appropriate source** of fresh water | low levels of dissolved salts and pollutants, reliable supply |
| 2. **Pass the water through filter beds** (layers of sand and gravel) | **removes solid particles** (insoluble material) |
| 3. **Sterilise** | **kills microbes** (bacteria and other pathogens) |

**Sterilising agents** include **chlorine**, **ozone** or **ultraviolet (UV) light**. Chlorine is cheap and stays in the water to keep it safe in the pipes, but is toxic in large amounts and can affect taste; ozone and UV leave no taste but give no lasting protection.

**Desalination.** If supplies of fresh water are limited (e.g. in hot, dry countries such as Saudi Arabia, or on islands), **desalination** of **salty water or sea water** may be required:

- **Distillation** — sea water is **boiled**; the steam is **condensed** to give water without the salts.
- **Reverse osmosis** — sea water is forced at **high pressure** through a **membrane** that lets water molecules through but not the salt ions.

**These processes require large amounts of energy** (to heat the water, or to pump it at high pressure), so desalination is **expensive** and, if the energy comes from fossil fuels, adds to carbon dioxide emissions. Reverse osmosis also needs membranes that must be replaced.

**Ground water vs salty water:**

| | Ground (fresh) water | Salty or sea water |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved salts | low | high (sea water about 35 g/dm³) |
| Treatment | filter, then sterilise | **desalination** (distillation or reverse osmosis), then usually sterilise |
| Energy and cost | low | **high** |

### Required practical 8 (Trilogy: Required practical 13): analysis and purification of water samples from different sources, including pH, dissolved solids and distillation

**Aim:** to compare water samples (e.g. tap water, rain water, sea water or salty water, pond/river water) by measuring **pH** and the **mass of dissolved solids**, and to **purify** salty water by **distillation**.

**Method:**
1. **pH:** test a sample of each water with **universal indicator paper** (compare the colour with the chart) or, better, a calibrated **pH probe/meter**, which gives a more precise numerical reading.
2. **Dissolved solids:** weigh a clean, dry **evaporating basin**. Add a measured volume (e.g. **10.0 cm³**, using a measuring cylinder or pipette) of the water sample. Heat gently — ideally over a **water bath** or with a Bunsen at a low flame — until all the water has **evaporated**. Allow to cool, then **reweigh**. Mass of dissolved solids = final mass − mass of empty basin. Repeat to check it is constant.
3. **Distillation:** put salty water in a round-bottomed or conical flask with a few **anti-bumping granules**. Fit a thermometer with its bulb **level with the side arm**, connect a **Liebig condenser** with cold water flowing **in at the bottom and out at the top**, and collect the **distillate** in a beaker or test tube (ideally sitting in ice). Heat until about half the water has distilled. The thermometer should read **100 °C**.
4. Test the **distillate**: its pH should be about **7** and when a sample is evaporated it should leave **no residue** — showing it is pure.

**Variables:** independent — **source of the water sample**; dependent — **pH** and **mass of dissolved solids**; control — **same volume** of each sample, same heating method, same balance.

**Risks and control measures:**

| Hazard | Control |
|---|---|
| hot apparatus and steam — burns | let apparatus cool; handle with tongs; keep hands away from steam |
| liquid **boiling over or "bumping"** violently | anti-bumping granules; heat gently |
| **spitting** of solid as the basin nears dryness | heat gently; use a water bath; stop when nearly dry and let residual heat finish |
| glassware breaking | clamp securely; do not heat a sealed system |
| pond or river water may contain **microbes** | do not drink samples; wash hands |
| general | **eye protection** |

**Typical results:**

| Sample | pH | Dissolved solids (g per 10 cm³) |
|---|---|---|
| distilled water | about 7 | 0.00 |
| tap water | 6.5–8 | about 0.002–0.005 (very small) |
| rain water | 5–6 (slightly acidic: dissolved CO₂) | almost none |
| sea water | about 8 | about 0.35 (≈ 35 g/dm³) |

**Calculation:** concentration of dissolved solids in g/dm³ $= \frac{\text{mass of solid (g)}}{\text{volume (dm}^3)}$. If 25.0 cm³ of sea water leaves 0.88 g, volume $= 25.0 \div 1000 = 0.0250$ dm³, so concentration $= \frac{0.88}{0.0250} = 35$ g/dm³ (2 s.f.).

**Errors and improvements:** the masses of solid from tap water are tiny, so use a **larger volume** and a **balance reading to 0.001 g** to reduce percentage error. Universal indicator gives only whole-number pH and colour judgement is subjective — a **pH meter** is more precise. Repeat each measurement and calculate a mean. Water lost by spitting gives a falsely low mass.

### Waste water treatment

**Urban lifestyles and industrial processes produce large amounts of waste water** that require **treatment before being released into the environment**.

- **Sewage** and **agricultural waste water** require removal of **organic matter and harmful microbes**.
- **Industrial waste water** may require removal of **organic matter and harmful chemicals** (e.g. heavy metals) as well.

**Sewage treatment includes:**

| Stage | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Screening and grit removal** | the sewage passes through a mesh screen; grit and sand settle out | removes large objects (rags, plastics, twigs) and grit that would damage equipment |
| 2. **Sedimentation** | sewage stands in large settlement tanks; heavier solids sink to form **sewage sludge**; the lighter liquid on top is the **effluent** | separates solids from liquid |
| 3. **Anaerobic digestion of sewage sludge** | bacteria break down the organic matter in the sludge **without oxygen** | produces **biogas (methane)**, which can be burned to generate electricity, and digested sludge that can be used as **fertiliser** |
| 4. **Aerobic biological treatment of effluent** | **air is bubbled through** the effluent so **aerobic bacteria** break down the remaining organic matter and kill harmful microbes | the treated water is safe to release into rivers or the sea |

**Relative ease of obtaining potable water:**

| Source | Treatment needed | Ease |
|---|---|---|
| **Ground water** (and lakes, rivers) | filtration and sterilisation only | **easiest** — few stages, little energy |
| **Waste water** | screening, sedimentation, anaerobic digestion, aerobic treatment, then further filtration and sterilisation (and removal of chemicals if industrial) | **harder** — many stages and processes; public may be reluctant to drink recycled water |
| **Salt (sea) water** | desalination by distillation or reverse osmosis | **hardest in terms of energy and cost** — needs very large amounts of energy |

Waste water is used where fresh water is scarce (e.g. Singapore, Namibia), and it uses less energy than desalination, but more treatment stages are needed than for ground water.

### Alternative methods of extracting metals (Higher tier)

**The Earth's resources of metal ores are limited.** **Copper ores are becoming scarce**, and new ways of extracting copper from **low-grade ores** (ores with only a small percentage of copper) include **phytomining** and **bioleaching**. These methods **avoid traditional mining methods of digging, moving and disposing of large amounts of rock**.

**Phytomining (Higher tier):**
1. **Plants** are grown on soil containing low-grade ore (or mine waste); they **absorb metal compounds** through their roots, which build up in their tissues.
2. The plants are **harvested** and **burned**.
3. The **ash** contains **metal compounds** (a "bio-ore") that can be processed.

**Bioleaching (Higher tier):**
- **Bacteria** are used to break down low-grade ore, producing **leachate solutions** that **contain metal compounds** (e.g. copper ions in solution).

**Getting copper metal from the solutions (Higher tier).** The metal compounds can be processed to obtain the metal. Copper can be obtained from solutions of copper compounds by:

- **Displacement using scrap iron** — iron is more reactive than copper:
  $$\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu} \qquad \text{ionic: } \text{Fe} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$$
  Scrap iron is cheap and this recycles waste iron.
- **Electrolysis** — copper ions are reduced at the **cathode**: $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$. This produces very pure copper but uses electricity.

**Evaluating biological methods (Higher tier):**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| can extract copper from **low-grade ores** and mine waste that would otherwise be uneconomic | **slow** — plants take time to grow (phytomining); bacteria act slowly (bioleaching) |
| **less digging, moving and disposal of rock** — less damage to the landscape, less noise and dust | yield of metal per tonne is small |
| use **less energy** than traditional mining and smelting | bioleaching leachate can be **toxic** and pollute ground water if it escapes |
| phytomining can clean up **contaminated land**; less sulfur dioxide than smelting | the metal still needs processing (displacement or electrolysis) |
| conserves the limited supply of high-grade ore | burning plants releases carbon dioxide |

::: terms
- **Finite resource**: a resource such as crude oil or a metal ore that will run out because it is not replaced at a useful rate.
- **Renewable resource**: a resource such as timber or crops that can be replaced as fast as it is used.
- **Sustainable development**: development that meets the needs of current generations without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- **Potable water**: water that is safe to drink, with low levels of dissolved salts and microbes.
- **Pure water**: water containing only water molecules and no dissolved substances.
- **Desalination**: removing dissolved salts from salty water or sea water, by distillation or reverse osmosis.
- **Reverse osmosis**: forcing salty water at high pressure through a membrane that lets water through but not salt ions.
- **Effluent**: the liquid that remains above the sludge after sewage has been left to settle in sedimentation tanks.
- **Phytomining**: growing plants to absorb metal compounds, then harvesting and burning them to give ash containing the metal compounds.
- **Bioleaching**: using bacteria to produce leachate solutions that contain metal compounds from low-grade ores.
:::

::: method
**"Give reasons for the steps" in water treatment:** pair every step with its purpose — **filter beds remove solid particles**; **sterilising (chlorine, ozone or UV) kills microbes**. Do not say filtering removes microbes or dissolved salts.

**Potable vs pure:** potable water **contains dissolved substances** but low levels of salts and microbes; pure water contains **only water molecules**. "Clean" is not enough.

**Compare ground water and salt water treatment:** ground water → filter + sterilise; sea water → **desalination** (distillation or reverse osmosis) which needs **large amounts of energy**, so it is expensive. Comment on **ease**: ground water easiest; waste water needs many stages; salt water needs most energy.

**Sewage treatment:** learn the four stages **in order** with the key word for each — screening, sedimentation (sludge + effluent), **anaerobic** digestion of **sludge**, **aerobic** treatment of **effluent**. Swapping anaerobic and aerobic loses marks.

**Required practical 8 (Trilogy 13):** for the dissolved solids part give **measured volume → weigh empty basin → evaporate → reweigh → subtract**. For distillation mention **thermometer at side arm**, **condenser with cold water in at the bottom**, **anti-bumping granules**, and testing the distillate (pH 7, boils at 100 °C, no residue). Improvements: pH meter, larger volume, more precise balance, repeats.

**Calculations:** concentration (g/dm³) = mass (g) ÷ volume (dm³) — convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000. For orders of magnitude, divide reserves by annual use and give the answer as a power of ten.

**(Higher tier) Evaluate phytomining or bioleaching:** give advantages **and** disadvantages from the information, then a **justified conclusion**. Include how copper is obtained from the product (scrap iron displacement or electrolysis).

**6-mark questions:** e.g. "Describe how potable water is produced in the UK and in a country with little fresh water." Cover source → filtration → sterilisation, then desalination methods and their energy demand, with reasons for each step.
:::

::: example Potable vs pure
Explain why potable water is not pure water. (2)

Potable water contains dissolved substances such as salts (1). Pure water contains only water molecules / nothing else (1).
:::

::: example Dissolved solids calculation
A student evaporates 20.0 cm³ of a water sample. The evaporating basin weighs 45.210 g empty and 45.918 g after evaporation. Calculate the concentration of dissolved solids in g/dm³. (3)

Mass of solid = 45.918 − 45.210 = 0.708 g (1). Volume = 20.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0200 dm³ (1). Concentration = 0.708 ÷ 0.0200 = **35.4 g/dm³** (1).
:::

::: example Sewage treatment
Describe what happens to sewage sludge and to effluent during sewage treatment. (4)

Sewage sludge undergoes anaerobic digestion (1) — bacteria break down the organic matter without oxygen, producing methane/biogas that can be used as a fuel (1). Effluent undergoes aerobic biological treatment (1) — air is bubbled through so aerobic bacteria break down the remaining organic matter and kill harmful microbes (1).
:::

::: example Bioleaching (Higher tier)
Copper ions are obtained in solution by bioleaching. Describe how copper metal could be obtained from the solution using scrap iron, and write the ionic equation. (3)

Add scrap iron to the solution; iron is more reactive than copper so it displaces copper (1). Copper metal forms and can be filtered off (1). Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Cu (1).

::: watch

- **"Pure" vs "potable".** Potable water is safe to drink but **not pure** — it contains dissolved substances.
- **Anaerobic vs aerobic.** **Sludge** → **anaerobic** digestion; **effluent** → **aerobic** treatment.
- **Forgetting energy.** The key disadvantage of desalination is that it needs **large amounts of energy**.

::: test
(Higher tier) Evaluate the use of phytomining to extract copper from the waste rock left at old copper mines. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a detailed description of phytomining including how copper is obtained, with several advantages and disadvantages weighed up and a justified conclusion. **Level 2 (3–4):** describes the process and gives some advantages and/or disadvantages, with a simple conclusion or none. **Level 1 (1–2):** simple points about the process or one advantage/disadvantage.

Indicative content: plants are grown on the waste rock and absorb copper compounds through their roots; plants are harvested and burned; the ash contains copper compounds; copper is obtained by dissolving the ash in acid and then displacing with scrap iron or by electrolysis. Advantages: uses low-grade ore/waste that would otherwise not be economic; avoids digging, moving and disposing of large amounts of rock; less damage to the landscape; less energy than traditional mining; cleans up contaminated land; conserves scarce high-grade ores; plants absorb CO₂ while growing. Disadvantages: very slow (plants take months or years to grow); small yield of copper; needs land; burning plants releases CO₂ and possibly pollutants; the copper compounds still need further processing. Conclusion: worthwhile for waste or low-grade sites where traditional mining is uneconomic, but too slow to replace traditional mining for large-scale demand.
