## What it is

**Separate science only:** the whole of AQA 4.7.2 (reactions of alkenes and alcohols, and carboxylic acids) is **(chemistry only)**. It is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. One part, explaining why carboxylic acids are weak acids, is **(Higher tier)** only.

This topic is about **functional groups**. A **functional group** is the atom or group of atoms that decides how a compound reacts. Every member of a **homologous series** has the same functional group, so they all react in the same way. AQA puts it like this: it is the **generality of reactions of functional groups** that determines the reactions of organic compounds. Learn the reactions of ethene, ethanol and ethanoic acid, and you can apply them to the other members.

| Homologous series | Functional group | General formula | First four members |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkenes | C=C | CₙH₂ₙ | ethene, propene, butene, pentene |
| Alcohols | –OH | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH | methanol, ethanol, propanol, butanol |
| Carboxylic acids | –COOH | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH (n can be 0) | methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic, butanoic acid |

The name stem tells you the number of carbons: **meth** = 1, **eth** = 2, **prop** = 3, **but** = 4, **pent** = 5. The ending tells you the series: **-ene** (alkene), **-anol** (alcohol), **-anoic acid** (carboxylic acid). You only need to name these first members (alkenes up to pentene; alcohols and acids up to four carbons).

### Structure and formulae of alkenes

**Alkenes** are hydrocarbons with a **carbon–carbon double bond**. The general formula is **CₙH₂ₙ**. They are **unsaturated** because they contain **two fewer hydrogen atoms** than the alkane with the same number of carbon atoms (ethane is C₂H₆, ethene is C₂H₄).

| Alkene | Molecular formula | Structural formula |
|---|---|---|
| Ethene | C₂H₄ | CH₂=CH₂ |
| Propene | C₃H₆ | CH₂=CHCH₃ |
| Butene | C₄H₈ | CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ |
| Pentene | C₅H₁₀ | CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃ |

There is no "methene": a double bond needs two carbons. You must be able to **recognise** an alkene from its name, from its molecular formula (fits CₙH₂ₙ) or from a displayed formula (has a C=C), and **draw the displayed formula** of the first four, showing every atom and every bond. In a displayed formula each carbon has **four** bonds (the double bond counts as two) and each hydrogen has **one**.

### Reactions of alkenes

**Combustion.** Alkenes burn in oxygen like any hydrocarbon, giving carbon dioxide and water when combustion is complete:

C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O

In air, though, they tend to burn with a **smoky (sooty), yellow flame**, because of **incomplete combustion**: they have a higher proportion of carbon than alkanes, so some carbon (soot) and carbon monoxide form.

**Addition reactions.** Alkenes react with **hydrogen**, **water** and the **halogens** by **addition**: atoms add **across the C=C double bond**, so the double bond becomes a **single** C–C bond. One atom (or group) joins **each** of the two carbons. There is only **one product** and nothing is lost.

| Adding | Conditions | Product (from ethene) | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydrogen** (hydrogenation) | heated, with a **nickel catalyst** | ethane, an **alkane** | C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ |
| **Water** as **steam** (hydration) | about **300 °C**, high pressure (about **60–70 atm**), **phosphoric acid catalyst** | ethanol, an **alcohol** | C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH |
| **Chlorine** | room temperature, no catalyst | 1,2-dichloroethane | C₂H₄ + Cl₂ → C₂H₄Cl₂ |
| **Bromine** (or bromine water) | room temperature, no catalyst | 1,2-dibromoethane | C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ |
| **Iodine** | room temperature, no catalyst | 1,2-diiodoethane | C₂H₄ + I₂ → C₂H₄I₂ |

The same pattern works for every alkene. Propene + hydrogen → propane; propene + bromine → 1,2-dibromopropane (C₃H₆Br₂); butene + steam → butanol (C₄H₉OH). For propene and the larger alkenes, the –OH from steam can join either carbon of the double bond, so two different alcohols can form; at GCSE "propanol" is enough.

**Test for an alkene (link to 4.7.1).** Shake with **bromine water**: an alkene turns it from **orange to colourless** because the bromine adds across the double bond and is used up. An alkane leaves it orange.

Hydrogenation is used to harden vegetable oils into spreads; hydration of ethene is the industrial way to make ethanol.

### Alcohols

Alcohols contain the functional group **–OH** (hydroxyl). Ethanol can be written C₂H₅OH, CH₃CH₂OH or as a displayed formula.

| Alcohol | Molecular formula | Structural formula |
|---|---|---|
| Methanol | CH₃OH | CH₃OH |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH | CH₃CH₂OH |
| Propanol | C₃H₇OH | CH₃CH₂CH₂OH |
| Butanol | C₄H₉OH | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH |

In the displayed formula the O is joined to a carbon on one side and to an H on the other: C–O–H. Do not write "OH⁻": the –OH in an alcohol is covalently bonded and is **not** a hydroxide ion, which is why alcohols are not alkalis.

**What the first four alcohols do:**

- **With sodium:** the sodium **fizzes** (bubbles of **hydrogen**), moves around and slowly disappears. The reaction is **gentler than sodium with water**. A salt (sodium ethoxide from ethanol) forms. The pop test confirms hydrogen.
- **Burning in air:** they burn with a **clean, blue flame** and are used as **fuels**; complete combustion gives carbon dioxide and water. Balanced equations are needed **only for combustion**:
  - 2CH₃OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 4H₂O
  - C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O
  - 2C₃H₇OH + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 8H₂O
  - C₄H₉OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O
- **Added to water:** they **dissolve** (mix) to form a **neutral** solution (pH 7). Solubility falls as the chain gets longer: methanol, ethanol and propanol mix completely; butanol is only partly soluble.
- **With an oxidising agent** (for example acidified potassium dichromate, which turns from **orange to green**): the alcohol is **oxidised to a carboxylic acid**. Ethanol → **ethanoic acid**; propanol → propanoic acid. Microbes in the air do the same thing slowly, which is why wine left open turns to vinegar.

**Main uses.** **Methanol**: making other chemicals (a chemical feedstock) and as a fuel. **Ethanol**: the alcohol in **alcoholic drinks**, a **solvent** (perfumes, aftershave, hand gels) and a **fuel** (mixed with petrol). Methanol and ethanol are both used as **solvents and fuels**; propanol and butanol are used as solvents and fuels too.

### Fermentation

**Aqueous solutions of ethanol** are made by **fermenting sugar solutions using yeast**. Enzymes in the yeast convert the sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide:

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

**Conditions:** sugar (glucose) dissolved in water, plus **yeast**; a **warm** temperature of about **30–35 °C** (too cold is slow; too hot denatures the yeast's enzymes); **no air** (anaerobic, so an airlock is used, otherwise the ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid); a slightly acidic pH. The ethanol concentration stops rising at about 15% because the ethanol then kills the yeast; **fractional distillation** concentrates it.

| | Fermentation | Hydration of ethene |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material | sugar from crops: **renewable** | ethene from crude oil: **non-renewable** |
| Conditions | 30–35 °C, normal pressure | 300 °C, 60–70 atm (high energy cost) |
| Rate and process | slow, batch | fast, continuous |
| Product | dilute ethanol solution that must be distilled | pure ethanol |

### Carboxylic acids

Carboxylic acids have the functional group **–COOH** (a carbon with a C=O and a C–O–H). Ethanoic acid can be written CH₃COOH or as a displayed formula.

| Acid | Formula | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Methanoic acid | HCOOH | 1 carbon, the C of –COOH |
| Ethanoic acid | CH₃COOH | the acid in vinegar |
| Propanoic acid | C₂H₅COOH | |
| Butanoic acid | C₃H₇COOH | |

The carbon in –COOH **counts** in the name: ethanoic acid has two carbons in total.

**What the first four carboxylic acids do** (no balanced equations are needed):

- **With carbonates:** they **fizz**, giving a **salt + water + carbon dioxide**, just like other acids. Ethanoic acid + sodium carbonate → **sodium ethanoate** + water + carbon dioxide. The salts are called **-anoates**.
- **Dissolving in water:** they form **acidic solutions** with a pH of about 3–4, which turn universal indicator orange. They are **weak acids**.
- **With alcohols:** heated with an alcohol and a **concentrated sulfuric acid catalyst** they form an **ester** and **water**. Ethanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ **ethyl ethanoate** + water. Esters have a functional group –COO–, are **volatile** and have **sweet, fruity smells**, so they are used in **perfumes and flavourings** (ethyl ethanoate is also a solvent, as in nail-varnish remover). Ethyl ethanoate is the only ester you need to name.

### Why carboxylic acids are weak acids (Higher tier)

A **weak acid** is only **partially ionised** in aqueous solution: most molecules stay whole and only a few release H⁺ ions. The reaction is reversible:

CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺

A **strong acid** such as hydrochloric acid is **completely ionised** (HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻). So, at the **same concentration**, ethanoic acid has a much **lower concentration of H⁺ ions** than hydrochloric acid, and therefore a **higher pH** (about 3 compared with about 1 for 0.1 mol/dm³ solutions). Every decrease of 1 pH unit means the H⁺ concentration is **10 times** greater, so pH 1 has 100 times the H⁺ concentration of pH 3. Weak acids react more slowly with metals and carbonates (fewer bubbles) for the same reason. Remember: **weak** describes the degree of ionisation; **dilute** describes the amount of acid per dm³. They are not the same thing.

::: terms
- **Functional group**: the atom or group of atoms that determines how an organic compound reacts, such as C=C, –OH or –COOH.
- **Homologous series**: a family of organic compounds with the same functional group, the same general formula and similar chemical reactions.
- **Unsaturated**: containing a carbon–carbon double bond, so alkenes have two fewer hydrogens than the alkane with the same number of carbons.
- **Addition reaction**: a reaction in which atoms add across a C=C double bond, which becomes a single bond, giving one product.
- **Hydration**: adding water (as steam) across the double bond of an alkene to make an alcohol, using a phosphoric acid catalyst.
- **Fermentation**: the conversion of sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide by enzymes in yeast, in warm conditions with no air.
- **Oxidising agent**: a substance that oxidises another; it converts an alcohol into a carboxylic acid.
- **Ester**: a sweet, fruity-smelling volatile compound with the –COO– group, made from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol.
- **Weak acid**: an acid that is only partially ionised in aqueous solution, such as ethanoic acid.
:::

::: method
**Recognise the series first.** Look for the functional group: C=C means alkene, –OH means alcohol, –COOH means carboxylic acid. Count every carbon (including the one in –COOH) to get the stem. Check a molecular formula against CₙH₂ₙ for an alkene.

**Command words.**
- **Describe** what happens when an alcohol or acid reacts: give **observations** (fizzing, sodium disappears, flame colour, colour change) and **name the products**. "Ethanol reacts with sodium" scores nothing; "bubbles of hydrogen are given off and the sodium slowly disappears" scores.
- **Give the conditions**: be specific. Hydration needs **steam**, **300 °C**, **high pressure** and a **phosphoric acid catalyst**; hydrogenation needs a **nickel catalyst**; fermentation needs **yeast**, about **30–35 °C** and **no air**.
- **Explain** (Higher tier) why ethanoic acid is weak: use the word **partially ionised** and link to **lower H⁺ concentration** and **higher pH** than a strong acid **of the same concentration**.
- **Compare** or **evaluate** fermentation and hydration: give a point for each method on raw material (renewable or not), energy/conditions, rate, and purity of product, then a justified conclusion.
- **Suggest** for an unfamiliar member of a series: apply the reaction of the functional group (butanoic acid fizzes with carbonates just like ethanoic acid).

**Drawing displayed formulae.** Show every atom and every bond. Carbon has four bonds, hydrogen one, oxygen two, halogens one. For an addition product, turn C=C into C–C and put one new atom on **each** carbon of the old double bond. Recount bonds before you move on.

**Equations.** You must balance **combustion** equations for alkenes and alcohols (balance C, then H, then O last; an alcohol already contains one O). You are **not** asked to balance other alcohol or carboxylic acid equations, but word equations and simple addition equations (C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂) are fair game.

**6-mark questions** on this topic are usually "Describe how ethanol can be produced and compare the two methods", or "Describe the reactions of propanol". Level 3 needs **conditions**, **products** and **comparisons** in a logical order, not a list of disconnected facts.
:::

::: example Naming and drawing an addition product
Propene reacts with bromine. Name the product and describe its displayed formula. (2)

**1,2-dibromopropane** (accept dibromopropane), C₃H₆Br₂ (1). Displayed formula: a chain of three carbons joined by **single** bonds; one Br on carbon 1 and one Br on carbon 2 (the two carbons that were double bonded); every other position is H, so carbon 1 has 2 H, carbon 2 has 1 H and carbon 3 has 3 H (1).
:::

::: example Balancing an alcohol combustion equation
Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of propanol, C₃H₇OH. (2)

C₃H₇OH + O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O (balance C and H first). Oxygen on the right: $3 \times 2 + 4 = 10$ atoms; the alcohol supplies 1, so 9 atoms are needed from O₂, which is 4.5 O₂. Double everything:
**2C₃H₇OH + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 8H₂O** (1 for correct formulae, 1 for balancing).
:::

::: example Comparing methods for making ethanol
Give two advantages of fermentation and two advantages of hydration of ethene for making ethanol. (4)

Fermentation: uses sugar from crops, a **renewable** resource (1); uses low temperature and normal pressure, so less **energy** is needed (1). Hydration: **fast** and **continuous** (1); produces **pure** ethanol, so no distillation is needed (1).
:::

::: example Weak acid explanation (Higher tier)
Solutions of hydrochloric acid and ethanoic acid both have a concentration of 0.1 mol/dm³. The hydrochloric acid has pH 1 and the ethanoic acid pH 3. Explain the difference and state how many times greater the H⁺ concentration is in the hydrochloric acid. (3)

Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid, **completely ionised**; ethanoic acid is a weak acid, only **partially ionised** (1), so ethanoic acid has a lower concentration of H⁺ ions and a higher pH (1). Two pH units lower means $10 \times 10 =$ **100 times** greater H⁺ concentration (1).
:::

::: example Identifying an unknown liquid
A colourless liquid X is neutral, burns with a clean blue flame and fizzes gently with sodium. With acidified potassium dichromate it gives a liquid that fizzes with sodium carbonate. Identify the type of compound X is and explain. (3)

X is an **alcohol** (1): it is neutral, burns as a fuel and gives hydrogen with sodium (1). Oxidation turns it into a **carboxylic acid**, which reacts with carbonates to give carbon dioxide (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Forgetting the carbon in –COOH.** CH₃COOH is **eth**anoic acid (two carbons), not methanoic acid.
- **Vague conditions.** "Heat and a catalyst" does not score for hydration. Write **steam, 300 °C, 60–70 atm, phosphoric acid**. For fermentation write **yeast, about 30–35 °C, no air (anaerobic)**.
- **"Water" instead of steam.** Hydration of ethene uses steam.
- **Addition products drawn with a double bond left in**, or with both new atoms on the same carbon. The C=C becomes C–C and one atom goes on each carbon.
- **"Clear" instead of colourless** for bromine water. Orange bromine water is already clear.
- **Calling ethanol an alkali** because it has "OH". The –OH is covalently bonded; alcohol solutions are neutral.
- **(Higher tier) "Weak means dilute."** Weak means **partially ionised**; dilute means a small amount of acid in a given volume. Always compare acids **at the same concentration**.
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A 0.1 mol/dm³ solution of a carboxylic acid has pH 3. A 0.1 mol/dm³ solution of nitric acid has pH 1. A student dilutes the nitric acid until it also has pH 3. (a) Calculate the factor by which the nitric acid was diluted and its new concentration. (b) Explain why the two pH 3 solutions do not neutralise the same amount of sodium carbonate. (4)
---
(a) pH rises by 2, so the H⁺ concentration falls by $10 \times 10 = 100$ times (1); new concentration $0.1 \div 100 = $ **0.001 mol/dm³** (1). (b) The carboxylic acid is **weak / partially ionised**, so it still contains 0.1 mol/dm³ of acid, 100 times as much as the nitric acid (1); as H⁺ ions are used up, more acid molecules ionise, so it neutralises far more carbonate (about 100 times as much) (1).
:::

---
Shake with **bromine water**: it turns from **orange to colourless** (1). Hydrogen **adds across** the C=C double bonds (1) so they become single bonds / the oil becomes saturated (1). Conditions: **nickel catalyst**, heated (about 60 °C) (1).
:::

::: test
Ethanol can be made by fermentation of sugar or by hydration of ethene. Describe each method and evaluate which is better for a country with plenty of farmland but no crude oil. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** both methods described with correct conditions and products, a comparison across several factors, and a justified judgement for the stated country. **Level 2 (3–4):** both methods outlined with some conditions and at least one comparison; judgement may be missing or weakly linked. **Level 1 (1–2):** some correct facts about one method, little comparison.

Indicative content. Fermentation: sugar solution + yeast; about 30–35 °C; no air (anaerobic, airlock); C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂; slow batch process; gives about 15% ethanol, needs fractional distillation. Hydration: ethene + steam; about 300 °C, 60–70 atm, phosphoric acid catalyst; C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH; fast continuous process; pure product. Ethene comes from cracking crude oil (non-renewable, finite); sugar crops are renewable. Hydration needs much more energy for high temperature and pressure. Judgement: fermentation is better for this country, because it can grow the sugar crops, has no crude oil to make ethene, and the process needs simple, low-energy equipment, even though it is slower and needs distillation.
