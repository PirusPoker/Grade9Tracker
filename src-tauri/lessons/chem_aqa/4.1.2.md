## What it is

The periodic table organises the elements so their properties can be explained and predicted from their atomic structure. This topic covers how the table is arranged, how it was developed, metals and non-metals, and three groups in detail: Group 0, Group 1 and Group 7. All of it is common to GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464.

### How the periodic table is arranged

- Elements are in order of **atomic (proton) number**.
- Elements with **similar properties are in columns, called groups**. It is called *periodic* because similar properties recur at **regular intervals**.
- Rows are **periods**. The period number = the **number of occupied shells**.
- Elements in the same group have the **same number of electrons in their outer shell**, and that gives them **similar chemical properties**. For Groups 1–7 the group number = the number of outer electrons.

So position, electron arrangement and atomic number all tell the same story. Sulfur (atomic number 16) is 2,8,6: three shells → **Period 3**; six outer electrons → **Group 6**.

**Predicting reactions:** an element will react like the others in its group, with reactivity following the group trend. Rubidium is below potassium in Group 1, so it reacts with water like potassium but **more violently**.

### Development of the periodic table

1. Before protons, neutrons and electrons were discovered, scientists classified elements by **atomic weight** (what we now call relative atomic mass).
2. **Early tables were incomplete**, and following the strict order of atomic weights put some elements into **inappropriate groups** with elements whose properties did not match.
3. **Mendeleev** (1869) overcame this by:
   - **leaving gaps** for elements he thought **had not yet been discovered**;
   - in some places **changing the order** based on atomic weights so elements sat with others of similar properties (e.g. he put tellurium, 127.6, before iodine, 126.9, so that iodine sat with the other halogens).
4. Elements with the properties he **predicted** for the gaps were later **discovered** — gallium and germanium matched his predictions closely. A successful prediction is strong evidence that a scientific idea is right, so his table was accepted.
5. **Knowledge of isotopes** explained why ordering by atomic weight was not always correct: the table is really ordered by **proton number**, and an element with a higher proportion of heavier isotopes can have a higher relative atomic mass than the next element (argon, 39.9, comes before potassium, 39.1).

### Metals and non-metals

- **Metals** are elements that **react to form positive ions**. **Non-metals** do **not** form positive ions.
- **Most elements are metals.** Metals are to the **left and towards the bottom**; non-metals to the **right and top**.
- **Atomic structure:** metal atoms have **few outer electrons (1, 2 or 3)** which they lose to form positive ions; non-metal atoms have **4 to 7 outer electrons** and gain or share electrons. Going down a group atoms get bigger and lose electrons more easily, so elements become more metallic (in Group 4, carbon is a non-metal but tin and lead are metals).

| Property | Metals | Non-metals |
|---|---|---|
| Melting/boiling points | mostly high | mostly low (many are gases) |
| Electrical and thermal conduction | good | poor (graphite is an exception) |
| Appearance | shiny | dull |
| Solid behaviour | malleable and ductile | brittle |
| Density | usually high | usually low |
| Ions formed | positive | negative (or share electrons) |
| Oxides | basic (react with acids) | acidic |

### Group 0 — the noble gases

- Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon.
- **Unreactive** and do **not easily form molecules** (they exist as single atoms) because their atoms have a **stable arrangement of electrons**: a **full outer shell** — **eight** outer electrons, except **helium, which has two**.
- They do not need to lose, gain or share electrons.
- **Boiling points increase going down the group** (with increasing relative atomic mass). All are colourless gases at room temperature.

| Element | Boiling point (°C) |
|---|---|
| Helium | −269 |
| Neon | −246 |
| Argon | −186 |
| Krypton | −153 |
| Xenon | −108 |

Prediction: radon, below xenon, boils above −108 °C (actually about −62 °C).

### Group 1 — the alkali metals

- Lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium.
- Each has **one electron in its outer shell**, which gives the group its characteristic properties. They lose it to form **1+ ions** (Na⁺), with a noble-gas electron arrangement.
- Physical: soft (cut with a knife), shiny when freshly cut but **tarnish quickly in air**, **low density** (Li, Na and K float on water), low melting points that **decrease** down the group. Stored under oil.

**Reactions of the first three** (you must describe these):

| Reacting with | Equation | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| **Water** | 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ | Li: floats, fizzes steadily, slowly disappears. Na: melts into a silvery ball, fizzes rapidly, moves around the surface. K: melts, fizzes violently, the hydrogen ignites with a **lilac** flame, may spit. All form an **alkaline** hydroxide solution (universal indicator turns purple) and **hydrogen**. |
| **Oxygen** | 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O | Freshly cut surfaces go dull as an oxide forms; heated, they burn (Li red flame, Na orange-yellow, K lilac) to form white metal oxides. |
| **Chlorine** | 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl | React vigorously (sodium burns with a bright orange-yellow flame) forming **white solid** metal chlorides, which are ionic and dissolve in water to give colourless solutions. |

The same equations work for lithium and potassium: just swap the symbol (2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂).

**Reactivity increases going down Group 1.** Explanation: going down the group the atoms have **more shells**, so the outer electron is **further from the nucleus** and **more shielded** by inner electrons. The **attraction** between the nucleus and the outer electron is **weaker**, so the outer electron is **lost more easily**.

### Group 7 — the halogens

- Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine. **Non-metals** consisting of **diatomic molecules** (pairs of atoms): F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.
- All have **seven electrons in the outer shell**, so they have **similar reactions**: each atom gains one electron to form a **1− halide ion** (Cl⁻ chloride, Br⁻ bromide, I⁻ iodide).

| Halogen | Appearance at room temperature | Boiling point (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Fluorine | pale yellow gas | −188 |
| Chlorine | green gas | −34 |
| Bromine | red-brown liquid (orange vapour) | 59 |
| Iodine | grey-black solid (purple vapour) | 184 |

- Going **down** the group: **relative molecular mass, melting point and boiling point increase**.
- Going **down** the group: **reactivity decreases**. Explanation: the atoms have more shells, so the outer shell is **further from the nucleus** and **more shielded**; the attraction for an incoming electron is **weaker**, so an electron is **gained less easily**.

**Compounds the halogens form:**
- With **metals** → **ionic** compounds (metal halides) containing **1− halide ions**, e.g. sodium chloride NaCl, iron(III) bromide FeBr₃. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl.
- With **non-metals** → **covalent** compounds made of **molecules**, sharing electrons, e.g. hydrogen chloride HCl (H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl), which dissolves in water to make hydrochloric acid; carbon tetrachloride CCl₄.

**Displacement reactions:** a **more reactive** halogen **displaces** a less reactive halogen from an **aqueous solution of its salt**.

| Added halogen | Potassium chloride | Potassium bromide | Potassium iodide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine water | — | **orange** solution (bromine formed) | **brown** solution (iodine formed) |
| Bromine water | no reaction | — | **brown** solution (iodine formed) |
| Iodine solution | no reaction | no reaction | — |

Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ and Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂. Higher tier ionic form: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂ (potassium ions are spectators).

**Predicting astatine:** below iodine, so it should be a **dark solid**, with a **higher** melting and boiling point and be the **least reactive** halogen; it will **not** displace iodine from iodide solution.

**Opposite trends:** Group 1 reactivity goes **up** down the group (losing an electron gets easier); Group 7 reactivity goes **down** (gaining an electron gets harder). The cause is the same in both — the outer shell is further from the nucleus.

::: terms
- **Group**: a vertical column of the periodic table; its elements have the same number of outer electrons and similar properties.
- **Period**: a horizontal row of the periodic table; the period number equals the number of occupied electron shells.
- **Metal**: an element that reacts to form positive ions.
- **Non-metal**: an element that does not form positive ions.
- **Noble gases**: the unreactive Group 0 elements, whose atoms have a stable full outer shell.
- **Alkali metals**: the Group 1 elements, which have one outer electron and react with water to form alkaline hydroxides.
- **Halogens**: the Group 7 non-metals, which have seven outer electrons and exist as diatomic molecules.
- **Halide ion**: the 1− ion formed when a halogen atom gains one electron, such as chloride.
- **Displacement reaction**: a reaction in which a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive one in a compound.
- **Shielding**: the reduction in attraction between the nucleus and outer electrons caused by inner shells of electrons.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** a reaction → give observations (fizzing, melts into a ball, lilac flame, colour change) and products. Do not explain.
- **Explain** a property or trend → link it to **outer electrons**. For reactivity trends always give the full chain: **more shells → outer electron further from the nucleus (more shielding) → weaker attraction → electron lost more easily (Group 1) / gained less easily (Group 7) → more / less reactive**.
- **Predict** → use the trend in the data or the group: state the value or behaviour **and** which direction it goes compared with its neighbour.
- **Compare** → mention both things: "potassium reacts more vigorously than sodium".
- **Evaluate** Mendeleev's table → say what it got right (gaps, predictions confirmed, swapping order) and why it was accepted.

**Writing equations:** Group 1 + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen; Group 1 + chlorine → metal chloride; Group 1 + oxygen → metal oxide (M₂O). Halogens are always X₂ in equations. Displacement: more reactive halogen + salt of less reactive halogen → salt of more reactive halogen + less reactive halogen.

**Data questions:** read the trend first (increasing or decreasing), then estimate the missing value between or beyond its neighbours. Give units.

**6-mark extended responses** (e.g. "Compare the reactions of lithium, sodium and potassium with water and explain the trend"). Level 3 (5–6): detailed observations for all three, a correct equation or products, and a full linked explanation of the trend in terms of electron arrangement. Level 2 (3–4): some observations and a partial explanation. Level 1 (1–2): isolated facts. Structure it: observations → products and test for alkali → trend → atomic-structure explanation.
:::

::: example Position from electron arrangement
An element has the electronic structure 2,8,7. State its group and period and explain your answer. (3)

Group 7 (1) because it has 7 electrons in its outer shell (1). Period 3 because it has electrons in three shells (1). (It is chlorine.)
:::

::: example Group 1 trend
Explain why potassium is more reactive than sodium. (3)

A potassium atom has one more occupied shell than a sodium atom, so its outer electron is further from the nucleus (1) and more shielded by inner electrons (1). The attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker, so the outer electron is lost more easily (1).
:::

::: example Halogen displacement
Chlorine water is added to colourless potassium iodide solution. Describe what you would see, write a balanced equation, and explain why the reaction happens. (4)

The solution turns brown (1) because iodine is formed. Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂ (1). Chlorine is more reactive than iodine (1) because its outer shell is closer to the nucleus, so it gains an electron more easily, and a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt (1).
:::

::: example Mendeleev
Explain why Mendeleev left gaps in his periodic table and why this helped scientists accept it. (3)

He left gaps for elements he believed had not yet been discovered, so that elements with similar properties would line up in groups (1). He used the gaps to predict the properties of the missing elements (1). When elements such as gallium and germanium were discovered with properties matching his predictions, this was evidence that his table was correct (1).
:::

::: example Noble gases
Explain why argon is unreactive. (2)

Argon has a full outer shell of eight electrons (a stable arrangement) (1), so it does not need to lose, gain or share electrons and does not easily react or form molecules (1).
:::

::: q
How are the elements arranged in the modern periodic table? (2)
---
In order of atomic (proton) number (1); with elements with similar properties in the same columns (groups) (1).
:::

::: q
Why do elements in the same group have similar chemical properties? (1)
---
They have the same number of electrons in their outer shell.
:::

::: q
Write a balanced equation for the reaction of lithium with water. (2)
---
2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂ (1 for correct formulae, 1 for balancing).
:::

::: q
Give two observations when sodium is added to water. (2)
---
Any two: floats; melts into a ball; fizzes/bubbles; moves around on the surface; gets smaller and disappears (1 each).
:::

::: q
Describe the nature of the compound formed when chlorine reacts with (a) sodium, (b) hydrogen. (2)
---
(a) An ionic compound (sodium chloride, a white solid containing Cl⁻ ions) (1). (b) A covalent compound made of small molecules (hydrogen chloride gas) (1).
:::

::: q
Bromine water is added to potassium chloride solution. What happens? Explain. (2)
---
No reaction / no colour change (1); bromine is less reactive than chlorine so it cannot displace it (1).
:::

::: q
State the trend in boiling point of the noble gases going down the group. (1)
---
Boiling point increases.
:::

::: q
Explain how knowledge of isotopes showed that ordering elements by atomic weight was not always correct. (2)
---
The table is ordered by proton number, but relative atomic mass depends on the abundance of isotopes (1); an element with more heavy isotopes (e.g. argon) can have a higher atomic weight than the next element (potassium), so ordering by weight would put them in the wrong groups (1).
:::

::: q
Give two differences in physical properties between metals and non-metals. (2)
---
Any two: metals conduct electricity/heat, non-metals generally do not; metals have high melting points, non-metals generally low; metals are malleable, non-metals brittle; metals shiny, non-metals dull (1 each — must state both sides).
:::

::: watch
- In trend explanations, say **outer electron/outer shell further from the nucleus** and **weaker attraction**. "The atom is bigger" alone is not enough; never say "more electrons so more reactive".
- Group 1 **loses** an electron more easily down the group; Group 7 **gains** one less easily. Mixing up "lose" and "gain" costs the mark.
- Halogens are **diatomic**: Cl₂, not Cl, in equations.
- Displacement observations are **colour changes in solution** (orange for bromine, brown for iodine), not fizzing or precipitates.
- Noble gases are unreactive because of a **full outer shell**, and helium has **two**, not eight, outer electrons.
- Mendeleev: say he **left gaps** for **undiscovered** elements and **predicted** their properties; just "he left gaps" usually earns only one mark.
:::
