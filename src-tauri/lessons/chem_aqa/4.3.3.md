## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection (4.3.3, yield and atom economy) is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. Trilogy students can skip this lesson.

In industry it is not enough for a reaction to work — it has to make as much product as possible from the starting materials, with as little waste as possible. Two measures tell chemists how well a reaction does this: **percentage yield** (how much product you actually got) and **atom economy** (how much of the starting materials could ever end up in the useful product).

### Why you never get 100% of the product

Even though **no atoms are gained or lost** in a chemical reaction, it is not always possible to obtain the calculated amount of a product because:

1. **the reaction may not go to completion because it is reversible** — some product turns back into reactants (e.g. making ammonia: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃);
2. **some of the product may be lost when it is separated from the reaction mixture** — left on filter paper, in the flask, spilt, or lost during evaporation or crystallisation;
3. **some of the reactants may react in ways different to the expected reaction** — side reactions make unwanted products.

(Impure reactants also reduce the yield, because some of the mass weighed out is not the reactant.)

### Percentage yield

The amount of a product obtained is the **yield**. Compared with the **maximum theoretical amount** as a percentage, it is the **percentage yield**:

$$\text{\% yield} = \frac{\text{mass of product actually made}}{\text{maximum theoretical mass of product}} \times 100$$

You need to **recall** this equation and **rearrange** it (MS 3b): actual mass = % yield × theoretical mass ÷ 100.

**Worked mini-example:** a reaction could make 8.0 g of a salt, but the student collects 6.0 g. % yield = $\frac{6.0}{8.0} \times 100$ = **75%**.

**Rearranged:** a process has a 90% yield and the theoretical mass is 50 kg. Actual mass = $\frac{90 \times 50}{100}$ = **45 kg**.

A % yield can never be more than 100%. A value above 100% means the product was **impure** — usually **still wet** (not fully dried) or contaminated.

### Calculating the theoretical mass (Higher tier)

To find the **maximum theoretical mass of product**, use a reacting-mass calculation from the balanced equation (moles of reactant → ratio → moles of product → mass).

**Example:** 10.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated. 4.2 g of calcium oxide is collected. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ ($A_r$: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16). Calculate the percentage yield.
1. Moles CaCO₃ = 10.0 ÷ 100 = 0.100 mol
2. Ratio 1 : 1 → 0.100 mol CaO
3. Theoretical mass CaO = 0.100 × 56 = 5.6 g
4. % yield = $\frac{4.2}{5.6} \times 100$ = **75%**

**Example:** 40 g of iron(III) oxide is reduced and 22.4 g of iron is obtained. Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ ($A_r$: Fe = 56, O = 16). Moles Fe₂O₃ = 40 ÷ 160 = 0.25 mol → Fe = 0.50 mol → theoretical mass = 28 g. % yield = $\frac{22.4}{28} \times 100$ = **80%**.

### Atom economy

The **atom economy** (atom utilisation) is a measure of **the amount of starting materials that end up as useful products**. It is calculated from the **balanced equation**:

$$\text{\% atom economy} = \frac{M_r \text{ of desired product from equation}}{\text{sum of } M_r \text{ of all reactants from equation}} \times 100$$

Use the $M_r$ values **multiplied by the balancing numbers**. (The sum of the reactants equals the sum of the products, by conservation of mass, so you can check your total.)

**Worked mini-examples**

| Reaction | Desired product | Calculation | Atom economy |
|---|---|---|---|
| CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ | CaO (lime) | $\frac{56}{100} \times 100$ | **56%** |
| Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ | Fe | $\frac{2 \times 56}{160 + (3 \times 28)} \times 100 = \frac{112}{244} \times 100$ | **45.9%** |
| C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ (fermentation) | ethanol | $\frac{2 \times 46}{180} \times 100$ | **51.1%** |
| C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH (hydration of ethene) | ethanol | $\frac{46}{28 + 18} \times 100$ | **100%** |
| N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ | ammonia | only one product | **100%** |

**Any reaction with only one product has an atom economy of 100%.** Reactions that make **by-products** (other substances as well as the one you want) have lower atom economies.

### Why atom economy matters

It is important for **sustainable development** and for **economic reasons** to use reactions with **high atom economy**:
- **Less waste** is produced — fewer unwanted by-products that need disposing of (which may be harmful).
- **Fewer raw materials** are used for the same mass of product, so **natural resources last longer** and costs are lower.
- If a by-product **can be sold or used** (e.g. CO₂ for fizzy drinks, or HCl as a reagent), the low atom economy matters less — effectively more of the atoms are useful.

**Atom economy and yield are different.** Atom economy is fixed by the **equation** (a theoretical maximum). Yield depends on **how well the reaction actually goes** in practice. A reaction can have 100% atom economy but a low yield (e.g. the Haber process, a reversible reaction with roughly 15% conversion per pass).

### Choosing a reaction pathway (Higher tier)

There is often more than one way to make a product. Given data, you must **explain why a particular reaction pathway is chosen**, weighing up:

| Factor | What makes a pathway better |
|---|---|
| **Atom economy** | high — less waste, fewer raw materials |
| **Yield** | high — more product from the same reactants |
| **Rate** | fast — more product per hour |
| **Equilibrium position** (reversible reactions) | lies to the right (towards the product) under practical conditions |
| **Usefulness of by-products** | by-products can be sold or used, not just thrown away |

Other things that matter in real questions: cost and renewability of raw materials, energy needed (temperature and pressure), and safety.

**Example — two ways to make ethanol:**

| | Fermentation of sugar | Hydration of ethene |
|---|---|---|
| Equation | C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ | C₂H₄ + H₂O ⇌ C₂H₅OH |
| Atom economy | 51.1% (CO₂ by-product) | 100% |
| Rate | slow | fast |
| Yield | ethanol stops at about 15% as yeast dies; needs distilling | about 5% per pass but unreacted ethene is recycled, so overall about 95% |
| Conditions | about 30 °C, normal pressure — low energy | about 300 °C, 60–70 atm, catalyst — high energy |
| Raw material | sugar from crops — **renewable** | ethene from crude oil — **non-renewable** |

A country with lots of crops and little oil might choose fermentation despite its lower atom economy; a country with cheap crude oil might choose hydration for its high atom economy, rate and purity. A good answer **uses the data** and **reaches a justified judgement**.

::: terms
- **Yield**: the amount of a product obtained from a reaction.
- **Theoretical yield**: the maximum mass of product that could be made, calculated from the balanced equation.
- **Percentage yield**: the mass of product actually made as a percentage of the maximum theoretical mass.
- **Atom economy**: a measure of the amount of starting materials that end up as useful products, worked out from the balanced equation.
- **Desired product**: the useful substance that a reaction is carried out to make.
- **By-product**: an extra substance made in a reaction alongside the desired product.
- **Reversible reaction**: a reaction in which the products can react to re-form the reactants, so it may not go to completion.
- **Sustainable development**: using resources to meet the needs of people today without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
:::

::: method
**Two equations to recall and rearrange:**
- % yield = actual mass ÷ maximum theoretical mass × 100
- % atom economy = $M_r$ of desired product ÷ sum of $M_r$ of all reactants × 100 (both **from the balanced equation**, so include the balancing numbers)

**Calculations:** write the equation → substitute → answer with % and sensible significant figures (usually 3). Check your total of reactant $M_r$ values equals the total of product $M_r$ values.

**(Higher tier) % yield from a reactant mass:** first do the reacting-mass calculation for the **theoretical** mass (mass → moles → ratio → moles → mass), then use the yield equation. Two separate, clearly labelled stages.

**Explain why the yield is less than 100%:** give the three AQA reasons — **reversible reaction** (does not go to completion), **product lost during separation** (e.g. filtering, transferring), **side reactions** (reactants react in unexpected ways). Pick ones that fit the context given.

**Explain why high atom economy matters:** **less waste** + **fewer raw materials / resources conserved** (sustainable) + **cheaper** (economic). Always mention both sustainability and economics.

**(Higher tier) Choose / evaluate a pathway:** use the data for each factor — atom economy, yield, rate, equilibrium position, usefulness of by-products — and say which pathway is better **for each**, then give an overall **judgement with a reason**. Quote numbers from the table. A one-factor answer will not reach the top level.

**Do not confuse** atom economy (theory, from the equation) with yield (practice, from the experiment).
:::

::: example Percentage yield
A student expects to make 12.0 g of copper sulfate crystals but collects 9.6 g. Calculate the percentage yield and give two reasons why it is less than 100%. (4)

% yield = $\frac{9.6}{12.0} \times 100$ (1) = **80%** (1). Reasons (any two): some crystals were left on the filter paper / in the evaporating basin (1); some product stayed dissolved in the remaining solution (1); some solution was spilt or lost when transferring (1).
:::

::: example Theoretical mass then yield (Higher tier)
12 g of magnesium is burned and 16 g of magnesium oxide is collected. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO ($A_r$: Mg = 24, O = 16). Calculate the percentage yield. (4)

Moles Mg = 12 ÷ 24 = 0.50 mol (1). Ratio 2 : 2 → 0.50 mol MgO; theoretical mass = 0.50 × 40 = 20 g (1). % yield = $\frac{16}{20} \times 100$ (1) = **80%** (1).
:::

::: example Atom economy
Titanium is extracted by the reaction TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂. Calculate the atom economy for titanium. ($A_r$: Ti = 48, Cl = 35.5, Mg = 24) (3)

Sum of reactants = $M_r$ TiCl₄ + 2 × 24 = 190 + 48 = 238 (1). $M_r$ Ti = 48 (1). Atom economy = $\frac{48}{238} \times 100$ = **20.2%** (1).
:::

::: example Comparing atom economies
Hydrogen can be made by (A) CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂ or (B) 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (electrolysis). Calculate the atom economy of each for hydrogen and suggest one advantage of the method with the lower value. ($A_r$: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16) (4)

A: $\frac{3 \times 2}{16 + 18} \times 100 = \frac{6}{34} \times 100$ = **17.6%** (1). B: $\frac{2 \times 2}{2 \times 18} \times 100 = \frac{4}{36} \times 100$ = **11.1%** (1). Method B has the lower atom economy (1). Advantage: its by-product (oxygen) is useful and harmless / it can use renewable electricity and does not use a fossil fuel or make carbon monoxide (1).
:::

::: example Rearranging the yield equation
A factory makes ammonia with an overall percentage yield of 96%. The maximum theoretical mass is 250 tonnes. What mass is actually made? (2)

Actual = $\frac{96 \times 250}{100}$ (1) = **240 tonnes** (1).
:::

Give three reasons why it is not always possible to obtain the calculated amount of a product. (3)
---
The reaction may be reversible so does not go to completion (1); some product is lost when it is separated from the reaction mixture (1); some reactants react in different ways to the expected reaction / side reactions (1).

::: q
A reaction should make 5.0 g of product but 3.5 g is collected. Calculate the percentage yield. (2)
---
$\frac{3.5}{5.0} \times 100$ (1) = 70% (1).
:::

::: q
A student calculates a percentage yield of 112%. Suggest a reason. (1)
---
The product was not completely dry / still contained water or another impurity, so its mass was too high.
:::

::: q
Calculate the atom economy for making sodium chloride: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O. ($M_r$: NaOH = 40, HCl = 36.5, NaCl = 58.5) Give your answer to 3 significant figures. (2)
---
$\frac{58.5}{40 + 36.5} \times 100$ (1) = 76.5% (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the reaction C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH has an atom economy of 100%. (1)
---
Ethanol is the only product, so all the atoms in the reactants end up in the desired product.
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why industry prefers reactions with a high atom economy. (2)
---
Less waste is produced / fewer by-products to dispose of (1); fewer raw materials are needed, so resources last longer and costs are lower (sustainable and economic) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between percentage yield and atom economy. (2)
---
Atom economy is calculated from the balanced equation — the proportion of reactant mass that could end up in the desired product (a theoretical value) (1). Percentage yield compares the mass actually obtained in practice with the maximum theoretical mass (1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) 14 g of ethene, C₂H₄, is reacted with steam and 20.7 g of ethanol, C₂H₅OH, is obtained. C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH. Calculate the percentage yield. ($A_r$: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16) (4)
---
Moles C₂H₄ = 14 ÷ 28 = 0.50 mol (1). Ratio 1 : 1 → theoretical ethanol = 0.50 × 46 = 23 g (1). % yield = $\frac{20.7}{23} \times 100$ (1) = 90% (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the atom economy for making copper by CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O. ($A_r$: Cu = 63.5, O = 16, H = 1) Give your answer to 3 significant figures. (2)
---
$\frac{63.5}{79.5 + 2} \times 100 = \frac{63.5}{81.5} \times 100$ (1) = 77.9% (1).
:::

::: watch
- Forgetting the **balancing numbers** in atom economy: in Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ the desired product is **2 × 56**, and the reactants include **3 × 28**.
- Putting only the reactant that "becomes" the product on the bottom of the atom economy fraction — it is the sum of **all** reactants.
- Mixing up the two ideas: **atom economy** comes from the **equation**; **yield** comes from the **experiment**. A reaction can have 100% atom economy and still a low yield.
- Giving vague reasons for low yield such as "human error". Use the AQA reasons: **reversible**, **lost in separation**, **side reactions**.
- Answering a pathway question using only atom economy. Use **every** factor in the data (yield, rate, equilibrium position, by-products) and end with a **justified** choice.
- (Higher tier) Using the actual mass instead of the **theoretical** mass in the yield calculation, or skipping the mole ratio.
:::

::: test
Calcium chloride can be made by CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Calculate the atom economy for calcium chloride. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. ($A_r$: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16, H = 1, Cl = 35.5) (3)

::: test
Explain why the atom economy of a reaction matters less if its by-products can be sold. Refer to sustainability and cost. (2)
---
The by-products are not waste, so fewer atoms from the raw materials are thrown away and resources are used more fully (sustainable) (1); selling them brings in money, offsetting the cost of the raw materials (economic) (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) 7.2 g of magnesium reacts with excess sulfuric acid. 30.6 g of magnesium sulfate is obtained. Mg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂. Calculate the percentage yield. ($A_r$: Mg = 24, S = 32, O = 16) (4)
---
Moles Mg = 7.2 ÷ 24 = 0.30 mol (1). Ratio 1 : 1; $M_r$ MgSO₄ = 120, so theoretical mass = 0.30 × 120 = 36 g (1). % yield = $\frac{30.6}{36} \times 100$ (1) = 85% (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A chemist needs to obtain 18 g of magnesium sulfate. The method gives a 75% yield. Calculate the minimum mass of magnesium she must use. Mg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂. ($A_r$: Mg = 24, S = 32, O = 16) (4)
---
Theoretical mass needed = 18 × 100 ÷ 75 = 24 g (1). Moles MgSO₄ = 24 ÷ 120 = 0.20 mol (1). Ratio 1 : 1 → 0.20 mol Mg (1). Mass Mg = 0.20 × 24 = 4.8 g (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A company can make a medicine by two routes. Route 1: one reversible step; atom economy 100%; equilibrium position lies well to the left at practical temperatures, giving a yield of 40%; needs 250 °C and is slow even with a catalyst; no by-products. Route 2: two steps; atom economy 65%; overall yield 85%; fast at 40 °C; the by-product is sodium chloride solution, which can be sold. Evaluate the two routes and recommend one. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a balanced comparison using data for most factors (atom economy, yield, rate, equilibrium position / conditions, by-products), with a clear recommendation justified by weighing the factors. **Level 2 (3–4):** comparison of several factors using some data, with a recommendation that is partly justified. **Level 1 (1–2):** simple statements about one or two factors; recommendation absent or unsupported.

Indicative content: Route 1 has the higher atom economy (100% vs 65%), so in theory no waste and fewer raw materials per tonne of product. But its equilibrium lies to the left, so only 40% yield — more than half the reactants do not become product each time (unless recycled). It needs 250 °C — high energy cost — and is slow, so less product per hour. Route 2 has a lower atom economy, but its yield is more than double (85%), it is fast and runs at 40 °C, saving energy. Its by-product, sodium chloride solution, can be sold, so the atoms in it are not wasted and it brings in money, reducing the effect of the lower atom economy. Two steps may add cost / time. Recommendation, e.g. Route 2, because the higher yield, faster rate, lower energy use and saleable by-product outweigh the lower atom economy; or Route 1 if unreacted material can be recycled to raise the overall yield — any judgement supported by the data.
:::
