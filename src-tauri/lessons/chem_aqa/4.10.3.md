## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection is in GCSE Chemistry 8462 only (chemistry only) — it is **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. There is no Higher-tier-only content. It covers **corrosion** and how to prevent it, **alloys**, and the properties and uses of **glass, clay ceramics, polymers and composites**.

### Corrosion and rusting

**Corrosion is the destruction of materials by chemical reactions with substances in the environment.** **Rusting** is an example of corrosion — the corrosion of **iron** (and steel).

**Both air (oxygen) and water are necessary for iron to rust.**

$$\text{iron} + \text{oxygen} + \text{water} \rightarrow \text{hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust)}$$

Rust is brittle and flaky, so it falls off and exposes fresh iron underneath — the iron keeps corroding. The iron is **oxidised** (it gains oxygen; in terms of electrons, Fe → Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻). **Salt** (e.g. sea water or road salt) speeds rusting up.

### Experiment: showing that air and water are both needed (WS 2.2, 2.7, 3.5)

Set up three test tubes, each with a clean **iron nail**, and leave them for about a week:

| Tube | Contents | What it tests | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | nail in tap water, open to the air | **air and water** present | **rusts** |
| **B** | nail in **boiled water** with a layer of **oil** on top, sealed with a bung | water but **no air** — boiling removes dissolved air; the oil stops air dissolving back in | **no rust** |
| **C** | nail with **anhydrous calcium chloride** (a drying agent), sealed with a bung | air but **no water** — the calcium chloride absorbs water vapour | **no rust** |

**Interpretation:** the nail rusts only in tube A, so **both air and water are needed**. Tube A is the **control**. A fourth tube with **salty water** rusts fastest. Control variables: same type and size of nail, cleaned with sandpaper, same temperature and time.

### Preventing corrosion

**1. Barrier methods** — a coating stops air and water reaching the metal:
- **greasing or oiling** (moving parts such as bicycle chains, machinery)
- **painting** (cars, bridges, railings)
- **electroplating** — coating with a thin layer of another metal using electrolysis (e.g. chromium plating on taps, tin plating on food cans)
- plastic coating (garden furniture, wire fences).

**Aluminium** has a natural **oxide coating** (aluminium oxide) that **protects the metal from further corrosion**. Although aluminium is more reactive than iron, the thin, tough oxide layer sticks to the surface and does not flake off like rust — so aluminium does not corrode further.

**2. Sacrificial protection** — some coatings are **reactive** and contain a **more reactive metal**. For example, **zinc is used to galvanise iron**: the iron is coated with zinc.

- Zinc is **more reactive** than iron, so it reacts with oxygen and water **instead of** the iron — zinc **loses electrons more readily** (is oxidised in preference): Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
- The zinc is "**sacrificed**" — it corrodes, while the iron is protected.
- It **still works if the coating is scratched**, because the nearby zinc is still more reactive than the exposed iron.
- Blocks of **magnesium** or zinc are bolted to ships' hulls, oil rigs and underground pipelines for the same reason.

**Contrast with tin plating:** tin is **less reactive** than iron, so it only acts as a barrier. If a tin can is scratched, the exposed iron rusts — in fact faster than unprotected iron, because the iron is now the more reactive metal.

### Alloys as useful materials

**Most metals in everyday use are alloys.** Pure metals are too soft for many uses because the layers of identical atoms slide over each other easily. In an alloy, atoms of different sizes **distort the layers**, so it is harder for them to slide — alloys are **harder and stronger** (topic 4.2.2).

| Alloy | Composition | Properties | Use you must recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bronze** | **copper and tin** | hard, strong, resists corrosion | statues, medals, bells, ship propellers |
| **Brass** | **copper and zinc** | hard, does not corrode, gold-coloured | musical instruments (trumpets), door handles, taps, plumbing fittings |
| **Gold alloys** | gold with **silver, copper and zinc** | harder than pure gold | **jewellery** |
| **High carbon steel** | iron with a high percentage of carbon | **strong but brittle** | cutting tools, blades, drill bits, hammers |
| **Low carbon steel** (mild steel) | iron with a low percentage of carbon | **softer and more easily shaped** | car body panels, nails, wire, girders |
| **Stainless steel** | iron with **chromium and nickel** | **hard and resistant to corrosion** | cutlery, saucepans, sinks, surgical instruments |
| **Aluminium alloys** | aluminium with other metals (e.g. copper, magnesium) | **low density**, strong | aircraft, bicycle frames, car parts |

**Steels** are alloys of iron that contain **specific amounts of carbon and other metals**.

**Carats (MS 1a, 1c).** The proportion of gold in an alloy is measured in **carats**. **24 carat is 100% (pure gold)**, and **18 carat is 75% gold**.

$$\text{percentage of gold} = \frac{\text{carats}}{24} \times 100$$

- 18 carat: $\frac{18}{24} \times 100 = 75\%$
- 9 carat: $\frac{9}{24} \times 100 = 37.5\%$
- A 12.0 g ring of 18 carat gold contains $0.75 \times 12.0 = 9.0$ g of gold.

You may be given an unfamiliar alloy (e.g. solder, titanium alloys, duralumin) with its composition and properties, and asked to **interpret and evaluate** its uses — link each property in the data to the use.

### Glass and clay ceramics

| Material | How it is made | Properties and uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Soda-lime glass** (most of the glass we use) | **heating a mixture of sand, sodium carbonate and limestone** | transparent, hard, brittle; windows, bottles, jars |
| **Borosilicate glass** | made from **sand and boron trioxide** | **melts at higher temperatures** than soda-lime glass; resists heat and sudden temperature changes — ovenware, laboratory glassware |
| **Clay ceramics** (pottery, bricks) | **shaping wet clay and then heating in a furnace** | hard, strong in compression, brittle, electrical and thermal insulators, high melting point — bricks, tiles, plates, mugs, sinks |

### Polymers

**The properties of polymers depend on what monomers they are made from and the conditions under which they are made.** For example, **low density (LD) and high density (HD) poly(ethene) are both produced from ethene**:

| | LD poly(ethene) | HD poly(ethene) |
|---|---|---|
| Monomer | ethene | ethene |
| Conditions | **high pressure** (about 1000–3000 atmospheres), about 200 °C, trace of oxygen as initiator | **low pressure** (a few atmospheres), lower temperature (about 50–60 °C), **catalyst** |
| Chain structure | **highly branched** chains | **straight chains** with very little branching |
| Packing | branches stop chains packing closely | chains **pack closely** together |
| Properties | **lower density**, flexible, softens at lower temperature | **higher density**, stronger, more rigid, higher softening point |
| Uses | carrier bags, cling film, squeezy bottles | milk bottles, washing-up bowls, water pipes |

**The difference is the conditions**, which change the **structure** (branching), which changes the **properties**.

**Thermosoftening and thermosetting polymers:**

| | Thermosoftening polymers | Thermosetting polymers |
|---|---|---|
| On heating | **melt** (soften) when heated; can be remoulded and recycled | **do not melt** when heated; they char or decompose at high temperature |
| Structure | individual tangled polymer chains held together by **weak intermolecular forces** | chains joined by strong **covalent cross-links** between them |
| Explanation | little energy is needed to overcome the weak forces, so chains slide past each other | the cross-links hold the chains in place; breaking them would mean breaking strong covalent bonds |
| Examples | poly(ethene), poly(propene), PVC, polystyrene | melamine, Bakelite, epoxy resin, polyurethane foam in some forms |
| Uses | bags, bottles, pipes, packaging | kitchen worktops, plug sockets, saucepan handles |

### Composites

**Most composites are made of two materials**: a **matrix or binder** surrounding and binding together **fibres or fragments** of the other material, which is called the **reinforcement**. A composite combines the useful properties of both.

| Composite | Reinforcement | Matrix / binder | Properties and uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reinforced concrete** | steel rods | concrete | strong in compression and tension — buildings, bridges |
| **Fibreglass** (glass-reinforced plastic) | glass fibres | polymer resin | low density, strong, can be moulded — boats, canoes, car bodies |
| **Carbon fibre** composite | carbon fibres | polymer resin | very strong, very low density — racing bikes, tennis rackets, aircraft parts |
| **Wood** (natural composite) | cellulose fibres | lignin | strong, low density — building, furniture |
| **Chipboard / MDF** | wood chips or fibres | resin glue | cheap flat-pack furniture |
| **Concrete** | aggregate (gravel, sand) | cement | strong in compression — foundations, paving |

### Comparing materials quantitatively

You must be able to **compare quantitatively** the physical properties of glass and clay ceramics, polymers, composites and metals from data, and **select an appropriate material**.

| Material | Density / g/cm³ | Tensile strength / MPa | Melting or softening point / °C | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| mild steel | 7.8 | 400 | 1400 | rusts; conducts electricity |
| aluminium alloy | 2.8 | 300 | 600 | corrosion resistant |
| soda-lime glass | 2.5 | 50 | 600 (softens) | brittle, transparent |
| clay brick | 1.9 | 5 | over 1000 | strong in compression, brittle |
| HD poly(ethene) | 0.96 | 30 | 130 | flexible, insulator |
| carbon fibre composite | 1.6 | 1500 | resin decomposes about 300 | very strong, expensive |

**Strength-to-weight (MS 1c):** tensile strength ÷ density. Carbon fibre $= \frac{1500}{1.6} \approx 940$; steel $= \frac{400}{7.8} \approx 51$; aluminium alloy $= \frac{300}{2.8} \approx 110$. Carbon fibre is about **18 times** better than steel per unit mass — which is why it is used for racing bikes, but its **cost** limits its use.

**Selecting a material:** match each required property to data, e.g. a window needs **transparency** (glass); a saucepan handle needs to be a **thermal insulator** that **does not soften** when hot (thermosetting polymer); an aircraft panel needs **low density and high strength** (aluminium alloy or carbon fibre composite).

::: terms
- **Corrosion**: the destruction of materials by chemical reactions with substances in the environment.
- **Rusting**: the corrosion of iron or steel, which needs both oxygen (air) and water.
- **Sacrificial protection**: protecting iron by attaching or coating it with a more reactive metal that corrodes instead.
- **Galvanising**: coating iron or steel with a layer of zinc to give barrier and sacrificial protection.
- **Alloy**: a mixture of a metal with other elements, usually other metals or carbon, that is harder than the pure metal.
- **Carat**: a measure of the proportion of gold in an alloy, where 24 carat is pure gold.
- **Thermosoftening polymer**: a polymer made of separate chains held by weak intermolecular forces, so it melts when heated.
- **Thermosetting polymer**: a polymer with covalent cross-links between chains, so it does not melt when heated.
- **Composite**: a material made of a reinforcement of fibres or fragments held together by a matrix or binder.
:::

::: method
**Rusting experiment questions:** say what each tube **tests** and **how** — boiled water removes dissolved air; the **oil layer** stops air redissolving; **anhydrous calcium chloride** removes water vapour. Conclude: "Rust forms only when both air and water are present." Identify the **control** tube.

**Explain sacrificial protection** in terms of **relative reactivity**: "Zinc is more reactive than iron, so zinc loses electrons / reacts with oxygen and water instead of the iron. The zinc corrodes, not the iron — even if the coating is scratched." Do not just say "zinc protects it".

**Barrier vs sacrificial:** paint, grease, plastic and tin plating are **barriers only** — they fail when scratched. Zinc and magnesium are **sacrificial**.

**Alloy questions:** recall compositions and **one use** each. For unfamiliar alloys, link a **property in the data** to the **use**. For carats: % gold = carats ÷ 24 × 100; mass of gold = fraction × mass of item.

**Polymer questions:** for LD vs HD poly(ethene), name the **same monomer**, the **different conditions** (pressure, catalyst), the **different structure** (branched vs straight chains) and the resulting **properties**. For thermosoftening vs thermosetting, explain using **weak intermolecular forces** vs **cross-links**.

**Comparing materials (calculate / compare / select):** quote values from the table, calculate ratios (e.g. strength ÷ density), and justify the choice with **at least two properties** — and mention cost if relevant.

**6-mark questions:** e.g. "Evaluate materials for a bike frame" — compare density, strength, corrosion and cost for each option using the data, then give a reasoned conclusion.
:::

::: example Rusting experiment
A student sets up three tubes: nail in water open to the air (A); nail in boiled water under a layer of oil (B); nail with anhydrous calcium chloride in a sealed tube (C). Only A rusts. Explain the purpose of the boiled water, the oil and the calcium chloride, and state the conclusion. (4)

Boiling the water removes dissolved air (1). The oil stops air dissolving back into the water (1). The anhydrous calcium chloride absorbs water vapour, so the air in tube C is dry (1). Conclusion: both air and water are needed for iron to rust (1).
:::

::: example Galvanising
Explain why a galvanised steel bucket does not rust even when the zinc coating is scratched. (3)

Zinc is more reactive than iron (1). So the zinc reacts with oxygen and water / loses electrons in preference to the iron (1). The zinc corrodes instead of the iron — this is sacrificial protection, which works even where iron is exposed (1).
:::

::: example Carat calculation
A 14 carat gold chain has a mass of 6.0 g. Calculate the mass of gold in the chain. (2)

Fraction of gold = 14 ÷ 24 = 0.583 (58.3%) (1). Mass of gold = 0.583 × 6.0 = **3.5 g** (1).
:::

::: example LD and HD poly(ethene)
Explain how low density and high density poly(ethene) can both be produced from ethene but have different properties. (4)

Both are made by addition polymerisation of ethene, but under different conditions (1): LD poly(ethene) at very high pressure; HD poly(ethene) at low pressure with a catalyst (1). LD poly(ethene) has highly branched chains that cannot pack closely, so it has a lower density and is more flexible (1). HD poly(ethene) has straight chains that pack closely, so it has a higher density and is stronger and more rigid (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Barrier vs sacrificial.** Painting and tin plating are barriers; only a **more reactive** metal (zinc, magnesium) gives sacrificial protection. Say "**more reactive than iron**" every time.
- **Rusting experiment details.** The oil keeps **air** out; the calcium chloride removes **water**. Mixing these up loses both marks.
- **High vs low carbon steel.** **High** carbon = **strong but brittle**; **low** carbon = **soft, easily shaped**. Students often swap these.
- **Carats.** Divide by **24**, not 100.
- **LD vs HD poly(ethene).** Same monomer — the difference is the **conditions**, giving **branched** or **straight** chains.
- **Thermosetting.** It is the **cross-links** that stop melting — not "stronger intermolecular forces".

::: test
A ship's steel hull has blocks of magnesium bolted to it. (a) Explain how the magnesium stops the hull rusting. (b) Suggest why the blocks must be replaced regularly. (3)
---
(a) Magnesium is more reactive than iron (1), so it loses electrons/corrodes instead of the iron — sacrificial protection (1). (b) The magnesium is used up as it corrodes (1).

::: test
Food cans are made of steel coated with tin. Tin is less reactive than iron. Explain why a scratched tin can rusts quickly, but a scratched galvanised bucket does not. (4)
---
Tin acts only as a barrier; when it is scratched, air and water reach the iron (1). Because iron is more reactive than tin, the iron corrodes (and may rust faster than unprotected iron) (1). Zinc on the bucket is more reactive than iron (1), so the zinc corrodes in preference to the iron even where the coating is scratched — sacrificial protection (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between thermosoftening and thermosetting polymers in terms of their structures, and suggest which type should be used for a saucepan handle. (4)
---
Thermosoftening polymers are made of separate chains held together by weak intermolecular forces (1); these are easily overcome on heating so the polymer melts (1). Thermosetting polymers have (covalent) cross-links between chains so they do not melt (1). A saucepan handle should be thermosetting, because it must not soften or melt when the pan is hot (1).
:::

::: test
Use the data to compare the suitability of aluminium alloy (density 2.8 g/cm³, tensile strength 300 MPa, cost £2 per kg) and carbon fibre composite (density 1.6 g/cm³, tensile strength 1500 MPa, cost £40 per kg) for a racing bike frame. Include a calculation of strength-to-density ratio for each. (4)
---
Aluminium alloy: 300 ÷ 2.8 = 107 (1). Carbon fibre: 1500 ÷ 1.6 = 938 (1). Carbon fibre is about nine times stronger per unit mass, so a lighter yet stronger frame can be made (1). But it is 20 times more expensive per kg; for a racing bike, low mass matters most so carbon fibre is better, while aluminium alloy suits a cheaper bike (1).
:::

::: test
A company makes window frames. It considers using wood, aluminium alloy, mild steel or a thermosoftening polymer (uPVC). Evaluate these materials for window frames, using your knowledge of corrosion and the properties of materials. (6)
---
Levels of response. **Level 3 (5–6):** a detailed comparison of all or most materials, linking specific properties (corrosion, density, strength, insulation, maintenance, cost) to the use, with a justified conclusion. **Level 2 (3–4):** compares some materials with relevant properties; a simple conclusion. **Level 1 (1–2):** a few relevant properties of one or two materials.

Indicative content: wood — renewable, good thermal insulator, but rots/needs painting regularly, natural composite (cellulose fibres in lignin).
