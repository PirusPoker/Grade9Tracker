## What it is

This subsection is in **both** GCSE Chemistry 8462 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 5.9.2). There is no Higher-tier-only content. It covers the **greenhouse effect**, the human activities that add **carbon dioxide** and **methane** to the air, the evidence for **global climate change** and its effects, and the **carbon footprint**.

### Greenhouse gases

**Greenhouse gases** in the atmosphere keep the Earth **warm enough to support life**. Without them the average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about 15 °C. The three you must name are:

- **water vapour**, H₂O
- **carbon dioxide**, CO₂
- **methane**, CH₄

Nitrogen and oxygen, the main gases in air, are **not** greenhouse gases.

### The greenhouse effect: short and long wavelength radiation

You must describe it in terms of how **short** and **long wavelength radiation** interact with matter.

1. The **Sun** emits radiation, mostly **short wavelength** (visible light and some ultraviolet).
2. Short wavelength radiation **passes through the atmosphere** — greenhouse gases do not absorb it much.
3. The radiation is **absorbed by the Earth's surface**, which warms up.
4. The warm Earth **emits radiation of longer wavelength** — **infrared**.
5. **Greenhouse gases absorb** some of this **long wavelength (infrared) radiation**.
6. They then **re-emit** it in **all directions**, including **back towards the Earth's surface**. This keeps the lower atmosphere and surface warmer than they would otherwise be.

The key contrast: the atmosphere is **transparent to the incoming short wavelength** radiation but greenhouse gases **absorb the outgoing long wavelength** radiation. More greenhouse gas means more infrared is absorbed and re-emitted, so the surface temperature rises — the **enhanced greenhouse effect**. (Greenhouse gases do not "trap heat like a blanket" or "reflect" radiation, and the effect is **not** caused by the hole in the ozone layer.)

### Human activities that increase greenhouse gases

You must recall **two** human activities for **each** gas.

| Gas | Human activities that increase it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbon dioxide** | **burning fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas) in power stations, vehicles, homes and industry | releases carbon that was locked up for millions of years |
| | **deforestation** (cutting down and especially burning forests) | fewer trees photosynthesise to remove CO₂; burning or decaying wood releases CO₂ |
| | making **cement** (heating limestone) | CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ |
| **Methane** | **cattle (livestock) farming** | cows and sheep release methane from digestion |
| | **rice paddy fields** | bacteria in waterlogged soil release methane |
| | **landfill sites** | waste decomposes anaerobically, producing methane |
| | extracting and transporting **natural gas** | leaks of natural gas, which is mainly methane |

The **growing world population** and **industrialisation** increase all of these. Methane is present in much smaller amounts than carbon dioxide but each molecule is a **much more powerful** greenhouse gas.

**Working with data (MS 1c, 2c, 4a):** carbon dioxide has risen from about **280 ppm** (parts per million) before the Industrial Revolution to over **420 ppm** today. Percentage increase $= \frac{420 - 280}{280} \times 100 = 50\%$. As a percentage of air, 420 ppm $= \frac{420}{1\,000\,000} \times 100 = 0.042\%$.

### Evidence, uncertainty and peer review (WS 1.2, 1.3, 1.6)

**Based on peer-reviewed evidence, many scientists believe that human activities will cause the temperature of the Earth's atmosphere to increase at the surface** and that this will result in **global climate change**. Evidence includes direct temperature and CO₂ measurements since the mid-1900s and older data from **ice cores** (bubbles of trapped air) and tree rings.

However, **it is difficult to model such complex systems** as the global climate. This leads to:
- **simplified models** that cannot include every variable (clouds, oceans, ice, the Sun's output, volcanoes)
- **speculation and opinions in the media** that may be based on **only parts of the evidence** and may be **biased**.

**Uncertainties in the evidence base:**
- Historical data (ice cores, tree rings) are **less precise** and come from **few locations**.
- Older temperature records were taken in fewer places, mainly on land in the northern hemisphere.
- There is **natural variation** in climate, so short-term trends can mislead.
- A **correlation** between CO₂ and temperature does not on its own prove causation — the mechanism (the greenhouse effect) is what links them.
- Predictions depend on **future emissions**, which are unknown.

**Peer review** means other scientists in the same field check the methods, data and conclusions before publication. It reduces the chance of errors or bias and makes findings more **trustworthy**. Results must also be **communicated to a wide range of audiences** (governments, the public) so decisions can be made — and simplified reporting can lose important detail.

**Evaluating a report on climate change:** ask who wrote or funded it (bias?), whether it is peer reviewed, how big the sample/data set is, how long a time period it covers, whether it uses data from many locations, and whether its conclusion matches the data or only part of it.

### Global climate change and its effects

**An increase in average global temperature is a major cause of climate change.** The average global surface temperature has risen by about 1.2 °C since pre-industrial times. You must describe **four potential effects**:

| Effect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sea level rise** | melting of polar ice caps and glaciers, and thermal expansion of seawater, cause **flooding** of low-lying land and increased **coastal erosion** |
| **More frequent and severe storms** | more energy in the atmosphere and warmer oceans |
| **Changes in the amount, timing and distribution of rainfall** | some areas get floods, others droughts |
| **Temperature and water stress** | for humans and wildlife; heatwaves, water shortages |
| **Changes in food production** | some regions can grow less food; others may grow more |
| **Changes in the distribution of wildlife** | species move to new areas, some may become extinct; pests and diseases (e.g. mosquitoes) spread |

**Scale, risk and environmental implications (WS 1.5):** the changes are **global**, affect billions of people, and are hard to reverse because CO₂ stays in the air for a long time. Poorer, low-lying and hot countries are at greatest **risk**. Implications include loss of habitats and **biodiversity**, ocean acidification as CO₂ dissolves, and migration of people.

### The carbon footprint and its reduction

The **carbon footprint** is the **total amount of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases emitted over the full life cycle of a product, service or event**. It is usually given in kilograms or tonnes of "CO₂ equivalent". It can be reduced by **reducing emissions of carbon dioxide and methane**.

**Actions to reduce emissions:**

| Action | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Use **renewable energy** (wind, solar, hydroelectric) or **nuclear** power | electricity without burning fossil fuels |
| **Energy conservation** — insulating homes, efficient appliances, public transport | less fuel burned |
| **Carbon capture and storage (CCS)** | CO₂ from power stations is captured and stored underground (e.g. in old oil and gas fields) |
| **Carbon taxes and licences** | make polluting more expensive, encouraging cuts |
| **Carbon offsetting**, including **planting trees** | trees absorb CO₂ by photosynthesis |
| **Carbon-neutral fuels** such as biofuels | the plants absorbed as much CO₂ as is released when burned (in theory) |
| Reduce **methane**: less landfill (recycle, compost), capture landfill gas to burn as fuel, eat less beef, reduce gas leaks | less methane released |

**Why actions may be limited:**
- **Scientific disagreement** about causes and consequences.
- **Lack of public information and education.**
- People are reluctant to **change their lifestyle** (e.g. driving less, flying less, eating less meat).
- **Economic considerations** — renewable energy, CCS and new equipment are expensive; countries may fear losing jobs or growth.
- **Lack of international cooperation** — emissions are global, so one country acting alone has limited effect; developing countries want to industrialise.
- Some technologies (CCS) are not yet developed at large scale.

**Mini-example:** a car emits 120 g of CO₂ per km. A journey of 15 km produces $120 \times 15 = 1800$ g = 1.8 kg of CO₂. Sharing the car between three people cuts each person's footprint for the trip to 0.6 kg.

::: terms
- **Greenhouse gas**: a gas, such as water vapour, carbon dioxide or methane, that absorbs long wavelength infrared radiation.
- **Greenhouse effect**: the warming of the Earth's surface because greenhouse gases absorb outgoing infrared radiation and re-emit it back towards the Earth.
- **Short wavelength radiation**: radiation from the Sun, mainly visible light, that passes through the atmosphere to the Earth's surface.
- **Long wavelength radiation**: infrared radiation emitted by the warm Earth, some of which is absorbed by greenhouse gases.
- **Global climate change**: long-term changes in weather patterns caused mainly by an increase in average global temperature.
- **Carbon footprint**: the total amount of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases emitted over the full life cycle of a product, service or event.
- **Peer review**: checking of a scientist's methods, data and conclusions by other experts in the field before publication.
- **Carbon capture and storage**: collecting carbon dioxide from power stations or industry and storing it underground instead of releasing it.
:::

::: method
**Describe the greenhouse effect** in a sequence using the key words: **short wavelength** radiation from the Sun **passes through** the atmosphere → **absorbed** by the Earth's surface → Earth **emits long wavelength (infrared)** radiation → greenhouse gases **absorb** it → **re-emit** in all directions, including back to Earth → surface warms. Never say greenhouse gases "reflect" radiation or "trap heat", and never bring in the ozone layer.

**Recall human activities:** two per gas. CO₂ — burning fossil fuels, deforestation. Methane — cattle farming, rice paddy fields (also landfill). Be specific: "burning fossil fuels in power stations", not "pollution".

**Evaluate a report or data:** comment on **peer review**, **source/bias**, **size of data set and number of locations**, **time span**, **natural variation**, and whether a **correlation** proves cause. End with a judgement: "The data show a correlation, and there is a known mechanism, so the conclusion is supported, but..."

**Describe effects of climate change:** four distinct effects, each with a consequence — "sea levels rise due to melting ice caps, causing flooding of low-lying land". Two variations of the same effect only score once.

**Carbon footprint questions:** "describe actions" → name the action **and** how it cuts CO₂ or methane. "Give reasons why actions may be limited" → cost/economics, lifestyle change, lack of education, scientific disagreement, international cooperation.

**Calculations (MS 1c, 2c, 4a):** percentage increase = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Read values carefully from graphs (check the axis units — ppm or %, and whether the axis starts at zero). Convert ppm to %: divide by 10 000.

**6-mark questions:** e.g. "Explain how human activities could lead to climate change." Link **activity → gas → greenhouse effect mechanism → temperature rise → effect**. Level 3 needs the full chain with the short/long wavelength explanation.
:::

::: example The greenhouse effect
Explain how greenhouse gases keep the Earth's surface warm. (4)

Short wavelength radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere (1) and is absorbed by the Earth's surface, which warms up (1). The Earth emits long wavelength (infrared) radiation (1). Greenhouse gases absorb some of this infrared radiation and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the Earth, keeping the surface warmer (1).
:::

::: example Percentage increase
The concentration of methane in the atmosphere was 0.72 ppm in 1750 and is 1.92 ppm now. Calculate the percentage increase. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. (2)

Increase = 1.92 − 0.72 = 1.20 ppm (1). Percentage increase = $\frac{1.20}{0.72} \times 100 = 166.7\%$, which is **170%** to 2 significant figures (1).
:::

:::

::: watch
- **Ozone hole confusion.** The greenhouse effect has nothing to do with the ozone layer. Do not mention it.
- **"Traps heat" or "reflects".** Say greenhouse gases **absorb long wavelength (infrared) radiation and re-emit it**, some back towards the Earth.
- **Wrong wavelengths.** Radiation **in** from the Sun is **short** wavelength; radiation **out** from the Earth is **long** wavelength. Swapping them loses the mark.
- **Vague activities.** "Pollution" or "cars" alone may not score — say "burning fossil fuels". For methane, give cattle farming and rice paddy fields.
- **Repeating effects.** "Floods" and "sea level rise" may count as one point; give four clearly different effects.
- **Percentage increase.** Divide by the **original** value, not the new one.
:::

::: test
A family's annual carbon footprint is 12.5 tonnes of CO₂ equivalent. Heating the home accounts for 28% of this. Fitting insulation cuts the heating emissions by one-quarter. (a) Calculate the emissions from heating. (b) Calculate the family's new total carbon footprint. (3)
---
(a) 0.28 × 12.5 = **3.5 tonnes** (1). (b) Saving = 3.5 ÷ 4 = 0.875 tonnes (1). New total = 12.5 − 0.875 = **11.6 tonnes** (11.625; accept 11.6 or 11.63) (1).

::: test
A newspaper headline says: "Cold winter proves global warming is not happening." Evaluate this claim. (3)
---
One winter is weather in one place / a single data point, not a long-term global average (1). Climate change is about the trend in average global temperature over decades, and there is natural year-to-year variation (1). Conclusion: the claim is not valid / is based on only part of the evidence and may be biased; the peer-reviewed long-term data show warming (1).
:::
