## What it is

**Gas pressure comes from collisions.** The molecules of a gas move **randomly** at high speed in all directions. Each time one hits the wall of its container it bounces off, changing momentum, and so exerts a tiny **force** on the wall. Billions of collisions every second over the whole surface add up to a steady **pressure** (force per unit area) on every part of the container — this is why gas pressure acts equally in all directions.

**Temperature and molecular motion.** Heating a gas makes its molecules move **faster**: the temperature of a gas is a measure of the **average kinetic energy** of its molecules. Faster molecules hit the walls harder and more often, so at fixed volume the pressure rises.

**Absolute zero.** Cooling a gas slows its molecules. If you plot pressure (at constant volume) against temperature in °C you get a straight line; extend it backward and it reaches zero pressure at **−273 °C**. At this temperature the molecules would have the **minimum possible energy** — they cannot move any slower, so the gas can be cooled no further. This is **absolute zero**, the lowest temperature possible.

**The Kelvin scale** starts at absolute zero, so **0 K = −273 °C** and there are no negative temperatures. A kelvin is the same size as a degree Celsius, so

$$T(\text{K}) = \theta(°\text{C}) + 273 \qquad \theta(°\text{C}) = T(\text{K}) - 273$$

Room temperature (20 °C) is 293 K; water boils at 373 K; ice melts at 273 K.

**Why kelvin matters.** On the Kelvin scale the **average kinetic energy of the molecules is directly proportional to the absolute temperature**: double the temperature in kelvin and the average kinetic energy doubles. This is *not* true in °C (doubling 10 °C to 20 °C barely changes the molecular energy). It is also why the gas laws (topic 5d) must use kelvin.

::: method
1. **Explain pressure**: molecules move randomly → collide with the walls → each collision exerts a force → force over area = pressure.
2. **Explain a temperature effect**: hotter → molecules faster (more KE) → hit harder and more often → pressure rises (at constant volume).
3. **Convert temperatures**: K = °C + 273 before any proportional reasoning or gas-law calculation.
4. **Absolute zero**: the temperature at which molecules have minimum energy (essentially stop moving), −273 °C = 0 K; found by extrapolating a pressure-temperature line to zero pressure.
5. Proportionality: average KE ∝ **kelvin** temperature — check any "double the temperature" statement is in K.
:::

::: example Pressure from particles
Explain how the molecules of a gas produce a pressure on the walls of a container. (3)

Molecules move randomly at high speed (1); when they collide with the wall they change momentum, exerting a force on it (1); the enormous number of collisions over the area of the wall produces a steady pressure (1).
:::

::: example Heating a sealed can
A sealed can of air is heated. Explain, in terms of molecules, why the pressure inside increases. (3)

The molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster (1); they hit the walls more often and with greater force (1); more force on the same area means a higher pressure (1).
:::

::: example Temperature conversion
Convert 27 °C to kelvin and 200 K to °C. (2)

27 + 273 = **300 K** (1). 200 − 273 = **−73 °C** (1).
:::

::: example Kinetic energy and temperature
A gas is warmed from 100 K to 300 K. State how the average kinetic energy of its molecules changes. Explain why the same statement cannot be made for a change from 100 °C to 300 °C. (3)

The average kinetic energy triples (1), because it is proportional to the kelvin temperature. From 100 °C to 300 °C is 373 K to 573 K (1), a factor of only about 1.5 — the Celsius scale does not start at absolute zero so ratios of °C values mean nothing (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how a graph could be used to find absolute zero. (3)
---
Measure the pressure of a fixed volume of gas at several temperatures (1); plot pressure against temperature in °C and draw the straight line (1); extend (extrapolate) the line back to zero pressure — it meets the temperature axis at −273 °C (1).
:::

::: q
Why does a bicycle tyre pressure rise on a hot day? (2)
---
The air molecules move faster (1) and collide with the tyre walls harder and more often (1).
:::

::: watch
- Pressure explanations need **collisions with the walls** and **force over area**; "the molecules push" is not enough.
- Temperature effects: molecules hit **harder AND more often** — both.
- **K = °C + 273.** Do not subtract, and do not use °C in proportional statements.
- Absolute zero is where molecules have **minimum** energy, not "no molecules" or "the gas disappears".
- Average KE ∝ **kelvin** temperature; any "double the temperature" claim in °C is wrong.
:::
