## What it is

**Classifying stars by colour.** A star's **colour tells you its surface temperature**. The hottest stars glow **blue-white** (surface 20 000 K or more), then white, yellow (like the Sun, about 6000 K), orange, and the coolest **red** (about 3000 K). The reason is that a hotter surface emits more of its radiation at shorter wavelengths — the peak shifts from red toward blue as temperature rises. So "red = cool, blue = hot" is the opposite of a tap's colour code and worth fixing in memory.

**The life of a star like the Sun.**

1. **Nebula** — a vast cloud of gas (mainly hydrogen) and dust. **Gravity** pulls the material together.
2. **Protostar** — as the cloud collapses it gets denser and hotter. When the core reaches millions of degrees, **fusion of hydrogen into helium** begins.
3. **Main sequence star** — a long stable period (the Sun's is about 10 billion years, half over) in which the **outward pressure from fusion** balances the **inward pull of gravity**. The star's size and brightness stay steady.
4. **Red giant** — when the core's hydrogen runs low, fusion slows, the core contracts and heats, and the outer layers **expand** hugely and **cool**, turning red. Helium fuses into heavier elements such as carbon.
5. **White dwarf** — fusion stops; the outer layers drift away as a planetary nebula and the core collapses under gravity into a small, very hot, very dense white dwarf, which slowly cools over billions of years into a **black dwarf**.

**The life of a more massive star.** The first three stages are the same, but faster — massive stars burn through their fuel in millions rather than billions of years.

4. **Red supergiant** — far larger than a red giant; fusion continues through heavier elements up to iron.
5. **Supernova** — the iron core cannot release energy by fusing, so it collapses catastrophically and the star **explodes**, briefly outshining its galaxy. The explosion forms and scatters the **elements heavier than iron** through space — the material of later stars and planets, including Earth.
6. **Neutron star** — if the remaining core is a few times the Sun's mass, gravity crushes it into an incredibly dense ball of neutrons a few kilometres across.
7. **Black hole** — if the core is more massive still, nothing can halt the collapse; gravity becomes so strong that not even light can escape.

::: terms
- **Star colour and temperature**: red stars are coolest, then orange, yellow, white, and blue stars are hottest.
- **Nebula**: a cloud of dust and gas from which stars form.
- **Main sequence star**: a stable star fusing hydrogen into helium, in which the outward pressure from fusion balances the inward pull of gravity.
- **Red giant**: a star like the Sun after its core hydrogen runs out, when it swells and cools.
- **White dwarf**: the small, hot, dense core left when a red giant sheds its outer layers.
- **Red supergiant**: a very large star formed when a massive star runs out of core hydrogen.
- **Supernova**: the explosion of a massive star, which scatters heavy elements into space.
- **Neutron star**: the extremely dense core left after a supernova.
- **Black hole**: a collapsed core so dense that not even light can escape its gravity.
:::

::: method
1. Colour ↔ temperature: **blue hottest, red coolest**, yellow (Sun) in between.
2. Learn the two sequences as chains and know **where they split** (after main sequence, by mass).
3. Main sequence stability: **fusion pressure outward = gravity inward**. Say both forces.
4. Red giant: hydrogen in the core running out → core contracts → outer layers expand and cool.
5. Massive star ending: supernova (explains heavy elements) → neutron star or black hole depending on the core's mass.
:::

::: example Colour and temperature
Star A appears blue-white and star B appears red. Which has the higher surface temperature? Explain. (2)

Star A (1); hotter surfaces emit more of their light at shorter (bluer) wavelengths, while cooler surfaces emit mainly longer, red wavelengths (1).
:::

::: example Stability of the Sun
Explain why the Sun has remained roughly the same size for billions of years. (3)

The Sun is a main sequence star fusing hydrogen into helium in its core (1); the outward pressure from the energy released by fusion (1) balances the inward force of gravity, so it neither expands nor collapses (1).
:::

::: example The Sun's future
Describe the stages the Sun will go through after its main sequence period ends. (4)

As core hydrogen runs out the core contracts and heats while the outer layers expand and cool, forming a red giant (1); helium fuses into heavier elements such as carbon (1); when fusion ends the outer layers drift away and the core collapses into a hot, dense white dwarf (1), which cools slowly to a black dwarf (1).
:::

::: example Massive stars
Describe how the end of a star much more massive than the Sun differs from the Sun's, and why supernovae matter. (4)

After the main sequence it becomes a red supergiant, fusing elements up to iron (1); the iron core collapses and the star explodes as a supernova (1); the remnant becomes a neutron star, or a black hole if its mass is large enough (1). Supernovae create and scatter the elements heavier than iron, from which later stars, planets and living things are made (1).
:::

::: q
What does the colour of a star indicate? (1)
---
Its surface temperature.
:::

::: q
Arrange these colours from coolest to hottest star: white, red, blue, yellow. (1)
---
Red, yellow, white, blue.
:::

::: q
What is a nebula? (1)
---
A large cloud of gas (mostly hydrogen) and dust in space.
:::

::: q
What force causes a nebula to collapse into a protostar? (1)
---
Gravity.
:::

::: q
What must happen in a protostar's core for it to become a main sequence star? (1)
---
The temperature must rise enough for hydrogen nuclei to begin fusing into helium.
:::

::: q
What are the two balanced forces in a main sequence star? (2)
---
Outward pressure from fusion (radiation/thermal) (1) and inward gravitational force (1).
:::

::: q
Why does a star expand into a red giant? (2)
---
Hydrogen in the core runs low so the core contracts and heats up (1); this makes the outer layers expand greatly and cool, glowing red (1).
:::

::: q
What is a white dwarf? (1)
---
The small, very hot, very dense core left when a Sun-like star's fusion ends.
:::

::: q
What is a supernova? (1)
---
The huge explosion of a massive star after its red supergiant stage, when the core collapses.
:::

::: q
What determines whether a supernova leaves a neutron star or a black hole? (1)
---
The mass of the remaining core — the most massive cores collapse into black holes.
:::

::: watch
- **Blue = hottest, red = coolest**; do not reverse it.
- Main sequence stability needs **both** forces named: fusion pressure out, gravity in.
- The two life cycles are identical until **after the main sequence**; the split is decided by **mass**.
- A supernova comes from a **red supergiant**, not a red giant; the Sun will never explode.
- Heavy elements (beyond iron) are made in **supernovae** — a common one-mark "why do supernovae matter" question.
:::
