## What it is

**Absolute magnitude** is a measure of how bright a star **really is** — its brightness as it would appear from a standard distance (10 parsecs, about 33 light years) — so that stars can be compared fairly regardless of how far away they are. (Apparent magnitude is how bright it looks from Earth, which depends on distance too.) The magnitude scale runs backwards: **the lower (or more negative) the number, the brighter the star**. The Sun's absolute magnitude is about +4.8; a bright supergiant might be −7; a faint dwarf +15.

**The Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram** plots stars' **absolute magnitude** (vertical axis, brightest at the top) against **surface temperature** (horizontal axis, hottest on the **left** — another backwards convention). Stars are not scattered randomly; they fall into groups that correspond to stages of their lives:

- **Main sequence** — a diagonal band from top-left (hot, bright, blue, massive) to bottom-right (cool, dim, red, small). Most stars, including the Sun (in the middle), sit here, fusing hydrogen.
- **Red giants and supergiants** — top-right: cool (red) but very bright because they are enormous.
- **White dwarfs** — bottom-left: hot (white) but dim because they are tiny.

A star moves around the diagram as it ages: onto the main sequence when fusion starts, up and right to the giant region when it expands, and down to the white dwarf corner when it collapses.

**The Big Bang.** The universe began about **13.8 billion years ago** from an extremely small, hot, dense point that has been **expanding** ever since: space itself stretches, carrying the galaxies apart. Two key pieces of evidence:

1. **Red-shift of galaxies** — the light from almost every galaxy is shifted toward longer (redder) wavelengths, showing they are moving away from us; the **further away** a galaxy, the **greater** its red-shift, so the faster it recedes. That is exactly what an expanding universe predicts: everything moves apart from everything else, with the most distant objects separating fastest.
2. **Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMB)** — faint microwave radiation coming uniformly from every direction in space. It is the leftover glow of the hot early universe, stretched by expansion from visible light into microwaves, and its existence was predicted before it was detected.

**Doppler shift for light.** Just as a receding siren sounds lower, light from a source moving **away** has its wavelength **stretched** to longer values (red-shift); from a source moving **toward** us it is squashed (blue-shift). The size of the shift depends on the speed:

$$\frac{\text{change in wavelength}}{\text{reference wavelength}} = \frac{\text{velocity of source}}{\text{speed of light}} \qquad \frac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0} = \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0} = \frac{v}{c}$$

where λ₀ is the wavelength measured in the laboratory, λ the wavelength observed from the galaxy, and c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Astronomers compare the dark absorption lines in a galaxy's spectrum with the same lines measured on Earth; the lines are all shifted by the same fraction. The **red-shift equation** therefore gives a galaxy's **recession speed**, and since speed is proportional to distance, red-shift also tells us how **far away** the galaxy is.

::: terms
- **Absolute magnitude**: the brightness a star would have if it were a standard distance (10 parsecs) from Earth.
- **Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram**: a graph of absolute magnitude (brightness) against surface temperature for stars.
- **Red-shift**: an increase in the wavelength of light from a galaxy moving away from us.
- **Red-shift equation**: Δλ ÷ λ₀ = v ÷ c.
- **Big Bang theory**: the universe began from a very small, very hot, very dense point and has been expanding ever since.
- **Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR)**: microwave radiation from all directions, the cooled remains of radiation from the early universe.
- **Evidence for expansion**: light from more distant galaxies is more red-shifted, so they are moving away faster.
:::

::: method
1. HR diagram: axes are **temperature decreasing to the right** and **brightness increasing upward**. Place main sequence (diagonal), giants (top right), white dwarfs (bottom left).
2. Locate a star from its description: hot + dim → white dwarf; cool + bright → red giant; anything on the diagonal → main sequence.
3. Big Bang evidence: **red-shift ∝ distance** (expansion) and **CMB** (leftover radiation). Give both with what each shows.
4. Red-shift calculation: $\Delta\lambda = \lambda - \lambda_0$; $v = c \times \Delta\lambda/\lambda_0$. Keep wavelengths in the same unit (nm is fine because it is a ratio).
5. Interpretation: larger red-shift → faster → further away.
:::

::: example Placing stars on the HR diagram
Star P is very hot but very dim. Star Q is cool but extremely bright. Where is each on an HR diagram, and what type of star is it? (4)

P: bottom-left — a white dwarf (hot surface, tiny size so low brightness) (2). Q: top-right — a red giant or supergiant (cool surface but huge size so very bright) (2).
:::

::: example Red-shift calculation
A hydrogen absorption line has a laboratory wavelength of 656 nm. In the spectrum of a distant galaxy the same line appears at 672 nm. Calculate the galaxy's speed and state whether it is approaching or receding. (4)

Δλ = 672 − 656 = 16 nm (1). $v = c \times \Delta\lambda/\lambda_0 = 3 \times 10^8 \times 16 \div 656$ (1) = **7.3 × 10⁶ m/s** (1). The wavelength has increased (red-shift), so the galaxy is moving away (1).
:::

::: example Red-shift as evidence for expansion
Explain how the red-shift of galaxies provides evidence that the universe is expanding. (3)

Light from nearly all galaxies is red-shifted, showing they are moving away from us (1); the more distant a galaxy, the larger its red-shift, so the faster it is receding (1). This is what would be seen if space itself were expanding and carrying all galaxies apart — as it would after a Big Bang (1).
:::

::: example Cosmic microwave background
What is the cosmic microwave background radiation, and why does it support the Big Bang theory? (3)

Microwave radiation detected coming from every direction in space at almost exactly the same intensity (1); it is the radiation left over from the very hot early universe, red-shifted by expansion from visible light into microwaves (1); the Big Bang theory predicted it before it was discovered, and no other theory explains it (1).
:::

::: example Explain — absolute magnitude
Two stars look equally bright from Earth, but one is much further away. Explain what this tells you about their absolute magnitudes. (3)

How bright a star looks depends on both its actual brightness and its distance from Earth (1). The more distant star must give out much more light to look as bright as the nearer one (1). So it has the brighter absolute magnitude — the brightness each would have at the same standard distance (1).
:::

What does the Big Bang theory state? (2)

::: q
What is red-shift? (2)
---
The increase in wavelength (shift toward the red end of the spectrum) (1) of light from a source moving away from the observer (1).
:::

::: q
Write the red-shift equation. (1)
---
Δλ / λ₀ = v / c (change in wavelength ÷ reference wavelength = velocity ÷ speed of light).
:::

::: q
A line at 500 nm is observed at 510 nm. Calculate the recession speed. (3)
---
Δλ = 10 nm (1); v = 3 × 10⁸ × 10 ÷ 500 (1) = 6 × 10⁶ m/s (1).
:::

::: q
How is the red-shift of a galaxy related to its distance from Earth? (1)
---
The greater the distance, the greater the red-shift (and the recession speed).
:::

::: q
Light from the Andromeda galaxy is slightly blue-shifted. What does this tell you? (1)
---
It is moving toward us (its light's wavelength is shortened).
