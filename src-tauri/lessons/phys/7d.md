## What it is

Radioactive sources are chosen for a job by matching **the type of radiation** (how far it penetrates, how ionising it is) and the **half-life** (long enough to be useful, short enough not to linger) to the task.

**Uses in industry.**

- **Thickness control**: paper, plastic film or aluminium foil passes between a **beta** source and a detector on the production line. If the sheet gets thicker, fewer beta particles get through and the rollers are automatically adjusted. Beta is chosen because alpha would be stopped by any thickness and gamma would barely notice a change; a long half-life keeps the reading steady.
- **Leak detection**: a **gamma**-emitting tracer with a short half-life is added to the liquid in an underground pipe; a detector above ground finds the leak where the count rate is highest. Gamma penetrates the soil; a short half-life means it soon becomes harmless.
- **Smoke detectors**: an **alpha** source (americium-241) ionises the air between two plates so a small current flows; smoke absorbs the alpha particles, the current drops and the alarm sounds. Alpha is safe here because it cannot penetrate the casing or the air in the room.
- **Sterilising** medical instruments and food: gamma rays kill bacteria without heating.
- **Dating**: carbon-14 (half-life 5730 years) for once-living material; uranium and potassium isotopes for rocks.

**Uses in medicine.**

- **Tracers**: a **gamma** emitter with a **short half-life** (e.g. technetium-99m, 6 hours) is injected or swallowed, concentrates in the organ of interest, and a gamma camera outside the body maps where it goes — blood flow, kidney function, tumours. Gamma escapes the body to be detected; the short half-life limits the dose.
- **Radiotherapy**: high-energy **gamma** beams from cobalt-60 (or X-rays) are aimed at a tumour from several directions to kill cancer cells while limiting the dose to healthy tissue; or a small **beta/gamma** source is implanted next to the tumour.

**Contamination and irradiation.**

- **Irradiation** — being exposed to radiation from an outside source. It stops when you move away or shield the source; it does **not** make you radioactive. Sterilised food is irradiated — it is safe to eat.
- **Contamination** — getting radioactive **material** on or in you (dust, gas, liquid). The source keeps decaying wherever it is, so the exposure continues until it is removed or decays. Contamination by an alpha emitter is the most dangerous, because inside the body all its energy is absorbed in a small volume of tissue.

**Dangers and how they are managed.** Ionising radiation damages cells: it can **kill** them (radiation burns, sickness at high doses) or **mutate** their DNA, which may cause **cancer**. Protection follows three rules: **time** (keep exposure short), **distance** (intensity falls rapidly with distance — use tongs, stand back), **shielding** (lead aprons and walls, thick concrete, the source in a lead-lined box). Workers wear film badges to monitor dose, and sources are stored in lead containers, handled with tongs, never pointed at people and labelled with the trefoil symbol.

::: method
1. For any use, justify **type** (penetration/ionisation) and **half-life** (long for steady industrial use, short for anything entering the body).
2. Tracers: **gamma** (escapes the body) + **short half-life** (low dose) — both, every time.
3. Thickness control: **beta** — alpha too weak, gamma too strong — plus long half-life.
4. Irradiation vs contamination: *exposure to* vs *having the material*; contamination is worse because it persists.
5. Safety: **time, distance, shielding** — name all three and give a concrete example of each.
:::

::: example Choosing a tracer
Explain why a medical tracer should emit gamma radiation and have a short half-life. (4)

Gamma is very penetrating, so it can pass out of the body to be detected by a gamma camera (1); alpha or beta would be absorbed inside the body, causing damage and giving no image (1). A short half-life means the activity falls quickly after the scan (1), so the patient receives only a small dose (1).
:::

::: example Thickness control
Explain why a beta source with a long half-life is used to monitor the thickness of paper in a mill. (3)

Beta is partly absorbed by paper, so a small change in thickness gives a measurable change in the count reaching the detector (1); alpha would be completely stopped and gamma would pass through almost unchanged (1). A long half-life keeps the source's activity constant, so the count only changes because the thickness changes (1).
:::

::: example Contamination versus irradiation
A worker spills a radioactive liquid on her gloves. Explain why this is more serious than briefly standing near the sealed source. (3)

Standing near the source is irradiation — it stops as soon as she moves away (1). The spilt liquid is contamination: the radioactive material is now on her and keeps decaying, exposing her continuously (1); it could also be transferred to skin, breathed in or swallowed, where alpha and beta would deliver all their energy inside the body (1).
:::

::: example Protecting radiographers
Describe three precautions taken by hospital staff who work with radioactive sources. (3)

Keep the time of exposure as short as possible (1); keep as far from the source as possible, using tongs and standing behind screens — intensity drops rapidly with distance (1); use shielding such as lead aprons, lead-lined containers and thick walls, and monitor dose with a film badge (1).
:::

::: q
Why is an alpha source used in a smoke detector rather than gamma? (2)
---
Alpha ionises the air strongly so the small current is easily disrupted by smoke (1); its short range means it cannot escape the detector and harm people (1).
:::

::: q
Which type of radiation is used to sterilise surgical instruments, and why? (2)
---
Gamma (1); it penetrates the packaging and kills bacteria without heat (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how a radioactive tracer can locate a leak in an underground pipe. (3)
---
A gamma emitter with a short half-life is added to the fluid (1); a detector is moved along the ground above the pipe (1); the count rate is highest where fluid has leaked into the soil (1).
:::

::: q
How is radiation used to treat cancer? (2)
---
Beams of gamma rays (or X-rays) are focused on the tumour from several directions (1) to kill the cancer cells while limiting damage to surrounding healthy tissue (1).
:::

::: q
Define irradiation. (1)
---
Being exposed to radiation from a source outside the body (without the source touching you).
:::

::: q
Define contamination. (1)
---
Radioactive material getting onto or into an object or person.
:::

::: q
Does irradiating food make it radioactive? Explain. (1)
---
No — radiation passes through and kills bacteria, but no radioactive material is left in the food.
:::

::: q
Why is an alpha emitter particularly dangerous inside the body? (2)
---
Alpha is the most ionising radiation (1), and inside the body all its energy is absorbed by cells in a very small volume, causing severe damage (1).
:::

::: q
State two effects of ionising radiation on living cells. (2)
---
It can kill cells (1) or damage DNA, causing mutations that may lead to cancer (1).
:::

::: q
State the three general principles for reducing exposure to a radioactive source. (3)
---
Minimise time (1); maximise distance (1); use shielding (1).
:::

::: watch
- Every "why this source" answer needs **both** the radiation type **and** the half-life justified.
- Tracer = **gamma + short half-life**; thickness gauge = **beta + long half-life**; smoke alarm = **alpha**.
- Irradiation does **not** make things radioactive; contamination does persist. Use the words precisely.
- Alpha is harmless outside the body and the **most dangerous inside** — say why (most ionising, all energy absorbed locally).
- Safety: **time, distance, shielding**, each with an example (tongs, lead box, short exposure).
:::

::: test
A radioactive tracer is injected to investigate a kidney. State the type of radiation and half-life it should have, with reasons. (3)
---
Gamma (1) so it can be detected outside the body without being absorbed (1); a short half-life of a few hours so the patient's dose is small but there is time to scan (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why gamma radiation is used to sterilise medical equipment, and why the equipment is not radioactive afterwards. (2)
---
It kills bacteria and can penetrate sealed packaging (1); irradiation transfers energy but no radioactive material, so nothing radioactive remains (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the thickness of paper can be controlled using a beta source and a detector. (3)
---
The paper passes between a beta source and a detector (1); thicker paper absorbs more beta, so the count falls (1); the reading controls the rollers to keep the thickness constant — alpha would be stopped completely and gamma would hardly change (1).
:::

::: test
Compare the dangers of an alpha source outside the body and inside the body. (2)
---
Outside it is almost harmless — stopped by skin or air (1); inside it is very dangerous, because all its ionising energy is absorbed by nearby cells (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how carbon-14 is used to date ancient wood. (3)
---
Living things take in carbon-14 while alive, at a fixed proportion (1); after death no more is taken in and the carbon-14 decays with a half-life of 5700 years (1); comparing the remaining activity with that of living wood gives the number of half-lives, so the age (1).
:::

::: test
Give two precautions taken by workers handling radioactive sources. (2)
---
Any two of: use tongs to keep the source at a distance; keep exposure time short; store sources in lead containers; wear a film badge; never point a source at anyone (2).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between contamination and irradiation, with an example of each. (2)
---
Contamination: radioactive material gets onto or into an object, e.g. dust from an accident on clothing, which keeps emitting (1). Irradiation: exposure to radiation from a source without touching it, e.g. an X-ray, which stops when the source is removed (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why radiotherapy for cancer uses beams from several directions. (2)
---
Each beam passes through healthy tissue, so a single beam would damage it as much as the tumour (1); rotating the beams around the tumour gives it the full dose while spreading the dose to healthy cells (1).
:::
