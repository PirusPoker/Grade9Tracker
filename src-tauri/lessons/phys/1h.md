## What it is

A force acting on a pivoted object makes it **turn**. The turning effect is called the **moment** of the force:

$$\text{moment (N m)} = \text{force (N)} \times \text{perpendicular distance from the pivot (m)}$$

The distance must be measured **at right angles** to the line of action of the force. A longer spanner, or pushing a door at its edge rather than by the hinge, gives a bigger moment for the same force — that is why levers work. Moments are described as **clockwise** or **anticlockwise**.

**The principle of moments**: when an object is **balanced** (in equilibrium) about a pivot,

$$\text{sum of clockwise moments} = \text{sum of anticlockwise moments}$$

and the resultant force on it is also zero. Use this to find an unknown force or distance on a seesaw, a beam or a crane.

**Centre of gravity** is the point through which the whole **weight** of an object can be considered to act. For a uniform ruler or beam it is at the **midpoint**; for a regular shape, at the geometric centre. An object placed so that its centre of gravity lies **over its base** is stable; tip it until the centre of gravity passes outside the base and it topples. Low centre of gravity and wide base = stable (racing cars, Bunsen burners). Hanging an irregular sheet from two different points with a plumb line locates its centre of gravity where the lines cross.

**A beam on two supports.** A horizontal beam resting on two supports has an **upward force** at each support. Their sum equals the total downward weight, and they share it according to where the load sits: take moments about **one support** (its own force has zero distance, so it drops out) to find the other force, then subtract from the total.

::: terms
- **Moment**: the turning effect of a force: moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot, in N m.
- **Pivot**: the point about which an object turns.
- **Centre of gravity**: the point through which the whole weight of an object appears to act.
- **Principle of moments**: for an object in equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about a pivot equals the sum of anticlockwise moments.
- **Stability**: an object is more stable with a low centre of gravity and a wide base.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the pivot.** Measure every distance from it, at right angles to the force.
2. Label each moment **clockwise or anticlockwise**.
3. **Balanced** → clockwise = anticlockwise. Write the equation with force × distance for every force, including the beam's own weight at its centre of gravity if the pivot is not there.
4. **Two supports**: take moments about one support (removes that unknown), solve for the other, then use *up = down* for the first.
5. Units: N and **m** give N m. Convert cm to m unless every distance stays in the same unit and the answer is a force or distance.
:::

::: example Moment of a force
A spanner is 0.25 m long. A force of 60 N is applied at its end, perpendicular to the spanner. Calculate the moment, and state how the moment changes if the force is applied at the middle. (3)

Moment = 60 × 0.25 (1) = **15 N m** (1). At the middle the distance halves, so the moment halves to 7.5 N m (1).
:::

::: example Balancing a seesaw
A 30 kg child sits 2.0 m from the pivot of a seesaw. Where must a 40 kg child sit on the other side to balance it? (g = 10 N/kg) (3)

Anticlockwise moment = 300 N × 2.0 m = 600 N m (1). Clockwise: 400 × d = 600 (1), so d = **1.5 m** from the pivot (1).
:::

::: example Beam on two supports
A uniform beam of weight 200 N and length 4 m rests on supports at each end. A 300 N load sits 1 m from the left end. Find the force at each support. (4)

Take moments about the left support: clockwise = 300 × 1 + 200 × 2 = 700 N m (1). Anticlockwise = R₂ × 4 (1). R₂ = 700 ÷ 4 = **175 N** (1). Up = down: R₁ = 500 − 175 = **325 N** (1).
:::

::: example Centre of gravity and stability
Explain why a double-decker bus is tested by tilting it, and why passengers are only allowed to stand on the lower deck. (3)

The bus topples once its centre of gravity moves outside its wheelbase (1); passengers upstairs raise the centre of gravity (1), so the bus would topple at a smaller angle — keeping them low keeps the centre of gravity low and the bus stable (1).
:::

Define the moment of a force. (2)
---
Force × perpendicular distance from the pivot (1) to the line of action of the force (1).
:::

::: q
Give the unit of a moment. (1)
---
Newton metre (N m).
:::

::: q
Calculate the moment of a 25 N force applied 0.4 m from a pivot. (2)
---
25 × 0.4 (1) = 10 N m (1).
:::

::: q
State the principle of moments. (2)
---
For a body in equilibrium (1), the sum of clockwise moments about a point equals the sum of anticlockwise moments (1).
:::

::: q
A metre rule is pivoted at its centre. A 2 N weight hangs 40 cm to the left. What weight 25 cm to the right balances it? (3)
---
2 × 40 = 80 N cm (1); W × 25 = 80 (1); W = 3.2 N (1).
:::

::: q
What is meant by the centre of gravity of an object? (1)
---
The point through which the object's whole weight appears to act.
:::

::: q
Where is the centre of gravity of a uniform metre rule? (1)
---
At its midpoint, the 50 cm mark.
:::

::: q
Describe how to find the centre of gravity of an irregular card. (3)
---
Hang the card freely from a pin through a hole near its edge (1); hang a plumb line from the same pin and mark the vertical line on the card (1); repeat from a second hole — the centre of gravity is where the lines cross (1).
:::

::: q
Give two features that make an object stable. (2)
---
Low centre of gravity (1); wide base (1).
:::

::: q
A 6 m uniform plank of weight 400 N rests on supports at both ends. A 600 N person stands 2 m from the right end. Find the force at the right support. (3)
---
Moments about the left end: 400 × 3 + 600 × 4 = 3600 N m (1); R × 6 = 3600 (1); R = 600 N (1).
:::

::: watch
- The distance in a moment is the **perpendicular** distance from the pivot to the force's line of action.
- Include the beam's **own weight at its centre** whenever the pivot is not at the centre — it is the most commonly forgotten moment.
- For two supports, take moments **about a support** so one unknown vanishes, then use forces up = forces down.
- Keep all distances in the **same unit**; N cm is acceptable in working, but a final *moment* should be in N m.
- "Balanced" means **both** zero resultant moment *and* zero resultant force.
:::

::: test
A spanner is 0.3 m long. A force of 80 N is applied at its end, at right angles. Calculate the moment about the nut. (2)
---
$M = Fd = 80 \times 0.3$ (1) $= 24$ N m (1).
:::

::: test
A seesaw is balanced on a pivot at its centre. A child of weight 300 N sits 1.5 m from the pivot. How far from the pivot must an adult of weight 750 N sit on the other side? (3)
---
Clockwise moment $= 300 \times 1.5 = 450$ N m (1); $750 \times d = 450$ (1); $d = 0.6$ m (1).
:::

::: test
A uniform beam of length 4 m and weight 200 N is supported at both ends. A 600 N load is placed 1 m from the left end. Calculate the force from the right-hand support. (4)
---
Take moments about the left end: $R \times 4 = 600 \times 1 + 200 \times 2$ (2); $4R = 1000$ (1); $R = 250$ N (1).
:::

::: test
Hence find the force from the left-hand support in the question above. (1)
---
Total upward $=$ total downward $= 800$ N, so left support $= 800 - 250 = 550$ N.
:::

::: test
Explain why a door handle is placed as far from the hinges as possible. (2)
---
Moment $=$ force $\times$ distance from the pivot (1), so the same push gives a bigger turning effect — the door opens more easily (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a racing car with a low, wide body is more stable than a tall, narrow van. (2)
---
Its centre of gravity is lower and its base is wider (1), so it must tilt much further before the line of action of its weight falls outside the base and it topples (1).
:::

::: test
Describe, with the moments involved, what happens to a tall box tilted so that its centre of gravity lies exactly above the edge it is pivoting on. (2)
---
The weight acts through the pivot, so it has no moment — the box is balanced (1); the slightest further tilt puts the weight beyond the edge and its moment topples the box (1).
:::

::: test
A crane has a counterweight on the opposite side of the tower from its load. Explain the purpose of the counterweight. (2)
---
It provides an anticlockwise moment to balance the clockwise moment of the load (1), so the resultant moment about the tower is close to zero and the crane does not topple (1).
:::

::: test
A 400 N child sits 1.2 m from the pivot of a seesaw. Calculate the distance from the pivot at which a 320 N child must sit to balance it. (3)
---

::: test
Explain why it is easier to open a door by pushing near the handle than near the hinges. (2)
---
The moment = force × distance from the pivot (hinge) (1); a larger distance means a smaller force is needed to give the same turning effect (1).
:::

::: test
A uniform plank 5.0 m long, weighing 300 N, rests on two supports, one at each end. A painter of weight 700 N stands 1.0 m from the left end. Calculate the upward force from each support. (6)
---
Take moments about the left support: clockwise = 700 × 1.0 + 300 × 2.5 = 700 + 750 = 1,450 N m (2). Right support force × 5.0 = 1,450, so right support = 290 N (2). Total upward force = total weight = 1,000 N, so left support = 1,000 − 290 = 710 N (2).
:::
