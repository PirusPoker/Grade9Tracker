## What it is

Units for this section: activity in **becquerels (Bq)**, distances in **cm**, times in **hours, minutes and seconds**.

**Atomic structure.** An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged **nucleus** of **protons** (charge +1, mass 1) and **neutrons** (charge 0, mass 1), surrounded by **electrons** (charge −1, negligible mass) in shells. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Nearly all the atom is empty space.

**Nuclide notation** describes a nucleus:

$$^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \qquad \text{e.g. } ^{238}_{92}\text{U},\ ^{14}_{6}\text{C},\ ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

- **Z, the proton (atomic) number** — the number of protons; it fixes which element it is.
- **A, the nucleon (mass) number** — protons + neutrons.
- Number of neutrons = A − Z.

**Isotopes** are atoms of the **same element** (same Z) with **different numbers of neutrons** (different A): carbon-12 and carbon-14, uranium-235 and uranium-238. They have identical chemistry but different masses, and some are unstable.

**Radioactivity.** Some nuclei are **unstable**; they **decay** by emitting radiation to become more stable. The decay is **random** — you cannot say when a particular nucleus will go — and unaffected by temperature or chemical state. The three radiations are all **ionising**: they knock electrons off atoms they pass, which is what makes them detectable and dangerous.

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons | Fast electron from the nucleus (a neutron → proton + electron) | High-frequency electromagnetic wave |
| Charge | +2 | −1 | 0 |
| Mass | 4 | ~1/2000 | 0 |
| Ionising power | Very high | Medium | Low |
| Penetration | Few cm of air; stopped by **paper** or skin | About 1 m of air; stopped by a few mm of **aluminium** | Very penetrating; reduced by thick **lead** or metres of concrete |
| Deflection by fields | Deflected (slightly) | Deflected strongly, the opposite way | Not deflected |

The pattern: the more ionising a radiation, the more energy it gives up per collision, so the **less** far it penetrates. Alpha is harmless outside the body but very damaging inside; gamma passes straight through you.

**Practical: penetrating powers.** Use a **Geiger-Müller tube and counter**. First measure the **background count** with no source. Place a source a fixed distance from the tube and record the count rate; then insert paper, then aluminium sheets, then lead between them. Subtract background each time. A large drop with paper means alpha is present; a drop with a few mm of aluminium means beta; a count that only lead reduces means gamma. Handle sources with tongs, keep them in lead-lined boxes, point them away from people, and keep exposure time short.

::: method
1. **Read the notation**: bottom number = protons, top = protons + neutrons; neutrons = top − bottom. Electrons = protons in a neutral atom.
2. **Isotope** = same protons, different neutrons. Say both parts.
3. **Identify a radiation** from what stops it (paper → α, aluminium → β, lead → γ) or from its nature (helium nucleus, electron, EM wave).
4. Link **ionising power inversely to penetration**: alpha most ionising, least penetrating.
5. In the practical: **background first**, subtract it, fixed distance, add absorbers in order, safety with tongs/lead box/distance.
:::

::: example Reading nuclide notation
For the nucleus $^{235}_{92}\text{U}$, state the number of protons, neutrons and (in the neutral atom) electrons. (3)

Protons = 92 (1); neutrons = 235 − 92 = 143 (1); electrons = 92 (1).
:::

::: example Isotopes
Explain what is meant by isotopes, using carbon-12 and carbon-14 as examples. (3)

Isotopes are atoms of the same element, with the same number of protons (1) but different numbers of neutrons (1); carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons (1).
:::

::: example Identifying a radiation
A source's count rate is unchanged by a sheet of paper but falls almost to background behind 5 mm of aluminium. What radiation is it emitting? Explain. (3)

Not alpha, since paper would have stopped it (1); the aluminium stopped it, which is characteristic of beta (1); no significant count remained, so no gamma is present — the source is a pure beta emitter (1).
:::

::: example Why alpha is stopped so easily
Explain why alpha particles are highly ionising but have a very short range in air. (3)

Alpha particles are relatively massive and carry a +2 charge (1), so they interact strongly with the electrons of atoms they pass, ionising many of them (1); each ionisation takes energy from the particle, so it loses all its energy within a few centimetres (1).
:::

::: q
State the charge and relative mass of a proton, a neutron and an electron. (3)
---
Proton +1, mass 1 (1); neutron 0, mass 1 (1); electron −1, mass ≈ 1/2000 (negligible) (1).
:::

::: q
What do the nucleon number and proton number tell you? (2)
---
Nucleon number = protons + neutrons (1); proton number = number of protons, which identifies the element (1).
:::

::: q
How many neutrons are in $^{60}_{27}\text{Co}$? (1)
---
60 − 27 = 33 neutrons.
:::

::: q
What is an alpha particle? (1)
---
A helium nucleus: 2 protons and 2 neutrons.
:::

::: q
What is a beta particle and where does it come from? (2)
---
A fast-moving electron (1) emitted from the nucleus when a neutron changes into a proton (1).
:::

::: q
What is gamma radiation? (1)
---
A high-frequency (high-energy) electromagnetic wave emitted from the nucleus.
:::

::: q
Which radiation is (a) most ionising, (b) most penetrating? (2)
---
(a) Alpha (1); (b) gamma (1).
:::

::: q
What is meant by ionising radiation? (1)
---
Radiation that removes electrons from atoms it passes, creating ions.
:::

::: q
Why is radioactive decay described as random? (1)
---
It is impossible to predict when any particular nucleus will decay.
:::

::: q
In the penetration experiment, why is the background count measured first? (1)
---
So it can be subtracted from all readings, leaving only the count due to the source.
:::

::: watch
- **Neutrons = A − Z.** Students regularly write the mass number as the neutron number.
- Isotopes: **same protons, different neutrons** — both halves for the mark.
- Beta particles come from the **nucleus** (neutron → proton + electron), not from the electron shells.
- Alpha: paper; beta: aluminium; gamma: lead — and the **most ionising is the least penetrating**.
- Always **subtract background**; and gamma is only *reduced* by lead, never fully "stopped".
:::

::: test
Describe the structure of an atom, including where the mass and charge are. (3)
---
A tiny central nucleus of protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral) (1) containing almost all the mass (1); electrons (negative) orbit in shells, and the atom is neutral because electron and proton numbers are equal (1).
:::

::: test
Uranium-235 and uranium-238 are isotopes. Explain what this means and give the numbers of neutrons in each. (Proton number 92) (3)
---
Same number of protons, different numbers of neutrons (1); U-235 has 143 (1); U-238 has 146 (1).
:::

::: test
Write the nuclide symbols for an alpha particle and a beta particle. (2)
---
$^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ (or $^{4}_{2}\alpha$) (1); $^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ (or $^{0}_{-1}\beta$) (1).
:::

::: test
Give the approximate range in air and the material needed to stop each of alpha, beta and gamma radiation. (3)
---
Alpha: a few cm, stopped by paper or skin (1); beta: about a metre, stopped by a few mm of aluminium (1); gamma: very long range, reduced by thick lead or concrete (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why alpha radiation is the most ionising but the least penetrating. (2)
---
It is large and has a charge of $+2$, so it pulls electrons off atoms very effectively (1); each ionisation costs it energy, so it is stopped after a short distance (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how you could identify which type of radiation a source emits using absorbers and a Geiger-Müller tube. (3)
---
Measure the count rate with no absorber, then with paper, then with aluminium (1); if paper cuts it to background it is alpha; if aluminium does, beta (1); if only thick lead reduces it, gamma; subtract background from every reading (1).
:::

::: test
What is meant by the term 'nucleon'? (1)
---
A particle in the nucleus — a proton or a neutron.
:::

::: test
An atom of sodium has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons. Write its nuclide symbol and state its mass number. (2)
---
$^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$ (1); mass number 23 (1).
:::
