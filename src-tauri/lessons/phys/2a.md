## What it is

An electric circuit needs a complete loop of conductor and a source of energy — a **cell** or battery — to push **charge** around it. The units: **current** in **amperes (A)**, **charge** in **coulombs (C)**, **voltage** (potential difference) in **volts (V)**, **resistance** in **ohms (Ω)**, energy in **joules (J)**, power in **watts (W)** and time in **seconds (s)**.

- **Current** (*I*) is the rate at which charge flows — how many coulombs pass a point each second. It is measured with an **ammeter** connected **in series** (in the loop). Conventional current flows from + to −.
- **Voltage / potential difference** (*V*) across a component is the energy transferred per coulomb of charge passing through it. It is measured with a **voltmeter** connected **in parallel** (across the component).
- **Resistance** (*R*) is how much a component opposes current. A larger resistance means a smaller current for the same voltage.

**Ohm's law** ties the three together:

$$V = I \times R \qquad \text{voltage (V) = current (A) × resistance (Ω)}$$

In a **series circuit** the current depends on the total voltage supplied and the total resistance: $I = V/R_{total}$. Increase the resistance (add a resistor, lengthen the wire, turn up a variable resistor) and the current **decreases**; increase the voltage and the current **increases**. Resistances in series simply **add**.

::: method
1. Write V = IR and the rearrangements: $I = V/R$, $R = V/I$. The triangle: V on top, I and R below.
2. Identify which quantity is **across a component** (voltage) and which **through it** (current).
3. In series: $R_{total} = R_1 + R_2 + \ldots$; the same current flows through every component; the supply voltage is shared.
4. Convert prefixes: mA ÷ 1000 → A; kΩ × 1000 → Ω.
5. "What happens to the current if…" — reason from $I = V/R$: more R, less I; more V, more I.
:::

::: example Ohm's law
A resistor carries a current of 0.30 A when the voltage across it is 6.0 V. Calculate its resistance. (2)

$R = V/I = 6.0 \div 0.30$ (1) = **20 Ω** (1).
:::

::: example Current in a series circuit
A 12 V battery is connected in series with resistors of 4 Ω and 8 Ω. Find the current and the voltage across the 8 Ω resistor. (4)

Total resistance = 4 + 8 = 12 Ω (1). $I = 12 \div 12 =$ **1 A** (1). Voltage across 8 Ω: $V = IR = 1 \times 8 =$ **8 V** (2).
:::

::: example Changing the resistance
A lamp of resistance 15 Ω is connected to a 3 V supply. A variable resistor is added in series and set to 15 Ω. Explain what happens to the current and calculate its new value. (3)

Total resistance doubles to 30 Ω, so the current halves (1). Original I = 3 ÷ 15 = 0.2 A; new I = 3 ÷ 30 (1) = **0.1 A** (1).
:::

::: q
Give the units of current, voltage, resistance and charge. (2)
---
Ampere (A), volt (V) (1); ohm (Ω), coulomb (C) (1).
:::

::: q
How are an ammeter and a voltmeter connected in a circuit? (2)
---
Ammeter in series with the component (1); voltmeter in parallel across it (1).
:::

::: q
Write Ohm's law as an equation. (1)
---
V = I × R.
:::

::: q
What current flows through a 50 Ω resistor connected to 10 V? (2)
---
I = 10 ÷ 50 (1) = 0.2 A (1).
:::

::: q
A current of 2.5 A flows through a heater of resistance 92 Ω. Find the voltage across it. (2)
---
V = 2.5 × 92 (1) = 230 V (1).
:::

::: q
Resistors of 6 Ω, 10 Ω and 14 Ω are in series. What is their total resistance? (1)
---
6 + 10 + 14 = 30 Ω.
:::

::: q
A lamp draws 250 mA from a 6 V battery. Calculate its resistance. (2)
---
I = 0.25 A; R = 6 ÷ 0.25 (1) = 24 Ω (1).
:::

::: q
State what happens to the current in a series circuit when a second identical resistor is added. Explain. (2)
---
It halves (decreases) (1) because the total resistance doubles while the voltage stays the same (1).
:::

::: q
What does a voltmeter reading across a component tell you? (1)
---
The energy transferred per coulomb of charge passing through the component (the potential difference).
:::

::: q
In a series circuit with a 9 V battery and two resistors, the voltage across one is 4 V. What is the voltage across the other? (1)
---
5 V (the supply voltage is shared).
:::

::: watch
- **Voltage is across, current is through.** A "current across a resistor" costs marks.
- Convert **mA to A** and **kΩ to Ω** before V = IR.
- In series the **current is the same everywhere**; the voltages add up to the supply. Do not divide the current between components.
- Explain changes with $I = V/R$ in words: "resistance increases, so for the same voltage the current decreases".
- Draw meters correctly: ammeter breaks the loop, voltmeter bridges the component.
:::

::: test
A 12 V battery drives a current of 0.4 A through a resistor. Calculate the resistance. (2)
---
$R = V \div I = 12 \div 0.4$ (1) $= 30\ \Omega$ (1).
:::

Describe how to connect an ammeter and a voltmeter to measure the resistance of a lamp, and state the calculation. (3)

Ammeter in series with the lamp (1); voltmeter in parallel across the lamp (1); $R = V \div I$ (1).

::: test
Resistors of 4 $\Omega$, 6 $\Omega$ and 20 $\Omega$ are connected in series to a 15 V supply. Calculate the current. (2)
---
Total resistance $30\ \Omega$ (1); $I = 15 \div 30 = 0.5$ A (1).
:::

::: test
In the series circuit above, calculate the voltage across the 20 $\Omega$ resistor. (2)
---
$V = IR = 0.5 \times 20$ (1) $= 10$ V (1).
:::

::: test
Explain, in terms of electrons, why a longer wire has a greater resistance. (2)
---
Electrons collide with the metal ions as they move (1); a longer wire means more collisions along the way, so more opposition to the current (1).
:::

::: test
State what happens to the current in a circuit if the voltage is doubled and the resistance is halved. (1)
---
The current becomes four times larger ($I = V/R$).
:::
