## What it is

Components can be joined in two ways, and the rules for current and voltage are different for each.

**Series** — one loop, components one after another.

- The **same current** flows through every component.
- The **supply voltage is shared** between the components: $V_{supply} = V_1 + V_2 + \ldots$ (the bigger resistance takes the bigger share).
- Total resistance = sum of the resistances; adding a component **increases** resistance and reduces the current.
- If one component fails (a bulb blows) the circuit is broken and **everything stops**.

**Parallel** — components on separate branches between the same two points.

- The **voltage across each branch is the same** as the supply voltage.
- The current **splits** at a junction and **rejoins**: current into a junction = current out (charge is conserved). The branch with the smaller resistance takes the larger current.
- Adding a branch gives the current another route, so the total resistance **decreases** and the total current from the supply increases.
- Each branch works independently: one bulb blowing leaves the others lit, and each can have its own switch.

**Choosing.** Series is used when components must act together or share a voltage (a string of identical fairy lights, a switch controlling a whole circuit, resistors to limit current). Parallel is used when each component needs the full voltage and independent control — household lighting and sockets, car headlights, anything where one failure must not switch off the rest.

**Two components with different resistances.** In series, the current is the same, so voltages are in the ratio of the resistances ($V = IR$). In parallel, the voltage is the same, so currents are in the *inverse* ratio ($I = V/R$): a 2 Ω and a 4 Ω resistor in parallel across 12 V carry 6 A and 3 A, total 9 A.

::: terms
- **Series circuit**: the current is the same everywhere; the supply voltage is shared between the components; total resistance = R₁ + R₂.
- **Parallel circuit**: each branch has the full supply voltage; the currents in the branches add up to the total current.
- **Junction rule**: the current entering a junction equals the current leaving it — current is conserved.
- **Advantage of parallel wiring**: each component can be switched on and off independently, and if one fails the others still work.
- **Adding resistors in parallel**: lowers the total resistance, so the total current from the supply increases.
:::

::: method
1. **Decide: series or parallel?** Trace the loops. Components on the same single loop are in series; components on separate branches between the same two junctions are in parallel.
2. **Series**: add resistances → $I = V/R_{total}$ → voltage across each = $IR$. Check the voltages add to the supply.
3. **Parallel**: each branch has the supply voltage → $I_{branch} = V/R$ for each → total current = sum of branches.
4. **Junction rule**: current in = current out. Use it to find a missing branch current.
5. Predict qualitatively: adding in series reduces current; adding in parallel increases total current.
:::

::: example Series circuit
A 6 V battery is in series with a 2 Ω and a 4 Ω resistor. Calculate the current and the voltage across each resistor. (4)

$R_{total} = 6$ Ω, $I = 6 \div 6 = 1$ A (1). $V_2 = 1 \times 2 = 2$ V (1); $V_4 = 1 \times 4 = 4$ V (1). Check: 2 + 4 = 6 V, the supply (1).
:::

::: example Parallel circuit
A 12 V battery supplies a 3 Ω and a 6 Ω resistor in parallel. Find the current in each branch and the current from the battery. (4)

Both branches have 12 V across them (1). $I_3 = 12 \div 3 = 4$ A (1); $I_6 = 12 \div 6 = 2$ A (1). Battery current = 4 + 2 = **6 A** (1).
:::

::: example Junction rule
At a junction, 5 A flows in and splits between two branches. One branch carries 1.8 A. What is the current in the other, and what happens to the total if that branch is removed? (3)

Current out must equal current in, so the other branch carries 5 − 1.8 = **3.2 A** (1). Removing the branch removes its route, so the total current falls to 1.8 A (1) — the remaining branch still has the full voltage, so its current is unchanged (1).
:::

::: example Why parallel at home
Explain two reasons why the lamps in a house are wired in parallel rather than in series. (2)

Each lamp receives the full mains voltage, so it works at full brightness whatever else is on (1). Each lamp can be switched independently, and if one fails the others stay on (1).
:::

::: q
In a series circuit, what can be said about the current through each component? (1)
---
It is the same through every component.
:::

::: q
How is the supply voltage related to the voltages across components in series? (1)
---
The supply voltage equals the sum of the voltages across the components.
:::

::: q
State the rule for voltage across components connected in parallel. (1)
---
It is the same across each branch (equal to the supply voltage).
:::

::: q
State the rule for current at a junction. (1)
---
Total current into the junction equals total current out (current is conserved).
:::

::: q
Three identical lamps are connected in series to a 9 V battery. What is the voltage across each? (1)
---
9 ÷ 3 = 3 V across each lamp.
:::

::: q
A 12 Ω and a 4 Ω resistor are in parallel across 12 V. Find the current in each. (2)
---
12 ÷ 12 = 1 A (1); 12 ÷ 4 = 3 A (1).
:::

::: q
Currents of 0.6 A and 0.9 A flow into a junction. What current leaves it? (1)
---
1.5 A.
:::

::: q
Explain why adding a resistor in parallel decreases the total resistance. (2)
---
It provides an extra path for the current (1), so more total current flows for the same voltage, which means lower resistance (1).
:::

::: q
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of connecting fairy lights in series. (2)
---
Advantage: one switch/simple wiring, voltage shared so low-voltage bulbs can run from mains (1). Disadvantage: if one bulb fails the whole string goes out (1).
:::

::: q
A 2 Ω and 8 Ω resistor are in series across a 5 V supply. Find the voltage across the 8 Ω resistor. (3)
---
I = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5 A (1); V = 0.5 × 8 (1) = 4 V (1).
:::

::: watch
- Series: **same current, shared voltage**. Parallel: **same voltage, split current**. Say which rule you are using.
- The larger resistor takes the **larger voltage** in series but the **smaller current** in parallel — easy to invert under pressure.
- Adding a parallel branch **lowers** total resistance and **raises** supply current; adding in series does the opposite.
- Check series answers: the component voltages must **add up to the supply**.
- "Independent switching" and "one failure doesn't stop the rest" are the standard reasons for household parallel wiring.
:::

::: test
Two 6 $\Omega$ resistors are connected in parallel. Calculate the combined resistance. (2)
---
$\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{6} = \dfrac{1}{3}$ (1); $R = 3\ \Omega$ (1).
:::

::: test
A 3 $\Omega$ and a 6 $\Omega$ resistor are connected in parallel across a 6 V battery. Calculate the current through each resistor and the total current. (3)
---
Each has 6 V across it: $2$ A through the $3\ \Omega$ (1), $1$ A through the $6\ \Omega$ (1); total $3$ A (1).
:::

Three lamps are connected in series to a 12 V supply and each has the same resistance. What is the voltage across each lamp? If one lamp breaks, what happens to the others? (2)

4 V each (1); all go out — the circuit is broken (1).
:::

Explain why the lights in a house are wired in parallel rather than in series. (2)
---
Each light gets the full mains voltage and can be switched independently (1); if one fails the others keep working (1).
:::

::: test
A circuit has a 2 $\Omega$ resistor in series with a parallel pair of 4 $\Omega$ resistors, all across a 12 V supply. Calculate the total resistance and the current from the supply. (3)
---
Parallel pair $= 2\ \Omega$ (1); total $= 4\ \Omega$ (1); $I = 12 \div 4 = 3$ A (1).
:::

::: test
At a junction, 1.5 A flows in along one wire and splits into two wires. If 0.9 A flows in one, what flows in the other? State the rule you used. (2)
---
$0.6$ A (1); current into a junction equals current out — charge is conserved (1).
:::

::: test
Two cells of 1.5 V each are connected in series, then a 5 $\Omega$ resistor is connected across them. Calculate the current. (2)
---
Total voltage $3$ V (1); $I = 3 \div 5 = 0.6$ A (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why adding more resistors in parallel increases the current drawn from the supply. (2)
---
Each new branch provides another path for charge, so the total resistance falls (1); with the supply voltage unchanged, $I = V/R$ increases (1).
:::
