## What it is

**Conductors** (metals, graphite, salty water) let charge move through them because they contain free electrons or ions. **Insulators** (plastics, rubber, glass, dry air) have no free charges, so charge placed on them **stays where it is put** — which is exactly what makes static electricity possible.

**Charging by friction.** Rub a polythene rod with a cloth and **electrons are transferred** from the cloth to the rod: the rod gains electrons and becomes **negatively charged**, the cloth loses electrons and becomes **positively charged**. Rub acetate (or glass) and electrons go the other way — the rod becomes positive. Only **electrons move**; the positive charges are the nuclei and stay put. Charge is conserved: the rod and cloth gain equal and opposite charges.

**Forces between charges.** **Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.** The force acts at a distance and gets weaker as the charges separate. A charged rod also attracts *uncharged* objects (small pieces of paper, a stream of water) by **induction**: the rod's charge repels like charges in the object to the far side, leaving the near side oppositely charged and therefore attracted.

**Practical: charging insulators.** Rub two polythene rods with a cloth, hang one from a thread and bring the other near: they **repel** (same charge). Bring a rubbed acetate rod near the hanging polythene rod: they **attract** (opposite charges). A gold-leaf electroscope or a charged rod picking up paper shows the same effects.

**Explaining electrostatic effects.** Everything comes down to **electron movement**: a shock from a car door is charge that built up on you (electrons rubbed on or off by the seat) jumping to earth; clothes crackle when electrons are pulled off one fabric onto another; a balloon sticks to a wall because its electrons repel the wall's electrons, leaving the wall's surface positive.

**Dangers of static.**

- **Refuelling** aircraft and tankers: fuel rushing through a pipe rubs off electrons and charge builds up; a spark could ignite fuel vapour. The prevention is an **earthing strap/bonding wire** between the vehicle and the fuel truck so charge flows harmlessly to earth.
- **Lightning**: ice and water droplets in a storm cloud rub together, the base of the cloud becomes strongly charged, and the enormous voltage causes a spark to the ground. Lightning conductors (metal strips to earth) give a safe path.
- Sparks near flammable gases in operating theatres, grain silos and paint spraying — floors and clothing are made slightly conducting.

**Uses of static.**

- **Photocopiers and laser printers**: a drum is charged, light discharges it where the page is white, negatively charged toner powder sticks to the remaining charged areas, and the image is transferred to paper and fused by heat.
- **Inkjet printers**: droplets of ink are given a charge and deflected by charged plates onto exactly the right spot of the paper.
- **Electrostatic paint spraying**: the spray nozzle charges the paint droplets, which repel each other into a fine even mist and are attracted to the earthed (oppositely charged) car body, coating even the back.
- Dust precipitators in chimneys, and removing dust from air.

::: terms
- **Conductor**: a material that lets charge (electrons) flow through it easily, such as a metal.
- **Insulator**: a material that does not let charge flow, such as plastic or rubber.
- **Charging by friction**: rubbing two insulators together transfers electrons from one to the other.
- **Negative charge**: gained when an object gains electrons.
- **Positive charge**: gained when an object loses electrons.
- **Like charges**: repel each other.
- **Unlike charges**: attract each other.
- **Earthing**: connecting a charged object to earth so the charge flows away safely.
:::

::: method
1. **Which way did the electrons go?** Whatever gained electrons is **negative**; whatever lost them is **positive**. Never say protons moved.
2. State the rule: **like charges repel, unlike attract**, then apply it to the objects in the question.
3. For attraction of a *neutral* object: charged rod → repels/attracts electrons in the object → near surface gets the opposite charge → attraction.
4. Danger questions: charge builds by friction → high voltage → **spark** → ignites fuel/vapour. Safety: **earth** it so charge leaks away.
5. Use questions (photocopier, inkjet, paint): say **what is charged**, and that **opposite charges attract** to explain where the toner/ink/paint goes.
:::

::: example Charging by friction
A polythene rod is rubbed with a woollen cloth and becomes negatively charged. Explain what happens to the rod and to the cloth. (3)

Friction transfers electrons from the cloth to the rod (1); the rod gains electrons so becomes negatively charged (1); the cloth has lost electrons so is left with an equal positive charge (1).
:::

::: example Attracting a neutral object
A charged balloon is held near small pieces of paper, which jump to it. Explain why the uncharged paper is attracted. (3)

The negative balloon repels electrons in the paper to the far side (1), leaving the near side of the paper positively charged (1); unlike charges attract, and the nearer positive side is attracted more strongly than the farther negative side is repelled, so the paper moves to the balloon (1).
:::

::: example Refuelling an aircraft
Explain why an aircraft is connected to the fuel tanker by a wire before refuelling begins. (3)

Fuel flowing through the hose rubs against it and transfers electrons, so static charge builds up (1); the charge could produce a spark, which could ignite the fuel vapour (1); the wire earths/bonds the aircraft so the charge flows away safely and no spark forms (1).
:::

::: example Electrostatic paint spraying
Explain how static electricity gives an even coat of paint when spraying a car body. (3)

The paint droplets are all given the same charge as they leave the nozzle, so they repel each other and spread into a fine, even mist (1); the car body is earthed or given the opposite charge (1); the droplets are attracted to the body, including surfaces facing away from the sprayer, so less paint is wasted and the coat is even (1).
:::

::: q
What is the difference between an electrical conductor and an insulator? (2)
---
A conductor contains charges (free electrons) that can move through it (1); an insulator has no free charges so charge cannot flow through it (1).
:::

::: q
Which particles are transferred when an insulator is charged by friction? (1)
---
Electrons.
:::

::: q
A cloth loses electrons when it rubs a rod. What charge does the rod have, and what charge is left on the cloth? (2)
---
The rod gains the electrons so it is negative (1); the cloth is left positive, with an equal and opposite charge (1).
:::

::: q
State the rule for forces between charges. (2)
---
Like charges repel (1); unlike charges attract (1).
:::

::: q
Two rubbed polythene rods are brought together. What happens and why? (2)
---
They repel (1) because both carry the same (negative) charge (1).
:::

::: q
Why does a charged comb attract a thin stream of water? (2)
---
The comb's charge induces the opposite charge on the near side of the water (1), and unlike charges attract (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how lightning occurs. (3)
---
Ice/water particles in the cloud rub together, transferring electrons (1); the cloud base builds up a large charge and a huge voltage to the ground (1); the air breaks down and a giant spark (discharge) jumps to earth (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how a photocopier uses static electricity. (3)
---
A drum is charged and an image of the page is projected onto it; light discharges the drum where the page is white (1); charged toner powder is attracted to the remaining charged (dark) areas (1); the toner is transferred to paper and fixed by heat (1).
:::

::: q
Why is a spark dangerous in a room where fuel is stored? (1)
---
It can ignite the flammable fuel vapour, causing a fire or explosion.
:::

::: q
How does earthing prevent the build-up of static charge? (2)
---
It connects the object to the ground with a conductor (1) so any charge flows away to earth instead of accumulating (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Electrons move, never protons.** Any answer with positive charge "moving onto" a rod is wrong.
- The two rubbed objects get **equal and opposite** charges — charge is transferred, not created.
- Attraction of a **neutral** object needs the induction explanation, not "it has the opposite charge".
- Danger answers must end at **spark → ignition**; safety answers must mention **earthing**.
- Photocopier/inkjet/paint: name **what is charged** and use **attraction of opposite charges** explicitly.
:::

Friction transfers electrons between balloon and jumper, charging the balloon (1); the charged balloon repels like charges in the wall's surface, leaving the nearer surface oppositely charged (1); opposite charges attract, holding it up (1).
:::

Negative (1); electrons are transferred from the rod to the cloth — only electrons move (1).

::: test
Explain why an aircraft is earthed before refuelling. (3)
---
Friction with the air charges the aircraft in flight (1); a spark from the charge could ignite the fuel vapour (1); an earth wire lets the charge flow safely to earth first (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how electrostatic paint spraying works and give one advantage. (3)
---
The paint droplets are given the same charge, so they repel and spread into a fine mist (1); the object is given the opposite charge (or earthed), so the paint is attracted to it (1); less paint is wasted and it coats the back and hidden surfaces (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a charged rod attracts small pieces of uncharged paper. (2)
---
The rod's charge repels like charges in the paper to the far side, leaving the near side oppositely charged (1); the attraction of the near side is stronger than the repulsion of the far side, so there is a net attraction (1).
:::

::: test
Why does a metal rod not become charged when rubbed while held in the hand? (2)
---
Metal conducts (1); any charge flows through the body to earth as fast as it is produced (1).
:::

::: test
Give one reason a person can get a small shock touching a car door after a journey. (2)
---
Friction between the seat and clothes (or tyres and air) charges the person or car (1); touching the metal lets the charge flow suddenly to earth through the body (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a lightning conductor protects a building. (2)
---
It provides a low-resistance metal path from the top of the building to earth (1), so the lightning current flows through it rather than through the building (1).
:::
