## What it is

When a force is applied to a spring, wire or rubber band it **extends** (or compresses). The **extension** is the *increase* in length — stretched length minus original length — never the total length.

**Hooke's law**: for a spring (and a metal wire) below its **limit of proportionality**, the extension is **directly proportional** to the force applied:

$$F = k \times x \qquad \text{force (N) = spring constant (N/m) × extension (m)}$$

A graph of force against extension is a **straight line through the origin** in this region; its gradient is the spring constant *k*, a measure of stiffness (a stiff spring needs a large force for a small extension). Beyond the limit of proportionality the graph curves — equal increases in force give bigger and bigger extensions.

**Elastic behaviour** means the object **returns to its original length when the force is removed**. Up to the **elastic limit** a spring is elastic; stretch it past that and it is permanently deformed (**plastic** behaviour) — it will not spring back fully. The limit of proportionality and the elastic limit are close together for a spring but are different points. Rubber bands are elastic but **do not obey Hooke's law**: their force-extension graph is curved throughout.

**Practical: extension against force.** Hang the spring from a clamp, fix a metre rule beside it, and read the position of a pointer at the bottom of the spring with **no load** — that is the original length. Add masses one at a time (each 100 g adds 1 N), reading the new position each time; extension = reading − original reading. Take readings on **unloading** too to check the spring has behaved elastically (same readings = no permanent stretch). Plot force (vertical) against extension (horizontal). Read the rule at **eye level** to avoid parallax, wait for the spring to stop bouncing, and keep away from the elastic limit so the spring is not ruined. The same method works for a wire (small extensions, so use a long wire) and a rubber band (curved graph).

::: method
1. **Extension = new length − original length.** Convert cm or mm to **m** for F = kx (k in N/m).
2. From a graph: *k* = gradient of the straight-line section = force ÷ extension. Read a point far from the origin.
3. **F = kx** rearranges to $x = F/k$ or $k = F/x$. Load from a hanging mass: F = mg with g = 10 N/kg.
4. "Does it obey Hooke's law?" — straight line through the origin = yes, up to the point where it curves.
5. "Is it elastic?" — does it return to its original length when unloaded? That is the test, and it is a different question from Hooke's law.
:::

::: example Spring constant from data
A spring is 8.0 cm long unloaded and 12.5 cm long with a 3 N load. Calculate the spring constant. (3)

Extension = 12.5 − 8.0 = 4.5 cm = 0.045 m (1). $k = F/x = 3 \div 0.045$ (1) = **66.7 N/m** (1).
:::

::: example Extension under a hanging mass
A spring with k = 250 N/m has a 400 g mass hung from it. Find its extension. (3)

Force = weight = 0.400 × 10 = 4 N (1). $x = F/k = 4 \div 250$ (1) = **0.016 m** (1.6 cm) (1).
:::

::: example Interpreting a graph
A force-extension graph for a spring is a straight line to (0.20 m, 30 N) and then curves upward-and-over. Explain what the shape shows and find k. (3)

Up to 0.20 m the extension is proportional to the force — the spring obeys Hooke's law (1). Beyond that point (the limit of proportionality) it no longer does; extensions grow more for each newton (1). $k = 30 \div 0.20 =$ **150 N/m** (1).
:::

::: example Elastic or not?
A spring is stretched to 20 cm and, when unloaded, returns to 17 cm instead of its original 12 cm. What has happened? (2)

It has been stretched beyond its **elastic limit** (1), so it has deformed plastically/permanently and no longer returns to its original length (1).
:::

::: q
Define the extension of a spring. (1)
---
The increase in length: stretched length minus original (unloaded) length.
:::

::: q
State Hooke's law. (2)
---
The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied (1), provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded (1).
:::

::: q
Write the equation linking force, spring constant and extension, with units. (2)
---
F = kx (1); N, N/m, m (1).
:::

::: q
A spring extends 0.05 m under a force of 12 N. Calculate its spring constant. (2)
---
k = 12 ÷ 0.05 (1) = 240 N/m (1).
:::

::: q
What force stretches a spring of constant 80 N/m by 15 cm? (2)
---
F = 80 × 0.15 (1) = 12 N (1).
:::

::: q
On a force-extension graph, what does the gradient of the straight section represent? (1)
---
The spring constant k.
:::

::: q
What is meant by elastic behaviour? (1)
---
The object returns to its original shape/length when the force is removed.
:::

::: q
Does a rubber band obey Hooke's law? Explain. (2)
---
No (1); its force-extension graph is curved, not a straight line through the origin (1).
:::

::: q
In the spring practical, give two ways of making the extension measurements more accurate. (2)
---
Any two: read the rule at eye level (avoid parallax); use a pointer on the spring; wait until the spring is still; measure the unloaded length carefully / repeat and average.
:::

::: q
Why should readings also be taken while unloading the spring? (1)
---
To check the spring returns to its original length — i.e. the elastic limit was not exceeded.
:::

::: watch
- **Extension, not length.** Subtracting the original length is the first mark in nearly every question.
- **Metres** in F = kx. A 4.5 cm extension is 0.045 m; leaving it in cm gives an answer 100× wrong.
- Hooke's law (proportional, straight line) and elastic (returns to shape) are **different properties**; a rubber band is elastic but not Hookean.
- The **limit of proportionality** is where the graph stops being straight; the **elastic limit** is where permanent stretch begins. Name the right one.
- A hanging mass gives a **force of mg** — 100 g is 1 N, not 100 N.
:::
