## What it is

**The particle model.**

- **Solids**: particles packed closely in a regular pattern, held by strong forces; they **vibrate** about fixed positions. Fixed shape and volume, hard to compress.
- **Liquids**: particles still close together but in no fixed pattern; they can **move past each other**. Fixed volume, takes the shape of the container, flows.
- **Gases**: particles far apart, moving **fast and randomly** in all directions with almost no forces between them. Fills any container, easily compressed, low density.

**Heating** transfers energy to a substance's thermal store. Either the particles move faster and the **temperature rises**, or — at the melting or boiling point — the energy goes into **breaking the bonds/forces** between particles and the substance **changes state** while the temperature stays **constant**.

- **Melting** (solid → liquid): energy lets particles break free of their fixed positions. **Freezing** is the reverse.
- **Boiling/evaporating** (liquid → gas): energy overcomes the remaining forces so particles escape and move freely. **Condensing** is the reverse. Evaporation happens at any temperature from the surface; boiling happens throughout at the boiling point.

A **temperature-time graph** for heating ice shows a rising slope (ice warming), a **flat section at 0 °C** (melting), a rising slope (water warming), a **flat section at 100 °C** (boiling), then a rising slope (steam). The plateaus are the changes of state: the temperature does not rise because the energy is being used to separate particles, not to speed them up.

**Practical: the temperature-time graph.** Heat crushed ice (or a solid such as stearic acid) gently with a thermometer in it, stirring, and record the temperature every 30 s; plot temperature against time and identify the plateau as the melting point. Cooling a liquid to a solid gives the mirror image, with a flat section as it freezes.

**Specific heat capacity, c,** is the energy needed to raise the temperature of **1 kg** of a substance by **1 °C** (unit **J/kg °C**). Water's is high — 4200 J/kg °C — which is why it takes a lot of energy to heat and makes a good coolant and heat store.

$$Q = m \times c \times \Delta T \qquad \text{energy (J) = mass (kg) × specific heat capacity (J/kg °C) × temperature change (°C)}$$

**Practical: specific heat capacity.** Put a known mass of water (or a metal block with holes for a heater and thermometer) in an insulated container; use an electric immersion heater connected to a **joulemeter** (or an ammeter, voltmeter and stopwatch, E = IVt); record the start and end temperatures. Then $c = Q / (m\Delta T)$. Insulate (lagging, lid) and stir to make the temperature uniform; the measured *c* comes out too high because some energy heats the container and escapes to the surroundings.

::: terms
- **Specific heat capacity (c)**: the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C, in J/kg °C.
- **Heating equation**: ΔQ = m × c × ΔT.
- **Melting point**: the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid.
- **Boiling point**: the temperature at which a liquid boils and turns into a gas throughout.
- **Change of state**: while a substance melts or boils, its temperature stays constant because the energy supplied breaks bonds between particles rather than increasing their kinetic energy.
- **Heating curve**: a temperature-time graph with flat sections at the melting and boiling points.
:::

::: method
1. Describe states with **arrangement, spacing and motion** of particles — three points for three marks.
2. On a heating graph: **sloping = one state warming, flat = changing state**; label the plateaus with the melting and boiling points.
3. Explain plateaus with: energy is used to **break the forces between particles**, not to increase their kinetic energy, so the temperature stays constant.
4. **Q = mcΔT**: mass in kg, ΔT is the *change* (final − initial); rearrange for *c* or ΔT as needed.
5. For the practical, know the measurements (mass, energy from the joulemeter/IVt, ΔT), the equation, and why the result is too high (heat loss).
:::

::: example Particle explanation of melting
Explain in terms of particles what happens when a solid is heated until it melts. (3)

Heating gives the particles more energy so they vibrate faster and the temperature rises (1). At the melting point the energy supplied breaks the forces holding the particles in fixed positions (1), so they can move past one another as a liquid, while the temperature stays constant until all the solid has melted (1).
:::

::: example Heating water
Calculate the energy needed to heat 1.5 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C (c = 4200 J/kg °C). (3)

ΔT = 80 °C (1). Q = 1.5 × 4200 × 80 (1) = **504 000 J** (1).
:::

::: example Finding c from experiment
An immersion heater transfers 30 000 J to a 1 kg aluminium block, raising its temperature from 18 °C to 51 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium and explain why the true value is lower. (4)

ΔT = 33 °C. c = Q ÷ (mΔT) = 30 000 ÷ (1 × 33) (1) = **909 J/kg °C** (1). Some of the 30 000 J heated the heater, thermometer and surroundings rather than the block (1), so the block received less energy than assumed and the calculated *c* is too high; the accepted value is about 900 J/kg °C (1).
:::

::: example Reading the cooling curve
A liquid cooling in a test tube has a temperature-time graph with a flat section at 55 °C. What is happening, and why does the temperature stay constant? (3)

The substance is freezing (changing from liquid to solid) at 55 °C, its freezing point (1). As particles settle into fixed positions and bonds form, energy is released (1), which balances the energy lost to the surroundings so the temperature does not fall until freezing is complete (1).
:::

Far apart, random arrangement (1); moving fast and randomly in all directions (1).

Why can a liquid flow but a solid cannot? (1)
---
In a liquid the particles can move past each other; in a solid they are held in fixed positions.

What happens to the temperature of a pure substance while it boils? Why? (2)
---
It stays constant (1) because the energy supplied is used to separate the particles rather than increase their kinetic energy (1).

Define specific heat capacity and give its unit. (2)
---
The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (1); J/kg °C (1).
:::

::: q
Write the equation linking energy, mass, specific heat capacity and temperature change. (1)
---
Q = m × c × ΔT.
:::

::: q
How much energy is needed to raise 0.5 kg of copper (c = 390 J/kg °C) by 40 °C? (2)
---
0.5 × 390 × 40 (1) = 7800 J (1).
:::

::: q
12 600 J of energy heats 0.3 kg of water. Calculate the temperature rise. (2)
---
ΔT = 12 600 ÷ (0.3 × 4200) (1) = 10 °C (1).
:::

::: q
Why is water used in central-heating systems? (1)
---
Its high specific heat capacity means it can carry a large amount of energy per kilogram (and per degree).
:::

::: q
In the specific heat capacity experiment, give two ways to reduce heat loss. (2)
---
Insulate/lag the container (1); use a lid / minimise the time / start below room temperature and finish equally above it (1).
:::

::: q
Sketch the shape of a temperature-time graph for ice heated until it becomes steam, and label the flat sections. (3)
---
Rising line, flat at 0 °C (melting) (1), rising line, flat at 100 °C (boiling) (1), rising line for steam (1).
:::

::: watch
- On a heating graph, **flat = change of state at constant temperature**; explain it with energy breaking bonds, not "the heat is used up".
- In Q = mcΔT the mass is in **kg** and ΔT is the **change** in temperature; the starting temperature alone is a trap.
- The experimental *c* is **too high** because of heat loss (less energy reached the substance than the joulemeter recorded).
- Describe particles with **all three**: arrangement, separation, motion.
- Evaporation happens **below the boiling point**, from the surface only; boiling happens **throughout** the liquid at one temperature.
:::

::: test
Calculate the energy needed to heat 2 kg of water from 20 °C to 80 °C. ($c = 4200$ J/kg °C) (2)
---
$Q = mc\Delta T = 2 \times 4200 \times 60$ (1) $= 504\,000$ J (1).
:::

::: test
A 1.5 kg block of iron absorbs 27 000 J and its temperature rises by 40 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of iron. (2)
---
$c = Q \div (m\Delta T) = 27\,000 \div 60$ (1) $= 450$ J/kg °C (1).
:::

::: test
A 2 kW heater warms 0.5 kg of oil for 3 minutes and the temperature rises by 360 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the oil, assuming no losses. (3)
---
$Q = 2000 \times 180 = 360\,000$ J (1); $c = 360\,000 \div (0.5 \times 360)$ (1) $= 2000$ J/kg °C (1).
:::

::: test
Explain, in terms of particles, why the temperature of boiling water stays at 100 °C even though it is still being heated. (2)
---
The energy supplied is used to break the bonds between particles as they turn into gas (1), not to make them move faster, so the average kinetic energy — the temperature — does not change (1).
:::

::: test
Describe the differences in the arrangement and movement of particles in a solid and a liquid. (2)
---
Solid: particles in fixed positions in a regular pattern, vibrating about those positions (1). Liquid: particles close together but able to move past each other in random motion (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the sea warms up and cools down more slowly than the land. (2)
---
Water has a much higher specific heat capacity than rock or soil (1), so it needs far more energy per kilogram for the same temperature change (1).
:::

Insulate the block or container (1); use a lid, and start the timer only once the heater is warm / keep the thermometer in contact with the material (1).

::: test
Describe how to measure the specific heat capacity of a metal block using an electric heater, and explain why the value obtained is usually higher than the true value. (6)
---
Any six: measure the mass of the block with a balance; place the heater and a thermometer in holes in the block; record the starting temperature; connect a joulemeter (or ammeter, voltmeter and stopwatch) to measure energy supplied; heat for a set time, e.g. 10 minutes; record the final temperature; c = E ÷ (m × ΔT); insulate the block; the value is too high because some energy is lost to the surroundings, so not all the energy supplied heats the block; add oil to the thermometer hole for good thermal contact.
Marking: 1 mark for each correct point, up to 6; for full marks the answer must be in a logical order.
:::
