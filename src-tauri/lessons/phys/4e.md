## What it is

Most electricity is generated by spinning a **turbine**, which turns a **generator** (a coil rotating in a magnetic field — see topic 6c). What differs between resources is how the turbine is made to spin, and the energy transfers that lead up to it.

**Non-renewable resources** (will run out):

- **Fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas): chemical store → burned → thermal store of water → steam → kinetic store of turbine → generator → electrical transfer. Reliable, cheap, can be switched on when needed; but release **CO₂** (climate change) and **SO₂** (acid rain), and are finite. Gas stations start up quickest.
- **Nuclear**: nuclear store of uranium → fission heats water → steam → turbine → generator. No CO₂ during operation, huge energy from little fuel, reliable; but produces **radioactive waste** that must be stored for thousands of years, risk of accidents, very expensive to build and decommission.

**Renewable resources** (replaced naturally):

- **Wind**: kinetic store of moving air → turbine blades → generator. No fuel, no emissions; but **unreliable** (no wind, no power), turbines are visually intrusive and noisy, and large numbers are needed.
- **Hydroelectric**: gravitational potential store of water in a high reservoir → kinetic → turbine → generator. Reliable, fast start-up, can store energy (pumped storage); but dams flood valleys, destroying habitats and displacing people, and need suitable hilly terrain.
- **Tidal**: gravitational/kinetic store of tides → turbines in a barrage. Predictable (twice daily), no emissions; but few suitable sites, barrages damage estuary habitats, expensive.
- **Wave**: kinetic store of waves → moves floats/air columns → generator. No emissions; but unreliable, hard to build robustly, small output.
- **Geothermal**: thermal store of hot rocks → water pumped down returns as steam → turbine. Reliable, no emissions; only in volcanic regions (Iceland, New Zealand).
- **Solar**: light → **photovoltaic cells** convert directly to electrical (no turbine); solar heating panels warm water. No emissions or fuel, good for remote places; but no output at night, less in cloud, cells are expensive per watt and need large areas.
- **Biomass/biofuels**: chemical store of plant material or waste → burned like fossil fuel. "Carbon-neutral" if replanted; but land use competes with food, and burning still releases gases.

**Comparing them.** The exam asks you to weigh **reliability** (can it supply on demand?), **environmental impact** (emissions, habitat, visual), **cost** (building and running), **availability** (does the site allow it?) and **scale** (how much power). A modern grid mixes them: renewables for low-carbon supply, gas and nuclear to keep the lights on when the wind drops.

::: method
1. For "describe the energy transfers", write the chain from the **original store** to **electrical**, via thermal (if burned/heated) → kinetic (turbine) → generator. Solar PV skips the turbine.
2. For "advantages and disadvantages", give at least one of each and make them **specific** to the resource: not "good for the environment" but "produces no carbon dioxide while operating".
3. Distinguish **renewable/non-renewable** (does it run out?) from **polluting/non-polluting** (nuclear is non-renewable but low-carbon).
4. Reliability is the key trade-off for wind, solar and wave; **predictability** is tidal's strength.
5. Use comparatives when asked to compare: "start-up time is shorter", "output is lower".
:::

::: example Energy transfers in a coal-fired station
Describe the energy transfers in a coal-fired power station. (4)

The chemical store of the coal is transferred by burning to the thermal store of the water in the boiler (1), producing steam; the steam's energy is transferred to the kinetic store of the turbine (1), which turns the generator (1); the generator transfers energy electrically to the grid, with a large fraction dissipated to the surroundings as heat (1).
:::

::: example Wind versus gas
A council is choosing between a gas power station and a wind farm. Give two advantages of each. (4)

Gas: reliable — can generate on demand whatever the weather (1); starts up quickly to meet peaks and produces a lot of power on a small site (1). Wind: no fuel costs and no carbon dioxide emitted during operation (1); the resource is renewable and will never run out (1).
:::

::: example Nuclear power
Explain why nuclear power is described as non-renewable but low-carbon, and give one major disadvantage. (3)

The uranium fuel is finite and will eventually run out, so it is non-renewable (1); but fission releases no carbon dioxide during generation, so it is low-carbon (1). Disadvantage: radioactive waste that stays dangerous for thousands of years and must be safely stored / the risk of accidents / very high construction and decommissioning costs (1).
:::

::: example Hydroelectric scheme
Describe the energy transfers in a hydroelectric station and give one environmental disadvantage. (3)

Water stored high in a reservoir has energy in its gravitational potential store; as it flows down through pipes this becomes kinetic (1), which turns turbines connected to generators that transfer energy electrically (1). Building the dam floods a valley, destroying habitats and possibly displacing communities (1).
:::

::: q
What is a renewable energy resource? (1)
---
One that is replenished naturally and will not run out (as it is used).
:::

::: q
Name three fossil fuels and one environmental problem of burning them. (2)
---
Coal, oil, natural gas (1); carbon dioxide causing climate change / sulfur dioxide causing acid rain (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the energy transfer in a solar photovoltaic cell. (1)
---
Light (radiation) is transferred directly to electrical energy, with no turbine.
:::

::: q
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of solar cells. (2)
---
No fuel or emissions, works in remote places (1); no output at night, reduced in cloud, expensive for the power produced (1).
:::

::: q
Why is tidal power more reliable than wind power? (1)
---
Tides are predictable — they occur twice a day regardless of weather.
:::

::: q
Give one disadvantage of a tidal barrage. (1)
---
Damages estuary habitats / few suitable sites / very expensive to build.
:::

::: q
Where can geothermal power be used, and why? (2)
---
Volcanic regions such as Iceland (1), where hot rocks are close to the surface to heat water into steam (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the turbine in a nuclear power station is the same as in a coal station. (2)
---
Both use the heat source to boil water into steam (1), and the steam drives the turbine in the same way — only the source of heat differs (1).
:::

::: q
Give two disadvantages of wind farms. (2)
---
Any two: unreliable output when there is no wind; visual/noise impact; many turbines needed for a large output; may harm birds.
:::

::: q
Why do countries keep gas power stations even as they build renewables? (2)
---
Renewables such as wind and solar are intermittent (1); gas stations can start quickly to supply demand when renewable output is low (1).
:::

::: watch
- Energy-transfer chains: **chemical/nuclear → thermal → kinetic (turbine) → electrical (generator)**. Solar PV is the exception with no turbine.
- Be **specific**: "no carbon dioxide during operation", "radioactive waste stored for thousands of years", not "bad for the environment".
- **Non-renewable ≠ polluting**: nuclear is non-renewable and low-carbon; biomass is renewable but still releases gases.
- Reliability is the standard disadvantage of wind, solar and wave; **predictable** is tidal's word.
- Compare with comparatives and address **both** sides when the question says "advantages and disadvantages".
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between renewable and non-renewable energy resources, giving one example of each. (2)
---
Renewable resources are replaced as fast as they are used, e.g. wind, solar, hydro (1); non-renewable ones will run out, e.g. coal, oil, gas, uranium (1).
:::

::: test
Give two advantages and two disadvantages of nuclear power. (4)
---
Advantages: no carbon dioxide emitted; very large energy output from little fuel; reliable (2). Disadvantages: radioactive waste stored for thousands of years; risk of accident; expensive to build and decommission (2).
:::

::: test
Explain how a hydroelectric power station generates electricity, and give one environmental disadvantage. (3)
---
Water stored behind a dam falls through pipes, transferring GPE to KE (1); the moving water turns a turbine connected to a generator (1). Disadvantage: flooding valleys destroys habitats and displaces people (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why solar cells cannot provide a country's entire electricity supply on their own. (2)
---
Output is zero at night and low in cloudy weather (1); electricity is hard to store in large amounts, so a backup supply is needed (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how wave power works and why it is difficult to use on a large scale. (2)
---
Moving water in waves drives floats or turbines connected to generators (1); the equipment must survive storms, output varies with the weather, and many devices are needed for a useful amount of power (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how burning fossil fuels causes global warming and acid rain. (2)
---
Burning releases carbon dioxide, which traps infrared in the atmosphere (1); sulfur dioxide from impurities dissolves in rainwater to form acid rain (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what is meant by the start-up time of a power station and why gas stations are useful for meeting sudden demand. (2)
---
The time from switching on to delivering full power (1); gas stations start up in minutes, unlike coal or nuclear, so they can cover peaks (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why hydroelectric and wind power stations produce no waste products in use but still have environmental impact. (2)
---
No fuel is burned, so nothing is emitted while running (1); but dams flood land and turbines spoil views, are noisy and can harm birds (1).
:::
