## What it is

**Friction** is the force that **opposes motion** between surfaces in contact (and between an object and the air or water it moves through). It always acts in the direction opposite to the movement, or the attempted movement. Friction is useful — grip for tyres and shoes, brakes — and a nuisance: it wastes energy as heat and wears moving parts, which is why engines are lubricated and shapes are streamlined.

**Newton's first law**: an object stays at rest, or keeps moving at **constant velocity**, unless a **resultant force** acts on it. A car at steady speed on a motorway has its driving force exactly balanced by air resistance and friction; take away the driving force and the unbalanced resistive forces slow it down.

**Newton's second law**: a resultant force makes an object accelerate in the direction of the force, and

$$F = m \times a \qquad \text{force (N) = mass (kg) × acceleration (m/s²)}$$

Double the resultant force and the acceleration doubles; double the mass and the acceleration halves. One newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s².

**Mass and weight** are different things:

- **Mass** (kg) is the amount of matter in an object; it is the same everywhere.
- **Weight** (N) is the **force of gravity** on that mass; it depends on where you are.

$$W = m \times g \qquad \text{weight (N) = mass (kg) × gravitational field strength (N/kg)}$$

On Earth **g ≈ 10 N/kg**, so a 60 kg person weighs 600 N. On the Moon g ≈ 1.6 N/kg: same mass, weight only 96 N. Weight acts through the object's centre of gravity, straight down. *g* is also the acceleration of free fall, 10 m/s² — the two uses of *g* are the same quantity in different units.

::: method
1. Draw the forces; find the **resultant** along the line of motion (driving force minus resistive forces).
2. Apply **F = ma** to the *resultant*, never to a single force when others are present. Rearrange: $a = F/m$, $m = F/a$.
3. Weight questions: **W = mg** with g = 10 N/kg on Earth unless told otherwise. Mass in kg — convert grams (÷1000).
4. Constant velocity → resultant zero → driving force equals total resistive force.
5. Check the unit: N for force and weight, kg for mass, m/s² for acceleration.
:::

::: example Acceleration from a resultant force
A 1200 kg car has a driving force of 3000 N and total resistive forces of 900 N. Calculate its acceleration. (3)

Resultant = 3000 − 900 = 2100 N (1). $a = F/m = 2100 \div 1200$ (1) = **1.75 m/s²** (1).
:::

::: example Force needed to decelerate
A 70 kg cyclist plus bike (total mass 85 kg) must slow from 8 m/s to rest in 4 s. Find the braking force required. (3)

Deceleration $= (0 - 8) \div 4 = -2$ m/s² (1). $F = ma = 85 \times 2$ (1) = **170 N** (1) (opposing the motion).
:::

::: example Mass and weight
An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg. Calculate her weight on Earth (g = 10 N/kg) and on Mars (g = 3.7 N/kg), and state her mass on Mars. (3)

Earth: $W = 80 \times 10 =$ **800 N** (1). Mars: $W = 80 \times 3.7 =$ **296 N** (1). Mass on Mars is still **80 kg** — mass does not change with location (1).
:::

::: example Friction and steady speed
A sledge is pulled at constant speed with a force of 40 N. State the size of the friction force and what happens if the pull rises to 55 N. (3)

Friction = 40 N (1) because constant speed means balanced forces. With a 55 N pull the resultant is 15 N forward (1), so the sledge accelerates (1).
:::

::: q
In which direction does friction act on a sliding object? (1)
---
Opposite to the direction of motion.
:::

::: q
State Newton's first law of motion. (2)
---
An object stays at rest or continues at constant velocity (1) unless acted on by a resultant force (1).
:::

::: q
Write the equation linking force, mass and acceleration, with units. (2)
---
F = m × a (1); N, kg, m/s² (1).
:::

::: q
A resultant force of 24 N acts on a 6 kg object. Find its acceleration. (2)
---
a = 24 ÷ 6 (1) = 4 m/s² (1).
:::

::: q
What resultant force is needed to give a 900 kg car an acceleration of 2.5 m/s²? (2)
---
F = 900 × 2.5 (1) = 2250 N (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between mass and weight. (2)
---
Mass is the amount of matter, in kg, and does not change with location (1); weight is the force of gravity on the mass, in N, and depends on g (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the weight of a 2.5 kg bag on Earth. (2)
---
W = 2.5 × 10 (1) = 25 N (1).
:::

::: q
An object weighs 48 N on the Moon where g = 1.6 N/kg. What is its mass? (2)
---
m = 48 ÷ 1.6 (1) = 30 kg (1).
:::

::: q
A car's engine force equals the resistive forces. Describe the car's motion. (1)
---
It moves at constant velocity (zero resultant force).
:::

::: q
Give one way of reducing friction in a machine and one situation where friction is essential. (2)
---
Lubricate with oil / use ball bearings / streamline (1); brakes or tyre grip on the road (1).
:::

::: watch
- **F = ma uses the resultant force.** If two forces are given, subtract first; putting the driving force straight in is the most common error.
- **Deceleration is still F = ma**: the force is in the opposite direction to motion.
- Weight is in **newtons**. "Weight = 60 kg" scores nothing. Use **g = 10 N/kg** unless the paper gives another value.
- Mass is **constant** everywhere; only weight changes on the Moon.
- Balanced forces → constant velocity, *not* "stops".
:::
