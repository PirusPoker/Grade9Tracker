## What it is

**Sound is a longitudinal wave**: a vibrating source (a loudspeaker cone, a guitar string, vocal cords) pushes and pulls on the air, sending out a series of **compressions** and **rarefactions** along the direction of travel. Because it needs particles to pass the vibration on, sound **cannot travel through a vacuum**. It travels at about **340 m/s in air**, faster in liquids (≈1500 m/s in water) and faster still in solids. Like all waves it **reflects** (an **echo** is reflected sound) and **refracts** (sound bends as it passes between warm and cold air).

**Hearing.** The human ear can detect frequencies from about **20 Hz to 20 000 Hz (20 kHz)**; the upper limit falls with age. Sound above 20 kHz is **ultrasound**, below 20 Hz **infrasound**.

**Pitch and loudness.**

- **Pitch** depends on **frequency**: a higher frequency is heard as a higher note.
- **Loudness** depends on **amplitude**: a bigger amplitude means more energy and a louder sound.

**Seeing sound on an oscilloscope.** A **microphone** turns the pressure variations of the sound into a varying voltage, and the **oscilloscope** displays that voltage against time as a wave trace — remember the trace is a graph of a longitudinal wave, not a picture of it. A higher pitch shows as **more waves across the screen** (shorter horizontal spacing); a louder sound shows as a **taller trace**.

**Practical: frequency from an oscilloscope trace.** The oscilloscope's **time base** sets how long each horizontal division represents (e.g. 2 ms/div). Count the divisions for one complete wave (crest to crest), multiply by the time base to get the **period T**, then $f = 1/T$. For accuracy, measure across several waves and divide.

**Practical: the speed of sound in air.** Two methods:

1. **Echo method** — stand a measured distance *d* (say 50 m) from a large wall, clap and start a stopwatch, and time until the echo returns; the sound has travelled 2*d*, so speed = 2d ÷ t. Better: clap in rhythm with the echoes and time, say, 20 claps. Timing error is the main weakness.
2. **Two microphones** — place two microphones a measured distance apart (e.g. 1.0 m), connected to an oscilloscope or data logger. Make a sharp sound in line with them; the logger records the time difference between the sound reaching each microphone. Speed = distance ÷ time difference. This removes reaction time and is the more precise method.

Repeat and average in either case.

::: terms
- **Sound wave**: a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions that needs a medium to travel through.
- **Echo**: a reflected sound wave.
- **Range of human hearing**: about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz).
- **Pitch**: how high or low a sound is; a higher frequency gives a higher pitch.
- **Loudness**: how loud a sound is; a larger amplitude gives a louder sound.
- **Oscilloscope**: a device that displays a sound wave (from a microphone) as a trace, showing its amplitude and period.
:::

::: method
1. **Describe sound correctly**: longitudinal, compressions and rarefactions, needs a medium, ≈340 m/s in air.
2. **Pitch ↔ frequency, loudness ↔ amplitude** — never mix them. Translate to the trace: more waves across = higher pitch; taller = louder.
3. **Frequency from a trace**: divisions per wave × time per division = T (convert ms to s), then f = 1/T.
4. **Echo calculations**: the sound goes there **and back** — use 2d.
5. Speed of sound experiments: name the measurements (distance, time), the equation, one source of error and one improvement.
:::

::: example Echo
A student claps 85 m from a cliff and hears the echo 0.5 s later. Calculate the speed of sound. (3)

Distance travelled = 2 × 85 = 170 m (1). Speed = 170 ÷ 0.5 (1) = **340 m/s** (1).
:::

::: example Frequency from an oscilloscope
An oscilloscope is set to 5 ms per division. One complete wave of a sound occupies 4 divisions. Find the frequency. (3)

T = 4 × 5 ms = 20 ms = 0.020 s (1). $f = 1/T = 1 \div 0.020$ (1) = **50 Hz** (1).
:::

::: example Interpreting traces
Two sounds are displayed on an oscilloscope with the same settings. Sound B has a trace twice as tall as A with the same number of waves across the screen. Compare the sounds. (2)

B is louder than A because its amplitude is greater (1); they have the same pitch because the frequency (number of waves in the same time) is the same (1).
:::

::: example Two-microphone method
Describe how to measure the speed of sound using two microphones and a data logger, and explain why this is better than using a stopwatch. (4)

Place the microphones a measured distance apart, e.g. 1.0 m, both connected to the data logger (1); make a sharp sound in line with them so the logger records the time between the sound arriving at each (1); speed = distance ÷ time difference, repeating and averaging (1). The logger measures milliseconds automatically, removing human reaction time that would swamp such a short interval (1).
:::

::: q
Why can sound not travel through a vacuum? (1)
---
It needs particles to vibrate and pass the vibration on; a vacuum has none.
:::

::: q
Describe how a sound wave travels through air. (2)
---
The source vibrates, pushing air particles together into compressions and apart into rarefactions (1); these travel outward along the direction of motion as a longitudinal wave (1).
:::

::: q
State the range of human hearing. (1)
---
About 20 Hz to 20 kHz.
:::

::: q
What property of a sound wave determines (a) its pitch, (b) its loudness? (2)
---
(a) Frequency (1); (b) amplitude (1).
:::

::: q
What does an oscilloscope connected to a microphone display? (1)
---
A graph of the microphone's voltage (the sound's pressure variation) against time.
:::

::: q
On a trace, one wave spans 2.5 divisions at 4 ms/div. Calculate the frequency. (3)
---
T = 2.5 × 4 = 10 ms = 0.01 s (2); f = 100 Hz (1).
:::

::: q
A ship's sonar pulse returns from the seabed after 0.8 s. Sound travels at 1500 m/s in water. How deep is the water? (2)
---
Total distance = 1500 × 0.8 = 1200 m (1); depth = 600 m (1).
:::

::: q
Give one source of error in the clap-and-echo method and how to reduce it. (2)
---
Human reaction time when starting/stopping the stopwatch (1); time many echoes in rhythm and divide, or use a data logger (1).
:::

::: q
What is an echo? (1)
---
A reflected sound wave.
:::

::: q
A note of 256 Hz is played, then one of 512 Hz at the same loudness. Describe the change in the oscilloscope trace. (2)
---
Twice as many waves across the screen (waves closer together) (1); the same height (1).
:::

::: watch
- Echo and sonar questions: the sound travels **there and back** — divide by 2 or use 2d.
- The oscilloscope shows **voltage against time**, so the trace is a graph; a sound wave itself is longitudinal.
- Pitch is **frequency**; loudness is **amplitude**. Writing "louder = higher frequency" is the standard lost mark.
- Time base: convert **ms to s** before f = 1/T.
- Speed of sound methods must name **distance and time measurements** and the **error** (reaction time) to earn full marks.
:::

::: test
Explain why an ultrasound scan can be used to image a foetus but X-rays are not. (2)
---
Ultrasound reflects at boundaries between tissues and is not ionising (1); X-rays are ionising and could damage the developing cells (1).

::: test

Sound is longitudinal — vibrations are along the direction of travel, so there is no plane to restrict (1); light is transverse and its vibrations can be restricted to one plane (1).

::: test
Describe how to measure the speed of sound in air using an echo from a wall. (6)
---
Any six: stand a measured distance, e.g. 50 m, from a large wall, using a trundle wheel or tape measure; clap and listen for the echo; clap in rhythm with the echoes; time 20 claps with a stopwatch; time for one clap interval = total ÷ 20; the sound travels to the wall and back, so distance = 2 × 50 m; speed = distance ÷ time; repeat and take a mean; timing many intervals reduces the effect of reaction time.
