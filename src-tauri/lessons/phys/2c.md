## What it is

A **current-voltage (I-V) graph** shows how the current through a component changes as the voltage across it changes. Its shape tells you how the component's resistance behaves. To obtain one, connect the component in series with an ammeter and a **variable resistor** (or a variable power supply), put a voltmeter across it, and record *I* for a range of *V*; reverse the supply to get the negative values.

- **Metal wire or fixed resistor at constant temperature**: a **straight line through the origin**. Current is proportional to voltage — it obeys Ohm's law and its resistance is constant (an *ohmic* conductor). The gradient is 1/R, so a steeper line means a lower resistance.
- **Filament lamp**: a curve that **flattens** (gets less steep) as the voltage rises, in both directions. As the current heats the filament, its **resistance increases**, so each extra volt gives less extra current. Not ohmic.
- **Diode**: current flows in **one direction only**. In the forward direction almost nothing flows until about 0.6–0.7 V, then the current rises steeply (very low resistance); in the reverse direction the current is essentially zero (very high resistance).

**LDRs and thermistors** are resistors whose resistance depends on the surroundings:

- A **light-dependent resistor (LDR)**: resistance **falls as light intensity rises** (very high in the dark, low in bright light). Used in automatic street lights, camera exposure meters, security lighting.
- A **thermistor**: resistance **falls as temperature rises**. Used in thermostats, fire alarms, car engine temperature sensors, digital thermometers.

Put either in series with a fixed resistor and the voltage across each shifts as conditions change — that is how a sensing circuit switches something on.

**Lamps and LEDs as indicators.** A filament lamp glowing shows that a current is flowing, and its brightness gives a rough idea of how much. An **LED** (light-emitting diode) lights when current flows through it in the forward direction and needs very little current, so it is the usual indicator on appliances and circuit boards; because it is a diode it must be connected the right way round, and it usually needs a series resistor to limit the current.

::: terms
- **I-V graph**: a graph of current against voltage for a component.
- **Ohmic conductor**: a component (such as a fixed resistor or a wire at constant temperature) whose current is directly proportional to voltage.
- **Filament lamp**: its resistance increases as it gets hotter, so its I-V graph curves.
- **Diode**: a component that lets current flow in one direction only — very high resistance in the reverse direction.
- **LED**: a light-emitting diode, which gives out light when current flows through it in the forward direction.
- **LDR (light-dependent resistor)**: a resistor whose resistance decreases as light intensity increases.
- **Thermistor**: a resistor whose resistance decreases as temperature increases.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the component from the graph shape**: straight line → resistor/wire; flattening curve → filament lamp; one-way steep rise → diode.
2. Resistance at any point = **V ÷ I** using the coordinates of that point (not the gradient of a curve).
3. For the lamp, explain *why*: more current → filament hotter → ions vibrate more → more collisions → resistance rises.
4. LDR: light ↑ → R ↓. Thermistor: temperature ↑ → R ↓. Then use $I = V/R$ or voltage-sharing to say what the circuit does.
5. Sketching: label axes I (vertical) and V (horizontal), draw through the origin, and show both quadrants for resistor and lamp.
:::

::: example Resistance from an I-V graph
On a filament lamp's I-V graph, the current is 0.5 A at 2 V and 0.8 A at 6 V. Calculate the resistance at each point and explain the change. (4)

At 2 V: R = 2 ÷ 0.5 = **4 Ω** (1). At 6 V: R = 6 ÷ 0.8 = **7.5 Ω** (1). The resistance increases with voltage (1) because the larger current heats the filament and the hotter metal resists the flow of electrons more (1).
:::

::: example Sketching a diode graph
Sketch the I-V graph for a diode and describe its resistance in each direction. (3)

Axes I against V; in the forward direction the current is ~zero until about 0.7 V then rises steeply (1); in the reverse direction the current is zero (1). Forward: very low resistance once conducting; reverse: very high resistance (1).
:::

::: example Thermistor circuit
A thermistor is connected in series with a fixed resistor and a 6 V battery. Explain what happens to the voltage across the fixed resistor as the room gets warmer. (3)

As temperature rises the thermistor's resistance falls (1), so the total resistance falls and the current increases (1); V = IR for the fixed resistor rises — it takes a larger share of the 6 V (1).
:::

::: example Automatic street light
Explain how an LDR can be used to switch a street light on at night. (3)

In daylight the LDR's resistance is low (1); as it gets dark the resistance rises sharply (1), changing the voltage across it (or the current in the circuit), which is used to trigger a switch that turns the lamp on (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the I-V graph of a fixed resistor at constant temperature. (2)
---
A straight line (1) through the origin (1).
:::

::: q
What does the gradient of a straight I-V graph tell you about resistance? (1)
---
Gradient = 1/R, so a steeper line means a lower resistance.
:::

::: q
Why does the I-V graph of a filament lamp curve? (2)
---
The filament heats up as the current increases (1), so its resistance increases (1).
:::

::: q
What is the main property of a diode? (1)
---
It allows current to flow in one direction only.
:::

::: q
A component has 0.2 A through it at 3 V. Calculate its resistance. (2)
---
3 ÷ 0.2 (1) = 15 Ω (1).
:::

::: q
How does the resistance of an LDR change with light intensity? (1)
---
It decreases as the light gets brighter.
:::

::: q
How does the resistance of a thermistor change with temperature? Give one use. (2)
---
It decreases as temperature rises (1); e.g. thermostat, fire alarm, engine temperature sensor (1).
:::

::: q
Give two advantages of an LED over a filament lamp as an indicator. (2)
---
Uses far less current/energy (1); lasts longer / does not get hot / switches instantly (1).
:::

::: q
Why must an LED be connected the right way round? (1)
---
It is a diode, so it only conducts (and lights) with current in the forward direction.
:::

::: q
Describe how you would obtain the I-V graph of a filament lamp. (3)
---
Connect the lamp in series with an ammeter and a variable resistor (or variable supply) and a voltmeter across the lamp (1); vary the voltage and record pairs of I and V readings (1); reverse the connections to obtain negative values, then plot I against V (1).
:::

::: watch
- For a **curve**, resistance is **V ÷ I at that point**; the gradient of a curve is not 1/R.
- The lamp's resistance **increases** with temperature because the filament is a metal — the opposite of a thermistor.
- Thermistor: **temperature up, resistance down**. LDR: **light up, resistance down**. Both fall; students often reverse one.
- Diode graph: essentially **zero** current in reverse, and a *threshold* before it conducts forward.
- Axes: I on the vertical, V on the horizontal, unless the paper says otherwise — and both negative quadrants for resistor and lamp.
:::

::: test
A component's I-V graph is a straight line through the origin. What does this tell you, and what is the component likely to be? (2)
---
Current is proportional to voltage — resistance is constant, it obeys Ohm's law (1); a fixed resistor (or metal wire at constant temperature) (1).
:::

::: test
Explain, in terms of particles, why the resistance of a filament lamp increases as the current increases. (3)
---
A larger current heats the filament (1); the metal ions vibrate more (1); the electrons collide with them more often, so resistance rises (1).
:::

::: test
Sketch or describe the I-V graph of a diode. (2)
---
Almost no current for negative (reverse) voltages (1); for forward voltages, current stays near zero until about 0.7 V and then rises steeply (1).
:::

::: test
A thermistor is used in a circuit to control a heater. Explain how its resistance changes as the room warms, and what this does to the current. (2)
---
As temperature rises the thermistor's resistance falls (1), so the current in the circuit increases (1).
:::

::: test
Give one practical use of an LDR and explain how it works in that use. (2)
---
Automatic street lights (1): in darkness the LDR's resistance rises, which the circuit uses to switch the lamp on (1).
:::

::: test
A filament lamp has 2 V across it at 0.5 A, and 10 V across it at 1.25 A. Calculate its resistance at each voltage and comment. (3)
---
$4\ \Omega$ (1) and $8\ \Omega$ (1); resistance increases with voltage because the filament gets hotter — it is not ohmic (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what a variable resistor does in a circuit and give one use. (2)
---
It changes the resistance and so controls the current (1); e.g. a dimmer switch or volume control (1).
:::

::: test
In an I-V experiment, why is it important to keep the wire from getting hot, and how can this be done? (2)
---
Heating changes the resistance, so the results would not be for a fixed resistance (1); use small currents and switch off between readings (1).
:::
