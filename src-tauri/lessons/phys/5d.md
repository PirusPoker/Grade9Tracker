## What it is

The behaviour of a **fixed mass of gas** depends on three quantities — **pressure, volume and temperature** — and the particle model explains how they are linked.

**Qualitatively.**

- **Temperature up at constant volume → pressure up.** Faster molecules hit the walls harder and more often. (A sealed can in a fire.)
- **Volume down at constant temperature → pressure up.** The same molecules are squeezed into less space, so they hit the walls more often. (A bicycle pump with the outlet blocked.)
- **Temperature up at constant pressure → volume up.** Faster molecules push the walls out until the collision rate falls back to balance the outside pressure. (A balloon expands in the sun.)

**The pressure law** (constant volume):

$$\frac{p_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2}{T_2} \qquad p \propto T \text{ (in kelvin)}$$

Pressure is directly proportional to the **absolute (kelvin) temperature**. A graph of *p* against *T* in K is a straight line through the origin — which is where absolute zero comes from.

**Boyle's law** (constant temperature):

$$p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2 \qquad p \propto \frac{1}{V}$$

Pressure is inversely proportional to volume: halve the volume and the pressure doubles. A graph of *p* against *V* is a curve (hyperbola); *p* against 1/*V* is a straight line through the origin.

Both laws only work with **temperatures in kelvin**. Pressure and volume can be in any units as long as the same units are used on both sides.

::: terms
- **Pressure and temperature (fixed volume)**: p₁ ÷ T₁ = p₂ ÷ T₂, with T in kelvin.
- **Pressure and volume (fixed temperature)**: p₁ × V₁ = p₂ × V₂ — Boyle's law.
- **Why heating raises pressure**: molecules move faster, hitting the walls more often and with more force.
- **Why compressing raises pressure**: in a smaller volume the molecules hit the walls more often.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify what is constant** (the question will say "at constant temperature" or "the volume does not change"). Constant V → pressure law; constant T → Boyle's law.
2. **Convert °C to K** (+273) before using p/T.
3. Write the law with the numbers substituted, then rearrange for the unknown. Keep the same units for p (kPa, Pa, atm) and V (cm³, m³) on both sides.
4. Sanity-check the direction: heating → higher p; compressing → higher p.
5. Explanations: always through **molecular collisions** — speed, frequency, and force on the walls.
:::

::: example Pressure law
A sealed container of gas at 27 °C and 100 kPa is heated to 127 °C. Find the new pressure. (3)

T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 400 K (1). $p_2 = p_1 T_2 / T_1 = 100 \times 400 \div 300$ (1) = **133 kPa** (1).
:::

::: example Boyle's law
A gas syringe holds 60 cm³ of air at 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in until the volume is 24 cm³ at the same temperature. Find the pressure. (2)

$p_2 = p_1 V_1 / V_2 = 100 \times 60 \div 24$ (1) = **250 kPa** (1).
:::

::: example Which law?
A diver's air bubble rises from where the pressure is 300 kPa to the surface at 100 kPa. Its volume at depth was 2 cm³. Assuming constant temperature, find its volume at the surface, and explain the change in molecular terms. (4)

Temperature constant → Boyle's law (1). $V_2 = p_1 V_1 / p_2 = 300 \times 2 \div 100 =$ **6 cm³** (1). As the outside pressure falls, the molecules' collisions push the bubble wall outward (1) until, in the larger volume, they hit the wall less often and the inside pressure matches the outside (1).
:::

::: example Tyre in the sun
A car tyre at 7 °C has a pressure of 220 kPa. After a journey the air is at 47 °C. Assuming constant volume, calculate the new pressure. (3)

280 K → 320 K (1). $p_2 = 220 \times 320 \div 280$ (1) = **251 kPa** (1).
:::

::: q
State what happens to the pressure of a fixed volume of gas when its temperature rises, and explain why. (3)
---
Pressure increases (1); molecules move faster (1) so they hit the walls more often and with more force (1).
:::

::: q
Write the equation relating pressure and temperature at constant volume. (1)
---
p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂ (T in kelvin).
:::

::: q
Write Boyle's law as an equation. (1)
---
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ (at constant temperature).
:::

::: q
A gas at 200 kPa occupies 0.5 m³. What is its volume at 50 kPa, same temperature? (2)
---
V₂ = 200 × 0.5 ÷ 50 (1) = 2 m³ (1).
:::

::: q
Gas at 150 kPa and 300 K is cooled to 200 K at constant volume. Find the new pressure. (2)
---
150 × 200 ÷ 300 (1) = 100 kPa (1).
:::

::: q
Why must kelvin temperatures be used in the pressure law? (1)
---
Pressure is proportional to absolute temperature; the Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, whereas 0 °C is not zero pressure.
:::

::: q
Explain in terms of molecules why compressing a gas at constant temperature raises its pressure. (2)
---
The same number of molecules occupy a smaller volume (1), so they collide with the walls more frequently (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the shape of a graph of pressure against volume for a gas at constant temperature. (1)
---
A downward curve (inverse proportion): as V increases, p decreases.
:::

::: q
A balloon of volume 2 dm³ at 20 °C is warmed to 40 °C at constant pressure. Does its volume double? Explain. (2)
---
No (1); 293 K to 313 K is only a 7% increase in absolute temperature, so the volume rises by about 7% (1).
:::

::: q
Air at 100 kPa is compressed from 500 cm³ to 100 cm³ at constant temperature. Calculate the final pressure. (2)
---
100 × 500 ÷ 100 (1) = 500 kPa (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Kelvin, always.** Using °C in p/T is the single biggest loss of marks in this topic.
- Choose the law by **what is held constant**; reading that phrase in the question is the first step.
- Boyle's law: pressure and volume are **inversely** proportional — compressing raises pressure.
- Keep p and V in the **same units on both sides**; no need to convert kPa or cm³ unless the answer must be in SI.
- Explanations must mention **collisions** — more frequent and/or harder — not "the gas wants to expand".
:::

::: test
A gas at 120 kPa has a volume of 3 m$^3$. It is compressed to 1.2 m$^3$ at constant temperature. Calculate the new pressure. (2)
---
$p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2$: $120 \times 3 = p_2 \times 1.2$ (1); $p_2 = 300$ kPa (1).
:::

::: test
A bubble of volume 2 cm$^3$ rises from a depth where the pressure is 250 kPa to the surface, where it is 100 kPa. Calculate its volume at the surface (constant temperature). (2)
---
$250 \times 2 = 100 \times V_2$ (1); $V_2 = 5$ cm$^3$ (1).
:::

::: test
A sealed container of gas is at 27 °C and 100 kPa. Calculate the pressure at 127 °C. (3)
---
Temperatures in kelvin: 300 K and 400 K (1); $\dfrac{p_2}{400} = \dfrac{100}{300}$ (1); $p_2 = 133$ kPa (1).
:::

::: test
The pressure in a car tyre is 220 kPa at 7 °C. After a journey it is 250 kPa. Calculate the temperature of the air in the tyre in °C, assuming constant volume. (3)
---
$280$ K start (1); $T_2 = 280 \times 250 \div 220 = 318$ K (1); $45$ °C (1).
:::

::: test
State Boyle's law in words. (2)
---
For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature (1), pressure is inversely proportional to volume — $pV$ is constant (1).
:::

::: test
Explain, in terms of particles, why the pressure of a gas increases when its volume is reduced at constant temperature. (2)
---
The particles have less distance to travel between collisions, so they hit the walls more often (1); more collisions per second on a smaller area means higher pressure (1).
:::

::: test
A student plots pressure against $1/V$ for a gas at constant temperature. Describe the graph and explain what it shows. (2)
---
A straight line through the origin (1); pressure is proportional to $1/V$, i.e. inversely proportional to volume — Boyle's law (1).
:::

::: test
A gas cylinder must not be left in the sun. Explain why, using the pressure law. (2)
---
Its volume is fixed, so as the temperature rises the pressure rises in proportion to the kelvin temperature (1); the pressure could exceed what the cylinder can withstand and it could burst (1).
:::

::: test
A syringe holds 50 cm³ of air at 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in until the volume is 20 cm³ at constant temperature. Calculate the new pressure. (3)

::: test
Explain why the pressure of a gas rises when its temperature increases at constant volume. (2)
---
Particles gain kinetic energy and move faster (1), so they hit the walls more often and with more force (1).
:::

::: test
An aerosol can contains gas at 300 kPa at 27 °C. It is thrown onto a fire and heated to 627 °C. Calculate the new pressure and explain, using particles, why this is dangerous. (6)
---
T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K and T₂ = 627 + 273 = 900 K (1). p₂ = p₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁ = 300 × 900 ÷ 300 (1) = 900 kPa (1). Particles gain kinetic energy and move faster (1), hitting the walls more often and harder, so the force on the can increases (1); the can may burst / explode (1).
:::
