## What it is

**Nuclear fusion** is the joining of **two light nuclei** to make a **heavier nucleus**, releasing energy. It is the opposite of fission, which splits a heavy nucleus. The simplest fusion reaction joins two isotopes of hydrogen:

$$^{2}_{1}\text{H} + ^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow ^{4}_{2}\text{He} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} + \text{energy}$$

(deuterium + tritium → helium + neutron). Check: 2 + 3 = 4 + 1; 1 + 1 = 2 + 0.

**Where the energy comes from.** The helium nucleus produced has slightly **less mass** than the two hydrogen nuclei that made it. The "missing" mass has been **converted into energy** (Einstein's *E = mc²* — a tiny mass loss gives an enormous energy release because *c²* is so large). The same is true of fission: the products have less mass than the original nucleus plus neutron.

**Fusion powers the stars.** The Sun and every other star release energy by fusing **hydrogen into helium** in their cores. This is the ultimate source of almost all the energy on Earth.

**Why fusion needs extreme conditions.** Nuclei are **positively charged**, so they **repel** each other strongly (electrostatic repulsion). To fuse, they must get close enough for the short-range nuclear force to take over, which means colliding at enormous speed. That requires:

- **Very high temperature** — millions of degrees (about 15 million K in the Sun's core; over 100 million K in a reactor) so the nuclei move fast enough to overcome the repulsion.
- **Very high pressure/density** — so collisions are frequent enough to sustain the reaction. In the Sun, gravity provides this by compressing the core.

On Earth these conditions are extremely hard to achieve and maintain: no container can hold matter at such temperatures, so experimental reactors (like ITER) confine the plasma with powerful **magnetic fields**. Fusion power is not yet a practical energy source, but its promise is huge — abundant fuel (hydrogen isotopes from water), no carbon dioxide, and far less long-lived radioactive waste than fission.

**Fission and fusion compared.**

| | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclei | Large nucleus splits (U-235, Pu-239) | Small nuclei join (hydrogen isotopes) |
| Trigger | Neutron absorption; chain reaction | Extreme temperature and pressure |
| Fuel | Uranium (mined, limited) | Hydrogen isotopes (abundant) |
| Waste | Long-lived radioactive daughter nuclei | Mainly short-lived; no daughter nuclei |
| Status | Used in power stations now | Experimental only |
| Energy per kg | Very large | Even larger |

::: method
1. Fusion = **two light nuclei join** to make a heavier one; fission = **one heavy nucleus splits**. State which is which in every comparison.
2. Energy source: **mass is lost** and converted to energy — say the products have less mass than the reactants.
3. Conditions: **high temperature** (to overcome electrostatic repulsion between positive nuclei) and **high pressure** (for enough collisions). Give the reason with each.
4. Stars: hydrogen → helium in the core; gravity supplies the pressure and temperature.
5. Balance fusion equations like any nuclear equation.
:::

::: example Defining fusion
Describe what happens in nuclear fusion and state where it occurs naturally. (3)

Two small (light) nuclei join together to form a larger nucleus (1), releasing a large amount of energy (1); it occurs in the cores of stars, where hydrogen fuses into helium (1).
:::

::: example Source of the energy
Explain where the energy released in fusion comes from. (2)

The mass of the nucleus formed is less than the total mass of the nuclei that fused (1); the lost mass is converted into energy (E = mc²) (1).
:::

::: example Conditions for fusion
Explain why fusion requires extremely high temperatures and pressures. (4)

Nuclei are positively charged and repel each other electrostatically (1); at very high temperatures they move fast enough to overcome this repulsion and get close enough for the nuclear force to bind them (1). High pressure means the nuclei are packed closely (1), so collisions are frequent enough for the reaction to release energy at a useful rate (1).
:::

::: example Comparing fission and fusion
Give two differences between fission and fusion, and one advantage of fusion as a future energy source. (3)

Fission splits a large nucleus; fusion joins two small nuclei (1). Fission is triggered by neutron absorption and can run as a chain reaction at ordinary temperatures; fusion needs millions of degrees (1). Advantage: the hydrogen fuel is almost unlimited / no carbon dioxide / little long-lived radioactive waste (1).
:::

::: q
What is nuclear fusion? (1)
---
Two light nuclei joining to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy.
:::

::: q
Which nuclei fuse in the Sun, and what do they form? (2)
---
Hydrogen nuclei (1) fuse to form helium (1).
:::

::: q
Complete: $^{2}_{1}\text{H} + ^{2}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow ^{3}_{2}\text{He} + \ ?$ (1)
---
$^{1}_{0}$n (a neutron): 2 + 2 = 3 + 1 and 1 + 1 = 2 + 0.
:::

::: q
Why do fusing nuclei need to be moving very fast? (2)
---
Both are positively charged and repel (1); only at high speed can they get close enough for the nuclear force to fuse them (1).
:::

::: q
What provides the pressure and temperature for fusion in a star? (1)
---
Gravity compressing the star's core.
:::

::: q
Why has fusion not yet been used to generate electricity commercially? (2)
---
The temperatures and pressures needed are extremely hard to produce and sustain (1); no material can contain the plasma, so complex magnetic confinement is required and reactors so far use more energy than they produce (1).
:::

::: q
State one similarity between fission and fusion. (1)
---
Both release energy because the products have less mass than the reactants (mass is converted to energy) / both are nuclear reactions releasing far more energy than chemical reactions.
:::

::: q
Give one reason fusion waste is less of a problem than fission waste. (1)
---
Fusion produces no long-lived radioactive daughter nuclei (the main product is stable helium).
:::

::: q
What happens to the total mass during a fusion reaction? (1)
---
It decreases slightly — the lost mass becomes the energy released.
:::

::: q
Compare the fuels used for fission and fusion. (2)
---
Fission uses uranium (or plutonium), which must be mined and is limited (1); fusion uses hydrogen isotopes, obtainable from water and effectively unlimited (1).
:::

::: watch
- Fusion **joins small nuclei**; fission **splits a large one**. Never describe fusion as "splitting hydrogen".
- The energy comes from **mass loss**: products lighter than reactants. Say it explicitly.
- Conditions need **reasons**: temperature → overcome repulsion of positive nuclei; pressure → enough collisions.
- The Sun fuses **hydrogen into helium** — not "burns hydrogen", which implies a chemical reaction.
- Fusion is **not yet** a practical power source; "fusion reactors provide electricity" is wrong.
:::
