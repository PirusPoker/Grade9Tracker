## What it is

A **charged particle moving through a magnetic field feels a force** — provided it is not moving along the field lines. A current is a stream of moving charges, so a **current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force** too: this is the **motor effect**. The wire's own magnetic field interacts with the external field; the two fields reinforce on one side of the wire and cancel on the other, pushing the wire toward the weaker side.

**Direction — Fleming's left-hand rule.** Hold the left hand with thumb, first finger and second finger at right angles:

- **First finger** → **Field** (N to S)
- **seCond finger** → **Current** (conventional, + to −)
- **thuMb** → **Motion** (force)

The force is at right angles to both the field and the current. Reverse either the current or the field and the force reverses. If the current is parallel to the field, there is no force.

**Size of the force.** It increases with a **larger current**, a **stronger magnetic field**, and a **longer length of wire** in the field. It is greatest when the wire is at right angles to the field.

**The d.c. motor.** A rectangular coil sits between the poles of a magnet. Current flows one way along one side of the coil and the opposite way along the other side, so by the left-hand rule the two sides feel **opposite forces** — one up, one down — producing a **turning effect** (a couple) that rotates the coil. After half a turn the forces would reverse and the coil would stop, so a **split-ring commutator** swaps the connections every half turn, reversing the current in the coil so the turning effect is always in the same direction; **brushes** press on the commutator to feed current into the spinning coil. The motor spins faster with more current, a stronger magnet, more turns on the coil, or a soft-iron core.

Loudspeakers use the same effect: an alternating current in a coil attached to the cone feels an alternating force in a permanent magnet's field, so the cone vibrates and produces sound.

::: method
1. Set up the left hand: **F**irst finger **F**ield (N→S), se**C**ond **C**urrent, thu**M**b **M**otion — three fingers at right angles.
2. Point the first finger and second finger as the diagram shows; the thumb gives the force. If the current comes out of the page, curl the hand so the second finger points at you.
3. "What happens if…": reversing current *or* field → force reverses; reversing both → unchanged; parallel to field → no force.
4. Size: **current, field strength, length of wire** (and angle).
5. Motor: opposite forces on opposite sides → rotation; commutator reverses current every half turn → keeps turning one way.
:::

::: example Using the left-hand rule
A wire between the poles of a magnet carries a current from left to right. The field points into the page. In which direction is the force on the wire? (2)

First finger into the page, second finger to the right (1); the thumb then points **up the page**, so the force is upward (1).
:::

::: example Changing the force
State what happens to the force on a current-carrying wire in a field when (a) the current is doubled, (b) the direction of the field is reversed, (c) the wire is turned to lie along the field lines. (3)

(a) The force doubles (1). (b) The force acts in the opposite direction (1). (c) The force falls to zero (1).
:::

::: example Why a motor turns
Explain why a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences a turning effect. (3)

The current flows in opposite directions along the two sides of the coil (1); by the left-hand rule the forces on the two sides are in opposite directions — one up, one down (1); these equal and opposite forces on either side of the axle produce a turning effect (couple) on the coil (1).
:::

::: example The commutator
Explain the purpose of the split-ring commutator in a d.c. motor. (3)

After half a turn the sides of the coil have swapped places, so the forces would now turn it back the other way (1). The commutator swaps the connections to the coil every half turn, reversing the current in it (1), so the forces keep the coil turning in the same direction continuously (1).
:::

::: q
What is the motor effect? (1)
---
A current-carrying wire (or moving charge) in a magnetic field experiences a force.
:::

::: q
State what each finger represents in Fleming's left-hand rule. (3)
---
First finger: magnetic field (N to S) (1); second finger: current (1); thumb: motion/force (1).
:::

::: q
Give three factors that increase the force on a wire in a magnetic field. (3)
---
Larger current (1); stronger field (1); longer wire in the field (1).
:::

::: q
When is there no force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field? (1)
---
When the current is parallel to the field lines.
:::

::: q
A wire carries a current up the page in a field pointing to the right. Find the direction of the force. (2)
---
Field to the right, current up (1); force out of the page (toward you) (1).
:::

::: q
A beam of electrons moves through a magnetic field. Why is it deflected? (2)
---
Electrons are moving charges, equivalent to a current (in the opposite direction) (1); a moving charge in a field experiences a force at right angles to its motion (1).
:::

::: q
What do the brushes do in a d.c. motor? (1)
---
They press against the commutator to keep the coil connected to the supply while it spins.
:::

::: q
Give two ways to make a d.c. motor turn faster. (2)
---
Any two: increase the current; use a stronger magnet; more turns on the coil; add a soft-iron core.
:::

::: q
How can the direction of rotation of a d.c. motor be reversed? (1)
---
Reverse the current (or reverse the magnetic field).
:::

::: q
Explain how a loudspeaker uses the motor effect. (3)
---
An alternating current flows in a coil attached to the cone, inside the field of a permanent magnet (1); the coil experiences a force that reverses as the current reverses (1), so the cone vibrates at the frequency of the current, producing a sound wave (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Left** hand for motors, and it is *conventional* current (+ to −). Electron flow is the opposite way.
- All three fingers at **right angles**; force is perpendicular to both field and current.
- Reverse **one** of current or field → force reverses. Reverse **both** → no change. **Parallel** → no force.
- Motor: the sides feel **opposite** forces because the current runs **opposite ways** — say both parts.
- Commutator = **reverses the current every half turn**; brushes = keep contact. Do not confuse the two.
:::
