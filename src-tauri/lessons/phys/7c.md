## What it is

**Detecting radiation.** Ionising radiation is invisible, so we detect it by its ionising effect.

- **Photographic film** darkens where radiation strikes it (this is how radioactivity was discovered). Workers wear **film badges**: the film behind windows of different absorbers shows both the dose received and the type of radiation.
- A **Geiger-Müller (GM) tube** contains low-pressure gas; radiation entering ionises the gas, the ions are pulled to electrodes by a high voltage, and each pulse of current is counted by the attached **counter** or heard as a click on a ratemeter. It measures the **count rate** (counts per second or per minute).

**Background radiation** is the low level of radiation always present around us. Sources include:

- **Radon gas** seeping from rocks and soil (the largest natural source in many areas, especially granite regions).
- **Rocks and building materials** containing uranium, thorium and their decay products.
- **Cosmic rays** from space (higher at altitude — pilots receive more).
- **Food and drink** (potassium-40, carbon-14) and our own bodies.
- Human-made: **medical** X-rays and radiotherapy, **nuclear weapons testing** fallout and **nuclear accidents**, nuclear power (very small).

Before using a source, measure the background count rate and **subtract** it from every reading.

**Activity** is the number of nuclei decaying per second, measured in **becquerels (Bq)**: 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. A source's activity **falls over time** as it runs out of unstable nuclei — but because decay is random, it falls in a particular way: a fixed **fraction** decays in each equal time interval, never a fixed number.

**Half-life** is the time taken for **half the unstable nuclei** in a sample to decay — equivalently, the time for the **activity (or count rate) to halve**. It is constant for a given isotope, regardless of how much you start with: after one half-life half remains, after two a quarter, after three an eighth, and so on. Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years. A short half-life means a very active source that soon dies away; a long half-life means low activity that persists.

::: method
1. **Subtract background** from measured count rates before doing anything else.
2. **Half-life from a graph**: read the starting activity, find where it has halved, read the time; check with a second halving (the intervals must be equal).
3. **Halving table**: write the activity at 0, 1, 2, 3… half-lives (÷2 each step) until you reach the target; count the steps and multiply by the half-life.
4. **Fraction remaining** after *n* half-lives = $(\tfrac{1}{2})^n$: ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16.
5. Time = number of half-lives × half-life; number of half-lives = total time ÷ half-life.
:::

::: example Half-life from data
A source's count rate (background already subtracted) falls from 1200 counts/min to 150 counts/min in 45 minutes. Find the half-life. (3)

1200 → 600 → 300 → 150 is **three halvings** (1). 45 ÷ 3 (1) = **15 minutes** (1).
:::

::: example Activity after a given time
A sample of iodine-131 (half-life 8 days) has an activity of 6400 Bq. What is its activity after 32 days? (2)

32 ÷ 8 = 4 half-lives (1). 6400 → 3200 → 1600 → 800 → **400 Bq** (1).
:::

::: example Correcting for background
With no source present a GM tube records 30 counts/min. With a source it records 510 counts/min, and 6 hours later 150 counts/min. Find the half-life. (3)

Corrected rates: 480 and 120 counts/min (1). 480 → 240 → 120 is two half-lives (1), so the half-life = 6 ÷ 2 = **3 hours** (1).
:::

::: example Why decay is a fraction, not a fixed number
Explain why a source's activity halves in each half-life rather than falling by a constant amount. (2)

Decay is random, so each nucleus has the same fixed chance of decaying in a given time (1); the number that decay is therefore proportional to the number left, so the activity falls by the same *fraction* in each equal interval (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how a Geiger-Müller tube detects radiation. (2)
---
Radiation entering the tube ionises the gas inside (1); the ions create a pulse of current that is counted (1).
:::

::: q
Give three sources of background radiation. (3)
---
Any three: radon gas from the ground; rocks and buildings; cosmic rays; food and drink; medical uses; nuclear fallout/accidents.
:::

::: q
Define activity and give its unit. (2)
---
The number of nuclei decaying per second (1); becquerel, Bq (1).
:::

::: q
Define half-life. (1)
---
The time taken for half the unstable nuclei in a sample to decay (or for the activity to halve).
:::

::: q
A source has a half-life of 2 hours. What fraction of it remains after 6 hours? (2)
---
3 half-lives (1); 1/8 (1).
:::

::: q
An activity of 2000 Bq falls to 250 Bq. How many half-lives have passed? (1)
---
Three (2000 → 1000 → 500 → 250).
:::

::: q
Cobalt-60 has a half-life of 5.3 years. Roughly how long until a source's activity falls to a quarter? (1)
---
Two half-lives ≈ 10.6 years.
:::

::: q
Why is the count rate measured with no source present before an experiment? (1)
---
To find the background count so it can be subtracted from the readings.
:::

::: q
Which is more dangerous to have nearby for a short time: a source with a half-life of 1 hour or one with 1000 years, of the same number of nuclei? Explain. (2)
---
The 1-hour source (1); its nuclei decay much faster, so its activity is far higher (1).
:::

::: q
How does a film badge indicate the dose a worker has received? (1)
---
The film darkens in proportion to the radiation it has absorbed when developed.
:::

::: watch
- **Subtract background** whenever a "no source" count is given — the halving must be done on corrected numbers.
- Half-life is the time to halve, **not** the time to disappear; a source never reaches exactly zero.
- Use a **halving table** and count steps; guessing "a third of the time for a third of the activity" is wrong.
- Fraction remaining is $(\tfrac{1}{2})^n$; "after two half-lives none is left" is a classic error (a quarter remains).
- Read graphs with **two** halvings to confirm the intervals are equal.
:::
