## What it is

A **transformer** changes the size of an alternating voltage. It has two coils of insulated wire — the **primary** (input) and the **secondary** (output) — wound on a laminated **soft iron core**, with no electrical connection between them.

**How it works.** Alternating current in the primary coil produces a **changing magnetic field**. The soft iron core carries this field through the secondary coil, where the **changing field induces an alternating voltage** (electromagnetic induction). The core is soft iron because it magnetises and demagnetises easily, following the changing field. A transformer **needs a.c.**: with d.c. the field would be steady, nothing would change through the secondary, and no voltage would be induced.

**Turns ratio.** The voltages are in the same ratio as the numbers of turns:

$$\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}$$

- **Step-up** transformer: more turns on the secondary → larger output voltage.
- **Step-down** transformer: fewer turns on the secondary → smaller output voltage.

**Power.** A transformer cannot create energy. For a 100% efficient transformer the power in equals the power out:

$$V_p \times I_p = V_s \times I_s$$

so stepping the voltage **up** steps the current **down** by the same factor, and vice versa. Real transformers are very efficient (over 95%) with small losses as heat in the coils and core.

**The National Grid.** Power stations generate at about 25 kV. **Step-up transformers** raise this to 132 kV–400 kV for the long transmission lines, and **step-down transformers** at substations reduce it in stages to 230 V for homes. The reason is **efficiency**: for a given power, a higher voltage means a **smaller current** (P = IV), and the energy wasted as heat in the cables depends on the **current squared** (P = I²R). Transmitting at high voltage cuts the current, so cable losses are tiny; the high voltage is stepped down again before use because it would be far too dangerous in a house.

::: terms
- **Transformer**: two coils wound on a soft iron core, which changes the size of an alternating voltage.
- **Primary coil**: the input coil of a transformer.
- **Secondary coil**: the output coil of a transformer.
- **Step-up transformer**: more turns on the secondary than the primary, so it increases the voltage.
- **Step-down transformer**: fewer turns on the secondary, so it decreases the voltage.
- **Turns ratio equation**: Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns.
- **Power in a transformer**: for 100% efficiency, Vp × Ip = Vs × Is.
- **National Grid**: the network that transmits electricity at very high voltage, so the current is small and less energy is wasted heating the cables.
:::

::: method
1. **Turns ratio**: $V_p/V_s = N_p/N_s$. Rearrange for the unknown: $V_s = V_p \times N_s/N_p$, or $N_s = N_p \times V_s/V_p$.
2. **Power**: $V_p I_p = V_s I_s$ (assume 100% efficient unless told otherwise) → $I_s = V_p I_p / V_s$.
3. Decide step-up (Ns > Np) or step-down (Ns < Np) and say so.
4. Explanation of operation: a.c. in primary → changing field → carried by iron core → changing field through secondary → induced alternating voltage. **Why a.c.**: d.c. gives a constant field → nothing induced.
5. Grid: high voltage → low current → less heat loss (I²R) in cables; step down for safety at the user's end.
:::

::: example Step-down transformer
A transformer has 2000 turns on the primary and 100 turns on the secondary. The primary is connected to the 230 V mains. Calculate the secondary voltage. (2)

$V_s = V_p \times N_s / N_p = 230 \times 100 \div 2000$ (1) = **11.5 V** (1).
:::

::: example Designing a step-up transformer
A transformer must step 12 V up to 240 V. The primary has 50 turns. How many turns does the secondary need? (2)

$N_s = N_p \times V_s / V_p = 50 \times 240 \div 12$ (1) = **1000 turns** (1).
:::

::: example Current in a transformer
A 100% efficient transformer steps 230 V down to 23 V. The current in the secondary is 5 A. Calculate the current in the primary. (2)

$V_p I_p = V_s I_s$: $230 \times I_p = 23 \times 5 = 115$ (1), so $I_p =$ **0.5 A** (1).
:::

::: example Why transmit at high voltage
Explain why electricity is transmitted across the country at very high voltage. (3)

For a given power, raising the voltage reduces the current in the cables (P = IV) (1); the energy wasted as heat in the cables depends on the current squared (1), so the smaller current means far less energy is lost and the transmission is more efficient (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why a transformer needs a.c.
Explain why a transformer works with an alternating current but not with a direct current. (3)

An alternating current in the primary coil produces a magnetic field that is constantly changing (1). The iron core carries this changing field to the secondary coil, where the changing field induces an alternating voltage (1). A direct current would give a constant field, so no voltage would be induced in the secondary (1).
:::

::: q
Why must a transformer be supplied with alternating current? (2)
---
Only a changing magnetic field induces a voltage in the secondary (1); d.c. would give a steady field and no induced voltage (1).
:::

::: q
Why is the core made of soft iron? (1)
---
It is easily magnetised and demagnetised, so it carries the changing field efficiently to the secondary.
:::

::: q
Write the transformer turns-ratio equation. (1)
---
Vp / Vs = Np / Ns.
:::

::: q
A transformer has 400 primary turns and 40 secondary turns. Is it step-up or step-down? What is the output for a 120 V input? (2)
---
Step-down (1); 120 × 40 ÷ 400 = 12 V (1).
:::

::: q
A transformer steps 10 V up to 250 V. If the primary has 200 turns, find the secondary turns. (2)
---
200 × 250 ÷ 10 (1) = 5000 turns (1).
:::

::: q
Write the equation relating primary and secondary power for an ideal transformer. (1)
---
Vp × Ip = Vs × Is.
:::

::: q
A step-up transformer raises 25 kV to 400 kV. The primary current is 800 A. Find the secondary current. (2)
---
Is = 25 000 × 800 ÷ 400 000 (1) = 50 A (1).
:::

::: q
Where are step-down transformers used in the National Grid, and why? (2)
---
At substations near towns and homes (1) to reduce the voltage to a safe level (230 V) for use (1).
:::

::: q
State one reason transformers are not 100% efficient. (1)
---
Energy is dissipated as heat in the coils (resistance) or in the core.
:::

::: q
Explain why a transformer cannot be used to increase the power delivered. (1)
---
Energy is conserved: increasing the voltage decreases the current in the same ratio, so power out cannot exceed power in.
:::

::: watch
- **a.c. only.** The reason — a *changing* field is needed for induction — must be stated, not just "transformers use a.c.".
- Turns ratio: put the primary values **together** on one side; mixing Np with Vs is the common slip.
- Step-up raises voltage but **lowers current** — power is conserved, never multiplied.
- Grid explanation: **high V → low I → less heat (I²R) in cables**, then step down for **safety**. All links needed.
- The two coils are **not connected** electrically — the link is the magnetic field in the iron core.
:::
