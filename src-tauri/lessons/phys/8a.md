## What it is

Units for astrophysics: distances in **m** (and km), time in **s**, speed in **m/s**, gravitational field strength in **N/kg**, mass in **kg**, force in **N**.

**The structure of the universe.** The **universe** contains billions of **galaxies**; each galaxy is a collection of billions of stars held together by gravity. Our galaxy is the **Milky Way**, a spiral galaxy of about 100 billion stars. The Sun is one of them, and its **solar system** — the Sun, eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets — sits in one of its spiral arms. So the order from small to large is: Earth → solar system → Milky Way → universe.

**Gravity and g.** Every mass attracts every other mass. The **gravitational field strength, g,** at a body's surface depends on the body's **mass** and **radius**: a more massive or more compact body has a larger g. Earth: 10 N/kg; the Moon: 1.6 N/kg (much less mass); Jupiter: about 25 N/kg. Your weight *W = mg* changes from body to body; your mass does not. Gravitational force also **weakens with distance**, which is why g is smaller high above a planet than at its surface.

**Orbits.** Gravity provides the force that keeps a body in orbit — it is the **centripetal force** pulling the orbiting body toward the centre, constantly changing its direction without changing its speed. The Sun's gravity keeps the **planets** in orbit; a planet's gravity keeps its **moons** and artificial **satellites** in orbit; the Sun's gravity also holds **comets**.

- **Planets** orbit the Sun in nearly circular paths (slightly elliptical) in the same direction and plane; the closer to the Sun, the stronger the gravitational pull and the **faster** the orbital speed and the shorter the year.
- **Moons** are natural satellites orbiting planets in near-circular orbits.
- **Artificial satellites**: **geostationary** satellites orbit above the equator with a period of exactly 24 hours, staying above one point (communications, TV); **low polar orbit** satellites circle in a few hours passing over the poles, scanning the whole Earth as it turns beneath them (weather, mapping, spying).
- **Comets** have highly **elliptical** (elongated) orbits. They move **fastest when closest to the Sun** — gravity is strongest and has been pulling them inward — and slowest at their far end, spending most of their time far out in the cold, where they are unseen. Their tails appear near the Sun as ice vaporises, always pointing away from the Sun.

**Orbital speed.** For a circular orbit of radius *r* (measured from the **centre** of the body being orbited) and period *T*,

$$v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$$

— the circumference divided by the time for one orbit. Use it for planets round the Sun (r = orbital radius, T = year length) or satellites round Earth (r = Earth's radius **plus** height, T = period in seconds).

::: terms
- **Universe**: everything that exists — a very large collection of billions of galaxies.
- **Galaxy**: a large collection of billions of stars held together by gravity.
- **Milky Way**: the galaxy containing our Solar System.
- **Solar System**: the Sun and everything orbiting it — planets, moons, comets, asteroids.
- **Gravitational field strength (g)**: different on different bodies because it depends on their mass and radius.
- **Orbit**: the path of a body around another, kept in place by the force of gravity.
- **Orbital speed**: v = 2 × π × r ÷ T.
- **Comet**: a body of ice and dust with a highly elliptical orbit around the Sun, moving fastest when closest to it.
:::

::: method
1. Order the structures: **planet < solar system < galaxy < universe**; Milky Way = our galaxy.
2. g depends on the body: bigger mass → bigger g. Weight changes, mass does not.
3. Orbits: the force is **gravity** acting toward the centre; closer orbit → stronger force → faster.
4. Comet orbits: **elliptical**, fastest near the Sun, slowest far away — and explain with gravity's strength varying with distance.
5. $v = 2\pi r/T$: *r* from the **centre** (add the planet's radius to a satellite's height), *T* in **seconds** (days × 86 400; years × 3.15 × 10⁷).
:::

::: example Orbital speed of the Earth
The Earth orbits the Sun at a mean distance of 1.5 × 10¹¹ m once every 365 days. Calculate its orbital speed. (3)

T = 365 × 24 × 3600 = 3.15 × 10⁷ s (1). $v = 2\pi \times 1.5 \times 10^{11} \div 3.15 \times 10^7$ (1) = **3.0 × 10⁴ m/s** (30 km/s) (1).
:::

::: example Satellite above the Earth
A satellite orbits 600 km above the Earth's surface with a period of 96 minutes. Earth's radius is 6400 km. Find its orbital speed. (3)

r = 6400 + 600 = 7000 km = 7.0 × 10⁶ m (1). T = 96 × 60 = 5760 s. $v = 2\pi \times 7.0 \times 10^6 \div 5760$ (1) = **7640 m/s** (1).
:::

::: example Comets
Explain why a comet travels much faster when it is near the Sun than when it is at the far end of its orbit. (3)

The comet's orbit is highly elliptical, so its distance from the Sun varies greatly (1); the Sun's gravitational pull is much stronger when the comet is close (1), so as it falls inward it accelerates, reaching its greatest speed nearest the Sun, then slows as gravity pulls back on it on the way out (1).
:::

::: example Weight on other bodies
A probe of mass 250 kg lands on Mars, where g = 3.7 N/kg. Compare its weight on Mars with its weight on Earth and explain why g differs. (3)

Mars: W = 250 × 3.7 = 925 N; Earth: 250 × 10 = 2500 N (1). Mars has much less mass than Earth (1), so its gravitational field at the surface is weaker; the probe's mass of 250 kg is the same on both (1).
:::

::: q
Put in order of size, smallest first: galaxy, universe, solar system, planet. (1)
---
Planet, solar system, galaxy, universe.
:::

::: q
What is the Milky Way? (1)
---
The galaxy containing our solar system (a spiral galaxy of about 100 billion stars).
:::

::: q
Why is g on the Moon smaller than on Earth? (1)
---
The Moon has much less mass than the Earth.
:::

::: q
What force keeps the planets in orbit around the Sun? (1)
---
The gravitational attraction of the Sun.
:::

::: q
Compare the orbits of planets and comets. (2)
---
Planets: nearly circular, roughly constant speed (1). Comets: highly elliptical, with speed varying — fastest near the Sun (1).
:::

::: q
What is a moon? (1)
---
A natural satellite orbiting a planet.
:::

::: q
Give one use of a geostationary satellite and one of a low polar orbit satellite. (2)
---
Geostationary: communications/TV broadcasting (1). Polar: weather monitoring, mapping, surveillance (1).
:::

::: q
Write the equation for orbital speed. (1)
---
v = 2πr / T.
:::

::: q
The Moon orbits the Earth at a radius of 3.8 × 10⁸ m in 27.3 days. Calculate its orbital speed. (3)
---
T = 27.3 × 86 400 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s (1); v = 2π × 3.8 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.36 × 10⁶ (1) = 1010 m/s (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why Mercury orbits the Sun faster than Neptune. (2)
---
Mercury is much closer to the Sun (1), so the Sun's gravitational pull on it is much stronger, requiring a greater speed to stay in orbit (1).
:::

::: watch
- The orbital radius is measured from the **centre** of the planet; add the planet's radius to a satellite's altitude.
- Period in **seconds**: days × 86 400. Forgetting the conversion is the main lost mark on $v = 2\pi r/T$.
- Comets: **elliptical** orbit, **fastest nearest the Sun** — and explain with gravity varying with distance.
- **Weight** changes between bodies; **mass** does not. Give W = mg with the right g.
- Solar system is inside the Milky Way, which is one galaxy among billions in the universe — keep the nesting right.
:::

::: test
Describe the orbit of a comet and explain how its speed changes. (3)
---
A highly elliptical orbit around the Sun (1); it moves fastest when nearest the Sun, where gravity is strongest (1), and slowest at the far end, where it spends most of its time (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the gravitational field strength at the top of a mountain is very slightly less than at sea level. (1)
---
Gravitational force weakens with distance from the centre of the Earth.
:::

::: test
Explain why the Moon does not fall into the Earth even though gravity pulls it inward. (2)
---
It is moving sideways fast enough that as it falls toward the Earth, the Earth's surface curves away beneath it (1); gravity only changes its direction, keeping it in orbit (1).
:::
