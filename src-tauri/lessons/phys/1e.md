## What it is

The **stopping distance** of a vehicle is the total distance it travels from the moment the driver sees a hazard to the moment it stops:

$$\text{stopping distance} = \text{thinking distance} + \text{braking distance}$$

- **Thinking distance** is the distance travelled during the driver's **reaction time** (typically 0.5–1 s) before the brakes are applied. Because the car is still at its full speed, thinking distance = speed × reaction time — it is **proportional to speed**. It is increased by tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distraction (phones) and age.
- **Braking distance** is the distance travelled while the brakes slow the car to rest. It depends on the speed **squared** (from $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$: double the speed and the braking distance is four times longer), and is increased by wet or icy roads, worn tyres or brakes, a heavier load, and going downhill.

Braking converts the car's kinetic energy into heat in the brakes: work done by the braking force = kinetic energy lost, $F \times s = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, which is why the distance scales with *v*².

**Falling objects and terminal velocity.** Near Earth's surface every falling object has a weight *W = mg* pulling it down and, once moving, **air resistance** pushing up. Air resistance **increases with speed** (and with the object's area).

1. At release: air resistance is zero, resultant = weight, acceleration = *g* = 10 m/s².
2. As speed rises, air resistance grows, the **resultant force decreases** and so the acceleration decreases — the object still speeds up, just more slowly.
3. Eventually air resistance **equals** the weight: resultant zero, acceleration zero, and the object falls at a constant **terminal velocity**.

A skydiver who opens a parachute suddenly has a huge air resistance, larger than the weight, so the resultant is upward and she **decelerates** — slower, but still moving downward — until air resistance again matches weight at a new, lower terminal velocity. In a vacuum there is no air resistance, so a feather and a hammer fall together with acceleration *g*.

::: method
1. **Stopping distance** questions: split into thinking + braking, and say which factor affects which. Reaction-time factors → thinking; road/tyre/brake/speed² factors → braking.
2. Thinking distance = speed × reaction time.
3. **Falling object**: reason in the order *weight constant → air resistance grows with speed → resultant shrinks → acceleration shrinks → forces balance → terminal velocity*. Every step is a mark.
4. On a velocity-time graph of a fall: curve getting less steep, then flat. A parachute gives a short steep *drop* then flat again.
5. Always state that at terminal velocity the resultant force is **zero**, not that "there are no forces".
:::

::: example Thinking distance
A driver with a reaction time of 0.8 s is travelling at 25 m/s. Calculate the thinking distance, and state the effect on it of the driver having drunk alcohol. (3)

Thinking distance = 25 × 0.8 (1) = **20 m** (1). Alcohol lengthens the reaction time, so the thinking distance increases (1).
:::

::: example Speed and braking distance
A car's braking distance at 15 m/s is 14 m. Estimate the braking distance at 30 m/s and explain. (3)

Braking distance is proportional to speed² (1). Speed doubles, so braking distance is 2² = 4 times longer (1): 14 × 4 = **56 m** (1).
:::

::: example Terminal velocity explanation
Explain, in terms of forces, why a skydiver reaches a terminal velocity. (4)

Initially the only force is weight, so she accelerates at *g* (1). As speed increases, air resistance increases (1), so the resultant force and hence the acceleration decrease (1). When air resistance equals weight the resultant is zero and she falls at a constant (terminal) velocity (1).
:::

::: example Opening the parachute
A skydiver falling at terminal velocity opens her parachute. Describe and explain her motion in the next few seconds. (3)

The large canopy greatly increases air resistance so it is now bigger than her weight (1); the resultant force is upward, so she decelerates (1) until air resistance falls back to equal her weight and she reaches a new, lower terminal velocity (1).
:::

::: q
Write the equation for stopping distance. (1)
---
Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance.
:::

::: q
Give two factors that increase thinking distance. (2)
---
Any two of: tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distractions such as a phone, higher speed.
:::

::: q
Give two factors, other than speed, that increase braking distance. (2)
---
Any two of: wet or icy road, worn tyres, worn brakes, heavier vehicle/load, downhill slope.
:::

::: q
A car travelling at 20 m/s has a driver with a 0.7 s reaction time. Calculate the thinking distance. (2)
---
20 × 0.7 (1) = 14 m (1).
:::

::: q
Why does doubling a car's speed roughly quadruple its braking distance? (2)
---
The kinetic energy (½mv²) to be removed by the brakes depends on v² (1), and work done = braking force × distance, so distance is proportional to v² (1).
:::

::: q
What is the acceleration of an object the instant it is released to fall (ignoring air resistance)? (1)
---
10 m/s² (g), downward.
:::

::: q
What can be said about the forces on an object falling at terminal velocity? (2)
---
Air resistance equals weight (1), so the resultant force is zero (1).
:::

::: q
Why does a feather reach a much lower terminal velocity than a stone? (2)
---
Its air resistance is large compared with its small weight (1), so the forces balance at a much lower speed (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the shape of a velocity-time graph for a skydiver from jumping until terminal velocity. (2)
---
Starts with a steep gradient (acceleration g) (1), the gradient decreases until the line becomes horizontal at terminal velocity (1).
:::

::: q
On the Moon there is no atmosphere. Compare how a hammer and a feather fall there. (2)
---
Both fall with the same acceleration (1) because there is no air resistance, so the only force is weight (1).
:::

::: watch
- Thinking distance is proportional to **speed**; braking distance to **speed squared**. Assign each factor to the right part.
- Terminal velocity: the resultant force is **zero**, but weight and air resistance are both still acting — never write "no forces".
- The sequence *air resistance increases → resultant decreases → acceleration decreases* is worth marks step by step; jumping straight to "forces balance" loses them.
- When the parachute opens the skydiver **decelerates while still falling** — she does not move upward.
- The **mass** of the falling object does not change; only the balance of forces does.
:::

::: test
A driver has a reaction time of 0.8 s and is travelling at 25 m/s. Calculate the thinking distance. (2)
---
$25 \times 0.8$ (1) $= 20$ m (1).
:::

::: test
A car's braking distance at 20 m/s is 30 m. Estimate the braking distance at 40 m/s, and explain your reasoning. (2)
---
About 120 m (1): braking distance depends on kinetic energy, which is proportional to speed squared, so doubling the speed quadruples it (1).
:::

::: test
The total stopping distance of a car is 75 m, of which 21 m is thinking distance. The car was travelling at 30 m/s. Find the braking distance and the driver's reaction time. (3)
---
Braking distance $= 75 - 21 = 54$ m (1); reaction time $= 21 \div 30$ (1) $= 0.7$ s (1).
:::

::: test
State two factors that affect the braking distance of a car but not the thinking distance. (2)
---
Any two of: road surface (wet/icy), tyre condition, brake condition, mass of the car (2).
:::

::: test
A ball is dropped and reaches terminal velocity. Describe how the forces on it change from the moment of release to terminal velocity. (3)
---
At release only weight acts, so acceleration is $g$ (1); as speed increases, air resistance increases, so the resultant force and acceleration decrease (1); at terminal velocity air resistance equals weight, resultant is zero, velocity constant (1).
:::

::: test
A skydiver opens her parachute while falling at terminal velocity. Describe and explain what happens to her motion immediately afterward. (3)
---
Air resistance suddenly exceeds her weight (1), giving an upward resultant force, so she decelerates (1); as she slows, air resistance falls until it equals weight again and she reaches a new, lower terminal velocity (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a person who has drunk alcohol has a longer stopping distance. (2)
---
Alcohol increases reaction time (1), so the thinking distance — and therefore the total stopping distance — is longer (1).
:::

::: test
Two identical balls are dropped on Earth and on the Moon from the same height. State which lands first and explain. (2)
---
The one on Earth: $g$ is 10 N/kg there against 1.6 N/kg on the Moon, so it accelerates more (1). Air resistance on a dense ball is negligible over a short drop, so the Moon's lack of air makes no real difference here (1).
:::
