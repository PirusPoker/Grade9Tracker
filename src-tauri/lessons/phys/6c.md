## What it is

**Electromagnetic induction** is the reverse of the motor effect: **moving a conductor through a magnetic field — or changing the field through a coil — induces a voltage** across the conductor. If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, the induced voltage drives a current. Nothing is induced while everything is stationary: it is the **cutting of field lines** (relative motion, or a changing field) that matters.

The induced voltage is made **larger** by:

- moving the wire or magnet **faster**;
- using a **stronger magnet**;
- using **more turns** on the coil (each turn adds its own induced voltage);
- a larger area of coil or a soft-iron core.

Its **direction** reverses if the motion reverses or the magnet's poles are swapped: push a magnet into a coil and the meter kicks one way; pull it out and it kicks the other way; hold it still and it reads zero. The induced current always flows so as to **oppose the change** producing it (which is why it takes effort to move the magnet — you are doing the work that becomes electrical energy).

**Generating electricity.** A **generator** (alternator) is a coil rotating in a magnetic field, or a magnet rotating inside a coil. As the coil turns, its sides cut the field lines, first one way then the other, so the induced voltage **alternates**: it is largest when the coil sides are moving at right angles to the field (coil horizontal in a vertical field) and zero when they move along the field lines. The result is **a.c.** — a sine-wave voltage that reverses direction every half turn. Spinning faster gives both a **larger** peak voltage (faster cutting) and a **higher frequency** (more cycles per second). Slip rings and brushes connect the rotating coil to the outside circuit without the wires twisting. A **dynamo** (bicycle lights) is the rotating-magnet version.

**Power stations** use exactly this: a turbine (steam, water or wind) spins an electromagnet inside huge stationary coils, inducing the alternating voltage that feeds the grid at 50 Hz.

::: method
1. Say **what changes**: the wire cuts field lines / the field through the coil changes. No change → no induced voltage.
2. To increase the induced voltage: **faster, stronger magnet, more turns** (and iron core). Give at least two when asked for ways.
3. Direction: reverse the motion **or** the poles → reverse the induced voltage.
4. Generator: describe the coil turning, cutting field lines, the voltage rising to a peak, falling to zero when the sides move along the field, then reversing → a.c.
5. Faster rotation → **higher peak voltage AND higher frequency**. Both changes on a sketch: taller and more squashed.
:::

::: example Magnet and coil
A bar magnet is pushed into a coil connected to a sensitive voltmeter. Describe what is observed as the magnet is pushed in, held still, and pulled out, and explain. (4)

Pushing in: the needle deflects one way (1). Held still: it returns to zero (1). Pulling out: it deflects the opposite way (1). A voltage is induced only while the magnetic field through the coil is changing (field lines are being cut); the direction of the change determines the direction of the induced voltage (1).
:::

::: example Increasing the induced voltage
Give three ways of increasing the voltage induced when a magnet is moved into a coil. (3)

Move the magnet faster (1); use a stronger magnet (1); use a coil with more turns (1).
:::

::: example How a generator works
Explain how a simple a.c. generator produces an alternating voltage. (4)

A coil rotates between the poles of a magnet (1); as it turns, the sides of the coil cut through the magnetic field lines, inducing a voltage (1). The voltage is greatest when the sides move at right angles to the field and zero when they move parallel to it (1); after each half turn the sides move the opposite way through the field, so the induced voltage reverses direction — the output alternates (1).
:::

::: example Effect of rotation speed
A generator's coil is made to rotate twice as fast. Describe two changes to the voltage output. (2)

The peak (maximum) voltage doubles because the coil cuts field lines faster (1); the frequency doubles because there are twice as many cycles per second (1).
:::

::: q
What is electromagnetic induction? (2)
---
A voltage is induced across a conductor (1) when it moves through a magnetic field or the field through it changes (1).
:::

::: q
A wire is held stationary between the poles of a magnet. What voltage is induced? Why? (2)
---
Zero (1); no field lines are being cut / the field through the wire is not changing (1).
:::

::: q
How can the direction of an induced current be reversed? (2)
---
Reverse the direction of motion (1) or reverse the magnetic field / swap the poles (1).
:::

::: q
Name the part of a generator that connects the rotating coil to the external circuit. (1)
---
Slip rings (with brushes).
:::

::: q
Why does a generator produce a.c. rather than d.c.? (1)
---
Each side of the coil moves alternately up and down through the field, so the induced voltage reverses every half turn.
:::

::: q
At what point in its rotation does a generator coil produce zero voltage? (1)
---
When the sides of the coil are moving parallel to the field lines (coil vertical in a horizontal field), so no lines are cut.
:::

::: q
Describe how a bicycle dynamo generates electricity. (2)
---
The wheel turns a magnet that rotates near or inside a coil (1); the changing magnetic field through the coil induces an alternating voltage which lights the lamp (1).
:::

::: q
What is the main difference between a motor and a generator? (2)
---
A motor uses a current in a field to produce motion (1); a generator uses motion in a field to produce a current (1).
:::

::: q
In a power station, what turns the generator? (1)
---
A turbine driven by steam, water or wind.
:::

::: q
Sketch how the output of a generator changes if the magnet is replaced by a stronger one at the same speed. (1)
---
Same frequency but a larger peak voltage (taller wave).
:::

::: watch
- Induction needs **change**: moving, or a changing field. A stationary magnet in a coil gives **nothing**.
- The three standard ways to increase the induced voltage: **speed, field strength, turns** — learn all three.
- A generator's output is **a.c.** because the coil sides swap direction through the field each half turn.
- Faster rotation changes **two** things — peak voltage and frequency.
- Do not use Fleming's *left*-hand rule for induction questions; the exam wants the "cutting field lines" explanation, not a hand rule.
:::

::: test
State three ways of increasing the size of the voltage induced when a magnet is pushed into a coil. (3)
---
Move the magnet faster (1); use a stronger magnet (1); use more turns on the coil (1).
:::

::: test
A magnet is pushed into a coil connected to a sensitive ammeter, then pulled out again. Describe what the ammeter shows. (2)
---
It deflects one way as the magnet goes in (1) and the opposite way as it comes out; nothing while the magnet is still (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a voltage is induced in a coil rotating in a magnetic field. (2)
---
The coil cuts through the magnetic field lines as it turns (1); cutting field lines induces a voltage across the coil (1).
:::

::: test
Sketch or describe the output of an a.c. generator over one rotation of the coil, and explain why it is zero twice. (3)
---
A sine wave: rises to a peak, falls through zero to a negative peak and back (1); it is zero when the coil is perpendicular to the field, moving parallel to the field lines and not cutting them (1); maximum when the coil is parallel to the field, cutting lines fastest (1).
:::

::: test
What are slip rings, and how do they differ from a commutator? (2)
---
Two separate rings that each stay connected to one end of the coil, giving a.c. output (1); a commutator is a split ring that swaps connections each half turn, giving d.c. (1).
:::

::: test
The speed of a generator's coil is doubled. State two changes to the output. (2)
---
The peak voltage doubles (1); the frequency doubles (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the induced current in a coil opposes the motion of the magnet producing it, and why this must be so. (2)
---
The induced current makes the coil an electromagnet whose pole repels the approaching magnet (1); otherwise the magnet would speed up and energy would be created from nothing (1).
:::

::: test
A bicycle dynamo lights a lamp. Explain why the lamp goes dim when the cyclist slows down. (2)
---
The magnet in the dynamo turns more slowly, so it cuts field lines at a lower rate (1); the induced voltage and current are smaller (1).
:::
