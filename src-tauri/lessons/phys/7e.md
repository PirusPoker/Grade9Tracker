## What it is

**Nuclear reactions as an energy source.** The nucleus stores enormous energy: a kilogram of uranium fuel releases millions of times more energy than a kilogram of coal. Nuclear power stations release it by **fission** — splitting large nuclei — and use the heat to make steam for a turbine, just like a fossil-fuel station.

**Fission of uranium-235.** A slow-moving **neutron** is absorbed by a $^{235}_{92}$U nucleus, making it unstable. The nucleus **splits** into two smaller **daughter nuclei** (for example barium and krypton), releasing **two or three more neutrons** and a large amount of **energy** — mostly as kinetic energy of the fragments, which heats the surroundings. A typical equation:

$$^{235}_{92}\text{U} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + ^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,^{1}_{0}\text{n} + \text{energy}$$

(Check: 235 + 1 = 141 + 92 + 3; 92 + 0 = 56 + 36 + 0.) The daughter nuclei are themselves **radioactive** — this is the source of nuclear waste.

**The chain reaction.** Each fission releases neutrons that can go on to cause **further fissions**, each of which releases more neutrons. Uncontrolled, the number of fissions grows exponentially in a fraction of a second — a nuclear bomb. In a **reactor** the reaction is controlled so that, on average, **exactly one** neutron from each fission goes on to cause another: a steady, self-sustaining rate.

**Inside the reactor.**

- **Fuel rods** of uranium (enriched to a few per cent U-235) are the source of fissions.
- The **moderator** (graphite, or the water in a pressurised-water reactor) **slows down** the fast neutrons released by fission, because slow neutrons are far more likely to be absorbed by U-235 and cause further fission.
- **Control rods** of boron or cadmium **absorb neutrons**. They are raised to speed the reaction up and lowered to slow it down; pushing them fully in shuts the reactor down. They keep the chain reaction steady at one neutron per fission.
- A **coolant** (water, or carbon dioxide gas) carries the heat away from the core to a heat exchanger, where it boils water into steam for the turbines.
- **Shielding**: the core is enclosed in a thick **steel pressure vessel** and a **concrete** shield several metres thick to absorb the neutrons and gamma rays and protect workers.

::: method
1. Fission story in order: **neutron absorbed → nucleus unstable → splits into two daughter nuclei → 2–3 neutrons + energy released**.
2. Balance a fission equation like any nuclear equation: top and bottom numbers must match on both sides — count the neutrons.
3. Chain reaction: neutrons from one fission cause **more** fissions; uncontrolled = bomb, controlled = one per fission.
4. Reactor parts and jobs: moderator **slows** neutrons; control rods **absorb** neutrons; coolant **carries heat**; shielding **absorbs radiation**. Never swap moderator and control rods.
5. Products: daughter nuclei are **radioactive waste** with long half-lives — the main disadvantage.
:::

::: example Describing fission
Describe what happens when a uranium-235 nucleus undergoes fission. (4)

The nucleus absorbs a slow neutron (1), becoming unstable; it splits into two smaller daughter nuclei (1), releasing two or three neutrons (1) and a large amount of energy, mostly as kinetic energy of the fragments (1).
:::

::: example Balancing a fission equation
Complete the equation: $^{235}_{92}\text{U} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{144}_{56}\text{Ba} + ^{?}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,^{1}_{0}\text{n}$. (2)

Nucleon numbers: 235 + 1 = 236 = 144 + A + 3, so A = **89** (1). Check protons: 92 = 56 + 36 (1). The fragment is $^{89}_{36}$Kr.
:::

::: example The chain reaction
Explain how a chain reaction can occur in uranium-235, and how it is controlled in a reactor. (4)

Each fission releases two or three neutrons (1); if these are absorbed by other U-235 nuclei they cause further fissions, releasing more neutrons, so the number of fissions grows (1). In a reactor, control rods absorb some of the neutrons (1) so that on average just one neutron from each fission causes another, keeping the reaction steady (1).
:::

::: example Moderator and control rods
Explain the different roles of the moderator and the control rods in a nuclear reactor. (4)

The moderator (graphite or water) slows the fast neutrons from fission (1) because slow neutrons are much more likely to be absorbed by U-235 and cause fission (1). The control rods (boron/cadmium) absorb neutrons (1); raising or lowering them changes how many neutrons are available, so the rate of the chain reaction can be controlled or the reactor shut down (1).
:::

::: q
What is nuclear fission? (1)
---
The splitting of a large, unstable nucleus into two smaller nuclei, releasing neutrons and energy.
:::

::: q
What triggers the fission of a uranium-235 nucleus? (1)
---
Absorbing a (slow) neutron.
:::

::: q
Name the products of a fission reaction. (2)
---
Two daughter nuclei (1); two or three neutrons plus energy (1).
:::

::: q
Why are the daughter nuclei a problem? (1)
---
They are radioactive, forming waste that must be stored safely for a long time.
:::

::: q
What is a chain reaction? (1)
---
A reaction in which the neutrons released by one fission go on to cause further fissions.
:::

::: q
What material are control rods made of, and what do they do? (2)
---
Boron or cadmium (1); they absorb neutrons to control the rate of the chain reaction (1).
:::

::: q
Why are the neutrons slowed down in a reactor? (1)
---
Slow neutrons are much more likely to be absorbed by U-235 and cause fission.
:::

::: q
Name the moderator in a typical reactor. (1)
---
Graphite (or water).
:::

::: q
How is the heat from the reactor core used to generate electricity? (2)
---
A coolant carries the heat to a heat exchanger, boiling water into steam (1); the steam drives a turbine connected to a generator (1).
:::

::: q
Why is the reactor surrounded by thick concrete? (1)
---
To absorb the neutrons and gamma rays emitted, protecting workers and the environment.
:::

::: watch
- Fission needs the full sequence: **neutron in → split → daughters + neutrons + energy**. Missing the neutrons loses the chain-reaction link.
- **Moderator slows; control rods absorb.** Confusing them is the most common error in this topic.
- Control = **one neutron per fission** on average; not "no neutrons".
- Equations: count the **three** (or two) neutrons on the right when balancing the nucleon numbers.
- Waste is the daughter nuclei — **radioactive with long half-lives** — not "the uranium".
:::

::: test
Explain why the neutrons released in fission are important. (2)
---
They can be absorbed by other U-235 nuclei and cause further fissions (1), giving a chain reaction that keeps the energy release going (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what would happen in a reactor if the control rods were fully withdrawn. (2)
---
More than one neutron from each fission would cause another fission (1); the number of fissions would grow rapidly, releasing energy too fast — overheating (1).
:::

::: test
Describe the energy transfers in a nuclear power station from the fuel to the generator. (3)
---
Nuclear store of the uranium → thermal store of the core by fission (1); coolant carries the thermal energy to a boiler where it makes steam (1); steam turns the turbine (kinetic), which turns the generator (electrical) (1).
:::

::: test
State one advantage and one disadvantage of nuclear power compared with a coal-fired station. (2)
---
Advantage: no carbon dioxide, and far less fuel needed (1). Disadvantage: radioactive waste, and the risk and cost of accidents (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the fuel used in a reactor must be enriched. (2)
---
Natural uranium is mostly U-238, which does not fission with slow neutrons (1); increasing the proportion of U-235 makes a chain reaction possible (1).
:::

::: test
Give two ways in which the reactor's design protects the workers. (2)
---
A thick concrete shield absorbs neutrons and gamma rays (1); the steel pressure vessel contains the core, and control rods can shut the reaction down (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a fission reactor produces waste that must be stored for thousands of years. (2)
---
The daughter nuclei are radioactive (1) and some have very long half-lives, so they stay dangerous for a very long time (1).
:::
