## What it is

Thermal energy moves from **hot to cold** by three processes.

**Conduction** — energy passed along through a material by **particles vibrating** and jostling their neighbours. In metals it is much faster because **free electrons** move through the lattice carrying energy as well. Metals are good conductors; non-metals, liquids and especially gases are poor conductors (**insulators**). Air trapped in fibres, foam or feathers is what makes most insulators work. A vacuum cannot conduct at all.

**Convection** — energy carried by the **movement of a fluid** (liquid or gas). Heat a region of fluid and its particles move faster and spread out, so it becomes **less dense** and **rises**; cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place, then is heated in turn, setting up a **convection current**. Solids cannot convect. Everyday examples: a radiator warms a room as air rises above it and circulates; hot water rises in a kettle or immersion heater so the element goes at the bottom; a freezer's cooling element is at the top so cold air sinks; sea breezes; hot-air balloons; fridge cooling units.

**Radiation** — energy transferred by **infrared electromagnetic waves**, which need **no medium**: this is how the Sun's energy crosses space. All objects emit infrared; the hotter the object, the **more** it radiates and at shorter wavelengths.

- **Emission**: **dark, matt** surfaces are the best emitters; **light, shiny** surfaces are the poorest.
- **Absorption**: dark, matt surfaces are also the best **absorbers**; light, shiny surfaces **reflect** most radiation.
- Hotter objects and larger surface areas radiate more.

So a black car heats up fastest in the sun and cools fastest at night; solar panels are black; a marathon runner's foil blanket is shiny to reflect body heat back; teapots and kettles are often shiny to keep heat in.

**Practical: thermal energy transfer.** Several standard experiments:

- **Leslie cube** — a metal cube with four different faces (matt black, shiny black, white, polished silver) filled with hot water; an infrared detector held the same distance from each face shows the matt black face emits the most.
- **Absorption** — two identical cans, one painted matt black and one shiny, placed the same distance from a lamp or radiant heater, with thermometers: the black can warms fastest.
- **Conduction** — rods of different metals with wax-fixed drawing pins, heated at one end: the pin falls first from the best conductor (copper).
- **Convection** — a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) in a beaker of water heated at one side shows the circulating current. Control variables: same volume of water, same starting temperature, same distance from the heater.

**Reducing unwanted transfer — insulation.** In a house: **loft insulation** (fibreglass traps air — cuts conduction and convection), **cavity wall insulation** (foam in the gap stops convection currents), **double glazing** (air or vacuum between panes cuts conduction/convection; the gap is thin so convection is minimal), **draught excluders** (stop convection through gaps), **reflective foil** behind radiators (reflects radiation back), and thick carpets/curtains. A **vacuum flask** uses every trick: a vacuum between double walls stops conduction and convection, silvered walls reduce radiation, and a plastic stopper cuts conduction and convection at the top.

::: method
1. **Name the process** and give the **particle explanation**: conduction (vibrations passed on, free electrons in metals), convection (heated fluid expands, less dense, rises), radiation (infrared waves, no medium needed).
2. Surfaces: **matt black = best emitter and absorber; shiny = worst emitter, best reflector.**
3. For "why is X positioned there" (element at the bottom, freezer compartment at the top) — reason with density and rising/sinking.
4. Insulation questions: say **which process** each feature reduces and **how** (trapped air is a poor conductor and cannot circulate).
5. Practicals: identify the variable changed, the variables controlled, and how the result is measured.
:::

::: example Conduction in metals
Explain why metals conduct thermal energy much better than plastics. (3)

In both, heated particles vibrate more and pass energy to neighbouring particles by collision (1). Metals also contain free electrons that move through the structure (1), carrying energy quickly from the hot end to the cold end, which plastics cannot do (1).
:::

::: example Convection in a kettle
Explain why the heating element in a kettle is placed at the bottom. (3)

Water next to the element is heated, its particles move faster and spread out, so it becomes less dense (1) and rises; cooler, denser water sinks to replace it and is heated in turn (1). This convection current circulates all the water so it heats evenly and quickly; an element at the top would only heat the top layer (1).
:::

::: example Radiation and surfaces
Two identical beakers of hot water are left to cool, one painted matt black and one polished silver. Predict and explain the result. (3)

The black beaker cools faster (1) because a matt black surface is a better emitter of infrared radiation (1) while a shiny surface is a poor emitter (it reflects), so it loses energy more slowly (1).
:::

::: example Vacuum flask
Explain how a vacuum flask keeps a drink hot. (4)

The vacuum between the double walls contains no particles, so no energy can pass by conduction or convection (1). The walls are silvered so radiation from the hot drink is reflected back and the outer surface emits little (1). The plastic or cork stopper is a poor conductor and stops convection currents escaping at the top (1). Only a small amount escapes by conduction through the neck, so the drink stays hot for hours (1).
:::

::: q
Name the three ways thermal energy is transferred. (3)
---
Conduction (1), convection (1), radiation (1).
:::

::: q
Which process can transfer energy through a vacuum? Explain. (2)
---
Radiation (1); it is carried by electromagnetic (infrared) waves, which need no particles (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a convection current forms when a liquid is heated from below. (3)
---
The heated liquid expands and becomes less dense (1), so it rises (1); cooler, denser liquid sinks to replace it and the cycle continues (1).
:::

::: q
Why are gases poor conductors? (1)
---
Their particles are far apart, so collisions that pass on energy are infrequent.
:::

::: q
Which surface is the best absorber of infrared: matt black or shiny white? (1)
---
Matt black.
:::

::: q
Why are the pipes at the back of a fridge painted black? (1)
---
Black surfaces are good emitters, so they radiate heat away from the fridge effectively.
:::

::: q
How does loft insulation reduce heat loss? (2)
---
Fibreglass traps air, which is a poor conductor (1), and prevents the air from circulating, reducing convection (1).
:::

::: q
Describe a test to compare how well different surfaces emit radiation. (3)
---
Fill a Leslie cube (faces matt black, shiny black, white, silver) with hot water (1); hold an infrared detector the same distance from each face (1); the highest reading shows the best emitter — matt black (1).
:::

::: q
Why is the freezer compartment at the top of a fridge? (2)
---
Air cooled at the top becomes denser and sinks (1), setting up a convection current that cools the whole fridge (1).
:::

::: q
Name the process by which energy from a hot drink reaches your hand through a ceramic mug. (1)
---
Conduction.
:::

::: watch
- Convection needs the words **less dense** and **rises**; "hot air rises because it is hot" scores nothing.
- Conduction in metals must mention **free electrons** for the extra mark.
- **Radiation needs no medium** — that is what distinguishes it, and why the Sun can heat the Earth.
- Matt black is best at **both** emitting and absorbing; shiny is poor at both (it reflects). Do not split them.
- For insulation, always say **which transfer** is reduced and **why trapped air** does it.
:::
