## What it is

A **wave** is a way of transferring **energy** (and information) from one place to another **without transferring matter**. The water in a ripple bobs up and down; it does not travel with the wave. Units for this section: angles in **degrees (°)**, frequency in **hertz (Hz)**, wavelength and distance in **metres (m)**, speed in **m/s**, time in **seconds (s)**.

**Two types of wave.**

- **Transverse**: the oscillations are at **right angles** to the direction the wave travels. Water ripples, waves on a rope, all **electromagnetic waves** (light, radio…) and S-waves in earthquakes.
- **Longitudinal**: the oscillations are **along** the direction of travel, as **compressions** (particles bunched up) and **rarefactions** (spread out). **Sound**, a pushed Slinky spring, ultrasound and P-waves.

**Describing a wave.**

- **Amplitude** — the maximum displacement from the rest (undisturbed) position; measured from the middle line to a crest, *not* crest to trough. Bigger amplitude = more energy (louder, brighter).
- **Wavelength (λ)** — the distance between two adjacent crests (or compressions), or any two identical points in step. In metres.
- **Frequency (f)** — the number of complete waves passing a point **per second**, in hertz. 1 kHz = 1000 Hz.
- **Period (T)** — the time for one complete wave. $T = 1/f$ and $f = 1/T$.
- **Wavefront** — a line joining points of the wave that are in step (a crest line). Wavefronts are at right angles to the direction of travel.

**The wave equation.** Speed, frequency and wavelength are linked by

$$v = f \times \lambda \qquad \text{wave speed (m/s) = frequency (Hz) × wavelength (m)}$$

It works for every wave. Sound in air travels at about **340 m/s**; light and all electromagnetic waves at **3 × 10⁸ m/s** in a vacuum (or air). For a given medium the speed is fixed, so a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength.

**The Doppler effect.** When a wave source moves **toward** you, each wave is emitted a little closer to you than the last: the waves are **bunched up**, the observed **wavelength is shorter** and the **frequency is higher**. Moving **away**, the waves are stretched: longer wavelength, lower frequency. You hear it as the pitch of a siren dropping as an ambulance passes; astronomers see it as the **red-shift** of light from receding galaxies. The source's own frequency does not change — only what the observer receives.

**All waves reflect and refract.** Reflection: a wave bounces off a barrier (an echo, a mirror). Refraction: a wave changes speed, and therefore direction, when it enters a different medium — water waves slow in shallow water, light slows in glass. Waves also **diffract** (spread out through gaps).

::: terms
- **Transverse wave**: a wave in which the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer — light, water waves.
- **Longitudinal wave**: a wave in which the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer — sound.
- **Amplitude**: the maximum displacement of a point on a wave from its rest position.
- **Wavelength (λ)**: the distance between a point on one wave and the same point on the next.
- **Frequency (f)**: the number of waves passing a point each second, in hertz (Hz).
- **Period (T)**: the time taken for one complete wave: T = 1 ÷ f.
- **Wavefront**: a line joining points on a wave that are at the same point in their cycle, such as the crests.
- **Wave equation**: wave speed = frequency × wavelength: v = f × λ.
- **Doppler effect**: the change in observed frequency and wavelength when a wave source moves towards or away from an observer.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the wave type** from what oscillates and in which direction relative to travel.
2. Read amplitude from the **middle line to the peak**; wavelength from **peak to peak**; period from a time graph, then $f = 1/T$.
3. **v = fλ**: convert kHz/MHz to Hz and cm/mm to m first. Rearrange: $f = v/\lambda$, $\lambda = v/f$.
4. Use the known speeds: sound 340 m/s, light 3 × 10⁸ m/s, unless the question gives others.
5. Doppler: decide **toward** (higher f, shorter λ) or **away** (lower f, longer λ) and state both changes.
:::

::: example Using the wave equation
A sound wave has a frequency of 500 Hz. Taking the speed of sound in air as 340 m/s, calculate its wavelength. (2)

$\lambda = v/f = 340 \div 500$ (1) = **0.68 m** (1).
:::

::: example Radio waves
A radio station broadcasts at 98 MHz. Calculate the wavelength of the radio waves. (3)

$f = 98 \times 10^6$ Hz (1). $\lambda = 3 \times 10^8 \div 9.8 \times 10^7$ (1) = **3.06 m** (1).
:::

::: example Frequency from period
An oscilloscope shows a sound wave with 4 complete cycles in 10 ms. Find the period and the frequency. (3)

One cycle takes 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 ms = 0.0025 s (1). $f = 1/T = 1 \div 0.0025$ (1) = **400 Hz** (1).
:::

::: example Doppler effect
Explain why the pitch of a police siren sounds higher as the car approaches and lower once it has passed. (3)

As the car approaches, each successive wave is emitted from a point nearer the listener, so the waves arrive bunched together — shorter wavelength, higher frequency, higher pitch (2). Once it has passed, the waves are stretched out — longer wavelength, lower frequency, lower pitch (1).
:::

::: example Compare — transverse and longitudinal waves
Describe the difference between a transverse and a longitudinal wave, giving one example of each. (4)

In a transverse wave the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction the wave transfers energy (1), e.g. light or a ripple on water (1).

In a longitudinal wave the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer, forming compressions and rarefactions (1), e.g. sound (1).
:::

::: q
What do waves transfer, and what do they not transfer? (2)
---
Energy (and information) (1); matter (1).
:::

::: q
State the difference between transverse and longitudinal waves and give an example of each. (4)
---
Transverse: oscillations at right angles to the direction of travel (1), e.g. light or water waves (1). Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of travel (1), e.g. sound (1).
:::

::: q
Define amplitude and wavelength. (2)
---
Amplitude: maximum displacement from the rest position (1). Wavelength: distance between two adjacent corresponding points, e.g. crest to crest (1).
:::

::: q
Define frequency and give its unit. (2)
---
The number of complete waves passing a point per second (1); hertz (1).
:::

::: q
A wave has a period of 0.02 s. Calculate its frequency. (2)
---
f = 1 ÷ 0.02 (1) = 50 Hz (1).
:::

::: q
Write the wave equation. (1)
---
v = f × λ.
:::

::: q
Water waves of wavelength 0.5 m pass a post at 4 waves per second. Find their speed. (2)
---
v = 4 × 0.5 (1) = 2 m/s (1).
:::

::: q
Light of wavelength 6 × 10⁻⁷ m travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Calculate its frequency. (2)
---
f = 3 × 10⁸ ÷ 6 × 10⁻⁷ (1) = 5 × 10¹⁴ Hz (1).
:::

::: q
What is a compression in a sound wave? (1)
---
A region where the air particles are pushed closer together (higher pressure).
:::

::: q
A star is moving away from Earth. State what happens to the observed wavelength and frequency of its light. (2)
---
Wavelength increases (red-shift) (1); frequency decreases (1).
:::

::: watch
- Amplitude is measured from the **rest position**, not crest to trough — halve it if you measured the full height.
- **Convert prefixes** before v = fλ: kHz, MHz, cm, mm all trip people up.
- Frequency is set by the **source**; when a wave enters a new medium its speed and wavelength change, but **f stays the same**.
- Doppler: give **both** the wavelength and frequency changes and the direction of motion.
- "Waves transfer energy **without transferring matter**" — the second half is the mark.
:::

::: test
A wave has a frequency of 250 Hz and a wavelength of 1.36 m. Calculate its speed. (2)
---
$v = f\lambda = 250 \times 1.36$ (1) $= 340$ m/s (1).
:::

::: test
Radio waves of frequency 100 MHz travel at $3 \times 10^8$ m/s. Calculate their wavelength. (2)
---
$\lambda = v \div f = 3 \times 10^8 \div 10^8$ (1) $= 3$ m (1).
:::

::: test
A wave passes a point 20 times in 4 seconds. Calculate its frequency and its period. (2)
---
$f = 20 \div 4 = 5$ Hz (1); $T = 1 \div f = 0.2$ s (1).
:::

::: test
State one example of a transverse wave and one of a longitudinal wave, and the direction of vibration in each. (2)
---
Transverse, e.g. light or water waves: vibrations at right angles to the direction of travel (1). Longitudinal, e.g. sound: vibrations along the direction of travel (1).
:::

::: test
Describe what happens to the wavelength, frequency and speed of water waves as they pass from deep water into shallow water. (3)
---
Speed decreases (1); wavelength decreases (1); frequency stays the same (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what diffraction is and when it is greatest. (2)
---
Waves spreading out as they pass through a gap or around an edge (1); greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength (1).
:::

::: test
A ripple tank is used to measure wave speed. Describe how you could find the wavelength and the frequency. (3)
---
Wavelength: measure the distance across several wavefronts (e.g. 10) with a rule and divide (1). Frequency: count the waves passing a point in a set time, or read the frequency of the vibrating motor / use a strobe (1). Then $v = f\lambda$ (1).
:::

::: test
Describe the Doppler effect for a source of sound moving toward an observer. (2)
---
The waves ahead of the source are squashed together, so the wavelength is shorter (1) and the observed frequency (pitch) is higher (1).
:::
