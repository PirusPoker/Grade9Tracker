## What it is

A **magnet** has two **poles**, north and south. **Like poles repel, unlike poles attract** — and repulsion is the only sure test that something is a magnet, because a magnet attracts *any* magnetic material, magnetised or not. The magnetic materials are **iron, steel, nickel and cobalt**; copper, aluminium and plastics are not magnetic.

- **Magnetically hard** materials (e.g. **steel**) are hard to magnetise but **keep** their magnetism: used for **permanent magnets**.
- **Magnetically soft** materials (e.g. **pure iron**) magnetise easily and **lose** their magnetism as soon as the field is removed: used for **electromagnet cores** and transformer cores, where the magnetism must switch on and off.

**Induced magnetism.** A magnetic material placed in a magnetic field becomes a magnet itself, with the pole nearest the permanent magnet **opposite** to it — which is why an unmagnetised nail is attracted, never repelled. Soft iron loses this induced magnetism when the magnet is removed; steel keeps some.

**Magnetic fields** are the regions around a magnet where a force acts on magnetic materials or other magnets. We draw them with **field lines**: they run **from north to south** outside the magnet, **never cross**, and are **closer together where the field is stronger** (at the poles). The direction of a field line is the direction a north pole would be pushed.

**Practical: field patterns.** Place a bar magnet under a sheet of paper and sprinkle **iron filings**, tapping gently — they line up along the field lines. For direction, use a small **plotting compass**: put it near one pole, mark dots at each end of the needle, move the compass so the tail sits on the previous head dot, and join the dots. Between two magnets: **unlike poles facing** give lines running straight across between them — a roughly **uniform field** in the middle (evenly spaced parallel lines, same strength everywhere); **like poles facing** give lines that bend away with a neutral point between them.

**Electromagnetism.** A wire carrying a **current has a magnetic field** around it: concentric circles centred on the wire, stronger near the wire and with larger current, direction given by the **right-hand grip rule** (thumb along the current, fingers curl in the field direction). A **flat coil** has a field like a set of concentric loops through its centre. A **solenoid** (long coil) has a field **like a bar magnet's** — nearly uniform and strong inside, with a north and south end (grip the coil with fingers following the current: the thumb points to the north pole). Reversing the current reverses the poles.

**Building an electromagnet**: wind insulated wire round a **soft iron core** and pass a current. Its strength is increased by a **larger current**, **more turns** on the coil, and the **iron core** (which becomes an induced magnet). Its great advantage over a permanent magnet is that it can be **switched off** and its strength controlled — scrapyard cranes, electric bells, relays, circuit breakers, MRI scanners.

::: terms
- **Magnetic poles**: the ends of a magnet, north and south, where the field is strongest.
- **Magnetic field**: the region around a magnet where a magnetic material experiences a force.
- **Field lines**: lines showing the direction of the field, from north to south; closer lines mean a stronger field.
- **Magnetically hard material**: a material such as steel that is hard to magnetise but keeps its magnetism — used for permanent magnets.
- **Magnetically soft material**: a material such as iron that is easily magnetised and loses its magnetism easily — used in electromagnets.
- **Induced magnetism**: magnetism acquired by a magnetic material placed in a magnetic field.
- **Uniform field**: a field of equal strength and direction everywhere, shown by parallel, equally spaced field lines.
- **Solenoid**: a long coil of wire; with a current, it has a field like a bar magnet's, uniform inside.
- **Electromagnet**: a solenoid with a soft iron core, magnetic only when current flows.
:::

::: method
1. **Attraction proves nothing; repulsion proves a magnet.**
2. Hard vs soft: **steel keeps** (permanent magnets), **iron loses** (electromagnets, transformer cores). Give the use with the property.
3. Field diagrams: arrows N → S, lines never cross, closer = stronger, uniform = parallel and evenly spaced.
4. Electromagnet strength: **current, turns, core** — the three levers, and "reverse current → reverse poles".
5. Field shapes: wire → concentric circles; solenoid → bar-magnet pattern with a uniform interior.
:::

::: example Testing for a magnet
A student has two steel bars; one is a magnet and one is not. Describe how to tell which, using only the bars. (3)

Bring one end of bar A near an end of bar B, then turn A round and bring the other end near (1). If one of A's ends **repels** B, A is the magnet, because only a magnet can repel (1); if both ends attract, B is the magnet and A is just a magnetic material being induced (1).
:::

::: example Choosing a core material
Explain why an electromagnet's core is made of soft iron rather than steel. (3)

Soft iron is magnetically soft: it magnetises easily, strengthening the field while the current flows (1), and loses its magnetism as soon as the current stops, so the electromagnet switches off (1). Steel is magnetically hard and would stay magnetised, so the magnet could not be turned off (1).
:::

::: example Plotting a field
Describe how to plot the magnetic field pattern of a bar magnet with a plotting compass. (3)

Place the magnet on paper and draw round it; put the compass near one pole and mark dots at the two ends of the needle (1); move the compass so its tail end is on the dot previously marked by its head, and mark again, repeating until the other pole is reached (1); join the dots to give one field line, and repeat from other starting points — the arrows point from N to S (1).
:::

::: example Making an electromagnet stronger
State three ways to increase the strength of an electromagnet. (3)

Increase the current in the coil (1); increase the number of turns (1); use a soft iron core (or a thicker core) (1).
:::

::: example Describe — the field of a solenoid
Describe the magnetic field produced by a current in a solenoid, and how it could be made stronger. (4)

Outside the solenoid the field is like that of a bar magnet, with a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other (1). Inside, the field lines are parallel and evenly spaced — a strong, uniform field (1).

It can be made stronger by increasing the current (1), adding more turns of wire, or putting a soft iron core inside (1).
:::

::: q
State the rule for forces between magnetic poles. (1)
---
Like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
:::

::: q
Name three magnetic materials. (3)
---
Iron (1), steel (1), nickel or cobalt (1).
:::

::: q
What is the difference between a magnetically hard and a magnetically soft material? Give an example of each. (4)
---
Hard: difficult to magnetise but keeps its magnetism (1), e.g. steel (1). Soft: easy to magnetise but loses it quickly (1), e.g. iron (1).
:::

::: q
What is induced magnetism? (2)
---
A magnetic material becomes magnetised when placed in a magnetic field (1), with the pole nearest the magnet opposite to it, so it is attracted (1).
:::

::: q
In which direction do magnetic field lines point? (1)
---
From north to south (outside the magnet).
:::

::: q
How can you tell from a field diagram where the field is strongest? (1)
---
Where the lines are closest together.
:::

::: q
Describe the field between the north pole of one magnet and the south pole of another facing it. (2)
---
Field lines run straight from N to S (1), parallel and evenly spaced: a uniform field (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the shape of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire. (2)
---
Concentric circles centred on the wire (1), closer together (stronger) nearer the wire (1).
:::

::: q
How is the field of a solenoid similar to that of a bar magnet? (1)
---
It has the same shape, with a north and south pole at the ends (and is nearly uniform inside).
:::

::: q
Give two advantages of an electromagnet over a permanent magnet. (2)
---
It can be switched on and off (1); its strength can be varied by changing the current (or its poles reversed) (1).
:::

::: watch
- Only **repulsion** is proof of a magnet — attraction happens with any magnetic material.
- Steel = permanent magnet; iron = electromagnet core. Get the pair the right way round and say **why**.
- Field lines **never cross**, go **N → S**, and are **closer where stronger** — the three things a diagram is marked on.
- Electromagnet strength: **current, turns, iron core** — all three, not "more electricity".
- Reversing the current **reverses the poles**; switching it off makes the (soft-iron) field disappear.
:::
