## What it is

No device transfers all the energy it takes in to the form we want. **Efficiency** measures the fraction that is useful:

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful energy output}}{\text{total energy input}}$$

It is a ratio, so it has **no units**; multiply by 100 to give a **percentage**. Efficiency can **never exceed 1 (100%)** — that would mean creating energy — and in practice is always less because some energy is always dissipated as heat (and sometimes sound). The same equation works with **power** (useful power output ÷ total power input) because power is energy per second.

Typical values: a filament lamp ≈ 5–10% (the rest is heat), an LED ≈ 40–50%, an electric motor ≈ 80–90%, a petrol engine ≈ 25–30%, a large power station ≈ 35–45%, an electric kettle ≈ 90%.

**Sankey diagrams** show energy transfers as arrows whose **widths are proportional to the energy** (or power). The input arrow enters on the left; it splits into the **useful** output(s), which continue to the right, and the **wasted** outputs, which peel off (usually downward). The widths must add up: input = useful + wasted. Drawing one to scale (e.g. 1 mm per joule) and labelling each arrow with its value lets you read the efficiency straight from the diagram.

**Reading and using them.** The useful energy is whichever output the device is *for*: light from a lamp, kinetic energy from a motor, thermal energy in the water from a kettle. Heat from a lamp is wasted; heat from a heater is useful. For a chain of devices (power station → cables → appliance) multiply the efficiencies.

::: terms
- **Efficiency**: useful energy output ÷ total energy input (× 100 for a percentage).
- **Useful energy**: the energy transferred to the store we want.
- **Wasted energy**: energy transferred to stores we do not want, usually heating the surroundings.
- **Sankey diagram**: a diagram showing energy transfers, with the width of each arrow proportional to the amount of energy.
:::

::: method
1. Identify the **useful** output for that device and the **total** input. Everything else is wasted.
2. Efficiency = useful ÷ total. As a decimal (0–1) or percentage (0–100%); never above 1.
3. Rearrange: useful = efficiency × total; total = useful ÷ efficiency; wasted = total − useful.
4. **Sankey**: draw the input arrow full width; split it so widths are in proportion; label every arrow with its value and the type of energy; wasted arrows point down.
5. Check: outputs **add up to the input** (conservation of energy).
:::

::: example Efficiency of a motor
A motor takes in 500 J of energy and does 400 J of useful work lifting a load. Calculate its efficiency and the energy wasted. (3)

Efficiency = 400 ÷ 500 (1) = **0.8 (80%)** (1). Wasted = 500 − 400 = **100 J**, dissipated as heat and sound (1).
:::

::: example Using efficiency to find input
An LED lamp is 40% efficient and gives out 6 W of light. What electrical power does it draw? (2)

Total = useful ÷ efficiency = 6 ÷ 0.4 (1) = **15 W** (1).
:::

::: example Reading a Sankey diagram
A Sankey diagram for a car engine shows an input of 2000 J, an output of 500 J as kinetic energy, 1200 J as heat to the exhaust and cooling system, and the rest as sound and friction. Find the efficiency and the missing energy. (3)

Missing = 2000 − 500 − 1200 = **300 J** (1). Efficiency = 500 ÷ 2000 (1) = **0.25 (25%)** (1).
:::

::: example Drawing a Sankey diagram
A kettle uses 100 kJ of electrical energy; 90 kJ heats the water. Describe how to draw its Sankey diagram to scale. (3)

Draw an input arrow 100 mm wide labelled "electrical energy in, 100 kJ" (1); continue it to the right as a 90 mm wide arrow labelled "thermal energy in water, 90 kJ" (useful) (1); branch a 10 mm wide arrow downward labelled "thermal energy to surroundings, 10 kJ" (wasted), so the widths add to 100 mm (1).
:::

::: q
Write the equation for efficiency. (1)
---
Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input.
:::

::: q
Why can no device be 100% efficient? (1)
---
Some energy is always dissipated (as heat, and sometimes sound) to the surroundings.
:::

::: q
A lamp transfers 60 J of electrical energy, of which 3 J becomes light. Calculate its efficiency as a percentage. (2)
---
3 ÷ 60 = 0.05 (1) = 5% (1).
:::

::: q
A generator is 90% efficient and produces 45 kW of electrical power. What is the input power? (2)
---
45 ÷ 0.9 (1) = 50 kW (1).
:::

::: q
What does the width of an arrow on a Sankey diagram represent? (1)
---
The amount of energy (or power) — widths are in proportion to the energy.
:::

::: q
On a Sankey diagram, which direction do the wasted-energy arrows usually take? (1)
---
They branch off downward (or away) from the main arrow.
:::

::: q
A heater takes in 2000 J and transfers 1900 J to the room by heating and 100 J by sound. Is the heat useful or wasted? What is the efficiency? (2)
---
Useful — heating the room is the heater's purpose (1); 1900 ÷ 2000 = 0.95 (95%) (1).
:::

::: q
Give one reason an LED is more efficient than a filament lamp. (1)
---
It transfers far less energy by heating (a filament must get white-hot to glow).
:::

::: q
A Sankey diagram has an input of 800 J and a useful output arrow of 200 J. What total width should the wasted arrows have, if 1 mm represents 10 J? (2)
---
Wasted = 600 J (1) = 60 mm (1).
:::

::: q
A student calculates an efficiency of 1.3. Explain why this must be wrong. (1)
---
It would mean more energy out than in, breaking conservation of energy; efficiency cannot exceed 1.
:::

::: watch
- **Useful ÷ total** — the other way up gives an answer over 1, which is impossible.
- Decide what is *useful* for **that** device: heat is useful from a heater but wasted from a lamp.
- Efficiency has **no unit**; write 0.8 or 80%, not 0.8 J.
- Sankey widths must **add up**: input = useful + wasted. Label every arrow with its energy and type.
- When both the input and outputs are given in the question, use the numbers given — do not re-estimate.
:::

::: test
A car engine has an efficiency of 30%. Explain where the other 70% of the energy goes. (2)
---
Transferred to the thermal store of the engine, exhaust gases and surroundings (1) — by heating, with some as sound; it is dissipated, not destroyed (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a Sankey diagram's arrows must add up on both sides. (1)
---
Energy is conserved — the input equals useful output plus wasted output.
:::

::: test

---

:::

::: test

Lubricate the moving parts (1); less friction, so less energy is transferred to thermal stores (1).

::: test
A television takes in 150 J each second. It gives out 45 J of light, 30 J of sound and the rest as thermal energy. Calculate the wasted energy and the efficiency. (3)
---
