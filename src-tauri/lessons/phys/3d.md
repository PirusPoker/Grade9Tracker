## What it is

How much a material bends light is measured by its **refractive index, n**:

$$n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}$$

where *i* is the angle of incidence in air and *r* the angle of refraction in the material, both measured from the normal. It is a ratio, so it has **no units**. Typical values: water 1.33, glass about 1.5, diamond 2.4. A bigger *n* means a slower speed of light in the material and more bending. (*n* is also the ratio of the speed of light in air to its speed in the material.)

**Practical: refractive index of glass.** Place a rectangular glass block on paper and draw round it. Use a ray box to send a narrow ray into one face at an angle; mark the incident and emergent rays with two dots each. Remove the block, join the dots and draw the refracted ray inside the block between the entry and exit points. Draw the normal at the entry point and measure *i* and *r* with a protractor. Repeat for five or six angles of incidence (say 20° to 70°) and either calculate *n* for each and average, or plot **sin i against sin r** — the gradient is *n*. Use a dim room, a thin ray, well-spaced dots and a sharp pencil; avoid very small angles where a 1° error is a large fraction.

**Total internal reflection (TIR).** When light travels from a **denser to a less dense** medium (glass → air) it bends away from the normal. Increase the angle of incidence and the refracted ray bends closer to the surface; at the **critical angle, c**, it skims along the surface (angle of refraction 90°). Beyond *c* there is no refracted ray at all — **all** the light is reflected back inside: total internal reflection. Two conditions: light must be going from the denser medium toward the less dense one, and the angle of incidence must be **greater than the critical angle**.

$$\sin c = \frac{1}{n}$$

For glass (*n* = 1.5), sin c = 0.667 so c ≈ 42°; for water c ≈ 49°; for diamond c ≈ 24°, which is why a cut diamond traps and sparkles with light.

**Uses of TIR.**

- **Optical fibres**: a thin glass core carries light (or infrared) by repeated total internal reflection along its length — the light always hits the wall at more than the critical angle. Used for high-speed **communications** (broadband, telephone), and in **endoscopes** to see inside the body (one bundle carries light in, another carries the image out).
- **Prisms**: a 45° right-angled glass prism reflects light through 90° or 180° by TIR, with less loss and no silvering compared with a mirror. Used in **periscopes**, **binoculars** (to fold the light path), bicycle reflectors and cats' eyes.

::: terms
- **Refractive index (n)**: sin i ÷ sin r for light entering a material from air.
- **Critical angle (c)**: the angle of incidence inside a denser medium at which the refracted ray travels along the boundary (angle of refraction 90°).
- **Critical angle equation**: sin c = 1 ÷ n.
- **Total internal reflection**: when light in a denser medium meets the boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle, all of it is reflected.
- **Optical fibre**: a thin glass fibre that carries light by repeated total internal reflection.
:::

::: method
1. **n = sin i / sin r** with both angles from the normal, *i* in air. Rearrange: $\sin r = \sin i / n$, then use inverse sin.
2. **sin c = 1/n**: $c = \sin^{-1}(1/n)$. Or, given *c*, $n = 1/\sin c$.
3. **Will TIR happen?** Check the direction (dense → less dense) and compare *i* with *c*: *i* > *c* → TIR; *i* < *c* → refracts out (with some partial reflection).
4. Explain optical fibres with: light enters, hits the wall at an angle greater than the critical angle, is totally internally reflected, repeats along the fibre.
5. Set the calculator to **degrees**.
:::

::: example Calculating refractive index
Light enters a glass block at an angle of incidence of 50° and is refracted at 30°. Calculate the refractive index. (2)

$n = \sin 50° / \sin 30° = 0.766 \div 0.500$ (1) = **1.53** (1).
:::

::: example Finding the angle of refraction
A ray hits water (n = 1.33) at 60° to the normal. Calculate the angle of refraction. (3)

$\sin r = \sin 60° / 1.33 = 0.866 \div 1.33 = 0.651$ (2). $r = \sin^{-1}(0.651) =$ **40.6°** (1).
:::

::: example Critical angle
Calculate the critical angle for a glass with refractive index 1.6, and state what happens to a ray inside the glass that meets the surface at 45°. (3)

$\sin c = 1/1.6 = 0.625$ (1), so c = **38.7°** (1). 45° is greater than the critical angle, so the ray is totally internally reflected (1).
:::

::: example Optical fibres
Explain how light travels along an optical fibre and give one advantage of optical fibres for telephone signals. (3)

Light entering the fibre strikes the glass-air (or core-cladding) boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle (1), so it is totally internally reflected, and this repeats all along the fibre even round bends (1). Advantages: very high data capacity / little signal loss over long distances / no electrical interference (1).
:::

Why does refractive index have no unit? (1)
---
It is a ratio of two sines (or two speeds).

::: q
A ray in air strikes glass at 40° and refracts at 25°. Find n. (2)
---
sin 40 ÷ sin 25 = 0.643 ÷ 0.423 (1) = 1.52 (1).
:::

::: q
Define the critical angle. (2)
---
The angle of incidence in the denser medium (1) for which the angle of refraction is 90° (the refracted ray runs along the surface) (1).
:::

::: q
State the two conditions for total internal reflection. (2)
---
Light travelling from a denser to a less dense medium (1); angle of incidence greater than the critical angle (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the critical angle for water, n = 1.33. (2)
---
sin c = 1 ÷ 1.33 = 0.752 (1); c = 48.8° (1).
:::

::: q
A material has a critical angle of 30°. What is its refractive index? (2)
---
n = 1 ÷ sin 30 = 1 ÷ 0.5 (1) = 2.0 (1).
:::

::: q
Give two uses of total internal reflection. (2)
---
Optical fibres (communications/endoscopes) (1); prisms in periscopes/binoculars/reflectors (1).
:::

::: q
In the refractive index practical, why is it better to plot sin i against sin r than to use one pair of readings? (2)
---
The gradient averages out random errors across all readings (1), and anomalous points can be spotted and ignored (1).
:::

::: q
Why is a prism preferred to a mirror in good-quality binoculars? (1)
---
TIR reflects nearly all the light with no loss or double image from a silvered surface (and prisms do not tarnish).
:::

::: watch
- Both angles from the **normal**, and *i* is in the **less dense** medium (air) for n = sin i / sin r.
- **Degrees mode.** A calculator in radians gives nonsense with no warning.
- TIR needs **both** conditions; "the angle is big" alone is not enough — say "greater than the critical angle" and "from glass to air".
- sin c = **1/n**, not n. If your critical angle comes out over 90° you have inverted it.
- "Total" internal reflection: below the critical angle some light *still* reflects, but partially — the word *total* matters.
:::
