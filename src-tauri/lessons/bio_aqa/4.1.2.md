## What it is

How cells copy themselves (the cell cycle and mitosis) and how unspecialised stem cells can become any of several cell types. Everything in this topic is the same for AQA Biology 8461 and Combined Science Trilogy 8464, and there is no Higher-tier-only content.

### Chromosomes

- The **nucleus** contains **chromosomes**, each made of one long **DNA** molecule.
- Each chromosome carries a **large number of genes**. A gene is a short section of DNA that codes for a particular protein.
- In body cells chromosomes are normally found in **pairs**: one of each pair came from the mother, one from the father. Human body cells have **46 chromosomes in 23 pairs**. Gametes (sperm and egg) have only one of each pair: 23.

### The cell cycle

Cells divide in a series of stages called the **cell cycle**. During it the genetic material is **doubled** and then **divided** into **two identical cells**. AQA expects the three overall stages (you do **not** need the named phases of mitosis such as prophase):

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Growth and DNA replication** (the longest stage) | The cell grows and increases the number of sub-cellular structures such as **ribosomes** and **mitochondria**. The **DNA replicates** so each chromosome becomes two identical copies (two chromatids joined together). |
| **2. Mitosis** | One set of chromosomes is **pulled to each end** of the cell, and the **nucleus divides**. |
| **3. Division of cytoplasm and membrane** (cytokinesis) | The **cytoplasm and cell membrane divide** to form **two identical daughter cells**. |

Why the extra ribosomes and mitochondria? Each new cell needs its own: ribosomes to make proteins and mitochondria to release energy. Why replicate DNA? So each daughter cell gets a complete, identical set of chromosomes.

The two daughter cells are **genetically identical** to each other and to the parent cell, and have the **same number of chromosomes** (46 in humans).

**Where mitosis happens** (you may have to recognise it in a given context):
- **growth** and **development** of multicellular organisms, e.g. an embryo growing, a child growing taller;
- **repair** of damaged tissue, e.g. a cut healing, a broken bone mending;
- **replacement** of worn-out cells, e.g. skin cells, cells lining the gut, red blood cells made in bone marrow;
- **asexual reproduction**, e.g. plant runners, cuttings.

Mitosis does **not** make gametes: those are made by meiosis (topic 4.6).

**Using cell counts (MS 1c).** In a tissue such as an onion root tip, the fraction of cells seen in mitosis is roughly the fraction of the cycle spent in mitosis:

$$\text{time in mitosis} = \dfrac{\text{cells in mitosis}}{\text{total cells counted}} \times \text{length of cell cycle}$$

*Mini-example*: 30 of 600 cells are in mitosis and the cycle lasts 20 hours. $\dfrac{30}{600} \times 20 = 1$ hour (60 minutes).

A useful model: think of DNA replication as photocopying a recipe book (every page copied), and mitosis as sharing the two copies out so each kitchen gets a complete book.

### Stem cells

A **stem cell** is an **undifferentiated** cell of an organism that can give rise to many more cells of the same type, and from which certain other cells can arise by **differentiation**.

| Source | What it can become | Function in the organism |
|---|---|---|
| **Embryonic stem cells** (human embryos) | can be cloned and made to differentiate into **most** types of human cell | form all the tissues of the developing embryo |
| **Adult stem cells** (e.g. **bone marrow**) | **many** types, including **blood cells** (fewer types than embryonic) | **repair and replace** cells, e.g. making new blood cells |
| **Meristems** (tips of roots and shoots in plants) | **any** type of plant cell, **throughout the life** of the plant | growth of roots and shoots |

You do **not** need to know the laboratory techniques for growing stem cells.

**Medical uses.** Treatment with stem cells may be able to help conditions such as **diabetes** (making new insulin-secreting cells) and **paralysis** (making new nerve cells).

**Therapeutic cloning**: an **embryo is produced with the same genes as the patient** (the nucleus of one of the patient's body cells is placed into an egg cell with its nucleus removed). Stem cells from this embryo are genetically identical to the patient, so they are **not rejected** by the patient's immune system and could be used for medical treatment.

**Risks and issues (WS 1.3: evaluate risks, benefits, social and ethical issues):**

| Benefits | Risks and objections |
|---|---|
| could treat or cure diseases with no current cure (diabetes, paralysis) | **transfer of viral infection** from the culture or donor to the patient |
| therapeutic cloning avoids rejection, so no lifelong immunosuppressant drugs | cells may divide uncontrollably and form **tumours** |
| could reduce waiting lists for donor organs | **ethical or religious objections**: an embryo is destroyed, and some people believe it has a right to life |
| embryos used are often spare ones from IVF that would be destroyed anyway | treatments are still largely experimental and very expensive |

**Plant meristems and cloning.** Stem cells from meristems can be used to produce **clones** of plants **quickly and economically**:
- **rare species** can be cloned to **protect them from extinction**;
- **crop plants with special features** such as **disease resistance** can be cloned to produce **large numbers of identical plants** for farmers.

The drawback of cloned crops is that all plants are genetically identical, so a new disease or a change in conditions could affect every plant.

::: terms
- **Chromosome**: a structure in the nucleus made of a long DNA molecule carrying a large number of genes.
- **Gene**: a small section of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a particular protein.
- **Cell cycle**: the series of stages in which a cell grows, copies its DNA and divides into two identical cells.
- **Mitosis**: the stage of the cell cycle in which one set of chromosomes is pulled to each end of the cell and the nucleus divides.
- **DNA replication**: copying of DNA before division so that each chromosome becomes two identical copies.
- **Stem cell**: an undifferentiated cell that can give rise to many more cells of the same type and can differentiate into other cell types.
- **Meristem**: plant tissue at root and shoot tips containing stem cells that can become any type of plant cell throughout life.
- **Therapeutic cloning**: producing an embryo with the same genes as a patient so its stem cells are not rejected when used in treatment.
:::

::: method
**Command words.** *Describe the cell cycle* = the three stages in order, with what happens in each. *Explain why* (e.g. the DNA is copied) = give the purpose ("so that each daughter cell receives a full set of chromosomes"). *Suggest* = use the context, e.g. "a cut on the skin heals" → mitosis for repair. *Evaluate* = benefits **and** risks, then a justified conclusion.

**Cell-cycle answers.** Use the three AQA stages: (1) the cell grows, makes more ribosomes and mitochondria, and the DNA replicates; (2) mitosis: one set of chromosomes pulled to each end, the nucleus divides; (3) the cytoplasm and cell membrane divide. Finish with "two genetically identical cells". Do not invent phase names; you will not get marks for them and they cost time.

**Recognising mitosis.** Growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction all use mitosis. Making sperm or eggs does not (that is meiosis). In an unfamiliar context, look for "identical cells", "growth" or "repair".

**Stem cell 6-markers.** Levels of response: Level 3 needs points **for and against**, linked to reasons, with a conclusion. Plan a three-part answer: benefits (treat diabetes and paralysis, no rejection with therapeutic cloning, uses spare IVF embryos); risks and ethics (viral infection, tumours, destruction of embryos, religious objections, cost); judgement ("on balance... because..."). Compare embryonic with adult stem cells when asked: embryonic → most cell types but ethical issues; adult → fewer types but no embryo destroyed.

**Calculations.** Cell-count questions: fraction of cells in mitosis × total cycle time = time in mitosis. Convert hours to minutes if asked (× 60). Show the fraction first, then the multiplication, then the unit.

**Data on cell number or DNA mass.** On a graph of DNA per cell against time, DNA doubles during the growth stage (replication) and halves when the cell divides. Describe the trend, quote values from the graph, then explain it.
:::

::: example Describe: the cell cycle
Describe what happens during the cell cycle. (4)

The cell grows and increases its number of sub-cellular structures such as ribosomes and mitochondria (1). The DNA replicates to form two copies of each chromosome (1). In mitosis, one set of chromosomes is pulled to each end of the cell and the nucleus divides (1). The cytoplasm and cell membrane then divide to form two genetically identical cells (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: time in mitosis
A student counted 500 cells in an onion root tip. 40 were in mitosis. The cell cycle takes 16 hours. Calculate the time spent in mitosis in minutes. (3)

Fraction in mitosis = 40 ÷ 500 = 0.08 (1).
Time = 0.08 × 16 = 1.28 hours (1).
1.28 × 60 = **76.8 minutes** (1).
:::

::: example Explain: therapeutic cloning
Explain why stem cells produced by therapeutic cloning are less likely to be rejected than stem cells from a donor embryo. (3)

In therapeutic cloning the embryo is produced with the same genes as the patient (1), so the stem cells are genetically identical to the patient's own cells (1). The patient's immune system does not recognise them as foreign, so they are not attacked and rejected (1).
:::

::: example Compare: sources of stem cells
Compare embryonic stem cells with adult stem cells from bone marrow. (3)

Embryonic stem cells can differentiate into most types of human cell, whereas adult bone marrow stem cells can form only some types, such as blood cells (1). Collecting embryonic stem cells destroys an embryo, which raises ethical and religious objections, whereas adult stem cells can be taken from a consenting donor without this objection (1). Both carry a risk of transferring viral infection, and donor cells from either source may be rejected unless they match the patient (1).
:::

Any two: growth (e.g. of a child or embryo) (1); repair of damaged tissue such as a cut (1); replacement of worn-out cells such as skin or blood cells (1).

Give two reasons why some people object to using embryonic stem cells. (2)

::: watch
- Use the **three AQA stages**: growth and DNA replication, mitosis (nucleus divides), division of cytoplasm and membrane. Phase names (prophase etc.) earn nothing here.
- Mitosis produces **two genetically identical cells** with the **same number** of chromosomes; it does **not** make gametes.
- Write "the **DNA replicates**", not "the chromosomes split", for the copying step.
- Stem cells are **undifferentiated**; say what each source can become: embryonic → most cell types, adult bone marrow → blood cells and some others, meristem → any plant cell.
- "Evaluate" questions need **both sides** and a **conclusion**; a list of benefits alone caps you at the middle level.
- Therapeutic cloning works because the embryo has the **patient's genes**, so the cells are **not rejected**.

::: test
A scientist measured the mass of DNA in a cell over time. It rose from 6 pg to 12 pg, then fell to 6 pg. Explain these changes. (3)
---
The DNA replicated before division, so each chromosome was copied, doubling the mass from 6 to 12 pg (1). During mitosis one set of chromosomes went to each end and the cell divided (1), so each new cell had half the doubled DNA: back to 6 pg, the same as the original cell (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a tadpole's tail cells can be repaired, but a damaged spinal cord in an adult human is very difficult to repair. Use ideas about differentiation. (3)
---
Most animal cells differentiate at an early stage and then lose the ability to become other types of cell (1). In adult humans, nerve cells are specialised and very few stem cells remain that can become nerve cells (1), so damaged nerve tissue cannot easily be replaced; stem cell treatments for paralysis aim to provide new nerve cells (1).
:::

::: test
40 of 800 cells in a root tip were in mitosis. The cell cycle takes 22 hours. Calculate the time spent in mitosis in minutes. (3)
---
40 ÷ 800 = 0.05 (1); 0.05 × 22 = 1.1 hours (1); 1.1 × 60 = 66 minutes (1).
:::

::: test
A farmer wants a large number of a disease-resistant variety of crop. Explain why cloning from meristems is useful, and give one risk. (4)
---
Meristem cells can differentiate into any type of plant cell (1), so many new plants can be grown quickly and cheaply (1); all are genetically identical, so they all have the disease resistance (1). Risk: no genetic variation, so a new disease or change in conditions could wipe out the whole crop (1).
:::

::: test
Suggest why a person treated with stem cells from an unrelated donor may need to take drugs for the rest of their life. (2)
---
The donor cells have different genes / antigens, so the patient's immune system recognises them as foreign and would reject them (1); drugs that suppress the immune system are needed to prevent rejection (1).
:::

::: test
Evaluate the use of human embryonic stem cells, including stem cells from therapeutic cloning, to treat conditions such as diabetes and paralysis. (6)
---
Indicative content. Benefits: embryonic stem cells can be cloned and made to differentiate into most types of human cell; could produce insulin-secreting cells for diabetes or nerve cells for paralysis; could cure rather than just treat; therapeutic cloning gives an embryo with the patient's genes, so cells are not rejected and no immunosuppressant drugs are needed; spare IVF embryos would otherwise be destroyed. Risks and issues: viral infection could be transferred to the patient; cells could divide uncontrollably and form tumours; embryo destroyed, ethical and religious objections; embryos cannot consent; treatments are experimental, costly and may not work; adult stem cells are an alternative with fewer ethical issues but can form fewer cell types. Conclusion: a justified judgement, e.g. worthwhile if strictly regulated because the potential benefits are large, or not acceptable because of ethical objections.
Level 3 (5–6): benefits and risks/ethical issues, each explained, with a reasoned conclusion.
Level 2 (3–4): some benefits and some risks with limited explanation, or one side well explained.
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements of a benefit or an objection.
0: nothing relevant.
