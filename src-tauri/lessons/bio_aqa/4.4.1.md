## What it is

**Photosynthesis** is the process in which plants and algae use **light** to make **glucose** from carbon dioxide and water, releasing **oxygen**. It happens in the **chloroplasts**, where the green pigment **chlorophyll** absorbs the light. Everything in this topic is in both Biology 8461 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464; the Higher-tier parts are flagged.

### The reaction

**Word equation:** carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light needed — written over the arrow)

You must **recognise the chemical symbols** $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{O}_2$ and $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$. The balanced symbol equation is:

$$6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2$$

Photosynthesis is an **endothermic reaction**: **energy is transferred from the environment to the chloroplasts by light**. That energy ends up stored in the glucose. (Respiration, 4.4.2, is the exothermic reaction that releases it again.)

### Rate of photosynthesis — the factors

| Factor | Effect on rate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Light intensity** | Rate increases as light intensity increases, until another factor limits. | Light provides the **energy** for the reaction. |
| **Carbon dioxide concentration** | Rate increases as CO₂ concentration increases, until another factor limits. | CO₂ is a **reactant** (raw material). |
| **Temperature** | Rate increases up to an **optimum**, then falls sharply. | The reactions are **controlled by enzymes**: more kinetic energy → more collisions up to the optimum; above it the enzymes' active sites change shape — they **denature**. |
| **Amount of chlorophyll** | More chlorophyll → faster rate. | More light is **absorbed**. Chlorophyll is reduced by magnesium deficiency (chlorosis), by disease such as TMV, or in white parts of variegated leaves. |

A **limiting factor** is the factor in shortest supply, which stops the rate increasing any further. On a graph of rate against one factor:
- where the line **rises**, *that* factor is limiting;
- where the line **levels off (plateaus)**, something **else** is now limiting.

**Measuring and calculating rates.** Rate is an amount of product (or reactant used) per unit time:

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\text{volume of oxygen produced}}{\text{time}}$$

Example: pondweed gives off $4.8\ \text{cm}^3$ of oxygen in 6 minutes. Rate $= 4.8 \div 6 = 0.80\ \text{cm}^3/\text{min}$. Counting bubbles: 45 bubbles in 3 minutes $= 15$ bubbles/min.

### The inverse square law and light intensity (Higher tier)

As a lamp is moved away, light spreads out, so light intensity falls with the **square** of distance — this is **inverse proportion**:

$$\text{light intensity} \propto \frac{1}{\text{distance}^2}$$

- Doubling the distance → intensity becomes $\frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4}$ of what it was.
- Tripling the distance → $\frac{1}{9}$.
- In practicals, **relative light intensity** $= \frac{1}{d^2}$ (arbitrary units). At $d = 20$ cm: $\frac{1}{20^2} = \frac{1}{400} = 0.0025$ au.

Example: a lamp is moved from 10 cm to 25 cm. New intensity $= \left(\frac{10}{25}\right)^2 = 0.16$ of the original — a fall of 84%.

### Greenhouse economics (Higher tier)

Growers can **add heat** (heaters), **light** (lamps) and **carbon dioxide** (CO₂ gas, or paraffin heaters that give both heat and CO₂) to get the **maximum rate of photosynthesis** — but each costs money. It is only worth paying for a factor if it is **the limiting factor**, and only up to the point where the extra crop is worth more than the extra cost. Adding light when CO₂ is limiting wastes money. (WS 1.4: use data to relate limiting factors to the **cost-effectiveness** of adding heat, light or CO₂.)

### Required practical activity 6 (Trilogy: required practical 5): the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis using pondweed

**Method**
1. Place a beaker of water (or a clear tank) between the lamp and the pondweed as a **heat shield**, or use an LED lamp, and check temperature with a **thermometer**.
2. Put a boiling tube containing **sodium hydrogencarbonate solution** (supplies CO₂) and a piece of pondweed (e.g. *Cabomba* or *Elodea*), cut end up, in a clamp.
3. Place the lamp at a measured distance (e.g. 10 cm) from the pondweed using a ruler. Leave for **5 minutes to acclimatise**.
4. **Count the bubbles** of oxygen released in 1 minute — or, better, collect the gas with an inverted measuring cylinder, gas syringe or capillary tube and measure the **volume**.
5. Repeat at 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm, and repeat each distance (multiple samples — WS 2.5) to calculate a mean.

| Variable | In this practical |
|---|---|
| Independent | Light intensity (distance from lamp) |
| Dependent | Rate of oxygen production (bubbles or $\text{cm}^3$ per minute) |
| Control | Temperature (heat shield, thermometer); CO₂ concentration (same sodium hydrogencarbonate solution); same piece/length of pondweed; same time; darkened room |

**Typical results:** the rate falls as the distance increases. Plotting rate against $\frac{1}{d^2}$ (light intensity) gives a line that rises and then may level off when CO₂ or temperature becomes limiting.

**Risks:** the lamp gets hot (do not touch; let it cool); water and mains electricity (keep apart, dry hands); wash hands after handling pondweed and dispose of it properly (AT 4).

**Errors and improvements:** bubbles vary in size, so counting them is inaccurate — collect and measure the **volume** instead. Other light in the room changes intensity — darken the room. The lamp heats the water — use a heat shield or LED. Some oxygen dissolves in the water, so the volume collected underestimates the true rate.

### Uses of glucose from photosynthesis

The glucose made may be:
- **used for respiration** — to release energy;
- **converted into insoluble starch for storage** (insoluble so it does not affect osmosis);
- **used to produce fat or oil for storage** (e.g. in seeds);
- **used to produce cellulose, which strengthens the cell wall**;
- **used to produce amino acids for protein synthesis** — and **to produce proteins, plants also use nitrate ions absorbed from the soil**.

Food tests (AT 8) let you show these products: **iodine** turns blue-black with starch; **Benedict's** solution, heated, goes green → yellow → brick red with glucose (a reducing sugar); **biuret** reagent turns lilac/purple with protein.

::: terms
- **Photosynthesis**: the endothermic reaction in which plants use light energy to make glucose and oxygen from carbon dioxide and water.
- **Endothermic reaction**: a reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings; in photosynthesis the energy is transferred by light.
- **Chlorophyll**: the green pigment in chloroplasts that absorbs light for photosynthesis.
- **Limiting factor**: the factor in shortest supply, which stops the rate of photosynthesis increasing further.
- **Rate of photosynthesis**: the amount of oxygen produced (or carbon dioxide used) per unit time, e.g. in cubic centimetres per minute.
- **Inverse square law**: light intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the light source.
- **Heat shield**: a beaker or tank of water between the lamp and the plant that stops the lamp warming the pondweed.
- **Sodium hydrogencarbonate**: a solution added to the water around pondweed to supply carbon dioxide so it is not limiting.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** a graph: say what happens to the rate as the factor increases, and quote values ("rate increases from 2 to 10 bubbles per minute between 10 and 40 au, then stays constant").
- **Explain** a graph: give the *reason* — light provides energy / CO₂ is a reactant / enzymes. For a plateau, say "**another factor** is now limiting" and, if you can, *name* it.
- **(Higher tier) Two- or three-factor graphs**: compare two lines at the **same** value on the x-axis. Whatever differs between those two lines is the factor that was limiting the lower one.
- **Suggest / evaluate (greenhouses)**: only the limiting factor is worth increasing; compare extra income with extra cost; mention paraffin heaters give both heat and CO₂.

**Calculations** — equation → substitute → answer with unit:
- rate $= \frac{\text{volume}}{\text{time}}$ (cm³/min, or bubbles/min);
- mean of repeats (leave out anomalies);
- (Higher tier) inverse square: $\frac{1}{d^2}$, or the factor $\left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)^2$ when a lamp moves from $d_1$ to $d_2$;
- gradient of a graph = change in y ÷ change in x.
Give sensible significant figures (usually the same as the data, 2 or 3).

**Required practical 6 (Trilogy 5)** — have ready: IV, DV, three control variables with *how* each is controlled, why the heat shield, why acclimatise, why measure volume rather than bubbles, and a risk with its control.

**6-mark answers** are usually the practical method or "explain the effect of temperature/light/CO₂". Level 3 needs a full, ordered, repeatable method (with measurements and control variables) or a fully linked explanation (factor → reason → effect on rate → plateau/limiting).
:::

::: example Rate calculation
A student collected $3.6\ \text{cm}^3$ of oxygen from pondweed in 8 minutes. Calculate the rate of photosynthesis. (2)

rate $= \frac{3.6}{8}$ (1) $= 0.45\ \text{cm}^3/\text{min}$ (1).
:::

::: example Inverse square law (Higher tier)
A lamp is moved from 15 cm to 30 cm from some pondweed. By what factor does the light intensity change? Calculate the relative light intensity at 30 cm. (3)

Distance doubles, so light intensity becomes $\frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4}$ of the original (1)(1). Relative light intensity at 30 cm $= \frac{1}{30^2} = \frac{1}{900} = 0.0011$ au (to 2 s.f.) (1).
:::

::: example Two-factor graph (Higher tier)
Look at the graph in What it is. Explain what is limiting the rate of photosynthesis on the plateau of the orange line (0.04% CO₂, 30 °C). (3)

On the plateau, increasing light intensity does not increase the rate, so light is not limiting (1). The green line (0.4% CO₂, 30 °C) has a higher rate at the same light intensity and temperature (1). The only difference is the CO₂ concentration, so carbon dioxide concentration is the limiting factor on the orange plateau (1).
:::

::: example Greenhouse economics (Higher tier)
A tomato grower's greenhouse is kept at 25 °C on bright summer days. She considers buying extra lamps. Explain why this may not be cost-effective. (3)

On bright days light intensity is high, so light is probably not the limiting factor (1). Adding more light would not increase the rate of photosynthesis, so yield would not rise (1). The cost of the lamps and electricity would not be repaid by extra crop; it would be better to add CO₂, which is more likely to be limiting (1).
:::

::: example Temperature
Explain why the rate of photosynthesis increases from 10 °C to 30 °C but falls rapidly at 45 °C. (3)

Photosynthesis is controlled by enzymes (1). As temperature rises, molecules have more kinetic energy and collide with active sites more often, so the rate increases (1). Above the optimum the enzymes denature — the active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits — and the rate falls (1).
:::

::: watch
- Light **energy** is transferred to the chloroplasts — photosynthesis is **endothermic**. Do not say plants "make energy".
- On a plateau, say "**another factor** is limiting" — and in Higher questions **name** it using the other lines on the graph.
- Temperature affects **enzymes**; light and CO₂ do not "denature". High temperature → enzymes **denature** (not "die").
- (Higher tier) Inverse **square**: doubling the distance gives **a quarter** of the light, not half.
- In the practical, **counting bubbles is inaccurate** because bubbles vary in size — the improvement is to **measure the volume** of gas.
- Starch is stored because it is **insoluble** — so it does not affect osmosis.
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A lamp is 10 cm from some pondweed. Calculate the relative light intensity, using $\frac{1}{d^2}$, at 10 cm and at 40 cm, and state how many times greater it is at 10 cm. (3)
---
At 10 cm: $\frac{1}{100} = 0.01$ au (1). At 40 cm: $\frac{1}{1600} = 0.000625$ au (1). $0.01 \div 0.000625 = 16$ times greater (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) In the experiment above, the rate of photosynthesis at 10 cm was only 4 times the rate at 40 cm, not 16 times. Suggest an explanation. (2)
---
At high light intensity (10 cm) light is no longer the limiting factor (1); another factor, such as carbon dioxide concentration or temperature, limits the rate, so the rate does not rise in proportion to light intensity (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A grower measured the yield of lettuces in a greenhouse with and without added carbon dioxide. Extra CO₂ raised the yield by 12% in winter but by 35% in summer. Explain this difference and comment on whether adding CO₂ in winter is worthwhile. (4)
---
In winter light intensity and temperature are low, so they are the limiting factors (1); adding CO₂ only increases the rate a little because CO₂ is not limiting (1). In summer light and temperature are higher, so CO₂ is the limiting factor and adding it greatly increases the rate (1). In winter adding CO₂ may not be cost-effective unless heat and light are also added — the grower should compare the cost with the value of the extra 12% yield (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how you would investigate the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis using pondweed. Include how you would make it a valid test. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- put pondweed in a boiling tube of water with sodium hydrogencarbonate (to supply CO₂)
- place a lamp at a measured distance (e.g. 10 cm) using a ruler
- leave for about 5 minutes to acclimatise
- count bubbles, or collect the gas and measure its volume, over a set time (e.g. 1 minute)
- repeat at several distances (e.g. 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 cm)
- repeat at each distance and calculate a mean
- control temperature with a heat shield (beaker of water) or LED lamp, checked with a thermometer
- keep CO₂ concentration the same (same sodium hydrogencarbonate solution)
- use the same piece of pondweed; darken the room
- calculate rate = volume ÷ time; relative light intensity = 1/d²; plot a graph.

Level 2 (3–4 marks): a method with key steps but some missing detail or only one control variable.
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple statements, e.g. "move the lamp and count bubbles".
0 marks: nothing worthy of credit.
