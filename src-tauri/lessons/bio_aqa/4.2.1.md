## What it is

Living things are built up in levels, from cells to whole organisms. This short topic gives you the vocabulary used throughout the Organisation unit (digestion, the heart and lungs, plant transport). It is the same for AQA Biology 8461 and Combined Science Trilogy 8464, with no Higher-tier-only content.

### The levels of organisation

| Level | Definition (AQA wording) | Animal example | Plant example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cell** | the basic building block of all living organisms | muscle cell, epithelial cell | palisade cell, root hair cell |
| **Tissue** | a group of cells with a **similar structure and function** | muscular tissue, glandular tissue, epithelial tissue | epidermal tissue, palisade mesophyll, xylem, phloem |
| **Organ** | an aggregation (collection) of **tissues** performing **specific functions** | stomach, heart, lungs | leaf, stem, root |
| **Organ system** | organs organised to **work together** | digestive system, circulatory system | transport system (xylem and phloem through roots, stem, leaves) |
| **Organism** | organ systems working together form an organism | a human | a sunflower |

Inside cells there is one further level: **sub-cellular structures** (organelles) such as the nucleus and mitochondria. The full sequence is:

**sub-cellular structure → cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism**

### Why organise like this?

A large multicellular organism cannot have every cell doing every job. Cells **differentiate** (topic 4.1.1) to become specialised. Specialised cells of the same type group together into a tissue, which can do one job very efficiently. Several tissues combine in an organ, so the organ can carry out a more complex function than any one tissue. Organs then cooperate in systems, so that, for example, food is both digested and absorbed, and the products are carried to every cell.

### An organ made of tissues: the stomach

| Tissue | Function in the stomach |
|---|---|
| **Muscular tissue** | contracts to churn food and mix it with digestive juices |
| **Glandular tissue** | produces digestive juices containing enzymes (protease) and hydrochloric acid |
| **Epithelial tissue** | covers the inside and outside of the stomach, lining and protecting it |

So the stomach is an organ (several tissues), and it belongs to an organ system (the digestive system), together with the mouth, oesophagus, liver, gall bladder, pancreas, small intestine and large intestine.

### Organ systems you will meet in this unit

| Organ system | Main organs | Overall job |
|---|---|---|
| **Digestive system** | salivary glands, stomach, pancreas, liver, small and large intestine | digest food into small soluble molecules and absorb them |
| **Circulatory system** | heart, blood vessels (arteries, veins, capillaries) | transport substances such as oxygen and glucose around the body |
| **Respiratory (breathing) system** | trachea, bronchi, lungs (alveoli) | gas exchange: oxygen in, carbon dioxide out |
| **Plant transport system** | roots, stem, leaves (xylem and phloem tissue) | move water, mineral ions and sugars around the plant |

Organ systems depend on each other: the digestive system provides glucose and the respiratory system provides oxygen, but the circulatory system has to carry both to cells for respiration.

### Size and scale (MS 1c)

Each level is much larger than the one before it. Typical sizes:

| Structure | Typical size | In metres |
|---|---|---|
| ribosome | 25 nm | $2.5 \times 10^{-8}$ m |
| mitochondrion | 1–2 µm | about $10^{-6}$ m |
| human cell | 10–30 µm | about $10^{-5}$ m |
| villus (tissue fold) | 1 mm | $10^{-3}$ m |
| stomach (organ) | 25 cm | $2.5 \times 10^{-1}$ m |
| digestive system (gut length) | about 8 m | about $10^{1}$ m |
| human (organism) | 1.7 m | about $10^{0}$ m |

To compare sizes, put both in the **same unit** and divide.
*Mini-example*: how many times longer is a 25 cm stomach than a 25 µm cell? 25 cm = 250 mm = 250 000 µm. 250 000 ÷ 25 = 10 000 = $10^4$, so four orders of magnitude.

You may also be asked to use ratios, fractions and percentages, e.g. "the small intestine is 6.0 m of a total gut length of 7.5 m: what percentage is this?" $6.0 \div 7.5 \times 100 = 80\,\%$.

::: terms
- **Cell**: the basic building block of all living organisms.
- **Tissue**: a group of cells with a similar structure and function.
- **Organ**: an aggregation of different tissues working together to perform specific functions.
- **Organ system**: a group of organs that work together to carry out a major function in the body.
- **Organism**: a living thing made of organ systems working together.
- **Epithelial tissue**: tissue that covers the inner and outer surfaces of organs, lining and protecting them.
- **Glandular tissue**: tissue that produces and releases substances such as enzymes and hormones.
- **Muscular tissue**: tissue that contracts to bring about movement, for example churning in the stomach.
:::

::: method
**Command words.** *Define* = give the AQA wording: tissue = "a group of cells with a similar structure and function"; organ = "an aggregation of tissues performing specific functions". *Identify* or *classify* = say which level something is at (e.g. "the heart is an organ, blood is a tissue"). *Explain* = say why each level helps, or how the tissues in an organ each contribute. *Calculate* = compare sizes in the same unit.

**Classifying levels.** Ask: is it one cell? (cell). Many similar cells doing one job? (tissue). Several different tissues forming a structure with a job? (organ). Several organs cooperating? (organ system). Typical traps: **blood is a tissue**, not an organ; the **leaf is an organ**; xylem and phloem are tissues; the **skin is an organ**.

**Describe an organ's tissues.** For the stomach: name each tissue and its job (muscular → churns, glandular → produces enzymes and acid, epithelial → lines and protects). This structure transfers to other organs: name the tissue, then its contribution.

**Ordering questions.** Put levels in order from smallest to largest: sub-cellular structure, cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism. Write them all; leaving one out loses the mark.

**Size and scale calculations.** Convert to the same unit (1 m = 1000 mm = 1 000 000 µm), divide, then state the factor or the order of magnitude (count the powers of ten). Use standard form for very large or small answers.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks).** For "explain how organisation into tissues, organs and systems helps a multicellular organism", link specialisation of cells to efficiency of tissues, then organs combining tissues, then systems cooperating, with a named example at each level. Levels of response reward a logical chain with examples.
:::

::: example Identify: levels of organisation
Classify each of the following as a cell, tissue, organ or organ system: (a) the heart; (b) a red blood cell; (c) the epithelium lining the small intestine; (d) the digestive system. (4)

(a) organ (1); (b) cell (1); (c) tissue (1); (d) organ system (1).
:::

::: example Explain: tissues in an organ
The stomach contains muscular, glandular and epithelial tissue. Explain how each tissue helps the stomach carry out its function. (3)

Muscular tissue contracts to churn the food, mixing it with digestive juices (1). Glandular tissue produces digestive juices containing protease enzymes and hydrochloric acid (1). Epithelial tissue lines the stomach, covering and protecting it (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: size and scale
A cell lining the small intestine is 20 µm long. The small intestine is 6 m long. How many of these cells, end to end, would stretch the length of the small intestine? Give your answer in standard form. (3)

6 m = 6 000 000 µm (1).
6 000 000 ÷ 20 = 300 000 (1).
= $3 \times 10^5$ cells (1).
:::

::: example Explain: why organ systems work together
Explain why the digestive system and the circulatory system must work together. (3)

The digestive system breaks food down into small soluble molecules such as glucose and absorbs them into the blood (1). The circulatory system then transports these molecules to all the cells of the body (1), where glucose is used in respiration or to build new molecules; neither system alone could supply the cells (1).
:::

An aggregation (collection) of different tissues that work together to perform specific functions.

::: q
Name the three main tissues in the stomach. (3)
---
Muscular tissue (1); glandular tissue (1); epithelial tissue (1).
:::

::: q
Is a leaf a tissue or an organ? Explain your answer. (2)
---
An organ (1), because it is made of several different tissues (e.g. epidermis, palisade mesophyll, xylem and phloem) working together to carry out a function (1).
:::

::: q
Name two organs, other than the stomach, that are part of the digestive system. (2)
---
Any two: small intestine; large intestine; liver; pancreas; oesophagus; gall bladder; salivary glands (1 each).
:::

::: q
A mitochondrion is 2 µm long and a cheek cell is 60 µm across. How many times larger is the cheek cell? (1)
---
60 ÷ 2 = 30 times.
:::

::: q
Suggest why blood is described as a tissue. (1)
---
It is made of cells (e.g. red and white blood cells) with related functions, working together, so it is a group of cells rather than an organ made of several tissues.
:::

::: watch
- Learn the AQA definitions word for word: tissue = cells with **similar structure and function**; organ = **tissues** performing specific functions; organ system = organs **working together**.
- An organ is made of **several different tissues**, not just "lots of cells".
- Common misclassifications: blood is a **tissue**, the leaf and the skin are **organs**, xylem and phloem are **tissues**.
- Size sums: get both lengths into the **same unit** before dividing; 1 m = 1 000 000 µm.
- Give the full order when asked: sub-cellular structure → cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism.
:::

::: test
A human is 1.8 m tall. A red blood cell is 7.5 µm across. Calculate how many times taller the human is than the diameter of the red blood cell. Give your answer in standard form. (3)
---
1.8 m = 1 800 000 µm (1). 1 800 000 ÷ 7.5 = 240 000 (1) = $2.4 \times 10^5$ (1).

::: test
The total length of a person's gut is 8.0 m. The small intestine is 6.4 m long. Calculate the percentage of the gut that is small intestine. (2)
---
6.4 ÷ 8.0 × 100 (1) = 80 % (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the heart is described as an organ. (2)
---
It is made of several different tissues (e.g. muscle tissue, nervous tissue, connective tissue, epithelial lining) (1) that work together to perform a specific function: pumping blood (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a multicellular organism such as a human needs organ systems, whereas a single-celled organism does not. (3)
---
A single cell can carry out all life processes itself and exchange substances across its surface by diffusion (1). In a multicellular organism, cells are specialised for particular jobs, so they rely on others; most cells are far from the surface (1). Organ systems, such as the digestive, respiratory and circulatory systems, work together to supply every cell with what it needs and remove waste (1).
:::

::: test
The pancreas contains glandular tissue that makes enzymes, and the liver makes bile. Both release their products into the small intestine. Use this information to explain why the digestive system is described as an organ system. (3)
---
The pancreas, liver and small intestine are all organs, each made of several tissues (1). They have different jobs, enzyme production, bile production, digestion and absorption (1), but they work together to achieve one overall function: digesting and absorbing food. A group of organs working together is an organ system (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the organisation of a human into cells, tissues, organs and organ systems allows the body to function efficiently. Use examples in your answer. (6)
---
Indicative content: cells differentiate and become specialised, each with structures suited to one job (e.g. muscle cells with many mitochondria); cells of the same type are grouped into tissues with a similar structure and function, so a job is done efficiently (e.g. muscular tissue contracts, glandular tissue secretes enzymes); different tissues form organs that carry out more complex functions (e.g. stomach: muscle churns, glands secrete, epithelium lines); organs work together in organ systems (e.g. digestive system digests and absorbs food); organ systems depend on each other (digestive provides glucose, respiratory provides oxygen, circulatory delivers both to cells); this division of labour means the large organism can supply every cell.
Level 3 (5–6): a logical sequence through all the levels, linked to efficiency, with relevant examples at most levels.
Level 2 (3–4): most levels described with some examples, limited linking.
Level 1 (1–2): some levels named or defined, few or no examples.
0: nothing relevant.
:::
