In Biology 8461 these are 4.5.3.4–4.5.3.7. **All of it is also in Combined Science: Trilogy** (numbered 4.5.3.3–4.5.3.6 there, where the last section is called "Feedback systems"), so nothing here is separate-science only — but watch the Higher tier flags.

### Hormones at puberty

During **puberty**, **reproductive hormones cause secondary sex characteristics to develop**.

| | Female | Male |
|---|---|---|
| Main reproductive hormone | **Oestrogen** | **Testosterone** |
| Made in | **Ovaries** | **Testes** |
| Key role | At puberty **eggs begin to mature** and **one is released about every 28 days** — this is **ovulation** | **Stimulates sperm production** |
| Secondary sex characteristics | Breasts develop, hips widen, pubic and underarm hair, menstruation starts | Voice deepens, facial and body hair, muscles develop, penis and testes enlarge |

### The menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle lasts about **28 days**. It prepares the body for a possible pregnancy: an egg matures and is released, and the **lining of the uterus** thickens ready to receive an embryo.

| Days (approx.) | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | **Menstruation** — the uterus lining breaks down and is lost (a period) |
| 5–13 | The uterus lining **builds up again**, becoming thick and full of blood vessels; an egg matures in the ovary |
| about 14 | **Ovulation** — an egg is released from the ovary into the oviduct |
| 15–28 | The lining is **maintained**. If the egg is not fertilised, the lining breaks down at the end and the cycle restarts |

**Four hormones control it:**

| Hormone | Made by | Effect (all students) |
|---|---|---|
| **FSH** (follicle stimulating hormone) | Pituitary gland | Causes **maturation of an egg** in the ovary |
| **LH** (luteinising hormone) | Pituitary gland | **Stimulates the release of the egg** (ovulation) |
| **Oestrogen** | Ovaries | Involved in **building and maintaining the uterus lining** |
| **Progesterone** | Ovaries (the empty follicle, after ovulation) | **Maintains the uterus lining** |

### How the four hormones interact (Higher tier)

1. **FSH** is released by the pituitary. It causes an egg to **mature** in a follicle in the ovary, and **stimulates the ovaries to produce oestrogen**.
2. **Oestrogen** rises. It makes the **uterus lining thicken**. It also **inhibits** (stops) further release of **FSH** and **stimulates the release of LH** from the pituitary.
3. **LH** peaks sharply (an "LH surge") around **day 14**, which **stimulates ovulation** — the release of the egg.
4. After ovulation, the empty follicle produces **progesterone**. Progesterone **maintains the uterus lining** and **inhibits the release of both FSH and LH**, so no more eggs mature.
5. If the egg is **not fertilised**, progesterone levels **fall**. The lining is no longer maintained, so it breaks down (**menstruation**), and because FSH is no longer inhibited, **FSH is released again** — a new cycle begins.
6. If the egg **is** fertilised, progesterone stays high, keeping the lining in place for the embryo.

**Reading hormone graphs (Higher tier, MS 2c).** On a typical graph of hormone levels against day of cycle:
- **FSH** rises at the start of the cycle (days 1–5) and has a small peak around day 13–14.
- **Oestrogen** rises steadily from about day 5, peaking just before day 14.
- **LH** shows a **tall, sharp peak at about day 13–14** — ovulation follows it.
- **Progesterone** is low until ovulation, rises to a peak around **day 21**, then falls before day 28.

Exam questions ask you to identify a hormone from its curve, say on which day ovulation happens, or explain what happens when progesterone falls. Quote the day and the hormone level from the graph.

### Contraception

Fertility can be controlled by **hormonal** and **non-hormonal** methods of contraception.

| Method | Type | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Oral contraceptive** ("the pill") | Hormonal | Contains hormones that **inhibit FSH production, so no eggs mature**. Most contain oestrogen and progesterone; some progesterone-only. |
| **Injection, implant or skin patch** | Hormonal | Slow release of **progesterone** to **inhibit the maturation and release of eggs** for **months or years** |
| **Barrier methods — condoms and diaphragms** | Non-hormonal | **Prevent the sperm reaching an egg**. Condoms also protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs). |
| **Intrauterine device (IUD, "coil")** | Either | **Prevents the implantation of an embryo**, or **releases a hormone** (progesterone) |
| **Spermicidal agents** | Non-hormonal | **Kill or disable sperm**; usually used with a barrier method |
| **Abstaining** when an egg may be in the oviduct (natural/rhythm method) | Non-hormonal | No intercourse around ovulation, so no sperm meet an egg |
| **Surgical sterilisation** — male (vasectomy: sperm ducts cut) and female (oviducts cut or tied) | Non-hormonal | Sperm cannot leave / eggs cannot reach the uterus; almost always permanent |

**Evaluating methods.** Consider:
- **Effectiveness** — the pill and implants are over 99% effective when used correctly; condoms about 98% with perfect use (lower in typical use); abstinence around ovulation is the least reliable because cycles vary.
- **Side effects** of hormonal methods — mood changes, headaches, weight change, irregular bleeding; a small increased risk of blood clots (combined pill).
- **Remembering** — the pill must be taken every day; implants and IUDs last for years, so no need to remember.
- **STI protection** — only condoms give it.
- **Reversibility** — sterilisation is usually permanent; most other methods are reversible.
- Hormonal methods put the responsibility on the woman.

**Why science alone cannot settle contraception issues (WS 1.3).** Science can say how effective a method is and what its side effects are, but whether people *should* use contraception involves **religious, moral, personal and cultural** beliefs that science cannot answer. Decisions also involve **social and economic** issues (cost, access to clinics, family size).

### The use of hormones to treat infertility (Higher tier)

Some women cannot become pregnant because they **do not produce enough FSH** (so no eggs mature) or LH.

**Fertility drug.** The woman is given **FSH and LH** in a "fertility drug" to stimulate eggs to mature and be released. She may then **become pregnant in the normal way**.

**In vitro fertilisation (IVF)** ("in glass" — fertilisation in the laboratory):
1. The mother is given **FSH and LH** to **stimulate the maturation of several eggs**.
2. The **eggs are collected** from the mother.
3. They are **fertilised by sperm from the father in the laboratory**.
4. The fertilised eggs **develop into embryos**.
5. At the stage when they are **tiny balls of cells**, **one or two embryos are inserted into the mother's uterus (womb)**.

**Developments in microscopy (WS 1.1)** have enabled IVF to develop — for example, a single sperm can be injected directly into an egg under a microscope (ICSI), and embryos can be examined before they are inserted.

**Problems with fertility treatment.** Although it gives a woman the chance to have a baby of her own:
- it is **very emotionally and physically stressful** (many injections, egg collection, repeated failure);
- **success rates are not high** — roughly 1 in 3 for younger women per embryo transfer, much lower over age 40;
- it can lead to **multiple births**, which are a **risk to both the babies and the mother** (premature birth, low birth weight, miscarriage, high blood pressure in the mother). This is why usually only one or two embryos are inserted.

**Social and ethical issues (WS 1.3, 1.4).** IVF is **expensive**, and free NHS cycles are limited; unused embryos are frozen or destroyed, which some people believe is wrong because an embryo has the potential for life; embryos can be screened for genetic disorders, which raises fears of "designer babies". From the **patient's** view: a chance of a biological child, against cost, stress and failure. From the **doctor's** view: helping patients, against the risks of multiple births and of raising false hope.

**Worked mini-example (percentages).** A clinic carried out 1250 IVF cycles in a year, giving 400 live births. Success rate $= \frac{400}{1250} \times 100 = 32\%$.

### Adrenaline and thyroxine (Higher tier)

**Adrenaline** is produced by the **adrenal glands** in times of **fear or stress**. It:
- **increases the heart rate** (and breathing rate);
- **boosts the delivery of oxygen and glucose to the brain and muscles** (it also makes the liver convert glycogen to glucose);
- prepares the body for **"flight or fight"**.

More oxygen and glucose to the muscles means a faster rate of **respiration**, releasing more energy for muscle contraction. Adrenaline is **not** controlled by negative feedback — its release is triggered by the nervous system in response to danger.

**Thyroxine** is produced by the **thyroid gland**. It:
- **stimulates the basal metabolic rate** (the rate of chemical reactions in the body at rest, including the rate at which energy is released by respiration and proteins are made);
- plays an **important role in growth and development** (especially of the brain and nervous system in children).

### Negative feedback (Higher tier)

**Negative feedback** is when a change from the normal level is detected and a response **reverses the change**, bringing the level back to normal. It keeps conditions within narrow limits.

**Thyroxine levels are controlled by negative feedback:**
1. When thyroxine levels in the blood are **low**, the brain (hypothalamus) detects this and the **pituitary gland releases TSH** (thyroid-stimulating hormone).
2. **TSH stimulates the thyroid** to release **more thyroxine**.
3. When thyroxine levels **return to normal or rise too high**, the **release of TSH is inhibited**, so the thyroid releases **less thyroxine**.
4. The level stays roughly constant.

**Interpreting negative feedback diagrams (WS 1.2).** They are usually a loop: *level rises → detected → response lowers it → level falls → detected → response raises it*. Look for a hormone that **inhibits** the gland that triggered it (e.g. thyroxine inhibiting TSH, or progesterone inhibiting FSH and LH in the menstrual cycle). Other examples you already know: insulin and glucagon controlling blood glucose; ADH controlling water levels (Biology only).

::: terms
- **Oestrogen**: the main female reproductive hormone, made in the ovaries; builds and maintains the uterus lining.
- **Testosterone**: the main male reproductive hormone, made in the testes; stimulates sperm production.
- **Ovulation**: the release of a mature egg from the ovary, about every 28 days (around day 14 of the cycle).
- **FSH**: follicle stimulating hormone from the pituitary gland; causes an egg to mature in the ovary.
- **LH**: luteinising hormone from the pituitary gland; stimulates the release of the egg (ovulation).
- **Progesterone**: a hormone from the ovary that maintains the uterus lining and inhibits FSH and LH.
- **Contraception**: methods that prevent pregnancy, either hormonal (pill, implant) or non-hormonal (condom, sterilisation).
- **IVF**: in vitro fertilisation; eggs are fertilised by sperm in the laboratory and embryos inserted into the uterus (Higher tier).
- **Adrenaline**: a hormone from the adrenal glands that prepares the body for fight or flight (Higher tier).
- **Thyroxine**: a hormone from the thyroid gland that stimulates the basal metabolic rate (Higher tier).
- **Negative feedback**: a control system in which a change from normal triggers a response that reverses the change.
:::

::: method
**Command words on this topic**
- **Describe** the roles of hormones in the menstrual cycle — name each hormone, **where it is made** and **its effect**. One clear sentence per hormone.
- **Explain** the interactions (Higher tier) — this is about what each hormone does to the **other** hormones: FSH → stimulates oestrogen; oestrogen → inhibits FSH and stimulates LH; LH → ovulation; progesterone → inhibits FSH and LH. Use the words **stimulates** and **inhibits**.
- **Graph questions (Higher tier, MS 2c)** — identify LH by its **sharp peak** just before ovulation; progesterone by its peak **after** ovulation (day ~21). State the day to the nearest day using the axis.
- **Evaluate** contraceptive methods — compare at least two methods using effectiveness, side effects, STI protection, ease of use and reversibility; end with a justified conclusion for the given person.
- **Explain** a contraceptive's action — link it to the hormone it affects (e.g. "the pill inhibits FSH, so no eggs mature, so none are released").
- **Describe** IVF (Higher tier) — the five steps in order, naming FSH and LH.
- **Evaluate** infertility treatment (Higher tier) — patient and doctor perspectives: chance of a child versus stress, low success rate, cost, risks of multiple births, ethical issues with spare embryos.
- **Explain** the role of adrenaline or thyroxine (Higher tier) — gland, trigger, effect, and *why* the effect is useful.
- **Explain** a negative feedback diagram (Higher tier) — describe the change, the detection, the response, and how the response reverses the change.

**Calculations.** Percentages (success rates, effectiveness), cycle-day arithmetic, and reading values from hormone graphs. Show the fraction, then multiply by 100, and give units or %.

**6-mark answers.** "Explain how hormones control the menstrual cycle" — Level 3 needs **all four** hormones with their **sources** and **interactions** in a logical sequence through the cycle, including what happens if no fertilisation occurs. "Evaluate IVF" — Level 3 needs advantages and several disadvantages with a reasoned conclusion.

::: example Hormone interactions (Higher tier)
Explain why a woman does not usually release a second egg during the second half of her menstrual cycle. (3)

After ovulation the ovary produces progesterone (1). Progesterone inhibits the release of FSH and LH from the pituitary gland (1). Without FSH no egg matures, and without LH no egg is released (1).
:::

::: example Contraception
Explain how the contraceptive pill prevents pregnancy, and give one advantage and one disadvantage of using it compared with a condom. (4)

The pill contains hormones that inhibit FSH production, so no eggs mature (1) and none are released, so no fertilisation (1). Advantage: more effective / does not interrupt intercourse (1). Disadvantage: no protection against STIs / side effects such as mood changes or risk of blood clots / must be taken every day (1).
:::

::: example IVF calculation (Higher tier)
In one year a clinic treated 820 women under 35 and 640 women aged 40–42. There were 279 live births in the younger group and 58 in the older group. Calculate the success rate for each group, to 2 significant figures, and suggest a reason for the difference. (3)

Under 35: $\frac{279}{820} \times 100 = 34\%$ (1). Aged 40–42: $\frac{58}{640} \times 100 = 9.1\%$ (1). Older women's eggs are less likely to be fertilised or to develop into healthy embryos / fewer eggs mature (1).
:::

::: example Adrenaline (Higher tier)
A person is frightened by a loud noise. Explain how adrenaline prepares the body for "fight or flight". (3)

The adrenal glands release adrenaline (1). It increases heart rate, so more oxygen and glucose are delivered to the brain and muscles (1). Muscles can respire faster, releasing more energy for contraction, so the person can run or fight (1).
:::

::: watch
- **FSH matures the egg; LH releases it.** Swapping them is the most common lost mark. FSH and LH come from the **pituitary**, not the ovary.
- Oestrogen and progesterone come from the **ovaries**. Progesterone **maintains** the lining; menstruation starts when progesterone **falls**.
- On hormone graphs, **LH has the sharp spike just before ovulation**; progesterone peaks **after** ovulation, around day 21.
- The pill works by **inhibiting FSH** — not by "killing the egg" or "stopping sperm".
- Only **condoms** protect against STIs. "Evaluate" needs both advantages and disadvantages **and** a conclusion.
- IVF: the eggs are **fertilised in the laboratory**, and the **embryos** (not the eggs) are put into the **uterus**. Mention **FSH and LH** to mature several eggs.
- Thyroxine controls **metabolic rate**, not "metabolism speed of the heart". Adrenaline increases **heart rate** and boosts **oxygen and glucose to the brain and muscles**.
- Negative feedback means the response **reverses** the change — say "levels return to normal".
:::

::: test
Higher tier: explain how thyroxine levels in the blood are kept constant by negative feedback. (4)
---
When thyroxine level is low, the pituitary gland releases TSH (1). TSH stimulates the thyroid gland to release more thyroxine (1). When thyroxine level rises back to normal/too high, TSH release is inhibited (1), so less thyroxine is released and the level falls back to normal — the response reverses the change (1).

::: test
Higher tier: explain how the hormones FSH, oestrogen, LH and progesterone interact to control the menstrual cycle. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- FSH is released by the pituitary gland
- FSH causes an egg to mature in the ovary
- FSH stimulates the ovary to produce oestrogen
- oestrogen causes the uterus lining to build up/thicken
- oestrogen inhibits FSH release and stimulates LH release from the pituitary
- LH peaks at about day 14 and stimulates ovulation (release of the egg)

- progesterone maintains the uterus lining and inhibits FSH and LH
