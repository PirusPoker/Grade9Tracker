This topic is about **how scientific ideas develop over time (WS 1.1)**: theories are proposed, argued about, tested against new evidence, and accepted gradually as evidence builds.

### Separate science only: Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection

**Charles Darwin** proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection as a result of:
- **observations on a round-the-world expedition** (HMS Beagle, 1831–36 — e.g. finches and tortoises on the Galápagos Islands that differed from island to island);
- **years of experimentation and discussion** (including breeding pigeons);
- **developing knowledge of geology and fossils** (rocks showed the Earth was very old, and fossils showed organisms had changed).

His theory in three points:
1. **Individual organisms within a particular species show a wide range of variation for a characteristic.**
2. **Individuals with characteristics most suited to the environment are more likely to survive to breed successfully.**
3. **The characteristics that have enabled these individuals to survive are then passed on to the next generation.**

Darwin published his ideas in **On the Origin of Species (1859)**. There was **much controversy**. The theory was **only gradually accepted** because:
- **it challenged the idea that God made all the animals and plants that live on Earth**;
- **there was insufficient evidence at the time** the theory was published to convince many scientists;
- **the mechanism of inheritance and variation was not known until 50 years after the theory was published** (genes were not understood until the early 20th century).

**Lamarck's theory.** Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (early 1800s) argued that **changes that occur in an organism during its lifetime can be inherited** — e.g. a giraffe stretching its neck to reach leaves would have offspring with longer necks; a part used a lot gets bigger, a part not used disappears. **We now know that in the vast majority of cases this type of inheritance cannot occur**: characteristics acquired during a lifetime (big muscles from training, a scar) do not change the DNA in the gametes, so they are not passed on.

| | Darwin (natural selection) | Lamarck (inheritance of acquired characteristics) |
|---|---|---|
| Where does the variation come from? | Already present in the population (we now know: mutations). | Produced by the organism during its life through use or disuse. |
| What is passed on? | Inherited characteristics (alleles) of individuals that survive. | Changes acquired during the lifetime. |
| Status today | Widely accepted, supported by evidence. | Rejected for almost all cases. |

### Separate science only: Wallace and speciation

**Alfred Russel Wallace** **independently proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection**. He **published joint writings with Darwin in 1858**, which **prompted Darwin to publish On the Origin of Species (1859) the following year**.

- Wallace **worked worldwide gathering evidence** for evolutionary theory (in the Amazon and the Malay Archipelago).
- He is best known for his work on **warning colouration** in animals (bright colours such as those of some caterpillars and butterflies warn predators that they are poisonous or taste bad — a feature favoured by natural selection) and his **theory of speciation**.
- Wallace did much pioneering work on speciation, but **more evidence over time has led to our current understanding** of the theory of speciation.

**Impact of Darwin and Wallace's ideas on biology:** they gave one explanation for the diversity of all life, linked all organisms through common ancestors, changed classification (organisms grouped by evolutionary relationships) and underpins modern biology, including understanding antibiotic resistance.

**The steps that give rise to new species (speciation):**
1. **Isolation** — two populations of one species become **separated**, so they cannot interbreed (e.g. by a mountain range, a river, the sea or an island).
2. **Variation** — each population has a range of alleles (genetic variation, from mutations).
3. **Different conditions** — the environments on each side are different (food, climate, predators).
4. **Natural selection** — in each population, different alleles give phenotypes best suited to *that* environment, so those individuals survive, breed and pass the alleles on. Over many generations the **allele frequencies change differently** in the two populations.
5. **New species** — eventually the populations are **so different in phenotype that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring**: they are two separate species.

### Separate science only: the development of our understanding of genetics

| Time | Development |
|---|---|
| **Mid-19th century** | **Gregor Mendel** carried out **breeding experiments on plants** (garden peas, e.g. tall × short, round × wrinkled seeds). He observed that **the inheritance of each characteristic is determined by "units" that are passed on to descendants unchanged** — and that some are dominant and some recessive (he found the 3 : 1 ratio). |
| **Late 19th century** | The **behaviour of chromosomes during cell division** was observed (with improved microscopes and stains). |
| **Early 20th century** | It was observed that **chromosomes and Mendel's "units" behaved in similar ways** (both come in pairs that separate when gametes form). This led to the idea that the **units, now called genes, were located on chromosomes**. |
| **Mid-20th century** | **The structure of DNA was determined** (1953, Watson and Crick using Rosalind Franklin's X-ray data) and the **mechanism of gene function worked out**. |

This scientific work by **many scientists** led to the **gene theory** being developed.

**Why Mendel's importance was not recognised until after his death (he died in 1884; his work was rediscovered in 1900):**
- He was a **monk**, not part of the scientific community, and published in an **obscure local journal** that few people read.
- **Chromosomes, genes and DNA had not been discovered**, so there was no mechanism to explain his "units".
- His use of **mathematics and statistics** in biology was unusual and not understood by many biologists.
- Other scientists could not see how his idea fitted with the ideas of the time (e.g. blending inheritance).

### Evidence for evolution

The theory of evolution by natural selection is **now widely accepted**. Evidence for Darwin's theory is available because:
- it has been shown that **characteristics are passed on to offspring in genes**;
- there is further evidence in the **fossil record**;
- and in the **knowledge of how resistance to antibiotics evolves in bacteria** — evolution observed happening now.

### Fossils

**Fossils are the "remains" of organisms from millions of years ago, which are found in rocks.** Fossils may be formed:

1. **from parts of organisms that have not decayed because one or more of the conditions needed for decay are absent** — e.g. no oxygen, too cold, too acidic or too dry (insects in amber, mammoths in ice, bodies in peat bogs);
2. **when parts of the organism are replaced by minerals as they decay** — hard parts like bones, teeth and shells are slowly replaced by minerals, forming a rock-like copy (the most common type);
3. **as preserved traces of organisms**, such as **footprints, burrows and rootlet traces**.

**Why the fossil record is incomplete (WS 1.3):**
- **Many early forms of life were soft-bodied**, so they have **left few traces behind** (soft tissue decays quickly).
- **What traces there were have been mainly destroyed by geological activity** (rocks folded, melted or eroded).
- Fossilisation needs rare conditions, so most organisms never became fossils, and many fossils have not been found.

**This is why scientists cannot be certain about how life began on Earth.** But **we can learn from fossils how much or how little different organisms have changed as life developed on Earth** — e.g. the horse sequence shows gradual change in size and toe number; sharks and crocodiles have changed little.

**Evolutionary trees (MS 2c, 4a).** Fossil data, with classification data for living organisms, are used to draw evolutionary trees. Read them like a family tree: species that **share a more recent branching point** (common ancestor) are **more closely related**. A branch that stops before the present day is an **extinct** species. Read the time axis carefully when asked "how long ago".

### Extinction

**Extinctions occur when there are no remaining individuals of a species still alive.** Factors that may contribute:
- **new predators** (e.g. rats and cats introduced to islands, as with the dodo);
- **new diseases**;
- **new, more successful competitors** (e.g. introduced grey squirrels outcompeting red squirrels in much of the UK);
- **changes to the environment over geological time** (e.g. climate change, rising/falling sea levels);
- **a single catastrophic event** (e.g. a massive volcanic eruption or an asteroid collision — thought to have caused the extinction of the dinosaurs about 66 million years ago);
- **human activity** — habitat destruction, hunting, pollution.

A species is most at risk when it **cannot adapt or evolve fast enough**: populations that are small, slow-breeding or have little genetic variation.

### Resistant bacteria

**Bacteria can evolve rapidly because they reproduce at a fast rate** (some divide every 20 minutes), so many generations pass quickly.

**How antibiotic resistance develops:**
1. **Mutations of bacterial pathogens produce new strains.** Some strains might be **resistant to antibiotics**.
2. When an antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria are killed, but the **resistant bacteria are not killed**.
3. **They survive and reproduce, so the population of the resistant strain rises.**
4. **The resistant strain will then spread because people are not immune to it and there is no effective treatment.**

**MRSA** (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) **is resistant to antibiotics** — a serious problem in hospitals.

**To reduce the rate of development of antibiotic-resistant strains:**
- **doctors should not prescribe antibiotics inappropriately**, such as for **non-serious or viral infections** (antibiotics do not kill viruses);
- **patients should complete their course of antibiotics** so all bacteria are killed and none survive to mutate and form resistant strains;
- **the agricultural use of antibiotics should be restricted** (e.g. not giving them routinely to farm animals to promote growth).

**The development of new antibiotics is costly and slow.** It is **unlikely to keep up with the emergence of new resistant strains**.

::: terms
- **Fossil**: the remains or traces of an organism from millions of years ago, found in rocks.
- **Fossil record**: the collection of all fossils found, which is incomplete because of soft bodies and geological activity.
- **Extinction**: when there are no remaining individuals of a species still alive.
- **Speciation**: the formation of a new species when isolated populations become unable to interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
- **Isolation**: separation of two populations of a species, for example by a river, mountain or sea, so they cannot interbreed.
- **Antibiotic resistance**: the ability of a strain of bacteria to survive treatment with an antibiotic.
- **MRSA**: a strain of Staphylococcus aureus bacteria that is resistant to many antibiotics.
- **Inheritance of acquired characteristics**: Lamarck's rejected idea that changes made during an organism's lifetime are passed on.
- **Warning colouration**: bright colours that warn predators an animal is poisonous or distasteful, studied by Wallace.
:::

::: example Resistance
Explain how a population of bacteria can become resistant to an antibiotic. (4)

A random mutation produces a bacterium that is resistant to the antibiotic (1). When the antibiotic is used, the non-resistant bacteria are killed but the resistant bacteria survive (1). The resistant bacteria reproduce rapidly, passing on the resistance allele (1). So the population of the resistant strain rises (and spreads, because there is no effective treatment) (1).
:::

::: example Bacterial growth calculation
A bacterium divides every 20 minutes. Starting from one resistant bacterium, how many bacteria would there be after 3 hours, assuming all survive? (2)

Number of divisions = 180 ÷ 20 = 9 (1). Number of bacteria = $2^9 = 512$ (1).
:::

::: example Separate science only: speciation
Two populations of mice are separated when a river changes course. Describe how this could lead to the formation of two species. (5)

The populations are isolated so cannot interbreed (1). There is genetic variation in each population due to mutations (1). The conditions on each side of the river are different, so different phenotypes are best suited on each side (1). Natural selection acts differently: the best-suited mice survive, reproduce and pass on their alleles, so allele frequencies change differently over many generations (1). Eventually the two populations are so different that they cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring — they are different species (1).
:::

::: example Fossil formation
Explain why fossils of soft-bodied organisms such as jellyfish are rare. (2)

Soft tissue decays quickly, so it is not usually preserved or replaced by minerals (1). Hard parts such as bones and shells are needed for most fossils, so soft-bodied organisms leave few traces (1).
