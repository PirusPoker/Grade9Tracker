## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.5.3.1 **the human endocrine system**, 4.5.3.2 **control of blood glucose concentration** (both for everyone, including Combined Science: Trilogy) and 4.5.3.3 **maintaining water and nitrogen balance** — which is **Biology 8461 only** and is under a "Separate science only:" heading below.

### The endocrine system

The **endocrine system** is made of **glands** that secrete chemicals called **hormones directly into the bloodstream**. The **blood carries the hormone to a target organ**, where it produces an effect.

| | Nervous system | Endocrine (hormonal) system |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Electrical impulses (chemical at synapses) | Chemical — hormones |
| Carried by | Neurones | The blood (plasma) |
| Speed of effect | Very fast | **Slower** |
| Duration | Short | **Acts for longer** |
| Area affected | Very precise (one muscle or gland) | Can be widespread — every organ with the right receptors |

**The pituitary gland** in the brain is called the **"master gland"**. It secretes several hormones into the blood **in response to body conditions**, and these hormones **act on other glands**, stimulating them to release their own hormones. For example, the pituitary releases TSH, which makes the thyroid release thyroxine, and FSH and LH, which act on the ovaries.

You must be able to **identify the position** of these glands on a diagram of the body:

| Gland | Position | Main hormone(s) and job |
|---|---|---|
| **Pituitary gland** | Base of the brain, just below the middle | Many: FSH, LH, TSH, ADH, growth hormone — controls other glands |
| **Thyroid** | Front of the neck, around the windpipe | Thyroxine — controls metabolic rate |
| **Adrenal glands** | On top of each kidney | Adrenaline — "fight or flight" |
| **Pancreas** | In the abdomen, just below and behind the stomach | Insulin and glucagon — control blood glucose |
| **Ovaries** (female) | In the lower abdomen, either side of the uterus | Oestrogen and progesterone |
| **Testes** (male) | In the scrotum, outside the abdomen | Testosterone |

### Control of blood glucose concentration

Blood glucose concentration is **monitored and controlled by the pancreas**. Glucose enters the blood from digested carbohydrate; cells remove it for respiration.

**If blood glucose is too high** (e.g. after a meal):
1. The pancreas detects the rise and **produces the hormone insulin**.
2. Insulin travels in the blood and **causes glucose to move from the blood into the cells**.
3. In **liver and muscle cells**, excess glucose is **converted to glycogen for storage**.
4. Blood glucose concentration falls back to normal.

**If blood glucose is too low (Higher tier)** (e.g. after exercise or not eating):
1. The pancreas **produces the hormone glucagon**.
2. Glucagon **causes glycogen to be converted into glucose** (in the liver) and **released into the blood**.
3. Blood glucose concentration rises back to normal.

**Negative feedback (Higher tier).** Insulin and glucagon work against each other in a **negative feedback cycle**: a rise in glucose triggers insulin, which lowers glucose; a fall triggers glucagon, which raises it. Whichever way glucose moves, the response reverses the change, so the concentration stays within narrow limits.

| | Insulin | Glucagon (Higher tier) |
|---|---|---|
| Released when | Blood glucose too **high** | Blood glucose too **low** |
| Made by | Pancreas | Pancreas |
| Effect | Glucose moves into cells; glucose → glycogen in liver and muscle | Glycogen → glucose in liver; glucose released into blood |
| Result | Blood glucose **falls** | Blood glucose **rises** |

Spelling matters: **glucose** (the sugar), **glycogen** (the storage carbohydrate), **glucagon** (the hormone). Examiners do not accept a mix-up.

### Diabetes

| | Type 1 diabetes | Type 2 diabetes |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | The **pancreas fails to produce sufficient insulin** | **Body cells no longer respond to insulin** produced by the pancreas |
| Result | Uncontrolled high blood glucose | Blood glucose stays high |
| Typical onset | Usually in childhood | Usually in later life, but increasingly in young people |
| Treatment | **Insulin injections** (often before meals), plus monitoring blood glucose, diet and exercise | **Carbohydrate-controlled diet** and an **exercise regime** (drugs may be added later) |
| Risk factors | Mainly genetic / autoimmune | **Obesity** is the main risk factor; also inactivity, age, family history |

**Why the treatments work:** insulin injections replace the hormone the pancreas cannot make; a carbohydrate-controlled diet stops large rises in glucose; exercise uses glucose in respiration and helps cells respond to insulin. Injecting insulin into a Type 2 patient is not the first treatment because the problem is that cells do not respond to it.

**Interpreting glucose graphs (MS 2c).** A typical glucose-tolerance graph shows blood glucose after a glucose drink:
- **Person without diabetes:** glucose rises (e.g. from 5 to 8 mmol/dm³), then insulin brings it back to normal within about 2 hours.
- **Person with Type 1:** glucose rises higher (e.g. to 15 mmol/dm³ or more) and stays high, because little or no insulin is released; insulin level on a second graph stays near zero.
- **Person with Type 2:** glucose rises high and falls slowly, even though insulin levels may be **normal or higher than normal**.

Quote values from the graph, give the time taken to return to normal, and link the shape to insulin.

**Obesity and diabetes (WS 1.3).** Data show a strong **correlation** between obesity (BMI over 30) and Type 2 diabetes. When evaluating recommendations (e.g. a sugar tax, banning junk-food adverts, free gym access), consider **social and ethical issues**: personal freedom of choice, cost to the NHS, fairness to people on low incomes, and that correlation alone does not prove one causes the other.

### Separate science only: maintaining water and nitrogen balance (Biology 8461)

**Effect of osmotic changes on cells.** If body fluids (tissue fluid, blood plasma) become too **concentrated**, cells **lose water by osmosis** and shrink; too **dilute**, cells **gain water by osmosis** and may swell and burst (animal cells have no cell wall). **If body cells lose or gain too much water by osmosis they do not function efficiently.** So the water and ion content of the body must be controlled.

**How water, ions and urea leave the body:**

| Route | What is lost | Controlled? |
|---|---|---|
| **Lungs** (exhalation) | Water (vapour) | **No** |
| **Skin** (sweat) | Water, ions and urea | **No** (sweating is controlled for temperature, not water balance) |
| **Kidneys** (urine) | **Excess** water, ions and urea | **Yes** — the kidneys adjust the amount |

**Making urea (Higher tier).** The digestion of protein produces amino acids. **Excess amino acids** cannot be stored and must be excreted safely. In the **liver** they are **deaminated** (the amino group removed) to form **ammonia**. Ammonia is **toxic**, so it is **immediately converted to urea**, which is carried in the blood to the kidneys for safe excretion.

**How the kidneys make urine.** The kidneys produce urine by:
1. **Filtration of the blood** — small molecules (water, glucose, ions, urea) are forced out of the blood into the kidney tubules. Large molecules (proteins) and blood cells stay in the blood.
2. **Selective reabsorption** of useful substances — **all the glucose**, **some ions** and **water** (as much as the body needs) are taken back into the blood. Glucose reabsorption needs active transport.
3. What is left — **urea, excess ions and excess water** — passes out as urine.

You do **not** need the structure of the kidney, the nephron, or other parts of the urinary system.

**Interpreting data (MS 4a).** You may get a table or bar chart like this (values in g per dm³):

| Substance | Blood entering kidney | Filtrate (after filtration) | Urine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein | 80 | 0 | 0 |
| Glucose | 1.0 | 1.0 | 0 |
| Urea | 0.3 | 0.3 | 20 |
| Ions | 7.0 | 7.0 | 15 |

Read it like this: **protein** is too large to be filtered; **glucose** is filtered and then **all reabsorbed**; **urea** is much more concentrated in urine because **water is reabsorbed** but urea is not. Concentration of urea in urine compared with filtrate: $\frac{20}{0.3} \approx 67$ times.

**ADH and water balance (Higher tier).** The water level in the body is controlled by the hormone **ADH** (anti-diuretic hormone), which acts on the **kidney tubules**:
- When the blood is **too concentrated** (e.g. after sweating, eating salty food, drinking little), receptors in the brain detect it and the **pituitary gland releases more ADH**.
- ADH makes the **kidney tubules more permeable to water**, so **more water is reabsorbed back into the blood**.
- Result: a **small volume of concentrated urine**; blood water level rises back to normal.
- When the blood is **too dilute** (after drinking a lot), **less ADH** is released, tubules are less permeable, less water is reabsorbed → **large volume of dilute urine**.

This is controlled by **negative feedback**: as the blood returns to normal, ADH release falls.

**Kidney failure: dialysis and transplant.** If the kidneys fail, urea and excess ions and water build up in the blood. Treatments are **kidney dialysis** or an **organ transplant**.

**Principles of dialysis:**
- The patient's blood flows through a machine, alongside **dialysis fluid**, separated by a **partially permeable membrane**.
- The dialysis fluid contains the **same concentration of glucose and useful ions as normal blood plasma**, so these do not diffuse out of the blood (no concentration gradient) — but **no urea**.
- **Urea diffuses** from the blood into the fluid down its concentration gradient; **excess ions** also diffuse out until their concentration matches.
- Blood cells and proteins are too large to cross the membrane, so they stay in the blood.
- The fluid flows in the **opposite direction** to the blood and is continually replaced, keeping a steep concentration gradient.
- Patients need sessions of several hours, about three times a week, and must control their diet (salt and protein) between sessions. Heparin is added to stop the blood clotting.

**Transplant vs dialysis (WS 1.5).**

| | Dialysis (mechanical device) | Transplant |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Available without waiting for a donor; no rejection risk; no major surgery | Long-term cure; free to lead a normal life and diet; cheaper over many years |
| Disadvantages | Hours several times a week; strict diet; expensive to run per year; risk of infection and blood clots; patient feels unwell between sessions | Shortage of donors (long waiting lists); needs a tissue match; risk of **rejection**, so lifelong **immunosuppressant drugs**, which increase the risk of infections; risks of major surgery |

A good evaluation ends with a justified conclusion, e.g. "a transplant is better long-term if a well-matched donor is available, but dialysis keeps the patient alive while they wait."

::: terms
- **Endocrine system**: glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream, which carries them to target organs.
- **Hormone**: a chemical messenger secreted by a gland and carried in the blood to a target organ, where it produces an effect.
- **Pituitary gland**: the master gland in the brain that secretes hormones which act on other glands.
- **Insulin**: a hormone from the pancreas that makes glucose move from the blood into cells, lowering blood glucose.
- **Glycogen**: the storage carbohydrate made from excess glucose in liver and muscle cells.
- **Glucagon**: a hormone from the pancreas that causes glycogen to be converted to glucose and released into the blood (Higher tier).
- **Type 1 diabetes**: a disorder in which the pancreas fails to produce sufficient insulin; treated with insulin injections.
- **Type 2 diabetes**: a disorder in which body cells no longer respond to insulin; treated with diet and exercise.
- **Deamination**: removal of the amino group from excess amino acids in the liver, forming ammonia (Higher tier, biology only).
- **Selective reabsorption**: the kidneys taking useful substances such as glucose, some ions and water back into the blood.
- **ADH**: a hormone from the pituitary that makes kidney tubules more permeable so more water is reabsorbed (Higher tier, biology only).
- **Dialysis**: treatment for kidney failure in which urea and excess ions diffuse from blood into dialysis fluid across a partially permeable membrane.
:::

::: method
**Command words on this topic**
- **Describe** the endocrine system — glands → hormones → secreted **into the blood** → carried to a **target organ** → effect.
- **Compare** nervous and hormonal control — make **paired** points: "hormonal is slower *but* lasts longer; nervous is faster *but* short-lived". A list about one system only gets no comparison marks.
- **Explain** how insulin controls blood glucose — detection by **pancreas** → **insulin** released into the blood → glucose moves **into cells** → converted to **glycogen** in **liver and muscle** → blood glucose falls.
- **Explain** the negative feedback cycle (Higher tier) — show both directions: high → insulin → falls; low → glucagon → glycogen to glucose → rises.
- **Compare** Type 1 and Type 2 — cause, then treatment, for each.
- **Explain** the effect of ADH (Higher tier, biology only) — blood too concentrated → pituitary → more ADH → tubules **more permeable to water** → more water **reabsorbed into the blood** → less, more concentrated urine.
- **Describe** dialysis — partially permeable membrane, dialysis fluid with **normal plasma concentrations of glucose and ions but no urea**, urea **diffuses** out down a concentration gradient.
- **Evaluate** dialysis versus transplant, or recommendations about obesity — both sides, then a justified conclusion.

**Graph and data questions (MS 2c, 4a).** Read peak values and times from glucose graphs and compare the person with and without diabetes using numbers. For kidney tables, ask for each substance: is it filtered? is it reabsorbed? Calculate how many times more concentrated something is (divide), or percentage changes.

**6-mark answers.** For "explain how blood glucose is controlled", Level 3 needs both insulin and glucagon (Higher), with correct target organ and the glycogen step, linked as negative feedback. For "evaluate dialysis and transplant", Level 3 needs advantages *and* disadvantages of each with a reasoned conclusion.
:::

::: example Insulin
Explain how the body responds after a person eats a meal rich in carbohydrate. (4)

Blood glucose concentration rises, which is detected by the pancreas (1). The pancreas releases insulin into the blood (1). Insulin causes glucose to move from the blood into body cells (1). In liver and muscle cells, excess glucose is converted to glycogen for storage, so blood glucose falls back to normal (1).
:::

::: example Comparing diabetes
Compare the causes and treatment of Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes. (4)

In Type 1 the pancreas fails to produce enough insulin (1), whereas in Type 2 the body cells no longer respond to insulin (1). Type 1 is treated with insulin injections (1), whereas Type 2 is treated with a carbohydrate-controlled diet and exercise (1).
:::

::: example Kidney data (separate science only)
The concentration of urea is 0.3 g/dm³ in the filtrate and 18 g/dm³ in the urine. Calculate how many times more concentrated urea is in the urine, and explain the difference. (3)

$\frac{18}{0.3} = 60$ times (1). Water is reabsorbed from the tubules back into the blood (1), but urea is not reabsorbed, so its concentration increases (1).
:::

::: example ADH (separate science only, Higher tier)
A runner sweats heavily during a race. Explain how her kidneys respond. (4)

Water is lost in sweat, so her blood becomes more concentrated (1). The pituitary gland releases more ADH into the blood (1). ADH makes the kidney tubules more permeable to water (1), so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and she produces a small volume of concentrated urine (1).
:::

::: example Dialysis (separate science only)
Explain why the dialysis fluid contains glucose at the same concentration as normal blood plasma but contains no urea. (3)

There is no concentration gradient for glucose, so glucose does not diffuse out of the blood and is not lost (1). There is no urea in the fluid, so there is a steep concentration gradient (1) and urea diffuses out of the blood into the fluid across the partially permeable membrane (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Glucose, glycogen, glucagon** — spell each correctly and use it in the right place. "Glucagon is stored in the liver" scores zero.
- Insulin makes glucose move **into cells**; it does not "turn into" glycogen itself, and it does not "break down" glucose.
- Type 1 = pancreas **does not make enough insulin**; Type 2 = cells **do not respond** to insulin. Do not say Type 2 "makes no insulin".
- Hormones travel in the **blood** — not along nerves.
- ADH makes tubules **more permeable** so **more water is reabsorbed into the blood** — the urine gets **less** and more concentrated. Do not say ADH "absorbs water".
- Dialysis fluid has glucose and ions at **normal blood levels** (so they are not lost) and **no urea**. Say substances **diffuse**, not "are filtered".
- Kidneys filter **all small** molecules and then **selectively reabsorb** — protein is never filtered; glucose is filtered then all reabsorbed.
:::

---

::: test

---

::: test
Separate science only (Higher tier): describe what happens in the body after a person eats a large meal of meat, from digestion of the protein to excretion. (4)
---
Protein is digested to amino acids, and excess amino acids cannot be stored (1). In the liver the excess amino acids are deaminated to form ammonia (1). Ammonia is toxic, so it is immediately converted to urea (1). Urea is carried in the blood to the kidneys, filtered and excreted in urine (1).
:::

::: test
Separate science only (Higher tier): a man drinks 2 litres of water. Explain how the volume and concentration of his urine change, including the role of ADH. (4)
---
His blood becomes more dilute (1). Less ADH is released by the pituitary gland (1). The kidney tubules become less permeable to water, so less water is reabsorbed into the blood (1). He produces a large volume of dilute urine; this is negative feedback returning blood water level to normal (1).
:::

::: test
Separate science only: evaluate the use of kidney dialysis compared with a kidney transplant for treating kidney failure. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- dialysis: blood passes along a partially permeable membrane next to dialysis fluid; urea and excess ions diffuse out
- dialysis advantages: available straight away, no need for a donor, no risk of rejection, no major surgery
- dialysis disadvantages: several hours, about three times a week; strict diet; costly to run each year; risk of infection and clots; patient often feels unwell; does not cure the disease
- transplant advantages: long-term cure; normal diet and lifestyle; cheaper over many years than continued dialysis
- transplant disadvantages: shortage of donors, long waiting lists; tissue must match; risk of rejection so lifelong immunosuppressant drugs, which increase risk of infection; risks of surgery; a transplanted kidney may eventually fail
- justified conclusion, e.g. transplant is better long-term if a matched donor is available, but dialysis keeps the patient alive meanwhile.

Level 3 (5–6 marks): advantages and disadvantages of **both** treatments, clearly compared, with a justified conclusion.
Level 2 (3–4 marks): advantages and disadvantages of both, or detailed points about one, without a clear conclusion.
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple points about one treatment, e.g. "dialysis takes a long time".
0 marks: nothing worthy of credit.
