## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.2.2.4 to 4.2.2.7: coronary heart disease and its treatments, health and how diseases interact, lifestyle risk factors, and cancer. All of it is in both GCSE Biology and Combined Science: Trilogy, and none of it is Higher-tier only.

A **non-communicable disease** cannot be passed from person to person (for example coronary heart disease, Type 2 diabetes, most cancers). A **communicable** (infectious) disease is caused by a pathogen and can spread.

### Coronary heart disease (CHD)

In **coronary heart disease, layers of fatty material build up inside the coronary arteries, narrowing them**. This **reduces the flow of blood** through the coronary arteries, **resulting in a lack of oxygen for the heart muscle**. Without enough oxygen the muscle cannot respire aerobically to release the energy it needs, causing chest pain; if an artery is blocked completely, part of the heart muscle dies — a heart attack.

Treatments you must be able to **evaluate** (weigh advantages against disadvantages):

| Treatment | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stents** | a small mesh tube inserted into a narrowed coronary artery (via a catheter) to **hold it open** | restores blood flow quickly; minor procedure, no major surgery, quick recovery; long-lasting | risk of infection, bleeding or a blood clot forming at the stent (so anti-clotting drugs are needed); fatty material can build up again; does not treat the cause |
| **Statins** | drugs that **reduce blood cholesterol levels**, which **slows down the rate of fatty material deposit** | no surgery; can prevent CHD developing; reduce risk of heart attack and stroke | must be **taken every day for life**; take time to work; possible **side effects** (e.g. muscle pain, liver problems); do not remove existing fatty deposits |

### Faulty heart valves

In some people heart valves may become faulty: the valve may **not open fully** (so the heart has to work harder to push blood through, and less blood flows), or the valve might **develop a leak** (so blood flows **backwards**). Consequences: less oxygenated blood reaches the body, the heart works harder, and the person becomes **breathless and tired**; it may lead to heart failure.

Faulty valves can be replaced using **biological valves** (from pigs, cows or human donors) or **mechanical valves** (artificial, made of metal or polymer).

| | Biological valve | Mechanical valve |
|---|---|---|
| Advantage | no need for lifelong anti-clotting drugs | lasts a very long time (often for life) |
| Disadvantage | wears out — may need replacing after 10–20 years | patient must take anti-clotting drugs for life (risk of clots); may make a clicking sound |

Both need major surgery, with risks of infection and complications.

### Heart failure: transplants and artificial hearts

In heart failure, a **donor heart, or heart and lungs, can be transplanted**. **Artificial hearts** are **occasionally** used to keep patients alive **while waiting for a heart transplant**, or to **allow the heart to rest** as an aid to recovery.

- Transplant — advantage: can give many years of good-quality life. Disadvantages: shortage of donors and long waiting lists; major surgery; risk of **rejection** by the immune system, so **immunosuppressant drugs** are taken for life, which increases the risk of infection.
- Artificial heart — advantage: less likely to be rejected (made of metal or plastic, so no immunosuppressant drugs are needed); available without a donor. Disadvantages: risk of blood clots (anti-clotting drugs needed), infection, needs a power supply, not a long-term solution, expensive.

**Evaluation (WS 1.3, 1.4)**: a good evaluation gives advantages AND disadvantages of each option and ends with a justified conclusion ("statins are best for preventing CHD in someone with high cholesterol because…, but a stent is better once an artery is badly narrowed because…").

### Health and interacting diseases

**Health is the state of physical and mental well-being.** Diseases, both communicable and non-communicable, are major causes of ill health. Other factors including **diet, stress and life situations** (e.g. poverty, housing, access to healthcare) may have a profound effect on both physical and mental health.

**Different types of disease may interact**:

- **Defects in the immune system** mean that an individual is **more likely to suffer from infectious diseases** (e.g. HIV damages the immune system).
- **Viruses living in cells can be the trigger for cancers** (e.g. human papillomavirus, HPV, and cervical cancer).
- **Immune reactions initially caused by a pathogen can trigger allergies** such as **skin rashes and asthma**.
- **Severe physical ill health can lead to depression** and other mental illness.

### Risk factors and lifestyle

**Risk factors are linked to an increased rate of a disease.** They can be **aspects of a person's lifestyle** (diet, smoking, alcohol, exercise) or **substances in the person's body or environment** (e.g. high cholesterol, asbestos, ionising radiation).

**A causal mechanism has been proven for some risk factors, but not in others.** A correlation (two things change together) does not on its own prove that one causes the other — scientists need to find the mechanism (e.g. chemicals in tobacco smoke are carcinogens that damage DNA in lung cells).

The examples AQA names:

| Risk factor | Disease / effect |
|---|---|
| **Diet, smoking and exercise** | **cardiovascular disease** — a diet high in saturated fat raises blood cholesterol; smoking damages artery walls and raises blood pressure; lack of exercise increases risk, regular exercise reduces it |
| **Obesity** | **Type 2 diabetes** |
| **Alcohol** | **liver** damage (cirrhosis) and impaired **brain function** |
| **Smoking** | **lung disease** (e.g. bronchitis, emphysema) and **lung cancer** |
| **Smoking and alcohol in pregnancy** | harm **unborn babies** — lower birth weight, premature birth; alcohol can cause lifelong brain damage (fetal alcohol syndrome) |
| **Carcinogens, including ionising radiation** | **cancer** (carcinogens damage DNA; radiation from e.g. UV light, X-rays, radon gas) |

**Many diseases are caused by the interaction of a number of factors** — for example CHD is linked to diet, smoking, lack of exercise, genetics and age together.

**Human and financial costs** — you should be able to discuss them at each scale:

- **Individual**: pain, reduced quality of life, shorter life expectancy, loss of income, emotional impact on family.
- **Local community**: carers needed, pressure on local GP surgeries and hospitals.
- **Nation**: NHS treatment costs (drugs, surgery, long-term care); fewer people working, so lower productivity and tax income.
- **Global**: non-communicable diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide; costs fall hardest on low-income countries, which may lack treatment.

Lifestyle factors also explain differences in **incidence** at local, national and global level: e.g. smoking rates and obesity differ between areas and countries, so rates of lung cancer and Type 2 diabetes differ too.

### Handling data on disease (MS 2c, 2d, 2g, 4a)

- **Translate between graphs and numbers** — read values from a bar chart or line graph accurately; turn a table into a chart.
- **Frequency tables, bar charts and histograms** — bar charts for categories (e.g. disease type); histograms for continuous data in class intervals (e.g. age groups).
- **Scatter diagrams** — plot two variables (e.g. number of cigarettes smoked per day and risk of lung cancer) to see whether there is a **positive**, **negative** or **no** correlation. A correlation does not prove causation.
- **Sampling (including epidemiological data)** — a study should use a **large**, **random**, **representative** sample (a mix of ages, sexes, ethnic groups, incomes) so results can be generalised; small or biased samples are not valid. Other variables (age, diet, etc.) should be matched or allowed for.

Worked mini-example: in a town of 40 000 people, 260 have Type 2 diabetes. Incidence per 100 000 $= \frac{260}{40\,000} \times 100\,000 = 650$ per 100 000. Percentage $= \frac{260}{40\,000} \times 100 = 0.65\%$.

### Cancer

**Cancer is the result of changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled growth and division.** The changes are mutations in the genes that control cell division. The mass of cells formed is a **tumour**.

| | Benign tumour | Malignant tumour |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | a growth of abnormal cells **contained in one area, usually within a membrane** | **cancer** |
| Spread | **does not invade** other parts of the body | **invades neighbouring tissues** and **spreads to different parts of the body in the blood**, where it forms **secondary tumours** |
| Danger | can still cause harm by pressing on organs (e.g. in the brain) | usually more dangerous; harder to treat once spread |

**Risk factors for cancer**: scientists have identified **lifestyle risk factors** for various types of cancer (smoking → lung, mouth, throat; obesity; UV exposure → skin cancer; alcohol → liver, mouth; diet), and there are also **genetic risk factors** for some cancers (inherited alleles, e.g. faulty BRCA genes increase the risk of breast and ovarian cancer).

::: terms
- **Coronary heart disease**: a non-communicable disease in which fatty material builds up inside the coronary arteries, narrowing them and reducing the oxygen supply to heart muscle.
- **Stent**: a mesh tube inserted into a narrowed coronary artery to hold it open so blood can flow.
- **Statin**: a drug that lowers blood cholesterol, slowing the rate at which fatty material is deposited in arteries.
- **Health**: the state of physical and mental well-being, not just the absence of disease.
- **Non-communicable disease**: a disease that cannot be passed from one person to another, such as coronary heart disease or Type 2 diabetes.
- **Risk factor**: an aspect of lifestyle, or a substance in the body or environment, that is linked to an increased rate of a disease.
- **Causal mechanism**: the proven biological process by which a factor actually causes a disease, not just a correlation.
- **Carcinogen**: a substance or agent, such as tobacco smoke chemicals or ionising radiation, that increases the risk of cancer.
- **Benign tumour**: a growth of abnormal cells contained in one area, usually within a membrane, that does not invade other parts of the body.
- **Malignant tumour**: a cancer whose cells invade neighbouring tissues and spread in the blood to form secondary tumours.
- **Correlation**: a relationship in which two variables change together; it does not prove that one causes the other.
:::

::: method
1. **Command words.** *Describe* a graph = state the trend and quote figures with units. *Explain* = give the biological reason (e.g. narrowed artery → less blood → less oxygen → less aerobic respiration in heart muscle). *Evaluate* = advantages AND disadvantages, then a **justified conclusion**. *Suggest* = apply ideas to unfamiliar data. *Compare* = same point for both options in one sentence.
2. **CHD chains.** Write the full chain: fatty material → coronary arteries narrowed → reduced blood flow → less oxygen to heart muscle → less respiration / muscle damaged. Missing "coronary" or "heart muscle" loses marks.
3. **Evaluating treatments.** Use the four comparison headings: how invasive (surgery?), how quickly it works, how long it lasts / ongoing drugs, risks (infection, rejection, side effects, clots). Finish with which you would choose for this patient and why.
4. **Data questions.** Quote values from the graph with units; calculate differences or percentages; say whether a correlation is positive or negative; never claim causation from correlation alone — say a causal mechanism is needed, or other factors may be involved.
5. **Sampling.** A valid study uses a large sample, randomly chosen, representative of the population, and controls other variables (age, sex, diet). Use these words when asked to evaluate a study's conclusion.
6. **4- and 6-mark answers** (evaluate, discuss costs, explain a disease interaction) are levels-marked. Level 3 needs a range of points, each developed (point → reason), and a conclusion when the command word is evaluate.
7. **Cancer.** Benign vs malignant: contained vs invades; does not spread vs spreads in the blood to form secondary tumours.
:::

::: example Explain — CHD
Explain how coronary heart disease can lead to a heart attack. (4)

Layers of fatty material build up inside the coronary arteries (1), narrowing them and reducing the flow of blood to the heart muscle (1). The heart muscle receives less oxygen, so it cannot respire aerobically to release enough energy (1). If the artery is blocked, the heart muscle cells beyond the blockage are starved of oxygen and die / stop contracting (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — stents vs statins
A 55-year-old man has high blood cholesterol and one coronary artery that is already badly narrowed. Evaluate the use of stents and statins to treat him. (6)

Indicative content — stents: hold the narrowed artery open, so blood flow and oxygen to the heart muscle are restored immediately; only a minor operation with a short recovery; but risk of infection or blood clots at the stent, and fatty material can still build up elsewhere. Statins: reduce blood cholesterol, slowing further deposits in all his arteries; no surgery; but must be taken every day for life, can have side effects such as muscle pain, and act slowly and do not clear the existing blockage. Conclusion: he would benefit from both — a stent to deal with the badly narrowed artery now, and statins to slow new deposits, plus lifestyle change.

Level 3 (5–6): advantages and disadvantages of both, linked to his case, with a justified conclusion. Level 2 (3–4): advantages and disadvantages of at least one, or both but undeveloped; conclusion may be missing. Level 1 (1–2): simple statements about one treatment.
:::

::: example Data — correlation and sampling
A study of 200 people found that those who smoked more cigarettes per day had a higher rate of lung cancer. A student concludes that smoking causes lung cancer. Evaluate this conclusion. (4)

The data shows a positive correlation between cigarettes per day and lung cancer (1). But correlation alone does not prove cause — other factors such as air pollution or occupation could be involved (1). 200 is a small sample; it may not be representative, so a larger random sample would make the conclusion more valid (1). The conclusion is supported because a causal mechanism is known: carcinogens in tobacco smoke damage the DNA of lung cells (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — incidence
In a country of 65 million people, 4.1 million have Type 2 diabetes. Calculate the percentage of the population with Type 2 diabetes. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. (2)

$\dfrac{4.1}{65} \times 100 = 6.307...$ (1) $= 6.3\%$ (1).
:::

::: example Describe — interacting diseases
Give two examples of how different types of disease can interact. (2)

Any two: a defective immune system (e.g. from HIV) makes a person more likely to suffer from infectious diseases (1); a virus living in cells can trigger cancer, e.g. HPV and cervical cancer (1); an immune reaction first caused by a pathogen can trigger an allergy such as asthma or a skin rash (1); severe physical ill health can lead to depression (1).
:::

::: q
A survey of heart disease in a town only asked people who attended a gym. Explain why its results may not be valid for the whole town. (2)
---

::: q

- Statins **lower cholesterol**; they do not "unblock" or "dissolve" fatty material.
- In "evaluate" questions give **both sides** and a **justified conclusion** — no conclusion, no top level.
- **Correlation is not causation.** Say a causal mechanism is needed, or that other factors could be involved.
- Benign tumours **can** still be harmful — they are just not cancer and do not spread.
- Malignant tumours spread **in the blood** to form **secondary tumours** — both phrases are marking points.

::: test
A scatter diagram shows that countries with higher mean alcohol consumption per person have higher rates of liver disease. Explain what this shows and whether it proves alcohol causes liver disease. (3)
---
There is a positive correlation between alcohol consumption and liver disease (1). A correlation alone does not prove causation, as other factors (e.g. diet, hepatitis infection, obesity) could vary between countries (1). However, a causal mechanism is known — alcohol is broken down in the liver and damages liver cells (cirrhosis) — so the evidence supports a causal link (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why patients who receive a heart transplant must take drugs for the rest of their lives, and suggest one problem this causes. (3)
---
The donor heart is recognised as foreign / has different antigens, so the immune system could attack and reject it (1); immunosuppressant drugs are taken to prevent rejection (1). Problem: the weakened immune system makes the patient more likely to catch infections (1).
:::

::: test
A 60-year-old woman has heart failure. Doctors are considering a heart transplant or an artificial heart. Evaluate the two options. (6)
---
Indicative content — transplant: a working human heart that can give many years of normal life; but donors are scarce so there may be a long wait and she may die waiting; major surgery with risk of infection; risk of rejection, so lifelong immunosuppressants, which increase infection risk. Artificial heart: available without waiting for a donor; less likely to be rejected, so no immunosuppressants; can keep her alive until a donor heart is available or let her own heart rest and recover; but risk of blood clots, so anti-clotting drugs; needs a power supply/battery and may limit activity; usually a temporary solution; expensive. Conclusion: e.g. an artificial heart as a bridge while she waits for a transplant, with the transplant as the long-term treatment.

Level 3 (5–6): advantages and disadvantages of both options, clearly explained, with a justified conclusion. Level 2 (3–4): some advantages and disadvantages, possibly one-sided or with an unsupported conclusion. Level 1 (1–2): simple statements about one option. 0: nothing worthy of credit.
:::

Nation (any two): NHS cost of treatment, drugs and long-term care; fewer people working, so lower productivity and less tax income; money spent on treatment cannot be spent on other services (2).
