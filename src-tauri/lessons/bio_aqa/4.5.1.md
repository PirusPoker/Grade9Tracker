## What it is

**Homeostasis is the regulation of the internal conditions of a cell or organism to maintain optimum conditions for function, in response to internal and external changes.** This subsection is in both Biology 8461 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464, at both tiers.

Cells can only survive within narrow physical and chemical limits. Your body is constantly being pushed away from its ideal state — by the weather (external change) or by exercise and digestion (internal change) — and homeostasis pulls it back.

### Why it matters

**Homeostasis maintains optimal conditions for enzyme action and all cell functions.**

- **Enzymes** work fastest at their optimum temperature and pH. If the body gets too hot, enzymes **denature** (the active site changes shape); too cold and reactions become too slow to keep cells alive.
- Cells need a **constant supply of glucose** for respiration — too little and they cannot transfer enough energy; too much and water leaves cells by osmosis and tissues are damaged.
- If the **water** concentration of the blood changes, water moves into or out of cells by **osmosis**: cells can swell and burst or shrink, and stop working properly.

### What the human body controls

In the human body homeostasis includes the control of:

| Condition | Typical value kept near | What pushes it away | Detailed in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Blood glucose concentration** | about 4–6 mmol/dm³ (about 90 mg per 100 cm³) | eating a meal (up); exercise and fasting (down) | 4.5.3.2 (hormones) |
| **Body temperature** | about 37 °C | hot or cold surroundings; exercise | 4.5.2.4 (*separate science only*) |
| **Water levels** | water and ion content of the blood | sweating, drinking, eating salty food | 4.5.3.3 (*separate science only*) |

### How control systems work

**These automatic control systems may involve nervous responses or chemical responses** (hormones). Nervous responses are fast and short-lived; hormonal responses are slower but last longer.

**All control systems include:**

1. **Receptors** — **cells which detect stimuli** (changes in the environment, inside or outside the body).
2. **Coordination centres** — such as the **brain**, **spinal cord** and **pancreas** — which **receive and process information from receptors**.
3. **Effectors** — **muscles or glands** — which **bring about responses which restore optimum levels**.

This gives a chain you can apply to any example:

**stimulus → receptor → coordination centre → effector → response**

| Part | Body temperature too high | Blood glucose too high |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus | Rise in blood temperature | Rise in blood glucose after a meal |
| Receptor | Temperature receptors (in the brain and skin) | Cells in the pancreas that detect glucose |
| Coordination centre | Brain (thermoregulatory centre) | **Pancreas** |
| Effector | **Sweat glands** (gland); blood vessels in skin | Liver and muscle cells respond to the hormone **insulin** released by the pancreas (a **gland**) |
| Response | More sweat evaporates, transferring energy away; temperature falls | Glucose moves into cells and is stored as glycogen; blood glucose falls |

Notice that the pancreas can be both the place that detects the change **and** the coordination centre — and, as a gland, it releases the hormone too.

**Effectors: muscles or glands.** A muscle responds by **contracting** (e.g. shivering, which transfers energy by respiration and warms the body). A gland responds by **secreting** a substance (e.g. sweat glands release sweat; the pancreas releases insulin).

### The response reverses the change

In homeostasis the response always acts to **restore the optimum level**: if a condition rises, the response brings it down; if it falls, the response brings it up. (Later topics call this **negative feedback**.) So body temperature, for example, fluctuates a little above and below 37 °C rather than staying perfectly constant.

**Mini-example.** On a cold day your skin temperature falls. Receptors in the skin detect the change and send nerve impulses to the brain (coordination centre). The brain sends impulses to skeletal muscles (effectors), which contract rapidly — shivering. More respiration transfers more energy as heat, so body temperature returns towards 37 °C.

**Data skills.** Expect graphs of body temperature or blood glucose against time. Describe the trend with values, identify where a change is detected and when the response begins, and calculate changes, e.g. blood glucose rising from 5.0 to 8.5 mmol/dm³ is a rise of $\frac{8.5 - 5.0}{5.0} \times 100 = 70\%$.

::: terms
- **Homeostasis**: the regulation of the internal conditions of a cell or organism to maintain optimum conditions for function.
- **Stimulus**: a change in the environment, inside or outside the body, that is detected by receptors.
- **Receptor**: a cell that detects a stimulus, such as a temperature receptor in the skin.
- **Coordination centre**: an organ such as the brain, spinal cord or pancreas that receives and processes information from receptors.
- **Effector**: a muscle or gland that brings about a response to restore optimum levels.
- **Response**: the action carried out by an effector, such as a muscle contracting or a gland secreting a hormone.
- **Optimum conditions**: the conditions at which enzymes and cells work best, such as a body temperature of about 37 °C.
- **Hormone**: a chemical messenger secreted by a gland and carried in the blood, used in chemical control systems.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Define / explain what is meant by homeostasis** — use AQA's wording: *regulation of internal conditions … to maintain optimum conditions for function … in response to internal and external changes*. "Keeping things the same" alone scores little.
- **Explain why homeostasis is important** — link to **enzymes** (optimum temperature/pH; denaturing) and **cell functions** (osmosis, glucose for respiration).
- **Describe the control system** — name the parts in order: stimulus → **receptor** → **coordination centre** → **effector** → response. Each part has a specific job; say what it does, not just its name.
- **Identify** — in an unfamiliar example, pick out which part is the receptor, coordination centre and effector. Effectors are **only muscles or glands**.
- **Suggest** — apply the pattern to a new situation (e.g. a fish detecting low oxygen, a thermostat in a house as an analogy).

**Extended answers.** A 4–6-mark "describe how the body responds to …" answer gains marks for each link in the chain, for saying that the response **restores** the optimum, and for being specific (e.g. "sweat glands secrete more sweat; evaporation transfers energy from the skin").

**Graphs and data.** Describe the pattern with values and times; say where the condition moves away from the optimum and where the response brings it back. Calculate changes as differences or percentages: $\frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}} \times 100$.
:::

::: example Definition
Explain what is meant by homeostasis. (2)

The regulation of the internal conditions of the body (or a cell) (1) to maintain optimum conditions for function, in response to internal and external changes (1).
:::

::: example Importance
Explain why it is important to keep body temperature close to 37 °C. (3)

Enzymes have an optimum temperature close to 37 °C (1). If the temperature rises too far, enzymes denature because the active site changes shape (1). If it falls too far, enzyme-controlled reactions such as respiration become too slow for cells to function (1).
:::

::: example Identify the parts
After a meal, cells in the pancreas detect a rise in blood glucose. The pancreas releases insulin, and liver cells take up glucose. Identify the receptor, coordination centre and effector. (3)

Receptor: cells in the pancreas that detect glucose (1). Coordination centre: the pancreas (1). Effector: the liver (accept: the pancreas as a gland releasing insulin) (1).
:::

::: example Control chain
Describe how the body responds when its temperature falls on a cold day. (4)

Temperature receptors in the skin detect the fall (stimulus) (1). Information is sent (as nerve impulses) to the brain, the coordination centre, which processes it (1). The brain sends impulses to skeletal muscles, the effectors (1), which contract rapidly (shiver), so more respiration transfers energy that warms the body back towards 37 °C (1).
:::

::: example Calculation
A person's blood glucose concentration was 4.8 mmol/dm³ before a meal and 7.2 mmol/dm³ one hour later. Calculate the percentage increase. (2)

$\frac{7.2 - 4.8}{4.8} \times 100$ (1) $= 50\%$ (1).
:::

::: watch
- Define homeostasis with **"optimum conditions for function"** and **"internal and external changes"** — "keeping the body the same" is too vague.
- Link importance to **enzymes**: they **denature** at high temperature (never "die") and work too slowly when cold.
- **Effectors are muscles or glands only.** The brain is a coordination centre, not an effector; a receptor is a **cell**, not an organ.
- Keep the chain in order: **stimulus → receptor → coordination centre → effector → response**, and say the response **restores the optimum level**.
- The detailed mechanisms (thermoregulation, kidneys and ADH) are later topics and are **separate science only**; blood glucose control by insulin is in both courses.
:::

::: test
A graph shows a person's blood glucose over 4 hours. It rises from 5 mmol/dm³ to 9 mmol/dm³ in the hour after a meal, then falls back to 5 mmol/dm³ by 3 hours and stays there. Explain the shape of the graph in terms of homeostasis, and suggest why the level does not keep falling below 5 mmol/dm³. (4)
---
Glucose is absorbed from the digested meal into the blood, so the concentration rises (1). The rise is detected (by the pancreas, the coordination centre) and a response — release of insulin — makes cells take up and store glucose, so it falls (1). The response restores the optimum level (1). Once it is back at the optimum the response stops / a fall below the optimum would trigger the opposite response, so the level stays close to 5 mmol/dm³ (1).
:::
