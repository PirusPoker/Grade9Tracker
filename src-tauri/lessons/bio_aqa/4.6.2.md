## What it is

This lesson covers spec subsection **4.6.2 Variation and evolution**: variation (4.6.2.1), evolution (4.6.2.2), selective breeding (4.6.2.3), genetic engineering (4.6.2.4) and cloning (4.6.2.5). Points 4.6.2.1–4.6.2.4 are in **both Biology 8461 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464** (same numbers). **Cloning (4.6.2.5) is separate science only.** The **main steps of genetic engineering are Higher tier only**.

### Variation

**Variation** is the differences in the characteristics of individuals in a population. It may be due to differences in:

- **the genes they have inherited** — *genetic causes* (e.g. blood group, eye colour, cystic fibrosis);
- **the conditions in which they have developed** — *environmental causes* (e.g. scars, language spoken, a plant in shade growing pale);
- **a combination of genes and the environment** — most characteristics (e.g. height and body mass: genes set a potential range, diet and health decide where you end up).

**How the genome and the environment produce the phenotype:** the genome (all the alleles) provides the instructions for proteins; the environment affects whether and how those instructions are used and how well the organism grows. Identical twins share the same genome, so differences between them must be environmental.

Key facts AQA wants you to state:

- There is usually **extensive genetic variation within a population** of a species.
- **All variants arise from mutations.** A **mutation** is a change in the DNA base sequence.
  - **Most** mutations have **no effect** on the phenotype;
  - **some** **influence** the phenotype;
  - **very few** **determine** the phenotype.
- Mutations occur **continuously**. Very rarely a mutation leads to a **new phenotype**. If that new phenotype is suited to an **environmental change**, it can lead to a **relatively rapid change in the species** (natural selection acts strongly on it).

### Evolution by natural selection

**Evolution** is **a change in the inherited characteristics of a population over time through a process of natural selection, which may result in the formation of a new species.**

The **theory of evolution by natural selection** states that **all species of living things have evolved from simple life forms that first developed more than three billion years ago.**

**How natural selection works** — learn this as a sequence (it is worth up to 6 marks):

1. **Variation**: individuals in a population show variation because of differences in their alleles (originally from **mutations**).
2. **Competition / selection pressure**: there is competition for resources (food, mates, territory), or a pressure such as a predator, disease or a change in climate.
3. **Survival of the best suited**: individuals with the **phenotype best suited to the environment** are **more likely to survive and reproduce**.
4. **Inheritance**: they **pass on their alleles** to their offspring.
5. **Over many generations** the advantageous allele becomes **more common** in the population.

**Example — peppered moths.** Before the Industrial Revolution most moths were pale and camouflaged on lichen-covered trees. Soot blackened the trunks; a mutation for dark wings already existed. Dark moths were now better camouflaged, so birds ate fewer of them; they survived to breed and passed on the dark allele, so the dark form became common in industrial areas.

**New species.** If **two populations of one species become so different in phenotype that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring**, they have formed **two new species**. (How this happens — isolation — is covered in speciation, 4.6.3.)

### Selective breeding (artificial selection)

**Selective breeding** is the process by which **humans breed plants and animals for particular genetic characteristics**. Humans have done this for **thousands of years**, since they first bred food crops from wild plants and domesticated animals.

**The process:**
1. Choose **parents with the desired characteristic** from a mixed population.
2. **Breed them together.**
3. From the offspring, choose those **with the desired characteristic** and breed them together.
4. **Repeat over many generations** until **all the offspring show the desired characteristic**.

**Characteristics chosen for usefulness or appearance:**
- **disease resistance in food crops**;
- **animals that produce more meat or milk**;
- **domestic dogs with a gentle nature**;
- **large or unusual flowers**.

**Impact on food plants and domesticated animals:** higher yields, more nutritious or tastier crops, crops that survive local conditions; but genetic variation in the population falls.

**Risks — inbreeding.** Breeding closely related individuals **reduces the gene pool (variation)**. Some breeds become **particularly prone to disease or inherited defects**, because harmful recessive alleles are more likely to be inherited from both parents (e.g. breathing problems in pugs and bulldogs, hip dysplasia in some large dog breeds). Low variation also means that if a **new disease** arrives, few or none of the population may be resistant. Ethical issues: animal welfare when breeds suffer for appearance.

### Genetic engineering

**Genetic engineering** is a process which involves **modifying the genome of an organism by introducing a gene from another organism to give a desired characteristic**. Genes from the chromosomes of humans and other organisms can be **"cut out" and transferred to cells of other organisms**.

**Uses:**
- **Plant crops** engineered to be **resistant to diseases** or to produce **bigger, better fruits**.
- **Bacterial cells** engineered to produce **useful substances such as human insulin** to treat diabetes.
- **GM crops** — crops whose genes have been modified in this way — include those **resistant to insect attack** or **to herbicides**. GM crops **generally show increased yields**.
- **Medicine**: research is exploring **genetic modification to overcome some inherited disorders** (gene therapy, e.g. for cystic fibrosis).

**Benefits and risks:**

| Benefits | Risks and objections |
|---|---|
| Higher crop yields to feed a growing population; less land needed. | **Effect on populations of wild flowers and insects** — e.g. herbicide-resistant genes spreading to wild relatives; fewer insects that feed on crop plants. |
| Less spraying of pesticides on insect-resistant crops. | Some people feel **the effects of eating GM crops on human health have not been fully explored**. |
| Crops with added nutrients (e.g. golden rice with a vitamin A precursor). | Some people object for ethical or religious reasons ("playing God"); concerns about large companies owning seed patents. |
| Cheap, plentiful human insulin with no animal-rights issues; possible cures for inherited disorders. | Gene therapy is experimental — long-term effects unknown. |

### The main steps of genetic engineering (Higher tier)

1. **Enzymes are used to isolate the required gene** (restriction enzymes cut it out of the DNA).
2. The gene is **inserted into a vector** — **usually a bacterial plasmid or a virus**. (Ligase enzymes join the gene into the plasmid, which has been cut with the same restriction enzyme so the "sticky ends" match.)
3. **The vector is used to insert the gene into the required cells.**
4. **Genes are transferred to the cells of animals, plants or microorganisms at an early stage in their development** so that they **develop with the desired characteristics** — every cell that grows from that cell contains the new gene.

For insulin: the human insulin gene is cut out, inserted into a plasmid, the plasmid is taken up by bacteria, and the bacteria are grown in large fermenters where they make human insulin that is extracted and purified.

### Separate science only: Cloning

Clones are **genetically identical** organisms produced by asexual reproduction (links to 4.6.1.3).

| Method | How it works | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Tissue culture** | Small groups of cells from part of a plant are grown on a nutrient medium (with plant hormones) into identical new plants. | **Preserving rare plant species**; **commercially in nurseries** to make many identical plants quickly. |
| **Cuttings** | An older, simple method: a piece of stem with leaves is cut and planted (often dipped in rooting powder) to grow into a new plant. | Gardeners making **many identical plants from a parent plant**. |
| **Embryo transplants** | **Cells are split apart from a developing animal embryo before they become specialised**, then the **identical embryos are transplanted into host mothers**. | Producing many calves from a prize cow and bull. |
| **Adult cell cloning** | See the steps below. | Cloning an adult animal with known features (e.g. Dolly the sheep, 1996). |

**Adult cell cloning — the steps:**
1. **The nucleus is removed from an unfertilised egg cell.**
2. **The nucleus from an adult body cell, such as a skin cell, is inserted into the egg cell.**
3. **An electric shock stimulates the egg cell to divide to form an embryo.**
4. **These embryo cells contain the same genetic information as the adult skin cell.**
5. **When the embryo has developed into a ball of cells, it is inserted into the womb of an adult female** to continue its development.

The clone is genetically identical to the animal that donated the **body-cell nucleus** — not to the egg donor or the surrogate mother.

**Benefits and risks of cloning in agriculture and medicine:**
- *Benefits*: rapid production of many animals or plants with desired features; saving endangered species; possible production of tissues and organs; preserving rare plants.
- *Risks*: **reduced genetic variation**, so one disease could wipe out the whole population; cloned animals may have **health problems** and shorter lives; it is inefficient (many attempts fail).
- *Ethical objections*: some people feel it is wrong to clone animals and fear it could lead to human cloning.

::: terms
- **Variation**: differences in the characteristics of individuals in a population, caused by genes, the environment or both.
- **Mutation**: a change in the DNA base sequence; the source of all genetic variants.
- **Evolution**: a change in the inherited characteristics of a population over time through natural selection.
- **Natural selection**: the process by which individuals best suited to their environment survive and reproduce, passing on their alleles.
- **Selective breeding**: humans choosing parents with desired characteristics and breeding them over many generations (artificial selection).
- **Inbreeding**: breeding closely related individuals, which reduces variation and makes inherited defects and disease more likely.
- **Genetic engineering**: modifying the genome of an organism by introducing a gene from another organism to give a desired characteristic.
- **Vector**: a bacterial plasmid or virus used to carry a gene into the required cells.
- **Plasmid**: a small ring of DNA found in bacteria, often used as a vector in genetic engineering.
- **Adult cell cloning**: inserting the nucleus of an adult body cell into an enucleated egg cell to grow a genetically identical animal.
:::

::: method
**Command words on this topic**
- **Describe evolution** — use the exact definition: "a change in the **inherited characteristics** of a **population** over time through **natural selection**". Individuals do not evolve; populations do.
- **Explain how [a feature] evolved** — the five-step chain every time: **variation (mutation) → selection pressure → best suited survive and reproduce → pass on alleles → allele becomes more common over many generations**. Use the context: name the feature, the pressure and the advantage.
- **Describe selective breeding** — choose parents with the trait → breed → select offspring with the trait → breed them → **repeat for many generations**.
- **Describe the main steps of genetic engineering (Higher tier)** — enzymes isolate the gene → insert into vector (plasmid or virus) → vector inserts gene into cells → done at an early stage of development so all cells have the gene.
- **Evaluate GM crops / cloning / selective breeding** — at least two benefits and two risks, use any data given, and a conclusion. Include "some people have objections" points: ethics, unknown long-term effects, effects on wildlife.
- **Compare natural and artificial selection** — both select individuals to breed and change allele frequencies over generations; in natural selection the **environment** chooses (traits that aid survival), in selective breeding **humans** choose (traits useful or attractive to us), often faster.

**6-mark answers.** Common 6-markers: "Explain how antibiotic resistance / a camouflage colour evolved" (natural selection chain in context), "Describe how scientists produce bacteria that make human insulin" (Higher-tier steps plus growing the bacteria), and "Evaluate the use of GM crops" (benefits, concerns, conclusion). Level 3 needs a full logical sequence — a missing step (usually "pass on alleles to offspring") holds you in Level 2.

**Words that lose marks.** Never write that organisms "adapt", "decide", "try" or "need to" change, or that a mutation happened *because* the environment changed. Mutations are random and already present; the environment only selects among them.
:::

::: example Natural selection in context
Explain how giraffes with long necks could have evolved from ancestors with shorter necks. (5)

There was variation in neck length in the ancestral population, caused by mutations / different alleles (1). When food was scarce, there was competition for leaves (1). Giraffes with longer necks could reach leaves higher up, so were more likely to survive and reproduce (1). They passed on the alleles for long necks to their offspring (1). Over many generations, the alleles for long necks became more common in the population (1).
:::

::: example Selective breeding
A farmer wants a herd of cows that produce more milk. Describe how she could use selective breeding to do this. (4)

Choose the cows that produce the most milk and a bull whose mother/daughters produced a lot of milk (1). Breed them together (1). From the offspring, select the cows that produce the most milk and breed them with high-yield bulls (1). Repeat over many generations until all offspring produce high milk yields (1).
:::

::: example Insulin from bacteria (Higher tier)
Describe how genetic engineering is used to make bacteria that produce human insulin. (4)

Enzymes are used to cut the insulin gene out of human DNA (1). The gene is inserted into a vector, a bacterial plasmid (1). The plasmid is used to insert the gene into bacterial cells (1). The bacteria multiply and make human insulin, which is extracted and purified (1).
:::

::: example Separate science only: adult cell cloning
A scientist clones a sheep using a skin cell from sheep A, an egg cell from sheep B and a surrogate mother, sheep C. Explain which sheep the clone is genetically identical to. (2)

Sheep A (1). The nucleus, which contains the genetic material, came from sheep A's skin cell; sheep B's nucleus was removed and sheep C only provided the womb (1).
:::

::: example Mutation statement
State how variants arise, and what effect most of them have on the phenotype. (2)

All variants arise from mutations (1). Most mutations have no effect on the phenotype (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Populations evolve, not individuals.** Never say an organism "adapted to survive" or "mutated because it needed to" — the mutation was already there by chance.
- The natural-selection chain loses most marks at **"passes on the allele to offspring"** and **"over many generations"** — write both every time.
- Selective breeding: "breed them **over many generations**" and "select **offspring** with the trait" are the marking points.
- **(Higher tier)** Vectors are a **plasmid or a virus**; genes go in **at an early stage of development**.
- **Separate science only:** in adult cell cloning, the clone matches the donor of the **body-cell nucleus**, and an **electric shock** makes the egg divide.
:::

::: test
A farmer grew a GM insect-resistant variety of maize in one field and a non-GM variety in a similar field. The non-GM maize yielded 8.0 tonnes per hectare and the GM maize yielded 9.4 tonnes per hectare. Calculate the percentage increase in yield. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. (3)

::: test
A rare orchid is threatened with extinction. Separate science only: a nursery plans to use tissue culture to grow thousands of plants and replant them in the wild. Evaluate this plan. (4)
---

::: test
Some strains of rice have been selectively bred and others genetically modified to resist a fungal disease. Compare selective breeding and genetic engineering, and evaluate the use of these techniques to produce disease-resistant food crops. (6)
---
Indicative content — comparison:
- both change the genetic make-up of the crop to give a desired characteristic
- selective breeding uses existing variation within a species; genetic engineering transfers a gene from another organism, possibly another species
- selective breeding takes many generations; genetic engineering is faster
- (Higher tier) genetic engineering: enzymes isolate gene, vector (plasmid/virus), inserted at early stage of development

Indicative content — evaluation:
- benefits: higher yields, less crop loss, less fungicide sprayed, more food for a growing population
- risks of selective breeding: inbreeding, reduced variation, crop vulnerable to a new disease
- risks of GM: effects on wild flowers and insects; health effects of eating GM crops not fully explored; ethical objections
- conclusion, e.g. both are useful; GM is quicker but needs monitoring

Level 3 (5–6 marks): clear comparison with at least one similarity and one difference, and a balanced evaluation with a justified conclusion.
Level 2 (3–4 marks): some comparison or description of one technique, with some benefits or risks.
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple statements about one technique.
0 marks: nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
A mutation arises in a population of beetles that gives a new, darker body colour. Explain why this mutation might lead to a relatively rapid change in the species, and why most mutations do not. (3)
---
Most mutations have no effect on the phenotype, or only influence it, so they are not selected for (1). If the new dark phenotype is suited to an environmental change (e.g. darker surroundings) (1), beetles with it are much more likely to survive and reproduce, so the allele spreads quickly through the population (1).
:::
