## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.3.1.1 to 4.3.1.5: what pathogens are, how they spread and how spread is reduced, and the named viral, bacterial, fungal and protist diseases. All of it is in both GCSE Biology and Combined Science: Trilogy, and none of it is Higher-tier only.

### Pathogens and communicable disease

**Pathogens are microorganisms that cause infectious disease.** Pathogens may be **viruses, bacteria, protists or fungi**. They may infect **plants or animals**. A disease caused by a pathogen is **communicable** — it can be passed from one organism to another.

Pathogens **can be spread by direct contact, by water or by air**:

| Route | How | Examples from the spec |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct contact** | touching, sexual contact, exchange of body fluids, contact with infected plant material | HIV, gonorrhoea, TMV |
| **Water** | drinking or contact with contaminated water; splashes of water carrying spores | rose black spot (spores in water) |
| **Air** | droplets from coughs and sneezes; spores carried by wind | measles, rose black spot (wind) |
| **Food** (ingested) | eating contaminated or undercooked food | *Salmonella* |
| **Vectors** | an animal that carries a pathogen between hosts without being harmed itself | malaria (mosquito) |

How pathogens make us ill:

- **Bacteria and viruses may reproduce rapidly inside the body.**
- **Bacteria may produce poisons (toxins) that damage tissues and make us feel ill.**
- **Viruses live and reproduce inside cells, causing cell damage** — the virus takes over the cell to make copies of itself, then the cell bursts, releasing new viruses that infect more cells.

| Pathogen | Size and structure | How it causes disease |
|---|---|---|
| **Virus** | very small; not a cell — genetic material in a protein coat | reproduces **inside** host cells, damaging them |
| **Bacterium** | single-celled, prokaryotic, much larger than a virus | reproduces rapidly by splitting in two; releases **toxins** |
| **Fungus** | single-celled (yeasts) or made of thread-like hyphae; spread by **spores** | grows on or in tissues |
| **Protist** | single-celled eukaryote | often carried by a **vector**; lives in host cells or blood |

Worked mini-example (rapid reproduction): if a bacterium divides every 20 minutes, after 2 hours (6 divisions) one bacterium becomes $2^6 = 64$. This is why bacterial numbers — and symptoms — build up so quickly. (Calculating cell numbers from a mean division time is taught in 4.1.1.8, which is **Separate science only**.)

### Reducing or preventing the spread of disease

You must be able to explain how spread can be reduced. Match each method to the route it blocks:

- **Hygiene** — hand washing, disinfecting surfaces, cooking food thoroughly, keeping raw meat separate → blocks direct contact and food routes.
- **Isolating infected individuals** → prevents passing on by contact or droplets.
- **Destroying or controlling vectors** (e.g. stopping mosquitoes breeding, insecticides, nets) → breaks the vector's link between hosts.
- **Vaccination** → people (or animals, such as poultry) become immune, so the pathogen cannot spread through them.
- **Barrier contraception** (condoms) → prevents spread of sexually transmitted infections.
- For plants: **removing and destroying infected leaves or plants**, chemical treatments (fungicides), crop rotation, controlling insect vectors.

### Viral diseases

**Measles** (humans)
- Symptoms: **fever and a red skin rash**.
- A **serious illness that can be fatal** if complications arise, so **most young children are vaccinated** against it.
- Spread: **inhalation of droplets from sneezes and coughs** (air).
- No specific treatment — prevention by vaccination.

**HIV** (humans)
- Initially causes a **flu-like illness**.
- **Unless successfully controlled with antiretroviral drugs**, the virus **attacks the body's immune cells**.
- **Late stage HIV infection, or AIDS**, occurs when the immune system is so badly damaged it **can no longer deal with other infections or cancers**.
- Spread: **sexual contact** or **exchange of body fluids such as blood**, which occurs when **drug users share needles**. (Also from mother to baby.)
- Control: antiretroviral drugs, condoms, not sharing needles, screening blood products.

**Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)** (plants)
- A widespread plant pathogen affecting **many species of plants including tomatoes**.
- Gives a distinctive **'mosaic' pattern of discolouration on the leaves**.
- This **affects the growth of the plant due to lack of photosynthesis** — the discoloured parts have less chlorophyll, so less light is absorbed, less glucose is made, and the plant grows less and gives a smaller crop.
- Spread: direct contact between plants, on hands and tools, and by insects. Control: remove infected plants, wash hands and tools, grow resistant varieties.

### Bacterial diseases

***Salmonella* food poisoning** (humans)
- Spread by **bacteria ingested in food, or on food prepared in unhygienic conditions** (e.g. undercooked poultry or eggs).
- Symptoms: **fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea**, caused by **the bacteria and the toxins they secrete**.
- Control: **in the UK, poultry are vaccinated against *Salmonella***; cook food thoroughly; good kitchen hygiene; keep raw and cooked food apart.

**Gonorrhoea** (humans)
- A **sexually transmitted disease (STD)**.
- Symptoms: **thick yellow or green discharge from the vagina or penis**, and **pain on urinating**.
- Caused by a **bacterium**; **easily treated with penicillin until many resistant strains appeared**.
- Spread by **sexual contact**.
- Control: **treatment with antibiotics** (a different antibiotic now that many strains resist penicillin) or the use of a **barrier method of contraception such as a condom**. Contact tracing also helps.

### Fungal disease

**Rose black spot** (plants)
- **Purple or black spots develop on leaves**, which **often turn yellow and drop early**.
- It **affects the growth of the plant as photosynthesis is reduced** (less leaf area with chlorophyll).
- **Spread in the environment by water or wind** (spores).
- Treated by **using fungicides and/or removing and destroying the affected leaves** (burning them so spores cannot spread).

### Protist disease

**Malaria** (humans)
- **The pathogens that cause malaria are protists.**
- **The malarial protist has a life cycle that includes the mosquito.** The female mosquito is the **vector**: when it bites an infected person it takes up the protist, which develops inside the mosquito and is passed to the next person it bites.
- Malaria causes **recurrent episodes of fever** and **can be fatal**.
- **Control**: **preventing the vectors, mosquitoes, from breeding** (draining standing water where larvae develop, insecticides) and **using mosquito nets** (often insecticide-treated) **to avoid being bitten**. Antimalarial drugs can be taken too.

| Disease | Pathogen | Host | Spread | Control / treatment |

| TMV | virus | plants (e.g. tomato) | contact, insects | remove infected plants; hygiene |

**Evaluating data (WS 1.4)**: you may be given data on cases of a disease before and after a control measure (e.g. a vaccination programme or mosquito nets). Describe the trend with figures, calculate a change or percentage change, and consider other factors that might also have changed.

::: terms
- **Pathogen**: a microorganism, such as a virus, bacterium, protist or fungus, that causes infectious disease.
- **Communicable disease**: an infectious disease caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one organism to another.
- **Toxin**: a poison produced by bacteria that damages tissues and makes us feel ill.
- **Vector**: an organism, such as the mosquito in malaria, that carries a pathogen from one host to another.
- **Antiretroviral drugs**: drugs used to control HIV so that the virus does not destroy the immune system.
- **AIDS**: late-stage HIV infection, when the immune system is so damaged it can no longer deal with other infections or cancers.
- **Fungicide**: a chemical used to kill fungi, such as the fungus that causes rose black spot.
- **Spores**: tiny reproductive structures released by fungi that are spread by wind or water.
- **Sexually transmitted disease**: an infection spread by sexual contact, such as gonorrhoea or HIV.
:::

::: method
1. **Command words.** *State* or *name* = a short fact (the pathogen type, a symptom). *Describe* how a disease spreads = the route and the process. *Explain* how spread is reduced = the method AND why it works (what link in the chain it breaks). *Suggest* = apply to an unfamiliar disease using the same principles. *Evaluate* a control method = pros, cons, conclusion.
2. **Learn each disease as five facts**: pathogen type, host, symptoms, how it spreads, how it is controlled or treated. Most marks come from exactly these words in the spec.
3. **"How does it make you ill?"** Bacteria → reproduce rapidly and release **toxins** that damage tissues. Viruses → reproduce **inside cells**, damaging or bursting them.
4. **Plant disease questions** (TMV, black spot): the chain is discolouration / spots → less chlorophyll or fewer leaves → **less photosynthesis** → less glucose → **reduced growth**.
5. **Control methods**: always link to the route. Mosquito nets stop the **vector biting**; draining water stops it **breeding**; condoms are a **barrier**; vaccination makes people **immune**; removing infected leaves removes the **source of spores**.
6. **Data questions**: describe the trend with numbers, calculate the change, and remember other factors (e.g. better hygiene, treatment) may also explain a fall in cases.
7. **4- and 6-mark answers** (compare two diseases, explain how to control an outbreak) are levels-marked: cover several diseases or methods, each with a reason.
:::

::: example Explain — TMV
Explain why a tomato plant infected with tobacco mosaic virus produces fewer tomatoes. (3)

TMV causes a mosaic pattern of discolouration on the leaves, where there is less chlorophyll (1). So less light is absorbed and the rate of photosynthesis falls (1). Less glucose is made for growth / for making fruit, so the plant grows less and produces fewer tomatoes (1).
:::

::: example Describe — HIV
Describe how HIV infection progresses if it is not treated, and how it is spread. (4)

It first causes a flu-like illness (1). The virus then attacks the body's immune cells (1). Eventually the immune system is so badly damaged that it cannot deal with other infections or cancers — late stage HIV / AIDS (1). It is spread by sexual contact or by exchange of body fluids such as blood, e.g. when drug users share needles (1).
:::

::: example Explain — controlling malaria
Explain two ways the spread of malaria can be reduced. (4)

Stop mosquitoes breeding, e.g. by draining standing water or using insecticide on the water where larvae develop (1); this reduces the number of vectors, so fewer people are bitten and infected (1). Use mosquito nets over beds (1), which stop mosquitoes biting people at night, so the protist is not passed from person to person (1).
:::

::: example Compare — Salmonella and gonorrhoea
Compare *Salmonella* food poisoning and gonorrhoea. (4)

Both are caused by bacteria (1). *Salmonella* is spread by eating contaminated food, whereas gonorrhoea is spread by sexual contact (1). *Salmonella* causes fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea, whereas gonorrhoea causes a thick yellow or green discharge and pain on urinating (1). *Salmonella* is controlled by vaccinating poultry and hygiene, whereas gonorrhoea is controlled with antibiotics and condoms (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — data on disease
In one region, there were 840 cases of measles in 2010 and 126 cases in 2020 after a vaccination campaign. Calculate the percentage decrease. (2)

Decrease $= 840 - 126 = 714$ (1). $\dfrac{714}{840} \times 100 = 85\%$ (1).
:::

::: watch
- Malaria is caused by a **protist**, not a bacterium or virus. The mosquito is the **vector**, not the pathogen.
- Rose black spot is a **fungus**; TMV is a **virus**. Both reduce growth because of **less photosynthesis** — say so.
- *Salmonella* symptoms come from **the bacteria and the toxins they secrete** — "toxins" is often the mark.
- Gonorrhoea: "resistant to **penicillin**", not "immune to antibiotics". Bacteria become **resistant**; people become **immune**.
- HIV and AIDS are not the same: AIDS is **late stage** HIV infection, when the immune system can no longer cope.
- For control methods, always say **which part of the spread** the method blocks.
:::

::: test
A bacterium divides every 30 minutes. Starting with one bacterium, calculate how many there would be after 4 hours, and explain why symptoms of a bacterial infection can appear quickly. (3)
---
4 hours = 8 divisions (1); $2^8 = 256$ bacteria (1). Bacteria reproduce rapidly inside the body, so large numbers quickly build up and release toxins that damage tissues (1).

::: test
An outbreak of a new disease is spreading in a city. Scientists do not yet know whether it is caused by a bacterium, a virus or a protist. Explain the methods that could be used to reduce its spread, and how each would work for different possible routes of transmission. (6)
---
Indicative content: if spread through the air by droplets — isolate infected people, wear masks, cover coughs and sneezes, ventilate rooms; if spread by direct contact or body fluids — hand washing, disinfecting surfaces, condoms, not sharing needles; if spread in food or water — cook food thoroughly, hygienic food preparation, treat or boil drinking water; if spread by a vector such as an insect — kill the vector or stop it breeding, use nets or repellents; vaccination (once a vaccine is developed) makes people immune so the pathogen cannot pass through them; treat infected people (antibiotics only if bacterial); each method breaks a link between infected and uninfected people.

Level 3 (5–6): a range of methods matched to different routes of transmission, each with a clear explanation of how it reduces spread. Level 2 (3–4): several methods with some explanation, possibly not linked to routes. Level 1 (1–2): simple list of methods with little explanation. 0: nothing worthy of credit.
