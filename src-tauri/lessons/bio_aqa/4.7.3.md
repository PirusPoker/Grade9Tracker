## What it is

Humans change ecosystems more than any other species. This topic covers what biodiversity is and why it matters, then the human activities that reduce it (pollution, land use, peat extraction, deforestation, global warming) and the programmes that try to protect it. It is the same for AQA Biology 8461 and Combined Science Trilogy 8464, with no Higher-tier-only content and no required practical. Much of the exam is about **using data** and **evaluating** conflicting needs.

### Biodiversity

**Biodiversity** is the variety of all the different species of organisms on Earth, or within an ecosystem.

A **great biodiversity ensures the stability of ecosystems** by reducing the dependence of one species on another for food, shelter and the maintenance of the physical environment. If a woodland bird eats twenty kinds of insect, the loss of one insect species hardly matters; if it eats only one, the loss could wipe the bird out.

**The future of the human species on Earth relies on us maintaining a good level of biodiversity.** We depend on other species for food, medicines (many drugs were first found in plants), materials, pollination of crops, clean water and air, and soil fertility. Many human activities are reducing biodiversity, and **only recently** have measures been taken to try to stop this reduction.

### Waste management

Rapid **growth in the human population** and an **increase in the standard of living** mean that more resources are used and more waste is produced. Unless waste and chemical materials are properly handled, more pollution is caused.

| Where | Sources of pollution | Effect on organisms |
|---|---|---|
| **Water** | sewage, fertiliser (run-off from fields), toxic chemicals (e.g. from industry, pesticides) | fertiliser and sewage cause algal growth that blocks light; plants die and bacteria decaying them use up the oxygen, so fish and invertebrates die; toxic chemicals poison organisms and can build up along food chains |
| **Air** | smoke, acidic gases (sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels) | smoke blocks light and damages lungs; acidic gases dissolve in rain to form **acid rain**, which damages trees and makes lakes too acidic for fish and other life |
| **Land** | landfill, toxic chemicals (pesticides, herbicides, industrial waste) | landfill destroys habitats; toxic chemicals can leach into soil and water and kill plants and animals |

**Pollution kills plants and animals, which can reduce biodiversity.** It hits the most sensitive species first, so a polluted river may contain only a few tolerant species.

### Land use

Humans **reduce the land available for other animals and plants** by **building, quarrying, farming and dumping waste**. Each of these removes habitats, so fewer species can live there.

**Peat bogs.** Peat forms in waterlogged, acidic bogs where there is too little oxygen for dead plants (mainly sphagnum moss) to decay fully, so carbon is stored for thousands of years. Peat is dug up to sell as cheap **garden compost** (and in some countries burned as fuel).

- Destroying peat bogs reduces the area of this habitat and so reduces the **variety of plant, animal and microorganism species** that live there: **biodiversity falls**.
- The **decay or burning of peat releases carbon dioxide** into the atmosphere, adding to global warming. Drained peat dries out, oxygen gets in, and microorganisms decay it aerobically.

**The conflict (WS 1.4, 1.5):** farmers and gardeners want cheap, available compost to increase food production, while conservationists want peat bogs conserved as habitats and as carbon stores. Peat-free composts (made from bark, coir, green waste) are the usual compromise, though they may cost more or give different results.

### Deforestation

Large-scale **deforestation** in tropical areas has occurred to:

- provide land for **cattle** and **rice fields** (food for a growing population)
- grow crops for **biofuels** (e.g. sugar cane or oil palm, used to make fuels).

**Environmental implications:**

| Effect | Why |
|---|---|
| **reduced biodiversity** | tropical rainforests have huge numbers of species; habitats and food sources are destroyed, and a monoculture crop supports very few species |
| **more carbon dioxide in the atmosphere** | trees are often burned to clear land (combustion releases CO₂), and dead wood decays as microorganisms respire |
| **less carbon dioxide removed** | fewer trees photosynthesise, so less CO₂ is taken out of the air and stored as wood |
| soil erosion and changes to the water cycle | without roots and leaf cover, rain washes soil away; less transpiration means less local rainfall |

The arguments for deforestation are economic and social: jobs, income, food and fuel for local people and countries. An evaluation should weigh these against the environmental costs.

### Global warming

**Levels of carbon dioxide and methane in the atmosphere are increasing**, and they contribute to **global warming**. (Burning fossil fuels and deforestation raise carbon dioxide; cattle, rice fields and landfill raise methane.)

**Biological consequences** you should be able to describe:

- **Loss of habitat**: low-lying areas flood as sea levels rise (ice melts and water expands); polar ice and cold mountain habitats shrink.
- **Changes in the distribution of species**: species that need warmth spread into areas that used to be too cold (towards the poles or uphill); species that need cold lose their range and may become extinct locally.
- **Changes in migration patterns**: birds migrate at different times or to different places.
- **Reduced biodiversity**, as species that cannot move or adapt quickly enough die out.
- **Effects on food and disease**: crop-growing areas change; pests and disease-carrying insects (e.g. mosquitoes) spread into new regions.

**How we know (WS 1.6, 1.3):** the scientific consensus about global warming and climate change is based on **systematic reviews of thousands of peer-reviewed publications**. Evidence about the exact effects is still **uncertain or incomplete** in places, because the climate is a complex system with many interacting factors, predictions rely on models, and long-term records from the past are indirect (ice cores, tree rings). Uncertainty about details does not mean uncertainty about the overall trend.

### Maintaining biodiversity

Human interactions can be **negative** (pollution, habitat destruction, introducing invasive species, over-fishing) or **positive**. Scientists and concerned citizens have put in place programmes to reduce the negative effects of humans on ecosystems and biodiversity:

| Programme | How it helps biodiversity | Problems or conflicts |
|---|---|---|
| **breeding programmes for endangered species** | captive breeding in zoos increases numbers; animals may be released back into the wild; keeps genetic variety | expensive; captive animals may not survive in the wild; their habitat may no longer exist |
| **protection and regeneration of rare habitats** | nature reserves, protected peat bogs, coral reefs and mangroves; replanting habitats lets species return | land cannot be used for farming or building; needs policing and money |
| **reintroduction of field margins and hedgerows** where farmers grow only one type of crop (monoculture) | provide habitats, food and corridors for insects, birds and small mammals; wild flowers support pollinators | less land for crops, so lower yield and income for the farmer |
| **reduction of deforestation and CO₂ emissions** by some governments | slows habitat loss and global warming | costs money and jobs; needs international agreement; hard to enforce |
| **recycling resources rather than dumping in landfill** | less land used for landfill; fewer raw materials extracted (less quarrying, mining, logging) | collection and sorting cost energy and money; relies on people taking part |

### Using data (MS and WS)

Exam questions give graphs or tables. *Mini-example:* a forest covered 4.0 million km² in 1990 and 3.4 million km² in 2020. Percentage decrease = (4.0 − 3.4) ÷ 4.0 × 100 = 0.6 ÷ 4.0 × 100 = **15 %**. Mean rate of loss = 0.6 million km² ÷ 30 years = **0.02 million km² per year** (20 000 km² per year).

When asked to evaluate, describe the trend with numbers, give advantages and disadvantages (biological, economic, social), and finish with a justified conclusion.

::: terms
- **Biodiversity**: the variety of all the different species of organisms on Earth, or within an ecosystem.
- **Pollution**: harmful substances released into water, air or land that can kill plants and animals.
- **Acid rain**: rain made acidic by sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides dissolving in it, damaging trees and lakes.
- **Peat bog**: a waterlogged acidic habitat where dead plants only partly decay, storing carbon as peat.
- **Deforestation**: the large-scale cutting down of forests, for example for cattle, rice or biofuel crops.
- **Biofuel**: a fuel made from plant material, such as ethanol from sugar cane or oil from oil palm.
- **Global warming**: the increase in Earth's average temperature linked to rising carbon dioxide and methane levels.
- **Monoculture**: farming in which a large area grows only one type of crop.
- **Breeding programme**: captive breeding of an endangered species to increase its numbers, sometimes with release into the wild.
:::

::: method
**Command words.** *Describe* = say what happens, e.g. a biological consequence of global warming ("species distribution changes as species move towards the poles"). *Explain* = give the reason, linking the human activity to biodiversity ("hedgerows provide habitats and food, so more species can survive"). *Suggest* = apply ideas to an unfamiliar example. *Evaluate* = give points for and against, using any data, then a justified conclusion. *Calculate* = percentage changes and rates from data.

**The biodiversity chain.** Most explanations follow one chain: human activity → habitat destroyed or polluted → organisms die or cannot find food or shelter → fewer species → lower biodiversity → less stable ecosystem. Name the step that links the activity to the effect.

**Carbon-dioxide links.** Peat, deforestation and global warming connect. For deforestation give two reasons CO₂ rises: **burning or decay releases CO₂**, and **fewer trees remove CO₂ by photosynthesis**. For peat: decay or burning of peat releases CO₂.

**Evaluate questions (4–6 marks).** These usually set conservation against food, money or jobs: peat compost versus peat bogs, deforestation for cattle or biofuels, field margins versus crop yield. Level 3 needs points on **both sides**, linked to the information given, and a **conclusion** that says which side outweighs the other and why. A list of advantages with no conclusion stays in Level 2.

**Data questions.** Read the axes, quote values with units, describe the trend and any change in the trend, and calculate percentage change as change ÷ original × 100. Do not claim a cause from a correlation alone; say what other factors might be involved.

**Evidence (WS 1.6, 1.3).** If asked why scientists agree on global warming: the consensus comes from systematic reviews of thousands of peer-reviewed publications. If asked why predictions are uncertain: the climate is complex, with many interacting factors, and models depend on assumptions.
:::

::: example Explain: deforestation and carbon dioxide
Explain how large-scale deforestation increases the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. (3)

Trees are often burned to clear the land, and combustion releases carbon dioxide (1). Remaining dead wood and roots are decayed by microorganisms, which respire and release carbon dioxide (1). There are fewer trees to photosynthesise, so less carbon dioxide is removed from the air (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: percentage change from data
The number of species of moth recorded in a farmland area fell from 240 in 1980 to 168 in 2020. Calculate the percentage decrease. (2)

Decrease = 240 − 168 = 72 (1).
72 ÷ 240 × 100 = 30 % (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate: peat compost
Gardeners like peat compost because it is cheap and good for growing plants. Evaluate the use of peat compost. (4)

For: it is cheap and widely available, and it helps grow plants for food and gardens (1). Against: digging peat destroys peat bog habitat, so the variety of plant, animal and microorganism species living there falls (1); the peat decays or is burned, releasing carbon dioxide, which contributes to global warming (1). Conclusion: the loss of a rare habitat and its long-term carbon store outweighs the small saving, so peat-free compost should be used where possible (1).
:::

::: example Explain: hedgerows and biodiversity
Explain how reintroducing hedgerows and field margins on farms with monoculture crops increases biodiversity. (3)

A monoculture provides food and habitat for very few species (1). Hedgerows and field margins provide habitats, shelter and nesting sites, and wild flowers provide food for insects such as pollinators (1). This supports a greater variety of insects, birds and small mammals, and the hedgerows act as corridors linking habitats (1).
:::

Carbon dioxide (1); methane (1).

Describe two biological consequences of global warming. (2)
---
Any two: changes in the distribution of species (moving towards the poles or uphill); changes in migration patterns; loss of habitat, e.g. from flooding as sea levels rise; reduced biodiversity / extinction of species that cannot adapt; spread of pests or disease-carrying insects.

Give one disadvantage of a captive breeding programme for an endangered species. (1)
---
Any one: expensive; animals bred in captivity may not survive when released; the natural habitat may no longer exist; small populations have low genetic variation.

::: watch
- Biodiversity is the variety of **species**, not the number of individual organisms. A field with a million wheat plants has low biodiversity.
- Deforestation raises CO₂ in **two** ways: burning or decay **releases** it, and fewer trees **remove** it by photosynthesis. Most students give only one.
- Peat: say **both** habitat loss (fewer species) **and** carbon dioxide released by decay or burning.
- "Evaluate" needs points for **and** against plus a **conclusion**; a one-sided list cannot reach the top level.
- Percentage change = change ÷ **original** value × 100, not ÷ the new value.
- Global warming consequences must be **biological** (distribution, migration, biodiversity, habitat), not just "the ice melts".
:::

::: test
An area of rainforest decreased from 2.5 million km² to 2.1 million km² in 20 years. Calculate (a) the percentage decrease and (b) the mean area lost per year in km². (4)
---
(a) Decrease = 0.4 million km² (1); 0.4 ÷ 2.5 × 100 = 16 % (1). (b) 0.4 million km² ÷ 20 = 0.02 million km² (1) = 20 000 km² per year (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why destroying peat bogs to produce garden compost is harmful to the environment. (4)
---
It reduces the area of peat bog habitat (1), so the variety of plant, animal and microorganism species living there falls / biodiversity is reduced (1). The peat is decayed (by microorganisms, once exposed to oxygen) or burned (1), which releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere, contributing to global warming (1).
:::

::: test
Most scientists agree that human activity is causing global warming. (a) What is this agreement based on? (b) Suggest why predictions about the effects of global warming are still uncertain. (3)
---
(a) Systematic reviews of thousands of peer-reviewed publications / research (1). (b) Any two: the climate is a complex system with many interacting factors (1); predictions rely on computer models with assumptions (1); past data is indirect or incomplete (1); future emissions are unknown (1).
:::

::: test
A government plans to clear 5000 hectares of tropical forest to grow oil palm for biofuel. Evaluate this plan. (4)
---
For: biofuel is renewable and can replace some fossil fuel (1); provides jobs and income for the country (1). Against: destroys habitats, reducing biodiversity, as an oil palm monoculture supports few species (1); clearing by burning releases CO₂ and fewer trees remove CO₂ by photosynthesis, which may cancel out the benefit of the biofuel and adds to global warming (1). A justified conclusion, e.g. the plan should not go ahead, or should use already-cleared land instead, credited within the above (max 4).
:::

::: test
Describe positive and negative human interactions in ecosystems and explain how each affects biodiversity. (6)
---
Indicative content: Negative: pollution of water by sewage, fertiliser or toxic chemicals kills organisms; air pollution (smoke, acidic gases, acid rain) damages trees and lakes; landfill and building destroy habitats; peat extraction destroys bog habitats and releases CO₂; deforestation for cattle, rice or biofuels destroys habitats and raises CO₂; global warming changes distribution and can cause extinction; all reduce the number of species. Positive: breeding programmes for endangered species increase numbers; protecting and regenerating rare habitats lets species return; field margins and hedgerows provide habitats and food on monoculture farms; governments reducing deforestation and CO₂ emissions slow habitat loss and warming; recycling reduces landfill and resource extraction; all help maintain or increase biodiversity.
Level 3 (5–6): both positive and negative interactions described, each clearly linked to its effect on biodiversity with explanation.
Level 2 (3–4): several interactions described with some explanation; may be mainly one-sided.
Level 1 (1–2): a few interactions named with little explanation of the effect on biodiversity.
0: nothing relevant.
:::
