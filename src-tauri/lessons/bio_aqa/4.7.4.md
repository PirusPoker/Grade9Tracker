## What it is

Separate science only: this whole topic (AQA 8461 section 4.7.4) is **biology only**, so Combined Science Trilogy 8464 students do not need it. It has no Higher-tier-only content.

It explains why food chains are short and why there are fewer large carnivores than plants: only a small fraction of the biomass at each level ends up in the next one. You need to describe trophic levels, construct and describe **pyramids of biomass**, explain where biomass is lost, and **calculate the efficiency** of biomass transfer.

### Trophic levels

A **trophic level** is the position an organism feeds at in a food chain. Levels are numbered, starting at level 1 with plants and algae, then according to how far along the food chain the organism is.

| Level | Name | What they are | Example (pond) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **producers** | plants and algae, which make their own food by photosynthesis | algae |
| **2** | **primary consumers** | **herbivores**, which eat plants or algae | water flea |
| **3** | **secondary consumers** | **carnivores** that eat herbivores | small fish (stickleback) |
| **4** | **tertiary consumers** | **carnivores** that eat other carnivores | pike |

**Apex predators** are carnivores with **no predators** of their own, at the top of the food chain (e.g. the pike in a pond, killer whales, eagles).

The same species can feed at more than one level: a fox eating rabbits is at level 3, but eating beetles that ate other insects it is at level 4.

### Decomposers

**Decomposers** (bacteria and fungi) are not given a numbered level: they feed on dead material from **every** level, and on faeces and urine. They break down dead plant and animal matter by **secreting enzymes into the environment** (digestion happens outside their cells). The **small soluble food molecules** produced then **diffuse into the microorganism**, which uses them for respiration and growth. This is how carbon is returned to the air and mineral ions to the soil.

### Pyramids of biomass

**Biomass** is the mass of living material. A **pyramid of biomass** shows the **relative amount of biomass in each trophic level** of a food chain at one time.

- Each level is a horizontal **bar**; the **width (length) of the bar is proportional to the biomass** at that level.
- **Trophic level 1 is at the bottom**, level 2 above it, and so on.
- Bars are centred, so the shape is (almost always) a pyramid.
- Biomass is usually given as dry mass in g/m² or kg.

**Constructing an accurate pyramid (MS 2c)**

1. Choose a scale that fits the largest value on the paper, e.g. 1 cm = 100 g/m².
2. Work out each bar length: biomass ÷ scale.
3. Draw the producer bar at the bottom, then each level above, centred, with the same bar height.
4. Label each bar with the organism (or trophic level) and its biomass.

*Mini-example:* phytoplankton 800 g/m², zooplankton 90 g/m², small fish 8 g/m², large fish 1 g/m². With 1 cm = 100 g/m², bars are 8.0 cm, 0.9 cm, 0.08 cm and 0.01 cm. The top bars are tiny: this is normal and shows how little biomass reaches the top. If they are too small to draw, a larger scale or a note of the values is acceptable, but the bars must still be in proportion.

**Why biomass, not numbers?** A **pyramid of numbers** counts organisms, so one large oak tree supporting thousands of caterpillars gives a strange shape. Biomass takes the size of the organisms into account, so it almost always gives a true pyramid.

**Describing a pyramid of biomass:** the biomass decreases at each trophic level going up; the producers have by far the most biomass; each level has roughly a tenth of the one below it.

### Transfer of biomass and energy

**Producers** (mostly plants and algae) transfer only about **1 % of the incident energy from light** for photosynthesis. The rest of the light is reflected, passes through the leaf, hits parts that are not green, or is the wrong wavelength.

Only approximately **10 % of the biomass from each trophic level is transferred to the level above it.**

**Why biomass is lost between trophic levels:**

| Loss | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Not all ingested material is absorbed** | some is **egested as faeces**, e.g. cellulose and bone that cannot be digested |
| **Some absorbed material is lost as waste** | **carbon dioxide and water** from **respiration** (breathed out); **water and urea** in **urine** |
| **Large amounts of glucose are used in respiration** | the energy released is used for movement and, in mammals and birds, keeping the body warm; this glucose does not become new biomass |
| **Not all of the organism is eaten** | roots, bones, fur and shells are often left; these go to decomposers |

### Calculating efficiency (MS 1c)

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{biomass transferred to the next level}}{\text{biomass available in the level below}} \times 100$$

This gives a **percentage**; you may also be asked for a **fraction**.

*Mini-example:* grass 2000 kg, rabbits 180 kg, foxes 15 kg.
- Grass → rabbits: 180 ÷ 2000 × 100 = **9 %** (as a fraction, 180/2000 = 9/100).
- Rabbits → foxes: 15 ÷ 180 × 100 = 8.33… = **8.3 %** (to 2 significant figures).
- Mass lost from rabbits to foxes: 180 − 15 = 165 kg; as a percentage of the rabbit biomass, 165 ÷ 180 × 100 = 91.7 %.

You may also be asked to work forwards: if 10 % is transferred, then from 5000 g of producer biomass, the primary consumers have about 500 g and the secondary consumers about 50 g.

### How this affects the number of organisms at each level

Because about 90 % of biomass is lost at each step, **each trophic level supports less biomass, so there are fewer organisms** (or fewer large organisms) at higher levels.

- Food chains rarely have more than **four or five trophic levels**: after a few transfers there is not enough biomass left to support another population.
- **Apex predators are rare** and each needs a large territory to find enough food.
- Shorter food chains are more efficient for humans: far more people can be fed by eating crops directly than by feeding the crops to animals and eating the meat (this links to food production, topic 4.7.5).
- Animals that move a lot or are **warm-blooded** (mammals and birds) use more glucose in respiration, so they transfer a **smaller** percentage of biomass than cold-blooded animals such as fish and insects.

::: terms
- **Trophic level**: the position at which an organism feeds in a food chain, numbered from 1 for producers.
- **Producer**: a plant or alga that makes its own food by photosynthesis; trophic level 1.
- **Herbivore**: an animal that eats plants or algae; a primary consumer at trophic level 2.
- **Carnivore**: an animal that eats other animals; a secondary or tertiary consumer.
- **Apex predator**: a carnivore at the top of a food chain that has no predators of its own.
- **Decomposer**: a microorganism that secretes enzymes onto dead matter and absorbs the small soluble molecules by diffusion.
- **Biomass**: the mass of living material in an organism or a trophic level.
- **Pyramid of biomass**: a diagram of bars drawn to scale showing the biomass at each trophic level, with level 1 at the bottom.
- **Egestion**: the removal of undigested food from the gut as faeces.
:::

::: method
**Command words.** *Describe* the differences between trophic levels = name the level, what the organisms eat, and give an example. *Describe* a pyramid = biomass decreases at each level up, producers have the most. *Explain* biomass loss = name the loss **and** say where the material goes (faeces, carbon dioxide and water from respiration, urea in urine). *Calculate* = efficiency with working. *Construct* / *draw* = an accurate, to-scale pyramid.

**Drawing pyramids.** Use a ruler. Pick a scale that uses most of the space; write it down. Every bar must be in proportion to its biomass, level 1 at the bottom, bars centred and labelled. A "sketch" that is not to scale loses the accuracy mark.

**Efficiency calculations.** Write the equation, substitute in the right order (the level **above** divided by the level **below**), multiply by 100, then round sensibly with a % sign. Check your answer is less than 100 %; typical values are 1–20 %. If asked for the mass lost, subtract first. If asked for a fraction, give it in simplest form.

**Explaining losses (3–4 marks).** Three separate ideas: (1) not all ingested material is absorbed: egested as **faeces**; (2) absorbed material lost as **waste**: CO₂ and water from respiration, water and urea in urine; (3) **lots of glucose used in respiration**, releasing energy for movement and heat rather than building biomass. "Energy is lost as heat" alone is too vague.

**Linking to numbers (extended answers).** Chain: about 10 % passed on → less biomass at each level → fewer organisms can be supported → food chains are short and apex predators are few. A 6-mark answer should also use any figures given.
:::

::: example Calculate: efficiency of biomass transfer
In a meadow, the biomass of grass is 1200 g/m², the biomass of grasshoppers is 96 g/m² and the biomass of shrews is 7.2 g/m². Calculate the efficiency of biomass transfer from grasshoppers to shrews. (2)

Efficiency = 7.2 ÷ 96 × 100 (1) = 7.5 % (1).
:::

::: example Construct: a pyramid of biomass
Draw an accurate pyramid of biomass for: oak trees 6000 kg, caterpillars 480 kg, blue tits 36 kg. (3)

Choose a scale, e.g. 1 cm = 500 kg (1): bars of 12 cm, 0.96 cm and 0.072 cm (about 0.7 mm). Draw the oak bar at the bottom, caterpillar bar centred above it, blue tit bar centred at the top, all the same height (1). Label each bar with the organism and its biomass, and state the scale (1).
:::

::: example Explain: why biomass is lost
Explain why only a small percentage of the biomass of the rabbits is transferred to the foxes that eat them. (4)

Not all of each rabbit is eaten, e.g. bones and fur are left (1). Not all of the eaten material is absorbed; some is egested as faeces (1). Some absorbed material is lost as waste: carbon dioxide and water from respiration, and water and urea in urine (1). Large amounts of glucose are used in respiration to release energy for movement and to keep the fox warm, so it is not built into new biomass (1).
:::

::: example Explain: decomposers
Describe how a fungus feeds on a dead leaf. (3)

The fungus secretes enzymes onto the dead leaf, into its environment (1). The enzymes digest large insoluble molecules into small soluble food molecules (1). These small molecules diffuse into the fungus (1).
:::

Which trophic level do herbivores belong to, and what are they called? (2)
---
Level 2 (1); primary consumers (1).

::: q
What is an apex predator? (1)
---
A carnivore with no predators (at the top of the food chain).
:::

::: q
Which trophic level is drawn at the bottom of a pyramid of biomass? (1)
---
Trophic level 1, the producers.
:::

::: q
About what percentage of the incident light energy do producers transfer for photosynthesis, and about what percentage of biomass passes from one trophic level to the next? (2)
---
About 1 % of light energy (1); about 10 % of biomass (1).
:::

::: q
Give two waste products lost from an animal's body that are produced by respiration. (2)
---
Carbon dioxide (1); water (1).
:::

::: q
A population of caterpillars has a biomass of 250 g. The birds feeding on them gain 20 g of biomass. Calculate the efficiency of transfer. (2)
---
20 ÷ 250 × 100 (1) = 8 % (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why food chains rarely have more than five trophic levels. (2)
---
About 90 % of biomass is lost at each level (1), so after a few levels there is too little biomass left to support another population (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why the efficiency of biomass transfer to a bird is lower than to a fish of the same mass. (2)
---
Birds keep a constant, warm body temperature and fly, so they use more glucose in respiration (1), leaving less to build new biomass (1).
:::

::: watch
- Trophic level 1 is at the **bottom** of the pyramid; bars must be drawn **to scale** with a ruler, centred and labelled.
- Efficiency = level **above** ÷ level **below** × 100. If your answer is over 100 %, you divided the wrong way.
- "Energy is lost as heat" is not enough: name the losses (faeces, CO₂ and water from respiration, urea and water in urine, glucose used in respiration).
- About **1 %** of light is used by producers; about **10 %** of biomass passes between levels. Do not swap them.
- Decomposers digest food **outside** their cells by secreting enzymes, then absorb the small soluble molecules by **diffusion**.
:::

::: test
In a lake, small fish have a biomass of 27 g/m² and the pike that eat them have a biomass of 2.4 g/m². Calculate the biomass lost between the small fish and the pike, and express the biomass transferred as a fraction of the small fish biomass in its simplest form. (3)
---
Lost = 27 − 2.4 = 24.6 g/m² (1). Fraction transferred = 2.4/27 (1) = 4/45 (1).

::: test
Explain how decomposers obtain the nutrients they need from dead organisms. (3)
---
They secrete enzymes onto the dead material / into the environment (1). The enzymes break down large insoluble molecules into small soluble molecules (1). These diffuse into the microorganism's cells (1).
:::

::: test
A field of wheat could feed 20 people if the wheat is eaten directly. Use ideas about biomass transfer to explain why the same field feeding cattle would provide meat for only about 2 people. (3)
---
Only about 10 % of the wheat biomass is transferred to the cattle (1), because the cattle egest some as faeces and use large amounts of glucose in respiration for movement and keeping warm (1). So there is about one tenth of the biomass available to humans as meat, feeding about 2 people instead of 20 (1).
:::
