## What it is

This lesson covers spec subsection **4.7.1 Adaptations, interdependence and competition**: communities (4.7.1.1), abiotic factors (4.7.1.2), biotic factors (4.7.1.3) and adaptations (4.7.1.4). It is in **both Biology 8461 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464** with the same numbers, and has no Higher-tier-only content. Much of the assessment is **"given appropriate information / data"** — you apply these ideas to an unfamiliar habitat, graph or table.

### Levels of organisation in an ecosystem

From smallest to largest:

| Level | Meaning | Example (a pond) |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual** | A single organism. | One frog. |
| **Population** | **All the organisms of one species** living in a habitat. | All the frogs in the pond. |
| **Community** | **All the populations of different species** living and interacting in a habitat. | Frogs, newts, pondweed, insects, algae, bacteria. |
| **Ecosystem** | **The interaction of a community of living organisms (biotic) with the non-living (abiotic) parts of their environment.** | The community plus the water, temperature, light, oxygen and mud. |

A **habitat** is the place where an organism lives. To **survive and reproduce, organisms require a supply of materials from their surroundings and from the other living organisms there.**

### Competition

Organisms compete for resources that are in **limited supply**.

| Plants compete for… | Animals compete for… |
|---|---|
| **Light** (for photosynthesis) | **Food** |
| **Space** | **Mates** |
| **Water** from the soil | **Territory** |
| **Mineral ions** from the soil | |

Competition can be **between individuals of the same species** (e.g. two robins for territory) or **between different species** (e.g. red and grey squirrels for food). The better competitor survives and breeds; a species that is outcompeted may decline or disappear from that habitat.

**"Suggest the factors for which organisms are competing"** — read the context. Seedlings growing under a parent tree compete for light, water and minerals (this is why seed dispersal is an advantage). Two bird species feeding on the same insects compete for food; male deer fight for mates and territory.

### Interdependence

**Within a community each species depends on other species for food, shelter, pollination, seed dispersal etc.** **If one species is removed it can affect the whole community. This is called interdependence.**

Examples:
- **Food**: predators depend on prey; herbivores depend on plants.
- **Shelter**: birds nest in trees; insects live in bark.
- **Pollination**: many flowering plants depend on bees; bees depend on flowers for nectar.
- **Seed dispersal**: birds eat berries and spread the seeds in their droppings.

**Knock-on effects.** Use food-web reasoning: if a predator is removed, its prey increase, then the prey's food plants decrease, and other animals that eat those plants decrease too. If bees decline, fewer plants are pollinated, so less fruit and seed is produced for other animals.

**A stable community** is one where **all the species and environmental factors are in balance so that population sizes remain fairly constant** (e.g. tropical rainforests, ancient oak woodland). Population sizes still go up and down a little, but around a steady level.

**Predator–prey cycles.** When prey numbers rise, predators have more food so their numbers rise **slightly later**; more predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall; predators then starve and fall. The predator peak always **lags behind** the prey peak, and there are usually fewer predators than prey.

### Abiotic factors

**Abiotic (non-living) factors** which can affect a community are:

1. **light intensity** — affects the rate of photosynthesis, so plant growth and distribution (fewer plants on a dark woodland floor);
2. **temperature** — affects enzymes, so the rate of photosynthesis and growth, and where species can live;
3. **moisture levels** — water for plants and animals; some species (e.g. mosses) need damp conditions;
4. **soil pH and mineral content** — e.g. nitrate for growth; some plants only grow in acid soil;
5. **wind intensity and direction** — affects transpiration rate and the shape/height of plants (trees bent away from prevailing wind);
6. **carbon dioxide levels for plants** — a limiting factor for photosynthesis;
7. **oxygen levels for aquatic animals** — fish need dissolved oxygen; polluted water with low oxygen supports only a few tolerant species (e.g. rat-tailed maggots, sludgeworms).

**Explaining an effect — the chain:** change in the abiotic factor → effect on a process (photosynthesis, respiration, enzyme activity, water loss) → effect on survival / growth / reproduction → effect on population → knock-on effect on others in the community.

### Biotic factors

**Biotic (living) factors** which can affect a community are:

1. **availability of food** — more food means more animals survive and breed, so the population rises;
2. **new predators arriving** — prey numbers fall (e.g. introduced mink reducing water vole populations in the UK);
3. **new pathogens** — disease can wipe out a population that has no resistance (e.g. ash dieback fungus killing ash trees; squirrelpox, carried by grey squirrels, killing red squirrels);
4. **one species outcompeting another so the numbers are no longer sufficient to breed** — e.g. grey squirrels outcompeting red squirrels for food.

### Interpreting data (MS 2c, 4a)

You will often get a graph of population size over time, or a table of species numbers at different values of an abiotic factor.
- **Describe** the trend with numbers from the graph (rose from 200 to 800 between years 2 and 5).
- **Explain** using the biotic or abiotic factor and a mechanism.
- **Calculate**: percentage change $= \frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}} \times 100$. Example: a hare population falls from 1,250 to 400. Change $= \frac{400 - 1250}{1250} \times 100 = -68\%$, a 68% decrease.
- Be careful about **correlation and causation**: two populations changing together does not prove one caused the other unless a mechanism is shown.

### Adaptations

**Organisms have features (adaptations) that enable them to survive in the conditions in which they normally live.** Adaptations may be:

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | Features of **body shape or structure**. | Polar bear: thick fur, layer of blubber, small ears (small surface area to volume ratio reduces heat loss), white fur for camouflage. Cactus: leaves reduced to **spines** (less surface for water loss, deters animals), thick stem stores water, **long or wide shallow roots** to absorb water. Camel: large flat feet for walking on sand. |
| **Behavioural** | **The way an organism behaves.** | Migration to warmer places; hibernation; desert animals staying in burrows in the day and coming out at night (nocturnal); penguins huddling to reduce heat loss. |
| **Functional** | **Processes inside the body** (physiology, metabolism, reproduction). | Desert animals producing very little sweat and small amounts of concentrated urine; cacti opening stomata at night to reduce water loss; some animals lowering their metabolic rate during hibernation; reproduction timed so young are born when food is plentiful. |

**Surface area to volume ratio** is a common link: small, compact animals (and small ears) in cold places lose less heat; large ears in hot places (fennec fox, African elephant) give a large surface area for heat loss.

**Extremophiles.** **Some organisms live in environments that are very extreme, such as at high temperature, pressure, or salt concentration. These organisms are called extremophiles.** **Bacteria living in deep-sea vents are extremophiles** — they survive temperatures above 100 °C and huge pressure, because their **enzymes do not denature** at those temperatures. Other examples: bacteria in hot springs, archaea in very salty lakes.

**Answering "suggest how this organism is adapted" (given appropriate information):** state the feature, then link it to the **condition** and **how it helps survival** — "thick fur *traps a layer of air, which insulates*, *reducing heat loss* in the cold Arctic".

::: terms
- **Population**: all the organisms of one species living in a habitat.
- **Community**: all the populations of different species living and interacting in a habitat.
- **Ecosystem**: the interaction of a community of living organisms with the non-living parts of their environment.
- **Interdependence**: the way species in a community depend on each other for food, shelter, pollination and seed dispersal.
- **Stable community**: a community where species and environmental factors are in balance, so population sizes stay fairly constant.
- **Abiotic factor**: a non-living factor that affects a community, such as light intensity, temperature or soil pH.
- **Biotic factor**: a living factor that affects a community, such as food availability, predators or pathogens.
- **Adaptation**: a structural, behavioural or functional feature that enables an organism to survive in its conditions.
- **Extremophile**: an organism that lives in very extreme conditions of temperature, pressure or salt concentration.
:::

::: method
**Command words on this topic**
- **Describe the levels of organisation** — individual → population → community → ecosystem, each with its definition. The ecosystem includes the **abiotic** parts; the community does not.
- **Suggest what the organisms are competing for** — plants: **light, space, water, mineral ions**; animals: **food, mates, territory**. Pick the ones that fit the context and say why they are limited.
- **Explain how a change in an abiotic / biotic factor would affect the community** — use the chain: factor changes → process affected (photosynthesis, food supply, predation, disease) → survival and reproduction → population size → knock-on effect via interdependence.
- **Explain the importance of interdependence** — removing one species affects others (food, shelter, pollination, seed dispersal); give a specific knock-on chain.
- **Suggest how an organism is adapted** — **feature + how it helps in this environment**. Classify as **structural, behavioural or functional** if asked.
- **Describe / explain the graph** — describe with figures (including units and time), then explain using a biotic or abiotic factor. For predator–prey graphs, mention the **time lag**.

**Calculations.** Percentage change, rates of population change (change ÷ time, e.g. "rabbits per year"), and reading values from graphs. Show the equation, substitute, give the unit, and round sensibly.

**6-mark answers.** A typical 6-marker gives you a habitat and data and asks you to "explain the changes in the populations" or "explain how organisms in this habitat are adapted". Level 3 needs **several factors**, each with a **mechanism** and **links between species**, using the data. A list of factors without explanation stays in Level 1–2.
:::

::: example Competition
Oak seedlings growing directly under a large oak tree often die. Suggest why. (3)

The parent tree's leaves block sunlight, so the seedlings get less light for photosynthesis (1). The parent's large root system absorbs most of the water (1) and mineral ions from the soil (1), so the seedlings cannot make enough glucose and proteins to grow.
:::

::: example Interdependence
In a woodland, a disease kills most of the rabbits. Explain the possible effects on foxes and on the grass. (3)

Foxes have less food, so fewer survive and breed and the fox population falls (1) (or they eat more of other prey, reducing those populations). Fewer rabbits eat the grass (1), so the grass grows more / its population increases, which could then provide more food for other herbivores (1).
:::

::: example Adaptation
A fennec fox lives in hot deserts and has very large ears. Explain how this adaptation helps it survive. (2)

Large ears give a large surface area (relative to volume) (1), so more heat is lost from the blood flowing through them, helping the fox stay cool (1).
:::

::: example Percentage change
A pond contained 240 frogs. After a new pathogen arrived, there were 54 frogs. Calculate the percentage decrease. (2)

$\frac{240 - 54}{240} \times 100$ (1) $= 77.5\%$ decrease (1).
:::

::: example Abiotic factor
A river is polluted with sewage and the oxygen concentration in the water falls. Explain how this affects the community of animals. (3)

Fish and many invertebrates need dissolved oxygen for aerobic respiration (1). With less oxygen they cannot release enough energy, so they die or move away and their populations fall (1). Only species adapted to low oxygen (e.g. sludgeworms, rat-tailed maggots) survive, so there is less variety of species (1).
:::

::: q
Some desert animals stay in burrows during the day and come out at night. What type of adaptation is this, and how does it help? (2)

Behavioural (1); they avoid the hottest part of the day, reducing water loss / overheating (1).

::: watch
- **Community = living only; ecosystem = living + non-living.** Swapping these is the most common lost mark.
- Plants compete for **light, space, water and mineral ions** — not "food" or "nutrients". Animals compete for **food, mates and territory**.
- "Explain the effect" needs a **mechanism** (photosynthesis, food supply, predation, oxygen for respiration), not just "the population goes down".
- An adaptation answer needs **feature + how it helps survival in that environment**. "It has thick fur" alone scores nothing.
- On predator–prey graphs, the predator peak **lags behind** the prey peak — say so and explain why.
:::

::: test
A polar bear has a thick layer of fat (blubber) and small ears. Explain how each adaptation helps it survive in the Arctic. (4)
---
Blubber insulates the body (1), reducing heat loss in the cold (and stores energy when food is scarce) (1). Small ears give a small surface area (1), so less heat is lost from them (1).

::: test
A population of red squirrels in a forest was 3,600 in 2010. By 2020 it had fallen to 900 after grey squirrels arrived. (a) Calculate the percentage decrease. (b) Calculate the mean rate of decrease in squirrels per year. (4)
---
(a) $\frac{3600 - 900}{3600} \times 100$ (1) $= 75\%$ decrease (1). (b) $\frac{3600 - 900}{10}$ (1) $= 270$ squirrels per year (1).
:::

::: test
Suggest and explain why grey squirrels caused the red squirrel population to fall. (3)
---
Grey squirrels outcompete red squirrels for food (1), so fewer red squirrels survive and their numbers become too low to breed successfully (1). Grey squirrels also carry a pathogen (squirrelpox) that kills red squirrels (1).
:::

::: test
A graph shows that the population of snowshoe hares peaks every 10 years and the population of lynx (which eat hares) peaks about 1–2 years after each hare peak. Explain the pattern. (4)
---
When there are many hares, lynx have plenty of food, so more lynx survive and breed (1). The lynx population rises after a delay because it takes time for them to reproduce and young to grow (1). More lynx eat more hares, so the hare population falls (1). With less food, lynx starve/breed less and their population falls, so hares can increase again (1).
:::

::: test
Cacti live in hot deserts. Suggest how the features of a cactus — spines instead of leaves, a thick swollen stem, a widespread shallow root system and stomata that open at night — adapt it to its environment. Classify each adaptation as structural or functional. (5)
---
Spines reduce surface area so less water is lost by transpiration, and deter animals from eating it — structural (1). Thick stem stores water — structural (1). Widespread shallow roots absorb rain water quickly over a large area before it evaporates — structural (1). Stomata opening at night reduces water loss because it is cooler — functional (1). Correct classification of all four (1).
:::

::: test
A farmer drains a wet meadow to grow crops, so the soil becomes much drier. The meadow had many species of wild flowers, insects that pollinate them and birds that feed on the insects. Explain how draining the meadow could affect this community. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- moisture level is an abiotic factor; drier soil means less water for plants
- plants adapted to wet conditions cannot absorb enough water, wilt, photosynthesise less and die; population falls
- some plants adapted to dry soil may outcompete others; species composition changes
- fewer flowers means less nectar/food for pollinating insects, so insect populations fall
- fewer insects means less food for birds; birds die or leave; bird population falls
- fewer pollinators means fewer flowers pollinated, so fewer seeds and even fewer plants next year
- interdependence: removal or reduction of one species affects the whole community
- the community becomes less stable / biodiversity falls

Level 3 (5–6 marks): a clear explanation linking the change in an abiotic factor to effects on plants, then knock-on effects on at least two other populations through interdependence.
Level 2 (3–4 marks): effects on plants and at least one other organism, with some explanation.
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple statements, e.g. "plants die" or "fewer insects".
0 marks: nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
Bacteria living around deep-sea vents can grow at temperatures above 80 °C. Suggest why most bacteria could not survive there, and explain how vent bacteria are able to. (3)
---
At high temperature the enzymes of most bacteria denature (1), so the active site changes shape and metabolic reactions stop (1). Vent bacteria are extremophiles whose enzymes keep their shape and work at high temperature and pressure (1).
:::
