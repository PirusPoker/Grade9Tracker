## What it is

**Separate science only: this whole subsection (4.3.3, Plant disease) is Biology 8461 only.** Combined Science: Trilogy students meet tobacco mosaic virus and rose black spot in 4.3.1, but not detection, identification, ion deficiencies or plant defences. Within it, **detection and identification of plant diseases is Higher tier only**.

Plants, like animals, are attacked by **viral, bacterial and fungal pathogens**, and also by **insects**. For AQA your knowledge of specific plant diseases is restricted to three examples:

| Problem | Type | Symptoms | Effect on the plant | Spread and control |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)** | Virus | A distinctive **mosaic pattern** of discoloured (pale/yellow) patches on the leaves. | Discoloured parts lack chlorophyll, so **less photosynthesis** → less glucose → **stunted growth**. | Spread by contact (and on hands, tools, insects). No cure — remove and destroy infected plants; grow resistant varieties; good hygiene. |
| **Rose black spot** | Fungus | **Purple or black spots** on leaves; leaves often turn yellow and drop early. | Less leaf area and less chlorophyll → **less photosynthesis** → poor growth. | Spores spread in the environment by **water or wind**. Treated with **fungicides** and by **removing and destroying affected leaves**. |
| **Aphids** | Insect (pest) | Small insects clustered on stems and young leaves; leaves may curl or wilt. | They pierce the **phloem** and feed on the sugary **sap**, depriving the plant of sugars → reduced growth. They can also carry (spread) viral pathogens. | Insecticides, or **biological control** — e.g. ladybirds eat aphids. |

### Detecting plant disease (Higher tier)

A gardener or farmer can spot that a plant is diseased from any of these signs:

- **stunted growth**
- **spots on leaves**
- **areas of decay (rot)**
- **growths** (lumps, galls)
- **malformed stems or leaves**
- **discolouration**
- **the presence of pests.**

### Identifying plant disease (Higher tier)

Seeing a symptom does not tell you *which* pathogen is responsible. Identification can be made by:

1. **reference to a gardening manual or website** — compare the symptoms with descriptions and photos;
2. **taking infected plants to a laboratory to identify the pathogen** — e.g. examining it under a microscope or growing it in culture;
3. **using testing kits that contain monoclonal antibodies** — the antibodies bind only to an antigen on one specific pathogen, so a positive result identifies it quickly, even in the field. (This links to 4.3.2.)

This is **WS 1.4**: the everyday application of science to detect and identify disease, so the right treatment can be chosen early and the spread stopped.

### Ion deficiency conditions

Not every sick-looking plant has a pathogen. Plants also need **mineral ions** absorbed from the soil by their root hair cells (by active transport). AQA limits you to two:

| Ion | Why the plant needs it | Deficiency symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrate** | Provides nitrogen for **protein synthesis** (to make amino acids and proteins), and therefore **growth**. | **Stunted growth.** |
| **Magnesium** | Needed to **make chlorophyll**. | **Chlorosis** — leaves turn yellow because they lack chlorophyll, so photosynthesis falls. |

WS 1.4: understanding ion deficiencies lets **horticulturists provide optimum conditions** — e.g. adding fertiliser containing nitrate or magnesium, or using hydroponics with a measured nutrient solution.

### Plant defence responses

Plants cannot run away, so they have evolved defences (links to *Adaptations*, 4.7).

**Physical defences — resist invasion of microorganisms:**
- **Cellulose cell walls** — a tough barrier around every cell that pathogens find hard to break through.
- **Tough waxy cuticle on leaves** — a waterproof barrier that pathogens cannot easily penetrate.
- **Layers of dead cells around stems (bark on trees) which fall off** — pathogens that land on the bark fall off with the dead cells.

**Chemical defences:**
- **Antibacterial chemicals** — kill or inhibit bacteria (e.g. mint and witch hazel produce them; pine trees produce antiseptic resin).
- **Poisons to deter herbivores** — e.g. foxglove, deadly nightshade and tobacco plants make toxins, so animals avoid eating them.

**Mechanical adaptations:**
- **Thorns and hairs deter animals** — e.g. rose thorns, cactus spines, stinging hairs on nettles.
- **Leaves which droop or curl when touched** — e.g. *Mimosa pudica*: the sudden movement can dislodge insects and startles grazers; the drooped plant looks less appealing.
- **Mimicry to trick animals** — e.g. some passion-flower leaves have yellow spots that look like butterfly eggs, so butterflies do not lay eggs there; *Lithops* ("living stones") look like pebbles so are not eaten; white deadnettle looks like a stinging nettle.

**Physical vs mechanical:** in AQA's grouping, *physical* defences stop **microorganisms** getting in; *mechanical* adaptations deter **animals**. Use the same grouping in your answers.

::: terms
- **Tobacco mosaic virus**: a plant virus that causes a mosaic pattern of discolouration on leaves, reducing photosynthesis and growth.
- **Rose black spot**: a fungal disease causing purple or black spots on leaves, which turn yellow and drop early; spread by water or wind.
- **Aphid**: an insect pest that feeds on phloem sap, reducing plant growth and spreading viruses.
- **Chlorosis**: yellowing of leaves due to a lack of chlorophyll, caused by magnesium deficiency.
- **Nitrate ions**: mineral ions absorbed from soil, needed for protein synthesis and therefore growth.
- **Magnesium ions**: mineral ions absorbed from soil that are needed to make chlorophyll.
- **Waxy cuticle**: a tough, waterproof layer on the leaf surface that acts as a physical barrier to pathogens.
- **Mimicry**: a plant adaptation in which it looks like something else to trick animals, e.g. leaf spots resembling butterfly eggs.
:::

::: method
**Command words on this topic**
- **Describe the symptoms** — name what you would *see*: mosaic pattern (TMV), purple/black spots and leaf drop (black spot), stunted growth (nitrate), yellow leaves (magnesium).
- **Explain why the plant grows less** — always link the symptom to **photosynthesis**: less chlorophyll / less leaf area → less light absorbed → less glucose → less energy and materials for growth. For nitrate: less protein synthesis → less growth. For aphids: sugars removed from the phloem.
- **Suggest how a gardener could identify the disease (Higher tier)** — choose from: gardening manual or website; send to a laboratory; monoclonal antibody testing kit. Say *why* the kit works (antibody binds only to that pathogen's antigen).
- **Describe plant defences** — sort into **physical** (cellulose wall, waxy cuticle, bark that falls off), **chemical** (antibacterial chemicals, poisons) and **mechanical** (thorns and hairs, drooping/curling leaves, mimicry). Then *explain* each: what it stops and how.
- **Evaluate a control method** — e.g. fungicide vs removing leaves vs resistant varieties: cost, effectiveness, harm to other organisms, whether it stops spread.

**6-mark answers.** A typical 6-marker is "Describe how plants defend themselves against pathogens and animals". Level 3 needs examples from **all three** groups **and** an explanation of how each works (not just a list). Another favourite: "A gardener's roses have yellow leaves. Explain possible causes and how they could find out which" — you need to separate **pathogen** (black spot: spots, spread by wind/water) from **ion deficiency** (magnesium: chlorosis, no spots), then describe identification.

**Data questions.** You may get a table of plants infected or yields with/without treatment. Calculate percentages (infected ÷ total × 100) or percentage change ((new − old) ÷ old × 100), and comment on whether a difference is large enough to be meaningful.
:::

::: example TMV
Explain why a plant infected with tobacco mosaic virus grows more slowly than an uninfected plant. (3)

TMV causes discoloured patches on the leaves, where chlorophyll is lost (1). Less light is absorbed so the rate of photosynthesis is lower (1). Less glucose is produced for respiration and for making new materials such as proteins and cellulose, so growth is stunted (1).
:::

::: example Black spot vs magnesium deficiency
A gardener notices that the leaves of a rose bush are yellow. Suggest how she could tell whether this is caused by rose black spot or magnesium deficiency. (3)

Black spot causes purple or black spots on the leaves before they yellow and fall (1); magnesium deficiency causes yellowing (chlorosis) without spots (1). She could compare with a gardening manual or website, or send a leaf to a laboratory / use a test kit to identify the fungus (1).
:::

::: example Monoclonal antibody kit (Higher tier)
Explain how a testing kit containing monoclonal antibodies can identify which pathogen is infecting a plant. (2)

The monoclonal antibodies are specific to an antigen on one particular pathogen, so they bind only to that pathogen (1). If the pathogen is present in a sample from the plant, the antibodies bind and a colour change/line shows a positive result, identifying the pathogen (1).
:::

::: example Defences
Describe one physical, one chemical and one mechanical defence of plants, and explain how each protects the plant. (6)

Physical: a tough waxy cuticle on the leaves (1) acts as a barrier so microorganisms cannot enter the leaf (1). Chemical: poisons such as those in foxgloves (1) deter herbivores because animals that eat them become ill, so avoid them (1). Mechanical: thorns on roses (1) hurt animals that try to eat the plant, so they are deterred (1).
:::

::: example Calculation
A farmer counted 360 tomato plants; 27 showed a mosaic pattern on their leaves. Calculate the percentage infected with TMV. (2)

$\frac{27}{360} \times 100$ (1) $= 7.5\%$ (1).
:::

Nitrate is needed to make amino acids/proteins (1); without protein synthesis the plant cannot make new cells, so growth is stunted (1).

Yellowing of the leaves due to lack of chlorophyll (1); caused by magnesium deficiency (1).

::: test

---

A gardener's tomato plants have stunted growth and yellow leaves, and small green insects are clustered on the stems. Suggest three possible causes and evaluate how she could find out which is responsible. (4)
---
Aphids (the insects) removing phloem sap and possibly spreading a virus such as TMV (1); nitrate deficiency causing stunted growth (1); magnesium deficiency causing chlorosis (1). Evaluation (1): several causes can produce similar symptoms, so identification needs more than looking — e.g. compare symptoms with a manual/website, test the soil for ions, or send plants to a lab / use a monoclonal antibody kit to check for a viral pathogen; removing the aphids and adding fertiliser separately would show which treatment helps.
