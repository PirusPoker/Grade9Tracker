## What it is

An ecosystem is organised by **who eats whom** and by **how materials go round**. This topic covers food chains and predator–prey cycles, how ecologists sample organisms with quadrats and transects (Required practical 9 in Biology 8461; Required practical 7 in Trilogy 8464), and the carbon and water cycles. Two parts are **separate science only**: decomposition (with Required practical 10) and the impact of environmental change, which is also **Higher tier**.

### Producers and food chains

**Photosynthetic organisms are the producers of biomass for life on Earth.** Every food chain starts with a **producer**, an organism that synthesises its own organic molecules. This is usually a green plant or alga, which makes glucose by photosynthesis and uses it to build the rest of its biomass.

grass → rabbit → stoat → fox

| Position | Name | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | **producer** | makes glucose by photosynthesis (grass) |
| 2nd | **primary consumer** | eats the producer (rabbit) |
| 3rd | **secondary consumer** | eats primary consumers (stoat) |
| 4th | **tertiary consumer** | eats secondary consumers (fox) |

The arrows show the **direction biomass (and energy) is transferred**: they point from the food to the feeder, meaning "is eaten by".

### Predators, prey and population cycles

Consumers that kill and eat other animals are **predators**; the animals eaten are **prey**. In a stable community the numbers of predators and prey **rise and fall in cycles**.

1. Prey numbers rise (plenty of food for the prey).
2. With more food available, more predators survive and breed, so predator numbers rise **a little later**.
3. More predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall.
4. With less food, predators starve or breed less, so predator numbers fall **a little later**.
5. With fewer predators, prey numbers recover, and the cycle repeats.

**Reading the graph (MS 4a):** the predator peak always comes **after** the prey peak (a lag), because predators take time to breed. The predator line is normally lower, because a large mass of prey is needed to feed each predator. The classic data set is the Canadian lynx (predator) and snowshoe hare (prey), which cycle roughly every 10 years. Real cycles are also affected by other factors (food for the prey, disease, weather), so they are never perfectly regular.

### Required practical 9 (Trilogy: Required practical 7): measure the population size of a common species and use sampling to investigate the effect of a factor on its distribution

Ecologists cannot count every organism, so they **sample**. **Abundance** is how many there are; **distribution** is where they are found.

**Part A: estimating population size with random quadrats**

1. Lay two tape measures at right angles along two edges of the area (for example, a field), to make a grid.
2. Use a **random number generator** to pick coordinates, and place a quadrat (often 0.5 m × 0.5 m = 0.25 m²) at each one. Randomness avoids bias, e.g. choosing the patches that look full of daisies.
3. Count the number of the chosen species (e.g. daisies or dandelions) in each quadrat.
4. Use at least 10 quadrats (more makes the mean more representative).
5. Calculate the mean per quadrat and scale up:

$$\text{estimated population} = \text{mean number per quadrat} \times \frac{\text{total area}}{\text{area of one quadrat}}$$

*Mini-example:* a 30 m × 20 m field (600 m²). Ten 0.25 m² quadrats contain 2, 5, 0, 3, 4, 8, 1, 3, 1, 3 daisies. Total = 30, so mean = 30 ÷ 10 = 3.0 per quadrat. Quadrats in the field = 600 ÷ 0.25 = 2400. Population ≈ 3.0 × 2400 = **7200 daisies**.

Data skills the spec names (MS 2b, 2f): in the same data, **mean** = 3.0, **median** (middle value when ordered 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 5, 8: average of 5th and 6th values) = 3, **mode** (most common value) = 3, range = 0 to 8.

**Part B: investigating distribution with a transect**

1. Stretch a tape measure (a **transect** line) across an area where a factor changes, e.g. from under a tree into open grass, or from a path into a field.
2. Place a quadrat at **regular intervals** (e.g. every 2 m) along the line.
3. Count the organisms (or estimate **percentage cover**, counting squares more than half covered, if individuals are hard to count, like grass or moss).
4. At each point measure the **abiotic factor**: light intensity with a light meter, soil moisture or pH with a probe, temperature with a thermometer.
5. Repeat with more transects nearby and average them.
6. Plot a graph of number (or % cover) against distance, and compare it with the factor. A scatter graph of number against light intensity can show a correlation.

| Variable | Part A | Part B |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | (location chosen randomly) | distance along transect / the abiotic factor |
| Dependent | number of organisms per quadrat | number or % cover per quadrat |
| Control | same quadrat size, same species, same time | same quadrat size, same interval, same day and same person identifying |

**Risks:** plants that sting or cause allergies (wear gloves), ticks or animal faeces (wash hands afterwards), uneven ground, tides or water near a pond or the shore (supervision). Treat organisms ethically: count them in place and return any you move.

**Typical results:** a shade-intolerant plant such as a daisy is rare under trees and increases in number as light intensity increases away from the tree. A correlation does not prove the light causes the change; other factors such as soil water or trampling also vary along a transect.

**Sources of error and improvements:** too few quadrats (use more); non-random placement (use random coordinates); misidentifying species (use an identification key); counting the same plant twice when it lies on the edge (rule: count only plants on two of the four edges).

### How materials are cycled

Many materials **cycle** through the **abiotic** (non-living: air, water, soil) and **biotic** (living) parts of an ecosystem. All materials in the living world are recycled to provide the **building blocks for future organisms**. You are not expected to study the nitrogen cycle.

**The carbon cycle** returns carbon from organisms to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, to be used by plants in photosynthesis.

| Process | Effect on carbon |
|---|---|
| **photosynthesis** by plants and algae | removes CO₂ from the air; carbon becomes glucose, then other carbon compounds in biomass |
| **feeding** (eating) | passes carbon compounds along food chains |
| **respiration** by plants, animals and microorganisms | releases CO₂ back into the air |
| **death, egestion and decay** | microorganisms (decomposers) feed on dead organisms and waste and respire, releasing CO₂ |
| **combustion** of wood and fossil fuels | releases CO₂ quickly |
| **fossilisation** (where decay is prevented, over millions of years) | locks carbon up in coal, oil and gas |

Why it matters: without the carbon cycle, all the carbon would end up locked in dead bodies and plants would run out of CO₂ for photosynthesis, so producers and then all other organisms would die.

**Role of microorganisms (decomposers):** bacteria and fungi break down dead organisms, faeces and urine. They respire the carbon compounds, **returning carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide**, and they release **mineral ions** (such as nitrate, phosphate and magnesium) **back into the soil**, where plant roots absorb them.

**The water cycle** provides fresh water for plants and animals on land before draining into the seas. Water is continuously **evaporated** and **precipitated**.

1. Heat from the Sun **evaporates** water from seas, lakes and soil; plants lose water vapour by **transpiration**.
2. The vapour rises, cools and **condenses** into clouds.
3. Water falls as **precipitation** (rain, snow, hail), which is fresh water, because the salts were left behind in the sea.
4. It is taken up by plants through roots, drunk by animals, and drains through soil and rivers (**run-off** and percolation) back into the sea.

Why it matters: living organisms need fresh water for photosynthesis, as a solvent for reactions, for transport in blood and xylem, and to keep cells turgid. Animals also lose water in sweat, urine and breathing, which re-enters the cycle.

### Separate science only: Decomposition

**The rate of decay** of biological material depends on the conditions for the decomposers (bacteria and fungi):

| Factor | Effect | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **temperature** | warmer → faster decay, up to an optimum; too hot → slower | enzymes in the microorganisms work faster with more kinetic energy; above the optimum the enzymes denature; when cold, enzymes work very slowly |
| **water** | moist → faster; dry → very slow | microorganisms need water to grow and reproduce, and enzymes work in solution; dried food keeps because there is too little water |
| **oxygen** | plenty of oxygen → faster | most decomposers respire aerobically, releasing more energy for growth and reproduction |

Gardeners and farmers provide **optimum conditions for rapid decay** of waste biological material: a compost heap is kept warm (the heat from respiration helps), moist, and turned or built with gaps so air gets in. The **compost** is used as a **natural fertiliser** for garden plants or crops, returning mineral ions to the soil.

**Anaerobic decay produces methane gas.** **Biogas generators** use this: waste such as animal manure, food scraps or sewage is fed into a sealed tank where microorganisms break it down without oxygen. The methane produced is burned as a **fuel** for heating, cooking or generating electricity. The remaining slurry can be used as fertiliser.

**Rate calculations (MS 1c, 4a, 4c):** rate = change ÷ time.
*Mini-example:* the mass of a bag of leaf litter fell from 45 g to 27 g in 6 weeks. Rate of decay = (45 − 27) ÷ 6 = 18 ÷ 6 = **3 g per week**. Percentage lost = 18 ÷ 45 × 100 = **40 %**. From a graph of mass against time, the rate at any point is the gradient.

### Separate science only: Required practical 10: investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of decay of fresh milk by measuring pH change

There is no Trilogy equivalent of this practical. Fresh milk contains fat (lipid). The enzyme **lipase** breaks down lipids into **fatty acids and glycerol**; the fatty acids lower the pH. This models decay.

**Method**

1. Put 5 cm³ of full-fat milk and 7 cm³ of dilute sodium carbonate solution (which makes the mixture alkaline) into a test tube, with 5 drops of **phenolphthalein** indicator. The mixture is **pink**.
2. Put this tube and a tube of 5% lipase solution in a water bath at the chosen temperature (e.g. 20 °C) and leave them to equilibrate for a few minutes; check with a thermometer.
3. Add 1 cm³ of lipase to the milk mixture, stir, and start the stopwatch.
4. Stop the clock when the pink colour **disappears** (the pH has dropped below about 8.3 as fatty acids neutralise the alkali).
5. Repeat at other temperatures (e.g. 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C), repeating each three times and calculating a mean.

A **pH probe and data logger** can be used instead of the indicator, to record pH against time without judging a colour change.

| Variable | Here |
|---|---|
| Independent | temperature |
| Dependent | time for the colour to change (or change in pH per minute) |
| Control | volume of milk, volume and concentration of lipase, volume of sodium carbonate, number of indicator drops, type of milk |

**Processing:** rate = 1 ÷ time (in s⁻¹), or rate = change in pH ÷ time. *Mini-example:* the pink colour disappeared after 250 s at 30 °C. Rate = 1 ÷ 250 = 0.004 s⁻¹ (= $4 \times 10^{-3}$ s⁻¹).

**Typical results:** the time falls (rate rises) as temperature increases to an optimum around 35–40 °C, then the time rises sharply as the lipase denatures.

**Risks:** sodium carbonate and phenolphthalein are irritants (wear eye protection); lipase can cause allergic reactions (avoid skin contact); do not drink the milk; take care with hot water baths.

**Errors and improvements:** judging the end point of a gradual colour change is subjective (compare against a colour standard, or use a pH probe); the reaction starts before the tube reaches the bath temperature (equilibrate both solutions first).

### Separate science only: Impact of environmental change (Higher tier)

You must be able to **evaluate** the impact of environmental changes on the **distribution of species** in an ecosystem when given information. The changes include:

- **temperature**
- **availability of water**
- **composition of atmospheric gases** (for example carbon dioxide, or pollutants such as sulfur dioxide).

The changes may be:

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **seasonal** | birds such as swallows migrate south in autumn as temperature and insect food fall |
| **geographic** | species change with altitude or latitude (different plants on a mountain top than in the valley) |
| **caused by human interaction** | climate change moving species' ranges towards the poles or uphill (e.g. UK butterflies such as the comma have spread north); drainage reducing water for wetland species; air pollution removing pollution-sensitive lichens |

When evaluating data: describe the pattern (with figures), give the likely cause (the changed factor affects survival or reproduction), and note limitations: correlation is not causation, other factors may have changed too, small samples or short time periods may not show a trend.

::: terms
- **Producer**: an organism, usually a green plant or alga, that makes its own glucose by photosynthesis and starts every food chain.
- **Primary consumer**: an animal that eats producers; a herbivore.
- **Predator**: a consumer that kills and eats other animals.
- **Prey**: an animal that is killed and eaten by a predator.
- **Quadrat**: a square frame of known area used to sample the organisms in one small part of a habitat.
- **Transect**: a line across a habitat along which quadrats are placed at regular intervals to study how distribution changes.
- **Abundance**: the number of individuals of a species in an area.
- **Distribution**: where in a habitat the organisms of a species are found.
- **Decomposer**: a microorganism such as a bacterium or fungus that breaks down dead material and waste.
- **Biogas generator**: a sealed tank in which waste decays anaerobically to produce methane used as a fuel.
:::

::: method
**Command words.** *Describe* a predator–prey graph = say what happens to each population, in order, with the lag. *Explain* = give the reason for each change ("prey increase, so more food for predators, so more predators survive and breed"). *Suggest* = apply the idea to an unfamiliar organism or habitat. *Evaluate* (Higher tier environmental change) = use the data on both sides and reach a judgement. *Calculate* = show the working.

**Predator–prey graphs.** Always mention that changes in predator numbers **lag behind** changes in prey numbers, and give the reason (predators take time to reproduce). Quote values and years from the graph.

**Sampling calculations.** Write the steps: total ÷ number of quadrats = mean; total area ÷ quadrat area = number of quadrats that would fit; mean × that number = estimate. Watch the units: a 50 cm quadrat is 0.25 m², not 50 m². Know mean, median and mode: order the data first for the median.

**Required practical questions.** Random sampling: "use a random number generator to give coordinates" (not "throw the quadrat"). Transects: "place quadrats at regular intervals along a line and measure the abiotic factor at each". Improvements: more quadrats, repeat transects, use a light meter or probe, use a key to identify species. For the milk practical (separate science only), name lipase, fatty acids and the falling pH; the rate is 1 ÷ time.

**Cycles.** For the carbon cycle, name the process for each arrow: photosynthesis removes CO₂; respiration and combustion return it; feeding passes it on; decomposers respire. For "role of microorganisms", say both: return **carbon dioxide to the air** and **mineral ions to the soil**.

**Decay (separate science only).** For each factor, link it to the decomposers: temperature → enzymes; water → growth and enzyme action; oxygen → aerobic respiration releasing energy.

**6-mark answers.** A carbon-cycle answer earns Level 3 by following carbon in a sequence: CO₂ in air → photosynthesis → glucose and biomass in plants → eaten by animals → respiration by plants, animals and decomposers → CO₂ back to air, plus combustion and fossil fuels. A practical-method answer needs a sequence a reader could follow, with named equipment, random or regular placement, repeats, and how the data is processed.
:::

::: example Calculate: estimating a population from quadrats
A student placed 8 quadrats, each 1 m², randomly in a meadow measuring 40 m × 25 m. The numbers of buttercups were 4, 7, 2, 0, 5, 6, 3, 5. Estimate the number of buttercups in the meadow. (3)

Mean = (4 + 7 + 2 + 0 + 5 + 6 + 3 + 5) ÷ 8 = 32 ÷ 8 = 4 per m² (1).
Area of meadow = 40 × 25 = 1000 m² (1).
Population ≈ 4 × 1000 = 4000 buttercups (1).
:::

::: example Explain: a predator–prey cycle
A graph shows the numbers of snowshoe hares and lynx over 30 years. Both rise and fall, and the lynx peaks come about 2 years after the hare peaks. Explain the pattern. (4)

When hare numbers increase there is more food for the lynx, so more lynx survive and breed (1). The lynx population rises after a delay because it takes time for lynx to reproduce (1). More lynx eat more hares, so the hare population falls (1). With less food, lynx starve or breed less, so lynx numbers then fall, allowing the hares to recover and the cycle repeats (1).
:::

::: example Describe: a transect investigation
Describe how a student could investigate how light intensity affects the distribution of daisies from under a large tree into an open field. (4)

Lay a tape measure from the base of the tree out into the field (1). Place a quadrat at regular intervals, e.g. every 2 m, along the tape (1). At each quadrat count the daisies and measure the light intensity with a light meter (1). Repeat with further transects and calculate means, then plot daisy number against light intensity (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: rate of decay (separate science only)
In the milk practical, the pink colour disappeared after 400 s at 20 °C and after 125 s at 40 °C. Calculate the rate at each temperature and how many times faster the reaction was at 40 °C. (3)

Rate at 20 °C = 1 ÷ 400 = 0.0025 s⁻¹ (1).
Rate at 40 °C = 1 ÷ 125 = 0.008 s⁻¹ (1).
0.008 ÷ 0.0025 = 3.2 times faster (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate: environmental change and distribution (separate science only, Higher tier)
In 1970 a bird species was found only south of a line across central England. By 2020 it bred 150 km further north, and the mean spring temperature had risen by 1 °C. Evaluate the claim that rising temperature caused the change in distribution. (4)

Supports: the range moved north at the same time as the temperature rose (1); warmer temperatures could let more insects (food) survive further north, so more birds survive and breed there (1). Against: this is only a correlation; other factors such as habitat change, more garden feeding or less hunting may have changed too (1). Conclusion: temperature is a likely cause, but more evidence (e.g. data for other species, or other years) is needed to be sure (1).
:::

What type of organism is found at the start of every food chain, and how does it make its food? (2)

A producer (usually a green plant or alga) (1), which makes glucose by photosynthesis (1).

In the food chain seaweed → limpet → crab → seagull, name the secondary consumer. (1)

The crab.

Why should quadrats be placed at random coordinates when estimating a population? (1)
---
To avoid bias (so the sample is representative of the whole area).

::: q
Six quadrats contained 6, 2, 9, 2, 6 and 2 snails. Find the mean, median and mode. (3)
---
Mean = 27 ÷ 6 = 4.5 (1). Ordered 2, 2, 2, 6, 6, 9: median = (2 + 6) ÷ 2 = 4 (1). Mode = 2 (1).
:::

::: q
Give two processes that return carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. (2)
---
Any two: respiration (by plants, animals or microorganisms); combustion / burning of fuels; decay by microorganisms (which respire).
:::

::: q
Explain the role of microorganisms in cycling materials. (2)
---
They decay dead organisms and waste, respiring and returning carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide (1), and releasing mineral ions back into the soil for plants to absorb (1).
:::

::: q
Separate science only: explain why a compost heap is turned regularly. (2)
---
Turning lets oxygen into the heap (1), so decomposers can respire aerobically, releasing more energy for growth and reproduction, so decay is faster (1).
:::

::: q
Separate science only: in the milk decay practical, why does the pH fall? (2)
---
Lipase breaks down the lipids (fats) in milk (1) into fatty acids and glycerol; the fatty acids lower the pH (1).
:::

::: watch
- On predator–prey graphs, say the predator peak **lags behind** the prey peak, and explain it by the time predators take to breed.
- Quadrat scaling: work out how many quadrats fit in the total area (convert 50 cm × 50 cm to 0.25 m²) before multiplying by the mean.
- "Random" means random number coordinates on a grid, not throwing the quadrat over your shoulder.
- Microorganisms return **both** carbon dioxide to the air **and** mineral ions to the soil; one alone is only half the marks.
- Decay (separate science only): "temperature affects enzymes", "water for microorganisms to grow", "oxygen for aerobic respiration"; never say decomposers "die" at low temperature, they just work slowly.
- Anaerobic decay makes **methane** (biogas), not carbon monoxide or hydrogen.
:::

::: test
Separate science only: the table shows the time for the pink colour to disappear in the milk decay practical. 10 °C: 820 s; 20 °C: 410 s; 30 °C: 200 s; 40 °C: 125 s; 50 °C: 330 s. Calculate the rate at 30 °C and explain the result at 50 °C. (4)

Describe how carbon is cycled through an ecosystem, starting with carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. Include the role of microorganisms. (6)

Level 2 (3–4): most processes named and linked, but the sequence or microorganism role is incomplete.
Level 1 (1–2): some processes named with little linking.
