## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.2.3.1 (plant tissues) and 4.2.3.2 (the plant organ system: transpiration and translocation). All of it is in both GCSE Biology and Combined Science: Trilogy, and none of it is Higher-tier only.

### Plant tissues and the leaf

A **tissue** is a group of cells with a similar structure and function. An **organ** is a group of tissues working together. **The leaf is a plant organ.** AQA limits leaf knowledge to the epidermis, palisade and spongy mesophyll, xylem and phloem, and guard cells surrounding stomata.

| Tissue | Where | Structure → function |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper epidermis** | top surface of the leaf | thin, **transparent** cells so light passes through to the palisade layer; covered by a **waxy cuticle** that reduces water loss |
| **Lower epidermis** | underside | contains most of the **stomata**, each between two **guard cells** |
| **Palisade mesophyll** | just below upper epidermis | tall cells tightly packed with **many chloroplasts**, near the top surface to absorb the most light — **main site of photosynthesis** |
| **Spongy mesophyll** | below palisade | loosely packed cells with **air spaces**, giving a large surface area for **gas exchange** — CO₂ diffuses to the photosynthesising cells, O₂ and water vapour diffuse out |
| **Xylem** | in veins (vascular bundles) | carries water and mineral ions to the leaf |
| **Phloem** | in veins | carries dissolved sugars away from the leaf |
| **Meristem** | **growing tips of shoots and roots** | cells that keep **dividing** and can **differentiate** into any type of plant cell (plant stem cells) |

Other leaf adaptations: broad and flat (large surface area for light); thin (short diffusion distance for gases).

**Practical skill (AT 7)**: observing and drawing a **transverse section of a leaf** under a light microscope (a prepared slide). Identify the layers in order from top to bottom: cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll (with a vein of xylem and phloem), lower epidermis with stomata and guard cells.

### The plant organ system

**The roots, stem and leaves form a plant organ system for transport of substances around the plant.** Two transport tissues run through all three organs: xylem and phloem.

**Root hair cells** are adapted for the efficient uptake of **water by osmosis** and **mineral ions by active transport**:

- a long, thin projection (the root hair) gives a **large surface area** for absorption;
- a thin cell wall and membrane — short distance;
- **many mitochondria** to release energy by respiration for **active transport** of mineral ions, which are at a **lower** concentration in the soil than in the cell (so they must be moved **against** the concentration gradient);
- no chloroplasts (underground, no light).

Water moves in by osmosis because the soil water is a more dilute solution than the cell sap.

### Xylem and transpiration

**Xylem tissue transports water and mineral ions from the roots to the stems and leaves.** It is composed of **hollow tubes strengthened by lignin**, adapted for the transport of water in the **transpiration stream**.

- The cells are **dead** and have no end walls, forming **continuous hollow tubes** — no cytoplasm to block the flow.
- **Lignin** strengthens the walls so the tubes do not collapse, and also supports the plant.
- Flow is **one way only** — upwards.

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from the leaves, mostly through the stomata. Water **evaporates** from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, then **diffuses** out through the stomata. This pulls water up the xylem from the roots in a continuous column — the **transpiration stream** — which also brings mineral ions to the leaves, keeps cells turgid, and cools the leaf.

### Stomata and guard cells

**The role of stomata and guard cells is to control gas exchange and water loss.**

- When the plant has plenty of water, the **guard cells** take in water, become **turgid** and bend, so the **stoma opens** — CO₂ can diffuse in for photosynthesis, but water vapour escapes.
- When the plant is short of water, guard cells lose water, become **flaccid**, and the **stoma closes** — water loss is reduced, but CO₂ uptake (and so photosynthesis) also falls.
- Stomata generally open in the light and close in the dark (no photosynthesis at night, so no need for CO₂).
- Most stomata are on the **lower surface**, which is cooler and shaded, so less water evaporates.

**Investigating the distribution of stomata (AT 6, 7)**: paint clear nail varnish on the lower and upper surfaces of a leaf, let it dry, peel it off with clear tape and stick it on a slide. Count stomata in the field of view under the microscope at several random positions and calculate the **mean** (MS 2a). Use the area of the field of view to work out stomatal density (stomata per mm²). Repeat with several leaves — the principle of **sampling** (MS 2d): more, randomly chosen samples give a more representative mean.

Worked mini-example: counts of 14, 17, 12, 15 and 17 stomata in a field of view of area 0.15 mm². Mean $= 75 \div 5 = 15$. Density $= 15 \div 0.15 = 100$ stomata per mm².

### Factors affecting the rate of transpiration

| Factor change | Effect on rate | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** increases | **increases** | water molecules have more kinetic energy, so evaporation and diffusion of water vapour are faster |
| **Humidity** increases | **decreases** | more water vapour in the air outside, so the **concentration gradient** between the air spaces and the air is **less steep** — slower diffusion |
| **Air movement** (wind) increases | **increases** | moving air carries water vapour away from the leaf surface, keeping a **steep concentration gradient** |
| **Light intensity** increases | **increases** | more stomata **open** (for photosynthesis), so more water vapour can diffuse out |

Each rise does not go on for ever: e.g. once all stomata are open, more light has no further effect, so the graph levels off.

### Measuring the rate of transpiration (AT 3, 4, 5)

The rate is measured by the **uptake of water** using a **potometer** — a leafy shoot sealed into a water-filled tube with a capillary tube and an air bubble. As the shoot takes in water, the bubble moves along the scale. (The uptake slightly overestimates transpiration, as a little water is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells.)

Method: cut the shoot **under water** (stops air entering the xylem); assemble the apparatus under water and seal joints with petroleum jelly so it is airtight; dry the leaves; let the plant settle; introduce a bubble; measure the distance it moves in a set time; use the reservoir to reset the bubble; repeat and calculate a mean. Change one factor (e.g. a fan at different distances for air movement, a lamp at different distances for light) while controlling the others.

**Rate is a compound measure** (MS 1a, 1c, 5c):

$$\text{rate of water uptake} = \frac{\text{volume of water taken up}}{\text{time}}$$

If you only have the distance the bubble moved, find the volume using the cross-sectional area of the capillary tube: volume $= \pi r^2 \times \text{distance}$.

Worked mini-example: the bubble moved 42 mm in 6 minutes along a capillary of radius 0.5 mm. Volume $= \pi \times 0.5^2 \times 42 = 33.0$ mm³. Rate $= 33.0 \div 6 = 5.5$ mm³/min.

Graph skills (MS 4a, 4c): plot the independent variable (e.g. wind speed) on the x-axis and rate on the y-axis, choose a scale that uses more than half the grid, plot points accurately, and draw a line or curve of best fit.

### Phloem and translocation

**Phloem tissue transports dissolved sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant for immediate use or storage.** The movement of food molecules through phloem is called **translocation**.

**Phloem is composed of tubes of elongated cells. Cell sap can move from one phloem cell to the next through pores in the end walls.** Unlike xylem, phloem cells are **living**, and sugars can move **both up and down** the plant — from the leaves (where sugar is made) to growing tips and roots (for respiration and growth) or to storage organs such as potatoes (stored as starch). You do not need the detailed structure of phloem or the mechanism of transport.

| | Xylem | Phloem |
|---|---|---|
| Transports | water and mineral ions | dissolved sugars (sucrose) |
| Direction | **up** only, roots → leaves | **up and down** |
| Cells | dead, hollow tubes, no end walls | living, elongated cells with **pores in end walls** |
| Walls | strengthened with **lignin** | not lignified |
| Process | transpiration stream | translocation |

::: terms
- **Palisade mesophyll**: the layer of tall leaf cells packed with chloroplasts near the upper surface, the main site of photosynthesis.
- **Spongy mesophyll**: loosely packed leaf cells with air spaces that allow gases to diffuse through the leaf.
- **Meristem**: tissue at the growing tips of shoots and roots whose cells divide and can differentiate into any type of plant cell.
- **Xylem**: tissue of dead, hollow tubes strengthened by lignin that carries water and mineral ions from the roots to the leaves.
- **Phloem**: tissue of living elongated cells with pores in the end walls that carries dissolved sugars around the plant.
- **Transpiration**: the loss of water vapour from the leaves, by evaporation and diffusion through the stomata.
- **Transpiration stream**: the continuous movement of water from the roots, up the xylem, to the leaves and out of the stomata.
- **Translocation**: the movement of dissolved food molecules such as sugars through the phloem.
- **Stoma**: a pore in the leaf epidermis, opened and closed by two guard cells, that controls gas exchange and water loss.
- **Potometer**: apparatus that measures the rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot, used to estimate the rate of transpiration.
:::

::: method
1. **Command words.** *Describe* a graph = trend plus figures. *Explain* a factor's effect = name the effect AND the reason (evaporation, diffusion, concentration gradient, stomata open). *Suggest* = apply to an unfamiliar plant (e.g. cactus: fewer stomata, thick cuticle). *Compare* xylem and phloem = paired differences.
2. **Transpiration factors need the mechanism.** Humidity and wind are about the **concentration gradient** of water vapour; temperature is about **kinetic energy / evaporation**; light is about **stomata opening**. "More light makes the plant thirstier" scores nothing.
3. **Root hair cells.** Water → **osmosis**; mineral ions → **active transport**, against the gradient, energy from **respiration** in **mitochondria**. Large surface area for both.
4. **Xylem vs phloem.** Water/minerals vs sugars; up only vs both ways; dead and lignified vs living with pores in end walls; transpiration vs translocation.
5. **Calculations.** Rate = volume ÷ time with a unit (mm³/min, cm³/h). If given distance, volume = $\pi r^2 \times$ distance. Means: add and divide by the number of readings, ignoring anomalies. Check significant figures.
6. **Practical questions.** Potometer: cut under water, airtight seals, dry leaves, settle time, control variables (temperature, light, air movement, same shoot), repeat for a mean. Name the independent, dependent and control variables. Limitation: measures uptake, not loss.
7. **6-mark answers** are levels-marked: describe the method in a logical order, or explain several linked factors. Level 3 needs complete, linked reasoning.
:::

::: example Explain — root hair cell
Explain how a root hair cell is adapted to absorb water and mineral ions. (4)

The root hair gives a large surface area for absorption (1). Water enters by osmosis because the soil water is more dilute than the cell sap (1). Mineral ions are absorbed by active transport, against their concentration gradient (1), using energy released by respiration in the cell's many mitochondria (1).
:::

::: example Explain — humidity
Explain why the rate of transpiration is lower on a humid day. (3)

On a humid day the air contains more water vapour (1), so the concentration gradient of water vapour between the air spaces in the leaf and the air outside is less steep (1), so water vapour diffuses out through the stomata more slowly (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — potometer
In a potometer, the air bubble moved 36 mm in 5 minutes. The capillary tube has a radius of 0.4 mm. Calculate the rate of water uptake in mm³ per minute, to 2 significant figures. (3)

Volume $= \pi r^2 \times d = \pi \times 0.4^2 \times 36 = 18.1$ mm³ (1). Rate $= 18.1 \div 5 = 3.62$ (1) $= 3.6$ mm³/min (1).
:::

::: example Compare — xylem and phloem
Compare the structure and function of xylem and phloem. (4)

Xylem transports water and mineral ions, whereas phloem transports dissolved sugars (1). In xylem the flow is only upwards from the roots, whereas in phloem substances move both up and down (1). Xylem is made of dead, hollow tubes, whereas phloem is made of living elongated cells (1). Xylem walls are strengthened with lignin, whereas phloem cells have pores in their end walls (1).
:::

::: example Explain — guard cells
Explain why stomata close when a plant is short of water, and the disadvantage of this. (3)

Guard cells lose water and become flaccid, so the stomata close (1), reducing the loss of water vapour by transpiration and preventing wilting (1). But less carbon dioxide can diffuse into the leaf, so the rate of photosynthesis falls (1).
:::

Describe how xylem is adapted for transporting water. (2)

Xylem forms continuous hollow tubes with no end walls, so water flows freely (1); the walls are strengthened with lignin so the tubes do not collapse (1).

::: q
Describe the process of transpiration. (3)
---
Water evaporates from the surfaces of mesophyll cells into the air spaces (1); water vapour diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata (1); this draws water up the xylem from the roots in the transpiration stream (1).

Explain the effect of increasing air movement on the rate of transpiration. (2)

Rate increases (1) because moving air removes water vapour from around the leaf, keeping a steep concentration gradient for diffusion (1).

::: watch
- Root hair cells: **water by osmosis**, **mineral ions by active transport**. Swapping these, or saying minerals "diffuse in", loses marks.
- Transpiration is **evaporation then diffusion of water vapour** — say "water vapour" for the gas leaving the leaf.
- Humidity **decreases** the rate. Explain it with a **less steep concentration gradient**, not "the air is full".
- Xylem cells are **dead**; phloem cells are **living**. Phloem moves sugar **both ways**; xylem only **up**.
- Transpiration stream is in the **xylem**; translocation is in the **phloem** — never the other way round.
- Potometer rate needs a **unit per time**; convert distance to volume with $\pi r^2 \times$ distance when given the radius.
:::

::: test
A potometer bubble moved 54 mm in 3 minutes at 20 °C and 54 mm in 2 minutes at 30 °C. Calculate both rates in mm per minute and explain the difference. (4)
---
20 °C: $54 \div 3 = 18$ mm/min (1). 30 °C: $54 \div 2 = 27$ mm/min (1). At the higher temperature water molecules have more kinetic energy (1), so water evaporates from mesophyll cells and diffuses out of the stomata faster, increasing the rate of uptake (1).

::: test
The capillary tube in a potometer has a radius of 0.5 mm. The bubble moves 60 mm in 10 minutes. Calculate the volume of water taken up, and the rate in mm³ per hour. (3)
---
Volume $= \pi \times 0.5^2 \times 60 = 47.1$ mm³ (1). Rate $= 47.1 \div 10 = 4.71$ mm³/min (1); $\times 60 = 283$ mm³ per hour (1). (Accept 280–283.)
:::

::: test
A graph shows that the rate of transpiration rises as light intensity increases, then levels off. Explain the shape of the graph. (3)
---
As light intensity increases, more stomata open (to let in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis) (1), so more water vapour can diffuse out and the rate rises (1). It levels off when all the stomata are fully open, so light is no longer the limiting factor / another factor such as temperature or humidity limits the rate (1).
:::

::: test
Plants in hot, dry deserts often have a thick waxy cuticle, few stomata and stomata sunk in pits. Suggest how each feature helps the plant survive. (3)
---
Thick waxy cuticle reduces evaporation of water from the leaf surface (1). Fewer stomata means fewer pores for water vapour to escape (1). Sunken stomata trap humid air, reducing the concentration gradient so less water vapour diffuses out (1).
:::
