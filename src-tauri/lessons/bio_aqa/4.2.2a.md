## What it is

The digestive system is an organ system in which several organs work together to **digest** food (break large insoluble molecules into small soluble ones) and **absorb** the products into the blood. Enzymes do the digesting. This topic (spec 4.2.2.1) is the same for AQA Biology 8461 and Combined Science Trilogy 8464, with no Higher-tier-only content; only the practical numbers differ.

### The organs of the digestive system

| Organ | Role |
|---|---|
| **Mouth and salivary glands** | teeth break food into smaller pieces; salivary glands release **amylase** |
| **Oesophagus** | carries food to the stomach by muscle contractions |
| **Stomach** | muscular walls churn food; glands release **protease** (pepsin) and **hydrochloric acid** (kills bacteria and gives the acid pH the stomach protease needs) |
| **Liver** | makes **bile** |
| **Gall bladder** | stores bile and releases it into the small intestine |
| **Pancreas** | makes **amylase, protease and lipase** and releases them into the small intestine |
| **Small intestine** | makes some enzymes too; digestion is completed here; small soluble molecules are **absorbed** into the blood through villi |
| **Large intestine** | water is absorbed from undigested food, forming faeces |

### Enzymes: what they are

- Enzymes are **biological catalysts**: they speed up reactions in living organisms without being used up.
- They are **large protein molecules**, each folded into a specific 3-D shape.
- Each enzyme has an **active site**, a region with a shape that fits only one substrate (or a small group of similar ones). That is why each enzyme catalyses a **specific reaction**.

**Lock and key theory** (a simplified model): the substrate (key) fits into the enzyme's active site (lock) because their shapes are **complementary**. An **enzyme–substrate complex** forms, the reaction happens, the products are released, and the enzyme is free to be used again.

Another model, the **induced fit model**, says the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, gripping it more tightly. It explains the evidence better than the rigid lock-and-key model, but both explain specificity. AQA lets you use other models, as long as you link shape to specificity.

**Link to metabolism**: metabolism is the sum of all the reactions in a cell or organism. Enzymes catalyse these reactions, both breaking molecules down (digestion, respiration) and building them up (e.g. glucose into starch, glycogen or cellulose; amino acids into proteins).

### Temperature and pH

| Factor | Effect on enzyme activity |
|---|---|
| **Temperature rises (below the optimum)** | molecules move faster with more kinetic energy, so more frequent successful collisions between substrate and active site → rate increases |
| **Optimum temperature** | the rate is highest (about 37 °C for most human enzymes) |
| **Above the optimum** | bonds holding the enzyme's shape break; the **active site changes shape**, so the substrate **no longer fits**: the enzyme is **denatured** and the rate falls quickly to zero. Denaturing is permanent. |
| **pH** | each enzyme has an **optimum pH**; too far either side changes the shape of the active site, so the substrate no longer fits and the enzyme is denatured |

Typical optimum pHs: stomach protease (pepsin) about **pH 2**; salivary amylase about **pH 7**; pancreatic enzymes in the small intestine about **pH 8** (alkaline, helped by bile).

A graph of rate against temperature rises gradually to a peak, then falls steeply. A graph of rate against pH is roughly a narrow peak at the optimum.

### Digestive enzymes: sites and actions

Digestive enzymes convert food into **small soluble molecules** that can be absorbed into the bloodstream. You must know these word equations (no symbol equations needed):

| Enzyme | Word equation | Made in | Works in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrases**, e.g. **amylase** | starch → simple sugars (maltose, then glucose) | **salivary glands**, **pancreas** (and small intestine) | mouth, small intestine |
| **Proteases** | proteins → **amino acids** | **stomach**, **pancreas**, small intestine | stomach, small intestine |
| **Lipases** | lipids (fats) → **glycerol** + **fatty acids** | **pancreas**, small intestine | small intestine |

The **products of digestion** are used to build new carbohydrates, lipids and proteins in the body's cells. Some **glucose** is used in **respiration** to release energy.

### Bile

- Bile is **made in the liver** and **stored in the gall bladder**, then released into the small intestine. It is **not an enzyme**.
- It is **alkaline**, so it **neutralises the hydrochloric acid** from the stomach, giving the alkaline conditions the enzymes in the small intestine work best in.
- It **emulsifies** fat: breaks large fat droplets into **small droplets**, which **increases the surface area** for lipase to act on.
- Both effects **increase the rate of fat breakdown by lipase**.

### Rate calculations (MS 1a, 1c)

- **Rate = amount of substrate used (or product made) ÷ time.**
- If only the time to finish is measured: **rate = 1 ÷ time**, with unit s⁻¹ (or min⁻¹).

*Mini-example*: starch digested in 60 s at pH 6 and in 240 s at pH 4. Rate at pH 6 = 1 ÷ 60 = 0.017 s⁻¹; at pH 4 = 1 ÷ 240 = 0.0042 s⁻¹. The rate at pH 6 is 4 times faster.

*Mini-example*: 5 cm³ of 1 % starch solution contains 0.05 g of starch. If it is digested in 2 minutes, rate = 0.05 ÷ 2 = 0.025 g/min.

### Required practical 4: food tests (Trilogy: Required practical 3)

Grind the food with distilled water using a pestle and mortar, then filter to make a food solution.

| Test for | Reagent and method | Positive result | Negative result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sugars** (reducing sugars such as glucose) | add **Benedict's solution**; heat in a **water bath** at about 75–80 °C for a few minutes | **green, yellow or brick-red** precipitate (more sugar → further along towards brick-red) | stays **blue** |
| **Starch** | add a few drops of **iodine solution** | **blue-black** | stays **orange-brown** |
| **Protein** | add **Biuret reagent** (or sodium hydroxide then copper sulfate solution) | **purple / lilac** | stays **blue** |
| **Lipids** | add a few drops of **Sudan III** and shake; or shake with **ethanol** then pour into water (emulsion test) | Sudan III: a **red-stained oily layer** on top; ethanol: a **cloudy white** emulsion | Sudan III: no red layer; ethanol: stays clear |

Risks: Benedict's and Biuret reagents are irritants (Biuret contains corrosive sodium hydroxide) so wear eye protection; use a water bath, not a naked flame, for Benedict's; ethanol is highly flammable (no flames nearby). These tests are **qualitative**: they show whether a substance is present, not exactly how much (Benedict's gives only a rough idea).

### Required practical 5: effect of pH on the rate of reaction of amylase (Trilogy: Required practical 4)

Method (continuous sampling):
1. Put one drop of **iodine** solution in each well of a **spotting tile**.
2. Place test tubes of **amylase**, **starch solution** and a **pH buffer** in a **water bath** (e.g. 35 °C) for a few minutes so they reach the same temperature.
3. Mix the amylase, buffer and starch in one tube, start the **stopwatch**, and keep the tube in the water bath.
4. Every **30 seconds**, take a drop of the mixture with a pipette and add it to a fresh well of iodine.
5. Record the time when the iodine **stays orange-brown**: all the starch has been digested.
6. Repeat with buffers of different pH (e.g. pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8) and calculate rate = 1 ÷ time.

Variables: independent = **pH**; dependent = **time taken for starch to be completely digested** (or rate); control = **temperature** (water bath or electric heater), volume and concentration of starch and amylase, volume of buffer.

Typical results: the shortest time (fastest rate) is around **pH 6–7**, the optimum for amylase; at very low or high pH the starch takes much longer or is never digested because the amylase is denatured.

Errors and improvements: sampling every 30 s means the true time could be up to 30 s earlier (sample more often, e.g. every 10 s); judging the colour change is subjective (compare against a control well, or use a colorimeter); repeat each pH and calculate a mean. Risks: iodine is an irritant; wear eye protection.

::: terms
- **Enzyme**: a large protein molecule that acts as a biological catalyst, speeding up a specific reaction without being used up.
- **Active site**: the region of an enzyme with a specific shape into which the substrate fits.
- **Lock and key theory**: a model in which the substrate fits the active site because their shapes are complementary, like a key in a lock.
- **Denatured**: describes an enzyme whose active site has permanently changed shape, so the substrate no longer fits.
- **Optimum**: the temperature or pH at which an enzyme works fastest.
- **Carbohydrase**: an enzyme that breaks down carbohydrates into simple sugars, for example amylase.
- **Protease**: an enzyme that breaks down proteins into amino acids.
- **Lipase**: an enzyme that breaks down lipids (fats) into glycerol and fatty acids.
- **Bile**: an alkaline liquid made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder that neutralises stomach acid and emulsifies fat.
- **Emulsify**: to break large fat droplets into many small droplets, increasing their surface area.
:::

::: method
**Command words.** *State* or *name* = a word or short phrase (e.g. "pancreas"). *Describe* a graph = the trend with figures from the graph (rises to a peak of X at Y °C, then falls to zero by Z °C). *Explain* a graph = the reason in terms of collisions, the **active site** and **denaturing**. *Suggest* = apply the ideas to an unfamiliar enzyme or organism. *Calculate* = rate with working and unit.

**Explaining enzyme graphs.** Below the optimum: more kinetic energy → more collisions between substrate and active site → more enzyme–substrate complexes per second. Above the optimum or away from the optimum pH: the **shape of the active site changes**, the **substrate no longer fits**, the enzyme is **denatured**. Never say the enzyme is "killed" or "dies"; enzymes are molecules, not living things.

**Specificity.** Explain using shape: "the active site has a specific shape that is complementary to the substrate, so only that substrate fits". Use the lock-and-key model, and mention induced fit if asked for another model.

**Sites and actions.** Lay out each enzyme as: name → substrate → products → where made → where it works. Bile is not an enzyme and does not digest fat chemically: it **emulsifies** (larger surface area) and **neutralises** acid (alkaline conditions for the enzymes).

**Rate calculations.** Equation → substitute → answer with unit. Rate = amount ÷ time (g/min, cm³/s) or 1 ÷ time (s⁻¹). Round to sensible significant figures (usually 2 or 3).

**Required-practical questions.** Food tests: reagent → method (heat for Benedict's) → positive colour → negative colour. Amylase and pH: independent, dependent and control variables; why the water bath; why sample every 30 s; why iodine stays orange-brown at the end point; improvements (shorter sampling interval, repeats, colorimeter).

**6-mark answers.** Levels of response want a logical, linked sequence. For "describe how fat is digested", follow food from the mouth onwards: bile released from the gall bladder, neutralises acid, emulsifies fat, larger surface area, lipase from the pancreas, glycerol and fatty acids, absorbed in the small intestine.
:::

::: example Explain: temperature graph
A graph shows the rate of an enzyme reaction rising from 10 °C to a maximum at 40 °C, then falling to zero at 60 °C. Explain the shape of the graph. (4)

From 10 to 40 °C the molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster, so there are more collisions between the substrate and the active site per second (1), and more enzyme–substrate complexes form, so the rate increases (1). 40 °C is the optimum temperature (1). Above 40 °C the active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits: the enzyme is denatured, so the rate falls to zero by 60 °C (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: rate from time
In Required practical 5 the starch was completely digested after 150 s at pH 7 and after 450 s at pH 5. Calculate the rate at each pH, as 1/time, and how many times faster the reaction was at pH 7. (3)

pH 7: rate = 1 ÷ 150 = 0.0067 s⁻¹ (1).
pH 5: rate = 1 ÷ 450 = 0.0022 s⁻¹ (1).
0.0067 ÷ 0.0022 = 3, so **3 times faster** at pH 7 (equivalently 450 ÷ 150 = 3) (1).
:::

::: example Explain: bile
Explain how bile increases the rate of fat digestion. (4)

Bile is alkaline, so it neutralises the hydrochloric acid from the stomach (1), giving the alkaline conditions in which lipase works best (1). Bile emulsifies fat, breaking large droplets into small droplets (1), which increases the surface area for lipase to act on, so fats are broken down into glycerol and fatty acids faster (1).
:::

::: example Describe: food tests
A student has a solution that may contain starch and protein. Describe how she could test for each, and give the positive results. (4)

Starch: add a few drops of iodine solution (1); it turns from orange-brown to blue-black if starch is present (1).
Protein: add Biuret reagent (1); it turns from blue to purple if protein is present (1).
:::

::: example Explain: specificity
Explain why amylase breaks down starch but does not break down proteins. (3)

The active site of amylase has a specific shape (1). Only starch molecules have a shape complementary to the active site, so only starch fits and forms an enzyme–substrate complex (1). Protein molecules have a different shape and do not fit the active site, so they are not broken down (1).
:::

::: watch
- Enzymes are **denatured**, never "killed": they are protein molecules, not living things.
- Explain denaturing through the **active site**: its **shape changes**, so the **substrate no longer fits**.
- Bile is **not an enzyme**. It **emulsifies** fat (bigger surface area) and **neutralises** stomach acid. It is made in the **liver** and stored in the **gall bladder**: do not swap them.
- Lipids → **glycerol and fatty acids** (both); proteins → **amino acids**; starch → **sugars**.
- Benedict's needs **heating**; iodine positive is **blue-black**; Biuret positive is **purple**. State the colour change, not just the end colour.
- Rate = 1 ÷ time: a **longer time means a slower rate**. Do not read times as rates on a graph.

---
