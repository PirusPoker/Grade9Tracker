## What it is

This lesson covers spec subsection **4.6.4 Classification of living organisms**. It is in **both Biology 8461 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464** (same number), with no Higher-tier-only content. It is about **how scientific methods and theories develop over time (WS 1.1)** and **interpreting evolutionary trees (WS 1.2)**.

### The Linnaean system

**Traditionally, living things have been classified into groups depending on their structure and characteristics**, in a system developed by **Carl Linnaeus** (Swedish, 18th century).

Linnaeus classified living things into seven levels, from the biggest group to the smallest:

| Level | Example: human | Example: lion |
|---|---|---|
| **Kingdom** | Animalia | Animalia |
| **Phylum** | Chordata | Chordata |
| **Class** | Mammalia | Mammalia |
| **Order** | Primates | Carnivora |
| **Family** | Hominidae | Felidae |
| **Genus** | *Homo* | *Panthera* |
| **Species** | *sapiens* | *leo* |

Memory aid: **K**ing **P**hilip **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup.

- Moving **down** the levels, each group contains **fewer organisms** that share **more features** with each other.
- Two organisms in the **same genus** are more closely related than two that only share the same family.
- The five **kingdoms** traditionally used are animals, plants, fungi, protists and prokaryotes.

**The binomial system.** Organisms are named by **the binomial system of genus and species**: a two-part Latin name, e.g. *Homo sapiens*, *Panthera leo*. Rules: the **genus** has a **capital letter**, the **species** a **lower-case letter**, and the name is written in *italics* (or underlined when handwritten).

**Why use binomial names?** Common names vary between languages and regions (a "robin" in the UK is a different bird from a "robin" in North America). A binomial name is **the same worldwide**, so scientists know exactly which organism is meant, and the genus shows its closest relatives.

**Using information given (exam skill).** If you are told that the lion is *Panthera leo*, the tiger *Panthera tigris* and the cheetah *Acinonyx jubatus*, you can say the lion and tiger are more closely related to each other than either is to the cheetah, because they share the genus *Panthera*. All three are in the same family (Felidae).

### How developments in biology changed classification

**As evidence of internal structures became more developed due to improvements in microscopes, and the understanding of biochemical processes progressed, new models of classification were proposed.**

- **Microscopes** revealed cell structures — e.g. that bacteria have **no nucleus** (prokaryotic), which separated them from all other organisms; and that fungi are not plants (no chlorophyll, cell walls made of chitin, not cellulose).
- **Biochemistry** — comparing chemicals such as DNA, RNA and proteins — showed that organisms that look alike are not always closely related, and vice versa.

### Carl Woese and the three-domain system

**Due to evidence available from chemical analysis**, there is now a **"three-domain system" developed by Carl Woese** (1990). Woese compared the sequences of **ribosomal RNA** in different organisms and found that some "bacteria" were as different from true bacteria as they were from us. Organisms are divided into:

| Domain | What it contains |
|---|---|
| **Archaea** | **Primitive bacteria usually living in extreme environments** (hot springs, salt lakes, deep-sea vents) — extremophiles. Prokaryotic, but with different chemistry from true bacteria. |
| **Bacteria** | **True bacteria** — prokaryotes such as *E. coli* and *Salmonella*. |
| **Eukaryota** | Organisms with a nucleus — this **includes protists, fungi, plants and animals**. |

The three domains sit **above** the kingdom level. This is a good example of WS 1.1: a classification system was **changed because of new evidence**.

### Evolutionary trees

**Evolutionary trees are a method used by scientists to show how they believe organisms are related.** They use **current classification data for living organisms** and **fossil data for extinct organisms**.

How to read one:
- The **base (root)** is the common ancestor of everything on the tree; time runs from the root to the tips (or along a time axis).
- Each **branching point (node)** represents a **common ancestor**.
- Two species are **more closely related** if they share a **more recent common ancestor** — i.e. their branches join **closer to the tips**.
- A branch that **ends before the present** is an **extinct** species.
- Distances on the page across the tips mean nothing — only the branching pattern and the time axis matter.

Example tree, described in words (species are labelled as an exam would): a common ancestor splits 60 million years ago into two lines. Line 1 splits 10 million years ago into **species A** and **species B**. Line 2 splits 25 million years ago into **species C** and **species D**; the branch for D stops 5 million years ago. A and B share the most recent common ancestor (10 million years ago), so they are the most closely related pair; B and C are only related through the ancestor 60 million years ago; D is **extinct** (it died out 5 million years ago, so it is known only from fossils).

::: terms
- **Classification**: sorting living organisms into groups based on shared features or evolutionary relationships.
- **Linnaean system**: classification into kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species, developed by Carl Linnaeus.
- **Binomial system**: naming an organism with two Latin words, its genus then its species, such as Homo sapiens.
- **Genus**: the classification group above species; the first word of a binomial name, written with a capital letter.
- **Species**: the smallest group; organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
- **Three-domain system**: Carl Woese's classification into Archaea, Bacteria and Eukaryota, based on chemical analysis.
- **Archaea**: primitive bacteria usually living in extreme environments, forming their own domain.
- **Evolutionary tree**: a diagram showing how scientists believe organisms are related through common ancestors.
:::

::: method
**Command words on this topic**
- **Give the order of the groups** — kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Learn the order both ways.
- **Use the information to explain which organisms are most closely related** — quote the evidence: "they share the same **genus**" (from the binomial name) or "they share the most **recent common ancestor**" (from the tree). Name the group or the time.
- **Explain why scientists use the binomial system** — the same name worldwide, avoids confusion from local common names, shows relationships (genus).
- **Describe how / explain why classification systems have changed** — new evidence: better **microscopes** showed internal structures; **biochemical analysis** (DNA, RNA) showed true relationships; this led to **Woese's three-domain system**. This is a "how science develops" (WS 1.1) answer: new evidence → new model.
- **Name the three domains and what each contains** — Archaea (primitive bacteria in extreme environments), Bacteria (true bacteria), Eukaryota (protists, fungi, plants, animals).
- **Interpret the evolutionary tree** — common ancestors are branching points; more recent branching point = more closely related; branch ending early = extinct. Read times off the axis precisely, with units (millions of years).

**Longer answers.** A typical 4–6 mark question: "Describe how and why the classification of living organisms has changed since Linnaeus." Structure it chronologically — Linnaeus (structure and characteristics, seven levels, binomial names) → improved microscopes and biochemistry → Woese's chemical analysis → three domains — with a reason at each step.

**Spelling.** Write the binomial name with a capital genus and lower-case species. Misspelt technical words (e.g. "eukaryota") still usually score, but swapping the order of genus and species does not.
:::

::: example Binomial names
The common frog is *Rana temporaria*. The marsh frog is *Pelophylax ridibundus* and the edible frog is *Pelophylax esculentus*. Which two frogs are most closely related? Explain your answer. (2)

The marsh frog and the edible frog (1), because they are in the same genus, *Pelophylax* (1).
:::

::: example Reading an evolutionary tree
On an evolutionary tree, species W and X share a common ancestor 4 million years ago; X and Y share a common ancestor 12 million years ago; Z's branch ends 2 million years ago. (a) Which species is most closely related to X? (b) What happened to Z? (2)

(a) W, because W and X share the most recent common ancestor (1). (b) Z became extinct about 2 million years ago (1).
:::

::: example Woese
Explain why Carl Woese proposed a three-domain system. (3)

Chemical analysis (of RNA sequences) showed that some organisms classed as bacteria were very different from true bacteria (1). These primitive bacteria, the Archaea, usually live in extreme environments (1). So he divided organisms into three domains — Archaea, Bacteria and Eukaryota — to reflect this new evidence (1).
:::

Put these groups in order from largest to smallest: family, kingdom, species, class, genus, order, phylum. (1)

::: watch
- Learn the **order** of the seven groups — mixing up **order** and **family**, or **phylum** and **class**, is the classic error.
- Binomial = **genus then species**; genus has a **capital**, species does not.
- The three domains are **Archaea, Bacteria, Eukaryota** — not "animals, plants, bacteria". Archaea are **primitive bacteria in extreme environments**.
- Woese's system came from **chemical analysis**, not from looking at structure.
- On evolutionary trees, relatedness comes from the **most recent common ancestor** (branching point), never from how close the names are on the page.
:::
