## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.3.1.6 to 4.3.1.9: the body's non-specific defences, the immune system, vaccination, antibiotics and painkillers, and how new drugs are discovered and tested. All of it is in both GCSE Biology and Combined Science: Trilogy, and none of it is Higher-tier only. (Monoclonal antibodies, 4.3.2, are a separate lesson and are Separate science only.)

### Non-specific defences: keeping pathogens out

These work against **any** pathogen — they are the body's first line of defence.

| Defence | How it stops pathogens |
|---|---|
| **Skin** | a tough **physical barrier** covering the body; produces **antimicrobial secretions** (oily sebum) that kill pathogens; covered in harmless microorganisms that compete with pathogens; cuts are sealed by **clotting** and scabs |
| **Nose** | **hairs and mucus** trap particles that may contain pathogens before they reach the lungs |
| **Trachea and bronchi** | lined with cells that secrete sticky **mucus**, which traps pathogens, and **ciliated cells** whose **cilia** beat to move the mucus **up** to the throat, where it is swallowed |
| **Stomach** | produces **hydrochloric acid**, which **kills** pathogens in food and drink |

### The immune system: destroying pathogens that get in

**If a pathogen enters the body the immune system tries to destroy the pathogen.** The key cells are **white blood cells**, which **help to defend against pathogens by**:

1. **Phagocytosis** — white blood cells (phagocytes) **engulf** pathogens, take them inside the cell and **digest** them with enzymes. This is non-specific.
2. **Antibody production** — white blood cells (lymphocytes) produce **antibodies**: proteins that are **specific** to one pathogen, because they fit the **antigens** (unique proteins) on its surface. Antibodies bind to the pathogen and help destroy it — e.g. by clumping pathogens together so phagocytes can engulf them more easily. It takes time to make enough of the right antibody the first time you meet a pathogen, so you become ill while this happens.
3. **Antitoxin production** — white blood cells produce **antitoxins**, which bind to and **neutralise the toxins** released by bacteria.

After an infection, **memory cells** remain. If the same pathogen enters again, they produce the right antibodies **much faster and in greater numbers**, destroying the pathogen before you become ill — you are **immune**.

### Vaccination

**Vaccination involves introducing small quantities of dead or inactive forms of a pathogen into the body to stimulate the white blood cells to produce antibodies. If the same pathogen re-enters the body the white blood cells respond quickly to produce the correct antibodies, preventing infection.**

Step by step:

1. A vaccine (usually an injection; some are oral or a nasal spray) contains dead or inactive pathogens, which carry the pathogen's **antigens** but cannot cause the disease.
2. White blood cells detect the antigens and produce the specific antibodies (and memory cells).
3. Later, if the live pathogen enters the body, white blood cells respond **quickly**, producing the correct antibodies in large numbers.
4. The pathogen is destroyed before it can reproduce enough to cause illness.

**Herd immunity — reducing spread in a population**: if **a large proportion of the population is immunised**, the pathogen has few people to infect, so it **cannot spread** easily. This also protects people who **cannot** be vaccinated (newborn babies, people with weak immune systems or allergies to the vaccine), because they are unlikely to meet an infected person.

You do not need to know vaccination schedules or side effects of specific vaccines.

**Evaluating the global use of vaccination (WS 1.4)**:

| For | Against / issues |
|---|---|
| Has controlled many serious diseases — **smallpox has been eradicated** worldwide, polio almost | some people choose not to be vaccinated, so herd immunity may be lost and outbreaks occur |
| Prevents illness and deaths, and protects those who cannot be vaccinated | rare side effects (e.g. a sore arm, mild fever; very rarely a bad reaction) |
| Prevents epidemics, saving healthcare costs and working days | vaccines can be costly to make, store (often refrigerated) and distribute in poorer or remote countries |
| Cheaper than treating the disease | pathogens that **mutate** quickly (e.g. flu) need new vaccines often; no vaccine yet for some diseases (e.g. HIV) |

### Antibiotics and painkillers

**Antibiotics, such as penicillin, are medicines that help to cure bacterial disease by killing infective bacteria inside the body.** **It is important that specific bacteria should be treated by specific antibiotics** — different antibiotics work against different bacteria, so doctors must use one that is effective against the bacterium causing that infection.

- **The use of antibiotics has greatly reduced deaths from infectious bacterial diseases.**
- **However, the emergence of strains resistant to antibiotics is of great concern.** (Overuse and misuse — e.g. prescribing them for viral infections, or patients not finishing the course — speed this up; this links to the lesson on resistant bacteria and evolution.)
- **Antibiotics cannot kill viral pathogens.** Viruses live and reproduce **inside** the body's cells, and antibiotics work on structures and processes that only bacteria have.
- **It is difficult to develop drugs that kill viruses without also damaging the body's tissues**, because viruses use the body's own cells to reproduce. (Antiretroviral drugs for HIV are an example of antiviral drugs that control rather than cure.)

**Painkillers and other medicines are used to treat the symptoms of disease but do not kill pathogens.** Examples: aspirin and paracetamol relieve pain and reduce fever; cough medicines soothe coughs. You get better because your **immune system** destroys the pathogen.

| | Antibiotics | Painkillers |
|---|---|---|
| Kill pathogens? | yes — **bacteria only** | no |
| What they do | cure bacterial disease | relieve **symptoms** |
| Work on viruses? | no | relieve symptoms of viral illness, e.g. a cold |

### Discovery and development of drugs

**Traditionally drugs were extracted from plants and microorganisms:**

- the **heart drug digitalis** originates from **foxgloves**;
- the **painkiller aspirin** originates from **willow** (bark);
- **penicillin** was discovered by **Alexander Fleming** from the ***Penicillium* mould** (1928) — he noticed bacteria did not grow around mould on a contaminated culture plate.

**Most new drugs are synthesised by chemists in the pharmaceutical industry. However, the starting point may still be a chemical extracted from a plant.**

**New medical drugs have to be tested and trialled before being used to check that they are safe and effective.** **New drugs are extensively tested for:**

- **toxicity** — is it harmful (safe)?
- **efficacy** — does it work?
- **dose** — what is the optimum amount to give?

The stages:

1. **Preclinical testing** — **done in a laboratory using cells, tissues and live animals**. Tests on cells and tissues find out if the drug works and whether it is toxic; tests on live animals show its effect on a whole body (and help find a safe starting dose).
2. **Clinical trials** — **use healthy volunteers and patients**.
   - **Very low doses of the drug are given at the start of the clinical trial**, to **healthy volunteers**, to check it is **safe** (no harmful side effects).
   - **If the drug is found to be safe, further clinical trials are carried out** on **patients** with the illness **to find the optimum dose** and check it works.
   - **In double blind trials, some patients are given a placebo.** Patients are randomly put into two groups: one gets the drug, the other a **placebo** (a dummy that looks like the drug but contains no active ingredient). In a **double blind** trial, **neither the patients nor the doctors know** who has which, so their expectations cannot bias the results. Comparing the groups shows whether the drug has a real effect beyond the **placebo effect**.
3. **Peer review (WS 1.6)** — **the results of testing and trials are published only after scrutiny by peer review**: other independent scientists in the same field check the methods, data and conclusions are valid before publication, which helps prevent false or biased claims.

Only after all of this is a drug licensed for use, and it is still monitored afterwards. The whole process takes many years and is very expensive.

Worked mini-example (data from a trial): in a trial of 400 patients, 200 got the drug and 200 a placebo. 128 in the drug group and 46 in the placebo group improved. Drug group $= \frac{128}{200} \times 100 = 64\%$; placebo $= \frac{46}{200} \times 100 = 23\%$. The drug improved 41 percentage points more patients than the placebo, so it is effective — and the 23% shows a placebo effect.

::: terms
- **Phagocytosis**: the process in which a white blood cell engulfs a pathogen and digests it.
- **Antibody**: a protein made by white blood cells that binds to a specific antigen on a particular pathogen, helping to destroy it.
- **Antitoxin**: a substance made by white blood cells that neutralises the toxins released by bacteria.
- **Antigen**: a unique protein on the surface of a pathogen that white blood cells recognise as foreign.
- **Vaccination**: introducing small quantities of dead or inactive pathogens into the body so white blood cells make antibodies and give future immunity.
- **Herd immunity**: protection of a whole population, including unvaccinated people, when a large proportion are immunised so the pathogen cannot spread.
- **Antibiotic**: a medicine, such as penicillin, that cures bacterial disease by killing bacteria inside the body; it does not work on viruses.
- **Painkiller**: a medicine that relieves the symptoms of a disease but does not kill the pathogen.
- **Placebo**: a dummy treatment that looks like the drug but contains no active ingredient, given to a control group in a trial.
- **Double blind trial**: a trial in which neither the patients nor the doctors know who is receiving the drug and who the placebo.
- **Peer review**: checking of scientific work by other independent experts in the field before it is published.
:::

::: method
1. **Command words.** *Describe* a defence = what it is and what it does. *Explain* = how it stops or destroys the pathogen (e.g. "cilia **move mucus up to the throat** where it is swallowed"). *Evaluate* vaccination or a trial = pros, cons, a judgement. *Suggest* = apply ideas to new data, e.g. a new drug or disease.
2. **Defences: be precise.** Mucus **traps**; cilia **move mucus**; stomach acid **kills**; skin is a **barrier**. Cilia do not "kill" or "catch" pathogens.
3. **White blood cells — three named jobs**: phagocytosis (engulf and digest), antibodies (specific, bind to antigens), antitoxins (neutralise toxins). Write the jobs, not just the names.
4. **Vaccination chain**: dead or inactive pathogen → white blood cells make antibodies (memory cells) → same pathogen re-enters → white blood cells respond **quickly** making the **correct** antibodies → pathogen destroyed **before you become ill**. For population questions add **herd immunity**.
5. **Antibiotics**: kill **bacteria** only, specific antibiotic for specific bacterium, **not viruses**, resistance a concern. Painkillers treat **symptoms**.
6. **Drug testing**: toxicity, efficacy, dose; preclinical (cells, tissues, live animals) → clinical (low dose to healthy volunteers → patients for optimum dose) → double blind with placebo → peer review. Give the **reason** for each step.
7. **Trial data**: calculate percentages for each group so groups of different size can be compared; compare drug with placebo; comment on sample size, repeats and blinding.
8. **6-mark answers** (drug development, how the body defends itself) are levels-marked: sequence the stages logically and give a purpose for each.
:::

::: example Explain — trachea and bronchi
Explain how the trachea and bronchi help to prevent pathogens reaching the lungs. (3)

Cells lining the trachea and bronchi produce sticky mucus (1), which traps pathogens in the air (1). Cilia on ciliated cells beat to move the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed (1).
:::

::: example Explain — vaccination
Explain how vaccination prevents a person becoming ill with measles. (4)

The vaccine contains small quantities of dead or inactive measles virus (1). This stimulates white blood cells to produce antibodies against it (1). If the live measles virus enters the body later, white blood cells respond quickly to produce the correct antibodies in large numbers (1), destroying the virus before it can cause illness (1).
:::

::: example Explain — antibiotics and a cold
A patient with a cold asks for antibiotics. Explain why the doctor refuses, and suggest what the patient could take instead. (3)

A cold is caused by a virus, and antibiotics cannot kill viruses (1); unnecessary use also increases the risk of antibiotic-resistant bacteria emerging (1). The patient could take painkillers to relieve the symptoms while their immune system destroys the virus (1).
:::

::: example Describe — double blind trial
Describe how a double blind trial of a new drug is carried out and why it is designed this way. (4)

Patients with the illness are randomly divided into two groups (1). One group is given the drug and the other a placebo that looks identical but contains no active ingredient (1). Neither the patients nor the doctors know which group each patient is in (1). This prevents bias from expectations, so any difference in results is due to the drug and not the placebo effect (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — trial data
In a trial, 35 of 250 patients on a new drug had side effects, compared with 12 of 150 patients on a placebo. Calculate the percentage with side effects in each group and comment. (3)

Drug: $\dfrac{35}{250} \times 100 = 14\%$ (1). Placebo: $\dfrac{12}{150} \times 100 = 8\%$ (1). Side effects were more common with the drug, so the benefits must be weighed against this risk / percentages are used because the groups are different sizes (1).
:::
