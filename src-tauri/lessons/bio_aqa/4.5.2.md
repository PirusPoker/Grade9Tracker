## What it is

This topic covers the whole of AQA 4.5.2 **The human nervous system**. The first part (structure, reflexes and the reaction-time practical) is for everyone, including Combined Science: Trilogy. **The brain, the eye and control of body temperature are Biology 8461 only** — they are under "Separate science only:" headings below.

### Why we need a nervous system

The nervous system lets humans **react to their surroundings** and **coordinate their behaviour**. It is one of the body's two control systems (the other is the hormonal, endocrine system in 4.5.3). Nervous responses are **fast, short-lived and act on a precise place**.

Information from **receptors** passes along cells called **neurones** as **electrical impulses** to the **central nervous system (CNS)**. The CNS is the **brain and spinal cord**. The CNS coordinates the response of **effectors**, which are either **muscles contracting** or **glands secreting hormones**.

The general pathway AQA wants you to know:

**stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response**

| Stage | What it is | Example (touching a hot pan) |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus | A change in the environment (inside or outside the body) | Heat from the pan |
| Receptor | Cells that detect the stimulus and turn it into impulses | Temperature/pain receptors in the skin |
| Coordinator | The CNS (brain or spinal cord) — receives and processes information | Spinal cord |
| Effector | Muscle or gland that brings about the response | Biceps muscle |
| Response | The action that results | Hand pulled away |

Receptors are often grouped in **sense organs**: light receptors in the eye, sound and balance receptors in the ear, chemical receptors on the tongue and in the nose, and touch, pressure, pain and temperature receptors in the skin.

### Neurones and how their structure suits their function

| Neurone | Carries impulses from… to… | Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensory neurone** | receptor → CNS | Long axon/fibre carries impulses over a long distance |
| **Relay neurone** | sensory neurone → motor neurone, inside the CNS | Short, with many connections to other neurones |
| **Motor neurone** | CNS → effector | Long axon; nerve endings at the muscle or gland |

All neurones are long and thin with branched endings (dendrites) so they can connect with many other cells. Many are wrapped in a fatty **myelin sheath** that insulates them and speeds up impulses.

### Synapses

A **synapse** is the **junction (tiny gap) between two neurones**. The electrical impulse cannot jump the gap, so:

1. The impulse arrives at the end of the first neurone.
2. **Chemicals** (neurotransmitters) are released and **diffuse across the gap**.
3. They bind to receptors on the next neurone, which **triggers a new electrical impulse** in it.

Synapses make sure impulses travel **in one direction only**, and they are where many drugs act (e.g. some painkillers block transmission). Because diffusion across the gap takes a little time, synapses are the slowest part of a nervous pathway.

### Reflex actions and the reflex arc

**Reflex actions are automatic and rapid; they do not involve the conscious part of the brain.** They protect the body from harm — for example pulling your hand off something hot, blinking when something approaches your eye, the pupil narrowing in bright light, coughing, or a baby's grasp reflex.

The pathway is called a **reflex arc**:

**stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → (synapse) → relay neurone in the spinal cord → (synapse) → motor neurone → effector → response**

| Structure | Function in the arc |
|---|---|
| Receptor | Detects the stimulus (e.g. pain receptor in the skin) |
| Sensory neurone | Carries the impulse from the receptor to the spinal cord |
| Synapse | Chemical passes the signal across the gap to the next neurone |
| Relay neurone | In the spinal cord; links sensory and motor neurones directly, so the conscious brain is bypassed |
| Motor neurone | Carries the impulse to the effector |
| Effector | Muscle contracts (or gland secretes) to produce the response |

**Why reflexes are important:** because the relay neurone links the sensory and motor neurones in the spinal cord, the impulse does not have to travel to the conscious brain and be "thought about". There are only a few synapses, so the response is **very fast**, which **reduces damage** to the body. (The brain does find out a moment later — that is when you feel the pain.)

### Required practical 7: the effect of a factor on human reaction time

(This is Required practical 7 in Biology 8461 and **Required practical 6 in Combined Science: Trilogy** — same practical.)

**Ruler-drop method:**

1. Person A holds a metre ruler vertically with the 0 cm mark level with the top of person B's thumb and finger, which are held apart (not touching the ruler).
2. A drops the ruler without warning; B catches it as fast as possible.
3. Record the distance (cm) at the top of B's thumb. A shorter distance = a faster reaction.
4. Repeat several times (e.g. 5) and calculate a **mean**, ignoring anomalous results.
5. Change the factor (e.g. after drinking a caffeinated drink, dominant vs non-dominant hand, before and after a distraction, after practice) and repeat.
6. Convert distance into time using a conversion table, or (Higher maths) $t = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}}$ with $d$ in metres and $g = 9.8$ m/s².

| Variable | Example |
|---|---|
| Independent | The factor changed: caffeine intake, hand used, distraction, amount of practice |
| Dependent | Reaction time (or distance the ruler falls before being caught) |
| Control | Same person catching; same hand (unless hand is the IV); same starting position of fingers; same ruler; same person dropping; same time of day; no warning given; same seated posture |

**Problems and improvements:** the catcher may **anticipate** the drop (vary the delay before dropping); people **improve with practice**, so give practice drops first or randomise the order; caffeine affects people differently and takes time to work (wait the same time after the drink). A **computer reaction-time test** (click when the screen changes colour) removes human judgement in reading the ruler and records time to the nearest millisecond — higher resolution.

**Risks:** low — a falling ruler can hurt toes; caffeine must not be given to anyone with a health condition, and doses should be small (ethics: participants must consent).

**Typical results:** reaction times are roughly 0.15–0.30 s. Caffeine is a stimulant and tends to **decrease** reaction time; alcohol and tiredness increase it; practice decreases it.

**Worked mini-example (MS 2c, 4a).** Five catches gave 14 cm, 16 cm, 15 cm, 29 cm and 13 cm. The 29 cm result is anomalous, so mean $= \frac{14 + 16 + 15 + 13}{4} = 14.5$ cm $= 0.145$ m. Reaction time $t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 0.145}{9.8}} = 0.17$ s (2 s.f.).

You must also be able to turn a table of reaction times into a **bar chart** (categoric independent variable, e.g. "with caffeine / without") or a **line graph** (continuous independent variable, e.g. hours without sleep), and read values back off a graph.

### Separate science only: the brain (Biology 8461)

The brain **controls complex behaviour**. It is made of **billions of interconnected neurones** and has different regions that carry out different functions. You must be able to identify these three on a diagram:

| Region | Position on a diagram | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Cerebral cortex** | The large, folded outer layer covering the top and front of the brain (the two cerebral hemispheres) | Consciousness, intelligence, memory, language, thinking and planning; conscious movement and the senses |
| **Cerebellum** | The small, wrinkled region at the back, underneath the cerebral hemispheres | Coordinates **muscular activity**, balance and posture |
| **Medulla** | The base of the brain, where it joins the spinal cord (the brain stem) | **Unconscious activities**: heart rate, breathing rate, peristalsis |

**How regions were mapped to functions (Higher tier).** Neuroscientists have used:
- **Studying patients with brain damage** — if one area is damaged (by a stroke or injury) and the person loses a particular ability, that area is linked to that function.
- **Electrically stimulating different parts of the brain** — a weak current to one area (during surgery, in conscious patients) may cause movement, a sensation or a memory.
- **MRI scanning** — shows which areas are most active (more blood flow) while a person carries out a task such as listening to music or recalling a memory. It is non-invasive.

**Difficulties of investigating and treating the brain (Higher tier).** The brain is **complex and delicate**:
- Many functions involve **several regions** working together, so it is hard to link a function to one place.
- Damaged neurones are **not easily repaired or replaced**.
- Treatments (surgery, drugs) cannot reach some areas easily — drugs may not cross from the blood into the brain, and surgery risks **damaging healthy tissue** next to the target, causing loss of speech, movement or memory, infection or bleeding.
- Ethical limits: you cannot experiment freely on living human brains, and patients may not be able to give consent.

**Evaluating brain procedures (WS 1.5):** weigh the **benefit** (e.g. removing a tumour, controlling epilepsy) against the **risk** (permanent damage to other functions, infection, death) and conclude whether the procedure is justified for that patient.

### Separate science only: the eye (Biology 8461)

The eye is a **sense organ containing receptors sensitive to light intensity and colour**.

| Structure | Function, linked to structure |
|---|---|
| **Retina** | Inner layer containing **light receptor cells** — rods (sensitive to dim light) and cones (sensitive to colour); detects the image |
| **Optic nerve** | Bundle of **sensory neurones** carrying impulses from the retina to the brain |
| **Sclera** | Tough, white outer layer that **protects** the eye and keeps its shape |
| **Cornea** | Transparent front part of the sclera; **refracts (bends) light** into the eye — it does most of the focusing |
| **Iris** | Coloured ring of muscle; **controls the size of the pupil** and so how much light enters |
| **Pupil** | The hole in the middle of the iris that lets light in |
| **Lens** | Transparent, flexible; **changes shape to finely focus** light onto the retina |
| **Ciliary muscles** | Ring of muscle around the lens; contract or relax to **change the lens shape** |
| **Suspensory ligaments** | Connect the ciliary muscles to the lens; when pulled tight they **pull the lens thin** |

**Adaptation to dim and bright light (the iris reflex).** The iris contains **circular** and **radial** muscles.
- **Bright light:** circular muscles **contract**, radial muscles **relax** → pupil **constricts** (gets smaller) → less light enters, protecting the retina from damage. This is a reflex.
- **Dim light:** radial muscles **contract**, circular muscles **relax** → pupil **dilates** (gets bigger) → more light reaches the retina so you can see.

**Accommodation** is the process of **changing the shape of the lens to focus on near or distant objects**.

| | Near object | Distant object |
|---|---|---|
| Ciliary muscles | **Contract** | **Relax** |
| Suspensory ligaments | **Loosen** (slacken) | **Pulled tight** |
| Lens | Becomes **thicker** (more rounded) | **Pulled thin** (flatter) |
| Refraction of light | Light rays refracted **strongly** | Light rays refracted **only slightly** |

Tip: the two always go in "opposite" pairs — muscles contract ↔ ligaments slacken.

**Defects of vision.** In both, rays of light **do not focus on the retina**.

| Defect | What the person sees clearly | Where the image forms | Cause | Corrected with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Myopia** (short-sightedness) | Near objects; distant objects blurred | **In front of** the retina | Lens too thick/curved, or eyeball too long | **Concave (diverging) lens** — spreads rays out slightly first |
| **Hyperopia** (long-sightedness) | Distant objects; near objects blurred | **Behind** the retina | Lens too thin/flat (or cannot thicken enough), or eyeball too short | **Convex (converging) lens** — bends rays inwards slightly first |

**Interpreting ray diagrams:** in a myopia diagram, the rays cross *before* reaching the retina; adding a concave lens (thinner in the middle, drawn as `)(`) makes rays diverge so the crossing point moves back onto the retina. In a hyperopia diagram, the rays would meet *behind* the retina; a convex lens (thicker in the middle, drawn as `()`) converges them so they meet on the retina.

**Treatments:** spectacle lenses (above); **hard and soft contact lenses** (sit on the cornea — soft ones are comfortable but carry a higher infection risk; hard ones last longer); **laser surgery** to change the shape of the cornea (permanent, but risk of complications, and not suitable for everyone); and a **replacement lens** in the eye (a clear plastic lens replacing the natural one, also used for cataracts — risks include infection or retinal damage).

### Separate science only: control of body temperature (Biology 8461)

Body temperature is kept near **37 °C** because that is the optimum for enzymes.
- It is monitored and controlled by the **thermoregulatory centre** in the **brain** (the hypothalamus).
- The thermoregulatory centre contains **receptors sensitive to the temperature of the blood**.
- The **skin** contains **temperature receptors** that send **nervous impulses** to the thermoregulatory centre.

| Too hot | Too cold |
|---|---|
| Blood vessels supplying skin capillaries **dilate** (**vasodilation**) | Blood vessels supplying skin capillaries **constrict** (**vasoconstriction**) |
| **Sweat** is produced by sweat glands | **Sweating stops** |
| — | **Skeletal muscles contract rapidly (shivering)** |

**How these work (Higher tier) — you must explain them in a given context:**
- **Vasodilation:** more blood flows through the capillaries near the skin surface, so **more energy is transferred** from the blood to the environment by radiation. (The vessels widen — they do not move closer to the skin.)
- **Sweating:** water in sweat **evaporates** from the skin; evaporation **transfers energy** from the skin to the environment, cooling it. (In humid air less sweat evaporates, so it cools less well.)
- **Vasoconstriction:** less blood flows near the skin surface, so **less energy is transferred** to the environment.
- **Shivering:** muscle contraction needs **respiration**, which **releases energy**, some of it warming the blood.
- Sweating stopping means less evaporation, so less energy is lost.

These are effectors controlled by nervous impulses from the thermoregulatory centre — an example of homeostasis by negative feedback (a change is detected and reversed).

::: terms
- **Receptor**: a cell that detects a stimulus (a change in the environment) and starts an electrical impulse.
- **Effector**: a muscle or gland that brings about a response; muscles contract and glands secrete hormones.
- **Central nervous system (CNS)**: the brain and spinal cord, which coordinate the response to a stimulus.
- **Sensory neurone**: a neurone that carries impulses from a receptor to the central nervous system.
- **Relay neurone**: a neurone inside the CNS that passes impulses from a sensory neurone to a motor neurone.
- **Motor neurone**: a neurone that carries impulses from the central nervous system to an effector.
- **Synapse**: the gap between two neurones, crossed by a chemical that diffuses across and starts a new impulse.
- **Reflex action**: an automatic and rapid response to a stimulus that does not involve the conscious part of the brain.
- **Accommodation**: changing the shape of the lens to focus on near or distant objects (biology only).
- **Myopia**: short-sightedness, where light focuses in front of the retina; corrected with a concave lens.
- **Hyperopia**: long-sightedness, where light would focus behind the retina; corrected with a convex lens.
- **Thermoregulatory centre**: the part of the brain that monitors blood temperature and controls body temperature.
- **Vasodilation**: widening of the blood vessels supplying skin capillaries so more energy is transferred to the surroundings.
:::

**Command words on this topic**
- **Describe** the pathway of a reflex — list the structures **in order** (receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector) and say what each does. Name the synapses.
- **Explain** why a reflex is fast or important — link: *relay neurone in the spinal cord* → *bypasses the conscious brain* → *few synapses* → *fast* → *reduces harm*.

- **Explain** temperature control in a context (Higher tier) — name the change (vasodilation, sweating) **and** say how it changes energy transfer to the environment.

::: example Reflex arc
A person touches a hot plate and pulls their hand away. Describe the pathway of the nerve impulses in this reflex. (4)

Pain/temperature receptors in the skin detect the heat (1). An impulse travels along a sensory neurone to the spinal cord (1). It crosses a synapse to a relay neurone and then another synapse to a motor neurone (1). The motor neurone carries the impulse to the effector — a muscle in the arm — which contracts to pull the hand away (1).
:::

::: example Reaction-time calculation
In a ruler-drop test a student's catches were 18 cm, 16 cm, 17 cm, 31 cm and 19 cm. Calculate the mean distance, giving a reason for any value you leave out. (3)

31 cm is anomalous — much larger than the others, so it is left out (1). Mean $= \frac{18 + 16 + 17 + 19}{4}$ (1) $= 17.5$ cm (1).
:::

::: watch
- Reflexes go through the **spinal cord** (or an unconscious part of the brain) — say they **do not involve the conscious part of the brain**, not "they do not involve the brain".
- Impulses are **electrical** along neurones but **chemical** across synapses. The chemical **diffuses** — do not say the impulse "jumps" the gap.
- Order matters: **sensory → relay → motor**. Reversing any two loses the mark.
- In the ruler-drop practical, a **shorter distance means a faster reaction**. Always exclude anomalies before taking a mean, and give **specific** control variables ("same hand", not "keep it fair").
- Accommodation: near = muscles **contract**, ligaments **slacken**, lens **thick**. Students who write "ligaments contract" lose the mark — ligaments are not muscles.
- Vasodilation: blood vessels **widen**, they do **not move** towards the skin. Say "more energy transferred to the environment" — never "heat is lost" alone, and never "capillaries dilate" (it is the vessels supplying them).
- Myopia → **concave**; hyperopia → **convex**. Say where the image forms relative to the **retina**.
:::

---

- the thermoregulatory centre in the brain contains receptors that detect the rise in blood temperature
