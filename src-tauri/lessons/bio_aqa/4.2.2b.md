## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.2.2.2 (the heart and blood vessels, plus the lungs) and 4.2.2.3 (blood). All of it is in both GCSE Biology and Combined Science: Trilogy, and none of it is Higher-tier only.

### The double circulatory system

The **heart is an organ that pumps blood around the body in a double circulatory system**: blood passes through the heart **twice** for every complete circuit of the body.

- The **right ventricle** pumps deoxygenated blood to the **lungs**, where gas exchange takes place (the pulmonary circuit).
- The **left ventricle** pumps oxygenated blood around the **rest of the body** (the systemic circuit).

Why two circuits? Blood loses pressure as it squeezes through the lung capillaries. Returning it to the heart lets the left ventricle re-pressurise it, so oxygenated blood reaches the body tissues quickly and at high pressure. The left ventricle wall is **thicker and more muscular** than the right, because it must pump blood all round the body rather than only to the nearby lungs.

### Structure of the heart

The heart is made mainly of **cardiac muscle**. It has four chambers: two thin-walled **atria** at the top that receive blood, and two thick-walled **ventricles** below that pump it out. A wall of muscle (the septum) keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart. **Valves** stop blood flowing backwards (you do not need their names).

The five blood vessels AQA expects you to know:

| Vessel | Carries | From → to |
|---|---|---|
| **Vena cava** | deoxygenated blood | body → right atrium |
| **Pulmonary artery** | deoxygenated blood | right ventricle → lungs |
| **Pulmonary vein** | oxygenated blood | lungs → left atrium |
| **Aorta** | oxygenated blood | left ventricle → body |
| **Coronary arteries** | oxygenated blood | branch off the aorta → the heart muscle itself |

Route of one circuit: vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body. A memory hook: **a**rteries carry blood **a**way from the heart; **v**eins carry blood back **in**to it. The pulmonary artery is the one artery with deoxygenated blood, and the pulmonary vein the one vein with oxygenated blood.

The heart muscle needs its own oxygen supply for respiration, which comes through the **coronary arteries** on its surface, not from the blood inside the chambers.

### Heart rate and pacemakers

The **natural resting heart rate is controlled by a group of cells located in the right atrium that act as a pacemaker**. They send out electrical impulses that make the heart muscle contract regularly.

**Artificial pacemakers are electrical devices used to correct irregularities in the heart rate.** A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin with a wire to the heart, and sends electrical impulses to keep the rhythm regular.

### The lungs: gas exchange

AQA limits you to the **trachea, bronchi, alveoli and the capillary network surrounding the alveoli**.

Air travels down the **trachea** (windpipe), which splits into two **bronchi** (one to each lung), which divide into smaller tubes ending in millions of tiny air sacs called **alveoli**. Each alveolus is wrapped in a network of capillaries. Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood; carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli to be breathed out.

How the lungs are adapted for gaseous exchange:

- **Very large surface area** — millions of alveoli (around 70 m² in total in an adult).
- **Thin walls** — alveolus wall and capillary wall are each **one cell thick**, giving a **short diffusion distance**.
- **Rich blood supply** — the capillary network carries oxygenated blood away and brings more carbon dioxide, **maintaining a steep concentration gradient**.
- **Ventilation** — breathing brings fresh air in and removes stale air, keeping oxygen concentration high in the alveoli.
- Moist lining — gases dissolve before diffusing.

### Blood vessels: structure related to function

| | Arteries | Veins | Capillaries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | carry blood **away** from the heart at **high pressure** | carry blood **back to** the heart at **low pressure** | exchange substances with body cells |
| Wall | **thick**, with layers of **muscle and elastic fibres** | **thin**, little muscle or elastic tissue | **one cell thick** |
| Lumen | narrow | **wide** (large lumen) | very narrow — just wide enough for red blood cells in single file |
| Valves | none (except at the heart) | **valves** to prevent backflow | none |

Explanations you must be able to give:

- **Arteries**: thick muscular walls withstand the high pressure from the ventricles without bursting; **elastic fibres stretch** as each surge of blood arrives and **recoil** to keep it moving (this is your pulse).
- **Veins**: pressure is low, so walls can be thin; the **wide lumen** reduces resistance to flow; **valves** stop blood flowing backwards. Blood is pushed along when body muscles squeeze the veins.
- **Capillaries**: the **one-cell-thick wall** gives a short diffusion distance, so oxygen and glucose diffuse out to cells and carbon dioxide and urea diffuse in; huge numbers of them give a large surface area, and they are permeable.

### Rate calculations for blood flow (MS 1a, 1c)

Rate is a **compound measure** — an amount per unit time:

$$\text{rate of blood flow} = \frac{\text{volume of blood}}{\text{time}}$$

Worked mini-example: 2400 cm³ of blood passes through an artery in 4 minutes. Rate $= 2400 \div 4 = 600$ cm³/min, or $2400 \div 240 = 10$ cm³/s.

Heart rate from a pulse count: 18 beats in 15 seconds → $18 \times 4 = 72$ beats per minute.

Questions sometimes give the equation **cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate** (it is not one you have to recall for AQA Biology, but you must be able to use it). Example: stroke volume 70 cm³, heart rate 72 beats/min → cardiac output $= 70 \times 72 = 5040$ cm³/min $= 5.04$ dm³/min. Watch the units and convert cm³ to dm³ (÷ 1000) when asked.

Percentage change (MS 1c): resting heart rate 64 → 96 beats/min during exercise is an increase of $\frac{96 - 64}{64} \times 100 = 50\%$.

### Blood

**Blood is a tissue** consisting of **plasma**, in which the **red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets** are suspended.

| Component | Function | Adaptations / recognising it |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasma** | the pale yellow liquid that transports everything: red and white cells, platelets, dissolved **carbon dioxide** (to the lungs), **glucose** and amino acids from the small intestine, **urea** (liver → kidneys), **hormones**, antibodies, and heat | mostly water, so substances dissolve |
| **Red blood cells** | carry **oxygen** from the lungs to the body | **no nucleus** → more room for **haemoglobin**, which binds oxygen (forming oxyhaemoglobin); **biconcave disc** shape → large surface area for oxygen to diffuse in and out; small and flexible → squeeze through capillaries |
| **White blood cells** | defend the body against **pathogens** (part of the immune system) | **have a nucleus** — the easiest way to spot them; larger than red cells and far fewer. Some engulf pathogens (phagocytosis); others make antibodies and antitoxins |
| **Platelets** | help the blood **clot** at a wound — this stops blood loss and keeps pathogens out | **small fragments of cells** with **no nucleus** |

Recognising cells in a photograph or diagram: red blood cells are the many small discs with paler centres and no nucleus; white blood cells are larger, fewer, and have a large stained (often lobed) nucleus; platelets are tiny specks.

**Practical skill (AT 7)**: observing and drawing blood cells on a prepared, stained slide under a light microscope. Start on the lowest power objective, focus with the coarse then fine focus, then move to high power. Draw with a sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading, and label with ruled lines. Add a magnification or scale. Use **prepared slides**, not fresh human blood, to avoid the risk of blood-borne infection.

**Evaluating risks of blood products (WS 1.5)**: blood transfusions and products such as plasma or clotting factors save lives (after accidents, surgery, for people with haemophilia) but carry risks: blood must be **matched** to the recipient's blood group or the immune system attacks it, and donated blood could carry pathogens such as HIV or hepatitis, so it is screened and tested. Today the benefits of tested, matched blood far outweigh the very small risks.

::: terms
- **Double circulatory system**: a system in which blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit of the body — once to the lungs, once to the rest of the body.
- **Ventricle**: one of the two lower, thick-walled chambers of the heart that pump blood out into the arteries.
- **Atrium**: one of the two upper, thin-walled chambers of the heart that receive blood from the veins.
- **Aorta**: the main artery, carrying oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the body.
- **Vena cava**: the main vein, carrying deoxygenated blood from the body into the right atrium.
- **Pulmonary artery**: carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs.
- **Pulmonary vein**: carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium.
- **Coronary arteries**: arteries that branch from the aorta and supply the heart muscle itself with oxygenated blood.
- **Pacemaker**: a group of cells in the right atrium that controls the natural resting heart rate; an artificial pacemaker is an electrical device that corrects an irregular rate.
- **Alveoli**: tiny air sacs at the ends of the lung airways where gas exchange with the blood takes place.
- **Capillary**: a tiny blood vessel with a wall one cell thick, where substances are exchanged with body cells.
- **Plasma**: the liquid part of blood, which carries cells, dissolved substances such as carbon dioxide, glucose, urea and hormones, and heat.
- **Haemoglobin**: the red protein in red blood cells that combines with oxygen to carry it round the body.
- **Platelets**: small fragments of cells with no nucleus that help blood to clot at a wound.
:::

::: method
1. **Command words.** *Describe* = say what happens or what something is like (e.g. the route of blood). *Explain* = give the reason, using "so" or "because" (structure → function). *Suggest* = apply what you know to an unfamiliar situation. *Compare* = state a similarity or difference for **both** things in the same sentence ("arteries have thick walls **but** veins have thin walls"). *Calculate* = show working and give a unit.
2. **Structure → function every time.** For a vessel, cell or the lungs, name the feature AND link it to its job: "one-cell-thick wall **so** a short diffusion distance"; "no nucleus **so** more room for haemoglobin **so** more oxygen carried". A feature on its own rarely scores.
3. **Heart route questions.** Name chambers and vessels in order and say which blood is oxygenated. Right side = deoxygenated, to the lungs; left side = oxygenated, to the body. Always say the left ventricle wall is thicker because it pumps blood **all around the body at higher pressure**.
4. **Gas exchange.** Four standard points: large surface area (many alveoli); thin walls / one cell thick → short diffusion distance; good blood supply → steep concentration gradient maintained; ventilation keeps gradient. Use the word **diffusion**.
5. **Calculations.** Write the equation (rate = volume ÷ time), substitute with units, give the answer with a unit (cm³/min, cm³/s, beats per minute) and the number of significant figures in the data. Check whether the answer wants seconds or minutes; convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000. For percentage change use (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
6. **4- and 6-mark answers** are marked by levels. Level 3 needs a full, logically ordered sequence with links (structure → function → consequence). A list of facts without links stays in Level 1–2. Plan two or three linked points per part of the question.
7. **Microscope/blood questions.** Identify cells by the nucleus (white = has one, red = none, platelet = fragment). For drawing: pencil, smooth lines, no shading, ruled label lines, scale or magnification. For risk: use prepared slides, not fresh blood.
:::

::: example Describe — route through the heart
Describe the path taken by blood from the vena cava until it leaves the heart in the aorta. (4)

Blood enters the right atrium from the vena cava (1). It passes into the right ventricle, which pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, where it is oxygenated (1). Oxygenated blood returns to the left atrium in the pulmonary vein (1). It passes into the left ventricle, which pumps it out through the aorta (1).
:::

::: example Explain — artery vs vein structure
Explain how the structures of arteries and veins are related to their functions. (4)

Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure, so they have thick walls with layers of muscle so they do not burst (1), and elastic fibres that stretch and recoil to keep blood flowing / even out the pressure (1). Veins carry blood back to the heart at low pressure, so they have thinner walls and a wide lumen to reduce resistance to flow (1), and valves to stop blood flowing backwards (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — rate of blood flow
In 2.5 minutes, 13 000 cm³ of blood flows through the aorta of a person at rest. Calculate the mean rate of blood flow in cm³ per second. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. (3)

Time $= 2.5 \times 60 = 150$ s (1). Rate $= \dfrac{13\,000}{150} = 86.67$ (1) $= 87$ cm³/s to 2 s.f. (1).
:::

::: example Explain — red blood cell adaptations
Explain how a red blood cell is adapted to carry oxygen. (3)

It contains haemoglobin, which combines with oxygen (1). It has no nucleus, so there is more room for haemoglobin and more oxygen can be carried (1). It is a biconcave disc, giving a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse in and out quickly (1).
:::

::: example Explain — alveoli (6-mark style)
Explain how the structure of the lungs is adapted for efficient gas exchange. (6)

Indicative content: the trachea divides into bronchi, then smaller tubes ending in millions of alveoli; this gives a very large surface area, so more oxygen and carbon dioxide can diffuse at once; alveolus walls and capillary walls are each one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is short and diffusion is fast; each alveolus is surrounded by a capillary network that carries oxygenated blood away and brings deoxygenated blood with carbon dioxide, maintaining a steep concentration gradient; ventilation (breathing) replaces air in the alveoli, keeping oxygen concentration high and carbon dioxide low; the moist lining lets gases dissolve.

Level 3 (5–6): several features, each clearly linked to how it increases the rate of diffusion, in a logical order from airway to blood. Level 2 (3–4): some features with some explanation. Level 1 (1–2): features listed with little or no explanation.
:::

::: q
What is meant by a double circulatory system? (2)
---
Blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit of the body (1): the right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs and the left ventricle pumps blood around the rest of the body (1).
:::

::: q
Name the blood vessel that carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart. (1)
---
Pulmonary vein.
:::

::: q
Explain why the wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. (2)
---
The left ventricle pumps blood all around the body, while the right only pumps to the nearby lungs (1), so the left needs more muscle to pump blood at higher pressure (1).
:::

::: q
Where is the natural pacemaker of the heart, and what does it do? (2)
---
A group of cells in the right atrium (1) that controls the natural resting heart rate (by sending electrical impulses that make the heart contract) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the heart muscle needs coronary arteries. (2)
---
Heart muscle cells respire continuously to release energy for contraction, so they need a constant supply of oxygen and glucose (1); the coronary arteries deliver oxygenated blood to the heart muscle itself (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how capillaries are adapted for exchanging substances with body cells. (2)
---
Their walls are one cell thick, giving a short diffusion distance (1); there are very many of them, giving a large surface area (and they pass close to every cell) (1).
:::

::: q
A student counts 21 pulse beats in 15 seconds. Calculate the heart rate in beats per minute. (1)
---
$21 \times 4 = 84$ beats per minute.
:::

::: q
How could you tell a white blood cell from a red blood cell in a photograph taken through a microscope? (2)
---
The white blood cell has a nucleus; the red blood cell does not (1). White blood cells are larger and there are far fewer of them (1).
:::

::: q
Name three substances transported in the plasma and say where each is carried to. (3)
---
Any three: carbon dioxide from body cells to the lungs; glucose / amino acids from the small intestine to body cells; urea from the liver to the kidneys; hormones from glands to target organs; antibodies; heat. (1 each.)
:::

::: q
Give one benefit and one risk of blood transfusions. (2)
---
Benefit: replaces blood lost in an accident or surgery, saving lives (1). Risk: blood of the wrong group can trigger an immune reaction, or donated blood could carry a pathogen such as HIV or hepatitis (so it must be screened) (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Arteries go away** from the heart, **veins go in**. The pulmonary artery carries **deoxygenated** blood and the pulmonary vein **oxygenated** — the classic trap.
- Do not say arteries have thick walls "to pump blood". Arteries do not pump; they **withstand high pressure**. Only the heart pumps.
- Valves in veins **prevent backflow** — "help blood flow" alone is too vague.
- Capillary and alveolus walls are **one cell thick**, not "one cell wall thick" or "thin cell walls" (animal cells have no cell walls).
- Red blood cells carry oxygen **using haemoglobin**; they do not carry carbon dioxide in AQA answers — CO₂ is carried in the **plasma**.
- In rate calculations, convert minutes to seconds (× 60) and always write a unit.
- The pacemaker is in the **right atrium**, not the ventricle or the left side.
:::

::: test
An athlete's stroke volume is 110 cm³ and their heart rate during exercise is 150 beats per minute. Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate. Calculate the cardiac output in dm³ per minute. (3)
---
$110 \times 150 = 16\,500$ cm³/min (1). Divide by 1000 to convert to dm³ (1): $16.5$ dm³/min (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why there is a steep concentration gradient of oxygen between the air in an alveolus and the blood arriving in the surrounding capillaries. Then explain how this gradient is maintained. (4)
---
Blood arriving at the lungs is deoxygenated because body cells used the oxygen in respiration, so its oxygen concentration is low (1); air in the alveolus has a high oxygen concentration (1). The gradient is maintained by the blood flow carrying oxygenated blood away and bringing more deoxygenated blood (1) and by ventilation, which replaces the air in the alveoli with fresh air (1).
:::

::: test
Blood flows through a vessel at a rate of 4.2 cm³/s. Calculate the volume of blood that flows through it in 3 minutes. (2)
---
Time $= 3 \times 60 = 180$ s (1). Volume = rate × time $= 4.2 \times 180 = 756$ cm³ (1).
:::

::: test
Suggest why people living at high altitude, where air contains less oxygen, often have more red blood cells per cm³ of blood than people at sea level. (2)
---
Each breath brings in less oxygen, so less oxygen diffuses into the blood (1); more red blood cells means more haemoglobin, so more oxygen can be picked up and carried to body cells for respiration (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the structures of the heart, the blood vessels and the blood work together to supply oxygen to a working muscle. (6)
---
Indicative content: oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the blood in the lung capillaries; it binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells, which have no nucleus and a biconcave shape for a large surface area; oxygenated blood returns in the pulmonary vein to the left atrium and left ventricle; the thick muscular left ventricle pumps blood at high pressure into the aorta; arteries have thick muscular, elastic walls to withstand and maintain the pressure; blood reaches capillaries in the muscle whose walls are one cell thick, so oxygen diffuses a short distance into the muscle cells for respiration; deoxygenated blood returns at low pressure in veins with valves to prevent backflow, through the vena cava to the right side, which pumps it to the lungs again (double circulation).

Level 3 (5–6): a coherent sequence from lungs to muscle covering heart, vessels and blood, with structures clearly linked to function. Level 2 (3–4): several correct structures with some linked functions, possibly missing one of heart, vessels or blood. Level 1 (1–2): isolated facts with little linking. 0: nothing worthy of credit.
:::
