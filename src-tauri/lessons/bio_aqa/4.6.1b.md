## What it is

This lesson covers spec points **4.6.1.6 Genetic inheritance, 4.6.1.7 Inherited disorders and 4.6.1.8 Sex determination** (numbered **4.6.1.4, 4.6.1.5 and 4.6.1.6** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464). **All of it is in both Biology and Trilogy.** Only *constructing* a Punnett square yourself and using it to make probability predictions is marked **Higher tier**; Foundation students complete squares that are partly drawn for them.

### The vocabulary you must be able to explain

AQA lists ten terms. Learn them as a chain from big to small, then the "how many / which ones" words.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Gamete** | A sex cell (sperm or egg in animals; pollen nucleus or ovule/egg cell in plants). Made by meiosis, so it carries **one** copy of each chromosome — **one allele** of each gene. |
| **Chromosome** | A long coiled molecule of **DNA** in the nucleus, carrying many genes. Human body cells have **23 pairs** (46). |
| **Gene** | A small section of DNA on a chromosome that **codes for a particular sequence of amino acids**, to make a specific protein. |
| **Allele** | A **different form (version) of the same gene**, e.g. the allele for brown fur and the allele for white fur. |
| **Dominant** | An allele that is **always expressed, even if only one copy is present**. Shown by a capital letter (B). |
| **Recessive** | An allele that is **only expressed if two copies are present** (no dominant allele). Shown by a lower-case letter (b). |
| **Homozygous** | The **two alleles are the same** (BB or bb). |
| **Heterozygous** | The **two alleles are different** (Bb). |
| **Genotype** | The **alleles an organism has** for a characteristic, e.g. Bb. |
| **Phenotype** | The **characteristic that is expressed** (what it looks like or how it works), e.g. brown fur. |

The genotype "operates at a molecular level": alleles code for proteins (enzymes, pigments, membrane channels) and those proteins produce the phenotype. That is the link between DNA and what you can see.

**Single-gene vs many-gene characteristics.** AQA's examples of characteristics controlled by **a single gene** are **fur colour in mice** and **red-green colour blindness in humans**. But **most characteristics are the result of multiple genes interacting** (height, skin colour, intelligence) — so simple crosses cannot predict them. Expect a mark for saying this when a question asks about the limits of genetic crosses.

### Genetic crosses and Punnett squares

Method for a monohybrid (single-gene) cross:

1. Choose a letter; capital = dominant allele, lower case = recessive allele. Write a **key**.
2. Write the **parents' phenotypes and genotypes**.
3. Write the **gametes** each parent can make (one allele each).
4. Draw the **Punnett square**: one parent's gametes across the top, the other's down the side, and fill in the offspring genotypes.
5. State the offspring **genotypes, phenotypes, and the ratio / probability / percentage**.

**Worked cross: two heterozygous brown mice.** B = brown (dominant), b = white (recessive). Parents Bb × Bb; gametes B, b and B, b.

| | **B** | **b** |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | BB (brown) | Bb (brown) |
| **b** | Bb (brown) | bb (white) |

- Genotypes: 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb.
- Phenotypes: **3 brown : 1 white**.
- Probability of a white offspring = $\frac{1}{4}$ = 0.25 = **25%**. Probability of brown = $\frac{3}{4}$ = 75%.

**Heterozygous × homozygous recessive** (Bb × bb) gives Bb, Bb, bb, bb — **1 brown : 1 white** (50% each). **Homozygous dominant × anything** gives 100% showing the dominant phenotype.

### Probability, ratio and proportion (MS 2e, 1c, 3a)

- Each fertilisation is an **independent event**. A 1 in 4 chance means *each* child has a 25% chance — if a couple have had three unaffected children, the fourth still has a 25% chance. It does **not** mean exactly one in every four children.
- Ratios predict proportions in **large numbers** of offspring. With 400 offspring from Bb × Bb you expect about 300 brown and 100 white, but small samples (e.g. a litter of 8) often deviate because fertilisation is random.
- Converting: a 3 : 1 ratio means $\frac{3}{4}$ (75%) and $\frac{1}{4}$ (25%) — divide each part by the **total number of parts**.
- Direct proportion: if 1 in 4 offspring are white, then 60 offspring would be expected to contain $60 \times \frac{1}{4} = 15$ white.

### Family trees (pedigrees)

Squares are males, circles are females, shaded symbols show the characteristic, a horizontal line joins parents and a vertical line leads to their children.

**How to spot a recessive allele:** two **unaffected** parents have an **affected** child. The parents must both be **heterozygous carriers** (Ff), and the child is ff.

**How to spot a dominant allele:** two **affected** parents have an **unaffected** child. Both parents must be heterozygous (Pp) and the unaffected child is pp. Also, with a dominant allele every affected person has at least one affected parent.

### Inherited disorders

Some disorders are **caused by the inheritance of certain alleles**.

| Disorder | Allele | What it does | Who is affected |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Polydactyly** | **Dominant** (P) | Extra fingers or toes. | PP or Pp. One affected parent (Pp) × unaffected (pp) → **50%** chance per child. |
| **Cystic fibrosis (CF)** | **Recessive** (f) | A **disorder of cell membranes**: thick, sticky mucus builds up in the lungs and digestive system, causing breathing problems, infections and poor digestion. | Only ff. Ff people are **carriers** — unaffected but can pass the allele on. Two carriers (Ff × Ff) → **25%** chance per child of CF, 50% chance of a carrier. |

**Cross for two carriers of CF:**

| | **F** | **f** |
|---|---|---|
| **F** | FF (unaffected) | Ff (carrier) |
| **f** | Ff (carrier) | ff (cystic fibrosis) |

### Embryo screening and gene therapy — informed judgements (WS 1.3)

**Embryo screening** checks embryos for alleles that cause disorders. In **pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD)** embryos are made by IVF, one cell is removed from each at an early stage and its DNA is tested; only embryos without the disorder are implanted. A fetus can also be tested during pregnancy (cells from the amniotic fluid or placenta), when parents may then choose to end the pregnancy.

**Gene therapy** aims to treat a disorder by inserting a working allele into the affected person's cells (research is exploring this for CF; see genetic engineering in 4.6.2).

| Arguments **for** screening | Arguments **against** screening |
|---|---|
| Reduces suffering — children are not born with a serious disorder. | **Ethical**: destroys embryos, which some people believe have a right to life. |
| **Economic**: treating a lifelong disorder such as CF is very expensive for the health service; screening costs less overall. | **Economic**: screening and IVF are expensive themselves. |
| **Social**: parents can prepare, or make an informed decision. | **Social/ethical**: fear of "designer babies" — selecting for non-medical traits such as sex or eye colour. |
| Laws (in the UK) only allow screening for serious disorders. | Implies people living with the disorder are less valued; tests are not 100% accurate; small risk of miscarriage with some prenatal tests. |

In a "make a judgement" question, give points on both sides, label them economic / social / ethical, and **finish with a justified conclusion**.

### Sex determination

- **Ordinary human body cells contain 23 pairs of chromosomes.**
- **22 pairs** control characteristics only; **one pair — the sex chromosomes — carries the genes that determine sex.**
- **Females: XX** (the two sex chromosomes are the same). **Males: XY** (different).
- All eggs carry **X**; sperm carry **X or Y** in equal numbers — so the father's sperm decides the sex.

| | **X** (from mother) | **X** (from mother) |
|---|---|---|
| **X** (from father) | XX (girl) | XX (girl) |
| **Y** (from father) | XY (boy) | XY (boy) |

Ratio **1 : 1**; probability of a boy at each birth $= \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$ = **50%**, whatever happened in previous births.

::: terms
- **Gamete**: a sex cell (sperm or egg) containing one allele of each gene, made by meiosis.
- **Allele**: a different form of the same gene, such as the alleles for brown and white fur.
- **Dominant allele**: an allele that is always expressed, even if only one copy is present.
- **Recessive allele**: an allele that is only expressed when two copies are present and no dominant allele.
- **Homozygous**: having two identical alleles of a gene, such as BB or bb.
- **Heterozygous**: having two different alleles of a gene, such as Bb.
- **Genotype**: the combination of alleles an organism has for a characteristic.
- **Phenotype**: the characteristic that is expressed as a result of the genotype, such as brown fur.
- **Carrier**: a heterozygous person with one recessive disorder allele, who is unaffected but can pass it on.
- **Embryo screening**: testing the DNA of embryos (or a fetus) for alleles that cause inherited disorders.
:::

::: method
**Command words on this topic**
- **Explain the term…** — give a precise definition: "alleles are *different forms of the same gene*", not "a gene". "Heterozygous = two *different alleles*", not "two different genes".
- **Complete / construct a Punnett square (Higher tier: construct)** — always write a **key**, the **parents' genotypes**, the **gametes** (circled, one allele each), the square, then **offspring genotypes and phenotypes**. Finish with the number the question asks for: ratio (3 : 1), probability (0.25 / $\frac{1}{4}$), or percentage (25%).
- **Calculate the expected number** — expected number = probability × total offspring. Use **direct proportion**: $\frac{1}{4}$ of 120 = 30.
- **Use the family tree to explain…** — name the two people you are using. "Persons 3 and 4 are both unaffected but their child 7 has the disorder, so the allele must be recessive and both 3 and 4 must be heterozygous."
- **Evaluate / make an informed judgement about embryo screening** — balanced points labelled economic, social and ethical, then a conclusion that follows from your points.

**Calculations.** Genetics maths is ratio and probability. Show the square, then convert: probability = favourable boxes ÷ 4. For an expected number: probability × total. Keep answers as fractions, decimals or percentages exactly as the question asks.

**6-mark answers.** A typical 6-marker: "Two parents without cystic fibrosis have a child with cystic fibrosis. Explain how this happened and evaluate whether they should use embryo screening for their next child." Level 3 needs a correct genetic explanation (recessive allele, both parents carriers, a full cross giving 25%) **and** a balanced evaluation (at least one economic, social or ethical point each way) with a conclusion. Listing points without linking them to this family stays in Level 2.

**Data questions.** You may be given real offspring counts (e.g. 289 brown, 103 white). Divide both by the smaller number to get a ratio (2.8 : 1, close to 3 : 1) and explain that random fertilisation means observed ratios are rarely exact.
:::

::: example Cross for mouse fur (Higher tier)
In mice, the allele for brown fur (B) is dominant to the allele for white fur (b). A heterozygous mouse is crossed with a white mouse. Construct a Punnett square and give the probability that an offspring has white fur. (4)

Parent genotypes Bb × bb (1). Gametes B and b; b and b (1).

| | **b** | **b** |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | Bb | Bb |
| **b** | bb | bb |

Offspring: 2 Bb (brown) and 2 bb (white) (1). Probability of white = $\frac{2}{4}$ = 0.5 / 50% (1).
:::

::: example Expected numbers
Two heterozygous pea plants (Tt, where T = tall is dominant) are crossed and produce 240 seeds. How many seeds would be expected to grow into short plants? (2)

Tt × Tt gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, so the probability of tt (short) = $\frac{1}{4}$ (1). Expected number = $\frac{1}{4} \times 240 = 60$ (1).
:::

::: example Family tree reasoning
In a family tree, a man and woman who both have polydactyly have a daughter with the normal number of fingers. Explain what this shows about the allele for polydactyly and give the parents' genotypes. (3)

The daughter does not have the condition although both parents do, so she must have inherited a different allele from each parent that is hidden in them (1). So polydactyly is caused by a **dominant** allele (1). Both parents must be heterozygous, Pp, and the daughter is pp (1).
:::

::: example Sex determination
Explain why there is always a 50% chance that a baby will be a boy. (3)

The mother is XX so all her eggs contain an X chromosome (1). The father is XY so half his sperm contain X and half contain Y (1). The egg has an equal chance of being fertilised by an X or a Y sperm, so XX : XY = 1 : 1, a probability of 0.5 for a boy (1).
:::

::: example Cystic fibrosis carriers
A woman is a carrier of cystic fibrosis. Her partner does not have the allele. What is the probability that their child will (a) have CF, (b) be a carrier? (2)

Ff × FF gives FF, FF, Ff, Ff. (a) Probability of CF (ff) = 0 (1). (b) Probability of a carrier (Ff) = $\frac{2}{4}$ = 0.5 / 50% (1).
:::

::: q
What is the difference between a gene and an allele? (2)
---
A gene is a small section of DNA that codes for a particular sequence of amino acids / a specific protein (1). An allele is a different form (version) of that same gene (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between genotype and phenotype. (2)
---
Genotype is the alleles an organism has, e.g. Bb (1). Phenotype is the characteristic that is expressed, e.g. brown fur (1).
:::

::: q
How many pairs of chromosomes are in an ordinary human body cell, and how many of these pairs carry genes that determine sex? (2)
---
23 pairs (1); one pair (the sex chromosomes) (1).
:::

::: q
Is the genotype Tt homozygous or heterozygous? Explain your answer. (1)
---
Heterozygous, because the two alleles are different.
:::

::: q
Polydactyly is caused by a dominant allele. A man with genotype Pp has children with a woman with genotype pp. What percentage of their children would be expected to have polydactyly? (2)
---
Gametes P, p and p, p; offspring 2 Pp : 2 pp (1). 50% expected to have polydactyly (1).
:::

::: q
Two parents who do not have cystic fibrosis have a child with cystic fibrosis. Explain how. (3)
---
Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele (1). Both parents are heterozygous carriers (Ff) and are unaffected because they also have a dominant allele (1). The child inherited the recessive allele from each parent, so is ff and has the disorder (1).
:::

::: q
Give two characteristics that AQA uses as examples of characteristics controlled by a single gene. (2)
---
Fur colour in mice (1); red-green colour blindness in humans (1).
:::

::: q
Two heterozygous brown mice had 8 offspring: 7 brown and 1 white. A student expected 6 brown and 2 white. Explain why the results differ from the expected ratio. (2)
---
Fertilisation is random / each offspring is an independent event (1). The 3 : 1 ratio is a probability that only applies closely to large numbers of offspring; 8 is a small sample (1).
:::

::: q
Give one economic and one ethical argument against embryo screening. (2)
---
Economic: IVF and screening are expensive (1). Ethical: unused or affected embryos are destroyed, which some people believe is wrong / it could lead to selecting embryos for non-medical reasons ("designer babies") (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Allele vs gene**: heterozygous means two different **alleles**, not two different genes.
- **Dominant does not mean common**, and **recessive does not mean rare** — dominance is only about which allele is expressed.
- In crosses, write the **gametes** and a **key**; marks are often given for these, not just the final answer.
- A ratio is not a guarantee: "1 in 4" is a **probability for each child**, not "exactly one of four children".
- 3 : 1 means **3 out of 4** (75%), not 3 out of 3 or "33%". Divide by the total number of parts.
- **Females XX, males XY** — the **sperm** decides the sex. And most characteristics are controlled by **many genes**, not one.
:::

Draw a genetic diagram to show that there is an equal chance of a child being a boy or a girl. (3)

::: test
In guinea pigs, the allele for rough coat (R) is dominant to the allele for smooth coat (r). Two heterozygous guinea pigs were bred together several times and produced 36 offspring. Calculate the number of offspring expected to have a smooth coat. Show your working. (3)
---
Rr × Rr gives RR, Rr, Rr, rr (1). Probability of smooth (rr) = $\frac{1}{4}$ / 0.25 (1). Expected number = $0.25 \times 36 = 9$ (1).
:::

::: test
Most human characteristics, such as height, cannot be predicted using a Punnett square. Give two reasons why. (2)
---
Most characteristics are controlled by multiple genes interacting rather than a single gene (1). They are also affected by the environment (e.g. diet), not just alleles (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A woman whose father had cystic fibrosis does not have the disorder herself. Her partner is a carrier. Construct a Punnett square and calculate the probability that their first child will have cystic fibrosis. (4)
---
The woman must have inherited f from her father but is unaffected, so she is Ff (1). Partner Ff; gametes F, f and F, f (1). Square: FF, Ff, Ff, ff (1). Probability of CF = $\frac{1}{4}$ = 0.25 / 25% (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Two black mice (B = black, dominant; b = brown) are crossed. Among their many offspring, some are brown. One black offspring is chosen. What is the probability that this black mouse is heterozygous? Explain your answer. (3)
---
Brown offspring (bb) means both parents must be Bb, so the cross gives 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb (1). Only the black offspring are considered: BB and the two Bb, so 3 possibilities (1). Probability heterozygous = $\frac{2}{3}$ (≈ 0.67) (1).
:::

::: test
A couple both know they are carriers of cystic fibrosis. They are considering IVF with embryo screening (pre-implantation genetic diagnosis) for their next child. Explain the genetic risk to their children and evaluate the use of embryo screening for this couple. (6)
---
Indicative content — genetics:
- CF is caused by a recessive allele; both parents are carriers (Ff)
- Punnett square Ff × Ff gives FF : Ff : Ff : ff
- 25% / 1 in 4 chance for each child to have CF; 50% chance of a carrier; risk is the same for every pregnancy
- CF is a disorder of cell membranes causing thick, sticky mucus in lungs and digestive system

Indicative content — evaluation:
- for: prevents a child suffering from a serious, lifelong disorder
- for (economic): avoids lifelong treatment costs to the health service
- for (social): parents can make an informed choice
- against (ethical): embryos with CF are destroyed; some believe embryos have a right to life
- against (economic): IVF and screening are expensive; IVF may fail
- against (social/ethical): could lead to "designer babies"; may imply people with CF are less valued
- conclusion consistent with the points made

Level 3 (5–6 marks): a correct genetic explanation with the 25% probability, and a balanced evaluation with points on both sides leading to a justified conclusion.
Level 2 (3–4 marks): some correct genetics and some points for or against screening, but evaluation one-sided or no conclusion.
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple statements, e.g. "CF is inherited" or "screening is expensive".
0 marks: nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
In a family tree, persons 1 (male) and 2 (female) are unaffected by a condition; their son, person 5, is affected. Person 5 has children with an unaffected woman who is homozygous dominant. (a) Explain whether the condition is caused by a dominant or recessive allele. (b) What is the probability that a child of person 5 will be affected? Explain. (4)
---
(a) Recessive (1): persons 1 and 2 are unaffected but have an affected child, so both must carry a hidden recessive allele (are heterozygous) (1). (b) Person 5 is aa; the woman is AA, so every child receives A from the mother and a from the father, giving Aa (1). Probability of an affected child = 0 (all children are carriers) (1).
:::
