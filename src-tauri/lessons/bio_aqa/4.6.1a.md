## What it is

This lesson covers the first half of AQA 4.6.1 **Reproduction**: 4.6.1.1 sexual and asexual reproduction, 4.6.1.2 meiosis and 4.6.1.4 DNA and the genome (for everyone, including Combined Science: Trilogy, where DNA and the genome is numbered 4.6.1.3). **4.6.1.3 advantages and disadvantages of sexual and asexual reproduction and 4.6.1.5 DNA structure are Biology 8461 only**, and protein synthesis and mutations within 4.6.1.5 are also **Higher tier only**.

### Sexual and asexual reproduction

**Meiosis leads to non-identical cells being formed, while mitosis leads to identical cells being formed.**

| | Sexual reproduction | Asexual reproduction |
|---|---|---|
| Parents | Two (male and female) | **Only one** |
| Gametes | **Fusion (joining) of male and female gametes**: sperm and egg cells in animals; **pollen and egg cells** in flowering plants | **No fusion of gametes** |
| Genetic information | **Mixing** of genetic information from two parents | **No mixing** |
| Offspring | **Varied** — genetically different from each other and from parents | **Genetically identical** to the parent and each other — **clones** |
| Cell division involved | **Meiosis** to form gametes (then mitosis for growth) | **Only mitosis** |

**Gametes** are sex cells. In animals the male gamete is the **sperm** and the female gamete is the **egg**. In flowering plants the male gamete is in the **pollen** and the female gamete is the **egg cell** in the ovule.

### Meiosis

**Cells in reproductive organs** (testes and ovaries in animals; anthers and ovaries in flowering plants) **divide by meiosis to form gametes**. Meiosis **halves the number of chromosomes** so that **fertilisation can restore the full number**.

When a cell divides by meiosis to form gametes:
1. **Copies of the genetic information are made** (each chromosome is copied).
2. The cell **divides twice** to form **four gametes**, each with a **single set of chromosomes**.
3. **All gametes are genetically different** from each other (chromosomes from each pair are shuffled into different combinations).

You do **not** need to know the stages of meiosis.

**Fertilisation.** Gametes join at fertilisation to **restore the normal number of chromosomes**. The fertilised egg (zygote) then **divides by mitosis**, so the **number of cells increases**. As the **embryo develops, cells differentiate** into specialised cells.

Human numbers:

| Cell | Number of chromosomes | Sets |
|---|---|---|
| Body cell (e.g. skin) | **46** (23 pairs) | Two sets |
| Gamete (sperm or egg) | **23** | **One set** |
| Fertilised egg | 23 + 23 = **46** | Two sets restored |

**Why halve the number?** If gametes had 46 chromosomes, the fertilised egg would have 92, and the number would double every generation.

**Comparing mitosis and meiosis:**

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Where | All body cells (growth, repair, asexual reproduction) | Reproductive organs only |
| Number of divisions | One | **Two** |
| Number of cells produced | Two | **Four** |
| Chromosome number | Same as parent (46 in humans) | **Halved** (23 in humans) |
| Genetic identity | **Identical** to parent and each other | **All different** |

**Modelling meiosis (WS 1.2).** You can model meiosis with pipe cleaners or paper chromosomes: two pairs of different colours, copied, then split into four groups, each with one of each pair. Different arrangements show why every gamete is different.

**Worked mini-example.** A dog body cell has 78 chromosomes. A dog sperm cell has $\frac{78}{2} = 39$ chromosomes; a fertilised dog egg has $39 + 39 = 78$.

### Separate science only: advantages and disadvantages of sexual and asexual reproduction (Biology 8461)

**Advantages of sexual reproduction:**
- It **produces variation in the offspring**.
- **If the environment changes**, variation gives a **survival advantage by natural selection** — some individuals are likely to have characteristics that let them survive (e.g. resistance to a new disease), so the species survives.
- Natural selection can be **speeded up by humans in selective breeding** to increase food production.

**Disadvantages of sexual reproduction** (the flip side of asexual's advantages): needs two parents, so time and energy are spent finding a mate; slower; produces fewer offspring; good combinations of genes can be lost.

**Advantages of asexual reproduction:**
- **Only one parent** is needed.
- **More time and energy efficient** as there is **no need to find a mate**.
- **Faster** than sexual reproduction.
- **Many identical offspring** can be produced **when conditions are favourable**.

**Disadvantages of asexual reproduction:** no variation, so if conditions change (a new disease, climate change) the whole population may be equally affected and could die out.

**Organisms that reproduce by both methods** (the only examples you need):

| Organism | Asexual | Sexual |
|---|---|---|
| **Malarial parasites** (the protist *Plasmodium*) | Reproduce asexually **in the human host** (in the liver and red blood cells) — rapidly builds up huge numbers | Reproduce sexually **in the mosquito** — gives variation |
| **Many fungi** | Reproduce asexually by **spores** | Also reproduce sexually **to give variation** |
| **Many plants** | Asexually by **runners** (e.g. **strawberry plants**) or **bulb division** (e.g. **daffodils**) | Produce **seeds** sexually (by pollination and fertilisation) |

These organisms use asexual reproduction to **multiply fast when conditions are good** and sexual reproduction to **produce variation**, which helps the species survive changes (for example, a fungus may reproduce sexually when conditions become harsh). Understanding the malaria life cycle has helped scientists prevent the disease (WS 1.1), e.g. by targeting mosquitoes.

You must be able to explain the advantages and disadvantages for **any organism** if you are given information about it — apply the same principles.

### DNA and the genome

- The genetic material in the **nucleus** of a cell is a chemical called **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid).
- **DNA is a polymer made up of two strands forming a double helix.**
- DNA is contained in structures called **chromosomes**.
- A **gene** is a **small section of DNA on a chromosome**. **Each gene codes for a particular sequence of amino acids, to make a specific protein.**
- The **genome** of an organism is **the entire genetic material of that organism**.

Size order, biggest to smallest: **nucleus → chromosome → gene → DNA bases**.

**The human genome** has now been studied (the Human Genome Project, completed in 2003, worked out the order of bases in all human DNA). Understanding the human genome is important for:

| Use | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Searching for genes linked to different types of disease** | Finding which genes (or variants) increase the risk of e.g. some cancers, heart disease or Alzheimer's, so people at risk can be screened, advised on lifestyle, or treated early |
| **Understanding and treatment of inherited disorders** | Knowing which gene causes a disorder such as cystic fibrosis helps diagnosis (genetic testing) and the development of new treatments, including gene therapy and targeted drugs |
| **Tracing human migration patterns from the past** | Comparing DNA from different populations shows how closely related they are and when groups split, revealing how early humans spread out of Africa across the world |

**Issues (WS 1.4):** genetic testing could lead to discrimination by insurers or employers, people may suffer anxiety about a risk that may never happen, and a gene linked to a disease does not guarantee the disease will develop.

### Separate science only: DNA structure (Biology 8461)

**DNA is a polymer made from four different nucleotides.** The DNA polymer is made up of **repeating nucleotide units**.

Each **nucleotide** consists of:
- a **common sugar**,
- a **phosphate group**, and
- **one of four different bases** attached to the sugar.

DNA contains **four bases: A, C, G and T** (adenine, cytosine, guanine, thymine — you only need the letters).

The **long strands** of DNA consist of **alternating sugar and phosphate sections** (the "backbone"). **Attached to each sugar is one of the four bases.** The two strands are joined by the bases in the middle and twisted into a double helix.

You must be able to **interpret a diagram** of DNA structure (identify sugar, phosphate, base and a nucleotide), but you will not be asked to draw it. In a typical diagram, a nucleotide is one sugar (pentagon) + one phosphate (circle) + one base (rectangle).

**The genetic code.** **A sequence of three bases is the code for a particular amino acid.** The **order of bases controls the order in which amino acids are assembled** to produce a particular protein. So a gene 900 bases long codes for $\frac{900}{3} = 300$ amino acids.

### Complementary base pairing (Higher tier)

In the two **complementary strands**, a **C is always linked to a G** on the opposite strand, and a **T to an A**.

So if one strand reads **A T G C C A**, the other strand reads **T A C G G T**.

Because they pair, in any double-stranded DNA the amount of A = amount of T, and the amount of C = amount of G.

**Worked mini-example (Higher tier).** In a DNA sample 30% of the bases are A. Then T = 30%; C + G = 100 − 60 = 40%, so C = 20% and G = 20%.

### Protein synthesis (Higher tier)

A simple description:
1. The gene's **DNA** in the nucleus acts as the code. A **template** copy of the gene is made and moves **out of the nucleus** to the cytoplasm.
2. **Proteins are synthesised on ribosomes, according to the template.**
3. **Carrier molecules bring specific amino acids** to add to the **growing protein chain in the correct order** — the order set by the sequence of bases (three bases per amino acid).
4. When the protein chain is complete, it **folds up to form a unique shape**.
5. **This unique shape enables proteins to do their job** as **enzymes**, **hormones** or **forming structures in the body such as collagen**.

AQA does **not** expect you to know or understand the structure of mRNA or tRNA, or the detailed structure of amino acids or proteins — "template" and "carrier molecules" are the words to use.

**How the structure of DNA affects the protein made:** the order of bases → the order of amino acids → how the chain folds → the shape of the protein → its function (e.g. the shape of an enzyme's active site).

### Mutations and genetic variants (Higher tier)

A **mutation** is a change in the DNA base sequence. **Mutations occur continuously.**
- **Most do not alter the protein**, or alter it only slightly, so its appearance or function is not changed (for example, the new triplet still codes for the same amino acid).
- **A few mutations code for an altered protein with a different shape.** For example, an **enzyme may no longer fit the substrate binding site** (active site), or a **structural protein may lose its strength** (e.g. collagen).

Types you can model (WS 1.2): a base **substituted** (swapped) for another changes one triplet — at most one amino acid changes; a base **inserted** or **deleted** shifts every triplet after it, so many amino acids change — usually a much bigger effect.

Example: original `TAC GGA TTC` (three amino acids). Delete the first G → `TAC GAT TC…` — every triplet after the deletion is read differently.

**How genetic variants influence phenotype:**
- **(a) In coding DNA** — a variant can **alter the activity of a protein**: a different amino acid sequence → different shape → the protein may work less well, not at all, or occasionally better.
- **(b) In non-coding DNA** — **not all parts of DNA code for proteins**. **Non-coding parts can switch genes on and off**, so variations in these areas **may affect how genes are expressed** — whether a protein is made, or how much of it — even though the protein itself is normal.

::: terms
- **Gamete**: a sex cell with a single set of chromosomes; sperm and egg in animals, pollen and egg cell in flowering plants.
- **Meiosis**: cell division in reproductive organs that halves the chromosome number, forming four genetically different gametes.
- **Fertilisation**: the fusion of a male and female gamete, restoring the normal number of chromosomes.
- **Clone**: an organism genetically identical to its parent, produced by asexual reproduction.
- **DNA**: the genetic material, a polymer made of two strands forming a double helix.
- **Gene**: a small section of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a particular sequence of amino acids to make a specific protein.
- **Genome**: the entire genetic material of an organism.
- **Nucleotide**: the repeating unit of DNA, made of a sugar, a phosphate group and one of four bases (biology only).
- **Complementary base pairing**: in DNA, A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G (Higher tier, biology only).
- **Mutation**: a change in the base sequence of DNA, which may change the protein made (Higher tier, biology only).
:::

::: example Meiosis and fertilisation
Explain why gametes must be produced by meiosis rather than mitosis. (3)

Meiosis halves the number of chromosomes, so gametes have a single set — 23 in humans (1). At fertilisation two gametes fuse, restoring the full number — 46 (1). If gametes were made by mitosis they would have 46, and the chromosome number would double every generation (1).
:::

::: example Asexual and sexual reproduction (separate science only)
Strawberry plants reproduce by runners and by seeds. Explain the advantage of each method to the plant. (4)

Runners are asexual: only one parent is needed and it is faster (1), so many identical plants can be produced quickly when conditions are favourable (1). Seeds are produced sexually, giving variation in the offspring (1); if the environment changes (e.g. a new disease), some offspring may survive by natural selection (1).
:::

::: example Genetic code (separate science only)
A gene is 1260 bases long. Calculate the number of amino acids in the protein it codes for. (2)

Three bases code for one amino acid (1), so $\frac{1260}{3} = 420$ amino acids (1).
:::

::: example Base pairing (Higher tier, separate science only)
In a DNA molecule 18% of the bases are guanine (G). Calculate the percentage of thymine (T). (2)

C = G = 18%, so C + G = 36% (1). A + T = 64%, so T $= \frac{64}{2} = 32\%$ (1).
:::

::: example Mutation (Higher tier, separate science only)
Explain how a mutation in the gene for an enzyme could stop the enzyme from working. (4)

The mutation changes the sequence of bases in the gene (1). This changes the sequence of amino acids in the protein (1). The protein folds into a different shape (1), so the active site no longer fits the substrate, and the enzyme cannot catalyse the reaction (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Meiosis** = gametes, **four** different cells, **half** the chromosomes. **Mitosis** = growth/asexual, **two** identical cells. Mixing them up costs most marks on this topic.
- Gametes have a **single set** (23 in humans) — not "23 pairs". Body cells have 23 **pairs** (46).
- Sexual reproduction in plants: male gamete = **pollen**, not "seed".
- A gene codes for a sequence of **amino acids** to make a **protein** — not "codes for a characteristic" alone.
- Each nucleotide = **sugar + phosphate + one base**. The backbone is **alternating sugar and phosphate**; the bases are attached to the **sugar**.
- **Three bases** code for **one amino acid**. Base pairs: **A–T**, **C–G**.
- Protein synthesis (Higher tier): proteins are made on **ribosomes**; **carrier molecules** bring amino acids. Do not write "DNA leaves the nucleus".
- Most mutations **have no effect** — the examiners reward saying so before describing a harmful one.
:::

::: test
Separate science only (Higher tier): describe how a protein is synthesised in a cell and explain how a change in the DNA base sequence can produce a protein that no longer works. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- a gene is a sequence of bases in DNA; three bases code for one amino acid
- a template of the gene is made and leaves the nucleus
- proteins are synthesised on ribosomes according to the template
- carrier molecules bring specific amino acids in the correct order
- amino acids join to form a protein chain
- the chain folds into a unique shape that lets it work as an enzyme, hormone or structural protein
- a mutation changes the base sequence (substitution, insertion or deletion)
- insertion or deletion changes every triplet after it, so many amino acids change
- the amino acid sequence changes, so the protein folds into a different shape
- e.g. an enzyme's active site no longer fits the substrate, or a structural protein such as collagen loses strength
- most mutations do not change the protein, or change it only slightly.

Level 2 (3–4 marks): protein synthesis partly described and the effect of a mutation partly linked (e.g. different amino acids → doesn't work, without shape).
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple statements, e.g. "DNA codes for proteins" or "mutations change DNA".
0 marks: nothing worthy of credit.
