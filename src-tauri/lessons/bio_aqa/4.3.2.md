## What it is

**Separate science only: this whole subsection (4.3.2) is Biology 8461 only, and it is Higher tier only.** Combined Science: Trilogy students do not need it. Foundation-tier Biology students are not examined on it either.

You already know (from 4.3.1.6 and 4.3.1.7) that **white blood cells called lymphocytes** make **antibodies**, and that each antibody has a shape that fits one specific **antigen** — a protein on the surface of a pathogen or cell. Monoclonal antibodies take that natural specificity and turn it into a tool.

### What "monoclonal" means (Higher tier)

- **Mono** = one; **clonal** = from a clone. Monoclonal antibodies are produced from **a single clone of cells**, so every antibody molecule is identical.
- They are specific to **one binding site on one protein antigen**. That means they can be used to target **a specific chemical or specific cells** in the body — and nothing else.
- Compare: when you are infected, many different lymphocytes respond to different parts of the pathogen, so your blood contains a *mixture* of antibodies (polyclonal). A monoclonal antibody is one pure type.

### Producing monoclonal antibodies (Higher tier)

The problem: a lymphocyte makes the antibody you want but **does not divide** in culture. A tumour (cancer) cell **divides rapidly and indefinitely** but makes no useful antibody. The trick is to fuse the two.

| Step | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A **mouse** is injected with the chosen **antigen** (e.g. a protein from a pathogen, or the pregnancy hormone hCG). | This **stimulates the mouse lymphocytes** to make the particular antibody that binds that antigen. |
| 2 | Lymphocytes are collected from the mouse (from its spleen). | These are the cells making the antibody. |
| 3 | The lymphocytes are **combined (fused) with a particular kind of tumour cell** (a myeloma cell). | The fused cell is called a **hybridoma cell**. |
| 4 | The hybridoma **can both divide and make the antibody**. | It has the lymphocyte's ability to make the antibody and the tumour cell's ability to divide. |
| 5 | **Single hybridoma cells are cloned** — each is allowed to divide into many genetically identical cells. | All cells in a clone produce **the same antibody**. The clone making the right antibody is selected. |
| 6 | A **large amount of the antibody is collected and purified**. | Gives a pure supply of identical, specific antibody. |

A simple way to remember it: **mouse → antigen → lymphocyte + tumour cell → hybridoma → clone → collect and purify.**

### Uses of monoclonal antibodies (Higher tier)

AQA says you are **not expected to recall any specific tests or treatments**, but given information you must be able to **explain how they work**. Every use relies on the same idea: *the antibody binds only to its one specific antigen*, and something attached to it (a dye, a radioactive label, a drug) goes along for the ride.

**1. Diagnosis — for example pregnancy tests.** A pregnant woman's urine contains the hormone **hCG** (human chorionic gonadotrophin). A pregnancy test stick works like this:

| Part of stick | What is there | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Wick (where urine goes) | Urine soaks in and moves up the strip. | Carries any hCG along. |
| Reaction zone | **Mobile** monoclonal antibodies to hCG, attached to **coloured beads**. | If hCG is present it binds to these antibodies; the hCG–antibody–bead complex moves on up the strip. |
| Test line ("T") | Monoclonal antibodies to hCG **fixed (immobilised)** in place. | They bind the hCG that is already carrying a bead, so the beads collect and a **coloured line** appears = pregnant. No hCG means no beads stick here. |
| Control line ("C") | Fixed antibodies that bind **the mobile antibodies themselves**. | The beads always collect here, whether or not hCG is present — a line here shows **the test has worked**. |

Result: two lines = pregnant; control line only = not pregnant; no control line = the test has failed.

**2. In laboratories — to measure levels of hormones and other chemicals in blood, or to detect pathogens.** The antibody binds only to the target hormone, chemical or pathogen, so a sample can be tested very specifically (for example, checking hormone levels, or confirming which bacterium or virus is present).

**3. In research — to locate or identify specific molecules in a cell or tissue.** The antibody is bound to a **fluorescent dye**. It attaches only to the molecule it is specific to, so under a microscope (with UV light) the glowing dye shows exactly where that molecule is.

**4. To treat some diseases, for example cancer.** Cancer cells carry antigens that normal cells do not. A monoclonal antibody against that antigen can be bound to:
- a **radioactive substance**,
- a **toxic drug**, or
- a **chemical which stops cells growing and dividing**.

The antibody delivers the substance **to the cancer cells without harming other cells in the body**. Compare this with ordinary chemotherapy, which reaches all dividing cells and damages healthy ones (hair follicles, gut lining) too. Some monoclonal antibodies on their own also mark cancer cells so that the immune system destroys them.

### Advantages and disadvantages (WS 1.5) and ethics (WS 1.3)

| Advantages | Disadvantages / issues |
|---|---|
| Bind to **only one** specific antigen, so highly specific — few false results in tests. | **Create more side effects than expected** (for example fever, vomiting, low blood pressure, allergic-type reactions). |
| Cancer treatment targets cancer cells only, so healthy cells are not harmed (unlike chemotherapy or radiotherapy). | **Not yet as widely used as everyone hoped** when they were first developed. |
| Can be made in large amounts once the hybridoma clone exists. | Expensive and time-consuming to develop. |
| Can be designed for many different antigens: diagnosis, research, treatment. | **Ethical issue:** mice are used to make them, which some people object to on animal-welfare grounds. |

When an exam question asks you to *evaluate*, give points on **both** sides and finish with a **justified conclusion** (for example: "Overall they are useful because their specificity reduces harm to healthy cells, but the side effects mean they must be tested carefully before use").

::: terms
- **Monoclonal antibodies**: identical antibodies produced from a single clone of cells, each specific to one binding site on one protein antigen.
- **Antigen**: a molecule, usually a protein on the surface of a cell or pathogen, that an antibody binds to.
- **Lymphocyte**: a type of white blood cell that produces antibodies; it makes the right antibody but does not divide in culture.
- **Tumour cell**: a cancer cell (myeloma) that divides rapidly and indefinitely; fused with a lymphocyte to make a hybridoma.
- **Hybridoma cell**: the cell formed by fusing a mouse lymphocyte with a tumour cell; it can both divide and make the antibody.
- **Clone**: a group of genetically identical cells produced from a single cell, so all make the same antibody.
- **hCG**: the hormone present in the urine of a pregnant woman, detected by the monoclonal antibodies in a pregnancy test.
- **Control line**: the line on a test stick where antibodies bind the mobile antibodies whatever the result, showing the test has worked.
:::

::: method
**Command words on this topic**
- **Describe how monoclonal antibodies are produced** — give the steps in order: mouse injected with antigen → lymphocytes make the antibody → lymphocytes fused with a tumour cell → hybridoma → single hybridoma cloned → antibody collected and purified. Use the key words *lymphocyte*, *tumour cell*, *hybridoma*, *clone*.
- **Explain how a test or treatment works** — AQA gives you the details of an unfamiliar test or drug. Your answer always hangs on one idea: *the antibody is specific to one antigen, so it binds only to its target*. Then say what is attached (coloured bead, fluorescent dye, radioactive substance, toxic drug) and what that achieves.
- **Suggest** — apply the idea to a new situation (e.g. "suggest why a radioactive monoclonal antibody could show where a tumour has spread": it binds only to cancer cells, so radiation is detected where cancer cells are).
- **Evaluate / give advantages and disadvantages** — balance specificity and targeting against side effects, cost and the use of mice; end with a justified conclusion.

**4- and 6-mark answers.** A 6-mark "describe how monoclonal antibodies are made and used to treat cancer" answer is marked in levels. Level 3 (5–6 marks) needs the **full sequence of production in the right order** *and* a clear **linked explanation of targeting** (antibody binds to antigen on cancer cells → carries drug → kills cancer cells → healthy cells are not harmed). Listing words without linking them stays in Level 1–2.

**Pregnancy test questions.** Track what moves and what is fixed: mobile antibodies with beads move up the strip; antibodies at the test line and control line are **fixed**. Explain both lines and what each result means.

**Data questions.** You may be given trial results (numbers of patients, side effects, survival rates). Calculate percentages carefully (part ÷ whole × 100), compare treatments, and comment on sample size and side effects.
:::

::: example Production sequence
Describe how monoclonal antibodies are produced. (4)

A mouse is injected with the antigen, which stimulates its lymphocytes to make the specific antibody (1). The lymphocytes are fused with tumour cells to make hybridoma cells (1). A single hybridoma cell is cloned to produce many identical cells (1), which all make the same antibody; the antibody is then collected and purified (1).
:::

::: example Why fuse with a tumour cell?
Explain why lymphocytes are fused with tumour cells in the production of monoclonal antibodies. (2)

Lymphocytes make the required antibody but do not divide (1). Tumour cells divide rapidly, so the hybridoma both divides and produces the antibody, giving large quantities (1).
:::

::: example Pregnancy test
A woman uses a pregnancy test. Only the control line turns blue. Explain this result. (3)

The urine contained no hCG (1), so no hCG was carried up the strip on the mobile, bead-labelled antibodies, and no beads were held at the test line by the fixed hCG antibodies — no colour (1). The mobile antibodies still reached the control line and were bound by antibodies fixed there, so it turned blue, showing the test worked; she is not pregnant (1).
:::

::: example Cancer treatment
A drug company attaches a toxic drug to a monoclonal antibody that binds to an antigen found only on breast cancer cells. Explain why this treatment causes less harm to the patient than standard chemotherapy. (3)

The monoclonal antibody is specific to the antigen on breast cancer cells, so it binds only to those cells (1). The toxic drug is therefore delivered directly to the cancer cells and kills them (1). Healthy body cells do not have the antigen, so they are not damaged, unlike chemotherapy which affects all dividing cells (1).
:::

::: example Data analysis
In a trial, 240 patients received a monoclonal antibody treatment. 54 of them reported side effects. Calculate the percentage who reported side effects. (2)

$\frac{54}{240} \times 100$ (1) $= 22.5\%$ (1).
:::

::: watch
- Monoclonal antibodies is **biology only and Higher tier only** — but on the Higher Biology paper it is a favourite 4–6 mark question.
- The fused cell is a **hybridoma** — not "hybrid cell" or "clone". Say what *each* parent cell contributes: lymphocyte = **makes the antibody**; tumour cell = **divides**.
- Do not stop after "fused". You must say a **single hybridoma is cloned** and the antibody is **collected and purified**.
- In any "explain how this works" question the marking point is **specificity**: the antibody binds **only** to its specific antigen. Without that word the rest of the answer usually scores nothing.
- Pregnancy tests: the **mobile** antibodies carry beads; the antibodies at the test and control lines are **fixed**. Do not say the hCG "turns blue".
- Cancer treatment: say healthy cells are **not harmed** — that is the comparison examiners want.
:::

::: test
Evaluate the use of monoclonal antibodies to treat cancer compared with chemotherapy, which kills all rapidly dividing cells. (4)
---
Advantages, any two: the antibody binds only to antigens on cancer cells so delivers the drug/radioactive substance directly to them (1); healthy cells are not harmed, so fewer of the side effects of chemotherapy such as hair loss (1); smaller doses may be needed because the drug is targeted (1).
Disadvantages, any one: monoclonal antibodies create more side effects than expected (1); expensive / not yet widely available (1); mice are used in production — ethical concern (1).
Justified conclusion (1): e.g. targeted treatment is better for the patient overall, provided side effects are monitored in trials.

::: test
Describe how monoclonal antibodies are produced and explain how they can be used to treat cancer. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- a mouse is injected with an antigen (e.g. a protein found on cancer cells)
- this stimulates mouse lymphocytes to make the specific antibody
- lymphocytes are collected and fused with tumour (myeloma) cells
- this forms hybridoma cells, which can divide and make the antibody
- a single hybridoma cell is cloned, producing many identical cells
- all make the same antibody, which is collected and purified
- the antibody is specific to (binds only to) an antigen on cancer cells
- it is bound to a radioactive substance, toxic drug or chemical that stops cell division
- it delivers the substance to cancer cells, which are killed or stop dividing
- healthy cells lack the antigen, so are not harmed.

Level 3 (5–6 marks): a detailed, correctly sequenced description of production **and** a clear linked explanation of how targeting kills cancer cells without harming other cells.
Level 2 (3–4 marks): production described with some steps missing or out of order, and/or treatment explained with an incomplete link (e.g. no mention of specificity).
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple, disconnected statements, e.g. "uses a mouse" or "kills cancer cells".
0 marks: nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
A new monoclonal antibody binds to a protein found on the surface of a virus. Suggest and explain how it could be used both to diagnose infection and to help treat it. Explain why this antibody would be useless against a different strain of the virus whose surface protein has a different shape. (4)
---
Diagnosis: attach a dye or label; it binds only to the viral protein, so a colour change/signal in a blood sample shows the virus is present (1). Treatment: injected antibodies bind to the virus, marking it so white blood cells destroy it / stopping it entering cells (accept: carry an antiviral drug to infected cells) (1). Antibodies are specific to one binding site — their shape is complementary to that protein only (1); a protein with a different shape will not fit/bind, so the antibody cannot detect or target the new strain (1).
:::
