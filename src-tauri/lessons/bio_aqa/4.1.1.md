## What it is

Cells are the basic unit of all life. This topic covers the two big types of cell, what the parts inside them do, how cells specialise, how microscopes let us see them, and (separate science only) how bacteria are grown safely in the lab.

### Eukaryotes and prokaryotes

| | Eukaryotic cells (plants, animals, fungi, protists) | Prokaryotic cells (bacteria) |
|---|---|---|
| Size | larger, typically 10–100 µm | much smaller, typically 1–10 µm |
| Genetic material | enclosed in a **nucleus** | **not** in a nucleus: a **single loop of DNA** free in the cytoplasm |
| Plasmids | not usually | may have one or more small rings of DNA called **plasmids** |
| Cell membrane and cytoplasm | yes | yes |
| Cell wall | plants (cellulose) and fungi only | yes (not made of cellulose) |
| Mitochondria, chloroplasts | yes (chloroplasts in plants) | no |

### Animal and plant cells: structure linked to function

| Structure | Found in | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Nucleus** | animal and plant | contains genetic material (chromosomes made of DNA) that controls the activities of the cell |
| **Cytoplasm** | all cells | jelly-like liquid where most chemical reactions happen; contains enzymes |
| **Cell membrane** | all cells | controls which substances pass in and out of the cell |
| **Mitochondria** | animal and plant | site of aerobic respiration, which releases energy for the cell |
| **Ribosomes** | all cells | site of protein synthesis |
| **Chloroplasts** | many plant and algal cells | contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light for photosynthesis |
| **Permanent vacuole** | plant cells | filled with cell sap; keeps the cell firm (turgid) |
| **Cell wall** | plant and algal cells (cellulose) | strengthens and supports the cell |
| **Plasmids** | bacterial cells | small rings of DNA carrying extra genes, e.g. for antibiotic resistance |

Not every plant cell has chloroplasts: root cells are underground, get no light, and have none.

### Size, scale and standard form

| Unit | In metres | Example |
|---|---|---|
| millimetre (mm) | $10^{-3}$ m | a large egg cell is about 0.1 mm |
| micrometre (µm) | $10^{-6}$ m | a red blood cell is about 8 µm |
| nanometre (nm) | $10^{-9}$ m | a ribosome is about 25 nm |

To convert: 1 mm = 1000 µm, 1 µm = 1000 nm. Each step down multiplies the number by 1000.

**Orders of magnitude**: each power of ten is one order of magnitude. A 20 µm animal cell is about 10 times (one order of magnitude) bigger than a 2 µm bacterium. A 100 µm plant cell is about $10^2$ times (two orders of magnitude) bigger than a 1 µm bacterium.

**Estimating relative size**: when you only need a rough comparison (e.g. how many mitochondria would fit across a cell, or what fraction of a cell's area the nucleus takes up), round to simple numbers and compare. Estimation is used when exact measurement is not possible or not needed, e.g. judging the size of a structure from a micrograph with a scale bar.

### Microscopy

| | Light microscope | Electron microscope |
|---|---|---|
| Uses | light | a beam of electrons |
| Maximum magnification | about ×2000 | about ×2 000 000 |
| Resolution (resolving power) | about 200 nm | about 0.2 nm or better |
| Specimens | can be living | must be dead (in a vacuum) |
| Shows | cells, nucleus, chloroplasts, vacuole | mitochondria in detail, ribosomes, plasmids, membranes |

- **Magnification**: how many times bigger the image is than the real object.
- **Resolution**: the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate; it decides how much **detail** you see.

Microscopy has developed over time: early light microscopes (1600s) let Hooke and van Leeuwenhoek see cells; electron microscopes (1930s onwards) have much higher magnification and resolving power, so biologists could study sub-cellular structures such as ribosomes and the inner membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts for the first time, and understand how they work.

**The magnification equation** (you must recall it):

$$\text{magnification} = \dfrac{\text{size of image}}{\text{size of real object}}$$

Rearranged: real size = image size ÷ magnification. **Both sizes must be in the same unit** before you divide.

*Mini-example*: a cell's image is 24 mm across at ×400. Real size = 24 ÷ 400 = 0.06 mm = 60 µm = $6 \times 10^{-5}$ m.

### Required practical 1: using a light microscope (same number in Trilogy)

Method (onion epidermis or cheek cells):
1. Peel a thin layer of onion epidermis (one cell thick, so light passes through) and lay it flat on a drop of water on a slide; add a drop of **iodine** stain (cheek cells: **methylene blue**).
2. Lower a coverslip at an angle with a mounted needle to avoid **air bubbles**.
3. Clip the slide on the stage; start with the **lowest-power objective** lens.
4. Use the **coarse focus** to bring the stage close to the lens, then move it away while looking until the cells come into focus; switch to a higher power and use only the **fine focus**.
5. Draw what you see: pencil, clear unshaded outlines, label lines drawn with a ruler, a title and the **magnification** (eyepiece × objective, e.g. ×10 × ×40 = ×400) or a scale bar.

Risks: iodine is an irritant (wear eye protection); take care with glass slides and the lamp. Typical result: onion cells are rectangular with a cell wall, nucleus and a large vacuole, but **no chloroplasts**; cheek cells are irregular with a nucleus and membrane but no wall.

### Cell specialisation and differentiation

| Specialised cell | Adaptations and how they help |
|---|---|
| **Sperm cell** | tail to swim to the egg; many mitochondria in the mid-piece to release energy for swimming; acrosome with enzymes to digest through the egg's outer layer; nucleus with half the genetic material |
| **Nerve cell** | long axon to carry impulses over long distances; branched dendrites to connect with many other cells |
| **Muscle cell** | protein fibres that contract; many mitochondria to release energy for contraction; can store glycogen |
| **Root hair cell** | long hair-like projection gives a large surface area for absorbing water and mineral ions; many mitochondria for active transport of ions; no chloroplasts |
| **Xylem cell** | dead cells joined end to end with no end walls, forming hollow tubes for water and mineral ions; walls strengthened with lignin |
| **Phloem cell** | sieve tubes with sieve plates (perforated end walls) so dissolved sugars can move; companion cells with many mitochondria supply energy |

**Differentiation** is the process by which a cell changes to become specialised: it gains the sub-cellular structures it needs for its job. It matters because a multicellular organism needs many cell types to form tissues and organs that carry out different functions.
- Most **animal** cells differentiate at an **early stage** of development. In mature animals cell division is mainly for **repair and replacement**.
- Many **plant** cells keep the ability to differentiate **throughout life**.

### Separate science only: culturing microorganisms (biology only)

Bacteria reproduce by **binary fission** (simple cell division), as often as once every **20 minutes** if they have enough nutrients and a suitable temperature. They are grown in a **nutrient broth** (liquid) or as **colonies on agar gel** plates. Uncontaminated cultures are needed to investigate the action of disinfectants and antibiotics fairly.

**Aseptic technique** and why each step matters:
- **Sterilise Petri dishes and culture media** before use (autoclave) to kill any microorganisms already present.
- **Pass the inoculating loop through a flame** until red hot (then let it cool) to kill microorganisms on it.
- **Secure the lid with adhesive tape** (two or four strips, not all the way round) to stop microorganisms from the air getting in, while letting oxygen in so harmful anaerobic bacteria do not grow.
- **Store the plate upside down** so condensation does not drip onto the agar and spread colonies.
- **Incubate at 25 °C** in school labs: microorganisms grow, but it is below body temperature (37 °C), so human pathogens are less likely to grow.
- Work near a lit Bunsen (rising air carries microbes away) and lift the lid only slightly.

**Number of bacteria after a time**: number of divisions = time ÷ mean division time; bacteria at end = bacteria at start × $2^{\text{number of divisions}}$.
*Mini-example*: 1 bacterium dividing every 20 minutes for 3 hours: 180 ÷ 20 = 9 divisions, so $2^9 = 512$ bacteria.

**(Higher tier) Express the answer in standard form**: 1 bacterium dividing every 20 minutes for 10 hours: 600 ÷ 20 = 30 divisions, $2^{30} = 1\,073\,741\,824 \approx 1.07 \times 10^9$ bacteria.

**Area of a colony or clear zone**: area $= \pi r^2$, where $r$ is half the measured diameter. A clear zone 14 mm across: $\pi \times 7^2 = 153.9 \approx 154$ mm².

### Separate science only: Required practical 2: effect of antiseptics or antibiotics on bacterial growth

1. Use aseptic technique to spread a bacterial culture evenly over a sterile agar plate (sterile spreader or swab).
2. Soak paper discs in different antiseptics (or different concentrations of one), or use antibiotic discs; include a **control disc** soaked in sterile water.
3. Place discs on the agar with sterile forceps, tape the lid (not fully sealed), label, store upside down and incubate at 25 °C for about 48 hours.
4. Measure the diameter of the clear **zone of inhibition** around each disc and calculate its area with $\pi r^2$.

Variables: independent = type or concentration of antiseptic/antibiotic; dependent = area of clear zone; control = type of bacteria, volume of culture, size of disc, time soaked, temperature and time of incubation, type of agar. A **larger clear zone** means the substance is **more effective** at killing bacteria or stopping their growth. The control disc should have no clear zone, showing it is the antiseptic, not the disc, causing the effect. Risks: treat all cultures as potential pathogens; never open the plate after incubation; wash hands; disinfect benches; dispose of plates by autoclaving.

::: terms
- **Eukaryotic cell**: a cell whose genetic material is enclosed in a nucleus, such as plant and animal cells.
- **Prokaryotic cell**: a bacterial cell, much smaller, whose genetic material is a single DNA loop not enclosed in a nucleus.
- **Plasmid**: a small ring of DNA found in bacterial cells, separate from the main DNA loop.
- **Mitochondrion**: the sub-cellular structure where aerobic respiration takes place, releasing energy.
- **Ribosome**: a tiny sub-cellular structure where proteins are made (protein synthesis).
- **Differentiation**: the process by which a cell becomes specialised by gaining the structures it needs for a particular function.
- **Magnification**: how many times bigger an image is than the real object; image size divided by real size.
- **Resolution**: the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as two separate points; it sets the level of detail.
- **Binary fission**: the simple cell division by which bacteria reproduce (separate science only).
- **Aseptic technique**: methods that prevent unwanted microorganisms contaminating a culture or escaping from it.
:::

::: method
**Command words.** *Describe* = say what is there or what happens (e.g. the features of a cell). *Explain* = give the reason, usually linking a structure to its function with "so" or "which means". *Compare* = give a similarity or difference **for both** things in the same sentence ("prokaryotes have no nucleus **but** eukaryotes do"). *Suggest* = apply what you know to an unfamiliar cell. *Calculate* = show the equation and working.

**Structure–function questions.** For any specialised cell, use the pattern: feature → what it does → why that helps the cell's job. "Sperm have many mitochondria **to release energy** for the tail **to swim** to the egg" earns more than "sperm have mitochondria". If the cell is unfamiliar, the question gives you information: pick out features such as large surface area, many mitochondria or a thin shape and link each to the function.

**Magnification calculations.**
1. Write the equation: magnification = image size ÷ real size.
2. Convert **both sizes into the same unit** first (mm → µm: × 1000).
3. Substitute, calculate, give the unit (magnification has **no unit**, just "×"; sizes need µm or mm).
4. Give standard form if asked, e.g. 0.000 05 m = $5 \times 10^{-5}$ m.

**Bacterial growth (separate science only).** Number of divisions = time ÷ division time (same time units); final number = start number × $2^n$. Higher tier: give large answers in standard form to the stated significant figures. Areas of clear zones: halve the diameter, then $\pi r^2$, unit mm² or cm².

**Required-practical questions.** Know the steps in order and the reason for each (thin layer so light passes through; stain to make structures visible; coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles; lowest power first). For the culture practical, name the independent, dependent and control variables, explain the control disc, and give a reason for each aseptic step.

**Extended responses (4–6 marks).** Levels of response: a top-level answer covers several points, each **linked** to a reason, in a logical order. For "compare light and electron microscopes", cover magnification, resolution, what each shows and what that taught biologists. For "evaluate", give points both for and against and finish with a justified judgement.
:::

::: example Calculate: magnification
A student's drawing of a cheek cell is 45 mm wide. The real cell is 60 µm wide. Calculate the magnification of the drawing. (2)

Convert: 45 mm = 45 × 1000 = 45 000 µm (1).
Magnification = 45 000 ÷ 60 = **×750** (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: real size in standard form
On an electron micrograph at ×20 000 a mitochondrion is 30 mm long. Calculate its real length in metres, in standard form. (3)

Real size = image ÷ magnification = 30 ÷ 20 000 = 0.0015 mm (1).
0.0015 mm = 0.0015 × $10^{-3}$ m = 0.000 001 5 m (1) = $1.5 \times 10^{-6}$ m (1). (That is 1.5 µm.)
:::

::: example Explain: specialised cell
Explain how a root hair cell is adapted to its function. (4)

It has a long hair-like extension that gives a **large surface area** (1) so more water can be absorbed by osmosis and more mineral ions taken up (1). It has **many mitochondria** (1) to release energy by respiration for the **active transport** of mineral ions from the dilute soil water (1). (It also has no chloroplasts, because it is underground and receives no light.)
:::

::: example Calculate: bacterial population (separate science only, Higher tier)
A culture starts with 200 bacteria. The mean division time is 30 minutes. Calculate the number of bacteria after 4 hours. Give your answer in standard form. (3)

Number of divisions = 240 ÷ 30 = 8 (1).
Number of bacteria = 200 × $2^8$ = 200 × 256 = 51 200 (1).
In standard form: $5.12 \times 10^4$ bacteria (1).
:::

::: example Compare: microscopes (4)
Compare light microscopes and electron microscopes.

An electron microscope has a much higher magnification (about ×2 000 000) than a light microscope (about ×2000) (1). It also has a much higher resolution, so it can distinguish points about 0.2 nm apart compared with about 200 nm for a light microscope (1). This means electron microscopes show sub-cellular structures such as ribosomes and the internal structure of mitochondria in detail, which light microscopes cannot (1). However, light microscopes can view living specimens and are cheap and portable, whereas electron microscopes need dead specimens in a vacuum (1).
:::

::: watch
- In magnification sums, convert **both sizes to the same unit** before dividing; 1 mm = 1000 µm, not 100.
- Magnification has **no unit**; write "×500", never "500 µm".
- Plasmids are in **bacteria**; the nucleus is **not** in bacteria. Do not say bacteria have "no DNA".
- Not all plant cells have chloroplasts: root hair cells do not.
- Structure–function answers need the **reason**: "many mitochondria **to release energy for**...", not just a list of parts.
- Resolution is about **detail** (seeing two points as separate), not about how big the image is.
- Culturing: the lid is taped but **not sealed all round**; plates are stored **upside down**; incubate at **25 °C** (not 37 °C).
:::

::: test
Evaluate the use of the electron microscope compared with the light microscope for studying cells. (6)
---
Indicative content. For the electron microscope: much higher magnification (up to about ×2 000 000 against about ×2000); much higher resolution (about 0.2 nm against about 200 nm); shows ribosomes, the inner membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, plasmids; this has greatly increased understanding of sub-cellular structures and their functions. Level 2 (3–4): some advantages and disadvantages with some explanation; a conclusion may be missing.
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements, e.g. "the electron microscope magnifies more".
