## What it is

Separate science only: this whole topic (AQA 8461 section 4.7.5, Food production) is **biology only**, so Combined Science Trilogy 8464 students do not need it. It has no Higher-tier-only content.

The world's population passed 8 billion in 2022 and the UN projects about 9.7 billion by 2050. This topic asks how we can feed everyone: what threatens **food security**, how **intensive farming** makes food production more efficient (and why some people object), how **fisheries** can be made sustainable, and how **biotechnology** (mycoprotein, GM bacteria and GM crops) can help. It builds on biomass transfer (4.7.4) and genetic engineering (4.6.2.4).

### Factors affecting food security

**Food security** is having enough food to feed a population. Biological factors threatening food security include:

| Factor | How it threatens food security |
|---|---|
| **increasing birth rate** | in some countries the population grows faster than food production |
| **changing diets in developed countries** | demand for foods such as meat, out-of-season fruit and exotic crops means scarce food resources are **transported around the world**, away from the people who grow them |
| **new pests and pathogens** | new diseases or pests of crops and livestock spread (helped by global trade and climate change) and destroy harvests |
| **environmental changes** | e.g. widespread **famine** in some countries if the **rains fail**; droughts, floods and rising temperatures reduce yields |
| **cost of agricultural inputs** | fertilisers, pesticides, seed, animal feed and fuel are expensive, so poorer farmers cannot afford them and yields stay low |
| **conflicts** | wars in some parts of the world affect the **availability of water or food**: farms are abandoned, supplies blocked |

**Sustainable methods must be found to feed all people on Earth.** "Sustainable" means meeting today's needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs: not exhausting soil, water or fish stocks, and not destroying biodiversity.

**Interpreting statistics (WS 1.4).** Exam questions give population or food production data. Compare the **rate** of increase in population with the rate of increase in food, calculate food per person (total food ÷ population), or percentage changes, and conclude whether food security is improving.

*Mini-example:* a country produced 12 million tonnes of grain for 30 million people in 2000, and 15 million tonnes for 40 million people in 2020. Grain per person: 2000: 12 000 000 ÷ 30 000 000 = 0.40 tonnes (400 kg); 2020: 15 000 000 ÷ 40 000 000 = 0.375 tonnes (375 kg). Total production rose by 25 %, but population rose by 33 %, so grain per person fell by 6.25 % and food security got **worse**.

### Farming techniques

From 4.7.4: animals use most of the biomass they eat for respiration, and much of the energy released is transferred to the surroundings as heat or used for movement. The **efficiency of food production can be improved by restricting energy transfer from food animals to the environment**:

- **limiting their movement**: animals kept in pens, cages or crowded sheds use less energy moving about, so less glucose is respired for movement;
- **controlling the temperature of their surroundings**: in warm buildings, animals use less energy keeping warm, so more of their food becomes biomass.
- Some animals are also **fed high-protein foods** to increase growth (protein is used to build muscle).

This is **intensive farming** (factory farming), used for chickens, pigs and fish farms.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| more of the food eaten becomes meat: higher **feed conversion efficiency** | **ethical objections**: animals cannot move freely or behave naturally; some people think this is cruel |
| cheaper meat, eggs and fish for consumers | crowded animals spread **disease** quickly, so **antibiotics** are often used, which can lead to antibiotic-resistant bacteria |
| more food from less land | heated buildings use a lot of **energy** (fuel), raising costs and CO₂ emissions |
| animals protected from predators and bad weather | waste (manure) builds up in one place and can pollute water |
| | high-protein feed (e.g. fishmeal, soya) may itself use land or fish stocks |

**Ethics (WS 1.3):** some people have ethical objections to some modern intensive farming methods because of animal welfare. Free-range farming gives animals more space and natural behaviour but produces more expensive food. An evaluation should weigh food supply and cost against welfare and environment.

*Mini-example (feed conversion):* an intensively reared chicken gains 2.4 kg of mass from 4.0 kg of feed; a free-range chicken gains 2.0 kg from 5.0 kg. Efficiency = mass gained ÷ mass of feed × 100. Intensive: 2.4 ÷ 4.0 × 100 = **60 %**. Free range: 2.0 ÷ 5.0 × 100 = **40 %**. The intensive bird converts more feed to meat because it moves less and is kept warm.

### Sustainable fisheries

**Fish stocks in the oceans are declining** because of over-fishing. It is important to **maintain fish stocks at a level where breeding continues**, or certain species may disappear altogether in some areas.

Two methods play important roles in conserving fish stocks at a sustainable level:

| Method | How it helps |
|---|---|
| **control of net size** (minimum **mesh size**) | larger holes let **young, small fish escape**, so they survive to **reach breeding age** and reproduce, replacing the fish that are caught |
| **fishing quotas** | limits on the **mass or number** of each species that each fishing boat or country may catch in a period, so fewer fish are removed than the population produces, and the stock can recover |

Other measures include closed seasons (no fishing during breeding) and protected areas. All of them reduce income for fishermen in the short term, but protect jobs and food in the long term. Quotas are hard to police, and fish caught over quota may be thrown back dead.

### Role of biotechnology

Biotechnical and agricultural solutions, including **genetic modification**, can help meet the demands of the growing human population. **Modern biotechnology techniques enable large quantities of microorganisms to be cultured for food.**

**Mycoprotein**

- The fungus **Fusarium** (*Fusarium venenatum*) is used to produce **mycoprotein**, a **protein-rich food suitable for vegetarians**.
- The fungus is grown on **glucose syrup** in **aerobic conditions** (air is pumped in) in large fermenters; conditions such as temperature and pH are controlled, and the vessel is kept sterile.
- The **biomass is harvested and purified**, then shaped and flavoured as a meat substitute.
- Advantages: grows quickly; needs little land; can use cheap carbohydrate feedstock; high in protein and fibre, low in fat; much more efficient than feeding crops to animals.

**Human insulin from GM bacteria**

- A **genetically modified bacterium produces human insulin**: the human insulin gene is cut out with enzymes and inserted into a bacterial **plasmid**, which is put back into bacteria.
- The bacteria are grown in large fermenters; the insulin is **harvested and purified**, and used to **treat people with diabetes**.
- This gives a large, reliable supply of human insulin without using animal pancreases.

**GM crops**

- GM crops could provide **more food** (e.g. crops resistant to insect pests or herbicides, or tolerant of drought) or **food with improved nutritional value**.
- **Golden rice** is rice genetically modified to produce **beta-carotene** in its grains; the body converts this to **vitamin A**. Vitamin A deficiency causes blindness and deaths in children in countries where rice is the main food.

| Benefits of GM crops | Concerns |
|---|---|
| higher yields; less pesticide needed for insect-resistant crops | possible effects on wild flowers and insects (and so biodiversity) |
| improved nutrition (golden rice) | genes might spread to wild relatives |
| can grow in poor conditions (drought) | some people worry about long-term effects of eating GM food (not fully established) |
| | seed costs and patents may make poor farmers dependent on companies |

::: terms
- **Food security**: having enough food to feed a population.
- **Sustainable**: meeting present needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Intensive farming**: rearing animals with restricted movement in controlled, warm conditions so more of their food becomes biomass.
- **Fishing quota**: a limit on the amount of each fish species that may be caught in a given area and time.
- **Mesh size**: the size of the holes in a fishing net; larger holes let young fish escape.
- **Mycoprotein**: a protein-rich food made from the fungus Fusarium grown on glucose syrup in aerobic conditions.
- **Golden rice**: rice genetically modified to make beta-carotene, which the body converts into vitamin A.
- **Genetically modified organism**: an organism whose genome has been changed by inserting a gene from another organism.
:::

::: method
**Command words.** *Describe* = state the factor or technique ("limiting movement", "quotas limit the number of fish caught"). *Explain* = give the biological reason, usually linked to biomass and respiration ("less energy used in movement, so less glucose respired, so more biomass"). *Evaluate* = advantages **and** disadvantages, with a conclusion. *Suggest* = apply to a new situation, e.g. a new fish species or a new GM crop. *Calculate* = percentages, food per person, feed conversion efficiency.

**Food security questions.** Use the AQA list: birth rate, changing diets, new pests and pathogens, environmental change (rains failing), cost of agricultural inputs, conflict. With statistics, compare the percentage increase in food with the percentage increase in population, or work out food per person.

**Intensive farming (4–6 marks).** Explain *why* it works: animals kept in small spaces move less and are kept warm, so less energy is transferred to the surroundings and less glucose is used in respiration; more biomass is made from the same food. High-protein feed increases growth. Then evaluate: cheaper, more food versus ethical objections (welfare), disease and antibiotic use, energy costs, pollution. Finish with a judgement.

**Fisheries.** Always link the method to **breeding**: bigger mesh lets young fish escape so they can **breed**; quotas leave enough fish to **breed** and replace those caught. "So the fish don't run out" is too vague.

**Biotechnology.** For mycoprotein, give all four details AQA lists: *Fusarium*, **glucose syrup**, **aerobic** conditions, biomass **harvested and purified**. For insulin, say a GM bacterium produces human insulin, which is harvested and purified to treat diabetes. For golden rice, say it improves **nutritional value** (vitamin A precursor).

**Evaluating GM crops.** Benefits: yield, nutrition, pest resistance, drought tolerance. Concerns: effects on biodiversity and wild relatives, uncertain long-term health effects, cost and dependence on seed companies. Conclude using the context given.
:::

::: example Explain: intensive farming and efficiency
Chickens reared for meat are often kept indoors in warm, crowded sheds. Explain how this increases the efficiency of food production. (3)

The chickens cannot move much, so less energy is used in movement (1). The warm sheds mean the chickens use less energy keeping warm, so less heat is transferred to the surroundings (1). Less glucose from their food is used in respiration, so more is converted into biomass (meat) (1).
:::

::: example Explain: sustainable fishing
Explain how increasing the minimum mesh size of fishing nets helps to conserve cod stocks. (2)

Young, small cod can escape through the larger holes (1), so they survive to reach breeding age and reproduce, keeping the population at a sustainable level (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: food statistics
In 1990 a country produced 9.0 million tonnes of food for 18 million people. In 2020 it produced 13.5 million tonnes for 25 million people. Calculate the food per person in each year and state whether food security has improved. (3)

1990: 9.0 ÷ 18 = 0.50 tonnes per person (1).
2020: 13.5 ÷ 25 = 0.54 tonnes per person (1).
Food per person has increased, so food security has improved (1).
:::

::: example Describe: producing mycoprotein
Describe how mycoprotein is produced. (4)

The fungus *Fusarium* is used (1). It is grown in a fermenter on glucose syrup (1) in aerobic conditions, with air supplied (1). The fungal biomass is harvested and purified (1).
:::

::: watch
- Intensive farming works by **reducing energy transfer to the surroundings**: less movement and kept warm → less glucose respired → more biomass. Saying "they get fatter" is not an explanation.
- Fishing methods must be linked to **breeding**: young fish escape to breed; quotas leave enough fish to breed.
- Mycoprotein: *Fusarium*, glucose syrup, **aerobic**, harvested and purified. "Anaerobic" or "yeast" loses the mark.
- Golden rice improves **nutritional value** (beta-carotene / vitamin A), not yield.
- With population and food data, compare **per person** or percentage changes; a rise in total food production does not prove food security improved.
- "Evaluate" needs a conclusion; welfare objections are about **ethics**, not science.
:::

::: test
The table shows a country's population and wheat production. 2000: 50 million people, 20 million tonnes. 2020: 65 million people, 24 million tonnes. Calculate the percentage change in wheat per person and use it to comment on food security. (4)
---
2000: 20 ÷ 50 = 0.40 t per person (1); 2020: 24 ÷ 65 = 0.369 t per person (1). Change = (0.369 − 0.40) ÷ 0.40 × 100 = −7.7 % (a decrease of about 8 %) (1). Wheat per person has fallen because population grew faster (30 %) than production (20 %), so food security has got worse (1).
:::
