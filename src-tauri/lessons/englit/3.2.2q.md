## What it is

The Power and Conflict question (Paper 2, Section B) is worth **30 marks** and asks you to compare how poets present an idea in the named poem and **one other**. The single biggest way to lose marks is to write about poem one, then poem two, and add a paragraph of "similarly" at the end. That structure caps the level because the comparison is not sustained. The fix is to **organise by idea, not by poem**: each paragraph takes one aspect of the question and moves between both poems inside it.

**The assessment objectives, in order of weight.** AO1 (response and evidence — 12 marks): a clear, developed argument with well-chosen quotations. AO2 (methods — 12 marks): analysis of language, form and structure, with effect. AO3 (context — 6 marks): ideas about the writers' situations and purposes, woven in. Comparison runs through all three — it is not a separate objective but the way the whole answer is built.

**Structure by idea.** Plan three or four points of comparison drawn from the question's focus, and for each one hold both poems together:

| Paragraph | Both poems, inside the paragraph |
|---|---|
| **Intro** | The idea in the question; a one-sentence statement of how each poet approaches it; your line of argument (the likeness and the key difference). |
| **1: the idea — what each poet says** | Poem A's presentation with a quotation → poem B's with a quotation → the comparison sentence. |
| **2: language / imagery** | A method both use (or use differently): personification, an image pattern, a key word — analysed in both. |
| **3: form and structure** | Form (monologue, sonnet, free verse) and structure (shift, refrain, ending) — compared. |
| **4: context and purpose** | Why each poet wrote as they did — position, time, audience — and what each wants the reader to take. |
| **Conclusion** | The most important difference, stated as an answer to the question. |

**Comparative connectives** make the comparison explicit — and examiners look for them. Likeness: *similarly, likewise, both, in the same way, equally, as in*. Difference: *whereas, in contrast, unlike, on the other hand, while, however, but where X does…, Y…*. Degree: *more explicitly, less directly, to a greater extent*. Use one in every paragraph, at the point of turning from one poem to the other.

**Compare methods, not just content.** "Both poems are about war" is content and earns AO1 only. "Both poets use a refrain, but Tennyson's 'six hundred' turns from action to honour while Owen's 'But nothing happens' refuses to change" is method and earns AO2. For every comparison of what, add a comparison of how: form, structure, voice, imagery, sound.

**Weight the poems roughly equally.** The named poem is printed; the other is from memory — so the temptation is to write more about the printed one. Resist it: an answer that is two-thirds one poem is not a comparison. Learn enough of your partner poems (three or four quotations each) to give them equal time.

**Timing.** About 45 minutes: 5 planning (choose the partner, list three points, note quotations for both), 35 writing, 5 checking. Plan the pair before you write a word — the second poem must be chosen for the focus word, not for familiarity.

**Sentence patterns that compare inside the paragraph:**

- "Both poets present power as temporary: Shelley's 'colossal Wreck' and Dharker's 'monoliths' that daylight 'break[s]' through are the same monument seen from two centuries."
- "Where Tennyson honours the obedience — 'Theirs but to do and die' — Owen shows what obedience costs: 'We turn back to our dying.'"
- "Rumens's 'sunlight' does for memory what Agard's 'beacon' does for history: both make the suppressed thing a light source, but Rumens's light is private and Agard's is political."

::: method
1. **Plan by idea**: three points from the focus word, each with a quotation from *both* poems, before you write.
2. **Every paragraph contains both poems** and a comparative connective at the turn.
3. **Content, then method**: each point compares what is said *and* how (language, form, structure).
4. **Equal weight**: if a paragraph is all poem A, add poem B before moving on.
5. **Conclude with the key difference** — the sharpest answer to the question is usually where the poets part.
:::

::: example An introduction
Question: Compare how poets present the effects of conflict in Remains and one other poem. Write the introduction.

Choose War Photographer: both are about people who witnessed violence and cannot leave it behind.

Introduction: "Both Armitage and Duffy present conflict's effects as something that arrives afterwards, at home, rather than on the battlefield. Armitage's soldier carries a looter 'probably armed, possibly not' in his head; Duffy's photographer carries 'a hundred agonies' in his darkroom. Where Armitage's speaker is destroyed by his own act, Duffy's is worn down by other people's indifference — and that difference in who is guilty is the argument of this answer."

Three ideas in one paragraph: the shared claim, a quotation from each, and the line of difference to be pursued.
:::

::: example A paragraph by idea
Write the "language" paragraph for the same pair.

"Both poets use the language of the body to make trauma physical. Armitage's refrain, 'his bloody life in my bloody hands', fuses a swear-word with literal blood, so that the colloquial voice cracks under the weight of the image; the hands recall Macbeth's, and like his they will not come clean. Duffy locates the damage in the hands too — 'which did not tremble then / though seem to now' — but the tremor is delayed, a professional detachment collapsing in safety. Where Armitage's soldier cannot stop seeing, Duffy's photographer cannot stop developing: 'a half-formed ghost' appears in the tray as the memory appears in his mind. Both poets make memory a haunting, but Armitage's ghost is a man the speaker killed and Duffy's is a man the speaker photographed — guilt of the act against guilt of the witness."

Both poems, three quotations, a method (body imagery), a connective ("Where … Duffy's"), and the difference restated at the end.
:::

::: example Comparing form
Write the form and structure paragraph.

"Formally the poems differ in ways that reflect their speakers. Armitage's loose quatrains and colloquial rhythm are a soldier talking — 'On another occasion' — and the final couplet stands apart, the residue that will not fit into the story. Duffy's six-line stanzas rhyme ABBCDD, 'ordered rows' like the photographer's spools: a composure that holds the horror in place. Both, however, are built on a turn: Armitage's 'End of story, except not really' divides the killing from the living-with-it, and Duffy's final stanza turns from the darkroom to the reader 'between the bath and pre-lunch beers'. Armitage's structure keeps the trauma inside the speaker; Duffy's pushes it outward, onto us."

Method compared, contrast then likeness ("Both, however"), and the consequence of the difference.
:::

::: example A conclusion
Write the conclusion.

"Both poets show that conflict's effects outlast the conflict, and both place the damage at home rather than at the front. The decisive difference is direction: Armitage's soldier is haunted by what he did, and the poem ends still holding the body; Duffy's photographer is haunted by what others will not see, and the poem ends by accusing them. Remains is a confession; War Photographer is an indictment — and the reader is the one indicted."

A conclusion that states the key difference as the answer to the question, without repeating the paragraphs.
:::

::: q
How many marks is the Power and Conflict comparison worth, and how long should you spend?
---
30 marks; about 45 minutes, including 5 to plan and choose the partner poem.
:::

::: q
What structure caps your marks, and why?
---
All of poem one, then all of poem two — the comparison is not sustained, so the level is capped.
:::

::: q
How should paragraphs be organised instead?
---
By idea: each paragraph takes one aspect of the question and moves between both poems inside it.
:::

::: q
Give three comparative connectives for difference and three for likeness.
---
Difference: whereas, in contrast, unlike (while, however). Likeness: similarly, likewise, both (equally, in the same way).
:::

::: q
What is the difference between comparing content and comparing methods?
---
Content = what the poems say (AO1); methods = how — form, structure, language, sound (AO2); you need both.
:::

::: q
Why must the two poems be weighted roughly equally?
---
An answer dominated by the printed poem is not a comparison; the partner needs equal evidence and analysis.
:::

::: q
How many quotations should you have ready for a partner poem?
---
Three or four short ones, learned — enough to give it equal weight from memory.
:::

::: q
What should the conclusion do?
---
State the most important difference between the poets as a direct answer to the question.
:::

::: q
Where does context belong in a comparison answer?
---
Woven into the paragraph on purpose — a sentence on each poet's position or time, tied to a quotation.
:::

::: q
What should you decide before writing a single word?
---
The partner poem (chosen for the focus word), three points of comparison, and a quotation for each poem under each point.
:::

::: watch
- **Idea by idea, never poem by poem** — every paragraph has both poets in it.
- A **connective at the turn** in every paragraph.
- **Methods compared**, not only content — form, structure, imagery, sound.
- **Equal weight**: the partner poem needs as much evidence as the printed one.
- **Plan first**: partner, three points, quotations for both.
:::
