## What it is

**Poppies** (Jane Weir, 2009) is a mother's monologue addressed to her son, who has left for the army. It was commissioned by Carol Ann Duffy for a collection of war poems written by women and moves between the moment of his leaving — pinning a poppy to his blazer, smoothing his collar — and the present, in which she stands at the war memorial and hears only "your playground voice". **The poem never says the son has died.** It works entirely by implication, and stating his death as a fact misses the technique.

**The time frame.** The poem shifts without warning between three times: the leaving (past: "Three days before Armistice Sunday … I pinned one onto your lapel"); the present (the memorial, the church yard, the dove); and a deeper past (his childhood — the playground, the "bedroom", the "gelled blackthorns" of his hair). The movement is dreamlike — association, not chronology — because it is grief's movement: every present object recalls a past one. Tracking the shifts is the structure paragraph.

**The domestic detail carrying the grief.** Weir's method is to put the whole loss into small, tactile acts a mother performs:

| Image | What it carries |
|---|---|
| "spasms of paper red, disrupting a blockade / of yellow bias binding around your blazer" | The poppy is "spasms" — a wound, a fit — against the "blockade" of the blazer's edging: sewing vocabulary meets war vocabulary. The mother's craft (Weir is also a textile designer) is the language she thinks in. |
| "Sellotape bandaged around my hand, / I rounded up as many white cat hairs / as I could" | "Bandaged" — a nursing verb for a domestic act; she is already treating a wound. Removing cat hairs is the last thing she can do for him. |
| "I wanted to graze my nose / across the tip of your nose, play at / being Eskimos like we did when / you were little" | The wish held back — "I wanted", "I resisted" — the mother stops herself from treating the soldier as a child. What she does not do is the grief. |
| "steeled the softening / of my face" | Metal against flesh — she makes herself hard so he can leave; "steeled" is a soldier's word for a mother's act. |
| "the world overflowing / like a treasure chest" | His world, as he leaves; "intoxicated" by it. Hers is the closed door. |
| "I listened, hoping to hear / your playground voice catching on the wind" | The final image: at the war memorial, she hears not the soldier but the child. The poem ends on the wind and nothing in it. |

**The textile and wounding imagery.** Weir's two vocabularies run together throughout: *bias binding, blazer, gelled, Sellotape, stitch, tucks, darts, pleats* against *blockade, bandaged, steeled, spasms, released a song bird from its cage*. The mother's world of making and mending is set against a war that unmakes; and the dove "released" from her stomach — an origami bird, a "song bird from its cage" — is both the son leaving and a soul departing. The stitching imagery also describes the poem's own form: pieces of time tacked together.

**What is left unsaid.** The son is never named; the war is never named; his death is never stated — only implied by the memorial, the dove, the "Armistice Sunday", the listening for a voice that is not there. The poem is about the mother's experience, and she does not know — or cannot say — the outcome. That uncertainty is closer to the real experience of the families of soldiers than a confirmed loss would be, and it is why the ending is a listening, not a conclusion.

**Form.** Free verse with irregular stanzas; heavy enjambment, so sentences run across lines the way the mother's mind runs across time; caesurae where she stops herself ("I resisted the impulse / to run my fingers through the gelled / blackthorns of your hair."). No rhyme: nothing chimes or closes. The second person "you" makes the whole poem an address to someone who cannot answer.

**Context in a sentence.** Written in 2009 for Duffy's *Exit Wounds* project, during the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan; Armistice Sunday and the poppy connect the present war to the First World War's remembrance rituals.

::: method
1. **Never state that the son died**: say the poem *implies* loss and show how (memorial, dove, listening).
2. **Track the time shifts**: leaving → present → childhood — and say why grief moves like this.
3. **Domestic detail = the grief**: pick two small acts and show what each carries.
4. **Textile vs wounding**: give a pair of words from each vocabulary and say what their collision does.
5. **The ending**: a listening for a child's voice on the wind — say why it is a child, and why nothing answers.
:::

::: example Domestic detail
How does Weir present the mother's grief? Build the paragraph.

Point: Weir puts the grief into small, physical acts of care rather than into statements of feeling.

Evidence: "Sellotape bandaged around my hand, / I rounded up as many white cat hairs / as I could".

Analysis: "bandaged" is the poem's method in one word — a mother removing cat hairs from a blazer is already dressing a wound. The act is trivial and the verb is medical; the grief is in the gap. "As many … as I could" is the limit of what she can do for him now.

Purpose: Weir was asked to write about war from a woman's perspective; her answer is that a mother's war is fought in collars and lint, and that the small acts are where the feeling is kept.
:::

::: example Time
How does Weir structure the poem's movement through time?

The poem begins in a specific past — "Three days before Armistice Sunday" — pinning the poppy, smoothing the collar. But almost at once it slips further back: the nose-rubbing "like we did when / you were little", the "playground".

Then it moves to a present without a marker: the mother goes to the church yard, the war memorial, and leans "against it like a wishbone". The son is gone from the poem as he is gone from the house.

The shifts have no signposts because grief has none — every object in the present opens a door into the past. The structure is associative, dreamlike, and it ends not in the present but in a listening for the deepest past, "your playground voice". Time in the poem moves the way a mother's mind moves at a memorial.
:::

::: example Two vocabularies
Analyse the textile and wounding imagery together.

The poppy is "spasms of paper red" against a "blockade / of yellow bias binding": "bias binding" is sewing; "blockade" and "spasms" are war and injury. The two vocabularies share the blazer.

"Steeled the softening of my face": steel is armour, softening is flesh — the mother arms herself against her own feeling so that the son can go. "Sellotape bandaged": mending becomes nursing.

Effect: Weir shows that the war has entered the mother's language — she cannot sew, smooth or hold without the words of injury getting in. The making and mending she does are set against a war that unmakes, and the collision is the poem's way of saying that the home front is a front.
:::

::: example The ending
Why does the poem end with "your playground voice"?

At the memorial the mother listens "hoping to hear / your playground voice catching on the wind" — not the soldier's voice, but the child's.

The son she wants back is the boy, not the man who left; the poem has been sliding towards childhood all along (the Eskimo noses, the gelled hair, the bedroom). Grief reaches for the earliest version.

And nothing answers. The poem does not say he is dead; it says she is listening, at a war memorial, on the wind. Weir leaves the outcome unsaid because the mother does not have it — and because the listening is truer than a verdict. The reader is left where she is, with hope and dread in the same sentence.
:::
