## What it is

Inspector Goole is the play's engine and its mystery. Priestley builds him to do three things at once: **drive** the investigation with a method no one can resist; **deliver** the play's argument in his final speech; and **remain unexplained**, so that the audience leaves arguing about what he was. The exam wants you to argue a reading and support it — not to solve him.

**How he is presented on entry.** The stage direction: "He need not be a big man but he creates at once an impression of massiveness, solidity and purposefulness … He speaks carefully, weightily, and has a disconcerting habit of looking hard at the person he addresses before actually speaking." The lighting changes when he arrives — from "pink and intimate" to "brighter and harder" — the room is put under interrogation.

**His method — one line of enquiry at a time.**

- He controls the order: "One person and one line of enquiry at a time. Otherwise there's a muddle." Each character is questioned alone; the photograph is shown to one person; the others are made to wait and, in waiting, to guess.
- He knows already, and lets them speak: Sheila sees it — "he's giving us the rope — so that we'll hang ourselves". He does not accuse; he asks, and lets silence do the work.
- He refuses Birling's status: "I don't play golf"; to threats about the Chief Constable, "I don't see much of him." Birling's weapons — rank, money, connections — do not touch him.
- He uses the girl's suffering as a weapon of plain speech: "Burnt her inside out, of course"; "a nasty mess"; "She died in misery and agony — hating life". Against the family's euphemisms, he is graphic.
- He controls pace and exits: "I haven't much time", and he times his questions to the doorbell and the interval — Eric's arrival is prepared by him.

**His final speech** — learn its shape, not the whole thing: "there are millions and millions of Eva Smiths and John Smiths still left with us … We don't live alone. We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other. And I tell you that the time will soon come when, if men will not learn that lesson, then they will be taught it in fire and blood and anguish." The rhythm is a sermon: the repeated "we", the tripling of "fire and blood and anguish", the direct "I tell you". The "lesson" is collective responsibility; the "fire and blood" is the war two years away — and, for the 1945 audience, the two wars they had just lived through. Then: "Good night" — and he is gone.

**What is he?** The name **Goole** sounds like "ghoul" (and is also a port on the Humber — Priestley lets you choose). The readings:

| Reading | Support | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **A real policeman** | He behaves like one; Birling assumes so. | No Goole on the force; no body at the Infirmary; he knew what had not happened yet. |
| **A ghost / supernatural agent** | Knows everything in advance; the name; the final call fulfils his prophecy. | The play never confirms it; he eats nothing, does nothing overtly ghostly. |
| **The family's conscience made visible** | He only says what the family already knows; each confession is theirs, not his discovery. | He knows Eva's diary, the charity minutes — external facts. |
| **Priestley's socialist voice / a preacher** | The final speech is a political sermon; he is the author's mouthpiece. | Reduces the play to a lecture, which it is careful not to be. |
| **A dramatic device — the morality-play figure** | He is the "Everyman" tester: he arrives, tests each soul, leaves, and judgement follows. | A reading of function, not identity — but it is the one that explains the ending best. |

The play leaves it open on purpose: the final phone call makes his reality irrelevant. Whether or not he was a policeman, the questions were true.

**Stagecraft around him**: he controls entrances (his own, Eric's), light, the telephone; the family's tempo speeds up when he speaks and slows when he pauses; his exits leave the family alone with what they have said. He is the only character who never changes — everyone else is changed by him or refuses to be.

::: terms
- **Inspector Goole**: the mysterious inspector whose name suggests "ghoul", a ghost.
- **Omniscient**: all-knowing; the Inspector seems to know everything before he is told.
- **Catalyst**: something that causes change — the Inspector's arrival sets off the confessions.
- **Stage directions**: instructions in the script about setting, lighting and how lines are delivered — "The lighting should be pink and intimate until the Inspector arrives, and then it should be brighter and harder".
- **Final speech**: the Inspector's warning — "We are members of one body… fire and blood and anguish".
:::

::: method
1. **Describe the entry** (stage direction, lighting) and say what Priestley signals by it.
2. **Name the method**: one at a time; lets them speak; immune to status; blunt about suffering. Quote one line for each.
3. **Analyse the final speech as rhetoric** — repetition, tripling, direct address, the "lesson" — and link "fire and blood" to 1914 and 1945.
4. **Argue a reading of what he is**: pick one, support it, acknowledge the alternative, and say why Priestley leaves it open.
5. **Say what he does to the audience**: he questions the theatre as well as the room.
:::

::: example His entry
How does Priestley establish the Inspector's authority before he speaks? Build the paragraph.

Point: Priestley gives the Inspector authority through presence, not rank.

Evidence: "He need not be a big man but he creates at once an impression of massiveness, solidity and purposefulness"; he has "a disconcerting habit of looking hard at the person he addresses before actually speaking."

Analysis: "need not be a big man" separates authority from size — his weight is moral. The hard look before speech is a method: silence that makes the other person fill it. And the lighting cue — "pink and intimate" to "brighter and harder" — turns the dining room into an interrogation room before a word is said.

Purpose: Birling's power is social (money, the Chief Constable, his knighthood); the Inspector's is something else, and the audience is meant to feel the difference from his first stage direction.
:::

::: example The method
Explain how the Inspector's questioning works, with Sheila as the example.

He shows Sheila the photograph alone and waits. She recognises the girl from Milwards and, "with a little cry", runs out — he has said almost nothing.

When she returns he does not accuse her; he lets her tell it: "I went to the manager … and I told him that if they didn't get rid of that girl, I'd never go near the place again." He supplies only the consequence: "And so she had to leave."

Sheila names the technique for the audience: "he's giving us the rope — so that we'll hang ourselves." Priestley's Inspector never needs to prove anything; the family convicts itself, one person at a time, which is why the confessions feel true even after the Inspector is shown to be false.
:::

::: example The final speech
Analyse "We are members of one body".

The speech is built like a sermon: "We don't live alone. We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other." Three short sentences, each beginning "We" — the pronoun is the argument. The Birlings have spent the play saying "I"; he answers with "we".

"Members of one body" echoes St Paul — the church as a body — which gives socialist responsibility the authority of scripture for a 1945 audience.

Then the warning, in a triple: "fire and blood and anguish". Priestley's audience knew what it referred to: the war Birling said would never come, and the second one they had just survived. The Inspector's "lesson" is aimed over the family's heads at the theatre — and his exit, "Good night", leaves the audience to answer it.
:::

::: example Arguing a reading
Is the Inspector real? Write a paragraph that argues without settling.

Priestley makes the supernatural reading available: the name Goole, his knowledge of a death that has not yet happened, and the final call that fulfils his warning. On this reading he is a ghost or an agent of judgement — the morality-play tester who visits, tries each soul and leaves.

But the play also works if he is simply the family's conscience given a hat and a voice: he discovers nothing; each confession is a thing they already knew.

What matters is why Priestley refuses to decide. If the Inspector were provably a policeman, the ending would be a plot; if provably a ghost, a fable. Left open, the question "was he real?" becomes "does it matter?" — and Sheila's answer, "it doesn't much matter now", is the play's. The audience is denied the loophole the Birlings reach for.
:::
