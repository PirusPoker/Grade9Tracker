## What it is

*A Christmas Carol* (1843) is a **novella in five staves** — Dickens calls them staves, not chapters, because the book is a carol: a song in prose with verses, meant to be sung again every year. Noticing that is an easy mark, and it tells you how the book is built: a musical, repeating structure with a moral at the end of each verse.

| Stave | Title | What happens | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | *Marley's Ghost* | Christmas Eve. Scrooge at his counting-house: refuses his nephew Fred's invitation, refuses the charity collectors ("Are there no prisons?"), grudges Bob Cratchit Christmas Day. At home, Marley's ghost in chains — "I wear the chain I forged in life" — warns him three spirits will come. | Nothing yet — but the warning frames everything. Scrooge is established as "solitary as an oyster"; the reader is made to dislike him thoroughly. |
| **2** | *The First of the Three Spirits* | The Ghost of Christmas **Past** — a child-old figure with a jet of light from its head — shows Scrooge his lonely schooldays, his sister Fan, Fezziwig's party, and Belle releasing him from their engagement: "another idol has displaced me … a golden one." | Scrooge **weeps** and begs the spirit to show no more. The first crack: he sees the boy he was and the man he chose to become. |
| **3** | *The Second of the Three Spirits* | The Ghost of Christmas **Present** — a giant in green robes on a throne of food — shows the Cratchits' Christmas (Tiny Tim: "God bless us every one!"), Fred's party where they laugh at him, miners, lighthouse-keepers, sailors keeping Christmas. Under the spirit's robe: **Ignorance and Want**. Scrooge's own words return: "Are there no prisons?" | Scrooge asks whether Tiny Tim will live; he is "overcome with penitence and grief" at hearing his own words. He begins to want to be part of what he sees. |
| **4** | *The Last of the Spirits* | The Ghost of Christmas **Yet to Come** — silent, shrouded, only a pointing hand. Businessmen joke about an unnamed dead man; his belongings are sold to Old Joe; a couple are relieved at his death; the Cratchits mourn Tiny Tim; the grave: EBENEZER SCROOGE. | Scrooge's resolution: "I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year." He grasps the spirit's hand and it collapses into a bedpost. |
| **5** | *The End of It* | Christmas morning. Scrooge is "as light as a feather"; sends the prize turkey to the Cratchits, gives generously to the collector, goes to Fred's, raises Bob's salary; becomes "a second father" to Tiny Tim. "Bah! Humbug!" becomes laughter. | The transformation complete — and the narrator confirms it lasted: "he knew how to keep Christmas well." |

**The circular structure.** Stave 5 replays Stave 1 in reverse: the same people (Fred, the collectors, Bob) receive the opposite response. The nephew who was refused is visited; the collector who got "Nothing!" gets a sum that makes him gasp; the clerk who was grudged a day gets a raise. Dickens chose the circle so the reader can measure the change exactly — the same tests, the opposite answers. It also mirrors the shape of a carol's refrain: we return to where we began, but changed.

**The three-spirits structure** is past → present → future, which is also cause → condition → consequence: how Scrooge became what he is; what his meanness costs others now; where it ends. Each spirit is more impersonal than the last — the Past is intimate, the Present is social, the Future is silent and shows only what others will say. The order matters: Scrooge cannot be moved by his own death (Stave 4) until he has been reminded that he was once capable of feeling (Stave 2) and shown the people his money touches (Stave 3).

**The ghost story as moral argument.** Dickens uses the popular Christmas ghost story — fireside, supernatural, frightening — to carry a social sermon he could not have made readers sit through as an essay. The ghosts are teachers; the fear is a method. And the narrator keeps stepping in to talk to us — "Marley was dead: to begin with. There is no doubt whatever about that" — so that the reader, like Scrooge, is being addressed and asked to change.

::: terms
- **Novella**: a short novel.
- **Stave**: a verse of a song; Dickens divides A Christmas Carol into five staves, like a carol.
- **Cyclical structure**: a structure that returns at the end to the situations of the beginning, showing how the character has changed.
- **Ghost story**: a Victorian genre, often read aloud at Christmas, which Dickens uses to carry a moral message.
- **Allegory**: a story in which characters and events stand for ideas, carrying a moral message.
- **Didactic**: intended to teach a moral lesson.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the stave** every time you quote: "In Stave 3, Dickens…" — it shows you know the structure and lets you talk about where a moment sits.
2. **Quote from every stave.** An answer that lives in Stave 1 has not shown the change; Stave 5 is your evidence that it worked.
3. **Use the circle**: for any Stave 1 moment, find its Stave 5 answer (the collectors, Fred, Bob, "Humbug").
4. **Say why the order of the spirits matters** — cause, condition, consequence — rather than just listing them.
5. **Call it a novella, in staves**, and say what Dickens gains from the ghost-story form (a moral the reader will sit through).
:::

::: example Naming the structure
Write an opening sentence for a question on how Dickens presents Scrooge's transformation.

Weak: "Scrooge changes a lot in the story."

Better: "Dickens structures the novella as a carol in five staves, so that Scrooge's transformation is measured stave by stave against the man of Stave 1."

Best: add the circle — "…and Stave 5 replays the encounters of Stave 1 — the nephew, the collectors, the clerk — with the opposite answers, so the reader can see the change tested on exactly the people it failed before."
:::

::: example The circle in evidence
Show the Stave 1 / Stave 5 mirror with quotations.

Stave 1, the charity collector asks for "some slight provision for the Poor"; Scrooge: "Are there no prisons? … Are there no workhouses?" — and gives "Nothing!"

Stave 5, Scrooge meets the same gentleman and whispers a sum that makes him say "My dear Mr Scrooge, are you serious?" — and "Not a farthing less. A great many back-payments are included in it, I assure you."

Analysis: the "back-payments" are the point — Scrooge is not just generous now but paying off the debt of every Christmas he refused. Dickens makes the change specific and quantifiable, and gives the reader the satisfaction of hearing Stave 1 answered in its own terms.
:::

::: example Why this order
Why does Dickens send the spirits in the order past, present, future?

The Past comes first because Scrooge must be reminded he was once a person who felt — the lonely boy, the brother who loved Fan, the clerk who loved Belle. Without that, the later lessons would land on stone.

The Present comes second to show the cost of his choices to others now — the Cratchits' small goose, Tiny Tim's crutch — and to turn his own words back on him.

The Future comes last because it is the consequence, and it can only frighten a man who has already begun to care: his death is unmourned, his things picked over, and the only grief in the stave is for Tiny Tim. Dickens builds the sequence as cause, condition, consequence, so that the final resolution is earned, not sudden.
:::

::: example The form
Explain what Dickens gains by writing a ghost story rather than a pamphlet on poverty.

In 1843 Dickens had planned a pamphlet — an appeal on behalf of poor children — and abandoned it for a story. A story could be read aloud at Christmas, to families, and would be felt where an argument would be skimmed.

The ghosts let him do what an essay cannot: put the reader inside Scrooge and take him to places a rich reader never went — the Cratchits' four-roomed house, the miners' hut, the rag-shop. Fear (Marley's chains, the shrouded Future) makes the moral urgent.

And the narrator talks directly to us — "Oh! But he was a tight-fisted hand at the grindstone, Scrooge!" — so the reader is addressed, entertained and accused at once. The form is the argument: if a ghost story can convert Scrooge, it can convert the reader.
:::

::: q
Why does Dickens call the sections staves rather than chapters?
---
Because the book is a carol — a song in verses (staves) meant to be repeated each year; the structure is musical and moral.
:::

::: q
What does Marley's ghost warn, and what does his chain represent?
---
That three spirits will visit; the chain is "forged in life" link by link from his own greed — the consequence of a life like Scrooge's.
:::

::: q
What does the Ghost of Christmas Past show Scrooge?
---
His lonely schooldays, his sister Fan, Fezziwig's party, and Belle ending their engagement.
:::

::: q
What are the two children under the robe of the Ghost of Christmas Present?
---
Ignorance and Want — "beware them both … but most of all beware this boy".
:::

::: q
How is the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come different from the other two?
---
It never speaks; it is shrouded and only points — impersonal and frightening, the consequence rather than a teacher.
:::

::: q
Name three encounters from Stave 1 that are repeated with the opposite outcome in Stave 5.
---
Fred's invitation (refused → accepted); the charity collectors (nothing → a large sum); Bob Cratchit (grudged a day off → given a raise).
:::

::: q
Quote Scrooge's resolution at the end of Stave 4.
---
"I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year. I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future."
:::

::: q
What is the first sign in Stave 2 that Scrooge can change?
---
He weeps at the sight of his younger self alone at school — "his lip was trembling".
:::

::: q
Why is the order past–present–future important to the structure?
---
It moves from cause (how Scrooge became this) to condition (what it costs others) to consequence (his unmourned death), so the change is earned.
:::

::: q
How does the narrator's voice contribute to the structure?
---
It addresses the reader directly from the first line, so we are being taught alongside Scrooge and invited to change too.
:::

::: watch
- **Stave, not chapter** — and say why: it is a carol.
- Quote from **every stave**; Stave 5 is your proof the change happened.
- The **circle** (Stave 1 answered in Stave 5) is the best structural point in the book — use it.
- Give the **reason for the spirit order**, not just the order.
- Retelling a stave is not analysis: every plot fact must show a **change or a method**.
:::

::: test
What happens in Stave 1 that Stave 5 answers, and how? (3)
---
Scrooge refuses the charity collectors — in Stave 5 he gives generously (1); refuses Fred's invitation — goes to Fred's party (1); grudges Bob Christmas Day — raises his salary (1).
:::

::: test
Describe the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come and explain why it is different from the others. (2)
---
Shrouded in black, silent, only a pointing hand (1); it does not explain — Scrooge must draw the moral himself, showing the change is now his own (1).
:::

::: test
Why does Dickens end Stave 4 with Scrooge at his own grave? (2)
---
Fear of his own death is the final push after memory and pity have failed to finish the job (1); it forces the vow — 'I will honour Christmas in my heart' (1).
:::

::: test
What does the order past, present, future do for the argument? (2)
---
Past shows the cause of Scrooge's coldness, present its cost to others, future its consequence for him (1); cause, condition, consequence — a reasoned conversion, not a sudden one (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the structural role of Marley's Ghost. (2)
---
It frames the story: warns Scrooge and announces the three spirits (1); its chain of cash-boxes is what Scrooge's own chain will be — the outcome shown before the journey (1).
:::

::: test
Give two ways the narrator's voice shapes the reader's experience. (2)
---
Direct address and jokes ('dead as a door-nail') make the reader Dickens's companion (1); the narrator judges Scrooge and later celebrates him, steering the reader's sympathy (1).
:::

::: test
What happens at Fezziwig's party, and what does it establish? (2)
---
Scrooge watches his old employer host a cheap, joyful party (1); it proves a kind master costs little — and Scrooge immediately thinks of Bob (1).
:::

::: test
Why is it wrong to call the book a novel in chapters? (2)
---
It is a novella in five staves (1); the term signals the carol form — a song with verses to be repeated each year, which is part of Dickens's method (1).
:::
