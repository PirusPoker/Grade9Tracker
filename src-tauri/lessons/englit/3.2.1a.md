## What it is

*An Inspector Calls* (1945) is a play built like a trap. Three acts, **one set, one evening, real time**: the Birling dining room in Brumley, spring 1912, from the engagement dinner to the final telephone call. Nothing happens offstage that we do not hear about; no time passes that we do not sit through. Paper 2 is **closed book with no extract**, so what you need is the shape of the play and a handful of short quotations per character — not long speeches.

**The real-time, single-set construction.** Priestley obeys the classical unities — one place, one action, one continuous stretch of time — so that the audience is locked in the room with the family. There is no escape into another scene, no cut to Eva, no flashback: everything she suffered is reconstructed through the confessions of the people who caused it. The set does the moralising: the "substantial and heavily comfortable" dining room, the port and cigars, and outside it a girl dying of disinfectant.

**The chain of confessions.** Each act is a link, and the order is a descent through the family's respectability: the employer, the customer, the lover, the charity chairwoman, the son. Each person's involvement is revealed **in turn**, one photograph at a time, because the Inspector insists on it — "One person and one line of enquiry at a time. Otherwise there's a muddle." The audience always knows more than the next character to be questioned, which is the play's engine of suspense: we watch them walk into it.

**The act endings** are cliffhangers by design — the curtain falls at the moment of revelation (Gerald's face; "Eric!"; the telephone), and the next act opens exactly where the last one stopped. The 1945 audience left each interval knowing who was next.

**The cyclical ending.** The final phone call restarts the play. Read as a ghost story it is uncanny: Goole/ghoul, the Inspector who knew everything before it was discovered. Read as a morality play it is judgement: the Birlings were given a rehearsal — a chance to learn — and the older ones failed it, so the lesson will now be "taught" for real. Read as social argument it says the questions do not stop because the questioner was a fake: an Eva Smith has still died. Priestley refuses to let the audience off with the family.

::: method
1. **Know the act endings**: they are the structure — say what each curtain reveals and why Priestley stops there.
2. **Explain the chain**: one character at a time, in descending order of self-righteousness; the audience ahead of the characters = dramatic irony.
3. **Say what the unities do**: no escape, no cut to Eva — the audience is shut in with the family and its evening.
4. **Argue the ending**: at least two readings (ghost story, morality play, social argument), and what each does to the audience.
5. **Three short quotations per character**, learned — Paper 2 has no extract.
:::

::: example Real time, one room
What does Priestley gain by setting the whole play in one room in real time? Build the paragraph.

Point: the single set and unbroken time make the audience prisoners of the Birlings' evening.

Evidence: the play opens on the "heavily comfortable" dining room and never leaves it; the Inspector's arrival is a doorbell heard from inside; Eva Smith exists only in the photograph the audience never sees and the confessions it hears.

Analysis: because there is no cut to Eva, the audience has to build her from the family's admissions — her suffering is assembled out of their complacency. And because no time is skipped, there is no relief: the Birlings cannot sleep on it, and neither can we.

Purpose: Priestley wants the audience to feel what the family feels — cornered — so that the Inspector's final speech is heard as addressed to the theatre as much as the room.
:::

::: example The chain of enquiry
Explain how Priestley reveals each character's involvement in turn.

The Inspector controls the order: "One person and one line of enquiry at a time." He shows the photograph to one character only, so each confession is private until it is public.

The order descends through the family: Birling (the employer who sacked her over a few shillings), Sheila (the customer who had her dismissed from spite), Gerald (the man who kept and dropped her), Mrs Birling (the charity that refused her), Eric (the father of her child). Each act ends on the next link — the audience sees Gerald's face, hears "Eric!" — before the character confesses.

Effect: dramatic irony. When Mrs Birling demands that the father be "dealt with very severely", the audience already suspects it is her son; Priestley lets her build the gallows for her own family. The structure turns the family's self-righteousness into the instrument of its exposure.
:::

::: example The act endings
Why does Act 2 end with Mrs Birling's "Eric!"?

Priestley has spent the act letting Mrs Birling refuse all responsibility — "I accept no blame" — and then demand the harshest punishment for the child's father.

The Inspector's questions narrow the father's identity — young, drunk, stole money — while Eric's absence from the stage becomes louder. Sheila sees it first: "Mother — stop — stop!"

The curtain falls on the realisation, not the confession, so the audience carries the knowledge through the interval. Priestley ends each act at the point of maximum dramatic irony: we know; the family is about to. The structure rewards the audience for paying attention and punishes the Birlings for not.
:::

::: example The cyclical ending
What does the final phone call do?

On the surface, it restarts the play: a girl has died, an inspector is coming, the questions will begin again — and this time there will be no doubt that the death is real.

As a morality play, it is judgement. The Inspector's "fire and blood and anguish" was a warning; the older Birlings' relief — "the whole thing's different now" — shows they learned nothing, so the lesson will be taught the hard way. Sheila and Eric, who accepted responsibility, are already changed.

As social argument, it tells the audience of 1945 that responsibility does not depend on whether the accuser was real. Priestley refuses the family — and the audience — the comfort of "it was a hoax". The ending is cyclical because the problem is: an Eva Smith dies every time the Birlings behave like Birlings.
:::

::: q
When and where is the play set, and when was it written?
---
Brumley, an industrial Midlands town, spring 1912; written and first performed in 1945.
:::

::: q
Name the three classical unities the play observes.
---
One place (the dining room), one time (a single continuous evening), one action (the investigation of Eva Smith's death).
:::

::: q
In what order does the Inspector question the family, and what did each do?
---
Birling (sacked her), Sheila (had her dismissed), Gerald (kept and left her), Mrs Birling (refused her charity), Eric (got her pregnant and stole money).
:::

::: q
How does Act 1 end?
---
Sheila realises Gerald knew Daisy Renton; the Inspector re-enters with "Well?"
:::

::: q
How does Act 2 end?
---
Mrs Birling realises the father of the child is Eric — "Eric!" — as the front door opens and he enters.
:::

::: q
Quote the Inspector's rule for questioning.
---
"One person and one line of enquiry at a time. Otherwise there's a muddle."
:::

::: q
What two discoveries make the family think the Inspector was a hoax?
---
There is no Inspector Goole on the Brumley police; the Infirmary has had no suicide that night.
:::

::: q
What is the final telephone message?
---
A girl has just died on her way to the Infirmary after swallowing disinfectant, and a police inspector is on his way to ask questions.
:::

::: q
Give two readings of the cyclical ending.
---
Ghost story (Goole knew what had not yet happened); morality play (a rehearsal failed, so the lesson is taught for real); social argument (responsibility does not depend on the accuser).
:::

::: q
Why should you learn short quotations rather than long speeches for Paper 2?
---
It is closed book with no extract; a handful of short, accurate quotations per character can be embedded anywhere in an answer.
:::

::: watch
- **Closed book, no extract**: three short quotations per character, learned cold.
- The **act endings** are the structure — say what each curtain reveals.
- Explain **why** the unities matter (no escape, no cut to Eva), not just that they exist.
- The ending is **deliberately open** — offer readings, do not "solve" it.
- Retelling confessions is not analysis; say what the **order** does to the audience.
:::
