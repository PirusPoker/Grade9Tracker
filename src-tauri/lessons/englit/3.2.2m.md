## What it is

**The Émigrée** (Carol Rumens, 1993) is spoken by a woman who left a city as a child and carries a memory of it that nothing can correct — not news of its tyranny, not the fact that she "may not" return, not the possibility that it no longer exists. Its subject is the **tension between memory and present reality**, and the identity that a person builds out of a place they can never go back to. The city is never named; the threat is never named. Those gaps are the poem.

**The three stanzas.**

| Stanza | Movement | Key phrases and analysis |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The memory established — and defended | "There once was a country" — the opening of a fairy tale; the country belongs to story now. "I left it as a child" — the memory is a child's, which is why it is all "sunlight". The news that arrives — "the worst news I receive of it" — "cannot break / my original view". The stanza's last word is "sunlight": the memory wins the sentence. |
| **2** | The language of the city | She carries the city's "child's vocabulary" like a treasure; the tongue is now banned — "It may by now be a lie, banned by the state" — but she cannot give it up. "The city takes a lot of space" — it fills her. Repeated "sunlight": the word is "clear" and bright. She holds the language like an object; "it tastes of sunlight". |
| **3** | The present intrudes — and is refused | "I have no passport, there's no way back at all"; the city itself comes to her "in its own white plane" — she cannot go, so it arrives. "They accuse me of absence, they circle me. / They accuse me of being dark in their free city." The "they" is unnamed — the state, the new country, the present. The last lines: "My city hides behind me. They mutter death, / and my shadow falls as evidence of sunlight." |

**"Sunlight."** The word ends every stanza. It is her memory's weather — a child's summer — and it becomes, by the end, the thing she is made of: her "shadow falls as evidence of sunlight". A shadow proves a light source; her existence proves the city she remembers. The repetition is refrain and refusal: whatever the present says, the stanza ends in sunlight.

**Memory against fact.** The speaker knows her memory may be false: "It may by now be a lie"; the city may be under a tyranny ("the worst news"), the language banned, the place perhaps gone from the map. She does not argue with the facts; she outlasts them. "That child's vocabulary I carried here / like a hollow doll" — the memory is a toy, hollow, and also the only thing she has. The gap between the city as she remembers it and the city as it is — if it is — is the poem's subject, and Rumens never closes it.

**The unnamed city and the unnamed threat.** No country, no war, no regime: only "the worst news", "banned by the state", "they mutter death". The vagueness is deliberate — the poem is about any exile, any émigrée, and about how the political present is always a "they" to a person whose home is a memory. Readers who try to identify the city miss the method: it could be anywhere a child left.

**Identity, exile, belonging.** She belongs to a place that cannot have her; the "free city" she now lives in accuses her of being "dark" — foreign, suspect. Her identity is therefore made of absence: the city "hides behind me", she is its evidence. Rumens presents exile not as loss of a home but as carrying one that has no address.

**Form.** Three stanzas of roughly equal length, free verse, with a loose iambic movement; long enjambed sentences that hold the memory together against the short accusations of "they". The refrain "sunlight" is the only formal repetition, and it does the work of rhyme.

**Context in a sentence.** Rumens is a British poet writing in 1993, as the Soviet bloc broke up and borders redrew; the émigrée's unnamed city belongs to that moment without belonging to any one country.

::: method
1. **Open with the fairy tale**: "There once was a country" — memory as story, and a child's story.
2. **Track "sunlight"** — three stanza endings, one word, its meaning growing.
3. **Memory vs fact**: quote "It may by now be a lie" and say that she does not argue, she persists.
4. **The unnamed**: say why no city and no "they" are identified — every exile, any state.
5. **The ending**: shadow as evidence of sunlight — identity made of absence.
:::

::: example The opening
How does Rumens present memory in the first stanza? Build the paragraph.

Point: Rumens makes the city a story before it is a place.

Evidence: "There once was a country … I left it as a child / but my memory of it is sunlight-clear."

Analysis: "There once was" is the grammar of fairy tale — the country exists in the past tense of story, which is the only tense an exile has for home. "As a child" explains the sunlight: it is a child's memory, unrevised. And the news that "cannot break / my original view" makes the memory a possession that facts cannot repossess.

Purpose: Rumens is not asking whether the memory is accurate; she is showing what it is for — the thing an émigrée is built from.
:::

::: example Sunlight
Track the word "sunlight" through the poem.

Stanza 1 ends "sunlight" — the memory's weather, "clear", a child's summer.

Stanza 2 ends "it tastes of sunlight": the city's language, carried "like a hollow doll", is sensory, in the mouth — memory has become the body's.

Stanza 3 ends "my shadow falls as evidence of sunlight". Now she is the light source's proof: a shadow needs a sun, and her existence needs the city. The word has moved from what she remembers to what she is.

The refrain is Rumens's structure: whatever "they" say in the stanza, the last word is the memory's. It is not an argument; it is a refusal to end on anything else.
:::

::: example Memory against fact
How does the poem present the conflict between memory and the present?

The speaker admits the facts: "It may by now be a lie, banned by the state"; "I have no passport, there's no way back at all"; the news is "the worst". She does not dispute that the city has changed, or that it may not exist.

But the admissions never win a sentence. "The worst news I receive of it cannot break / my original view" — the enjambment holds "break" back until it is denied.

Rumens presents memory not as denial but as endurance: the speaker outlasts the facts rather than contesting them. The gap between the city she carries and the city that is — "if" it is — stays open; the poem's honesty is that it never pretends to close it.
:::

::: example The unnamed
Why does Rumens leave the city and the threat unnamed?

There is no country, no regime, no war — only "the state", "they", "the worst news". The reader cannot place the émigrée on a map.

The vagueness makes her every exile: the poem is about the structure of leaving, not a particular history. And "they" — who "accuse me of absence" and "mutter death" — are the present in any form: the old regime, the new country, anyone who wants her to give up the city.

Effect: the accusation "being dark in their free city" is the immigrant's experience anywhere. Rumens keeps the poem portable, and that portability is the point — as a reader you may supply the city, but the poem will not.
:::

- **"Sunlight"** ends each stanza — track its growing meaning.
- Memory does not **argue** with fact; it **outlasts** it — quote "cannot break".
- The gap between memory and the real city stays **open**; do not close it.
- The ending makes her **evidence** of the city — identity built from absence.
