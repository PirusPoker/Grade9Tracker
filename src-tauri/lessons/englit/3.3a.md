## What it is

**AO2** — analysing the writer's methods: language, form and structure — is worth as much as AO1 across the whole qualification and more than AO3 by a long way. It is also where most answers leak marks, in one specific way: **feature-spotting**. "Shakespeare uses a metaphor here" is a label; "the metaphor of the 'borrow'd robes' makes Macbeth's new title a costume he has not earned, and the image will return in Act 5 as a 'giant's robe / Upon a dwarfish thief'" is analysis. The difference is the answer to **"so what?"** — and you must answer it every time you name anything.

**Language.** The unit of analysis is the **word**, not the device. Pick a word inside your quotation and press on it:

- what it literally means;
- what it connotes (suggests, associates);
- what other word could have been used, and why this one is better;
- what pattern it belongs to (is it one of several images of blood, of light, of hands?);
- what it does to the reader — the effect;
- and for the top band, how the effect serves the writer's purpose.

Devices are useful only as names for what you have already noticed: metaphor, simile, personification, oxymoron, hyperbole, sibilance, plosives, caesura, enjambment, anaphora. Use the term after the analysis, not instead of it: "…the image of blood dyeing the sea — a hyperbole that says guilt is larger than the world."

**Form.** Form is the kind of thing the text is, and it always carries meaning:

| Form | What to say about it |
|---|---|
| **Sonnet** | Fourteen lines, a turn (volta), a form for love and praise — so what does the poet do with that expectation? (Ozymandias: a sonnet for a ruin, with a broken rhyme scheme.) |
| **Dramatic monologue** | One voice, a silent listener, a speaker who reveals more than they intend — the reader judges (the Duke, Remains). |
| **Blank verse** | Unrhymed iambic pentameter — the rhythm of speech raised to dignity; nobles in Shakespeare; epic in Wordsworth. |
| **Free verse** | No metre or rhyme — the writer chooses every line-break; ask what the breaks and stanza shapes do (Tissue's refusal to close; Poppies' drift). |
| **Ballad / regular rhymed stanzas** | Song-like, memorable, often ironic against dark content (London's hymn-form; Charge's gallop). |
| **Novella in staves / play in three acts in real time** | Prose and drama have form too: Dickens's carol structure; Priestley's unities and act endings. |
| **Soliloquy, aside, stage direction** | Drama's forms — say what each lets the audience know or feel. |

**Structure.** Structure is *what comes when*. The questions to ask: where does it **shift** (tense, tone, speaker, setting, a "but" or "when")? Is there a **volta** (a turn, in a sonnet or elsewhere)? What does the **opening** set up and the **ending** do — resolve, reverse, refuse? Is it **cyclical** (Exposure; An Inspector Calls; the Carol's Stave 1 to Stave 5)? How are **stanzas or acts** shaped — regular, broken, one long paragraph? What is **repeated** and does the repetition change (the six hundred; "sunlight"; "Are there no prisons?")? What is **withheld** or delayed (the murder in six words; Eva never seen)?

**Embed quotations.** Short quotations inside your own sentence, grammatically continuous, so the analysis and the evidence are one thing: *Lady Macbeth's belief that "a little water clears us of this deed" is the line the sleepwalking scene overturns.* Not: *Lady Macbeth says "A little water clears us of this deed." This shows she thinks it is easy.* Three to six words; never a whole line unless every word of it matters; never a quotation that stands alone as a sentence.

**The paragraph that scores.** Point (an argument about the writer's presentation) → embedded evidence → analysis of a word or a structural choice with its effect → the writer's purpose or the reader's response → a link to elsewhere in the text if it strengthens the point. Not PEE as a ritual — a paragraph that keeps asking "so what?" until it reaches purpose.

::: terms
- **AO2**: analysing the language, form and structure writers use to create meanings and effects.
- **Language**: the writer's choice of words and imagery — metaphor, simile, personification, semantic field.
- **Form**: the type of text and its shape — play, novella, sonnet; verse or prose.
- **Structure**: how a text is organised — order, shifts, repetition, openings and endings.
- **Feature-spotting**: naming a technique without explaining its effect, which earns few marks.
- **Subject terminology**: accurate technical terms used to support analysis, not replace it.
:::

::: method
1. **Word, not device**: choose a word inside the quotation and press it — literal, connotation, alternative, pattern, effect, purpose.
2. **Name the form and say what it does** — one sentence per answer at least.
3. **Structure = what comes when**: find the shift and describe the ending in every answer.
4. **Embed**: quotation inside your sentence, three to six words.
5. **"So what?"** until you reach the writer's purpose — that is the top band.
:::

::: example Feature-spotting fixed
Turn "Owen uses personification of the wind" into analysis.

Spotting: "Owen uses personification when he says the winds 'knive us'."

Pressing the word: "knive" — a knife as a verb; the wind is not like a weapon, it *is* one, and it is used on "us" — the collective body of the soldiers. The alternative "cut" would be weaker: a knife is held by someone; the wind has become the enemy soldier.

So what: the personification makes the weather the antagonist so completely that the real enemy barely appears — which is Owen's argument: this war is being lost to cold, not to Germans.

Purpose: written from the front to a public fed on glory, the line replaces the enemy with the elements and the charge with waiting. Now the device is named inside an argument.
:::

::: example Writing about form
Write a form sentence for My Last Duchess.

"Browning casts the poem as a dramatic monologue in rhyming couplets: one uninterrupted voice, a silent envoy, and a rhyme scheme buried under enjambment so that the Duke sounds natural while every line is locked to the next — the form is his own combination of easy manner and total control."

Form named; what it consists of; what it does; how it serves the character. One sentence, and it is worth more than a paragraph of plot.
:::

::: example Writing about structure
Write a structure paragraph for A Christmas Carol.

"Dickens builds the novella as a circle: Stave 5 replays Stave 1 with the same three encounters — the nephew, the charity collector, the clerk — and reverses each response. The structure lets the reader measure Scrooge's change exactly, against the people it failed before, and the 'back-payments' he now offers the collector turn the arithmetic of Stave 1 into restitution. The five staves also move the spirits from personal to social to final — cause, condition, consequence — so that the resolution is earned step by step rather than granted."

Structure as an argument about meaning, with a quotation, and no retelling.
:::

::: example Embedding
Rewrite a clumsy quotation.

Clumsy: "Macbeth says 'Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand?' This shows he feels guilty."

Embedded and analysed: "Macbeth's fear that not 'all great Neptune's ocean' could wash his hand clean measures guilt against the largest thing he can imagine and finds guilt larger; the hyperbole reverses in the next line — the hand will 'incarnadine' the sea — so that the stain spreads outward from him to the world."

The quotation sits inside the sentence; the analysis presses on a word and follows the image; the effect is stated.
:::

::: q
What is the difference between feature-spotting and analysis?
---
Spotting names a device; analysis says what the specific word or choice does — the effect and purpose.
:::

::: q
What is the unit of language analysis?
---
The word — pressed for literal meaning, connotation, alternative, pattern and effect.
:::

::: q
Give three forms and one thing to say about each.
---
Sonnet (a form for praise, with a volta); dramatic monologue (a self-revealing voice, silent listener); free verse (every line-break is a choice).
:::

::: q
What questions define "structure"?
---
What comes when: where it shifts, how it opens and ends, what repeats and changes, what is withheld.
:::

::: q
What is a volta?
---
The turn in a poem — where the argument or feeling changes direction, classically in a sonnet.
:::

::: q
How long should an embedded quotation be?
---
Three to six words, inside your own sentence, grammatically continuous.
:::

::: q
When should you use a technical term?
---
After the analysis, as a name for what you have shown — not instead of it.
:::

::: q
Which two structural features should appear in every answer?
---
The shift (where the text turns) and the ending (what it does).
:::

::: q
What does a top-band paragraph reach that a middle-band one stops short of?
---
The writer's purpose — why the effect matters to what the writer is doing.
:::

::: q
Why is "This shows he feels guilty" not enough?
---
It states content without method — it does not explain how the words create the guilt or what that does to the reader.
:::

::: watch
- **Feature-spotting is the commonest AO2 loss** — always answer "so what?".
- Analyse a **word**, not a line; a **pattern**, not a device.
- **Form** earns a sentence in every answer; **structure** earns the shift and the ending.
- **Embed** quotations — three to six words inside your sentence.
- Terms **follow** analysis; they never replace it.
:::

::: test
How does Priestley use the character of Mr Birling to present his ideas about society? Write about:

- the ideas Priestley explores
- how Priestley presents them through the ways he writes.

(34)
---
Indicative content (reward any valid reading):

- Birling as a 'hard-headed practical man of business' — capitalism personified
- dramatic irony about the Titanic and war discredits his judgement
- his speech on 'look after himself and his own' is interrupted by the doorbell — structure as argument
- his concern for scandal and his knighthood, not Eva's death
- he does not change: at the end he mocks Sheila and Eric
- context: Priestley's socialism, 1912 class attitudes seen from 1945

Mark with the levels (AO1 response and references, AO2 writer's methods, AO3 ideas and context):
- Level 6 (26–30): a critical, exploratory argument driven by a clear idea; precise, well-chosen references; close analysis of methods, with terms used where they help; context explored as part of the argument.
- Level 5 (21–25): thoughtful and developed; apt references woven into sentences; methods examined in detail; context used thoughtfully.
- Level 4 (16–20): clear and sustained; effective references; methods clearly explained with the right terms; clear links to context.
- Level 3 (11–15): explained and relevant; references support the points; some methods explained; some understanding of context.
- Level 2 (6–10): supported comments; some awareness of methods and context.
- Level 1 (1–5): simple comments with some reference to the text.
- Plus AO4 (4 marks) for spelling, punctuation, grammar and vocabulary: 4 for consistent accuracy and a precise, ambitious vocabulary; 2–3 for considerable accuracy; 1 for reasonable accuracy.
