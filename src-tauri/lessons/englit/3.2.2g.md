## What it is

**Storm on the Island** (Seamus Heaney, 1966) is a single-stanza poem in the voice of an islander explaining, at first confidently, how the community is prepared for storms — and then, as the storm arrives, admitting fear. Nature is described in the language of **war**: the sea "exploding", the wind "bombarded", the air a "salvo". The subject is the **power of nature over human confidence** — and, for many readers, something about Northern Ireland, where Heaney was writing as the Troubles began.

**The shape — a turn from confidence to fear.** The poem is nineteen lines of blank verse with no stanza break, but it has a clear structure:

1. **Lines 1–5: preparation.** "We are prepared" — the opening statement, collective and calm. Houses are built "squat", with "rock" and "slate"; there is no hay to lose, no trees to fall. The tone is practical: the island has nothing the storm can take.
2. **Lines 6–13: the turn.** The wind rises; the absence of trees is reconsidered — a tree would at least give "company", a sound to accompany the fear. The sea, which the islander assumed was "company" too, turns: it "exploding comforts" (an oxymoron — the comfort explodes) and spits "like a tame cat / Turned savage".
3. **Lines 14–19: the siege.** The wind is described as an attacking force — a "salvo", "bombarded" — and the islanders "sit tight" while "space is a salvo". The final line admits the fear: "it is a huge nothing that we fear" — the enemy is air; there is nothing to fight.

**Military imagery for nature**: the storm is an artillery attack — "blast", "strafes", "salvo", "bombarded", "exploding". The islanders are besieged. The effect is double: it makes the weather violent and purposeful, and it makes a war out of something no one can fight or win. The last line's "nothing" — the wind is empty air — is what makes the fear "huge": there is no enemy to defeat, only endurance.

**The colloquial opening and the voice.** "We are prepared: we build our houses squat" — a speaker talking to us as if explaining local practice. The conversational asides ("you know what I mean", "as you can see") make the reader a visitor being reassured — and then the reassurance fails, so the reader feels the confidence collapse from inside. The **collective "we"** makes it a community's experience, and a community's fear.

**Blank verse**: unrhymed iambic pentameter, the rhythm of speech, which lets the poem sound like talk — until the metre is disturbed by the enjambment and monosyllables of the storm passages. There is no stanza break because the storm does not pause. The pattern of the argument — assurance, doubt, fear — is carried in one continuous breath.

**The Northern Ireland reading.** The poem's first eight letters spell **Stormont** — the seat of the Northern Ireland parliament. Written as the Troubles were beginning, the poem can be read as a community under a siege it cannot name, fearing "a huge nothing" — an atmosphere of violence rather than a visible enemy. Worth a sentence, not a paragraph: the poem works completely as a poem about weather, and the political reading is an extra layer, not a replacement.

**Context in a sentence.** Heaney grew up on a farm in County Derry; his early poems (*Death of a Naturalist*, 1966, which includes this one) are about rural life and the violence inside nature.

::: terms
- **Single stanza**: the poem is one unbroken block, like the stone cottage it describes.
- **Extended metaphor**: a metaphor developed throughout a poem — the storm as an attack or war.
- **Oxymoron**: two contradictory words together — "exploding comfortably".
- **Colloquial voice**: an everyday, conversational tone ("you know what I mean").
- **Blank verse**: unrhymed iambic pentameter, loosely used.
- **Stormont**: the Northern Ireland parliament building; the title's pun hints at the political troubles.
:::

::: example The turn
How does Heaney present the shift from confidence to fear? Build the paragraph.

Point: Heaney structures the poem as a reassurance that fails.

Evidence: the opening "We are prepared" is flat, collective, certain; but by the middle the sea that seemed like "company" is "exploding comforts", a "tame cat / Turned savage".

Analysis: the oxymoron "exploding comforts" is the turn in two words — the thing that reassured now attacks. The cat simile domesticates the sea and then reverses the domestication: something that lived with you has turned. The final admission — "it is a huge nothing that we fear" — completes the movement from having nothing to lose to fearing nothing itself.

Purpose: Heaney shows that preparation is not the same as safety; the community's confidence was built on an illusion of control.
:::

::: example War imagery
Analyse Heaney's use of military language for the storm.

The wind "strafes", the gusts are a "salvo", the house is "bombarded", the spray "exploding". Every verb is artillery; the islanders are a garrison under fire.

The effect is to give the weather intention — it attacks, it aims. But the comparison also exposes its own limit: you cannot fight wind. "We just sit tight" is what a garrison does when it cannot fire back.

That is why the final line matters: the enemy is "a huge nothing". Heaney builds a war and then removes the opponent, so the fear is not of destruction but of powerlessness — the war metaphor collapses into pure endurance.
:::

::: example Voice and form
Comment on the conversational voice and the single stanza.

The speaker talks as if to a visitor — "you know what I mean", "as you can see" — a tone of local expertise, with the collective "we" of a community that has done this before.

The blank verse carries that talk: unrhymed pentameter is as close as verse comes to speech. But there is no stanza break, so when the storm arrives it arrives inside the same breath, and the enjambments and monosyllables — "sit tight", "huge nothing" — roughen the rhythm.

Effect: the reader is being reassured by someone who stops being sure. Heaney lets the voice keep its manner while its content changes, which is more unsettling than panic would be — calm people admitting fear.
:::

::: example Stormont
How far should the Northern Ireland reading be used?

The title's first eight letters spell Stormont, the Northern Irish parliament, and the poem was published in 1966 as sectarian violence was beginning. On that reading, the island is a community bracing for a violence that has no single face — "a huge nothing" — and the war vocabulary is not metaphor but premonition.

The reading adds a layer: "We are prepared" becomes a community's false confidence before the Troubles; "sit tight" the only option.

But the poem is complete without it: the storm is real, the island is real, and the psychology of confidence collapsing into fear needs no politics. Use Stormont in one sentence as a possible dimension — the examiner rewards a reading held lightly, not a theory imposed.
:::
