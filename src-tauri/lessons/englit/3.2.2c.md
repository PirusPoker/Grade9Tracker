## What it is

**Extract from The Prelude** (William Wordsworth, written 1799, published 1850) — the "stealing the boat" episode from Book 1 of Wordsworth's fourteen-book autobiographical poem about "the growth of a poet's mind". A boy takes a boat out on a lake at night, confident and alone; as he rows, a mountain peak appears to rise up behind the ridge and follow him; he turns back, and the memory troubles him for days. The subject is **nature's power over the human mind** — and the shift from confidence to fear as the speaker discovers something bigger than himself.

**It is an extract** — say so. The 44 lines are a fragment of a poem about how experiences like this one made Wordsworth the poet he became; the boy's "spots of time" are the raw material of the adult's imagination. That context is worth a mark and explains the retrospective voice.

**The three movements:**

| Movement | Lines | What happens | Quotation and analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Confidence** | 1–20 | The boy unties a boat "led by her" (nature, or the boat, as a guide); rows out with "troubled pleasure"; fixes his eye on a ridge and the stars; the boat is "an elfin pinnace", he rows "lustily", proud, "like a swan". | "One summer evening (led by her) I found / A little boat" — the parenthesis makes nature the instigator. "It was an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure" — the guilt is present from the start, small. "Proud of his skill" — the boy's power is the subject of this section; the nature imagery is gentle: "small circles glittering idly in the moon". |
| **The peak** | 21–29 | "When, from behind that craggy steep till then / The horizon's bound, a huge peak, black and huge, / As if with voluntary power instinct, / Upreared its head." The mountain seems to rise as the boat moves (a trick of perspective) and to pursue him. | "huge … black and huge" — the repetition is the boy's inability to describe it; "voluntary power instinct" — the peak seems alive and willed; "Upreared its head" — a creature. "I struck and struck again" — the rhythm of panic. "With trembling oars I turned" — the trembling has moved from the water to him. |
| **The aftermath** | 30–44 | He returns "with grave / And serious mood"; for "many days" his brain "Worked with a dim and undetermined sense / Of unknown modes of being"; "huge and mighty forms, that do not live / Like living men, moved slowly through the mind". | "unknown modes of being" — the boy has met something outside the human; "No familiar shapes … no pleasant images of trees" — nature's gentle face is gone; "huge and mighty forms" haunt him. The lesson is not stated; it is felt. |

**Form and voice.** **Blank verse** — unrhymed iambic pentameter — the metre of Milton and of epic, used for a boy in a boat: the ordinary raised to the epic, and the mind treated as a landscape. The **first-person retrospective** voice is the adult remembering the child — "I" throughout, but with the adult's vocabulary ("voluntary power instinct") describing what the boy could only feel. The **single long verse paragraph** with no stanza breaks matches the single continuous experience; enjambment carries the rowing forward and then the panic. The **turn** is at "When" — the sentence changes direction as the peak appears.

**Nature's power.** For Wordsworth, nature is a teacher, and its lessons include fear. The boy's pride ("proud of his skill") is corrected not by punishment but by scale: the mountain does nothing — it only rises — and that is enough to reorder his sense of his place. The "Sublime" (the Romantic idea of nature as vast, terrifying and awe-inspiring) is the frame: beauty pleases; the sublime overwhelms, and the overwhelming is what makes a poet.

**Context in a sentence.** Wordsworth, born 1770 in the Lake District, wrote *The Prelude* to trace how his childhood among the mountains made him; Romantic poetry (with Coleridge, Shelley, Keats) turned from reason and society to feeling, nature and the individual mind.

4. **The peak does nothing**: nature's power is scale and presence, not violence — and the effect is on the mind.

::: example The shift
How does Wordsworth present the change from confidence to fear? Build the paragraph.

Point: Wordsworth structures the extract as a single sentence of confidence broken by a single word.

Evidence: before — "lustily / I dipped my oars", "proud of his skill", the boat "like a swan"; then "When, from behind that craggy steep … a huge peak, black and huge … Upreared its head."

Analysis: "When" is the turn; the syntax stops rowing forward and reverses. The repetition "huge … black and huge" is a boy running out of words, and "Upreared its head" makes the mountain an animal rising. His response is rhythm: "I struck and struck again" — the metre becomes panic.

Purpose: nature has not attacked him; it has simply appeared at its true size, and the boy's pride cannot survive the comparison. Wordsworth shows the sublime correcting the self.
:::

::: example The aftermath
Explain the effect of the experience on the speaker's mind.

The boy rows home "with grave / And serious mood" — the "troubled pleasure" has become trouble only.

For days afterwards "my brain / Worked with a dim and undetermined sense / Of unknown modes of being": the vagueness is exact — he has encountered something he has no category for. What is lost is nature's familiar face — "no pleasant images of trees, / Of sea or sky, no colours of green fields" — replaced by "huge and mighty forms, that do not live / Like living men".

Wordsworth's point is that this is how a poet's mind is made: not by pretty scenes but by fear, by a sense of powers beyond the human. The lesson is never stated; the adult poet leaves the child's confusion intact because the confusion was the education.
:::

::: example Form
Comment on the blank verse and the voice.

Blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter — is the metre of *Paradise Lost*; Wordsworth borrows the epic's dignity for a boy in a stolen boat. The claim is that the growth of a mind is as large a subject as the fall of man.

The voice is retrospective first person: the adult's words ("voluntary power instinct", "undetermined sense") describe what the child only felt. So the reader gets both — the fear as it was, and the understanding of what it meant.

There are no stanzas: one continuous verse paragraph, enjambed, so the rowing and the panic run without a pause, and the only structural break is the pivot on "When". The form is the mind moving.
:::

::: example Nature's power
How is nature presented as powerful?

At first nature is gentle and complicit — the boat is found "led by her", the lake shows "small circles glittering idly in the moon". The boy is at ease in it because he thinks he is its master: "proud of his skill".

The peak reverses this without violence. It is "As if with voluntary power instinct" — power that is willed, alive — and it "strode after me": nature is personified as a pursuer, though in fact it has not moved at all; the boy has.

Effect: Wordsworth's nature does not need to act to overpower; it only needs to be seen at its true scale. The real power is over the mind — the "huge and mighty forms" that move through it for days. This is the Romantic sublime: awe that is close to terror, and that reorders the self.
:::
