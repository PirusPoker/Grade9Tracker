## What it is

Context (AO3) is worth the fewest marks of the three main objectives and is the easiest to waste. It scores only when it **explains the writing**: a fact about 1606 that tells the examiner why Shakespeare wrote a line the way he did. Language and stagecraft (AO2) are worth far more — so this lesson gives you the context in usable pieces, and then the patterns of imagery and staging that carry most of the marks.

**Jacobean context — one sentence each, ready to attach to a quotation:**

| Fact | Attach it to |
|---|---|
| **James I** came to the English throne in 1603, a Scottish king who claimed descent from **Banquo**. The play flatters him: Banquo is honourable and his line "shall be kings" — the show of eight kings in 4.1 is James's ancestry. | Banquo's virtue; the apparitions; why Banquo is not the historical accomplice he was in Holinshed's chronicle. |
| **The Gunpowder Plot** (November 1605): Catholic conspirators tried to blow up James and Parliament. Their trial made "equivocation" — lying by technical truth — notorious. | The Porter's "equivocator" (2.3); the witches' equivocal prophecies in Act 4; the theme of treachery punished. |
| **Witchcraft**: James wrote *Daemonologie* (1597) and took part in witch trials; witchcraft was a capital crime. | The witches' real menace; Lady Macbeth's summoning of "spirits"; the audience's fear. |
| **Divine right and the Great Chain of Being**: the king is God's deputy; regicide is sacrilege; disorder above causes disorder below. | Duncan's "angels, trumpet-tongued"; 2.4's unnatural night; Malcolm's restoration. |
| **Gender**: a wife should be obedient and gentle; ambition and violence were masculine virtues. | "unsex me here"; "When you durst do it, then you were a man". |

The rule: **name the specific thing, then the line, then what the fact explains about the line.** "James I claimed descent from Banquo, which is why Shakespeare changes Holinshed's Banquo from accomplice to innocent — 'I lose none / In seeking to augment it'."

**Imagery patterns — the play's own vocabulary:**

- **Blood**: from honour ("What bloody man is that?" — the wounded Captain) to guilt ("Neptune's ocean") to habit ("in blood / Stepp'd in so far"). Track the shift.
- **Darkness and light**: "Stars, hide your fires"; "Come, thick night"; Duncan's murder at night; the "travelling lamp" strangled; Lady Macbeth who "has light by her continually" in 5.1 — she once called for darkness and now cannot bear it. Light is grace and conscience; dark is what the Macbeths ask for.
- **Clothing**: "Why do you dress me in borrow'd robes?"; "like a giant's robe / Upon a dwarfish thief" (Angus, 5.2). The crown is a costume that does not fit — kingship is something Macbeth wears, not is.
- **Disease and healing**: Scotland's "disease"; the Doctor who cannot "minister to a mind diseased"; Edward's healing touch; Malcolm's army as "medicine". Tyranny is sickness; the rightful king is the cure.
- **Sleep**, **hands**, **children/babies** (the "naked new-born babe", Lady Macbeth's infant, Macduff's "pretty chickens", Banquo's line — the future Macbeth cannot control) and **planting/growth** (Duncan's "I have begun to plant thee").

**Stagecraft — what the play does because it is a play:**

- **Soliloquy and aside**: the audience shares Macbeth's mind and no one else's (Lady Macbeth's speeches are addressed to spirits, then to him; her private mind we see only in 5.1, observed by others). Shakespeare rations intimacy.
- **Dramatic irony**: Duncan on Cawdor — "There's no art / To find the mind's construction in the face" — as Macbeth enters; Duncan's "This castle hath a pleasant seat". The audience knows; the victim does not.
- **The banquet scene (3.4)**: the one moment guilt goes public. The ghost is visible to Macbeth (and the audience) but to no one on stage — so the audience shares his hallucination and watches the court watch him. Lady Macbeth's improvisation ("my lord is often thus") and collapse ("Stand not upon the order of your going") show her losing control of the room.
- **Offstage violence**: Duncan's murder, Lady Macbeth's death — Shakespeare keeps the worst out of sight so the audience imagines it, and so the focus stays on consequence.
- **The Porter**: comic prose straight after the murder — release of tension, and a hell-gate that names the castle for what it is.

**Verse and prose.** Nobles speak **blank verse** (unrhymed iambic pentameter). The **witches** speak in trochaic tetrameter with rhyme — "Double, double toil and trouble" — a chant, unnatural against the human rhythm. **Prose** marks a drop in status or control: the Porter, the murderers, and Lady Macbeth in 5.1 — her fall from verse to prose is her breakdown in form. Watch for **broken lines**: "Macbeth shall sleep no more" shared between speakers, or the short line that leaves silence — the rhythm breaks where the mind does.

::: terms
- **AO3 (context)**: the relationship between a text and the context in which it was written and received.
- **Jacobean**: the period of James I's reign (1603–1625), when Macbeth was written.
- **James I**: king of England from 1603, who claimed descent from Banquo and wrote Daemonologie, a book on witchcraft.
- **Gunpowder Plot (1605)**: a failed Catholic plot to kill James I, which made treason and regicide topical.
- **Blank verse**: unrhymed iambic pentameter — used by high-status characters.
- **Iambic pentameter**: a line of ten syllables in five stressed beats, "da-DUM" × 5.
- **Trochaic tetrameter**: a rhythm of four "DUM-da" beats, used for the witches' chants ("Double, double toil and trouble").
- **Stagecraft**: how the play works on stage — entrances, props, lighting, sound, the ghost's appearance.
:::

::: method
1. **Context goes in a clause, not a paragraph**: "Writing for James I, who traced his line to Banquo, Shakespeare…" and then the quotation.
2. **Pick one imagery pattern per paragraph** and show it *moving* — the same image in two places with a changed meaning.
3. **Say what the stagecraft does to the audience**: "the soliloquy makes us complicit", "the dramatic irony makes Duncan's trust painful".
4. **Comment on form only where it changes**: prose for the Porter and for Lady Macbeth's collapse; rhyme for the witches; a broken line at a moment of shock.
5. **Ration terms**: one accurate term, explained, beats five names.
:::

::: example Context that explains
Use the Gunpowder Plot in a sentence that earns a mark.

Weak: "The Gunpowder Plot happened in 1605 and people were scared of treason." (History with no text.)

Better: "Writing months after the Gunpowder Plot trials, Shakespeare has the Porter admit an 'equivocator' to hell — the word the prosecutors used for the plotters' technique of lying by half-truth."

Best: add what it explains — "…which prepares the audience for the witches' own equivocation in Act 4: 'none of woman born' is a technically true statement built to deceive. The play punishes equivocators, human and supernatural, as James's court had just punished them."
:::

::: example An imagery pattern
Analyse the clothing imagery. Build the paragraph.

Point: Shakespeare uses clothing to present Macbeth's kingship as a costume that never fits.

Early: "Why do you dress me in borrow'd robes?" — on hearing he is Cawdor. The title is borrowed; he did not earn it. Banquo: "New honours come upon him, / Like our strange garments, cleave not to their mould / But with the aid of use."

Late: Angus, 5.2 — "now does he feel his title / Hang loose about him, like a giant's robe / Upon a dwarfish thief." The robe has become a giant's; the wearer has shrunk to a thief.

Analysis: Shakespeare's point is that legitimacy cannot be put on. Duncan's kingship was a body; Macbeth's is a garment. The image also carries the audience's Jacobean sense that rank was God-given — a robe of state on the wrong man is a visible lie.
:::

::: example Stagecraft in the banquet
How does Shakespeare use staging in Act 3 Scene 4 to present Macbeth's guilt?

The ghost enters and sits in Macbeth's place — a stage direction that puts the murdered man in the king's seat. Only Macbeth sees it; the audience sees it too. Shakespeare makes us share the hallucination while the guests share Lady Macbeth's view of a man talking to an empty stool.

The double perspective is the point: guilt is private and public at once. Macbeth's "Thou canst not say I did it" is a confession the court cannot decode but the audience can.

Lady Macbeth's stagecraft fails inside the scene — "my lord is often thus" buys time, "Are you a man?" no longer works, and "Stand not upon the order of your going" dissolves the ceremony she was trying to hold together. Shakespeare shows the court, the marriage and the king's composure breaking in a single scene, using nothing but a chair and a ghost.
:::

::: example Verse and prose
Comment on the form of Lady Macbeth's speech in Act 5 Scene 1.

Every earlier speech she makes is in blank verse — controlled, iambic, complete: "Leave all the rest to me." In 5.1 she speaks prose: "Out, damned spot! out, I say! One; two: why, then 'tis time to do't."

The prose is fragmented — commands, numbers, questions — and it revisits every murder out of order, including Lady Macduff's, which she did not plan. The loss of metre is the loss of the mind that made the metre.

Shakespeare also frames her: the Doctor and Gentlewoman watch and comment, so the audience sees her as a case, not a queen. The formal collapse is the psychological collapse — and it is the only time the audience is given her private mind, which arrives only when there is nothing left to hide.
:::

::: q
Why does Shakespeare make Banquo innocent when the historical Banquo helped kill Duncan?
---
James I claimed descent from Banquo; presenting him as honourable flatters the king who was Shakespeare's patron.
:::

::: q
What is equivocation, and where does it appear in the play?
---
Lying by technical truth (the Gunpowder plotters' defence); the Porter's "equivocator" and the witches' misleading prophecies.
:::

::: q
Give an example of dramatic irony from Act 1.
---
Duncan says "There's no art / To find the mind's construction in the face" about Cawdor — as Macbeth, the next traitor, enters.
:::

::: q
Quote a clothing image from late in the play and explain it.
---
"like a giant's robe / Upon a dwarfish thief" (Angus) — the kingship is too big for the man who stole it.
:::

::: q
What does light come to mean for Lady Macbeth by Act 5?
---
She who called for "thick night" now "has light by her continually" — light is the conscience she cannot escape.
:::

::: q
What metre do the witches use, and why?
---
Trochaic tetrameter with rhyme ("Double, double toil and trouble") — a chant that sounds unnatural beside the nobles' blank verse.
:::

::: q
Name two characters who speak prose and say what prose signals.
---
The Porter and Lady Macbeth in 5.1 (also the murderers); prose signals low status or loss of control.
:::

::: q
Why does Shakespeare keep Duncan's murder offstage?
---
So the audience imagines it and the focus stays on the Macbeths' reactions and the consequences.
:::

::: q
What does the ghost sitting in Macbeth's chair signify?
---
The murdered man occupies the king's place — guilt takes the seat Macbeth killed for.
:::

::: q
How should context appear in an answer?
---
As a clause attached to a quotation that explains why the line is written as it is — never as a separate history paragraph.
:::

::: watch
- Context is **AO3 — the fewest marks**. A history paragraph with no quotation scores **nothing**.
- Formula: **specific fact → line → what the fact explains**.
- Imagery answers must show the image **changing** across the play, not just appearing.
- Stagecraft = what it does to the **audience**: complicity, irony, the double view in the banquet.
- Comment on verse/prose only **where the form changes** — that is where the meaning is.
:::

Why does Shakespeare give Lady Macbeth prose in Act 5 but verse everywhere else? (2)

::: test
What was the Gunpowder Plot, and how does it connect to Macbeth? (3)
---
A Catholic plot in 1605 to blow up Parliament and kill King James I (1). The play, first performed around 1606, shows regicide leading to chaos and punishment (1), and the Porter's 'equivocator' recalls the trial of the plotters, whose defence of lying under oath was called equivocation (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the dramatic effect of the knocking at the gate at the end of Act 2 Scene 2 and in the Porter scene. (2)
---
It raises tension — the murderers fear discovery at once (1). The Porter imagines himself the keeper of hell's gate, so the castle becomes hell; the comedy briefly releases tension while keeping the dark theme (1).
:::

::: test
What is iambic pentameter, and what do shared or broken lines show in Act 2 Scene 2? (2)
---
A line of ten syllables in five unstressed-stressed pairs, used by the nobles (1). In Act 2 Scene 2 the lines are split between the Macbeths in short, frantic questions, showing panic and fear (1).
:::
