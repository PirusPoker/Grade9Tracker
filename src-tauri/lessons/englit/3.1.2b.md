## What it is

Scrooge is the whole book: an **allegory** of the wealthy reader Dickens wanted to convert, taken through a change that must be **gradual** and **evidenced** if it is to convince. The commonest failing in Scrooge answers is to jump from mean to generous. The marks are in the steps between.

**Stave 1 — making him repellent.** Dickens front-loads the dislike so the change has distance to travel:

- Physical description as moral description: "a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner!" — six participles of taking; "The cold within him froze his old features, nipped his pointed nose, shrivelled his cheek" — the cold is *within* him and works outward; "solitary as an oyster" — shut, hard, and (the reader will discover) with something worth finding inside.
- Speech as creed: "Bah! Humbug!"; to Fred, "What right have you to be merry? … You're poor enough"; to the collectors, "Are there no prisons?" and "If they would rather die … they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population" — the Malthusian argument in its cruellest form; to Bob, Christmas Day is "a poor excuse for picking a man's pocket every twenty-fifth of December".
- The narrator's mockery: "Oh! But he was a tight-fisted hand at the grindstone, Scrooge!" — the reader is invited to laugh at him before being asked to pity him.

**The change, stave by stave — evidence for each step:**

| Stave | Step | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Fear, not feeling | Marley's ghost: "Mercy! Dreadful apparition, why do you trouble me?" — he is frightened, and tries to reason it away ("more of gravy than of grave"). No change yet, but the armour is dented. |
| **2** | Memory reopens feeling | The schoolroom: "his lip was trembling"; he "sobbed". At Fezziwig's, he defends his old master — "he has the power to render us happy or unhappy" — and immediately thinks of Bob: "I should like to be able to say a word or two to my clerk just now." Belle: "Spirit! remove me from this place … I cannot bear it!" |
| **3** | Concern for others | He asks about Tiny Tim — "tell me if Tiny Tim will live" — and hears his own words returned: "If he be like to die, he had better do it, and decrease the surplus population." He "hung his head … and was overcome with penitence and grief." At Fred's he wants to join the game. Ignorance and Want: "Have they no refuge or resource?" — "Are there no prisons?" comes back a second time. |
| **4** | Resolution under fear | He tries to find himself in the future and cannot; the dead man's things are sold; "the case of this unhappy man might be my own." At the grave: "I am not the man I was. I will not be the man I must have been but for this intercourse." He vows to "honour Christmas in my heart". |
| **5** | Action | "I'm quite a baby. Never mind. I don't care. I'd rather be a baby." The turkey, the collectors, Fred's house, Bob's raise, "a second father" to Tiny Tim. The narrator: "He became as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew." |

**Is the transformation convincing?** Arguments that it is: the steps are laid out and each has a cause; the childhood scenes give the reader a Scrooge who was once loving, so the change is a return, not a conversion from nothing; Belle's lost future makes the loneliness a choice he can see; and Dickens makes the fear real. Arguments that it is too sudden: it takes one night; Stave 5's Scrooge is almost hysterical with joy; the world's response ("Some people laughed") is easy. The strongest answers say the *book's* purpose settles it: Dickens needs the reader to believe change is possible in a night, because the book is asking the reader to change by Boxing Day.

**Scrooge as allegory.** He is not a psychological study; he is the Victorian man of business — the reader Dickens was writing for — and his conversion is the model. The narrator's last words are instructions: "May that be truly said of us, and all of us!" The "us" is the point.

::: terms
- **Protagonist**: the main character — Scrooge.
- **Redemption**: being saved from sin or error; Scrooge's transformation.
- **Transformation**: a complete change in character.
- **Miser**: a person who hoards money and spends as little as possible.
- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as" — "solitary as an oyster".
- **Characterisation**: the methods a writer uses to present a character — description, speech, actions, others' reactions.
:::

::: method
1. **Start with Stave 1 in full**: quote the description, the speech and the narrator's tone so the starting point is measured.
2. **One step per stave**, each with a quotation and the cause of the step (memory, others' suffering, fear of his own death).
3. **Use the returned words** — "Are there no prisons?", "surplus population" — they are Dickens's own device for showing Scrooge hearing himself.
4. **Argue "convincing"**: both sides, then the purpose — Dickens needs the reader to believe it.
5. **End on allegory**: Scrooge is the reader; the last line's "all of us" is the target.
:::

::: example The early Scrooge
How does Dickens make Scrooge repellent in Stave 1? Build the paragraph.

Point: Dickens builds the early Scrooge out of cold — a man whose meanness is a physical temperature.

Evidence: "The cold within him froze his old features, nipped his pointed nose, shrivelled his cheek … He carried his own low temperature always about with him."

Analysis: the cold is internal and causal — it works from the inside out, so his appearance is his character made visible. "Carried … about with him" makes his coldness portable: he chills every room he enters. Dickens then compounds it with the list — "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous" — six verbs of taking with no verb of giving.

Purpose: the reader must dislike Scrooge completely so that the conversion has something to overcome — and the narrator's exclamation marks invite us to enjoy disliking him.
:::

::: example A step in the change
Show the Stave 2 step with cause and evidence.

Cause: the Ghost of Christmas Past shows Scrooge his younger self "alone" in the schoolroom at Christmas, reading.

Evidence: "Scrooge sat down upon a form, and wept to see his poor forgotten self as he used to be" — and, moments later, "I wish … but it's too late now … There was a boy singing a Christmas Carol at my door last night. I should like to have given him something: that's all."

Analysis: the tears are the first feeling; the boy at the door is the first regret about an action. Dickens makes memory the mechanism — Scrooge cannot pity others until he pities himself as a child. And "that's all" is exactly what Scrooge would say: the change is real but still embarrassed, which is what makes it believable.
:::

::: example The returned words
Explain how Dickens uses Scrooge's own words against him in Stave 3.

In Stave 1 Scrooge tells the collectors that the poor who would rather die than go to the workhouse "had better do it, and decrease the surplus population."

In Stave 3 he asks whether Tiny Tim will live, and the Spirit answers with his own sentence: "If he be like to die, he had better do it, and decrease the surplus population." Scrooge "hung his head to hear his own words quoted by the Spirit, and was overcome with penitence and grief."

The Spirit does it again for Ignorance and Want: "Are there no prisons? … Are there no workhouses?" Dickens's method is to make the abstraction concrete — "surplus population" now has a crutch and a name — and to make Scrooge the audience of himself. The reader, who laughed at those lines in Stave 1, is caught in the same trap.
:::

::: example Convincing or too sudden?
Argue whether the transformation convinces.

Against: it happens in a single night, and Stave 5's Scrooge — "I'm quite a baby" — is so giddy that some readers find it comic rather than moral.

For: Dickens has prepared every step. The childhood scenes establish that Scrooge was once affectionate; the Fezziwig scene shows him admiring generosity; the Cratchit scene shows him caring about a stranger; the grave shows him what indifference costs. The change is a return to a self we have been shown, not a conversion from nothing.

Resolution: the question is really about purpose. Dickens is asking his readers to change by the time they finish the book, so he must show that a change of heart can be fast and complete. The speed is the argument — "It's all right, it's all true, it all happened" — and the narrator vouches for it lasting: "he knew how to keep Christmas well."
:::

::: q
Quote the list of adjectives Dickens uses for Scrooge in Stave 1.
---
"a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner!"
:::

::: q
What does "solitary as an oyster" suggest beyond loneliness?
---
That Scrooge is shut and hard — but that there may be something worth finding inside, as the later staves prove.
:::

::: q
What does Scrooge say about the poor who would rather die than enter a workhouse?
---
"they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population."
:::

::: q
What is the first physical sign of feeling in Scrooge, and where?
---
Weeping at his younger self alone at school in Stave 2 — "his lip was trembling".
:::

::: q
What does Scrooge say after watching Fezziwig that shows the lesson landing?
---
"I should like to be able to say a word or two to my clerk just now."
:::

::: q
How does the Ghost of Christmas Present make Scrooge feel "penitence and grief"?
---
By quoting his own words about the surplus population back to him when he asks whether Tiny Tim will live.
:::

::: q
Quote Scrooge's vow at the grave in Stave 4.
---
"I am not the man I was. I will not be the man I must have been but for this intercourse."
:::

::: q
Give three actions from Stave 5 that prove the change.
---
Sending the prize turkey to the Cratchits; a large donation to the collector; going to Fred's dinner; raising Bob's salary (any three).
:::

::: q
Why is Scrooge better understood as an allegory than a realistic character?
---
He stands for the wealthy Victorian reader whose conversion Dickens wants — "May that be truly said of us, and all of us!"
:::

::: q
What single mistake costs most marks in a Scrooge answer?
---
Jumping from mean to generous without evidencing the gradual steps in between.
:::

::: watch
- The change is **gradual**: one step per stave, each with a quotation and a cause.
- **Measure Stave 1 first** — the conversion is only as impressive as the starting point.
- Use the **returned words** ("surplus population", "Are there no prisons?") — Dickens's own device.
- "Convincing?" needs **both sides and the purpose** — Dickens needs the reader to believe in fast change.
- Scrooge is **an allegory of the reader**: say so, and quote "all of us".
:::

::: test
What does 'more of gravy than of grave' show about Scrooge in Stave 1? (2)
---
He tries to joke away Marley's ghost as indigestion (1); the armour of reason still holds, but the pun shows fear underneath (1).
:::

::: test
How does Dickens use Belle to explain Scrooge's coldness? (2)
---
She releases him because 'another idol has displaced me' — 'a golden one' (1); money replaced love — the cause of the man we met in Stave 1 (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the effect of Scrooge hearing his own words about the 'surplus population' from the Ghost of Christmas Present. (2)
---
He is made to hear how his creed sounds applied to Tiny Tim (1); he 'hung his head' — the first time his own philosophy shames him (1).
:::

::: test
Quote Scrooge's reaction in Stave 5 that shows the change is joyful, not dutiful. (2)
---
'I am as light as a feather, I am as happy as an angel' (1); the similes of weightlessness reverse the heavy chain and the cold of Stave 1 (1).
:::

::: test
Why does Scrooge laugh at himself in Stave 5? (2)
---
'I don't know what to do! … I'm quite a baby' — he is reborn, starting again (1); laughter replaces the 'Humbug' — his voice has changed as well as his actions (1).
:::

::: test
What does 'I will not be the man I must have been but for this intercourse' promise? (2)
---
He rejects the future the spirit showed (1); 'must have been' admits the death he saw was the natural end of his old self (1).
:::

::: test
Give one argument that Scrooge's change is convincing and one that it is not. (2)
---
Convincing: it is shown in steps across four staves, each with a cause (1); not: it happens in one night and the narrator asserts it — but Dickens needs the reader to believe fast change is possible (1).
:::
