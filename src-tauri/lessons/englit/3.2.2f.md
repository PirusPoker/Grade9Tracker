## What it is

**Exposure** (Wilfred Owen, written 1917–18) is a poem about a war in which the enemy is the **weather**. Owen's soldiers lie in a trench in the winter of 1917 waiting for an attack that never comes; the wind, snow and cold do what the Germans do not. The refrain — "But nothing happens" — is the point: the boredom, the cold and the waiting are the war, and Owen writes against every poem that made war glorious.

**The structure.** Eight five-line stanzas, each ending in a short line — half a line, often the refrain. The poem is **circular**: it opens and ends in the same cold, with "But nothing happens" as the last words. Nothing changes because nothing happens — the form refuses the reader the progress that a narrative of battle would give.

| Stanza | Quotation | Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Our brains ache, in the merciless iced east winds that knive us … / Wearied we keep awake because the night is silent … / But nothing happens." | "Knive" — the wind is a weapon; "merciless" personifies it as the enemy. "Our" — collective voice; the whole army is the speaker. The first line half-echoes Keats's "My heart aches" — Romantic beauty turned to pain. |
| 2 | "Watching, we hear the mad gusts tugging on the wire, / Like twitching agonies of men among its brambles." | The wire "twitches" like dying men — the landscape is already a corpse. "Northward, incessantly, the flickering gunnery rumbles, / Far off, like a dull rumour of some other war. / What are we doing here?" — the real battle is elsewhere and unreal; the question has no answer. |
| 3 | "The poignant misery of dawn begins to grow … / Dawn massing in the east her melancholy army / Attacks once more in ranks on shivering ranks of grey" | Dawn — the Romantic symbol of hope — is an army; the clouds are "ranks", grey like uniforms. The weather has taken the enemy's place. "But nothing happens." |
| 4 | "Sudden successive flights of bullets streak the silence. / Less deadly than the air that shudders black with snow" | The only bullets in the poem — and they are "less deadly" than the snow. "Sidelong flowing flakes that flock, pause, and renew" — sibilance; the snow has a will. |
| 5 | "Pale flakes with fingering stealth come feeling for our faces — / We cringe in holes, back on forgotten dreams, and stare, snow-dazed, / Deep into grassier ditches. So we drowse, sun-dozed, / Littered with blossoms trickling where the blackbird fusses. / Is it that we are dying?" | The snow "fingers" them like a lover or a thief; the men retreat into memory — "grassier ditches", "blossoms". The hallucination of warmth is hypothermia. The question is asked plainly. |
| 6 | "Slowly our ghosts drag home: glimpsing the sunk fires, glozed / With crusted dark-red jewels; crickets jingle there; / For hours the innocent mice rejoice: the house is theirs; / Shutters and doors, all closed: on us the doors are closed, — / We turn back to our dying." | Home is imagined and locked — "on us the doors are closed": the men are ghosts already; England has shut them out. "We turn back to our dying" — the refrain's grammar: dying is the job. |
| 7 | "Since we believe not otherwise can kind fires burn; / Nor ever suns smile true on child, or field, or fruit … / Therefore, not loath, we lie out here; therefore were born, / For love of God seems dying." | The stanza of belief: they suffer so that home can be warm — the justification, offered and doubted. "For love of God seems dying" — either God's love is dying, or they die for love of God. Owen keeps both. |
| 8 | "Tonight, this frost will fasten on this mud and us, / Shrivelling many hands, puckering foreheads crisp. / The burying-party, picks and shovels in shaking grasp, / Pause over half-known faces. All their eyes are ice, / But nothing happens." | The dead described from outside — "half-known faces" — and the living burying them are shaking with cold too. "All their eyes are ice": the weather has won. Then the refrain, unchanged. |

**Sound.** **Pararhyme** (half-rhyme) throughout — knive us / nervous, silent / salient, wire / war, snow / renew, faces / fusses — consonants match, vowels do not: the rhymes almost close and never do, which unsettles the ear the way the waiting unsettles the men. Owen's pararhyme is his signature and his argument — no resolution. Heavy **sibilance** and long lines with **caesurae** slow the poem to the pace of waiting. The **short last line** of each stanza drops away — "But nothing happens", "What are we doing here?", "Is it that we are dying?" — questions and refrains that the next stanza cannot answer.

**Owen's purpose.** He wrote from the front — he was killed a week before the Armistice in November 1918 — to tell "the pity of War" to a public fed on Tennyson and recruiting posters. *Exposure* is the anti-Charge: no enemy, no action, no glory, and a title that means both being exposed to the weather and exposing the truth. The soldiers' passivity is the point; hunting for action in the poem is the mistake of readers who expect war to look like a charge.

::: method
1. **Name the antagonist**: the weather, personified as the army — quote "merciless iced east winds that knive us" and "Dawn massing … her melancholy army".
2. **Refrain and circular structure**: "But nothing happens" begins and ends — say why nothing happening *is* the subject.
3. **Pararhyme**: give a pair, explain the effect (almost-rhyme, no resolution).
4. **The home stanza**: "on us the doors are closed" — the men shut out of the country they die for.
5. **Owen's purpose** against glory — one sentence, and compare the Charge if asked.
:::

::: example The weather as enemy
How does Owen present the weather? Build the paragraph.

Point: Owen makes the weather the enemy so completely that the real enemy barely appears.

Evidence: "the merciless iced east winds that knive us"; "Dawn massing in the east her melancholy army / Attacks once more in ranks on shivering ranks of grey."

Analysis: "knive" turns wind into a blade, and "merciless" gives it a soldier's cruelty. Dawn — hope's traditional image — is reversed into an attacking army; the grey clouds are "ranks", the colour of German uniforms. The only bullets in the poem are "less deadly than the air".

Purpose: Owen's soldiers are not fighting; they are being killed by exposure while they wait. That inversion is his answer to the war poetry that promised action and glory.
:::

::: example The refrain
Explain what "But nothing happens" does across the poem.

The line ends stanzas 1, 3, 4 and 8, and the poem's last words are the same as its first stanza's — the structure is a circle.

Each time it lands after a description of suffering — the wind, the dawn attack, the snow — the "But" is almost sarcastic: all this, and still nothing. The men want something to happen, even an attack, because waiting in the cold is worse.

In the final stanza it follows the burial party and "All their eyes are ice": men have died, and still "nothing happens" — death by exposure does not count as an event. Owen's structure refuses the reader a climax because the war refused the soldiers one; the circle is the futility.
:::

::: example Pararhyme
Analyse Owen's use of half-rhyme.

"knive us / nervous", "silent / salient", "wire / war", "faces / fusses": the consonants match and the vowels miss, so each rhyme nearly closes and does not.

The effect is unease — the ear waits for the full rhyme as the men wait for the attack, and neither comes. It also flattens the tone: full rhyme would make the lines sing, and Owen will not let this material sing.

Pararhyme is Owen's signature; here it is also his argument. Nothing resolves — not the rhyme, not the night, not the war. The technique does what the refrain says.
:::

::: example Home
How does Owen present home in stanza 6?

The men's "ghosts drag home" — they are already dead in imagination — to a house with "sunk fires" and "crusted dark-red jewels" of coal; but the house belongs to "innocent mice", and "Shutters and doors, all closed: on us the doors are closed."

The repetition of "closed" locks them out. England is warm and shut; the soldiers are outside in every sense — physically in France, and excluded from the life they are supposedly defending.

"We turn back to our dying" makes dying the task they return to, like a shift. Owen's point is bitter: the country has closed its doors on the men who die for its fires — which is stanza 7's justification ("not otherwise can kind fires burn") already undermined.
:::

::: q
What is the enemy in Exposure?
---
The weather — wind, snow and frost, personified as an attacking army.
:::

::: q
Quote the refrain and say how many stanzas end with it.
---
"But nothing happens" — four stanzas (1, 3, 4 and 8), including the last.
:::

::: q
What is pararhyme? Give one example from the poem.
---
Half-rhyme in which consonants match but vowels differ — "knive us / nervous", "silent / salient".
:::

::: q
How is dawn presented, and why is that surprising?
---
As an army "massing in the east" that "attacks" — dawn, usually hope, becomes the enemy.
:::

::: q
What does "Less deadly than the air" say about the bullets?
---
That the weather kills more men than the enemy's fire.
:::

::: q
What happens to the men in stanza 5, and what is really happening?
---
They drift into dreams of grass and blossom — the hallucinations of hypothermia.
:::

::: q
Quote the line showing the soldiers shut out of home.
---
"on us the doors are closed".
:::

::: q
What two meanings does the title have?
---
Exposure to the elements, and exposing the truth of the war.
:::

::: q
What was Owen's stated purpose as a war poet?
---
To show "the pity of War" — to tell the truth against the glory of recruiting-poster patriotism.
:::

::: q
Name two poems to compare with Exposure.
---
The Charge of the Light Brigade (glory vs futility), Bayonet Charge (the soldier's experience), Storm on the Island (nature as attacker).
:::

::: watch
- **Nothing happens on purpose** — do not hunt for action; the waiting is the subject.
- The **weather is the enemy**: quote the personification.
- **Pararhyme** = no resolution; give a pair and the effect.
- The **circular structure** is the futility — say the last words repeat the first stanza.
- Owen writes **against glory**; if comparing with Tennyson, say who was there.
:::
