## What it is

Paper 2, Section C gives you a poem you have never seen and asks, for **24 marks**, how the poet presents a feeling or idea — "How does the poet present the speaker's feelings about…". You have nothing but the text and about 30 minutes. The skill is to **read for the central idea first** and only then look at how it is made — because a device you notice before you know what the poem is about will be described, not analysed, and description scores nothing.

**The reading routine — five minutes before you write:**

1. **Read it twice.** Once straight through for the sense; once slowly, marking anything that surprises you.
2. **Say in one sentence what it is about** — the situation (who is speaking, to whom, about what) and the feeling. "A daughter watches her father grow old and feels both tenderness and fear." If you cannot write this sentence, read it again; everything else depends on it.
3. **Find the shift.** Almost every poem turns — a change of tense, tone, address, or a "but". Mark where. The shift is your structure paragraph.
4. **Choose three or four moments** that carry the feeling most strongly. Underline a word or phrase in each — not a line, a word.
5. **Note the form in a phrase**: stanzas (regular or not), rhyme (or not), line length, whether sentences run over lines. Then ask what the form is doing for the feeling.

**Build a reading, not a device list.** The question is about a feeling or idea; your answer is an argument about how the poet builds it. Every paragraph: *the poet presents the feeling as X; here is the evidence; here is how the language or form creates X; here is why it matters to the poem's idea.* Devices appear only when they explain something — "the repeated 'still' is the speaker's refusal to accept change" — never as a heading.

**The language paragraph.** Pick a word and press on it: what does it literally mean, what does it suggest, what other word could have gone there, what does the choice do? Image patterns (several words of cold, of light, of hands) are stronger than single devices. Sound counts if it does something — a run of hard consonants at a moment of anger; a long vowel where the speaker lingers.

**The form and structure paragraph.** Say what the form is and connect it to the feeling: a regular rhyme scheme that breaks where the speaker breaks; couplets that make a memory feel tidy; free verse that will not settle; a last line shorter than the rest; a poem that begins and ends in the same place. The **ending** is always worth a sentence — how the poet leaves the feeling: resolved, open, reversed.

**The "so what" test.** After every observation ask "so what?" and write the answer. "The poem is in the present tense" — so what? — "so the loss is still happening as the speaker writes; there is no 'after'." The second half is the mark.

**Timing.** 24 marks, roughly 30 minutes: 5 reading and planning, 22 writing, 3 checking. The comparison question that follows (8 marks) needs its own 10 minutes — leave them. Rushing Q1 to save Q2 is the wrong trade; rushing Q2 because Q1 overran is the commonest.

**Marks.** AO1 — a clear response with well-chosen quotations; AO2 — analysis of language, form and structure with effect and accurate terms. No context: an unseen poem has none you can know, so do not invent it.

::: method
1. **One sentence of meaning** before anything else — situation and feeling.
2. **Mark the shift** and plan to use it.
3. **Three or four words**, not lines — press on each: literal, suggested, alternative, effect.
4. **Form → feeling**: say what the shape does, and how the poem ends.
5. **"So what?"** after every observation; the answer is the mark.
:::

::: example Reading for the idea
An unseen poem describes a mother's hands — kneading bread, then folded in a hospital bed — and ends "I hold them now, and they are light as flour." What is the central idea, and where is the shift?

Idea in a sentence: a child remembers a mother's working hands and holds them at her death; the feeling is love inseparable from loss.

The shift: from the kitchen (past, active — "kneading") to the hospital (present, still — "folded"). The verbs change from doing to being done to.

The plan follows: paragraph one on the hands as work and care (kitchen images); two on the change of tense and setting; three on the last line — "light as flour" — where the past's flour returns as the weight of a dying hand, and the simile makes the memory into the loss.
:::

::: example Pressing on a word
The line is "the kitchen held its breath". Analyse it properly.

Literal: the kitchen was silent and still. Suggested: the room is personified — it waits, as a person does before something happens; breath is life, so the kitchen is alive and anxious.

Alternative: "the kitchen was quiet" would report; "held its breath" makes the silence an act, a suspense.

Effect and so what: the poet gives the speaker's dread to the room, so the fear is everywhere at once and cannot be located in one person — which is what dread feels like. The line prepares the reader for the hospital before the poem gets there.
:::

::: example Form and feeling
The poem has four regular quatrains rhyming ABAB — until the last stanza, which has five lines and no rhyme. What do you write?

State it: four regular rhymed quatrains, then a final stanza that breaks the pattern — an extra line, no rhyme.

Connect it: the regularity is the mother's ordered life, the routine of bread and kitchen; the broken final stanza is her death breaking the routine — the poem's shape does what the loss does.

So what: the extra line is the thing that does not fit — the moment of holding the hands — and the missing rhyme is the closure the speaker cannot have. Form is not decoration here; it is the last stanza's argument.
:::

::: example An opening paragraph
Write the first paragraph of an answer to "How does the poet present the speaker's feelings about her mother?"

"The poet presents the speaker's feelings as love that has become inseparable from loss. The poem moves from a kitchen where the mother's hands are 'kneading' — a verb of work, care and repetition — to a hospital where the same hands are 'folded', passive and already arranged as if for burial. The feeling is carried by the hands rather than stated: the speaker never says 'I loved her', but the poem's attention never leaves what those hands did, which is a way of saying it."

An argument, a shift, two words pressed, and the method named (feeling carried by an image rather than stated).
:::

::: q
What is the first thing to do with an unseen poem?
---
Read it twice and write one sentence saying what it is about — situation and feeling — before analysing anything.
:::

::: q
Why does spotting a device score nothing on its own?
---
Marks are for effect — what the device does for the poem's idea; naming it without "so what" is description.
:::

::: q
What is the "shift", and why find it?
---
The point where the poem turns (tense, tone, address, a "but"); it gives you the structure paragraph.
:::

::: q
How many marks is the first unseen question worth, and how long should it take?
---
24 marks; about 30 minutes including planning.
:::

::: q
What should you quote — lines or words?
---
Words or short phrases, pressed for meaning; whole lines invite paraphrase.
:::

::: q
Give the four steps for analysing a single word.
---
Literal meaning; what it suggests; what else could have gone there; what the choice does for the feeling.
:::

::: q
What should a form paragraph always include?
---
What the form is, and how it serves the feeling — including how the poem ends.
:::

::: q
Should you write about context for an unseen poem?
---
No — there is no AO3 for unseen poetry; do not invent biography.
:::

::: q
What is the "so what" test?
---
After every observation, write what it does for the poem's meaning; the answer to "so what?" is where the mark is.
:::

::: q
Why must you keep time for the second unseen question?
---
It is a separate 8 marks and comes last; overrunning on the first is the commonest way to lose them.
:::

::: watch
- **Meaning first**, devices second — a device before an idea is a list.
- Quote **words**, and press on them; do not paraphrase lines.
- The **shift** and the **ending** are your structure marks — always use both.
- **"So what?"** every time; the effect is the mark.
- **No context** for unseen poems; and leave **ten minutes** for the comparison.
:::

::: test
What is the shift in a poem, and how do you use it? (2)
---
The point where tone, time or focus changes — often signalled by 'but', 'then', 'now' (1); it gives a structure point: what changes and what the change means (1).
:::

::: test
List the four steps for analysing a single word. (2)
---
Literal meaning; what it suggests (connotation) (1); an alternative word the poet did not choose; the effect (1).
:::

::: test
What should a paragraph on form always include? (2)
---
What the shape of the poem does — stanza length, rhyme or its absence, line breaks (1); and how the poem ends (1).
:::

::: test

---
There is no AO3 in the unseen questions; anything you say about the poet's life is a guess and earns nothing.
:::
