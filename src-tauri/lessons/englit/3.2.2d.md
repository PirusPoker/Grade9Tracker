## What it is

**My Last Duchess** (Robert Browning, 1842) is a **dramatic monologue**: the Duke of Ferrara shows a visitor a portrait of his dead wife and, while negotiating his next marriage, reveals — without meaning to — that he had her killed for smiling too freely. The whole poem depends on the **gap between what the Duke says and what the reader understands**: he condemns himself with every line of self-justification.

**The situation.** The visitor is an envoy from a Count whose daughter the Duke means to marry ("The Count your master's known munificence"). The Duke's tour of his art collection is also a warning: this is what happens to a duchess who does not behave. He draws a curtain to show the portrait ("since none puts by / The curtain I have drawn for you, but I") — he controls who sees her now, as he could not control who she smiled at when alive.

| Quotation | Analysis |
|---|---|
| "That's my last Duchess painted on the wall, / Looking as if she were alive." | "Last" — the previous one; there will be a next. "As if she were alive" — he prefers the painting: it can be owned. "My" is the first word after "That's". |
| "Sir, 'twas not / Her husband's presence only, called that spot / Of joy into the Duchess' cheek" | Her blush was for everyone — the sunset, a bough of cherries, a mule. His grievance is that she was happy without him. |
| "She had / A heart — how shall I say? — too soon made glad, / Too easily impressed" | The hesitation ("how shall I say?") performs modesty while accusing her of the crime of being pleased. |
| "as if she ranked / My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name / With anybody's gift" | Status: his ancient name should have outranked cherries and sunsets. Possessiveness disguised as pride. |
| "Even had you skill / In speech — (which I have not) — to make your will / Quite clear to such an one" | He claims to lack skill in speech in the most skilful speech in the poem — the reader sees through him. |
| "and I choose / Never to stoop." | He would not ask her to change; explaining himself would be beneath him. The word "stoop" is repeated — his rank is the only thing he values. |
| "I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together." | The murder, in six words, in a subordinate clause. The passive vagueness — "commands", "stopped" — is the horror: a death delivered as an administrative detail. |
| "Notice Neptune, though, / Taming a sea-horse … cast in bronze for me!" | He moves straight to the next artwork: a god taming a creature — his self-image; and "for me" — art as possession. The Duchess is one item in a collection. |

**Form.** Rhyming **couplets in iambic pentameter** — but the couplets are hidden by **enjambment** and caesura, so the poem sounds like speech while the rhyme locks underneath. The effect is the Duke: a man who believes he speaks freely and naturally while every line is controlled by a pattern he does not acknowledge. Some read the couplets as his control; others as the rhyme "he cannot contain" — the form giving away the compulsion he thinks he hides. The **single unbroken stanza** is one man's uninterrupted voice; the envoy never speaks, and the Duchess never spoke at all — she is a painting, "as if".

**What the poem reveals that the Duke does not intend.** He intends to show refinement, rank and a proper firmness with wives. He reveals: possessiveness ("my", "mine"), jealousy of a blush, a need to own what he cannot control, murder, and a plan to do it again. The dramatic irony is total, and Browning never intrudes — the reader is the only judge in the room.

**Context in a sentence.** Alfonso II, Duke of Ferrara, whose young wife Lucrezia de' Medici died in 1561 (poison was suspected); Browning, writing in 1842, gives a Victorian audience a study of male power over women and of the collector's mentality — art as ownership — with the objectification of the Duchess (she becomes a painting he can curtain) as its subject.

::: terms
- **Dramatic monologue**: a poem spoken by one character to a silent listener, revealing more than the speaker intends.
- **Rhyming couplets**: pairs of rhyming lines; their control mirrors the Duke's.
- **Enjambment**: running a sentence over the line end — here it hides the neatness of the couplets, like the Duke's hidden menace.
- **Caesura**: a pause in the middle of a line — "I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together."
- **Unreliable narrator**: a speaker whose account cannot be fully trusted.
- **Objectification**: treating a person as an object — the Duchess becomes a painting behind a curtain.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the form and what it does**: dramatic monologue — one voice, a silent listener, a speaker who reveals more than he intends.
2. **The gap is the method**: for every quotation, say what the Duke means and what the reader hears.
3. **"I gave commands" is your centre**: analyse the vagueness and the subordinate clause.
4. **Couplets under enjambment**: control (or compulsion) hidden in natural-sounding speech.
5. **End with Neptune**: the next artwork, the self-image, "for me".
:::

::: example The gap
How does Browning use dramatic monologue to reveal the Duke? Build the paragraph.

Point: Browning lets the Duke condemn himself by making him the only voice in the poem.

Evidence: "She had / A heart — how shall I say? — too soon made glad, / Too easily impressed; she liked whate'er / She looked on, and her looks went everywhere."

Analysis: the Duke intends this as a complaint; the reader hears a description of a warm, alive young woman. "How shall I say?" performs delicacy while he searches for a way to make happiness a fault. "Her looks went everywhere" is his jealousy, but it is her generosity.

Purpose: Browning never comments; the envoy never replies. The reader is left to do the judging — and the form makes us complicit listeners, like the envoy, who will carry this warning to the next duchess.
:::

::: example The murder
Analyse "I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together."

The murder occupies half a line. "Commands" — not "I had her killed"; the Duke does not name the act because to him it was administrative, a matter of giving orders as he gives orders for bronzes.

"All smiles stopped together" is passive and collective: the smiles stop, as if of their own accord, and "all" — every smile, for the mule, the cherries, the painter, the sunset — is what he needed to end. He killed a woman to stop a facial expression.

Browning places it mid-line, enjambed, so the Duke can move on at once: "There she stands / As if alive." The speed is the horror: the reader stops; the Duke does not.
:::

::: example The form
How do the rhyming couplets work?

The poem is in heroic couplets — AA BB — but the rhymes are buried by enjambment: "Fra Pandolf's hands / Worked busily a day, and there she stands." The ear hears speech; the pattern is there underneath.

One reading: the concealed couplets are the Duke's control — a man who appears to speak freely while every line is locked to the next. Another: the rhyme is what he cannot contain — the compulsion showing through the polish, the way his possessiveness shows through his courtesy.

Either way, form and character are the same thing: a surface of ease over a structure of control. Browning makes the reader hear both, which is exactly what the poem asks us to do with the Duke's words.
:::

::: example Neptune
Why does the poem end with the bronze of Neptune?

Having settled the dowry — "no just pretence / Of mine for dowry will be disallowed" — the Duke turns to his next object: "Notice Neptune, though, / Taming a sea-horse … cast in bronze for me!"

Neptune is a god subduing a wild creature — the Duke's picture of himself and of marriage. "Taming" is what he failed to do to the Duchess and intends to do to the next. "For me" is the poem's last phrase: everything, including wives, exists for him.

The Duchess is thus filed between two artworks. Browning ends on the collector's pride so that the reader sees the pattern the Duke cannot: he does not want a wife, he wants a possession that smiles only when told.
:::

::: q
What is a dramatic monologue?
---
A poem in a single character's voice addressed to a silent listener, revealing more about the speaker than they intend.
:::

::: q
Who is the Duke speaking to, and why is the visitor there?
---
An envoy from a Count whose daughter the Duke intends to marry; they are negotiating the dowry.
:::

::: q
What was the Duchess's "fault" in the Duke's eyes?
---
She was pleased by everything — "too soon made glad" — and did not rank his "nine-hundred-years-old name" above other pleasures.
:::

::: q
Quote the lines that reveal the murder.
---
"I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together."
:::

::: q
What does "I choose / Never to stoop" reveal?
---
His pride: he would not lower himself even to tell her what displeased him — rank matters more than the marriage.
:::

::: q
What form is the poem, and how is it disguised?
---
Iambic pentameter rhyming couplets, hidden by enjambment so it sounds like natural speech.
:::

::: q
Why does the Duke control the curtain over the portrait?
---
Now that she is a painting he controls who sees her — the ownership he could not have while she was alive.
:::

::: q
What is the significance of the Neptune bronze?
---
A god taming a creature — the Duke's self-image; "for me" makes art, and wives, his possessions.
:::

::: q
Who was the historical Duke?
---
Alfonso II of Ferrara, whose young wife Lucrezia de' Medici died in 1561, possibly poisoned.
:::

Name two poems to compare with My Last Duchess.
---
Ozymandias (power, art and pride), Checking Out Me History (control of another's identity), London (institutional control).
