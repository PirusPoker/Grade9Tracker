## What it is

Four ghosts, four jobs. Dickens designs each one — its appearance, its voice, what it shows and how it leaves — to do one specific piece of the work of converting Scrooge, and the order is not accidental. An answer on the ghosts is an answer on **method**: what each is *for*, and how its description implies its purpose.

| Ghost | Appearance | What it shows | What it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Marley** (Stave 1) | Scrooge's dead partner, "in his pigtail, usual waistcoat, tights and boots", transparent, bandaged jaw, and a **chain** "made of cash-boxes, keys, padlocks, ledgers, deeds, and heavy purses wrought in steel". | The consequence of a life like Scrooge's: "I wear the chain I forged in life. I made it link by link." Warns that Scrooge's chain "was full as heavy and as long as this, seven Christmas Eves ago". Outside the window, phantoms in chains who want to help the living and cannot. | **Warning and frame.** Marley is Scrooge's future if nothing changes — his mirror. His regret is precise: "Mankind was my business. The common welfare was my business." He sets the terms of the whole book and announces the three spirits. |
| **Christmas Past** (Stave 2) | "like a child: yet not so like a child as like an old man" — both at once; a "bright clear jet of light" springs from its head, with an extinguisher-cap under its arm. | Scrooge's own past: the lonely schoolboy, Fan ("Father is so much kinder than he used to be"), Fezziwig, Belle. | **Memory as the source of feeling.** The light is truth/memory; Scrooge ends the stave forcing the cap down over it — "he could not hide the light" — because he cannot bear what it shows. Child-and-old-man: memory is both. |
| **Christmas Present** (Stave 3) | A "jolly Giant" in a green robe trimmed with white fur, bare-chested, holding a "glowing torch" shaped like a horn of plenty, on a throne of turkeys, geese, puddings, punch; "girded round its middle was an antique scabbard; but no sword was in it, and the ancient sheath was eaten up with rust." | The Cratchits, Fred's party, the miners, the lighthouse, the ship — Christmas kept everywhere, by everyone, however poor. Then, under the robe, **Ignorance and Want**. The spirit ages through the stave and dies at midnight. | **The present as generosity and as accusation.** Abundance is the point — there is enough, and it is not shared. The empty scabbard: peace, not war. The torch sprinkles goodwill on quarrels. Ignorance and Want turn celebration into indictment. |
| **Christmas Yet to Come** (Stave 4) | "shrouded in a deep black garment, which concealed its head, its face, its form, and left nothing of it visible save one outstretched hand"; it **never speaks**. | The unnamed dead man: businessmen's jokes, Old Joe's rag-shop where his bed-curtains and shirt are sold, a couple relieved their creditor is dead, the Cratchits without Tiny Tim, the grave. | **Consequence, and fear.** Silence forces Scrooge to do the interpreting — "the case of this unhappy man might be my own" — so the moral is drawn by him, not told to him. The hand only points; the future is what you make it. |

**Ignorance and Want** are the most political moment in the book, and they appear under the *Present* spirit for a reason: they are not a future threat but a present fact, hidden under the robe of Christmas plenty. "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware them both … but most of all beware this boy, for on his brow I see that written which is Doom, unless the writing be erased." They are "wretched, abject, frightful, hideous, miserable" — "where graceful youth should have filled their features out … a stale and shrivelled hand, like that of age, had pinched, and twisted them." They are children made old by neglect — the inversion of the Past spirit's child-old face. Dickens's argument: ignorance (no education) is more dangerous than want, because it makes the poor a threat to society as well as its victims; and the spirit answers Scrooge's pity with his own words: "Are there no prisons?"

**Why this order.** Marley frames; Past reopens feeling; Present shows the world and the cost; Future shows the end. Each spirit is less human than the last — a friend, a child-elder, a giant, a shroud — as the lesson moves from personal to social to final. And each stave ends with Scrooge more active: Stave 2 he fights the light; Stave 3 he asks questions; Stave 4 he vows and "held up his hands in a last prayer".

::: terms
- **Marley's Ghost**: Scrooge's dead partner, whose chains show the consequences of a selfish life.
- **Ghost of Christmas Past**: shows Scrooge scenes from his earlier life, reminding him of lost feeling.
- **Ghost of Christmas Present**: shows Christmas in the present, including the Cratchits and Ignorance and Want.
- **Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come**: a silent, hooded spirit that shows Scrooge his lonely death.
- **Ignorance and Want**: two wretched children beneath the Ghost of Christmas Present's robe, symbols of society's neglect of the poor.
- **Symbolism**: using objects, people or colours to represent ideas.
:::

::: method
1. **Description implies purpose**: for each ghost, quote one detail of appearance and say what it means (light = memory; scabbard = peace; shroud = death and the unknown).
2. **Say what the ghost shows and what Scrooge does** — the change is measured in his responses.
3. **Marley is a mirror**: use his regret ("Mankind was my business") as the book's thesis.
4. **Ignorance and Want get their own paragraph** in any social-responsibility answer — and note where they appear (under the Present).
5. **Explain the order** — personal, social, final; friend to shroud — not just list it.
:::

::: example Marley's function
How does Dickens use Marley's ghost? Build the paragraph.

Point: Dickens uses Marley as Scrooge's mirror — the consequence of his life shown before the lessons begin.

Evidence: the chain is "made of cash-boxes, keys, padlocks, ledgers, deeds, and heavy purses wrought in steel" — the tools of a counting-house turned into fetters; "I wear the chain I forged in life … I girded it on of my own free will."

Analysis: "of my own free will" is the theme in miniature: the chain is chosen, so it can be unchosen. Marley's regret — "Mankind was my business" — corrects Scrooge's word "business" before Scrooge has used it in his own defence.

Purpose: Marley is the frame — he explains why the spirits come and what is at stake — and a warning to the reader, who is meant to check the length of their own chain.
:::

::: example Reading the description
What does the appearance of the Ghost of Christmas Present tell us about its purpose?

The green robe, the holly, the throne of food, the "glowing torch" — everything is abundance. Dickens's point is that plenty exists; the question is who shares it.

The scabbard with no sword, "eaten up with rust", makes the spirit a figure of peace — Christmas as the suspension of quarrels, which the torch enacts when it sprinkles goodwill on arguing people.

And the spirit ages: "My life upon this globe is very brief. It ends tonight." The present is always passing, so the time to act is now — which is why Ignorance and Want are revealed at the end of *this* stave, not the next: they are a present emergency, not a future one.
:::

::: example Ignorance and Want
Explain their significance in Stave 3.

Dickens hides two children under the robe of the spirit of plenty — the poverty concealed beneath Victorian Christmas cheer. They are "yellow, meagre, ragged, scowling, wolfish", children with the pinched hands of the old: society has stolen their childhood.

The spirit names them as abstractions — "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want" — and singles out the boy: "most of all beware this boy, for on his brow I see that written which is Doom." Dickens's argument is that an uneducated poor is a danger to everyone, not just a misery to themselves.

Then the reversal: Scrooge asks "Have they no refuge or resource?" and hears "Are there no prisons? … Are there no workhouses?" The reader who agreed with Scrooge in Stave 1 is now looking at what those answers do to actual children. This is the book's political centre, and any answer on social responsibility that leaves it out has left out the best evidence.
:::

::: example The silent spirit
Why does Dickens make the last ghost silent?

The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come is "shrouded in a deep black garment" and only points; it is death, and the unknown.

Silence makes Scrooge interpret: he has to work out for himself whose corpse lies under the sheet and whose name is on the stone — "Before I draw nearer to that stone … answer me one question. Are these the shadows of the things that Will be, or … that May be, only?" The moral is drawn by Scrooge, which is why it holds.

Dickens also uses the silence to control fear: a lecture can be argued with; a pointing hand cannot. The stave ends with Scrooge clutching the hand — "it shrunk, collapsed, and dwindled down into a bedpost" — the future dissolving into the ordinary present, where change is still possible.
:::

::: q
What is Marley's chain made of, and what does it represent?
---
Cash-boxes, keys, padlocks, ledgers, deeds and purses — the tools of his greed, become the burden he carries for eternity.
:::

::: q
Quote Marley's correction of the word "business".
---
"Mankind was my business. The common welfare was my business."
:::

::: q
Describe the Ghost of Christmas Past and explain the light from its head.
---
Like a child and an old man at once; the "bright clear jet of light" is memory/truth, which Scrooge tries to cap at the end of Stave 2.
:::

::: q
What does the empty, rusted scabbard on the Ghost of Christmas Present signify?
---
Peace — Christmas as the suspension of quarrels; the sword has not been used for a long time.
:::

::: q
Where do Ignorance and Want appear, and why there?
---
Under the robe of the Ghost of Christmas Present — they are a present fact hidden beneath Christmas plenty, not a future threat.
:::

::: q
Which of the two children does the spirit say to fear most, and why?
---
The boy, Ignorance — "on his brow I see that written which is Doom" — an uneducated poor endangers the whole of society.
:::

::: q
How does the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come communicate?
---
It never speaks; it is shrouded and points with one hand.
:::

::: q
Whose bed-curtains are sold at Old Joe's, and what does the scene show?
---
Scrooge's own; his death is unmourned and his possessions are picked over — the consequence of a loveless life.
:::

::: q
How does Scrooge's behaviour change from Stave 2 to Stave 4?
---
From resisting (forcing the cap over the light) to asking (about Tiny Tim) to vowing and praying at the grave.
:::

::: q
How does Dickens make the appearance of each ghost express its purpose?
---
Each detail is symbolic — chain (consequence), light (memory), abundance and scabbard (plenty, peace), shroud (death, the unknown).
:::

::: watch
- Every ghost: **appearance → what it shows → what it is for**. Description is never just description in Dickens.
- **Ignorance and Want** are essential to any social-responsibility answer — and note they are under the **Present**.
- Marley is the **frame and the mirror**; quote "Mankind was my business".
- The silent Future spirit makes **Scrooge draw the moral** — say why that matters.
- Explain the **order** (personal → social → final); do not just list the spirits.
:::
