## What it is

**Bayonet Charge** (Ted Hughes, 1957) follows one unnamed soldier through the seconds of a charge across a field under machine-gun fire. Hughes was born in 1930; his father had fought at Gallipoli, and the poem reconstructs a First World War experience from a generation's distance. Its subject is what happens to **thought, patriotism and the self** when a body is running towards bullets: instinct takes over, time stretches, and the ideals that sent the man there — "King, honour, human dignity" — are dropped like "luxuries".

**The structure — three stanzas, three states:**

| Stanza | What happens | Method |
|---|---|---|
| **1: Motion** | Opens *in medias res* — "Suddenly he awoke and was running" — as if the soldier wakes into the charge already happening. He runs through mud towards a hedge, rifle "lugged" like a burden, sweat "heavy", bullets "smacking the belly out of the air". The patriotism he arrived with is "sweating like molten iron" from his chest. | Enjambment and dashes keep the lines running; verbs of struggle ("lugged", "stumbling"); the simile of tears and "molten iron" makes feeling physical and molten — patriotism melting under heat. |
| **2: Suspension** | Time stops. "In bewilderment then he almost stopped" — a question surfaces: what is the "cold clockwork of the stars and the nations" that set him running? He is "a hand pointing" on a clock's "second" — a mechanism's part. His foot hangs "like / Statuary in mid-stride". | The stanza is a held breath: the present tense of a mind questioning in the middle of action; the clock and statue images freeze him. Hughes puts the poem's only thought here — and it is bewilderment, not understanding. |
| **3: Instinct** | A yellow hare bursts from the hedge, "crawled in a threshing circle", mouth open "silent", eyes "standing out" — an animal in agony, the war's true image. The soldier "plunged past" it: "King, honour, human dignity, etcetera / Dropped like luxuries". What drives him now is "his terror's touchy dynamite". | The hare is the poem's centre — nature caught in the machine, screaming without sound. "Etcetera" dismisses the ideals with a shrug; "luxuries" makes them things a running man cannot afford. He becomes a weapon: terror as explosive. |

**Instinct over thought.** The soldier barely thinks; the one moment of thought (stanza 2) is a question with no answer, and it stops him — thinking is dangerous in a charge. Hughes's argument is that war reduces a man to the body's reflexes: the patriotism that "sweats" out in stanza 1 is gone by stanza 3, and what remains is terror, running, and a bayonet.

**Time.** Stanza 1 is fast — a single sentence of running; stanza 2 slows to a stop — clockwork, statuary, "mid-stride"; stanza 3 accelerates again. The middle stanza's stretched present is the mind's experience of extreme fear, in which a second contains a whole question. Hughes controls pace with syntax: long enjambed sentences to run, a stopped foot to freeze.

**The anonymous soldier.** He has no name, no country, no rank; "he" throughout. The anonymity is Hughes's method: this is every soldier in every charge, and the poem refuses to make him a hero or a person — he is a body in a field. Do not fill the gap with a character.

**Patriotism reduced.** He began with ideals — the "patriotic tear" of stanza 1 — and ends with "etcetera". The list "King, honour, human dignity, etcetera" is the poem's verdict on the reasons men are given: they are the first thing dropped when the running starts. What is left is the "dynamite" of fear — the man has become the weapon the nation needed.

**Context in a sentence.** Hughes wrote a generation after the war his father survived; the poem is imagined rather than witnessed, and belongs with his poems about nature's violence (the hare belongs to that world too).

::: method
1. **In medias res**: start with "Suddenly he awoke and was running" — say what waking into a charge does to the reader.
2. **Three stanzas, three tempos**: motion, suspension, instinct — with a quotation each.
3. **The hare** deserves a paragraph: nature caught in the machine, the war's true image.
4. **"Etcetera"**: the ideals dismissed; "luxuries" as what a running man cannot carry.
5. **Anonymity as method** — the soldier is nobody so that he is everybody.
:::

::: example The opening
How does Hughes use the opening of the poem? Build the paragraph.

Point: Hughes begins in the middle of the action so that the reader, like the soldier, has no preparation.

Evidence: "Suddenly he awoke and was running" — the poem's first word is "Suddenly"; the soldier wakes already in motion.

Analysis: "awoke" makes the charge a nightmare he wakes into rather than out of; there is no before. The long first sentence, enjambed and dashed, does not let the reader stop any more than the soldier can. The bullets "smacking the belly out of the air" turn even the air into a body being struck.

Purpose: Hughes wants disorientation, not narrative — a war poem that gives the reader the soldier's confusion instead of an explanation.
:::

::: example Time stopped
Analyse the second stanza.

The stanza opens with hesitation — "In bewilderment then he almost stopped" — and the tempo drops from a sprint to a frozen frame: his foot hangs "like / Statuary in mid-stride".

The question he asks is the poem's only thought: what "cold clockwork of the stars and the nations" set him running? He sees himself as a mechanism — "a hand pointing" on a clock — the nation's instrument, not its citizen.

Hughes stretches the second so that a whole reflection fits inside it — the psychology of extreme fear — and then denies it an answer. Thought stops the body; the body cannot afford it. The stanza ends with the question unanswered and the running resumed.
:::

::: example The hare
Explain the significance of the hare.

A yellow hare "crawled in a threshing circle", mouth open, eyes "standing out" — an animal caught by the machine-gun fire, thrashing in silent agony.

The hare is nature, which has no side in the war and is destroyed by it anyway; its silent scream is the sound the poem's men cannot make. Hughes puts the war's whole horror into a creature that does not know what a nation is.

The soldier "plunged past" it. He does not stop for the hare any more than for his thought; pity is another luxury. Hughes uses the hare to show what the charge does to feeling — the man sees the agony and keeps running towards his own.
:::

::: example Patriotism
How does Hughes present patriotism?

In stanza 1 it is already melting: the "patriotic tear" that filled his eye is now "sweating like molten iron from the centre of his chest" — feeling turned to heat and running out of him.

By stanza 3 the ideals are a list with a shrug: "King, honour, human dignity, etcetera / Dropped like luxuries". "Etcetera" is the poem's most contemptuous word — the slogans are interchangeable, not worth finishing; "luxuries" are what you cannot carry when you are running for your life.

What replaces them is "his terror's touchy dynamite": the man has become an explosive, which is what the nation needed him to be. Hughes's argument is that the ideals were the packaging; the product was fear with a bayonet.
:::
