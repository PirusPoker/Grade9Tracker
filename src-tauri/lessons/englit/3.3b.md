## What it is

**AO3** — "the relationships between texts and the contexts in which they were written" — is the smallest of the three main objectives, and the one most often done badly in the same way: a paragraph of history with no text in it. Context scores when it **illuminates a specific moment**: a fact, a line, and the link between them. It costs marks when it replaces analysis, because every minute spent on the Gunpowder Plot is a minute not spent on the word "equivocator".

**What counts as context.** Not just dates and events. AO3 includes:

- **The writer's situation** — Tennyson as Laureate; Owen at the front; Priestley broadcasting for a fairer Britain; Dickens's blacking factory; Agard's colonial schooling.
- **The writer's purpose and audience** — who the text was for and what it was meant to do: a play for James I's court; a five-shilling book for Christmas; a poem for a documentary on veterans.
- **The ideas of the time** — divine right; Malthus and the Poor Law; Jacobean expectations of women; Edwardian confidence; Romantic ideas of nature.
- **Literary context** — the form's history: a sonnet for a ruin; a morality play in a dining room; Songs of Experience; the tradition Owen wrote against.
- **Reception** — how readers then and now respond differently: a 1606 audience terrified of witches; a 1945 audience who knew the Titanic sank; a modern reader who hears Lady Macbeth's "unsex me" as a woman refused any other route to power.

**The rule: fact → line → link.** Every piece of context must be attached to a quotation and must explain something about it. Compare:

| Bolted on | Woven in |
|---|---|
| "In Jacobean times people believed in the divine right of kings, which meant the king was chosen by God. This is important context for the play." | "Because James I's audience held that the king was God's deputy, Macbeth's fear that Duncan's virtues will 'plead like angels, trumpet-tongued' is not exaggeration but theology — the murder is sacrilege, and Macbeth knows it before he commits it." |
| "Dickens was very concerned about poverty in Victorian England." | "Dickens had visited a Ragged School months before writing, which is why Ignorance — the boy — is the child the Spirit says to fear 'most of all': the poem's target is not hunger but the education the poor were denied." |
| "Priestley was a socialist." | "Writing for an audience about to vote in the 1945 election, Priestley gives the Inspector the vocabulary of collective responsibility — 'members of one body' — that the welfare state would be built on." |

**Keep it to a sentence.** A clause of context inside an analytical sentence is worth more than a paragraph of it: "Writing months after the Gunpowder Plot trials, Shakespeare has the Porter admit an 'equivocator' to hell." The context is the subordinate clause; the analysis is the main one.

**Different readers, then and now.** A quick way to earn AO3 without history: say how the original audience would have responded and how a modern one might. "Shakespeare's audience would have seen Lady Macbeth's invocation as witchcraft — a capital crime; a modern audience is more likely to read it as the only way a woman could imagine power in a world that gave her none." Two responses, one line, no dates needed.

**Where context does not belong.** The unseen poetry questions have no AO3. The Power and Conflict comparison has it, but only six of thirty marks — one sentence per poet. Do not open any answer with a context paragraph: the first paragraph should be about the text.

**Purpose is context.** The most reliable form of AO3 is the writer's purpose: what the text was written to do and for whom. "Owen wanted a public raised on Tennyson to know what the war was" is context, and it links directly to method — the refrain, the pararhyme, the absence of an enemy. Purpose sits at the end of the analytical paragraph, as the answer to the final "so what?".

::: terms
- **AO3 (context)**: showing the relationship between texts and the contexts in which they were written.
- **Historical context**: the events and conditions of the time a text was written or set.
- **Social context**: attitudes to class, gender, religion and morality at the time.
- **Writer's purpose**: what the writer wanted readers or audiences to think or feel.
- **Reception**: how audiences then and now respond to the text.
- **Bolt-on context**: context added as a separate fact rather than linked to the text, which earns little credit.
:::

::: method
1. **Fact → line → link** every time: a specific thing, a quotation, what the fact explains about the words.
2. **A clause, not a paragraph**: put the context in a subordinate clause of an analytical sentence.
3. **Purpose and audience** are the safest context — who it was for, what it was meant to do.
4. **Then and now**: two readers' responses to one line, when history is thin.
5. **Never open with context**; never use it in unseen poetry; keep it to a sentence per poet in the comparison.
:::

::: example Fact, line, link
Use the 1834 Poor Law properly.

Fact: the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 abolished relief outside workhouses, which were made deliberately harsh to deter the poor from claiming.

Line: Scrooge's "Are there no workhouses?" and the collector's "Many can't go there; and many would rather die."

Link, in one sentence: "Scrooge's question is not ignorance but the policy of the 1834 Poor Law — the institutions exist, so the poor have no further claim — and Dickens answers the policy through the collector's 'would rather die', which Scrooge then turns into 'they had better do it'." The fact explains what the line means; the analysis continues.
:::

::: example A clause of context
Rewrite a bolted-on paragraph as a clause.

Bolted on: "Tennyson was the Poet Laureate. This meant he had to write poems about important national events. The Charge of the Light Brigade was one of these."

As a clause: "As Laureate, expected to give the nation something to be proud of, Tennyson still places 'Someone had blundered' in the second stanza, in a rhyme position — honouring the men without hiding the mistake."

The context is now the reason for a specific choice, and the sentence is about the poem.
:::

::: example Then and now
Write a two-reader sentence for Lady Macbeth.

"To a Jacobean audience, for whom witchcraft was a capital crime and a wife's duty was obedience, 'unsex me here' is monstrous — a woman summoning spirits to overrule her nature; to a modern audience it reads as the only route to power available to a woman in a world that offered her none, which makes her later collapse less a punishment than a cost."

Two responses to one line: AO3 earned twice in one sentence, with the analysis intact.
:::

::: example Purpose as context
End an analytical paragraph on Exposure with purpose.

"…The refrain 'But nothing happens' closes the poem where it began, in the same cold, so that the structure refuses the reader any climax. Owen, writing from the trenches for a public raised on Tennyson's charge, gives them the opposite poem: no enemy, no action, no glory — because the war he knew was made of waiting, and the pity of it was that the waiting killed."

Purpose in the last sentence, as the reason for the method — the strongest place for AO3.
:::

::: q
What is AO3, and how much is it worth compared with AO2?
---
Context — the relationship between the text and its situation, purpose and audience; worth far less than AO2 (analysis).
:::

::: q
Give the three-part formula for using context.
---
Fact → line → link: a specific fact, a quotation, what the fact explains about the words.
:::

::: q
Name four kinds of context beyond historical events.
---
The writer's situation, purpose and audience, ideas of the time, literary form and tradition, reception then and now.
:::

::: q
What is wrong with opening an essay with a context paragraph?
---
It delays the analysis that earns most marks and usually has no text in it.
:::

::: q
How long should a piece of context be?
---
A clause or a sentence, inside an analytical sentence.
:::

::: q
Which questions carry no AO3?
---
The unseen poetry questions.
:::

::: q
How much context belongs in the Power and Conflict comparison?
---
About a sentence per poet — six of thirty marks.
:::

::: q
What is the "then and now" technique?
---
Stating how the original audience and a modern reader respond differently to the same line.
:::

::: q
Why is the writer's purpose the safest form of context?
---
It links directly to method — the reason for the choices you have analysed — and needs no dates.
:::

::: q
Turn "Victorian times were hard" into context that scores.
---
Name a specific fact (the 1834 Poor Law, Malthus, the Ragged School) and attach it to a line it explains.
:::

::: watch
- **Fact → line → link** — history without a quotation scores nothing.
- **A clause, not a paragraph**; never the opening paragraph.
- **Purpose and audience** are context — and the best kind.
- **Then and now** earns AO3 without dates.
- **No context in unseen poetry**; one sentence per poet in the comparison.
:::
