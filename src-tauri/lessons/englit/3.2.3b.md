## What it is

The second unseen question gives you a **second poem** on a related subject and asks, for **8 marks**, how the two poets present the idea — "In both poems the speakers describe… What are the similarities and/or differences between the ways the poets present…". It is worth a third of the first question, comes last on the paper, and is the question most often rushed or left blank. It also has a narrower target: the mark scheme rewards **comparison of methods** (AO2 only) — how the poets do it — not a second full analysis.

**What the 8 marks are for.** AO2 alone: comparing the writers' use of language, form and structure and their effects. There is no AO1 mark for a "response" here and no context. So the answer is short, method-focused, and comparative in every sentence: *both poets use…; the first… whereas the second…*.

**Find a genuine point of comparison.** The two poems share a subject (the examiners chose them for it), so the comparison is not "both are about mothers" — that is given. It is *how* each poet handles it: one through an image pattern, the other through direct statement; one in a regular form, the other in free verse; one ending in resolution, the other open. Aim for **two or three points of method** with a quotation from each poem under each.

| Point of method | What to compare |
|---|---|
| **Imagery** | Do both use a controlling image (hands, light, weather)? Is one concrete and one abstract? |
| **Voice and address** | First person to a "you"? Third person? Present or past tense? Who is speaking to whom, and how close does that make the reader? |
| **Form** | Regular stanzas and rhyme against free verse; line length; a couplet ending against an open one. |
| **Structure** | Where each poem shifts; how each ends; whether one is a narrative and the other a moment. |
| **Sound** | Repetition, rhythm, a run of consonants — only if it does something. |
| **Tone** | Tender, bitter, detached, ironic — and the words that make it so. |

**Handle the mark allocation with a tighter answer.** Three short paragraphs, each one point of method with both poems in it, and a one-sentence conclusion on the key difference. No introduction restating the question; no summary of either poem. Roughly 10–12 minutes and half a side.

**Comparative sentence patterns** — practise these so they are automatic:

- "Both poets use [method] — [poem A quotation]; [poem B quotation] — but where A's [effect], B's [effect]."
- "Unlike A, which [method], B [method], so that [effect]."
- "A's [form] gives the feeling [quality]; B's [form], by contrast, [quality]."

**Timing.** Leave **ten minutes**. If the first unseen question is overrunning at 30 minutes, stop it — an unfinished 24-mark answer loses a couple of marks; an unattempted 8-mark answer loses eight. Read the second poem once, quickly; you are looking for two or three methods, not a full reading.

**Similarities and/or differences.** The question allows either; the best answers do both — a shared method, then how its use differs. Similarity alone is thin; difference alone can miss the ground the poems share. "Both… but…" is the shape.

::: method
1. **Read poem two once**, looking for how it differs in method from poem one: image, voice, form, ending.
2. **Two or three points of method**, each with a quotation from both poems.
3. **Every sentence compares** — a connective in each, "both… whereas…".
4. **No introduction, no summary, no context** — start with the first method.
5. **Ten minutes, half a side** — and always attempt it.
:::

::: example Finding the point
Poem one presents a mother through her hands (kneading, folded); poem two presents a father through his coat left on a hook. What is the genuine comparison?

Both poets present a parent through an object or body part that stands for them — a synecdoche — rather than by description. The hands are the mother's care; the coat is the father's absence.

Method difference: the hands are the mother, present in the poem and then still; the coat is what remains of the father — one poet keeps the parent in the poem, the other keeps only a trace.

That is a comparison of method, not subject — it earns AO2 and takes one paragraph.
:::

::: example A full answer, paragraph one
Write the first paragraph.

"Both poets present the parent through a single physical detail rather than describing them. In the first poem it is the hands, 'kneading' and later 'folded', so the mother is her work and then her stillness; in the second it is the 'coat on the hook', which the father is no longer inside. Where the first poet keeps the mother present until the last line — 'I hold them now' — the second removes the father before the poem begins, so the reader meets only what he left. The same method carries opposite feelings: presence turning to loss, and loss from the start."

One method, both poems, two quotations each, a connective ("Where…"), and the effect of the difference.
:::

::: example Comparing form
Write the form paragraph.

"The forms differ in a way that matches the feelings. The first poem's regular rhymed quatrains hold the mother's routine in place until the final stanza breaks the pattern — an extra line, no rhyme — so the form performs the loss. The second poem is in free verse throughout, with short lines and no stanza breaks, as if the speaker cannot find a shape for the absence at all. One poet uses order and then breaks it; the other refuses order from the start."

Two forms, two effects, and a closing sentence that names the difference in method.
:::

::: example The conclusion
End the answer in one sentence.

"The key difference is that the first poet lets the reader watch the loss happen, through the change in the hands, while the second presents a loss already complete, through what the father left behind — presence becoming absence against absence itself."

One sentence, comparative, about method and effect. Then stop; there are no marks for a summary.
:::

::: q
How many marks is the second unseen question worth, and which assessment objective?
---
8 marks; AO2 only — comparison of the poets' methods and their effects.
:::

::: q
How long should you spend on it?
---
About ten minutes — half a side, two or three points.
:::

::: q
Why is "both poems are about loss" not a valid point of comparison?
---
The shared subject is given; marks are for comparing how the poets present it — methods.
:::

::: q
Name four aspects of method you could compare.
---
Imagery, voice/address and tense, form, structure (shifts and endings) — also sound and tone.
:::

::: q
What shape should each paragraph take?
---
One method, both poems, a quotation from each, a comparative connective, and the difference in effect.
:::

::: q
Should you write an introduction?
---
No — start with the first point of method; there are no marks for restating the question.
:::

::: q
Should you include context for the second poem?
---
No — there is no AO3 for unseen poetry.
:::

::: q
What is the shape "similarities and/or differences" is best answered with?
---
"Both… but…" — a shared method, then how its use or effect differs.
:::

::: q
What is the worst outcome for this question, and how do you avoid it?
---
Leaving it blank because Q1 overran; stop Q1 at 30 minutes and always attempt it.
:::

::: q
Give one comparative sentence pattern.
---
"Both poets use [method] — [A]; [B] — but where A's [effect], B's [effect]."
:::

::: watch
- **8 marks, AO2 only**: methods and effects, compared — nothing else.
- **Ten minutes, always attempted** — stop Q1 to protect it.
- Every sentence has **both poems** and a connective.
- **No intro, no summary, no context.**
- "Both… **but**…" — likeness then difference, in method.
:::
