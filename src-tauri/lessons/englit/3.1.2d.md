## What it is

*A Christmas Carol* is an argument dressed as a ghost story. Its thesis is Marley's: "Mankind was my business." Dickens attacks the way the comfortable classes **thought** about the poor, and offers **redemption** — not as a happy ending but as the thing he is asking of his reader. A theme answer must therefore say what Dickens wants the reader to *do*, not only what he wants them to feel.

**The attack on attitudes to the poor.** Scrooge's Stave 1 lines are not his private cruelty; they are the respectable opinions of the 1840s, given to a man the reader is invited to despise:

- "Are there no prisons? … And the Union workhouses? … The Treadmill and the Poor Law are in full vigour, then?" — the institutions exist, so the problem is dealt with.
- "It's not my business … It's enough for a man to understand his own business, and not to interfere with other people's." — the doctrine of non-interference.
- "If they would rather die, they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population." — Malthus: the poor are too numerous, and charity only makes more of them.

Dickens's method is to let Scrooge state the case in its own words and then make him **watch what it means**: Tiny Tim is the "surplus population"; Ignorance and Want are what "no refuge or resource" produces. The Ghost of Christmas Present adds the counter-doctrine when Scrooge blames religion for closing bakeries on Sundays: "There are some upon this earth of yours … who lay claim to know us … who are as strange to us … as if they had never lived." Charity, for Dickens, is a duty of the rich, not a weakness of the poor.

**The Cratchits.** Dickens's evidence that the poor are not "surplus": a family with almost nothing who keep Christmas better than anyone. Bob on "fifteen 'Bob' a-week", the goose that "eked out by the apple-sauce and mashed potatoes … was a sufficient dinner for the whole family", the pudding "like a speckled cannon-ball", Mrs Cratchit's "twice-turned gown", Martha's late arrival, Peter in his father's collar. "They were not a handsome family; they were not well dressed … But they were happy, grateful, pleased with one another, and contented with the time." The point of the scene is the *contrast* with Scrooge's wealth and misery: happiness is not made of money, but survival is, and Tiny Tim's survival depends on Scrooge's.

**Tiny Tim as a device.** He barely speaks — "God bless us every one!" and a hope that people in church saw "he was a cripple", so they might remember "who made lame beggars walk and blind men see". He is not a character but a **lever**: the emblem of the innocent poor, the test of Scrooge's heart ("tell me if Tiny Tim will live"), and the measure of the change (the "second father" of Stave 5). Dickens uses sentiment on purpose: the reader who can be made to cry at an empty stool and a crutch "carefully preserved" can be made to give.

**Redemption as the argument.** The book is not "Scrooge is redeemed" but "redemption is available" — to Scrooge, to Marley's fellow phantoms if only they could act, and to the reader. Marley's chain was forged "of my own free will", so a will can unforge it; the Future spirit's shadows are "the things that May be, only". Fred's speech is the creed: Christmas is "the only time … when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely, and to think of people below them as if they really were fellow-passengers to the grave, and not another race of creatures bound on other journeys." The ending is instruction: "he knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed the knowledge. May that be truly said of us, and all of us!"

**Family, generosity and community across the staves.** Stave 1: Fred's invitation refused, Bob's family unknown. Stave 2: Fan — "I have come to bring you home"; Fezziwig's party, where a small sum buys enormous happiness. Stave 3: the Cratchits; Fred's party; miners, lighthouse-keepers and sailors who "had remembered those he cared for at a distance". Stave 4: a world with no family for Scrooge — only creditors relieved and dealers haggling. Stave 5: Scrooge joins Fred's family, becomes Tiny Tim's second father, and is "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man" — friend, master, man: the three communities he had shut out.

::: terms
- **Social responsibility**: the idea that people, especially the rich, have a duty to help others.
- **Poor Law Amendment Act (1834)**: the law that forced the destitute into harsh workhouses.
- **Workhouse**: an institution where the poor were housed and made to work in grim conditions.
- **Malthus**: an economist who argued that population growth would outstrip food; Scrooge echoes him with "decrease the surplus population".
- **Christmas spirit**: generosity, family and goodwill — the values the Fezziwigs, Fred and the Cratchits represent.
- **Tiny Tim**: the Cratchits' disabled son, whose likely death makes the cost of poverty personal.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the attitude Dickens attacks** — Malthusian "surplus population", the Poor Law, "not my business" — in Scrooge's words, then show the scene that refutes it.
2. **Treat Tiny Tim as a device**: say what Dickens uses him for, not what he is like.
3. **Contrast is the method**: Cratchit table against Scrooge's gruel; Fezziwig's cheap party against Belle's "golden idol".
4. **Redemption = argument**: quote the "free will" chain, the "May be" shadows, and the closing "all of us".
5. **Say what the reader is asked to do** — give, visit, raise wages, look under the robe.
:::

::: example The attack
How does Dickens attack Victorian attitudes to the poor? Build the paragraph.

Point: Dickens gives the respectable economics of his day to Scrooge, and then makes Scrooge look at what it means.

Evidence: "If they would rather die … they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population" — Malthus's argument that population outruns food, so charity is futile.

Analysis: "surplus" turns people into a quantity; "decrease" is an accountant's verb. Dickens lets the doctrine speak in its own cold vocabulary — and then, in Stave 3, gives the surplus a name and a crutch. The Spirit's repetition of the line over Tiny Tim converts an abstraction into a child.

Purpose: Dickens's readers had used those arguments. The book makes them hear the words in Scrooge's mouth and watch them applied to a boy they have been made to love.
:::

::: example Tiny Tim
Explain how Dickens uses Tiny Tim.

Tiny Tim is given almost no character — a crutch, an iron frame, "God bless us every one!" — because he is a lever, not a person.

He is the emblem of innocent poverty: he hopes people in church saw his lameness so that they would remember Christ healing the lame — a child who thinks of others' faith rather than his own pain, which is exactly what the reader is meant to find unbearable.

He is the test of Scrooge's change: "tell me if Tiny Tim will live" is Scrooge's first question about someone else. And he is the measure of it: the empty stool and preserved crutch in Stave 4, the "second father" of Stave 5. Dickens uses sentiment deliberately — a reader who weeps for Tim can be moved to act — and the vagueness of the character is what lets every reader put a child they know in his place.
:::

::: example The Cratchits
Why does Dickens present the Cratchit Christmas in such detail?

The detail is the argument. Dickens counts everything: fifteen shillings a week, the "twice-turned gown", a goose "eked out" with potatoes, "one small atom of a bone" left over — the reader is made to do the sums Scrooge refuses to do.

Against that arithmetic, the happiness: "the two young Cratchits crammed spoons into their mouths, lest they should shriek for goose"; Bob's "God bless us!" for his family; the toast to Scrooge, "the Founder of the Feast", which Mrs Cratchit resents and drinks anyway.

Purpose: the scene refutes "surplus population" without an argument. These people are not surplus; they are the best society in the book. But it also refuses to make poverty picturesque: Tiny Tim will die unless "these shadows remain unaltered" — happiness does not pay for medicine. The rich reader is shown both what the poor have and what they are owed.
:::

::: example Redemption as instruction
How does Dickens make redemption the book's argument rather than just its ending?

Marley makes the chain a choice — "I girded it on of my own free will" — so what was chosen can be unchosen. That is the premise: no one is too far gone.

The Future spirit shows "shadows of the things that May be, only", and dissolves into a bedpost when Scrooge grasps its hand: the future is not fixed; the present is where it is decided.

The narrator ends by turning to the reader — "May that be truly said of us, and all of us!" Dickens has spent five staves converting one man in order to convert the person holding the book. Redemption is the book's demand, and Christmas — the one day when hearts are "shut-up" no longer — is when he expects it to be paid.
:::

::: q
Name the three institutions Scrooge cites as dealing with the poor.
---
Prisons, Union workhouses, the Treadmill (and the Poor Law).
:::

::: q
Whose theory lies behind "decrease the surplus population"?
---
Thomas Malthus — that population outgrows food, so helping the poor only increases their numbers.
:::

::: q
How much does Bob Cratchit earn, and why does Dickens tell us?
---
Fifteen shillings a week; the exact figure makes the reader do the arithmetic of poverty.
:::

::: q
Quote the sentence summing up the Cratchits' happiness despite poverty.
---
"But they were happy, grateful, pleased with one another, and contented with the time."
:::

::: q
Why does Tiny Tim hope people in church noticed he was lame?
---
So they would remember "who made lame beggars walk and blind men see" — he thinks of others' faith, not his own suffering.
:::

::: q
What does the empty stool and the "carefully preserved" crutch in Stave 4 do?
---
It shows the cost of Scrooge's indifference and is designed to move the reader to grief and then to action.
:::

::: q
Quote Fred's description of what Christmas does to people.
---
It makes them "open their shut-up hearts freely, and … think of people below them as … fellow-passengers to the grave".
:::

::: q
How does Marley's chain support the idea of redemption?
---
It was forged "of my own free will" — a chosen burden can be unchosen; no one is beyond change.
:::

::: q
What are the last words of the book, and who are they aimed at?
---
"God bless Us, Every One!" (after "May that be truly said of us, and all of us!") — aimed at the reader.
:::

::: q
What does Dickens want the reader to do, not just feel?
---
Act — give, visit, pay fairly, see the poor as "fellow-passengers" — as Scrooge does in Stave 5.
:::

::: watch
- Say what Dickens **wants the reader to do**; feeling sorry is not the aim.
- Name the **specific attitude** attacked (Malthus, Poor Law, "not my business") in Scrooge's words.
- **Tiny Tim is a device** — explain his use, not his personality.
- **Contrast** does the arguing: Cratchit table vs counting-house; Fezziwig vs Belle's "golden idol".
- Redemption is **available**, not just achieved: "free will", "May be", "all of us".
:::

::: test
Explain how the empty stool and crutch in Stave 4 work on Scrooge and on the reader. (2)
---
Tiny Tim's death is shown only by objects, more affecting than a deathbed (1); it converts the 'surplus population' from a number to a child, for Scrooge and reader alike (1).
:::

::: test
What does Dickens ask the reader to do, and where does he say it? (2)
---
To act — give, visit, pay properly, look at the poor — like Scrooge in Stave 5 (1); the final 'God bless Us, Every One!' is addressed to 'all of us' (1).
:::

::: test
How does Dickens present the Cratchit family's Christmas dinner? (3)
---
They are poor — the goose is small and eked out — but they celebrate it as a feast (1). The family are loving and grateful, praising the pudding (1). Bob toasts Scrooge as 'the Founder of the Feast', showing generosity even to the man who underpays him (1).
:::

::: test
What does Scrooge say about Fezziwig's power as an employer, and what is Dickens's point? (2)
---
'He has the power to render us happy or unhappy' — kindness in 'words and looks' is worth as much as a fortune (1). Employers are responsible for their workers' happiness, and kindness costs little (1).
:::

::: test
Why does the Ghost of Christmas Present take Scrooge to miners, a lighthouse and a ship at sea? (2)
---
To show the Christmas spirit reaching people in the harshest, most isolated places (1), so fellowship is universal and not just for the comfortable (1).
:::
