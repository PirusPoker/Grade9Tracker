## What it is

Ambition is the play's engine and kingship is what it is measured against. A theme question is not "where does ambition appear?" but **"what does Shakespeare say about it, and how?"** — so every paragraph needs the writer's purpose: what he warns his audience about, and why a 1606 audience needed the warning.

**Ambition as the central force.** Shakespeare shows ambition in three forms: Macbeth's — "Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself"; Lady Macbeth's — sharper and faster than his, "the nearest way"; and its absence in Banquo, who hears the same prophecy and holds back — "If you shall cleave to my consent … It shall make honour for you" / "So I lose none / In seeking to augment it". The play **condemns** ambition when it overrides loyalty and conscience: Macbeth names his own desires "black and deep", and the only reason he can find for the murder is the one he condemns in the same breath. What is condemned is not wanting the crown but **what he is willing to do for it** — the disordered priority, not the appetite.

**Three kings, one question — what makes a rightful ruler?**

| Ruler | Presentation | What he stands for |
|---|---|---|
| **Duncan** | "so clear in his great office", "meek", generous: "I have begun to plant thee, and will labour / To make thee full of growing." Trusting to a fault — "There's no art / To find the mind's construction in the face." | The **anointed** king: virtue, order, growth (the planting imagery). His murder is a crime against nature. |
| **Macbeth** | Rules by fear: spies "in every house", murderers on retainer; Scotland "sinks beneath the yoke; / It weeps, it bleeds". "Tyrant" is his title by Act 4. | **Usurpation**: power without legitimacy, which must keep killing to survive. |
| **Malcolm** | Tests Macduff by pretending to be worse than Macbeth, then lists "the king-becoming graces" — "justice, verity, temperance, stableness, / Bounty, perseverance, mercy, lowliness". Ends by making thanes earls and calling all home "by the grace of Grace". | **Restoration**: a king who knows what a king should be, chosen by right and confirmed by victory. |

Edward the Confessor, the English king who heals with a touch (4.3), is the fourth: a king as holy healer, the opposite of Macbeth as disease.

**Divine right and regicide.** James I, on the throne when the play was written, argued in his own writings that kings were appointed by God — the **divine right of kings** — so that killing a king was not just murder but sacrilege, an attack on the order God had set. Macbeth himself knows it: Duncan's virtues "Will plead like angels, trumpet-tongued, against / The deep damnation of his taking-off". The murder is followed by **disorder in nature** — darkness at noon, an owl killing a falcon, Duncan's horses eating each other (2.4) — because the universe itself has been violated. The **Gunpowder Plot** of 1605, a Catholic attempt to blow up James and Parliament, was fresh in every mind; the Porter's "equivocator" (2.3) is a reference to the plotters' trial. An audience that had just seen an attempt on their king's life watched a play about a king's murder and its punishment.

**Quotations that serve more than one theme** — learn these because they will work for ambition, kingship, guilt or the supernatural depending on the question:

- "Stars, hide your fires; / Let not light see my black and deep desires" (ambition, darkness, secrecy, conscience)
- "I have no spur … but only / Vaulting ambition" (ambition, self-knowledge)
- "Nought's had, all's spent, / Where our desire is got without content" (ambition's emptiness, marriage)
- "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far" (guilt, tyranny, ambition's momentum)
- "this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen" (kingship restored, judgement)

::: method
1. **Define the theme in the writer's terms** in your first sentence: "Shakespeare presents ambition as a force that destroys the person it drives."
2. **Three contrasts beat three examples**: Macbeth against Banquo (same prophecy), Duncan against Macbeth (same crown), Macbeth against Malcolm (same throne, different right).
3. **Name the purpose** every paragraph: what a Jacobean audience is being warned of, and what James I would have wanted them to conclude.
4. **Use the imagery patterns**: planting and growth for legitimate kingship; disease, blood and darkness for usurpation.
5. **Finish with the ending**: Malcolm's speech is Shakespeare's verdict, so quote it and say whether the verdict feels complete.
:::

::: example Ambition condemned
How does Shakespeare present ambition as dangerous? Build the paragraph.

Point: Shakespeare presents ambition as self-destroying — it defeats the person who yields to it.

Evidence: "I have no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on the other."

Analysis: the extended riding metaphor makes ambition both the spur and the rider — it drives Macbeth on, and it is what unseats him. "O'erleaps itself" predicts the whole play in three words: the jump is too far, and the landing is a fall. Shakespeare gives Macbeth this insight *before* the murder so that the audience knows he acts against his own judgement.

Purpose: to a Jacobean audience, ambition that reaches above one's God-given place was a sin against order; Shakespeare dramatises the sin from inside so the warning is felt, not preached.
:::

::: example Duncan against Macbeth
Contrast the two kings using the play's imagery.

Duncan speaks in images of cultivation: "I have begun to plant thee, and will labour / To make thee full of growing." Kingship, for him, is nurture — he grows his thanes.

Macbeth's Scotland is a body in agony: it "weeps, it bleeds, and each new day a gash / Is added to her wounds", and Macbeth himself asks the Doctor to "cast / The water of my land, find her disease". His rule is sickness.

Analysis: Shakespeare sets the two vocabularies against each other so that the audience feels the difference between a rightful and a usurping king in the language itself — one makes things grow, the other makes them bleed. The cure is Malcolm and the English army, described as "the medicine of the sickly weal".
:::

::: example Regicide and the natural order
Explain how Shakespeare shows Duncan's murder as a crime against nature.

In 2.4 the Old Man and Ross describe the night of the murder: darkness "strangles the travelling lamp" at midday, a "mousing owl" kills a falcon, and Duncan's horses "Turn'd wild in nature … 'Tis said they eat each other."

Every image is a hierarchy overturned — night over day, the lesser bird over the greater, beasts devouring their own kind. This is the Great Chain of Being breaking.

Shakespeare's purpose is to make the divine right of kings visible: because the king is God's deputy, killing him disorders creation. For James I's audience, a year after the Gunpowder Plot, the scene is both a warning and a reassurance — the universe itself rejects the regicide.
:::

::: example Malcolm's verdict
Does the ending fully restore order? Argue it.

Yes on the surface: Malcolm, the rightful heir, is crowned; the thanes become earls; the tyrant is "this dead butcher"; the closing rhyme ("Scone" / "one") ties the play shut.

But Shakespeare leaves loose threads. Fleance is alive, and the witches promised Banquo's line the crown, not Malcolm's; Malcolm's own testing of Macduff shows a king already practised in deception; and his verdict on Lady Macbeth as "fiend-like" is simpler than what the audience has just watched.

Conclusion: Shakespeare gives James's audience the restoration they needed and lets a careful reader see it as provisional — order is restored, but the play has shown how easily it breaks.
:::

::: q
Quote Macbeth's own name for his ambition and the image he attaches to it.
---
"Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on the other" — a rider who jumps too far.
:::

::: q
How does Shakespeare use Banquo to comment on ambition?
---
Banquo hears the same prophecy and refuses to act dishonourably — "I lose none / In seeking to augment it" — so the play shows ambition can be held in check.
:::

::: q
Give one quotation presenting Duncan as a good king.
---
"so clear in his great office" or "I have begun to plant thee, and will labour / To make thee full of growing".
:::

::: q
What imagery describes Scotland under Macbeth?
---
Disease and wounding — "It weeps, it bleeds, and each new day a gash / Is added to her wounds"; Macbeth asks the Doctor to find "her disease".
:::

::: q
Name four of the "king-becoming graces" Malcolm lists in Act 4 Scene 3.
---
Any four of: justice, verity, temperance, stableness, bounty, perseverance, mercy, lowliness, devotion, patience, courage, fortitude.
:::

::: q
What is the divine right of kings, and why does it make regicide worse than murder?
---
The belief that kings are appointed by God; killing one is therefore an attack on God's order, a sacrilege as well as a crime.
:::

::: q
What unnatural events follow Duncan's murder in Act 2 Scene 4?
---
Darkness at midday; an owl kills a falcon; Duncan's horses turn wild and eat each other.
:::

::: q
Which real event of 1605 lies behind the play, and where does it surface in the text?
---
The Gunpowder Plot; the Porter's "equivocator" refers to the plotters' trial.
:::

::: q
Why is Edward the Confessor mentioned in Act 4?
---
As a king who heals by touch — the holy healer set against Macbeth the disease.
:::

::: q
What does a theme answer need that a "where it appears" answer lacks?
---
The writer's purpose — what Shakespeare says about the theme and what he wants the audience to conclude.
:::

::: watch
- Every theme paragraph needs **purpose**: what Shakespeare warns of, and what the 1606 audience takes away.
- **Contrast** is the method: Macbeth/Banquo, Duncan/Macbeth, Macbeth/Malcolm.
- Ambition is condemned for **what it overrides** — loyalty, conscience, order — not for existing.
- Use the **planting vs disease** imagery for kingship; it is the play's own argument.
- Keep the Gunpowder Plot and divine right to **one sentence each, tied to a quotation**.
:::
