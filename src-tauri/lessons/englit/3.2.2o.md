## What it is

**Kamikaze** (Beatrice Garland, 2013) tells the story of a Japanese kamikaze pilot who sets out on his suicide mission, looks down at the sea, and turns back — and of what his family and community do to him for surviving. Its subject is the **conflict between duty and life**, and the unusual fact that the punishment is cultural and domestic, not military: he is shamed by his neighbours and his wife, and his children learn to treat him as if he had died. **He is punished for surviving** — that is what makes the poem unusual in the cluster.

**The narrative distance.** The poem is told at second hand: the pilot's **daughter** narrates, remembering what "her father" did and what "we" did afterwards. The first five stanzas are third-person description of his flight — what he "must have" seen; the last two shift into the daughter's own voice, in italics, telling how "my mother never spoke again / in his presence" and how the children "learned to be silent". So the poem has three layers: the pilot (who never speaks), the daughter (who imagines his flight and confesses her part), and the reader. The distance is the point: the pilot's decision is reconstructed by someone who was taught to ignore him.

**The flight — stanzas 1–5.**

| Detail | Analysis |
|---|---|
| "Her father embarked at sunrise / with a flask of water, a samurai sword / in the cockpit, a shaven head / full of powerful incantations" | The rituals of the mission — sunrise (Japan, the rising sun), the sword (honour), the shaven head and incantations (the pilot as a monk of death). "One-way journey into history" — the mission is a story already written. |
| "he must have looked far down / at the little fishing boats / strung out like bunting / on a green-blue translucent sea" | "Must have" — the daughter is guessing; she was not there. The boats are "bunting" — festive, fragile, domestic; the sea is "translucent" — life seen through. |
| "a shoal of fishes / flashing silver as their bellies / swivelled towards the sun" | The fish turn to the sun as the pilot is supposed to; the "silver" is beauty, not glory. The dark "prince" of a tuna — the sea's own royalty. |
| "the dark shoals of fishes / … and beneath them, arcing in swathes / like a huge flag waved first one way / then the other" | The shoal moves like a flag — nature's flag against the nation's; the sea is a country he could belong to instead. |
| "his brothers waiting on the shore, / the little boats, the fishermen / built cairns of pearl-grey pebbles" | Memory of childhood: brothers, boats, pebbles — the life the sea holds. This is what turns him back: not fear but the pull of the ordinary. |

**The turn — and the punishment.** "And though he came back / my mother never spoke again / in his presence, nor did she meet his eyes / and the neighbours too" — the italic stanzas. "We too learned / to be silent, to live as though / he had never returned" — the children are taught the shaming. The final lines: he "must have wondered / which had been the better way to die" — the daughter's guess again, and the poem's verdict: survival was a death too.

**Sea imagery and the pull of memory and family.** The sea is everything the mission is not — living, moving, plural, beautiful; it holds his childhood (the fishermen, the "pearl-grey pebbles" cairns), and it "swivels" and "arcs" while the plane flies a straight line "into history". The pilot's choice is between a fixed story and a moving life. Garland makes the sea the argument for turning back without a word of argument.

**Shame, honour and the cost of turning back.** Kamikaze pilots were expected to die; to return was disgrace for the family. The poem never judges the mother or neighbours — the daughter reports "we too learned" — but the phrase "as though / he had never returned" makes the punishment a living death. Honour is presented as a system that kills either way; the tragedy is the daughter's — she was made to help.

**Form.** Seven six-line stanzas, free verse, unpunctuated and enjambed so that the flight stanzas run in one long sentence — the pilot's continuous descent of looking; the shift to italics marks the daughter's own voice and the change from description to confession. The final line's question — "which had been the better way to die" — is left unanswered.

**Context in a sentence.** Kamikaze ("divine wind") pilots flew suicide missions against Allied ships in 1944–45; Garland, a British poet, wrote the poem in 2013 from a family's rather than a soldier's point of view.

1. **Name the narrator** — the daughter, at second hand — and say what the distance does.
2. **The sea stanzas**: pick two images (bunting, the shoal as flag, the cairns) and show how nature and childhood pull him back.
3. **The turn is never described** — say that Garland skips the decision and shows only the consequence.
4. **The punishment is domestic**: mother, neighbours, children — "as though / he had never returned".
5. **The ending**: an unanswered question; the daughter's guilt inside the report.
:::

::: example Narrative distance
How does Garland use the daughter as narrator? Build the paragraph.

Point: Garland tells the pilot's story through a daughter who was taught to ignore him, so the telling is itself an act of late recognition.

Evidence: "Her father embarked at sunrise"; "he must have looked far down"; and then the italic shift — "my mother never spoke again / in his presence … We too learned / to be silent".

Analysis: "must have" admits she is imagining the flight — she reconstructs the choice that shamed the family, giving him the interior life the family denied him. The move from "her father" to "my mother" and "we" is the narrator stepping out of the report into her own complicity.

Purpose: the pilot never speaks; the poem is his daughter finally speaking for him — and confessing that she was part of the silence.
:::

::: example The sea
Analyse the sea imagery in the flight stanzas.

The boats are "strung out like bunting" — festive, fragile, domestic: the sea is a celebration, not a battlefield. The shoal of fish flashes "silver" and moves "like a huge flag waved first one way / then the other" — nature's flag, waving both ways, against the nation's flag that points one way only.

Beneath the beauty is memory: "his brothers waiting on the shore", the fishermen building "cairns of pearl-grey pebbles". The sea holds his childhood.

Garland never states why he turns back. She puts the living, moving, plural sea under a plane flying a straight line "into history", and lets the reader feel the pull. The choice is between a story and a life.
:::

::: example The punishment
How does Garland present what happens after the pilot returns?

The return is given in a subordinate clause — "And though he came back" — and the consequence takes the rest of the stanza: "my mother never spoke again / in his presence, nor did she meet his eyes / and the neighbours too".

The children are recruited: "we too learned / to be silent, to live as though / he had never returned" — "learned" makes the shaming an education, like a lesson. He is treated as dead while alive.

Garland's point is that honour is a system that kills either way: the mission would have killed him; the family's shame kills him socially. The conflict is not military at all — it is cultural and domestic, which is what makes the poem unlike any other in the cluster.
:::

::: example The ending
Explain the final lines.

"he must have wondered / which had been the better way to die" — the daughter's guess again, and the poem's last word is "die".

The question has two answers and the poem gives neither: death in the plane, with honour; or death in the house, ignored for decades. The pilot chose life and was given a slower death.

The daughter's "must have wondered" is her own wondering: she was one of the children who "learned to be silent", and the poem is the silence breaking. Garland ends on a question because the daughter cannot answer it either — she can only tell it now, too late.
:::

::: q
Who narrates the poem, and why does that matter?
---
The pilot's daughter, at second hand — she reconstructs a choice she was taught to shame, and confesses her part.
:::

::: q

---
A flask of water, a samurai sword, a shaven head "full of powerful incantations" — the ritual of an honourable death.
:::

"bunting" — festive, fragile, domestic.

What memory pulls him back?

His brothers on the shore, the fishermen, the cairns of "pearl-grey pebbles" — his childhood by the sea.

Does the poem describe the moment he decides to turn back?
---
No — Garland skips the decision and shows only "And though he came back" and its consequences.
:::

::: q
How is the pilot punished?
---
His wife never speaks to him again, the neighbours shun him, and his children learn to live "as though / he had never returned".
:::

---
Italics and the change from "her father" to "my mother" and "we".
:::

---
"which had been the better way to die" — both choices were deaths; the daughter cannot answer for him.
