## What it is

*An Inspector Calls* is set in **1912** and was written in **1945** — and the whole play lives in the gap between those dates. Priestley chose 1912 so that the audience would know what the Birlings do not: the Titanic will sink, the war will come, the world Birling calls permanent has two years left. The dramatic irony is not decoration; it is the play's way of discrediting a whole class before it is put on trial.

**1912 — the Birlings' world.** The last confident years of Edwardian England: rigid class, wealth from industry (Birling's works), women without the vote, strikes met with sackings, "the Poor Law" and charities like Sybil's for the destitute. Birling's speeches are a catalogue of the era's certainties: "the Titanic — forty-six thousand eight hundred tons … unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable"; "the Germans don't want war. Nobody wants war"; "in twenty or thirty years' time … you'll be living in a world that'll have forgotten all these Capital versus Labour agitations". He is wrong about everything, in the order the audience remembers it.

**1945 — the audience's world.** Two world wars, the Depression, the Blitz; a country that had just voted Labour to build a welfare state — the NHS, national insurance, nationalised industry — on exactly the principle the Inspector preaches: "we are responsible for each other." Priestley, a broadcaster whose wartime *Postscripts* had argued for a fairer post-war Britain, wrote the play in a week and, when no London theatre was free, opened it in Moscow. His audience had learned the "fire and blood and anguish" the Inspector predicts; the play asks them whether they will go back to 1912 or forward.

**Dramatic irony as method.** Because the audience sits in 1945, every Birling certainty is heard as its opposite: "unsinkable" means sinking; "impossible" means 1914; the "silly little war scares" are the audience's fathers' deaths. Priestley uses the irony to do two things: make Birling laughable (so his economics are laughable) and make the audience feel their own superior knowledge — which the Inspector then turns on them: *you* know what happens next; what will you do about it?

**A modern morality play.** Medieval morality plays (*Everyman*) put a representative human through a test by allegorical figures — Death, Good Deeds — and ended in judgement. Priestley's version: a representative family (the Birlings as Everyman, one type each), a visitor who tests each in turn, a warning speech, an exit, and a judgement that arrives by telephone. The Inspector's "Goole" is the play's allegorical name. Reading it this way explains the ending: the family has been given a rehearsal, and those who failed it now face the real thing.

**Stagecraft — what the play does because it is on a stage:**

- **The single set**: "the dining-room of a fairly large suburban house … substantial and heavily comfortable, but not cosy and homelike." The room is the class it belongs to; the family cannot leave it, and neither can the audience.
- **Lighting**: "pink and intimate until the INSPECTOR arrives, and then it should be brighter and harder" — the play's only lighting cue, and it turns celebration into examination.
- **The doorbell**: it rings in the middle of Birling's speech on self-interest — "The lighting … a sharp ring of a front door bell" — interrupting the creed with its refutation.
- **Entrances and exits**: the Inspector controls them — Eric's return is timed to the end of Act 2; the Inspector's exit leaves the family without a referee; Gerald's exit and return bring the loophole. Sheila's exit "with a little cry" after the photograph is the first crack.
- **The photograph**: shown to one person at a time, never to the audience — a prop that keeps Eva representative and the family divided.
- **The telephone**: Birling's call to the Chief Constable, Gerald's to the Infirmary, and the last ring — the outside world arriving through a single instrument.
- **Act endings on revelation**: Gerald's face, "Eric!", the telephone — cliffhangers that make the audience carry knowledge through the intervals.
- **The Inspector's timing**: "I haven't much time" — the pressure of the clock in a real-time play.

::: terms
- **1912 setting**: just before the First World War and the Titanic sinking — a time of rigid class divisions.
- **1945 writing**: at the end of the Second World War, when Labour won a landslide election promising a welfare state.
- **Dramatic irony**: when the audience knows something a character does not — Birling calling the Titanic "unsinkable".
- **Morality play**: a medieval play teaching a moral lesson through characters representing sins and virtues; An Inspector Calls works like one.
- **Seven deadly sins**: pride, greed, lust, envy, gluttony, wrath and sloth; each Birling can be linked to one.
- **Stagecraft**: Priestley's use of lighting, the doorbell, the telephone, exits and entrances to control tension.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the irony, not the date**: "Birling's 'unsinkable' is heard by an audience that knows the Titanic sank" beats "the play is set in 1912".
2. **Two audiences**: say what 1912 believed, and what 1945 knew — and what Priestley wants 1945 to decide.
3. **Morality play** in one sentence — Everyman, tester, judgement — then apply it to the ending.
4. **Stagecraft = effect**: for each device (light, doorbell, photograph, telephone) say what it does to the audience.
5. **Keep Priestley's biography to a clause**: broadcaster, socialist, wrote for the 1945 election.
:::

::: example The irony in Birling's speech
How does Priestley use dramatic irony in Act 1? Build the paragraph.

Point: Priestley lets Birling predict the future in front of an audience that has already lived it.

Evidence: "The Titanic … unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable"; "there isn't a chance of war"; "in 1940 you may be giving a little party like this".

Analysis: the 1945 audience knows the Titanic sank on its maiden voyage, that the war came in 1914, and that in 1940 London was being bombed. Every certainty lands as its opposite, and the repetition of "unsinkable" makes the confidence itself the joke.

Purpose: Priestley needs Birling's world-view discredited before the Inspector arrives, so that "a man has to look after himself" is heard as one more wrong prediction. The doorbell that interrupts the speech is history knocking.
:::

::: example Why 1912 and 1945
Explain why the play is set in 1912 but written in 1945.

1912 is the last moment of the old order's confidence — before the Titanic, before two wars, before the vote for women and the welfare state. Priestley puts the Birlings there so that their values are already, to the audience, obsolete.

1945 is the moment of choice. The war has just ended; the general election that summer returned a Labour government committed to collective responsibility — health, insurance, housing. Priestley, whose wartime broadcasts had argued for exactly that, wrote the play as the case for not going back.

The gap between the dates is the argument: the Inspector's "fire and blood and anguish" is the future for the Birlings and the past for the audience. The question the play leaves is whether the audience will be Birlings or Sheilas in the world they are about to build.
:::

::: example Morality play
Read the ending as a morality play.

In *Everyman* a representative man is visited by Death, tested, and judged. Priestley's family is Everyman split into types — the businessman, the matron, the daughter, the son, the suitor — and the Inspector is the tester who takes each in turn.

His final speech is the sermon; his exit is the withdrawal of grace; and the telephone is judgement. Those who repented (Sheila, Eric) are changed; those who did not (Arthur, Sybil, and Gerald by choice) are about to be tested for real — "a police inspector is on his way".

This reading dissolves the "was he real?" problem: in a morality play the tester is an allegory, not a policeman, and his reality is beside the point. Priestley modernises the form by making the sin social rather than personal — not greed or lust but the refusal of responsibility for others.
:::

::: example Stagecraft
Analyse the use of the doorbell and the lighting at the Inspector's arrival.

Birling is mid-sentence — a man must "look after himself and his own" — when "we hear the sharp ring of a front door bell". The interruption is the refutation: the creed is cut off by the thing it denies, a stranger with a claim on the family.

The lighting shifts from "pink and intimate" to "brighter and harder". Priestley gives the play a single lighting cue and spends it here: the warm glow of an engagement party becomes the flat light of an interrogation, and the audience's view of the room changes with it.

Effect: before the Inspector says a word, the stage has told the audience what he is for. Priestley's stagecraft does the work of a page of dialogue — and it does it to the audience's eyes, which cannot argue back.
:::
