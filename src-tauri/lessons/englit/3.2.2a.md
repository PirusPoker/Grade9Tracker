## What it is

**Ozymandias** (Percy Bysshe Shelley, 1818) is a sonnet about a ruined statue in the desert and the king it commemorates — Ozymandias is the Greek name for Ramesses II of Egypt. Its subject is **the temporariness of power**: the tyrant who ordered his greatness carved into stone is now a broken face in the sand, and the only thing that survives him is the work of the sculptor who mocked him.

**The layers of narration.** Shelley builds three frames: the poet ("I met a traveller"), the traveller ("who said"), and the inscription on the pedestal ("My name is Ozymandias"). The king's voice is the innermost — and the most distant. We hear him third-hand, through a stranger, from a stone, about a statue of a man no one remembers. The frame is the argument: power that once commanded is now hearsay.

**Key quotations and what to do with them:**

| Quotation | Analysis |
|---|---|
| "Two vast and trunkless legs of stone" | The statue is introduced by what is missing — no body, no head yet. "Vast" and "trunkless" in one line: size without substance. |
| "a shattered visage lies" | The face is on the ground; "shattered" is violent — time has done to the king what he did to others. |
| "sneer of cold command" | Alliterative plosives; the sculptor caught the tyrant's contempt so well that it outlasts him. |
| "its sculptor well those passions read / Which yet survive … The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed" | The **sculptor's** art survives; the king's power does not. "Mocked" = copied *and* ridiculed — the artist had the last word. |
| "My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings: / Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!" | Imperatives, capital letters, the boast — carved for rivals to read. **Irony**: the "Works" are gone, so the despair now belongs to any king who reads it and sees his own future. |
| "Nothing beside remains. Round the decay / Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare / The lone and level sands stretch far away." | The sentence after the boast is three words long. "Colossal Wreck" — size and ruin together. The alliteration of "boundless and bare … lone and level" flattens everything; the sand is the only thing that is "boundless" now. |

**Form.** A **sonnet** — the form of love and praise — used for a mockery of praise: fourteen lines, but the rhyme scheme is irregular (neither properly Petrarchan nor Shakespearean), as if the form itself is eroding. Iambic pentameter that breaks at the caesura after "despair!" — the boast is cut off by the sand. The **volta** comes late and small: "Nothing beside remains."

**Context in a sentence.** Shelley was a radical who despised George III and monarchy in general; the poem was written in a friendly competition with Horace Smith in 1817, as the British Museum was acquiring a colossal head of Ramesses. The tyrant is Egyptian; the target is every king.

**The reading that lifts an answer**: it is not only "power fades". Art outlasts power — the sculptor's mocking hand survives; and so does the poem, which is Shelley's own "work" set against the king's. Ozymandias is remembered only because a sculptor and a poet chose to remember his failure.

::: method
1. **Name the frames** in your first paragraph: poet → traveller → inscription. Say why Shelley distances the king.
2. **Irony of the inscription**: quote "Look on my Works … and despair" and set it against "Nothing beside remains".
3. **Sculptor paragraph**: "mocked" has two meanings — art survives power.
4. **Form**: a sonnet with a broken rhyme scheme; the late, three-word volta.
5. **Two quotations under pressure**: "sneer of cold command" and "Nothing beside remains" — learn them cold.
:::

::: example The frame
How does Shelley use the structure of narration? Build the paragraph.

Point: Shelley distances the king through layers of reported speech so that his power is reduced to hearsay.

Evidence: "I met a traveller from an antique land, / Who said—" and, inside that, the words on the pedestal, "My name is Ozymandias".

Analysis: three voices stand between the reader and the king; the man who commanded "ye Mighty" now speaks only through a stranger's memory of a stone. The frame performs the poem's argument — power that was once direct becomes, over time, a story someone heard.

Purpose: for a radical like Shelley, the frame is also a warning to living kings: this is how you will be spoken of.
:::

::: example The inscription
Analyse the irony of "Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!"

The line is an imperative addressed to rival kings — Ozymandias assumes an audience of the powerful, and commands their envy.

Shelley then gives the pedestal's reply: "Nothing beside remains." The "Works" are absent; the only thing left to look on is sand. So the sentence still works, but its meaning has inverted: the Mighty should despair because *this* is what their power comes to.

The full stop after "remains" is the poem's hinge — a boast in capitals answered by a sentence of three words. Shelley makes the tyrant's own words the instrument of his mockery.
:::

::: example The sculptor
Explain the significance of "The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed".

"Those passions" — the sneer, the frown, the cold command — "yet survive" because the sculptor "well … read" them: the artist understood the tyrant better than the tyrant understood himself.

"Mocked" carries both senses: the hand *copied* the passions in stone, and it *ridiculed* them. The king commissioned a monument and received a satire.

Shelley's larger point is that art outlives power: the sculptor's work is still legible when the empire is gone — and the poem itself is another such work, a poet's hand mocking a king two thousand years later. The reading that "power fades" is true; the reading that "art survives power" is the one the poem is actually making.
:::

::: example Form
Comment on Shelley's use of the sonnet.

The sonnet is a form for praise and love; Shelley uses it for a monument that praises no one. The rhyme scheme is irregular — ABAB ACDC EDE FEF — neither the Petrarchan nor the Shakespearean pattern, so the form is as broken as the statue.

The volta is delayed and small: "Nothing beside remains" arrives late in line 12, after the boast, and the poem's last three lines widen out into the desert — "boundless and bare", "lone and level" — with alliteration that flattens the line into sand.

Effect: the form that should have contained a tribute contains an erosion; the sonnet ends not with a couplet's click but with the sands stretching "far away", an ending that refuses to end.
:::

::: q
Who was Ozymandias?
---
The Greek name for Ramesses II, pharaoh of Egypt.
:::

::: q
Name the three voices layered in the poem.
---
The poet ("I"), the traveller ("who said"), and the inscription on the pedestal.
:::

::: q
Quote the line that describes the king's expression.
---
"sneer of cold command."
:::

::: q
What two meanings does "mocked" have in the poem?
---
Copied (in stone) and ridiculed — the sculptor both imitated and satirised the king.
:::

::: q
What is the inscription on the pedestal?
---
"My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings: / Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!"
:::

::: q
Which three words answer the inscription, and what do they do?
---
"Nothing beside remains." — they turn the boast into its own mockery.
:::

::: q
What form is the poem, and how is it unusual?
---
A sonnet with an irregular rhyme scheme — the form is eroded like the statue.
:::

::: q
Give the alliteration in the final lines and its effect.
---
"boundless and bare", "lone and level sands" — it flattens the sound into the emptiness of the desert.
:::

::: q
What reading lifts an answer above "power fades"?
---
That art outlasts power — the sculptor's mocking hand and the poem itself survive the king.
:::

::: q
Name two poems to compare with Ozymandias.
---
My Last Duchess (power and art), Tissue (fragility of human power), London (institutional power), Kamikaze (memory outlasting the person).
:::
