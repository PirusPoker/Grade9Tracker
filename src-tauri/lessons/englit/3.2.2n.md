## What it is

**Checking Out Me History** (John Agard, 2007) is a poem about **whose history gets taught**. The speaker, in Caribbean Creole, lists what British schooling gave him — "1066 and all dat", Dick Whittington and his cat, the cow who jumped over the moon — and sets against it the Black and colonial history he was never told: Toussaint L'Ouverture, Nanny de Maroon, Shaka the Great, Mary Seacole. The poem's form makes the argument: two strands, two typefaces, two rhythms, and a spelling that is a **deliberate assertion of voice, not an error**.

**The two strands.**

| Strand | Content | Form | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Imposed history** (the "Dem tell me" stanzas) | "Dem tell me bout 1066 and all dat", "dem tell me bout Dick Whittington and he cat", "de man who discover de balloon", "Columbus and 1492", Florence Nightingale, Robin Hood, the cow that jumped over the moon | Short, rhymed, sing-song quatrains; nursery-rhyme rhythm; repetition of "Dem tell me" — the passive pupil being told | The British syllabus is made to sound like nursery rhymes: trivial, childish, mixed indiscriminately with fairy tales. The rhymes are mocking — history as jingle. "Dem" is the unnamed authority; "me" is what it did not see. |
| **Reclaimed history** (the italicised stanzas) | "Toussaint / a slave / with vision / lick back / Napoleon / battalion / and first Black / Republic born"; Nanny de Maroon, "see-far woman / of mountain dream"; Shaka "the great Zulu"; Mary Seacole, "a healing star / among the wounded / a yellow sunrise / to the dying" | Italics; short free-verse lines, unrhymed, stepped down the page; imagery of light — "beacon", "fire", "star", "sunrise" | The suppressed history is given the poem's most beautiful language and its own typeface — literally set apart, and literally shining. The lines are terse, like an inscription; the figures are named where the British ones are dismissed. |

**The phonetic spelling as a political act.** "Dem tell me wha dem want to tell me" — the Creole spelling, dropped verb endings ("dem", "wha", "de") are Agard's Guyanese English written as it is spoken. In a poem about who gets to define history, writing in one's own language is the first act of resistance: the poem refuses the "standard" that the syllabus enforced. Saying this is central to any decent answer. It also makes the voice **oral** — the poem is meant to be performed, chanted, and the reader hears a person, not a textbook.

**The two typographies and rhythms.** The roman "Dem tell me" stanzas bounce; the italic stanzas fall in short lines with pauses between. Reading aloud, the rhythm changes from jingle to incantation. The visual contrast on the page — regular vs italic, blocks vs stepped lines — is the argument made visible: two histories, one of them literally in a different type.

**Imagery of light and sight.** The reclaimed figures are light — Toussaint "a beacon / of de Haitian Revolution"; Nanny "a fire-woman"; Seacole "a healing star", "a yellow sunrise". Against that: "Blind me to me own identity", "Bandage up me eye with me own history". The British syllabus is a bandage over the eyes; the reclaimed history is what he sees when he takes it off. The final stanza: "I carving out me identity" — "carving" is an act of making, with effort, in something hard.

**Identity, education, cultural erasure.** The poem's claim is that education shaped identity by omission: what was left out taught the speaker he had no history. Reclaiming the names is reclaiming a self — hence "me own history", "me identity". The title's "Checking Out" is double: examining, and leaving (checking out of the imposed version).

**Context in a sentence.** Agard was born in British Guiana (now Guyana) in 1949, when it was a colony with a colonial curriculum, and moved to Britain in 1977; the poem belongs to the argument about decolonising what schools teach, and to the Caribbean performance-poetry tradition.

::: terms
- **Phonetic spelling**: writing words as they are spoken in Caribbean dialect ("Dem tell me"), asserting identity.
- **Dialect**: a form of a language particular to a region or group.
- **Repetition**: "Dem tell me", attacking the colonial education that told him what to learn.
- **Contrasting forms**: the rigid, unpunctuated stanzas about British history against the freer italic stanzas about Black heroes.
- **Colonialism**: one country controlling another; the poem criticises British colonial education in the Caribbean.
- **John Agard**: a poet born in Guyana, who moved to Britain in 1977.
:::

::: example The spelling
How does Agard use language to make his argument? Build the paragraph.

Point: Agard writes in Caribbean Creole so that the poem's voice is itself the resistance it describes.

Evidence: "Dem tell me / Dem tell me / Wha dem want to tell me"; "Bandage up me eye with me own history".

Analysis: "Dem", "wha", "me" for "my" — the spelling records speech, not error. In a poem about an education that told him his own history was not worth teaching, writing in his own English refuses the standard that education imposed. The repetition of "Dem tell me" makes "dem" an anonymous authority and the speaker a passive receiver — until the italic stanzas, where he does the telling.

Purpose: the form enacts the content: reclaiming history begins with reclaiming the language you tell it in.
:::

::: example Two forms
Contrast the two kinds of stanza.

The "Dem tell me" stanzas rhyme and bounce — "Dick Whittington and he cat", "de cow who jump over de moon" — the metre of nursery rhymes. Real history (1066, Columbus, Nightingale) is mixed with fairy tales, so the whole syllabus sounds like something told to a child to keep him quiet.

The italic stanzas are different on the page and in the ear: short unrhymed lines stepped downward — "Toussaint / a slave / with vision" — each line a stroke of a chisel. No jingle; an inscription.

Effect: the history he was given is made trivial by its rhythm; the history he was denied is given weight by its form and light by its imagery. The reader hears which one matters before understanding why.
:::

::: example Light and blindness
Analyse the imagery of sight.

The syllabus is a bandage: "Bandage up me eye with me own history / Blind me to me own identity". Education did not just omit; it covered his eyes with a false version and called it his.

The reclaimed figures are light sources: Toussaint "a beacon", Nanny "a fire-woman", Mary Seacole "a healing star" and "a yellow sunrise / to the dying". Each is something you see by.

Agard's argument in images: the imposed history blinds, the reclaimed history illuminates. The final stanza removes the bandage — "But now I checking out me own history" — and the verb "carving" makes the new sight an act of work, not a gift.
:::

::: example The ending
Explain "I carving out me identity".

The poem's last lines turn from "Dem tell me" to "I" — the grammatical subject changes hands. He is no longer being told; he is doing.

"Carving" is slow, effortful, permanent — identity cut into something hard, like a name into stone. It answers the earlier "Bandage": where they covered, he cuts.

And the present continuous — "I carving", "I checking" — means the work is ongoing. Agard does not present identity as recovered but as under construction; the poem ends mid-action, which is where a history still being reclaimed should end.
:::
