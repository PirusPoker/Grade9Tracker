## What it is

The play has one claim — **we are responsible for each other** — and three lenses through which it makes it: **class**, **gender** and **generation**. A theme question wants Priestley's argument, the evidence, and what the 1945 audience was meant to take from it. The girl at the centre of all three, Eva Smith, never appears; that absence is a choice and is worth a paragraph.

**Collective responsibility.** The Inspector's thesis: "We don't live alone. We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other." Against it, Birling's: "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own" — and his sneer at "community and all that nonsense", "as if we were all mixed up together like bees in a hive". Priestley structures the play so that Birling's philosophy is refuted by the plot: five separate, individually defensible acts — a sacking, a dismissal, an affair ended, a charity refused, a drunken encounter — add up to a death. No one killed her; everyone did. "Each of you helped to kill her," the Inspector says, and Eric repeats it: "we all helped to kill her". The chain is the argument.

**Class.** Eva Smith is working-class, and every Birling treats her as a category rather than a person:

- Birling: a worker who asked for "twenty-five shillings" instead of "twenty-two and six"; "if you don't come down sharply on some of these people, they'd soon be asking for the earth"; "cheap labour" — Sheila's correction: "they're people".
- Sheila: a shop assistant who could be dismissed on a customer's word — "I was absolutely furious … I told him that if they didn't get rid of that girl…". Class as the power to have someone removed.
- Gerald: a girl in the Palace bar to be rescued and kept — "young and fresh and charming" — and then dropped: her class made her available, and disposable.
- Mrs Birling: "Girls of that class"; "a girl of that sort"; and charity as class discipline — help given to the deserving, refused to the "impertinent".
- Eric: "I wasn't in love with her or anything"; the money he offered was stolen, and she refused it — the working-class girl has the play's only moral scruple about money.

Priestley's point is not that the Birlings are cruel but that the class system lets ordinary selfishness become lethal because the people at the bottom have no protection.

**Gender.** Three women, three positions:

- **Eva/Daisy**: a working woman with no power — sacked for organising, dismissed on a whim, kept as a mistress, refused by a charity run by a woman, made pregnant by a drunk. Every institution she meets is run by, or for, men; the one run by a woman is the harshest.
- **Sheila**: begins as a possession — "I'm not a child, don't forget", she says, and the engagement ring is the plot's opening gift; Birling's speeches barely include her; Gerald "was away all last summer" and she is expected not to ask. She ends by refusing the ring and telling her parents they are childish: the play's clearest growth is a woman's.
- **Sybil**: power exercised through respectability and charity — a woman who polices other women. Her contempt for Eva is class and gender at once: "as if a girl of that sort would ever refuse money".

Priestley shows a world in which women's security depends on men (marriage for Sheila, Gerald's rooms for Daisy) and in which a woman without a man is prey — Alderman Meggarty in the Palace bar. The Inspector's "young women counting their pennies in their dingy little back bedrooms" is the play's other half of the population.

**Generation.** The older Birlings are Priestley's pessimism — they cannot learn even when their own son is the father; the younger ones are his hope — "the younger ones … more impressionable", the Inspector says. Sheila and Eric accept responsibility when there is no longer any pressure to; that is the definition of having learned. Gerald, young but of the old order, is the warning that age is not enough.

**Eva Smith's absence.** She never appears; we never see the photograph; we do not even know her name is her name — Eva Smith, Daisy Renton, "Mrs Birling". The Inspector: "there are millions and millions of Eva Smiths and John Smiths". Priestley keeps her offstage so that she stays representative: she is every working girl, and the Birlings' inability to be sure it was one girl is the point — they treated every girl the same. Her silence is the play's loudest voice.

::: method
1. **State the claim** — collective responsibility — and set Birling's creed against the Inspector's in your first paragraph.
2. **Use the chain**: five acts, one death; "each of you helped to kill her".
3. **For class and gender, quote the labels**: "cheap labour", "girls of that class", "a girl of that sort" — the language is the prejudice.
4. **Make Eva's absence a method**: name it, quote "millions of Eva Smiths", say what it does.
5. **Finish with the audience**: 1945, the welfare state ahead, the Inspector's "we" addressed to the theatre.
:::

::: example Collective responsibility
How does Priestley present responsibility? Build the paragraph.

Point: Priestley presents responsibility as collective by making a death out of five acts that no one thought mattered.

Evidence: Birling's "look after himself and his own" against the Inspector's "We are responsible for each other"; and the structure — Birling sacks her, Sheila has her dismissed, Gerald leaves her, Sybil refuses her, Eric gets her pregnant — each one a small, ordinary decision.

Analysis: the plot is the argument. Individually every act is defensible (Birling: "I was quite justified"); together they are lethal. Priestley's method is arithmetic — the Inspector adds up what the family kept separate — and the sum is "Each of you helped to kill her."

Purpose: the 1945 audience is invited to add up its own small decisions. The play is not asking whether the Birlings are wicked; it is asking what a society of Birlings does to an Eva Smith.
:::

::: example Class
How does Priestley use language to expose the Birlings' attitude to class?

The Birlings never call Eva a person; they name her by category. Birling: "cheap labour", "some of these people"; Sybil: "girls of that class", "a girl of that sort"; Gerald: "women of the town".

Sheila's correction is the play's: "these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people." The word "people" is the whole argument in one syllable.

Priestley makes the prejudice audible so the audience hears it as language, not policy — and then makes the girl with the least power the only one who refuses stolen money, so that the class the Birlings despise turns out to hold the moral standard they lack. Sybil's disbelief — "as if a girl of that sort would ever refuse money" — condemns her, not Eva.
:::

::: example Gender
Discuss how Priestley presents Eva's position as a working woman.

Every stage of Eva's life is decided by someone else's power. She is sacked for asking for a rise; dismissed because a customer "was in a bad temper"; picked up in a bar where Alderman Meggarty had "wedged her into a corner"; kept as a mistress in rooms that were not hers; refused charity by a committee; left pregnant by a drunk.

The Inspector names the class she belongs to as female: "young women counting their pennies in their dingy little back bedrooms." Her only refuges are men, and every man leaves.

Priestley sets Sheila's arc against this. Sheila begins as the object of an engagement and ends refusing the ring — "I must think" — the one woman in the play who gains a choice. Eva had none. The contrast is the gender argument: in 1912 a woman's fate depended on which class she was born into and which men she met.
:::

::: example Eva's absence
Why does Priestley keep Eva Smith offstage?

She never appears, the photograph is never shown to the audience, and by the end the family cannot prove the five stories were about one girl.

Priestley wants that uncertainty. The Inspector says there are "millions and millions of Eva Smiths and John Smiths"; if she were on stage she would be one girl, and the Birlings could argue that this one was special or unlucky.

Offstage, she is all of them — and the family's confusion about whether it was one girl or several convicts them further: they treated every working girl the same. Her silence is also her dignity: she is the only character who is never made to perform for the audience, and the only one with a moral act to her name — refusing stolen money. Priestley's method is to make the absent centre the play's conscience.
:::

How does the plot's structure argue for collective responsibility?

Five separate, ordinary acts by five people add up to one death — "Each of you helped to kill her".

"they're people."

Why did Eva refuse Eric's money?

Give two phrases Mrs Birling uses that reveal class prejudice.

"Girls of that class"; "a girl of that sort".

::: q
How does Sheila's position as a woman change across the play?
---
She begins as the object of an engagement and ends refusing the ring — "I must think" — gaining a choice Eva never had.
:::

::: q
Which characters does Priestley present as able to change, and what does that say about generation?
---
Sheila and Eric — the young can learn; the older Birlings cannot; the future belongs to the "more impressionable" young.
:::

::: q
Why does the audience never see Eva Smith or her photograph?
---
So she remains representative — "millions of Eva Smiths" — and the family's confusion over whether it was one girl exposes how they treated all such girls alike.
:::

::: q
What was Priestley's 1945 audience about to do, and why does it matter?
---
Elect the Labour government that built the welfare state; the play argues for exactly that collective responsibility.
:::
