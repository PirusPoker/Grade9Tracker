## What it is

**Tissue** (Imtiaz Dharker, 2006) is a meditation on **paper** — the paper of holy books, maps, receipts, architects' plans — and on what would happen if buildings, borders and human power were as thin and translucent as paper. The title works three ways: tissue paper, the tissue of living skin, and the tissue of connections between things. Its subject is the **fragility of human power** and the beauty of that fragility: the poem argues *for* thinness, for light coming through, against the solid structures people build to last.

**The movement of the poem.** Dharker moves through kinds of paper, each carrying a kind of power:

| Stage | Paper | What it stands for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Paper that lets the light / shine through" — the thin pages of the Koran ("the Koran … well-used", handwritten names in the family record) | Sacred and personal record: religion, ancestry — paper that is precious because it is old and worn, "smoothed and stroked / and turned transparent with attention". |
| 2 | Maps: "the sun shines through / their borderlines" | Political power — borders drawn on paper; the light passing through them exposes them as lines, not walls. Rivers and roads shown as "the marks that rivers make". |
| 3 | Receipts: "Fine slips from grocery shops", "paid by credit card" | Economic power — money as paper; "might fly our lives like paper kites" — what we buy and owe could be let go. |
| 4 | Architecture: "An architect could use all this" — buildings made of paper, "never meant to last", "let the daylight break / through capitals and monoliths" | The reversal: if buildings were paper, light would enter the monuments of power; "capitals and monoliths" are the structures of state. |
| 5 | Skin: "turned into your skin" | The final transformation — paper becomes the living tissue of a human being; the fragile, translucent thing was us all along. |

**Paper as the metaphor for human power.** Every institution that claims permanence — religion recorded, nations mapped, wealth receipted, cities built — is made of paper in the poem, and paper is thin, tearable, and lets light through. Dharker does not lament this; she recommends it: "this / is what could alter things". The thinness is truth. Solid monuments pretend to last; paper admits it will not, and is more beautiful for it — like the worn Koran "turned transparent with attention".

**Transience and light.** "Light" and "shine" recur: light through pages, sun through borders, daylight through monoliths. Light is what passes through when things are thin enough — it stands for truth, for what is shared, for the life that goes on when structures fail. Transience — "never meant to last" — is presented as the condition of being alive, and the poem's ending ("turned into your skin") makes the reader the last, most fragile, most living page.

**Form — free verse that refuses to close.** Unrhymed, mostly four-line stanzas that do not correspond to sentences; enjambment runs across stanza breaks ("the sun shines through / their borderlines"); the tone is quiet and conditional — "could", "might", "if" — the grammar of possibility rather than statement. The final stanza is a single line: "turned into your skin." The poem does not resolve, and the form refuses to force it: no couplet, no rhyme, no conclusion — because the argument is against permanence. **Do not impose a neat ending** on it; say that the openness is the method.

**Context in a sentence.** Dharker was born in Pakistan, raised in Glasgow, lives between Britain and India; her work crosses borders and religions, and *Tissue* — from *The Terrorist at My Table* (2006) — turns that crossing into a vision of a world whose walls let light through.

::: method
1. **Name the metaphor**: paper = human power, in its four kinds (sacred, political, economic, architectural).
2. **Show the reversal**: not "power is fragile, alas" but "fragility is what could alter things" — quote the conditional.
3. **Light**: track it through the poem and say what passes through when things are thin.
4. **Form**: free verse, short stanzas, enjambment across breaks, a one-line ending — the poem refuses closure on purpose.
5. **The skin ending**: paper becomes the reader; say why that is where the argument arrives.
:::

::: example The metaphor
How does Dharker use paper to present human power? Build the paragraph.

Point: Dharker makes paper the material of every human structure so that all of them share its fragility.

Evidence: the Koran's pages "turned transparent with attention"; maps where "the sun shines through / their borderlines"; receipts "paid by credit card"; buildings an architect could make so that "daylight break[s] / through capitals and monoliths".

Analysis: each is a form of power — faith, nation, money, state — reduced to what it is physically written on. Borders are "lines"; wealth is "slips"; monuments are "monoliths" until the light gets in. The metaphor demotes every claim to permanence.

Purpose: Dharker, who lives between countries and cultures, presents thinness as honesty — the structures that admit they are paper are the ones that let light through.
:::

::: example Light
Track the image of light.

It begins with "Paper that lets the light / shine through" — the thin pages of an old holy book, worn by hands. Light here is reverence: the more the page is used, the more transparent it becomes.

Then the sun "shines through" the borderlines of maps: light exposes political lines as lines. In the architect's vision, "daylight break[s] / through capitals and monoliths" — the verb is "break": light as a gentle destroyer of the solid.

By the end light is what the human body admits — skin is tissue the light passes through. Dharker's light is truth and life together: what remains when structures are thin enough to be seen through, and what the poem asks us to let in.
:::

::: example The conditional
Analyse the grammar of the architect stanzas.

"An architect could use all this, / place layer over layer, luminous / script over numbers over line" — "could", not "does". Buildings "never meant to last" would let daylight through the monoliths.

The conditional mood runs through the poem: "might fly our lives like paper kites"; "this / is what could alter things". Nothing is asserted as fact; everything is offered as possibility.

Effect: the poem's argument is a proposal, not a description — what would happen if we built as paper builds. Dharker's refusal to state is consistent with her refusal to close: a poem against permanence cannot end in a declaration.
:::

::: example The ending
Why does the poem end "turned into your skin"?

The final stanza is one line, set apart. After paper of every kind — pages, maps, receipts, plans — the last transformation is into the reader's body.

Skin is tissue: living, thin, light-admitting, temporary. The metaphor turns round — it was never only about paper; the fragile, translucent, beautiful thing is the human being holding the poem.

And the poem stops there, without rhyme or summary. Dharker leaves the ending open because the argument is against things that pretend to last; a neat conclusion would be a monolith. The right reading says the openness is deliberate, and that the reader has become the last page.
:::
