## What it is

Lady Macbeth is Shakespeare's study of what ambition looks like in a woman who has no legitimate route to power. She reads her husband's letter, decides the murder in a heartbeat, drives it through his doubts — and then, having got everything she wanted, is destroyed by it more completely than he is. The best answers hold **two readings at once**: she is the play's driving force *and* its casualty.

**Her arc, with evidence:**

| Stage | Where | What Shakespeare shows |
|---|---|---|
| **The reader of the letter** | 1.5 | Her first judgement is of him: "too full o' th' milk of human kindness / To catch the nearest way". She sees the crown before he has said the word. |
| **The invocation** | 1.5 | "Come, you spirits / That tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here" — she asks to be stripped of womanhood, "Make thick my blood", "take my milk for gall". She believes she must become unnatural to do this. |
| **The planner** | 1.5–1.6 | "Look like th' innocent flower, / But be the serpent under 't." She greets Duncan with perfect courtesy — "All our service" — the serpent performing the flower. |
| **The manipulator** | 1.7 | When he withdraws, she attacks his manhood — "When you durst do it, then you were a man" — and his love: "Such I account thy love." The infant image: she would have "dash'd the brains out" of her own baby rather than break such a promise. He folds. |
| **The practical one** | 2.2 | Cool where he panics: "Give me the daggers"; "A little water clears us of this deed." But one crack — "Had he not resembled / My father as he slept, I had done 't." |
| **The excluded queen** | 3.2 | "Nought's had, all's spent, / Where our desire is got without content." He now plans without her: "Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck." She has to manage the banquet alone — "Are you a man?" — the same taunt, now failing. |
| **The sleepwalker** | 5.1 | Prose, fragments, repetition: "Out, damned spot! Out, I say!", "Here's the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand." The water that "clears" has become blood that cannot be washed. |
| **The offstage death** | 5.5 | "The Queen, my lord, is dead." Malcolm's "by self and violent hands". Shakespeare denies her a death scene: she disappears from the play as she has disappeared from Macbeth's mind. |

**Her language early and late.** Early she speaks in confident blank verse, imperatives and metaphors of control — "Leave all the rest to me." Late she speaks in **prose**, in broken phrases and questions — "The Thane of Fife had a wife: where is she now?" — replaying every murder, including one she did not commit. The change in form is the change in the woman.

**Jacobean expectations.** A wife was expected to be obedient, gentle, fertile and pious; ambition, command and violence were masculine. Lady Macbeth inverts every one: she commands her husband, calls on spirits (witchcraft was a capital crime and a royal obsession), rejects motherhood, and dominates the marriage. To a 1606 audience she would be monstrous — but Shakespeare then shows the monster is a performance that costs her everything, and the invocation to be "unsexed" turns out to have been a request the play refuses: her conscience is exactly what she asked to have removed.

**Two readings to hold together:** (1) she is the instigator — without her taunts in 1.7 Macbeth "will proceed no further"; (2) she is the victim — of a society that gives her no outlet, of a husband who abandons her once he no longer needs her, and of a guilt she never planned for. Neither reading alone gets to the top band.

::: method
1. **Compare early and late**: every good Lady Macbeth answer sets 1.5 or 1.7 beside 5.1 and names what has changed — verse to prose, commands to questions, water to blood.
2. **Show the manipulation working**: quote the taunt and quote Macbeth's surrender ("I am settled") so the cause and effect are on the page.
3. **Bring in gender as method, not history**: "Shakespeare has her ask to be unsexed because…", linked to a quotation.
4. **Hold both readings**: use "and yet" or "at the same time" to turn a driving-force paragraph into a casualty paragraph.
5. **Notice absences**: no death scene, no last words, dismissed in a subordinate clause. What Shakespeare withholds is evidence.
:::

::: example The invocation
How does Shakespeare present Lady Macbeth in "unsex me here"? Build the paragraph.

Point: Shakespeare presents her ambition as something she believes requires the destruction of her own nature.

Evidence: the imperatives — "Come", "unsex me", "Make thick my blood", "Stop up th' access and passage to remorse" — are addressed to "spirits", which to a Jacobean audience is a witch's summoning; and what she asks for is the removal of pity and of femininity, "take my milk for gall".

Analysis: "remorse" is the key word — she is not asking for courage but for the removal of conscience, which is exactly what she will fail to lose. The dramatic irony only becomes visible in Act 5, when remorse returns in the sleepwalking.

Purpose: Shakespeare makes her transgressive in the specific ways James I's audience feared — witchcraft, unnatural womanhood — and then shows the transgression fail. She cannot be unsexed; she can only be broken.
:::

::: example The manipulation
Explain how Shakespeare shows Lady Macbeth persuading Macbeth in Act 1 Scene 7.

Macbeth has just decided against the murder: "We will proceed no further in this business." Shakespeare then gives Lady Macbeth three weapons in sequence.

First, contempt for his courage and love: "Art thou afeard / To be the same in thine own act and valour / As thou art in desire?" — she splits his wish from his will and calls the gap cowardice.

Second, manhood: "When you durst do it, then you were a man." Masculinity is redefined as the willingness to kill — a definition Macbeth accepts and lives by for the rest of the play ("Bring forth men-children only", he answers, admiringly).

Third, the baby: she would have "dash'd the brains out" of her nursing child sooner than break her word. The image is so violent that his last objection — "If we should fail?" — is practical, not moral. Shakespeare shows a will overpowered, and the moment is his: "I am settled."
:::

::: example Early against late
Compare Lady Macbeth's language in Act 2 Scene 2 with Act 5 Scene 1.

Act 2: she speaks in controlled blank verse and instructs — "Give me the daggers", "A little water clears us of this deed: / How easy is it, then!" Guilt is a practical problem with a practical solution.

Act 5: she speaks in prose, in fragments and questions: "Out, damned spot!", "What, will these hands ne'er be clean?", "Here's the smell of the blood still." The water has failed; the hand is the same hand.

Analysis: Shakespeare gives her the same images in reverse — water, hands, blood — so that the audience hears Act 2 being answered. The move from verse to prose is the loss of control made audible; the Doctor's "unnatural deeds / Do breed unnatural troubles" delivers the play's verdict on her invocation.
:::

::: example Both readings
Write a concluding paragraph that holds "driving force" and "casualty" together.

Shakespeare makes Lady Macbeth the engine of the first two acts: without her, Macbeth's "We will proceed no further" would have stood, and the play would have ended in 1.7.

And yet from Act 3 she is shut out — "Be innocent of the knowledge" — and the ambition she set in motion runs on without her. The murders she did not commit (Banquo, Lady Macduff) are the ones she replays in her sleep.

So she is both cause and casualty, and the two are connected: the strength she performed was the thing that isolated her, and when the performance ended there was no one left to see the collapse — not even her husband, who greets her death with "She should have died hereafter." Shakespeare's verdict is not that she was evil but that she was consumed by what she summoned.
:::

::: q
What is Lady Macbeth's first judgement of her husband in Act 1 Scene 5?
---
That he is "too full o' th' milk of human kindness / To catch the nearest way".
:::

::: q
Quote the line in which she asks to have her womanhood removed.
---
"Come, you spirits / That tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here."
:::

::: q
What image does she use to tell Macbeth how to behave in front of Duncan?
---
"Look like th' innocent flower, / But be the serpent under 't."
:::

::: q
How does she attack Macbeth's masculinity in Act 1 Scene 7?
---
"When you durst do it, then you were a man" — she defines manhood as the willingness to kill.
:::

::: q
Which line in Act 2 Scene 2 does the sleepwalking scene overturn?
---
"A little water clears us of this deed" — in Act 5 "all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand".
:::

::: q
Give one sign in Act 2 that Lady Macbeth is not as hard as she claims.
---
She could not kill Duncan herself because he "resembled / My father as he slept".
:::

::: q
What does "Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck" show about the marriage in Act 3?
---
Macbeth now plans murders without her; she has been excluded from the partnership she created.
:::

::: q
Why does she speak in prose in Act 5 Scene 1?
---
Prose marks the collapse of control — the ordered verse of her earlier commands has broken into fragments and questions.
:::

::: q
How does Shakespeare treat her death, and what is the effect?
---
Offstage, reported in one line ("The Queen, my lord, is dead"); she vanishes from the play as she has vanished from Macbeth's concern.
:::

::: q
Name two Jacobean expectations of women that Lady Macbeth inverts.
---
Obedience to a husband (she commands him); gentleness and motherhood (she rejects both — "unsex me", the infant image).
:::

- Show the manipulation **working** — quote her taunt and his surrender.

Explain the contrast between 'A little water clears us of this deed' and 'all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand'. (3)
---
In Act 2 she believes guilt washes off (1); in Act 5 nothing can remove it — the smell of blood is permanent (1); the same hand, the same 'little', but the confidence has become obsession (1).

::: test
What does 'Nought's had, all's spent, / Where our desire is got without content' reveal? (2)
---
She has everything she wanted and no joy from it (1); the first sign that the crown has cost her more than it gave (1).
:::

::: test
Give two features of the sleepwalking scene's language and what each shows. (2)
---
Prose and broken fragments — her control and status have collapsed (1); repetition ('Out, damned spot! Out, I say!') — her mind circling the same guilt (1).
:::

::: test
What is the effect of the Doctor and Gentlewoman watching her in Act 5 Scene 1? (2)
---
Her private guilt is witnessed and reported — the secret is out (1); their helplessness ('More needs she the divine than the physician') shows this is a sin, not an illness (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a Jacobean audience would have reacted to 'unsex me here'. (2)
---
A wife calling on spirits to strip her womanhood is both unnatural and close to witchcraft (1); it would have been shocking and sinful, marking her as dangerous from the start (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how Lady Macbeth can be read as both the play's driving force and its casualty. (2)
---
In Act 1 she plans the murder and pushes Macbeth through his doubts (1); by Act 5 she is excluded, sleepless and dies offstage, destroyed by what she began (1).
