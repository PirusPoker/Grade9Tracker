## What it is

**London** (William Blake, 1794, from *Songs of Experience*) is a walk through the capital by a speaker who sees suffering in every face and hears it in every sound — and who blames not fate but **institutions**: the crown, the church, the law, the marriage market. It is a dramatic monologue in four quatrains, the shape of a hymn or a nursery rhyme, carrying an accusation.

**The journey.** The speaker "wander[s]" — stanza 1 sees; stanza 2 hears; stanza 3 names the guilty institutions; stanza 4 arrives at night and the "Harlot", where the poem's anger is at its most bitter. Tracking that movement through the stanzas gives structure marks cheaply: the poem narrows from streets to faces to sounds to a single curse.

| Quotation | Analysis |
|---|---|
| "I wander thro' each charter'd street, / Near where the charter'd Thames does flow" | "Charter'd" twice: chartered = licensed, owned, mapped. Even the river is property. The repetition is the point — everything, including nature, has been bought. |
| "And mark in every face I meet / Marks of weakness, marks of woe" | "Mark" as verb (notice) then noun (sign) — the speaker's noticing becomes the people's scars. "Every" begins the poem's habit of totality. |
| "In every cry of every Man, / In every Infant's cry of fear, / In every voice, in every ban" | "Every" seven times across two stanzas — anaphora that leaves no exception; the rhythm becomes a chant, or a march. |
| "The mind-forg'd manacles I hear" | The poem's central image: the chains are made ("forg'd") in the mind — by ideology, by acceptance, by fear — and they are *heard*, in the cries. Blake's argument: oppression is internalised; people wear the chains they were taught to accept. "Forg'd" also means faked. |
| "How the Chimney-sweeper's cry / Every blackning Church appalls" | The child sweep (Blake's recurring victim) against the church that should protect him — "blackning" from soot and from moral corruption; "appalls" = shocks, and also *pales* (from *pall*, a shroud): the church is shamed, or whitened like a corpse. |
| "And the hapless Soldier's sigh / Runs in blood down Palace walls" | The soldier dies for a monarchy that stays behind walls; the sigh becomes blood — sound made visible, guilt on the building. Written during the French Revolution, when palace walls had recently run with blood. |
| "But most thro' midnight streets I hear / How the youthful Harlot's curse / Blasts the new-born Infant's tear / And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse" | The climax: a teenage prostitute's curse (her swearing, and the venereal disease she carries) reaches the baby and the marriage. "Marriage hearse" — an oxymoron: marriage as death; the institution of marriage produces the prostitution it condemns. |

**Form and sound.** Four quatrains, ABAB, iambic tetrameter — a **regular, simple form** like a hymn or a ballad, so that the ugliness is delivered in a nursery rhythm. The regularity mimics the "manacles": the form marches; the repetition of "every" and "cry" and "charter'd" is the sound of a system that repeats itself. The final stanza's verbs — "Blasts", "blights" — are plosive, violent, and end on the oxymoron.

**Context, one sentence each.** Blake was a radical, hostile to organised religion, monarchy and commerce; *Songs of Experience* pairs with *Songs of Innocence* to show what the world does to the innocent. 1794: the French Revolution five years old, Britain at war with France, fear of revolt at home — and child chimney sweeps, press-ganged soldiers, and a London in which the poor were visibly dying.

::: method
1. **Track the walk** stanza by stanza — sight, sound, institutions, night — for structure marks.
2. **"Mind-forg'd manacles" is your centre**: explain forged (made / faked), mind (internalised), heard (in the cries).
3. **Institutions by name**: church, palace, marriage — each with its victim (sweep, soldier, harlot/infant).
4. **Form as meaning**: the hymn-like regularity and the anaphora of "every" enact the system's totality.
5. **Two quotations cold**: "mind-forg'd manacles", "Marriage hearse".
:::

::: example The central image
Analyse "The mind-forg'd manacles I hear". Build the paragraph.

Point: Blake's central claim is that oppression is not only imposed but internalised — the chains are made in the mind.

Evidence: "manacles" are literal restraints; "mind-forg'd" says they are manufactured by thought — by the ideas people are taught (obedience, deference, sin).

Analysis: "forg'd" carries a second sense, *faked*: the chains are counterfeit, which means they could be broken by seeing through them. And they are *heard*, not seen — in "every cry", the sound of people accepting their condition. Blake makes the abstract (ideology) audible.

Purpose: for a radical in 1794, the line is a diagnosis and a call: the revolution has to happen in the mind before it can happen in the street.
:::

::: example The journey as structure
How does Blake structure the poem?

Stanza 1 is sight: the speaker "wander[s]" the "charter'd" streets and "mark[s]" faces. Stanza 2 is sound: cries, voices, bans — and the manacles. The poem moves from what can be seen to what can only be heard, from surfaces to the system.

Stanza 3 names the institutions and their victims in pairs — sweep/church, soldier/palace. Stanza 4 goes to "midnight streets" and the harlot: the day's walk ends in the dark, at the point where the system produces its own destruction, the "Marriage hearse".

Effect: the poem narrows and darkens. Tracking the movement shows Blake building an argument, not a list — and the final stanza's "But most" signals the climax as a deliberate ranking of horrors.
:::

::: example The final stanza
Explain "blights with plagues the Marriage hearse".

The harlot's "curse" is both her swearing and the disease she carries; it "blasts" the infant (born blind or diseased) and "blights" the marriage — the husband who visited her brings the plague home.

"Marriage hearse" is an oxymoron: the carriage of a wedding becomes the carriage of a funeral. Blake's point is that the institution of marriage — loveless, economic — creates the demand for prostitution and then is destroyed by it.

The stanza's verbs are plosive and violent; the poem ends not on a resolution but on a system feeding on itself. Blake refuses the reader a way out — which is why the poem is placed in *Experience*, not *Innocence*.
:::

::: example Form
How does the form work against the content?

Four regular quatrains, ABAB, four beats a line: the shape of a hymn or a nursery rhyme, the forms Blake's readers learned as children in church and at home.

Into that innocent form Blake puts sweeps, soldiers' blood and venereal disease. The regularity does two things: it makes the horror sing, so it cannot be skipped; and it mimics the "manacles" — the metre is locked, the repetitions ("every", "charter'd", "cry") are the system's monotony.

Effect: the form is the cage, and the content is what it holds. The last line's oxymoron breaks the sense while the metre goes on — the machine keeps marching over the corpse.
:::

What does "charter'd" mean, and why is it repeated?
---
Licensed, owned, mapped for profit; repeating it for the street and the Thames shows even nature is property.
:::

---
The verb (to notice) and the noun (a sign or scar) — the speaker's noticing becomes the people's wounds.

Seven — anaphora that admits no exception; the suffering is total and the rhythm becomes a chant.
