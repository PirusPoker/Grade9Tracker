## What it is

**Remains** (Simon Armitage, 2008) is a soldier's monologue about shooting a looter in Iraq and then being unable to stop seeing it. Armitage wrote it for a documentary (*The Not Dead*) from the testimony of a real Guardsman, so the voice is colloquial, conversational, almost casual — and the casualness is the point: the killing is told in a few lines, and **living with the killing takes the rest of the poem**. That imbalance is the poem's argument about trauma.

**Two halves.**

**The event (stanzas 1–4).** "On another occasion" — the poem opens as if mid-anecdote, one story among many. The looter is running from a bank; three soldiers open fire; the speaker is careful to spread the responsibility — "all three of us", "myself and somebody else and somebody else" — and then to note the ambiguity that will never leave him: the looter was "probably armed, possibly not". The killing is described physically and flatly: the rounds "rip through his life", the body is "sort of inside out", "tossed" into a lorry. Then: "End of story, except not really."

**The aftermath (stanzas 5–8).** The event returns — "his bloody life in my bloody hands" — first as a memory when he walks over the spot, then at home, "on leave", where the man is "dug in behind enemy lines" in the speaker's head. Drink and drugs cannot "flush him out". The final couplet: "his bloody life in my bloody hands" — repeated from the middle — and the poem ends still holding the body.

| Feature | Evidence | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Colloquial voice** | "On another occasion", "legs it", "sort of inside out", "End of story, except not really" | A soldier talking, not a poet composing — the everyday register makes the horror ordinary, and shows a man who has no elevated language for what he did. |
| **Monologue / testimony** | First person throughout; the poem addresses no one in particular | Confession with no priest — he tells it because he cannot stop telling it. |
| **Ambiguity** | "probably armed, possibly not" — repeated | The doubt is the wound: if the looter was unarmed, it was murder; if armed, it was war. The speaker can never know, so the question loops. |
| **Distributed blame** | "all three of us", "somebody else and somebody else" | He shares the shooting, but the memory is his alone — the rounds may have been anyone's, the image is not. |
| **"Bloody"** | "his bloody life in my bloody hands" | Both a swear-word and literal blood — the colloquial and the graphic in one word. Macbeth's blood on hands stands behind it. |
| **Military metaphor for the mind** | "dug in behind enemy lines", "flush him out" | The looter has invaded his head and holds a position there; the war continues inside him. |
| **Structure** | Four-line stanzas with loose, irregular rhythm; the last two lines a separate couplet | The quatrains keep a soldier's order; the isolated couplet is what will not fit — the residue, the "remains". |
| **Enjambment across stanzas** | "and I swear // I see every round" | The memory will not stay inside its stanza — it spills, as it spills into his life. |

**Guilt and post-traumatic stress.** The poem is a case study in PTSD without ever naming it: intrusive memory ("every round as it rips through his life / I see broad daylight on the other side"), avoidance failing ("the drink and the drugs won't flush him out"), the return of the image in sleep and on leave. Armitage's title carries it: *remains* as the body, as what is left of the speaker, and as what remains — permanently — in his mind. "Some distant, sun-stunned, sand-smothered land" — the alliteration flattens Iraq into a place with no name; the war has come home instead.

**Context in a sentence.** From *The Not Dead* (2008), a documentary about veterans' trauma, based on the words of Guardsman Tromans who served in Basra; Armitage gives testimony the shape of a poem without smoothing the voice.

::: example Two halves
How does Armitage structure the poem to present trauma? Build the paragraph.

Point: Armitage makes the aftermath larger than the event, so the structure itself says what trauma is.

Evidence: the shooting is over by the end of stanza 3 — "End of story, except not really" — and the remaining five stanzas are the looter returning: on patrol, "on leave", "in my head".

Analysis: "End of story" is where a war anecdote should stop; "except not really" is where the poem actually begins. The killing was seconds; the memory is permanent, and the poem's proportions are the proportions of the speaker's life.

Purpose: Armitage is writing for a documentary about veterans — the argument is that the war's real damage is done afterwards, at home, where no one can see it.
:::

::: example The ambiguity
Analyse "probably armed, possibly not".

The phrase appears twice — once in the event, once in the aftermath — and both times it is the whole moral weight of the poem in four words.

"Probably" and "possibly" are the language of estimation: the speaker is still trying to calculate a thing that cannot be resolved. If armed, the looter was a combatant; if not, he was a thief shot dead for stealing. The speaker fired without knowing and will never know.

Armitage makes the doubt itself the trauma. It is not the image alone that returns but the question attached to it — and a question with no answer cannot be finished, which is why the poem ends still holding it.
:::

::: example Voice
Explain the effect of the colloquial language.

"Legs it up the road", "sort of inside out", "tossed" into a lorry: the vocabulary is a squaddie's, not a poet's. Armitage keeps the real Guardsman's register.

The effect is double. First, authenticity — the reader believes this happened because no one would invent "sort of" for a disembowelment. Second, the mismatch between the casual words and the appalling content shows a man without a language for what he did; he tells it as an anecdote because that is the only form he has.

Then the register cracks: "his bloody life in my bloody hands" is both swearing and blood, and it is the one line he repeats. The everyday voice can carry the killing but not the guilt — that needs to be said twice.
:::

::: example The enemy inside
How does Armitage present the mind after the event?

The looter is "dug in behind enemy lines" — a military phrase for a position that cannot be taken; the speaker's head has become the battlefield and the dead man holds it.

"The drink and the drugs won't flush him out" — another military verb, for clearing a position, and it fails. The war's language has followed the speaker home because the war has.

The ending places the body in the speaker's hands and leaves it there: "his bloody life in my bloody hands", set apart as a final couplet — the remains that will not fit into the stanzas, or the life. Armitage does not resolve it because the soldier cannot.
:::
