## What it is

Guilt in *Macbeth* is not a feeling the characters describe — Shakespeare makes it **physical**: it is on their hands, it keeps them awake, it stands at the table. The supernatural works the same way: it is atmosphere (the witches, the storm, the dark), but it is also **moral disorder made visible** — the outward sign that the natural order has been broken.

**Guilt made physical:**

| Image | Where | What Shakespeare does with it |
|---|---|---|
| **Blood** | 2.2, 3.4, 5.1 | "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine." Guilt is ineradicable and spreading — the hand dyes the sea, not the sea the hand. Later "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far"; and her "Out, damned spot!" |
| **Hands** | 2.2, 5.1 | "What hands are here? Ha! they pluck out mine eyes." Then "A little water clears us of this deed" — answered by "all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand". The hands are the same; what changes is who can bear to look at them. |
| **Sleep** | 2.2, 3.2, 5.1 | "Macbeth does murder sleep … the innocent sleep". He has killed a sleeping man and killed his own rest: "Macbeth shall sleep no more." Lady Macbeth's punishment is sleep without rest — sleepwalking, "a great perturbation in nature". |
| **Hallucination** | 2.1, 3.4 | The dagger — "a false creation, / Proceeding from the heat-oppressed brain"; Banquo's ghost, whom only Macbeth sees. Shakespeare stages the conscience: guilt appears as a thing in the room. |
| **The unsaid "Amen"** | 2.3 | "I could not say 'Amen' … I had most need of blessing, and 'Amen' / Stuck in my throat." Guilt cuts him off from God at once. |

**The witches: cause or revelation?** The evidence that they *cause*: they know the future; they appear at the moments of decision; they "juggle" him with equivocation in Act 4. The evidence that they *reveal*: they never instruct — the greeting "king hereafter" is a fact, not a command; Macbeth's reaction is his own ("why do I yield"); Banquo's warning shows the danger is in how men use the truth — "The instruments of darkness tell us truths, / Win us with honest trifles, to betray's / In deepest consequence." The play's answer: the witches are catalysts, not causes. They tell Macbeth what he already wants to hear and let his ambition do the rest.

**Supernatural as atmosphere**: the play opens in "thunder and lightning" with three figures who "look not like th' inhabitants o' th' earth"; it is set largely at night; "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" announces a world where values are inverted. Shakespeare's audience believed in witches — James I had written *Daemonologie* (1597) and presided over witch trials — so the atmosphere is not decoration; it is a real threat.

**Supernatural as disorder**: after the murder, nature revolts — darkness at noon, the owl and the falcon, the horses eating each other (2.4). Lennox's "The night has been unruly … strange screams of death". These are not the witches' doing; they are the universe reacting to regicide. The **Great Chain of Being** — God, king, nobles, commons, beasts — has been snapped at the second link, and everything below it convulses. The supernatural is therefore Shakespeare's way of showing that killing a king is a crime against creation, not merely a crime.

**Fate and the equivocation of Act 4**: "none of woman born", "until Great Birnam wood" — true statements engineered to mislead. Macbeth's error is to hear a promise where there is only a riddle: "That will never be." Shakespeare lets him trust the supernatural absolutely at the moment it is deceiving him most.

::: terms
- **Motif**: a recurring image or idea that develops a theme — blood, sleep, darkness in Macbeth.
- **Imagery**: descriptive language that creates pictures in the reader's or audience's mind.
- **The supernatural**: forces beyond nature — the witches, the dagger, Banquo's ghost, the apparitions.
- **Pathetic fallacy**: weather or nature reflecting human events — the storm and unnatural events after Duncan's murder.
- **Equivocation**: speaking in half-truths or with double meanings to deceive — the witches' prophecies.
- **Hallucination**: seeing what is not there — the dagger and the ghost show Macbeth's guilty mind.
:::

::: method
1. **Follow one image across the play**: blood or hands or sleep, in three scenes, showing how its meaning shifts (water clears → water fails; sleep murdered → sleep without rest).
2. **Argue the witches** rather than decide: cause and revelation, with Banquo as the control experiment.
3. **Separate the two supernatural jobs** — atmosphere (fear, night, "fair is foul") and moral disorder (nature revolting) — and say which the extract is doing.
4. **Tie disorder to the king**: name the Great Chain of Being in one clause, then show it breaking in a quotation.
5. **Use "physical"**: guilt is on hands, in blood, in sleep — say Shakespeare *embodies* it, and why that works on stage.
:::

::: example Following the blood
How does Shakespeare use blood to present guilt across the play? Build the paragraph.

Point: Shakespeare uses blood as the physical form of guilt, and its meaning grows as the play goes on.

In 2.2 blood is horror — "this my hand will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine": the hyperbole says the stain is bigger than the ocean; one hand dyes the world.

In 3.4 blood is momentum — "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o'er": guilt has become a river Macbeth has decided to cross rather than recross. The horror has gone; only the arithmetic remains.

In 5.1 blood is the thing that cannot be washed — "Here's the smell of the blood still." Lady Macbeth, who said "A little water clears us", now smells what no water reaches. Shakespeare passes the image from husband to wife so the audience watches guilt travel between them.
:::

::: example The witches
Do the witches cause Macbeth's downfall? Argue in one paragraph.

Shakespeare gives the witches real foreknowledge — Cawdor arrives within the scene — so a Jacobean audience, for whom witches were real, would feel their power.

But he is careful about what they say: "All hail, Macbeth, that shalt be king hereafter" is a prophecy, not a plan. The first mention of murder comes from Macbeth himself, in an aside — "my thought, whose murder yet is but fantastical". Banquo hears the same words and warns against them.

Conclusion: the witches reveal a desire and supply the occasion; Macbeth supplies the deed. Shakespeare keeps the responsibility on the human side of the line, which is why the play is a tragedy and not a fairy tale.
:::

::: example Disorder in nature
The extract is Act 2 Scene 4 (the Old Man and Ross). How does Shakespeare present the supernatural here?

This scene contains no witches. What Shakespeare shows instead is nature itself behaving unnaturally: "by th' clock 'tis day, / And yet dark night strangles the travelling lamp"; a falcon "was by a mousing owl hawk'd at and kill'd"; Duncan's horses "eat each other".

Each image reverses a hierarchy — night over day, the weak bird over the noble, animals against their kind. Shakespeare is showing the Great Chain of Being convulsing because its second link, the king, has been cut.

So the supernatural here is not atmosphere but judgement: the universe registers the regicide before any human has accused Macbeth. Ross's "unnatural, / Even like the deed that's done" makes the link explicit. To an audience that believed a king was God's deputy, this is the world confirming what they already knew.
:::

::: example Sleep
Explain how Shakespeare uses sleep to present guilt.

The moment after the murder, Macbeth hears a voice: "Macbeth does murder sleep" — and Shakespeare gives him a whole catalogue of what sleep is: "the innocent sleep, / Sleep that knits up the ravell'd sleeve of care … Chief nourisher in life's feast."

The point is exact: he killed a man asleep, and the punishment fits — "Macbeth shall sleep no more." Guilt is sleeplessness; by Act 3 they "sleep / In the affliction of these terrible dreams / That shake us nightly."

Lady Macbeth's version is worse: she sleeps but does not rest, walking and washing "a quarter of an hour" every night. The Doctor's diagnosis — "unnatural deeds / Do breed unnatural troubles" — is the play's law of guilt: the crime returns in the body.
:::

Guilt cuts him off from grace — "'Amen' / Stuck in my throat" — he is already damned in his own mind.
