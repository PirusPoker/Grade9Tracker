## What it is

Priestley's characters are **attitudes on legs**. Each Birling stands for a position on responsibility, and the play sorts them by **generation**: the older Birlings cannot learn; the younger ones can. Gerald, older than Sheila and Eric but younger than her parents, is the hinge — and his choice at the end tells you which side the play thinks he belongs to. Treating "the Birlings" as one group throws away Priestley's whole argument.

| Character | Stands for | Evidence (short quotations for a closed-book paper) | Learns? |

| **Arthur Birling** | Capitalist individualism; the self-made man; the complacent Edwardian | "hard-headed, practical man of business"; "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own"; the Titanic "unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable"; on Eva: "I can't accept any responsibility"; "there'll be a public scandal"; on Eric's theft — his first concern is the money and his knighthood. | **No.** Ends laughing: "the whole thing's different now"; "Look, you'd better ask Gerald for that ring you gave back to him". |
| **Sybil Birling** | Class prejudice made respectable; charity as power; denial | "Girls of that class"; "a girl of that sort"; "I accept no blame for it at all"; "I did nothing I'm ashamed of"; demands the father be made "an example of" — her own son. Her only shock is the word "Eric". | **No.** "He certainly didn't make me confess"; refuses to see any of it as real once the Inspector is exposed. |
| **Sheila Birling** | The capacity to change; the audience's conscience on stage | Starts "very pleased with life"; "But these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people"; sees the Inspector's method before anyone — "he's giving us the rope — so that we'll hang ourselves"; returns Gerald's ring; "I'm not a child, don't forget"; at the end, "it's you two who are being childish". | **Yes.** The play's model of a person who accepts responsibility and keeps it after the pressure is gone. |
| **Eric Birling** | The damage done by the older generation; guilt without the vocabulary for it | "half shy, half assertive"; drinks — "I was in that state when a chap easily turns nasty"; "you're not the kind of father a chap could go to when he's in trouble"; steals to help her; "the money's not the important thing. It's what happened to the girl … that matters"; "we all helped to kill her". | **Yes**, painfully — and turns on his parents: "You're beginning to pretend now that nothing's really happened at all." |
| **Gerald Croft** | The upper class's charm and its limits; the older generation's values in a younger man | Sees Daisy as "young and fresh and charming"; kept her "as my mistress", "I didn't feel about her as she felt about me"; the only one who showed her kindness, and the only one whose kindness came with an ending. Leaves, investigates the Inspector, comes back triumphant — "there isn't any such inspector". | **No.** Offers Sheila the ring again; sides with the parents. His moment of feeling — "she died a rather terrible death" — does not survive the evening. |

**The generational split** is the point. Priestley wrote in 1945 for an audience about to vote in the Labour landslide: the old order (Arthur, Sybil) is shown as unteachable; the young (Sheila, Eric) as the future. The dividing line is not what each did — Sheila's act was petty, Eric's serious — but whether they **accept responsibility when the external pressure is removed**. That is the test the Inspector's hoax exposes, and it is why the ending sends the Inspector back: the older generation needed a real one.

**Gerald between the generations.** By age he belongs with Sheila and Eric; by class and instinct with Arthur — Birling's "the son-in-law I always wanted", the Croft family "older and richer" than the Birlings. Priestley uses him to show that youth alone does not guarantee change: it is a choice, and Gerald chooses the ring and the old world. Sheila's refusal — "No, not yet. It's too soon. I must think" — is the play's last act of responsibility.

**Who accepts responsibility?** Track it explicitly: Sheila (fully, early); Eric (fully, late); Gerald (briefly, then withdraws); Arthur (never — "a man has to make his own way"); Sybil (never — she is the only character who refuses even when confronted with her own son). The pattern is the argument.

::: method
1. **Sort by generation first**: two who learn, two who do not, one who chooses not to. State this in your first paragraph.
2. **One attitude per character**: Birling = individualism; Sybil = class; Sheila = conscience; Eric = damage; Gerald = charm without change.
3. **Quote short and precise** — three per character, embedded. This is the closed-book paper.
4. **Test them by the ending**: what each says after the Inspector is exposed is the evidence of whether they learned.
5. **Make Gerald a paragraph on his own** — his choice is Priestley's point about youth and class.
:::

::: example Arthur Birling
How does Priestley present Mr Birling in Act 1? Build the paragraph.

Point: Priestley makes Birling the voice of Edwardian capitalism and undermines him with the audience's hindsight.

Evidence: "The Titanic … unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable"; war "impossible" because "the Germans don't want war"; "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own".

Analysis: every prediction is wrong, and the 1945 audience knows it — the Titanic sank in 1912, the war came in 1914. Priestley discredits Birling's economics by attaching them to his prophecies: if he is wrong about ships and wars, he is wrong about "his own". The Inspector's doorbell interrupts him mid-creed.

Purpose: Birling is not a villain but a type — the "hard-headed" businessman the audience might have respected — and Priestley needs him laughable before the play can make him culpable.
:::

::: example The generational split
Contrast the parents' and children's reactions once the Inspector is exposed.

Arthur and Sybil are relieved: "the whole thing's different now"; Birling laughs and mimics the Inspector; Sybil says "he certainly didn't make me confess". For them, no inspector means no guilt.

Sheila and Eric refuse the relief. Sheila: "it doesn't much matter … whether it's just a bit of a hoax or not"; Eric: "the fact remains that I did what I did … and mother did what she did". The girl is still dead, or a girl is.

Analysis: Priestley makes the test of character the moment the pressure lifts. The children hold to responsibility without a policeman; the parents never had it. "It's you two who are being childish" reverses the family's hierarchy — the young are now the adults. The final phone call then punishes the ones who needed to be watched.
:::

::: example Gerald
Where does Gerald belong, and how does Priestley show it?

Priestley gives Gerald the most sympathetic confession: he rescued Daisy from Alderman Meggarty, gave her money and a home, and "she told me she'd been happier than she'd ever been before". Sheila even says "that's probably the best thing you've done".

But his kindness had a limit — "I didn't feel about her as she felt about me" — and when it was inconvenient it ended. He then leaves the house, checks the Inspector's credentials, and returns with the loophole: "there isn't any such inspector." His energy goes into escaping the charge, not answering it.

Conclusion: by age Gerald is young; by choice he is old. Offering Sheila the ring — "Everything's all right now" — shows he has understood nothing, and her refusal is Priestley's verdict on him.
:::

::: example Sybil Birling
How does Priestley use Mrs Birling to attack class prejudice?

Sybil's language sorts people by class before she knows anything about them: "Girls of that class", "a girl of that sort", "as if a girl of that sort would ever refuse money".

Her charity is a form of power — she can grant or refuse — and she refuses because the girl used her name: "impertinent". The girl's honesty in refusing stolen money is, to Sybil, proof of "elaborate fine feelings … that were simply absurd in a girl in her position".

Priestley's irony is total: she demands the father be made an example of, and the father is Eric. The one person who "accepts no blame" is the one whose prejudice finally condemns her own family. Her refusal to learn even then — "I was perfectly justified" — makes her the play's hardest case, and its clearest warning.
:::

::: q
Quote two of Mr Birling's confident predictions in Act 1.
---
The Titanic is "unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable"; war is impossible — "the Germans don't want war".
:::

::: q
What is Mr Birling's creed of responsibility?
---
"a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own."
:::

::: q
Why did Mrs Birling refuse to help the girl?
---
Because the girl called herself "Mrs Birling", which Sybil found "impertinent", and because of her prejudice against "a girl of that sort".
:::

::: q
Quote Sheila's line that recognises the girls as people.
---
"But these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people."
:::

How does Sheila describe the Inspector's method?
---
"he's giving us the rope — so that we'll hang ourselves."
:::

::: q
What does Eric say to his father about their relationship?
---
"you're not the kind of father a chap could go to when he's in trouble."
:::

::: q
What does Gerald do when he leaves the house in Act 3?
---
He checks with a police sergeant and finds there is no Inspector Goole on the force.
:::

::: q
How do Sheila and Eric respond to the news that the Inspector may be a hoax?
---
They refuse to treat it as a let-off — "the fact remains that I did what I did"; the girl is still dead.
:::

::: q
Which characters accept responsibility by the end of the play?
---
Sheila and Eric; Gerald briefly and then withdraws; Arthur and Sybil never.
:::

::: q
Why must you not write about "the Birlings" as a single group?
---
The play's argument is the generational split — the older generation cannot learn, the younger can — and grouping them erases it.
:::
