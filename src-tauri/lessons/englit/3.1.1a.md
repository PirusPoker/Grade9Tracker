## What it is

*Macbeth* (c. 1606) is Shakespeare's shortest tragedy: a loyal Scottish general hears a prophecy that he will be king, murders his way to the crown, and is destroyed by what the crown costs him. Paper 1 gives you an **extract** and asks you to write about it **and the play as a whole** — so you need the whole shape in your head, act by act, with a quotation ready for every turning point.

| Act | What happens | Turning point and a quotation |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Three witches plan to meet Macbeth. Macbeth and Banquo, fresh from victory, hear the prophecy: Macbeth will be Thane of Cawdor and **king**; Banquo's sons will be kings. Cawdor comes true at once. Duncan names his son **Malcolm** heir. Lady Macbeth reads Macbeth's letter and summons the spirits to "unsex me here". Duncan arrives at Inverness. Macbeth wavers; she shames him into it. | **The prophecy**: "All hail, Macbeth, that shalt be king hereafter!" Macbeth's first reaction is fear — "why do I yield to that suggestion / Whose horrid image doth unfix my hair" — the thought of murder is already his own. |
| **2** | The dagger soliloquy. Macbeth murders **Duncan** offstage; Lady Macbeth returns the daggers and smears the grooms. Macduff discovers the body; Macbeth kills the grooms. Malcolm and Donalbain flee, so suspicion falls on them. Macbeth is crowned. | **The murder**: "Is this a dagger which I see before me". Afterwards: "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" — she answers, "A little water clears us of this deed." |
| **3** | Macbeth, now king, fears Banquo's line and hires murderers: **Banquo** is killed, **Fleance** escapes. At the banquet Banquo's ghost appears to Macbeth alone; Lady Macbeth dismisses the guests. Macbeth resolves to return to the witches. | **The banquet**: "Thou canst not say I did it: never shake / Thy gory locks at me." "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far that … returning were as tedious as go o'er." |
| **4** | The witches' apparitions: beware Macduff; none of woman born shall harm Macbeth; he is safe until Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane. Macbeth has **Macduff's wife and children** slaughtered. In England, Malcolm tests Macduff; Macduff learns of the murders. | **The apparitions**: "none of woman born / Shall harm Macbeth" — false security. Macduff: "He has no children." |
| **5** | Lady Macbeth sleepwalks, rubbing her hands. Malcolm's army carries branches from Birnam Wood. Lady Macbeth dies (offstage, probably suicide). "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow". Macduff, "from his mother's womb / Untimely ripp'd", kills Macbeth. Malcolm is hailed king. | **The end**: "Out, damned spot!" Then "Life's but a walking shadow … signifying nothing." Macduff enters with Macbeth's head; Malcolm calls them "this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen". |

**The structure** is a rise and a fall with the crown in the middle: Acts 1–2 are Macbeth deciding and killing; Act 3 is the top of the arc and the first crack (the ghost); Acts 4–5 are the collapse. Each murder is worse than the last — a king (for the crown), a friend (for security), a family (for nothing) — and each one Macbeth commits with less hesitation, which is the play's measure of his corruption. The play **begins and ends with a traitor's death** (Cawdor, Macbeth), and Malcolm's closing speech restores the order the witches' opening chaos disturbed.

Only about half of the marks on Paper 1 come from the extract. The rest come from what you do with the rest of the play — so every extract question is really a whole-play question with a starting point.

::: terms
- **Tragedy**: a play in which a great figure falls to destruction through a flaw of their own, arousing pity and fear.
- **Act and scene**: the divisions of a play; Macbeth has five acts.
- **Extract question**: Paper 1 Section A prints a passage and asks about it and about the play as a whole.
- **Frame**: the way the play opens and closes on similar images (battle, a traitor executed, a new king), giving it a circular shape.
- **Exposition**: the opening part of a play, which sets up the situation, characters and conflict.
- **Climax**: the turning point of highest tension — in Macbeth, the murder of Duncan.
:::

::: method
1. **Locate the extract**: which act, what has just happened, what happens next. One sentence of placement shows you know the play — "This comes moments after Duncan names Malcolm heir, so Macbeth's aside is the first time he admits murder to himself."
2. **Read the question's focus word** (ambition, guilt, Lady Macbeth, kingship) and find it in the extract first, then in two or three other places in the play.
3. **Move outward in time**: something earlier that set this up, something later that it leads to. That is what "the play as a whole" means — cause and consequence, not a plot summary.
4. **Quote short**: three to six words, embedded in your sentence, from at least three different scenes.
5. **Every paragraph names Shakespeare** doing something on purpose: "Shakespeare structures", "Shakespeare withholds", "Shakespeare gives Macbeth a soliloquy here because".
:::

::: example Placing an extract
The extract begins "Is this a dagger which I see before me". Write the first sentence of an answer on how Shakespeare presents Macbeth's state of mind.

Place it first. This is Act 2 Scene 1: Lady Macbeth has settled the plan, Banquo has just left, and Macbeth is alone on the way to Duncan's chamber.

Then name what Shakespeare is doing: he gives Macbeth a soliloquy so the audience is inside his mind at the exact moment before the murder — the dagger is a hallucination "proceeding from the heat-oppressed brain", and the fact that the handle points "toward my hand" shows a mind already reaching for the crime.

A whole-play link finishes the opening: this is the first of the play's hallucinations; the ghost at the banquet in Act 3 and the "damned spot" in Act 5 will show guilt taking the same physical form in both Macbeths.
:::

::: example Using structure
How does Shakespeare use the order of the murders to show Macbeth's decline? Build the paragraph.

Point: Shakespeare escalates the murders so that each one is less justified and more easily committed than the last.

Evidence in sequence: Duncan's murder takes an act of persuasion and a soliloquy of doubt — "We will proceed no further in this business"; Banquo's is ordered through hired killers with a speech of cold self-justification; Macduff's family are killed on a whim — "The castle of Macduff I will surprise".

Analysis: the hesitation shrinks as the victims become more innocent. By Act 4 Macbeth acts before he thinks — "The very firstlings of my heart shall be / The firstlings of my hand" — which reverses the Act 1 Macbeth, who thought so much that he could barely act.

Purpose: Shakespeare shows a Jacobean audience that regicide does not end with one death — it must go on defending itself, and the killer loses the capacity for conscience that made him hesitate in the first place.
:::

::: example The frame
Explain how the ending mirrors the beginning of the play. (A whole-play structure paragraph.)

The play opens with the execution of a traitor: the Thane of Cawdor, of whom Duncan says "He was a gentleman on whom I built / An absolute trust", and whose title passes to Macbeth.

It closes with Macbeth, the new Cawdor, beheaded as a traitor and his title passing to Malcolm's new earls — "this dead butcher".

Shakespeare frames the play so that treachery is punished at both ends: the circle suggests the natural order (a rightful king, loyal thanes) reasserting itself, but the fact that it had to happen twice leaves a question over whether the cycle is really closed — Fleance is still alive, and Banquo's line, not Malcolm's, is what the witches promised.
:::

::: example Quoting from outside the extract
The extract is the sleepwalking scene. The question is about guilt. List three places outside the extract you would use, with a quotation each.

Act 2 Scene 2, straight after the murder: Lady Macbeth's "A little water clears us of this deed" — the line the sleepwalking scene overturns, since no water can now clear her hands.

Act 2 Scene 2, Macbeth's version of the same image: "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" — Shakespeare gives the two of them the same image at opposite moments.

Act 3 Scene 4, the banquet: "Thou canst not say I did it" — Macbeth's guilt is public and visible while hers is still private; by Act 5 the positions have reversed and she is the one who cannot hide.
:::

::: q
In which act is Duncan murdered, and who discovers the body?
---
Act 2; Macduff discovers it.
:::

::: q
What are the three prophecies the witches make in Act 1?
---
Macbeth will be Thane of Cawdor; Macbeth will be king; Banquo's sons will be kings (though Banquo will not).
:::

::: q
Why does Duncan naming Malcolm as Prince of Cumberland matter?
---
It blocks Macbeth's path to the crown — "a step / On which I must fall down, or else o'erleap" — so the prophecy will not come true by itself.
:::

::: q
Who escapes when Banquo is murdered, and why does that matter?
---
Fleance; the witches' prophecy that Banquo's line will be kings stays possible, so Macbeth's security is never complete.
:::

::: q
What three things do the apparitions tell Macbeth in Act 4?
---
Beware Macduff; none of woman born shall harm him; he is safe until Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane.
:::

::: q
How is each of the last two prophecies fulfilled?
---
Malcolm's soldiers carry branches from Birnam Wood as camouflage; Macduff was born by caesarean — "from his mother's womb / Untimely ripp'd".
:::

::: q
Give a quotation from Malcolm's final speech that sums up the Macbeths.
---
"this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen".
:::

::: q
Roughly what proportion of Paper 1 marks come from beyond the extract?
---
About half — the question always asks about the extract and the play as a whole.
:::

::: q
Name the three murders in order and say what each one was for.
---
Duncan (to gain the crown); Banquo (to secure it against the prophecy); Macduff's family (for nothing — revenge and panic).
:::

::: q
How does the play's opening scene mirror its closing scene?
---
It opens with a traitor (Cawdor) executed and his title given to Macbeth; it ends with Macbeth executed as a traitor and Malcolm restoring order.
:::

::: watch
- Retelling the plot scores nothing. Every plot fact must be **doing a job** — showing a cause, a consequence or a contrast.
- Place the extract in **one sentence**, then leave the plot alone.
- Quote from **at least three scenes**, not just the extract — half the marks live outside it.
- "Shakespeare shows", "Shakespeare structures" — the writer is the subject of your sentences, not the characters.
- The witches **never say "kill Duncan"**; Macbeth supplies the murder himself. Get this right or every later point about responsibility collapses.
:::

::: test
In which act and scene does Macbeth first appear, and what does the audience already know about him? (2)
---
Act 1 Scene 3 (1); he has been praised as a brave, loyal soldier in 1.2 — 'brave Macbeth' (1).
:::

::: test
What happens at the banquet in Act 3, and why is it a turning point? (3)
---
Banquo's ghost appears, seen only by Macbeth, who raves in front of the thanes (1); Lady Macbeth sends the guests away (1); his guilt is now public and he resolves to return to the witches — the tyrant stage begins (1).
:::

::: test
Who is Fleance, and why does his escape matter to the plot? (2)
---
Banquo's son (1); the prophecy that Banquo's line will be kings can still come true, so Macbeth's crimes have bought nothing (1).
:::

::: test
Give two ways Act 5 answers Act 1. (2)
---
The play opens and closes in battle, with Macbeth's severed enemy replaced by his own severed head (1); Macbeth's rise from Thane to king is reversed as Malcolm, named heir in Act 1, takes the throne (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the murder of Macduff's family (Act 4) is different from the earlier murders. (2)
---
Duncan and Banquo were killed for a reason — the crown, the prophecy (1); the Macduffs gain Macbeth nothing; it is pure spite, showing thought and act have fused (1).
:::

::: test
Where is the extract most likely to come from in an exam question on Lady Macbeth, and which three other scenes should you bring in? (2)
---
1.5, 1.7 or 5.1 (1); the other two of those plus 2.2 or 3.2 — the early–late contrast (1).
:::

::: test
What does Malcolm call Macbeth and Lady Macbeth in the final speech, and why does Shakespeare end on it? (2)
---
'This dead butcher and his fiend-like queen' (1); it is the verdict of the restored order — the audience is invited to judge, though it is simpler than what they have seen (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the structure of the play makes the audience complicit in Macbeth's crimes. (2)
---
The soliloquies let us hear him reason toward each murder before it happens (1); we know his intentions while other characters do not, so we watch from inside his mind (1).
:::
