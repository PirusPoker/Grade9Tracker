## What it is

Macbeth is a **tragic hero**: a man of real greatness with a flaw — his **ambition** — that the play sets out to test to destruction. Shakespeare's method is to keep the audience inside his head through **soliloquies**, so we watch him talk himself into each crime, and then watch what the crimes do to him. The exam wants the **presentation** — how Shakespeare builds him — not a character study of a real person.

**His arc, with a quotation at each stage:**

| Stage | Where | Shakespeare's presentation |
|---|---|---|
| **The loyal soldier** | 1.2 | Before he appears we hear of him: "brave Macbeth — well he deserves that name", "Bellona's bridegroom", unseaming Macdonwald "from the nave to th' chops". Violence is his virtue when it serves the king. |
| **The tempted man** | 1.3–1.4 | The prophecy makes him "start"; his aside — "why do I yield to that suggestion / Whose horrid image doth unfix my hair" — shows the murder is his own thought, not the witches'. "Stars, hide your fires; / Let not light see my black and deep desires." |
| **The doubter** | 1.7 | The great soliloquy: "If it were done when 'tis done" — he lists every reason not to: Duncan is his king, kinsman and guest; Duncan is virtuous; "I have no spur … but only / Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself". He decides against it — "We will proceed no further" — and is shamed back by his wife. |
| **The murderer** | 2.1–2.2 | The dagger; then instant horror: "I am afraid to think what I have done", "Macbeth shall sleep no more", "Neptune's ocean". Guilt arrives with the deed. |
| **The tyrant** | 3.1–3.4 | Fear replaces guilt: "To be thus is nothing, / But to be safely thus." He no longer confides in Lady Macbeth — "Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck". The ghost breaks him publicly. "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far…" |
| **The butcher** | 4.1 | "From this moment / The very firstlings of my heart shall be / The firstlings of my hand" — thought and action fused; the Macduffs die for nothing. |
| **The empty man** | 5.3–5.5 | "I have lived long enough", "I have almost forgot the taste of fears"; on his wife's death, "She should have died hereafter", then "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow … signifying nothing". Nihilism — the crown has cost him meaning itself. |
| **The soldier again** | 5.8 | "I will not yield / To kiss the ground before young Malcolm's feet" — he dies fighting, which is why some sympathy survives. |

**Conscience and self-deception.** Shakespeare gives Macbeth a conscience that never stops working — that is what the hallucinations are — and a mind that keeps finding ways round it. In 1.7 he reasons honestly and reaches the right answer; then lets his wife's taunt about manhood override it. After Duncan he cannot say "Amen"; after Banquo he tells himself he is only "young in deed". The self-deception is what makes him tragic rather than simply villainous: he knows, at every stage, exactly what he is doing.

**Fate or responsibility?** The play sets the question deliberately. For fate: the witches know his future, the prophecies come true, "chance may crown me". For responsibility: the witches never instruct him; Banquo hears the same prophecy and does nothing; Macbeth chooses — "I go, and it is done". The strongest answers hold both: the witches reveal a desire that was already there, and Macbeth is responsible for acting on it.

**Terms that earn their place**: *soliloquy* (when a speech is one, and what it lets the audience see); *hamartia* (the fatal flaw — ambition — only if you then show it working); *hubris*; *tragic hero*; *aside*; *dramatic irony* (Duncan's "absolute trust" in Cawdor, said as Macbeth enters).

::: method
1. **Choose three moments from different parts of the arc** — early, middle, late — so the answer shows change. A Macbeth answer without Act 5 has no ending.
2. **Quote the soliloquies**: they are where Shakespeare exposes him. Say what the soliloquy *does* — reveals doubt, exposes reasoning, shows a mind at war with itself.
3. **Keep Shakespeare as the subject**: "Shakespeare presents Macbeth's ambition as…", "Shakespeare gives him the language of…".
4. **Argue the fate/responsibility question** rather than settle it: show both, then say which the play weights more heavily and why.
5. **Use one term per paragraph at most**, and only when explaining it adds something.
:::

::: example The 1.7 soliloquy
How does Shakespeare present Macbeth's conscience in "If it were done when 'tis done"? Write the paragraph.

Point: Shakespeare presents a Macbeth who knows exactly why the murder is wrong before he commits it.

Evidence: the soliloquy is a list of reasons against — Duncan is "here in double trust", as king and as guest; he "Hath borne his faculties so meek" that his virtues "Will plead like angels, trumpet-tongued". The only reason for is named and condemned in the same breath: "Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on the other."

Analysis: the horse image is telling — ambition is a rider who jumps too far and lands on the far side of the saddle. Macbeth sees his own fall before it happens. Shakespeare's point is not that he lacks a conscience but that he has one and overrides it.

Whole play: this is why the later hallucinations arrive — the conscience he silenced here returns as a dagger, a ghost and a spot that will not wash out.
:::

::: example Tracking the change
Write a two-part comparison of Macbeth's language early and late in the play.

Early: his speech is full of images of heaven and judgement — "heaven's cherubin", "angels, trumpet-tongued", "the deep damnation of his taking-off". He measures the murder against a moral universe.

Late: "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" measures life against nothing at all — "a tale / Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing." The heaven has gone from his vocabulary.

Analysis: Shakespeare charts the cost of the crown in the shrinking of Macbeth's world. The man who once feared damnation now cannot feel anything: "I have almost forgot the taste of fears." The tragedy is that he gets the thing he wanted and finds it empty.
:::

::: example Fate or responsibility
Argue the question in one paragraph that holds both sides.

Shakespeare allows the reading that Macbeth is fate's victim: the witches greet him with a title he does not yet hold, and it arrives within the scene — "What, can the devil speak true?"

But Shakespeare places Banquo beside him to close that reading down. Banquo hears the same prophecy and warns that "instruments of darkness tell us truths … to betray's in deepest consequence"; he does nothing, and remains innocent. The prophecy is identical; the choice is not.

Conclusion: the witches reveal, they do not command. Macbeth's own aside — "My thought, whose murder yet is but fantastical" — shows the murder already in his mind before anyone has mentioned it. Shakespeare gives us the temptation and the man who yields, and asks the audience to judge the man.
:::

::: example Sympathy at the end
Does Shakespeare want the audience to feel anything for Macbeth in Act 5?

Yes, and he engineers it. Macbeth's last soliloquies are the most beautiful verse in the play — Shakespeare gives a butcher the language of a poet, and the audience cannot help hearing a mind still capable of understanding its own ruin.

He also restores the soldier: "At least we'll die with harness on our back", and he refuses to yield to Macduff even knowing the prophecy has turned.

But Shakespeare frames that death with Malcolm's judgement — "this dead butcher" — so the sympathy is contained. The audience feels pity for the man and relief at the outcome, which is what tragedy is for.
:::

::: q
Give a quotation showing Macbeth admired before he appears on stage.
---
"brave Macbeth — well he deserves that name" (the Captain, 1.2) or "Bellona's bridegroom".
:::

::: q
What does Macbeth's aside "why do I yield to that suggestion" show about responsibility?
---
That the thought of murder is his own, arising from the prophecy but not commanded by the witches.
:::

::: q
In "If it were done", what is the only reason Macbeth gives for the murder?
---
"Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself" — and he immediately sees it as self-defeating.
:::

::: q
Quote Macbeth's decision in 1.7 before Lady Macbeth changes his mind.
---
"We will proceed no further in this business."
:::

::: q
What does "To be thus is nothing, / But to be safely thus" reveal about Macbeth in Act 3?
---
That gaining the crown has replaced guilt with fear; his ambition has become a need for security that drives Banquo's murder.
:::

::: q
Which line in Act 4 marks the end of Macbeth's hesitation?
---
"The very firstlings of my heart shall be / The firstlings of my hand" — he will act on impulse without thought.
:::

::: q
What does the "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" speech show about what the crown has cost him?
---
Meaning itself — life is "a tale / Told by an idiot … Signifying nothing"; he has gained power and lost any sense that anything matters.
:::

::: q
How does Shakespeare use Banquo to answer the question of fate?
---
Banquo hears the same prophecy, warns that the "instruments of darkness" betray, and does nothing — showing the choice was Macbeth's.
:::

::: q
What is a soliloquy, and why does Shakespeare give Macbeth so many?
---
A speech alone on stage voicing private thought; it lets the audience watch Macbeth reason, doubt and deceive himself, which is the engine of the tragedy.
:::

::: q
Why does "Shakespeare shows" score and "Macbeth felt" not?
---
The exam assesses the writer's methods and purpose; Macbeth is a construction, and marks come from explaining how and why Shakespeare builds him.
:::

::: watch
- Macbeth is a **construct** — write "Shakespeare presents him as", never "Macbeth was a nice man who".
- Cover the **whole arc**: an answer that stops at Act 2 misses the tyrant and the nihilist.
- The **soliloquies** are your best evidence — say what each one lets the audience see.
- Do not choose between fate and responsibility; show Shakespeare **weighting** the choice through Banquo.
- Terms like *hamartia* earn nothing unless you show the flaw **at work** in a quotation.
:::

::: test
Quote the image Macbeth uses for his new title in Act 1, and explain it. (2)
---
'Why do you dress me in borrow'd robes?' (1); the title is a costume he has not earned — clothing imagery follows him to Act 5 (1).
:::

::: test
What does Macbeth mean by 'Stars, hide your fires; / Let not light see my black and deep desires'? (2)
---
He wants darkness to hide his ambition to kill (1); he already knows the desire is 'black' — his own conscience judges it before he acts (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how Shakespeare shows Macbeth's guilt arriving immediately after Duncan's murder. (3)
---
He cannot say 'Amen' (1); he hears a voice cry 'Macbeth shall sleep no more' (1); he asks whether 'all great Neptune's ocean' can wash the blood away — guilt as blood on hands (1).
:::

::: test
What changes between 'I am afraid to think what I have done' and 'I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far'? (2)
---
Horror at one murder becomes acceptance of endless murder (1); guilt has been replaced by fear and calculation — going back is 'as tedious' as going on (1).
:::

::: test
How does Shakespeare use 'Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck' to show Macbeth's change? (2)
---
He now plans Banquo's murder without his wife, reversing Act 1 (1); the endearment is patronising — he has taken over and shut her out (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why some sympathy for Macbeth survives to the end. (2)
---
He dies fighting — 'I will not yield' — recovering the soldier of Act 1 (1); the soliloquies have kept us inside his suffering mind throughout (1).
:::

::: test
What is meant by hamartia, and how would you show it 'at work' in a quotation? (2)
---
The tragic hero's fatal flaw (1); e.g. 'Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself' — Macbeth naming the flaw and predicting its fall (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why 'Shakespeare presents Macbeth as' is a better sentence opening than 'Macbeth is'. (2)
---
It keeps the writer as the subject, showing the character is a construction (1); it leads naturally to method — how and why Shakespeare builds him that way (1).
:::
