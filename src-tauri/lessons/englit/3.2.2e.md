## What it is

**The Charge of the Light Brigade** (Alfred, Lord Tennyson, 1854) was written within weeks of the event it describes: at Balaclava in the Crimean War, a misunderstood order sent about 600 British cavalry charging down a valley into Russian guns on three sides. Over a hundred were killed and many more wounded; the charge achieved nothing. Tennyson, **Poet Laureate**, read the report in *The Times* and wrote a poem that does two things at once: it **honours the soldiers' obedience and courage**, and it **admits the order was a blunder**. Answers that pick only one side flatten the poem.

**The shape.** Six stanzas that follow the charge in time: into the valley (1–2), the guns (3), the fight (4), the retreat (5), the memorial (6). The refrain "the six hundred" ends every stanza and changes as it goes.

| Stanza | Quotation | Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Half a league, half a league, / Half a league onward" | Dactylic rhythm (DUM-da-da) — hoofbeats; the repetition is the relentless advance. "Into the valley of Death / Rode the six hundred" — biblical (Psalm 23); the destination is named before the charge begins. |
| 2 | "'Forward, the Light Brigade!' / Was there a man dismayed? / Not though the soldier knew / Someone had blundered." | The admission — "Someone had blundered" — is in the second stanza, not hidden. "Someone": unnamed; the poem does not accuse, but it does not excuse. "Theirs not to make reply, / Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die" — anaphora; duty as the whole of a soldier's life; "do and die", not "do or die". |
| 3 | "Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them, / Cannon in front of them / Volleyed and thundered" | Repetition builds the trap; "jaws of Death", "mouth of Hell" — the valley personified as a devouring monster. |
| 4 | "Flashed all their sabres bare … / Sabring the gunners there, / Charging an army, while / All the world wondered" | The moment of glory: light on steel, action verbs, the world watching. "Cossack and Russian / Reeled from the sabre-stroke / Shattered and sundered. / Then they rode back, but not / Not the six hundred." | The refrain breaks: "Not the six hundred" — the number is now a loss. |
| 5 | "Cannon behind them" | The same lines as stanza 3 with "behind" — the guns have not moved; the survivors ride back through them. "They that had fought so well / Came through the jaws of Death, / Back from the mouth of Hell, / All that was left of them, / Left of the six hundred." |
| 6 | "When can their glory fade? / O the wild charge they made! / All the world wondered. / Honour the charge they made! / Honour the Light Brigade, / Noble six hundred!" | Rhetorical question, imperatives to the reader ("Honour"), exclamation. The poem turns from narrative to memorial; the refrain becomes "Noble six hundred". |

**The rhythm.** **Dactylic dimeter** — two feet of stressed-unstressed-unstressed per line ("HALF a league, HALF a league") — galloping, driving, relentless; it carries the reader into the valley as the order carried the men. The **rhyme** is irregular, mostly on "-undred" and "-ed" verbs (blundered, thundered, wondered, sundered), so that "blundered" chimes with "thundered" and "wondered": the blunder is woven into the sound of the glory.

**"The six hundred."** The refrain shifts: "Rode the six hundred" (stanzas 1–3, a unit in motion) → "Not the six hundred" (4, loss) → "Left of the six hundred" (5, remnant) → "Noble six hundred" (6, memorial). The same three words go from action to absence to honour. Tracking that is a structure paragraph in itself.

**Tennyson's position.** As Laureate he was expected to celebrate; he does — but he also puts "Someone had blundered" in the poem's second stanza, in a rhyme position, and the phrase was controversial enough that some editions dropped it. The poem's honesty is that it honours obedience while showing what obedience cost: "Theirs but to do and die" is both praise and indictment, depending on where you stand. Compare Owen's *Exposure* — written by a soldier, sixty years later, with no glory left.

**Context in a sentence.** The Crimean War (1853–56) was the first war reported by telegraph and newspaper correspondents, so the public knew about the blunder within weeks; Tennyson wrote for a public that needed both to mourn and to be proud.

::: terms
- **Dactylic rhythm**: a stressed syllable followed by two unstressed ones ("HALF a league"), imitating galloping hooves.
- **Refrain**: a line or phrase repeated through a poem — "Rode the six hundred".
- **Anaphora**: repetition at the start of lines — "Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them".
- **Biblical allusion**: "the valley of Death", recalling Psalm 23.
- **Patriotism**: love of one's country; the poem honours the soldiers' courage while admitting "Someone had blundered".
- **Crimean War**: the war (1853–56) in which the Charge of the Light Brigade took place in 1854.
:::

::: example Honour and blunder together
How does Tennyson present the charge? Build a paragraph that holds both sides.

Point: Tennyson honours the soldiers' courage while refusing to hide that the order was a mistake.

Evidence: "Someone had blundered" sits in stanza 2, before the charge; and "Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die."

Analysis: "Someone" is unnamed — the poem does not prosecute — but "blundered" rhymes with "hundred", so the error is bound to the men in the sound. The triple "Theirs" makes obedience a creed; "do and die" (not "or") admits that death was the job.

Purpose: as Laureate, Tennyson had to give the nation a way to be proud of a disaster. His solution is to separate the men from the order: the courage was theirs; the blunder was "someone's".
:::

::: example The rhythm
Analyse the effect of the metre.

The poem is in dactylic dimeter: "HALF a league, HALF a league, / HALF a league ONward" — a stressed syllable followed by two unstressed, twice per line.

The effect is hoofbeats: the metre gallops, and the repetition of "Half a league" three times drives the reader forward without pause, as the order drove the men. The rhythm does not slow for the guns; "Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them" keeps the same beat, so the trap closes at full speed.

Tennyson's rhythm is also his argument: the momentum is magnificent and unstoppable, which is exactly what made the blunder fatal. The reader cannot stop the poem any more than the men could stop the charge.
:::

::: example The refrain
Track "the six hundred" through the poem.

Stanzas 1–3 end "Rode the six hundred": a unit, in motion, a number that is also a name.

Stanza 4 breaks it — "Then they rode back, but not / Not the six hundred": the repeated "not" is the stumble of the line realising what it is saying. Stanza 5: "All that was left of them, / Left of the six hundred" — the number has become a remnant.

Stanza 6: "Noble six hundred!" — the verb is gone; they are no longer riding or returning but fixed in memory. Tennyson uses one phrase to carry the reader from charge to loss to monument, which is the poem's whole structure in three words.
:::

::: example The valley
How does Tennyson use imagery to present the danger?

The valley is personified as a devouring creature: "the valley of Death", "the jaws of Death", "the mouth of Hell". The men ride into a mouth — and, in stanza 5, "Back from the mouth of Hell": the same image, reversed, for the survivors.

"Valley of Death" is Psalm 23 — "though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death" — so the charge is given a scriptural frame: the soldiers are the faithful walking through.

The cannon stanzas surround the men grammatically — "to right of them … to left of them … in front of them" — and then "behind them", so the reader is placed inside the trap. Tennyson's imagery makes the blunder visible without a word of blame: three sides of guns is not a battle plan.
:::
