## What it is

Medieval prevention and treatment followed directly from medieval **ideas** about disease. If God sent illness, you prayed; if the humours were unbalanced, you drained the excess; if bad air carried disease, you purified the air. The examiner wants you to make that **connection** every time. This topic also covers **who** treated the sick — physicians, apothecaries, barber surgeons, hospitals and, above all, women at home — and the case study of the **Black Death, 1348–49**.

### Prevention and treatment, linked to ideas

| Idea about cause | Prevention | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **God's punishment / test of faith** | prayer, fasting, going to church, giving to charity, living a good life, pilgrimage | prayer, pilgrimage to a saint's shrine (e.g. Thomas Becket at Canterbury), holy water, touching relics, flagellation, paying for Masses |
| **Astrology** | following the stars' advice on when to act | timing bloodletting and remedies by the zodiac |
| **Four Humours** | the *regimen sanitatis* — advice on diet, exercise, sleep, keeping clean and emptying the bowels — to keep the humours balanced | **bloodletting** (cutting a vein, leeches, cupping) and **purging** (laxatives and emetics to make the patient go to the toilet or vomit); Galen's Theory of Opposites in food and remedies |
| **Miasma** | cleaning the streets, removing dung and rotting rubbish, carrying posies of sweet-smelling herbs, burning herbs or lighting fires, keeping away from bad smells | purifying the air around the patient with herbs and smoke |
| **Supernatural (charms, magic)** | amulets and charms | charms and incantations, often alongside herbal remedies |

**Bloodletting** was the most common treatment. A barber surgeon or physician cut a vein, used leeches or used **cupping** (a heated cup placed on a small cut draws out blood as it cools). The amount and timing were decided by the humours and the stars. **Purging** removed excess humours through vomiting or the bowels. Both usually weakened the patient — but they were logical given the theory.

**Keeping clean.** The *regimen sanitatis* was mostly followed by the wealthy, who could pay a physician to write personal advice. Towns took some action: London employed **rakers** to clear the streets, and in **1388** Parliament passed a law (the Statute of Cambridge) making it an offence to throw filth and rubbish into ditches and rivers — England's first national law of this kind. Monasteries had some of the best hygiene in England: fresh running water, drains, and separate washing and toilet areas. But enforcement in towns was weak: kings did not fund public health, and most people lived with open cesspits and waste in the streets.

### Who treated the sick?

- **Physicians** — university-trained (often seven years or more at Oxford, Cambridge or abroad), studied Galen and Hippocrates, diagnosed using urine, pulse and astrology. Expensive and few in number, so they treated mainly the rich. They diagnosed and advised but rarely did practical work themselves.
- **Apothecaries** — mixed and sold herbal remedies and ointments, sometimes on a physician's instructions; trained by apprenticeship; cheaper than physicians and used by ordinary townspeople.
- **Barber surgeons** — trained by apprenticeship, not at university; did bloodletting, pulled teeth, lanced boils and did simple operations such as setting bones or amputations; also cut hair. Surgery was limited because there was no effective anaesthetic, no understanding of infection and no way to stop serious bleeding. Some surgeons were skilled — **John of Arderne** (14th century) wrote about operating on anal fistulas and used a pain-relieving mixture.
- **Women at home** — most sick people were cared for at home by female relatives, who used traditional **herbal remedies** passed down through families, often mixed with prayers and charms. Some remedies had real value: honey is antibacterial, and plants such as willow bark (containing a natural painkiller) were used. Local "wise women" and midwives also treated people in their communities.

### Hospitals

Hundreds of hospitals were founded in England between 1100 and 1500, most of them run by the **Church** — monks and nuns — and funded by donations from the wealthy, who hoped to earn salvation. **St Bartholomew's** in London was founded in 1123. But a medieval hospital was mainly a place of **care, not cure**:

- they gave rest, warmth, food, clean conditions and, above all, **prayer** — care for the soul was as important as care for the body;
- many admitted only the elderly, poor, pilgrims and travellers, and **refused people with infectious or incurable diseases**;
- **almshouses** housed the poor and old;
- separate **leper houses** (lazar houses) kept lepers away from the community, usually outside towns;
- physicians rarely worked in them — care came from nuns, monks and lay sisters.

### Case study: the Black Death, 1348–49

**What it was.** The Black Death reached England at **Melcombe (Weymouth) in Dorset in June 1348** and London by the autumn. It spread along trade routes and killed perhaps **a third to a half** of England's population. It came in two forms: **bubonic plague** (spread by fleas on black rats; swellings called buboes in the armpit and groin; death within about a week) and **pneumonic plague** (spread through coughing and breathing; attacked the lungs; death within days).

**What people thought caused it:**
- **God's punishment** for sin — the most common explanation;
- **the planets** — the conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars in 1345;
- **miasma** — bad air from rotting matter, or from earthquakes and swamps;
- **an imbalance of the humours** in those who caught it;
- in parts of Europe, Jews were blamed and massacred — but Jews had been expelled from England in 1290.

**Attempts to prevent its spread:**
- prayer, fasting, processions and confessing sins;
- **flagellants** — groups who whipped themselves in public to show God their repentance (a group came to London in 1349);
- carrying herbs and posies, lighting fires and ringing church bells to drive away bad air;
- **cleaning the streets** — in **1349 Edward III ordered the Mayor of London** to remove the filth from the streets because its smell was thought to spread the disease;
- avoiding the sick, fleeing to the countryside (for those who could afford it), and some towns tried to shut out outsiders (Gloucester tried to stop people from Bristol entering).

**Attempts at treatment:**
- prayer and holy relics;
- bloodletting and purging to rebalance the humours;
- lancing (cutting open) the buboes to drain them;
- herbal remedies, vinegar and sweet-smelling substances;
- very little else — physicians were helpless and many fled.

**Significance.** None of these methods worked, because nobody knew about germs, fleas or rats. The Black Death did **not** change medical ideas — people interpreted it through the ideas they already had. It shows how the Church, the Humours and miasma together shaped every response, and how limited government action was (a royal order to clean streets, but no organised national plan). Compare it later with the **Great Plague of 1665** — more organised action by the authorities, but ideas about the cause almost unchanged.

::: terms
- **Bloodletting**: removing blood by cutting a vein, using leeches or cupping, to rebalance the humours.
- **Purging**: making a patient vomit or empty their bowels, using emetics or laxatives, to remove excess humours.
- **Regimen sanitatis**: a set of instructions for keeping healthy — diet, exercise, sleep and cleanliness — to keep the humours in balance.
- **Physician**: a university-trained doctor who diagnosed illness using Galen's ideas, urine and astrology; expensive and rare.
- **Apothecary**: a trained mixer and seller of herbal remedies and medicines, cheaper than a physician.
- **Barber surgeon**: a man trained by apprenticeship who did bloodletting, pulled teeth and carried out simple operations.
- **Flagellant**: a person who whipped themselves in public to show repentance and ask God to lift the plague.
- **Bubo**: a painful swelling in the armpit or groin, the sign of bubonic plague.
- **Lazar house**: a hospital or house outside a town where people with leprosy were kept apart from the community.
:::

::: method

**Q3 — "Explain one way in which [X] was similar to / different from [X in another period]." (4 marks)**
- Level 1 (1–2): simple or general comment. Level 2 (3–4): the similarity/difference explained with **specific knowledge of both periods**.
- Structure: state the way; precise evidence for period one (e.g. 1348–49); precise evidence for period two (e.g. 1665); tie them together. Only one way.

**Q4 — "Explain why…" (12 marks).** Two stimulus points; **add at least one of your own** or you are capped at 8 and shut out of Level 4.
- Level 1 (1–3) simple; Level 2 (4–6) some explanation; Level 3 (7–9) analysis with accurate knowledge; Level 4 (10–12) analysis consistently focused on the question, links between factors, sustained reasoning and wide-ranging precise knowledge.
- For treatment and prevention questions, the winning move is to **connect each method to an idea** ("because they believed in miasma, they…") and to a **factor** (Church, government, attitudes, science and technology).

**Q5/Q6 — "'Statement.' How far do you agree?" (16 + 4 SPaG).**
- Level 3 (9–12): analysis, accurate knowledge, a judgement with some justification. Level 4 (13–16): sustained analysis of both sides and a judgement justified by a **criterion** ("most people", "longest-lasting", "most effective"). Capped at 11 without a point beyond the stimulus.
- Structure: answer in the introduction; argue for; argue against (with your own point); conclude by weighing.
- SPaG: High (4) accurate writing and a wide range of specialist terms (physician, apothecary, purging, miasma); Intermediate (2–3); Threshold (1).
:::

::: example 4-mark difference
Explain one way in which the care of the sick in hospitals in the fourteenth century was different from care in hospitals in the nineteenth century. (4)

One difference is who provided the care and what it aimed at. In the fourteenth century most hospitals were run by the Church, and monks and nuns offered rest, food and above all prayer — care for the soul — while many hospitals refused people with infectious diseases. By the late nineteenth century, after Florence Nightingale founded her training school at St Thomas' Hospital in 1860, hospitals were staffed by trained professional nurses who focused on cleanliness, ventilation and treating the body, and they admitted the seriously ill for medical treatment.
:::

::: example 12-mark explain why
Explain why people in England were unable to stop the spread of the Black Death in 1348–49. (12)
You may use: miasma; flagellants. You must also use information of your own.

The first reason is that people believed the Black Death was caused by **miasma**, bad air from rotting matter. They therefore carried posies of herbs, lit fires and cleaned the streets — in 1349 Edward III ordered the Mayor of London to remove filth from the streets. These actions targeted the smell, not the fleas on black rats that actually spread bubonic plague, so they could not stop it.

The second reason is **religion**. Most people believed the plague was God's punishment for sin, so the Church's answer was prayer, fasting and processions, and the **flagellants** whipped themselves in public, including in London in 1349. Gathering in crowds to pray may even have helped pneumonic plague spread from person to person. Because the cause was seen as God's will, there was little reason to look for a natural one.

A third reason, of my own, is the **lack of scientific knowledge and technology**. No one could see bacteria — there were no microscopes — and physicians trained in Galen explained the disease as an imbalance of the humours, prescribing bloodletting and purging, which weakened patients. Government was also too weak to organise anything more than orders to clean the streets.

These factors fed each other: without the technology to see the real cause, people fell back on religious and humoral explanations taught by the Church and universities, and every method of prevention followed from those wrong ideas.
:::

::: example 16-mark judgement
"There was little change in how people tried to prevent and treat the plague between 1348 and 1665." How far do you agree? (16 + 4 SPaG)
You may use: prayer; the Plague Orders. You must also use information of your own.

**Introduction.** I mostly agree: ideas and treatments stayed almost the same, though the organisation of prevention by the authorities changed noticeably.

**Continuity.** In both outbreaks people blamed God and responded with prayer and fasting; in both they believed in miasma and carried herbs and lit fires; in both, doctors bled, purged and lanced buboes. The comet of 1664 was blamed just as the planets were in 1345. None of these methods tackled the fleas on rats, so both epidemics burned out rather than being stopped.

**Change.** In 1665 the Lord Mayor and aldermen issued detailed Plague Orders: infected houses were shut up with a red cross on the door and watchmen outside, searchers examined bodies, burials were in pits at night, and dogs and cats were killed. Bills of Mortality counted deaths. In 1348–49 government action amounted to a royal order to clean the streets. Some flagellant-style religious extremes had also faded.

**Conclusion.** Judged by *ideas* and *treatment*, there was almost no change, because nobody yet knew about germs. Judged by *organisation*, there was real change, because local government in 1665 was stronger and more willing to act. Since organisation still rested on the old ideas of miasma and contagion, and neither approach stopped the disease, the statement is largely correct.

::: q
How did the Theory of the Four Humours lead to bloodletting and purging?
---
If illness was caused by an excess of one humour, removing the excess would restore balance — bloodletting removed excess blood; purging (vomiting or laxatives) removed excess bile or phlegm.
