## What it is

In 1558 England and Spain were allies: Philip II of Spain had been married to Mary I, and he even proposed to Elizabeth in 1559. By 1585 the two countries were at war. This topic explains how that happened — through **political, religious and commercial rivalry**, the **privateering** of men like Francis Drake, and finally England's **direct military involvement in the Netherlands** under Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, followed by Drake's **raid on Cadiz** in 1587.

### Why England and Spain drifted apart

**Political rivalry.** Philip II ruled the richest empire in Europe: Spain, much of Italy, the Netherlands and a vast American empire whose silver paid for his armies. In 1580 he also became King of Portugal, adding the Portuguese empire. For England, the key area was the **Spanish Netherlands**, just across the Channel. A Spanish army there could invade England; Antwerp was also the main market for English cloth. When the Dutch Protestants revolted against Spanish rule (from 1566), Philip sent the **Duke of Alba** with about 10,000 troops in 1567. Alba's "Council of Troubles" (nicknamed the Council of Blood) executed around a thousand rebels. Elizabeth now had a large Catholic army on her doorstep.

In **December 1568** Spanish ships carrying about **£85,000** in gold — money borrowed from Genoese bankers to pay Alba's army — sheltered from storms and pirates in English ports. Elizabeth **seized the money**, arguing that it was still the bankers' property until it reached Alba. Spain seized English ships and goods in the Netherlands; England retaliated; English trade with Antwerp was stopped by an embargo that lasted until 1573. Relations were damaged but not yet broken.

**Religious rivalry.** Philip saw himself as the defender of the Catholic Church; Elizabeth was the Protestant Supreme Governor of the Church of England. After the Pope excommunicated Elizabeth in **1570** (the bull *Regnans in Excelsis*), Catholic rulers had papal approval to act against her. Philip was linked to the **Ridolfi plot (1571)**, in which Alba was to land troops in England, and the **Throckmorton plot (1583)**; after Throckmorton, the Spanish ambassador **Bernardino de Mendoza** was expelled in **January 1584**, and England had no Spanish ambassador again during the rest of the reign. English sailors captured by Spain could be handed to the **Spanish Inquisition** and tried as heretics.

**Commercial rivalry.** Under the **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)** the Pope had divided the newly discovered lands between Spain and Portugal. Spain claimed a **monopoly** over trade with its American colonies: foreigners could trade there only with a licence. English merchants wanted a share of the profits in slaves, sugar, spices and silver; they also depended on the Netherlands for cloth exports. Every time Spanish-Dutch fighting or an embargo hit Antwerp, English merchants suffered.

### Privateering and the activities of Drake

A **privateer** was a privately owned, armed ship whose captain had permission from a monarch to attack enemy ships and keep a share of the plunder. To Spain, English privateers were simply **pirates**. Elizabeth never openly declared war before 1585, but she **invested in** privateering voyages, shared their profits and rewarded the captains — which let her hurt Spain while denying responsibility.

- **John Hawkins** made slaving voyages to the Spanish Caribbean in 1562–63, 1564–65 and 1567–69, selling enslaved Africans to Spanish colonists illegally. Elizabeth invested in the second voyage and lent a royal ship.
- In **1568** at **San Juan de Ulúa** (Mexico) a Spanish fleet attacked Hawkins's ships even though a truce had been agreed. Only two English ships escaped — one of them commanded by the young **Francis Drake**. Drake regarded this as treachery and nursed a grudge against Spain.
- **1572–73:** Drake attacked **Nombre de Dios** in Panama and, with the help of escaped enslaved people (the Cimarrons), captured a Spanish **mule train carrying silver** in 1573. He returned rich and famous.
- **1577–80:** Drake's **circumnavigation** in the *Golden Hind* included raids on Spanish ports on the Pacific coast of South America and the capture of the treasure ship *Nuestra Señora de la Concepción* (nicknamed *Cacafuego*) in March 1579. He returned with treasure worth around **£400,000**. In **April 1581** Elizabeth knighted him on board the *Golden Hind* at Deptford — a public insult to Philip, who had demanded Drake's punishment. (The circumnavigation itself is covered in B4.3b.)
- **1585–86:** once war had begun, Drake led a large fleet to the Caribbean, attacking **Santo Domingo** and **Cartagena**. The raid made little profit but humiliated Spain and damaged its credit with bankers.

**Significance of privateering:**

| Significance | Why it mattered |
|---|---|
| Wealth | Elizabeth and investors (courtiers such as Leicester and Walsingham) made large profits without the cost of official war. |
| Damaged Spain | Losses of silver hurt Philip's finances and credit; attacks showed his empire was vulnerable. |
| Worsened relations | Philip saw Elizabeth's rewards to Drake as proof that she approved of piracy against him. |
| Naval experience | English captains and crews gained skill in ocean sailing and fighting that paid off in 1588. |
| National pride | Drake became a Protestant hero; England's reputation as a sea power grew. |

### The outbreak of war, 1585–88

**The situation in the Netherlands.** Elizabeth did not want to support rebels against their lawful king — it set a dangerous example — and war was expensive. But she could not let Spain crush the Dutch and use the Netherlands as a base. For years she secretly sent money and allowed English volunteers to fight. Three events in 1584–85 forced her hand:

1. **July 1584:** the Dutch Protestant leader **William of Orange** was assassinated. The revolt lost its leader.
2. **December 1584:** in the **Treaty of Joinville**, Philip allied with the French Catholic League (the Guise family). France could no longer be relied on to balance Spain.
3. **1585:** the Spanish commander, the **Duke of Parma**, was reconquering the southern Netherlands; **Antwerp fell in August 1585**. In **May 1585** Philip seized English ships in Spanish ports.

**The Treaty of Nonsuch (August 1585).** Elizabeth agreed to send an army of about **7,400 men (6,400 infantry and 1,000 cavalry)** and to pay a large annual subsidy to the Dutch. In return England held the towns of **Flushing and Brill** as security. This was England's **direct involvement**: it was, in effect, a declaration of war. England and Spain were at war from 1585 until 1604.

**The actions of Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester.** Leicester, Elizabeth's favourite, commanded the army and arrived in December 1585. In **January 1586**, without Elizabeth's permission, he accepted the title of **Governor-General of the Netherlands**. Elizabeth was furious: it implied that she was claiming to rule the Netherlands — something she had deliberately refused, because it would make her look like a conqueror and would make peace with Philip impossible. She forced him into a humiliating climb-down.

Leicester's campaign also went badly. He was short of money and his troops were badly paid and supplied; he quarrelled with the Dutch leaders. At **Zutphen (September 1586)** the English won a skirmish but the poet-soldier **Sir Philip Sidney** was fatally wounded. In **January 1587** two English officers, Sir William Stanley and Rowland Yorke, betrayed **Deventer** and a fort near Zutphen to the Spanish. In 1587 Leicester failed to save **Sluys**, which fell to Parma. He was recalled in December 1587.

**Why this worsened relations:** English soldiers were now fighting Spanish soldiers. Philip took Nonsuch as an act of war and began serious planning for the **"Enterprise of England"** — the Armada. The execution of Mary, Queen of Scots in **February 1587** then removed the Catholic claimant Philip might have put on the throne, and gave him another motive: to avenge a Catholic queen.

### Drake and the raid on Cadiz, April 1587

In April 1587 Drake sailed with a fleet of over twenty ships to disrupt the Armada being prepared in Spanish ports. On **19 April 1587** (English date) he sailed into **Cadiz** harbour and over about two days destroyed or captured around **30 Spanish ships** and large quantities of supplies. Drake called it **"singeing the King of Spain's beard"**.

He then attacked shipping near **Cape St Vincent**, destroying around **1,600–1,700 tons of barrel staves** — the seasoned wood used to make casks for food and water. In June he captured the Portuguese carrack **San Felipe** near the Azores, with a cargo worth about **£114,000**.

**Significance of Cadiz:**
- It **delayed the Armada by about a year** — it could not sail in 1587, giving England time to prepare.
- The loss of seasoned barrel staves meant the Armada's casks in 1588 were made from **unseasoned wood**, so food rotted and water leaked — a real weakness in the campaign.
- It showed the English navy could strike Spain in its own harbours and boosted morale.
- But it also made Philip more determined: the Armada was now certain.

### Making a judgement

Historians usually see the drift to war as **gradual**: rivalry in the 1560s–70s created mistrust, but both rulers avoided war because it was costly. The **turning point** was 1584–85, when the collapse of the Dutch revolt and the Franco-Spanish alliance made Spanish victory in the Netherlands likely. Elizabeth's decision at Nonsuch was mainly **defensive and strategic** (to keep a Spanish army away from the Channel), while religion and trade supplied long-term hostility and privateering supplied constant provocation.

::: terms
- **Privateer**: a privately owned armed ship, or its captain, given permission by a ruler to attack enemy ships and keep part of the plunder.
- **Treaty of Tordesillas**: the 1494 agreement dividing newly discovered lands outside Europe between Spain and Portugal.
- **Monopoly**: exclusive control of a trade — Spain claimed the sole right to trade with its American colonies.
- **Spanish Netherlands**: the provinces (modern Netherlands and Belgium) ruled by Philip II, where the Dutch Protestants rebelled from 1566.
- **Treaty of Nonsuch**: the August 1585 agreement by which Elizabeth sent an army of about 7,400 men to help the Dutch rebels.
- **Governor-General**: the title Leicester accepted in the Netherlands in January 1586 without Elizabeth's permission.
- **Embargo**: an official ban on trade with a country, such as the 1569–73 halt to trade with the Netherlands.
- **Singeing the King of Spain's beard**: Drake's description of his April 1587 raid on Cadiz, which delayed the Armada.
:::

::: method
This topic sits in **Paper 2, Booklet B: the British depth study (Early Elizabethan England)**. Three kinds of question.

**Q "Describe two features of…" (4 marks).** Two marks per feature: 1 for identifying a feature, 1 for a supporting detail. Write two short paragraphs, each starting "One feature was…" / "A second feature was…", and add one precise fact (a name, date or number) to each. No explanation or judgement needed. Spend about five minutes.

**Q "Explain why…" (12 marks).** Two stimulus points are printed; you may use them, but to reach the top level (Level 4, 10–12) you must also bring in **your own point** — mark schemes cap answers that go no further than the stimulus points at 8 marks. Write three paragraphs, each on a different reason, each: **point → precise evidence → explanation of how it caused the outcome** ("this meant that…", "as a result…").
- Level 1 (1–3): simple or general comments, little knowledge.
- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation, some accurate knowledge, but loosely organised or descriptive.
- Level 3 (7–9): explanation linked to the question, a mostly sustained line of reasoning, well-chosen knowledge.
- Level 4 (10–12): analytical explanation focused on the question throughout, a logical structure, precise and wide-ranging knowledge. Linking reasons ("Nonsuch mattered because the fall of Antwerp had already…") is the mark of Level 4.

**Q "'Statement.' How far do you agree? Explain your answer." (16 marks + 4 SPaG).** You choose one of two. Again two stimulus points; the cap for sticking to them is 11 marks.

- Paragraphs 2–3: **other** factors, each with detail and explanation.
- Conclusion: a clear judgement with a **criterion** — for example "the most important cause was the one that turned rivalry into actual fighting" or "without X, Y would not have happened". Say how far, not just yes/no.
- Level 1 (1–4), Level 2 (5–8), Level 3 (9–12: judgement given but only partly justified), Level 4 (13–16: sustained analysis, judgement with criteria clearly justified).
- SPaG (4 marks): accurate spelling and punctuation, clear grammar, and a wide range of specialist terms (privateer, embargo, excommunication, Governor-General).

Timing: about 50 minutes for the whole depth study section — roughly 5 / 15 / 25 minutes, plus checking.

You may use the following in your answer:
- privateering
- the Netherlands

You must also use information of your own.

Model answer:

One reason relations worsened was **privateering**. Elizabeth secretly invested in voyages that raided Spanish shipping and colonies. Drake captured a Spanish silver mule train in Panama in 1573 and returned from his circumnavigation in 1580 with treasure worth around £400,000, much of it Spanish. When Elizabeth knighted him on the *Golden Hind* in 1581, Philip saw that the Queen herself approved of piracy against him. This turned commercial rivalry into personal hostility between the two rulers.

A second, and in the end decisive, reason was the **Netherlands**. Spain's army in the Netherlands was a threat to England's security and to the cloth trade through Antwerp. The seizure of Alba's pay ships in 1568 led to a trade embargo until 1573. By 1584–85 the situation was critical: William of Orange was assassinated in July 1584, Parma took Antwerp in August 1585 and Spain had allied with the French Catholic League at Joinville. Elizabeth feared a victorious Spanish army just across the Channel, so in August 1585 she signed the Treaty of Nonsuch and sent about 7,400 troops under Leicester. Because English soldiers now fought Spanish soldiers, rivalry became open war.

A third reason was **religion**. After Elizabeth's excommunication in 1570, Philip saw himself as the champion of Catholic Europe, and Spain was involved in the Ridolfi (1571) and Throckmorton (1583) plots to put Mary, Queen of Scots on the throne. The expulsion of the Spanish ambassador Mendoza in 1584 ended normal diplomacy. Religion made each side assume the worst about the other, which made the political and commercial disputes much harder to settle — and so linked all three causes together.

::: example How far do you agree (16)
"The main reason for the outbreak of war between England and Spain in 1585 was English involvement in the Netherlands." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16 + 4 SPaG)

You may use: the Treaty of Nonsuch; Drake's activities. You must also use information of your own.

Model answer (outline of a Level 4 response):

**Agree.** Involvement in the Netherlands was the immediate cause. Nonsuch (August 1585) committed about 7,400 English soldiers to fight Philip's army, and handed England Flushing and Brill. Leicester's acceptance of the title of Governor-General in January 1586 made it look as if Elizabeth was taking Philip's lands. Philip regarded this as an act of war and began planning the Armada. Before 1585 there had been rivalry but no fighting between armies.

**Disagree — privateering.** Drake and Hawkins had been attacking Spanish ships for twenty years. San Juan de Ulúa (1568), the Panama raids (1572–73) and the circumnavigation (1577–80) cost Spain money and pride, and Drake's knighthood in 1581 showed Elizabeth's approval. This created the hostility that made war likely.

**Disagree — religion and plots.** The 1570 excommunication and Spanish involvement in the Ridolfi and Throckmorton plots meant Elizabeth saw Philip as a threat to her life and her Church; Mendoza's expulsion in 1584 ended diplomacy.

**Judgement.** I largely agree. Privateering and religion created long-term hostility, but both rulers had tolerated that for years because war was expensive. What changed in 1584–85 was the Netherlands: the death of William of Orange and the fall of Antwerp meant that without intervention Spain would win and have a base across the Channel. So the Netherlands was the **trigger that turned rivalry into war**, and it was only important because the long-term causes had already destroyed trust.
