## What it is

Between 1941 and 1946 the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union went from being wartime allies to suspicious rivals. This topic covers the **Grand Alliance**, the three wartime conferences (**Tehran, Yalta, Potsdam**), the **ideological differences** and the attitudes of **Stalin, Truman and Churchill**, and the three things that turned suspicion into hostility: the **atomic bomb**, the **Long and Novikov telegrams**, and the creation of **Soviet satellite states** in Eastern Europe.

### The Grand Alliance, 1941

The Grand Alliance was a "marriage of convenience". It formed in 1941 only because all three powers were fighting Nazi Germany:

- **June 1941** — Hitler invaded the USSR (Operation Barbarossa), so Britain and the USSR became allies.
- **December 1941** — Japan attacked Pearl Harbor and Germany declared war on the USA, bringing the USA in.

The "Big Three" leaders were **Franklin D. Roosevelt** (USA), **Winston Churchill** (Britain) and **Joseph Stalin** (USSR). They shared one aim — defeating Hitler — but little else. Stalin had not forgotten that Britain and the USA had sent troops to fight against the Bolsheviks in the Russian Civil War (1918–21), and he resented how long the Western allies took to open a **second front** in France while the Red Army did most of the fighting and dying. The USSR lost well over 20 million people in the war.

### The three conferences

| Conference | Date | Leaders | Key outcomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tehran** (Iran) | Nov–Dec 1943 | Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin | Second front agreed: the USA and Britain would invade France (this became D-Day, June 1944). USSR to declare war on Japan once Germany was beaten. Eastern Europe accepted as a Soviet sphere of influence. Poland's borders to move westward. A United Nations organisation agreed in principle. |
| **Yalta** (Crimea, USSR) | February 1945 | Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin | Germany and Berlin each to be divided into four zones (USA, Britain, France, USSR). Nazi war criminals to be tried. Germany to pay reparations, half to the USSR. **Declaration on Liberated Europe**: free elections in countries freed from the Nazis. Poland in the Soviet sphere, but its government to include non-communists and hold free elections. USSR to join the war against Japan three months after Germany's defeat, and to join the United Nations. |
| **Potsdam** (near Berlin) | 17 July–2 August 1945 | **Truman**, Stalin, Churchill then **Attlee** | Division of Germany and Berlin confirmed; Nazi Party banned; Germany demilitarised; war criminals to be tried (the Nuremberg trials). Poland's western border set on the Oder–Neisse line. Each power to take reparations from its own zone; the USSR also to receive a quarter of industrial equipment from the western zones. |

**Why Yalta looked like success.** At Yalta the Big Three still needed each other: the war in Europe was not over and the USA wanted Soviet help against Japan. Compromise was possible — Stalin got his sphere of influence, Roosevelt and Churchill got a promise of free elections. The problem was that the two promises contradicted each other in Poland, and nobody defined what "free elections" meant.

**Why Potsdam was tense.** Five months later, almost everything had changed:

1. **New leaders.** Roosevelt died on **12 April 1945**. **Harry Truman** was far more suspicious of Stalin. Churchill lost the British general election and was replaced mid-conference by **Clement Attlee**. The personal trust between Roosevelt and Stalin had gone.
2. **Germany had surrendered (May 1945).** The common enemy was gone, so there was less reason to compromise.
3. **The Red Army occupied Eastern Europe.** Stalin had set up a pro-communist government in Poland (the Lublin government) and was arresting non-communists — which Truman saw as a breach of Yalta.
4. **Reparations.** Stalin wanted huge reparations to rebuild the USSR; Truman and Attlee did not want Germany crippled (they remembered the effects of the Treaty of Versailles) and would have to feed the German population in their own zones.
5. **The bomb.** On **16 July 1945**, the day before Potsdam opened, the USA successfully tested an atomic bomb in New Mexico. Truman told Stalin during the conference that the USA had a new weapon of unusual destructive force. Stalin already knew from Soviet spies.

### Ideology: capitalism versus communism

| | USA — capitalism | USSR — communism |
|---|---|---|
| Politics | Democracy: free elections, several parties, free press, free speech | One-party state run by the Communist Party; no opposition, censorship, secret police |
| Economy | Private ownership of business; free market; profit | State ownership of land and industry; a planned economy |
| Values | Individual freedom matters most, even if it produces inequality | Equality of the working class matters more than individual freedom |
| World view | Wanted open markets for American trade across the world | Believed communism would eventually spread worldwide as capitalism collapsed |

Each side believed the other wanted to spread its system. Americans feared communist revolution; Soviet leaders feared **capitalist encirclement**. Ideology meant every action could be read as aggressive.

### Attitudes of the leaders

- **Stalin** wanted **security**. Russia had been invaded from the west through Poland twice in 30 years (1914 and 1941). He wanted a **buffer zone** of friendly (communist) states in Eastern Europe, a weak Germany and reparations to rebuild. He distrusted the West because of the Civil War intervention and the delay over the second front.
- **Truman** was a firm anti-communist. He believed Stalin was breaking the Yalta agreements and that the USA should be tough rather than conciliatory. The atomic monopoly gave him confidence.
- **Churchill** had been an opponent of communism since 1917. In **March 1946** at Fulton, Missouri (with Truman on the platform) he gave the **"Iron Curtain" speech**: "From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent." He called for a British–American alliance against Soviet expansion. Stalin denounced the speech as a call to war.

### The atomic bomb

The USA dropped atomic bombs on **Hiroshima (6 August 1945)** and **Nagasaki (9 August 1945)**. Japan surrendered in August 1945. Impact on relations:

- Truman had not shared the secret with his ally; Stalin saw this as proof of distrust. Some historians argue the bombs were also a warning to the USSR.
- The American **monopoly** made the USA confident it could pressure Stalin; it made Stalin feel threatened and determined to catch up. The Soviet programme was accelerated and the USSR tested its own bomb in **August 1949**.
- Japan surrendered before the USSR could play a large part in the Pacific war, so the USSR was denied a role in occupying Japan, which the USA ran alone.
- It began the **arms race** — the core of the Cold War.

### The Long and Novikov telegrams, 1946

| | **Long Telegram** | **Novikov Telegram** |
|---|---|---|
| Written by | **George Kennan**, US diplomat in Moscow | **Nikolai Novikov**, Soviet ambassador in Washington |
| Date | February 1946 | September 1946 |
| Sent to | Washington (the State Department) | Moscow (Foreign Minister Molotov) |
| Claimed | Stalin saw the world as a struggle between capitalism and communism; the USSR was building up its military power and wanted to spread communism; it would back down when faced with firm resistance. | The USA was seeking world supremacy; it was building up its armed forces and military bases around the world; it was preparing its people for war against the USSR. |
| Impact | Confirmed Truman's view that Stalin must be resisted; laid the ground for the policy of **containment** (stopping communism spreading), announced in 1947. | Convinced Stalin that the USA was an aggressive threat, so he tightened his grip on Eastern Europe. |

Together the telegrams show **mutual misunderstanding**: each side's experts told their leaders the other side was aggressive and expansionist, and each side's response confirmed the other's fears.

### Soviet satellite states in Eastern Europe

A **satellite state** is a country that is officially independent but controlled by another power. Between 1945 and 1948 Stalin turned the countries occupied by the Red Army into communist states loyal to Moscow:

| Country | How it became communist |
|---|---|
| **Poland** | Pro-Soviet Lublin government; opponents arrested; rigged elections in January 1947 gave the communists control. |
| **Bulgaria** | Communist-led government from 1945; opposition leaders executed; monarchy abolished 1946. |
| **Romania** | Soviet pressure installed a communist-led government in 1945; the king was forced to abdicate at the end of 1947. |
| **Hungary** | Communists lost the 1945 election but gained control of the police; they removed rivals piece by piece ("salami tactics") and dominated after 1947. |
| **Czechoslovakia** | A coalition until **February 1948**, when a communist coup took full power. |
| **East Germany** | The Soviet zone; became the German Democratic Republic in October 1949. |

(Yugoslavia became communist without the Red Army, under Tito, and broke with Stalin in 1948.)

Communists typically took the **Ministry of the Interior** (police and security), then used arrests, intimidation and rigged elections to remove opponents. To Stalin this was a defensive buffer; to the West it was proof that he had broken the Yalta promise of free elections and wanted to spread communism westward — Churchill's "iron curtain". It led directly to the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan in 1947.

::: terms
- **Grand Alliance**: the wartime alliance of the USA, Britain and the USSR formed in 1941 to defeat Nazi Germany.
- **Superpower**: a state with enough military, economic and political power to influence events worldwide — after 1945, the USA and the USSR.
- **Capitalism**: an economic system based on private ownership, free markets and profit, linked in the USA with democracy and individual freedom.
- **Communism**: an ideology in which the state owns land and industry for the benefit of all, run in the USSR as a one-party dictatorship.
- **Sphere of influence**: an area where one power has dominant control or influence, though the countries are not formally part of it.
- **Satellite state**: a country that is officially independent but is in practice controlled by another power, as Eastern Europe was by the USSR.
- **Buffer zone**: a ring of friendly states Stalin wanted between the USSR and Germany to protect against invasion from the west.
- **Iron Curtain**: Churchill's 1946 phrase for the dividing line between communist Eastern Europe and the capitalist West.
- **Containment**: the US policy of stopping communism spreading to new countries, grounded in Kennan's Long Telegram.
- **Reparations**: compensation for war damage paid by a defeated country, a major dispute over Germany at Potsdam.
:::

::: method
Paper 2 period study (Booklet P, 32 marks, about 50 minutes). Every question is worth 8 marks and is marked on AO1 (knowledge) and AO2 (explanation/analysis). There is no SPaG here.

**1. "Explain two consequences of…" (8)** — two separate consequences, each marked out of 4.
- Level 1 (1–2 per consequence): a simple or general statement ("it made relations worse").
- Level 2 (3–4 per consequence): a specific consequence explained with precise, accurate supporting knowledge.
- Structure each: *Consequence* → *specific evidence* → *so what — how it changed things*. Use a new paragraph and a clearly different consequence for the second, e.g. one about relations, one about Eastern Europe.

**2. "Write a narrative account analysing…" (8)** — two stimulus bullets are given; you must add information of your own.
- Level 1 (1–2): a simple narrative with limited organisation.
- Level 2 (3–5): some sequence and some links between events.
- Level 3 (6–8): a clear chronological sequence in which each event is **linked by cause and consequence** to the next ("this led to…", "as a result…", "which meant that…"). **Answers that do not go beyond the two bullets are capped at 5 marks.** Aim for three stages: beginning, turning point, outcome.

**3. "Explain two of the following: the importance of X for Y" (8 each, choose two of three)**
- Level 1 (1–2): general comment on X.
- Level 2 (3–5): some explanation of X's importance, with some relevant knowledge.
- Level 3 (6–8): explains **how X changed Y** — the named situation (e.g. "US–Soviet relations") — with precise knowledge, ideally showing two or three strands of importance and saying which mattered most or what would have been different without it.
- Keep hammering the words of the question: "This was important for US–Soviet relations because…".

**Judgement.** Period-study answers do not need a "how far" conclusion, but top-level importance answers often end with a line weighing the strands ("its most important effect was…because…").
:::

::: example Consequences — the Potsdam Conference
Explain two consequences of the Potsdam Conference (1945). (8)

**Consequence 1:** One consequence was the formal division of Germany into four zones of occupation, with Berlin, deep inside the Soviet zone, also split four ways. The conference also agreed that each power would take reparations from its own zone. This meant the Soviet zone and the western zones were run in increasingly different ways — the USSR stripped its zone of industry while the West began to rebuild — so the division that was meant to be temporary hardened, leading eventually to two German states in 1949.

**Consequence 2:** A second consequence was a sharp rise in distrust between the USA and the USSR. Truman had told Stalin of the successful atomic test of 16 July and argued with him over reparations and the pro-communist government in Poland. Because the USA now had a weapon the USSR lacked, Stalin felt threatened and speeded up the Soviet atomic programme, while Truman felt able to take a tougher line. As a result, cooperation between the former allies had largely broken down by 1946.
:::

::: example Narrative — the breakdown of the Grand Alliance
Write a narrative account analysing the key developments in relations between the USA and the Soviet Union in the years 1943–46. You may use the following in your answer: the Yalta Conference (1945); the Long Telegram (1946). You must also use information of your own. (8)

In 1943, at Tehran, the Grand Alliance was still united by the need to defeat Hitler. Roosevelt and Churchill agreed to open a second front in France, and Stalin was allowed a sphere of influence in Eastern Europe. Because they needed each other, they compromised.

This cooperation continued at Yalta in February 1945, where they agreed to divide Germany and Berlin into four zones and signed the Declaration on Liberated Europe promising free elections. However, the agreement over Poland contained the seeds of conflict: Stalin was to have influence there, yet Poland was also to hold free elections. As the Red Army occupied Poland and installed the Lublin communists, the West believed Stalin was breaking his word.

This distrust grew at Potsdam in July 1945 because circumstances had changed. Roosevelt had died and the more hostile Truman had replaced him; Germany had surrendered, removing the common enemy; and the USA had tested the atomic bomb. Disputes over reparations and Poland were left unresolved, and the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August left Stalin feeling threatened by the American monopoly.

By 1946 the suspicion had become a settled hostility. Kennan's Long Telegram of February 1946 told Washington that the USSR was expansionist but would retreat before strength, which pushed Truman towards containment. Churchill's "Iron Curtain" speech in March, and the Novikov Telegram of September telling Moscow the USA wanted world supremacy, meant that each side now saw the other as a threat. The alliance had turned into a Cold War.
:::

::: example Importance — the atomic bomb
Explain the importance of the atomic bomb for relations between the USA and the Soviet Union in the years 1945–49. (8)

The atomic bomb was important first because it destroyed trust. Truman tested the bomb on 16 July 1945 and only hinted to Stalin at Potsdam that the USA had a powerful new weapon; he had not shared the project with his wartime ally. Stalin saw this as proof that the USA regarded the USSR as a future enemy, not a partner.

Second, the bomb changed the balance of power. After Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 the USA had a monopoly, which made Truman confident enough to take a tough line over Poland and Eastern Europe. It also meant the war with Japan ended before the USSR could take a major part, so the USA occupied Japan alone. Stalin, feeling vulnerable, was determined to strengthen his grip on Eastern Europe as a buffer.

Most importantly, it started the nuclear arms race. Stalin poured resources into a Soviet bomb, tested in August 1949. From then on both sides competed to build more and more destructive weapons, so the bomb turned political rivalry into a military stand-off that shaped relations for the rest of the Cold War.
:::

::: q
Name the three leaders of the Grand Alliance in 1941 and say why the alliance formed.
---
Roosevelt (USA), Churchill (Britain), Stalin (USSR). It formed because all three were at war with Nazi Germany after Germany invaded the USSR (June 1941) and the USA entered the war after Pearl Harbor (December 1941). Their only shared aim was defeating Hitler.

::: q
Give three things agreed at the Yalta Conference (February 1945).
---
Any three: Germany and Berlin divided into four zones; Nazi war criminals to be tried; reparations with half going to the USSR; free elections in liberated countries (Declaration on Liberated Europe); Poland in the Soviet sphere but with free elections and non-communists in government; USSR to join the war against Japan three months after Germany's defeat; USSR to join the United Nations.

::: q
Explain two reasons why relations were worse at Potsdam than at Yalta.
---
Any two, explained: Roosevelt died (April 1945) and Truman was far more anti-communist, so the personal trust was lost; Germany had surrendered so there was no common enemy forcing compromise; the USA had tested the atomic bomb (16 July 1945) and Truman felt he did not need Soviet help; Stalin had installed a pro-communist government in Poland, which Truman saw as breaking Yalta; disagreement over reparations.
:::

::: q
Compare the Long Telegram and the Novikov Telegram: who wrote each, when, and what did each claim?
---
Long Telegram: George Kennan, US diplomat in Moscow, February 1946 — the USSR was expansionist, building its military and hostile to capitalism, but would back down when faced with strength (basis for containment). Novikov Telegram: Nikolai Novikov, Soviet ambassador in Washington, September 1946 — the USA wanted world supremacy, was building bases and armed forces and was preparing for war with the USSR.
:::

::: q
Explain why Stalin wanted a buffer zone in Eastern Europe.
---
Russia had been invaded from the west through Poland twice (1914 and 1941), and the USSR lost more than 20 million people in the Second World War. Stalin wanted friendly communist governments between the USSR and Germany so that no future invasion could come through them; he also distrusted the West because of its intervention in the Russian Civil War and its delay in opening a second front.
:::

::: q
What were "salami tactics", and in which satellite state are they most associated?
---
The step-by-step removal of opponents "slice by slice" — communists took control of the police, then used arrests, intimidation and splits to remove rival parties one at a time until they held full power. Most associated with Hungary (the communist leader Rákosi), though similar methods were used across Eastern Europe.
:::

::: q
Explain why the Iron Curtain speech (March 1946) damaged relations.
---
Churchill, speaking at Fulton, Missouri, with Truman present, said an iron curtain had descended across Europe and called for an Anglo-American alliance against Soviet expansion. Truman's presence suggested US approval, so Stalin denounced it as a call to war against the USSR. It publicly defined Europe as divided into two hostile camps.
:::

- Consequence answers that just say "it made relations worse" stay in Level 1 — name **what specifically changed** and give a date, figure or name.
- Mixing up the conferences: Tehran = second front; Yalta = four zones and free elections; Potsdam = Truman, Attlee, Oder–Neisse, reparations from own zone, bomb announced.
- Stating Churchill was at Potsdam throughout — Attlee replaced him part-way.
- Narrative answers that only describe the two bullet points are capped at 5 — add at least one event of your own and link each event with "this led to…".
- Importance answers that describe the event without tying it back to **the named focus** (usually US–Soviet relations).
- Getting the telegrams the wrong way round: Kennan (American, February) → Washington; Novikov (Soviet, September) → Moscow.
:::
