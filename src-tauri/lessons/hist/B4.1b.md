## What it is

Elizabeth's first great decision was religion. In 1559 Parliament passed the laws that created the Protestant **Church of England** that still exists — the **religious settlement**. The spec asks for its **features and impact**, and for **the role of the Church of England in society**.

### Why a settlement was needed

In 25 years England's official religion had changed four times: Henry VIII broke with Rome in 1534; Edward VI (1547–53) made the Church firmly Protestant; Mary I (1553–58) restored Catholicism and burned nearly 300 Protestants. Elizabeth was a Protestant, but she faced Catholic bishops in the House of Lords, a largely Catholic nobility and north, Catholic powers abroad (France, Spain, the Pope) — and returning Protestant exiles who wanted to go much further. She wanted a Church that **most people could accept**, that put **her** in charge, and that avoided rebellion.

| Catholic belief/practice | Protestant belief/practice |
|---|---|
| The Pope is head of the Church | The Bible is the highest authority; no Pope |
| Services and Bible in Latin | Services and Bible in English |
| Bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ (**transubstantiation**) | Bread and wine are symbols, or Christ is present only spiritually |
| Priests are special intermediaries, wear rich vestments, cannot marry | Priests guide the people, wear plain clothes, may marry |
| Churches richly decorated with statues, paintings, candles | Churches plain, so nothing distracts from God's word |

### Passing the settlement

Parliament met in January 1559. The House of Commons, with many Protestant MPs, passed the bills easily; the **House of Lords** was the problem because it contained Catholic bishops. After a public debate between Catholic and Protestant churchmen (the Westminster Disputation, spring 1559), **two Catholic bishops were sent to the Tower** for disobedience, and about ten bishoprics were empty because their holders had recently died. Even so, the **Act of Uniformity passed the Lords by only 21 votes to 18** — a sign of how strong Catholic feeling still was among the elite.

### The features of the settlement (1559)

**1. The Act of Supremacy (1559)**
- Made Elizabeth **Supreme Governor** of the Church of England. The title "Governor" rather than Henry VIII's "Supreme Head" was a compromise: it was less offensive to Catholics, who believed only the Pope could head the Church, and to Protestants who believed only Christ could — and to those who felt a woman could not.
- Everyone holding public or Church office — clergy, royal officials, JPs, anyone taking a university degree — had to swear an **oath of supremacy** recognising her as Supreme Governor. Refusal meant losing office.
- Set up the **Ecclesiastical High Commission** to enforce the settlement and discipline the clergy.

**2. The Act of Uniformity (1559)**
- Set out the **appearance of churches and the form of services**.
- Everyone had to use the new **Book of Common Prayer** (in English), based on Edward VI's 1552 book.
- The communion service combined wording from the 1549 and 1552 prayer books, so it could be understood either as the real presence of Christ (acceptable to Catholics) or as a remembrance (acceptable to Protestants) — a deliberate **ambiguity**.
- **Church attendance was compulsory** on Sundays and holy days; anyone absent was fined **12 pence (one shilling)** per time, the money going to the poor.
- Clergy had to wear **special clothing (vestments)**, and churches could keep some decoration.

**3. The Royal Injunctions (1559)**
Issued by Cecil on the queen's behalf, they gave detailed instructions to clergy:
- clergy must teach the royal supremacy and report anyone who refused to attend church;
- every church must have an **English Bible**;
- no one could preach without a **licence** from a bishop;
- pilgrimages, processions and the worship of relics and images were banned;
- clergy could marry, but only with approval;
- traditional features were kept — clergy wore vestments, communion used unleavened wafers, and church music continued.

**Later: the Thirty-Nine Articles (1563)** set out the Church's beliefs in a clearly Protestant way, consolidating the settlement.

### A "middle way" (via media)

| Protestant features | Catholic-looking features |
|---|---|
| Queen, not Pope, governs the Church | Bishops kept — a traditional hierarchy |
| Services and Bible in English | Vestments worn by clergy |
| Clergy allowed to marry | Some decoration kept in churches; Elizabeth kept a crucifix and candles in her own chapel |
| Book of Common Prayer based on the Protestant 1552 book | Ambiguous communion wording; wafers used |
| No pilgrimages, relics or image worship | Kneeling for communion; church music |

The settlement was **Protestant in doctrine but traditional in appearance**. That was deliberate: it aimed to be acceptable to the Catholic majority without alienating Protestants.

### The impact of the settlement

**On the clergy and bishops.**
- **Bishops**: almost all of Mary's surviving bishops refused the oath of supremacy and were **removed**. Only one, **Anthony Kitchin of Llandaff**, took it. Elizabeth appointed new Protestant bishops; **Matthew Parker** became Archbishop of Canterbury in December 1559.
- **Parish clergy**: the great majority — of roughly 9,000 — took the oath. Only a few hundred lost their posts in the 1560s (one careful count gives 261 deprivations). Many were not enthusiastic but conformed to keep their livings.

**On ordinary people.**
- Most parishes accepted the new Church, especially in the south and east. Change came gradually: **royal visitations** in 1559 sent commissioners around the country to check that clergy and churches followed the Injunctions and to remove Catholic items.
- In strongly Catholic areas — the north, Lancashire — many kept Catholic practices, hid vestments and statues, or attended church while privately remaining Catholic ("**church papists**"). Enforcement of the 12-pence fine was patchy at first; Elizabeth did not want to force the issue.
- A small minority, **recusants**, refused to attend at all — their numbers grew later in the 1560s and 1570s.

**On Elizabeth.**
- She gained control over the Church and, through it, a powerful means of controlling what people heard.
- The settlement brought **stability** in the short term: there was no religious rising in the 1560s until 1569.
- But it pleased neither extreme fully. **Puritans** thought it too Catholic (leading to the crucifix and vestments controversies); committed **Catholics** could not accept a Protestant queen as head of the Church. These challenges are covered in the next topic.

### The Church of England: its role in society

In Elizabethan England the parish church was the centre of community life, and the Church was also an arm of the state.

- **Life events**: baptism, marriage and burial all took place in church and were recorded in parish registers.
- **The calendar**: Sundays and holy days structured the year; festivals such as Christmas and Easter, and parish celebrations, were community events.
- **Teaching and control**: clergy read official sermons (**homilies**) that taught obedience to the monarch and warned against rebellion. Only licensed preachers could preach, so the government controlled the message.
- **Information**: the pulpit was used to pass on government announcements and news.
- **Moral discipline**: **Church courts** dealt with moral and religious offences — adultery, drunkenness, swearing, working on Sundays, failing to attend church — and could impose penances.
- **Parish officials**: **churchwardens** reported absentees and helped look after the poor.
- **Money**: parishioners paid **tithes** (a tenth of produce or income) to support the Church.

Because church attendance was compulsory and the monarch governed the Church, **religious conformity and political loyalty were closely linked**: refusing to attend church looked like disloyalty to the queen.

::: terms
- **Religious settlement**: the laws of 1559 that set up Elizabeth's Protestant Church of England — the Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity and the Royal Injunctions.
- **Supreme Governor**: Elizabeth's title as leader of the Church of England, chosen instead of "Supreme Head" to be less provocative.
- **Oath of supremacy**: an oath recognising the queen as Supreme Governor, required of clergy and public officials.
- **Book of Common Prayer**: the English service book that every church had to use from 1559.
- **Vestments**: the special clothing worn by clergy during services.
- **Royal Injunctions**: instructions issued in 1559 telling clergy how to run their churches and services.
- **Recusant**: a Catholic who refused to attend Church of England services.
- **Via media**: the "middle way" — a settlement Protestant in belief but traditional in appearance.
- **Transubstantiation**: the Catholic belief that the bread and wine become the actual body and blood of Christ.
- **Homily**: an official sermon that clergy read to their congregations.
:::

::: example Explain why — 12 marks
Explain why Elizabeth's religious settlement of 1559 took the form of a "middle way". (12)

You may use the following in your answer: the House of Lords; foreign Catholic powers. You must also use information of your own.

**Opposition in the House of Lords.** One reason was that Elizabeth needed laws that could get through Parliament. The Lords contained Catholic bishops and nobles who would block a radical Protestant Church. Even after two bishops were sent to the Tower and with several bishoprics empty, the Act of Uniformity passed by only 21 votes to 18. A more extreme Protestant settlement would probably have failed, so compromises — such as the ambiguous communion wording and keeping vestments — were necessary.

**Fear of foreign Catholic powers.** A second reason was the danger from abroad. England was in debt by about £300,000 and had just lost Calais to France, while Mary, Queen of Scots, married to the French heir, claimed the throne. A settlement that looked violently anti-Catholic might give France, or the Pope, an excuse to act, and could lose the friendship of Philip II of Spain. Keeping bishops and traditional appearances made the Church look less threatening.

**Keeping the Catholic majority loyal.** A third reason, linked to the first two, was the need to avoid rebellion at home. Most people, particularly in the north and Lancashire, were still Catholic in outlook and many nobles were Catholic. By making Elizabeth "Supreme Governor" rather than "Supreme Head", keeping decoration and music, and at first enforcing the 12-pence fine lightly, Elizabeth made it easy for Catholics to conform outwardly.

**Elizabeth's own beliefs.** Finally, the middle way reflected Elizabeth herself. She was a Protestant, which is why the Church had English services, clergy marriage and no Pope, but she liked traditional worship — she kept a crucifix and candles in her own chapel. The settlement therefore combined her Protestant faith with her taste for order and ceremony, and above all with her aim of uniting the country under her control.
:::

::: example How far do you agree — 16 marks
"Elizabeth's religious settlement was successful in the years 1559–68." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)

You may use the following in your answer: the parish clergy; the vestments controversy. You must also use information of your own.

**Criterion.** A successful settlement would be accepted by most people, give Elizabeth control of the Church and prevent religious conflict.

**Success.** By these tests the settlement worked well. The great majority of the roughly 9,000 parish clergy took the oath of supremacy, and only a few hundred were removed in the 1560s. Elizabeth replaced the Catholic bishops with Protestants led by Archbishop Matthew Parker, giving her control of the Church's leadership. Most parishes accepted the Prayer Book, and there was no religious rebellion in the 1560s — a striking contrast with the Western Rising of 1549 over Edward VI's Prayer Book.

**Limits — Puritans.** However, the settlement did not satisfy committed Protestants. Puritans objected to the crucifix in the queen's chapel and to clerical vestments; in 1566, after Archbishop Parker's *Advertisements* enforced the surplice, 37 London clergy refused and lost their posts. This was a challenge from within the Church's own leadership.

**Limits — Catholics.** Nor did it win over committed Catholics. Almost all Marian bishops refused the oath. In the north and Lancashire many remained Catholic in private or became church papists, and some refused to attend at all. The arrival of Mary, Queen of Scots in 1568 gave them a Catholic claimant to rally around.

**Judgement.** I largely agree. By the criteria of acceptance and stability, the settlement succeeded: most people conformed and there was no rebellion before 1569. But its success was partly surface-deep — outward conformity hid Catholic loyalties, and the Puritan quarrels showed that it satisfied neither extreme. It was a success in the short term, but it had not settled religion for good.
