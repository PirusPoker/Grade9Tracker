## What it is

This lesson covers the first part of the historic environment **The British sector of the Western Front, 1914–18: injuries, treatment and the trenches** — the **context**: where the British fought in Flanders and northern France (the **Ypres salient, the Somme, Arras and Cambrai**), how the **trench system** was organised, and why the **terrain** and the **transport and communications** infrastructure mattered for medical treatment; and the **conditions requiring medical treatment**: ill health caused by the trench environment, **wounds from rifles and explosives**, **shrapnel**, **wound infection**, the rise in **head injuries**, and the effects of **gas**. (The RAMC, the chain of evacuation and new surgical techniques are in the next lesson.) Everything here matters because *where* and *how* men were wounded decided *how hard* they were to treat.

### The British sector of the Western Front

After the German advance through Belgium in 1914 was stopped at the Battle of the Marne (September 1914), both sides dug in. By the end of 1914 a line of trenches — the **Western Front** — ran about **400 miles** from the Belgian coast to the Swiss border. The **French** held most of it; the **British Expeditionary Force (BEF)** held a sector in **Flanders** (Belgium) and **northern France**, which lengthened as the army grew from a small professional force in 1914 to about **two million men by 1917**.

### The trench system and its organisation

The British built **lines** of trenches, usually three, linked by **communication trenches**:
- **Front-line (fire) trench** — faced the enemy across **no man's land** (anything from a few dozen metres to several hundred metres wide), protected by **barbed-wire** entanglements. It had a **fire step** to stand on to shoot, a **parapet** of sandbags at the front and a **parados** at the back, and **duckboards** on the floor. It was dug in a zigzag or with **traverses** (fire bays), so that a shell burst or an enemy who got in could only hit one section.
- **Support trench** — a short distance behind, where men moved back to when the front line was shelled and from which reinforcements came.
- **Reserve trench** — further back again, holding troops ready to counter-attack.
- **Communication trenches** — ran back from the front, used to bring up men, food and ammunition and to carry the **wounded** out.
- **Saps** ran forward into no man's land to listening posts; **dugouts** cut into the trench walls gave shelter; latrines were dug in side-trenches.
- Soldiers rotated: typically a few days in the front line, then support, then reserve, then rest behind the lines.

**Why this mattered medically:** trenches were **narrow, deep and zigzagged**, so stretcher bearers had to carry the wounded round tight corners, often lifting the stretcher above their heads — slow, exhausting and dangerous. Front-line medical posts had to be **within reach of the front** but sheltered from shellfire, which is why the first stage of treatment was a small **Regimental Aid Post** in a dugout or ruined building, and bigger facilities were set further back.

### The terrain

- **Flanders (Ypres):** flat, low-lying **clay** farmland with a **high water table** — water lay close to the surface. Trenches **flooded**, so the British often built up breastworks of sandbags rather than digging deep. Constant shelling destroyed the old field drainage system, turning the ground into **mud** and water-filled **shell craters**, worst at Passchendaele in the wet autumn of 1917. Wounded men could **drown** in craters; stretcher parties sank to their knees.
- **The Somme and Arras:** **chalk** — easier to drain and to dig deeply. The Germans built **deep dugouts** on the Somme which survived the British bombardment of June 1916; at Arras, chalk made the tunnels possible.
- **Farmland soil** everywhere had been fertilised with **manure** for centuries. It was full of bacteria (including the ones that cause **gas gangrene** and **tetanus**), so any wound contaminated by soil, mud or dirty uniform was likely to become **infected**.
- **Ridges and high ground** (Messines, Passchendaele, Vimy) gave the enemy observation: movement in daylight — including collecting the wounded — drew fire.

### Transport and communications

- **Roads** behind the lines were narrow, often cobbled, **cratered by shells**, and **congested** with troops, guns, ammunition and supplies. In winter and at Passchendaele they turned to mud.
- The wounded were moved first **on foot or by stretcher**, then by **horse-drawn** and increasingly **motor ambulances**, and by **light (narrow-gauge) railways** and **ambulance trains** from railheads. Some serious cases were moved by **canal barge** — slow but smooth.
- **Communications** were fragile: **telephone** wires were cut by shellfire, so messages relied on **runners**, signal lamps and **carrier pigeons**. Medical units could not always know where the wounded were or how many were coming.
- **Consequences for treatment:** delay — wounded men often lay in no man's land until they could be collected at **night**; delay allowed **infection** to set in and blood loss to continue; during big battles such as the first day of the Somme the system was **overwhelmed**. This is why the RAMC built a **chain of evacuation** with treatment at every stage (next lesson).

### Conditions requiring medical treatment: ill health from the trenches

Many men needed treatment not for wounds but for illnesses caused by living in the trenches.

- **Trench foot:** standing for days in cold water and mud cut off the circulation in the feet; they swelled, went numb, turned red or blue, and could become **gangrenous** and need **amputation**. By the end of the first winter (1914–15) about 20,000 British soldiers had been treated for it. **Prevention:** rubbing feet with **whale oil**, changing into **dry socks** (men were told to carry spare pairs), keeping trenches drained with duckboards and pumps, and officers **inspecting feet**; units with many cases could be treated as negligent. Cases fell sharply once these measures were enforced.
- **Trench fever:** caused by bacteria carried by **body lice**, which infested soldiers' clothing and seams. Symptoms: high temperature, headaches, aching muscles and severe pain in the shins; recovery took weeks and it often returned. It affected hundreds of thousands of men. The link with lice was confirmed only in **1918**; **delousing** stations, baths and laundering uniforms then helped.
- **Shell shock:** the mental and physical effects of constant bombardment and fear — shaking, nightmares, paralysis, loss of speech or sight. It was poorly understood; some were accused of cowardice. The army recorded about **80,000** cases by the end of the war.
- **Other conditions:** **frostbite** and **exposure** in winter; **dysentery** and diarrhoea from contaminated water and food; **respiratory** infections, bronchitis and pneumonia; the **rats** (attracted by food waste and corpses) and poor sanitation spread disease.

### Wounds from rifles and explosives

- **Rifles and machine guns.** The British **Lee-Enfield** and German **Mauser** rifles fired **high-velocity bullets**. At long range a bullet might make a clean wound through the flesh, but at close range, or if it hit **bone**, it shattered the bone and caused a large exit wound. **Machine guns** fired hundreds of rounds a minute, so men were often hit several times. **Snipers** targeted anyone who raised his head above the parapet.
- **Explosives — artillery shells, mortars, grenades and mines.** Artillery was the **biggest killer and wounder** of the war: most estimates suggest well over half of British wounds were caused by shells. High-explosive shells blew bodies apart, buried men alive, and caused severe internal injuries and broken bones. **Fractures of the femur** (thigh bone) from shell or bullet wounds were especially deadly — until the Thomas splint (next lesson).

### The problem of shrapnel

- **Shrapnel shells** were designed to burst in the air above the trenches and scatter **hundreds of small metal balls**; high-explosive shells also broke into jagged **fragments** of casing (also loosely called shrapnel).
- Shrapnel caused **deep, ragged wounds**, often many at once, and carried **pieces of dirty uniform and soil** deep into the flesh.
- Fragments were **hard to find and remove** — which is why **x-rays** became so important — and any left behind caused infection.

### Wound infection

- Because of the manured soil, the delay before treatment, and the fragments of cloth driven into wounds, **infection** was the major danger after being wounded.
- **Gas gangrene** was the most feared: bacteria from the soil multiplied in the damaged, oxygen-starved flesh, producing gas bubbles under the skin, a foul smell, and rapid spread. It could kill within days; often the only treatment was **amputation**.
- **Tetanus** (also from soil bacteria) caused muscle spasms and death; from late 1914 wounded men were routinely given **anti-tetanus serum**, and tetanus rates fell sharply.
- Nineteenth-century **antiseptic** methods (carbolic acid) and **aseptic** hospital surgery were not enough on a battlefield where wounds were already dirty before a doctor saw them — which led to new techniques (debridement, Carrel-Dakin irrigation) in the next lesson.

### Head injuries

- In 1914–15 British soldiers wore **cloth caps**, which gave no protection. Men's heads were the part most often **exposed** above the trench, to snipers and to shrapnel bursting from above, so the number of **head injuries** rose sharply.
- The steel **Brodie helmet** was introduced from **late 1915** and was standard issue by the Battle of the Somme in 1916. It reduced deaths from head wounds, though head injuries remained common and were very hard to treat — brain surgery was in its infancy, and wounded skulls easily became infected.
- Survivors often had **facial injuries**; this led to pioneering **plastic surgery** by Harold Gillies at Aldershot and later Sidcup (next lesson).

### The effects of gas attacks

| Gas | First used | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Chlorine** | by the Germans at **Second Ypres, 22 April 1915**; by the British at **Loos, 25 September 1915** | a greenish-yellow cloud; attacked the lungs, causing choking and death by suffocation; easy to see and smell |
| **Phosgene** | by the Germans from **December 1915** | more deadly and harder to detect; symptoms could be **delayed** for up to two days, so men who seemed well could suddenly collapse |
| **Mustard gas** | by the Germans at **Ypres, July 1917** | almost odourless; caused **blisters** on skin and inside the lungs, **blindness** (usually temporary), internal and external bleeding; lingered in the soil for weeks; caused the most gas casualties, though usually not fatal |

**Protection developed quickly:** from cotton pads soaked in water, urine or bicarbonate of soda (1915), to flannel **gas helmets** soaked in chemicals, to the **small box respirator** (from 1916), which filtered air through chemicals in a box. **Treatment** was limited: remove the man from the gas, strip contaminated clothing, wash the skin and eyes (with bicarbonate solution), give oxygen, and rest. Gas killed a small proportion of those it affected, but it caused **huge numbers of casualties**, great fear, and **long-term lung damage** for many survivors.

### Summary: why this environment made medical treatment so difficult

1. **Numbers**: huge offensives (the Somme, Arras, Passchendaele) produced thousands of casualties a day.
2. **Terrain**: mud and water, narrow trenches and shell craters slowed collection of the wounded.
3. **Infrastructure**: shelled, congested roads and cut communications delayed evacuation.
4. **Infection**: manured soil and dirty clothing made wounds septic before treatment began.
5. **New types of wound**: shrapnel, head injuries and gas needed new methods — which is why the Western Front became a place of medical **experiment** (next lesson).

::: terms
- **Salient**: a bulge in the front line pushing into enemy territory, so the enemy can fire on it from three sides, as at Ypres.
- **No man's land**: the ground between the opposing front-line trenches, covered by barbed wire and enemy fire.
- **Communication trench**: a trench running back from the front line, used to move men and supplies forward and carry the wounded out.
- **Traverse**: a zigzag or bay in a trench, built so that a shell burst or enemy raid only affected one section.
- **Shrapnel**: small metal balls scattered by a shell bursting in the air; the term is also used for jagged fragments of shell casing.
- **Gas gangrene**: a deadly infection caused by soil bacteria in wounds, producing gas in the tissues; often treated by amputation.
- **Trench foot**: swollen, numb and sometimes gangrenous feet caused by standing for long periods in cold water and mud.
- **Trench fever**: a fever with headaches and shin pain, spread by body lice; its cause was confirmed in 1918.
- **Shell shock**: the mental and physical breakdown caused by the strain of bombardment and combat.
- **Small box respirator**: the British gas mask used from 1916, which filtered air through chemicals in a box.
:::

::: method
**Where this sits:** Paper 1, **Section A** — the historic environment (16 marks, about 25 minutes). There are three questions, and this topic feeds all of them.

**Q1 — "Describe two features of…" (4 marks)** e.g. "Describe two features of the trench system on the Western Front."
- **1 mark** for each valid **feature** identified (maximum two), and **1 mark** for **supporting information** about each feature. So: feature + detail, twice.
- No levels, no analysis, no introduction. Two short paragraphs, each: *a feature* (one sentence) + *a precise supporting detail* (one sentence).
- Pick features you can back with a fact: "The front line had a fire step (1). This allowed soldiers to stand and fire over the parapet, while the rest of the trench was deep enough to shelter them (1)."

**Q2(a) — "How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into…?" (8 marks)** — the sources are printed in a separate booklet with their provenance (who, what, when, why).
- **Level 1 (1–2):** a simple judgement on usefulness, with undeveloped comment on content and/or provenance; paraphrase; little context.
- **Level 2 (3–5):** judgements on usefulness **for the enquiry**, using valid criteria, supported by **developed** comment on content and/or provenance; contextual knowledge used to support comments.
- **Level 3 (6–8):** judgements that **take account of how the provenance affects the usefulness of the content**; the sources are analysed; **contextual knowledge is used to interpret** the sources and apply criteria.
- Marking rules to remember: **no credit** for own knowledge that is not linked to evaluating the sources; **no credit** for generic provenance ("it could be biased") that is not used to evaluate the content.
- Structure for each source (A, then B): **Content** — what it shows that is useful *for this enquiry* ("Source A is useful because it shows…"), checked against **own knowledge** ("this is accurate because I know…"); **Provenance** — nature, origin, purpose — and **how that affects** the value of that content ("because this was an official photograph, it may have been chosen to show… so it may not show…"); **Limits** — what it does not show. Finish with a brief overall judgement for each (you do not need to say which is more useful, but it helps).

**Q2(b) — follow-up enquiry (4 marks)** — a four-part frame on the paper:
- **Detail in Source A that I would follow up** (1): quote or pinpoint a specific detail.
- **Question I would ask** (1): a question that **follows on from that detail** and is about the enquiry.
- **What type of source I would look for** (1): a **specific, realistic** type — e.g. RAMC war diaries, army medical statistics, hospital admission records, casualty clearing station records, a nurse's diary, newspaper reports, government reports on trench foot, the Official Medical History of the War.
- **How this might help answer my question** (1): explain what information that source would give that answers **your** question.
- The four parts must **link** — a question unconnected to the detail, or a source that could not answer the question, loses the marks.

**Useful source knowledge for this topic:** *national* sources — army records and statistics, government reports, national newspapers (censored by the government), medical journals such as *The Lancet* and the *British Medical Journal*; *local* sources — personal diaries and letters (censored if sent home), photographs (official photographers; many images posed or censored), hospital and unit records, trench maps.

::: example 8-mark usefulness
How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into the problems caused by the trench environment for soldiers' health? Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context. (8)

*Source A: a summary of an order issued to British units on the Western Front in 1915 about trench foot. It instructs officers to inspect men's feet daily, orders men to rub their feet with whale oil before going into the line and to carry dry socks, and warns that units with many cases of trench foot will be investigated.*

*Source B: a description of an official British army photograph taken during the Third Battle of Ypres in August 1917. It shows a team of stretcher bearers, up to their knees in mud, carrying a wounded soldier on a stretcher across flooded, shell-cratered ground.*

Source A is useful because it shows that trench foot was a serious and widespread problem — the army would not issue detailed orders about daily foot inspections and whale oil unless many men were being affected. This fits what I know: by the winter of 1914–15 about 20,000 British soldiers had been treated for trench foot. Its provenance makes it reliable for showing *official* concern and the prevention methods used, because it is an order written for officers to follow, not for the public. The warning that units would be investigated suggests the army saw trench foot as partly the men's fault, which is useful for understanding attitudes. However, it is limited: it shows what was ordered, not whether the orders were obeyed or how conditions felt, and it does not mention other trench illnesses such as trench fever, which was spread by lice.

Source B is useful because it shows how the flooded, cratered terrain made evacuating the wounded slow and exhausting — a team was needed to carry a single stretcher. This matches my knowledge of Flanders: the clay soil and high water table, made worse by shelling that destroyed the drainage, turned the ground at Passchendaele into mud in which men could drown. As a photograph taken at the time, it gives direct visual evidence of conditions. But it was an official photograph, taken by an army photographer and passed by the censor, so it may have been chosen to show the bearers' heroism rather than the worst suffering, and it shows only one moment in one place — the Somme's chalk trenches, for example, drained better.

Overall, Source A is more useful for the problem of illness caused by the trenches because it reveals the scale of trench foot and how the army responded, while Source B is more useful for the way the environment affected the wounded and their treatment. Together they are useful but need other sources, such as medical statistics, to show how many men were affected.
