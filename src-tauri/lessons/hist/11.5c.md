## What it is

The historic environment is not only about *what happened* on the Western Front — it is about **how historians find out**. The second strand of the specification is *knowledge, selection and use of sources for historical enquiries*. You must know:

- **national sources** relevant to the Western Front — e.g. army records, national newspapers, government reports, medical articles;
- **local sources** — e.g. personal accounts, photographs, hospital records, army statistics;
- the **strengths and weaknesses** of different types of source **for specific enquiries**;
- how to **frame questions** relevant to an enquiry;
- how to **select appropriate sources** for a specific investigation.

This is tested directly by the two-part source question in Section A of Paper 1: **Q2(a)** "How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into…?" (8 marks) and **Q2(b)** "How could you follow up Source A/B to find out more about…?" (4 marks). Together they are 12 of the 16 Section A marks, so this topic is worth as much as all the Western Front content put together.

### The golden rule: usefulness depends on the enquiry

No source is useful or useless in itself. A soldier's diary is excellent for an enquiry into **what it felt like** to be wounded, but weak for an enquiry into **how many** men died of gas gangrene. Army statistics are the reverse. Always ask: *useful for what?*

A source can be **useful even if it is biased** — a propaganda photograph is very useful for an enquiry into how the government wanted the public to see the medical services. And a reliable source can be of **little use** if it simply does not cover the enquiry.

### What makes a source useful: content, provenance, context

| Test | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| **Content** | What does it tell me about *this* enquiry? What does it show directly, and what can I infer? What does it leave out? |
| **Provenance — Nature** | What type of source is it (diary, photograph, war diary, newspaper, statistics)? Private or public? Written at the time or later? |
| **Provenance — Origin** | Who produced it, and when? Were they in a position to know (an eyewitness, a senior officer, a journalist who was not there)? |
| **Provenance — Purpose** | Why was it produced — to record, to inform the army, to persuade the public, to comfort a family, to publish later? |
| **Contextual knowledge** | Does the content fit what I know? Is it typical of the Western Front, or a special case (e.g. the Somme, Passchendaele)? |

### Censorship and propaganda: the key context for provenance

Many Western Front sources were shaped by **censorship**:
- The **Defence of the Realm Act (DORA)**, passed in **August 1914**, gave the government power to censor the press.
- Soldiers' **letters home** were read and censored by their officers; men knew this and often wrote cheerfully to avoid worrying their families.
- Private **cameras** were banned for soldiers from early in the war; most photographs we have were taken by **official photographers**, whose images were checked before publication and were sometimes posed or reconstructed.
- The official film **The Battle of the Somme** (1916) was seen by huge audiences in Britain; some of its scenes were staged.

So an official or published source may **play down** suffering and failures; a private source written later may be more frank but less precise.

### National sources

National sources were produced by or for the country as a whole — the army, government, national press or the medical profession.

| Source type | Examples | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Army records** | **Unit war diaries** (daily records kept by every battalion, field ambulance and casualty clearing station, now at The National Archives); service records; orders and reports | Written at the time by people involved; not meant for publication, so less reason to distort; precise dates, places, numbers | Brief and matter-of-fact; little about individual experience; many service records were destroyed by bombing in 1940; quality depends on who wrote them |
| **National newspapers** | *The Times*, *Daily Mail*, illustrated papers | Show what the public was told; published quickly; reports from war correspondents | Censored under DORA; often patriotic or propaganda; journalists were kept away from the front line for much of the war |
| **Government reports** | Reports on casualties, medical services, shell shock; the official *History of the Great War: Medical Services* compiled after the war | Based on large amounts of evidence; overview of the whole army; statistics | May present the government and army favourably; written after the event; less about individual experiences |
| **Medical articles** | Articles in *The Lancet* and the *British Medical Journal* by army doctors, e.g. on wound treatment, splints or blood transfusion | Written by experts, often with case details and results; show how new techniques spread | Technical; written for doctors, so may focus on successes; may cover only a small number of cases |

### Local sources

Local sources relate to a particular place, unit or person.

| Source type | Examples | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal accounts** | Letters, diaries, memoirs of soldiers, nurses, doctors and stretcher bearers; later oral-history interviews | Eyewitness detail and feelings; show what treatment was like for the individual | One person's view; letters censored and self-censored; memoirs and interviews written years later — memory may fade or be shaped by later views |
| **Photographs** | Official war photographs; a few private ones taken early in the war | Show conditions directly — mud, trenches, aid posts, ambulances | Official, censored and sometimes posed; show one moment only; may be chosen to give a positive impression; no sound, no before or after |
| **Hospital records** | Admission and discharge registers, case notes, CCS and base hospital records | Precise: names, dates, wounds, treatments, outcomes; can be used to find patterns | Incomplete survival; brief; written by busy staff; may not explain why something happened |
| **Army statistics** | Casualty returns; the War Office's *Statistics of the Military Effort of the British Empire* (1922) | Show scale and trends over time — e.g. numbers wounded, types of wound, deaths from disease | Categories may be inconsistent; figures can be incomplete in major battles; say nothing about experience |

Remember that the same source can be both: a war diary is an **army record** (national) but relates to one **local** unit. The labels matter less than knowing what each type can and cannot tell you.

### Framing questions for an enquiry

A good enquiry question is:
- **focused** — on one issue, place or time ("How long did it take to move a wounded man from the aid post to the casualty clearing station at Arras in April 1917?", not "What was the war like?");
- **answerable** from sources that probably exist;
- **linked** to a detail in the source you have been given, when you are following up.

Ways to generate questions: *How many…? How often…? How typical was…? How long…? Why did…? What happened next…? Did this change over time…?*

### Selecting appropriate sources

Match the type of source to the type of question:

| If the question is about… | Choose… |
|---|---|
| Numbers, scale, trends (how many, how often) | Army statistics, government reports, hospital admission registers |
| What happened on a particular day in a particular place | Unit war diaries, hospital records |
| Experience, feelings, conditions | Letters, diaries, memoirs, oral-history interviews, photographs |
| Whether a technique worked | Medical articles, hospital case notes, statistics before and after |
| What the public was told | National newspapers, official film and photographs |

### The follow-up question (Q2b): the chain of four

In Q2(b) you complete four linked steps. Think of it as a chain, where each step must connect to the one before:

1. **Detail in the source I would follow up** — pick something specific and quotable from the source (a place, a number, a claim), not a vague theme.
2. **Question I would ask** — a focused question directly about that detail.
3. **Type of source I could use** — a real, specific type of source (e.g. "the war diary of the field ambulance", "admission registers of the base hospital at Étaples"), not "the internet" or "a book".
4. **How this might help answer my question** — say exactly what information the source would contain and how it would answer your question.

A common trap: choosing a source that could not answer the question (e.g. a photograph to find out how many men died), or asking a question that is not linked to the detail.

### Building a Q2(a) answer

1. **Source A — content:** what it shows about the enquiry; test against own knowledge (a precise fact).
2. **Source A — provenance:** how the nature, origin and purpose make it more or less useful **for this enquiry**.
3. **Source B** — the same.
4. **Short judgement** — which is more useful and why, or how they are useful together (one covers what the other misses).

Weak answers list generic weaknesses ("it might be biased", "it is only one person"). Strong answers explain the **specific effect** on the enquiry ("because letters were censored and she was writing to her mother, she may have left out the deaths she saw, so the source is less useful for the enquiry into how many men died at the CCS, but still useful for what the work involved").

### Example sources you might meet

- A photograph of stretcher bearers in mud near Ypres (official photographer, 1917).
- Extracts from a nurse's diary or a later memoir — the VAD **Vera Brittain** served at Étaples in 1917 and published her memoir *Testament of Youth* in **1933**, over fifteen years later.
- A page from a field ambulance war diary for the Somme, July 1916.
- An article in the *British Medical Journal* by an army surgeon on the Thomas splint or on blood transfusion — Oswald Robertson reported his stored-blood transfusions in the *BMJ* in **1918**.
- Army statistics of wounds by cause (shell, bullet, gas).
- A newspaper report of the Battle of Arras, 1917.

::: terms
- **Enquiry**: the specific question an investigation is trying to answer; usefulness is always judged against it.
- **Provenance**: where a source comes from — its nature, origin and purpose (NOP).
- **Utility**: how useful a source is for a particular enquiry, taking into account content, provenance and context.
- **National source**: a source produced by or for the country as a whole, such as army records, national newspapers, government reports or medical articles.
- **Local source**: a source about a particular place, unit or person, such as a diary, photograph, hospital register or unit statistics.
- **War diary**: the official daily record kept by every army unit, including field ambulances and casualty clearing stations.
- **Censorship**: official control of what could be published or written home, e.g. under the Defence of the Realm Act (August 1914).
- **Contextual knowledge**: your own knowledge of the Western Front used to test whether a source's content is accurate and typical.
:::

::: method
This topic is the skills half of **Paper 1, Section A (historic environment)**: Q2(a) and Q2(b) are always based on two sources about the Western Front. Q1 (4 marks) is the "Describe two features" knowledge question covered in 11.5a and 11.5b.

**Q2(a) "How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into…? Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context." (8 marks, about 12 minutes).**
- Read the **enquiry** first and underline it — everything you write must be about usefulness for *that* enquiry.
- For each source write a paragraph: **content** (what it tells you, backed or challenged by a precise piece of your own knowledge), then **provenance** (how its nature, origin and purpose affect its value for this enquiry — strength and limitation).
- Finish with a sentence comparing or combining them.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple comments on usefulness; content or provenance taken at face value; little or no contextual knowledge.
- Level 2 (3–5): judgements on usefulness based on content and provenance, with some relevant contextual knowledge; may be unbalanced between the sources or undeveloped.
- Level 3 (6–8): judgements on the usefulness of **both** sources that take into account how provenance affects the value of the content for the enquiry, supported by precise and relevant contextual knowledge.
- Generic statements ("it is only one person's view", "it may be biased") without explaining the effect on the enquiry keep you in Level 1–2.

**Q2(b) "How could you follow up Source A to find out more about…? In your answer, you must give the question you would ask and the type of source you could use." (4 marks, about 5 minutes).** The answer is printed as a table with four prompts; 1 mark for each linked part:
- *Detail in Source A that I would follow up* — a specific detail taken from the source (1).
- *Question I would ask* — a focused question linked to the detail and the enquiry (1).
- *What type of source I could use* — a specific, realistic type of source (1).
- *How this might help answer my question* — explain what the source would contain that answers the question (1).
If the parts do not link, marks are lost down the chain — the question must come from the detail, the source must be able to answer the question.

**Knowledge you need ready:** the strengths and weaknesses of army records, national newspapers, government reports, medical articles, personal accounts, photographs, hospital records and army statistics; the effect of censorship (DORA, censored letters, official photographers).
:::

::: example Usefulness (8)
How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into the effectiveness of the chain of evacuation during the Battle of Arras (April 1917)? Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context. (8)

Source A: a summary of a report in a national newspaper, April 1917. It praises the arrangements for the wounded at Arras, describing an underground hospital in the old quarries and saying that the wounded were being treated within hours.

Source B: a summary of the war diary of a British casualty clearing station near Arras for 9–14 April 1917. It records the numbers admitted each day, that the unit was overwhelmed on 10–11 April, that operating teams worked in shifts around the clock, and that ambulance trains were delayed.

Model answer:

Source A is useful because it describes a real feature of the chain of evacuation at Arras: the underground hospital in the chalk quarries, which I know had about 700 beds, operating theatres, electric lighting and running water, and was close enough to the front for men to be treated quickly. However, its provenance limits its value for judging effectiveness. It is a national newspaper published during the war, when the press was censored under the Defence of the Realm Act, and its purpose was partly to reassure families at home. It is therefore likely to stress successes and leave out problems, so it tells me more about what the public was told than about how well the system really worked.

Source B is more useful for this enquiry. A war diary was an official record kept by the unit itself, written day by day and not intended for publication, so it has little reason to hide problems. Its content shows both strengths — surgeons operating around the clock, which fits my knowledge that from 1915 more surgery was done at casualty clearing stations — and weaknesses, such as being overwhelmed and delays to ambulance trains. The numbers admitted each day give precise evidence. Its limitation is that it covers just one casualty clearing station for six days, so it may not be typical of the whole Arras front.

Overall Source B is more useful because its purpose was to record rather than to reassure, but the two together are valuable: A shows the facilities the army had built, while B shows how those facilities coped with the pressure of a major battle.
:::

::: example Follow-up (4)
How could you follow up Source B (above) to find out more about the effectiveness of the chain of evacuation during the Battle of Arras? In your answer, you must give the question you would ask and the type of source you could use. (4)

- Detail in Source B that I would follow up: the casualty clearing station was overwhelmed on 10–11 April. (1)
- Question I would ask: How many wounded men died at or on the way to casualty clearing stations near Arras on 10–11 April compared with the rest of the battle? (1)
- What type of source I could use: army casualty statistics for the Battle of Arras, or the admission and death registers of the casualty clearing stations. (1)
- How this might help answer my question: the registers record each man's date of admission and whether he died, so I could compare death rates on the busiest days with quieter days and see whether being overwhelmed made the system less effective. (1)
:::

::: example Choosing sources (4)
A historian is investigating what it was like for stretcher bearers carrying the wounded at Passchendaele in 1917. Explain why a stretcher bearer's diary would be more useful than army statistics for this enquiry, and give one limitation of the diary. (4)

The enquiry is about *experience* — what the work was like — so a diary is more useful (1). A stretcher bearer was an eyewitness who wrote at the time about his own work, so it could describe the mud, the exhaustion and the danger, such as needing several men to carry one stretcher through flooded shell holes (1). Army statistics could show how many were wounded but nothing about what carrying them felt like (1). A limitation is that it records only one man's experience in one unit, so it may not be typical, and he may have left things out in case the diary was found (1).
:::

::: q
Give two examples of national sources and two examples of local sources for a study of the Western Front. (4)
---
National (any two): army records such as war diaries or orders; national newspapers such as The Times; government reports such as the official history of the medical services; medical articles in The Lancet or British Medical Journal (1 each). Local (any two): personal accounts such as letters, diaries or memoirs; photographs; hospital records such as admission registers; army statistics for a unit (1 each).
:::

::: q
Why might a soldier's letter home be of limited use for an enquiry into the conditions in a casualty clearing station? (3)
---
Letters were read by officers and censored (1), so details of problems or locations could be removed (1); soldiers also often wrote cheerfully to avoid worrying their families, so the letter may play down suffering (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one strength and one weakness of official photographs as evidence of treatment on the Western Front. (4)
---
Strength: a photograph shows conditions directly, e.g. mud around stretcher bearers or the layout of an aid post (1), detail that words may not convey (1). Weakness: official photographers worked under censorship and some scenes were posed or chosen to give a positive impression (1), and a photograph shows only one moment in one place, so may not be typical (1).
:::

::: q
Which type of source would be most useful for finding out how many soldiers were treated at a particular base hospital in 1916, and why? (2)
---
The hospital's admission registers (or army statistics) (1), because they record every patient admitted with dates, so the number can be counted precisely (1).
:::

::: q
What is wrong with this follow-up answer? Detail: "the wounded waited hours for ambulances". Question: "What was the Battle of the Somme?" Source: "the internet". (3)
---
The question is not linked to the detail — it should ask something like how long the wounded waited and why (1). The question is too broad/general to be a useful enquiry question (1). "The internet" is not a type of source; a specific type such as a field ambulance war diary or army statistics is needed (1).
:::

::: q
Why is a memoir written in the 1930s by a former nurse both useful and limited for an enquiry into nursing at Étaples in 1917? (4)
---
Useful: written by an eyewitness who nursed there (1), so it can describe the work and feelings in detail and more frankly than a censored letter (1). Limited: written many years later, so memory may be inaccurate or shaped by later views of the war (1); written for publication, so it may be dramatised to interest readers or to make a point (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what "usefulness depends on the enquiry" means, using army statistics as an example. (3)
---
A source's value changes with the question being asked (1). Army statistics would be very useful for an enquiry into how many men died of wounds or the proportion of shell wounds (1), but of little use for an enquiry into what it felt like to be treated at an aid post, because they contain no personal experience (1).
:::

::: watch
- Judge usefulness **for the enquiry in the question** — answers that discuss the source in general drop to Level 1–2.
- Do not dismiss a source as "biased so useless": explain **how** its provenance affects its value for this enquiry, and what it is still useful for.
- Use **precise own knowledge** to test content (e.g. Arras hospital about 700 beds; about 57,000 British casualties on 1 July 1916) — Level 3 needs contextual knowledge.
- Evaluate **both** sources; one excellent paragraph on Source A alone cannot reach Level 3.
- In the follow-up question, keep the **chain linked**: the question must come from the detail, and the source must be able to answer the question. "The internet", "a textbook" or "Google" are not types of source.
- Remember the effects of **censorship** (DORA, censored letters, official photographers) when judging wartime sources.
:::

::: test
Describe two features of the problems of transporting the wounded on the Western Front. (4)
---
Award 1 mark for each valid feature and 1 for supporting detail (max 2 per feature). For example:
- Mud and shell holes slowed stretcher bearers (1): at Passchendaele in 1917 six or more men could be needed for one stretcher (1).
- Trenches were narrow and zigzagged (1), making it hard to carry stretchers along them (1).
- Roads were damaged by shelling and crowded with military traffic (1), so motor ambulances were delayed (1).
- Horse-drawn ambulances were slow and bumpy (1), causing pain and bleeding for badly wounded men (1).
:::

::: test
Describe two features of casualty clearing stations on the Western Front. (4)
---
Award 1 mark per feature and 1 for supporting detail. For example:
- They were a distance behind the lines (1), usually about 7–12 miles, near railways or roads (1).
- They carried out triage (1), sorting the wounded into walking wounded, urgent cases and those unlikely to survive (1).
- Surgery was performed there (1), increasingly from 1915, to excise wounds before gas gangrene set in (1).
- They had nursing staff and equipment (1), such as QAIMNS nurses and, later, X-ray machines (1).
:::

::: test
How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into the problem of wound infection on the Western Front? Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context. (8)

Source A: a summary of an article by a British army surgeon in a medical journal in 1915. He describes cases of gas gangrene in men who had waited a long time before treatment, and explains how he cut away damaged tissue from wounds.

Source B: a summary of a page from the admissions register of a base hospital, 1915, listing each soldier's name, unit, wound, date of admission and outcome. Several entries record death from gas gangrene.
---
Level 3 (6–8): judgements on the usefulness of both sources, taking into account how provenance affects the value of the content for the enquiry; supported by precise, relevant contextual knowledge.
Level 2 (3–5): judgements on usefulness based on content and/or provenance, with some contextual knowledge, but not fully developed or for one source only.
Level 1 (1–2): simple judgement, content or provenance taken at face value; little or no contextual knowledge.

Indicative content:
- A — content: gas gangrene, delays in treatment, wound excision; fits knowledge that bacteria in the manured soil infected shrapnel wounds and that excision (debridement) became standard, leading to surgery moving forward to CCSs. Provenance: expert surgeon writing at the time for other doctors, so informed and detailed; but may focus on his own cases and methods, possibly those that worked; only one surgeon's experience.
- B — content: shows infection killed men and gives precise dates and outcomes; could be used to count cases. Provenance: official hospital record written at the time, no reason to distort; but brief, one hospital, one page, gives no explanation of causes or treatment.
- Together: A explains causes and treatment, B shows the effects and scale in one hospital.
:::

::: test
How could you follow up Source A (in the previous question) to find out more about the problem of wound infection on the Western Front? In your answer, you must give the question you would ask and the type of source you could use. (4)
---
1 mark for each linked part: a relevant detail from Source A; a question linked to that detail; a relevant type of source; an explanation of how the source would help answer the question. For example:
- Detail: men who had waited a long time before treatment developed gas gangrene. (1)
- Question: Did the number of deaths from gas gangrene fall after more surgery was carried out at casualty clearing stations from 1915? (1)
- Source: army medical statistics of causes of death from wounds for each year of the war, or the official history of the medical services. (1)
- How it helps: comparing the figures for 1915 with those for 1916–17 would show whether earlier treatment reduced deaths from infection. (1)
:::

::: test
How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into the work of nurses on the Western Front? Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context. (8)

Source A: a description of an official British photograph from 1917 showing nurses in clean uniforms standing beside neatly made beds in a ward of a base hospital at Étaples.

Source B: a summary of the private diary of a VAD nurse at a casualty clearing station in 1917. She writes about working through the night after an attack, dressing wounds infected with gas gangrene, and nursing men who died.
---
Levels as for the previous 8-mark question (Level 3 6–8; Level 2 3–5; Level 1 1–2).

Indicative content:
- A — content: shows nurses at a base hospital and the ordered facilities there; fits knowledge that QAIMNS and VAD nurses staffed base hospitals near the coast such as Étaples. Provenance: official photograph, censored and possibly posed; purpose to reassure the public, so shows a calm scene not the pressure after a battle; one moment, no sense of the work itself.
- B — content: nurses working near the front, heavy workload, infection and death; fits knowledge that nurses increasingly served at CCSs and faced gas gangrene. Provenance: private diary, written at the time for herself, so likely to be frank; but one nurse, one unit, and VAD perspective (less trained than QAIMNS).
- Judgement: B more useful for what the work involved; A useful for how the work was presented to the public and for the setting.
:::

::: test
A student is investigating how successful new medical techniques were on the Western Front. Explain which two types of source they should select, and what the strengths and weaknesses of each would be for this enquiry. (8)
---
Level 3 (6–8): selects two appropriate source types and explains precisely how the strengths and weaknesses of each affect their value for measuring success; uses specific examples of techniques (e.g. Thomas splint, blood transfusion, wound excision).
Level 2 (3–5): selects relevant source types with some explanation of strengths and weaknesses, not always linked to the enquiry.
Level 1 (1–2): names source types with generic comments.

Indicative content:
- Army statistics / official history: can show before-and-after figures, e.g. femur-fracture deaths falling from around 80% to around 20% after the Thomas splint; large-scale and systematic; but compiled officially, categories may change, and figures in big battles may be incomplete.
- Medical articles (The Lancet, BMJ): written by the doctors who used the techniques, with case details — e.g. Robertson's 1918 BMJ report on stored blood at Cambrai; but small numbers of cases and a tendency to report successes.
- Hospital records could also be credited: precise outcomes for individual patients, but limited to one hospital.
- Best answers explain that combining statistics (scale) with medical articles or hospital records (detail) gives a fuller judgement of success.
:::
