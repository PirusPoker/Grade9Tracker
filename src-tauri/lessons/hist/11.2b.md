## What it is

This lesson covers how people in Renaissance England were **treated and cared for** (continuity and change from the medieval period), the **key individual William Harvey** and his discovery of the circulation of the blood, and the case study of the **Great Plague in London, 1665**. The theme that links all three: discoveries were made, and authorities became more organised, but **ordinary treatment barely changed**, because nobody yet knew what caused disease.

### Continuity and change in prevention and treatment

**Continuity — most treatments were medieval:**
- **Bloodletting and purging** remained the standard treatments, still based on the Four Humours — even after Harvey.
- **Herbal remedies** made at home stayed the main treatment for most people. Nicholas Culpeper's *Complete Herbal* (1653), printed in English, was a bestseller.
- **Religious and supernatural remedies** continued. The **"Royal Touch"**: people believed a monarch's touch could cure scrofula (a tuberculous swelling of the neck glands, called "the King's Evil"); Charles II is recorded as touching tens of thousands of sufferers. **Transference** — the idea a disease could be passed to something else (e.g. rubbing a wart with a piece of meat and burying it) — was common.
- **Prevention** still relied on the *regimen* (diet, exercise, rest) and on purifying the air.

**Change — new remedies and new sellers:**
- **New ingredients from overseas**, especially the Americas: **cinchona bark** (quinine) against fevers such as malaria, tobacco (wrongly thought to be a cure-all), ipecacuanha to cause vomiting. Their arrival was driven by trade and exploration.
- **Chemical cures**: followers of Paracelsus used chemicals such as mercury (for syphilis, which reached Europe in the 1490s) and antimony.
- **More people selling medicine**: apothecaries gained their own organisation (the Society of Apothecaries, chartered in 1617); unlicensed "quacks" sold cures at fairs and markets, helped by printed advertisements.

### Care in the community and in hospitals

- **The Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536–41)** shut down most hospitals because they were run by the Church. Care for the sick was badly disrupted, especially for the poor.
- **Some hospitals were re-founded as charities** run by town authorities or funded by wealthy donors: **St Bartholomew's** (re-founded 1546) and **St Thomas's** (re-founded 1551) in London. They were no longer run by monks, so care of the soul mattered less and **treating the body** mattered more; physicians increasingly **visited** and treated patients. **Pest houses** were set up for those with infectious disease such as plague.
- **Most care still happened at home**, given by women, using herbal remedies. Wealthy women sometimes acted as healers for their whole community — **Lady Grace Mildmay** (1552–1620) recorded preparing remedies and treating local people on her Northamptonshire estate.
- Apothecaries and barber surgeons continued to be the everyday medical help for townspeople; physicians remained expensive.

**Judgement:** the *organisation* of care changed (from Church to charity and town) and hospitals began to focus more on cure, but the *methods* of care were largely the same as in 1400.

### Key individual: William Harvey (1578–1657)

**Background.** Harvey studied at Cambridge and then at **Padua**, where his teacher **Fabricius** had described the **valves in the veins** — the tradition of Vesalius. He became physician at **St Bartholomew's Hospital** and royal physician to **James I and Charles I**.

**What Galen had said:** blood was made in the liver from food and was **used up** as fuel by the body; it passed from one side of the heart to the other through invisible holes in the septum; veins and arteries carried different blood.

**Harvey's method** — experiment, observation and measurement:
- dissected living **cold-blooded animals** (such as frogs and snakes), whose hearts beat slowly enough to watch, and also human corpses;
- showed the heart acts as a **pump**, pushing blood out into the arteries;
- used **ligatures** (tight bandages) on the arm to show that blood in the veins flows only **towards** the heart — the valves stop it flowing back;
- **calculated** that the heart pumps out far more blood in an hour than the body could possibly make from food — so the same blood must be **circulating**.

**Publication:** *An Anatomical Account of the Motion of the Heart and Blood* (*De Motu Cordis*), **1628**.

**Reaction and impact:**
- Many doctors rejected it at first — it contradicted Galen — and the writer John Aubrey later recorded that Harvey's practice declined after he published.
- He could not explain **how blood passed from arteries to veins** — the capillaries were only seen with a microscope by Marcello Malpighi in **1661**.
- It had **little immediate effect on treatment**: bloodletting continued for 200 years. Attempts at blood transfusion in the 1660s failed, because blood groups were not discovered until 1901.
- **Long-term significance**: it was the foundation of modern **physiology**, destroyed another part of Galen's system, and made later surgery and transfusion possible.

**Factors behind Harvey:** individual genius and persistence; **science and technology** — the use of water pumps in London (for fire-fighting and water supply) helped him see the heart as a pump; the culture of **dissection** after Vesalius; **printing** spread his book across Europe; **royal patronage** gave him status and protection.

### Case study: the Great Plague in London, 1665

**The outbreak.** Plague began in the poor suburb of St Giles-in-the-Fields in spring 1665 and peaked in **September 1665**, when more than 7,000 deaths were recorded in a single week. The official **Bills of Mortality** recorded about **68,600** plague deaths that year, but the real total was probably closer to **100,000** — about a fifth of London's people. The rich, including **Charles II and the court**, left London; so did many physicians.

**What people thought caused it:** God's punishment; **miasma**; the stars — a **comet** seen in late 1664 was thought to be a sign; an imbalance of the humours; and **contagion** — being near the sick.

**Attempts to prevent its spread — the Plague Orders.** The **Lord Mayor and aldermen of the City of London** issued orders (based on earlier national plague orders), enforced much more systematically than anything in 1348:
- **Examiners and searchers** (usually old women) inspected the dead and reported plague cases;
- **infected houses were shut up** with everyone inside for a month or more, with a **red cross** and the words "Lord have mercy upon us" painted on the door, and **watchmen** outside;
- the dead were buried at **night** in **plague pits**, at least six feet deep;
- **dogs and cats were killed** in large numbers because they were thought to spread infection — which may have helped the rats;
- **fires** were lit in the streets to purify the air; streets were cleaned;
- **public gatherings were banned** — theatres closed — and beggars removed;
- people needed a **certificate of health** to leave the city;
- **pest houses** isolated some of the sick.

**Individual precautions:** carrying herbs and posies, smoking **tobacco**, wearing amulets and charms (such as the word "abracadabra" written in a triangle), prayer and fasting, fleeing the city.

**Attempts at treatment:** bloodletting and purging; lancing buboes; herbal remedies and "plague water"; strapping a live chicken to a bubo to draw out the poison; the Royal College of Physicians published advice on remedies.

**End:** deaths fell as the weather turned colder in late 1665 and into 1666. The Great Fire of September 1666 is often said to have ended the plague, but it had already declined.

### Comparing 1348 and 1665

| | Black Death 1348–49 | Great Plague 1665 |
|---|---|---|
| **Ideas about cause** | God, planets, miasma, humours | God, a comet, miasma, humours, contagion — almost unchanged |
| **Treatment** | prayer, bloodletting, purging, lancing buboes, herbs | much the same, plus tobacco and chemical remedies |
| **Government action** | Edward III's 1349 order to clean London's streets | detailed Plague Orders: searchers, shut-up houses, watchmen, night burials, killing dogs and cats, bans on gatherings, health certificates |
| **Record keeping** | none | weekly Bills of Mortality |

The big change is in **organisation by the authorities** (government as an institution), not in understanding — which is why neither outbreak was stopped.

::: terms
- **Circulation of the blood**: Harvey's discovery that blood is pumped by the heart around the body in a continuous loop, not made and used up.
- **Capillaries**: tiny blood vessels connecting arteries and veins, first seen by Malpighi in 1661.
- **Ligature**: a tight bandage or thread tied round a limb or blood vessel, used by Harvey to show the direction of blood flow.
- **Plague Orders**: rules issued by the Lord Mayor and aldermen of London in 1665 to stop the plague spreading, such as shutting up infected houses.
- **Searcher**: a person, usually an older woman, paid to inspect dead bodies and report whether plague was the cause of death.
- **Bills of Mortality**: weekly printed lists of deaths and their causes in London parishes.
- **Royal Touch**: the belief that the touch of a king or queen could cure scrofula, the "King's Evil".
- **Transference**: the belief that a disease could be passed from a person into an object, animal or other person.
- **Pest house**: a building where people with infectious diseases such as plague were isolated.
:::

::: example 4-mark similarity
Explain one way in which treatments for the plague in 1665 were similar to treatments in 1348–49. (4)

In both outbreaks treatments were based on the Theory of the Four Humours. In 1348–49 physicians and barber surgeons tried to rebalance the humours through bloodletting and purging, and lanced buboes to drain the poison. In 1665, more than 300 years later, London doctors still bled and purged plague victims and lanced their buboes, even though Harvey had shown in 1628 that blood circulates — so treatment rested on the same humoral theory in both.
:::

::: example 12-mark explain why
Explain why Harvey's discovery of the circulation of the blood had little impact on the treatment of patients in the seventeenth century. (12)
You may use: Galen; blood transfusion. You must also use information of your own.

The first reason is that doctors remained loyal to **Galen**. Harvey's 1628 book contradicted Galen's idea that blood was made in the liver and used up, and many physicians trained on Galen refused to accept it; John Aubrey recorded that Harvey's practice fell away after he published. Since their treatments rested on the Four Humours, they kept bloodletting and purging.

The second reason is that Harvey's discovery gave doctors **nothing new to do**. Knowing that blood circulates did not explain what caused disease or suggest a cure. When doctors tried **blood transfusions** in the 1660s, they failed because blood groups were unknown until 1901, so the most obvious practical use of the discovery ended in failure.

A third reason, of my own, is the **limits of technology**. Harvey could not show how blood passed from arteries to veins; the capillaries were only seen by Malpighi through a microscope in 1661, so his theory had a gap critics could exploit.

These factors combined: an incomplete theory that offered no treatment was easy for conservative doctors to ignore. Its importance was long-term — it became the basis of physiology — rather than immediate.
:::

::: example 16-mark judgement
"Government action was the main difference between the responses to the Black Death (1348–49) and the Great Plague (1665)." How far do you agree? (16 + 4 SPaG)
You may use: shutting up houses; religious responses. You must also use information of your own.

**For.** In 1348–49 government action was minimal — Edward III's 1349 order to clean London's streets. In 1665 the Lord Mayor's Plague Orders shut up infected houses with a red cross and watchmen, appointed searchers, ordered night burials in plague pits, killed dogs and cats, banned gatherings and required health certificates. The Bills of Mortality even measured the outbreak week by week.

**Against — other differences.** Some responses changed for other reasons: new remedies like tobacco reflected trade with the Americas; printed advice from the Royal College of Physicians reflected the printing press; flagellant processions had disappeared. But **religious responses** remained — prayer, fasting, the belief the plague was God's punishment — and **ideas** hardly changed: miasma, the humours, even the stars (the comet of 1664).

**Judgement.** I agree. Measured by what was actually done to stop the spread, government action was the biggest difference by far; other changes were small or superficial. But it was a difference in organisation built on the same ideas — contagion and miasma — which is why the Great Plague still killed perhaps 100,000 Londoners.
:::
