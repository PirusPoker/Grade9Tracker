## What it is

Between c1250 and c1500 people in England explained disease in two broad ways: **supernatural and religious** explanations (God, sin, the stars) and **rational** explanations (the Four Humours and miasma). The two were not rivals in most people's minds — a physician could blame the stars, check a patient's humours and advise prayer all in the same visit. What held the whole system together was the enormous authority of the ancient writers, above all **Galen**, and the power of the **Church**.

### Supernatural and religious explanations

- **God's punishment for sin.** The Church taught that God sent disease to punish sinful individuals or a sinful community. Leprosy was widely seen as a punishment for sin, and the Black Death of 1348–49 was interpreted by many as God's judgement on a wicked world.
- **A test of faith.** Illness could also be a trial sent by God — something to be endured patiently, with prayer, like the sufferings of the saints.
- **Astrology.** Educated physicians believed the movement of the planets and stars affected the body. Each sign of the zodiac governed a part of the body (shown in the **"Zodiac Man"** diagrams in medical books). Physicians carried a **vademecum** — a folding handbook with star charts and urine charts — to decide when to bleed a patient. The Paris medical faculty's report on the Black Death blamed a conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars in 1345. Astrology looks "supernatural" to us, but to medieval doctors it was learned science taught at universities.
- **Evil spirits and bad luck** — ordinary people also believed in charms, curses and witchcraft, and wore amulets for protection.

Why were religious explanations so strong? The **Church** controlled education (monks copied the books; the universities were Church institutions) and ran most hospitals; most people could not read and learned what the priest told them; and nobody could see the real cause of disease — there were no microscopes. A religious explanation also gave meaning to suffering in a society where death was everywhere.

### Rational explanations 1: the Theory of the Four Humours

The **Theory of the Four Humours** came from the Greek doctor **Hippocrates** (c460–c370 BC) and his followers. The body contained four fluids, or humours: **blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile**. Good health meant the humours were in **balance**; illness meant one had built up or become corrupted. The theory linked each humour to one of the four elements and seasons, which made it feel like a complete explanation of nature:

| Humour | Element | Season | Qualities |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blood | Air | Spring | hot and wet |
| Yellow bile | Fire | Summer | hot and dry |
| Black bile | Earth | Autumn | cold and dry |
| Phlegm | Water | Winter | cold and wet |

It was a **rational** theory — it looked for a natural cause, and it fitted what people could see (blood in wounds, phlegm with a winter cold, vomit containing bile). It was wrong, but it was logical, and doctors could act on it.

### Galen and his continuing influence in England

**Galen** (c129–c216 AD) was a Greek doctor who worked in Rome, including as doctor to gladiators and emperors. He built on Hippocrates:

- He developed the **Theory of Opposites** — treat an imbalance with its opposite: a cold (too much phlegm, cold and wet) should be treated with hot, dry things such as pepper.
- He dissected animals — especially pigs and Barbary apes — because human dissection was banned in Rome, and wrote detailed anatomy books. Some of his anatomy was wrong for humans (for example, he said the human jawbone was made of two bones).
- He wrote hundreds of works, which survived through Arab and Byzantine scholars and were translated into Latin.

Why did Galen stay dominant in England for over 1,000 years?

1. **The Church approved.** Galen believed the body was designed by one creator, which fitted Christian teaching. The Church kept and copied his books, and disagreeing with Galen could look like disagreeing with the Church.
2. **Training was based on books.** Physicians trained at universities (Oxford, Cambridge, or abroad at Paris, Montpellier, Bologna and Padua) by reading Galen and Hippocrates, not by experiment. Human dissection was rare and, when it happened, a lecturer read Galen aloud while an assistant cut — the body was used to *illustrate* Galen, not to test him.
3. **His ideas worked well enough.** Treatments like rest, diet and keeping warm often helped, and most illnesses get better on their own, so patients credited the treatment.
4. **Attitudes in society** respected ancient authority — people assumed the ancients knew more than they did.

### Rational explanations 2: miasma

**Miasma** was the idea that disease was caused by **bad air** — poisonous vapours given off by rotting matter such as corpses, dung, cesspits and stagnant water. It also came from the ancient world (Hippocrates and Galen both wrote about it). It made sense in medieval towns, which stank: animals were slaughtered in the streets, human waste was thrown out of windows, and rivers were open sewers. Miasma was often combined with the Humours — bad air entered the body and corrupted the humours. It led to real action (cleaning streets, carrying sweet-smelling herbs), and it is the explanation that lasted longest: it was still the main theory until the 1860s.

### Diagnosis

A physician would examine the patient's **urine** (colour, smell and even taste) against a **urine chart**, take the **pulse**, ask about symptoms, and consult the **stars** before deciding a treatment. This combined rational observation with astrology — a good example of how the two kinds of explanation worked together.

### Change and continuity, and the factors

| Factor | How it shaped ideas c1250–c1500 |
|---|---|
| **Church (institution)** | Controlled universities and book-copying; supported Galen; taught disease as God's punishment; discouraged dissection and challenges to authority |
| **Government** | Weak role — kings did not fund medical research; some local action on filth, but no national health policy |
| **Science and technology** | No microscope, no way to see microbes; dissection rare; learning came from copied manuscripts, so ideas spread slowly and errors were repeated |
| **Attitudes in society** | Deep religious faith; respect for ancient authority; most people illiterate; ordinary people mixed prayer, charms and folk remedies |

The key judgement: **continuity** dominated. The Four Humours and Galen went unchallenged throughout the period; miasma survived for another 350 years. What changed was little — astrology became more elaborate and medical books more widely available in the later 1400s — so the medieval period is a clear example of the Church and attitudes in society **holding back** change.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| c400 BC | Hippocrates and followers develop the Four Humours |
| c129–c216 AD | Galen: Theory of Opposites, animal dissection, huge written output |
| 1100s–1200s | Galen's works translated into Latin; universities teach them |
| 1345 | Conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars — later blamed for the Black Death |
| 1348–49 | Black Death reaches England — explained through God, the stars and miasma |
| 1476 | William Caxton sets up the first printing press in England |

::: terms
- **Four Humours**: the theory that the body contains blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile, and illness comes from an imbalance of them.
- **Miasma**: the belief that disease is caused by bad air — poisonous vapours rising from rotting matter, corpses and filth.
- **Theory of Opposites**: Galen's idea that an imbalance of humours should be treated with its opposite, e.g. a cold with hot pepper.
- **Astrology**: the study of the movement of the planets and stars, believed by medieval physicians to affect health and the body.
- **Vademecum**: a folding handbook physicians carried, with star charts, urine charts and other information for diagnosis.
- **Urine chart**: a chart of urine colours used by physicians to diagnose which humour was out of balance.
- **Supernatural explanation**: explaining disease through forces beyond nature — God's punishment, evil spirits or the stars.
- **Rational explanation**: explaining disease through natural causes that can be observed and reasoned about, like the Humours or miasma.
:::

::: method
**Where this sits:** Paper 1, Section B — the thematic study *Medicine in Britain, c1250–present* (36 marks + 4 SPaG; spend about 55 minutes of the 1 hour 20). Three question types:

**Q3 — "Explain one way in which [X in one period] was similar to / different from [X in another period]." (4 marks)**
- Level 1 (1–2 marks): a simple or general comment with limited knowledge ("both believed in God").
- Level 2 (3–4 marks): the similarity or difference is *explained* with **specific knowledge of both periods**.
- Structure: one sentence naming the way ("In both periods, ideas about the cause of disease were based on…"), one or two sentences of precise evidence for the first period, the same for the second, then a closing clause tying them together. One way only — a second way earns nothing.

**Q4 — "Explain why…" (12 marks).** Two stimulus bullet points are given; you do not have to use them, but you **must add a point of your own**.
- Level 1 (1–3): simple or general comments. Level 2 (4–6): some explanation, limited knowledge. Level 3 (7–9): an explanation showing analysis, with accurate, relevant knowledge. Level 4 (10–12): an analytical explanation directed consistently at the question, with links between factors, wide-ranging precise knowledge and a sustained, logically structured line of reasoning.
- If you only use the stimulus points, you are capped at **8 marks** and cannot reach Level 4.
- Structure: three paragraphs (two stimulus + one own, or one stimulus + two own). Each paragraph: a reason in the first sentence, precise evidence (names, dates, examples), then explain *how* that led to the outcome. Finish with a line that links the factors — e.g. how the Church and attitudes reinforced each other.

**Q5/Q6 — "'Statement.' How far do you agree? Explain your answer." (16 marks + 4 SPaG).** Choose one of two.
- Level 1 (1–4) and Level 2 (5–8): description, limited analysis, judgement weak or missing. Level 3 (9–12): analysis with accurate knowledge, a judgement given with some justification. Level 4 (13–16): sustained analysis of both sides, precise wide-ranging knowledge, and a **judgement reached with criteria and fully justified**.
- Capped at **11 marks** without a point beyond the stimulus.
- Structure: introduction giving your answer; two paragraphs developing the side of the statement; one or two paragraphs on the other side (including your own point); a conclusion that **weighs** — use a criterion such as "how long it lasted", "how many people it affected", or "whether it changed what doctors actually did".
- SPaG (4): High (4) — accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar and a wide range of specialist terms (humours, miasma, physician); Intermediate (2–3); Threshold (1).
:::

::: example 4-mark similarity
Explain one way in which ideas about the causes of disease were similar in the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries. (4)

One similarity is that in both centuries many people believed disease was caused by **miasma**, bad air from rotting matter. In the fourteenth century, during the Black Death in 1348–49, people carried posies of sweet herbs and in 1349 Edward III ordered the Mayor of London to clean filth from the streets because its smell was thought to spread disease. In the seventeenth century, during the Great Plague of 1665, the authorities still lit fires in the streets to purify the air and people still carried herbs, so the same theory of bad air shaped how people understood the plague 300 years later.
:::

::: example 12-mark explain why
Explain why the ideas of Galen remained so important in medieval England. (12)
You may use the following in your answer: the Church; medical training. You must also use information of your own.

One reason Galen's ideas lasted is that the **Church** supported them. Galen believed the body had been designed by a single creator, which fitted Christian teaching. Because the Church controlled education and monks copied the manuscripts, Galen's books were preserved and treated almost as sacred texts. Questioning Galen could therefore look like questioning the Church, and very few people were in a position to do so.

A second reason is the way physicians were **trained**. At universities such as Oxford and Paris, students spent years reading Galen and Hippocrates rather than experimenting. Dissections were rare, and when they took place a lecturer read Galen aloud while an assistant cut, so any difference between the body and the book was blamed on the body. The training system reproduced Galen's ideas generation after generation.

A third reason, of my own, is that **Galen's ideas seemed to work**. His Theory of Opposites produced sensible advice — rest, warmth, changes of diet — and most illnesses cure themselves, so patients and doctors credited the treatment. Nor could anyone offer an alternative: without microscopes, no one could see the real causes of disease.

These factors reinforced each other: the Church shaped the universities, the universities trained the physicians, and the physicians' apparent success confirmed the authority of both. That is why Galen dominated for over a thousand years.
:::

::: example 16-mark judgement (plan and conclusion)
"Religion was the main reason for the lack of progress in medicine in the period c1250–c1500." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16 + 4 SPaG)
You may use: the Four Humours; hospitals. You must also use information of your own.

**Plan.** Agree: the Church supported Galen and controlled universities; taught disease as God's punishment, so prayer and pilgrimage rather than inquiry; discouraged dissection. Disagree: the Four Humours was a rational theory and itself blocked progress because it seemed to explain everything; lack of technology (no microscope); attitudes of respect for ancient authority; the Church's hospitals actually improved care.

**Conclusion.** I largely agree. The lack of scientific technology meant no one could have discovered the true cause of disease in this period whatever the Church thought, and the Four Humours was a secular Greek theory. But the reason that theory went unchallenged for so long was the Church: it preserved Galen, trained the physicians, limited dissection and taught people to accept illness as God's will. The other factors explain why progress was *difficult*; religion explains why no one *tried*. Judged by what stopped people questioning old ideas, religion was the main reason.
:::

::: example Diagnosis in one paragraph
Describe how a medieval physician would diagnose a patient. (Knowledge check)

The physician would ask the patient about symptoms, take the pulse and examine the urine — its colour, smell and sometimes taste — comparing it with a urine chart to work out which humour was out of balance. He would then consult his vademecum to check the position of the stars and the Zodiac Man, because the planets were thought to govern parts of the body and decide the best time to treat, for example by bloodletting.
:::

---
Blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile.

::: q
What was Galen's Theory of Opposites? Give an example.
---
Treat an imbalance of the humours with its opposite. For example, a cold (too much cold, wet phlegm) should be treated with something hot and dry, such as pepper; a fever (hot) with something cold, like cucumber.
:::

::: q
Give two supernatural explanations of disease believed in medieval England.
---
Any two: God's punishment for sin; a test of faith sent by God; the movement of the planets and stars (astrology); evil spirits, curses or witchcraft.
:::

::: q
What was miasma, and why did it seem convincing in a medieval town?
---
The belief that disease was caused by bad air — poisonous vapours from rotting matter. Medieval towns smelled strongly of dung, rotting food, animal slaughter and cesspits, and illness was often common where the smell was worst, so the link seemed obvious.
:::

::: q
Give three reasons why the Church supported Galen's ideas or helped keep them dominant.
---
Galen believed the body was designed by one creator, which fitted Christianity; monks copied and preserved his books; the Church controlled the universities where physicians trained; it discouraged dissection, so his errors went untested.
:::

::: q
Why is the Theory of the Four Humours called a "rational" explanation even though it was wrong?
---
Because it explained disease through natural causes — an imbalance of bodily fluids — that could be observed and reasoned about, rather than through God or magic. It also led to logical treatments aimed at rebalancing the humours.
:::

::: q
Explain one way in which the Church and attitudes in society worked together to stop change in ideas about disease.
---
The Church taught that illness was sent by God and that Galen was an authority; a mostly illiterate population respected the Church and ancient writers and learned their ideas from priests, so almost nobody was in a position or willing to question them — the Church set the ideas and society's respect for authority kept them in place.
:::
