## What it is

The 1559 settlement pleased the majority but not the committed at either end. This topic covers three spec points: **the nature and extent of the Puritan challenge**, **the nature and extent of the Catholic challenge** (including the role of the **nobility, the Papacy and foreign powers**), and **Mary, Queen of Scots** — her claim to the English throne and her relations with Elizabeth in **1568–69**.

"Nature" means *what kind* of challenge it was (who, what they wanted, how they acted). "Extent" means *how serious* it was. Always answer both.

### The Puritan challenge

**Who were the Puritans?** Protestants who wanted to "purify" the Church of England of everything Catholic that remained. Many were **Marian exiles** — Protestants who had fled abroad under Mary I and learned Calvinist ideas in cities such as **Geneva** and Frankfurt. They wanted plain churches, no vestments, no crucifixes, an emphasis on preaching and the Bible, and (for the more radical) a Church without bishops.

**Nature of the challenge.**
- **The crucifix controversy (1559–60).** Elizabeth kept a silver crucifix and candles in her Chapel Royal and wanted crucifixes kept in churches. Several of her new Protestant bishops objected strongly, some threatening to resign. Elizabeth could not afford to lose them — there were few qualified Protestants to replace them — so she **backed down** and did not insist on crucifixes in every church, though she kept her own.
- **The vestments controversy (1566).** Puritans saw the surplice and other vestments as Catholic "rags". Elizabeth ordered uniformity; Archbishop **Matthew Parker** issued his **Book of Advertisements** (1566), setting out what clergy must wear. When more than 100 London clergy were summoned to Lambeth in **March 1566**, **37 refused** and lost their posts. Senior figures such as **Thomas Sampson**, Dean of Christ Church, Oxford, had already been removed for refusing.
- **Influence at the top.** Puritans were not a fringe: they included bishops (such as Edmund Grindal, Bishop of London), MPs and university scholars, and they had sympathisers on the Privy Council, notably Robert Dudley and later Francis Walsingham.

**Extent of the challenge (to 1569).**
- It was a challenge to **the settlement and to Elizabeth's authority over the Church**, not to her throne — Puritans were loyal Protestants who wanted her as queen.
- Elizabeth gave ground over crucifixes but **held firm on vestments**, and the Puritan clergy who defied her were removed. Most Puritans conformed.
- It was significant because it came from **educated, influential people** inside the Church and Parliament; it grew in the 1570s (Presbyterian ideas, "prophesyings"). But in 1558–69 it was **limited**: Elizabeth kept control.

### The Catholic challenge

**Nature of the challenge.** Catholics believed the Pope was head of the Church and that Elizabeth was illegitimate. In the 1560s most Catholics outwardly conformed ("**church papists**"), attending services while keeping their beliefs. A minority became **recusants**, refusing to attend. Catholic practice survived strongly in the **north** and **Lancashire**, where the settlement was enforced lightly.

**The role of the nobility.**
- Many old noble families were Catholic, especially in the north — the **Percys** (Earl of Northumberland), **Nevilles** (Earl of Westmorland) and **Dacres**. They had great local power and armed tenants who would follow them.
- In 1558–68 they largely **conformed**, and Elizabeth relied on them to govern the north. Their resentment grew as Protestant "new men" such as Cecil gained influence at court and the Council of the North, which governed the region, was run by southerners loyal to the government rather than by the old northern families.
- The nobility were the key: without noble leaders, Catholic discontent could not become rebellion. When the Northern Earls did rebel in 1569, it became the most serious threat of the early reign.

**The role of the Papacy.**
- At first the Papacy was **cautious**, hoping England could be won back peacefully; Pope Pius IV invited Elizabeth to send representatives to the Council of Trent (she refused).
- The **Council of Trent (1545–63)** launched the Counter-Reformation, and by the mid-1560s Rome was telling English Catholics that they must not attend Protestant services — undermining the church papists and encouraging recusancy.
- The Pope did **not** excommunicate Elizabeth in the 1560s. That came only in **1570** (*Regnans in Excelsis*) after the Northern Revolt. So in 1558–69 papal pressure was real but limited.

**The role of foreign powers.**
- **France** was the leading Catholic threat in 1558–60 through Mary, Queen of Scots and the Auld Alliance, but from **1562** it was paralysed by the **Wars of Religion** between Catholics and Protestant Huguenots.
- **Spain** under **Philip II** at first wanted friendship with England against France. Relations worsened in the later 1560s: in 1566 the Protestant Dutch revolted against Spain, and in **1567** the **Duke of Alba** arrived in the Netherlands with about **10,000 troops** — a large Catholic army just across the North Sea. In **late 1568** Elizabeth seized Spanish ships carrying silver (a loan from Genoese bankers to pay Alba's army) that had sheltered in English ports, and the new Spanish ambassador, **Guerau de Spes**, began encouraging plots.

**Extent of the Catholic challenge (to 1569).** In 1558–67 the Catholic challenge was **potentially dangerous but mostly dormant**: most Catholics conformed, France was distracted and Spain friendly. It became much more serious in **1568–69** because three things combined: **Mary, Queen of Scots arrived** as a Catholic claimant on English soil; **Spain turned hostile** with an army in the Netherlands; and the **northern nobility** were alienated. The result was the Revolt of the Northern Earls in November 1569.

### Mary, Queen of Scots: her claim

- Mary was the granddaughter of **Margaret Tudor**, Henry VIII's elder sister — so the great-granddaughter of **Henry VII**. By blood she was the **next heir** after Elizabeth.
- To Catholics, who thought Elizabeth illegitimate, Mary was the **rightful queen already**.
- She was a Catholic, had been queen of France (1559–60) and had strong links to the powerful French **Guise** family.
- In 1559–60 she displayed the English royal arms, and she **never ratified the Treaty of Edinburgh** (1560), which would have required her to give up her claim while Elizabeth lived.
- Against her claim: **Henry VIII's will** had passed over his sister Margaret's descendants in favour of the line of his younger sister Mary (the Greys), and she was foreign-born.

### Mary's downfall in Scotland

Mary returned to Scotland in 1561 after her husband Francis II died. In **1565** she married her cousin **Lord Darnley** (also a descendant of Margaret Tudor), and in **June 1566** gave birth to a son, **James** — which strengthened her claim, since she now had an heir. But in **February 1567** Darnley was murdered at **Kirk o' Field** in Edinburgh, and three months later Mary married the **Earl of Bothwell**, widely believed to be the murderer. Scotland's Protestant lords rebelled, imprisoned her at **Lochleven Castle**, and in **July 1567** forced her to **abdicate** in favour of her baby son, **James VI**, with her half-brother the **Earl of Moray** as regent. In **May 1568** Mary escaped, was defeated at the **Battle of Langside**, and fled across the border into England.

### Relations between Elizabeth and Mary, 1568–69

Elizabeth had four options — all bad:

| Option | Problem |
|---|---|
| Help Mary regain the Scottish throne | Would mean war with Scotland's Protestant lords and put a Catholic, pro-French queen back on England's border |
| Hand her to the Scottish lords | Would endorse subjects overthrowing their anointed queen — a dangerous precedent for Elizabeth herself |
| Let her go abroad to France or Spain | She could return with a Catholic army to claim the English throne |
| Keep her in England | She would be a focus for Catholic plots — but at least Elizabeth could watch her |

Elizabeth chose to **keep Mary in England** under guard — first at Carlisle, then **Bolton Castle** in Yorkshire, and from early 1569 **Tutbury** in Staffordshire, in the custody of the Earl of Shrewsbury.

**The inquiry (October 1568 – January 1569).** Elizabeth set up an inquiry, first at **York** then at **Westminster**, into whether Mary had been involved in Darnley's murder. Moray produced the **"Casket Letters"**, supposedly written by Mary to Bothwell, suggesting she was involved. Mary's supporters said they were forgeries, and Mary refused to answer charges before a court of a fellow monarch. In January 1569 the inquiry reached **no verdict** — she was neither found guilty nor cleared.

This outcome suited Elizabeth: Mary could not be restored (the suspicion remained), but Elizabeth had not approved the overthrow of a queen. Moray kept governing Scotland for the Protestant James VI. **Mary stayed a prisoner in England for 19 years.**

**Why Mary was a threat in 1569.** Mary in England became the focus for discontent. In 1569 a plan developed for her to marry the **Duke of Norfolk**, England's only duke. Elizabeth learned of it in September 1569 and sent Norfolk to the Tower. Within weeks the Northern Earls rose in Mary's name — the link to the next topic.

::: terms
- **Puritan**: a Protestant who wanted to purify the Church of England of all remaining Catholic features.
- **Marian exile**: a Protestant who fled abroad under Mary I and often returned with Calvinist ideas.
- **Vestments controversy**: the 1566 dispute when Puritan clergy refused to wear the surplice; 37 London clergy lost their posts.
- **Crucifix controversy**: the 1559–60 dispute between Elizabeth and Protestant bishops over crucifixes in churches.
- **Church papist**: a Catholic who attended Church of England services outwardly while keeping Catholic beliefs.
- **Recusant**: a Catholic who refused to attend Church of England services.
- **Counter-Reformation**: the Catholic Church's campaign to reform itself and win back Protestant lands, launched by the Council of Trent.
- **Casket Letters**: letters said to be written by Mary, Queen of Scots to Bothwell, used against her at the 1568–69 inquiry.
- **Abdicate**: to give up the throne; Mary was forced to abdicate in favour of James VI in 1567.
:::

::: example Explain why — 12 marks
Explain why Mary, Queen of Scots' arrival in England in 1568 caused problems for Elizabeth. (12)

You may use the following in your answer: Mary's claim to the throne; the Scottish lords. You must also use information of your own.

**A rival claimant on English soil.** The main problem was that Mary had a strong claim to the English throne: as the granddaughter of Margaret Tudor she was next in line by blood, and to Catholics who thought Elizabeth illegitimate she was already the rightful queen. While she was in Scotland she was at a distance; once in England she could become the focus for Catholic plots — which is exactly what happened in 1569 when a plan developed for her to marry the Duke of Norfolk.

**No good way to deal with her.** Mary's arrival also caused problems because every option was dangerous. Restoring her to the Scottish throne meant fighting the Scottish Protestant lords, who had forced her to abdicate in 1567 after the murder of Darnley and her marriage to Bothwell. Handing her to those lords would approve of subjects overthrowing their queen — a precedent that threatened Elizabeth herself. Letting her go to France or Spain risked her returning with an army. Elizabeth therefore had to keep her prisoner, which meant living with the danger.

**Foreign Catholic powers.** A further problem was the timing. By 1568 Spain was becoming hostile: the Duke of Alba had around 10,000 troops in the Netherlands, and Elizabeth seized Spanish treasure ships at the end of 1568. A Catholic claimant in England gave Spain, and the Pope, a candidate to back. This made the Catholic threat far more serious than it had been earlier in the decade.

**The Darnley question.** Finally, the inquiry at York and Westminster in 1568–69 put Elizabeth in a difficult position. Moray's Casket Letters suggested Mary was involved in Darnley's murder, but finding a fellow queen guilty would undermine monarchy itself. The inquiry reached no verdict, which kept Mary a prisoner — but also left her grievance, and her claim, alive.
:::

::: example How far do you agree — 16 marks
"The Catholic threat to Elizabeth was more serious than the Puritan threat in the years 1558–69." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)

You may use the following in your answer: the vestments controversy; the Catholic nobility. You must also use information of your own.

**Criterion.** The most serious threat is the one that endangered Elizabeth's throne, not just her Church.

**The Puritan threat.** Puritans did challenge Elizabeth's authority over the Church. In the crucifix controversy of 1559–60 her bishops threatened to resign and she backed down over churches; in 1566, 37 London clergy refused to wear vestments. Because Puritans included bishops, MPs and privy councillors, the challenge came from within the government. But it had limits: Puritans were loyal Protestants who never questioned her right to rule, Elizabeth held firm over vestments and the rebels were removed.

**The Catholic threat, 1558–67.** For most of the 1560s the Catholic threat was also limited. Most Catholics conformed as church papists, the Papacy was cautious and did not excommunicate Elizabeth, France was absorbed in its own Wars of Religion from 1562 and Philip II of Spain wanted friendship.

**The Catholic threat, 1568–69.** However, the Catholic threat had something the Puritans lacked: the potential to overthrow Elizabeth. Catholics regarded her as illegitimate; the northern nobility — the Percys and Nevilles — had armed followers; and in 1568 Mary, Queen of Scots arrived in England as an alternative Catholic queen, just as Spain turned hostile with Alba's army in the Netherlands. In 1569 these came together in the Revolt of the Northern Earls.

**Judgement.** I strongly agree. The Puritan challenge was a quarrel about how Protestant the Church should be; it caused friction but never endangered the Crown. The Catholic challenge was quieter for most of the decade but, by 1568–69, combined a rival claimant, noble leaders and foreign backing — a threat to Elizabeth's throne itself.
:::
