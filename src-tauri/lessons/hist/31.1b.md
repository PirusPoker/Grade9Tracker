## What it is

Between 1919 and 1923 the Weimar Republic was attacked from every side. The spec covers three things:
1. **Why the Republic was unpopular early on**, including the stab-in-the-back theory and the key terms of the Treaty of Versailles.
2. **Challenges from the Left and the Right**: the Spartacists, the Freikorps and the Kapp Putsch.
3. **The crises of 1923**: hyperinflation, and the reasons for and effects of the French occupation of the Ruhr.

(The Munich Putsch of November 1923 belongs to Key Topic 2.)

### The Treaty of Versailles (signed 28 June 1919)

Germany was not allowed to take part in the negotiations and was given the terms to accept or face invasion. Germans called it a **Diktat**, a dictated peace. The Reichstag accepted it because the army admitted it could not resist. Chancellor Scheidemann resigned rather than sign.

| Area | Key terms |
|---|---|
| **Land** | Alsace-Lorraine to France; West Prussia and Posen to Poland, creating the **Polish Corridor** that cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany; Danzig a free city under the League; Eupen-Malmedy to Belgium; North Schleswig to Denmark after a vote; the **Saar** run by the League for 15 years with its coal going to France; all overseas **colonies** taken as League mandates; union (**Anschluss**) with Austria forbidden. Germany lost about **13 per cent** of its European territory, about 6 million people, and much of its iron ore and coal |
| **Army** | Army limited to **100,000** men; no conscription; no tanks, military aircraft or submarines; navy limited to 6 battleships; the **Rhineland demilitarised**, with Allied troops there for 15 years |
| **Blame** | **Article 231, the "war guilt" clause**: Germany accepted responsibility for causing the war and all its loss and damage |
| **Money** | **Reparations**, fixed in 1921 at **132 billion gold marks** (£6.6 billion) |
| **League** | Germany was excluded from the new League of Nations |

**Why it made the Republic unpopular:**
- **Humiliation.** War guilt was seen as a lie and an insult to national honour.
- **Weakness.** A small army left Germany unable to defend itself, and the demilitarised Rhineland left its industrial heartland exposed.
- **Economic damage.** Lost coal and iron plus reparations were blamed for Germany's economic problems.
- **Resentment of the signatories.** The politicians who signed were the same "November Criminals" who had signed the armistice.

### The stab-in-the-back theory (Dolchstoss)

Nationalists claimed the German army had **not been defeated in battle** but had been betrayed at home by socialists, communists, Jews and the politicians who made peace. In **November 1919 Hindenburg told a Reichstag committee** investigating the defeat that the army had been "stabbed in the back". The myth was believable because:
- the army was still on foreign soil at the armistice;
- wartime propaganda had promised victory until the end;
- the generals had kept their names off the armistice.

It gave the right a simple story: **democracy equals defeat and betrayal**. Hitler later used it relentlessly.

### Challenges from the Left: the Spartacist uprising, January 1919

The **Spartacist League**, led by **Rosa Luxemburg** and **Karl Liebknecht**, wanted a communist revolution like Russia's, with power in the hands of workers' councils. At the turn of 1918–19 it founded the **German Communist Party (KPD)**. When Ebert dismissed Berlin's left-wing police chief **Emil Eichhorn**, the Spartacists and their allies rose in Berlin on **5–6 January 1919**. They called a general strike and occupied newspaper offices and public buildings. Ebert's defence minister **Gustav Noske** used the army and the **Freikorps** to crush the rising in about a week. **Luxemburg and Liebknecht were captured and murdered by Freikorps officers on 15 January 1919.** Further left-wing risings followed: the Bavarian Soviet Republic (April–May 1919) and a "Red Army" in the Ruhr (1920). All were crushed by the Freikorps and the army.

**Significance:** the Republic survived, but only by relying on right-wing ex-soldiers who hated it. The KPD never forgave the SPD for the murders, which left the left permanently split.

### The Freikorps

The Freikorps were **volunteer units of ex-soldiers**, led by former officers. They were nationalist, anti-communist and anti-democratic, and there were possibly 250,000 members by 1919. The government used them against the left, but they were loyal to their own leaders, not the Republic. Versailles cut the army to 100,000, so in **1920 the government ordered Freikorps units to disband**. That order caused the Kapp Putsch.

### Challenges from the Right: the Kapp Putsch, March 1920

- **13 March 1920:** about **5,000 Freikorps**, led by the **Ehrhardt Brigade**, marched into Berlin. **Wolfgang Kapp**, a nationalist civil servant, declared himself head of a new government, backed by General **Walther von Lüttwitz**.
- **The army refused to defend the government.** General von Seeckt made it clear that soldiers would not fire on fellow soldiers. The government fled to Dresden and then Stuttgart.
- **Defeat:** the government called a **general strike**. Workers stopped gas, water, electricity and transport in Berlin, making it impossible to govern. Civil servants refused to cooperate with Kapp. After about four days (**17 March**) Kapp fled to Sweden.
- **Consequences:** the Republic survived thanks to the **workers**, not the army, which exposed how unreliable the army and civil service were. Most putschists were not punished. Right-wing violence continued: **Matthias Erzberger** was murdered in 1921 and foreign minister **Walther Rathenau** in 1922. Of **354** right-wing political murders in 1919–22, **326 went unpunished**, while left-wing killers received heavy sentences. The judges' bias undermined faith in the Republic's justice.

### 1923: the occupation of the Ruhr

**Reasons:**
- Germany paid the first reparations instalment in 1921 but then fell behind.
- At the end of 1922 it defaulted on deliveries of goods, including timber and coal.
- France believed Germany was deliberately avoiding payment and wanted to take what it was owed directly.

On **11 January 1923** French and Belgian troops (about 60,000, later more) **occupied the Ruhr**, Germany's industrial heartland, which produced most of its coal, iron and steel.

**Effects:**
- **Passive resistance.** Chancellor **Wilhelm Cuno**'s government ordered workers not to cooperate. Strikers were paid by the government.
- **French retaliation.** The French brought in their own workers, took over the mines and railways, and expelled many Germans. More than 100 Germans were killed in clashes.
- **Economic damage.** Industrial output collapsed, taxes fell and goods became scarce, while the government was paying strikers. It **printed money** to cover the gap.
- **Politically,** the occupation united Germans in anger at France, but the government was humiliated when **Stresemann called off passive resistance on 26 September 1923** and resumed payments. Nationalists, including Hitler, called this another betrayal.

### 1923: hyperinflation

**Causes:**
1. **War debts.** The war had been financed by borrowing, meant to be repaid from victory and reparations from the Allies, and by printing money. Prices were already rising before 1923.
2. **Reparations** drained gold and foreign currency.
3. **The Ruhr occupation.** Lost production plus payments to strikers were covered by printing more money: by late 1923 printing works were running day and night just to produce banknotes.
4. **Too much money chasing too few goods.** Prices rose faster than money could be printed, and confidence in the mark collapsed.

| Date | Price of a loaf of bread |
|---|---|
| 1918 | under 1 mark |
| January 1923 | about **250** marks |
| November 1923 | about **200,000 million** marks |

In 1914 a US dollar cost about 4 marks; by November 1923 it cost about **4.2 trillion** marks.

**Effects:**
- **Losers:** people with savings, pensioners and anyone on fixed incomes, especially the **middle classes**, whose savings were wiped out. People on wages saw prices rise faster than their pay. Food ran short as farmers held back produce.
- **Daily life:** workers were paid twice a day and rushed to spend their wages. People used barter and burned money as fuel, and the elderly and sick suffered most.
- **Winners:** debtors and those with loans or mortgages, which could be paid off with worthless money; businessmen like **Hugo Stinnes**, who borrowed to buy up firms; foreigners with hard currency; and farmers with food to sell.
- **Politically,** the middle classes, the Republic's natural supporters, blamed the Republic, and many never trusted it again. This made them open to extremists after 1929.

**1923 as a whole:** the Ruhr, hyperinflation, the Hamburg communist rising (October), separatist movements in the Rhineland and the Munich Putsch (November) made 1923 the Republic's worst year before 1929. It survived because Stresemann took decisive action, the army stayed loyal against the Putsch, and the extremists were divided.

::: terms
- **Diktat**: a dictated peace; the German name for the Treaty of Versailles, which Germany had no part in negotiating.
- **War guilt clause**: Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, making Germany accept responsibility for the war.
- **Reparations**: compensation Germany had to pay the Allies, fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks.
- **Stab-in-the-back myth**: the false claim that Germany's army was undefeated in 1918 and was betrayed by politicians at home.
- **Spartacists**: German communists led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht, who rose in Berlin in January 1919.
- **Freikorps**: volunteer paramilitary units of right-wing ex-soldiers, used by the government against the left.
- **Putsch**: an attempt to seize power by force, such as the Kapp Putsch of March 1920.
- **Passive resistance**: refusing to cooperate with an occupier without using violence, as Ruhr workers did in 1923.
- **Hyperinflation**: extremely rapid price rises that make money almost worthless, as in Germany in 1923.
:::

::: method
**Paper 3 at a glance.** 1 hour 30 minutes, 52 marks (48 + 4 SPaG). **Section A:** Q1 inference from Source A (4) and Q2 "Explain why…" (12). **Section B:** one enquiry on Sources B and C and Interpretations 1 and 2: 3(a) usefulness (8), 3(b) main difference (4), 3(c) why they differ (4), 3(d) how far do you agree with Interpretation 2 (16 + 4 SPaG). Timings: roughly 6, 18, 15, 5, 5 and 30–35 minutes.

**Q1: "Give two things you can infer from Source A about…" (4).** Two rows, each worth 1 mark for an inference and 1 for the supporting detail quoted or described from the source. On this topic, a hyperinflation photograph might let you infer that money was worthless or that savers were ruined; a poster about Versailles might let you infer nationalist anger. Say what the source *suggests*, not what it literally shows, and never swap in your own knowledge for the detail.

**Q2: "Explain why…" (12).** Two stimulus points (for example "the Treaty of Versailles" and "hyperinflation") and you must add your own.
- Level 1 (1–3): simple or generalised comments, limited knowledge.
- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation with some accurate knowledge; focus may drift.
- Level 3 (7–9): explanation mainly focused on the question, with accurate, relevant knowledge.
- Level 4 (10–12): analytical explanation, sustained focus, wide-ranging precise knowledge and clear links between causes. You cannot reach Level 4 without going beyond the two stimulus points.

Write three paragraphs, each naming a cause, backing it with precise detail (13 per cent of land, 100,000 men, Article 231, 132 billion gold marks, 326 of 354 murders unpunished, bread at 200,000 million marks) and finishing with "so this made the Republic…". Good own-knowledge points here are the stab-in-the-back myth, the Kapp Putsch exposing the army's disloyalty, and the Ruhr humiliation. Conclude by linking causes: Versailles fed the myth, and reparations and the Ruhr fed hyperinflation.

**Q3(a): "How useful are Sources B and C for an enquiry into…?" (8).**
- Level 1 (1–2): simple comments on content.
- Level 2 (3–5): judgements on content and/or provenance with some context.
- Level 3 (6–8): judgements on **both** sources using **content and provenance** (nature, origin, purpose), checked against your own knowledge, reaching a judgement about usefulness for this enquiry.

A Freikorps veteran's memoir about crushing the Spartacists is biased, but that makes it useful evidence of right-wing attitudes to the left. A government poster during the Ruhr occupation is propaganda, but useful for how Cuno's government wanted Germans to see passive resistance.

**Q3(b): main difference (4).** Level 2 (3–4) explains the main difference in the *overall view*, with a detail from each interpretation. For example: one sees 1919–23 as proof the Republic was fatally weak; the other stresses that it survived every challenge.

**Q3(c): why they differ (4).** Level 2 (3–4) gives a developed reason: the historians emphasise different evidence or aspects (one focuses on the army's refusal to act in March 1920, the other on the general strike that defeated Kapp), or the extracts are partial. You may use Sources B and C to show which evidence supports which view.

**Q3(d): "How far do you agree with Interpretation 2…?" (16 + 4 SPaG).**
- Level 1 (1–4): simple answer, little analysis of the interpretations.
- Level 2 (5–8): some analysis, limited knowledge, weakly supported judgement.
- Level 3 (9–12): explains agreement and disagreement using both interpretations and relevant knowledge; judgement partly justified.
- Level 4 (13–16): evaluates the interpretations with precise knowledge throughout; sustained, fully justified judgement.

Structure: an introduction stating what Interpretation 2 argues and your view; a paragraph supporting it with its own details plus your knowledge; a paragraph challenging it using Interpretation 1 and your knowledge; a conclusion with a clear criterion (for example "the Republic was weak because it depended on its enemies to survive, but it did survive, so 'doomed' goes too far"). Use precise terms such as Diktat, Freikorps and hyperinflation, spelled correctly, for SPaG.

::: example Explain why: the Republic was unpopular 1919–23 (12)
Explain why the Weimar Republic was unpopular in the years 1919–23. You may use the following in your answer: the Treaty of Versailles; hyperinflation. You must also use information of your own. (12)

**The Treaty of Versailles.** The Republic's politicians accepted a treaty drawn up without German involvement, a Diktat. It took about 13 per cent of Germany's European land, including the Polish Corridor, cut the army to 100,000 men and forced Germany to accept war guilt under Article 231 and pay 132 billion gold marks. Because the Republic signed it, the government was held responsible for the humiliation, and nationalists could present democracy as the regime of national shame.

**The stab-in-the-back myth.** Hindenburg's claim in 1919 that the army had been "stabbed in the back" gave people a reason to believe the Republic's founders were traitors. Most Germans had not seen their country invaded, so the idea that the "November Criminals" had betrayed an undefeated army made sense to them. The Republic was unpopular not only for what it did but for what it was believed to be.

**Hyperinflation.** In 1923 printing money to pay striking Ruhr workers turned inflation into hyperinflation: bread rose from 250 marks in January to about 200,000 million marks by November. The middle classes lost their savings and pensioners their incomes, while speculators like Stinnes grew rich. This turned the respectable middle classes, who should have been the Republic's firmest supporters, against it.

**Judgement.** Versailles was the deepest cause, because it linked the Republic to defeat and fed both the stab-in-the-back myth and the reparations that contributed to hyperinflation. Hyperinflation, however, turned resentment into personal suffering for millions.
:::

::: example 3(b) and 3(c): the Kapp Putsch
Interpretation 1 (a summary of a textbook view): the Kapp Putsch showed that the Republic had strong support among ordinary Germans, who defeated the putsch with a general strike. Interpretation 2 (a summary of another historian's view): the Kapp Putsch exposed the Republic's weakness, because its own army refused to defend it and most rebels escaped punishment. Explain the main difference, then suggest one reason for it. (4 + 4)

**3(b):** Interpretation 1 sees the putsch as evidence of the Republic's **strength**, because workers defended it by striking. Interpretation 2 sees it as evidence of **weakness**, because the army refused to fire on the rebels and the courts let most of them go. The difference is whether the putsch shows the Republic was secure or vulnerable.

**3(c):** They emphasise different parts of the story. Interpretation 1 focuses on how the putsch ended: the general strike paralysed Berlin, and Kapp fled after about four days. Interpretation 2 focuses on how it began and its aftermath: the government had to flee to Dresden, and 326 of 354 right-wing political murders went unpunished. A source such as a photograph of strikers would support Interpretation 1, while a report of the army's refusal to act would support Interpretation 2.
:::

::: q
Why was the stab-in-the-back myth believable to many Germans?
---
The army was still on foreign soil in November 1918, and Germany had not been invaded. Wartime propaganda had promised victory. The generals had avoided signing the armistice, so civilians appeared responsible, and Hindenburg himself repeated the claim in 1919.
:::

::: q
How was the Spartacist uprising defeated, and what happened to its leaders?
---
Ebert's government, through defence minister Noske, used the army and the Freikorps, which crushed the rising in about a week in January 1919. Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were captured and murdered by Freikorps officers on 15 January 1919.
:::

::: q
What caused the Kapp Putsch, and why did it fail?
---
Cause: the government ordered Freikorps units, including the Ehrhardt Brigade, to disband to meet the Versailles army limits, and they marched on Berlin in March 1920. It failed because the government called a general strike: workers shut down transport and utilities, civil servants would not cooperate, and Kapp fled after about four days.
:::

::: q
Why did the French occupy the Ruhr in 1923?
---
Germany had fallen behind with reparations and in late 1922 defaulted on deliveries of goods such as coal and timber. France (with Belgium) occupied the Ruhr on 11 January 1923 to take the goods it was owed, directly from Germany's main industrial region.
:::

::: q
Explain how the occupation of the Ruhr led to hyperinflation.
---
The government ordered passive resistance and paid the striking workers, while output and tax revenue from the Ruhr collapsed and goods became scarce. To cover the gap the government printed ever more money. More money chasing fewer goods pushed prices up out of control, and confidence in the mark collapsed.
:::

::: q
Who gained and who lost from hyperinflation?
---
Losers: savers, pensioners and people on fixed incomes, especially the middle classes, whose savings were wiped out; workers whose wages lagged behind prices. Winners: debtors and people with mortgages, who could pay them off with worthless money; businessmen like Hugo Stinnes, who bought up firms; people with foreign currency; and farmers, who could sell or barter food.
:::

::: watch
- **Putting hyperinflation in 1919.** Inflation built up from the war onwards, but hyperinflation was 1923, set off by the Ruhr occupation.
- **Listing Versailles terms without explaining the resentment.** Say why each term angered Germans.
- **Saying "the army crushed the Kapp Putsch".** It refused to act. The general strike defeated it.
- **Mixing up Left and Right.** The Spartacists were the Left; Kapp and the Freikorps were the Right. The Freikorps crushed the Spartacists.
- **Stating that hyperinflation hurt everyone.** Top answers show the winners too, and why the middle classes mattered politically.
