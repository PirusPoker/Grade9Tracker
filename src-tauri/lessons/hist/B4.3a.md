## What it is

Key topic 3 turns from court and war to **Elizabethan society**. This lesson covers two spec areas: **education and leisure** (education in the home and schools; sport, pastimes and the theatre) and **the "problem" of the poor** (why poverty and vagabondage increased, and how attitudes and policies changed). A good answer always says **who** — Elizabethan society was a hierarchy, and experiences depended on rank and gender.

### Education in the home

For most children, **the home was the only school**. Children of labourers and craftsmen learned by working alongside their parents: boys learned a trade or farm work, girls learned cooking, spinning, brewing and running a household. Apprenticeships (usually seven years, regulated by the **Statute of Artificers, 1563**) trained boys in a craft.

In **wealthy and noble families**, education at home was more formal:
- **Private tutors** taught Latin, Greek, French, music and dancing. Elizabeth herself had been taught by the humanist scholar **Roger Ascham**, whose book on teaching, *The Scholemaster*, was published in 1570.
- Boys learned the skills of a gentleman: riding, hunting, fencing and archery, as well as how to manage an estate.
- Girls of noble families learned languages, music, needlework and household management; a few, like Elizabeth, were very highly educated, but most girls were prepared for marriage.
- Boys were often sent to live in another great household to learn manners and make connections. As Master of the Court of Wards, **William Cecil**'s household trained many noble wards.
- Discipline was strict; children were expected to obey and respect their parents absolutely.

**Protestantism** encouraged literacy: people were meant to read the Bible and prayer book in English, and parents were expected to teach children the catechism.

### Education in schools

| Type | Who went | What was taught |
|---|---|---|
| **Petty schools** | Young boys (and some girls) of families who could afford small fees | Reading, writing, basic arithmetic, the catechism; often run by an educated local person in their own home (a "dame school" if run by a woman) |
| **Grammar schools** | Boys aged about 7–14, sons of gentry, yeomen, merchants and prosperous craftsmen | Mainly **Latin** (grammar, translation, classical authors), some Greek; learned by rote and repetition |
| **Universities** — Oxford and Cambridge | Boys from about 14–15, from wealthy families or with scholarships; many became clergy, lawyers or officials | Latin, theology, philosophy, law and medicine |
| **Inns of Court**, London | Young gentlemen | The law — useful for managing estates and for careers in government |

**Features of grammar schools:** long days (often from early morning to late afternoon, six days a week); strict discipline, with beating common; teaching in Latin because it was the language of the law, the Church, scholarship and diplomacy. The number of grammar schools **grew** under Elizabeth, many founded by merchants or towns — for example **Rugby (1567)** and **Harrow (1572)**. At Cambridge, **Emmanuel College** was founded in **1584** by Sir Walter Mildmay to train Protestant (Puritan) clergy.

**Why education expanded:** Protestantism and Bible reading; the growth of trade, needing literate and numerate merchants; the gentry wanting sons who could serve as JPs, MPs and royal officials; the printing press making books cheaper. Education was still a privilege: **most girls and most poor boys received no formal schooling**.

### Sport and pastimes

| Group | Sports and pastimes |
|---|---|
| **Nobility and gentry** | **Hunting** deer with hounds; **hawking** (falconry); **real tennis**; **fencing**; jousting — especially the **Accession Day tilts** held on 17 November to celebrate Elizabeth's accession; bowls; dancing; music; cards and chess |
| **Ordinary people** | **Football** — rough village games with few rules and many players, often causing injuries; **wrestling**; **archery** (men were required by law to practise it); running; fishing |
| **All classes** | **Bear-baiting**, **bull-baiting** and **cock-fighting**, often with gambling; fairs, feast days, May Day and Christmas celebrations; the alehouse; card and dice games |

Elizabeth enjoyed hunting, dancing and watching bear-baiting. The most famous baiting arena was **Paris Garden** in Southwark; in **January 1583** a stand collapsed there on a Sunday, killing several spectators — **Puritans** said it was God's judgement on people who broke the Sabbath. Puritans such as **Philip Stubbes** (*The Anatomie of Abuses*, 1583) attacked football, baiting and theatres as sinful, violent and a distraction from worship.

### The theatre

Before the 1570s, plays were performed by **travelling companies of actors** in inn yards, great houses and town squares. Several developments transformed theatre under Elizabeth:

- **1572:** the Act for the Punishment of Vagabonds treated wandering actors as vagabonds unless they had a **licence from a nobleman**. Acting companies therefore attached themselves to noble patrons — **Leicester's Men** (who received a royal patent in 1574), later **the Queen's Men (1583)**.
- **1576:** **James Burbage** built **The Theatre** in Shoreditch, the first successful purpose-built playhouse. **The Curtain** (1577) and **The Rose** (1587, on Bankside) followed.
- Playhouses were built **outside the City of London's authority**, because the City authorities opposed them.
- **Audiences** came from all classes: "groundlings" paid a penny to stand in the yard; wealthier people paid more for seats in the galleries.
- Plays were **licensed and censored** by the **Master of the Revels**, so nothing could attack the Queen, the government or the Church.
- By the late 1580s new playwrights such as **Christopher Marlowe** (*Tamburlaine*, about 1587) and **Thomas Kyd** (*The Spanish Tragedy*) were drawing huge crowds; Shakespeare's career began soon after.

**Why theatre was popular:** cheap entertainment for all classes; exciting stories of history, revenge and comedy; royal and noble patronage — Elizabeth watched plays at court. **Why it was opposed:** Puritans thought plays encouraged immorality, idleness and missing church; City authorities feared crowds, crime, riot, pickpockets and **the spread of plague**; apprentices skipped work.

### The "problem" of the poor: why poverty increased

Poverty was not new, but it **grew** in Elizabeth's reign and became more visible, especially in towns. The reasons:

1. **Population growth** — from roughly **3 million in the 1550s to about 4 million by 1600**. More people competed for jobs, land and food.
2. **Rising prices (inflation)** — food prices rose much faster than wages, so **real wages fell**. Henry VIII's debasement of the coinage had fuelled inflation (Elizabeth's recoinage of 1560–61 helped, but prices kept rising with population).
3. **Bad harvests** — a poor harvest could double the price of grain; there were bad years in the 1550s and again in **1586–87**. For the poor, bread was most of their spending.
4. **Enclosure and sheep farming** — some landowners fenced off common land and switched from arable to **sheep**, which needed far fewer workers; families lost jobs and common grazing.
5. **Rack-renting** — landlords raised rents sharply, pushing tenants off the land.
6. **Decline of the cloth trade** — trade disputes and wars with Spain in the Netherlands (the 1569–73 embargo, the fall of Antwerp in 1585) caused unemployment in cloth-making areas.
7. **The loss of monastic charity** — the dissolution of the monasteries (1536–40) had removed an important source of help for the poor.
8. **Disease and war** — plague (for example **1563**) killed breadwinners; demobilised soldiers returning from the Netherlands after 1585 added to the unemployed.

Many poor people took to the roads looking for work, becoming **vagabonds** (vagrants). Towns such as London grew rapidly as migrants arrived.

### Changing attitudes

Elizabethans divided the poor into groups:
- The **"deserving" (or "impotent") poor**: the old, sick, disabled, orphans and widows — people who could not work. They deserved help.
- The **"undeserving" poor**: able-bodied people thought to be **idle** — "sturdy beggars" and vagabonds. They were feared as criminals, spreaders of disease and potential rebels.

Pamphlets fed the fear. **Thomas Harman's *A Caveat for Common Cursitors* (1566)** described the tricks of vagabonds such as the "counterfeit crank", who faked illness to beg. Historians note such books exaggerated to sell.

**The change** during the reign was a gradual recognition that some able-bodied people were poor **because there was no work**, not because they were lazy — so the answer was to provide work, not only punishment. Responsibility also moved from the Church and private charity to the **parish and the state**, funded by compulsory taxation.

### Changing policies

| Date | Policy | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| **1563** | Act for the Relief of the Poor (Statute of Artificers the same year regulated wages and apprenticeships) | Everyone who could afford it had to contribute to parish poor relief; JPs could imprison people who refused to pay |
| **1572** | Vagabonds Act | Vagrants over 14 were whipped and **burned through the right ear** for a first offence; repeat offenders could be treated as felons and executed. But it also required JPs to register the deserving poor and introduced a **compulsory local poor rate**, with overseers of the poor to run it |
| **1576** | Poor Relief Act | Towns had to provide a stock of **wool, flax, hemp and iron** so the able-bodied poor could **work** for relief; those who refused were sent to a **house of correction** |

**Local initiatives** often went further than national law:
- **London:** Christ's Hospital for orphans, St Thomas's and St Bartholomew's hospitals for the sick, and **Bridewell** as a house of correction for idle vagrants (founded or refounded between the 1540s and 1550s).
- **Norwich:** in **1570** the city carried out a **census of the poor**, recording hundreds of poor households with their ages, jobs and illnesses, then reorganised relief — work for the able-bodied, help for the sick and old, education for poor children, and a compulsory rate.
- **Ipswich** and **York** also experimented with compulsory rates and work schemes.

The Elizabethan system was completed after this period by the **Poor Laws of 1598 and 1601**, which lasted until 1834 — but the key principles (compulsory rates, work for the able-bodied, punishment for the idle, relief for the deserving) were in place by 1576.

### Making a judgement

When weighing causes of poverty, a strong answer distinguishes **long-term structural causes** (population growth and inflation, which affected the whole country for the whole period) from **short-term triggers** (bad harvests, trade slumps, plague). When judging change in policy, note both **continuity** — harsh punishment of vagrants continued throughout — and **change** — compulsory rates and work schemes show a new acceptance of state responsibility.

::: terms
- **Grammar school**: a school for boys, usually aged about 7–14, teaching mainly Latin; many new ones were founded under Elizabeth.
- **Petty school**: a small local school teaching young children reading, writing and the catechism, often run in a teacher's home.
- **Vagabond**: a homeless, unemployed person who travelled from place to place, often begging; feared as a criminal or a threat to order.
- **Deserving poor**: people who were poor through no fault of their own, such as the old, sick, disabled and orphans, and so deserved help.
- **Enclosure**: fencing off open fields or common land, often for sheep farming, which reduced jobs and common grazing.
- **Poor rate**: a local tax on better-off parishioners to pay for poor relief, made compulsory by the 1572 Act.
- **House of correction**: an institution where able-bodied poor who refused to work were sent to be punished and made to work.
- **Master of the Revels**: the royal official who licensed and censored plays and organised court entertainments.
- **Inflation**: a general rise in prices; under Elizabeth, food prices rose faster than wages, so real wages fell.
:::

::: example Explain why (12)
Explain why poverty increased in Elizabethan England. (12)

You may use the following in your answer:
- population growth
- enclosure

You must also use information of your own.

Model answer:

The most important long-term reason was **population growth**. England's population rose from roughly 3 million in the 1550s to about 4 million by 1600. More people competed for the same jobs and land, so employers could keep wages low, while the growing demand for food pushed prices up. This meant that real wages fell: a labourer's pay bought less bread every decade, and families with no savings slipped into poverty.

A second reason was **enclosure and rack-renting**. Some landowners fenced off common land and turned arable land into sheep pasture, because wool was profitable. Sheep farming needed far fewer workers than growing crops, and villagers lost the common land where they grazed animals. Landlords also raised rents sharply. As a result, some families were forced off the land and took to the roads as vagabonds looking for work.

A third reason, which made the others worse, was **bad harvests and trade slumps**. When harvests failed, as in 1586–87, grain prices could double, and because the poor spent most of their income on bread they were hit hardest. At the same time the cloth industry suffered when trade with the Netherlands was disrupted by the embargo of 1569–73 and the fall of Antwerp in 1585, throwing weavers and spinners out of work. Because population growth had already pushed many families to the edge, these short-term crises turned hardship into destitution — and without the monasteries, dissolved in the 1530s, there was less charity to fall back on.
:::

::: example How far do you agree (16)
"Attitudes and policies towards the poor changed completely in the years 1558–88." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16 + 4 SPaG)

You may use: the 1572 Vagabonds Act; the 1576 Poor Relief Act. You must also use information of your own.

Model answer (outline of a Level 4 response):

**Agree — change.** The 1572 Act made the poor rate compulsory and required JPs to register the deserving poor, so poor relief became a local tax rather than voluntary charity. The 1576 Act went further: towns had to provide wool, flax, hemp and iron so that the able-bodied could work for relief. This showed a new recognition that some people were poor because there was no work, not because they were idle. Norwich's census of 1570 shows towns investigating poverty systematically.

**Disagree — continuity.** Harsh punishment continued: under the 1572 Act vagrants were whipped and burned through the ear, and repeat offenders could be hanged. Houses of correction punished those who refused work. Pamphlets such as Harman's *Caveat for Common Cursitors* (1566) kept alive the fear of vagabonds as criminals. The division between deserving and undeserving poor remained.

**Judgement.** Policies changed significantly but attitudes did not change completely. The **method** of relief changed most — from charity to compulsory rates and work schemes — but the underlying attitude, that idle vagrants were dangerous and must be punished, stayed the same. The best criterion is how the able-bodied poor were treated: by 1576 they could be given work, but the whip was still waiting for anyone judged idle, so the change was real but partial.
:::
