## What it is

In 1947–49 the Cold War set hard. The USA announced a policy of **containment** through the **Truman Doctrine** and backed it with money through the **Marshall Plan**. Stalin replied with **Cominform** and **Comecon**. The first great crisis came over **Berlin** — the **blockade and airlift of 1948–49** — which led directly to **NATO** and to the division of Germany into the **Federal Republic of Germany (FRG)** and the **German Democratic Republic (GDR)**. By the end of 1949 Europe was split into two armed, organised blocs.

### The Truman Doctrine, March 1947

**Background.** Britain had been supporting the Greek government against communist guerrillas in the Greek Civil War. In **February 1947** a nearly bankrupt Britain told the USA it could no longer afford to. Turkey was also under Soviet pressure over access to the Mediterranean. Truman feared that if Greece fell, communism would spread country by country (later called the "domino theory").

**The speech.** On **12 March 1947** Truman asked Congress for **400 million US dollars** in aid for Greece and Turkey. He said the world faced a choice between two ways of life — one based on free institutions and elections, the other on terror, oppression and a controlled press — and declared: "It must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures."

**Impact on US–Soviet relations:**
- It made **containment** official US policy: the USA would stop communism spreading beyond where it already was.
- It ended American **isolationism** — the USA committed itself to permanent involvement in Europe and the world.
- It divided the world publicly into two opposed ideologies, with the USA as leader of the "free" side. The USSR saw this as the USA claiming the right to interfere anywhere.
- In Greece it worked: with US money and advisers the government defeated the communists by 1949.

### The Marshall Plan, 1947

The Truman Doctrine was the idea; the Marshall Plan was the money. Secretary of State **George Marshall** visited Europe in early 1947 and found it in ruins after a terrible winter — cities bombed, industry wrecked, food rationed. Poverty helped communists: communist parties were strong in **France and Italy**. In **June 1947** he announced the **European Recovery Programme**.

- About **13 billion US dollars** went to 16 Western European countries from 1948 to 1952 (Britain and France received the most).
- Aid came as money, food, machinery and goods — much of it bought from American firms, which also helped the US economy.
- Congress hesitated until the **communist coup in Czechoslovakia (February 1948)** frightened it into approving the plan in April 1948.

**Soviet response.** The plan was offered to all of Europe, including the East. At a conference in Paris in July 1947 the Soviet delegation under **Molotov** walked out. Stalin forbade the satellite states — Czechoslovakia and Poland had been interested — to accept. He saw Marshall Aid as **"dollar imperialism"**: a way of using American money to buy influence, open markets to US goods and pull Eastern Europe out of the Soviet sphere.

**Impact on relations:** Europe was now divided **economically** as well as politically. Western Europe recovered and became tied to the USA; Eastern Europe did not. It made the Iron Curtain a line between rich and poor as well as between two ideologies.

### Cominform (1947) and Comecon (1949)

| | **Cominform** — Communist Information Bureau | **Comecon** — Council for Mutual Economic Assistance |
|---|---|---|
| Set up | September 1947 | January 1949 |
| What it did | Brought together the communist parties of Eastern Europe (and of France and Italy) under Soviet direction; spread Soviet policy; made sure satellite governments followed Moscow and rejected the Marshall Plan | Soviet alternative to the Marshall Plan: coordinated the economies of the Eastern bloc and encouraged trade within it |
| Members | Communist parties of the USSR, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Yugoslavia, France and Italy | Founding members: USSR, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria; Albania joined in 1949 and East Germany in 1950 |
| Significance | Tightened Stalin's political control. When Yugoslavia's Tito refused to obey, Yugoslavia was **expelled in 1948** — a warning to others. Showed the West that the East was a closed, disciplined bloc. | Tied the satellites' economies to the USSR and to each other, and kept Western trade out. But the USSR had far less to give than the USA, so it mainly served Soviet interests. It confirmed the **economic** division of Europe. |

### Berlin: division and the Berlin Crisis, 1948–49

**The division.** After Potsdam, Germany was split into four zones (US, British, French, Soviet). **Berlin** lay about **160 km (100 miles) inside the Soviet zone**, and was itself split into four sectors. The Western Allies had agreed road, rail, canal and air access routes to their sectors.

**Why a crisis developed:**
1. **Different aims.** Stalin wanted Germany weak, paying reparations, never able to invade again. The West wanted a prosperous Germany that could trade, feed itself and resist communism.
2. **Bizonia and Trizonia.** In **January 1947** Britain and the USA merged their zones economically (Bizonia); France joined in 1948, and Marshall Aid flowed into the western zones. The West was clearly building a separate West German state.
3. **The new currency.** In **June 1948** the West introduced a new currency, the **Deutschmark**, in its zones and then in West Berlin, without consulting the USSR. Stalin feared it would destabilise the Soviet zone's economy.
4. **West Berlin as a showcase.** Prosperous West Berlin, in the middle of the poorer Soviet zone, was an embarrassment and an advertisement for capitalism.

**The blockade.** On **24 June 1948** Stalin cut all road, rail and canal links into West Berlin. About **two million** West Berliners had supplies of food and fuel for only a few weeks. Stalin's aim was to force the West either to abandon West Berlin or to drop its plans for a separate West Germany.

**The airlift.** Truman would not withdraw (that would look like appeasement) but would not force the roads open (that could start a war). Instead the Western allies supplied the city by air — Stalin could only stop the planes by shooting them down, which would be an act of war.
- American and British planes (and crews from other Commonwealth countries) flew into Tempelhof, Gatow and, from late 1948, a new airport at Tegel.
- In total around **2.3 million tons** of supplies — food, coal, medicine — were flown in over about **278,000 flights**. At its peak planes were landing every few minutes.
- Truman also sent B-29 bombers, the type that had dropped the atomic bombs, to bases in Britain as a warning.
- Around 100 people died, mostly in air accidents.

On **12 May 1949** Stalin lifted the blockade. It had failed.

**Impact of the crisis:**
- **Propaganda victory for the West**: it looked humane and determined; the USSR looked as though it was trying to starve civilians.
- **Humiliation for Stalin**: the blockade had achieved neither of its aims.
- **The first direct confrontation** between the superpowers — it showed neither side would risk actual war, a pattern for the rest of the Cold War.
- **NATO** was formed during the crisis, in April 1949.
- **Germany was permanently divided** into two states.
- Berlin remained a flashpoint — it returned as a crisis in 1958–61.

### NATO, April 1949

The **North Atlantic Treaty Organisation** was signed on **4 April 1949** in Washington by 12 countries: the **USA, Canada, Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, Italy and Portugal**. Its key principle (Article 5) was that **an armed attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all**.

**Significance:**
- It was the USA's first peacetime military alliance: the US committed itself to defend Western Europe, with troops stationed there.
- Containment now had a **military** arm as well as an economic one (Marshall) and a political one (Truman Doctrine).
- Stalin saw it as an aggressive alliance aimed at the USSR. When West Germany joined NATO in **1955**, the USSR created the **Warsaw Pact** in response.

### Two Germanys, 1949

- **Federal Republic of Germany (FRG, West Germany)** — formed **May 1949** from the three western zones; capital **Bonn**; first chancellor **Konrad Adenauer** (from September 1949); a democratic, capitalist state allied to the West.
- **German Democratic Republic (GDR, East Germany)** — formed **October 1949** from the Soviet zone; capital East Berlin; a one-party communist state dominated by the SED under **Walter Ulbricht**.

The division, meant to be temporary in 1945, now lasted until 1990. Germany became the front line of the Cold War.

::: terms
- **Containment**: the US policy, announced in the Truman Doctrine, of preventing communism spreading beyond the countries where it already existed.
- **Truman Doctrine**: Truman's March 1947 pledge that the USA would support free peoples resisting takeover by armed minorities or outside pressure.
- **Marshall Plan**: the European Recovery Programme of about 13 billion US dollars in aid to rebuild Western Europe, 1948–52.
- **Dollar imperialism**: the Soviet description of Marshall Aid as the USA using its wealth to gain control over European countries.
- **Cominform**: the Communist Information Bureau (1947), which coordinated communist parties under Soviet direction.
- **Comecon**: the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (1949), the Soviet bloc's economic organisation and answer to the Marshall Plan.
- **Bizonia**: the economic merger of the British and US zones of Germany in January 1947, later joined by France.
- **Berlin Airlift**: the Western supply of West Berlin by air during the Soviet blockade of June 1948 to May 1949.
- **NATO**: the North Atlantic Treaty Organisation (April 1949), a Western military alliance in which an attack on one member was an attack on all.
- **FRG and GDR**: West Germany (Federal Republic, May 1949) and East Germany (Democratic Republic, October 1949).
:::

::: method
Paper 2 period study (Booklet P, 32 marks, about 50 minutes). All questions are worth 8 marks and test AO1 (knowledge) and AO2 (explanation). No SPaG.

**1. "Explain two consequences of…" (8)** — each consequence out of 4.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple or general ("it caused tension").
- Level 2 (3–4): a specific consequence explained using precise knowledge.
- Pattern: *state the consequence* → *support it with a fact* → *explain what difference it made*. Pick two genuinely different consequences (e.g. for Germany, and for the superpowers).

**2. "Write a narrative account analysing…" (8)** — two stimulus points given; own knowledge required.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple story, little organisation.
- Level 2 (3–5): some sequencing and some links. **Capped at 5 if you use only the two bullets.**
- Level 3 (6–8): a coherent sequence (beginning → development → outcome) with every step linked by cause and consequence. Use connectives: "in response…", "this led to…", "as a result…".

**3. "Explain two of the following: the importance of X for Y" (8 each, two of three)**
- Level 1 (1–2): general comment about X.
- Level 2 (3–5): some explanation of importance with relevant knowledge.
- Level 3 (6–8): shows **what difference X made to Y** (the named focus — relations, the division of Germany, Soviet control of Eastern Europe) with precise knowledge, typically two or three strands, and a closing line on which was most important.
:::

::: example Consequences — the Berlin Crisis
Explain two consequences of the Berlin Crisis (1948–49). (8)

**Consequence 1:** One consequence was the formation of NATO in April 1949. The blockade convinced Western governments that Stalin might use force to expand, so the USA, Canada and ten Western European countries signed the North Atlantic Treaty, agreeing that an attack on one would be an attack on all. This committed the USA to defending Western Europe in peacetime for the first time and gave containment a military form, which in turn led the USSR to form the Warsaw Pact in 1955.

**Consequence 2:** A second consequence was the permanent division of Germany. Because the crisis ended any chance of agreement on Germany's future, the three western zones became the Federal Republic of Germany in May 1949, with Adenauer as chancellor, and the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic in October 1949. Germany therefore became two rival states on the front line of the Cold War, and Berlin remained a divided city inside the GDR, setting up the later crises of 1958–61.
:::

::: example Narrative — from Truman Doctrine to divided Europe
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of 1947–49 that led to the division of Europe. You may use the following in your answer: the Marshall Plan (1947); Comecon (1949). You must also use information of your own. (8)

In March 1947 Britain's withdrawal of support from Greece prompted Truman to announce the Truman Doctrine, promising help to free peoples resisting communism and asking Congress for 400 million US dollars for Greece and Turkey. This made containment official US policy and publicly divided the world into two ways of life.

Containment was then given economic force. In June 1947 George Marshall announced the European Recovery Programme, because poverty was making communism attractive in countries such as France and Italy. Stalin saw it as dollar imperialism, so Molotov walked out of the Paris talks and the satellite states were forbidden to join. Europe was now divided economically.

In response, Stalin tightened control. Cominform was set up in September 1947 to keep communist parties in line, and in February 1948 a communist coup took Czechoslovakia — which in turn pushed Congress to approve Marshall Aid in April. In January 1949 Comecon was created as the East's alternative, binding the satellite economies to the USSR.

Germany was where the division became final. The western zones' new currency in June 1948 led Stalin to blockade West Berlin; the Western airlift defeated the blockade by May 1949. During the crisis the West formed NATO in April 1949, and by October two German states existed. By the end of 1949 Europe was split into two political, economic and military blocs.
:::

::: example Importance — the Marshall Plan
Explain the importance of the Marshall Plan for relations between the USA and the Soviet Union. (8)

The Marshall Plan was important because it turned containment from words into action. The Truman Doctrine had promised support; the Marshall Plan delivered about 13 billion US dollars to 16 Western European countries between 1948 and 1952, rebuilding industry and reducing the poverty that had made communist parties strong in France and Italy. This showed Stalin the USA intended to be a permanent power in Europe.

It was also important because Stalin interpreted it as an attack on his sphere of influence. Aid was offered to Eastern Europe too, and Czechoslovakia and Poland were interested. Stalin forced them to refuse, and Molotov walked out of the Paris talks, denouncing the plan as dollar imperialism. The USSR then answered with Cominform in 1947 and Comecon in 1949, so the plan directly led to rival Soviet organisations.

Most importantly, it divided Europe economically. Western Europe recovered and became tied to the USA; the East stayed poorer and tied to the USSR. The western zones of Germany benefited too, which helped cause the Berlin Crisis of 1948. The plan therefore made the Iron Curtain an economic as well as a political line.
:::

::: q
Why did Truman announce the Truman Doctrine in March 1947, and what did he ask Congress for?
---
Britain could no longer afford to support the Greek government against communist guerrillas (February 1947), and Turkey was under Soviet pressure. Truman feared communism would spread; he asked Congress for 400 million US dollars for Greece and Turkey and announced the USA would support free peoples resisting subjugation — containment.
:::

::: q
Give three reasons why the USA introduced the Marshall Plan.
---
Any three: Europe was devastated and poor after the war; poverty made communism attractive (strong communist parties in France and Italy); to put containment into practice; to rebuild markets for American exports; the Czech coup (February 1948) showed the danger of communist takeovers, pushing Congress to agree.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between Cominform and Comecon.
---
Cominform (1947) was political: it coordinated communist parties under Soviet direction and made sure they followed Moscow (Yugoslavia expelled 1948). Comecon (1949) was economic: it coordinated the Eastern bloc's economies and trade as the Soviet alternative to Marshall Aid.
:::

::: q
Give three reasons why Stalin blockaded West Berlin in June 1948.
---
Any three: the West introduced the Deutschmark without consulting him; the western zones were being merged (Bizonia/Trizonia) into a separate West German state; Marshall Aid was rebuilding the West while the Soviet zone stayed poor; prosperous West Berlin was an embarrassing showcase for capitalism inside the Soviet zone; he wanted to force the West out of Berlin or make it abandon plans for West Germany.
:::

::: q
Why did the West choose an airlift rather than sending troops or tanks along the roads?
---
Forcing the roads open risked a shooting war with the USSR; abandoning Berlin would look like appeasement and damage US credibility. An airlift supplied the city without force — Stalin could only stop it by shooting down planes, which would make him the aggressor.
:::

::: q
List the 12 founding members of NATO and state its key principle.
---
USA, Canada, Britain, France, Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, Italy, Portugal. Key principle (Article 5): an armed attack on one member is an attack on all.
:::

::: q
When were the FRG and GDR created, and who led each?
---
FRG (West Germany): May 1949, capital Bonn, Chancellor Konrad Adenauer. GDR (East Germany): October 1949, a communist state dominated by the SED under Walter Ulbricht.

- Treating the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan as the same thing — the Doctrine is the **policy** (containment, March 1947); the Plan is the **money** (about 13 billion US dollars, June 1947 onward).
- Forgetting that Marshall Aid was **offered to the East too** — Stalin's refusal is the key to its impact on relations.
- Confusing Cominform (political, 1947) with Comecon (economic, 1949).
- Berlin answers that skip the **causes** of the blockade (currency, Bizonia, Marshall Aid) or its **results** (NATO, FRG/GDR).
- Narrative answers that stop at the two bullets — capped at 5.
- Importance answers that never say "this was important for US–Soviet relations because…".
