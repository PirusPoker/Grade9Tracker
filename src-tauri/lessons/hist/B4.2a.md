## What it is

Between 1569 and 1587 Elizabeth faced a series of **Catholic rebellions and plots**, almost all of them aimed at replacing her with **Mary, Queen of Scots**, who had been Elizabeth's prisoner since 1568. This topic covers the spec points: the **reasons for, and significance of, the Revolt of the Northern Earls (1569–70)**; the **features and significance of the Ridolfi (1571), Throckmorton (1583) and Babington (1586) plots**; **Walsingham and the use of spies**; and the **reasons for, and significance of, Mary's execution in 1587**. The story is one of an increasing threat — from a regional rising to international conspiracies involving Spain, France and the Pope — met by an increasingly effective government response, ending with the removal of Mary herself.

### The Revolt of the Northern Earls, 1569–70

**Who:** Thomas Percy, **Earl of Northumberland**, and Charles Neville, **Earl of Westmorland** — heads of two great Catholic families in the north of England. Their plans were linked with the **Duke of Norfolk**, England's only duke.

**Reasons for the revolt:**

1. **Religion.** The north was the most Catholic part of England. The earls wanted to restore Catholicism and saw the 1559 religious settlement as heresy. Many ordinary northerners still followed the old faith.
2. **Loss of power and influence in the north.** The northern nobles had traditionally governed the region, but Elizabeth relied on southern "new men" loyal to her. The **Council of the North** was run by Protestant southerners; important posts on the Scottish border (the wardenships of the Marches) went to outsiders such as Sir John Forster and Lord Hunsdon. Northumberland also lost income when the Crown claimed the profits of copper mines discovered on his land.
3. **Resentment of Cecil and the "new men".** Some nobles at court, including Norfolk, blamed William Cecil for Elizabeth's policies and wanted him removed.
4. **Mary, Queen of Scots and the succession.** Mary's arrival in 1568 gave Catholics an alternative queen on English soil. The plan was for **Mary to marry Norfolk**, be recognised as Elizabeth's heir and, for some rebels, replace Elizabeth altogether. Elizabeth was unmarried and had no heir, so the question of who came next mattered enormously.
5. **Hope of foreign support.** The Spanish ambassador, **Guerau de Spes**, encouraged the plotters and led them to hope that Spanish troops from the Netherlands would help.

**Events:**

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| September–October 1569 | Elizabeth discovers the Norfolk marriage plan; Norfolk leaves court, then submits and is sent to the Tower |
| November 1569 | The earls, summoned to court, rise instead |
| 14 November 1569 | The rebels take **Durham Cathedral**, tear up the English Bible and Book of Common Prayer, and hold a **Catholic Mass** |
| Late November | They march south towards **Tutbury** to free Mary; Mary is moved to Coventry; the rebels (about **4,600** men) turn back in Yorkshire |
| December 1569 | They besiege and take **Barnard Castle**; a large royal army under the **Earl of Sussex**, Lord President of the Council of the North, advances; the earls **flee to Scotland** |
| February 1570 | A separate rising by **Leonard Dacre** is defeated by **Lord Hunsdon** |
| February 1570 | Pope Pius V issues the bull **Regnans in Excelsis**, excommunicating Elizabeth — too late to help the rebels |

**Outcome:** Around **450** rebels were executed. Northumberland was handed over by the Scots and **executed at York in 1572**; Westmorland escaped abroad and died in exile. Norfolk was released in 1570 but went on to plot again.

**Significance:**
- It was the **most serious rebellion of the reign** — the only real armed rising against Elizabeth — and showed that Mary could be a rallying point for Catholic nobles.
- It **failed**: most Catholics did not join; the earls had no clear plan, no foreign help arrived, and the government responded quickly. It showed the strength of Elizabeth's position.
- Elizabeth **tightened control of the north**: the Council of the North was reformed and from 1572 led by the Puritan **Earl of Huntingdon**; the power of the Percys and Nevilles was broken.
- The **excommunication (1570)** that followed changed the situation for English Catholics: the Pope now released them from obedience to Elizabeth, so the government increasingly saw Catholics as potential traitors. Harsher laws followed (e.g. the 1571 Treason Act made it treason to deny Elizabeth was the rightful queen).

### The Ridolfi plot, 1571

**Features:**
- **Roberto Ridolfi** was an Italian (Florentine) banker living in London who acted as a go-between for the Pope, Spain and Mary.
- The plan: English Catholics would rise; the **Duke of Alba** would invade from the Netherlands with about **10,000** Spanish troops; Elizabeth would be overthrown (and probably killed); **Mary would marry Norfolk** and become queen, restoring Catholicism.
- It had the support of **Pope Pius V** and of **Philip II**, though Alba doubted it could work.
- It was uncovered in 1571 by **William Cecil** (made **Lord Burghley** that year) and his agents, when a messenger, **Charles Baillie**, was arrested at Dover carrying letters, and Norfolk's involvement was discovered through intercepted money and servants' confessions.

**Significance:**
- **Norfolk was executed in June 1572** — the only duke in England, so the nobility lost its most powerful Catholic sympathiser.
- It showed **foreign powers were now involved** — the Pope and Spain — making Mary part of an international Catholic threat. The Spanish ambassador de Spes was **expelled**.
- **Parliament demanded Mary's execution**; Elizabeth refused, but Mary was kept under closer guard.
- England sought a defensive alliance with France: the **Treaty of Blois (1572)**.

### The Throckmorton plot, 1583

**Features:**
- **Francis Throckmorton** was a young English Catholic who carried letters between Mary and the Spanish ambassador **Bernardino de Mendoza**.
- The plan: the French Catholic **Duke of Guise** (Mary's cousin) would invade England, with money from **Philip II** and the Pope; English Catholics would rise; Mary would be freed and made queen.
- **Walsingham** had Throckmorton watched for months. He was arrested in **November 1583**; papers found at his house included a **list of Catholic supporters** and harbours suitable for landing troops. Under **torture on the rack** he confessed. He was executed in **1584**.

**Significance:**
- It showed that **Spain was directly involved** — Mendoza was **expelled in January 1584**, and no Spanish ambassador returned to England for the rest of the reign.
- The government cracked down on Catholics: many were arrested, and some leading Catholic nobles fled abroad. Laws against Catholic priests were tightened (e.g. the **1585 Act** against Jesuits and seminary priests made it treason for them to be in England).
- The **Bond of Association (1584)**: thousands of gentlemen swore to kill anyone who plotted against Elizabeth and anyone in whose favour a plot was made — clearly aimed at Mary.
- The **Act for the Preservation of the Queen's Safety (1585)** turned this into law: anyone who plotted, or in whose name a plot was made, could be tried and executed. This made it possible to execute Mary legally if she were implicated.
- Mary was moved to stricter confinement under the Puritan **Sir Amias Paulet**, and her correspondence was cut off — setting up the trap that caught her in 1586.

### The Babington plot, 1586

**Features:**
- **Anthony Babington** was a young Catholic gentleman; the priest **John Ballard** was also central.
- The plan: **six gentlemen** would **assassinate Elizabeth**; English Catholics would rise; a foreign Catholic invasion led by the **Duke of Guise**, backed by Spain, would follow; Mary would become queen.
- The trap: Walsingham used a double agent, **Gilbert Gifford**, to set up a secret channel for Mary's letters at **Chartley**, hidden in **beer barrels**. Every letter was copied and decoded by Walsingham's code-breaker **Thomas Phelippes** before being passed on.
- On **17 July 1586** Mary replied to Babington, **approving the plot**, including the assassination. Phelippes added a forged postscript asking for the names of the six assassins.
- Babington and his fellow conspirators were arrested in August 1586; **fourteen were executed** in September 1586.

**Significance:**
- For the first time there was **written evidence** that Mary had approved Elizabeth's murder — treason under the 1585 Act.
- It led directly to Mary's **trial at Fotheringhay Castle** in October 1586 and her **execution in 1587**.

### Walsingham and the use of spies

**Sir Francis Walsingham** was Elizabeth's **Principal Secretary** from **1573** until his death in 1590. A committed Protestant, he built the most effective intelligence network in Europe to protect Elizabeth.

His methods:
- **A network of spies and informers** in England and abroad — in ports, in foreign courts and in the Catholic seminaries training English priests (such as Douai).
- **Double agents** and **agents provocateurs** — men like Gilbert Gifford who pretended to be Catholic plotters to gain trust and gather evidence.
- **Intercepting letters** and **code-breaking**: Thomas Phelippes decoded Mary's ciphers.
- **Surveillance** of suspects over long periods (Throckmorton for about six months) rather than arresting them at once, to uncover the whole network.
- **Torture** (with royal authority) to obtain confessions, as with Throckmorton.

**Significance:** Walsingham uncovered the Throckmorton and Babington plots before they could be carried out, deterred other conspirators, and — by trapping Mary — provided the evidence that finally persuaded Elizabeth to act. His network also gathered intelligence on Spanish preparations for the Armada.

### The execution of Mary, Queen of Scots, 1587

**Events:** Mary was tried by a commission of peers and councillors at **Fotheringhay Castle** in **October 1586** and found guilty. Parliament petitioned for her execution. Elizabeth hesitated for months, then signed the death warrant on **1 February 1587**. Her secretary **William Davison** passed it to the Privy Council, which sent it to Fotheringhay without telling her. Mary was **beheaded on 8 February 1587**. Elizabeth claimed she had not meant the warrant to be used: Davison was **imprisoned in the Tower and heavily fined**, and Burghley was temporarily banished from court.

**Reasons for the execution:**
1. **Clear evidence** — the Babington letters proved Mary had approved Elizabeth's assassination.
2. **The law now allowed it** — the Act for the Preservation of the Queen's Safety (1585).
3. **Pressure from the Privy Council and Parliament** — Burghley and Walsingham had long argued that Elizabeth would never be safe while Mary lived.
4. **The international situation** — England had been at **war with Spain since 1585**; a Spanish invasion could put Mary on the throne, so she was more dangerous than ever. At the same time the risks of executing her were lower: **James VI** of Scotland, Mary's son, had signed the **Treaty of Berwick (1586)** and received an English pension, so he was unlikely to go to war over his mother; **France** was divided by civil war.

**Why Elizabeth delayed:** executing an anointed queen set a dangerous precedent that monarchs could be put to death; Mary was her cousin; she feared the reaction of Catholic Europe; and she did not want to take personal responsibility.

**Significance:**
- It **removed the focus of Catholic plots** at home: Mary's heir was the **Protestant James VI**, so English Catholics had no Catholic claimant to rally around, and there were no more major plots of this kind.
- It gave **Philip II** a further motive and justification for invading. Mary had written that she would pass her claim to Philip if James did not become Catholic; Philip could now present the Armada as avenging a Catholic martyr and claim the throne for himself or his daughter. **Pope Sixtus V** promised money for the invasion.
- Relations with **Scotland** survived: James protested but did not break the alliance.
- The immediate result was the **Spanish Armada (1588)**, covered in B4.2c.

### Making a judgement

The threat **changed over time**. In 1569 it was a **domestic, regional** rising of nobles with local grievances as well as religious ones. From 1571 the plots were **international conspiracies**, backed by the Pope, Spain and the Guise family, and aimed at **assassination** rather than just pressure. The government's response also grew more effective — from military force in 1569 to Walsingham's **intelligence system** and new **laws** by the 1580s. Mary was the **common thread** in every plot: the key turning point was 1586–87, when Walsingham's trap produced evidence that made her execution possible.

::: terms
- **Revolt of the Northern Earls**: the 1569–70 Catholic rising led by the Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland, the most serious rebellion of Elizabeth's reign.
- **Regnans in Excelsis**: the papal bull of February 1570 that excommunicated Elizabeth and released Catholics from obeying her.
- **Ridolfi plot**: the 1571 plan, backed by the Pope and Philip II, for a Spanish invasion under Alba and for Mary to marry Norfolk and become queen.
- **Throckmorton plot**: the 1583 plan for a French invasion led by the Duke of Guise, funded by Spain, to put Mary on the throne.
- **Babington plot**: the 1586 plan to assassinate Elizabeth, in which Mary's letter of approval was intercepted by Walsingham.
- **Bond of Association**: the 1584 oath by which thousands of gentlemen swore to kill anyone who plotted against Elizabeth or in whose name a plot was made.
- **Act for the Preservation of the Queen's Safety**: the 1585 law allowing anyone in whose name a plot was made to be tried and executed.
- **Double agent**: a spy who pretends to work for one side while secretly working for the other, like Gilbert Gifford.
- **Principal Secretary**: the leading royal minister running government business and intelligence, the post Walsingham held from 1573.
:::

::: example Explain why (12)
Explain why Mary, Queen of Scots was executed in 1587. (12)

You may use the following in your answer:
- the Babington plot
- the Act for the Preservation of the Queen's Safety (1585)

You must also use information of your own.

Model answer:

The most immediate reason was the **evidence from the Babington plot**. Mary had been at the centre of plots since 1569, but Elizabeth had always refused to execute her without proof. In 1586 Walsingham's double agent Gilbert Gifford set up a secret channel for Mary's letters in beer barrels at Chartley, and his code-breaker Thomas Phelippes read them all. On 17 July 1586 Mary wrote to Anthony Babington approving a plan in which six gentlemen would assassinate Elizabeth. This was written proof of treason in her own words, so Mary could no longer be presented as an innocent prisoner, and she was tried and found guilty at Fotheringhay in October 1586.

A second reason was that **the law now made execution possible**. After the Throckmorton plot, thousands of gentry signed the Bond of Association in 1584, and in 1585 Parliament passed the Act for the Preservation of the Queen's Safety, which allowed anyone in whose name a plot was made to be tried and put to death. Without this Act, trying a foreign queen would have had no clear legal basis. The Act meant that once the Babington evidence appeared, a legal process followed almost automatically, and Parliament petitioned for the sentence to be carried out.

A third reason, from my own knowledge, was the **changed international situation**. Since the Treaty of Nonsuch in 1585 England had been at war with Spain, and Philip II was preparing an invasion that could put Mary on the throne. So Mary alive was now more dangerous than ever. At the same time, the risks of executing her had fallen: James VI had signed the Treaty of Berwick in 1586 and took an English pension, so Scotland was unlikely to fight, and France was divided by civil war. This is why the evidence of 1586 was decisive when earlier plots, such as Ridolfi in 1571, had not led to Mary's death: the evidence gave the justification, the 1585 Act gave the legal means, and the international situation meant Elizabeth could no longer afford to keep Mary alive. Even so, it took pressure from Burghley and Walsingham, and Davison's sending of the warrant, before Mary was beheaded on 8 February 1587.

Model answer (outline of a Level 4 response):

**Agree.** The Northern Revolt was the only occasion when rebels actually took up arms. Led by the Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland, about 4,600 men took Durham Cathedral on 14 November 1569 and celebrated a Catholic Mass, showing open defiance of the religious settlement. They marched towards Tutbury to free Mary and took Barnard Castle. **Disagree — the international plots.** The Ridolfi plot (1571) involved the Pope and Philip II, with Alba to invade with about 10,000 troops from the Netherlands. The Throckmorton plot (1583) planned a French invasion under the Duke of Guise funded by Spain, and the Babington plot (1586) aimed to assassinate Elizabeth. The Babington plot was more serious because it targeted Elizabeth's life directly and was linked to a foreign invasion at a time when England was already at war with Spain; only Walsingham's spy network prevented it. However, the revolt was significant because it began the pattern — Mary as a focus for Catholic plots, and the excommunication — that made the later plots possible.
