## What it is

This lesson covers the first half of **c1900–present: Medicine in modern Britain** — how our understanding of the **causes** of illness changed (genetics and lifestyle), how **diagnosis** improved (blood tests, scans, monitors), how **care and treatment** changed with the **NHS** and new medicines (**magic bullets** and **antibiotics**), and the key-individuals case study of **Fleming, Florey and Chain's development of penicillin**. It is the period where all three factors — individuals and institutions (now above all **government**), science and technology, and attitudes in society — work together faster than ever before.

### Where the story starts in 1900

By 1900 doctors accepted **germ theory** (Pasteur 1861, Koch's identification of specific bacteria from the 1870s–80s). They knew *what* caused infectious disease but could do little to *cure* it once someone was ill: there were vaccines for a few diseases, antiseptic and aseptic surgery, and anaesthetics, but no drug that killed bacteria inside the body. Most people paid for a doctor, so the poor often went without. The twentieth century changed both halves of that — cure and access.

### 1. Causes: genetic and lifestyle factors

**Genetics.** Doctors had long noticed that some conditions ran in families, but the mechanism was unknown until the structure of **DNA** was worked out.

- In **1953**, **James Watson and Francis Crick**, at the Cavendish Laboratory in **Cambridge**, published the **double-helix** structure of DNA. Their model depended on X-ray diffraction images of DNA made at King's College London by **Rosalind Franklin** (the famous "Photo 51") and the work of **Maurice Wilkins**. Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize in 1962; Franklin had died in 1958.
- Knowing that genes are instructions carried on DNA explained **genetic (inherited) conditions** — for example **cystic fibrosis**, **Huntington's disease** and **sickle-cell anaemia** — and chromosomal conditions such as **Down's syndrome**. It also explained **genetic predisposition**: some genes, such as faulty **BRCA1/BRCA2** genes, greatly raise the risk of breast and ovarian cancer.
- The international **Human Genome Project (1990–2003)** mapped all the genes in human DNA; Britain's Sanger Centre near Cambridge sequenced about a third of it. The UK's **100,000 Genomes Project** (Genomics England, completed 2018) sequenced patients with rare diseases and cancers.
- Impact: genetic **screening and testing** (e.g. the newborn heel-prick test includes cystic fibrosis), family counselling, preventive surgery for high-risk BRCA carriers, and the hope of **gene therapy** and treatments targeted at a tumour's own mutations. The limit: understanding a cause is not the same as a cure — most genetic conditions still cannot be cured.

**Lifestyle.** As infectious diseases were controlled, the big killers became **heart disease, cancers and strokes**, and research showed that how people live causes much of them.

- **Smoking** — in **1950** **Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill** published a study of lung cancer patients in 20 London hospitals showing a strong link between smoking and lung cancer; their **British Doctors Study** (from 1951) confirmed it. The **Royal College of Physicians' report *Smoking and Health* (1962)** made the causal link public.
- **Diet** (too much fat, salt and sugar → obesity, heart disease, type 2 diabetes); **alcohol** (liver disease, some cancers); **lack of exercise**; **sunbathing** (skin cancer); **unprotected sex** (sexually transmitted infections, HIV).
- Significance: if behaviour causes disease, then prevention means **changing behaviour** — which leads to government **lifestyle campaigns** (next lesson). It also shifts responsibility partly onto the individual, a real change in attitudes.

### 2. Improvements in diagnosis

Medieval doctors diagnosed by looking at urine and taking the pulse; Sydenham in the seventeenth century stressed careful observation of symptoms. The twentieth century gave doctors the ability to see **inside** the body and to **measure** it.

| Technology | When | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **X-rays** | discovered by Wilhelm Röntgen, **1895**; in hospitals within a year | images of bones and dense objects (fractures, bullets, some tumours) |
| **Blood tests** | blood groups discovered by Karl Landsteiner, **1901**; routine testing grows through the century | measure blood sugar (**diabetes**), cholesterol, infections, anaemia, hormone levels, liver and kidney function, some cancer markers, genetic conditions |
| **Ultrasound** | pioneered in obstetrics by **Ian Donald** in **Glasgow** in the late 1950s | sound waves image soft tissue and babies in the womb, with no radiation |
| **CT (computed tomography) scans** | **Godfrey Hounsfield** at EMI; first patient scan **1971** in London | combines many X-rays into cross-section images of the body and brain |
| **MRI scans** | developed in the **1970s**; Peter Mansfield of Nottingham shared the 2003 Nobel Prize | magnetic fields and radio waves give detailed images of soft tissue — brain, spine, tumours — with no radiation |
| **Monitors** | **ECG** (electrocardiograph, Willem Einthoven, 1903); modern bedside monitors | record heart rhythm, blood pressure, oxygen levels and blood sugar continuously; home monitors let diabetics check glucose themselves |
| **Endoscopes** | flexible fibre-optic endoscopes from the 1950s–60s | a camera on a tube looks inside the gut or lungs and can take a biopsy |

The impact: diagnosis became **faster, more accurate and earlier**. Many conditions — high blood pressure, raised cholesterol, early cancers — can be found **before** symptoms appear, which is why screening programmes (breast screening from 1988, bowel screening from 2006) are possible. The factors: **science and technology** made the machines; the **NHS** made them available to everyone free.

### 3. Care and treatment: the NHS

**Before 1948.** Doctors charged fees. The **National Insurance Act (1911)**, introduced by the Liberal Chancellor **David Lloyd George**, gave lower-paid workers free GP care and sick pay in return for weekly contributions — but it did **not** cover their wives, children or the elderly, and it did not cover hospital treatment. Hospitals were a patchwork of **voluntary** (charity) hospitals and **council (municipal)** hospitals of very uneven quality.

**Why it came in 1948.**
- **The Second World War**: the government ran hospitals nationally through the **Emergency Medical Service** (1939), which showed that central organisation worked and exposed how poor many hospitals were. Evacuation revealed how unhealthy many poor city children were.
- **The Beveridge Report (1942)** identified five "giants" to be defeated — **Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness** — and proposed a comprehensive health service. It was hugely popular.
- **Attitudes**: after a "people's war", voters expected the state to look after them; Labour won the **1945** election with a landslide.
- **Individual**: **Aneurin Bevan**, Labour's Minister of Health, pushed the NHS through despite strong opposition from many doctors and the **British Medical Association (BMA)**, who feared losing income and independence. He won over hospital consultants by letting them keep private patients alongside NHS work.

**The NHS began on 5 July 1948.** It was **free at the point of use**, paid for by general taxation, and available to **everyone**. Hospitals were nationalised and organised regionally; GPs, dentists, opticians and pharmacists joined it.

**The impact.**
- **Access**: for the first time the whole population — including women, children and the elderly — could see a doctor, get glasses, dentures and prescriptions without paying. Demand in the first years far exceeded forecasts, which showed how much illness had gone untreated.
- **Cost**: spending rose so fast that **charges** were soon introduced — for spectacles and dentures in 1951, and a prescription charge in 1952.
- **Scale**: the NHS could afford expensive technology (scanners, transplant units, radiotherapy) and organise national **vaccination** and **screening** programmes, and train doctors and nurses nationally.
- **Limits and continuity**: waiting lists, regional variation, pressure from an ageing population and expensive new drugs. Since 1999 **NICE** decides which new drugs and treatments are cost-effective enough for the NHS to fund.

### 4. Advances in medicines: magic bullets and antibiotics

A **magic bullet** is a chemical that kills a specific microbe inside the body without harming the patient — the idea came from **Paul Ehrlich**, who had worked in Koch's institute.

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| **1909** | Ehrlich and **Sahachiro Hata** find compound number **606** kills the bacterium that causes **syphilis**; sold from **1910** as **Salvarsan** — the first magic bullet. |
| **1932** | **Gerhard Domagk** (Bayer, Germany) finds the red dye **Prontosil** kills streptococcal bacteria (blood poisoning) in mice; he later uses it to save his own daughter's arm, and it is published in 1935. |
| **1938** | British firm **May & Baker** produces **M&B 693** (a sulphonamide) against pneumonia; it helped **Winston Churchill** recover from pneumonia in 1943. |
| **1928–45** | Penicillin — the first **antibiotic** (see case study). |
| **1940s onwards** | more antibiotics, e.g. **streptomycin** (1943) against tuberculosis; later drugs for high blood pressure, heart disease (beta-blockers, statins), **insulin** for diabetes (1921–22), chemotherapy for cancer. |

**Limits.** Sulphonamides had side effects and did not work on the most common staphylococcus germs; antibiotics do nothing against **viruses**; and overuse has produced **antibiotic resistance** — "superbugs" such as **MRSA**. The **thalidomide** disaster (a morning-sickness drug that caused birth defects around 1958–61) led to much tighter drug testing and the **Medicines Act (1968)**.

### 5. Case study: Fleming, Florey and Chain and penicillin

**Alexander Fleming (discovery, 1928).** A bacteriologist at **St Mary's Hospital, London**, Fleming had seen soldiers die from infected wounds in the First World War and had shown that antiseptics damaged body tissue. In **September 1928**, returning from holiday, he noticed that a mould — ***Penicillium notatum*** — on an old **Petri dish** of **staphylococcus** had killed the bacteria around it. He grew the mould, found the "mould juice" killed many bacteria and did not harm white blood cells, named it **penicillin** and published his findings in **1929**. But he could not produce it pure or in quantity, it seemed unstable, and he did **not** test it on infected animals, so he did not show it could work inside the body; he used it mainly as a laboratory tool. Chance played a part: the mould spore, the cool weather while he was away and his observant eye.

**Florey and Chain (development, 1938–41).** At the **Sir William Dunn School of Pathology, Oxford**, **Howard Florey** (an Australian) and **Ernst Chain** (a German-Jewish refugee chemist) began researching antibacterial substances in 1938 and read Fleming's paper. **Norman Heatley** devised ways to grow and extract the drug, using bedpans, milk churns and bathtubs.
- **May 1940**: they infected **eight mice** with deadly streptococci and treated four with penicillin; the four treated mice survived and the four untreated died.
- **February 1941**: first human patient, **Albert Alexander**, an Oxford policeman dying of an infection that began with a scratch. He improved dramatically, but the supply ran out — even though penicillin was recovered from his urine — and he died.
- Lesson: it worked, but it needed **mass production**, and British chemical firms were fully committed to the war effort and at risk from bombing.

**Mass production (1941–44).** In **1941** Florey and Heatley went to the **USA**. The US government funded research at a government laboratory in **Peoria, Illinois**, where a better growth medium (corn steep liquor) and a higher-yielding strain of mould (from a mouldy cantaloupe melon) were found, and **deep-tank fermentation** allowed large-scale production. After **Pearl Harbor (December 1941)** the US government paid drug companies to make it as a war priority; by **D-Day (June 1944)** there were **2.3 million doses** ready for the Allied invasion. **Fleming, Florey and Chain** shared the **Nobel Prize in 1945**. After the war penicillin became widely available — in Britain, through the NHS.

**Factors** (the heart of most exam answers):
- **Individuals**: Fleming's observation; Florey's leadership, Chain's chemistry, Heatley's ingenuity.
- **Chance**: the contaminated dish, and Chain happening to find Fleming's paper.
- **Science and technology**: germ theory; the Petri dish and microscopes; deep-tank fermentation and freeze-drying.
- **Government and war**: the Second World War created the need (infected wounds) and the **US government** provided the money and industrial organisation; this is the decisive factor in turning a laboratory curiosity into a medicine. Attitudes — the wartime acceptance of huge state spending — made it possible.

### Change and continuity, c1900–present (for comparisons)

- **Change**: cause of illness now understood at the level of genes and lifestyle; diagnosis by machine and blood test; for the first time, drugs that **cure** bacterial infections; care free for all through the NHS.
- **Continuity**: people still self-treat (the modern pharmacy and herbal remedies echo medieval home care); alternative medicine remains popular; cost still limits treatment (NICE rationing); and diseases still outpace science — antibiotic resistance, new viruses.
- **Comparison hooks**: medieval Church-run hospitals vs the state-run NHS; Jenner's vaccination (1796) vs magic bullets (a cure rather than prevention); Pasteur and Koch (identifying germs) vs Ehrlich and Fleming (killing them); Harvey's discovery (1628) with no practical use for centuries vs penicillin, turned into a treatment in 16 years because government and industry backed it.

::: terms
- **Genetic condition**: an illness caused by a fault in a person's genes or chromosomes, often inherited, such as cystic fibrosis or Huntington's disease.
- **Lifestyle factor**: an aspect of how someone lives — smoking, diet, alcohol, exercise — that raises or lowers their risk of disease.
- **Double helix**: the twisted-ladder structure of DNA published by Watson and Crick in 1953, using Rosalind Franklin's X-ray images.
- **Magic bullet**: a chemical that kills a specific microbe inside the body without harming the patient; Ehrlich's Salvarsan 606 was the first.
- **Sulphonamide**: a family of chemical drugs, beginning with Domagk's Prontosil (1932), that attack bacteria such as streptococci.
- **Antibiotic**: a drug made from a living organism, such as a mould, that kills or stops the growth of bacteria; penicillin was the first.
- **NHS**: the National Health Service, launched on 5 July 1948 by Aneurin Bevan, providing care free at the point of use and funded by taxation.
- **Beveridge Report**: the 1942 government report naming five giants (Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness) that laid the plan for the welfare state.
- **Deep-tank fermentation**: the industrial method, developed in the USA in the 1940s, of growing mould in huge tanks so penicillin could be mass-produced.
- **Antibiotic resistance**: the evolution of bacteria, such as MRSA, that are no longer killed by antibiotics, largely because of overuse.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1, Section B (thematic study) — the three question types, with how each level is reached.**

**Question 3 — "Explain one way in which X was similar to / different from Y." (4 marks)**
- Level 1 (1–2): a simple or general statement of a similarity or difference.
- Level 2 (3–4): the similarity or difference is **explained** and supported with **specific knowledge of both periods**.
- Build: state the one way in the first sentence; give a precise fact from period 1; give a precise fact from period 2; finish with a sentence that ties them back ("so in both periods…" / "whereas…"). One way only — a second earns nothing.

**Question 4 — "Explain why…" (12 marks).** Two stimulus bullet points are printed.
- Level 1 (1–3): simple or general comments, little knowledge.
- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation, some accurate knowledge.
- Level 3 (7–9): an analytical explanation directed at the question, with accurate and relevant knowledge; a line of reasoning that is generally sustained. Answers that use **only** the stimulus points are capped in this level.
- Level 4 (10–12): analytical explanation throughout, well-selected **precise** knowledge, and a line of reasoning that is coherent and logically structured. **You must bring in at least one point of your own beyond the stimulus** to reach Level 4.
- Build: three paragraphs — stimulus 1, stimulus 2, your own factor — each opening with the *reason*, then the evidence (names, dates, figures), then the link: "…and this is why…". No conclusion needed, but a closing sentence showing how the factors connect helps Level 4.

**Questions 5/6 — "'Statement.' How far do you agree? Explain your answer." (16 marks + 4 SPaG).** You choose one of two.
- Level 1 (1–4) / Level 2 (5–8): description or limited explanation; judgement asserted.
- Level 3 (9–12): explanation of both sides with accurate knowledge; a judgement with some justification. Stimulus-only answers are capped in this level.
- Level 4 (13–16): sustained analysis, precise knowledge **beyond the stimulus**, and a judgement whose **criteria are explained** and which is justified throughout.
- Build: introduction that gives your line; two or three paragraphs agreeing; two or three disagreeing; conclusion that weighs them by a **criterion** — e.g. "which factor was *necessary* — without which the others could not have acted?" or "which change reached the *most people*?". Say "to a large extent / partly" and why.
- **SPaG (4)**: accurate spelling and punctuation, clear grammar and **specialist terms** (bacteriology, sulphonamide, NHS, diagnosis). Proofread the last two minutes.

**On this topic** the factors question is king: penicillin (individual vs chance vs government/war vs technology), the NHS (government vs attitudes vs war), and "the most important factor in improving diagnosis/treatment" (science and technology vs government).
:::

::: example 4-mark difference — hospitals
Explain one way in which hospitals in the medieval period were different from hospitals in the period c1900–present. (4)

One difference was who ran and paid for them. In medieval England most hospitals were run by the **Church** and paid for by charity; they were staffed by monks and nuns and offered rest, food and prayer rather than medical treatment, and many refused infectious patients. After **1948**, hospitals were run by the **state** through the **NHS**, funded by taxation, and offered free, highly technical treatment — surgery, scans, transplants — to everyone. So control shifted from the Church, whose priority was caring for the soul, to the government, whose aim was curing the body.
:::

::: example 12-mark explain why — penicillin
Explain why penicillin became widely available in the years 1928–45. (12)
You may use the following in your answer: Fleming's discovery; the Second World War. You **must** also use information of your own.

**Fleming's discovery** was the starting point. In 1928, at St Mary's Hospital, Fleming noticed that *Penicillium* mould had killed staphylococcus bacteria on a Petri dish and published his findings in 1929. This mattered because it identified a substance that killed bacteria without harming white blood cells — but on its own it did not make penicillin available, because Fleming could not purify it or make it in quantity and never tested it on infected animals. His discovery explains why the drug *existed*, not why it reached patients.

The **work of Florey and Chain** turned it into a medicine. Working at Oxford from 1938, they purified penicillin with Heatley's improvised equipment and proved it worked inside the body: in May 1940 four treated mice survived while four untreated mice died, and in 1941 Albert Alexander recovered dramatically until the supply ran out. This proved to governments and companies that penicillin was worth producing, which is why the next step — mass production — was attempted at all.

The **Second World War** and the **US government** made mass production happen. Britain's chemical industry was stretched and under bombing, so Florey went to America in 1941. After Pearl Harbor the US government treated penicillin as a war priority: it funded research at Peoria, where a better mould strain and corn steep liquor raised yields, and paid drug companies to build deep-tank fermentation plants. By D-Day in June 1944 there were 2.3 million doses. War created both the demand — millions of soldiers with infected wounds — and the political will to spend on an unprecedented scale.

So the factors worked together: chance and Fleming's observation began it, Florey and Chain's science proved it, and government money driven by war made it widely available — the last was decisive, since without it penicillin would have stayed a laboratory curiosity.
:::

::: example 16-mark factor judgement — the NHS
"The main reason for the improvement in people's access to medical care in the period c1900–present was the work of the government." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)
You may use the following in your answer: the National Insurance Act (1911); new technology in diagnosis. You **must** also use information of your own.

**Introduction.** I largely agree: science created new treatments, but only government action made them available to the whole population rather than to those who could pay.

**Agree.** The **National Insurance Act (1911)** first gave lower-paid workers a free GP and sick pay, though it left out wives, children and the elderly. The decisive step was the **NHS (5 July 1948)**: Bevan's Ministry of Health made GPs, hospitals, dentists and opticians **free at the point of use** for everyone, funded by taxation — so a widow or child could now see a doctor who before could not. Demand in the first years far exceeded forecasts, proving how many had gone untreated. Government also organised access nationally: vaccination and screening programmes (breast screening from 1988) reach whole age groups, and NICE decides which drugs everyone can have.

**Disagree.** Access means little without something effective to access. **Science and technology** created it: X-rays, **CT scans** (Hounsfield, 1971), MRI and blood tests allow diagnosis that did not exist in 1900, and **penicillin** and later antibiotics gave doctors real cures. **Attitudes** mattered too: the Beveridge Report (1942) was popular because after a "people's war" the public expected fair treatment, and Labour's 1945 landslide gave Bevan the mandate. And the **Second World War** itself, through the Emergency Medical Service, showed the state could run hospitals.

**Judgement.** Science determined *what* care existed; government determined *who got it*. The question is about **access**, and by that criterion government was the main reason: a CT scanner in a private clinic would have reached only the wealthy. But government action depended on attitudes and war — Bevan could create the NHS in 1948 because voters demanded it — so government was the main reason, working through a political climate it did not create alone.
:::
