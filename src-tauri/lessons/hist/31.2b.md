## What it is

In the Reichstag election of **May 1928** the Nazis won just **12 seats** and **2.6%** of the vote. By **July 1932** they were the **largest party in Germany**, with **230 seats** and **37.3%**. Six months later, on **30 January 1933**, Hitler was Chancellor. The spec asks you to explain four things:
1. **The growth of unemployment** after 1929 — its causes and its impact.
2. **The growth of support for the Communist Party (KPD).**
3. **The reasons for the growth in support for the Nazi Party**, including the appeal of Hitler and the Nazis, the effects of propaganda and the work of the SA.
4. **The presidential and Reichstag elections of 1932**, and **why Hitler became Chancellor in 1933**, including the roles of **Hindenburg** and **von Papen**.

The big idea for a grade 9 answer: the Depression made millions of Germans desperate and willing to vote for extremists, but **votes alone did not make Hitler Chancellor**. In November 1932 his vote was actually falling. He was put into power by a small group of conservative politicians who thought they could use him.

### The Depression: why unemployment grew

The "Golden Years" had been built on **American loans** under the Dawes Plan (1924). Much of this money was lent **short-term**, meaning it could be demanded back at short notice. After the **Wall Street Crash of October 1929**, American banks were in trouble themselves and **recalled their loans**.

The causes of unemployment in Germany:
- **Loss of American money**: German banks and firms had borrowed heavily. When the loans were called in, companies could not pay wages or invest, and many went bankrupt.
- **Collapse of world trade**: as the world slumped, demand for German exports fell. Countries such as the USA raised tariffs, so German goods became harder to sell abroad. Industrial production fell by about **40%** between 1929 and 1932.
- **Banking crisis**: in **July 1931** the Danatbank, one of Germany's largest banks, collapsed and the government briefly closed the banks. People lost savings and firms lost credit.
- **Government policy made it worse**: Chancellor **Heinrich Brüning** (Centre Party, March 1930 – May 1932) feared inflation after 1923, so he **cut government spending, raised taxes, cut wages and salaries of public employees and reduced unemployment benefit**. This cut demand still further. He became known as the **"Hunger Chancellor"**.

**How fast it grew**: registered unemployment rose from about **1.3 million in September 1929** to around **3 million in 1930** and over **6 million by early 1932** — roughly one in three of the workforce. Millions more were on short time or not registered.

### The impact of unemployment

- **On workers**: unemployment insurance (set up in 1927) could not cope. Benefit was cut and paid for a limited period, after which people fell back on poor relief from their local council. Families could not pay rent; homeless people slept in parks or in shanty towns on the edge of cities; soup kitchens fed thousands.
- **On the young**: school-leavers found no jobs at all. Bored, angry young men were easy recruits for the SA and the KPD's fighting groups.
- **On the middle classes**: shopkeepers, small businessmen and professionals saw their incomes collapse. Having already lost savings in the **hyperinflation of 1923**, many felt the Republic had ruined them twice.
- **On farmers**: food prices had been low since the late 1920s; the slump pushed many into debt and bankruptcy.
- **On politics**: in March 1930 the "Grand Coalition" of Chancellor **Hermann Müller** (SPD) split over whether to raise contributions or cut unemployment benefit. After that, no government had a Reichstag majority. Brüning, Papen and Schleicher all governed through **Article 48** presidential decrees. The number of decrees rose sharply while the Reichstag met less and less — democracy was already being hollowed out before Hitler took power.

### The growth of support for the Communist Party (KPD)

The KPD, led by **Ernst Thälmann**, gained from the Depression too:

| Reichstag election | KPD seats |
|---|---|
| May 1928 | 54 |
| Sept 1930 | 77 |
| July 1932 | 89 |
| Nov 1932 | **100** (16.9%) |

- **Why it grew**: the KPD appealed above all to the **unemployed industrial workers** of the big cities. It promised that a Soviet-style revolution would end capitalism, which seemed to have failed. Many voters moved from the SPD, which was blamed for supporting Brüning's cuts.
- **Why it mattered**: the growth of the KPD **frightened** the middle classes, farmers, industrialists, landowners and the army, who remembered the Spartacist Rising of 1919 and feared a revolution like Russia's in 1917. Many of them turned to the Nazis as the strongest anti-communist force. The Nazis in effect **used the KPD's growth** to win support.
- **A divided left**: on orders from Moscow, the KPD attacked the SPD as "social fascists" and refused to cooperate with it. The two workers' parties together won more votes than the Nazis in November 1932, but they never united against Hitler.

### Reasons for the growth in Nazi support

**Nazi Reichstag results**

| Election | Seats | Share of vote |
|---|---|---|
| May 1928 | 12 | 2.6% |
| Sept 1930 | 107 | 18.3% |
| July 1932 | **230** | **37.3%** |
| Nov 1932 | 196 | 33.1% |

**1. The appeal of Hitler.** Hitler was a gifted, passionate speaker who seemed to offer strong leadership at a time when Weimar politicians looked weak and divided. He presented himself as a man of the people who had served as an ordinary soldier in the war. In the 1932 campaigns he **flew from city to city** by aeroplane ("Hitler over Germany"), sometimes speaking in several places in one day — something no politician had done before. Many voters did not study the Nazi programme; they voted for Hitler.

**2. The appeal of Nazi ideas to different groups.** The Nazis were deliberately vague and promised something to almost everyone:
- **The unemployed**: "work and bread"; the Nazis promised jobs.
- **Farmers**: help for agriculture and praise for the peasant as the heart of the nation — Protestant rural areas in the north and east became Nazi strongholds.
- **The middle classes**: protection from communism and from big department stores (often Jewish-owned) that hurt small shops.
- **Big business**: an end to communism and strong trade unions. In January 1932 Hitler spoke to industrialists at the **Düsseldorf Industry Club**, and some businessmen gave money.
- **Nationalists**: overturning the **Treaty of Versailles**, restoring Germany's greatness and blaming the "November Criminals".
- **Scapegoats**: the Nazis blamed Germany's problems on the Jews, the communists and the Weimar politicians. This gave simple answers to complicated problems.
- The idea of a **Volksgemeinschaft** (people's community) promised to end class division and unite Germans. The Nazis were the first party to present themselves as a party of **all classes**, not of one class or one religion.

The Nazis did less well among industrial workers loyal to the SPD or KPD, and among Catholics loyal to the Centre Party — but they still won some votes from both.

**3. Propaganda.** From 1930 **Joseph Goebbels** ran Nazi propaganda. The Nazis used:
- Simple, repeated slogans and bold **posters** aimed at specific groups (for example workers, mothers, farmers).
- Mass **rallies** with flags, uniforms, music and torchlight, designed to look powerful and disciplined.
- Modern technology: **loudspeakers**, **films**, gramophone records and aeroplanes.
- **Newspapers**: the party's own paper, the *Völkischer Beobachter*, and — importantly — publicity in the newspapers owned by the DNVP leader **Alfred Hugenberg**. In 1929 Hitler had joined Hugenberg in a campaign against the **Young Plan**, which made the Nazis look respectable to conservatives and gave them national coverage.
- Propaganda was organised centrally but adapted locally: speakers were trained and sent out to small towns and villages that other parties ignored.

**4. The work of the SA.** By 1932 the SA had several hundred thousand members (around **400,000**).
- **Rallies and marches**: the SA made the Nazis look organised, disciplined and strong — the opposite of the squabbling Reichstag.
- **Violence**: the SA broke up opponents' meetings and fought communists in the streets. In **1932** there were hundreds of political killings; on **17 July 1932** ("Altona Bloody Sunday") a Nazi march through a communist district of Hamburg-Altona left 18 dead. Many voters blamed the communists and believed the Nazis would restore order.
- **Support for the unemployed**: the SA ran soup kitchens and hostels and gave jobless young men a uniform, food and a sense of purpose.

**5. The failures of Weimar.** The other parties seemed to have no answer to the Depression; government by decree made the Reichstag look irrelevant; and memories of 1923 made many Germans lose faith in democracy altogether.

### The elections of 1932

**The presidential election (March–April 1932).** Hindenburg's seven-year term ended in 1932. Hitler, who became a German citizen only in February 1932, stood against him.

| Candidate | First round (13 Mar) | Second round (10 Apr) |
|---|---|---|
| **Hindenburg** (backed by the SPD, Centre and moderates) | 49.6% | **53.0%** |
| **Hitler** (Nazi) | 30.1% | **36.8%** |
| **Thälmann** (KPD) | 13.2% | 10.2% |

Hitler lost, but the result made him a national figure: over **13 million** people voted for him in the second round. Ironically, Hindenburg — a conservative monarchist — was re-elected by the votes of the SPD and Centre, the parties of the Republic.

**The Reichstag elections of July and November 1932.** In **July** the Nazis won **230 seats** and became the largest party, but not a majority. Hitler demanded to be Chancellor; on **13 August 1932** Hindenburg refused, offering only the post of Vice-Chancellor, which Hitler rejected. Papen's government lost a vote of no confidence (512 to 42) in September, so another election followed. In **November** the Nazi vote **fell** to 196 seats — they lost around **2 million votes**. The party was short of money, some voters were put off by SA violence and by Hitler's refusal to join a government, and the economy was beginning very slightly to improve. Goebbels privately worried that the movement's moment might be passing.

### How Hitler became Chancellor, 1932–33

The key point: Hitler did not seize power. He was **appointed**, legally, by President Hindenburg, after a series of backstairs deals among a small group of conservative politicians.

- **Heinrich Brüning** (Chancellor 1930–May 1932) lost Hindenburg's support after he banned the SA and proposed to break up bankrupt estates in the east for settlers, which angered landowners.
- **Franz von Papen** (Chancellor June–November 1932) was an aristocratic Centre Party politician and friend of Hindenburg. He led a "cabinet of barons" with almost no support in the Reichstag. He lifted the SA ban to win Nazi toleration, but Hitler would not support him without the chancellorship.
- **Kurt von Schleicher** (Chancellor December 1932 – January 1933), a general and political schemer, had helped to remove both Brüning and Papen. He tried to split the Nazis by offering the Vice-Chancellorship to **Gregor Strasser**, but Hitler kept control and Strasser resigned his party posts in December. Schleicher's plans for public works and land settlement alarmed industrialists and landowners.
- **The Papen–Hitler deal**: Papen, eager for revenge on Schleicher, met Hitler at the Cologne home of the banker **Kurt von Schröder** on **4 January 1933**. They agreed that Hitler would be Chancellor and Papen Vice-Chancellor, in a cabinet dominated by conservatives. Papen then worked on Hindenburg, helped by the President's son **Oskar von Hindenburg** and his state secretary **Otto Meissner**.
- **Schleicher's fall**: Schleicher asked Hindenburg for emergency powers to dissolve the Reichstag and postpone new elections. Hindenburg refused, and Schleicher resigned on **28 January 1933**.

**Why Hindenburg agreed**, though he personally despised Hitler and had refused him in August:
- The Nazis were still the **largest party** (196 seats), so a Hitler government could claim a parliamentary base that Papen's and Schleicher's never had.
- Papen persuaded him that Hitler could be **controlled**: only **three of the cabinet's members** would be Nazis (Hitler, **Frick** and **Göring**), and Papen as Vice-Chancellor would keep Hitler "boxed in".
- **Fear of communism** and of civil war: the KPD had reached 100 seats, and a war game run by the army in late 1932 had suggested the army might not cope with a Nazi and a communist uprising at the same time.
- **Pressure from the elites**: in **November 1932** a group of industrialists, bankers and landowners had petitioned Hindenburg to appoint Hitler; the landowners' organisations were furious with Schleicher.
- **No alternative**: two conservative chancellors had failed and Hindenburg, aged 85, did not want to rule by decree indefinitely or use the army against the people.

### The judgement a grade 9 answer needs

- The **Depression** was the essential background: without it the Nazis would have stayed a fringe party. It explains the **votes**.
- The **appeal of Hitler, propaganda and the SA** explain why angry voters chose the Nazis rather than another party.
- But the **backstairs intrigue** of Papen, Schleicher and Hindenburg explains the **appointment**. When Hitler became Chancellor, his vote was falling. Historians such as Alan Bullock have stressed that Hitler was brought into office by intrigue rather than carried in by a popular majority — no Nazi ever won more than 37.3% in a free election.
- The best answers link the factors: the Depression → votes for the Nazis and the KPD → fear of communism among the elites → elites turn to Hitler as a mass-supported anti-communist they thought they could control.

::: terms
- **Wall Street Crash**: the collapse of share prices on the New York stock exchange in October 1929, which led American banks to recall their loans to Germany.
- **Depression**: the world economic slump of 1929–33, which in Germany brought mass unemployment of over 6 million by early 1932.
- **Hunger Chancellor**: the nickname of Heinrich Brüning, whose spending cuts and benefit reductions (1930–32) deepened the suffering.
- **Article 48**: the emergency clause of the Weimar Constitution letting the President rule by decree; used constantly by Brüning, Papen and Schleicher.
- **KPD**: the German Communist Party, led by Ernst Thälmann, which rose to 100 Reichstag seats in November 1932.
- **Volksgemeinschaft**: the "people's community" — the Nazi promise of a united Germany without class divisions.
- **SA**: the Nazi stormtroopers, who marched, protected Nazi meetings, fought communists and ran soup kitchens for the unemployed.
- **Backstairs intrigue**: the private deals among Papen, Schleicher, Oskar von Hindenburg and others that led to Hitler's appointment in January 1933.
- **Cabinet of barons**: Papen's government of 1932, made up mainly of aristocrats with almost no support in the Reichstag.
:::

::: example Q2 — Explain why Nazi support grew
Explain why support for the Nazi Party grew in the years 1929–32. You may use the following in your answer: unemployment; propaganda. You must also use information of your own. (12)

The main reason Nazi support grew was the unemployment caused by the Depression. After the Wall Street Crash of October 1929, American banks recalled their short-term loans to Germany, firms went bankrupt and registered unemployment rose from about 1.3 million in September 1929 to over 6 million by early 1932. Brüning's government made things worse by cutting benefits and wages, earning him the name "Hunger Chancellor". Desperate people lost faith in the Weimar parties and turned to extremists who promised radical solutions. This is why the Nazi vote rose from 2.6% in 1928 to 18.3% in September 1930 and 37.3% in July 1932.

A second reason is that the Nazis' propaganda, run by Joseph Goebbels from 1930, was far more effective than that of other parties. It used simple slogans such as "work and bread", posters aimed at particular groups such as farmers and the unemployed, mass rallies and modern technology — loudspeakers, films and, in 1932, Hitler flying from city to city. Propaganda promised something to everyone and blamed Germany's problems on the Jews, communists and the "November Criminals". This mattered because it turned general anger into Nazi votes, including among Protestant farmers and the middle classes, and presented Hitler as the strong leader Germany needed.

A third reason was fear of communism, which the SA exploited. The KPD's support also grew with unemployment, from 54 seats in 1928 to 89 in July 1932. Middle-class voters, farmers and businessmen feared a Soviet-style revolution. The SA, around 400,000 strong by 1932, fought communists in the streets and marched in disciplined columns, so the Nazis looked like the strongest defence against revolution. Some businessmen gave money after Hitler spoke to the Düsseldorf Industry Club in January 1932. Overall, the Depression created the desperation, but it was the Nazis' propaganda and their image as the party of order against communism that ensured it was the Nazis, not another party, who gained.

::: example Q3(d) — how far do you agree
Interpretation 1 (a summary of the view of historians who stress popular support): Hitler came to power because millions of Germans, ruined by the Depression, voted for him; by 1932 the Nazis were the largest party and could not be kept out of government. Interpretation 2 (a summary of the view of historians such as Alan Bullock): Hitler did not win power but was given it, through backstairs intrigue by a small group of conservative politicians around Hindenburg who wrongly believed they could control him. (16)

I largely agree with Interpretation 2, because the final decision was made by Hindenburg and Papen at a time when Nazi support was falling; however, Interpretation 1 explains why the conservatives needed Hitler in the first place.

Interpretation 2 is supported by the events of 1932–33. In August 1932, when the Nazis had 230 seats, Hindenburg refused to make Hitler Chancellor and offered him only the Vice-Chancellorship, which shows that votes alone were not enough. Overall I agree with Interpretation 2 more, because it explains the timing and the manner of Hitler's appointment: he came to power when his vote was falling, and only because Hindenburg changed his mind.
