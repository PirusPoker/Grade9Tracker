## What it is

The period c1500–c1700 is called the **Medical Renaissance**. "Renaissance" means rebirth: scholars rediscovered original Greek texts, questioned inherited authority and began to trust **observation and experiment** over old books. In medicine this produced real change in **knowledge** — anatomy (Vesalius), physiology (Harvey, in 11.2b) and diagnosis (Sydenham) — and in how ideas **spread** (the printing press and the Royal Society). But the key exam point is the gap between knowledge and practice: **ideas about the cause of disease changed only a little**, and most treatment stayed the same.

### Continuity and change in explanations of disease

| | Continuity | Change |
|---|---|---|
| **Religious ideas** | Many still believed God sent disease — the Great Plague of 1665 was widely seen as punishment | The Church's power weakened after the **Reformation** (Henry VIII broke with Rome in the 1530s and dissolved the monasteries 1536–41); religious explanations were used less by physicians |
| **Astrology** | Still used by some physicians and in popular almanacs | Declining among educated doctors by the late 1600s |
| **Four Humours** | Still the main explanation for most doctors and patients; treatments still based on it | Challenged by some — e.g. **Paracelsus** (a Swiss doctor, d.1541) who publicly burned Galen's books and argued for chemical causes and cures |
| **Miasma** | Still widely believed — fires and herbs in 1665 | — |
| **New ideas** | — | Sydenham's view that diseases are **separate things** with their own patterns, not just a general imbalance in one patient; microscopes revealed tiny organisms (not yet linked to disease) |

So by 1700 educated people were more willing to look for **natural** causes and to test ideas by observation, but the true cause of infectious disease — germs — was still unknown.

### A scientific approach: Thomas Sydenham (1624–89)

Sydenham was a London physician (he had fought for Parliament in the Civil War), often called **"the English Hippocrates"** because, like Hippocrates, he stressed **observation at the bedside**.

- He argued doctors should learn from **observing patients' symptoms** closely, not from reading old books.
- He believed each disease was a **separate entity** with its own pattern of symptoms, which could be observed, described and **classified**, like plants. This was a major change from the Humours, where illness was a personal imbalance unique to each patient.
- He recorded the course of diseases carefully and **distinguished between illnesses** that had been confused — for example, he described scarlet fever as a disease separate from measles.
- He published *Observationes Medicae* (1676), which became a standard text for doctors.
- He used some new remedies, such as **cinchona bark** (containing quinine) from South America for malaria-type fevers ("agues"), and laudanum (opium) for pain.

**Limits:** Sydenham still used bloodletting and some humoral treatments; he did not know what *caused* diseases; and many physicians preferred the prestige of book learning. His influence was on **diagnosis and method** — the idea that the patient, not the book, is the evidence — which later medicine built on.

### The printing press and the transmission of ideas

Johannes Gutenberg developed printing with moveable type in Germany in the 1440s; **William Caxton** set up the first press in England at Westminster in **1476**.

- **Speed and quantity**: hundreds of identical copies instead of one hand-copied manuscript; new ideas could reach doctors across Europe within months.
- **Accuracy**: every copy of a detailed anatomical drawing, like Vesalius's, was the same — copying errors disappeared.
- **Loss of Church control**: monks no longer controlled which books were copied, so ideas that challenged Galen could circulate.
- **Books in English**: medical books were printed in English, not just Latin. **Nicholas Culpeper** translated the Royal College of Physicians' official list of remedies into English (1649), angering physicians, and his *Complete Herbal* (1653) let ordinary people treat themselves.
- **Debate**: doctors could read and respond to each other's work, building knowledge cumulatively.

### The Royal Society

The **Royal Society** was founded in **1660** and received a royal charter from **Charles II** in **1662**. It brought together scientists to share experiments and ideas; its motto *Nullius in verba* means roughly "take nobody's word for it" — test it yourself.

- It published a journal, ***Philosophical Transactions***, from **1665**, spreading scientific findings across Britain and Europe.
- Members included Robert Boyle (chemistry) and **Robert Hooke**, whose *Micrographia* (1665) showed detailed drawings of things seen through a microscope and first used the word "cell".
- It published the letters of the Dutchman **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek**, who from the 1670s described tiny "animalcules" (micro-organisms) seen through his microscopes — though no one linked them to disease for almost 200 years.
- Royal backing gave science **status**; Charles II himself was interested in experiments.

**Significance:** the Royal Society encouraged a **scientific method** — observation, experiment, publishing and peer discussion — and made it respectable. But it had little direct effect on treatment in the 1600s.

### Improvements in medical training

- The **Royal College of Physicians** (founded 1518) licensed physicians in London and set standards.
- In **1540** the barbers and surgeons were united as the **Company of Barber-Surgeons** and were granted the bodies of **four executed criminals a year** to dissect; in **1565** Elizabeth I gave the Royal College of Physicians the same right, and it began anatomy lectures.
- Many English physicians studied at **Padua** (Italy), and later Leiden, where dissection was central — including **John Caius**, who had lodged with Vesalius at Padua and later lectured on anatomy in London; and **William Harvey**.
- **Continuity:** Oxford and Cambridge still taught mainly through Galen's texts; practical dissection was limited by the number of bodies; training was still more about reading than doing.

### Andreas Vesalius and his influence in England

**Vesalius** (1514–64) was born in Brussels and became professor of surgery and anatomy at the University of **Padua** in Italy, where human dissection was allowed.

- Unlike lecturers who read Galen while an assistant cut, he **dissected human bodies himself**, and taught students to trust what they saw.
- In **1543** he published ***On the Fabric of the Human Body*** (*De Humani Corporis Fabrica*), with detailed, accurate illustrations produced by skilled artists and printed in large numbers.
- He showed Galen had made mistakes — Galen's anatomy was largely based on animals. For example, the **human jawbone is one bone, not two**; the breastbone has three parts, not seven; and (in his 1555 second edition) he doubted that blood passed through holes in the **septum** of the heart.
- He was criticised by followers of Galen, some of whom claimed the human body must have changed since Galen's time.

**Influence in England:**
- Printing spread the *Fabrica* rapidly; in **1545** Thomas Geminus published a copied version of its illustrations in England, and an English-language edition followed.
- English doctors trained at Padua brought his methods home (Caius, Harvey), and dissection became a larger part of training.
- He showed that **Galen could be wrong**, encouraging others to investigate — Harvey built directly on this.

**Limits:** Vesalius improved knowledge of **anatomy**, not of the causes of disease or its treatment. Patients in 1600 were treated in much the same way as in 1500.

### Factors in the Medical Renaissance

| Factor | Role |
|---|---|
| **Individuals** | Vesalius, Sydenham and Harvey challenged old ideas through observation |
| **Institutions** | Weaker Church after the Reformation; Royal Society and Royal College of Physicians; royal patronage from Charles II |
| **Science and technology** | Printing press; microscopes; better artistic techniques for illustration |
| **Attitudes in society** | Renaissance questioning of authority and interest in the human body; but most doctors and patients stayed conservative, and the Humours survived |

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1476 | Caxton's printing press at Westminster |
| 1518 | Royal College of Physicians founded |
| 1536–41 | Dissolution of the monasteries |
| 1540 | Company of Barber-Surgeons formed; four bodies a year for dissection |
| 1543 | Vesalius publishes *On the Fabric of the Human Body* |
| 1565 | Royal College of Physicians granted bodies for dissection |
| 1628 | Harvey publishes his work on the circulation of the blood |
| 1660 / 1662 | Royal Society founded / royal charter |
| 1665 | *Philosophical Transactions* first published; Hooke's *Micrographia* |
| 1676 | Sydenham's *Observationes Medicae* |

::: terms
- **Medical Renaissance**: the period c1500–c1700 when renewed interest in learning and observation led to new discoveries about the body.
- **Anatomy**: the study of the structure of the body — its bones, organs and muscles.
- **Dissection**: cutting open a dead body to study its structure.
- **Royal Society**: a scientific society founded in 1660, chartered by Charles II in 1662, which shared and published experiments.
- **Printing press**: a machine using moveable type to make many identical copies of a text, brought to England by Caxton in 1476.
- **Reformation**: the split from the Catholic Church in the 1530s, which weakened the Church's control over learning in England.
- **Diagnosis**: identifying what illness a patient has from their symptoms.
- **Observation**: learning by carefully watching and recording what happens, rather than relying on books.
:::

::: example 4-mark difference
Explain one way in which the spread of medical ideas in the seventeenth century was different from the medieval period. (4)

In the medieval period medical ideas spread slowly through manuscripts hand-copied by monks, so the Church controlled which books survived and errors crept in with each copy — which helped keep Galen's ideas unchallenged. By the seventeenth century printed books spread identical copies quickly, and after 1665 the Royal Society published its journal *Philosophical Transactions*, so new findings such as Hooke's microscope drawings in *Micrographia* could be shared and debated by scientists across Europe without Church control.
:::

::: example 12-mark explain why
Explain why there was progress in medical knowledge in the period c1500–c1700. (12)
You may use: the printing press; Vesalius. You must also use information of your own.

One reason is the work of individuals like **Vesalius**. By dissecting human bodies himself at Padua, he found that Galen was wrong about human anatomy — for example the jawbone is one bone, not two — and published his findings in *On the Fabric of the Human Body* in 1543. This showed that ancient authority could be challenged by observation, which encouraged later doctors such as Harvey.

A second reason is the **printing press**, set up in England by Caxton in 1476. It meant Vesalius's detailed illustrations could be reproduced accurately in large numbers; by 1545 a copied version was published in England. Ideas that once took generations to spread now reached doctors in years, and the Church could no longer control which books were copied.

A third reason, of my own, is the **Royal Society**, founded in 1660 and chartered by Charles II in 1662. Its motto urged members to take nobody's word for it, and its journal from 1665 spread experimental results, such as Leeuwenhoek's observations of micro-organisms. Royal backing made science respectable.

These factors worked together: individuals made the discoveries, technology spread them, and new institutions — alongside a Church weakened by the Reformation — gave them a platform. Without printing, Vesalius's findings might have stayed in Padua.
:::

::: example 16-mark judgement (conclusion)
"The work of Vesalius was the most important development in medicine in the period c1500–c1700." How far do you agree? (16 + 4 SPaG)
You may use: dissection; the Royal Society. You must also use information of your own.

**Conclusion.** Vesalius was important because he was the first to show, in print, that Galen was wrong, and he made dissection central to training — without that, Harvey's discovery is hard to imagine. But judged by the criterion of **effect on ordinary patients**, neither Vesalius nor any Renaissance figure changed much: bloodletting, purging and herbal remedies carried on. Judged by **long-term change in method**, the printing press was more important, because it spread Vesalius's work and every later discovery. So I only partly agree: Vesalius was the most important *discovery* in anatomy, but the printing press was the most important *development*, because it multiplied the effect of every individual.
