## What it is

Once Hitler had made himself Führer in August 1934, the Nazis needed to keep control of some 65 million Germans. They used two methods side by side: **fear** (the police state) and **persuasion** (propaganda and control of culture). They also tried to control the one set of institutions that still commanded millions of people's loyalty: the **Churches**. The spec covers:
1. **The police state**: the role of the **Gestapo**, the **SS** and **concentration camps**; Nazi control of the **legal system**.
2. **Controlling and influencing attitudes**: **Goebbels** and the Ministry of Propaganda — censorship, Nazi use of the media, rallies and sport, including the **Berlin Olympics (1936)**; Nazi control of **culture and the arts** — art, architecture, literature and film.
3. **The Churches**: Nazi attempts to control the Catholic and Protestant Churches — the **Concordat** and the **Reich Church**.

### The police state

A **police state** is one where the government uses the police (often secret police) to control people's lives, and where the police can arrest and imprison people without being checked by the courts.

**The SS (Schutzstaffel — "protection squad")**
- Set up in 1925 as Hitler's bodyguard; from **1929** led by **Heinrich Himmler**. Members wore black uniforms and had to prove their "racial purity".
- It grew from a few hundred men to hundreds of thousands. After the **Night of the Long Knives (1934)** it became independent of the SA and became the main instrument of terror.
- The SS could **arrest and search** without warrants and send people to concentration camps. It ran the camps (through the **Death's Head Units**) and, through the **SD** (security service) under **Reinhard Heydrich**, spied on opponents and on Nazis alike.
- In **1936** Himmler was made **Chief of the German Police**, bringing the SS and all the police under one man.

**The Gestapo (secret state police)**
- Set up by **Göring** in Prussia in **April 1933**; Himmler took it over in **1934**, with Heydrich running it day to day. Legally it was part of the state, but in practice it was controlled by the SS.
- It could hold people in **"protective custody"** — imprisonment without trial — and send them to concentration camps. Its methods included phone tapping, opening mail and brutal interrogation.
- It was actually **quite small** — often only a few dozen officers for a large city. It relied heavily on **informers** and on **denunciations** by ordinary Germans: neighbours, colleagues and even family members reporting "suspicious" remarks, sometimes out of personal grudges. **Block wardens** (Blockleiter), each responsible for a block of households, watched their neighbourhood.
- The belief that the Gestapo was everywhere was more important than its real size: **fear** stopped people speaking out.

**Concentration camps**
- The first camps were set up in 1933 in empty factories and warehouses ("wild camps" run by the SA). **Dachau**, near Munich, opened in **March 1933** and became the model SS camp; others followed, including **Sachsenhausen (1936)**, **Buchenwald (1937)**, **Mauthausen (1938)** and **Ravensbrück (1939)**, a camp for women.
- At first the inmates were mainly **political opponents** — communists, socialists and trade unionists. Later, other groups were sent: "asocials" such as beggars and alcoholics, homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses, "work-shy" people, and, after **Kristallnacht (November 1938)**, around 30,000 Jewish men.
- Conditions were harsh: hard labour, poor food, beatings and executions. Prisoners wore coloured badges to show their "category".
- The camps were **publicised**, not hidden: newspapers reported that opponents were being "re-educated", which deterred others. They were **not** yet the extermination camps of the war years.

**Nazi control of the legal system**
- **Judges**: they had to belong to the **National Socialist League for the Maintenance of Law**. Judges who did not follow Nazi ideas could be dismissed, and Jewish judges and lawyers were removed from 1933. Judges were told to judge according to the will of the Führer and "healthy popular feeling", not just the letter of the law.
- **Special Courts** (from **March 1933**) tried political cases without a jury; defendants had few rights and could not appeal.
- **The People's Court** (set up in **April 1934**) tried cases of treason. It was created after the acquittal of four of the communists accused of the Reichstag Fire angered Hitler. Its judges were chosen for loyalty to the Nazis, and trials were often held in secret. By the war it was sentencing large numbers of people to death.
- **Harsher laws**: the number of offences carrying the **death penalty** rose from **3 in 1933 to 46 by 1943**. Telling anti-Nazi jokes or listening to foreign radio could be a crime.
- **Above the law**: the Gestapo could re-arrest people as soon as a court released them and send them to a camp. The legal system could no longer protect anyone from the state.

### Goebbels and the Ministry of Propaganda

**Joseph Goebbels** had run the party's propaganda since 1930. In **March 1933** he became head of the new **Ministry of Popular Enlightenment and Propaganda**. His aims were to make Germans believe in Nazi ideas and in Hitler, and to make sure no other ideas could be heard.

**Censorship**
- **Books**: on **10 May 1933** students and SA men burned tens of thousands of books by Jewish, communist and other "un-German" authors in Berlin's Opernplatz and in other cities. Libraries were purged; books had to be approved before publication.
- **Newspapers**: communist and socialist papers were closed in 1933; Jewish editors were removed. The **Editors Law (October 1933)** made editors responsible for publishing nothing harmful to the state. Goebbels held daily press briefings telling papers what to print. Hundreds of newspapers closed, and the rest printed much the same news.
- **The Reich Chamber of Culture** (September 1933): every writer, artist, musician, actor and journalist had to be a member. Jews and political opponents were excluded — so they could not work.

**Nazi use of the media**
- **Radio**: the Nazis took control of all radio stations. Cheap **"People's Receivers"** (Volksempfänger) were mass-produced so that most families could afford one; by 1939 about **70%** of households had a radio. The sets had a short range, so it was hard to pick up foreign stations. Loudspeakers were set up in streets, cafés and factories so that Hitler's speeches could be heard by everyone.
- **Film**: all film scripts had to be approved by Goebbels. Newsreels showing Nazi achievements were shown before every film. Most feature films were light entertainment carrying subtle Nazi messages; some were openly political, such as *Hitlerjunge Quex* (1933), about a Hitler Youth "martyr" killed by communists.
- **Posters** were everywhere, with simple images: the strong Aryan family, the heroic worker and soldier, Hitler as the nation's saviour, and anti-Jewish images.

**Rallies**
- The annual **Nuremberg Rally**, held each September, brought hundreds of thousands of Nazi members, the SA, SS, Hitler Youth and the army together for parades, speeches and displays. The architect **Albert Speer** designed huge rally grounds and a "**cathedral of light**" made from well over a hundred anti-aircraft searchlights pointing into the night sky.
- **Leni Riefenstahl**'s film **Triumph of the Will (1935)**, about the 1934 rally, spread the impression of a united, powerful nation around Germany.
- Rallies made individuals feel part of a mighty movement and showed Germany's strength to foreigners.

**Sport and the Berlin Olympics, 1936**
- The Nazis used sport to promote physical fitness, discipline and national pride. Jewish athletes were excluded from German sports clubs.
- **Berlin hosted the Olympics in August 1936**. Goebbels used the Games as a showcase for the "new Germany": a huge new **Olympic stadium** was built; the Games were broadcast on radio and filmed by Riefenstahl (**Olympia**, 1938); and the **torch relay** from Greece was used for the first time.
- **Germany won the most medals** (33 gold), which the Nazis claimed proved Aryan superiority.
- To impress foreign visitors, **anti-Jewish signs were temporarily removed** and one athlete with a Jewish father, the fencer Helene Mayer, was included in the German team.
- **Limits**: the black American athlete **Jesse Owens** won **four gold medals** (100m, 200m, long jump and 4x100m relay), undermining Nazi racial theories. Still, most foreign visitors went home impressed by Germany's efficiency.

### Nazi control of culture and the arts

The Nazis wanted culture to express **Nazi values**: the heroic German people, the family, the soldier, the peasant and the land ("blood and soil"). Modern, experimental and "Jewish" culture was attacked as **degenerate** (Entartete Kunst).

- **Art**: modern art — Expressionism, abstract art, the **Bauhaus** school (closed in 1933) — was banned. Paintings were removed from galleries. In **July 1937** the **Degenerate Art exhibition** in Munich mocked modern works; at the same time the **Great German Art Exhibition** opened in the new **House of German Art** to show approved art — realistic, heroic images of workers, farmers, mothers and soldiers.
- **Architecture**: Hitler, who had wanted to be an architect, favoured **monumental** buildings in a stripped classical style — huge, solid and imposing, to show the power of the state and to last a thousand years. **Albert Speer** designed the Nuremberg rally grounds and the new **Reich Chancellery** in Berlin (1939), and planned to rebuild Berlin as "Germania". Homes and youth hostels were often built in a traditional German rural style.
- **Literature**: books by Jewish, communist and liberal writers were burned or banned; many leading writers, such as **Thomas Mann** and **Bertolt Brecht**, left Germany. Approved books praised the war, the peasant and the Volk; **Mein Kampf** sold millions of copies.
- **Film**: Goebbels controlled the film industry and approved every script (see above). He understood that entertainment which people enjoyed was often better propaganda than open lectures.
- **Music**: works by Jewish composers such as Mendelssohn were banned; **jazz** was condemned as "negro music". Traditional German composers such as **Wagner**, Beethoven and Bach were promoted.

### The Churches: the Concordat and the Reich Church

Germany was a Christian country: roughly **two-thirds** of Germans were Protestants and about **one-third** Catholics. Nazi ideas — racism, glorification of violence, loyalty to the Führer above God — clashed with Christian teaching, but Hitler was careful: attacking the Churches head-on could turn millions against him.

**The Catholic Church and the Concordat**
- Catholics were loyal to the **Pope** in Rome, had their own schools and youth groups, and had supported the Centre Party.
- On **20 July 1933** Hitler signed a **Concordat** (agreement) with **Pope Pius XI**. Hitler promised not to interfere with the Catholic Church, its schools and its youth organisations; in return the Church agreed to **stay out of politics**, and bishops swore loyalty to the state. The Centre Party had already dissolved itself.
- For Hitler, it gave the regime international respectability and removed the Catholic political threat.
- **Hitler soon broke the Concordat**: Catholic priests were harassed and arrested, and many were sent to concentration camps; Catholic schools were brought into line and replaced by state schools, and crucifixes were removed from some; Catholic youth groups were restricted and finally dissolved; Catholic newspapers were censored.
- In **March 1937** Pius XI issued the letter **"With Burning Concern"** (Mit brennender Sorge), smuggled into Germany and read out in Catholic churches, criticising the Nazis for breaking the Concordat and for their racial ideas. The Nazis responded with more arrests and closures.

**The Protestant Churches and the Reich Church**
- Protestants were divided into 28 regional churches. Some Protestants, the **German Christians**, supported the Nazis and wore Nazi uniforms; they wanted to remove Jewish influences from Christianity.
- In **1933** the Nazis united the Protestant churches into a single **Reich Church** under a pro-Nazi **Reich Bishop, Ludwig Müller** (September 1933). The idea was to bring Protestantism under state control (part of Gleichschaltung).
- Many Protestants objected, especially when pastors of Jewish descent were to be removed. **Martin Niemöller** set up the **Pastors' Emergency League** (1933), and in **1934** opponents formed the **Confessing Church**, which declared (in the Barmen Declaration) that the Church was answerable to God, not to the state.
- The Reich Church **failed** to win over most Protestants. In 1935 the Nazis created a **Ministry for Church Affairs** under Hanns Kerrl to bring the churches into line. Leaders of the Confessing Church were watched and arrested: **Niemöller was arrested in 1937** and spent the rest of the Nazi period in concentration camps (see 31.3c).

### The judgement a grade 9 answer needs

- **Terror and propaganda worked together**: propaganda won genuine support or at least acceptance for most Germans; terror dealt with the minority who would not be persuaded. Fear of denunciation meant that people policed themselves.
- **Propaganda worked best where it built on existing beliefs** — nationalism, resentment of Versailles, anti-communism, the Hitler "cult" — and on real successes such as falling unemployment and the Olympics. It was less successful at changing deeply held beliefs, such as religion.
- **Control of the Churches was the least successful**: the Nazis could not replace Christianity, and the Churches remained the only large organisations outside Nazi control.
- Historians debate the balance: some (such as Robert Gellately) stress how much the police state depended on ordinary Germans' cooperation and consent; others (such as Richard Evans) stress that coercion and fear were far more widespread than that suggests.

::: terms
- **Police state**: a state in which the government uses the police, often secret police, to control people, with no protection from the courts.
- **SS**: the Schutzstaffel, Hitler's black-uniformed elite force led by Himmler, which ran the police and the concentration camps.
- **Gestapo**: the secret state police, set up in 1933 and controlled by the SS, which relied heavily on denunciations by ordinary Germans.
- **Protective custody**: imprisonment without trial, used by the Gestapo and SS to send people to concentration camps.
- **People's Court**: the court set up in 1934 to try treason cases, with judges chosen for loyalty to the Nazis.
- **Reich Chamber of Culture**: the body set up in 1933 that every artist, writer, musician and actor had to join in order to work.
- **Degenerate art**: the Nazi label for modern, experimental or "Jewish" art, mocked in a Munich exhibition in 1937.
- **Concordat**: the July 1933 agreement between Hitler and Pope Pius XI protecting the Catholic Church if it stayed out of politics.
- **Reich Church**: the single Protestant church created in 1933 under the pro-Nazi Reich Bishop Ludwig Müller.
- **Confessing Church**: the Protestant church formed in 1934 by pastors, including Niemöller, who rejected Nazi control of the Church.

::: example Q2 — Explain why the Nazis controlled attitudes
Explain why the Nazis were able to control the attitudes of the German people in the years 1933–39. You may use the following in your answer: the Ministry of Propaganda; the Gestapo. You must also use information of your own. (12)

One reason was the work of Goebbels and the Ministry of Popular Enlightenment and Propaganda, set up in March 1933. The Nazis controlled every source of information. Cheap People's Receivers meant that by 1939 about 70% of households had a radio, and loudspeakers in streets and factories carried Hitler's speeches everywhere. Every film was preceded by a newsreel, and huge events such as the Nuremberg rallies and the 1936 Berlin Olympics, when Germany won the most medals, created pride in the regime. Because Germans heard only one message, repeated constantly, many came to accept the Nazi view of the world.

A second reason was censorship, which ensured that no alternative ideas could be heard. On 10 May 1933 books by Jewish and left-wing authors were burned, and the Editors Law of October 1933 made editors responsible for printing nothing harmful to the state. Every writer, artist and musician had to belong to the Reich Chamber of Culture, from which Jews and opponents were excluded, and modern art was attacked as "degenerate" in the 1937 Munich exhibition. This meant that even people who disliked the Nazis had no other source of ideas and could not easily express different views.

A third reason was fear created by the police state. The Gestapo, under the control of Himmler's SS, could hold people in "protective custody" without trial and send them to camps such as Dachau. Although it was quite small, it relied on denunciations by neighbours and colleagues and on block wardens who watched every street, so people believed they could be reported for a careless remark. The legal system gave no protection: judges had to join the National Socialist League for the Maintenance of Law, and the People's Court dealt with treason. So while propaganda won over the majority, fear silenced those who were not persuaded — the combination is what gave the Nazis such control.

::: example Q3(d) — how far do you agree
Interpretation 1 (a summary of the view of historians such as Richard Evans): the Nazis controlled Germany above all by terror; the threat of the Gestapo, the camps and the courts hung over every German, and fear explains why so few resisted. Interpretation 2 (a summary of the view of historians such as Robert Gellately): the Nazi police state was small and depended on the cooperation of ordinary Germans, most of whom supported the regime; control rested mainly on consent. How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about how the Nazis controlled Germany in the years 1933–39? Explain your answer, using both interpretations and your knowledge of the historical context. (16)

I partly agree with Interpretation 2: for most Germans, control rested on consent and cooperation, but that consent existed within a system of terror that made open disagreement dangerous.

Interpretation 2 is supported by what we know of the Gestapo. It was quite small, with often only a few dozen officers for a large city, so it depended on information from ordinary people. Many investigations began with denunciations by neighbours, colleagues and relatives, sometimes for personal reasons. Block wardens were ordinary party members watching their own streets. There is also evidence of genuine support: plebiscites gave huge majorities, unemployment fell, and events such as the 1936 Olympics and the Nuremberg rallies were popular. Goebbels' propaganda, with 70% of households owning a radio by 1939, built on beliefs many Germans already held.

However, Interpretation 1 is convincing too. The Nazis used terror from the start: Dachau opened in March 1933, and communists, socialists and trade unionists were imprisoned without trial in "protective custody". The Gestapo, controlled by Himmler's SS, could re-arrest anyone a court released, and the number of crimes carrying the death penalty kept rising. People who denounced others did so in a climate where any "wrong" remark could lead to a camp, and apparent consent may have been fear. Even the Churches' leaders were not safe: Niemöller was arrested in 1937 and sent to a concentration camp.

Overall I agree with Interpretation 2 to a large extent for the majority of Germans, who were won over by propaganda and real successes and cooperated willingly. But I do not fully agree, because the consent was shaped by terror: the camps and the Gestapo removed the organised opposition in 1933–34, so later "consent" was given by a population that knew the price of refusing it.
