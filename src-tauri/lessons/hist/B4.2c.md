## What it is

In summer 1588 Philip II sent the **Spanish Armada** — the largest fleet yet seen in European waters — to invade England. It failed. This topic covers the **Spanish invasion plans**, the **key events** of the campaign, and the **reasons for the English victory**. Dates below are the English (Old Style) dates used in most English sources; Spain already used the new calendar, ten days ahead.

### Why Philip launched the Armada

By 1587 Philip had every reason to act: England had been at war with him since the **Treaty of Nonsuch (1585)**, English soldiers were fighting his army in the Netherlands, Drake had raided the Caribbean (1585–86) and **Cadiz (April 1587)**, and Elizabeth had executed the Catholic **Mary, Queen of Scots (February 1587)**. Philip believed it was his religious duty to restore Catholicism in England; **Pope Sixtus V** promised to help pay once Spanish troops landed. He called the plan the **Enterprise of England**.

### The Spanish invasion plan

- A fleet of about **130 ships** carrying roughly **30,000 men** — most of them **soldiers**, not sailors — would sail from Lisbon up the English Channel.
- It would meet the **Duke of Parma**, whose army of roughly **27,000 veteran troops** was waiting in the Spanish Netherlands.
- The Armada would protect Parma's army as it crossed the narrow sea in **flat-bottomed barges**, to land in **Kent** and march on **London**.
- English Catholics were expected to rise and support the invasion.

**Weaknesses in the plan:**
- **No deep-water port.** The Armada's large ships could not get close to the shallow Flemish coast, and Parma's barges could not sail out to meet it — they would be sunk by Dutch rebel "fly-boats" (small, fast warships in the shallows) that were blockading Parma's ports.
- **Communication.** Messages between Medina Sidonia and Parma took days, so the two forces could not coordinate a meeting.
- **Leadership.** The experienced commander, the **Marquis of Santa Cruz**, died in **February 1588**. His replacement, the **Duke of Medina Sidonia**, was a great nobleman and good administrator but had **no experience of commanding at sea** and asked to be excused.
- **Supplies.** After Drake destroyed the seasoned barrel staves in 1587, the Armada's casks were made of **unseasoned wood**; food rotted and water leaked.
- **Spanish tactics** relied on grappling and boarding enemy ships so that soldiers could fight hand to hand. Spanish guns sat on heavy two-wheeled carriages designed for land use, which made reloading at sea slow.

### Key events

| Date (English) | Event |
|---|---|
| May 1588 | The Armada leaves Lisbon; storms force it into Corunna for repairs and fresh supplies |
| 19 July | Armada sighted off **the Lizard**, Cornwall; beacons carry the news across England |
| 20 July | The English fleet under **Lord Howard of Effingham** (with **Drake** as vice-admiral) works its way out of Plymouth and gets **upwind** (to windward) of the Armada |
| 21 July onwards | Skirmish off Plymouth; the Armada keeps its defensive **crescent formation**. Drake captures the damaged *Nuestra Señora del Rosario* (breaking formation to do so) |
| 23 July | Fighting off **Portland Bill** |
| 25 July | Fighting off the **Isle of Wight**; the Armada is prevented from anchoring in the Solent |
| 27 July | The Armada anchors off **Calais** to wait for Parma — who is not ready |
| 28 July (night) | The English send **eight fireships** into the anchored Armada. Spanish captains cut their anchor cables in panic; the fleet scatters and the crescent is broken. The galleass *San Lorenzo* runs aground at Calais |
| 29 July | **Battle of Gravelines**: the English attack at close range for hours. Several Spanish ships are sunk or driven aground and many men killed; the English lose no ships |
| 30 July onwards | A south-westerly wind (the **"Protestant wind"**) drives the Armada into the North Sea; the English follow as far as the Firth of Forth, then turn back, short of ammunition |
| 9 August | Elizabeth addresses her troops at **Tilbury** |
| August–September | The Armada sails home round Scotland and Ireland; storms wreck many ships, some two dozen on the Irish coast |

**Outcome.** Only about **65 ships** of the Armada returned to Spain, and perhaps **15,000–20,000** Spanish sailors and soldiers died, most from shipwreck, disease and starvation rather than in battle. The English lost **no ships** and fewer than 100 men in combat — but thousands of English sailors died afterwards from diseases such as typhus and dysentery, many of them unpaid.

**Tilbury.** Elizabeth rode among the army gathered at Tilbury in Essex, where she is recorded as saying she had "the heart and stomach of a king, and of a king of England too". The speech became a key part of her image as a warrior queen, though by then the danger had largely passed.

### Reasons for the English victory

| Factor | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **English ships** | Under **John Hawkins**, Treasurer of the Navy from 1577, many royal ships were rebuilt or built as **"race-built" galleons**: lower, longer and narrower, without the tall castles at bow and stern. They were **faster and more manoeuvrable** and could sail closer to the wind. The English fleet grew to about **197 ships**, 34 of them royal warships. |
| **Guns and tactics** | English guns such as culverins had a **longer range**, and English gun carriages had **four small wheels**, so guns could be pulled back, reloaded and fired more quickly. The English kept their distance, refusing to be boarded, then closed in at Gravelines once the formation was broken. |
| **Fireships** | The fireships at Calais were the turning point: they broke the crescent formation that had protected the Armada all the way up the Channel. |
| **Leadership** | Howard, Drake, Hawkins and Frobisher were experienced seamen. Medina Sidonia kept his fleet together skilfully but had no experience of naval warfare, and his decision to anchor at the exposed roads of Calais gave the English their chance. |
| **Spanish plan and supplies** | The plan depended on meeting Parma without a deep-water port; Dutch fly-boats kept Parma's barges in harbour. Food and water spoiled in unseasoned casks. Both sides ran short of shot, but the English could resupply from their own coast. |
| **Weather and luck** | The wind after Gravelines drove the Armada north, making a return to meet Parma impossible, and autumn storms off Scotland and Ireland caused most of the losses. English propaganda called this God's judgement — the "Protestant wind". |

### Making a judgement

Most historians argue that the Armada was **defeated at Gravelines and broken by the fireships**, so English ships, guns and tactics were decisive in stopping the invasion — the Armada **never met Parma**. However, the **weather** did most of the actual destruction on the way home, and the **Spanish plan** was so flawed that it might have failed even without English skill. A strong answer weighs "what stopped the invasion" (ships, tactics, fireships, the flawed plan) against "what destroyed the fleet" (storms).

**Significance:** England's independence and its Protestant Church were secure; Elizabeth's prestige soared (the "Armada portrait"); but the war continued until 1604, and Spain rebuilt its fleet and sent further armadas in 1596 and 1597.

::: terms
- **Armada**: the Spanish word for a war fleet; the 1588 Armada was Philip II's fleet of about 130 ships sent to invade England.
- **Enterprise of England**: Philip II's name for his plan to invade England and restore Catholicism.
- **Crescent formation**: the defensive half-moon shape the Armada kept as it sailed up the Channel, with the strongest ships on the wings.
- **Fireship**: an old ship filled with flammable material, set alight and sent downwind into an enemy fleet at anchor; the English used eight at Calais.
- **Galleon**: a large sailing warship; the English "race-built" galleons were lower, longer and more manoeuvrable than Spanish ships.
- **Protestant wind**: the English name for the wind that drove the Armada into the North Sea, presented as a sign of God's favour.
- **Culverin**: a long-barrelled cannon with a long range, used widely on English ships.
- **Gravelines**: the battle off the Flemish coast on 29 July 1588 where the English attacked the scattered Armada at close range.
:::

::: method
This topic sits in **Paper 2, Booklet B: the British depth study (Early Elizabethan England)**. Three kinds of question.

**Q "Describe two features of…" (4 marks).** Two marks per feature: 1 for identifying a feature, 1 for a supporting detail. Two short paragraphs — "One feature was… / A second feature was…" — each with a precise fact (a date, a place, a number). No explanation needed.

**Q "Explain why…" (12 marks).** Two stimulus points are given. Use them if you like, but you must add **at least one reason of your own**: answers that stay within the stimulus points are capped at 8 marks. Three paragraphs, each a separate reason: **point → precise evidence → how it led to the outcome**.
- Level 1 (1–3): simple or general comments.
- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation, accurate but loosely organised or descriptive.
- Level 3 (7–9): explanation linked to the question with a mostly sustained line of reasoning.
- Level 4 (10–12): analytical explanation focused throughout on "why", logically structured, with precise knowledge and links between reasons (for example, showing how the fireships made Gravelines possible).

**Q "'Statement.' How far do you agree? Explain your answer." (16 marks + 4 SPaG).** Choose one of two. Two stimulus points; capped at 11 marks if you go no further than them.
- Paragraph 1: case for the named factor.
- Paragraphs 2–3: other factors, each fully explained.
- Conclusion: a clear judgement using a **criterion** — for Armada questions, distinguish **what stopped the invasion** from **what destroyed the fleet**, or ask **which factor the others depended on**.
- Level 1 (1–4), Level 2 (5–8), Level 3 (9–12: judgement given, partly justified), Level 4 (13–16: sustained analysis, justified judgement with criteria).
- SPaG (4 marks): accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar and precise terms (galleon, fireship, crescent formation, culverin).

Descriptive answers that retell the whole Channel campaign rarely rise above Level 2 — select events that prove your argument.
:::

::: example Explain why (12)
Explain why the Spanish Armada failed to invade England in 1588. (12)

You may use the following in your answer:
- the fireships at Calais
- the Duke of Parma

You must also use information of your own.

Model answer:

The Armada failed firstly because of the **plan to meet the Duke of Parma**. The whole invasion depended on the fleet protecting Parma's barges as they crossed to Kent, but there was no deep-water port on the Flemish coast where the large ships could meet them, and Dutch rebel fly-boats blockaded Parma's harbours. Messages between Medina Sidonia and Parma took days, so when the Armada anchored at Calais on 27 July Parma's army was not ready. This meant the Armada had to wait at an exposed anchorage, which gave the English their chance.

Secondly, the **fireships at Calais** broke the Armada's defence. Up the Channel the crescent formation had protected it, and English attacks had done little damage. On the night of 28 July eight fireships drifted into the anchored fleet; the Spanish cut their cables and scattered. As a result, the next day at Gravelines the English could attack isolated ships at close range, sinking or wrecking several and killing many men. After this the wind drove the Armada into the North Sea, so it could never return to meet Parma.

A third reason was **English ships and guns**. Hawkins, Treasurer of the Navy from 1577, had rebuilt many royal ships as race-built galleons that were faster and more manoeuvrable than the Spanish ships, and English guns on four-wheeled carriages could be reloaded more quickly. This allowed the English to avoid being boarded — the tactic Spain relied on — and to exploit the break-up of the formation at Gravelines. Without these ships the fireship attack could not have been followed up, so English naval strength turned the flaws of the Spanish plan into defeat.
:::

::: example How far do you agree (16)
"The main reason for the defeat of the Spanish Armada was the weather." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16 + 4 SPaG)

You may use: the "Protestant wind"; English ships. You must also use information of your own.

Model answer (outline of a Level 4 response):

**Agree.** The weather caused most of the Spanish losses. After Gravelines a south-westerly wind drove the Armada into the North Sea, so it could not turn back to meet Parma. On the voyage home round Scotland and Ireland, storms wrecked many ships — around two dozen on the Irish coast alone — and only about 65 ships returned to Spain. The English lost no ships; most Spanish deaths came from shipwreck, disease and hunger, not battle.

**Disagree — English ships and tactics.** Race-built galleons were faster and more manoeuvrable; English guns had a longer range and were reloaded faster on four-wheeled carriages. The fireships at Calais on 28 July broke the crescent formation and Gravelines was fought before the wind carried the Armada away.

**Disagree — the Spanish plan and leadership.** With no deep-water port and no reliable communication with Parma, the invasion was unlikely to succeed. Santa Cruz's death left the fleet to Medina Sidonia, who had no naval experience; supplies had been spoiled by the unseasoned casks made after Cadiz.

**Judgement.** I disagree that the weather was the main reason for the **defeat of the invasion**. The Armada had already failed in its purpose — it never met Parma — before the storms struck: the flawed plan put it at Calais, and the English fireships and Gravelines broke it. The weather turned a failed invasion into a disaster, so it explains the **scale of Spanish losses** but not the defeat itself.
:::

He had no experience of commanding at sea (1); he replaced the experienced Marquis of Santa Cruz, who died in February 1588, and he himself asked not to be given the command (1).
