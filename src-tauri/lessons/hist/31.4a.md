## What it is

The Nazis did not only want to control Germany — they wanted to remake German society as a **Volksgemeinschaft** (people's community): racially "pure", loyal to the Führer and ready for war. Women and young people were at the heart of this plan. Women were to produce and raise the next generation of Aryan Germans; the young were to be turned into that generation — fit, obedient and fanatically Nazi. This lesson covers Nazi views and policies on **women and the family**, the **Hitler Youth and the League of German Maidens**, and **Nazi control of education**.

### Nazi views on women and the family

Nazi ideas about women were **traditional and racial**. Hitler believed that men and women had separate "spheres": a man's world was the state, work and war; a woman's world was her husband, her family and her home. The slogan that summed this up was **"Kinder, Küche, Kirche"** — children, kitchen, church.

Why did the Nazis think this?
- **The falling birth rate**: in 1900 there had been over 2 million births a year in Germany; by 1933 there were fewer than 1 million (about 971,000). The Nazis believed a great nation needed a growing population — soldiers, workers and settlers for *Lebensraum* in the east.
- **Race**: women's highest duty was to have healthy, *Aryan* children. Motherhood was a patriotic service equal to a man fighting in battle.
- **Reaction against Weimar**: the Nazis disliked the "new woman" of the 1920s — educated, working, voting, wearing short hair and make-up, smoking and having fewer children. Under Weimar women had gained the vote (1918), dozens sat as Reichstag deputies and thousands worked as teachers, doctors and civil servants. The Nazis saw this as a sign of decadence.
- **Unemployment**: in 1933 there were about 6 million unemployed. Sending women home freed jobs for men.

### Policies on marriage and the family

- **The Law for the Encouragement of Marriage (June 1933)** introduced **marriage loans** of **1,000 Reichsmarks** (about nine months' average wages) to young couples, provided the wife **gave up her job**. For each child born, **a quarter of the loan was cancelled** — so four children meant the loan was paid off. This was nicknamed *abkindern* ("childing it off").
- **The Mother's Cross (Honour Cross of the German Mother)**, instituted in 1938: **bronze for 4–5 children, silver for 6–7, gold for 8 or more**. It was awarded on 12 August, the birthday of Hitler's mother. Holders were saluted by the Hitler Youth and given priority in queues.
- **Propaganda and welfare**: Mother's Day became a public celebration; family allowances and tax cuts were given to large families; the Nazi women's organisations ran courses in cookery, childcare and household management (the Reich Mothers' Service).
- **Lebensborn (1935)**: Himmler's SS organisation ran maternity homes where "racially valuable" women — some unmarried — could give birth, often to children fathered by SS men. It shows that race mattered more to the Nazis than traditional morality.
- **Divorce law (1938)**: a husband could divorce his wife if she was **infertile** or refused to have children; divorce was also made easier for "racial" reasons.
- **Contraception and abortion**: birth-control clinics were closed and abortion was punished harshly — for "Aryan" women. At the same time the regime **sterilised** women it judged "unfit" (see lesson 31.4c).

**Results**: marriages rose from about **517,000 in 1932 to about 740,000 in 1934**, and births rose from about **971,000 in 1933 to about 1.4 million in 1939**. But historians point out that the birth rate was already recovering as the economy improved, and the average family size did not return to the levels of the early 1900s — most couples still chose to have one or two children.

### Policies on employment

- **1933 onwards**: women doctors and civil servants were dismissed, especially married women whose husbands earned a salary ("double earners"). Women teachers were encouraged or pressured to leave.
- A limit was placed on women entering **university** (no more than 10% of new students).
- From **1936** women could not be **judges or lawyers**. There were no women deputies in the Nazi Reichstag.

**The U-turn**: by **1937** rearmament and full employment had created a **labour shortage**. The regime quietly reversed direction:
- In 1937 the marriage-loan rule changed so a wife could keep working.
- In **1938** the **Duty Year (Pflichtjahr)** required young women (under 25) to do a year of work on farms or in households before taking other jobs.
- The number of women in employment rose from about **4.85 million in 1933 to 7.14 million in 1939** — the opposite of what Nazi ideology wanted. Many were in low-paid jobs in factories and farms, while the higher-status professions stayed largely closed.

This contradiction — ideology versus the needs of the economy and rearmament — is a key point for a grade 9 answer.

### Policies on appearance

The ideal Nazi woman was blonde, sturdy and "natural": broad-hipped (for childbearing), hair in plaits or a bun, flat shoes, no make-up and plain clothes. Women were **discouraged** (not generally banned) from wearing make-up and trousers, dyeing their hair, slimming (thin women were thought to be less fertile) and **smoking** — smoking was banned in some public places and party offices for women. Nazi women's organisations under **Gertrud Scholtz-Klink**, such as the **National Socialist Womanhood (NSF)** and the **German Women's Enterprise (DFW)**, spread these ideals.

**How far did the policies work?** Many women welcomed the status given to motherhood, the loans and the welfare. Others — especially educated, professional women — lost careers and independence. And after 1936 the Nazis needed women in the factories, so the policies were never consistent.

### Nazi aims towards the young

Hitler said the future belonged to youth. The regime aimed to:
- make young people **loyal to Hitler** and the Nazi Party, above loyalty to parents or churches;
- make boys **physically fit, tough and ready to fight** as soldiers;
- make girls **healthy future mothers** of Aryan children;
- teach Nazi racial ideas and belief in the Volksgemeinschaft.

The two tools were the **youth movement** and the **schools**.

### The Hitler Youth and the League of German Maidens

The **Hitler Youth (Hitlerjugend, HJ)** was founded in 1926 but was small: about **108,000** members at the end of 1932. From **June 1933** it was led by **Baldur von Schirach**, Youth Leader of the German Reich. Other youth groups were closed down or absorbed (Protestant youth groups were merged in 1933; Catholic groups held out longest, protected at first by the Concordat).

| Age | Boys | Girls |
|---|---|---|
| 10–14 | German Young People (Deutsches Jungvolk) | League of Young Girls (Jungmädelbund) |
| 14–18 | Hitler Youth (Hitlerjugend) | **League of German Maidens (Bund Deutscher Mädel, BDM)** |

**Growth**: membership rose to about **2.3 million in 1933** and **5.4 million by the end of 1936**. The **Law on the Hitler Youth (December 1936)** banned all other youth organisations and made the HJ responsible for educating young people "physically, intellectually and morally". A further law in **March 1939** made membership **compulsory**; by 1939 over 7 million young people belonged.

**Boys' activities**: sport and athletics, hiking and camping, marching and drill, **military training** (map-reading, shooting with small-bore rifles, signalling), and evening meetings with Nazi ideology — the life of Hitler, the "betrayal" of Versailles, race and anti-Semitism. Boys competed for badges and faced tests of courage. Older members could join specialist units (motor, flying, naval).

**Girls' activities (BDM)**: sport and fitness, hiking and camping, but above all **domestic skills** — cooking, sewing, childcare and housework — and teaching on racial hygiene, such as how to choose a racially suitable husband. From 1938 **Faith and Beauty** was added for young women aged 17–21.

**Why did young people join?** Excitement, camps, uniforms and sport; a sense of belonging and status; the chance to escape parents; and increasingly because non-membership meant pressure at school and harmed job prospects. **Limits**: by the late 1930s many found the endless drill and ideological lectures boring; compulsory membership made it feel like a duty; some parents and churches disliked the loss of family control; and a minority rejected it altogether — the **Edelweiss Pirates** and **Swing Youth** (see the opposition lesson). Reports from HJ leaders themselves complained about poor attendance.

### Nazi control of education

**Teachers**
- The **Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service (April 1933)** removed Jewish teachers and those thought politically unreliable.
- Teachers had to join the **National Socialist Teachers' League (NSLB)**; by 1937 about **97%** of teachers were members.
- Teachers took an oath of loyalty to Hitler and attended **training courses** (often a month at camp) on Nazi ideology and physical education.
- Pupils in the Hitler Youth were encouraged to **report** teachers who did not follow the Nazi line, so teachers had to be careful what they said.

**The curriculum**
- **Physical education** was expanded to about **15%** of the timetable; boxing became compulsory for boys in upper schools, and pupils could fail school for poor fitness.
- **History** taught the "stab in the back", the injustice of Versailles, the heroism of the Nazi rise to power and the greatness of Germany.
- **Biology** taught **race science** — the superiority of the Aryan race, heredity and eugenics, and why "races" should not mix.
- **Geography** taught *Lebensraum* and the lands Germany had lost.
- **German** used Nazi texts; even **maths** problems carried propaganda (for example, sums about the cost of caring for disabled people or about bombing targets).
- **Religious education** was reduced and could be dropped; school prayers were removed.
- **Girls** were taught domestic science and needlework; girls' grammar schools did not offer Latin, which was needed for university entrance, so fewer girls could go on to higher education.
- Textbooks were rewritten and approved centrally; *Mein Kampf* was a set text. Lessons began and ended with the Hitler salute ("Heil Hitler").

**Elite schools**
- **Napolas** (National Political Institutes of Education), from 1933, trained future leaders with a military-style education.
- **Adolf Hitler Schools** (from 1937), run by the Hitler Youth, took boys from age 12 for leadership training.
- **Order Castles** (Ordensburgen) trained the Party's future elite.

**Effects**: academic standards fell as time was taken from academic subjects for PE and ideology; the number of university students dropped sharply during the 1930s; and many teachers were resentful of the Hitler Youth's interference. But a generation grew up surrounded by Nazi ideas at school, in the youth movement and through propaganda at home — which is why the regime saw its youth policy as a long-term investment.

::: method
Paper 3 (Weimar and Nazi Germany) is 1 hour 30 minutes, 52 marks. Section A: Q1 inference (4) and one "Explain why" question with two prompts (12; recent papers such as Summer 2024 set it as a single compulsory question). Section B: one four-part question on Sources B and C and Interpretations 1 and 2: (a) usefulness (8), (b) how the interpretations differ (4), (c) why they might differ (4), (d) how far you agree with Interpretation 2 (16 + 4 SPaG). Life in Nazi Germany is a favourite Section B enquiry — for example "the Nazi treatment of women" or "the success of Nazi youth policies".

1. **Q1 "Give two things you can infer from Source A" (4)**: for each inference write "What I can infer: …" (1 mark) and "Details in the source that tell me this: …" quoting or closely paraphrasing (1 mark). An inference goes *beyond* what the source literally says. A photo of a BDM camp might let you infer that the Nazis wanted girls to be healthy future mothers. About 6 minutes.
2. **Q2 "Explain why…" (12)**: two stimulus points are given and you **must add a factor of your own** to reach Level 4. Three paragraphs: cause → precise detail (laws, dates, numbers) → link back to the question. Level 1 (1–3): simple or general points. Level 2 (4–6): some explanation, limited knowledge. Level 3 (7–9): clear analytical explanation, accurate knowledge. Level 4 (10–12): sustained analysis, well organised, wide-ranging precise knowledge beyond the stimulus. About 18 minutes.
3. **Q3(a) usefulness of Sources B and C (8)**: judge each source on **content** (what it shows about the enquiry, checked against own knowledge), **provenance** (nature, origin, purpose — a Nazi propaganda poster, a BDM leader's memoir, a Sopade exile report) and how the provenance **affects** usefulness. A Nazi poster of the ideal mother is highly useful for what the regime *wanted* women to be, but not for how many women actually lived. Level 1 (1–2): simple comments. Level 2 (3–5): judgements based on content and/or provenance with some knowledge. Level 3 (6–8): judgements on both sources, using content and provenance, with contextual knowledge. About 12 minutes.
4. **Q3(b) how do Interpretations 1 and 2 differ (4)**: identify the **main difference in view** — for example "success versus failure" or "women were victims versus women gained" — with a supporting detail from **each**. Level 2 (3–4) needs the key difference explained with support from both.
5. **Q3(c) why might they differ (4)**: a reason such as **different focus** (one historian looks at official policy, the other at what happened in practice; one at the early years, the other at the late 1930s) or **different evidence** — show that Source B supports one and Source C the other. Level 2 (3–4) needs an explained reason linked to content.
6. **Q3(d) "How far do you agree with Interpretation 2?" (16 + 4 SPaG)**: judgement in your first sentence. Paragraph 1: evidence supporting Interpretation 2 (from the interpretation and your own knowledge). Paragraph 2: evidence challenging it, using Interpretation 1 and own knowledge. Conclusion: weigh with a criterion ("more convincing because the figures after 1937 show…"). Level 1 (1–4) simple, Level 2 (5–8) some analysis, Level 3 (9–12) explained evaluation and partly justified judgement, Level 4 (13–16) sustained evaluation and fully justified judgement. Good SPaG and specialist terms (Volksgemeinschaft, Gleichschaltung, Lebensborn) earn up to 4 more. About 25–30 minutes.

**The analysis that lifts answers on this topic**: always separate **aims** from **results**, and **ideology** from **economic need**. Women's policies changed after 1936–37 because rearmament needed workers; youth policies were very successful in membership numbers, but less so in winning hearts by the late 1930s.
:::

::: example Q2 — Explain why the Nazis tried to control young people
Explain why the Nazis tried to control the young in the years 1933–39. You may use the following in your answer: the Hitler Youth; the school curriculum. You must also use information of your own. (12)

One reason was that the Nazis wanted to create a generation of loyal Nazis who would keep the regime going for the future. This is why the Hitler Youth was expanded under Baldur von Schirach from 1933: other youth groups were closed or absorbed, membership rose from about 108,000 at the end of 1932 to 5.4 million by the end of 1936, and the Law on the Hitler Youth (December 1936) banned all rivals. Evening meetings taught the life of Hitler, the injustice of Versailles and racial ideas, so young people's first loyalty would be to the Führer rather than their parents or churches.

A second reason was that Hitler's foreign policy aims required soldiers. Boys in the Hitler Youth did drill, map-reading and shooting, and in schools physical education rose to about 15% of the timetable, with boxing compulsory for older boys. Geography lessons taught *Lebensraum* and history taught the "stab in the back", so boys learned both the skills and the attitudes needed for war. This mattered because conscription had been reintroduced in 1935 and the army was expanding fast.

A third reason, from my own knowledge, was Nazi racial and family policy. Girls were to become mothers of healthy Aryan children, so the League of German Maidens stressed fitness, cooking, sewing and childcare, and biology lessons taught race science and how to choose a racially suitable partner. Teachers were controlled to deliver this: Jewish and "unreliable" teachers were removed in 1933, and about 97% had joined the National Socialist Teachers' League by 1937. So control of youth combined the Nazis' political, military and racial aims — the young were the key to building the Volksgemeinschaft.

::: example Q3(d) — how far do you agree (women)
Interpretation 1 (a summary of the view of historians who stress the failure of policy): Nazi policies towards women failed, because the needs of the economy forced the regime to bring women back into work and most families stayed small. Interpretation 2 (a summary of the view of historians who stress the effects on women's lives): Nazi policies pushed women out of public life and into the home, where their role was to have children for the state. How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about Nazi policies towards women? Explain your answer, using both interpretations and your knowledge of the historical context. (16)

I agree with Interpretation 2 to some extent, because it is accurate about Nazi aims and about professional women, but Interpretation 1 is more convincing about what happened to most women by 1939.

Interpretation 2 is supported by many policies from 1933. Women doctors and civil servants were dismissed, especially "double earners"; only 10% of new university students could be women; and from 1936 women could not be judges or lawyers. There were no women in the Nazi Reichstag. At the same time the regime pushed women towards motherhood: marriage loans of 1,000 Reichsmarks required the wife to leave work, with a quarter cancelled per child, and the Mother's Cross rewarded large families. Marriages rose to about 740,000 in 1934 and births to about 1.4 million by 1939, so the policies did change many women's lives.

However, Interpretation 1 challenges this. From 1937, rearmament created a labour shortage and the regime reversed its policy: the marriage-loan rule was changed so wives could work, the Duty Year (1938) put young women to work on farms and in homes, and the number of women in employment rose from about 4.85 million in 1933 to 7.14 million in 1939. The birth rate was already recovering with the economy, and most couples still had one or two children, so the "three-child family" ideal was not achieved.

Overall I agree with Interpretation 2 only partly. It describes Nazi ideology and the fate of educated women accurately, but by 1939 more women were in work than in 1933, which shows that economic need beat ideology. Interpretation 1 is therefore more convincing about the results, while Interpretation 2 is better about the aims.
