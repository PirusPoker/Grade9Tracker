## What it is

Cuba is the moment the Cold War came closest to nuclear war. The story runs in a chain you must be able to tell in order: **Castro's revolution (1959) → the USA turns hostile → Cuba turns to the USSR → the Bay of Pigs (1961) → Soviet missiles in Cuba → the Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962) → the hotline and the arms-control treaties (1963–68)**. Every link caused the next — which is exactly what the narrative and consequence questions reward.

### Why Cuba mattered to the USA

- Cuba lies about **90 miles (145 km) off Florida**. The USA saw the Caribbean as its own backyard and had dominated Cuba since 1898; it kept a naval base at **Guantanamo Bay**.
- American companies owned much of Cuba's **sugar plantations, mines, railways, telephone and electricity** networks, and US tourists and casinos filled Havana.
- The USA backed the corrupt dictator **Fulgencio Batista**, who was friendly to American business. Most Cubans were poor, and resentment of both Batista and the USA was strong.

### The Cuban Revolution and its impact on relations

**Fidel Castro**, with his brother Raúl and **Che Guevara**, fought a guerrilla war from the Sierra Maestra mountains from 1956. Batista fled on **1 January 1959** and Castro took power. At first he was a nationalist, not a declared communist, and in April 1959 he visited the USA and met Vice-President Nixon (Eisenhower avoided him).

**Relations with the USA collapsed** because Castro attacked American interests:
- He **nationalised** land and businesses, including US-owned sugar estates (land reform, 1959) and, in 1960, US oil refineries and companies — without compensation Americans thought fair.
- When US-owned refineries refused to process Soviet oil (June 1960), Castro seized them.
- Eisenhower hit back: he **cut the US quota of Cuban sugar** it would buy (July 1960) and imposed a **trade embargo** on most US exports to Cuba (October 1960).
- The CIA was authorised (March 1960) to train Cuban exiles to overthrow Castro.
- In **January 1961** the USA **broke off diplomatic relations** and from then on **refused to recognise Castro's government** as legitimate. (The USA had recognised the new government in January 1959; the refusal came as relations broke down — the spec calls it "the refusal of the USA to recognise Castro's government".)

**Relations with the USSR grew closer** because Castro needed a buyer for his sugar and a protector:
- In **February 1960** Soviet deputy premier **Anastas Mikoyan** visited Havana and signed a trade agreement: the USSR would buy **5 million tons of Cuban sugar over five years** and gave **100 million US dollars** in credit.
- Full diplomatic relations with the USSR followed in May 1960; the USSR supplied oil, and later weapons and military advisers.
- For **Khrushchev** this was a gift: a communist-friendly state in the USA's own sphere of influence, and proof that communism could spread to the developing world.

The key analytical point: **US hostility pushed Castro towards Moscow, and Castro's move to Moscow made the USA more hostile** — a spiral.

### The Bay of Pigs, April 1961

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| March 1960 | Eisenhower approves CIA plan to train anti-Castro exiles (in Guatemala) |
| January 1961 | **John F. Kennedy** becomes president and inherits the plan; he approves it but insists US involvement must be hidden |
| 15 April 1961 | Air strikes by exile pilots in old US B-26 bombers (painted in Cuban colours) fail to destroy Castro's air force |
| 17 April 1961 | About **1,400 Cuban exiles** (Brigade 2506) land at the Bay of Pigs; Kennedy refuses further US air cover |
| 19 April 1961 | Invasion crushed within about three days by Castro's much larger forces; over 100 exiles killed and roughly 1,200 captured |

Why it failed: poor secrecy (Castro knew an attack was coming), a swampy landing site with no escape route into the mountains, air strikes that missed, no US air support, and no popular Cuban uprising — most Cubans backed Castro.

**Significance of the Bay of Pigs:**
1. **Humiliation for Kennedy** — the USA was exposed as trying to overthrow a neighbouring government, and failed. Khrushchev judged Kennedy young, inexperienced and weak (reinforced at the Vienna summit, June 1961).
2. **It drove Castro fully into the Soviet camp** — on 16 April 1961, the day after the air strikes, he declared the revolution socialist, and in December 1961 he announced he was a Marxist-Leninist. He asked for Soviet military protection.
3. **Kennedy did not give up** — Operation Mongoose (from November 1961) used CIA sabotage and assassination plots against Castro, convincing Castro and Khrushchev that a real US invasion was coming.
4. **It led directly to the missiles** — Khrushchev could defend Cuba, and redress the nuclear balance, by putting missiles there.

### Why Khrushchev put missiles in Cuba

- **Protect Cuba** from a second US invasion and keep a communist ally in the Americas.
- **Close the missile gap** — the USA had far more long-range missiles than the USSR; missiles in Cuba could hit most US cities with little warning.
- **Retaliate for the US Jupiter missiles in Turkey**, on the Soviet border, operational in 1962.
- **Test Kennedy**, whom he thought weak, and perhaps gain leverage over **Berlin**.

In summer 1962 (Operation Anadyr) the USSR secretly shipped medium-range nuclear missiles, bombers and tens of thousands of troops to Cuba.

### The events of the Cuban Missile Crisis — the "Thirteen Days"

| Date (1962) | Event |
|---|---|
| 14 October | A US **U-2 spy plane** photographs missile sites under construction at San Cristóbal |
| 16 October | Kennedy is shown the photographs; he sets up **ExComm** (Executive Committee of the National Security Council) |
| 16–21 October | ExComm debates options: do nothing, diplomacy, air strikes, invasion, or a **naval blockade** |
| 22 October | Kennedy announces on television a **"quarantine"** (blockade) of Cuba and demands removal of the missiles; US forces go on high alert |
| 24 October | Blockade begins; Soviet ships heading for Cuba stop or turn back |
| 25 October | At the UN, US ambassador Adlai Stevenson shows the photographs to the world |
| 26 October | **Khrushchev's first letter**: missiles removed if the USA promises not to invade Cuba |
| 27 October | **"Black Saturday"**: a second, tougher letter demands removal of US missiles from Turkey; a U-2 is **shot down over Cuba** and its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson, killed. Kennedy publicly accepts the first letter; **Robert Kennedy secretly tells Soviet ambassador Dobrynin** that the Jupiters in Turkey will go within months |
| 28 October | Khrushchev announces on Moscow radio that the missiles will be dismantled |
| 20 November | Blockade lifted once Soviet bombers are also removed; the Turkish Jupiters are removed by April 1963 |

Why the blockade was clever: it was firm but not an act of war on Soviet forces, and it gave Khrushchev **time and a way out**. An air strike might have killed Soviet troops; an invasion risked nuclear retaliation.

Who "won"? **Kennedy** appeared the winner publicly — the missiles left and he looked firm. **Khrushchev** gained a promise that Cuba would not be invaded and the secret removal of the Jupiters, but because that part was kept secret he looked to have backed down; hardliners in Moscow removed him in **October 1964**. **Castro** was furious he had not been consulted, but his regime survived.

### The consequences of the Cuban Missile Crisis

Both leaders had seen how close they came to nuclear war, and how easily miscommunication (letters took hours to arrive) could cause catastrophe. Relations improved — a first step towards the later détente.

| Consequence | Date | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| **The hotline** | Agreed June 1963; working August 1963 | A direct teleprinter link between the White House and the Kremlin so leaders could communicate instantly in a crisis |
| **Limited Test Ban Treaty** | 5 August 1963 | USA, USSR and Britain banned nuclear tests **in the atmosphere, in outer space and under water**; underground tests still allowed. France and China did not sign |
| **Outer Space Treaty** | January 1967 | Banned nuclear weapons in orbit and on the Moon or other bodies; space to be used for peaceful purposes |
| **Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty** | 1 July 1968 | Nuclear powers agreed not to help other states get nuclear weapons; non-nuclear states agreed not to develop them, in return for help with peaceful nuclear power; nuclear powers promised to work towards disarmament |

Other consequences worth using:
- **The arms race continued** — the USSR, humiliated, built up its nuclear forces to reach rough parity with the USA by the end of the 1960s. The treaties limited testing and spread, not the number of weapons.
- **Khrushchev fell** (1964), partly because the crisis looked like a retreat.
- **Castro's Cuba survived** as a communist state under the US non-invasion pledge, and the US embargo stayed.
- **Allies were unsettled** — France's de Gaulle felt Europe could be dragged into war without being consulted, one reason France left NATO's integrated military command in 1966.
- Both superpowers learned to **avoid direct confrontation**, preferring to manage crises — a key reason for 1970s détente.

::: terms
- **Nationalisation**: a government taking private businesses or land into state ownership — Castro's seizure of US-owned property in 1959–60.
- **Trade embargo**: a ban on trade with a country; the USA's embargo on Cuba began in October 1960.
- **Bay of Pigs**: the failed CIA-backed invasion of Cuba by about 1,400 Cuban exiles in April 1961.
- **ExComm**: the Executive Committee of the National Security Council, Kennedy's crisis team during the Cuban Missile Crisis.
- **Quarantine (blockade)**: Kennedy's use of the US Navy from 24 October 1962 to stop Soviet ships carrying weapons reaching Cuba.
- **Jupiter missiles**: US nuclear missiles based in Turkey near the Soviet border, secretly removed as part of the settlement of the crisis.
- **Hotline**: the direct communication link between Washington and Moscow set up in 1963 so leaders could talk quickly in a crisis.
- **Limited Test Ban Treaty**: the 1963 agreement banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, space and under water.
- **Non-Proliferation Treaty**: the 1968 agreement to stop nuclear weapons spreading to more countries.
:::

::: method
The Cold War is the **period study** in Booklet P of Paper 2 (about 50 minutes for 32 marks). There are three question types, all worth 8 marks, and **no SPaG marks**. The mark schemes have two levels (consequences) or three levels (narrative and importance) — there is no Level 4 on this booklet, so full marks come from a Level 2 or Level 3 answer.

**1. "Explain two consequences of…" (8 marks: 4 per consequence)**
- Level 1 (1–2 marks per consequence): a simple or general statement ("it made relations worse").
- Level 2 (3–4 marks per consequence): the consequence is **explained** with specific, accurate knowledge showing *how* the event led to it.
- Structure: "One consequence of X was Y. This happened because… [detail]. As a result… [effect]." Then repeat for a second, *different* consequence. Two paragraphs, no introduction or conclusion needed.

**2. "Write a narrative account analysing…" (8 marks)**
- You get two stimulus bullet points and **must add your own information**. Answers that do not go beyond the stimulus points are capped at Level 2 (5 marks).
- Level 1 (1–2): simple story with little sense of links. Level 2 (3–5): events in sequence with some links. Level 3 (6–8): a **coherent, analytical narrative** — events in the right order, each **linked by cause and consequence** to the next ("this led to…", "as a result…", "which meant that…"), heading to an outcome.
- Aim for at least three well-linked events. Stay inside the dates in the question.

**3. "Explain the importance of X for Y" (8 marks each; you answer two of three)**
- Level 1 (1–2): general comment about X. Level 2 (3–5): some explanation of how X affected Y. Level 3 (6–8): **analysis of the impact** of X on Y, supported by precise knowledge, often shown in two or three distinct ways.
- The mark goes on "for Y". Do not just describe X — keep asking "so what did this mean for Y?" Use a short judgement at the end ("its greatest importance was…").
:::

::: example Two consequences
Explain two consequences of the Bay of Pigs incident (1961). (8)

One consequence was that Cuba moved firmly into the Soviet camp. The failed invasion by about 1,400 CIA-trained exiles in April 1961 proved to Castro that the USA was determined to overthrow him. On 16 April, the day after the exile air strikes, he declared the revolution socialist, and in December 1961 he announced he was a Marxist-Leninist. He asked Khrushchev for military protection, which gave the USSR a close ally only 90 miles from Florida.

A second consequence was that Khrushchev saw Kennedy as weak, which encouraged him to take risks. Kennedy had approved the plan but refused to send US air cover, so the invasion collapsed within about three days and the USA was humiliated. Khrushchev judged the new president inexperienced, which was a key reason he dared to send nuclear missiles to Cuba in 1962, believing Kennedy would not respond firmly. This led directly to the Cuban Missile Crisis.
:::

::: example Narrative account
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962. You may use the following in your answer: the U-2 photographs (14 October); Khrushchev's letters (26–27 October). You must also use information of your own. (8)

The crisis began when Khrushchev, wanting to protect Cuba after the Bay of Pigs and to answer US Jupiter missiles in Turkey, secretly sent nuclear missiles to Cuba in the summer of 1962. On 14 October a US U-2 spy plane photographed missile sites at San Cristóbal. This discovery forced Kennedy to act, so he set up ExComm on 16 October to consider options, from air strikes to invasion.

Because an air strike or invasion risked killing Soviet troops and starting nuclear war, Kennedy chose a naval blockade, announced on television on 22 October. As a result, when the blockade began on 24 October, Soviet ships approaching Cuba stopped and turned back, which showed Khrushchev was unwilling to risk a clash at sea. However, work on the sites continued, so tension rose further.

This pressure led Khrushchev to seek a way out. On 26 October he wrote offering to remove the missiles if the USA promised not to invade Cuba, but on 27 October a second letter also demanded the removal of US missiles from Turkey. The same day a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, bringing the crisis to its most dangerous point. Kennedy responded by publicly accepting the first letter while Robert Kennedy secretly promised Soviet ambassador Dobrynin that the Jupiters would go. Consequently, on 28 October Khrushchev announced the missiles would be dismantled, ending the crisis — though the secret part of the deal meant he looked to have backed down.
:::

::: example Importance
Explain the importance of the Cuban Missile Crisis for the development of the Cold War. (8)

The crisis was important because it made both superpowers recognise the danger of nuclear war and try to manage their rivalry. The confusion of the thirteen days — when letters between leaders took hours to arrive — led to the hotline in 1963, a direct link between Washington and Moscow so a future crisis could be defused quickly.

It was also important because it began the first attempts at arms control. In August 1963 the USA, USSR and Britain signed the Limited Test Ban Treaty, banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, space and under water. This was followed by the Outer Space Treaty (1967) and the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), which tried to stop nuclear weapons spreading. These agreements laid the foundations for détente in the 1970s.

However, the crisis did not end the arms race. Khrushchev appeared to have backed down and was removed in 1964; his successors built up Soviet nuclear forces to reach parity with the USA by the late 1960s. So the crisis was most important as a turning point in how the Cold War was conducted — from brinkmanship to cautious management — rather than an end to rivalry.
:::

::: q
Give two ways in which Castro damaged US interests in Cuba in 1959–60.
---
Any two: nationalised US-owned land and sugar estates; seized US oil refineries after they refused to process Soviet oil (1960); nationalised other US businesses; made a trade deal with the USSR (February 1960).
:::

::: q
What did the USSR agree with Cuba in February 1960, and why did it matter?
---
Mikoyan's trade agreement: the USSR would buy 5 million tons of sugar over five years and gave 100 million US dollars in credit. It gave Castro an alternative to the USA and brought communist influence into the Americas.
:::

::: q
Give three reasons why the Bay of Pigs invasion failed.
---
Any three: poor secrecy — Castro knew it was coming; air strikes failed to destroy the Cuban air force; Kennedy refused US air cover; the exiles (about 1,400) were heavily outnumbered; the swampy landing site; no popular uprising — most Cubans supported Castro.
:::

::: q
Why did Khrushchev place missiles in Cuba? Give three reasons.
---
To protect Cuba from US invasion; to close the missile gap with the USA; to respond to US Jupiter missiles in Turkey; to test Kennedy, whom he thought weak; possibly to gain leverage over Berlin.
:::

::: q
Why did Kennedy choose a blockade rather than an air strike?
---
An air strike might not destroy every missile and could kill Soviet personnel, risking nuclear retaliation. A blockade showed strength but was not a direct attack; it gave Khrushchev time and a way to back down.
:::

::: q
What happened on "Black Saturday", 27 October 1962?
---
Khrushchev's second letter demanded removal of US missiles from Turkey; a U-2 was shot down over Cuba and its pilot killed; Kennedy publicly accepted the first letter while Robert Kennedy secretly told Dobrynin the Jupiters in Turkey would be removed.
:::

::: q
State what each of these banned or limited: the Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963); the Outer Space Treaty (1967); the Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968).
---
LTBT: nuclear tests in the atmosphere, outer space and under water (underground allowed). Outer Space Treaty: nuclear weapons in orbit or on the Moon and other bodies. NPT: the spread of nuclear weapons to states that did not have them.
:::

::: q
Why is it misleading to say Kennedy simply "won" the Cuban Missile Crisis?
---
Khrushchev gained a public US promise not to invade Cuba and a secret promise to remove the Jupiter missiles from Turkey (gone by April 1963). Castro stayed in power. Kennedy only looked the winner because the Turkey deal was secret.
:::

::: watch
- **Consequence answers that describe the event** — the Bay of Pigs story is not a consequence; say what it *led to*.
- **Mixing up the order** — revolution 1959, embargo 1960, Bay of Pigs April 1961, Missile Crisis October 1962, hotline and Test Ban 1963. Narratives lose Level 3 if the sequence is wrong.
- Forgetting the **secret Turkey deal** — without it you cannot explain why Khrushchev agreed or why he looked weak.
- Confusing the treaties: the **Test Ban Treaty did not ban underground tests** and did not cut weapon numbers.
- Narratives that **only use the two stimulus points** are capped at 5 marks — add at least one event of your own.
- Writing "Cuba was communist from 1959" — Castro declared himself a Marxist-Leninist in December 1961, after the Bay of Pigs.
:::
