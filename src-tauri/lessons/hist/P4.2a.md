## What it is

Berlin was the most dangerous point in Europe between 1958 and 1963. East Germans were escaping through West Berlin in huge numbers; Khrushchev tried to force the West out with an **ultimatum** (1958); a series of **summit meetings (1959–61)** failed to solve the problem; in **August 1961** the East German government built the **Berlin Wall**; and in **June 1963** President Kennedy visited West Berlin and declared his support for its people. This topic covers the Berlin strands of Key Topic 2: the refugee problem, the ultimatum, the summits, the construction of the Wall, its impact on US–Soviet relations, and Kennedy's visit.

### The refugee problem

After 1949 Germany was divided into the **FRG** (West Germany — capitalist, democratic, booming thanks to Marshall Aid) and the **GDR** (East Germany — communist, poorer, run by **Walter Ulbricht**). Berlin, deep inside the GDR, was still divided into a Soviet sector (East Berlin) and three Western sectors (West Berlin).

- The main border between East and West Germany was sealed in 1952, but **inside Berlin people could still move freely** between sectors — by foot, tram or train. An East German could take the train into West Berlin and fly on to West Germany.
- Between 1949 and 1961 roughly **2.7 million** East Germans left — about one in six of the population. In **July 1961** alone more than **30,000** went.
- Many were **young and skilled**: doctors, engineers, teachers, skilled workers. The GDR was losing the people it most needed — a **"brain drain"** that damaged its economy.
- It was a **propaganda disaster** for communism: people were voting with their feet for capitalism.
- West Berlin was also a base for Western **spying** and radio propaganda inside the Eastern bloc, and its prosperity was a constant advertisement for the West.

Ulbricht pressed Khrushchev to act, and Khrushchev wanted to solve the problem to protect the GDR and Soviet prestige.

### Khrushchev's Berlin ultimatum, November 1958

Khrushchev was confident: the USSR had tested the first ICBM and launched Sputnik in 1957, and he believed the "missile gap" gave him leverage. In **November 1958** he issued an **ultimatum**:

- The Western powers must withdraw their troops from West Berlin **within six months**.
- Berlin should become a demilitarised **"free city"**.
- If not, the USSR would sign a separate peace treaty with the GDR and hand control of all access routes into West Berlin to the East Germans — so the West would have to deal with (and in effect recognise) the GDR, which it refused to do.

**The Western response.** Eisenhower refused to withdraw, as leaving Berlin would abandon two million West Berliners and damage American credibility across Europe. But he agreed to talk, and the six-month deadline passed without the USSR carrying out its threat.

### The summit meetings, 1959–61

| Summit | Date | Who | What happened | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Geneva** | May–August 1959 | Foreign ministers of the USA, USSR, Britain, France | Discussed Berlin and Germany; no agreement, but talks were a step forward | Deadline quietly dropped; paved the way for a leaders' meeting |
| **Camp David** (USA) | September 1959 | Eisenhower and Khrushchev | Khrushchev toured the USA — the first Soviet leader to do so; talks at Camp David, the president's retreat | No solution, but Khrushchev **withdrew the ultimatum** and they agreed to meet again in Paris; a friendlier "spirit of Camp David" |
| **Paris** | May 1960 | Eisenhower, Khrushchev, Macmillan, de Gaulle | On **1 May 1960** a US **U-2 spy plane** was shot down over the USSR and its pilot, **Francis Gary Powers**, captured. The USA first claimed it was a weather plane; Khrushchev revealed the pilot was alive. Eisenhower admitted the spying but refused to apologise. | Khrushchev **walked out**; the summit collapsed; Eisenhower's planned visit to the USSR was cancelled |
| **Vienna** | June 1961 | **Kennedy** and Khrushchev | Kennedy had just suffered the humiliating **Bay of Pigs** failure in Cuba (April 1961). Khrushchev saw him as young and inexperienced and **renewed the ultimatum** — the West must leave Berlin by the end of 1961. | Kennedy refused. Both left expecting a crisis. Kennedy went home and increased US military strength. |

**After Vienna.** In July 1961 Kennedy announced a major build-up: an extra **3.25 billion US dollars** for defence, more troops, and a programme of fallout shelters. He made clear the USA would defend **West Berlin** — its presence there and access to it — but not East Berlin. This left Khrushchev a way out: action on the Eastern side of the line would not mean war.

### The construction of the Berlin Wall, August 1961

- On the night of **12–13 August 1961** East German soldiers and police sealed the border between East and West Berlin with **barbed wire** and roadblocks. Trains and roads were cut.
- Within days a **concrete wall** began to replace the wire. Over time it became a complex system of walls, watchtowers, searchlights, dogs, mines and a "death strip", around **155 km** long, surrounding West Berlin.
- Families and friends were separated overnight. People jumped from windows of buildings on the border, dug tunnels and swam canals to escape. Guards had orders to shoot. In **August 1962** eighteen-year-old **Peter Fechter** was shot while trying to cross and left to bleed to death in view of Western journalists. At least 140 people were killed at the Wall between 1961 and 1989.
- **The American reaction** was cautious. The USA sent Vice-President Lyndon Johnson and General Lucius Clay (hero of the Berlin Airlift) to Berlin and reinforced the garrison with 1,500 more troops, but did not tear the Wall down.
- **Checkpoint Charlie, October 1961**: after a dispute over the right of US officials to enter East Berlin, American and Soviet tanks faced each other across the crossing point for around 16 hours before both withdrew — the closest the Berlin crisis came to war.

**Why build a wall?** It stopped the refugee flow and the brain drain overnight, saved the GDR from collapse, and removed the need for a showdown with the West: Khrushchev had failed to push the Western powers out of Berlin, so he walled them in instead.

### Impact of the Wall on US–Soviet relations

| Effect | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Reduced the risk of war over Berlin** | The refugee problem was solved, so Khrushchev dropped his demand for a peace treaty. Kennedy is reported to have said privately that a wall was a lot better than a war. The Berlin question stopped being the main flashpoint. |
| **Symbol of the Cold War** | The Wall became the physical form of the Iron Curtain — the clearest symbol of a divided Europe. |
| **Propaganda victory for the West** | A communist state had to wall its own people in to stop them leaving. The West could present the Wall as proof that communism failed. |
| **Kennedy's position** | He had not stopped the Wall, which critics saw as weakness — but he had kept West Berlin free and avoided war. |
| **Khrushchev's position** | He had failed to get the West out of Berlin, but secured the GDR; still, the Wall was an admission of failure. |
| **Relations stayed tense** | The Checkpoint Charlie stand-off showed how dangerous things remained; within a year the Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962) brought the superpowers even closer to nuclear war. |
| **Division accepted** | Both sides now effectively accepted the division of Berlin and Germany as a fact. The Wall lasted until **9 November 1989**. |

### Kennedy's visit to West Berlin, June 1963

On **26 June 1963** Kennedy visited West Berlin. Huge crowds lined the streets and hundreds of thousands gathered to hear him speak outside the city hall at Schöneberg. He viewed the Wall and declared his solidarity with the city, saying "Ich bin ein Berliner" ("I am a Berliner"). Addressing those who thought communism was the future, he repeated "Let them come to Berlin."

**Significance:**
- It showed the USA's **continuing commitment** to defending West Berlin — reassuring West Berliners who had felt abandoned when the Wall went up.
- It was a **propaganda triumph**: Kennedy used the Wall to contrast Western freedom with communist repression in front of the world's press.
- It raised Kennedy's standing after the Bay of Pigs and the Wall.
- But it **changed nothing on the ground**: the Wall stayed, and Kennedy was accepting the status quo. The Soviet Union saw the speech as provocative.
- It came just after the Cuban Missile Crisis, as relations were starting to ease (the hotline and Limited Test Ban Treaty were agreed in 1963) — Berlin remained a symbol, but no longer a trigger for war.

::: terms
- **Refugee problem**: the flight of East Germans to the West through Berlin, about 2.7 million between 1949 and 1961.
- **Brain drain**: the loss of skilled and educated people — doctors, engineers, teachers — from the GDR to the West.
- **Ultimatum**: a final demand with a deadline and a threat, such as Khrushchev's 1958 six-month demand that the West leave Berlin.
- **Free city**: Khrushchev's proposal that West Berlin become a demilitarised city with no Western troops.
- **Summit**: a meeting between heads of government, such as Camp David (1959), Paris (1960) and Vienna (1961).
- **U-2 incident**: the shooting down of an American spy plane over the USSR on 1 May 1960, which wrecked the Paris summit.
- **Berlin Wall**: the barrier built from August 1961 around West Berlin to stop East Germans escaping.
- **Checkpoint Charlie**: the best-known crossing point between East and West Berlin, site of the October 1961 tank stand-off.
- **Status quo**: the existing state of affairs; after 1961 both superpowers effectively accepted Berlin's division.
:::

::: method
Paper 2 period study (Booklet P, 32 marks, about 50 minutes, no SPaG). All questions are 8 marks, targeting AO1 (knowledge) and AO2 (explaining and analysing).

**1. "Explain two consequences of…" (8)** — each consequence out of 4.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple or general ("it divided Berlin").
- Level 2 (3–4): specific consequence explained with accurate supporting detail.
- Choose two **different kinds** of consequence — e.g. for East Germany (refugees stopped) and for superpower relations (reduced risk of war / propaganda).

**2. "Write a narrative account analysing…" (8)** — two stimulus bullets; own knowledge required.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple narrative.
- Level 2 (3–5): some sequence and links. **Capped at 5 if you do not go beyond the bullets.**
- Level 3 (6–8): events in order with **explicit causal links** between them: refugees → ultimatum → summits fail → Vienna → Wall. Each paragraph should explain why the next event happened.

**3. "Explain two of the following: the importance of X for Y" (8 each, choose two of three)**
- Level 1 (1–2): general comment on X.
- Level 2 (3–5): some explanation of importance with relevant detail.
- Level 3 (6–8): explains **what difference X made to Y** — e.g. the importance of the U-2 incident *for relations between the superpowers* — with precise detail, two or three strands, and a closing sentence on which mattered most. Keep using the wording of the question.
:::

::: example Consequences — the Berlin Wall
Explain two consequences of the construction of the Berlin Wall (1961). (8)

**Consequence 1:** One consequence was that the refugee problem was ended almost overnight. Before August 1961 about 2.7 million East Germans, many of them skilled workers such as doctors and engineers, had escaped through Berlin — over 30,000 in July 1961 alone. Once the border was sealed on 13 August, the GDR stopped losing its workforce, which allowed Ulbricht's regime to stabilise its economy and survive.

**Consequence 2:** A second consequence was a reduction in the immediate danger of war over Berlin. Because the Wall solved the refugee problem, Khrushchev no longer needed to force the West out and dropped his demand for a separate peace treaty. Kennedy accepted the Wall rather than risk war, since it did not threaten West Berlin itself. Although the Checkpoint Charlie stand-off showed tension remained, Berlin stopped being the main trigger for a superpower conflict.
:::

::: example Narrative — the Berlin crisis, 1958–61
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of 1958–61 that led to the construction of the Berlin Wall. You may use the following in your answer: Khrushchev's Berlin ultimatum (1958); the Vienna summit (1961). You must also use information of your own. (8)

By 1958 large numbers of East Germans were escaping to the West through Berlin, where movement between sectors was still free. This brain drain of skilled workers was damaging the GDR's economy and embarrassing communism, so Khrushchev, confident after Sputnik, issued an ultimatum in November 1958 demanding the West leave Berlin within six months and threatening to hand access routes to the GDR.

Eisenhower refused but agreed to negotiate, which led to a series of summits. The Geneva conference of 1959 produced no agreement, but at Camp David in September 1959 Khrushchev withdrew his ultimatum and agreed to meet again in Paris. However, the U-2 spy plane shot down on 1 May 1960 wrecked the Paris summit when Eisenhower refused to apologise and Khrushchev walked out, so the Berlin problem remained unsolved.

With a new president in office, Khrushchev tried again. Kennedy had been humiliated at the Bay of Pigs in April 1961, so at Vienna in June Khrushchev saw him as weak and renewed the ultimatum with an end-of-year deadline. Kennedy refused and announced a large increase in US military spending, making clear he would defend West Berlin but not East Berlin.

Meanwhile the refugee flow grew to over 30,000 in July 1961. Since the West would not leave, and force risked war, Khrushchev allowed Ulbricht to seal the border on 12–13 August 1961 with barbed wire, soon replaced by a concrete wall.
:::

::: example Importance — Kennedy's visit to West Berlin
Explain the importance of Kennedy's visit to West Berlin (1963) for relations between the USA and the Soviet Union. (8)

Kennedy's visit was important first because it restated the American commitment to West Berlin. Many West Berliners felt abandoned when the USA did not stop the Wall in 1961. By visiting in June 1963, viewing the Wall and declaring "Ich bin ein Berliner" to a vast crowd, Kennedy showed the USSR that the USA would not give up West Berlin, closing off any further Soviet attempt to force the West out.

It was also important as propaganda. Kennedy used the Wall to contrast Western freedom with a communist system that had to imprison its own people, telling those who believed in communism to "come to Berlin". This damaged the USSR's image worldwide, and the Soviet leadership saw the speech as provocative.

However, its importance was limited, because it changed nothing on the ground. Kennedy accepted the Wall and the division of Berlin; the visit came as relations were easing after the Cuban Missile Crisis, with the hotline and Test Ban Treaty agreed that summer. Its greatest importance was therefore symbolic: it confirmed the status quo in Berlin rather than challenging it.
:::

---
