## What it is

This lesson covers the first half of **c1700–c1900: Medicine in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Britain** — how explanations of the **cause of disease** changed (and how much stayed the same), the influence in Britain of **Louis Pasteur's germ theory** and **Robert Koch's work on microbes**, and the **key individual Edward Jenner** and the development and use of **vaccination**. The theme that links them: for the first time since Galen, the *cause* of infectious disease was correctly identified — but it took decades for new knowledge to change what British doctors and the government actually did.

### Where the story starts in 1700

By 1700 the Medical Renaissance had damaged Galen's authority (Vesalius on anatomy, Harvey on the blood) and scientists such as those in the **Royal Society** (1660) valued observation and experiment. But nobody knew what caused infectious disease.

- **Continuity:** most doctors still thought in terms of **miasma** — bad air from rotting matter, sewage and corpses. The **Four Humours** gradually lost credibility among educated physicians during the eighteenth century, but bloodletting and purging were still widely used into the 1800s. Ordinary people relied on herbal remedies, patent medicines and **quacks**.
- **Spontaneous generation** was the accepted scientific idea: microbes (first seen by **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** through his home-made microscope in the 1670s) were thought to be **produced by** decay and disease — a *result* of illness, not its *cause*.
- **Change:** the idea that disease might be passed on by **contagion** (contact with the sick) grew, and doctors increasingly used observation and experiment — for example **John Hunter** (1728–93), the Scottish surgeon in London who taught Jenner to test ideas by experiment.

**Why disease became a bigger problem.** The **Industrial Revolution** drew millions into fast-growing towns with overcrowded back-to-back housing, shared privies, cesspits and polluted water. Diseases such as **typhus, typhoid, tuberculosis (TB)** and, from **1831**, **cholera** spread rapidly. This made finding the true cause urgent.

**Better microscopes.** In **1830 Joseph Jackson Lister** (father of the surgeon Joseph Lister) designed an **achromatic** lens that removed the blurred, coloured edges of earlier microscopes, so scientists could see microbes clearly at much higher magnification. Without this, neither Pasteur nor Koch could have done their work — a key example of **science and technology** as a factor.

### Louis Pasteur and the germ theory (1861)

**Who:** Louis Pasteur (1822–95) was a French **chemist**, not a doctor — so he approached the problem as a scientist with a laboratory.

**How it happened:**
- In **1857**, in Lille, he was asked by an industrialist to find out why beet alcohol was going sour. Using a microscope he showed that **microbes caused fermentation and souring**, and that different microbes produced different results. Industry (brewing, wine, vinegar, milk) funded and encouraged this work.
- To disprove spontaneous generation, he used **swan-necked flasks**. Boiled broth left in a flask with a long curved neck stayed fresh, because microbes from the air were trapped in the bend; when the neck was broken, or the flask was tilted so the broth touched the neck, the broth went bad. So **microbes come from the air — decay does not create them**.
- He published his **germ theory in 1861**: **microbes (germs) in the air cause decay**, and he suggested that germs might also cause disease.
- From **1865** he helped the French silk industry, showing that a disease of silkworms was caused by a microbe — evidence that germs could cause disease in living things.

**The limit of 1861:** Pasteur proved that germs cause decay, and argued they cause disease, but he had **not yet linked a specific germ to a specific human disease**. That was Koch's achievement.

**Pasteur's later work on vaccines** (competing with Koch):
- **1879 — chicken cholera.** His assistant **Charles Chamberland** was meant to inject chickens with a fresh culture of chicken cholera but went on holiday; the culture was left for weeks and **weakened**. Chickens injected with it did not die — and when later given a fresh culture, they were **immune**. Pasteur had found a way to make a vaccine by **weakening (attenuating) the germ**.
- **1881 — anthrax.** At **Pouilly-le-Fort** in May 1881, in a public experiment in front of journalists, he vaccinated half of a flock of sheep; after all were injected with anthrax, the vaccinated animals survived and the unvaccinated ones died.
- **1881** — at the **International Medical Congress in London**, Pasteur proposed that the word **"vaccination"** be used for all such preventive injections, **in honour of Jenner**.
- **1885 — rabies.** Pasteur treated **Joseph Meister**, a nine-year-old boy bitten by a rabid dog, with his experimental rabies vaccine; the boy survived. The **Pasteur Institute** in Paris opened in 1888.

### Robert Koch and the work on microbes

**Who:** Robert Koch (1843–1910) was a German **doctor**, a country doctor who began research in his own home laboratory.

**What he did:**
- **1876 — anthrax.** He identified the **specific bacterium that causes anthrax** and worked out its life cycle, showing it could be transferred from one animal to another. This was the first time a **specific microbe was proved to cause a specific disease**.
- **New methods, which became the standard toolkit of bacteriology:**
  - **staining** bacteria with **aniline dyes** (a by-product of the German chemical industry) so they could be seen and identified;
  - **photographing** bacteria through the microscope so others could check the results;
  - growing pure cultures on a **solid medium** — first potato and gelatin, then **agar jelly** (suggested in 1881 by **Fanny Hesse**, wife of one of his team); the shallow **Petri dish** was designed by his assistant **Julius Petri** in 1887.
- **1882 — tuberculosis.** He identified the bacterium that causes **TB**, one of the biggest killers in Victorian Britain.
- **1883 — cholera.** He identified the **cholera bacterium** (in Egypt and India), confirming that it spread through **contaminated water** — as John Snow had argued in 1854.
- His methods allowed his team and other scientists to identify the microbes behind many diseases within about twenty years — including typhoid, diphtheria, tetanus and plague.
- **Koch's postulates** — his rules for proving that a particular microbe causes a particular disease (find it in every case, grow it in pure culture, reproduce the disease with it, recover it again) — gave bacteriology a **scientific method**.

**The rivalry.** After France's defeat by Prussia in the **Franco-Prussian War (1870–71)**, national pride made Pasteur and Koch bitter rivals. Both **governments funded** their research and gave them teams and laboratories (Koch's Institute for Infectious Diseases opened in Berlin in 1891). The competition speeded up discoveries — a good example of **government** and **attitudes in society** (nationalism) working together with **science and technology**.

### The influence of germ theory and Koch's work in Britain

Germ theory did **not** transform British medicine overnight.

**Slow acceptance:**
- Many British doctors went on believing in **spontaneous generation** and **miasma** into the 1870s and 1880s. The London physician **Henry Charlton Bastian** was a leading public defender of spontaneous generation in the 1870s, while the physicist **John Tyndall** supported Pasteur in lectures and experiments.
- Germ theory was hard to accept: microbes were tiny and, to most people, invisible; it was hard to believe something so small could kill a grown man; and before Koch it could not name the germ behind any human disease.
- Public health reform in the 1850s–70s (Chadwick, Bazalgette's sewers) was driven mainly by **miasma** thinking — the smell of sewage — not by germ theory.

**Real influence (mainly from the late 1860s onwards):**
- **Surgery:** in **1865** the surgeon **Joseph Lister** in Glasgow read Pasteur's work and reasoned that germs in the air caused wounds to go septic. His **carbolic acid antiseptic** method (published in *The Lancet* in **1867**) was a direct application of germ theory, and led on to **aseptic** surgery in the 1890s (see the next lesson).
- **Identifying and preventing disease:** once Koch's methods identified specific bacteria, doctors could **diagnose** diseases in the laboratory and scientists could develop **vaccines** against them. In Britain, **Almroth Wright** developed a **typhoid vaccine** (1896) at the Army Medical School at Netley; it was offered to soldiers in the Boer War (1899–1902) and used on a large scale in the First World War.
- **Public health:** by the 1880s–90s germ theory gave a scientific reason for clean water, sewers, disinfection, and isolating infectious patients, and supported the work of the **Medical Officers of Health** required by the **Public Health Act (1875)**.
- **By 1900** germ theory was accepted by the medical profession — but there were still almost **no cures** for bacterial diseases once caught. Treatment only changed with magic bullets (1909) and antibiotics in the twentieth century.

**Institutions that spread new ideas in Britain:** the medical journal ***The Lancet*** (founded 1823) and the **British Medical Association** (named in 1856) circulated research; the **Medical Act (1858)** created the **General Medical Council** and a register of qualified doctors, making medicine more scientific and professional.

### Key individual: Edward Jenner and vaccination

**The problem — smallpox.** Smallpox was one of the most feared diseases of the eighteenth century: it killed large numbers, especially children, and survivors were often scarred or blinded. It was highly infectious and there was no cure.

**Inoculation (variolation)** was the existing method of prevention. **Lady Mary Wortley Montagu** had seen it in the Ottoman Empire and had her daughter inoculated in London in **1721**. A doctor made a cut in a healthy person's skin and rubbed in pus from a smallpox sufferer's scab, giving a (usually) **mild dose of smallpox** that left the person immune. It was popular but **risky**: some people died, and inoculated people could spread full smallpox to others. Inoculators such as the **Sutton family** made good money from it.

**Edward Jenner** (1749–1823) was a country doctor in **Berkeley, Gloucestershire**. He had trained under **John Hunter** in London, who encouraged him to observe and experiment, and he was a Fellow of the Royal Society.

**What he did:**
- He investigated the local country belief that **milkmaids who caught cowpox** (a mild disease caught from cows) **did not catch smallpox**.
- On **14 May 1796** he took pus from a cowpox blister on the hand of the milkmaid **Sarah Nelmes** and inserted it into cuts on the arm of **James Phipps**, the eight-year-old son of his gardener. Phipps caught a mild dose of cowpox.
- In **July 1796** Jenner **inoculated Phipps with smallpox** — and he did not catch it. He repeated the test on other patients, recording **23 cases** in all, including his own infant son.
- The **Royal Society** refused to publish his paper (1797), so in **1798** he published it himself: *An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae*. From the Latin *vacca* (cow) came the word **vaccination**.

**Opposition:**
- **Inoculators** feared losing their income.
- Some doctors could not **repeat** his results, often because the cowpox they used was contaminated with smallpox, and some patients died.
- Many people thought it **unnatural or ungodly** to give humans a disease from an animal; the cartoonist **James Gillray** mocked vaccination in **1802** with a picture of vaccinated people sprouting cows' heads from their bodies.
- **Jenner could not explain how it worked** — germs were unknown until Pasteur's work over sixty years later — so critics could dismiss it.
- The idea of a government **forcing** people to be vaccinated clashed with the **laissez-faire** belief that government should not interfere in people's lives.

**Support and spread:**
- Jenner sent vaccine free to anyone who asked; vaccination spread quickly across Europe and to America (Thomas Jefferson supported it; Napoleon had his army vaccinated).
- **Parliament** gave Jenner **£10,000 in 1802** and **£20,000 in 1807** to continue his work.

**Government action on vaccination — the slow shift from laissez-faire:**

| Date | Law or event |
|---|---|
| 1796 | Jenner vaccinates James Phipps |
| 1798 | Jenner publishes his *Inquiry* |
| 1802 / 1807 | Parliament grants Jenner £10,000 and £20,000 |
| 1840 | Vaccination Act: **free vaccination** for infants; **inoculation banned** |
| 1853 | Vaccination made **compulsory** for infants, with fines for parents who refused |
| 1871 | Local **vaccination officers** appointed to enforce the law |
| 1885 | Mass anti-vaccination demonstration in **Leicester** |
| 1898 | Parents allowed to opt out as **conscientious objectors** |
| 1980 | The World Health Organization declares smallpox **eradicated** worldwide |

**Significance of Jenner:**
- It was the **first safe, scientific method of preventing** a deadly disease, based on observation and testing; smallpox deaths in Britain fell sharply once vaccination spread.
- It led to the **first compulsory government intervention in health** in Britain (1853) — a turning point for the role of government.
- **But** Jenner did not know *why* it worked, so the method **could not be applied to other diseases**. Only after germ theory did **Pasteur** (1879–85) show how to make vaccines for other diseases by weakening the germ — which is why Pasteur honoured Jenner by extending the word "vaccination".

### Factors: why did understanding change in this period?

| Factor | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Individuals** | Pasteur's experiments; Koch's methods; Jenner's observation and testing; Lister applying germ theory |
| **Science and technology** | achromatic microscope (1830); aniline dyes; agar and Petri dishes; photography |
| **Institutions — government** | French and German governments funded research teams; British Parliament rewarded Jenner, then made vaccination free (1840) and compulsory (1853) |
| **Attitudes in society** | nationalism after 1870–71 drove the rivalry; industry wanted answers; but laissez-faire, religious objections and loyalty to old ideas slowed acceptance in Britain |
| **Communication** | printed journals (*The Lancet*), scientific societies, international congresses (London 1881) spread ideas quickly |

**Judgement:** this was a genuine **turning point in knowledge** — germ theory is the basis of modern medicine — but in Britain before 1900 the change in **practice** was limited: prevention (vaccination, antiseptics, clean water) improved far more than **treatment**, and there were still no cures for most infections.

::: terms
- **Germ theory**: Pasteur's 1861 theory that microbes (germs) in the air cause decay, extended to the idea that specific germs cause specific diseases.
- **Spontaneous generation**: the older belief that microbes were created by decay and disease, rather than causing them.
- **Microbe**: a living organism too small to see without a microscope, such as a bacterium.
- **Bacteriology**: the scientific study of bacteria, founded on Koch's methods of staining, culturing and identifying microbes.
- **Culture**: microbes deliberately grown in the laboratory on a nutrient medium such as agar jelly.
- **Inoculation (variolation)**: deliberately giving a healthy person a mild dose of smallpox to make them immune; banned in Britain in 1840.
- **Vaccination**: giving a person a harmless or weakened form of a disease (first cowpox, later attenuated germs) so that the body becomes immune.
- **Attenuation**: weakening a germ in the laboratory so that it can be used safely as a vaccine, as Pasteur did with chicken cholera in 1879.
- **Laissez-faire**: the nineteenth-century belief that the government should not interfere in people's lives or the economy.
:::

::: example 4-mark difference
Explain one way in which ideas about the cause of disease in the late nineteenth century were different from ideas in the Renaissance period (c1500–c1700). (4)

In the Renaissance, people believed disease was caused by things they could not identify precisely: during the Great Plague of 1665 people blamed miasma, an imbalance of the Four Humours, God's punishment and even a comet seen in 1664. By the late nineteenth century, scientists could identify the exact cause of a disease: after Pasteur's germ theory (1861), Robert Koch used stained samples and agar cultures to prove that specific bacteria caused specific diseases, such as tuberculosis (1882) and cholera (1883). So the difference is that explanations had moved from general, unproven ideas to specific microbes proved in the laboratory.
:::

::: example 12-mark explain why
Explain why there was rapid progress in understanding the causes of disease in the years c1860–c1900. (12)
You may use: Louis Pasteur; microscopes. You must also use information of your own.

One reason was the work of **Louis Pasteur**. Asked in 1857 to find out why alcohol went sour, he showed that microbes caused fermentation, and his swan-necked flask experiments disproved spontaneous generation. His germ theory of 1861 argued that germs in the air cause decay and could cause disease. This changed the question scientists were asking: instead of looking for bad air, they began looking for the germ behind each disease.

A second reason was **better technology**. Joseph Jackson Lister's achromatic lens (1830) removed the blurred edges of older **microscopes**, so bacteria could be seen clearly at high magnification. Koch added staining with aniline dyes, photography through the microscope and pure cultures grown on agar. This meant a specific microbe could be isolated, shown to others, and proved to cause a disease — anthrax in 1876, TB in 1882 and cholera in 1883.

A third reason, of my own, is **government funding and national rivalry**. After the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, the French and German governments paid for laboratories and teams of scientists for Pasteur and Koch, and each side raced to beat the other. This competition is why the microbes behind diseases such as typhoid, diphtheria and plague were identified within about twenty years.

These factors worked together: Pasteur's theory gave scientists a direction, technology gave them the tools to prove it, and government money and nationalism gave them the resources and motive to move fast.

You may use: vaccination; the Public Health Act (1875). You must also use information of your own.

I partly agree: Jenner produced the first scientifically tested method of preventing a deadly disease, although in the long term Pasteur's work was more widely significant.

**Against — the Public Health Act.** The Public Health Act of 1875 forced local councils to provide clean water, remove sewage and appoint Medical Officers of Health. **Judgement.** Measured by *being first* and by forcing the government to take responsibility for health, Jenner was the most significant. Overall I partly agree: Jenner was the breakthrough, but his method stayed a one-off until germ theory made it a system.
