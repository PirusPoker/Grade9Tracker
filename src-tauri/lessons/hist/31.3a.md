## What it is

When Hitler became Chancellor on **30 January 1933** he was not a dictator. Only **three of the twelve** cabinet members were Nazis (Hitler, Wilhelm **Frick** as Interior Minister and Hermann **Göring** as minister without portfolio and Prussian Interior Minister). Franz von **Papen**, the Vice-Chancellor, boasted that the conservatives had "hired" Hitler and would box him in. President **Hindenburg** could still sack him, the army swore loyalty to the constitution, and the Nazis had no majority in the Reichstag. Within **nineteen months** all of those limits had gone. This lesson explains how.

### The Reichstag Fire, 27 February 1933

Hitler had persuaded Hindenburg to call a new election for **5 March 1933**, hoping for a majority. During the campaign the Reichstag building in Berlin burned down on the night of 27 February. **Marinus van der Lubbe**, a young Dutch communist, was arrested at the scene and confessed. He was tried at Leipzig, found guilty and executed in **January 1934**. Four other communists (including the Bulgarian Georgi Dimitrov) were **acquitted** — which angered Hitler and is one reason he later created the People's Court.

**Who started it?** Historians still disagree. Some (for example Fritz Tobias in the 1960s) argued that van der Lubbe acted alone; others argue that the Nazis were involved or at least knew. For the exam the key point is that it does not matter much: **Hitler exploited the fire** whoever lit it.

**How Hitler used it:**
- He told Hindenburg the fire was the signal for a communist uprising. On **28 February** Hindenburg signed, under **Article 48**, the **Decree for the Protection of the People and the State** (the "Reichstag Fire Decree"). It suspended freedom of speech, of the press and of assembly, allowed the police to search homes and open post, and allowed people to be held in "protective custody" **without trial**. It also let the national government take over state governments.
- Around **4,000 communists** were arrested, including leading KPD deputies; communist newspapers were shut down and SPD papers were also banned.
- Göring had already enrolled about **50,000** SA and SS men in Prussia as **auxiliary police**, so Nazi thugs now had legal authority.
- The decree was never withdrawn: it remained the legal basis of the police state until 1945.

**The election of 5 March 1933**: despite the terror and propaganda, the Nazis won only **43.9%** of the vote and **288 seats**. With their allies the DNVP (52 seats) they had a small majority — but not the **two-thirds** needed to change the constitution.

### The Enabling Act, 23 March 1933

The Enabling Act allowed the Chancellor to pass laws **without the Reichstag's consent** and without the President's signature, for **four years**. Because it changed the constitution it needed a two-thirds majority. Hitler got it by:
- **Removing opponents**: the **81 KPD deputies** were under arrest, in hiding or barred; some SPD deputies were also absent.
- **Intimidation**: the Reichstag met in the **Kroll Opera House**, surrounded by SA and SS men chanting threats.
- **A deal with the Centre Party**: Hitler promised to respect the rights of the Catholic Church, so the Centre Party voted in favour.

The Act passed by **444 votes to 94**. Only the SPD voted against; its leader **Otto Wels** made a brave speech defending democracy. The Reichstag now became a rubber stamp — it met only a handful of times more to hear Hitler's speeches. Historians often call this the **"legal revolution"**: the dictatorship was built using the Weimar constitution's own procedures.

### Gleichschaltung: the banning of trade unions and other parties

The Nazi word for bringing every institution under Nazi control was **Gleichschaltung** ("coordination").
- **Trade unions**: on **1 May 1933** Hitler declared a paid national holiday for workers; on **2 May** the SA and SS occupied trade union offices, arrested leaders and seized union funds. Unions were banned and replaced (10 May) by the **German Labour Front (DAF)** under **Robert Ley**. Strikes and wage bargaining ended — a major gain for employers.
- **Political parties**: the **KPD** had effectively been crushed after the fire and its property was seized. The **SPD** was banned on **22 June 1933**. The others — the DNVP, the Centre Party and the rest — **dissolved themselves** by early July. The **Law against the Formation of New Parties (14 July 1933)** made the Nazis the only legal party. In the November 1933 election voters were offered a single Nazi list.
- **Local government**: in 1933 the states (Länder) were given Nazi **Reich governors**, and in **January 1934** the state parliaments were abolished.

### The threat from Röhm and the SA

By 1934 the **SA** (stormtroopers) had around **3 million** members — far larger than the **100,000**-man army allowed by Versailles. Its leader, **Ernst Röhm**, was one of Hitler's oldest comrades, but he had become a problem:
- **Röhm wanted the SA to absorb the army** under his command. The army's generals, proud and aristocratic, despised the SA as street brawlers and refused.
- **A "second revolution"**: many SA men came from the working class and took the "socialist" part of National Socialism seriously. They wanted attacks on big business and the old elites — exactly the people Hitler now needed.
- **Violence and indiscipline**: SA beatings, "wild" camps and drunkenness embarrassed the regime.
- **Timing**: Hindenburg was 86 and dying. To succeed him Hitler needed the **army's support**. In June 1934 Papen's **Marburg speech** criticised the SA's violence, and it was widely believed Hindenburg might declare martial law and hand power to the army if Hitler did not act.
- **Rivals pushed Hitler**: **Himmler** (SS) and **Göring** wanted to destroy the SA and fed Hitler rumours that Röhm was planning a coup.

### The Night of the Long Knives, 30 June – 2 July 1934

On **30 June 1934** Hitler flew to Munich and went with SS men to **Bad Wiessee**, where Röhm and other SA leaders were staying. Röhm was arrested; SA leaders were shot by **SS** squads over the next two days. Röhm was shot in his cell on **1 July** after refusing to kill himself.

Hitler also settled old scores: former Chancellor **Kurt von Schleicher** (and his wife), **Gregor Strasser** (the former Nazi organiser who had quarrelled with Hitler), **Gustav von Kahr** (who had crushed the Munich Putsch in 1923), and members of Papen's staff including **Edgar Jung**, author of the Marburg speech. The official death toll was under a hundred; historians believe the real number ran into the **hundreds**.

A law of **3 July** declared the killings legal as "self-defence of the state". On **13 July** Hitler told the Reichstag that in that hour he had been responsible for the fate of the German people and had therefore become its "supreme judge" — in other words, **Hitler's will was now above the law**.

**Consequences:**
- The **SA** was reduced to a minor role under a new leader, Viktor Lutze.
- The **SS** became independent of the SA (July 1934) and rose to become the regime's main instrument of terror.
- The **army** was relieved and grateful — it had supplied weapons and transport and now backed Hitler.
- Every German learned that Hitler would **murder** opponents, even inside his own party.

### Hindenburg's death, the Führer and the army oath

**Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934.** A law passed the day before merged the offices of **President and Chancellor**; Hitler became **Führer** (leader) and **Reich Chancellor**, and therefore **supreme commander of the armed forces**. The same day every soldier swore a **personal oath of loyalty to Adolf Hitler** — not to Germany or the constitution. This mattered enormously: the army, the one force that could have removed Hitler, was now bound to him by a personal oath that many officers took seriously. A **plebiscite on 19 August 1934** approved the change with about **90%** voting yes.

### Why Hitler succeeded: the analysis a grade 9 answer needs

- **Legality**: every step had a legal cover (Article 48, the Enabling Act, the 3 July law), which reassured conservatives, civil servants and judges.
- **Terror**: SA and SS violence, protective custody and the first camps (Dachau opened in March 1933) removed and frightened opponents.
- **Exploiting events**: the fire and the Röhm "plot" were turned into opportunities.
- **Weakness and division of opponents**: the KPD and SPD had never cooperated; the Centre Party and DNVP chose to cooperate and then dissolved themselves.
- **Support from elites**: the army and big business preferred Hitler to Röhm and to communism.
- **Hindenburg**: he signed the February decree and did not stop the Enabling Act; his death removed the last constitutional check.

::: terms
- **Reichstag Fire Decree**: the Decree for the Protection of the People and the State (28 February 1933), which suspended civil rights and allowed arrest without trial.
- **Enabling Act**: the law of March 1933 giving Hitler's government the power to make laws without the Reichstag for four years.
- **Gleichschaltung**: "coordination" — bringing every organisation in Germany (states, unions, parties, clubs) under Nazi control.
- **German Labour Front (DAF)**: the Nazi organisation led by Robert Ley that replaced the banned trade unions in May 1933.
- **SA**: the Sturmabteilung or stormtroopers — the Nazis' brown-shirted paramilitary force, led by Ernst Röhm.
- **Second revolution**: the SA's demand for a more radical, anti-business, socialist transformation after 1933.
- **Night of the Long Knives**: the purge of 30 June – 2 July 1934 in which Röhm and other SA leaders and political enemies were murdered.
- **Führer**: "leader" — Hitler's title after he merged the offices of President and Chancellor in August 1934.
- **Army oath**: the personal oath of loyalty to Hitler sworn by every soldier from 2 August 1934.
- **Plebiscite**: a national vote on a single question, used by the Nazis to show apparent public approval.
:::

Section A: Q1 inference (4) and one "Explain why" question with two prompts (12; recent papers such as Summer 2024 set it as a single compulsory question). ::: example Q2 — Explain why Hitler was able to consolidate power
Explain why Hitler was able to establish a dictatorship in the years 1933–34. You may use the following in your answer: the Reichstag Fire; the Enabling Act. You must also use information of your own. (12)

One reason was that Hitler exploited the Reichstag Fire of 27 February 1933. A Dutch communist, van der Lubbe, was arrested at the scene, and Hitler persuaded Hindenburg that it was the start of a communist rising. The next day Hindenburg signed the Decree for the Protection of the People and the State under Article 48, suspending freedom of speech and the press and allowing arrest without trial. About 4,000 communists were arrested. This removed the Nazis' most determined opponents just before the March election and gave the police state its legal foundation.

A second reason was the Enabling Act of March 1933. Although the Nazis won only 43.9% in the March election, Hitler secured the two-thirds majority needed by barring the 81 communist deputies, surrounding the Kroll Opera House with SA and SS men and winning over the Centre Party with promises to protect the Catholic Church. The Act passed by 444 votes to 94 and let Hitler make laws without the Reichstag. This mattered because he could now destroy other institutions legally: trade unions were abolished on 2 May 1933 and, by the Law against the Formation of New Parties in July, Germany was a one-party state.

A third reason, and the one that removed the final threats, was the Night of the Long Knives in June 1934. Röhm's 3-million-strong SA wanted to absorb the army and carry out a "second revolution", which alarmed the generals and big business. By having Röhm and other SA leaders shot, Hitler won the army's gratitude. So when Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934, Hitler could merge the offices of President and Chancellor and the army swore a personal oath of loyalty to him. Hitler's success therefore depended on combining legal methods, terror and the support of the elites: legality reassured the conservatives, while terror silenced everyone else.
:::

::: example Q3(d) — how far do you agree
Interpretation 1 (a summary of the view of historians who stress legality): Hitler's dictatorship was built above all through legal measures — the emergency decree, the Enabling Act and the merging of offices — which persuaded conservatives and civil servants to accept it. Interpretation 2 (a summary of the view of historians who stress violence): the dictatorship was created mainly through terror, as the SA and SS intimidated and removed anyone who might resist. How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about how Hitler created a dictatorship in 1933–34? Explain your answer, using both interpretations and your knowledge of the historical context. (16)

I agree with Interpretation 2 to a large extent, because terror made each "legal" step possible — but Interpretation 1 is right that legality was what made the dictatorship acceptable to the people who mattered most.

Interpretation 2 is supported by the role of violence at every stage. After the Reichstag Fire around 4,000 communists were arrested and held without trial; Göring had already made about 50,000 SA and SS men auxiliary police. The Enabling Act was passed in the Kroll Opera House with stormtroopers surrounding the building and the 81 communist deputies absent. Trade unions were destroyed when the SA occupied their offices on 2 May 1933, and Dachau opened in March 1933. Finally the Night of the Long Knives showed Hitler would murder even Röhm and former Chancellor Schleicher.

However, Interpretation 1 is also convincing. The terror itself was legalised: the Reichstag Fire Decree was signed by Hindenburg under Article 48, and the Night of the Long Knives was declared legal by the law of 3 July 1934. The Enabling Act was passed by 444 votes to 94, so Hitler could claim constitutional authority, which is why the Centre Party voted for it and why most other parties dissolved themselves rather than being banned. The army oath of August 1934 was sworn because the generals accepted Hitler as legal head of state, and the plebiscite gave about 90% approval.

Overall I agree with Interpretation 2 more than Interpretation 1, because the "legal" steps only worked because opponents had first been removed or frightened: without the arrests there would have been no two-thirds majority. But terror alone would not have won the army and the civil service; it was the combination — violence given a legal disguise — that created the dictatorship so quickly.
