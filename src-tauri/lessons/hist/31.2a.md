## What it is

This lesson covers the first two parts of Key topic 2, Hitler's rise to power 1919–33:
1. **The early development of the Nazi Party, 1920–22**: Hitler's early career, joining the German Workers' Party and setting up the Nazi Party (1919–20); the **Twenty-Five Point Programme**; the role of the **SA**.
2. **The Munich Putsch and the Nazi Party, 1923–28**: the **reasons for, events and consequences of the Munich Putsch**; ***Mein Kampf***; the **reasons for limited support for the Nazis, 1924–28**; and **party reorganisation**, including the **Bamberg Conference of 1926**.

The big idea: in 1919–23 the Nazis were a small, violent Bavarian party that tried to seize power by force and failed. In 1924–28 Hitler rebuilt the party as a national organisation under his total control, aiming to win power legally, but while the Republic was prosperous, few Germans voted for it.

### 1. Hitler's early career, 1889–1919

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **20 April 1889** | Born in **Braunau am Inn, Austria**, the son of a customs official |
| **1907–08** | Twice rejected by the **Vienna Academy of Fine Arts**; lived in poverty in Vienna, where he absorbed extreme **German nationalism** and **antisemitism** |
| **1913** | Moved to **Munich**, in Bavaria |
| **1914–18** | Volunteered for the Bavarian army; served on the Western Front as a **regimental dispatch runner**; wounded in 1916; awarded the **Iron Cross (First Class)** in 1918, a rare honour for a corporal |
| **Oct–Nov 1918** | Temporarily blinded in a British gas attack; in hospital at **Pasewalk** when he heard of the armistice. He came to believe the army had been **"stabbed in the back"** by the "November criminals": socialists, democrats and Jews |
| **1919** | Stayed in the army in Munich, working for its political department as an **informant**, reporting on the many small political groups there |

### 2. Joining the German Workers' Party and setting up the Nazi Party, 1919–20

- The **German Workers' Party (DAP)** was founded in Munich in **January 1919** by a railway locksmith, **Anton Drexler**. It was nationalist, antisemitic, anti-communist and anti-Weimar, but tiny.
- In **September 1919** the army sent Hitler to observe a DAP meeting. He intervened angrily in a debate; Drexler was impressed and invited him to join. Hitler joined later that month (his membership number was 555, because the numbering began at 501 to make the party look bigger).
- Hitler quickly proved to be a gifted, passionate **public speaker**, and he was put in charge of **propaganda**. Meetings grew from dozens to hundreds and then thousands.
- On **24 February 1920**, at a mass meeting of about 2,000 people in the **Hofbräuhaus** beer hall in Munich, Hitler presented the party's **Twenty-Five Point Programme**, written with Drexler. The party changed its name to the **National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP)**, the Nazi Party. Hitler left the army in March 1920 to work for the party full time.
- In **1920** the party adopted the **swastika** flag and in December bought a newspaper, the ***Völkischer Beobachter*** (People's Observer).
- In **July 1921**, after a dispute with the founders, Hitler threatened to resign. The party gave in and he became **party leader (Führer)** with near-total power; Drexler was given an honorary title.

### 3. The Twenty-Five Point Programme (1920)

The programme mixed **nationalism**, **racism** and some **socialist** ideas designed to appeal to workers.

| Theme | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Nationalism** | Abolish the **Treaty of Versailles**; unite all German-speaking people in a **Greater Germany**; land and colonies to feed the people and settle surplus population (later **Lebensraum**) |
| **Racism** | Only people of **German blood** could be citizens, so **no Jew could be a citizen**; non-citizens could be expelled; stop non-German immigration |
| **Socialism** | Nationalise large businesses (trusts); share profits of large industries; abolish unearned income; generous old-age pensions; land reform; the death penalty for profiteers and usurers |
| **Strong government** | A strong central government with unconditional authority; a national army instead of the small professional army; control of the press |

Why it mattered: it gave the party a clear programme with something for many groups (nationalists, workers, the lower middle class, people who blamed the Jews and the Treaty for Germany's problems). Hitler later made little use of the socialist points, which alarmed businessmen, but he refused to change the programme officially.

### 4. The role of the SA

- The **SA (Sturmabteilung, "storm division")**, also called the **stormtroopers** or **Brownshirts** from their uniform, was set up in **1921**. Many members were ex-soldiers and ex-**Freikorps** men; many were unemployed young men who liked the uniform, comradeship and violence. **Ernst Röhm** was a key organiser, and **Hermann Göring** became its leader in 1923.
- **Roles:** protecting Nazi meetings and speakers; **disrupting opponents' meetings**, especially those of the communists and socialists; street fighting and intimidation; marching and parading to give an image of strength, discipline and order; giving young men a sense of purpose and belonging.
- **Significance:** the SA made the Nazis visible and feared, and it helped the party grow. But it also gave the party a reputation for thuggery, and its members were hard to control, a problem that returned in 1934.

By 1923 the party had grown to tens of thousands of members, mainly in Bavaria (a figure of about 55,000 is often quoted).

### 5. The Munich Putsch, 8–9 November 1923

**Reasons (why Hitler attempted it).**
- **Long-term:** Hitler's hatred of the Republic, the "November criminals" and the Treaty of Versailles; his belief in taking power by force.
- **The crisis of 1923:** the French and Belgian occupation of the **Ruhr** (January), **hyperinflation**, and then Stresemann's decision to **call off passive resistance** (September 1923), which nationalists saw as a surrender. The Republic seemed weak and unpopular.
- **The situation in Bavaria:** Bavaria had a right-wing government hostile to Berlin. The state commissioner **Gustav von Kahr**, army commander **Otto von Lossow** and police chief **Hans von Seisser** were themselves discussing action against Berlin, so Hitler hoped they would join him.
- **Mussolini's example:** Mussolini's "March on Rome" in October 1922 had brought the Fascists to power in Italy. Hitler planned a "march on Berlin".
- **Nazi strength and impatience:** membership and the SA had grown; the war hero **General Ludendorff** supported Hitler; and Hitler feared that if he waited, his followers would lose enthusiasm or Kahr would act without him.

**Events.**

| Time | What happened |
|---|---|
| **8 Nov, evening** | Kahr was addressing about 3,000 people in the **Bürgerbräukeller**, a Munich beer hall. Hitler burst in with about 600 SA men, fired a shot into the ceiling and announced that the "national revolution" had begun |
| | Hitler took Kahr, Lossow and Seisser into a side room and, with Ludendorff's help, pressured them into agreeing to support him. They were allowed to leave |
| **Night of 8–9 Nov** | Röhm's men occupied the army headquarters, but Kahr, Lossow and Seisser withdrew their support and called in the army and police |
| **9 Nov** | Hitler and Ludendorff led about 2,000–3,000 Nazis on a march into central Munich. At the **Odeonsplatz** they were met by armed police. Shots were fired: **16 Nazis and 4 police officers were killed** |
| **11 Nov** | Hitler, who had injured his shoulder and fled, was **arrested** two days later. The party was banned |

**Consequences.**
- **Short term: failure.** The putsch collapsed within hours. The Nazi Party and its newspaper were banned, and its leaders arrested.
- **The trial (February–April 1924)** was a propaganda gift. Hitler was allowed to make long speeches attacking the Republic and the "November criminals", and they were reported across Germany. He became a nationally known figure.
- **Lenient sentence:** on 1 April 1924 Hitler was found guilty of **treason** and sentenced to **five years** in **Landsberg Prison**, the minimum sentence; Ludendorff was acquitted. The judges were sympathetic to the nationalist right. Hitler was released in **December 1924** after serving about nine months.
- **Mein Kampf.** In prison Hitler dictated ***Mein Kampf*** ("My Struggle") to **Rudolf Hess**. It was published in two volumes, in 1925 and 1926.
- **A change of strategy.** Hitler concluded that power could not be seized by force against the army and police. The Nazis would instead **win power legally, through elections**, and destroy democracy from within.
- **A Nazi legend.** The dead marchers were later honoured as martyrs, and the putsch became part of Nazi mythology.

### 6. Mein Kampf: the main ideas

- **Race:** the **Aryans** were a "master race"; other races, and above all the **Jews**, were inferior and dangerous. Hitler blamed the Jews for Germany's defeat and for communism.
- **Lebensraum (living space):** Germany needed land in **eastern Europe**, especially Russia, to be taken by force.
- **Nationalism:** overturn the **Treaty of Versailles** and unite all Germans in one state.
- **Anti-communism and anti-democracy:** democracy was weak and divisive.
- **The Führer principle:** Germany should be ruled by one all-powerful leader.
- **Propaganda:** simple messages repeated constantly were the way to win the masses.

Why it mattered: it set out the ideas Hitler later tried to carry out, and it gave the party an ideology. Sales were modest at first but huge after 1933.

### 7. The "lean years": reasons for limited support, 1924–28

| Election | Nazi result |
|---|---|
| **May 1924** | A Nazi-backed alliance, the National Socialist Freedom Movement, won 32 seats (6.5 per cent), a protest vote during the 1923 crisis |
| **December 1924** | 14 seats (3 per cent) |
| **May 1928** | **12 seats (2.6 per cent)**, about 810,000 votes |

**Reasons for limited support.**
- **Economic recovery.** Under Stresemann, the Rentenmark, the Dawes Plan and American loans brought prosperity. Rising wages, better housing and unemployment insurance (lesson 31.1d) meant fewer people were desperate enough to vote for extremists. The Nazis did best when Germany was in crisis.
- **Foreign policy success.** Locarno (1925) and League membership (1926) restored Germany's pride, weakening Nazi attacks on the "November criminals".
- **Hitler out of action.** He was in prison for most of 1924, the party was banned until February 1925, and afterwards he was **banned from public speaking** in most German states (in Bavaria until 1927 and in Prussia until 1928), removing the party's biggest asset.
- **Divisions.** While Hitler was in prison the party split into rival factions. Even after 1925 the northern wing under **Gregor Strasser** wanted more socialist policies than Hitler.
- **The failed putsch** had made the Nazis look like violent, foolish extremists to many voters.
- **Competition:** the conservative nationalist **DNVP** offered a respectable home for nationalist voters and joined governments in the mid-1920s.

### 8. Party reorganisation, 1925–28, and the Bamberg Conference

- **Refounding.** Hitler refounded the party at the Bürgerbräukeller on **27 February 1925**, insisting on his total control.
- **The Bamberg Conference, 14 February 1926.** Hitler called party leaders to Bamberg to settle the split with the northern, more socialist wing led by **Gregor Strasser** and his secretary **Joseph Goebbels**, who wanted to change the programme (for example, to support taking the property of the former German princes). Hitler spoke for hours and won: the **Twenty-Five Points were confirmed** as unchangeable, and his authority as sole leader was established. This was the **Führerprinzip**, the leader principle: the Führer decided, and members obeyed. Strasser accepted the result and became a key party organiser, and Goebbels switched to Hitler's side; he was made **Gauleiter of Berlin** later in 1926.
- **A national structure.** The party was divided into regions called **Gaue**, each run by a **Gauleiter** loyal to Hitler, with local branches below them. This gave the party a presence across Germany instead of mainly in Bavaria.
- **New organisations.** The **SS** (Schutzstaffel) was set up in **1925** as Hitler's personal bodyguard, loyal to him rather than to the SA. The **Hitler Youth** was set up in **1926**, and organisations for students, women and professional groups followed, spreading the Nazi message to particular groups.
- **Propaganda.** The party worked on its message and organisation, ready to exploit any new crisis; after the 1928 result it focused more attention on rural areas, where farmers were suffering.
- **Membership grew** from about **27,000 in 1925** to about **108,000 in 1928**, even though the vote stayed small.

### Overall judgement

The years 1919–28 turned a tiny Munich debating group into a national, disciplined party under a single leader, with a programme, a newspaper, a private army, a bodyguard and a nationwide organisation. The Munich Putsch failed as a revolution but succeeded as publicity, and it taught Hitler to seek power legally. The lean years show that the Nazis could not win while the Republic was stable; what they had built was ready to take advantage when the Depression struck in 1929.

::: terms
- **German Workers' Party (DAP)**: the small nationalist party founded by Anton Drexler in Munich in January 1919, which Hitler joined that September.
- **NSDAP**: the National Socialist German Workers' Party, the name the DAP took in February 1920; the Nazi Party.
- **Twenty-Five Point Programme**: the Nazi programme of February 1920, combining nationalism, racism and some socialist ideas.
- **SA (Sturmabteilung)**: the Nazi stormtroopers or Brownshirts, set up in 1921 to protect Nazi meetings and attack opponents.
- **Putsch**: an attempt to seize power by force, like Hitler's failed Munich Putsch of 8–9 November 1923.
- **Mein Kampf**: "My Struggle", the book Hitler dictated in Landsberg Prison in 1924, setting out his ideas on race, Lebensraum and leadership.
- **Lebensraum**: "living space"; the land in eastern Europe that Hitler believed Germany must conquer for its people.
- **Führerprinzip**: the leader principle, under which the Führer had total authority and party members owed him complete obedience.
- **Gauleiter**: the Nazi Party leader of a Gau, one of the regional divisions of the party created after 1925.
- **Bamberg Conference**: the meeting of Nazi leaders on 14 February 1926 at which Hitler defeated the Strasser wing and confirmed his total control.
:::

- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation with some accurate knowledge.
- Level 3 (7–9): explanation mainly focused on the question with accurate knowledge.
- Level 4 (10–12): analytical explanation, sustained focus, precise knowledge and clear links between reasons.
Write three reason paragraphs (point, precise evidence, explain how it caused the outcome), then link them: for example, the 1923 crisis made the Republic look weak, but Hitler only acted because he expected Kahr's support.

::: example Explain why: the Munich Putsch (12)
Explain why Hitler attempted to seize power in Munich in November 1923. You may use the following in your answer: hyperinflation; Gustav von Kahr. You must also use information of your own. (12)

**The crisis of 1923.** In 1923 the Weimar Republic faced its worst crisis. French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr in January, and the government's policy of paying striking workers led to hyperinflation, with the mark collapsing to over four trillion to the US dollar by November. Savings were wiped out and many Germans blamed the Republic. When Stresemann called off passive resistance in September 1923, nationalists saw it as a surrender to France. This made Hitler believe the Republic was so unpopular that Germans would welcome a nationalist revolution.

**The situation in Bavaria and von Kahr.** Bavaria had a right-wing government that was hostile to Berlin. The state commissioner Gustav von Kahr, the army commander von Lossow and the police chief von Seisser were themselves talking about moving against the national government. Hitler expected that if he forced their hand they would join him, bringing the Bavarian army and police with them. This mattered because without their support, the Nazis alone could not hope to take power.

**Nazi strength and Mussolini's example.** The Nazi Party had grown to tens of thousands of members and had its own private army, the SA, while the war hero General Ludendorff supported Hitler. Mussolini had come to power in Italy after the "March on Rome" in October 1922, showing that a nationalist leader could seize power by threatening force. Hitler also feared that if he did not act soon his SA would lose enthusiasm or Kahr would act without him.

**Links.** The crisis provided the opportunity and Mussolini the model, but the key reason Hitler acted in November was his belief that Kahr, Lossow and Seisser would back him. That is also why the putsch failed: when they withdrew their support overnight, the army and police stood against him.
:::

::: example 16-mark paragraph: consequences of the putsch (part answer)
Interpretation 2 (a summary of a historian's view): the Munich Putsch was a complete failure that left Hitler and the Nazi Party in ruins. How far do you agree? Write the "disagree" paragraph. (part of a 16-mark answer)

Interpretation 2 is convincing about the short term but ignores the putsch's long-term benefits for Hitler. His trial in February–April 1924 gave him a national platform: newspapers across Germany reported his speeches attacking the "November criminals", turning a Bavarian agitator into a national figure. The sympathetic judges gave him the minimum five-year sentence, and he served only about nine months in Landsberg, where he dictated Mein Kampf, giving the movement a clear ideology. Above all, the failure taught Hitler that he could not seize power against the army and police; he refounded the party in 1925 to win power legally, reorganising it into Gaue under loyal Gauleiter and asserting the Führerprinzip at Bamberg in 1926. As Interpretation 1 suggests, the putsch was a failure as a revolution but a turning point in the Nazis' route to power.

---
Set up in 1921 and made up largely of ex-soldiers, ex-Freikorps and unemployed young men, the SA protected Nazi meetings and speakers, disrupted opponents' (especially communists') meetings, fought in the streets and intimidated opponents, and paraded to give an image of strength and order. ::: q
Why did the Munich Putsch fail?
---
