## What it is

When Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933, Germany was in the depths of the Depression: about **6 million** people were registered as unemployed — roughly one worker in three. Hitler had promised "work and bread", and reducing unemployment was vital to keep public support. By 1939 the Nazis claimed to have almost **ended unemployment**. This lesson explains **how** they did it — labour service, the autobahns, rearmament and "invisible unemployment" — and then asks whether German workers were actually **better off**, looking at the **Labour Front (DAF)**, **Strength Through Joy (KdF)** and **Beauty of Labour (SdA)**.

### Unemployment in Germany, 1933–39 (annual averages, millions)

| 1933 | 1934 | 1935 | 1936 | 1937 | 1938 | 1939 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.8 | 2.7 | 2.2 | 1.6 | 0.9 | 0.4 | 0.1 |

(The figure of about 6 million is the peak registered in early 1933; the annual average for 1933 was lower because the economy had begun to recover.) The fall was real and dramatic, but the official figures also hid some unemployment.

### Nazi policies to reduce unemployment

**1. The National Labour Service (Reichsarbeitsdienst, RAD)**
- Began as a voluntary scheme (inherited from the Weimar years) and was expanded under the Nazis. From **June 1935** six months' service was made **compulsory for all men aged 18–25**.
- Workers lived in **camps**, wore **uniforms**, did **military-style drill** and were paid only pocket money.
- They did public works: **planting forests, digging ditches, draining marshes**, repairing river banks and building roads.
- Many resented the low pay, poor food, hard labour and lack of freedom, but the RAD took hundreds of thousands of young men out of the unemployment figures, got them physically fit and prepared them for army service.

**2. Public works and the autobahns**
- The Nazis spent heavily on public works: public buildings, schools, hospitals, housing and, above all, **motorways (autobahns)**.
- The autobahn programme began in **September 1933** under engineer **Fritz Todt**. Around **3,000 km** of road had been completed by the end of the 1930s, and at the peak about **125,000** men worked directly on it, with many more jobs created in supplying steel, concrete and machinery.
- The autobahns had a propaganda value (modern Germany) and a military value (moving troops quickly).
- Work was deliberately labour-intensive: men were often used instead of machines to create more jobs.

**3. Rearmament**
- Rearmament was the biggest single cause of falling unemployment. Hitler broke the Treaty of Versailles openly: **conscription** was reintroduced in **March 1935**. The armed forces grew from the **100,000** allowed by Versailles to about **1.4 million** men by 1939, directly taking many men off the register.
- Spending on arms rose from about **3.5 billion Reichsmarks in 1933 to about 26 billion in 1939**. Factories making weapons, tanks, ships and aircraft took on hundreds of thousands of workers, and the **coal, iron, steel and chemical** industries boomed.
- From **1936** the **Four-Year Plan**, under **Hermann Göring**, aimed to make Germany ready for war within four years and self-sufficient (**autarky**), for example by developing synthetic rubber and oil. Göring's phrase "guns or butter" summed up the choice: resources went to weapons rather than consumer goods.

**4. "Invisible unemployment"**
The official figures were massaged. Groups not counted as unemployed included:
- **Women** dismissed from their jobs or encouraged to leave work to marry (see 31.4a);
- **Jewish people** dismissed from their jobs (for example from the civil service in 1933);
- **Men in the RAD** and those conscripted into the army;
- **Opponents** of the regime held in **concentration camps**;
- **Part-time workers**, who were counted as fully employed.
Historians call this hidden unemployment "invisible unemployment". It means the Nazi achievement was genuine but smaller than the propaganda claimed.

**Other factors**: world trade was recovering after 1932, and schemes started by the governments of Papen and Schleicher were already in place — so the Nazis inherited some of the recovery. Tax cuts and grants encouraged businesses to take on workers.

### Changes in the standard of living, especially for workers

**The Labour Front (Deutsche Arbeitsfront, DAF)**
- Set up in **May 1933** after the trade unions were banned (2 May). Led by **Robert Ley**. It had about **22 million** members by 1939 — membership was effectively compulsory for workers.
- It replaced unions **and** employers' organisations. Workers lost the right to **strike** and to bargain over pay; the DAF, with employers and state "Trustees of Labour", set wages and hours.
- It could discipline workers and, from **1935**, workers needed a **work book (Arbeitsbuch)** recording their employment; without one they could not be employed, and it made changing jobs harder.
- The DAF also ran the two schemes below to win workers' loyalty.

**Strength Through Joy (Kraft durch Freude, KdF)**, set up in **November 1933**
- Provided cheap **leisure**: subsidised holidays, **cruises** (for example to Norway and Madeira), theatre and concert tickets, sports facilities, hiking, day trips and evening classes.
- Millions of workers took part in its activities, and cruises gave some workers holidays that had been unthinkable before — though the best trips often went to Nazi party members and better-paid workers.
- **The Volkswagen ("people's car") scheme** (from 1938): workers could pay **5 Reichsmarks a week** towards a car costing **990 Reichsmarks**. Over 300,000 people paid in, but **no worker ever received a car** — the factory switched to military vehicles in 1939 and the money was never returned.
- Purposes: reward loyalty, keep workers content without raising wages, keep leisure under Nazi control, and show off the Volksgemeinschaft.

**Beauty of Labour (Schönheit der Arbeit, SdA)**, a department of KdF
- Campaigned to improve **working conditions**: better lighting, less noise, cleaner air, **washrooms**, **canteens** with hot meals, sports facilities and green spaces at factories.
- Improved conditions without having to raise pay; employers often expected **workers to do the improvements themselves** in their own time, unpaid.

### So were workers better off?

| Better off | Worse off |
|---|---|
| **Jobs**: unemployment fell from about 6 million to almost nothing — security mattered hugely after the Depression | **Longer hours**: the average working week rose from about **43 hours in 1933 to about 49 in 1939** |
| Average weekly wages rose from about **86 Reichsmarks (1932) to about 109 (1939)**, mainly through overtime | **Prices**, especially of food, rose; wages were effectively frozen, so **real wages** barely rose and only returned to 1928 levels around 1938 |
| Cheap holidays and leisure through KdF; improved workplaces through SdA | **No trade unions**, no right to strike; the work book restricted freedom to change jobs |
| Paid holidays increased | **Consumer goods** were in short supply ("guns or butter"); diets were plainer (more bread and potatoes, less meat, fat and fruit) |
| A sense of national revival and belonging in the Volksgemeinschaft | The Volkswagen savings were lost; KdF was a substitute for real pay rises |

**Who gained most?** Big business (armaments firms such as Krupp and IG Farben) made large profits and benefited from the end of unions. **Farmers** were helped at first by the Reich Food Estate and the 1933 Reich Entailed Farm Law, but farm labourers drifted to better-paid factory jobs. **Small businesses** were promised help against department stores but many suffered.

**The judgement a grade 9 answer needs**: workers gained **security** — a job — which after the Depression many valued above everything. But they lost **freedom**, worked longer for little real gain, and were compensated with leisure rather than money. Whether they were "better off" depends on what you measure: employment and morale, or wages, hours and rights.

::: terms
- **National Labour Service (RAD)**: the Reichsarbeitsdienst — from 1935 six months' compulsory labour for men aged 18–25, in camps and uniforms, on public works.
- **Autobahns**: the motorway network begun in 1933 under Fritz Todt, creating jobs and roads useful for moving troops.
- **Rearmament**: the build-up of Germany's armed forces and weapons, including conscription from March 1935 — the biggest cause of falling unemployment.
- **Invisible unemployment**: people left out of the official unemployment figures, such as women, Jews, men in the RAD or army, and prisoners in camps.
- **German Labour Front (DAF)**: the Nazi organisation under Robert Ley that replaced trade unions in May 1933 and controlled wages and hours.
- **Strength Through Joy (KdF)**: the DAF scheme (from November 1933) offering workers cheap holidays, cruises, outings and entertainment.
- **Beauty of Labour (SdA)**: a KdF department that campaigned to improve factories with better lighting, canteens and washrooms.
- **Volkswagen scheme**: the KdF plan letting workers pay 5 Reichsmarks a week for a 990-Reichsmark car that none of them ever received.
- **Real wages**: what wages can actually buy once price rises are taken into account.
- **Four-Year Plan**: Göring's plan from 1936 to prepare the economy for war and make Germany self-sufficient (autarky).
:::

::: example Q2 — Explain why unemployment fell
Explain why unemployment fell in Germany in the years 1933–39. You may use the following in your answer: the National Labour Service (RAD); rearmament. You must also use information of your own. (12)

One reason was the National Labour Service. From June 1935 all men aged 18–25 had to do six months in the RAD, living in camps, wearing uniform and doing drill, for pocket money. They planted forests, drained marshes and dug ditches. Because these young men were occupied they no longer counted as unemployed, and the scheme also prepared them for the army — which is why unemployment fell from 4.8 million in 1933 to 2.2 million by 1935 without the regime needing to pay full wages.

A second and more important reason was rearmament. Conscription was reintroduced in March 1935 and the armed forces grew from 100,000 to about 1.4 million by 1939, taking many men off the register directly. Spending on arms rose from about 3.5 billion to about 26 billion Reichsmarks between 1933 and 1939, and from 1936 Göring's Four-Year Plan pushed the economy towards war. Factories producing tanks, aircraft and ships, and the coal, iron and steel industries that supplied them, took on huge numbers of workers, so by 1938 unemployment was under half a million.

A third reason was that the figures themselves were manipulated — "invisible unemployment". Women pushed out of work to marry, Jews dismissed from their jobs, men in the RAD and army, and prisoners in concentration camps were not counted, and part-time workers counted as fully employed. Public works such as the autobahns, begun in 1933 under Fritz Todt, also created jobs, with about 125,000 men working on them at the peak. So unemployment fell because of real job creation, above all rearmament, but also because the Nazis changed who was counted as unemployed.
:::

::: example Q3(d) — how far do you agree (workers)
Interpretation 1 (a summary of the view of historians who stress gains): German workers were better off under the Nazis — they had secure jobs, cheap holidays and better workplaces, and most were grateful after the misery of the Depression. Interpretation 2 (a summary of the view of historians who stress losses): German workers lost out under the Nazis — they worked longer hours for wages that bought little more, and they lost their unions and their freedom. How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about the lives of German workers in the years 1933–39? Explain your answer, using both interpretations and your knowledge of the historical context. (16)

I agree with Interpretation 2 to a large extent about wages, hours and freedom, but Interpretation 1 is right that job security mattered most to many workers after the Depression.

Interpretation 2 is supported by evidence on hours and pay. The average working week rose from about 43 hours in 1933 to about 49 in 1939. Weekly wages rose from about 86 to 109 Reichsmarks, but mainly through overtime, and food prices rose too, so real wages barely returned to 1928 levels by 1938. Workers lost their trade unions on 2 May 1933; the German Labour Front under Robert Ley set wages with employers, strikes were banned and, from 1935, the work book restricted changing jobs. Consumer goods were short because of "guns or butter", and the Volkswagen scheme took workers' savings without delivering a single car.

However, Interpretation 1 has real strength. Unemployment fell from about 6 million in early 1933 to almost nothing by 1939, and for families who had suffered in the Depression a regular wage was a huge gain. Strength Through Joy gave millions cheap theatre tickets, outings and even cruises to Norway and Madeira, and Beauty of Labour brought canteens, washrooms and better lighting to many factories. Sopade reports admitted that many workers were grateful for work even while they grumbled.

Overall I agree with Interpretation 2 more, because the best measure of whether workers were better off is what their labour earned them and what rights they had, and on both they lost out. But Interpretation 1 explains why there was little resistance: the regime offered security and leisure in place of pay and freedom, and many workers accepted that bargain.
:::

::: q
Explain how the Volkswagen scheme worked and why it has been called a con.
---
Workers could pay 5 Reichsmarks a week into a savings scheme towards a "people's car" costing 990 Reichsmarks (1–2). Over 300,000 people paid in, but no worker received a car because the factory switched to military vehicles when war began in 1939, and the money was not returned (1–2). So workers' savings effectively funded the regime.

::: watch
- Treating the fall in unemployment as entirely genuine — you must mention **invisible unemployment** (women, Jews, RAD, army, camps, part-time workers).
- Forgetting that **rearmament** (conscription from 1935, arms spending to about 26 billion Reichsmarks by 1939, the Four-Year Plan) was the biggest cause, not the autobahns.
- Saying wages "went up" without discussing **prices and hours**: money wages rose mainly through overtime, and real wages barely rose.
- Confusing **KdF** (leisure: holidays, cruises, the Volkswagen) with **SdA** (improving workplaces) — SdA was part of KdF but did a different job.
- Writing about the DAF as if it were a trade union — it **replaced** the unions and served the regime and employers.
- In "were workers better off?" answers, failing to reach a judgement with a criterion — say *what* you are measuring (security, pay, freedom).
:::
