## What it is

This topic is the second half of the knowledge you need for the **historic environment** in Paper 1: *The British sector of the Western Front, 1914–18: injuries, treatment and the trenches*. It covers three spec points: **how the wounded were treated** (the RAMC, nurses, transport and the stages of the chain of evacuation, including the underground hospital at Arras); **why the Western Front mattered for experiments in surgery and medicine** (wound treatment, the Thomas splint, mobile X-rays, the blood bank at Cambrai); and the **historical context** of medicine in the early twentieth century (infection and aseptic surgery, X-rays, blood transfusion and storage). The terrain, the trenches and the types of wound are covered in 11.5a — here we look at what the army did about them.

### The big picture

In 1914 the British Army expected a short war of movement. Instead it got four years of static trench warfare in which artillery caused most wounds, the soil was heavily manured farmland full of bacteria, and hundreds of thousands of casualties had to be moved from muddy, shell-swept trenches to hospitals miles away. The army's medical services had to **grow enormously**, **organise a system** that sorted casualties quickly, and **invent or adopt new techniques** under pressure. The Western Front was, in effect, a huge medical laboratory — but most of the "new" techniques built on scientific knowledge that already existed before 1914.

### The work of the RAMC

The **Royal Army Medical Corps (RAMC)**, formed in **1898**, was the army's medical service: doctors (medical officers), orderlies, stretcher bearers and the staff of field ambulances, clearing stations and hospitals. It expanded massively during the war as volunteers and civilian doctors joined. Its work included:

- running every stage of the **chain of evacuation**, from the medical officer in the front line to the base hospitals;
- **triage** — sorting the wounded by urgency so that treatment went where it could do most good;
- **preventing disease** as well as treating wounds: sanitation, clean water, delousing, and inoculation against typhoid. Because of this, disease killed far fewer British soldiers on the Western Front than in earlier wars such as the Boer War;
- keeping **records** — unit war diaries, admission registers and casualty returns — which are now important sources (see 11.5c).

Every infantry **battalion** had its own medical officer and at least **16 stretcher bearers** (at first often the regimental bandsmen). During big battles this was nowhere near enough, and extra men were used.

### Nurses

Women played a vital role, mostly **behind the front line**, at casualty clearing stations, base hospitals, on ambulance trains and in Britain.

| Organisation | Who they were | What they did |
|---|---|---|
| **QAIMNS** (Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service), founded 1902 | Professionally trained army nurses — just under 300 in 1914, over 10,000 by 1918 | Nursed at base hospitals and, as the war went on, at casualty clearing stations close to the fighting; supervised VADs |
| **Territorial Force Nursing Service** | Trained nurses from civilian hospitals | Staffed territorial hospitals in Britain and served in France |
| **VADs** (Voluntary Aid Detachments), set up 1909 | Mainly middle-class volunteers with basic first-aid training | Cleaning, feeding, dressing wounds and general care; increasingly given more skilled work as the war went on |
| **FANY** (First Aid Nursing Yeomanry), founded 1907 | Volunteer women, many able to drive | Drove ambulances, ran field hospitals and soup kitchens; from **1916** officially drove ambulances for the British Army |

Nurses worked long shifts, faced shelling and air raids on hospitals behind the lines, and dealt with gas gangrene, burns and dying men. Their work helped change **attitudes** to women's professional abilities.

### Transport in the chain of evacuation

Moving the wounded was one of the hardest problems on the Western Front, because of the **terrain** (mud, shell holes, flooded ground around Ypres) and because the roads and railways behind the lines were also needed for troops and ammunition.

- **Stretcher bearers** carried men from no man's land and the trenches to the aid post. Trenches were narrow and zigzagged, so stretchers were hard to turn. In deep mud, as at Passchendaele in 1917, it could take **six men or more** to carry one stretcher and several hours to cover a short distance. Bearers usually worked at night or during lulls, and many were killed.
- **Horse-drawn ambulances** were used early in the war and on rough ground. They were slow and bumpy, which caused pain and made wounds bleed.
- **Motor ambulances** quickly became common. They were faster and carried more men, though the ride on shell-damaged roads was still rough. Many were donated by the public or charities, and were driven by men of the RAMC, the Army Service Corps and, increasingly, women such as the FANY.
- **Ambulance trains** carried men from casualty clearing stations to base hospitals; **canal barges** carried the most delicate cases (for example chest wounds or broken thigh bones) because the ride was smooth, though slow.
- **Hospital ships** carried "Blighty" cases across the Channel to hospitals in Britain.

### Stages of treatment in the chain of evacuation

The system was designed to **treat each man only as much as was needed at each stage**, then move him back to where better facilities were available.

| Stage | Where | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Regimental Aid Post (RAP)** | In or just behind the support trenches, within about **200 m** of the front line, often in a dugout or ruined building | The battalion's medical officer and orderlies gave **first aid**: cleaning and dressing wounds, stopping bleeding, splints, morphine. Lightly wounded men returned to the line; walking wounded walked back; others were carried on. No surgery. |
| **2. Field ambulance: Advanced Dressing Station (ADS)** | About **400 m** further back, in dugouts, ruins or bunkers | A **field ambulance** was a mobile RAMC unit, not a vehicle. At the ADS the wounded got more treatment — fresh dressings, splints, anti-tetanus injections — and were sent on. Limited emergency surgery only. |
| **3. Main Dressing Station (MDS)** | Further back again, often in tents or buildings | Run by the field ambulance; could hold more men and give more treatment before loading them into ambulances. |
| **4. Casualty Clearing Station (CCS)** | Usually about **7–12 miles** behind the lines, near a railway or road | Large, well-equipped units with hundreds of beds, surgeons, nurses and later X-ray machines. From **1915** more and more **surgery** (including amputations and wound excision) was done here, as early surgery prevented infection. Staff carried out **triage** into three groups: the walking wounded; those who needed hospital treatment urgently; and those who were unlikely to survive, who were made comfortable. |
| **5. Base hospital** | On or near the French coast near the Channel ports — e.g. **Étaples**, Boulogne, Rouen | Large hospitals (some in former hotels or huge hutted camps) for major operations and longer care. Men who would recover quickly went back to the front; the most serious cases were sent home to Britain by hospital ship. |

Key point for answers: moving surgery **forward** to the casualty clearing stations was one of the most important changes, because the key to preventing gas gangrene was treating a wound within hours rather than days.

### The underground hospital at Arras

Arras was close to the front line. Under the town were ancient chalk **quarries**. In 1916–17, ahead of the **Battle of Arras (which began on 9 April 1917)**, the **New Zealand Tunnelling Company** linked and extended them into a network of tunnels that could shelter thousands of troops. Inside was an **underground hospital** with:

- about **700 beds**;
- **operating theatres**;
- **running water, electric lighting** and a **light railway** to move men and supplies;
- accommodation for medical staff and a mortuary.

It was deep enough to be safe from shelling and close enough to the front for men to be treated quickly. It shows the army **adapting the terrain** to medical needs — but it also had problems: it depended on generators, and once the offensive began and the front moved, it was a long way from some of the fighting. The site is now the Wellington Quarry museum.

### The Western Front and experiments in surgery and medicine

**1. Treating wounds and infection**

- **The problem.** Shrapnel and bullets carried mud, cloth and bacteria deep into wounds. In the manured soil of Flanders and northern France this caused **gas gangrene** (a bacterial infection that produced gas in the tissues and spread fast) and **tetanus**. Aseptic surgery, which worked in clean hospitals, could not prevent infection in wounds that were dirty before the soldier reached a doctor.
- **Wound excision (debridement).** Surgeons learned to cut away all dead, damaged and contaminated tissue around a wound, then leave it open or close it later. This became standard practice and reduced gas gangrene — but only if done quickly, which is why surgery moved to the casualty clearing stations.
- **Amputation** was used when a limb could not be saved, to stop gangrene spreading.
- **The Carrel–Dakin method (1915).** Alexis Carrel and Henry Dakin developed a way of flushing wounds continuously with an antiseptic solution (a sterile sodium hypochlorite solution) through rubber tubes. It was effective but time-consuming and needed careful nursing, so was used mainly in hospitals.
- **Anti-tetanus injections** (antitoxin) were given routinely to the wounded and greatly reduced tetanus.
- **Head injuries.** The Brodie steel helmet (introduced from late 1915) reduced head wounds, and surgeons developed better techniques for brain surgery — for example, the American surgeon **Harvey Cushing** used magnets to remove metal fragments from the brain and reduced death rates for his brain operations from around 50% to around 29%.
- **Plastic surgery.** Horrific facial injuries led **Harold Gillies** to develop skin-grafting techniques; the Queen's Hospital at Sidcup, Kent, opened in 1917 for facial reconstruction.

**2. The Thomas splint**

A compound fracture of the **femur** (thigh bone) was one of the deadliest wounds: the broken bone moved, cutting muscles and blood vessels, causing blood loss, shock and infection. Early in the war around **80%** of men with this wound died. The **Thomas splint**, designed in the 1870s by the Welsh surgeon **Hugh Owen Thomas**, held the leg straight and kept the bone still. His nephew **Robert Jones**, a leading army orthopaedic surgeon, promoted its use on the Western Front from **1916**. It was fitted at the aid post or dressing station so that the leg was immobilised during the whole journey. The death rate from these fractures fell to around **20%** (some estimates put it even lower). It is a clear example of an **old invention applied on a large scale because of war**.

**3. Mobile X-ray units**

X-rays (discovered in 1895) let surgeons find bullets and shrapnel deep in the body, so they could remove them without cutting around blindly. Base hospitals had X-ray machines from early in the war, and **mobile X-ray units** in vehicles were used to bring X-rays closer to the front and to casualty clearing stations. In France, **Marie Curie** organised a fleet of radiological cars. Problems: X-ray tubes **overheated** and had to be rested; X-rays did not show fragments of **cloth** that could cause infection; patients had to keep still for some time; and staff were exposed to radiation before its dangers were fully understood.

**4. Blood transfusion and the blood bank at Cambrai**

Blood loss and shock killed many wounded men. At first, transfusions were given **directly** from donor to patient, which meant the donor had to be present, and blood clotted quickly. During the war:

- **sodium citrate** was found to stop blood clotting (1914–15), so blood could be collected and given indirectly;
- in **1916** Francis Peyton Rous and J. R. Turner found that adding a **citrate-glucose** solution allowed blood to be **stored for longer** (about four weeks if refrigerated);
- **Geoffrey Keynes**, a British surgeon, developed a portable transfusion apparatus so that transfusions could be given near the front;
- in **1917**, the American doctor **Oswald Robertson** set up the first **blood depot** (an early blood bank). Before the **Battle of Cambrai (November 1917)** he collected and stored about **22 units of type O** blood (which can be given to most people) in glass bottles, kept cool in ice-packed boxes. Some had been stored for 26 days when it was used. Robertson treated about 20 badly wounded men with it — a result he later reported in the medical press.

This meant blood could be ready **before** a battle, instead of searching for a donor when a man was dying.

### The historical context: medicine in the early twentieth century

The Western Front developments did not come from nowhere. By 1914:

- **Understanding of infection.** Louis Pasteur's **germ theory** (1861) and Robert Koch's work identifying specific bacteria (from the 1870s–80s) meant doctors knew infection was caused by microbes. **Joseph Lister** introduced **antiseptic** surgery with carbolic acid in **1867**.
- **Moves towards aseptic surgery.** By the 1890s–1900s surgeons aimed to keep germs out of the operating theatre altogether: sterilised instruments (steam sterilisers), rubber gloves, gowns and masks, and clean theatres. On the Western Front, however, wounds were already infected, so surgeons **combined** aseptic operating conditions with antiseptic solutions and wound excision.
- **X-rays.** Wilhelm **Röntgen** discovered X-rays in **1895**; hospitals quickly began using them to see broken bones and objects in the body. War made X-ray machines far more common and mobile.
- **Blood transfusions.** Early transfusions often failed because the patient's body rejected the blood. **Karl Landsteiner** discovered **blood groups** in **1901**, making safe matching possible. Before the war, clotting and storage were still unsolved — the war drove the solutions.

### Change and continuity: how far did the Western Front improve medicine?

| Change / progress | Continuity / limits |
|---|---|
| A properly organised chain of evacuation with triage | Transport still slow in mud; many wounded lay out for hours or died before reaching an aid post |
| Surgery moved forward to CCSs; wound excision reduced gas gangrene | Infection could still not be cured — no antibiotics until the Second World War |
| Thomas splint cut femur-fracture deaths | Mostly an old invention used more widely, not new science |
| Mobile X-rays, stored blood, the Cambrai blood depot | Blood storage was small-scale in 1917; X-ray machines were unreliable |
| Huge expansion of trained nurses and RAMC staff | Most techniques relied on pre-war discoveries (germ theory, X-rays, blood groups) |

Factors: **war** created the scale and urgency; **science and technology** (X-rays, citrate, refrigeration) provided the means; **government and the army** (the RAMC, funding, organisation) spread the methods; **individuals** (Jones, Robertson, Keynes, Gillies, Cushing) developed them.

::: terms
- **RAMC**: the Royal Army Medical Corps, formed in 1898, the army's medical service that ran the chain of evacuation.
- **Chain of evacuation**: the system of stages (aid post, dressing stations, casualty clearing station, base hospital) that moved and treated the wounded.
- **Regimental Aid Post**: the first stage, within about 200 m of the front line, where the battalion medical officer gave first aid.
- **Field ambulance**: a mobile RAMC medical unit (not a vehicle) that ran the advanced and main dressing stations.
- **Casualty Clearing Station**: a large, well-equipped unit about 7–12 miles behind the lines where triage and, from 1915, much surgery took place.
- **Base hospital**: a large hospital near the Channel coast, such as those at Étaples, for major operations and longer care.
- **Triage**: sorting casualties by how urgently they need treatment and whether they are likely to survive.
- **Wound excision (debridement)**: cutting away dead and contaminated tissue from a wound to prevent gas gangrene.
- **Thomas splint**: a splint that held a broken leg straight and still, cutting deaths from femur fractures from about 80% to about 20%.
- **Blood depot**: a store of donated blood kept ready for use, first set up by Oswald Robertson before the Battle of Cambrai in 1917.
- **Aseptic surgery**: surgery that keeps germs out of the operating area altogether, using sterilised instruments, gloves and gowns.
:::
