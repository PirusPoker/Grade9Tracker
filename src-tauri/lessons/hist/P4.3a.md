## What it is

**Détente** (French for "relaxation") is the easing of tension between the superpowers in the 1970s. It produced real agreements — **SALT 1 (1972)**, the **Helsinki Accords (1975)** and **SALT 2 (1979)** — but it rested on mistrust, and it collapsed when the USSR **invaded Afghanistan in December 1979**. President Carter responded with the **Carter Doctrine**, sanctions and the **boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics**; the USSR answered by boycotting **Los Angeles 1984**. By 1980 the Cold War had returned — the "Second Cold War" that Reagan would intensify (see P4.3b).

### Why détente happened

| Pressures on the USA | Pressures on the USSR |
|---|---|
| The **Vietnam War** was costly, unpopular and being lost; US forces withdrew in 1973 | The **economy** was stagnating; it needed Western technology and **grain** |
| Arms spending was huge while domestic problems (inflation, protest) grew | Rough **nuclear parity** by around 1970 meant the USSR could negotiate as an equal |
| President **Nixon** and his adviser **Henry Kissinger** favoured practical deals (realpolitik) | **Sino-Soviet split** — border clashes in 1969; the USSR feared a US–China alliance |
| Nixon's visit to **China (February 1972)** gave the USA leverage over Moscow | **Brezhnev** wanted Western recognition of Soviet control of Eastern Europe |

Both sides also remembered how close they had come in **Cuba (1962)**, and both wanted to control the spiralling cost of the arms race. West Germany's **Ostpolitik** under Willy Brandt (treaties with the USSR and Poland in 1970) eased tension in Europe too.

### SALT 1 (1972)

The **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks** began in Helsinki in November 1969. **Nixon became the first sitting US president to visit Moscow**, and he and Brezhnev signed SALT 1 on **26 May 1972**:
- **The ABM Treaty**: each side limited to two anti-ballistic missile sites (reduced to one in 1974), with no more than 100 interceptors each. This kept both sides vulnerable — so **mutually assured destruction** still deterred attack.
- **The Interim Agreement**: froze the number of **ICBM launchers** at existing levels for five years; new submarine missile launchers only if older ones were scrapped. The USSR was allowed more launchers because the USA had more bombers and multiple-warhead missiles.
- **Basic Principles**: both agreed to avoid confrontation and to seek peaceful coexistence.

**Significance of SALT 1**:
- It was the **first agreement to limit the number of nuclear weapons**, not just testing or spread — a major step on from the 1963 Test Ban Treaty.
- It **built trust** and opened the way to trade (a large US grain deal in 1972), cultural contact and the **Apollo–Soyuz** joint space mission (July 1975).
- **Limits**: it did not cover **MIRVs** (missiles carrying several warheads) or bombers, so the number of warheads kept rising; it froze numbers, it did not cut them.

### The Helsinki Accords (1975)

The **Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe** ended with the Helsinki Final Act, signed on **1 August 1975** by **35 states** — almost every European country (Albania refused), plus the USA and Canada. It had three "baskets":

| Basket | Agreement | Who gained |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Security | Europe's post-war **borders recognised as fixed**; disputes settled peacefully; notice of large military exercises | The **USSR**: the West accepted the division of Europe, including East Germany and the Soviet-drawn borders of Poland |
| 2 — Co-operation | Trade, science, technology, environment | Both; the USSR wanted Western technology |
| 3 — Human rights | Freedom of speech, religion, movement and information; reuniting divided families | The **West**: the USSR signed up to human rights standards |

**Significance of Helsinki**:
- It was the **high point of détente** — the widest agreement of the Cold War.
- The USSR saw it as a victory (its sphere of influence legitimised), but **Basket 3 backfired on Moscow**: dissidents used it to hold the regime to account. The **Moscow Helsinki Group** (1976) and **Charter 77** in Czechoslovakia monitored abuses; the USSR arrested and exiled them. The physicist **Andrei Sakharov** was exiled to Gorky in 1980.
- In the USA, critics said Ford had **legitimised Soviet domination** of Eastern Europe for promises Moscow would not keep. When Carter (president from 1977) made **human rights** central to US foreign policy, Soviet abuses soured relations.

### SALT 2 (1979)

Talks began in 1972; a framework was agreed by Ford and Brezhnev at Vladivostok (1974). **Carter and Brezhnev signed SALT 2 in Vienna on 18 June 1979**:
- Each side limited to **2,400 strategic nuclear delivery vehicles** (missiles and bombers), falling to **2,250**.
- Limits on **MIRVed** missiles and a ban on new types of ICBM.

**But it was never ratified by the US Senate.** Many senators thought it favoured the USSR and could not be verified; Soviet actions in Africa (support for Cuban forces in Angola and Ethiopia) and the deployment of new **SS-20** missiles targeted at Western Europe increased suspicion; NATO decided in December 1979 to deploy US **Pershing II and cruise missiles** in Europe. After the invasion of Afghanistan, **Carter withdrew SALT 2 from the Senate** (January 1980). Its significance is therefore that it showed **détente was already fading** — the agreement was signed, but the trust needed to make it law had gone.

### The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979)

**Background**:
- In **April 1978** a communist coup brought the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan to power. Its reforms (land, women's rights, attacks on Islam) provoked revolt by Muslim fighters, the **mujahideen**.
- In 1979 the communist leader Taraki was overthrown and killed by **Hafizullah Amin**. Moscow distrusted Amin and feared he might turn to the USA.
- The **Iranian Revolution** (early 1979) created an Islamic republic; the USSR feared Islamic revolt spreading to its own Muslim Central Asian republics on the Afghan border.

**The invasion**: from **24 December 1979** Soviet troops entered Afghanistan; on 27 December Soviet special forces killed Amin and installed **Babrak Karmal**. The force grew to around 100,000. The war lasted until **February 1989** and cost around 15,000 Soviet lives.

**Why it mattered — its significance**:
- The USA saw it as **Soviet expansion**, not a defensive move: the first time since 1945 Soviet troops had invaded a country outside the Eastern bloc, bringing them closer to the **oil of the Persian Gulf**.
- It **ended détente**: SALT 2 was abandoned and the superpowers returned to confrontation.
- It became the USSR's own "Vietnam" — a long, costly war that drained its economy and morale, contributing to the Soviet collapse (P4.3b).

### The Carter Doctrine (January 1980)

In his State of the Union address on **23 January 1980**, Carter declared that any attempt by an outside force to gain control of the **Persian Gulf** region would be treated as an assault on the vital interests of the USA and would be repelled **by any means necessary, including military force**. Alongside it:
- SALT 2 withdrawn from the Senate;
- a **grain embargo** — sales of 17 million tonnes of grain to the USSR cancelled — and a ban on selling high-technology goods;
- increased defence spending and a **Rapid Deployment Force** for the Gulf;
- **military aid to the mujahideen** through Pakistan, and closer ties with Pakistan and China;
- the **Olympic boycott**.

**Significance**: it was a clear return to **containment** — Carter, who had begun his presidency hoping to cut arms, now committed the USA to fight for the Gulf. It showed détente was over and began the arms build-up that Reagan expanded.

### The Olympic boycotts

| Games | Boycott | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Moscow 1980** | Led by the USA in protest at Afghanistan; **around 60 countries** stayed away, including West Germany, Japan and China. Some Western teams (e.g. Britain's) went against their government's wishes, several competing under the Olympic flag | A propaganda blow to the USSR, which had planned the Games as a showcase for communism |
| **Los Angeles 1984** | The USSR and **14 allies** (e.g. East Germany, Cuba) boycotted, officially over "security" fears; Romania went anyway | A tit-for-tat — showed relations were as bad as ever in Reagan's "Second Cold War" |

The boycotts mattered because they showed **the Cold War had spread into every area of life**, including sport; they were symbolic rather than decisive, but they marked the end of the co-operation that détente had brought.

::: terms
- **Détente**: the easing of tension between the USA and USSR in the 1970s, shown by arms control, trade and summit meetings.
- **SALT 1**: the 1972 Strategic Arms Limitation agreements — the ABM Treaty and an interim freeze on ICBM launchers.
- **ABM Treaty**: part of SALT 1 limiting each side's anti-ballistic missile defences, so both stayed vulnerable to attack.
- **MIRV**: a missile carrying several nuclear warheads, each able to hit a different target; not limited by SALT 1.
- **Helsinki Accords**: the 1975 agreement of 35 states recognising Europe's borders, promoting co-operation and promising human rights.
- **SALT 2**: the 1979 agreement limiting each side to 2,400 strategic delivery vehicles; signed but never ratified by the US Senate.
- **Mujahideen**: Afghan Islamic fighters who resisted the communist government and Soviet forces, armed by the USA via Pakistan.
- **Carter Doctrine**: Carter's January 1980 pledge to use military force to stop any outside power controlling the Persian Gulf.
- **Ratification**: formal approval of a treaty — in the USA, a two-thirds vote of the Senate.
:::

::: method
The Cold War is the **period study** in Booklet P of Paper 2 (about 50 minutes for 32 marks). Three question types, all worth 8 marks, **no SPaG**. The mark schemes use two levels (consequences) or three levels (narrative and importance) — there is no Level 4 on this booklet, so an answer at the top of Level 2 or Level 3 gains full marks.

**1. "Explain two consequences of…" (8 marks: 4 per consequence)**
- Level 1 (1–2 each): a simple or generalised comment ("it ended détente").
- Level 2 (3–4 each): the consequence is **explained** with specific knowledge showing *how* the event caused it.
- Structure: "One consequence of X was Y. This was because… As a result…". Two different consequences in two paragraphs.

**2. "Write a narrative account analysing…" (8 marks)**
- Use the two stimulus points and **add your own** — answers that do not go beyond them are capped at 5 marks.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple story. Level 2 (3–5): a sequence with some links. Level 3 (6–8): a **coherent, analytical narrative** — events in the right order, each **linked by cause and consequence**, heading to an outcome ("so by 1980 détente was over").

**3. "Explain the importance of X for Y" (8 marks each; answer two of three)**
- Level 1 (1–2): general comment. Level 2 (3–5): some explanation of how X affected Y. Level 3 (6–8): **analysis of the impact** of X on Y with precise knowledge — two or three distinct effects, ideally with a limit and a closing judgement.
- For treaties, say what they **achieved** and what they **did not** — that balance is what lifts SALT and Helsinki answers into Level 3.
:::

::: example Two consequences
Explain two consequences of the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979). (8)

One consequence was the Carter Doctrine. The USA saw the invasion as Soviet expansion towards the oil of the Persian Gulf, so in January 1980 Carter announced that the USA would use military force if necessary to stop any outside power controlling the Gulf. He increased defence spending, created a Rapid Deployment Force and sent aid to the mujahideen through Pakistan, which marked a return to containment.

A second consequence was the end of détente. SALT 2 had been signed in June 1979 but faced opposition in the Senate, and after the invasion Carter withdrew it, so it was never ratified. Carter also cancelled grain sales to the USSR and led a boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics by around 60 countries. Relations sank to their lowest level since the early 1960s, beginning the "Second Cold War".
:::

::: example Narrative account
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of détente in the years 1969–75. You may use the following in your answer: SALT 1 (1972); the Helsinki Accords (1975). You must also use information of your own. (8)

By 1969 both superpowers had reasons to reduce tension: the USA was trapped in Vietnam and the USSR had reached nuclear parity but faced economic problems and a hostile China. As a result, the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks began in Helsinki in November 1969. Nixon's visit to China in February 1972 increased the pressure on Brezhnev to reach agreement with the USA rather than risk a US–Chinese alliance.

This led to Nixon's visit to Moscow, where SALT 1 was signed in May 1972. The ABM Treaty limited missile defences and the Interim Agreement froze ICBM launchers for five years — the first limit on nuclear weapon numbers. The trust this built encouraged further co-operation, such as US grain sales to the USSR and the Apollo–Soyuz space docking in July 1975.

Détente reached its high point when 35 states signed the Helsinki Final Act in August 1975. The USSR gained Western recognition of Europe's post-war borders, and in return it promised human rights in Basket 3. However, this agreement also contained the seeds of détente's decline, because Soviet persecution of the dissidents who tried to monitor those promises angered the West.
:::

::: example Importance
Explain the importance of the Helsinki Accords (1975) for relations between the USA and the Soviet Union. (8)

The Helsinki Accords were important because they marked the high point of détente. Signed in August 1975 by 35 states including both superpowers, they recognised Europe's post-war borders as fixed. This gave the USSR what it had long wanted — Western acceptance of its control of Eastern Europe — and showed that the superpowers could reach agreement on the most sensitive issue of the Cold War.

However, they were also important because they created new tension. In Basket 3 the USSR promised freedom of speech and movement, but it arrested members of the Moscow Helsinki Group and Czechoslovakia persecuted Charter 77. When Carter became president in 1977 he made human rights central to US policy, so Soviet abuses became a constant source of friction and made the US Senate distrust later agreements such as SALT 2.

Overall, Helsinki's importance was double-edged: in the short term it improved relations, but in the longer term Basket 3 gave the West a weapon against the USSR and helped undermine détente.
:::

::: watch
- Treating **détente as friendship** — it was managed rivalry; both sides kept building weapons (MIRVs) and competing in the developing world.
- Saying SALT 2 "reduced weapons" or "came into force" — it was **signed but never ratified**.
- Helsinki answers that only mention human rights: say **what each side gained** — borders for the USSR, Basket 3 for the West.
- Explaining the Afghanistan invasion without its **significance for the USA** — the Gulf's oil and the end of détente.
- Mixing up the **boycotts**: the USA boycotted Moscow 1980; the USSR boycotted Los Angeles 1984.
- Narratives beyond the question's dates, or using only the two stimulus points (capped at 5).
:::
