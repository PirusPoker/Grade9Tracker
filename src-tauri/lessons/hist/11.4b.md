## What it is

This lesson finishes **c1900–present**: **high-tech medical and surgical treatment in hospitals**, the **new approaches to prevention** — **mass vaccination** and **government lifestyle campaigns** — and the case study **the fight against lung cancer in the twenty-first century**: how science and technology are used in its **diagnosis and treatment**, and what **government action** has been taken. The theme running through all of it: the modern state does not just treat illness, it tries to *prevent* it — and it does so by changing behaviour, which is much harder than building a machine.

### 1. High-tech treatment in hospitals

Surgery in 1900 had already solved the old problems of **pain** (anaesthetics — Simpson's chloroform, 1847) and **infection** (Lister's carbolic antiseptic, 1867, then aseptic surgery). The third problem, **blood loss**, was solved in the twentieth century with blood groups (Landsteiner, 1901) and stored blood (the First World War; the National Blood Transfusion Service from 1946). That opened the way to operations deep inside the body.

| Treatment | Key date / person | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Plastic surgery** | **Harold Gillies**, Queen's Hospital, Sidcup (1917); Archibald McIndoe's burns work in the Second World War | rebuilt faces and bodies with skin grafts |
| **Kidney dialysis** | Willem Kolff's machine (1943, Netherlands) | a machine does the work of failed kidneys |
| **Pacemaker** | first implanted 1958 (Sweden) | regulates the heartbeat |
| **Kidney transplant** | first successful UK transplant, **1960**, Edinburgh (Michael Woodruff), between identical twins | organ replacement becomes possible |
| **Hip replacement** | **John Charnley**, Wrightington, **1962** | now one of the most common operations; restores mobility |
| **Heart transplant** | Christiaan Barnard, South Africa, **1967**; first UK operation **1968**; long-term success at **Papworth** from **1979** (Terence English) | anti-rejection drugs (ciclosporin, 1980s) made transplants routine |
| **IVF** | **Louise Brown**, the first "test-tube baby", born **1978** in Oldham (Steptoe and Edwards) | treats infertility |
| **Keyhole surgery** | laparoscopic surgery spread in the **1980s–90s** | tiny cuts, camera and instruments; less pain, faster recovery |
| **Radiotherapy and chemotherapy** | radiation beams and cancer drugs developed through the century | shrink or destroy tumours |
| **Robotic surgery** | e.g. the da Vinci system (2000s) | surgeon controls precise robotic arms |

**Impact.** Many conditions that meant death or disability in 1900 — kidney failure, arthritis, heart failure, some cancers — became treatable. **Limits**: the treatments are **expensive** (an NHS problem — NICE decides what is cost-effective), there are **not enough donor organs** (the NHS Organ Donor Register began in 1994; England moved to an "opt-out" system in **May 2020**), and they depend on specialist hospitals, so access has not always been equal.

**Factors.** **Science and technology** (fibre optics, computers, immunosuppressant drugs); **the NHS and government** (funding, training, national transplant networks); **individuals** (Charnley, Gillies); **war** (plastic surgery and blood transfusion developed in both world wars); **attitudes** (public willingness to donate organs and blood).

### 2. New approaches to prevention: mass vaccination

Jenner's smallpox vaccination (1796) was the only vaccine for most of the nineteenth century; Pasteur made the principle general (chicken cholera, anthrax, rabies in the 1880s). In the twentieth century the **government** turned vaccination into a **mass, free, organised** campaign — first through local authorities, then through the NHS.

| Vaccine / campaign | Date | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Diphtheria** immunisation campaign | national campaign from the early 1940s | diphtheria, once a major child killer, became rare within a decade |
| **BCG** against tuberculosis | offered to schoolchildren from **1953** | TB deaths fell sharply (alongside better housing and streptomycin) |
| **Polio** | Salk (injected) vaccine **1956**; Sabin oral vaccine on a sugar lump from **1962** | polio, which crippled thousands of children, disappeared from Britain |
| **Smallpox** | worldwide WHO vaccination campaign | declared **eradicated in 1980** — the only human disease ever wiped out |
| **MMR** (measles, mumps, rubella) | **1988** | measles cases fell to very low levels |
| **HPV** for girls (later boys) | **2008** | protects against cervical and other cancers |
| **Covid-19** | **Margaret Keenan**, Coventry, 8 December **2020** — the world's first person to receive the approved Pfizer vaccine outside a trial | mass programme of tens of millions of doses in months |

**Why mass vaccination worked**: the vaccines were free on the NHS, given through schools and GPs, and recorded so children could be recalled; it relies on **herd immunity** — if enough people are immune, the disease cannot spread.

**Attitudes as a limit**: in **1998** Andrew Wakefield published a claim (later discredited, and he was struck off) linking MMR to autism; uptake fell and measles outbreaks followed. The government cannot force vaccination in Britain — it must persuade — so public trust is a factor in its success, exactly as it was in the opposition to compulsory smallpox vaccination in the nineteenth century.

### 3. New approaches to prevention: government lifestyle campaigns

Because modern killers (heart disease, cancers, strokes, type 2 diabetes) are linked to **lifestyle** (see 11.4a), the government has tried to change behaviour using three tools: **information** (advertising campaigns), **law** (bans and age limits), and **tax** (price).

- **Diet and exercise**: **5 A Day** (from 2003, eat five portions of fruit and vegetables); **Change4Life** (from **2009**, against childhood obesity); traffic-light food labels; the **Soft Drinks Industry Levy** ("sugar tax", **April 2018**).
- **Smoking** — see the case study below: the most sustained and successful campaign.
- **Alcohol**: recommended weekly limits and drink-drive campaigns; minimum unit pricing in Scotland (2018).
- **Sexual health**: the 1980s AIDS campaign ("Don't Die of Ignorance", with leaflets sent to every household in 1987).
- **Air**: the **Clean Air Act (1956)**, after the Great Smog of London in 1952 killed thousands.

**How effective?** Laws and taxes usually work better than advertising: the smoking ban and tax rises cut smoking far more than posters did, while obesity has continued to rise despite Change4Life. This echoes a long-running tension from the nineteenth century (the 1875 Public Health Act vs *laissez-faire*): how far **should** government interfere in people's private choices?

### 4. Case study: the fight against lung cancer in the twenty-first century

**The problem.** Lung cancer is the **UK's biggest cancer killer**, causing roughly **35,000 deaths a year**. Most cases — around **seven in ten** — are caused by **smoking**; others by passive smoking, radon gas, air pollution, asbestos and genetic factors. It often has no symptoms until it is advanced, which is why survival rates have historically been low.

**How we know smoking causes it.** **Doll and Hill (1950)** — lung cancer patients in 20 London hospitals — showed heavy smokers were far more likely to have lung cancer; their **British Doctors Study** (from 1951) followed thousands of doctors for decades; the **Royal College of Physicians' *Smoking and Health* (1962)** made it public. In the 1950s smoking was normal — most men smoked — so it took decades of evidence to change attitudes.

**Science and technology in diagnosis.**
- **Chest X-ray** (often the first test) and **CT scans** — detailed images that can find tumours too small for an X-ray.
- **PET-CT scans** show how active a tumour is and whether it has spread.
- **Bronchoscopy** — a flexible camera passed into the airways — and **biopsy** (taking a sample of cells, sometimes guided by CT or ultrasound) confirm the diagnosis.
- **Genetic testing of the tumour** identifies the mutation driving it (for example an **EGFR** mutation), which decides which targeted drug will work.
- **Screening**: the NHS **Targeted Lung Health Check** (from **2019**) offered people aged **55–74** who had ever smoked a check and a low-dose CT scan; most cancers it found were at an **early stage**, when they are far more treatable. In **June 2023** the government announced a **national lung cancer screening programme** for England, to reach all eligible people by **2029/30**.

**Science and technology in treatment.**
- **Surgery** — removing a lobe (lobectomy) or the whole lung (pneumonectomy), increasingly by **keyhole** or **robotic** surgery.
- **Radiotherapy** — including highly targeted **stereotactic** radiotherapy for small tumours in people unfit for surgery.
- **Chemotherapy** — drugs that kill dividing cells.
- **Targeted therapies** — drugs aimed at the specific genetic mutation in the tumour — and **immunotherapy**, which helps the patient's own immune system attack the cancer. These are the modern version of Ehrlich's "magic bullet" idea.
- Limits: new drugs are very expensive, and NICE must judge them cost-effective; survival for late-diagnosed cancer remains poor, so **prevention and early diagnosis** matter most.

**Government action.**

| Date | Action |
|---|---|
| **1965** | cigarette advertising banned on television |
| **1971** | health warnings on cigarette packets (larger, then picture warnings, followed) |
| **2003** | most tobacco advertising banned (Tobacco Advertising and Promotion Act 2002) |
| **2007** | **smoking ban** in enclosed public places and workplaces in England (**1 July**; Scotland 2006); legal age to buy tobacco raised from **16 to 18** |
| **2012–15** | tobacco displays hidden in shops |
| **2012** | **Stoptober** quit-smoking campaign begins; free NHS Stop Smoking Services and nicotine replacement |
| **2015** | smoking banned in cars carrying children |
| **2016–17** | **plain (standardised) packaging** |
| **2019–23** | Targeted Lung Health Checks; national screening programme announced (2023) |
| **2026** | the **Tobacco and Vapes Act** (Royal Assent 29 April 2026) makes it illegal to sell tobacco to anyone born on or after **1 January 2009**, from 2027 — a "smoke-free generation" |

Plus **high taxation** on tobacco, which raises the price. **Result**: adult smoking in Britain fell from about **45% in 1974** to around **12% by 2023**. Lung cancer rates in men have fallen substantially since the 1970s, following the fall in smoking.

**Factors in the fight against lung cancer.** **Science** identified the cause (Doll and Hill) and built the scanners and drugs; **government** passed the laws, raised the taxes and funds screening; **attitudes** changed from smoking as glamorous and normal to smoking as antisocial — which made the 2007 ban politically possible. Tobacco companies' opposition and advertising slowed change, a reminder that attitudes can *hinder* as well as help.

### Change and continuity (for comparisons)

- **Change**: prevention is now organised by the state on a national scale — compare the 1665 plague orders issued locally by the Lord Mayor, or the voluntary smallpox vaccination of the early 1800s, with free universal vaccination and screening.
- **Continuity**: government has always struggled to control people's behaviour (plague orders were widely ignored; people still smoke and eat badly); compulsion provokes resistance (anti-vaccination leagues in the 1800s, MMR fears in 1998).
- **Turning point?** 1948 (the NHS made national prevention possible); 1950 (Doll and Hill — the first proof that lifestyle kills).

::: terms
- **Mass vaccination**: a government-organised campaign to vaccinate most of the population against a disease, free, through schools and GPs.
- **Herd immunity**: protection for a whole community when enough people are immune that a disease cannot spread.
- **Lifestyle campaign**: a government effort — adverts, laws or taxes — to change behaviour such as smoking, diet or drinking in order to prevent disease.
- **Transplant**: surgery that replaces a failed organ with one from a donor, made practical by anti-rejection drugs.
- **Keyhole surgery**: surgery through tiny incisions using a camera and fine instruments, so patients recover faster.
- **CT scan**: computed tomography — many X-rays combined by computer into cross-section images, used to find small tumours.
- **Biopsy**: taking a small sample of tissue to examine under a microscope and confirm whether it is cancer.
- **Targeted therapy**: a cancer drug aimed at a specific genetic mutation inside the tumour, identified by testing the tumour's genes.
- **Screening**: testing people with no symptoms, such as smokers aged 55–74 with low-dose CT, to find disease early.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1, Section B (thematic study).**

**Q3 — "Explain one way in which X was similar/different…" (4).** Level 1 (1–2): a general statement. Level 2 (3–4): the way is explained with **specific knowledge of both periods**. One way; a fact from each period; a linking sentence.

**Q4 — "Explain why…" (12).** Two stimulus points are given.
- Level 1 (1–3) simple; Level 2 (4–6) some explanation; Level 3 (7–9) analytical explanation with accurate knowledge — capped here if you use only the stimulus; Level 4 (10–12) analysis throughout, precise knowledge, coherent reasoning, **including a factor of your own**.
- Three paragraphs: reason → evidence → "this is why…". For prevention questions, a strong third point is almost always **attitudes** (public acceptance, trust, resistance) or **government law/tax**.

**Q5/Q6 — "How far do you agree?" (16 + 4 SPaG).**
- Level 3 (9–12): both sides explained, a judgement with some justification; stimulus-only capped. Level 4 (13–16): sustained analysis, precise knowledge beyond the stimulus, judgement justified with a **criterion**.
- Useful criteria on this topic: *Which reached the most people?* (mass vaccination, national laws); *Which prevented rather than treated?* (prevention saves more lives than cure — smoking fell from about 45% to 12%); *Which was necessary for the others?* (science had to identify the cause before government could act on it).
- Structure: introduction with a line → agree → disagree → weighed conclusion. SPaG: specialist terms (immunotherapy, screening, herd immunity) spelled right.

**Using the lung-cancer case study**: it is the perfect evidence bank for "science and technology vs government" — scanning and drugs on one side, the 2007 ban and plain packaging on the other — and for "attitudes" (from normal habit to social stigma). Comparisons often pair it with Snow and cholera (1854) or the Black Death: how far government action has changed.
:::

::: example 4-mark similarity — prevention
Explain one way in which attempts to prevent disease in the period c1700–c1900 were similar to attempts in the period c1900–present. (4)

One similarity is that government used vaccination to prevent disease in both periods. After Jenner's discovery in 1796, the government funded free smallpox vaccination (1840) and made it compulsory for babies from 1853. In the twentieth century the government again organised vaccination on a mass scale — the diphtheria campaign of the 1940s, polio vaccine from 1956 and MMR from 1988, free through the NHS. So in both periods the state used vaccination as its main weapon against infectious disease, even though it faced public resistance each time (the anti-vaccination movement then, the MMR scare of 1998 now).
:::

::: example 12-mark explain why — lung cancer
Explain why the fight against lung cancer has made progress in the twenty-first century. (12)
You may use the following in your answer: CT scans; the smoking ban (2007). You **must** also use information of your own.

**Better diagnosis through technology** has helped because lung cancer is most treatable when found early. CT scans produce detailed cross-sections that reveal tumours too small for an ordinary X-ray, and the NHS Targeted Lung Health Check (from 2019) used low-dose CT to scan people aged 55–74 who had smoked — most of the cancers found were at an early stage, when surgery or radiotherapy can cure them. This is why the government announced a national screening programme in 2023.

**Government action on smoking** has helped because smoking causes around seven in ten lung cancers. The 2007 ban on smoking in enclosed public places, the same year the buying age rose to 18, made smoking less convenient and less socially acceptable, and it was reinforced by advertising bans (2003), plain packaging (2016–17), high taxes and, in 2026, the Tobacco and Vapes Act banning sales to anyone born on or after 1 January 2009. Adult smoking fell from about 45% in 1974 to around 12% by 2023, so fewer people develop the disease at all.

**New treatments** built on genetic science have improved survival. Testing a tumour's genes can reveal a mutation such as EGFR, so doctors can choose a **targeted drug**; **immunotherapy** helps the immune system attack the cancer; keyhole and robotic surgery and stereotactic radiotherapy mean more patients can be treated. These have given patients with advanced disease longer survival than chemotherapy alone.

These factors reinforce each other: science proved the cause (Doll and Hill, 1950) and built the tools, while government turned the science into laws and funded screening — prevention by government has saved the most lives, since a cancer that never develops needs no treatment.
:::

::: example 16-mark judgement — prevention
"Government action has been the most important factor in preventing disease in the period c1900–present." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)
You may use the following in your answer: mass vaccination; the discovery of the link between smoking and lung cancer. You **must** also use information of your own.

**Introduction.** I mostly agree: science identified what to prevent, but only government could turn that knowledge into protection for the whole population.

**Agree.** Mass vaccination is a government achievement: the diphtheria campaign of the 1940s, BCG in schools from 1953, polio vaccine from 1956, MMR from 1988 and the Covid-19 programme from December 2020 were all organised and paid for by the state through the NHS, reaching nearly every child. On lifestyle, the government used law and tax: the 1956 Clean Air Act, the 1965 TV advertising ban, the 2007 smoking ban, the sugar tax (2018). Smoking fell from about 45% of adults in 1974 to around 12% by 2023 — a change no advert alone achieved.

**Disagree.** Every one of these depended on **science** first: vaccines had to be developed (Salk, Sabin), and the government acted on smoking only after **Doll and Hill (1950)** and the RCP's 1962 report proved the link. **Attitudes** limited government: the MMR scare (1998) cut vaccination rates, and obesity has kept rising despite 5 A Day and Change4Life, because government cannot force people to change their diet. **Individuals** such as Doll and Hill, or campaigners, pushed reluctant governments into action.

**Judgement.** Science was necessary — you cannot prevent what you do not understand — but by the criterion of how many people were actually protected, government action was more important: the polio vaccine existed in laboratories, but polio disappeared from Britain because the state vaccinated every child. The limits of lifestyle campaigns show government is most effective when it uses law and tax rather than persuasion, so I agree to a large extent.
:::

::: q

---
Their 1950 study of lung cancer patients in 20 London hospitals, and the British Doctors Study from 1951, gave the first strong statistical proof that a lifestyle choice — smoking — caused a major disease. It shifted prevention from germs to behaviour and gave governments the evidence (via the RCP's 1962 report) to act with warnings, bans and taxes.

---
Any two: very expensive, so NICE must ration new drugs and procedures; shortage of donor organs (hence opt-out in England, 2020); treatment concentrated in specialist centres so access varies; it treats disease after it develops rather than preventing it.
