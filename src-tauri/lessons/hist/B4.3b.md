## What it is

This lesson covers the last two parts of Key topic 3 of the Early Elizabethan England depth study: **exploration and voyages of discovery**, and the **attempted colonisation of Virginia**. The spec asks for four things:
1. **The factors prompting exploration**, including new technology in ships and sailing and the drive to expand trade.
2. **The reasons for, and significance of, Drake's circumnavigation of the globe** (1577–80).
3. **The reasons for the attempted colonisation of Virginia**, including the significance of **Sir Walter Raleigh**.
4. **The reasons for the failure of the first settlement** in Virginia (Roanoke, 1585–86).

At the start of Elizabeth's reign England was a minor sea power. Spain and Portugal had divided the "New World" between them (Treaty of Tordesillas, 1494) and grown rich on American silver and Asian spices. By 1588 English sailors had sailed round the world, raided Spain's empire and tried to plant a colony in North America. The question behind this whole topic is **why England turned outwards**, and how much it achieved.

### 1. Factors prompting exploration

**Trade: the need for new markets.** England's main export was **woollen cloth**, most of it sold through **Antwerp** in the Spanish Netherlands. That market became unreliable: trade was disrupted by embargoes in the 1560s, the Dutch Revolt against Spain from 1566 turned the Netherlands into a war zone, and Spanish troops sacked Antwerp in 1576 (the "Spanish Fury"). Merchants needed new markets and direct access to luxury goods (spices, silk, sugar) instead of buying them second-hand at high prices. Joint-stock trading companies were set up, in which investors pooled money and shared the profits:

| Company | Founded | Trade |
|---|---|---|
| **Muscovy Company** | 1555 | Russia, via the White Sea |
| **Eastland Company** | 1579 | the Baltic |
| **Levant (Turkey) Company** | 1581 | the Ottoman Empire and the eastern Mediterranean |
| **Barbary Company** | 1585 | Morocco |

**The search for a route to Asia.** Spain and Portugal controlled the southern routes round Cape Horn and the Cape of Good Hope, so English explorers looked for a **Northwest Passage** round the top of America. **Martin Frobisher** made three voyages (1576–78) and **John Davis** three more (1585–87). They failed to find a passage, but they mapped parts of Canada and Greenland.

**Profit from plunder and the slave trade.** **John Hawkins** made three voyages (1562–69) carrying enslaved Africans to sell to Spanish colonists in the Caribbean: the start of England's involvement in the **triangular trade**. His third voyage ended in disaster when the Spanish attacked his fleet at **San Juan de Ulúa** (Mexico) in **1568**. After this, **privateers** such as **Francis Drake** attacked Spanish treasure ships and ports, often with secret investment from Elizabeth and her councillors. Plunder could make investors spectacularly rich.

**New technology in ships and sailing.**
- **Ship design.** From the 1570s, **John Hawkins**, as Treasurer of the Navy (from 1577), promoted the **"race-built" galleon**: longer and narrower, with the high castles at the front and back cut down. These ships were faster, more manoeuvrable, more stable in rough ocean water and carried heavy cannon along their sides. Galleons could make long ocean voyages and fight when they got there.
- **Sails.** A mix of **square sails** (for speed with the wind behind) and triangular **lateen sails** (for sailing closer into the wind) made ships much easier to steer on long voyages.
- **Navigation instruments.** The **magnetic compass** for direction; the **astrolabe** and **quadrant** (and later the cross-staff) for measuring the height of the sun or Pole Star to work out **latitude**; the **log and line** for measuring speed; the **traverse board** for recording course and speed.
- **Maps and knowledge.** Printed books spread navigational knowledge: Spanish navigation manuals were translated into English (Richard Eden translated Martín Cortés's *Art of Navigation* in 1561). **Mercator's projection (1569)** gave sailors a map on which a straight line was a constant compass bearing. **Richard Hakluyt** collected and published the accounts of voyages to encourage English exploration.

**Other factors.** A growing population and fears about the numbers of poor and vagabonds (see B4.3a) led writers such as Hakluyt to argue that colonies could absorb "idle" people. National rivalry with **Catholic Spain**, the wish to spread **Protestantism**, and the personal ambition of courtiers wanting wealth and royal favour also mattered.

### 2. Drake's circumnavigation, 1577–80

**Reasons for the voyage.**
- **Profit.** The voyage was a private venture funded by investors, including (secretly) **Elizabeth herself**, **Walsingham**, **Leicester**, **Sir Christopher Hatton** and **Hawkins**. The main aim was to raid Spain's poorly defended Pacific coast, where treasure from Peru was shipped north unguarded.
- **Weakening Spain.** Elizabeth wanted to damage Philip II without open war; she could deny responsibility for a private voyage.
- **Exploration and trade.** The official aims included exploring the Pacific coast of South America and possibly the unknown southern continent, and looking for trading opportunities. Drake also hoped to find the Pacific end of a Northwest Passage.
- **Drake's personal motives.** He was a committed Protestant who hated Spain, and wanted revenge for San Juan de Ulúa (1568), where he had served under Hawkins.

**Timeline.**

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **Dec 1577** | Drake leaves **Plymouth** with five ships and about 160 men; his flagship is the **Pelican** |
| **July 1578** | At **Port St Julian** (Patagonia), Drake has **Thomas Doughty**, a gentleman adventurer, tried and executed for mutiny: Drake asserts total command |
| **Aug–Sept 1578** | The Pelican is renamed the **Golden Hind** (after the crest of investor **Christopher Hatton**); the fleet passes through the **Strait of Magellan** in about 16 days |
| **Autumn 1578** | Storms: the **Marigold** is lost with all hands and the **Elizabeth** turns back to England. Only the Golden Hind goes on |
| **1579** | Drake raids Spanish ports such as **Valparaíso** and **Callao** (the port of Lima), and in **March 1579** captures the treasure ship **Nuestra Señora de la Concepción** (nicknamed *Cacafuego*), loaded with silver, gold and jewels |
| **June 1579** | Lands on the coast of California and claims it for Elizabeth as **Nova Albion** ("New England") |
| **Nov 1579** | Crosses the Pacific to the **Moluccas** (Spice Islands); agrees a trade deal with the **Sultan of Ternate** and loads cloves |
| **Jan 1580** | The Golden Hind runs aground on a reef; Drake lightens her by throwing guns and spices overboard, and she floats free |
| **26 Sept 1580** | Returns to **Plymouth** via the Cape of Good Hope, nearly three years after leaving |
| **4 April 1581** | Elizabeth boards the Golden Hind at **Deptford** and Drake is **knighted** on deck |

**Significance.**
- **Wealth.** The treasure has been estimated at up to about £600,000. Investors received about **£47 for every £1 invested (roughly 4,700 per cent)**. The Queen's share, at least **£160,000**, was enough to pay off the Crown's debts.
- **A first for England.** Drake was the first English captain to sail round the world, and the first captain to complete a circumnavigation in command of his own ship (Magellan had been killed on the way in 1521).
- **National pride and reputation.** The voyage showed that English seamanship and English ships could reach anywhere. It boosted national confidence and made Drake a hero, and encouraged further investment in exploration.
- **Relations with Spain.** Philip II and his ambassador **Bernardino de Mendoza** demanded the treasure back and called Drake a pirate. Elizabeth refused and **knighted him publicly** instead: a deliberate snub that was a clear sign of worsening Anglo-Spanish relations on the road to war in 1585.
- **Spanish weakness exposed.** Spain's empire, especially the Pacific coast, was shown to be vulnerable, encouraging more privateering.
- **Future trade and empire.** The contact with Ternate pointed towards the spice trade, later pursued by the **East India Company (1600)**, and the claim to Nova Albion was an early English claim in North America.

### 3. The attempted colonisation of Virginia

**Background.** In 1578 **Sir Humphrey Gilbert** received a royal patent to settle land in North America. In 1583 he claimed **Newfoundland** for Elizabeth but drowned on the voyage home. His half-brother **Walter Raleigh**, a favourite of the Queen, took over the idea.

**Reasons for the attempted colonisation.**
- **Wealth.** Investors hoped to find **gold and silver**, as Spain had in Mexico and Peru, and valuable raw materials: timber, furs, fish, and crops that England had to import.
- **A base against Spain.** A colony on the North American coast could serve as a base for **privateers** attacking Spanish treasure fleets sailing home from the Caribbean. This became more important once war with Spain began in 1585.
- **Trade.** A colony would create a new market for English cloth and new goods to trade.
- **Solving social problems.** Richard Hakluyt's ***Discourse of Western Planting*** **(1584)**, written at Raleigh's request to persuade the Queen, argued that colonies would give work to England's growing numbers of unemployed poor and vagabonds.
- **Religion and rivalry.** A colony would spread **Protestantism** among the Native Americans and stop the Catholic Spanish claiming the whole continent. It would also bring national prestige.
- **A route to Asia.** Some still hoped the colony would help in the search for a passage to the Pacific.

**The significance of Raleigh.**
- In **March 1584** Raleigh was granted a **royal patent** to explore and settle land not already ruled by a Christian prince.
- He organised and financed the expeditions with other investors, and sent a reconnaissance voyage under **Philip Amadas and Arthur Barlowe** in **1584**. They reached **Roanoke Island** off the coast of modern North Carolina and returned with glowing reports and two Native Americans, **Manteo** and **Wanchese**.
- He named the land **Virginia** in honour of Elizabeth, the "Virgin Queen", which flattered her and helped win her support; he was knighted in January 1585.
- He recruited experts: the scientist **Thomas Harriot** and the artist **John White**, who recorded the land, plants and people. Harriot's *Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia* (1588) promoted the colony.
- **Limits:** Elizabeth would not let him leave court, so **Raleigh never went to Virginia himself**. The expeditions depended on his private money and investors rather than the Crown, and he chose commanders (Grenville and Lane) who quarrelled.

### 4. The first settlement, 1585–86, and why it failed

**What happened.** In **April 1585** a fleet of seven ships left Plymouth under **Sir Richard Grenville**, with **Ralph Lane** as governor of the colony. In the summer about **108 men** settled on **Roanoke Island**. Grenville sailed home in August, promising to return with supplies by Easter 1586. Lane's men built a fort, explored the region and searched for gold and copper, but they relied on the local Native Americans, led by **Wingina**, for food. Relations broke down, and in **June 1586** Lane attacked Wingina's people and killed him. When **Drake** arrived that month after raiding the Spanish Caribbean, he offered supplies, but a storm hit, and the colonists decided to go home with him. They reached England in July 1586. Grenville's relief fleet arrived shortly after, found Roanoke deserted, and left **15 men** to hold it; they were never seen again.

**Reasons for the failure.**

| Reason | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Poor planning and timing** | The colonists arrived in summer, too late to plant crops, so they could not feed themselves through the winter |
| **Loss of supplies** | Grenville's flagship, the **Tiger**, ran aground entering the Outer Banks, and much of the food and seed was ruined by seawater |
| **A poor site** | The shallow waters and sandbanks of the **Outer Banks** made Roanoke hard for large ships to reach; it was no good as a harbour or privateering base |
| **The wrong colonists** | All men, mostly **soldiers** and **gentlemen**, with few farmers or craftsmen; many would not do manual work and spent their time looking for gold |
| **Dependence on Native Americans** | The colonists relied on local people for food; as their demands grew, the Native Americans' own stores ran short and hostility grew |
| **Violence** | In 1585 Grenville burned the village of **Aquascogoc** after a silver cup went missing; in 1586 Lane killed **Wingina**. Relations collapsed |
| **Poor leadership** | Grenville and Lane quarrelled; Lane ran the colony like a military camp |
| **Late relief** | Grenville's supplies did not arrive by Easter 1586, so the colonists took Drake's offer to leave |
| **War with Spain** | From 1585 England was at war with Spain; ships, men and money went to the war rather than to Virginia |

**What happened next.** In **1587** Raleigh sent a second colony of about 117 men, women and children under **John White**, meant to be a settled farming community. White's granddaughter, **Virginia Dare**, was the first English child born in America. White returned to England for supplies, but the **Armada crisis of 1588** meant all ships were kept at home. When he reached Roanoke in **1590** the colony had vanished, leaving only the word "CROATOAN" carved on a post: the "**Lost Colony**". No permanent English colony succeeded until **Jamestown (1607)**.

**Overall judgement.** Exploration in 1558–88 was driven mostly by **trade and profit**, made possible by **new technology** and encouraged by **rivalry with Spain**. Drake's voyage was a spectacular success in profit and prestige, but the Virginia venture shows the limits of what private investors could achieve without royal money, good planning or good relations with the people already living there.

::: terms
- **Circumnavigation**: sailing all the way round the world; Drake did it in 1577–80, the first English captain to do so.
- **Privateer**: a privately owned ship or captain licensed or secretly backed by the Crown to attack enemy shipping and keep a share of the plunder.
- **Galleon**: a large ocean-going sailing warship; the English "race-built" design was longer and lower, faster and more manoeuvrable.
- **Joint-stock company**: a trading company in which many investors buy shares, pool their money and share the profits and risks.
- **Northwest Passage**: a hoped-for sea route to Asia around the north of America, searched for by Frobisher and Davis.
- **Colonisation**: settling people permanently in a new land and claiming it for your own country.
- **Patent**: a royal grant giving a person the right to do something, such as Raleigh's 1584 right to settle unclaimed land in North America.
- **Astrolabe**: a navigation instrument used to measure the height of the sun or stars above the horizon and so work out latitude.
- **Triangular trade**: the trade route from England to West Africa (to buy enslaved people), to the Americas (to sell them), then home with goods.
:::

::: example Explain why: the failure of the first Virginia settlement (12)
Explain why the first English settlement in Virginia (1585–86) failed. You may use the following in your answer: the Tiger; relations with the Native Americans. You must also use information of your own. (12)

**Loss of supplies and poor timing.** The expedition was badly planned. Grenville's flagship, the Tiger, ran aground as it entered the shallow waters of the Outer Banks in 1585, and much of the food and seed on board was ruined by seawater. The colonists also arrived in summer, too late to plant and harvest crops before winter. This mattered because it meant the 108 men on Roanoke could not feed themselves from the start, which left them dependent on others.

**Breakdown in relations with the Native Americans.** Because they could not grow their own food, the colonists relied on Wingina's people. As their demands grew, the local people's own supplies ran low and resentment rose. English violence made this worse: Grenville burned the village of Aquascogoc in 1585 after a silver cup went missing, and in June 1586 Lane attacked and killed Wingina. This destroyed the colony's food supply and left it surrounded by enemies, so leaving became the only safe option.

**The wrong colonists and late relief.** The settlers were all men, mostly soldiers and gentlemen who expected to find gold rather than to farm. There were few farmers or craftsmen, and Lane ran the colony like a military camp. They were therefore unable to become self-sufficient. Grenville had promised to return with supplies by Easter 1586, but he was late, partly because war with Spain from 1585 was drawing ships and money away. When Drake arrived in June 1586 and a storm struck, the hungry colonists chose to go home with him.

**Links.** These causes fed each other: the loss of supplies on the Tiger created dependence, dependence created conflict, and the kind of colonists Raleigh sent meant they could never break that dependence. The late relief was the final trigger rather than the root cause.
:::

::: example How far do you agree: significance of Drake's voyage (16)
"The main significance of Drake's circumnavigation of the globe was the wealth it brought to England." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. You may use the following in your answer: the Cacafuego; relations with Spain. You must also use information of your own. (16 + 4 SPaG)

**Introduction.** The wealth was the most immediate result of the voyage, but I will argue that its most important significance was its effect on relations with Spain, because it brought England a step closer to open war.

**For the statement.** The voyage made a vast profit. Drake's capture of the treasure ship Cacafuego in March 1579, along with raids on Valparaíso and Callao, brought back treasure that has been estimated at up to £600,000. Investors received roughly £47 for every £1 invested, and Elizabeth's share of at least £160,000 was enough to pay off the Crown's debts. This mattered because Elizabeth's government was always short of money, and it showed courtiers and merchants that overseas ventures could be hugely profitable, encouraging further investment.

**Against: relations with Spain.** The voyage also had a major political significance. Philip II's ambassador Mendoza demanded the return of the treasure and called Drake a pirate. Instead, Elizabeth boarded the Golden Hind at Deptford in April 1581 and had Drake knighted, publicly rewarding an attack on Spain. This told Philip that Elizabeth was behind the privateers, and it was one of the steps that led to open war in 1585 and to the Armada.

**Against: prestige and future exploration.** Drake was the first English captain to sail round the world. This boosted national pride, made Drake a hero and proved that English ships and navigation, using galleons, compasses and astrolabes, could reach anywhere. His trade agreement with the Sultan of Ternate pointed towards the spice trade later pursued by the East India Company, and his claim to Nova Albion was an early English claim in North America.

**Conclusion.** The wealth was real, but it was a one-off: the money was spent within a few years. The damage to relations with Spain was more significant because it had longer-lasting consequences, pushing the two countries towards the war that dominated the rest of the 1580s. So I largely disagree with the statement: wealth was the most visible result, but not the most significant.
