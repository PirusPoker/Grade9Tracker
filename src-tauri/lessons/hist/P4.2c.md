## What it is

In 1968 Czechoslovakia tried to reform communism from within — the **Prague Spring** — and was crushed by a Warsaw Pact invasion. Brezhnev then announced the **Brezhnev Doctrine**: the USSR would intervene in any Eastern bloc state that threatened to abandon socialism. The West protested but did nothing. The chain to learn: **discontent → Dubček's reforms → Soviet alarm → failed talks → invasion (20–21 August 1968) → normalisation → Brezhnev Doctrine → international reaction**.

### Why there was opposition to Soviet control

- Czechoslovakia had been communist since the **February 1948** coup and was run by the hardline Stalinist **Antonín Novotný** (party leader from 1953).
- **The economy stagnated** in the 1960s: central planning produced shortages and poor-quality goods, and living standards fell behind the West.
- **Lack of freedom**: strict censorship, a powerful secret police (StB), no free elections, and travel to the West banned.
- **Criticism grew openly in 1967**: writers attacked censorship at the Writers' Congress (June 1967) and students protested in Prague. **Slovaks** resented domination by Czechs in Prague.
- In **January 1968** Novotný was replaced as party leader by **Alexander Dubček**, a Slovak reformer. Brezhnev accepted the change, believing Dubček was loyal. In March 1968 Novotný also lost the presidency to **Ludvík Svoboda**.

### The Prague Spring: Dubček's reforms

Dubček called his aim **"socialism with a human face"** — communism that people would support willingly. The **Action Programme** (April 1968) promised:

| Reform | What it meant |
|---|---|
| Relaxed censorship | Press, radio and television could criticise the government; censorship was formally ended in June 1968 |
| Freedom of speech and assembly | Open debate, public meetings, new clubs and societies |
| Freedom to travel | Czechoslovaks could travel abroad, including to the West |
| Economic reform | Some market elements, less central planning, more trade with the West |
| Curbs on the secret police | Fewer arbitrary arrests; victims of the 1950s purges rehabilitated |
| More power to parliament and regions | A plan to make Czechoslovakia a federation of Czech and Slovak republics |

Dubček insisted that Czechoslovakia would **stay communist and stay in the Warsaw Pact** — he was trying to reform the system, not leave it. This is the key difference from Hungary in 1956, where Nagy announced withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact.

But reform ran ahead of him:
- Non-communist groups formed, such as **KAN** (the Club of Committed Non-Party Members) and **K231** (former political prisoners), and there were calls to revive other parties.
- On **27 June 1968** the writer Ludvík Vaculík published the **"Two Thousand Words"** manifesto, signed by many prominent figures, urging faster change and warning against Soviet interference. Dubček distanced himself from it, but Moscow saw it as counter-revolution.

### Why the USSR was alarmed

- **Domino effect**: if Czechoslovakia gained freedoms, people in Poland, East Germany and the USSR itself might demand the same. Walter Ulbricht (East Germany) and Władysław Gomułka (Poland) pressed Brezhnev to act.
- **Strategic position**: Czechoslovakia bordered **West Germany**, a NATO state. A weak link there threatened the whole Warsaw Pact.
- **Independent communists**: Tito of Yugoslavia and Ceaușescu of Romania — both outside Moscow's full control — visited Prague in August 1968 to cheers.
- **Loss of the party's monopoly**: free debate and new clubs looked like the start of multi-party politics.

### From talks to invasion

| Date (1968) | Event |
|---|---|
| June | Warsaw Pact military exercises held inside Czechoslovakia — a warning |
| 15 July | **Warsaw Letter**: five Warsaw Pact states warn that reform threatens the whole socialist bloc |
| 29 July–1 August | Brezhnev and Dubček meet at **Čierna nad Tisou** on the border |
| 3 August | **Bratislava Declaration**: the bloc's leaders affirm loyalty to socialism; Dubček thinks he has bought time. Hardliners secretly send Moscow a letter asking for "help" |
| Night of 20–21 August | **Invasion**: around half a million troops from the USSR, Poland, Hungary and Bulgaria (East German units were mobilised but, apart from a few, held back) |

### The invasion and the re-establishment of Soviet control

- **Resistance was mostly non-violent**, because Dubček urged people not to fight. Citizens removed road signs to confuse troops, painted slogans, argued with soldiers and staged strikes. Around **100 Czechs and Slovaks were killed**.
- **Dubček and other leaders were arrested and flown to Moscow**, where on 26 August they were pressured into signing the **Moscow Protocol**, which reversed most reforms. They returned and were allowed to stay in post for a time.
- **Soviet troops stayed** in Czechoslovakia (until 1991).
- In January 1969 the student **Jan Palach** set himself on fire in Wenceslas Square in protest and died; the protests were suppressed.
- In **April 1969 Dubček was replaced by Gustáv Husák**, who began **"normalisation"**: censorship restored, reformers purged from the party, secret police powers restored. Only the federal Czech–Slovak structure survived. Dubček was later expelled from the party and given a job in forestry.

### The Brezhnev Doctrine

Set out in the Soviet newspaper *Pravda* (September 1968) and in Brezhnev's speech in Warsaw (**November 1968**), it said that when forces hostile to socialism try to turn a socialist country towards capitalism, this becomes the concern of **all** socialist countries — so the Warsaw Pact had the right to intervene. In effect, Eastern European states had **limited sovereignty**: they could not leave the bloc or abandon one-party communism.

Its significance:
- It justified the invasion after the event and **warned every Eastern bloc state** — reform would be crushed. It held Eastern Europe together for twenty years.
- It confirmed the **division of Europe** — each superpower would control its own sphere.
- It was only abandoned when **Gorbachev** in the late 1980s let Eastern European states choose their own way (see P4.3b).

### International reaction to Soviet measures in Czechoslovakia

| Who | Reaction |
|---|---|
| **USA** | Condemned the invasion but took **no action**. President Johnson was bogged down in **Vietnam**, it was an election year, and he wanted arms-control progress — the NPT had just been signed (July 1968). A planned US–Soviet summit on arms talks was called off, but talks soon resumed and became SALT (1969). |
| **United Nations** | A Security Council resolution condemning the invasion was **vetoed by the USSR**. |
| **Western Europe** | Protests and condemnation, but NATO did not intervene — Czechoslovakia was accepted as inside the Soviet sphere. |
| **Western communist parties** | The Italian and French communist parties criticised the USSR — a blow to its reputation with the left. |
| **Communist states** | **Yugoslavia** and **Romania** condemned it (Romania had refused to take part); **Albania** formally left the Warsaw Pact (September 1968); **China** denounced it, and Sino-Soviet relations worsened into border clashes in 1969. |

**Analysis**: the Western reaction showed that neither superpower would risk war over the other's sphere. In a sense this made the Cold War more **stable** — both accepted the line across Europe — which helped lead to **détente** in the 1970s. But it also damaged the USSR's image abroad and showed its bloc was held together by force, not consent.

::: terms
- **Prague Spring**: the period of reform in Czechoslovakia from January to August 1968 under Alexander Dubček.
- **Socialism with a human face**: Dubček's slogan for a reformed communism with more freedom, while keeping the Communist Party in charge.
- **Action Programme**: Dubček's April 1968 reform plan — less censorship, travel abroad, economic reform, curbs on the secret police.
- **Two Thousand Words**: the June 1968 manifesto by Ludvík Vaculík calling for faster reform, which alarmed Moscow.
- **Warsaw Pact**: the 1955 military alliance of the USSR and its Eastern European satellite states.
- **Normalisation**: Gustáv Husák's policy from 1969 of reversing the reforms and restoring strict communist control.
- **Brezhnev Doctrine**: the 1968 Soviet claim of a right to intervene in any Eastern bloc state that threatened to abandon socialism.
- **Sphere of influence**: an area where one power has dominant control, accepted in practice by its rival.
:::

::: method
The Cold War is the **period study** in Booklet P of Paper 2 (about 50 minutes for 32 marks). Three question types, all worth 8 marks, **no SPaG**. The mark schemes have two levels (consequences) or three levels (narrative and importance) — there is no Level 4 on this booklet.

**1. "Explain two consequences of…" (8 marks: 4 per consequence)**
- Level 1 (1–2 each): a simple or generalised statement ("the Soviets took control").
- Level 2 (3–4 each): the consequence is **explained** with accurate, specific knowledge showing how the event led to it.
- Structure: "One consequence of X was Y. This was because… As a result…". Two separate, different consequences; no introduction.

**2. "Write a narrative account analysing…" (8 marks)**
- Two stimulus points are given; **add your own information** — answers using only the stimulus points are capped at 5 marks.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple story. Level 2 (3–5): a sequence with some links. Level 3 (6–8): a **coherent analytical narrative** — events in order, each linked to the next by cause and consequence ("this alarmed…", "as a result…"), leading to an outcome.

**3. "Explain the importance of X for Y" (8 marks each; answer two of three)**
- Level 1 (1–2): general comment. Level 2 (3–5): some explanation of how X affected Y. Level 3 (6–8): **analysis of the impact** of X on Y with precise knowledge, ideally in two or three ways, ending with which was most important.
- Keep returning to Y. "The Prague Spring was important for Soviet control of Eastern Europe because…" not "The Prague Spring was…".
:::

::: example Two consequences
Explain two consequences of the Prague Spring (1968). (8)

One consequence was the Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia. Dubček's Action Programme relaxed censorship, allowed travel abroad and let non-communist clubs form, and the "Two Thousand Words" manifesto called for faster change. Brezhnev feared reform would spread to Poland and East Germany and weaken the bloc on the border with West Germany, so on the night of 20–21 August 1968 around half a million Warsaw Pact troops invaded. Dubček was arrested and taken to Moscow, and by April 1969 he had been replaced by the hardliner Husák.

A second consequence was the Brezhnev Doctrine. To justify the invasion and warn other states, Brezhnev declared in November 1968 that the socialist countries had the right to intervene wherever socialism was threatened. This meant Eastern European states had only limited sovereignty, and it kept them under Soviet control until Gorbachev abandoned the doctrine in the late 1980s.
:::

::: example Narrative account
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the Prague Spring and its suppression, 1968–69. You may use the following in your answer: Dubček becomes leader (January 1968); the invasion of August 1968. You must also use information of your own. (8)

Economic stagnation and resentment of censorship led to growing criticism in 1967, so in January 1968 the hardliner Novotný was replaced by Alexander Dubček. Dubček wanted "socialism with a human face", and in April his Action Programme relaxed censorship, allowed travel abroad and promised economic reform, while keeping Czechoslovakia in the Warsaw Pact.

These reforms unleashed demands for more. Non-communist clubs such as KAN appeared, and in June the "Two Thousand Words" manifesto urged faster change. This alarmed Brezhnev and hardline neighbours like Ulbricht, who feared the ideas would spread. As a result, Warsaw Pact exercises were held in Czechoslovakia, the Warsaw Letter of July warned Dubček, and talks at Čierna nad Tisou and Bratislava followed. Dubček believed he had reassured Moscow, but the talks only delayed action.

On the night of 20–21 August around half a million Warsaw Pact troops invaded. Because Dubček urged non-violent resistance, citizens removed road signs and argued with soldiers rather than fighting, but around 100 were killed. Dubček was flown to Moscow and forced to sign the Moscow Protocol reversing the reforms. Consequently, the Prague Spring was dismantled: in April 1969 Husák replaced Dubček and began "normalisation", and Brezhnev justified it all with his doctrine of the right to intervene.
:::

::: example Importance
Explain the importance of the Brezhnev Doctrine for Soviet control of Eastern Europe. (8)

The Brezhnev Doctrine was important because it gave the USSR a stated right to intervene in any Eastern bloc state. Announced in November 1968 to justify the invasion of Czechoslovakia, it declared that a threat to socialism in one country was the concern of all socialist states. This turned the invasion from a single action into a permanent rule.

It was also important as a deterrent. After the crushing of both Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968, other Eastern European governments knew that serious reform or leaving the Warsaw Pact would bring Soviet tanks. This meant that for twenty years opposition, such as Solidarity in Poland in the early 1980s, was contained by the governments themselves under the threat of intervention.

Finally, it was important because the West's weak reaction to it confirmed the division of Europe into spheres of influence. The USA condemned the invasion but did nothing, which showed that the Doctrine would be respected in practice. Its greatest importance, therefore, was that it held the bloc together by threat — which is why Soviet control collapsed so quickly once Gorbachev abandoned it in 1989.
:::

::: q
Give three reasons for discontent in Czechoslovakia before 1968.
---
Any three: a stagnating economy and poor living standards; strict censorship; a powerful secret police; no freedom to travel; Slovak resentment of Czech domination; the unpopular hardline leader Novotný.
:::

::: q
Name four reforms in Dubček's Action Programme (April 1968).
---
Any four: relaxed censorship / free press; freedom of speech and assembly; freedom to travel abroad; economic reform with some market elements and more trade with the West; curbs on the secret police; more power to parliament; a federal Czech–Slovak structure.
:::

::: q
How was Dubček's aim different from Nagy's in Hungary in 1956?
---
Dubček intended to keep Czechoslovakia communist and in the Warsaw Pact; Nagy announced Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact.
:::

::: q
Why did the USSR decide to invade Czechoslovakia? Give three reasons.
---
Any three: fear of a domino effect on other satellite states and the USSR; Czechoslovakia bordered West Germany; the Communist Party was losing control (non-communist clubs, Two Thousand Words); pressure from Ulbricht and Gomułka; Tito and Ceaușescu's visits suggested drift away from Moscow.
:::

::: q
How did the people of Czechoslovakia respond to the invasion?
---
Mostly non-violent resistance: removing road signs, graffiti, talking to and arguing with soldiers, strikes. Around 100 were killed. Jan Palach set himself on fire in January 1969.
:::

::: q
What did the Brezhnev Doctrine state?
---
That when a socialist country is threatened by forces trying to turn it to capitalism, this is a concern of all socialist countries, which have the right to intervene — so Eastern bloc states had limited sovereignty.
:::

::: q
Why did the USA not intervene in Czechoslovakia in 1968?
---
It was fighting in Vietnam; it wanted arms-control progress (NPT signed July 1968); it accepted Czechoslovakia was in the Soviet sphere; intervention risked nuclear war; 1968 was an election year.
:::

::: q
Which communist states criticised the invasion, and how?
---
Yugoslavia and Romania condemned it (Romania refused to join in); Albania left the Warsaw Pact in September 1968; China denounced it. Western communist parties in Italy and France also criticised it.
:::

::: watch
- **Confusing 1956 and 1968**: Hungary tried to leave the Warsaw Pact; Czechoslovakia did not. Hungarians fought; Czechoslovaks mostly resisted non-violently.
- **Describing the reforms without linking them** to Soviet fear — the importance and narrative questions want the *why it mattered* link.
- Getting the **Brezhnev Doctrine** wrong — it came *after* the invasion, to justify it and warn others; it did not cause the invasion.
- Forgetting **Husák and normalisation** — the story ends with Soviet control re-established in 1969, not on 21 August 1968.
- International reaction answers that say "the West did nothing" without explaining **why** (Vietnam, arms control, spheres of influence).
:::
