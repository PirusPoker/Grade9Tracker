## What it is

How popular was the Nazi regime, and who dared to oppose it? The spec covers three points:
1. **The extent of support** for the Nazi regime.
2. **Opposition from the Churches**, including the role of **Pastor Martin Niemöller**.
3. **Opposition from the young**, including the **Swing Youth** and the **Edelweiss Pirates**.

The big idea for a grade 9 answer: most Germans **supported or conformed** — some with enthusiasm, many because life seemed to be improving, others out of fear. Opposition existed, but it was **small, scattered and mostly defensive**: the Churches mainly protected their own freedom, and young non-conformists mainly wanted to live their own way. None of it came close to threatening Hitler's rule before 1939.

### A scale of behaviour: from support to resistance

Historians distinguish between different kinds of behaviour. Using these words precisely will lift your answers:

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Support** | Genuine enthusiasm for Hitler or the regime | Crowds at rallies; many voted yes in plebiscites willingly |
| **Conformity** | Going along with the regime, outwardly obeying, whatever you privately think | Giving the Hitler salute; joining the DAF or the Hitler Youth because it was expected |
| **Dissent / grumbling** | Private complaining without action | Moaning about food prices, wages or local party officials |
| **Non-conformity** | Refusing to live the Nazi way, but not trying to overthrow the regime | The Swing Youth listening to banned jazz |
| **Opposition** | Openly disagreeing with a Nazi policy | Niemöller's sermons against the Reich Church |
| **Resistance** | Actively working to weaken or overthrow the regime | Underground communist cells distributing leaflets |

### The extent of support for the Nazi regime

**Evidence of wide support**
- **Plebiscites**: in November 1933 about **95%** approved leaving the League of Nations; in August 1934 about **90%** approved Hitler as Führer; in March 1936, after the remilitarisation of the Rhineland, about **99%** voted yes; in April 1938 about **99%** approved the **Anschluss** with Austria. These results were not free — voting was watched, and there was no real alternative — but historians agree that many voters did genuinely approve.
- **Economic recovery**: registered unemployment fell from around **6 million** in early 1933 to **under half a million** by 1938, thanks to public works (such as the autobahns), rearmament and conscription. For families who had suffered in the Depression, a steady job counted for a great deal.
- **Foreign policy success**: tearing up Versailles — rearmament, the Rhineland (1936), the Anschluss (1938), the Sudetenland (1938) — without war made Hitler hugely popular and restored national pride.
- **Order and stability**: after the street fighting and weak governments of 1930–32, many Germans welcomed an end to political violence and strikes.
- **Benefits for particular groups**: big business gained from the end of trade unions and from rearmament contracts; many workers enjoyed **Strength Through Joy** (KdF) holidays and outings; farmers were praised by propaganda.
- **The "Hitler myth"**: the historian **Ian Kershaw** showed that Hitler personally was far more popular than the Nazi Party. Many Germans blamed problems on corrupt local Nazi officials and believed "if only the Führer knew". Propaganda built Hitler up as a selfless leader standing above everyday politics.

**Reasons to doubt how deep support went**
- **Fear**: the Gestapo, denunciations, concentration camps and the People's Court made it dangerous to say anything critical. Conformity does not prove belief.
- **Grumbling**: secret reports — both the regime's own and the **Sopade reports** gathered by the SPD in exile from contacts inside Germany — recorded widespread complaints about low wages, long hours, food shortages and prices, and the arrogance of local party officials. But these same reports also admitted that Hitler himself remained popular.
- **Propaganda**: Germans heard only the Nazi version of events, so their "support" was shaped by censorship.
- **Apathy**: many people simply kept their heads down and concentrated on private life. Some writers called this "inner emigration".

**Why opposition was weak**
- The **left was crushed** in 1933: the KPD and SPD were banned, thousands of their members were sent to camps, and the trade unions were destroyed. Small underground **communist cells** printed leaflets and **social democrats** kept secret networks, but the Gestapo broke up many groups and arrested thousands.
- The left remained **divided**: communists and social democrats still did not cooperate.
- There were **no legal organisations** left through which to oppose — no parties, no free press, no unions.
- Many Germans **agreed** with much of what the Nazis were doing, or benefited from it.
- The **army** had sworn a personal oath to Hitler (1934), and most officers welcomed rearmament.

### Opposition from the Churches

The Churches were the only large organisations in Germany not fully under Nazi control. But their opposition was **limited**: most clergy of both Churches conformed, many welcomed the fight against communism, and when they protested it was mainly to **defend the Church's own independence**, not to attack the regime as a whole.

**Pastor Martin Niemöller (1892–1984)**
- A **First World War U-boat commander** who became a Lutheran pastor in **Dahlem**, a wealthy district of Berlin. He was a nationalist and at first **welcomed** the Nazi regime.
- He turned against the Nazis when they tried to control the Protestant Church: the creation of the **Reich Church** under Reich Bishop **Ludwig Müller** in 1933 and the plan to remove pastors of Jewish descent (the "Aryan paragraph").
- In **September 1933** he set up the **Pastors' Emergency League** to defend the Church's independence; within months thousands of pastors had joined.
- In **January 1934** he was one of the Protestant leaders who met Hitler; Göring produced a transcript of one of Niemöller's telephone calls, which the Gestapo had tapped — showing that the regime was watching him.
- In **1934** he was a leader in founding the **Confessing Church**, which declared (the **Barmen Declaration**, May 1934) that the Church owed obedience to God, not to the state.
- He continued to preach against Nazi interference in the Church to large congregations. He was **arrested in July 1937**. At his trial in **1938** he was given a short sentence that he had already served in custody — but on release he was **immediately re-arrested by the Gestapo** as Hitler's "personal prisoner" and held in **Sachsenhausen** and later **Dachau** concentration camps until **1945**.
- **Significance**: he showed that open opposition was possible, and the Confessing Church stopped the Reich Church from taking over Protestantism. But his arrest showed the price of opposition, and he was mainly defending the Church rather than opposing Nazism as a whole. After the war he became famous for a statement admitting that he, like many others, had not spoken up when the Nazis came for communists, trade unionists and Jews, because he did not belong to those groups.
- **Other Confessing Church opposition**: hundreds of Confessing Church pastors were arrested in the later 1930s; the young theologian **Dietrich Bonhoeffer** trained pastors for the Confessing Church in secret, later joined the resistance and was executed in April 1945.

**Catholic opposition**
- Catholic opposition was also mainly about defending the Church's rights under the **Concordat** (1933): protests against the closure of Catholic schools and youth groups and the arrest of priests.
- In **March 1937** Pope Pius XI's letter **"With Burning Concern"**, criticising Nazi breaches of the Concordat and racist ideas, was read from Catholic pulpits across Germany. The regime responded by arresting priests and seizing printing presses.
- Some individual priests spoke out and were sent to concentration camps. Later, in **1941**, **Bishop Clemens von Galen** of Münster preached publicly against the Nazi killing of disabled people — one of the few open protests that made the regime change course.

**Assessment**: Church opposition was the most significant open opposition in Nazi Germany in 1933–39, and it kept Christianity alive as an alternative set of values. But it was the work of a **minority** of clergy, it rarely challenged Nazi policies outside religion (for example the persecution of the Jews), and it did not weaken Hitler's hold on power.

### Opposition from the young

The Nazis put enormous effort into winning the young through schools and the **Hitler Youth**, which was made the official state youth organisation in **1936** and compulsory in **1939**. Yet by the late 1930s some young people had grown bored of the drill and discipline, and a minority formed their own groups.

**The Swing Youth (Swingjugend)**
- Mainly **middle- and upper-class** teenagers in big cities such as **Hamburg**, Berlin and Frankfurt.
- They loved **American swing and jazz music**, which the Nazis condemned as "negro music", and danced the jitterbug at clubs and private parties.
- They copied British and American styles: boys grew their hair long and wore long jackets; girls wore make-up; they used English slang. They welcomed Jewish and black musicians and mocked the Hitler Youth's military style.
- They were **not political** in the sense of trying to overthrow the regime: they rejected Nazi culture and wanted freedom and fun. But in a state that tried to control everything, this was itself a challenge.
- The Nazis banned their events and arrested members; in the early 1940s **Himmler** ordered that ringleaders be sent to **concentration camps**.

**The Edelweiss Pirates (Edelweisspiraten)**
- Mainly **working-class** teenagers, roughly **14 to 18**, in the industrial cities of western Germany such as **Cologne**, **Düsseldorf** and **Essen**. Local groups had their own names, such as the **Navajos** in Cologne and the **Kittelbach Pirates**.
- They wore an **edelweiss flower badge** and their own style of clothes (checked shirts, dark shorts), and they refused to join or left the Hitler Youth.
- They went on **hikes and camping trips** at weekends, where **boys and girls mixed freely** — unlike the separate Hitler Youth and League of German Maidens — and sang songs, including songs altered to mock the Nazis.
- They **fought Hitler Youth patrols** and scrawled anti-Nazi slogans.
- During the war some groups went further: they helped deserters, escaped prisoners and forced labourers, and some committed sabotage. In **November 1944**, thirteen people, including several Edelweiss Pirates, were **publicly hanged** in Cologne.

**Assessment of youth opposition**
- It showed that **Nazi control of the young was not complete**: the Hitler Youth failed to win over every teenager, and by the late 1930s many found it boring and regimented.
- But it was **small** — a few thousand compared with millions in the Hitler Youth — and mostly **non-conformity** rather than resistance before 1939. It had no national organisation and no plan to overthrow the regime.
- The historian **Detlev Peukert** argued that such youth cultures matter because they reveal the limits of Nazi claims to have created a united "people's community".

### The judgement a grade 9 answer needs

- **Support was real but not universal**: economic recovery, foreign policy successes and the Hitler myth won genuine approval; fear and propaganda account for much of the rest.
- **Opposition was limited**: the Churches defended themselves, not the victims of Nazism; the young non-conformists wanted their own culture; the left was crushed.
- **Why opposition was so weak** is as important as what it was: terror, the destruction of every independent organisation, the popularity of Hitler, and the fact that many Germans benefited.
- Be precise with **words**: grumbling is not resistance, and the Swing Youth were non-conformists, not revolutionaries.

::: terms
- **Conformity**: outwardly going along with the regime and obeying its rules, whatever a person privately believed.
- **Non-conformity**: refusing to live in the way the Nazis demanded, without trying to overthrow the regime.
- **Resistance**: active attempts to weaken or overthrow the regime, such as underground leaflet campaigns or sabotage.
- **Plebiscite**: a national vote on a single question, such as approving the Anschluss in 1938, used to show public approval.
- **Sopade reports**: secret reports on conditions and opinion inside Germany, gathered by the SPD leadership in exile.
- **Hitler myth**: Ian Kershaw's term for Hitler's personal popularity, which stood above Germans' complaints about the Nazi Party.
- **Pastors' Emergency League**: the group founded by Martin Niemöller in 1933 to defend the Protestant Church from Nazi interference.
- **Confessing Church**: the Protestant church founded in 1934 that declared the Church answerable to God, not to the state.
- **Swing Youth**: mainly middle-class city teenagers who defied Nazi culture by listening and dancing to American swing and jazz.
- **Edelweiss Pirates**: mainly working-class teenagers in western German cities who rejected the Hitler Youth, went hiking and fought Hitler Youth patrols.
:::

::: example Q2 — Explain why there was little opposition
Explain why there was little opposition to the Nazi regime in Germany in the years 1933–39. You may use the following in your answer: the Gestapo; economic recovery. You must also use information of your own. (12)

One reason was fear of the police state. The Gestapo, controlled by Himmler's SS, could hold people in "protective custody" without trial and send them to concentration camps such as Dachau. Although it was small, it relied on denunciations from neighbours and colleagues and on block wardens watching every street, so Germans believed that any critical remark could be reported. This meant that even people who disliked the regime kept quiet, and that underground groups were constantly broken up.

A second reason was that many Germans genuinely approved of what the Nazis had achieved. Registered unemployment fell from around 6 million in early 1933 to under half a million by 1938, and foreign policy successes such as the remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936) and the Anschluss (1938) restored national pride without war. In plebiscites around 99% voted yes, and although these votes were not free, Sopade reports admitted that Hitler himself remained popular even when people grumbled about wages and prices. People who felt their lives were improving had little reason to oppose.

A third reason was that the Nazis had destroyed every organisation that could have led opposition. In 1933 the KPD and SPD were banned, the trade unions were abolished, and the other parties dissolved themselves. The left remained divided even underground. The only large independent organisations left were the Churches, and even there opposition was limited: Niemöller's Confessing Church defended the Church's independence rather than attacking the regime, and Niemöller himself was arrested in 1937. Opposition was therefore weak because potential opponents were frightened, satisfied or leaderless — and the most dangerous had already been removed in 1933.

::: example Q3(d) — how far do you agree
Interpretation 1 (a summary of the view of historians who stress youth resistance): groups such as the Edelweiss Pirates were a real challenge to the Nazis, fighting the Hitler Youth, defying the regime's control of young people and, in some cases, paying with their lives. Interpretation 2 (a summary of the view of historians such as Detlev Peukert): youth groups were mainly about teenagers wanting freedom, music and fun; they rejected Nazi culture but were not political resistance and never threatened the regime. How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about opposition from young people in Nazi Germany? Explain your answer, using both interpretations and your knowledge of the historical context. (16)

I mostly agree with Interpretation 2 for the years before 1939, although Interpretation 1 is more convincing for some Edelweiss Pirate groups during the war.

Interpretation 2 is supported by the Swing Youth. They were mainly middle-class teenagers in cities such as Hamburg who loved American swing and jazz, danced the jitterbug and copied British and American fashions. Their aim was to enjoy themselves, not to overthrow Hitler. The Edelweiss Pirates, too, mostly went hiking and camping at weekends, where boys and girls could mix freely, and sang their own songs. Both groups were small compared with the millions in the Hitler Youth, which became compulsory in 1939, and they had no national organisation or political programme. Before 1939 this was non-conformity rather than resistance.

However, Interpretation 1 has some strength. In a state that claimed total control of young people, simply refusing to join the Hitler Youth was a challenge, and the Nazis treated it as one: Swing Youth ringleaders were later sent to concentration camps on Himmler's orders. The Edelweiss Pirates attacked Hitler Youth patrols and mocked the regime in their songs, and during the war some groups sheltered deserters and escaped prisoners and carried out sabotage. In November 1944 thirteen people, including several Edelweiss Pirates, were publicly hanged in Cologne, which shows that the regime saw them as a real threat.

Overall I agree with Interpretation 2 because, judged by whether they threatened the regime, the youth groups did not: they were small, local and mostly cultural. But Interpretation 1 is right that they exposed the failure of Nazi attempts to control the young, and by the war some had crossed the line from non-conformity into resistance.
