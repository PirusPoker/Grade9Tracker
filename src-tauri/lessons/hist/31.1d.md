## What it is

The "Golden Years" of 1924–29 were not only about Stresemann's economic and foreign policies (lesson 31.1c). The spec also asks about **changes in society**:
1. **Changes in the standard of living**: wages, housing, unemployment insurance and welfare.
2. **Changes in the position of women** in work, politics and leisure.
3. **Cultural changes**: developments in architecture, art and the cinema.

A grade 9 answer does two things with this: it gives precise evidence of change, and it asks **who benefited**. Many industrial workers and young urban women gained; farmers, the old middle classes and conservatives often did not, and many Germans saw the new culture as a threat to traditional values. That resentment matters later, because the Nazis exploited it after 1929.

### 1. Changes in the standard of living

| Area | What changed | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Wages** | Real wages (what wages can buy) rose by about **25 per cent between 1925 and 1928**; by 1928 German industrial workers were among the best paid in Europe | Rises were concentrated among industrial workers in trade unions; wages were decided by arbitration that employers resented |
| **Working hours** | The average working week fell from about **50 hours (1925) to 46 hours (1928)**, so workers had more leisure time | Still long hours by modern standards |
| **Unemployment insurance** | The **Unemployment Insurance Act (1927)** created a national scheme: about **16–17 million workers** paid in **3 per cent** of their wages and could claim cash benefit if they lost their job | The fund was designed to support about 800,000 unemployed; unemployment was never below about 1.3 million and reached 2 million in 1926, so the scheme was under strain from the start |
| **Housing** | Article 155 of the Constitution promised decent housing. A **rent tax** from 1924, government loans, tax breaks and land grants funded housing associations and council building. **Over 2 million new homes were built between 1924 and 1931**, and by **1928 homelessness had fallen by more than 60 per cent**. Estates such as Ernst May's "New Frankfurt" gave workers modern flats with bathrooms, electricity and kitchens | Housing shortages remained in the cities; the building was funded partly by foreign loans |
| **Welfare** | By 1926 the state was supporting about **800,000 disabled war veterans**, **360,000 war widows** and **900,000 war orphans**; health insurance; help for the old and sick | Spending was expensive and raised taxes, which the better-off resented |

**Who did not benefit?**
- **The middle classes** (civil servants, shopkeepers, pensioners, people with savings) had lost their savings in the 1923 hyperinflation, and they were never fully compensated. Many resented a Republic that seemed to help organised workers and big business at their expense.
- **Farmers** suffered from falling world food prices and debt throughout the 1920s, even in the "golden" years.
- **The unemployed**: at least 1.3 million people were out of work even in the best year, 1928.

So living standards improved for many working-class Germans, but the improvement was uneven and depended on the American loans that kept the economy going.

### 2. Changes in the position of women

**Politics.**
- The **Weimar Constitution (1919)** gave women the **vote** on the same terms as men (everyone over 20) and the right to stand for election. **Article 109** declared that men and women have "basically the same" civic rights and duties.
- In **1919, 41 women were elected** to the Reichstag, about **10 per cent** of the deputies, a higher proportion than in the British House of Commons at the time. The number fell back in later elections (32 in 1926).
- Women were also elected to state and local assemblies. Most women deputies, however, sat for the left-wing parties (SPD, KPD); many women voters supported conservative and religious parties such as the Centre Party and the DNVP.

**Work.**
- Many women had worked in factories during the First World War. After 1918 many were pushed out to make jobs for returning soldiers, but women's employment was higher than before the war: about **a third of the workforce** in the mid-1920s was female.
- The biggest growth was in **new white-collar jobs**: typists, secretaries, telephonists, shop assistants and the civil service. The number of women in **professions** grew: by **1933** there were about **100,000 women teachers** and **3,000 women doctors**.
- **Limits:** women were usually paid less than men for the same work, and most women who worked were in traditional, low-paid roles (farming, domestic service, textiles). There was hostility to married women working when men were unemployed, and married women could be dismissed from state jobs.

**Leisure and the "New Woman".**
- In the cities, especially Berlin, a new image appeared: the **"New Woman" (Neue Frau)**. She was young, single and earned her own money; she wore her hair short (the bob), wore make-up and shorter skirts, smoked and drank in public, went out to cafés, cinemas and dance halls without a chaperone, and played sport.
- Women married later and had fewer children; the birth rate fell and divorce became more common.
- **Limits and reaction:** the New Woman was mostly an urban, middle-class or young phenomenon, and much of the image came from magazines and films. Most women lived traditional lives. Conservatives, the Churches and the Nazis attacked the New Woman as a sign of moral decline that threatened the family and the birth rate, and many women themselves disapproved.

### 3. Cultural changes

Article 118 of the Constitution guaranteed **freedom of expression**, and the end of the imperial regime freed artists to experiment. Berlin became one of the cultural capitals of Europe, famous for its theatres, cabarets and nightlife. Much of this culture was **modern, experimental and critical of society**.

**Architecture: the Bauhaus.**
- The **Bauhaus** school of art and design was founded by the architect **Walter Gropius** in **Weimar in 1919**. It moved to **Dessau in 1925**, into a new building designed by Gropius, and later to Berlin, where it was closed under Nazi pressure in 1933.
- Its style was **functional**: simple shapes, no decoration, and modern materials such as **steel, glass and concrete**. Design should serve its purpose ("form follows function"). It combined art with craft and industry, designing furniture (for example **Marcel Breuer's** tubular steel chairs), lamps and household goods for mass production.
- Other examples: Erich Mendelsohn's curved **Einstein Tower** in Potsdam (completed 1921), and the new council housing estates in Frankfurt and Berlin.

**Art: New Objectivity (Neue Sachlichkeit).**
- Artists turned away from romantic images to show everyday life realistically and often harshly: the poor, war cripples, prostitutes and profiteers.
- **Otto Dix**, a war veteran, painted maimed ex-soldiers begging while the rich enjoyed themselves, for example his *Metropolis* triptych (1927–28), which contrasts glamorous Berlin nightlife with crippled veterans in the street.
- **George Grosz** satirised the Republic's elites, for example in *Pillars of Society* (1926), showing a nationalist, a newspaper owner, a politician and a clergyman as empty-headed or violent.
- Art was used to criticise society and to make people think about its problems.

**Cinema.**
- Germany had one of the world's most important film industries, based at the **UFA** studios near Berlin, and cinema-going became hugely popular.
- Famous films: ***The Cabinet of Dr Caligari*** **(1920)**, an expressionist horror film; ***Nosferatu*** **(1922)**, F. W. Murnau's vampire film; ***Metropolis*** **(1927)**, Fritz Lang's science-fiction epic about a city divided between rich rulers and underground workers; and ***The Blue Angel*** **(1930)**, starring **Marlene Dietrich**.
- German directors pioneered new techniques in lighting, camera movement and special effects, and many went on to work in Hollywood.

**Other culture.** Cabaret clubs mocked politicians; **Bertolt Brecht's** *The Threepenny Opera* (1928, music by Kurt Weill) mocked capitalism; jazz music and new dances came from America.

**Reactions.** Many Germans loved the new culture, but many others hated it:
- Conservatives, the Churches and rural Germans saw Berlin's nightlife, cabaret, jazz and the New Woman as **immoral and "un-German"**, a sign of **decadence**.
- Nationalists thought artists such as Dix and Grosz insulted the army and the nation.
- Some organisations campaigned against "trashy" literature and films, and in 1926 a law was passed to protect young people from "trash and filth" in publications.
- The **Nazis** attacked Weimar culture as **"degenerate"** and linked it to Jews and communists. After 1933 the Bauhaus was closed and many artists and film-makers left Germany.

### Overall judgement

| Group | Better off by 1929? |
|---|---|
| Industrial workers in work | Yes: higher real wages, shorter hours, better housing, insurance |
| The unemployed and war victims | Some help, but limited |
| Middle classes | Mixed: savings lost in 1923 never recovered; resentment |
| Farmers | No: falling prices and debt |
| Young urban women | More freedom and work, but lower pay and hostility |
| Artists and intellectuals | Great freedom and creativity |
| Traditional and rural Germans | Many felt threatened by modern culture |

Weimar society changed a great deal in 1924–29, but the changes were **uneven and divisive**. They improved life for many workers and young women in the cities, but they also widened the gap between modern, urban Germany and traditional, rural and conservative Germany, a gap the Nazis exploited after 1929.

::: terms
- **Standard of living**: how well people live, measured by things like real wages, working hours, housing and welfare.
- **Real wages**: what wages can actually buy once price changes are taken into account; they rose by about 25 per cent in 1925–28.
- **Unemployment Insurance Act (1927)**: the law creating a national scheme in which workers paid 3 per cent of wages in return for benefit if unemployed.
- **New Woman (Neue Frau)**: the image of the young, independent urban woman of the 1920s, with short hair, make-up, a job and an active social life.
- **Bauhaus**: the school of art, design and architecture founded by Walter Gropius in Weimar in 1919, known for simple, functional design.
- **New Objectivity (Neue Sachlichkeit)**: a 1920s art movement that showed everyday life realistically and critically, as in the work of Dix and Grosz.
- **Expressionism**: a style using distortion and exaggeration to show emotion, seen in films such as The Cabinet of Dr Caligari (1920).
- **UFA**: Germany's leading film studio, near Berlin, which made films such as Metropolis (1927).
- **Decadence**: moral decline; the charge conservatives and Nazis made against Weimar culture and the nightlife of Berlin.
:::

::: method
**Paper 3 at a glance.** 1 hour 30 minutes, 52 marks (48 + 4 SPaG). **Section A:** Q1 inference from Source A (4) and Q2 "Explain why…" (12). **Section B:** one enquiry based on Sources B and C and Interpretations 1 and 2: 3(a) usefulness (8), 3(b) main difference (4), 3(c) why they differ (4), 3(d) how far do you agree with Interpretation 2 (16 + 4 SPaG). Roughly 6, 18, 15, 5, 5 and 30–35 minutes. Society topics like this one are very common in Section B enquiries, for example "changes in the position of women" or "the standard of living".

**Q1: "Give two things you can infer from Source A about…" (4).** Two inferences, each worth 1 mark, and each backed by 1 mark of detail **quoted or paraphrased from the source**. An inference goes beyond what the source literally says: a photo of women in short skirts in a Berlin café lets you infer "some women had more social freedom", not just "women went to cafés". Use the layout: "Inference 1: … Detail in the source that tells me this: …".

**Q2: "Explain why…" (12).** For example: why living standards improved 1924–29; why the position of women changed; why Weimar culture was controversial. Two stimulus points are given; you must add at least one of your own to reach Level 4.
- Level 1 (1–3): simple or general comments.
- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation with some accurate knowledge.
- Level 3 (7–9): explanation mainly focused on the question with accurate knowledge.
- Level 4 (10–12): analytical explanation, sustained focus, precise knowledge and clear links between reasons.
Write three paragraphs, each a separate reason, each ending with how it caused the outcome. Precise detail here means numbers and names: 25 per cent rise in real wages, 50 to 46 hours, the 1927 Act, 2 million homes, 41 women deputies in 1919, Gropius, Dix, Metropolis.

**Q3(a): "How useful are Sources B and C for an enquiry into…?" (8).**
- Level 1 (1–2): simple comments on content.
- Level 2 (3–5): judgements on content and/or provenance with some context.
- Level 3 (6–8): judgements on **both** sources using **content** and **provenance** (nature, origin, purpose), with your own knowledge used to check them, and a judgement about usefulness for **the enquiry named**.
For a magazine image of a New Woman, ask: who made it and why? A fashion or advertising image may exaggerate how common the lifestyle was, but it is useful for the ideal being sold to young urban women. A conservative or Nazi source attacking modern women is one-sided, but useful evidence of the hostility changes provoked.

**Q3(b): main difference (4).** Level 2 (3–4): identify the difference in the **overall view** of each interpretation and support it with a detail from each. For example, one says women's lives were transformed; the other says change was limited to a minority of young city women.

**Q3(c): why they differ (4).** Level 2 (3–4): give a developed reason. Usually the historians emphasise **different aspects or groups** (urban versus rural, politics versus pay), or use **different evidence**, or the extracts are **partial** selections. You can use Sources B and C to show that evidence exists for both views.

**Q3(d): "How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about…?" (16 + 4 SPaG).**
- Level 1 (1–4): simple judgement, little analysis of the interpretations.
- Level 2 (5–8): some analysis of the interpretations, limited knowledge, weakly supported judgement.
- Level 3 (9–12): explains agreement and disagreement using both interpretations and relevant knowledge; judgement partly justified.
- Level 4 (13–16): evaluates the interpretations using precise knowledge throughout; a sustained judgement fully justified.
Structure: introduction stating what Interpretation 2 argues and your view; a paragraph agreeing, using its details plus your own knowledge; a paragraph disagreeing, using Interpretation 1 plus your knowledge; a conclusion with a **criterion** (for example, "how many women actually saw their lives change?"). Use specialist terms accurately for SPaG.
:::

::: example Explain why: living standards 1924–29 (12)
Explain why the standard of living of many Germans improved in the years 1924–29. You may use the following in your answer: housing; unemployment insurance. You must also use information of your own. (12)

**Higher wages and shorter hours.** The economic recovery after 1924, funded by American loans under the Dawes Plan, created jobs and profits. Arbitration of wage disputes and strong trade unions pushed wages up: real wages rose by about 25 per cent between 1925 and 1928, and the average working week fell from about 50 hours to 46. This improved living standards because workers could buy more and had more leisure time for sport, cinema and clubs.

**Housing.** The Republic treated housing as a duty, promising decent homes in Article 155 of the Constitution. A rent tax from 1924, cheap loans and land grants paid for housing associations and council building, and over 2 million new homes were built between 1924 and 1931. By 1928 homelessness had fallen by more than 60 per cent, and estates such as New Frankfurt gave families flats with bathrooms and electricity. This directly raised the quality of daily life for many working families.

**Welfare and unemployment insurance.** The Unemployment Insurance Act of 1927 meant about 16–17 million workers who paid 3 per cent of their wages could claim benefit if they lost their jobs, removing some of the fear of unemployment. The state also paid pensions to about 800,000 disabled veterans and 360,000 war widows. This improved security as well as income.

**Links and limits.** All of this depended on economic recovery and American loans, which provided the jobs, the tax revenue and the building money. That is why the improvement was fragile, and it did not reach everyone: farmers and the middle classes who had lost their savings in 1923 gained much less.
:::

::: example 16-mark paragraph: women (part answer)
Interpretation 2 (a summary of a historian's view): the Weimar years brought a revolution in the lives of German women, who gained political rights, new careers and personal freedom. How far do you agree? Write the "disagree" paragraph. (part of a 16-mark answer)

Interpretation 2 exaggerates how far women's lives changed, because the gains were real but limited to a minority. Women did gain the vote in 1919 and 41 were elected to the Reichstag, but the number fell to 32 by 1926, and most female voters backed conservative parties that wanted women to stay at home. At work, women were still paid less than men for the same job, most remained in low-paid traditional work such as farming and domestic service, and married women faced pressure to give up jobs to men. The "New Woman" with bobbed hair and a job in an office was mainly a young, urban image, largely created by magazines and films; in rural and small-town Germany, most women lived much as before. As Interpretation 1 suggests, the period brought opportunities rather than a revolution, and it also provoked a strong conservative backlash that the Nazis later exploited.
:::

The middle classes, who had lost their savings in the 1923 hyperinflation and never recovered them; farmers, hit by falling food prices and debt; and the unemployed, at least 1.3 million even in 1928 (2 million in 1926). Many of these groups resented the Republic.

She was controversial because conservatives, the Churches and the Nazis saw her as a threat to traditional family values and the birth rate, and linked her to moral decline.

::: watch
- **Treating change as universal.** Always say which group: industrial workers, farmers, the middle classes, young urban women or rural women had very different experiences.
- **Vague evidence.** "Life got better" earns little; use numbers such as 25 per cent real wage rise, 50 to 46 hours, 2 million homes, the 1927 Act, 41 women deputies.
- **Overstating the New Woman.** She was mainly young, urban and middle-class, and largely a media image.
- **Describing culture without the reaction.** Examiners reward answers that explain why modern culture divided Germany and fed the Nazi attack on "degenerate" art.
- **Inference answers that just copy the source.** An inference must go beyond what the source says, backed by a detail from it.
- **Mixing up dates.** The Bauhaus was founded in Weimar in 1919 and moved to Dessau in 1925; Metropolis was 1927.
:::
