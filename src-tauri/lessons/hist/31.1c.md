## What it is

After the disasters of 1923, the Weimar Republic enjoyed five years of relative stability, 1924–29, often called the **"Golden Years"**. The spec asks for two things:
1. **The reasons for economic recovery**, including the work of Stresemann, the Rentenmark, the Dawes and Young Plans, and American loans and investment.
2. **Stresemann's achievements in gaining international acceptance for Germany** through the Locarno Pact and joining the League of Nations.

A grade 9 answer also weighs **how real the recovery was**: it rested on American money, and Stresemann himself warned it was fragile.

### Gustav Stresemann

Stresemann led the right-liberal **German People's Party (DVP)**. He was a nationalist and a former monarchist who became a *Vernunftrepublikaner*, a republican by reason rather than by heart: he backed the Republic because he saw it as the only realistic way to rebuild Germany. He was **Chancellor from August to November 1923** (about 100 days) and then **Foreign Minister in every government until his death on 3 October 1929**. His strategy was **fulfilment**: cooperate with the Allies and meet treaty obligations, to win trust and then revise the treaty peacefully.

### Economic recovery

| Date | Measure | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| **26 Sept 1923** | **Passive resistance called off** | Ended payments to striking Ruhr workers, the main cause of money printing; reparations resumed, restoring the Allies' trust |
| **Nov 1923** | **Rentenmark** introduced by a new Rentenbank | A new currency in strictly limited supply, backed by a mortgage on German land and industrial assets. Confidence returned and hyperinflation ended |
| 1923–24 | Cuts in spending | Government spending was cut and many public employees dismissed, to balance the budget |
| **Aug 1924** | **Reichsmark** | A permanent currency backed by gold, controlled by an independent **Reichsbank** under **Hjalmar Schacht** |
| **1924** | **Dawes Plan** (US banker **Charles Dawes**) | Annual reparations cut temporarily, rising gradually from 1 billion to 2.5 billion marks a year over five years; the Reichsbank reorganised under Allied supervision; a loan of **800 million gold marks** from the USA; French and Belgian troops to leave the Ruhr (completed 1925) |
| **1929** | **Young Plan** (US businessman **Owen Young**) | Total reparations cut from 132 billion to **37 billion** gold marks; Germany given **59 years** to pay (until 1988); lower annual payments; Allied troops to leave the Rhineland by 1930, five years early |

**American loans and investment.** The Dawes Plan unlocked a flood of American money. Between 1924 and 1930 Germany borrowed about **25 billion marks**, mostly from the USA. The money funded new factories and machinery, roads, housing, schools, swimming pools and sports stadiums built by local councils. Big firms such as the chemicals giant **IG Farben** grew. By **1928 industrial production had passed pre-war (1913) levels**, and exports and wages rose.

**Political effects.** Stability was reflected in voting: support for extreme parties fell. In the **May 1928 election the Nazis won just 2.6 per cent (12 seats)**. The pro-republican SPD won nearly 30 per cent and led a "grand coalition" under Hermann Müller. There were no attempted revolutions between 1924 and 1929. When President Ebert died in February 1925, **Hindenburg** was elected President. He was a monarchist, but he respected the Constitution in these years, which reconciled some conservatives to the Republic.

**Opposition to the plans.** Nationalists attacked both plans for accepting reparations at all. In **1929** the DNVP leader **Alfred Hugenberg**, a press baron, joined with Hitler to force a **referendum** against the Young Plan, the "Freedom Law". It won only about 14 per cent of the vote and failed, but it gave Hitler national publicity and respectability through Hugenberg's newspapers.

### How far was the recovery real?

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Hyperinflation ended; a stable currency returned | Recovery depended on **short-term American loans**, which could be recalled at any time |
| Industrial output above 1913 levels by 1928 | **Unemployment** never fell below about 1.3 million, and reached 2 million in 1926 |
| Reparations reduced and spread out | **Farmers** suffered from falling world food prices and debt throughout |
| New housing, welfare and public works | The middle classes never recovered the savings lost in 1923 |
| Extremist support fell | Reparations still had to be paid, and Germany was borrowing to pay them |

Stresemann saw the danger. In 1928–29 he warned that Germany was "dancing on a volcano": if the short-term American loans were called in, much of the economy would collapse. That is exactly what happened after the Wall Street Crash in October 1929, weeks after his death.

### Stresemann's foreign policy: international acceptance

| Date | Achievement | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **1925** | **Locarno Pact**, negotiated at Locarno in Switzerland in October and signed in London on 1 December | Germany, France and Belgium accepted their **western borders** as fixed; Britain and Italy guaranteed them; Germany accepted the demilitarised Rhineland; disputes over the eastern borders with Poland and Czechoslovakia to be settled by arbitration. Germany was treated as an **equal partner**, not a defeated enemy. The "spirit of Locarno" brought a new mood of cooperation, and Allied troops left the Cologne zone in 1926 |
| **1926** | **Germany joins the League of Nations** (September) as a **permanent member of the Council** | Restored Germany's status as a great power and gave it a voice in international decisions, for example about German minorities abroad |
| 1926 | **Nobel Peace Prize** shared with French foreign minister **Aristide Briand** | International recognition of Stresemann's reconciliation work |
| **1928** | **Kellogg–Briand Pact** | Germany was among more than 60 countries that agreed not to use war to settle disputes, showing it was trusted again |
| **1929** | Young Plan agreed | Reduced reparations and secured the early withdrawal of Allied troops from the Rhineland (completed 1930) |

**Criticisms.** Nationalists said Locarno **confirmed the loss of Alsace-Lorraine** and accepted Versailles. They also complained that joining the League meant accepting a Versailles institution. The eastern borders were left unresolved, which Stresemann privately hoped to revise. The achievements depended heavily on one man and did not change nationalist hostility to the Republic.

**Overall.** Stresemann turned Germany from an outcast into a respected power without firing a shot, and reduced reparations, won the Allies' withdrawal from German soil and restored confidence. But the prosperity underneath rested on foreign loans. The Golden Years were real, but brittle.

::: terms
- **Rentenmark**: the temporary currency introduced in November 1923, in limited supply and backed by German land and industry.
- **Reichsmark**: the permanent gold-backed currency introduced in 1924 and controlled by the Reichsbank.
- **Dawes Plan**: the 1924 agreement that reduced annual reparations for five years and arranged American loans to Germany.
- **Young Plan**: the 1929 agreement that cut total reparations to 37 billion gold marks, payable over 59 years.
- **Locarno Pact**: the 1925 treaties in which Germany accepted its western borders and was treated as an equal by Britain, France and Italy.
- **League of Nations**: the international peace organisation set up in 1920, which Germany joined in 1926 as a permanent Council member.
- **Fulfilment**: Stresemann's policy of carrying out the Treaty of Versailles to win the Allies' trust and then revise it.
- **Kellogg–Briand Pact**: the 1928 agreement in which over 60 countries, including Germany, renounced war as a way of settling disputes.
:::

::: method
**Paper 3 at a glance.** 1 hour 30 minutes, 52 marks (48 + 4 SPaG). **Section A:** Q1 inference from Source A (4) and Q2 "Explain why…" (12). **Section B:** one enquiry on Sources B and C and Interpretations 1 and 2: 3(a) usefulness (8), 3(b) main difference (4), 3(c) why they differ (4), 3(d) how far do you agree with Interpretation 2 (16 + 4 SPaG). Timings: roughly 6, 18, 15, 5, 5 and 30–35 minutes.

**Q1: "Give two things you can infer from Source A about…" (4).** Two rows, each worth 1 mark for an inference and 1 for the supporting detail from the source. A source praising new factories or the Locarno Pact might let you infer that confidence was returning, or that recovery depended on American money. Go beyond what the source literally says, and take the supporting detail from the source, not from your own knowledge.

**Q2: "Explain why…" (12).** Typical questions: why the economy recovered 1924–29, or why Germany's international position improved. You get two stimulus points (for example "the Rentenmark" and "the Dawes Plan") and must add your own.
- Level 1 (1–3): simple or generalised comments, limited knowledge.
- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation with some accurate knowledge; focus may drift.
- Level 3 (7–9): explanation mainly focused on the question, with accurate, relevant knowledge.
- Level 4 (10–12): analytical explanation, sustained focus, wide-ranging precise knowledge and clear links between causes. Going beyond the stimulus is required for Level 4.

Write three cause paragraphs with precise detail: the Rentenmark (November 1923) and Reichsmark (1924); the Dawes Plan (1924, 800 million gold marks loan, payments rising from 1 to 2.5 billion marks a year); the Young Plan (1929, 37 billion over 59 years); American loans (about 25 billion marks, output above 1913 by 1928); Locarno (1925); League membership (1926). End each with "so this helped Germany recover because…". In the conclusion, show how causes link: the stable currency and Dawes Plan unlocked the loans, and the loans were both the engine and the weakness.

**Q3(a): "How useful are Sources B and C for an enquiry into…?" (8).**
- Level 1 (1–2): simple comments on content.
- Level 2 (3–5): judgements on content and/or provenance with some context.
- Level 3 (6–8): judgements on **both** sources using **content and provenance** (nature, origin, purpose), checked against your own knowledge, with a judgement about usefulness for the named enquiry.

A speech by Stresemann praising Locarno was made to win support, so it may play down nationalist criticism, but it is very useful for what the government hoped Locarno would achieve. A nationalist cartoon attacking the Young Plan is one-sided, but useful evidence of the opposition Hugenberg and Hitler exploited in 1929.

**Q3(b): main difference (4).** Level 2 (3–4) identifies the main difference in the *overall view*, supported by a detail from each: for example, one sees the Golden Years as genuine recovery and success; the other sees them as a fragile illusion built on borrowed money.

**Q3(c): why they differ (4).** Level 2 (3–4) explains a reason in a developed way: the historians emphasise different aspects (industrial output and Locarno versus unemployment, farmers' debt and short-term loans), or chose different evidence, or the extracts are partial. Use Sources B and C to show which evidence supports which view.

**Q3(d): "How far do you agree with Interpretation 2…?" (16 + 4 SPaG).**
- Level 1 (1–4): simple answer, little analysis of the interpretations.
- Level 2 (5–8): some analysis, limited knowledge, weakly supported judgement.
- Level 3 (9–12): explains agreement and disagreement using both interpretations and relevant knowledge; judgement partly justified.
- Level 4 (13–16): evaluates the interpretations with precise knowledge throughout; sustained, fully justified judgement.

Structure: an introduction stating what Interpretation 2 argues and your overall view; a paragraph supporting it with its details and your knowledge; a paragraph challenging it using Interpretation 1 and your knowledge (Stresemann's own "dancing on a volcano" warning is a strong point against an over-optimistic view); a conclusion that states your criterion, for example whether the recovery could survive without American loans. Use specialist terms such as Rentenmark, reparations and fulfilment accurately for SPaG.

::: example Explain why: economic recovery 1924–29 (12)
Explain why the German economy recovered in the years 1924–29. You may use the following in your answer: the Rentenmark; the Dawes Plan. You must also use information of your own. (12)

**Ending hyperinflation: the Rentenmark.** Stresemann first called off passive resistance in September 1923, ending the payments to strikers that were driving the printing presses. In November 1923 the Rentenmark was introduced in strictly limited quantities, backed by German land and industry, and in 1924 it was replaced by the gold-backed Reichsmark under Schacht's Reichsbank. This mattered because recovery was impossible while money lost its value by the hour. A stable currency restored the confidence people needed to save, lend and invest.

**Reducing the reparations burden: the Dawes and Young Plans.** The Dawes Plan (1924) cut annual payments for five years, starting at 1 billion marks and rising gradually, and got the French out of the Ruhr, returning Germany's industrial heartland. The Young Plan (1929) cut the total from 132 billion to 37 billion gold marks over 59 years. Lower, predictable payments let the government balance its budget and made Germany look like a safe place to invest.

**American loans.** Most importantly, the Dawes Plan unlocked American money: about 25 billion marks of loans between 1924 and 1930, starting with 800 million gold marks. This paid for new factories, machinery, housing and public works. By 1928 industrial output had passed its 1913 level. The loans also let Germany keep paying reparations: in effect, American money went round in a circle.

**Judgement.** The stable currency was the precondition, but American loans were the engine of recovery. That is also why it was fragile: when the loans were recalled after October 1929, the recovery collapsed.
:::

::: example 16-mark paragraph: Stresemann's achievements
Interpretation 2 (a summary of a historian's view): Stresemann's foreign policy was a great success that restored Germany's position as a respected European power. How far do you agree? Write the "disagree" paragraph. (part of a 16-mark answer)

Interpretation 2 overstates Stresemann's success, because his gains were limited and divided Germans. The Locarno Pact of 1925 treated Germany as an equal, but it also meant Germany **voluntarily accepted** the loss of Alsace-Lorraine and the demilitarisation of the Rhineland. Nationalists such as Hugenberg's DNVP saw this as confirming the Versailles Diktat. Joining the League in 1926 gave Germany a permanent Council seat, but it did nothing about the eastern borders, the Polish Corridor or the 100,000-man army. As Interpretation 1 suggests, what Germany gained was **acceptance, not revision**, and the goodwill depended on Stresemann personally. Within four years of his death in 1929, Hitler was pursuing the opposite policy. So Stresemann restored Germany's respectability, but he did not restore its power.
:::

::: q
What was the Rentenmark and why did it end hyperinflation?
---
A new temporary currency introduced in November 1923 by the Rentenbank. It was issued in strictly limited amounts and backed by a mortgage on German land and industrial assets. Because people trusted it would keep its value, prices stabilised. It was replaced in 1924 by the gold-backed Reichsmark.
:::

::: q
Give three terms of the Dawes Plan (1924).
---
Any three: annual reparations reduced for five years, starting at 1 billion marks and rising to 2.5 billion; the Reichsbank reorganised under Allied supervision; American loans, starting with 800 million gold marks; French and Belgian troops to leave the Ruhr.
:::

::: q
How did the Young Plan (1929) change reparations, and why did some Germans oppose it?
---
It cut the total from 132 billion to 37 billion gold marks, paid over 59 years (until 1988), with lower annual payments, and Allied troops were to leave the Rhineland by 1930. Nationalists such as Hugenberg and Hitler opposed it because it still accepted reparations and war guilt and committed future generations to paying. They forced a referendum in 1929, which failed.
:::

::: q
What did Germany agree to in the Locarno Pact (1925), and why was it important?
---
Germany, France and Belgium accepted their western borders; Britain and Italy guaranteed them; Germany accepted the demilitarised Rhineland; and disputes in the east would go to arbitration. It was important because Germany negotiated as an equal for the first time since 1919, which led to League membership in 1926 and eased French fears.
:::

::: q
Why did joining the League of Nations in 1926 matter for Germany?
---
Germany was given a permanent seat on the League Council, alongside Britain, France, Italy and Japan, which showed it was accepted as a great power again. It could influence international decisions and was no longer treated as an outcast.
:::

::: q
Give two weaknesses of the economic recovery of 1924–29.
---
Any two: dependence on short-term American loans that could be recalled (Stresemann's "dancing on a volcano" warning); unemployment still over 1.3 million and 2 million in 1926; farmers hit by falling prices; middle-class savings never restored; Germany borrowing to pay reparations.
:::

::: q
Why was Stresemann called a "republican by reason" (Vernunftrepublikaner)?
---
He was a nationalist and former monarchist who did not love democracy, but he supported the Republic because he believed it offered the only realistic way to restore Germany's economy and international position.
:::

::: watch
- **Treating the Golden Years as a total recovery.** Always add the dependence on American loans and the continuing problems of farmers and unemployment.
- **Mixing up the plans.** Dawes (1924) cut annual payments temporarily and brought loans. Young (1929) cut the total to 37 billion over 59 years.
- **Muddling the currencies.** The Rentenmark came in November 1923; the Reichsmark in 1924.
- **Saying Locarno fixed all Germany's borders.** It fixed the western borders only.
- **Explain-why answers that list measures without explaining how each one caused recovery,** for example stable currency leading to confidence leading to investment.
:::
