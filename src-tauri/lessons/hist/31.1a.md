## What it is

This topic covers how the Weimar Republic began. Germany went from an empire ruled by a Kaiser to a democratic republic in a few weeks in late 1918. The spec wants two things: **the situation in Germany at the end of the First World War** (political unrest, the Kaiser's abdication, the armistice and the new republic) and **the strengths and weaknesses of the Weimar Constitution**. Everything that follows in the course, from the stab-in-the-back myth to Hitler's use of Article 48, starts here.

### Germany in autumn 1918: a country at breaking point

- **Military defeat.** Germany's Spring Offensive (March 1918) failed. The Allied counter-attack from **8 August 1918** (Amiens, which Ludendorff called the "black day" of the German army) pushed the army back. Allied strength was growing as American troops arrived. On **29 September 1918** the two men who really ran Germany, **Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg** and **General Erich Ludendorff**, told Kaiser Wilhelm II that the war was lost. They advised asking for an armistice and handing power to a more democratic government, so that the civilian politicians would take the blame for defeat.
- **Human cost.** About **2 million** German soldiers had been killed and over 4 million wounded, leaving huge numbers of widows, orphans and disabled veterans.
- **Hunger.** The British naval blockade cut off food imports. After the "turnip winter" of 1916–17, many people lived on turnips, black bread and ersatz (substitute) foods. Around **750,000** German civilians are estimated to have died from malnutrition and related diseases during the war.
- **Economic strain.** The war had been paid for mostly by borrowing and printing money, so prices had roughly quadrupled and the mark had lost much of its value. War profiteers grew rich while workers' real wages fell.
- **Political divisions.** The left (SPD, the more radical USPD and the revolutionary Spartacists) wanted peace and reform or revolution. The right (army officers, landowners, industrialists) wanted to keep the old order.

**Prince Max of Baden** became Chancellor on **3 October 1918** and introduced the October reforms, making the Chancellor answerable to the Reichstag for the first time. This came too late to stop the unrest.

### The revolution of November 1918

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| late Oct – 3 Nov 1918 | Sailors at **Wilhelmshaven and Kiel** refuse orders to sail against the British navy; the Kiel mutiny spreads |
| early Nov 1918 | Workers' and soldiers' councils take control in many towns, copying the Russian soviets |
| 7–8 Nov 1918 | **Kurt Eisner** (USPD) declares Bavaria a socialist republic in Munich |
| **9 Nov 1918** | **Kaiser Wilhelm II's abdication is announced**; **Friedrich Ebert** (SPD leader) becomes Chancellor; **Philipp Scheidemann** proclaims a German republic from the Reichstag; Karl Liebknecht proclaims a "free socialist republic" |
| 10 Nov 1918 | The Kaiser goes into exile in the Netherlands; the **Ebert–Groener pact**: the army will support the new government against revolution if the government leaves the officer corps alone |
| **11 Nov 1918** | **Armistice** signed in a railway carriage at Compiègne; the German delegation is led by **Matthias Erzberger** (Centre Party), a civilian |
| 15 Nov 1918 | Stinnes–Legien agreement: employers recognise trade unions and accept an eight-hour day |
| **19 Jan 1919** | National Assembly elections; women and all adults over 20 vote |
| 6 Feb 1919 | National Assembly meets in **Weimar**, because Berlin is too dangerous |
| 11 Feb 1919 | Ebert elected first President |
| 31 Jul 1919 | Constitution approved (signed 11 August 1919) |

The Kaiser's abdication was forced by the threat of revolution, pressure from the SPD and the army's refusal to back him. Ebert set up a temporary **Council of People's Representatives** of SPD and USPD members. His priority was order: he feared a Russian-style Bolshevik revolution. This is why he made the deal with General Groener, and it matters later: the new democracy relied on an army led by officers who despised it.

The armistice was signed by civilians, not generals. **Hindenburg and Ludendorff avoided putting their names to defeat**, and many Germans had not seen their country invaded (the army was still on French and Belgian soil in November 1918). This created the conditions for the **stab-in-the-back myth**: the claim that the army had not been beaten but betrayed by politicians at home. The politicians who signed became known as the **"November Criminals"**, and the Republic was linked with defeat from the day it was born.

The January 1919 election was a success for moderate parties. The **SPD** won about **38 per cent** (163 seats), the Catholic **Centre Party** about 20 per cent and the liberal **DDP** about 19 per cent. Together they formed the **Weimar Coalition**. Turnout was over 80 per cent, and **37 women** were elected.

### The Weimar Constitution

| Part | How it worked |
|---|---|
| **President** | Elected by the people every **7 years**; head of state; supreme commander of the armed forces; appointed the Chancellor; could dissolve the Reichstag and call new elections |
| **Article 48** | In an emergency the President could suspend civil rights and rule by **decree** without the Reichstag |
| **Chancellor** | Head of government; chose ministers; needed the support of a majority in the Reichstag |
| **Reichstag** | Main parliament; elected every **4 years** by **proportional representation**; all men and women over **20** voted; made laws and controlled the budget |
| **Reichsrat** | Second chamber of representatives of the regional states (Länder) such as Prussia and Bavaria; could delay laws, but the Reichstag could overrule it |
| **Länder** | The states kept their own governments, police and courts |
| **Basic rights** | Freedom of speech, religion, assembly and the press; equality before the law, including for men and women; referendums possible |

**Proportional representation** gave a party one seat for roughly every **60,000 votes**, so even small parties got seats.

**Strengths**
- **Democratic.** It was one of the most democratic constitutions in the world: universal suffrage at 20 (older in Britain), and women could vote and stand.
- **Fair representation.** Proportional representation meant every vote counted and every opinion was represented.
- **Rights.** Civil rights were written down and guaranteed.
- **Checks and balances.** Power was spread out. The President balanced the Reichstag, the Reichstag could hold the Chancellor to account, and the Reichsrat protected regional interests. This was meant to prevent another Kaiser.

**Weaknesses**
- **Proportional representation led to weak coalitions.** No party ever won a majority, so every government was a coalition. Parties with different aims found it hard to agree, and governments collapsed often: there were **20 different cabinets** between 1919 and 1933. Small extremist parties, including the Nazis later, won seats.
- **Article 48.** It was meant for real emergencies but was vague. A President could bypass the Reichstag, which is exactly what Hindenburg did from 1930 onwards.
- **The President's power.** The right to appoint and dismiss the Chancellor and to dissolve the Reichstag put huge power in one person's hands. This became a fatal weakness when the President was an anti-democratic nationalist like Hindenburg (elected 1925).
- **The old elites stayed in place.** Army officers, judges and civil servants from the Kaiser's time kept their jobs. Many were hostile to democracy, as the lenient treatment of right-wing extremists in 1919–23 would show.
- **The states.** The Länder could resist the central government. Bavaria in 1923 is the best example.
- **Its origins.** It was created by the men associated with defeat. Many Germans, used to authoritarian rule, had no experience of democracy and did not trust it.

**Judgement.** Most historians argue that the Constitution was not doomed in itself. It worked reasonably well in 1924–29. Its weaknesses mattered when combined with circumstances: a lost war, a hated peace treaty, economic crisis and an anti-democratic President.

::: terms
- **Armistice**: an agreement to stop fighting, signed on 11 November 1918, before a formal peace treaty.
- **Abdication**: giving up the throne; Kaiser Wilhelm II's abdication was announced on 9 November 1918.
- **Republic**: a country without a monarch, where the head of state is elected.
- **Proportional representation**: an electoral system where the share of seats a party wins matches its share of the vote.
- **Coalition**: a government made up of two or more parties because no single party has a majority.
- **Article 48**: the clause of the Weimar Constitution letting the President rule by decree in an emergency.
- **Reichstag**: the German parliament, elected every four years by all adults over 20.
- **November Criminals**: the insulting name nationalists gave to the politicians who signed the armistice and accepted defeat.
- **Ebert–Groener pact**: the November 1918 deal in which the army promised to support Ebert's government if the government left the officer corps untouched.
:::

::: method
**Paper 3 at a glance.** The paper lasts 1 hour 30 minutes and is worth 52 marks: 48 for content and 4 for SPaG (spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms). **Section A** has Question 1, an inference question on Source A (4 marks), and Question 2, a 12-mark "Explain why…" question with two prompts to use (the specification allows a choice of two, but recent papers such as Summer 2024 set a single compulsory question). **Section B** is one enquiry in four parts, based on Sources B and C and Interpretations 1 and 2, which are printed in a separate booklet. Rough timings: Q1 6 minutes, Q2 18, 3(a) 15, 3(b) 5, 3(c) 5, 3(d) 30–35.

**Q1: "Give two things you can infer from Source A about…" (4).** The answer grid has two rows, each with "What I can infer" and "Details in the source that tell me this". Each row gets 1 mark for a valid inference and 1 for a detail from the source that supports it. An inference is something the source *suggests* but does not say outright. Do not just copy or describe the source, and do not replace the source with your own knowledge.

**Q2: "Explain why…" (12).** You get two stimulus points and are told to use information of your own as well.
- Level 1 (1–3): a simple or general answer with limited knowledge.
- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation, with some accurate knowledge, but not always focused on the question.
- Level 3 (7–9): an explanation that shows some analysis and is mainly focused on the question, with accurate and relevant knowledge.
- Level 4 (10–12): an analytical explanation that stays focused on the question, with wide-ranging, precise knowledge and clear links between causes.
- If you do not go beyond the stimulus points, you cannot reach Level 4.

Write three paragraphs, one cause each: the two stimulus points plus one of your own, or three of your own. Each paragraph should name the cause, give precise evidence, then build a chain ("this meant that… so…") back to the question. End by saying which cause mattered most and how the causes linked.

**Q3(a): "How useful are Sources B and C for an enquiry into…?" (8).**
- Level 1 (1–2): simple judgements based on the content of the sources.
- Level 2 (3–5): judgements based on content and/or provenance, with some context.
- Level 3 (6–8): judgements on **both** sources based on **content and provenance** (nature, origin, purpose), with contextual knowledge used to check or question what they say.

Usefulness is always *for the named enquiry*. A one-sided source can still be very useful, for example as evidence of what nationalists believed. Say what each source is useful for and where it is limited, then give an overall judgement.

**Q3(b): "What is the main difference between these views?" (4).** Level 1 (1–2) picks out differences of detail. Level 2 (3–4) explains the main difference in the *argument*, using details from both interpretations. One paragraph is enough: "Interpretation 1 argues… for example… whereas Interpretation 2 argues… for example…".

**Q3(c): "Suggest one reason why Interpretations 1 and 2 give different views about…" (4).** You may use Sources B and C. Level 2 (3–4) explains a reason in a developed way. Good reasons are that the historians have given weight to different evidence (show that Source B supports one view and Source C the other), that they emphasise different aspects, or that the extracts are only partial. Do not just say "one historian is biased" without explaining how.

**Q3(d): "How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about…?" (16 + 4 SPaG).**
- Level 1 (1–4): a simple answer with little analysis of the interpretations.
- Level 2 (5–8): some analysis of the interpretation, limited knowledge, and a judgement with limited support.
- Level 3 (9–12): explains agreement and disagreement using both interpretations and relevant knowledge, with a judgement that is partly justified.
- Level 4 (13–16): evaluates the interpretations using precise contextual knowledge throughout, with a sustained, fully justified judgement.

Suggested structure:
1. An introduction that states what Interpretation 2 argues and your overall view.
2. A paragraph agreeing with Interpretation 2, using its details and your own knowledge.
3. A paragraph challenging it, using Interpretation 1 and your own knowledge.
4. A conclusion giving your judgement and the criterion you used to reach it.

Make the SPaG marks easy: use accurate specialist terms and write in full sentences.
:::

::: example Inference: the situation in November 1918
Source A is a summary of a report from a German army officer in Berlin, November 1918. It describes sailors and soldiers with red armbands taking over railway stations, workers marching with banners demanding peace and bread, and officers having the badges torn from their uniforms. Give two things you can infer from Source A about the situation in Germany in November 1918. (4)

**Inference 1:** The authorities had lost control of the capital (1). **Detail:** sailors and soldiers were taking over the railway stations (1).

**Inference 2:** Many ordinary Germans were suffering from hunger and war-weariness (1). **Detail:** workers marched with banners demanding "peace and bread" (1).

(Also acceptable: the army's discipline had broken down, shown by officers having their badges torn off.)
:::

::: example Explain why: the Kaiser abdicated (12)
Explain why Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated in November 1918. You may use the following in your answer: the Kiel mutiny; the armistice negotiations. You must also use information of your own. (12)

**Military defeat and the loss of the army's support.** By late September 1918 Germany's last offensive had failed and the Allied advance since 8 August was pushing the army back. On 29 September Hindenburg and Ludendorff told the Kaiser the war was lost. This mattered because the Kaiser's power rested on the army. Once its commanders admitted defeat and advised a more democratic government under Prince Max of Baden, the Kaiser had lost the institution that had kept him in power.

**The Kiel mutiny and revolution at home.** At the start of November sailors at Kiel refused to sail on a suicidal attack on the British navy. The mutiny spread, and workers' and soldiers' councils took over towns across Germany. On 7–8 November Kurt Eisner declared a republic in Bavaria. Because four years of blockade and hunger had made people desperate for peace, the revolt spread fast. The Kaiser now faced a Russian-style revolution, and his continued rule was the main obstacle to calming it.

**The armistice.** President Wilson made it clear that the Allies would not negotiate with the old autocratic regime. The SPD, led by Ebert, threatened to withdraw its support from the government unless the Kaiser went. This linked the two pressures: peace needed the Kaiser gone, and so did preventing revolution. So on 9 November Prince Max announced the abdication, and Ebert became Chancellor.

**Judgement.** The root cause was military defeat, because it destroyed the Kaiser's authority with the army. But it was the revolution from Kiel onwards that made abdication urgent. Without the threat of revolution he might have tried to cling on as a figurehead.
:::

::: example 16-mark plan: the Constitution
Interpretation 1 (a summary of a modern textbook view): the Weimar Constitution was one of the most democratic in the world and worked adequately in normal times; the Republic's problems came from defeat, the Treaty and economic crisis. Interpretation 2 (a summary of another historian's view): the Constitution contained the seeds of its own destruction, because proportional representation produced weak governments and Article 48 gave the President the power to bypass democracy. How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about the weaknesses of the Weimar Constitution? (16 + 4 SPaG)

**Introduction:** Interpretation 2 blames the design of the Constitution itself. I agree it had real weaknesses, but they became fatal only because of the circumstances Interpretation 1 describes.

**Agree:** Proportional representation (one seat per roughly 60,000 votes) meant no party ever had a majority. There were 20 cabinets between 1919 and 1933, so governments could not take firm action. Article 48 was vague. From 1930 Hindenburg used it to govern through Chancellor Brüning without the Reichstag, which opened the door to Hitler.

**Disagree:** In 1924–29 the same Constitution produced stable coalitions and economic recovery. Coalitions work in many democracies, and Ebert used Article 48 in 1923 to *defend* the Republic. The Constitution kept the army, judges and civil servants who hated democracy in place, but that was a political decision rather than a flaw in the text. Defeat, the stab-in-the-back myth and the Depression explain why people turned against it.

**Conclusion:** I partly agree. Interpretation 2 identifies the weaknesses that were *used* to destroy the Republic. But Interpretation 1 is more convincing about *why* they became fatal: the Constitution survived crises in 1919–23 and failed only when the Depression and an anti-democratic President came together.
:::

::: q
Why were Hindenburg and Ludendorff happy for civilians to request the armistice?
---
They knew the war was lost (they told the Kaiser so on 29 September 1918). By handing power to civilian politicians, the army avoided the blame for defeat. The politicians who signed, such as Erzberger, were later attacked as "November Criminals".
:::

::: q
Describe what happened on 9 November 1918.
---
The Kaiser's abdication was announced by Prince Max of Baden. Friedrich Ebert, leader of the SPD, became Chancellor. Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed a German republic from the Reichstag, and Karl Liebknecht proclaimed a socialist republic. The Kaiser fled to the Netherlands the next day.
:::

::: q
Why did the National Assembly meet in Weimar rather than Berlin?
---
Berlin was unsafe because of street fighting and the Spartacist uprising of January 1919. Weimar, a small town associated with Goethe and German culture, was quieter and easier to protect.
:::

::: q
Give two powers of the President under the Weimar Constitution.
---
Any two: appointed and dismissed the Chancellor; could dissolve the Reichstag and call elections; was supreme commander of the armed forces; could use Article 48 to rule by decree in an emergency.
:::

::: q
Explain one strength and one weakness of proportional representation in the Weimar Republic.
---
Strength: it was fair, because a party's seats matched its share of the vote, so every vote counted and small groups were represented. Weakness: no party won a majority, so governments were coalitions that often collapsed (20 cabinets in 1919–33), and extremist parties gained seats and publicity.
:::

::: q
Why did the survival of the old elites weaken the new Republic?
---
Army officers, judges and civil servants from the Kaiser's time kept their jobs (the Ebert–Groener pact protected the officer corps). Many were monarchists who despised democracy. Judges gave light sentences to right-wing extremists: of 354 right-wing political murders in 1919–22, 326 went unpunished. The army refused to fire on the Kapp rebels in 1920. So the Republic could not rely on the people meant to defend it.
:::

::: q
Explain why the Weimar Republic was associated with defeat from the beginning.
---
Its politicians signed the armistice in November 1918, while the generals kept their names off it, and the same politicians accepted the Treaty of Versailles in 1919. Most Germans had not seen their country invaded and did not believe the army had lost. Nationalists therefore blamed the Republic's founders, the "November Criminals", for a stab in the back, so the new democracy was tied to humiliation from the start.
:::

::: watch
- **Mixing up dates.** The abdication was 9 November 1918, the armistice 11 November 1918, the Assembly election 19 January 1919 and the Constitution July/August 1919.
- **Writing "the Constitution was weak because it was democratic".** You must name the mechanism: proportional representation leading to coalitions, Article 48, or the President's power to appoint Chancellors.
- **Weaknesses with no consequence.** Link each one forward, for example Article 48 to Hindenburg and Brüning in 1930–32.
- **Inference answers that just repeat the source.** An inference goes one step beyond what the source says.
- **3(c) answers that say "the historians are biased".** Explain different emphasis or different evidence instead, using Sources B and C.
:::
