## What it is

At the centre of Nazi ideology was **race**. The Nazis believed humanity was divided into races of unequal value, locked in a struggle for survival, and that Germany's strength depended on keeping the "Aryan" race pure. The Volksgemeinschaft (people's community) was only for those judged racially and biologically "fit"; everyone else was an outsider. Between 1933 and 1939 this led to the persecution of **Slavs, Roma and Sinti, homosexuals, people with disabilities** and, above all, **Jewish people** — step by step, through propaganda, law and violence, with each stage making the next easier. This lesson covers Nazi racial beliefs, the treatment of minority groups, and the persecution of the Jews including the **boycott of 1933**, the **Nuremberg Laws (1935)** and **Kristallnacht (1938)**.

### Nazi racial beliefs

- **The Aryan master race (Herrenvolk)**: Nazis claimed that northern Europeans — tall, fair-haired, blue-eyed "Aryans" — were the superior race that had created civilisation. Germans were to keep their "blood" pure.
- **Untermenschen ("sub-humans")**: races the Nazis ranked as inferior, including **Slavs** (Poles, Russians and other eastern Europeans), **Roma and Sinti**, Black people and, at the very bottom, **Jews**.
- **Anti-Semitism**: Hitler portrayed Jews not as a religion but as a "race" that was a parasite on other nations, blaming them for Germany's defeat in 1918 (the "stab in the back"), for communism and for the Depression. These ideas are set out in *Mein Kampf*.
- **Eugenics and "racial hygiene"**: the belief that a nation could be improved by encouraging the "fit" to have children and preventing the "unfit" — the disabled, the "asocial" — from doing so.
- **Lebensraum**: the German master race needed "living space" in the east, to be taken from the "inferior" Slavs.

These ideas were taught in schools (race science in biology — see 31.4a), spread by **Goebbels's** propaganda (the newspaper *Der Stürmer*, edited by Julius Streicher, printed vicious anti-Jewish cartoons), and enforced by the **SS**, whose members had to prove their Aryan ancestry.

### The treatment of minority groups

**Slavs**
- Nazi ideology regarded Slavs as Untermenschen, fit only to work for the Aryan master race. Their lands in eastern Europe were the intended Lebensraum.
- Before 1939 Germany had relatively few Slavs, though Polish workers in Germany faced discrimination. The full consequences of these beliefs came after the invasion of Poland in **September 1939** and of the USSR in 1941, when millions were killed, enslaved or deported.

**Roma and Sinti ("Gypsies")**
- A small community — roughly 26,000–30,000 people in Germany in 1933. The Nazis saw them as racially inferior and as "asocial" because of their travelling way of life.
- The **Nuremberg Laws** of 1935 were extended to them in **November 1935**: they could not marry Aryans.
- In **1936**, before the Berlin Olympics, hundreds of Roma in Berlin were forced into a camp at **Marzahn** on the edge of the city; other cities set up similar camps.
- Many were **sterilised** under the 1933 sterilisation law.
- In **1938** Himmler set up a central office to deal with the "Gypsy menace", and his **December 1938 decree on "Combating the Gypsy Plague"** ordered all Roma and Sinti to be registered and racially assessed. Some were sent to concentration camps as "asocials" in 1938. From 1939 plans were made to deport them; during the war most German Roma and Sinti were murdered.

**Homosexuals**
- The Nazis saw homosexual men as a threat to the Volksgemeinschaft because they would not father children, and as "degenerate". (Lesbianism was not made a crime, since women were not seen as a threat in the same way.)
- Gay bars and clubs were closed in 1933 and gay organisations and publications banned.
- **1935**: **Paragraph 175** of the criminal code was widened so that almost any contact between men could be prosecuted.
- **1936**: Himmler set up the **Reich Central Office for the Combating of Homosexuality and Abortion**.
- Between 1933 and 1945 an estimated **100,000** men were arrested as homosexuals and about **50,000** convicted. Between **5,000 and 15,000** were sent to **concentration camps**, where they had to wear a **pink triangle**; many were castrated or died.

**People with disabilities**
- **The Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring (July 1933)** allowed **compulsory sterilisation** of people with conditions judged hereditary: "feeble-mindedness", schizophrenia, epilepsy, blindness, deafness, physical deformities and severe alcoholism. Doctors had to report cases, and special Hereditary Health Courts ruled on them. About **400,000** people were sterilised by 1945.
- **Propaganda** stressed the cost of caring for disabled people — posters and school maths problems calculated what a disabled person "cost" the community in Reichsmarks.
- From **1939** the regime moved to murder. A **"euthanasia" programme for children** began in 1939 (doctors and midwives had to report babies born with disabilities), and the **T4 programme** for adults began in autumn 1939, backdated by Hitler to **1 September 1939**. Around **70,000** people were killed by gas or lethal injection by **August 1941**, when the official programme was stopped after protests, notably by Bishop **von Galen** of Münster. Killings continued in secret.

**Other "asocials"**: beggars, the homeless, alcoholics, the "work-shy" and prostitutes were rounded up; many were sent to concentration camps, where they wore a black triangle.

### The persecution of Jewish people

There were about **500,000 Jews** in Germany in 1933 — **less than 1%** of the population. Most were well integrated: many had fought for Germany in the First World War (about 100,000 served), and they were prominent in law, medicine, business, journalism and the arts. Persecution grew in stages.

| Date | Measure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Apr 1933 | **Boycott** of Jewish shops and businesses | First national anti-Jewish action |
| 7 Apr 1933 | Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service | Jews dismissed from the civil service, including teachers and judges |
| 1933–34 | Jews excluded from journalism, the arts, broadcasting; Jewish shops marked | Jews pushed out of public life |
| May 1935 | Jews banned from the armed forces | Excluded from national service |
| 15 Sep 1935 | **Nuremberg Laws** | Jews lost citizenship; marriage with Aryans banned |
| Aug 1936 | Berlin Olympics — anti-Jewish signs temporarily removed | Persecution hidden from foreign visitors |
| 1938 | Jews register property; doctors and lawyers lose licences; passports stamped "J"; forced names "Israel" and "Sara" | Economic ruin and identification |
| 9–10 Nov 1938 | **Kristallnacht** | Nationwide violence; about 30,000 Jewish men sent to camps |
| Nov 1938 onwards | Jews excluded from economic life; Jewish children expelled from German schools | Complete exclusion; pressure to emigrate |
| Jan 1939 | Reich Central Office for Jewish Emigration (under Heydrich) | Forced emigration organised |

**The boycott of Jewish shops and businesses, 1 April 1933**
- Organised by the Nazi Party, led by **Julius Streicher**, officially as a response to "atrocity propaganda" about Germany abroad.
- **SA men stood outside Jewish shops, doctors' surgeries and lawyers' offices**, telling people not to go in. The **Star of David** was painted on windows, with slogans such as "Germans! Defend yourselves! Don't buy from Jews!"
- It lasted **one day**. Many Germans ignored it and shopped as normal, some deliberately; foreign reaction was hostile and the economy was fragile, so the regime called it off. But it signalled that Jews were now outsiders — and within a week came the civil service law, which dismissed Jewish officials (with exemptions at first for First World War veterans, pressed by Hindenburg).

**The Nuremberg Laws, 15 September 1935**
Announced at the Nazi Party rally at Nuremberg:
- **The Reich Citizenship Law**: only people of "German or related blood" could be citizens. Jews became **"subjects"** of the state, not citizens — they lost the vote and the right to hold public office.
- **The Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour**: **marriages and sexual relations between Jews and German citizens were banned**; marriages made in breach of the law were void, even if they took place abroad. Jews could not employ German women under 45 as domestic servants or fly the German flag.
- A **supplementary decree (November 1935)** defined a Jew as anyone with **three or four Jewish grandparents** (or two, if they practised the Jewish religion or were married to a Jew); people of mixed descent were called **Mischlinge**.
- The laws gave **legal form** to the persecution: Jews were now officially second-class, and the definitions later made it possible to identify people for deportation.

**Growing persecution, 1936–38**
- During the **1936 Olympics** the regime removed "Jews not wanted" signs and toned down *Der Stürmer* to impress the world — then resumed.
- **1938** was the turning point. After the **Anschluss** with Austria (March 1938), violence against Viennese Jews was open. Jews had to **register all property** worth more than 5,000 Reichsmarks (April); Jewish businesses were **"Aryanised"** — sold cheaply to non-Jews; Jewish **doctors** and then **lawyers** lost their licences; Jews had to carry identity cards and take the extra names **"Israel"** or **"Sara"**; and passports were stamped with a red **"J"** (October).
- In **October 1938** some 17,000 Polish-born Jews were expelled from Germany and dumped at the Polish border.

**Kristallnacht ("the Night of Broken Glass"), 9–10 November 1938**
- **Trigger**: **Herschel Grynszpan**, a 17-year-old Polish Jew living in Paris, whose family had been among the Jews expelled to the Polish border, shot the German diplomat **Ernst vom Rath** on **7 November 1938**. Vom Rath died on **9 November**.
- **Goebbels** used the news, at a party gathering in Munich that evening, to call for "spontaneous" action. In reality SA and SS men, often in plain clothes, and Hitler Youth members carried out attacks across Germany and Austria while police and fire brigades were ordered not to intervene except to protect German property.
- **Results**: about **91 Jews were killed** (many more died in the camps afterwards); around **7,500 Jewish shops and businesses** were destroyed or damaged; at least **267 synagogues** were burned or wrecked; homes, schools and cemeteries were vandalised; about **30,000 Jewish men** were arrested and sent to **Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen**.
- **Aftermath**: the Jewish community was ordered to pay a "fine" of **1 billion Reichsmarks**, and insurance payments for the damage were **confiscated by the state**. On **12 November** a decree **excluded Jews from economic life** (they could no longer run shops or businesses from January 1939). Jewish children were **expelled from German schools** (15 November), and Jews were banned from cinemas, theatres and many public places.
- **Reactions**: some Germans were shocked by the destruction and disorder, and a few helped Jewish neighbours, but there was little open protest. Foreign opinion was outraged: the USA recalled its ambassador, and Britain began the **Kindertransport**, which took about 10,000 Jewish children to safety.

**Emigration**: the Nazi aim before 1939 was to force Jews to leave. In **January 1939** the **Reich Central Office for Jewish Emigration** was set up under **Heydrich**. By **September 1939** about **282,000** Jews had left Germany — more than half — but emigration was hard: Jews had to leave most of their wealth behind, and many countries restricted entry (the **Évian Conference**, July 1938, showed that few nations would take more refugees).

### Why did persecution increase, and why did so few Germans resist?

- **Ideology**: race was the core of Nazism; persecution was always the aim, only the pace changed.
- **Growing security**: by 1938 the regime was secure at home, the economy was strong and foreign powers were appeasing Hitler, so it cared less about opinion abroad (compare the cautious one-day boycott of 1933 with Kristallnacht).
- **Rivalry among Nazi leaders**: Goebbels, Himmler and Heydrich competed to show radical zeal.
- **Propaganda** made persecution seem normal; the **police state** made opposition dangerous; and many Germans **benefited** from Jewish jobs, shops and property.
- Ian Kershaw's famous verdict captures German opinion: "the road to Auschwitz was built by hate, but paved with indifference".

::: terms
- **Aryan**: the Nazi term for the supposedly superior "master race" of northern Europeans, whose blood Germans were meant to keep pure.
- **Untermenschen**: "sub-humans" — the Nazi label for groups they ranked as racially inferior, such as Slavs, Roma and Sinti, and Jews.
- **Eugenics**: the belief that a population can be "improved" by controlling who has children — used to justify sterilising the disabled.
- **Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring**: the July 1933 law allowing compulsory sterilisation; about 400,000 people were sterilised by 1945.
- **T4 programme**: the secret "euthanasia" programme from 1939 that murdered around 70,000 disabled adults by August 1941.
- **Paragraph 175**: the section of the criminal code against homosexuality, widened by the Nazis in 1935 to make prosecution easier.
- **Boycott of 1933**: the one-day Nazi boycott of Jewish shops and businesses on 1 April 1933, enforced by SA men outside the premises.
- **Nuremberg Laws**: the September 1935 laws that took German citizenship from Jews and banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans.
- **Kristallnacht**: "the Night of Broken Glass", 9–10 November 1938 — nationwide attacks on Jewish shops, homes and synagogues.
- **Aryanisation**: the forced transfer of Jewish-owned businesses and property to non-Jews, usually at far below their value.

::: example Q2 — Explain why persecution of the Jews increased
Explain why the persecution of Jewish people in Germany increased in the years 1933–39. You may use the following in your answer: the Nuremberg Laws; Kristallnacht. You must also use information of your own. (12)

One reason was Nazi racial ideology, which made persecution the aim from the start. Hitler's *Mein Kampf* portrayed Jews as a "race" that had caused Germany's defeat in 1918 and threatened the Aryan people. In power, the Nazis moved quickly: the boycott of 1 April 1933 and the civil service law a week later removed Jews from public life. The Nuremberg Laws of September 1935 then took away their citizenship and banned marriage with Aryans, and the November decree defined a Jew by grandparents. Because the goal was a racially "pure" Volksgemeinschaft, each measure prepared the way for the next.

A second reason was that the regime grew more secure and more confident, so it no longer needed to hold back. In 1933 the boycott was called off after a day because of hostile foreign reaction and fears for the fragile economy, and in 1936 anti-Jewish signs were removed for the Olympics. By 1938 unemployment had almost gone, the army was loyal and Austria had been annexed, while Britain and France were appeasing Hitler. That year Jews had to register property, doctors lost their licences and passports were stamped "J". This mattered because Hitler could now persecute openly without fearing the consequences.

A third reason was that Nazi leaders competed to be the most radical and used events as opportunities. When Herschel Grynszpan shot Ernst vom Rath in Paris, Goebbels used his death on 9 November 1938 to launch Kristallnacht: about 91 Jews were killed, 7,500 businesses and at least 267 synagogues were attacked, and 30,000 Jewish men were sent to camps. Afterwards Jews were fined 1 billion Reichsmarks and excluded from economic life, and Heydrich's emigration office (January 1939) forced them to leave. So persecution increased because ideology set the direction, while security and rivalry among Nazi leaders set the speed — and weak public opposition allowed it to continue.

::: example Q3(d) — how far do you agree (persecution)
Interpretation 1 (a summary of the view of historians who stress Hitler's ideology): the persecution of the Jews followed Hitler's long-held plan; every step from 1933 to 1939 was part of a clear programme to remove Jews from Germany. Interpretation 2 (a summary of the view of historians who stress circumstances): persecution grew unevenly, speeding up or slowing down depending on events, the economy, foreign opinion and competition between Nazi leaders. How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about the persecution of the Jews in the years 1933–39? Explain your answer, using both interpretations and your knowledge of the historical context. (16)

I largely agree with Interpretation 2, because the pace of persecution clearly changed with circumstances, although Interpretation 1 is right that the direction was fixed by Hitler's ideology.

Interpretation 2 is supported by the uneven pattern of persecution. The boycott of 1 April 1933 was called off after one day because of hostile foreign reaction and fear for the economy, and Jewish war veterans were at first exempted from the civil service law because Hindenburg insisted. Anti-Jewish signs were removed for the 1936 Olympics. The biggest escalation came in 1938, when the regime felt secure after the Anschluss, and Kristallnacht was sparked by an unplanned event — the shooting of vom Rath — and launched by Goebbels, who wanted to regain Hitler's favour. Even the Nuremberg Laws were drafted hastily at the 1935 rally.

However, Interpretation 1 is also convincing. Hitler's hatred of Jews was clear in *Mein Kampf* and the 1920 party programme, which already said Jews could not be citizens — exactly what the Reich Citizenship Law of 1935 did. Every stage moved in one direction: exclusion from the civil service (1933), loss of citizenship (1935), loss of property and businesses (1938), expulsion of Jewish children from schools, and forced emigration through Heydrich's office in 1939. About 282,000 Jews had been driven out by September 1939.

Overall I agree with Interpretation 2 more, because the evidence shows that timing depended on the economy, foreign opinion and rival leaders; nothing in 1933 guaranteed the violence of 1938. But Interpretation 1 explains why persecution never went into reverse: the ideology set the goal, and circumstances only set the pace.

Interpretation 2 (a summary of the view of historians who stress violence): the most important feature of Nazi persecution of the Jews before the war was violence and terror, which culminated in Kristallnacht and drove Jews out of Germany. How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 about the persecution of the Jews in the years 1933–39? Explain your answer, using both interpretations and your knowledge of the historical context. (16 + 4 SPaG)
---
Level 4 (13–16): a sustained evaluation of Interpretation 2, using Interpretation 1 and precise contextual knowledge, reaching a fully justified overall judgement.
Level 3 (9–12): an explained evaluation with relevant knowledge; a judgement that is partly justified.
Level 2 (5–8): some analysis of the interpretation with some knowledge; a judgement with limited support.
Level 1 (1–4): simple comments, limited knowledge.
Indicative content — supporting Interpretation 2: SA intimidation during the 1 April 1933 boycott; local SA violence in 1933 and 1935; open violence in Vienna after the Anschluss (1938); Kristallnacht, 9–10 November 1938 — about 91 killed, 7,500 businesses and at least 267 synagogues attacked, 30,000 men sent to Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen; emigration surged after Kristallnacht, with about 282,000 gone by September 1939.
Challenging it / Interpretation 1: civil service law (April 1933); exclusion from journalism, the arts and the army (1935); Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935) — loss of citizenship, marriage ban, the November 1935 definition; 1938 decrees — property registration, Aryanisation, doctors' and lawyers' licences, "Israel"/"Sara" names, "J" passports; after Kristallnacht it was a decree (12 November) that excluded Jews from economic life and a fine of 1 billion Reichsmarks that ruined them; the law gave persecution permanence and the appearance of legitimacy, which made most Germans accept it.
Judgement: any justified view, e.g. that legal measures did more lasting damage and affected every Jew, while violence was occasional but accelerated emigration — or that the two worked together, with laws following each outbreak of violence.
SPaG (4): high performance 4; intermediate 2–3; threshold 1.
