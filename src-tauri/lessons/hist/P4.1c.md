## What it is

In the 1950s the Cold War became more dangerous. Both superpowers raced to build ever more powerful nuclear weapons and ways to deliver them (**the arms race**); the Eastern bloc formalised its military alliance (**the Warsaw Pact, 1955**); and in **1956** a popular uprising in Hungary was crushed by Soviet tanks while the West watched. This topic covers the significance of the arms race, the formation of the Warsaw Pact, the events of 1956 that led to the Hungarian Uprising, Khrushchev's response and the international reaction.

### The arms race

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| July–August 1945 | USA tests and uses the atomic bomb (Hiroshima, Nagasaki) — a US monopoly |
| August 1949 | USSR tests its first atomic bomb — the monopoly ends years earlier than the USA expected |
| 1950 | US report **NSC-68** recommends a massive increase in military spending to contain communism |
| November 1952 | USA tests the first **hydrogen bomb** (H-bomb), hundreds of times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb |
| August 1953 | USSR tests its own H-bomb; a more advanced design follows in 1955 |
| August 1957 | USSR tests the first **intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM)**, able to hit the USA from Soviet territory |
| 4 October 1957 | USSR launches **Sputnik**, the first artificial satellite — proof its rockets could reach anywhere |
| 1958–59 | USA develops its own ICBMs; NASA set up in 1958; claims of a "missile gap" in the Soviet Union's favour |

**Why the arms race happened.** Each side believed the other was aggressive and could only be deterred by strength. Once one side developed a weapon, the other felt it had to match it — a cycle of fear. Each side also wanted the **prestige** of technological leadership.

**Significance of the arms race:**
1. **It increased fear and tension.** Each new weapon made the other side feel more threatened. Sputnik and the Soviet ICBM caused real alarm in the USA: for the first time American cities could be hit directly within minutes. Americans built fallout shelters and schools ran nuclear drills.
2. **It made direct war too dangerous.** Paradoxically, because a war could destroy both sides, the superpowers avoided fighting each other directly. The Cold War was fought through crises, propaganda, alliances and proxy wars instead. This idea of **deterrence** later became known as mutually assured destruction (MAD).
3. **It shaped policy.** US leaders relied on nuclear threats (the Eisenhower administration's "massive retaliation"); leaders on both sides practised **brinkmanship** — pushing a crisis to the edge of war to force the other to back down.
4. **It was enormously expensive**, draining resources from both economies — especially the smaller Soviet economy.
5. **It spread the Cold War into space** — the "space race" began with Sputnik.

### The Warsaw Pact, 1955

**Background.** Stalin died in **March 1953**. Several leaders competed to succeed him; by 1955–56 **Nikita Khrushchev** had emerged as the dominant figure. Meanwhile, in **May 1955** the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) joined **NATO** and was allowed to rearm. For the USSR, which had lost millions of people to German invasion, a rearmed West Germany inside a hostile alliance was alarming.

**The Pact.** On **14 May 1955**, days after West Germany joined NATO, the **Warsaw Pact** was signed. Members: **the USSR, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Albania and East Germany**. It was a military alliance: members would defend each other if attacked, and forces were under a unified command headed by a Soviet commander, with headquarters in Moscow.

**Significance:**
- It was a direct **response to NATO** and to West German rearmament — Europe was now divided into **two armed military alliances**.
- It gave the USSR **legal grounds** to station troops in Eastern Europe and to keep a tight grip on its satellites' armed forces.
- It strengthened Soviet control: leaving the Pact would be treated as a threat to the whole bloc — which is exactly what happened when Hungary tried to leave in 1956.
- It confirmed the Cold War division as permanent; the Pact lasted until 1991.

### Khrushchev and de-Stalinisation

Khrushchev seemed to offer a new start:
- **Peaceful coexistence**: he said capitalism and communism could exist side by side without war, and competition should be economic, not military.
- **The Secret Speech, February 1956**: at the 20th Party Congress Khrushchev attacked Stalin's crimes — the purges, the terror, the "cult of personality". The speech quickly became known across Eastern Europe.
- He improved relations with Tito's Yugoslavia and closed Cominform in April 1956, suggesting there could be **different roads to socialism**.

To many in Eastern Europe this suggested Soviet control would loosen. In **Poland**, riots in **Poznań (June 1956)** led to the return of the reformer **Władysław Gomułka** as party leader in October. Khrushchev accepted this because Gomułka promised Poland would remain communist and in the Warsaw Pact. Hungarians hoped for the same — but went much further.

### Events of 1956 leading to the Hungarian Uprising

**Long-term causes.**
- **Mátyás Rákosi**, Hungary's hardline Stalinist leader, ruled through the secret police (the **ÁVH**); thousands were arrested, imprisoned or executed.
- Hungarians resented Soviet control: Soviet troops were stationed in the country, Russian was taught in schools, and Hungarian goods were exported to the USSR while Hungarians faced food and fuel shortages and low living standards.
- Hungarians were strongly Roman Catholic and nationalist; the communists persecuted the Church (Cardinal Mindszenty was imprisoned).

**Short-term causes and events.**

| Date (1956) | Event |
|---|---|
| February | Khrushchev's Secret Speech raises hopes of change |
| June–October | Poznań riots and Gomułka's return in Poland show reform might be possible |
| July | Moscow removes Rákosi, but replaces him with another hardliner, **Ernő Gerő** — this disappoints reformers |
| 23 October | Students and workers demonstrate in Budapest demanding reform and the return of **Imre Nagy**; the giant statue of Stalin is pulled down; the ÁVH fires on crowds at the radio station and fighting spreads |
| 24 October | **Imre Nagy** becomes prime minister; Soviet troops enter Budapest |
| Late October | Ceasefire; Soviet troops withdraw from Budapest; Nagy promises reforms, including free elections and an end to one-party rule |
| 1 November | Nagy announces Hungary will **leave the Warsaw Pact** and become neutral, and appeals to the United Nations |

Nagy's reforms — multi-party elections, and especially leaving the Warsaw Pact — crossed the line Khrushchev had set in Poland.

### Khrushchev's response

**Why Khrushchev acted:**
- Leaving the Warsaw Pact would open a hole in the Soviet buffer zone and could encourage other satellites to follow — the whole bloc might unravel.
- Ending one-party rule meant the end of communist control in Hungary.
- Khrushchev had to show hardliners in the Kremlin that de-Stalinisation did not mean weakness.
- The timing was favourable: Britain and France were engaged in the **Suez Crisis** in Egypt (from late October 1956), dividing the West and distracting the UN; the USA was in the middle of a presidential election (6 November).

**What he did.** On **4 November 1956** Soviet troops and around **1,000 tanks** entered Budapest. Hungarians fought back with rifles and petrol bombs but were crushed within days. About **2,500–3,000 Hungarians** were killed and some **200,000** fled to the West, mostly through Austria. Soviet losses were around 700.

- **János Kádár** was installed as leader of a new pro-Soviet government; he ruled Hungary until 1988.
- Nagy took refuge in the Yugoslav embassy; he left after being promised safe passage, was arrested, secretly tried and **executed in June 1958**.
- Kádár kept Hungary loyal to the Warsaw Pact but later allowed some limited economic freedom.

### International reaction

- **The United Nations**: the USSR vetoed a Security Council resolution; the General Assembly condemned the invasion and called for Soviet withdrawal, but the USSR ignored it and UN observers were not allowed in.
- **The USA**: Eisenhower condemned the invasion but did **not** intervene. Hungary was inside the Soviet sphere; intervention risked nuclear war. Yet Radio Free Europe (US-funded) had broadcast encouragement, and US leaders had spoken of "liberating" Eastern Europe, so many Hungarians expected help and felt betrayed. The USA did accept tens of thousands of Hungarian refugees and sent food and medical aid.
- **Western Europe**: Britain and France were preoccupied with Suez, which weakened their moral authority to criticise the USSR.
- **Olympic protests**: at the Melbourne Olympics (November–December 1956) the Netherlands, Spain and Switzerland withdrew in protest; the water polo match between Hungary and the USSR became violent.
- **Western communists**: many members left Western communist parties in disgust.

**Significance:**
- It showed that **the USA would not use force inside the Soviet sphere** — containment, not "rollback". In effect, the West accepted that Eastern Europe was Soviet.
- It showed that **Khrushchev's de-Stalinisation had limits**: reform was tolerated only if countries stayed communist and in the Warsaw Pact.
- It **strengthened Soviet control** and Khrushchev's position; the satellites learned that resistance would be crushed (a lesson repeated in Czechoslovakia in 1968).
- It **damaged the USSR's image** abroad, handing the West a propaganda victory.

::: terms
- **Arms race**: the competition between the USA and USSR to build more and more powerful nuclear weapons and delivery systems.
- **Hydrogen bomb**: a thermonuclear weapon far more powerful than the atomic bomb, tested by the USA in 1952 and the USSR in 1953.
- **ICBM**: an intercontinental ballistic missile, able to carry a nuclear warhead from one continent to another; first tested by the USSR in 1957.
- **Sputnik**: the first artificial satellite, launched by the USSR in October 1957, showing its rocket technology could reach the USA.
- **Deterrence**: the idea that having powerful weapons stops an enemy attacking, because the retaliation would be unacceptable.
- **Warsaw Pact**: the military alliance of the USSR and its satellite states formed in May 1955 in response to West Germany joining NATO.
- **De-Stalinisation**: Khrushchev's policy of criticising Stalin's crimes and loosening some of his harshest controls after 1956.
- **Peaceful coexistence**: Khrushchev's idea that capitalist and communist states could compete without going to war.
- **Secret Speech**: Khrushchev's February 1956 speech to the 20th Party Congress attacking Stalin's crimes and cult of personality.
- **ÁVH**: the Hungarian communist secret police, hated for the arrests and terror of the Rákosi years.
:::

::: method
Paper 2 period study (Booklet P, 32 marks, about 50 minutes, no SPaG). Every question is worth 8 marks and assesses AO1 (knowledge) and AO2 (analysis).

**1. "Explain two consequences of…" (8)** — marked as two separate answers of 4.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple or generalised comment ("people died").
- Level 2 (3–4): the consequence is explained with specific, accurate knowledge.
- For Hungary, pick consequences that are clearly different: one for **Hungary**, one for **international relations** (e.g. the US refusal to intervene).

**2. "Write a narrative account analysing…" (8)**
- Level 1 (1–2): simple story.
- Level 2 (3–5): a sequence with some links. **Capped at 5 if you do not go beyond the two stimulus bullets.**
- Level 3 (6–8): a clear chronological account — cause → event → consequence — where every paragraph explains **why** the next thing happened. For 1956: Secret Speech → Rákosi/Gerő → 23 October → Nagy's reforms → leaving the Warsaw Pact → 4 November.

**3. "Explain two of the following: the importance of X for Y" (8 each, choose two of three)**
- Level 1 (1–2): general comment on X.
- Level 2 (3–5): some explanation of importance with relevant detail.
- Level 3 (6–8): shows **how X changed Y** with precise knowledge — e.g. the importance of the Hungarian Uprising *for relations between the USA and the USSR* means the US refusal to act and what it revealed. Two or three developed strands, ending with a line on which mattered most.
:::

::: example Consequences — the Hungarian Uprising
Explain two consequences of the Hungarian Uprising (1956). (8)

**Consequence 1:** One consequence was that Soviet control over Hungary was restored and strengthened. After Nagy announced on 1 November that Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact, Khrushchev sent in around 1,000 tanks on 4 November; the resistance was crushed within days and about 2,500–3,000 Hungarians were killed. János Kádár was installed as a loyal leader and Nagy was executed in 1958. This showed every satellite state that leaving the Warsaw Pact would not be tolerated.

**Consequence 2:** A second consequence was that it became clear the USA would not intervene inside the Soviet sphere. Radio Free Europe had encouraged the rebels and US leaders had talked of liberating Eastern Europe, but Eisenhower only condemned the invasion and supported a UN resolution the USSR ignored, because military action risked nuclear war. This meant the West had in practice accepted Soviet domination of Eastern Europe, and Hungarians felt betrayed.
:::

::: example Narrative — the road to the Hungarian Uprising
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of 1956 that led to the Hungarian Uprising. You may use the following in your answer: Khrushchev's Secret Speech (1956); Imre Nagy. You must also use information of your own. (8)

Hungarians had long resented Soviet control under Mátyás Rákosi, whose ÁVH secret police imprisoned and executed opponents while living standards stayed low. In February 1956, Khrushchev's Secret Speech denounced Stalin's crimes, which encouraged Hungarians to hope that Stalinist leaders like Rákosi would go and that change was possible.

This hope grew in the summer. Moscow removed Rákosi in July, but replaced him with Ernő Gerő, another hardliner, which frustrated reformers. At the same time events in Poland, where the reformer Gomułka returned to power in October, suggested that the USSR might accept a more independent path.

As a result, on 23 October students and workers in Budapest demonstrated for reform and pulled down the statue of Stalin. When the ÁVH fired on the crowd, the protest became an armed uprising. To calm the situation the popular reformer Imre Nagy was made prime minister, and after a ceasefire Soviet troops withdrew from Budapest.

However, Nagy's reforms went further than Khrushchev would accept. He promised free elections and, on 1 November, announced that Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact and become neutral. This threatened the whole Soviet buffer zone, so on 4 November Khrushchev sent Soviet tanks into Budapest, crushing the uprising and installing Kádár.
:::

::: example Importance — the arms race
Explain the importance of the arms race for relations between the USA and the Soviet Union in the years 1949–58. (8)

The arms race was important first because it increased fear and mistrust. The Soviet atomic test of August 1949 ended the American monopoly years earlier than expected, and the USA responded by building the hydrogen bomb in 1952; the USSR matched it in 1953. Each new weapon made the other side feel less secure and more convinced of its rival's aggressive intentions.

It was also important because it shifted the balance of prestige. In 1957 the USSR tested the first ICBM and launched Sputnik, showing that its missiles could now reach American cities. This caused alarm in the USA, fears of a "missile gap", and huge new spending on rockets and the creation of NASA, drawing the rivalry into space.

Most importantly, the arms race changed the way the Cold War was fought. Because nuclear war threatened to destroy both sides, the superpowers avoided direct conflict and instead used deterrence, alliances and brinkmanship. This explains why, for example, the USA did not intervene militarily in Hungary in 1956: the risk of nuclear war was too great.
:::

- Saying Hungary was invaded because Nagy wanted reform is only half the answer — the trigger was **leaving the Warsaw Pact**.
- Arms-race answers that only list dates — the marks are for the **significance**: fear, deterrence, brinkmanship, cost, prestige.
- Forgetting the **Suez Crisis** when explaining why the West did not respond more strongly.
- Narrative answers capped at 5 because they only discuss the Secret Speech and Nagy — add Rákosi/Gerő, 23 October and 4 November.
