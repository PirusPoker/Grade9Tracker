## What it is

This lesson covers the second half of **c1700–c1900: Medicine in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Britain** — the **extent of change in care and treatment**: better hospital care and **Florence Nightingale's** influence on nursing and hospitals; the impact of **anaesthetics** and **antiseptics** on surgery; the new approach to prevention through the **Public Health Act (1875)**; and the case study of **fighting cholera in London in 1854**, with **John Snow** and the **Broad Street pump**. (Vaccination and Jenner are in the previous lesson.) The big idea: the nineteenth century saw real change in **surgery, nursing and public health** — but much of it came **before** germ theory was accepted, driven by individuals, war, the growth of towns, and changes in government.

### Hospitals before Nightingale

- The eighteenth century saw many new **voluntary hospitals**, paid for by charity and wealthy subscribers — for example **Guy's Hospital** in London (1721). Patients often needed a **subscriber's letter** to be admitted, and the infectious and the incurable were usually turned away.
- After the **Poor Law Amendment Act (1834)**, the poorest were treated in **workhouse infirmaries**. **Specialist hospitals** appeared (Great Ormond Street Hospital for Children, 1852), and small **cottage hospitals** in country towns from 1859.
- Conditions were often dirty and overcrowded, and infection spread easily between patients. **Nurses** were untrained, poorly paid and had a bad reputation for drunkenness — Charles Dickens mocked them in the character of Sarah Gamp in *Martin Chuzzlewit* (1843–44).

### Florence Nightingale (1820–1910)

**Background.** From a wealthy family, Nightingale insisted on becoming a nurse against her parents' wishes. She trained at **Kaiserswerth** in Germany (1851) and became superintendent of a women's hospital in Harley Street, London (1853).

**The Crimean War (1854–56).** Newspaper reports — especially in *The Times* — shocked the public with the conditions for wounded British soldiers. **Sidney Herbert**, the Secretary at War, asked Nightingale to take a team of **38 nurses** to the army hospital at **Scutari** (near Constantinople), where she arrived in **November 1854**.
- She found filthy wards, blocked drains, rats and lice, few beds, and men dying far more often from **diseases** such as cholera, typhus and dysentery than from their wounds.
- She organised cleaning and laundry, better food and kitchens, clean bedding and ventilation, and kept careful records. She was nicknamed **"the Lady with the Lamp"** because she checked on patients at night.
- The death rate at Scutari fell from about **42% (February 1855)** to about **2% (June 1855)**. Part of this was due to the government's **Sanitary Commission**, which arrived in **March 1855** and flushed out the sewers and improved ventilation — but Nightingale's reputation made her a national heroine.
- (**Mary Seacole**, a Jamaican nurse and businesswoman, also cared for soldiers in the Crimea, near the front at Balaclava.)

**Her influence on nursing and hospitals in Britain:**
- The public raised money for a **Nightingale Fund**, which set up the **Nightingale School of Nursing at St Thomas' Hospital, London, in 1860**. Trainees were taught discipline, hygiene and practical skills; they went on to train others and to run hospitals across Britain and the Empire. By 1900 nursing was a **respectable, trained profession** for women.
- ***Notes on Nursing*** and ***Notes on Hospitals*** (both **1859**) spread her ideas: cleanliness, fresh air and light, good food, and careful observation of patients.
- **Hospital design — the pavilion plan**: separate ward blocks (pavilions) with large windows for light and ventilation, beds spaced well apart, easily cleaned walls and floors, and separate areas for the infectious. St Thomas' Hospital was rebuilt on these lines (opened 1871).
- She used **statistics** — including her famous diagrams of the causes of death in the Crimea — to persuade the government to reform army hospitals. In 1858 she became the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society.

**Limits:** Nightingale believed in **miasma**, not germ theory, for most of her career; her cleanliness worked for the wrong reason. Her reforms improved **care**, not **cures**. And nurses remained subordinate to (male) doctors.

### Surgery c1800: the three problems

Before 1846, surgery faced three problems: **pain**, **infection** and **bleeding**. Patients were held down, given alcohol or opium, and operations had to be **fast** — the London surgeon **Robert Liston** was famous for amputating a leg in a few minutes. Only simple operations (amputations, removing bladder stones, lancing) were possible. Anaesthetics and antiseptics solved the first two; **blood loss** was not solved until blood groups (1901) and blood storage (First World War).

### The impact of anaesthetics

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| 1799 | **Humphry Davy** notes that **nitrous oxide** ("laughing gas") relieves pain |
| 1846 | **William Morton** uses **ether** in a public operation in Boston, USA (October); **Robert Liston** uses ether in London (December) |
| 1847 | **James Simpson**, professor at Edinburgh, discovers the effect of **chloroform** by testing it on himself and friends at home (November) |
| 1848 | **Hannah Greener**, aged 15, dies under chloroform during a minor operation |
| 1853 | **John Snow** gives chloroform to **Queen Victoria** at the birth of Prince Leopold |
| 1884 | **Cocaine** first used as a **local** anaesthetic |

**Problems with ether:** it irritated the lungs and made patients cough, caused vomiting, and was highly **flammable** near candles and gas lamps. **Chloroform** was easier to use and quicker, but it was hard to get the **dose** right — it could affect the heart and kill, as with Hannah Greener. Snow designed an inhaler to control the dose.

**Opposition:**
- **Religious**: some argued pain in childbirth was God's will (from the Bible's story of Eve).
- **Safety**: early deaths frightened patients and surgeons.
- **Tradition**: some surgeons and army doctors thought a conscious patient was safer, or that pain kept soldiers alert.
- **Queen Victoria's** use of chloroform in 1853 (and again in 1857) made it respectable and helped win acceptance.

**Impact — the "black period" of surgery (c1846–c1870):** with patients unconscious, surgeons attempted **longer and more complex operations**, deeper inside the body. But nobody yet understood infection, so instruments, hands and clothing were dirty, and **death rates from infection and blood loss rose** in some hospitals. Anaesthetics solved **pain** but made the **infection** problem worse.

### The impact of antiseptics

- **Ignaz Semmelweis**, in **Vienna** in **1847**, showed that doctors carrying infection from the dissecting room caused **childbed fever**; making them wash their hands in **chlorinated lime** cut deaths sharply. He could not explain why, and he was ignored.
- **Joseph Lister** (1827–1912), surgeon at **Glasgow Royal Infirmary**, read **Pasteur's germ theory** in **1865** and reasoned that germs in the air caused wounds to go septic. He knew **carbolic acid** had been used to treat sewage in Carlisle.
- In **August 1865** he treated **James Greenlees**, aged 11, who had a **compound fracture** of the leg (bone through the skin — normally leading to infection and amputation), with dressings soaked in carbolic acid. The wound healed.
- He published his results in ***The Lancet*** in **1867**. In his wards, the death rate after amputation fell from about **46% (1864–66)** to about **15% (1867–70)**. From **1871** he used a **carbolic spray** to kill germs in the air during operations.

**Opposition:**
- Carbolic acid **irritated** surgeons' skin and lungs; the spray made the theatre unpleasant.
- It **slowed operations** down and added work and cost.
- Some surgeons who copied him badly got poor results; Lister kept changing his methods, which made it look unreliable.
- Many British surgeons still did not accept **germ theory** in the late 1860s–70s.

**Acceptance and aseptic surgery:** as germ theory — and Koch's identification of the bacteria that infect wounds (1878) — gained acceptance, surgeons moved from **antiseptic** surgery (killing germs present) to **aseptic** surgery (keeping germs out altogether) by the **1890s**: instruments **sterilised** by steam (from 1886), operating theatres built to be cleaned thoroughly, and surgeons wearing **gowns, masks, caps** and, from **1890**, **rubber gloves** (introduced by William Halsted in the USA). Lister abandoned the spray in 1890 and was made a peer in 1897.

**Judgement on surgery:** by 1900 surgery was **painless** and much **safer from infection** — a huge change from 1800 — but **blood loss** still limited what was possible.

### New approaches to prevention: public health and the Act of 1875

**The problem.** Industrial towns grew rapidly; working families lived in **back-to-back housing** with shared privies, overflowing **cesspits**, rubbish in the streets, and water from wells or rivers polluted by sewage. Private water companies often supplied dirty water only a few hours a day.

**Chadwick and the 1848 Act.** **Edwin Chadwick's** *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population* (**1842**) showed that the poor in towns died much younger than the better-off — he found the average age of death of labourers in Liverpool was 15, compared with 35 for the gentry. A believer in **miasma**, he argued that cleaning towns would save money on poor relief. After the second cholera epidemic began, Parliament passed the **Public Health Act (1848)**: a General Board of Health was created, and towns **could** set up local boards of health — but it was mostly **voluntary** (only compulsory where the death rate was above 23 per 1,000). Many towns refused because of cost and laissez-faire attitudes; the Board was abolished in 1854 after strong opposition, including from *The Times*.

**Why government changed its mind by 1875:**
- **Repeated cholera epidemics** (1831–32, 1848–49, 1853–54, 1866) frightened all classes.
- **The Great Stink (summer 1858):** the smell of sewage in the Thames in a hot summer was so bad that Parliament could hardly sit. It funded **Joseph Bazalgette's** sewer system for London — about **82 miles of main intercepting sewers** and **1,100 miles of street sewers**, carrying waste east of the city; the main system opened in **1865** and was completed in **1875**. It was built to remove smells (miasma), but it ended cholera in London: the last outbreak, in **1866**, hit an area of the East End not yet connected.
- **Statistics:** William Farr at the General Register Office published death rates that showed the scale of the problem; in 1866 he accepted that cholera spread through water.
- **The Second Reform Act (1867)** gave the vote to many skilled working-class men in towns, so politicians had to respond to their demands for better living conditions.
- **Germ theory** (1861) and later Koch's work gave scientific backing to cleanliness — though this mattered more after 1875 than before.
- **Attitudes** shifted away from strict laissez-faire towards the idea that the state should protect people from dangers they could not avoid themselves.

**The Public Health Act (1875)**, passed by **Disraeli's** Conservative government (Home Secretary **Richard Cross**), was **compulsory**. Local councils had to:
- provide **clean water** supplies;
- build and maintain **sewers and drains**, and dispose of sewage;
- **remove rubbish** and keep streets clean;
- appoint a **Medical Officer of Health** and **sanitary inspectors** to report on and deal with disease and nuisances;
- set and enforce **building regulations** for new housing, and inspect lodging houses and food such as unsound meat.

The same year saw the **Artisans' Dwellings Act** (allowing councils to clear slums) and the **Sale of Food and Drugs Act**.

**Significance:** it marked a **turning point in the role of government** — from laissez-faire to **compulsory** local action to prevent disease. Death rates in towns fell by 1900 and diseases spread by dirty water (cholera, typhoid) declined. **Limits:** it depended on local councils, some of which acted slowly; housing for the poor stayed bad; and infant mortality was still high in 1900.

### Case study: fighting cholera in London, 1854

**Cholera** arrived in Britain in **1831** (first at Sunderland). It caused violent diarrhoea and vomiting and could kill within a day, from dehydration. There were four major epidemics: **1831–32, 1848–49, 1853–54 and 1866**, each killing tens of thousands. Most people blamed **miasma**.

**John Snow** (1813–58) was a London doctor and a leading **anaesthetist** (he gave Queen Victoria chloroform in 1853). After studying the 1848–49 epidemic, he published *On the Mode of Communication of Cholera* (**1849**), arguing that cholera was **spread by contaminated water**, not bad air — because it attacked the gut, not the lungs.

**The Broad Street outbreak (Soho, 1854):**
- At the end of **August 1854** cholera broke out around **Broad Street**; **more than 500 people died in about ten days**.
- Snow asked the local registrar for the addresses of the dead and **plotted every death on a map**. The deaths **clustered around the water pump in Broad Street**.
- He interviewed families and explained apparent exceptions:
  - workers at the **Lion Brewery** in Broad Street had no deaths — they drank beer, and the brewery had its **own well**;
  - the **workhouse in Poland Street** had very few deaths — it also had its own well;
  - a widow in **Hampstead**, miles away, died — she had water fetched from the Broad Street pump because she liked its taste.
- On **7 September 1854** he persuaded the parish Board of Guardians to **remove the handle of the pump** (it was taken off the next day). The outbreak — already declining as people fled — ended.
- Later the **Reverend Henry Whitehead**, a local curate who had at first doubted Snow, found the likely source: a baby at 40 Broad Street had died of cholera, and her nappies had been washed into a **cesspit only a few feet from the well**, which leaked into the water.

**The "Grand Experiment" (1854).** Snow compared households in south London supplied by two water companies: the **Southwark and Vauxhall Company** took water from the Thames where it was polluted by sewage, while the **Lambeth Company** had moved its intake upstream (1852). Deaths from cholera were about **eight times higher** among Southwark and Vauxhall customers.

**Significance of Snow:**
- **Short term — limited.** A government committee in **1855** rejected his conclusions and stuck to miasma; he could not identify **what** in the water caused cholera, because germ theory had not yet been published. Snow died in 1858.
- **Long term — very important.** His use of **mapping and statistics** laid the foundations of **epidemiology**; William Farr accepted the water link after 1866; Koch identified the cholera bacterium in **1883**, proving Snow right. His work added to pressure for **clean water**, which became a legal duty of councils under the **1875 Act**.
- Note the irony: London was protected from cholera mainly by Bazalgette's sewers — built for the wrong reason (miasma) — rather than by Snow's correct theory.

### Factors and the extent of change c1700–c1900

| Factor | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Individuals** | Nightingale (nursing and hospitals), Simpson (chloroform), Lister (antiseptics), Snow (cholera), Chadwick (public health) |
| **Government** | Sanitary Commission (1855); 1848 Act (voluntary) → 1875 Act (compulsory); funding for Bazalgette; but the 1855 rejection of Snow |
| **Science and technology** | chloroform and ether; carbolic acid and later steam sterilisation; germ theory and Koch's work; statistics and mapping |
| **Attitudes in society** | fear of cholera; the Great Stink; the 1867 Reform Act; newspapers in the Crimean War; opposition from laissez-faire, religion and conservative doctors |
| **War** | the Crimean War created Nightingale's reputation and exposed army medicine |

**Extent of change:** by 1900 surgery was painless and far safer, nursing was professional, hospitals were cleaner and better designed, and the state had taken responsibility for clean water and sewage. **Continuity:** there were still few **cures** for disease, blood loss limited surgery, and most of the poor still could not afford a doctor.

::: terms
- **Anaesthetic**: a substance that removes pain (general anaesthetics such as ether and chloroform make the patient unconscious).
- **Antiseptic**: a chemical, such as carbolic acid, used to kill germs in a wound or on instruments during surgery.
- **Aseptic surgery**: surgery in which germs are kept out of the operating theatre altogether, by sterilising instruments and wearing gowns, masks and gloves.
- **Black period**: c1846–c1870, when anaesthetics allowed longer operations but death rates from infection and blood loss rose.
- **Compound fracture**: a broken bone that pierces the skin, which in the nineteenth century usually became infected.
- **Pavilion plan**: Nightingale's hospital design of separate, well-ventilated ward blocks with large windows and widely spaced beds.
- **Medical Officer of Health**: a doctor employed by a local council to monitor and report on disease; required under the 1870s Public Health Acts.
- **Epidemiology**: the study of how diseases spread through a population, using statistics and mapping as John Snow did in 1854.
- **Great Stink**: the summer of 1858, when the smell of sewage in the Thames led Parliament to fund Bazalgette's London sewers.
:::

::: example 4-mark similarity
Explain one way in which the government's response to cholera in 1854 was similar to the response to the Great Plague in 1665. (4)

In both cases the authorities' actions were shaped by the belief that disease spread through bad air rather than by its real cause. In 1665 the Lord Mayor of London ordered fires to be lit in the streets to purify the air, along with shutting up infected houses. In 1854, even after John Snow showed that deaths in Broad Street clustered around a water pump, a government committee in 1855 rejected his theory and held on to miasma, so the problem of contaminated water supplies was not tackled directly. So in both periods, government action was limited by a mistaken belief in miasma.

::: example 12-mark explain why
Explain why surgery became safer in the second half of the nineteenth century. (12)
You may use: chloroform; carbolic acid. You must also use information of your own.

The first reason was the development of effective **anaesthetics**. After ether was used in 1846, James Simpson discovered **chloroform** in Edinburgh in 1847; it was quicker and easier to use. Once Queen Victoria accepted chloroform in 1853, opposition weakened. Patients no longer died of shock from pain or struggled on the table, so surgeons could work carefully rather than at great speed.

However, anaesthetics alone made surgery more dangerous for a time, because longer operations meant more infection — the "black period". The second reason was therefore **antiseptics**. In 1865 Joseph Lister, having read Pasteur, used **carbolic acid** on the compound fracture of James Greenlees, who recovered. In his wards the death rate after amputation fell from about 46% to about 15%, and he published in *The Lancet* in 1867.

A third reason, of my own, was the move to **aseptic surgery** by the 1890s, which rested on **germ theory**. Once Koch had shown that specific bacteria caused wound infection (1878), surgeons accepted that germs had to be kept out: instruments were sterilised by steam from 1886, theatres were built to be cleaned, and surgeons wore gowns, masks and, from 1890, rubber gloves.

The reasons were linked: anaesthetics allowed ambitious operations, which exposed the infection problem, which germ theory explained and antiseptic and then aseptic methods solved. Surgery was still limited by blood loss, but by 1900 it was far safer than in 1850.

::: example 16-mark judgement
"The Public Health Act of 1875 was the most important development in the prevention of disease in nineteenth-century Britain." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16 + 4 SPaG)
You may use: clean water; John Snow. You must also use information of your own.

**For.** The 1875 Act was the first **compulsory** public health law: every local council had to provide **clean water**, build sewers and drains, remove rubbish, enforce building regulations and appoint a Medical Officer of Health. Unlike the voluntary Act of 1848, which many towns ignored, it applied across the country and reached everyone, rich or poor. It helped reduce deaths from diseases spread by dirt and water, such as cholera and typhoid, and marked the end of laissez-faire in public health.

**Against — John Snow.** The Act might not have focused on clean water without the evidence built up since 1854. Snow's map of the Broad Street outbreak and his comparison of the Southwark and Vauxhall and Lambeth water companies proved that cholera spread through water, and after 1866 William Farr accepted this. Without that knowledge, the Act's water provisions would have lacked their scientific basis — although Snow was rejected in 1855 and died before his ideas were accepted.

**Against — vaccination (own).** Vaccination against smallpox, developed by Jenner in 1796, was made free in 1840 and compulsory in 1853 — more than twenty years before the 1875 Act. It targeted one of the biggest killers directly and was the first time the government forced people to act for their health.

**Judgement.** If the criterion is **how many people and diseases were protected**, I agree: the 1875 Act improved conditions in every town and cut many diseases at once, while vaccination prevented only smallpox. But if the criterion is **what came first and made later action possible**, Snow's evidence and the 1853 vaccination law were the real breakthroughs; the 1875 Act built on both. Overall, the 1875 Act was the most important in impact, but it was the culmination of earlier developments rather than a new idea.

Judgement: e.g. by the criterion of making surgery possible, anaesthetics came first; by the criterion of reducing deaths, antiseptic and aseptic methods mattered more, and they depended on germ theory. The best answers explain that each development solved a different problem.
SPaG: High 4, Intermediate 2–3, Threshold 1.
