## What it is

Elizabeth I became queen on **17 November 1558**, the day her half-sister Mary I died. She was 25, unmarried and inherited a kingdom that was in debt, divided over religion and threatened by France. This topic covers three spec points: **England in 1558** (society and government), **the "Virgin Queen"** (legitimacy, gender, marriage, character and strengths) and **the challenges at home and abroad** (finance, religion, France).

### Elizabethan society in 1558

England had about **3 million people** and roughly nine in ten lived in the countryside, working the land. Society was a strict **hierarchy**, and people believed God had placed everyone in their position — so challenging your place (or the monarch's) was a challenge to God's order.

- **Monarch** — at the top, chosen by God.
- **Nobility** — a small group of titled families (dukes, earls, barons) with huge estates, private retinues and great local power. Many in the north were still Catholic.
- **Gentry** — landowners below the nobility; knights and squires. The gentry were growing in wealth and number and supplied MPs and Justices of the Peace.
- **Yeomen** — farmers who owned their own land.
- **Tenant farmers** — rented land from landowners.
- **Labourers, servants and the poor** — the majority; vulnerable to bad harvests and rising prices. At the very bottom were **vagabonds**.

In towns the hierarchy was merchants at the top, then professionals (lawyers, doctors), skilled craftsmen and unskilled labourers. **London** was by far the largest city and the centre of trade and government.

### Government in 1558

| Part | Who | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| **The monarch** | Elizabeth | Ruled by **royal prerogative**: declared war and made peace, called and dismissed Parliament, chose advisers, granted titles, lands, offices and monopolies (**patronage**), gave pardons and decided whom she would marry |
| **The Court** | Nobles, advisers and servants around the monarch | Centre of power and patronage; a place to win the queen's favour |
| **Privy Council** | About 19 members (Mary's had been far larger) | Advised the queen, ran day-to-day government, oversaw law and order and local government, discussed policy — but the queen did not have to take its advice |
| **Secretary of State** | **William Cecil** from 1558 (Lord Burghley from 1571) | The most important minister: ran the Privy Council's business, controlled correspondence, advised on policy |
| **Parliament** | House of Lords and House of Commons | Passed laws and granted **extraordinary taxation**; met only when the monarch called it — about ten times in Elizabeth's 45-year reign |
| **Lord Lieutenants** | Nobles appointed for each county | Raised and trained the county militia |
| **Justices of the Peace (JPs)** | Local gentry, several per county | Kept law and order, tried minor cases, enforced government orders |

Elizabeth's Council mixed experienced Marian officials with Protestants she trusted; she deliberately chose a **smaller** Council so it was easier to control. Cecil — cautious, hard-working and Protestant — became her chief adviser for 40 years. Robert Dudley (Earl of Leicester from 1564) joined the Council in 1562.

### The Virgin Queen: legitimacy

Catholics believed Henry VIII's first marriage, to Catherine of Aragon, had never been lawfully ended, because the Pope had not agreed to annul it. So to them Henry's marriage to Anne Boleyn in 1533 was invalid and **Elizabeth was illegitimate** — the daughter of a bigamous marriage — with no right to the throne. An Act of Parliament of 1536 had actually declared her illegitimate after her mother's execution; the 1544 Act of Succession restored her place in the line but never reversed that.

To many Catholics at home and abroad the rightful queen was **Mary, Queen of Scots**, granddaughter of Henry VIII's elder sister Margaret Tudor. When Mary I died, the French king **Henry II** had his daughter-in-law Mary proclaimed Queen of England, and Mary and her husband Francis displayed the English royal arms on their own.

### Gender

Tudor society expected women to obey fathers and husbands. People assumed a woman could not lead an army, control powerful men or rule by her own judgement. **Mary I's** reign seemed to prove the point: her marriage to **Philip II of Spain** dragged England into a Spanish war that lost Calais, and she burned nearly 300 Protestants. In 1558 the Scottish Protestant preacher **John Knox** published *The First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women*, attacking female rule — it was aimed at Catholic women rulers, but it made Elizabeth's position harder too.

### Marriage

Everyone expected Elizabeth to marry quickly: a husband would, people assumed, help her rule, and above all she needed an **heir** to secure the succession and stop a civil war or a Catholic succession (Mary, Queen of Scots). But marriage posed dilemmas:

- **A foreign prince** could drag England into his wars and make it a satellite of his country — exactly what Philip II had done under Mary I.
- **An English nobleman** would raise one faction above the others and cause jealousy at court.
- **A husband** would expect to rule; a wife was supposed to obey.

Suitors included **Philip II** (who proposed in 1559), **Archduke Charles of Austria**, **Eric XIV of Sweden** and, closest to her heart, **Robert Dudley** — whose chances collapsed when his wife **Amy Robsart** died after a fall down stairs in **September 1560**, causing a scandal. In **October 1562** Elizabeth nearly died of **smallpox**, which terrified the Council because there was no agreed heir; **Parliament petitioned her to marry in 1563 and again in 1566**. Elizabeth refused to be pushed, telling Parliament that marriage and the succession were matters for her prerogative. By never marrying she kept control, used the *possibility* of marriage as a diplomatic tool, and later turned virginity into an image — the Virgin Queen, "married" to England.

### Her character and strengths

- **Well educated** — taught by the humanist scholar **Roger Ascham**; she spoke and read several languages, including Latin, French and Italian, which helped her in diplomacy.
- **Experienced in danger** — imprisoned in the Tower in 1554 and suspected of treason under Mary, she had learned caution and how to keep her own counsel.
- **Intelligent and decisive when she needed to be**, but often **cautious**, preferring to delay rather than risk a wrong decision.
- **Charismatic** — she understood the power of image, ceremony and public appearances (processions, progresses, portraits).
- **Chose able advisers** — above all Cecil — and played factions against each other so no single one dominated.
- **A Protestant, but a moderate one** who wanted to avoid the religious bloodshed of the previous reigns.

### Challenges at home and abroad

**1. Financial weakness.** The Crown was about **£300,000 in debt** — more than its ordinary yearly income of roughly £287,000 — mostly because of Mary's war with France. Much of the debt was owed to money-lenders in **Antwerp** at around **14% interest**. Earlier monarchs had sold Crown lands and **debased** the coinage (reduced the silver in coins), which fed **inflation**. Parliamentary taxation was unpopular and made a monarch dependent on Parliament. Elizabeth responded with thrift: she cut household spending, sold some Crown lands, and in **1560–61 recoined** the currency to restore its value. A monarch without money could not defend the country or reward supporters — so finance mattered to every other problem.

**2. Religious divisions.** In 25 years England had swung from Catholic to Protestant (Henry VIII's break with Rome, Edward VI's Protestantism) and back again (Mary I). The north and Lancashire were strongly Catholic; London and the south-east were more Protestant. Protestant **Marian exiles** returned from Calvinist cities such as Geneva wanting a far more radical Church. The House of Lords contained Catholic bishops who would resist change. Whatever Elizabeth chose, someone powerful would be angry.

**3. The French threat.** France was the biggest danger in 1558:
- It had just taken **Calais** (January 1558), and under the **Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis** (April 1559) it kept it.
- The **"Auld Alliance"** linked France and Scotland. Scotland was ruled by Mary of Guise, regent for her daughter Mary, Queen of Scots, with French troops present — an enemy on England's northern border.
- Mary, Queen of Scots, married the French heir and became queen of France in July 1559 when Francis II succeeded. She had a strong claim to the English throne.
- **Ireland** was a possible back door for invasion by a Catholic power.

Elizabeth (urged by Cecil) sent a fleet and an army in 1560 to help the Scottish Protestant lords; after Mary of Guise died, the **Treaty of Edinburgh (July 1560)** removed French troops from Scotland. France was then weakened by its own **Wars of Religion** from 1562. An English attempt to use the Wars of Religion to win back Calais — occupying **Le Havre (Newhaven) in 1562–63** — failed, and in the **Treaty of Troyes (1564)** Elizabeth gave up the claim to Calais.

**Spain** was not yet an enemy: Philip II wanted England as a friendly Catholic-ish ally against France and hoped to marry Elizabeth. That changed in the later 1560s.

### Linking the problems

The challenges fed each other. Doubts about **legitimacy** made the **Catholic** powers and Mary, Queen of Scots dangerous; **gender** made people think she needed a husband, but **marriage** could hand England to a foreign power; **debt** meant she could not afford a war with **France**; and **religious division** meant any decision could provoke rebellion. A top answer shows these links rather than treating each problem in isolation.

::: terms
- **Hierarchy**: a system in which people are ranked one above another, from the monarch down to the poorest labourer.
- **Royal prerogative**: the monarch's personal powers — to make war and peace, call and dismiss Parliament, choose advisers and grant patronage.
- **Privy Council**: the monarch's inner group of about 19 advisers who helped run government day to day.
- **Patronage**: the monarch's power to give titles, lands, offices and monopolies, used to reward and control loyal subjects.
- **Legitimacy**: the lawfulness of a ruler's claim — Catholics said Elizabeth was illegitimate because they did not accept Henry VIII's marriage to Anne Boleyn.
- **Succession**: who would inherit the throne next; an unmarried queen with no heir left it uncertain.
- **Debasement**: reducing the amount of precious metal in coins, which made money worth less and fuelled inflation.
- **Auld Alliance**: the long-standing alliance between France and Scotland, which threatened England from two sides.
- **Justice of the Peace (JP)**: a local gentleman appointed to keep law and order and enforce government orders in his county.
:::

::: method
This topic sits in **Paper 2, Booklet B (British depth study)**. You answer three questions in about 55 minutes of the 1 hour 50 minute paper. Paper 2 has **no SPaG marks** (those are on Papers 1 and 3), but clear, accurate writing still helps you reach the top level.

**Question 1 — "Describe two features of…" (4 marks, about 5 minutes).**
- 2 marks per feature: 1 for identifying a valid feature, 1 for supporting detail that develops it.
- Write two short paragraphs, each starting "One feature was…" / "A second feature was…", then add a specific fact (a name, date or number).
- Do not explain causes or give opinions — describe only.

**Question 2 — "Explain why…" (12 marks, about 18 minutes).** Two stimulus points are printed; you must use at least one piece of your own knowledge beyond them to reach Level 4.
- Level 1 (1–3): simple or general reasons; little knowledge.
- Level 2 (4–6): some explanation, some accurate knowledge. Maximum 5 if you do not go beyond the stimulus points.
- Level 3 (7–9): an analytical explanation directed at the question, with accurate and relevant knowledge. Maximum 8 if you do not go beyond the stimulus points.
- Level 4 (10–12): analytical explanation consistently focused on *why*, a coherent and sustained line of reasoning, wide-ranging accurate knowledge — and a third factor of your own.
- Structure: three paragraphs, one factor each. Start each with the reason ("One reason Elizabeth's marriage was a problem was…"), prove it with precise detail, then finish with "This meant that…" to link it back to the question. Links between factors lift you to the top.

**Question 3 or 4 — "'Statement.' How far do you agree? Explain your answer." (16 marks, about 25 minutes).** Two stimulus points are given; again, add your own.
- Level 1 (1–4): simple, general comments.
- Level 2 (5–8): some analysis, some knowledge, a judgement that is asserted. Maximum 7 without going beyond the stimulus.
- Level 3 (9–12): analytical explanation, accurate knowledge, a judgement given with some justification. Maximum 11 without going beyond the stimulus.
- Level 4 (13–16): sustained analysis, wide-ranging precise knowledge, and a judgement with **clearly stated criteria** that is justified throughout.
- Structure: introduction giving your line; a paragraph for the statement's factor; two paragraphs for other factors; a conclusion that weighs them using a criterion (for example "which problem threatened her hold on the throne most directly" or "which problem made the others worse"). Your judgement must answer "how far", not just "yes".
:::

::: example Describe two features — 4 marks
Describe two features of the Privy Council in the early years of Elizabeth's reign. (4)

One feature was that it was small: Elizabeth reduced it to about 19 members, far fewer than under Mary I, so it was easier for her to control and to keep business moving (2).

A second feature was that it was led by the Secretary of State, William Cecil, who managed its business and correspondence and became Elizabeth's most trusted adviser; the Council advised the queen and ran day-to-day government, but she did not have to follow its advice (2).
:::

::: example Explain why — 12 marks
Explain why Elizabeth's marriage was an important issue in the years 1558–69. (12)

You may use the following in your answer: the succession; Robert Dudley. You must also use information of your own.

**The succession.** The most important reason was that Elizabeth had no heir. If she died childless, the throne could pass to Mary, Queen of Scots, a Catholic married into the French royal family, or be disputed, risking civil war. This fear became real in October 1562 when Elizabeth nearly died of smallpox; afterwards Parliament petitioned her to marry in 1563 and again in 1566. So marriage mattered because the whole stability of the Protestant regime rested on her producing a child.

**Choosing a husband.** Marriage was also important because any choice carried danger. A foreign Catholic prince such as Philip II, who proposed in 1559, or Archduke Charles could drag England into his wars, just as Mary I's marriage to Philip had led to the loss of Calais. An English noble would raise one faction over the rest: Robert Dudley was her favourite, but after his wife Amy Robsart died falling down stairs in 1560 rumours of murder made marrying him politically impossible. Because no candidate was safe, the question stayed open and kept dividing the Council.

**Gender and authority.** Finally, marriage mattered because contemporaries believed a woman needed a husband to rule. John Knox's attack on female rulers in 1558 reflected a common view. Yet a husband would expect to share power, and a wife was expected to obey. This links to the succession issue: marrying might secure an heir but cost Elizabeth her authority, which is why she used the possibility of marriage as a diplomatic tool instead.

Overall, marriage was important because it tied together the three biggest weaknesses of 1558 — the lack of an heir, the threat from Catholic powers and doubts about a woman's ability to rule.
:::

::: example How far do you agree — 16 marks
"The main challenge facing Elizabeth in 1558 was the threat from France." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)

You may use the following in your answer: Mary, Queen of Scots; the Crown's debts. You must also use information of your own.

**Introduction.** France was a serious threat in 1558, but I will argue that religious division was the greater challenge, because it affected Elizabeth's authority at home and made the French threat more dangerous.

**France.** France had just taken Calais in January 1558 and kept it under the Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis. Through the Auld Alliance it had troops in Scotland under Mary of Guise, so England faced enemies on two sides. Worse, Mary, Queen of Scots — married to the French heir — had a strong claim to the English throne, and Henry II had her proclaimed Queen of England. This was a direct threat to Elizabeth's crown.

**Finance.** However, the French threat was only as dangerous as it was because of England's weakness. The Crown owed about £300,000, much of it to Antwerp lenders at 14%, and debasement had fuelled inflation. Without money Elizabeth could not fight a war. This limited her options, although careful spending and the 1560–61 recoinage showed it could be managed.

**Religion.** Religious division was arguably the deepest challenge. After swings between Catholicism and Protestantism, the nobility and the north were largely Catholic, the Lords contained Catholic bishops, and returning Marian exiles wanted radical Protestant change. Catholics regarded Elizabeth as illegitimate — which is exactly why Mary, Queen of Scots, and therefore France, were so dangerous. Religion also shaped her marriage: any husband's faith would upset one side.

**Conclusion.** Judged by which problem made the others worse, religion was the main challenge. The French threat was real but was eased relatively quickly — the Treaty of Edinburgh removed French troops from Scotland in 1560 and France fell into civil war from 1562. Religious division, by contrast, underlay the legitimacy question, the Mary, Queen of Scots problem and the danger of rebellion, and it was still threatening her in 1569. So I agree only to a limited extent.
:::
