## What it is

In the early 1980s the Cold War grew as tense as at any time since Cuba — the **"Second Cold War"** under President **Ronald Reagan**. Yet by 1991 it was over: the Berlin Wall had fallen, Germany was reunited, the **Warsaw Pact** had dissolved and the **Soviet Union** itself had broken into fifteen states. The spec asks you to explain the significance of **Reagan and SDI**, of **Reagan and Gorbachev in reducing tensions**, of **Gorbachev's "new thinking"** for Eastern Europe, of the **fall of the Berlin Wall**, and of the **collapse of the USSR** for the end of the Warsaw Pact.

### Reagan and the "Second Cold War", 1981–85

Reagan became president in **January 1981**. He rejected détente as a one-way street that had let the USSR expand, and set out to confront communism from a position of strength.

- **Rhetoric**: in March 1983 he called the USSR an **"evil empire"**.
- **Military build-up**: defence spending rose sharply — to over 250 billion US dollars a year by the mid-1980s — on new bombers, missiles, submarines and a larger navy.
- **Missiles in Europe**: following NATO's 1979 decision, US **Pershing II and cruise missiles** arrived in Western Europe from late 1983 (for example at Greenham Common in Britain), able to hit Soviet targets within minutes. The USSR walked out of the Geneva arms talks in November 1983.
- **The Reagan Doctrine**: US support for anti-communist forces around the world — the **mujahideen** in Afghanistan (including Stinger anti-aircraft missiles from 1986) and the **Contras** in Nicaragua; the US invasion of **Grenada** (October 1983) removed a Marxist government.
- **1983, the most dangerous year**: in September the USSR shot down Korean Air Lines flight 007, killing all **269** on board; in November the NATO exercise **Able Archer 83** was mistaken by some Soviet leaders for preparation for a real nuclear attack.
- **Soviet weakness**: Brezhnev died in 1982; his successors **Andropov** (died 1984) and **Chernenko** (died 1985) were old and ill. The USSR had no leader capable of a new approach until 1985.

### The Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI)

Announced by Reagan on **23 March 1983** and nicknamed **"Star Wars"**, SDI was a plan for a space-based shield — satellites and lasers — to destroy Soviet missiles in flight.

Its significance:
- **It threatened MAD**: if the USA could shoot down Soviet missiles, it could strike first without fear of retaliation. The balance that had kept the peace — and the logic of the ABM Treaty (1972) — would be broken.
- **The USSR could not match it**: Soviet computer and electronics technology lagged far behind, and its stagnating economy could not afford a new arms race. This put **pressure on Soviet leaders to negotiate**.
- **It was a bargaining chip and an obstacle**: Gorbachev's demand that SDI be confined to the laboratory wrecked the Reykjavik summit (1986). The technology was never built, but the threat mattered.
- Historians disagree: some argue SDI and Reagan's pressure "won" the Cold War; others argue the Soviet collapse came mainly from internal weakness and Gorbachev's choices, and that SDI may even have slowed arms reductions.

### Gorbachev and "new thinking"

**Mikhail Gorbachev** became General Secretary in **March 1985**, aged 54. He faced a USSR in crisis:
- a **stagnant economy**, shortages and falling living standards, with a large share of national wealth (estimates vary widely) spent on the military;
- the costly, unwinnable **war in Afghanistan**;
- the **Chernobyl** nuclear disaster (April 1986), which exposed secrecy and incompetence.

His reforms at home were **perestroika** (restructuring the economy, with some market elements) and **glasnost** (openness — less censorship, honest discussion of problems). His **"new thinking"** in foreign policy followed from them: the USSR could only fix its economy if it cut military spending, which meant ending the Cold War.

New thinking meant:
- **Arms reduction** through agreement with the USA.
- **Withdrawal from Afghanistan** — announced in 1988, completed **February 1989**.
- **No more propping up Eastern European regimes by force**. In a speech to the **United Nations in December 1988** he announced a unilateral cut of **500,000** Soviet troops, withdrawals from Eastern Europe, and said nations should have **freedom of choice**. In October 1989 a Soviet spokesman joked that the Brezhnev Doctrine had been replaced by the **"Sinatra Doctrine"** — each country could do it "my way".

### Reagan and Gorbachev: reducing tensions

| Summit | Date | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Geneva** | November 1985 | No treaty, but the two leaders built a personal relationship and agreed in principle to seek deep cuts in nuclear weapons |
| **Reykjavik** | October 1986 | Came close to agreeing to scrap huge numbers of nuclear weapons, but collapsed over SDI |
| **Washington** | December 1987 | **INF Treaty** signed: all intermediate-range nuclear missiles (500–5,500 km range) to be destroyed |
| **Moscow** | May–June 1988 | Reagan walked in Red Square; relations warm |

The **INF Treaty (8 December 1987)** was the **first treaty to abolish a whole category of nuclear weapons** — 2,692 missiles were destroyed by 1991, with on-site inspection by each side. Earlier treaties had only limited growth.

**Significance of each leader**:
- **Reagan**: his build-up and SDI put pressure on the USSR; but he was also willing to negotiate — he genuinely wanted to abolish nuclear weapons — and he built trust with Gorbachev. Margaret Thatcher, after meeting Gorbachev in December 1984, famously said he was a man she could "do business" with, and encouraged Reagan to engage.
- **Gorbachev**: his decisions — to withdraw from Afghanistan, accept the INF Treaty on terms close to the West's, cut troops and refuse to use force in Eastern Europe — were the most direct cause of the Cold War's end. Without a Soviet leader willing to let go, pressure alone would not have ended it.
- A balanced judgement: **Reagan created pressure; Gorbachev chose how to respond**. Both were needed, but the Soviet economic crisis explains *why* Gorbachev chose as he did.

### The loosening Soviet grip on Eastern Europe, 1989

| Country | What happened in 1989 |
|---|---|
| **Poland** | The banned trade union **Solidarity** was legalised (April); in partly free elections in June it won almost every seat it could contest; in August **Tadeusz Mazowiecki** became the first non-communist prime minister in the Eastern bloc |
| **Hungary** | Began dismantling its border fence with **Austria** in May, and in September let East Germans cross freely — the first hole in the Iron Curtain |
| **East Germany** | Tens of thousands fled west via Hungary and Czechoslovakia; mass protests (Leipzig, October); **Honecker** was replaced in October; the **Berlin Wall opened on 9 November** |
| **Czechoslovakia** | The peaceful **"Velvet Revolution"** (November–December); the dissident playwright **Václav Havel** became president |
| **Bulgaria** | Long-time leader Zhivkov forced out (November) |
| **Romania** | A violent revolution; **Ceaușescu** was executed on 25 December |

The crucial point: **Gorbachev did not send in troops**, unlike 1956 and 1968. Once people saw that the Soviet threat had gone, the communist regimes — which rested on that threat — collapsed like dominoes within months.

### The fall of the Berlin Wall, 9 November 1989

On 9 November an East German official, Günter Schabowski, announced new travel rules at a press conference and wrongly said they applied immediately. Crowds gathered at the checkpoints; overwhelmed border guards opened the gates, and Berliners began to break down the Wall.

Its significance:
- **Symbol**: the Wall had stood since 1961 as the symbol of a divided Europe; its fall showed the Iron Curtain had gone.
- **Proof that the Brezhnev Doctrine was dead**: the USSR did not intervene, so the other regimes lost their last protection.
- **German reunification**: East Germany collapsed; Germany was reunited on **3 October 1990**, and a united Germany joined **NATO** — something Stalin and his successors had always opposed.
- **End of the Cold War in Europe**: at the **Malta summit** (December 1989) President **George H. W. Bush** and Gorbachev spoke of a new era; the Cold War division of Europe, begun in 1945–49, was over.

### The collapse of the Soviet Union and the end of the Warsaw Pact

- With the satellite states now non-communist, the **Warsaw Pact** had no purpose. Its military structures were dismantled in **March 1991** and it was formally **dissolved on 1 July 1991**; **Comecon**, the Soviet-bloc trading organisation, had been dissolved days earlier (June 1991).
- Inside the USSR, glasnost let nationalism erupt. **Lithuania** declared independence in March 1990; the **Baltic states**, Georgia and others pushed to leave.
- In **August 1991** hardline communists attempted a **coup**, holding Gorbachev in the Crimea. It collapsed within three days, largely because **Boris Yeltsin**, president of the Russian republic, rallied resistance in Moscow. The coup destroyed the Communist Party's authority and Gorbachev's power.
- Republics declared independence one after another. In **December 1991** Russia, Ukraine and Belarus formed the **Commonwealth of Independent States**; Gorbachev resigned on **25 December 1991** and the USSR ceased to exist.

**Significance for the Warsaw Pact**: note the order — the Pact was dissolved in July 1991, **before** the USSR finally broke up in December. The link is that the **collapse of Soviet power and will** — economic failure, new thinking, the loss of Eastern Europe in 1989 and the weakening of the centre — removed everything that held the alliance together. The end of the USSR then made the end of the Pact permanent: there was no longer a superpower to lead a rival bloc, and the bipolar Cold War was over. NATO survived; the USA was left as the only superpower.

::: terms
- **Second Cold War**: the renewed period of high tension from about 1979 to 1985, after the collapse of détente.
- **Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI)**: Reagan's 1983 plan for a space-based missile defence system, nicknamed "Star Wars".
- **Perestroika**: Gorbachev's policy of restructuring the Soviet economy, introducing some market elements.
- **Glasnost**: Gorbachev's policy of openness — less censorship and more honest discussion of the USSR's problems.
- **New thinking**: Gorbachev's foreign policy of cutting arms, withdrawing from Afghanistan and letting Eastern Europe choose its own path.
- **INF Treaty**: the 1987 agreement to destroy all US and Soviet intermediate-range nuclear missiles.
- **Sinatra Doctrine**: the joking 1989 Soviet name for letting Eastern European states go "their own way", replacing the Brezhnev Doctrine.
- **Velvet Revolution**: the peaceful overthrow of communist rule in Czechoslovakia in November–December 1989.
- **Commonwealth of Independent States**: the loose association of former Soviet republics formed in December 1991 as the USSR dissolved.
:::

::: method
The Cold War is the **period study** in Booklet P of Paper 2 (about 50 minutes for 32 marks). Three question types, all worth 8 marks, **no SPaG**. The mark schemes use two levels (consequences) or three levels (narrative and importance) — there is no Level 4 on this booklet.

**1. "Explain two consequences of…" (8 marks: 4 per consequence)**
- Level 1 (1–2 each): simple or generalised ("it ended the Cold War").
- Level 2 (3–4 each): the consequence is **explained** with specific knowledge showing *how* the event led to it.
- Two paragraphs, two different consequences: "One consequence of X was Y. This was because… As a result…".

**2. "Write a narrative account analysing…" (8 marks)**
- Use both stimulus points **and add your own** — answers that do not go beyond the stimulus are capped at 5 marks.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple story. Level 2 (3–5): a sequence with some links. Level 3 (6–8): a **coherent analytical narrative** — events in order, each linked to the next by cause and consequence, reaching an outcome ("so by December 1991 the USSR had ceased to exist").
- 1989 moves fast: get the order right — Poland (June), Hungary (May–September), East Germany (October–November), Czechoslovakia (November–December), Romania (December).

**3. "Explain the importance of X for Y" (8 marks each; answer two of three)**
- Level 1 (1–2): general comment. Level 2 (3–5): some explanation of how X affected Y. Level 3 (6–8): **analysis of the impact** of X on Y with precise knowledge, two or three distinct effects, and a short judgement.
- For Reagan, Gorbachev and SDI, weigh the factor against others (the Soviet economy, people power) — a sentence of balance helps show analysis.
:::

::: example Two consequences
Explain two consequences of the fall of the Berlin Wall (1989). (8)

One consequence was the reunification of Germany. When the Wall opened on 9 November 1989, East Germans could leave freely, and the East German state lost its authority and population. Free elections in March 1990 were won by parties favouring unity, and on 3 October 1990 Germany was reunited. The united Germany joined NATO, which meant the division of Germany that had begun with the zones of 1945 was over.

A second consequence was that communist rule collapsed across Eastern Europe. The fact that Gorbachev did not send Soviet troops to save the Wall showed that the Brezhnev Doctrine was dead. Within weeks the Velvet Revolution brought Havel to power in Czechoslovakia, Zhivkov was forced out in Bulgaria and Ceaușescu was overthrown and executed in Romania. As a result the Warsaw Pact lost its purpose and was dissolved in July 1991.
:::

::: example Narrative account
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of 1985–91 that led to the collapse of the Soviet Union. You may use the following in your answer: Gorbachev becomes leader (1985); the August coup (1991). You must also use information of your own. (8)

Gorbachev became General Secretary in March 1985 and faced a stagnating economy and a costly war in Afghanistan. To revive the economy he introduced perestroika and glasnost, and because military spending was crippling the USSR he adopted "new thinking", which led to the INF Treaty with Reagan in 1987 and the withdrawal from Afghanistan by February 1989.

Glasnost allowed open criticism, and new thinking meant Gorbachev would no longer use force in Eastern Europe; he announced troop cuts at the UN in December 1988. As a result, in 1989 communist regimes collapsed across Eastern Europe, and the fall of the Berlin Wall in November showed the Soviet empire was gone. This encouraged nationalists inside the USSR itself: Lithuania declared independence in March 1990, and other republics pressed to leave.

Alarmed that the USSR was falling apart, hardline communists launched a coup in August 1991 while Gorbachev was in the Crimea. However, Boris Yeltsin led resistance in Moscow and the coup collapsed within three days. This fatally weakened both the Communist Party and Gorbachev, so republics rushed to declare independence. Consequently, in December 1991 Russia, Ukraine and Belarus formed the Commonwealth of Independent States, Gorbachev resigned on 25 December and the Soviet Union ceased to exist.
:::

::: example Importance
Explain the importance of the Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI) for relations between the USA and the Soviet Union. (8)

SDI was important because it increased tension in the early 1980s. Announced by Reagan in March 1983, the same month he called the USSR an "evil empire", it proposed a space-based shield to shoot down Soviet missiles. Soviet leaders saw it as an attempt to escape mutually assured destruction and gain the ability to strike first, which made the year of Able Archer 83 one of the most dangerous of the Cold War.

It was also important because it put pressure on the USSR to negotiate. Soviet technology was far behind, and the economy could not afford to compete in a new high-technology arms race. This encouraged Gorbachev to seek arms reductions so he could cut military spending, leading to the summits at Geneva and Reykjavik.

However, SDI was also an obstacle. At Reykjavik in 1986 the two leaders came close to agreeing enormous cuts, but talks collapsed because Reagan would not confine SDI to the laboratory. Only when Gorbachev separated the issues did the INF Treaty follow in 1987. So SDI's greatest importance was as pressure that pushed the USSR towards talks — though Soviet economic weakness, not SDI alone, explains Gorbachev's choices.
:::

::: q
Why did SDI alarm Soviet leaders?
---
It threatened to make the USA invulnerable to Soviet missiles, destroying mutually assured destruction and allowing a US first strike; the USSR lacked the technology and money to build its own.
:::

::: q
Give three problems facing the USSR when Gorbachev came to power in 1985.
---
Any three: a stagnating economy and shortages; heavy military spending; the costly war in Afghanistan; an ageing leadership; lagging technology; (from 1986) Chernobyl.
:::

::: q
What was Gorbachev's "new thinking" in foreign policy?
---
Ending the arms race through agreements with the USA, withdrawing from Afghanistan, cutting troops (500,000, announced at the UN in December 1988) and letting Eastern European states choose their own path instead of propping them up by force.
:::

::: q
Why was the INF Treaty (1987) a turning point?
---
It was the first treaty to abolish a whole category of nuclear weapons — all intermediate-range missiles — rather than just limit growth; 2,692 missiles were destroyed with on-site inspection, building trust.
:::

::: q
When was the Warsaw Pact dissolved, and why is the date important?
---
1 July 1991 (military structures ended March 1991). It came before the USSR's collapse in December 1991, so the Pact ended because Soviet power and control had already collapsed, and the USSR's end made it permanent.

::: watch
- **Muddling the order of 1989–91** — Wall falls November 1989; Germany reunited October 1990; Warsaw Pact dissolved July 1991; coup August 1991; USSR ends December 1991.
- Saying **Reagan alone** ended the Cold War: examiners reward analysis that links his pressure to Gorbachev's choices and the Soviet economy.
- Describing SDI without its **significance** — it threatened MAD, strained the Soviet economy and blocked agreement at Reykjavik.
- Confusing **perestroika** (economic restructuring) with **glasnost** (openness).
- Treating the fall of the Wall as just a party: its importance is that it **proved the USSR would not intervene** and led to reunification and the Pact's end.
- Narratives that only use the two stimulus points are capped at 5 marks.
:::
