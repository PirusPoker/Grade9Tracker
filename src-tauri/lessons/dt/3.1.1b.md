## What it is

New technology does not only change factories; it changes the planet, jobs, fashions and people's lives. This part of 3.1.1 covers **sustainability**, **people**, **culture**, **society**, the **environment**, and the **critical evaluation** of new technologies from different points of view. It is tested by 1-mark multiple choice (inclusive design, market pull, finite resources), 2–4 mark "explain" questions, and longer answers where you weigh arguments, such as the case for and against planned obsolescence.

### Sustainability: the impact of resource consumption

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Finite resource** | exists in a limited amount and cannot be replaced within a human lifetime; will eventually run out | crude oil (the raw material for most polymers), natural gas, coal, metal ores such as bauxite and iron ore |
| **Non-finite resource** | can be replaced or regrown, so it will not run out if managed properly | timber from managed forests, cotton, wool, bamboo, wind and solar energy |
| **Disposal of waste** | what happens to products and materials at the end of their life | landfill, incineration (sometimes with energy recovery), recycling, composting |

Using finite resources fast has consequences: supplies become scarcer and more expensive, extraction (mining, drilling) damages landscapes and habitats, and processing uses a lot of energy. Even non-finite resources are only sustainable if they are replaced as fast as they are used: a forest that is felled and not replanted is not sustainable. **Waste** matters because landfill sites are filling up, some materials (many polymers) take hundreds of years to break down, toxic substances (from batteries and electronics) can leak into soil and water, and incineration releases carbon dioxide. Designers reduce these impacts by choosing recyclable or recycled materials, using less material, designing for disassembly and making products last longer. This links to the **ecological footprint** of a material: the land, water and energy used, and the pollution caused, across its whole life.

### People: technology push, market pull and changing jobs

- **Technology push**: a new product is developed because a **new technology, material or process** has been invented, and the company then looks for uses and customers. Examples: products using touch screens when capacitive touch screens became cheap; products using lithium-ion batteries; early smart watches.
- **Market pull**: a new product is developed because **consumers want or need** something, found through market research, complaints or changing lifestyles. Examples: longer battery life in phones; more eco-friendly packaging; child-proof medicine lids.

Most successful products combine both: a new technology is used to meet a need that people already have.

**Changing job roles.** Automation, CAD/CAM and the internet remove some jobs (manual assembly, drafting by hand, many shop and warehouse roles) and create others (CAD technicians, robot programmers, app developers, data analysts, online marketers, repair and recycling specialists). Workers must retrain, and lifelong learning becomes normal. Home working and the "gig economy" change how people are employed.

### Culture: fashion, trends, faiths and beliefs

**Fashion and trends** change faster because of new technology: social media spreads a trend worldwide in days, CAD and fast manufacturing let companies copy it quickly, and online shopping makes it easy to buy. New materials create new looks (e.g. performance sportswear worn as everyday clothing, LED and smart fabrics). The downside is "fast fashion": cheap clothes worn a few times and thrown away, creating huge amounts of textile waste.

**Respecting people of different faiths and beliefs** means designers research their users and avoid causing offence: for example, colours, symbols, numbers or images that carry meaning in some cultures; imagery on clothing; modest-wear options; and materials that some people avoid, such as leather or animal-derived materials for vegetarians and some faiths, or products from certain animals. Getting this wrong can offend users, damage a brand and lose sales in a whole market.

### Society: designing to avoid a negative impact on others

**Inclusive design** means designing products that as many people as possible can use, whatever their age, ability or background.

| Group | Design responses |
|---|---|
| **People with disabilities** | large, high-contrast controls; tactile markings and Braille; audible and visual signals together; voice control; one-handed operation; adjustable heights; wheelchair access |
| **The elderly** | large clear text and buttons; easy-grip, cushioned handles for weaker grip or arthritis; lightweight products; simple controls with few functions; non-slip surfaces; good lighting |
| **Different religious groups** | products that respect dietary laws, dress codes and practices (e.g. modest sportswear, prayer mats, labelling of ingredients or materials); avoiding offensive symbols; features such as timers that suit religious practice |

Inclusive products are often better for everyone: a lever tap designed for arthritic hands is also easier for a child or someone with dirty hands.

### Environment: positive and negative impacts of new products

| Impact | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous improvement** | each new version is refined: more efficient, lighter, less material, easier to recycle | encourages people to replace products that still work, adding to waste |
| **Efficient working** | more efficient motors, LEDs and insulation reduce energy use; efficient factories use less material and energy per product | making new efficient products still uses resources and energy |
| **Pollution** | cleaner production processes and filters; electric vehicles cut local air pollution | mining, manufacturing, transport and disposal release harmful chemicals, microplastics and toxic e-waste |
| **Global warming** | renewable-powered products, lightweight materials and local production cut carbon dioxide emissions | burning fossil fuels for energy and transport releases greenhouse gases that trap heat in the atmosphere |

### Critical evaluation: looking at a new technology from different perspectives

A designer judges a new technology by thinking about everyone it affects: the user, the manufacturer, the worker, the community and the environment.

- **Planned obsolescence**: deliberately designing a product to have a limited useful life, so the customer must replace it. It can be **built-in failure** (parts that wear out, batteries that cannot be replaced, glued-shut cases) or **style/perceived obsolescence** (new models and fashions that make the old one look out of date). *For:* keeps sales and jobs, drives innovation, keeps prices low, lets customers get the latest safety features and efficiency. *Against:* wastes finite resources, creates huge amounts of e-waste, costs customers money, is seen as unethical.
- **Design for maintenance**: designing products to be easily repaired, serviced and upgraded: screws instead of glue, standard replaceable parts, modular construction, available spare parts and repair guides. It extends product life and reduces waste, but products may cost more and be bulkier, and manufacturers sell fewer replacements.
- **Ethics**: whether the product and the way it is made are morally right: fair pay and safe conditions for workers, no child labour, honest marketing, data privacy, animal welfare, not exploiting people who cannot afford repairs.
- **The environment**: the whole-life impact, from extraction through manufacture, transport and use to disposal.

::: terms
- **Finite resource**: a resource that exists in a limited amount and cannot be replaced once used, such as crude oil or metal ores.
- **Non-finite resource**: a resource that can be replaced or regrown, such as timber from managed forests or cotton.
- **Technology push**: new products developed because a new technology, material or process has been invented.
- **Market pull**: new products developed because consumers want or need them, often found through market research.
- **Inclusive design**: designing products that can be used by as many people as possible, whatever their age, ability or background.
- **Planned obsolescence**: deliberately designing a product so it fails or goes out of date after a limited time, so it must be replaced.
- **Design for maintenance**: designing products so they can be easily repaired, serviced and upgraded, extending their life.
- **Continuous improvement**: making small, regular improvements to a product or process to increase quality and efficiency.
- **Global warming**: the rise in the Earth's average temperature caused by greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide trapping heat.
- **Ethics**: the moral principles used to judge whether a product, and the way it is made and sold, is right or wrong.
- **Ecological footprint**: the effect of a product or material on the environment across its whole life, from extraction to disposal.
:::

::: method
1. **Define the key term first** (market pull, planned obsolescence, finite) in one accurate sentence.
2. **Give a real product example** and say how it shows the idea: examples turn a 1-mark point into a 2-mark one.
3. **For design-for-users questions**, name the user's need (weak grip, poor eyesight, religious practice), then the design feature, then how it helps.
4. **For environment questions**, think across the life cycle: materials, manufacture, transport, use, disposal.
5. **For "discuss" or "evaluate" questions**, look from at least two perspectives (manufacturer, consumer, environment) and give both sides before a reasoned conclusion.
:::

::: example Define: planned obsolescence
Explain what is meant by planned obsolescence. (2)

A product is deliberately designed to have a limited life or to go out of date (1), so that the customer has to replace it and buy a new one, e.g. a phone with a battery that cannot be replaced (1).
:::

::: example Explain: technology push
Explain one example of technology push. (2)

When capacitive touch screens became cheap and reliable, manufacturers put them into phones, tablets and cooker controls (1): the products were created because the technology existed, not because consumers had asked for them (1).
:::

::: example Explain: designing for elderly users
Explain two ways a kettle could be designed for elderly users. (4)

A large, cushioned handle that is easy to grip (1) helps users with arthritis or weak hands to hold it safely without it slipping (1). A clear water gauge with large, high-contrast markings (1) lets users with poor eyesight fill it to the right level, so they do not overfill it and risk scalding (1).
:::

::: example Explain: disposal of waste
Explain why the disposal of electronic products is an environmental problem. (3)

Electronic products contain toxic substances such as lead and those in batteries (1), which can leak into soil and water if they are sent to landfill (1). They also contain finite metals such as copper and gold, which are lost if the products are not recycled (1).
:::

::: example Discuss: planned obsolescence
Discuss the arguments for and against planned obsolescence. (6)

For: manufacturers sell more replacement products, which keeps the company profitable and protects jobs (1). Regular new models drive innovation, so consumers get improved features, safety and energy efficiency (1). Products can be made cheaply because they do not need to last, keeping them affordable (1).
Against: products are thrown away while they could still work, using up finite resources such as oil and metals (1). The discarded products add to landfill and toxic e-waste, much of it shipped to poorer countries (1). Consumers are forced to spend money on replacements, which many see as unethical; designing for maintenance, with replaceable batteries and standard parts, would reduce waste while still allowing upgrades (1).
Level 3 (5–6): both sides, developed, with a supported conclusion. Level 2 (3–4): some developed points, may be one-sided. Level 1 (1–2): simple points.
:::

::: q
State the difference between a finite and a non-finite resource. (2)
---
A finite resource is limited and will run out because it cannot be replaced (1); a non-finite resource can be replaced or regrown, so it will not run out if managed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one example of market pull. (2)
---
Consumers complained that phone batteries did not last a day (1), so manufacturers developed longer-lasting batteries and power-saving features to meet this demand (1). Accept any valid product driven by consumer need.
:::

::: q
Explain how new technology has changed job roles in manufacturing. (4)
---
Automation and robots have replaced many manual assembly jobs (1), so fewer unskilled workers are needed (1). New roles have been created in programming, operating and maintaining CNC machines and robots (1), so workers need higher technical skills and must retrain throughout their careers (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why designers must respect people of different faiths and beliefs. (3)
---
Symbols, colours, images or materials can have religious meaning (1); using them wrongly can offend users (1), which damages the brand's reputation and loses sales in that market (1).
:::

::: q
Describe what is meant by design for maintenance and give one example. (3)
---
Designing products so they can be easily repaired, serviced or upgraded (1) to extend their life and reduce waste (1). Example: a laptop with a battery and memory that can be removed with standard screws (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one positive and one negative impact of new products on the environment. (4)
---
Positive: new products are often more energy efficient, e.g. LED lamps (1), so less electricity is generated from fossil fuels and fewer greenhouse gases are released (1). Negative: making new products uses finite resources and energy (1), and the old products they replace are often sent to landfill, causing pollution (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Planned obsolescence is deliberate.** "When a product becomes out of date" without saying it is designed that way loses the mark.
- **Technology push starts with the technology; market pull starts with the consumer.** Check which comes first in your example.
- Timber and cotton are **non-finite only if replaced**; oil and metal ores are **finite**.
- For inclusive design, link **feature to need**: "big buttons" alone is weak; "big buttons so users with poor eyesight or shaky hands can press the right one" earns two marks.
- In a "discuss" question, give **both sides** and a conclusion; one-sided answers stay in the middle level.
:::

Landfill sites are filling up and take up land (1); many polymers take hundreds of years to break down, and toxic chemicals from electronics and batteries can leak into soil and water (1); incineration releases carbon dioxide, adding to global warming, and valuable materials are lost if not recycled (1).

Each new version of a product is refined to use less material or less energy (1), so fewer resources are used and fewer emissions are produced over its life (1).

::: test
Explain how the use of fossil fuels in manufacturing and transport contributes to global warming. (3)
---
Burning coal, oil or gas releases carbon dioxide (1). Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas that traps heat in the atmosphere (1), raising average global temperatures and causing climate change (1).

---
Social media spreads new styles around the world very quickly (1), so trends change more often and people want new products sooner (1). ---

Level 2 (3–4): some developed arguments, may be one-sided or lack a conclusion.
Level 1 (1–2): simple points.
Indicative content.
