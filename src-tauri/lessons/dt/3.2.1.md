## What it is

Every product starts with a **selection** decision: which material, and which bought-in components, will do the job best? AQA wants you to weigh eight factors. No material wins on every one, so good designers **trade them off** against the product and its user. In the exam you must name the factor *and* link it to a real product and material. A factor on its own earns almost nothing.

| Factor | What it covers | Questions a designer asks |
|---|---|---|
| **Functionality** | **application of use** (does it have the properties the job needs?) and **ease of working** (can it be cut, formed, joined and finished with the processes available?) | Is it strong, light, rigid, flexible, waterproof, conductive or insulating enough? Can we laser cut, bend, mould or sew it? |
| **Aesthetics** | **surface finish**, **texture** and **colour** | Will it look and feel right to the target market? Does it need a finish, or does it look good straight from the mould? |
| **Environmental factors** | **recyclable** or **reused** materials; what happens at **end of life** | Can it be recycled or reused? Is it a single material, or a mix that is hard to separate? Does it have recycled content? |
| **Availability** | **ease of sourcing and purchase** | Is it sold in standard stock forms? How long is the lead time? Could supply run out, or the component be discontinued? |
| **Cost** | the price of the material and of working it; **bulk buying** | What does each unit cost? How much cheaper does it get if we buy in bulk? Is a cheaper material still good enough? |
| **Social factors** | **social responsibility**: the effect on people who make, use and live near the product | Is it safe and non-toxic? Were workers treated fairly? Does it exclude any users? |
| **Cultural factors** | being **sensitive to cultural influences** | Could a colour, symbol or material cause offence, or carry an unintended meaning, in the market where it is sold? |
| **Ethical factors** | buying from **ethical sources** such as **FSC** | Is the timber or paper from a certified, well-managed forest? Is the cotton fair trade? Are the minerals conflict-free? |

**Functionality comes first.** A material that looks good but snaps, rots or melts in use is the wrong choice; once a shortlist *works*, the other factors decide. **Ease of working** matters as much as in-use properties: MDF machines cleanly with no grain to split, acrylic laser cuts with a polished edge, and a metal for a deep-drawn can must be malleable enough to stretch without tearing.

**Aesthetics** sells the product. Colour can be built into the material (pigmented polymers, dyed yarn) so no paint is needed; texture comes from the material (oak grain) or is added (a textured mould, embossing); finish runs from high gloss to matt. Premium products show off their material; budget ones hide a cheap core under a laminate or print.

**Environmental factors** favour materials that recycle easily (metals, thermoforming polymers, paper and board) and **recycled or reused content** (reclaimed timber, recycled PET fleece). Thermosets, composites and bonded layers (a board-polymer-foil drinks carton) are hard to recycle. Designing for **end-of-life disposal** means fewer material types, marked polymer codes, and screws rather than glue so parts separate.

**Availability**: standard **stock forms** are cheap and quick to buy; odd sizes, exotic hardwoods or specialist components bring long lead times and minimum orders, and electronic parts can go **obsolete**, so designers prefer parts made by several suppliers. **Cost** is the whole cost (material, working, finishing, waste). **Bulk buying** lowers the **unit price**, but the cash is paid up front and the stock must be stored. A cheaper material that is *good enough* may win, such as oak-veneered MDF instead of solid oak.

**Social, cultural and ethical factors** are about people:
- **Social responsibility**: the product must be safe and non-toxic, for example a child's toy with lead-free paint and no small parts that could come loose. It must be made in **safe working conditions**, and it should not harm the community where it is made or thrown away.
- **Cultural sensitivity**: colours and symbols carry different meanings in different places. In parts of East Asia white is linked with mourning, while red is linked with luck and celebration. Some materials are unacceptable to some groups, such as leather to many Hindus or pigskin to many Muslims and Jews. So a product sold worldwide may need different colours, materials or graphics in different markets.
- **Ethical sourcing**: the **FSC** (Forest Stewardship Council) label shows that timber, paper or board comes from forests managed so that trees are replanted, wildlife is protected and local people's rights are respected. Other examples are **Fairtrade** cotton, and avoiding **conflict minerals** (tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold mined where the profits fund armed groups) in electronics.

### The factors in each material category

| Category | Typical product | How the factors shape the choice |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | cereal box, flyer | **Folding boxboard** or **duplex board**: stiff enough to protect the food yet easy to crease and die cut (function); a smooth white face that prints well (aesthetics); recyclable, often with recycled content (environment); FSC-certified board is widely available and cheap in bulk. |
| **Timbers** | child's toy, flat-pack wardrobe | **Beech** for toys: close grain, hard and tough, doesn't splinter, takes a non-toxic finish (function, social). **Pine** and **MDF** for flat pack: cheap, plentiful and easy to machine (cost, availability). **Oak** for premium furniture, for its grain and colour (aesthetics). FSC certification for all of them (ethics). |
| **Metals** | drinks can, saucepan, bike frame | **Aluminium** can: light, malleable enough to deep draw, doesn't rust, and can be recycled again and again (function, environment). **Stainless steel** pan: corrosion resistant and easy to clean (function). The polished or brushed surface sells it (aesthetics). |
| **Polymers** | lunch box, garden chair | **Polypropylene**: food safe, tough, and able to form a flexible **living hinge**; coloured right through, so no painting; recyclable (code 5). A garden chair needs a UV-stabilised polymer (function). |
| **Textiles** | running top, sofa cover | **Polyester/elastane** for sportswear: wicks sweat, stretches and dries fast (function). **Recycled** polyester from bottles, or **Fairtrade/organic cotton** (environment, ethics). Dyed colour and print suit the market (aesthetics). A furnishing fabric needs to be flame retardant (social: safety). |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | torch, toy robot | **LEDs** rather than filament lamps: efficient and long lasting (function, environment). A **microcontroller** does the work of many separate chips (cost, ease of assembly). Standard parts are sold on reels, much cheaper in bulk (cost, availability). **Lead-free** solder, as required by **RoHS** (the Restriction of Hazardous Substances rules), and conflict-free minerals (ethics). Products are designed so they can be taken back and recycled at end of life (environment). |

**Cost calculations** in Section B usually involve bulk pricing or a percentage discount: find the number of packs, then the full price, then multiply by $(1 - \text{discount})$, and round at the end (usually to the nearest penny).

::: terms
- **Functionality**: whether a material has the properties a product needs in use, and whether it can be worked with the processes available.
- **Ease of working**: how readily a material can be cut, shaped, joined and finished with the tools and machines available.
- **Aesthetics**: how a product looks and feels, controlled by its colour, texture and surface finish.
- **Availability**: how easily a material or component can be sourced and bought, in the stock forms and quantities needed.
- **Bulk buying**: buying in large quantities so the supplier charges a lower price per unit.
- **Unit cost**: the cost of one item, found by dividing a total cost by the number of items.
- **FSC**: the Forest Stewardship Council, whose label shows timber, paper or board comes from responsibly managed forests.
- **Social responsibility**: making sure a product and its manufacture do not harm the people who make, use or live near it.
- **Cultural sensitivity**: designing so colours, symbols and materials do not offend or confuse people from different cultures.
- **Ethical sourcing**: buying materials from suppliers who treat workers fairly and do not damage the environment.
- **Recyclable material**: a material that can be reprocessed into new material at the end of a product's life.
- **End-of-life disposal**: what happens to a product and its materials once it is no longer used: reuse, recycling, incineration or landfill.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the product and its user**, because the factors are weighed differently for a toy, a hand tool or a sofa.
2. **Name the factor** the question asks about, using AQA's words: functionality, aesthetics, environmental, availability, cost, social, cultural or ethical.
3. **Name a specific material or component**: beech, duplex board, PP, stainless steel, polyester, an LED. "Wood" or "plastic" is too vague for Section B.
4. **Link the factor to a property and to the product**: "beech is tough and close grained, so a toy won't splinter when dropped."
5. **Give the consequence** for the user, the maker or the environment. That is the second mark in every "explain".
6. For **calculations**, lay out the packs, full price, discount and final price, with units and £ signs, rounded as asked.
7. For the **8-mark analyse and evaluate** question, cover several factors, show how they **conflict** (cheap against ethical, good-looking against recyclable), use **named products**, and finish with a judgement on which factors matter most for that product.
:::

::: example Explain: aesthetics
Explain why a designer would consider aesthetics when selecting a material for a product. Give an example in your answer. (3)

Customers often choose between similar products by how they look and feel, so the right colour, texture and finish helps a product sell to its target market (1). For example, a high-end kitchen utensil set might use brushed stainless steel with oak handles (1), because the metal's sheen and the visible wood grain suggest quality and justify a higher price, while a budget set uses bright polypropylene coloured right through, which looks cheerful and needs no finish (1).
:::

::: example Explain: ethical sourcing
Explain why a manufacturer of paper notebooks would buy FSC-certified paper. (3)

FSC certification shows the wood pulp came from forests where felled trees are replanted and wildlife and local people are protected (1). This reduces the manufacturer's part in deforestation and in illegal logging (1). Many customers and retailers look for the FSC logo, so using certified paper protects the brand's reputation and can help the notebooks sell (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: bulk discount
A manufacturer needs 1500 LEDs. They cost £4.80 per bag of 50. The supplier offers 15% off orders over 1000. Calculate the total cost. Give your answer to the nearest penny. (3)

Bags needed $= 1500 \div 50 = 30$ (1)
Full price $= 30 \times £4.80 = £144.00$ (1)
With the discount: $£144.00 \times 0.85 = £122.40$ (1)
:::

::: example Choose one: two factors
Choose one material: corrugated card, pine, aluminium, polypropylene, cotton. Explain two factors a designer would consider when selecting it for a named product. (4)

Chosen material: aluminium, for a drinks can.
Functionality: aluminium is lightweight and malleable, so it can be deep drawn into a thin seamless can (1), keeping the can cheap to transport and easy to hold (1).
Environmental: aluminium can be recycled over and over without losing quality (1), and recycling uses far less energy than extracting new metal, which reduces the can's footprint (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate: extended
Analyse and evaluate the factors a designer must consider when selecting materials for a child's toy. Give examples in your answer. (8)

Functionality is the priority. The toy will be dropped, chewed and thrown, so it needs a tough, hard-wearing material, such as beech for a wooden stacking toy or ABS for a construction brick. Both resist impact and hold their shape, so the toy lasts and stays safe. Social responsibility is almost as important. The finish must be non-toxic, parts must be too large to swallow, and edges rounded. A cheaper softwood such as pine is easier to dent and splinter, so it is less suitable even though it costs less. Aesthetics matter because parents and children are drawn to bright colours. Polymers can be pigmented right through, so the colour never chips off, whereas painted timber can chip and needs a toy-safe paint. Environmental factors pull the other way. Many parents now prefer FSC beech because it is renewable and biodegradable, while mixed polymer toys often end up in landfill. Cost and availability favour polymers for mass-produced toys, because injection moulding makes millions of identical parts cheaply. Solid timber suits smaller batches sold at a higher price. Overall, safety and durability cannot be traded away. After that, a designer weighs cost against environmental and aesthetic appeal to suit the target market: a budget brand chooses a polymer, and a premium, eco-conscious brand chooses FSC hardwood. (Level 4: 7–8 marks)
:::

::: q
Give two factors, other than cost, that a designer considers when selecting a material. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): functionality/application of use; ease of working; aesthetics (surface finish, texture, colour); environmental factors (recyclable or reused materials); availability; social; cultural; ethical.
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by "ease of working" when selecting a material. Give an example. (2)
---
How easily the material can be cut, shaped, joined or finished with the tools and processes available (1); e.g. MDF has no grain, so it machines and routs cleanly without splitting (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a designer might choose a material that is available in standard stock sizes. (2)
---
Standard stock is easy to buy quickly from many suppliers (1), so the product is not delayed by long lead times, and it is cheaper than a special size (1).
:::

::: q
Timber for a batch of stools costs £6.40 per length. The workshop buys 60 lengths and receives a 10% discount. Each length makes the legs for two stools. Calculate the timber cost per stool to the nearest penny. (3)
---
$60 \times £6.40 = £384.00$ (1); with the discount $£384.00 \times 0.9 = £345.60$ (1); stools $= 60 \times 2 = 120$, so $£345.60 \div 120 = £2.88$ per stool (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why bonding several different materials together can make a product harder to dispose of responsibly. (3)
---
At the end of life the materials must be separated before they can be recycled (1); bonded layers, such as board, polymer and foil in a drinks carton, are hard and costly to separate (1), so the product is more likely to go to landfill or incineration (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways a designer can show social responsibility in the choice of materials and components for a product. (4)
---
Choosing non-toxic materials and finishes, e.g. lead-free paint on a toy (1), protects users, especially children, from harm (1). Buying from suppliers with safe, fairly paid working conditions, e.g. Fairtrade cotton (1), avoids exploiting workers in the supply chain (1). Also accept: avoiding materials that pollute communities near factories; designing for inclusive use.
:::

::: watch
- A factor with no product earns little. Always write **factor + specific material + property + product + consequence**.
- **"It's cheap"** is not an explanation. Say *why*: bulk buying, a cheaper process, or less waste.
- **Environmental** means recyclable or reused content and end of life. Don't drift into energy sources unless the question asks.
- **FSC** is about responsibly managed **forests**, for timber, paper and board. It is not a recycling logo.
- In calculations, multiply by $(1 - \text{discount})$ **once**. Work out packs first, and round only at the end.
- In the 8-mark question, a list of factors with no weighing up stays in the lower levels. **Evaluate**: say which factor matters most for this product, and why.
:::

::: test
Explain one benefit and one drawback to a manufacturer of bulk buying materials. (4)
---
Benefit: suppliers give a lower price per unit on large orders (1), reducing the unit cost of each product and raising profit or allowing a lower price (1). Drawback: a large sum must be paid up front and the stock stored (1), tying up cash and space, and the stock may be wasted if the design changes or demand falls (1).

::: test
A manufacturer needs to buy 750 sheets of card. The card costs £8.40 per pack of 50 sheets. An 18% discount is offered. Calculate the total price. Give your answer to the nearest penny. Show your working. (3)
---
Packs $= 750 \div 50 = 15$ (1); full price $= 15 \times £8.40 = £126.00$ (1); with the discount $£126.00 \times 0.82 = £103.32$ (1). Accept a discount of £22.68 subtracted from £126.00.
:::

::: test
Supplier A sells a switch at £0.32 each. Supplier B sells the same switch at £27.50 per 100, plus a £6.00 delivery charge per order. A company needs 500 switches. Calculate which supplier is cheaper, and by how much. (4)
---
A: $500 \times £0.32 = £160.00$ (1). B: $5 \times £27.50 = £137.50$ (1), plus £6.00 delivery gives £143.50 (1). B is cheaper by £16.50 (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a designer might select a recycled material rather than a virgin (new) material for a product. (3)
---
Recycled material reduces the demand for new raw materials such as trees, ore or oil (1); reprocessing usually uses less energy than extraction, so less carbon is produced (1); it also diverts waste from landfill and can appeal to environmentally aware customers, e.g. polyester fleece from recycled PET bottles (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how cultural factors could affect the selection of materials for a range of tableware sold in several countries. (3)
---
Some cultures avoid certain materials or animal products, e.g. bone china contains animal bone, which some vegetarians and religious groups avoid (1); colours and patterns carry different meanings, e.g. white linked with mourning in parts of East Asia, red with celebration (1); so the designer may change materials, colours or decoration for each market to avoid offence and to suit local tastes (1).
:::

::: test
Table 1 shows a range of materials and components: duplex board · beech · stainless steel · polypropylene (PP) · polyester fleece · microcontroller. Choose one. (a) Name a product that uses your chosen material or component. (b) Explain how functionality influenced its selection for that product. (1 + 2) (3)
---
(a) A suitable product (1), e.g. duplex board: cereal box; beech: toy or chair; stainless steel: cutlery or sink; PP: lunch box or chair shell; polyester fleece: jacket; microcontroller: toy robot or thermostat.
(b) A relevant property (1) linked to how it helps the product work (1), e.g. duplex board is stiff but creases cleanly, so it forms a box that protects the food; beech is tough and close grained, so it resists knocks without splintering; stainless steel resists corrosion, so it stays hygienic when washed; PP flexes repeatedly without cracking, so it forms a living hinge lid; fleece traps air, so it insulates while staying light; a microcontroller can be programmed, so one chip controls timing and inputs, cutting part count.
:::

::: test
Explain why a designer of electronic products would prefer standard components available from several suppliers. (2)
---
If one supplier runs out or stops making the part, it can be bought elsewhere, so production is not stopped (1); competition and bulk supply keep the price low (1).
:::

::: test
A designer is choosing between solid oak and MDF with an oak veneer for a bookcase. Explain two factors that would influence the choice. (4)
---
Cost: veneered MDF is much cheaper than solid oak and is available in large standard sheets (1), so the bookcase can sell at a lower price or make more profit (1). Aesthetics/function: solid oak shows real grain and can be sanded and refinished (1), while MDF is stable, doesn't warp, and machines easily, but its veneer can chip, and it swells if it gets wet (1). Also accept an environmental point, e.g. MDF uses waste wood fibre, while oak may be FSC-certified and biodegradable.
:::

::: test
Explain why a thermosetting polymer is a less environmentally friendly choice than a thermoforming polymer. (2)
---
Thermosets have cross-links that stop them softening again when heated (1), so they cannot be melted and remoulded, and they usually end up in landfill or incinerated (1).
:::

::: test
Analyse and evaluate the factors a designer must consider when selecting materials and components for a product. Give examples of products in your answer. (8)
---
Levels:
7–8: Detailed analysis of several factors, with clear evaluation of how they conflict and which matter most, supported by relevant named products and materials throughout.
5–6: Good analysis of several factors with some evaluation (personal judgement) and some examples.
3–4: Basic analysis of generic factors with little or no evaluation; examples limited or generic.
1–2: One or two simple points showing limited understanding.
A response with no product examples can gain a maximum of 6 marks.
Indicative content: functionality (application of use and ease of working, e.g. PP for a living hinge, MDF for routed flat pack); aesthetics (colour, texture, finish, e.g. oak grain, brushed aluminium); environmental (recyclable or reused materials, single-material design, end of life, e.g. aluminium cans, recycled PET fleece); availability (stock forms, lead times, obsolete components); cost (bulk buying lowers unit cost, a cheaper material may be "good enough"); social (safety, non-toxic finishes on toys, working conditions); cultural (colour meanings, materials some groups avoid); ethical (FSC timber and paper, Fairtrade cotton, conflict minerals). Evaluation: factors conflict, e.g. the cheapest material may not be ethical or recyclable; function and safety usually come first; the balance depends on the market and scale, e.g. a budget, mass-produced product against a premium, eco-branded one.
:::
