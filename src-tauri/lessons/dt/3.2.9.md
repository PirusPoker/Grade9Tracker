## What it is

A **surface treatment or finish** is applied to the outside of a material to improve how it **works** (function) and how it **looks and feels** (aesthetics).
- **Functional reasons**: protection from moisture, **corrosion**, rot, insects, UV light, stains and wear; making a surface hygienic or easy to clean; reducing friction; insulating.
- **Aesthetic reasons**: colour, gloss or matt, texture, pattern, branding and information.

A finish is only as good as the **preparation** underneath it, so the exam asks both *how the surface is prepared* and *how the finish is applied*. The spec names three finishes per category (two for systems).

### The science: corrosion and oxidation
- **Oxidation** is a reaction with oxygen. Most metals oxidise at their surface.
- **Rusting** is the corrosion of iron and steel. It needs **both water and oxygen**, and it forms flaky iron oxide that falls away, exposing fresh metal to rust further.
- A finish protects in one of two ways:
  - As a **barrier** (paint, plastic coating, lacquer), it keeps water and air off the surface. If it is scratched, rust can start.
  - As **sacrificial protection**, a more reactive metal coats the steel. **Zinc** in galvanising corrodes **instead of** the iron, even where the coating is scratched.
- Aluminium's natural oxide layer protects it (and anodising thickens it). Timber rots through moisture and fungi, not corrosion, so it needs sealing or preservatives.

### General preparation
Whatever the material, prepare the surface first. **Clean and degrease** it so the finish bonds. **Remove defects** by filling, sanding, filing or de-burring. **Smooth** it through progressively finer abrasives. **Remove dust**. Make sure it is **dry**. A finish applied over dirt, grease or rust will flake, blister or show every flaw.

### Finishes across the categories

| Category | Finish | Preparation and application | What it achieves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | **Printing** | artwork is prepared as CMYK files; printed by offset lithography (long runs), digital (short runs), screen printing or flexography | colour, images, branding and information |
| | **Embossing** | the card is pressed between matched **male and female dies**, so part of the surface is pushed up into a **raised** design (**debossing** pushes it down); it can be combined with foil or print, or left plain (**blind embossing**) | a tactile, premium feel and look, e.g. on wedding invitations, luxury packaging and book covers |
| | **UV varnishing** | liquid varnish is applied all over or to selected areas (**spot UV**), then passed under **ultraviolet light**, which cures (hardens) it instantly | a high-gloss, scuff- and moisture-resistant surface; spot UV makes logos and images stand out |
| **Timbers** | **Painting** | sand smooth, working through the glasspaper grades with the grain; fill holes; seal knots; remove dust with a tack cloth; apply **primer**, then **undercoat**, then one or two **top coats**, sanding lightly between coats; brush, roller or spray | opaque colour; hides the grain and cheaper timber or MDF; a protective barrier against moisture |
| | **Varnishing** | prepare as for painting; apply a thin first coat (as a sealer), then further coats, **denibbing** (lightly sanding) between coats; polyurethane varnishes are tough and water resistant, and yacht varnish suits outdoor use | a clear, hard-wearing, waterproof layer that **shows and enhances the grain**; gloss, satin or matt |
| | **Tanalising** | an **industrial pressure treatment**: timber is sealed in a vessel, a **vacuum** removes the air from the cells, then a (copper-based) **preservative** is forced deep into the wood under **pressure** | long-lasting protection against **rot, fungi and insects** for outdoor timber (fence posts, decking, sheds); a greenish or brown tint |
| **Metals** | **Dip coating** (plastic) | the metal is cleaned, degreased and **heated** in an oven, then dipped into a **fluidised bed** of polymer powder (air blown through the powder makes it behave like a liquid); the powder melts onto the hot metal; it is returned to the oven to flow smooth, then cooled | a thick, soft-feel, coloured, electrically insulating barrier coat, e.g. on tool handles, dish racks and wire baskets |
| | **Powder coating** | the metal is cleaned and pre-treated; charged polymer powder is **sprayed electrostatically** onto the earthed metal, so it clings evenly, even in corners; the part is **baked** so the powder melts and cures into a tough coat | a hard, even, colourful and corrosion-resistant finish with no solvents, and overspray can be recovered; bikes, garden furniture, radiators, car wheels |
| | **Galvanising** | steel is degreased, **pickled** in acid to remove rust and scale, fluxed, then **dipped in molten zinc**; the zinc bonds to the steel as a coating | **barrier plus sacrificial protection**: zinc corrodes in preference to the steel, so even scratched areas don't rust; street furniture, crash barriers, fencing, buckets, nails |
| **Polymers** | **Polishing** | mainly the cut edges of acrylic: **draw file** smooth, rub with **wet-and-dry paper** (used wet, coarse to fine), then buff with **polishing compound** on a cloth or buffing wheel (or flame polish) | clear, glossy, smooth edges that match the factory surface |
| | **Printing** | **screen printing** onto flat items, **pad printing** onto curved ones (e.g. pens, keyboard keys), in-mould labels, and digital UV printing | logos, text, instructions and decoration on polymer products |
| | **Vinyl decals** | the design is cut from self-adhesive **vinyl** on a CNC **vinyl cutter** from CAD; the waste is **weeded** away; application tape lifts the decal; it is positioned on a clean surface, **squeegeed** to remove bubbles, and the tape is peeled off | cheap, colourful graphics and labels, easily applied to finished products, signs and vehicles |
| **Textiles** | **Printing** | screen printing (one screen per colour; ink forced through the mesh with a squeegee, then heat set), digital inkjet, heat transfer, sublimation (on polyester), block printing | patterns, logos and images on selected areas |
| | **Dyes** | fabric or yarn is washed to remove finishes, then immersed in a **dye bath**; the colour is fixed with a **mordant**, by heat, or by **oxidation** (e.g. indigo); resist methods (tie-dye, batik) create patterns | whole-fabric colour that should be **colourfast** (it won't wash out or fade) |
| | **Stain protection** | a chemical finish is sprayed or padded onto the fabric and heat cured, coating the fibres | liquids and oily spills **bead up** instead of soaking in, so they wipe away; used on upholstery, carpets, school uniform and outdoor wear; may need re-applying and can reduce breathability |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | **PCB lacquering** | after soldering, the board is cleaned of flux residues; connectors are masked; a thin clear **lacquer (conformal coating)** is sprayed, brushed or dipped over the board and left to cure | protects the copper and joints from **oxidation**, moisture, dust and short circuits, so electronics in damp or harsh places last longer |
| | **Lubrication** | **oil** or **grease** applied to moving parts: gears, bearings, chains, hinges, slides; oil for light, fast-moving parts; grease stays in place for slow, heavy loads | reduces **friction** and **wear**, heat and noise, and keeps moisture off so parts don't rust; must be maintained, and can attract dirt |

### Coverage calculations
Paint and varnish are rated by **coverage**, the area one litre covers (m²/L).

$\text{litres needed} = \frac{\text{area} \times \text{number of coats}}{\text{coverage}}$

Then **round up** to whole tins.

::: terms
- **Surface finish**: a coating or treatment applied to a material's surface to improve its function and/or appearance.
- **Corrosion**: the gradual destruction of a metal by chemical reaction with its surroundings, such as rusting of iron.
- **Oxidation**: a reaction with oxygen; in metals it forms an oxide layer on the surface.
- **Sacrificial protection**: coating steel with a more reactive metal such as zinc, which corrodes first to protect the steel.
- **Galvanising**: dipping cleaned steel into molten zinc to give barrier and sacrificial protection against rust.
- **Powder coating**: electrostatically spraying charged polymer powder onto metal, then baking it into a tough, even coat.
- **Dip coating**: dipping heated metal into a fluidised bed of polymer powder, which melts to form a thick plastic coating.
- **Tanalising**: forcing preservative deep into timber under vacuum and pressure to protect it from rot and insects.
- **Embossing**: pressing card between matched dies to create a raised design on the surface.
- **UV varnishing**: applying liquid varnish to printed card and curing it instantly with ultraviolet light for a glossy protective layer.
- **Vinyl decal**: a self-adhesive vinyl graphic cut by a CNC vinyl cutter and applied to a surface.
- **Stain protection**: a chemical finish that makes fabric fibres repel water and oil so spills do not soak in.
- **Conformal coating**: a thin protective lacquer applied over a finished PCB to keep out moisture, dust and corrosion.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the finish and the material**: "galvanising a steel gate", "varnishing an oak table top".
2. **Preparation first**: clean and degrease, remove defects, smooth through the grades, remove dust, dry.
3. **Application stages in order**, with the specific equipment and conditions (primer → undercoat → top coat; heat → dip in fluidised powder → reheat; degrease → pickle → flux → molten zinc).
4. **Say what it achieves** functionally (barrier, sacrificial protection, rot resistance, lower friction) and aesthetically (colour, gloss, grain, texture).
5. For metals, use the **science**: rust needs water and oxygen; barrier against sacrificial protection.
6. In "choose one" or 6-mark questions, **one mark per correct stage**, so give preparation, application and curing or drying as separate stages, plus a safety point.
7. For coverage: area × coats ÷ coverage, then **round up** to whole tins.
:::

::: example Explain: galvanising
Explain why steel garden railings are galvanised. (3)

Steel rusts when it is exposed to water and oxygen outdoors (1). Galvanising coats the railings in zinc, which acts as a barrier keeping out moisture and air (1). Zinc is more reactive than iron, so if the coating is scratched the zinc corrodes instead of the steel (sacrificial protection), and the railings last for many years with little maintenance (1).
:::

::: example Describe: varnishing
Describe how to prepare and varnish a pine shelf. (5)

Fill any holes and sand the surface smooth, working with the grain through coarse to fine grades of glasspaper on a cork block (1). Remove all dust with a brush and a tack cloth, so the varnish is not rough (1). Apply a thin first coat with a clean brush, working with the grain, to seal the wood (1). When it is dry, lightly sand (denib) with very fine glasspaper and remove the dust (1). Apply two or more further coats in the same way, in a well-ventilated, dust-free area, letting each dry fully (1).
:::

::: example Choose one: describe a finish
Choose one finish: powder coating · embossing · vinyl decals. Using notes and/or sketches, describe how your chosen finish is applied. (4)

Chosen finish: vinyl decals.
Draw the graphic in CAD software and send it to a CNC vinyl cutter, which cuts through the vinyl but not its backing sheet (1). Weed away the unwanted vinyl with a craft knife or tweezers, leaving the design on the backing (1). Cover the design with application tape and peel it off the backing, so the whole decal lifts in position (1). Clean and dry the product surface, position the decal, and press it down from the centre outwards with a squeegee to remove bubbles, then peel off the tape (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: paint coverage
A fence has a total area of 18 m². It needs two coats of wood stain. One litre covers 10 m², and the stain is sold in 2.5 litre tins at £21.00 each. Calculate the cost of the stain. (4)

Area to cover $= 18 \times 2 = 36$ m² (1)
Litres needed $= 36 \div 10 = 3.6$ L (1)
Tins $= 3.6 \div 2.5 = 1.44$, so **2 tins** (round up) (1)
Cost $= 2 \times £21.00 = £42.00$ (1)
:::

::: example Explain: extended
Explain how to prepare and apply a finish to a product in a material category of your choice, and why it is used. (6)

Category: metals. Product: a steel bike frame, powder coated. Steel rusts when exposed to rain and air, and a bike frame is constantly chipped and scratched, so it needs a tough, protective, attractive finish. First the frame is thoroughly degreased to remove oil from manufacturing. Any rust or scale is removed by shot blasting or pickling, which also gives a slight key, and the frame is dried. Areas that must stay bare, such as threads, are masked. The frame is earthed and sprayed with electrostatically charged polymer powder. The charge makes the powder cling evenly, even inside corners, and overspray is collected and reused. The frame is then baked in an oven, where the powder melts, flows and cures into a hard, even coat. This gives a thick barrier against water and oxygen, so the frame doesn't rust, and the coating resists chips better than wet paint. It also gives the bike its bright colour and gloss, which matter to buyers. Operators wear masks and use extraction to avoid breathing powder. (Level 3: 5–6 marks)
:::

::: q
Give two reasons for applying a finish to a product. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): protect from corrosion or rust; protect from moisture, rot or insects; resist wear or stains; improve appearance (colour, gloss); add texture or branding; hygiene; reduce friction.
:::

::: q
State the two conditions needed for iron or steel to rust. (2)
---
Water (moisture) (1); oxygen (air) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why fence posts are tanalised. (2)
---
Preservative is forced deep into the timber under pressure (1), protecting the part in the ground from rot, fungi and insects, so the post lasts much longer outdoors (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a manufacturer might apply spot UV varnish to a printed box. (2)
---
It gives selected areas, e.g. the logo, a high-gloss finish that contrasts with the matt surface (1), making the branding stand out on the shelf, while also resisting scuffs (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a PCB used in an outdoor weather station is lacquered. (2)
---
A thin lacquer coating seals the copper tracks and joints (1) from moisture, dust and oxidation, preventing corrosion and short circuits so the circuit keeps working (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how to polish the cut edge of an acrylic sheet. (3)
---
Draw file the edge flat and remove saw marks (1); rub with wet-and-dry paper used wet, working from coarse to fine grades (1); buff with polishing compound on a cloth or buffing wheel until the edge is clear and glossy (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Galvanising is a zinc coating, not a paint.** Name the zinc and explain **sacrificial protection**.
- **Always include preparation**: clean, degrease, smooth, de-dust. Many answers jump straight to "paint it".
- Dip coating: the metal is **heated first**, and the powder melts onto it. Powder coating: the powder is **electrostatically sprayed**, then **baked**.
- **Tanalising** is an industrial vacuum-pressure treatment, not brushing on preservative.
- Give **both** function and aesthetics when asked "why", and link them to the product's use (outdoors, food, handling).
- Coverage: multiply by the number of **coats**, and **round up** to whole tins.
:::

---

::: test
Explain why a stain-protection finish is applied to a sofa fabric. (2)
---
It coats the fibres so liquids and oily spills bead on the surface instead of soaking in (1), so they can be wiped off before they stain, keeping the sofa looking new for longer (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why surfaces must be prepared before a finish is applied. (2)
---
Dirt, grease, dust or rust stop the finish bonding, so it flakes, peels or blisters (1); any scratches or defects show through the finish, so a smooth, clean surface gives a better-looking, longer-lasting result (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between dip coating and powder coating a metal product. (3)
---
In dip coating the metal is heated and dipped into a fluidised bed of polymer powder, which melts onto the hot surface (1). In powder coating, charged powder is sprayed electrostatically onto the cold, earthed metal (1), which is then baked so the powder melts and cures; dip coating gives a thicker, softer coat, and powder coating a thinner, harder one (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why steel crash barriers at the side of roads are galvanised rather than painted. (3)
---
Paint is only a barrier, so once it is scratched in an impact, water and oxygen reach the steel and it rusts (1). Galvanising coats the steel in zinc, which corrodes in preference to the iron (1), so even scratched or damaged areas are protected and the barriers need little maintenance over many years (1).
:::

::: test
A steel storage unit has a surface area of 6.4 m². It needs three coats of paint. One litre covers 12 m². Paint is sold in 0.75 litre tins. Calculate how many tins are needed. (3)
---
$6.4 \times 3 = 19.2$ m² (1); $19.2 \div 12 = 1.6$ L (1); $1.6 \div 0.75 = 2.13$, so 3 tins (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of painting a pine toy box rather than varnishing it. (4)
---
Advantage: paint gives bright, opaque colour that appeals to children and hides knots and blemishes (1), so a cheaper timber or MDF can be used (1). Disadvantage: the natural grain is hidden (1), and chips show the bare wood clearly, needing touching up; the paint must also be toy-safe and non-toxic (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how a fabric is dyed so that the colour is colourfast. (3)
---
The fabric is washed to remove any finishes or dirt so the dye can penetrate (1); it is immersed in a dye bath at the correct temperature and time so the fibres absorb the dye evenly (1); the dye is fixed with a mordant, heat or oxidation, then the fabric is rinsed until the water runs clear (1).
:::

::: test
Choose one of the surface treatments or finishes: UV varnishing · varnishing · galvanising · vinyl decals · printing (textiles) · lubrication. Using notes and/or sketches, describe how your chosen finish is prepared and applied. (6)
---
Up to 6 marks for correct stages in a logical order (1 each), including preparation, application, curing or drying, and a relevant quality or safety point. E.g. UV varnishing: printed sheet dry and clean; varnish applied by roller or screen, all over or spot; passed under UV lamps; cured instantly; inspected for coverage. Varnishing: fill, sand through the grades with the grain, de-dust, thin sealing coat, denib, further coats, ventilation. Galvanising: degrease, rinse, pickle in acid, flux, dip in molten zinc, withdraw and cool, inspect coating. Vinyl decals: CAD design, vinyl cutter, weed, application tape, clean surface, position and squeegee, peel tape. Textile printing: prepare the screen or design, fix the fabric flat, ink through the mesh with a squeegee (one colour per screen), dry and heat set. Lubrication: clean away old grease and dirt, select oil or grease, apply to the contact surfaces, work the mechanism to spread it, wipe off excess.
:::

::: test
Explain why a bicycle chain is lubricated rather than painted. (2)
---
The links move against each other constantly, so paint would crack and wear off and would increase friction (1); oil reduces friction and wear between the moving parts while also keeping out water, so the chain runs smoothly and does not rust (1).
:::
