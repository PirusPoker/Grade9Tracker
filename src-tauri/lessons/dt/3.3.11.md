## What it is

This topic is about **doing** the making: selecting the right **specialist techniques and processes** for a material and task, using them to the required accuracy, and working safely to shape, fabricate and construct a high-quality prototype. The spec names four families of process: **wastage**, **addition**, **deforming** and **reforming**. It also covers **surface treatments and finishes**: why they are applied (function and aesthetics), how to **prepare** the surface, and how to **apply** the finish. Exam questions typically ask you to describe how to join two parts (4 marks), explain why a surface is prepared (2 marks), or describe how to prepare and apply a finish safely (6 marks, levelled).

**The four families of process**

| Family | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Wastage** | removing material to create a shape | sawing, drilling, filing, planing, chiselling, turning, milling, laser cutting, routing, cutting fabric |
| **Addition** | joining or adding material | gluing, screwing, nailing, riveting, soldering, brazing, welding, sewing, knock-down fittings, 3D printing, laminating |
| **Deforming** | changing shape without adding or removing material, usually by force and/or heat | bending acrylic on a strip heater, vacuum forming, steam bending or laminating timber, bending and folding metal, pleating and heat-setting fabric, scoring and folding card |
| **Reforming** | changing the state of a material (melting, liquid) and forming it again in a mould | casting pewter or aluminium, injection moulding, blow moulding, rotational moulding, pulp moulding |

**Selecting a technique**

Choose by: the **material** and its properties (thermoplastics soften with heat; metals can be cast; timber can be jointed), the **shape** needed, the **accuracy** and **finish** required, the **number** of parts (a jig or mould is worth making for a batch), the **equipment** available, **time**, **cost** and **safety**.

**Techniques by material area (a selection to describe step by step)**

| Material | Technique | Key steps and accuracy points |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | cutting and creasing a net | mark out with a sharp pencil; cut on the outside of the line with a craft knife and safety rule on a cutting mat; score fold lines with a blunt tool so folds are crisp; glue tabs with PVA or double-sided tape |
| **Timbers** | cutting a comb (finger) or dowel joint; gluing up | mark with knife and gauge from the face side; cut on the waste side; pare to the line; dry-fit; glue with PVA and clamp with sash cramps, checking for square by measuring the diagonals; wipe off excess glue |
| **Metals** | riveting, brazing, casting | riveting: drill matching holes, insert pop rivet, pull with riveter; brazing: clean with emery cloth, apply flux, heat on the brazing hearth, feed in brass spelter; pewter casting: make a mould (MDF or silicone), melt pewter in a ladle, pour steadily, cool, remove and file sprue |
| **Polymers** | line bending acrylic; vacuum forming | line bending: heat along the bend line on a strip heater, turning the sheet to heat both sides, bend over a former or jig to the correct angle, hold until cool; vacuum forming: tapered mould with no undercuts, clamp sheet, heat until it sags, raise the platen, switch on the vacuum, cool, release and trim |
| **Textiles** | plain seam; applying a zip; appliqué | pin and tack; sew on the seam line using the machine's guide for an even 15 mm allowance; press seams open; neaten edges by overlocking or zigzag |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | soldering components to a PCB | clean the board; insert component and bend legs; heat the pad and leg together with the iron, then feed in solder to form a small cone-shaped joint; trim legs; test with a multimeter |

**Working safely**: every process has specific hazards, such as hot metal and flux fumes (brazing, casting), hot polymer sheet (vacuum forming, strip heater), sharp blades (knives, saws), rotating parts (drills, lathes), and adhesive and solvent fumes. The control measures are the same kinds as for tools: PPE (goggles, apron, heat-resistant gloves and a visor for casting), guards, securing the work, extraction and ventilation, training and risk assessment.

**Surface treatments and finishes**

A **finish** is a surface treatment applied at the end of making. Reasons:

- **Functional**: protect against **corrosion** and oxidation, moisture, rot, insect attack, UV fading, stains and wear; make a surface hygienic, easy to clean, grippy or slippery; insulate electrically; reduce friction (lubrication).
- **Aesthetic**: add colour, pattern, texture or shine; enhance natural grain; give a brand image or information (printing, decals).

**Corrosion and oxidation (science link).** **Oxidation** is a reaction between a material and oxygen. When **iron or steel** reacts with **oxygen and water**, it forms **rust** (hydrated iron oxide), which flakes off and exposes fresh metal, so corrosion continues and weakens the part. Some metals, such as **aluminium**, **copper** and **zinc**, form a thin oxide layer that sticks to the surface and protects the metal beneath. Finishes prevent corrosion by forming a **barrier** that keeps water and oxygen away (paint, powder coating, dip coating, oil, lacquer). **Galvanising** coats steel in **zinc**, which is both a barrier and **sacrificial protection**: zinc is more reactive than iron, so if the coating is scratched the zinc corrodes instead of the steel.

**Finishes by material**

| Material | Preparation | Finishes |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | clean, dust-free surface | printing, **embossing** (raised pattern), **UV varnishing** (gloss or spot varnish), lamination |
| **Timbers** | plane, then sand with progressively finer glass paper along the grain; remove dust with a tack cloth | **painting** (primer, undercoat, top coat), **varnishing**, oil or wax, stain; **tanalising** (pressure-treating with preservative, done industrially to protect outdoor timber from rot and insects) |
| **Metals** | remove burrs with a file; clean with emery cloth or wire wool; **degrease** with a solvent such as white spirit or methylated spirit | **dip coating** (heat the metal, dip in fluidised polymer powder), **powder coating** (electrostatically sprayed powder, then oven-cured), **galvanising** (dip in molten zinc), painting, lacquer, polishing |
| **Polymers** | often need little: remove scratches with wet-and-dry paper, then polish | **polishing** (buffing wheel or polish), **printing**, **vinyl decals**, self-finishing from the mould |
| **Textiles** | wash to remove size and dirt; press | **printing** (screen, block, sublimation), **dyeing**, **stain protection** coatings, water-repellent finishes, flame retardant |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | clean the PCB, remove flux residue | **PCB lacquering** (protects tracks from moisture and corrosion), **lubrication** of moving parts (oil or grease to reduce friction and wear, and resist corrosion) |

**Why prepare the surface?** A finish only adheres properly to a **clean, dry, smooth, grease-free** surface. Dust, grease, oxide or rust stop the finish sticking, so it peels or flakes, and corrosion can continue underneath. Scratches and tool marks show through (especially under gloss finishes), and raised grain gives a rough result. Good preparation is the difference between a high-quality and a poor finish.

**How to apply a finish: a model sequence (painting a mild steel bracket)**

1. File off burrs and sharp edges.
2. Remove rust and mill scale with emery cloth or wire wool.
3. Degrease with white spirit on a cloth and let it dry; don't touch it with bare hands afterwards.
4. Apply a metal primer in a well-ventilated area or spray booth, wearing a mask and gloves as the COSHH sheet advises.
5. When dry, apply thin, even top coats with a brush or spray, allowing drying time and lightly rubbing down between coats.
6. Inspect for runs, missed areas and drips.

**Coverage calculations (maths link).** Paint coverage is given in m² per litre. To find the paint needed: total area to cover × number of coats ÷ coverage. For example, a 1.2 m × 0.8 m panel painted on both sides with 2 coats needs $1.2 \times 0.8 \times 2 \times 2 = 3.84$ m² of coverage; at 12 m² per litre that is $3.84 \div 12 = 0.32$ litres.

::: terms
- **Wastage**: shaping a material by removing parts of it, such as sawing, drilling or filing.
- **Addition**: joining or adding material, such as gluing, soldering, welding, riveting or sewing.
- **Deforming**: changing the shape of a material by force or heat without adding or removing material, such as bending.
- **Reforming**: changing a material's state, usually by melting, and forming it in a mould, such as casting.
- **Finish**: a surface treatment applied to protect a material and/or improve its appearance.
- **Oxidation**: a chemical reaction between a material and oxygen, which on iron and steel forms rust with water present.
- **Corrosion**: the gradual destruction of a metal by chemical reaction with its surroundings, such as rusting.
- **Galvanising**: coating steel with zinc, which acts as a barrier and sacrificially protects the steel.
- **Powder coating**: spraying electrostatically charged polymer powder onto metal, then curing it in an oven.
- **Tanalising**: pressure-treating timber with preservative to protect it from rot and insects outdoors.
- **Degreasing**: removing oil and grease from a surface with a solvent so a finish will adhere.
- **Flux**: a paste used in soldering and brazing that stops oxidation and helps the joint flow.
- **PCB lacquer**: a clear coating that protects a circuit board's tracks from moisture and corrosion.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the process and its family** (wastage, addition, deforming or reforming).
2. **Give the steps in order**, with the specific tools, and include how accuracy is achieved (marking out, jigs, checking).
3. **Add a specific safety point** at each hazardous stage.
4. **For finishes, always start with preparation**: clean, remove burrs or rust, sand or degrease, and say why.
5. **Then the application** (method, number of coats, drying) and the **reason**: function (protect against named hazard) and/or aesthetics.
6. **For corrosion**, name water and oxygen, and say how the finish stops them reaching the metal.
:::

::: example Joining two parts
Describe how to join two pieces of 3 mm mild steel strip using pop rivets. (4)

Mark the hole positions on both strips from a datum and centre punch them (1). Clamp the strips together and drill matching holes of the rivet's diameter on a pillar drill, with the work held in a machine vice and wearing goggles (1). De-burr the holes with a file or countersink bit so the parts sit flat (1). Push the rivet through both holes from one side and use the pop riveter to pull the mandrel until it snaps, spreading the rivet to lock the strips together (1).
:::

::: example Preparing a surface
Explain why a surface should be prepared before a finish is applied. (2)

Dirt, grease, rust or dust stop the finish from bonding to the material (1), so it would peel or flake and leave the material unprotected, while scratches and marks would show through the finish and spoil its appearance (1).
:::

::: example Galvanising
Explain why steel garden furniture is often galvanised. (3)

Garden furniture is left outside, exposed to rain and air, so untreated steel would rust (1). Galvanising coats the steel in zinc, which forms a barrier that keeps water and oxygen away from the steel (1). Zinc is more reactive than iron, so if the coating is scratched the zinc corrodes first and sacrificially protects the steel underneath (1).
:::

::: example Coverage
A timber planter has four sides, each 0.6 m × 0.4 m, to be varnished on the outside only with three coats. The varnish covers 15 m² per litre. Calculate the volume of varnish needed in millilitres. (4)

Area of one side $= 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.24$ m² (1). Four sides $= 0.96$ m², three coats $= 2.88$ m² (1). $2.88 \div 15 = 0.192$ litres (1) $= 192$ ml (1).
:::

::: example Finish, prepare and apply
Choose one material area from timbers, metals or polymers. Describe how to prepare and apply a suitable finish, and explain why it is used. (6)

Timbers, varnishing a pine bookshelf: plane the surfaces and remove pencil marks, then sand along the grain with progressively finer glass paper, 120 then 240 grit, wrapped round a cork block so the surface stays flat (2). Remove all dust with a brush and tack cloth, because dust trapped in the varnish would leave a rough finish; work in a ventilated area and wear gloves as the varnish label advises (1). Apply a thin first coat with a clean brush, working along the grain, and let it dry; lightly rub down with fine abrasive to remove raised grain and dust nibs, then apply two more thin coats (2). Varnish seals the timber against moisture and dirt, making it hard-wearing and easy to wipe clean, while enhancing the natural grain and colour of the pine (1).
:::

State the two conditions needed for iron to rust. (2)
---

::: q
Give two reasons for applying a finish. (2)
---
Any two: protect from corrosion, moisture, rot, UV, stains or wear; improve appearance with colour, texture or shine; make hygienic or easy to clean; provide information or branding.
:::

::: q
Explain why a PCB is lacquered after the components have been soldered. (2)
---
The lacquer forms a sealed coating over the copper tracks and joints (1), protecting them from moisture and oxidation that would corrode them and cause the circuit to fail (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how to bend a piece of 3 mm acrylic to 90° using a strip heater. (4)
---
Mark the bend line with a chinagraph pencil (1). Heat along the line on the strip heater, turning the sheet to heat both sides evenly until it softens (1). Bend it over a former or jig set at 90°, wearing heat-resistant gloves (1). Hold it in position until it cools and sets, then check the angle with a square (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why aluminium does not need a protective finish as often as mild steel. (2)
---
Aluminium forms a thin oxide layer that sticks to the surface (1) and seals the metal underneath from further oxidation, whereas rust on steel flakes off and exposes fresh metal (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Preparation first.** A finish answer that skips cleaning, sanding or degreasing loses the first marks.
- Galvanising is **zinc**, not paint: give **barrier + sacrificial protection**.
- Rust needs **both water and oxygen**; saying only "water" is half an answer.
- Name the **family** correctly: bending is deforming, casting is reforming.
- Describe processes **in order**, with named tools and a **specific** safety point.
- Coverage: multiply area by **coats and sides** before dividing by coverage, and convert litres to ml if asked.
:::

::: test
Describe how to join two pieces of pine using a dowel joint. (4)
---
Mark matching hole centres on both pieces from the face side, using a dowel jig or centre points for alignment (1). Drill holes of the dowel diameter to the correct depth using a depth stop (1). Apply PVA to the holes and dowels and assemble (1). Clamp with sash cramps, check for square by measuring diagonals and wipe off excess glue (1).

::: test
Describe how to solder a resistor onto a PCB. (4)
---
Insert the resistor legs through the holes and bend them slightly to hold it (1). Clean the soldering iron tip; heat the pad and leg together (1). Feed solder onto the joint, not the iron, to make a small shiny cone, then remove solder then iron (1). Trim the legs with side cutters; iron in its stand, fume extraction and goggles for safety (1).
:::

::: test
A steel gate panel measures 1.5 m × 1.0 m. Both sides are painted with two coats. The paint covers 10 m² per litre and costs £14.50 per litre. Calculate the cost of paint needed, to the nearest penny. (4)
---
Area of one side $= 1.5$ m² (1). Both sides, two coats $= 1.5 \times 2 \times 2 = 6$ m² (1). Paint $= 6 \div 10 = 0.6$ litres (1). Cost $= 0.6 \times 14.50 = £8.70$ (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between deforming and reforming, using an example of each. (4)
---
Deforming changes the shape of a material without changing its state, usually by force or heat (1), e.g. bending acrylic on a strip heater (1). Reforming changes the material's state, usually melting it, and forming it in a mould (1), e.g. casting pewter or injection moulding (1).
:::

::: test
Discuss how a manufacturer could protect a steel product used outdoors from corrosion, comparing at least two finishes. (6)
