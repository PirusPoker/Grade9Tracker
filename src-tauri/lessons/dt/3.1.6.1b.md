## What it is

This lesson covers the other three material groups in 3.1.6.1: **metals and alloys**, **polymers**, and **textiles**. For each you need the categories (ferrous or non-ferrous; thermoforming or thermosetting; natural, synthetic, blended; woven, non-woven, knitted), the named examples, a property or two and a typical use. The exam loves 1-mark classification questions (which is ferrous, which is thermoforming, which fibre comes from an animal), "explain why this material is used for this product", and comparisons of polymer types, including what happens at the end of a product's life.

### Metals: the science

Metals are **elements** with **metallic bonding**: positive metal ions sit in a regular lattice surrounded by a "sea" of free (delocalised) electrons. That structure explains their typical properties: they **conduct electricity and heat** (free electrons carry charge and energy), are **malleable and ductile** (layers of ions can slide without the bonds breaking), are **shiny** when freshly cut, and are usually **dense** with **high melting points**. **Non-metals**, and the materials made from them such as polymers, timber and textiles, are usually **insulators**, less dense and not malleable.

**Metal ores** are finite resources, mined and then refined, which uses a lot of energy. Metals can be recycled again and again without losing their properties.

### Ferrous, non-ferrous and alloys

- **Ferrous metals** contain **iron**. Most rust unless protected, and most are magnetic.
- **Non-ferrous metals** contain **no iron**. They do not rust (although some tarnish or oxidise) and are usually not magnetic.
- An **alloy** is a **mixture of a metal with one or more other elements** (usually metals, sometimes carbon), made to improve its properties: harder, stronger, more corrosion resistant, or with a lower melting point. The different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers, so they cannot slide as easily, which makes the alloy harder and stronger than the pure metal.

| Metal | Group | Composition / properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low carbon steel** (mild steel) | ferrous | iron with a small amount of carbon (up to about 0.3%); tough, ductile, malleable, easily welded, cheap; rusts easily | car bodies, nuts and bolts, girders, gates, appliance panels |
| **Cast iron** | ferrous | iron with a high carbon content (roughly 2–4%); hard and strong in compression but brittle; runs well into moulds | engine blocks, vice bodies, manhole covers, cast cookware, machine bases |
| **High carbon / tool steel** | ferrous | iron with more carbon than mild steel (roughly 0.8–1.4%); can be hardened and tempered to be very hard; less ductile | chisels, screwdrivers, hammer heads, saw blades, springs |
| **Aluminium** | non-ferrous | light, good strength-to-weight ratio, corrosion resistant (forms a protective oxide layer), good conductor, malleable | drinks cans, foil, window frames, bike frames, aircraft parts (as alloys), saucepans |
| **Copper** | non-ferrous | excellent conductor of electricity and heat, ductile, malleable, corrosion resistant | electrical wiring, water pipes, PCB tracks, pan bases |
| **Tin** | non-ferrous | soft, low melting point, corrosion resistant | coating steel to make tinplate for food cans; solder |
| **Zinc** | non-ferrous | corrosion resistant, low melting point, casts well | galvanising (coating) steel, die-cast parts, batteries, roofing |
| **Brass** | alloy (non-ferrous) | **copper + zinc**; gold colour, corrosion resistant, casts and machines well, conducts electricity | door and window fittings, plumbing fittings, musical instruments, plug pins |
| **Stainless steel** | alloy (ferrous) | **steel + chromium (and usually nickel)**; hard, tough, highly corrosion resistant, hygienic | cutlery, sinks, kitchen and catering equipment, surgical instruments |
| **High speed steel** | alloy (ferrous) | **steel + tungsten, chromium and vanadium**; stays hard even when hot from friction | drill bits, lathe cutting tools, milling cutters |

### Polymers

Polymers are made of **very long chain molecules** built from repeating units (monomers). Most are made from **crude oil**, a finite resource; some bio-based polymers are made from plants. They are usually light, good electrical and thermal insulators, water resistant and easily moulded.

**Thermoforming polymers** (thermoplastics) have long chains held together by weak forces between them, with **no cross-links**. When heated they **soften and can be reshaped**, then harden when cooled, and this can be **repeated many times**, so they can be recycled. Processes: vacuum forming, injection moulding, blow moulding, line bending.

**Thermosetting polymers** undergo a chemical change as they set, forming **strong cross-links** between the chains. Once set they **cannot be softened or reshaped by heat**: they char or burn instead. They are hard, rigid and heat resistant, but difficult to recycle (they can only be ground up for use as fillers).

| Thermoforming | Properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Acrylic (PMMA)** | hard, rigid, glossy; transparent or coloured; weather resistant; scratches, and can crack or shatter (though less dangerously than glass) | display stands, signs, light diffusers, car rear light lenses, laser-cut products |
| **High impact polystyrene (HIPS)** | light, rigid, impact resistant, cheap, easy to vacuum form | packaging trays and blister packs, toys, product casings, models |
| **High density polythene (HDPE)** | tough, stiff, chemical resistant, food safe, light | milk and detergent bottles, bins, pipes, chopping boards |
| **Polypropylene (PP)** | tough, light, flexible, food safe, excellent fatigue resistance (can form living hinges) | food containers, flip-top bottle lids, garden chairs, medical equipment |
| **Polyvinyl chloride (PVC)** | rigid (uPVC) or plasticised and flexible; weather and chemical resistant; electrical insulator | window frames, drainpipes, cable insulation, flooring, rainwear |
| **Polyethylene terephthalate (PET)** | tough, clear, light, good barrier to gas and moisture, widely recycled | drinks bottles, food packaging, fibres for polyester fleece |

| Thermosetting | Properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Epoxy resin (ER)** | strong adhesive; hard; good electrical insulator; bonds to many materials | two-part adhesives, the matrix in CRP, PCB boards, protective coatings |
| **Melamine-formaldehyde (MF)** | hard, scratch and heat resistant, food safe, can be brightly coloured | laminates on worktops, picnic tableware |
| **Phenol formaldehyde (PF)** | heat resistant, good electrical insulator, dark in colour, brittle | saucepan handles, electrical fittings |
| **Polyester resin (PR)** | rigid, weather resistant; sets when a hardener is added | with glass fibre to make GRP (boat hulls, car panels), casting and encapsulation |
| **Urea-formaldehyde (UF)** | hard, rigid, good electrical insulator, heat resistant, usually white | plugs, sockets and switches; the adhesive in MDF and chipboard |

### Textiles

**Fibres** are spun into **yarns**, and yarns are made into **fabrics**.

| Fibre | Source | Key properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cotton** | natural: plant (the seed pods of the cotton plant) | soft, absorbent, cool, breathable, strong (stronger when wet); creases, can shrink, slow to dry | jeans, T-shirts, towels, bedding |
| **Wool** | natural: animal (sheep's fleece) | warm (traps air), resilient and elastic, absorbent yet water-repellent on the surface, naturally flame resistant; can shrink and felt when washed hot | jumpers, coats, suits, carpets, blankets |
| **Silk** | natural: animal (silkworm cocoons) | smooth, lustrous, soft, drapes well, strong for its fineness; expensive; weakened by sunlight | luxury garments, ties, scarves, bedding |
| **Polyester** | synthetic (from oil) | strong, durable, crease resistant, quick drying, low absorbency; can be heat-set | sportswear, fleece, bedding, fillings, blended with cotton |
| **Polyamide (nylon)** | synthetic (from oil) | very strong, tough, abrasion resistant, light, elastic, low absorbency | tights, sportswear, rucksacks, ropes, carpets |
| **Elastane (Lycra)** | synthetic (from oil) | extremely stretchy with excellent recovery; usually blended in small amounts | swimwear, sportswear, stretch jeans, underwear |

**Blended and mixed fibres.** A **blend** mixes two or more fibres before spinning into one yarn; a **mixed** fabric uses yarns of different fibres in the weave or knit. Blending combines the best of each fibre. **Cotton/polyester** (polycotton) keeps cotton's comfort and absorbency, while polyester adds strength, crease resistance, quicker drying and lower cost: ideal for school shirts, uniforms and bed sheets.

**Fabric construction**

| Construction | How it is made | Properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Woven: plain weave** | two sets of yarn interlaced at right angles; the **weft** passes over one, under one **warp** yarn | strong, firm, hard-wearing, same on both sides; frays at cut edges | shirts, sheets, calico, canvas bags |
| **Non-woven: bonded** | fibres (not yarns) held together by adhesive, heat or stitching | cheap, does not fray, not very strong, little drape | interfacing, disposable cloths, medical masks, tea bags |
| **Non-woven: felted** | wool fibres matted together using heat, moisture, pressure and agitation | does not fray, warm, can be moulded; weak and not elastic | hats, craft, snooker table covers, insulation |
| **Knitted** | loops of yarn interlocked, either across (weft knit) or down (warp knit) the fabric | stretchy, comfortable, warm (traps air), crease resistant; weft knits can ladder or unravel | T-shirts, jumpers, socks, sportswear |

### Maths link: alloy composition as a ratio

A brass is made from copper and zinc in the ratio 3 : 2. To make a 600 g casting: total parts $= 3 + 2 = 5$; one part $= 600 \div 5 = 120$ g; copper $= 3 \times 120 = 360$ g; zinc $= 2 \times 120 = 240$ g.

A common grade of stainless steel contains 18% chromium and 8% nickel. In a 500 g pan: chromium $= 0.18 \times 500 = 90$ g; nickel $= 0.08 \times 500 = 40$ g; the rest, $500 - 130 = 370$ g, is mostly iron.

::: terms
- **Ferrous metal**: a metal that contains iron, such as mild steel or cast iron; most rust and are magnetic.
- **Non-ferrous metal**: a metal that contains no iron, such as aluminium, copper, tin or zinc; it does not rust.
- **Alloy**: a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, made to improve its properties.
- **Thermoforming polymer**: a polymer without cross-links that softens when heated and can be reshaped and recycled many times.
- **Thermosetting polymer**: a polymer with strong cross-links formed as it sets, so it cannot be softened or reshaped by heat.
- **Cross-link**: a strong chemical bond joining neighbouring polymer chains, found in thermosetting polymers.
- **Natural fibre**: a fibre from a plant or animal source, such as cotton, wool or silk.
- **Synthetic fibre**: a human-made fibre made from chemicals, mostly from oil, such as polyester, polyamide or elastane.
- **Blended fibre**: a yarn made by spinning two or more different fibres together to combine their properties.
- **Plain weave**: the simplest weave, with each weft yarn passing over one warp yarn and under the next.
- **Non-woven fabric**: a fabric made directly from fibres by bonding or felting rather than from yarns.
- **Knitted fabric**: a fabric made from interlocking loops of yarn, making it stretchy and comfortable.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify precisely**: ferrous, non-ferrous or alloy; thermoforming or thermosetting; natural (plant or animal) or synthetic; woven, non-woven or knitted.
2. **Name the specific material**: "HDPE", "stainless steel", "polyamide", not "plastic", "metal" or "fabric".
3. **Link property to function**: property, then "so" or "which means", then the benefit to the product or user.
4. **For alloy questions**, name the metals in the alloy and the property that is improved.
5. **For polymer questions about heat, moulding or recycling**, explain using cross-links.
6. **For "choose one material" questions**, pick the one you know best and give property, product and reason separately.
:::

::: example Explain: alloy over pure metal
Explain why stainless steel is used for cutlery rather than plain steel. (3)

Stainless steel is an alloy of steel with chromium (and nickel) (1). The chromium makes it highly resistant to corrosion, so it does not rust when repeatedly washed and in contact with food and water (1). It stays hygienic, attractive and hard-wearing, whereas plain steel would rust and stain (1).
:::

::: example Explain: thermoset for a plug
Explain why urea-formaldehyde is used for electrical plugs. (2)

It is a good electrical insulator, so the user is protected from electric shock (1); as a thermoset it does not soften if the plug gets hot, so it keeps its shape and holds the pins securely (1).
:::

::: example Choose one: property, product, reason
Choose one material from: aluminium, HIPS, cotton. Name one property of it, give one product that uses it, and describe why it is used in that product. (4)

Aluminium (chosen). Property: lightweight / corrosion resistant (1). Product: drinks can (1). Why: it is light, which keeps transport costs down (1), and it resists corrosion, so it does not react with the drink; it is also easily recycled (1).
(HIPS: rigid and easy to vacuum form, e.g. a packaging tray. Cotton: absorbent and soft, e.g. a towel.)
:::

::: example Calculate: alloy ratio
A casting alloy is made from copper and zinc in the ratio 7 : 3. Calculate the mass of each metal needed to make a 2.5 kg casting. (3)

Total parts $= 7 + 3 = 10$; one part $= 2.5 \div 10 = 0.25$ kg (1). Copper $= 7 \times 0.25 = 1.75$ kg (1). Zinc $= 3 \times 0.25 = 0.75$ kg (1).
:::

::: example Extended: thermoforming vs thermosetting
Compare thermoforming and thermosetting polymers for use in products and for disposal at the end of their life. (6)

Thermoforming polymers have no cross-links, so they soften when heated and can be vacuum formed, injection moulded or bent into shape (1), making them ideal for products such as HDPE bottles and PP food containers (1). Thermosetting polymers form cross-links as they set, so once moulded they stay rigid and do not soften with heat (1); this makes them suitable for hot or electrical products such as PF saucepan handles and UF plug casings (1). At the end of life, thermoforming polymers can be melted and remoulded into new products, so they are easier to recycle (1), whereas thermosets cannot be remelted and usually end up in landfill, incinerated or ground up as filler, which is worse for the environment (1).
Level 3 (5–6): a detailed, balanced comparison of structure, properties, products and disposal. Level 2 (3–4): some valid comparisons, partly explained. Level 1 (1–2): simple statements about one type.
:::

::: q
Name two non-ferrous metals. (2)
---
Any two: aluminium; copper; tin; zinc (also lead, silver, gold, titanium).
:::

::: q
Explain why copper is used for household electrical wiring. (2)
---
It is an excellent conductor of electricity, so little energy is lost (1); it is ductile, so it can be drawn into thin wire that bends easily around a building (1).
:::

::: q
State what brass is made from and give one use. (2)
---
Copper and zinc (1). Use: door handles, hinges, plumbing fittings, plug pins, musical instruments (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why polypropylene is used for flip-top lids on sauce bottles. (2)
---
It has excellent fatigue resistance, so a thin living hinge can be opened and closed thousands of times without breaking (1); it is food safe, so it does not contaminate the sauce (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the difference between a woven and a knitted fabric. (2)
---
Woven: two sets of yarns (warp and weft) interlaced at right angles, giving a firm fabric (1). Knitted: loops of yarn interlocked, giving a stretchy fabric (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why polycotton is used for school shirts. (3)
---
Cotton gives comfort, softness and absorbency, so the shirt is cool to wear (1). Polyester adds strength and crease resistance, so it lasts longer and needs little ironing (1). It dries quickly and is cheaper than pure cotton (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Ferrous means contains iron.** Aluminium, copper, tin and zinc are **non-ferrous**; brass is a **non-ferrous alloy**; stainless steel and high speed steel are **ferrous alloys**.
- **Thermoforming can be reheated and reshaped; thermosetting cannot**, because of **cross-links**. Get the reason right.
- **Silk and wool come from animals**; cotton from a plant; polyester, nylon and elastane are synthetic.
- Always name the **specific** material. "Plastic" or "metal" earns nothing.
- Felt and bonded fabrics are **non-woven** and are made from **fibres, not yarns**.
- An alloy is a **mixture**, not a compound or a coating: galvanised steel is coated, not alloyed.
:::

::: test
Explain why an alloy is used rather than a pure metal. Use an example in your answer. (3)
---
Alloying combines a metal with other elements to improve its properties (1), e.g. making it harder, stronger, more corrosion resistant or easier to cast (1). Example developed: steel alloyed with chromium makes stainless steel, which does not rust, so it is used for sinks and cutlery (1). ::: test
Explain why cast iron is used for the body of an engineer's vice. (3)
---
It is very strong in compression, so it withstands the large clamping forces (1); it is hard and rigid, so the vice does not distort (1); it flows well when molten, so the complex shape can be cast in one piece cheaply (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a thermosetting polymer is used for the handle of a saucepan. (2)
---
Thermosets have cross-links, so they do not soften or melt when heated by the hob (1); PF is also a poor conductor of heat, so the handle stays cool enough to hold safely (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why elastane is blended with other fibres in swimwear. (3)
---
Elastane is extremely stretchy and recovers its shape (1), so the swimsuit fits closely and moves with the body without restricting the swimmer (1); it is blended in small amounts with polyamide or polyester, which add strength, abrasion resistance and quick drying (1).
:::

::: test
Solder for electronics is made from tin and copper. One type contains 99.3% tin and 0.7% copper. Calculate the mass of each metal in a 250 g reel. (2)
---
Tin $= 0.993 \times 250 = 248.25$ g (1). Copper $= 0.007 \times 250 = 1.75$ g (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why aluminium is used for the frames of lightweight bicycles. (3)
---
Aluminium has a low density, so the frame is light and easier to ride and carry (1); it has a good strength-to-weight ratio (when alloyed), so the frame is strong enough to take the rider's weight (1); it is naturally corrosion resistant, so it does not rust when ridden in the rain (1).
:::
