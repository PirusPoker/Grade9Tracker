## What it is

Every product leaves a mark on the planet and on people. Its **ecological footprint** is the damage it does to the environment across its whole life: the land cleared, resources used, energy burned, pollution released and waste left behind. Its **social footprint** is its effect on people: the workers who make it, the communities near factories and dumps, and the users. Designers set most of both footprints at the drawing board, by choosing materials, processes, where things are made and how the product ends its life.

### Ecological issues: getting the raw materials

| Activity | What it supplies | Ecological damage | What a designer can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deforestation** | timber, paper and board; land cleared for farms | habitat and biodiversity lost; fewer trees to absorb CO₂; soil erosion and flooding; indigenous communities displaced | specify **FSC**-certified timber and paper; use **recycled** paper and board; use manufactured boards that use up waste wood |
| **Mining** | metal ores (iron ore, bauxite for aluminium, copper), and minerals for electronics | open-cast pits scar the landscape and destroy habitats; dust, noise and toxic run-off pollute water; spoil heaps; high energy use | use **recycled** metal; use less metal (thinner sections, smaller parts); design so metals can be separated and recovered |
| **Drilling** | crude oil and gas, the raw material for most **polymers** and synthetic fibres, and fuel | oil spills and leaks harm marine life; drilling disturbs habitats; burning and processing release CO₂ | use less polymer; choose **recyclable** thermoforming polymers; use recycled polymers or bio-based alternatives |
| **Farming** | natural fibres (cotton, wool, silk), and plant-based polymers | cotton needs large amounts of water and often pesticides and fertilisers, which pollute rivers; land cleared for crops and grazing; methane from livestock | specify **organic** or responsibly farmed fibres; use recycled or blended fibres; design for long life so less new fibre is needed |

### Mileage and carbon

A product's **mileage** is the total distance it and its materials travel: from the **raw material source**, to processing, to **manufacture**, through **distribution** to the shop or warehouse, to the **user's location**, and finally to **disposal** or recycling. Most of this is by lorry, ship or plane burning fossil fuel, so **more miles means more CO₂** and other air pollution. Designers and manufacturers cut mileage by:
- sourcing materials locally and manufacturing near the market;
- making products lighter and more compact, e.g. **flat-pack** furniture, so more fit in each lorry or container;
- choosing sea or rail freight rather than air;
- using local recycling or take-back schemes at the end of life.

**Carbon is also produced in manufacture.** Almost every process uses energy, and where that energy comes from fossil fuels it releases CO₂. Examples are smelting ore, cracking crude oil, pulping and drying paper, kiln-drying timber, and running moulding machines. A product's **carbon footprint** adds up the CO₂ from every stage. Designers reduce it by using **less material**, choosing **recycled** material (recycling aluminium takes a small fraction of the energy of extracting it from bauxite), choosing processes that use less energy, and making the product in factories powered by **renewable** energy.

### The six Rs

The six Rs are a checklist for cutting a product's footprint. Each one needs an **action** you can describe.

| R | Meaning | Design examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce** | use less material and energy, and fewer parts, and make less waste | thinner walls with ribs; less packaging; nesting parts to cut waste; low-energy components such as LEDs |
| **Refuse** | don't use or buy materials or products that are unsustainable or unnecessary | refusing single-use plastic packaging; refusing timber without certification; refusing a product you don't need |
| **Reuse** | use a product or part again, for the same or a new purpose, **without reprocessing** it | refillable bottles; reusable bags and cups; upcycling pallets into furniture; reusing components from old devices |
| **Repair** | fix a product rather than throw it away | screws instead of glue; replaceable batteries, zips and fuses; spare parts and repair guides; modular designs |
| **Recycle** | reprocess the **material** into a new product | single-material designs; polymer identification codes; aluminium cans; recycled PET into polyester fleece; recycled board |
| **Rethink** | question the whole approach and design it differently | products that do several jobs; hiring or sharing instead of owning; wind-up or solar power instead of batteries; designing for disassembly from the start |

### Social issues

- **Safe working conditions.** Workers in the supply chain need fair pay, reasonable hours, protective equipment, safe buildings, freedom from child labour, and protection from toxic chemicals such as dyes, solvents and solder fumes. Garment-factory disasters, such as the collapse of the Rana Plaza building in Bangladesh in 2013, which killed more than a thousand workers, showed the human cost of chasing the cheapest production. Designers and brands can choose audited suppliers and Fairtrade-certified materials.
- **Reducing oceanic and atmospheric pollution.** Polymer waste breaks down into **microplastics** that end up in the sea. So do the synthetic fibres shed when clothes are washed. Dye run-off pollutes rivers, and burning fuel and waste releases CO₂ and toxic gases. Designers can respond by avoiding single-use polymers, choosing biodegradable or recyclable materials, using water-based finishes, and making products that last.
- **Reducing the detrimental (negative) impact on others.** This covers noise and pollution near factories and mines, **e-waste** shipped abroad and burned or picked apart by hand, and **conflict minerals** whose profits fund violence. It also covers products that harm users, such as toxic paint on toys.

### Across the material categories

| Category | Main footprint issues | Ways to reduce them |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | deforestation; water and energy in pulping; chemicals in bleaching; packaging waste | FSC or recycled board; unbleached board; less packaging; easy-to-recycle single-material packs |
| **Timbers** | deforestation and illegal logging; transport of heavy logs; kiln-drying energy | FSC timber; local species; manufactured boards using waste; flat-pack to cut mileage; repairable joints |
| **Metals** | mining damage; very high smelting energy; toxic run-off | recycled metal; less metal; designs that separate easily for recycling |
| **Polymers** | drilling for oil; energy in cracking; ocean plastic and microplastics; many are not recycled | recyclable thermoforming polymers with codes; recycled content; avoid single use; bio-based polymers |
| **Textiles** | water, pesticides and fertiliser for cotton; dye pollution; microfibre shedding; fast-fashion waste; sweatshops | organic or Fairtrade cotton; recycled polyester; durable, repairable garments; take-back schemes |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | mining for rare metals; conflict minerals; toxic e-waste; energy used in use | replaceable batteries and modules (repair); lead-free solder; low-power components; take-back and recycling of old equipment |

### Calculations

Footprint questions can include **mileage totals** and **percentage reductions**. Percentage reduction = (old − new) ÷ old × 100. Always use the *original* value as the denominator.

::: terms
- **Ecological footprint**: the total environmental impact of a product across its life, from raw materials to disposal.
- **Social footprint**: the effect a product and its manufacture have on people: workers, communities and users.
- **Deforestation**: the permanent clearing of forests for timber, paper or farmland, destroying habitats and carbon stores.
- **Mining**: extracting ores and minerals from the ground, which damages landscapes and can pollute water and air.
- **Product mileage**: the total distance a product and its materials travel from source, through manufacture and use, to disposal.
- **Carbon footprint**: the total carbon dioxide released by a product's materials, manufacture, transport, use and disposal.
- **Six Rs**: reduce, refuse, reuse, repair, recycle and rethink: a checklist for lowering a product's footprint.
- **Reuse**: using a product or part again, for the same or a different purpose, without reprocessing its material.
- **Recycle**: reprocessing a product's material so it can be made into something new.
- **Rethink**: questioning the way a product works or is used, and redesigning it to be more sustainable.
- **Microplastics**: tiny polymer fragments and fibres that pollute oceans and enter food chains.
- **Conflict minerals**: minerals such as tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold mined where profits fund armed conflict.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the issue precisely**: deforestation, mining, drilling, farming, mileage, carbon in manufacture, working conditions or pollution.
2. **Link it to a material and a product**: "bauxite mining for aluminium drinks cans".
3. **Explain the impact**: what is damaged (habitat, water, air, climate, people), and why.
4. **Give a design response**: a specific R or action (recycled content, FSC, local sourcing, flat pack, repairable design).
5. For six Rs questions, **define the R and give a product example**. Never confuse reuse (same form) with recycle (reprocessed material).
6. For calculations, total the distances or values, then use (old − new) ÷ old × 100 for a percentage change.
7. For the **8-mark analyse and evaluate** question, cover ecological *and* social points, give **named product examples**, and weigh which actions make the biggest difference, and at what cost.
:::

::: example Explain: two Rs
Explain how two of the six Rs could be applied to the design of a kettle. (4)

Repair: the kettle could be assembled with standard screws rather than glued or clipped shut, and sold with a replaceable element and lid hinge (1), so a fault can be fixed cheaply instead of the whole kettle going to landfill (1). Reduce: an internal level mark and a small-quantity "one cup" setting stops users boiling more water than they need (1), reducing the electricity used every time it is boiled over its lifetime (1).
:::

::: example Explain: mileage
Explain how a furniture manufacturer could reduce the mileage of its products. (3)

Buy timber from local, FSC-certified forests rather than importing it (1). Design the furniture as flat pack, so many more units fit in each lorry and fewer journeys are needed to reach shops and customers (1). Make the furniture in factories close to the main markets, and offer a local take-back scheme so old furniture is recycled nearby rather than shipped away (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: mileage and percentage reduction
A toy's journey is: forest to sawmill 120 km, sawmill to factory 340 km, factory to warehouse 85 km, warehouse to shop 60 km. The company moves production to a factory only 90 km from the sawmill. Calculate the percentage reduction in total distance, to 1 decimal place. (4)

Original total $= 120 + 340 + 85 + 60 = 605$ km (1)
New total $= 120 + 90 + 85 + 60 = 355$ km (1)
Reduction $= 605 - 355 = 250$ km (1)
$\frac{250}{605} \times 100 = 41.3\%$ (1)
:::

::: example Explain: social footprint
Explain two ways a clothing brand can reduce the social footprint of its products. (4)

Use suppliers whose factories are independently audited for safe buildings, fair pay and reasonable hours (1), so the workers making the clothes are not put in danger or exploited (1). Choose Fairtrade or organic cotton and low-impact dyes (1), which gives farmers a fair price and cuts the pesticide and dye pollution that harms communities living near farms and dye works (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate: extended
Analyse and evaluate how designers can reduce the ecological footprint of products. Give examples in your answer. (8)

The biggest impacts often come from getting raw materials. Designers can specify recycled aluminium for drinks cans, which avoids bauxite mining and uses a small fraction of the energy of new metal, and FSC-certified or recycled board for packaging, which reduces deforestation. These choices are effective because they cut both habitat damage and manufacturing carbon, but recycled materials are not always available in the quality or colour needed. Reducing material also works: ribs in a polypropylene crate let the walls be thinner, saving oil-based polymer and energy, and lighter products burn less fuel in transport. Mileage can be cut by flat-pack designs, as with self-assembly bookcases, which fit many more units per lorry, although the customer then has to assemble them. Designing for repair, with screws instead of glue and replaceable batteries in phones and toothbrushes, keeps products in use longer, which is often the most effective step of all because a new product's footprint is never incurred. It can conflict with companies' wish to sell replacements. Overall, the choice of material and a long, repairable life have the biggest effect, because most of a typical product's footprint is set by its materials and by how soon it is replaced. Transport and packaging changes help but are smaller. (Level 4: 7–8 marks)
:::

::: q
Give two ecological issues linked to obtaining raw materials. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): deforestation; mining; drilling for oil; intensive farming (water, pesticides); habitat loss; water pollution.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between reuse and recycle. (2)
---
Reuse is using a product again in its current form, for the same or another purpose, e.g. a refillable bottle (1); recycling reprocesses the material into a new product, e.g. melting PET bottles into polyester fibre (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by "rethink" in the six Rs, with an example. (2)
---
Questioning how a product meets a need and redesigning it to be more sustainable (1), e.g. a wind-up radio instead of one using disposable batteries, or hiring tools instead of each person buying one (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why carbon dioxide is produced during the manufacture of a steel product. (2)
---
Extracting and processing steel needs large amounts of heat and energy, e.g. in blast furnaces (1); most of this comes from burning fossil fuels such as coke, gas or coal, which releases CO₂ (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way synthetic textiles contribute to oceanic pollution. (2)
---
Washing synthetic garments such as polyester fleece sheds tiny microfibres (1), which pass through water treatment into rivers and the sea, where they are eaten by marine life and enter food chains (1).
:::

::: q
A lorry emits 0.9 kg of CO₂ per km. It carries 150 assembled wardrobes or 400 flat-packed wardrobes on a 600 km journey. Calculate the CO₂ saved per wardrobe by flat packing. (4)
---
Journey CO₂ $= 600 \times 0.9 = 540$ kg (1); assembled $540 \div 150 = 3.6$ kg each (1); flat pack $540 \div 400 = 1.35$ kg each (1); saving $= 3.6 - 1.35 = 2.25$ kg per wardrobe (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Reuse ≠ recycle.** Reuse keeps the product's form; recycling reprocesses the material.
- **Refuse** is about the user and designer declining unnecessary or unsustainable items. It isn't "refusing to recycle".
- Say **what is damaged and why**: "mining destroys habitats and toxic run-off pollutes rivers", not just "mining is bad".
- Mileage covers the **whole journey**: source, manufacture, distribution, user and disposal.
- Social footprint is about **people**: workers, communities and users. Don't answer a social question with only CO₂.
- In the 8-mark question, examples are compulsory for the top band; **no product examples caps you at 6**.
:::
