## What it is

Designers must **develop, communicate, record and justify** their ideas: to themselves, to clients, and to the people who will make the product. Different techniques suit different jobs. A quick freehand sketch captures an idea in seconds; a third angle orthographic drawing tells a manufacturer every size; a card model lets a client hold the idea. The real paper includes a drawing question (completing an orthographic view, around 4 marks) and a 1-mark "which symbol shows third angle" question. This app is text-only, so here you learn the **rules** behind those drawings in words: where each view goes, what each line type means, and how dimensions and scales work.

**Drawing techniques**

| Technique | What it looks like | Best used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Freehand sketching** | quick drawings without instruments, 2D or 3D, often with notes | generating and recording early ideas fast; exploring many options |
| **Isometric drawing** | 3D view: vertical lines stay vertical; the two horizontal directions are drawn at **30° to the horizontal**; lengths along the three axes are true | a clear, measurable 3D view for presenting a design; drawn with a 30°/60° set square or isometric grid paper |
| **Perspective drawing** | 3D view where parallel lines converge to **vanishing points** on a **horizon line**; one-point (one face flat to you, e.g. a room interior) or two-point (a corner towards you, e.g. a building or product) | the most realistic view; how the object looks to the eye; presentations and client images |
| **Oblique drawing** (also common) | front face drawn true shape; depth lines at 45°, often at half length | quick 3D views of objects with a detailed front face |
| **2D drawings** | flat views of one face or side | outlines, templates, patterns, nets, orthographic views |
| **3D drawings** | shows three faces or dimensions at once | showing the overall form to a client |

**Rendering** adds tone, colour and texture (shading from a light source, lines to show wood grain or shine) so that a sketch shows form and material.

**Annotated drawings** are drawings with notes that **explain**, not just label: why this material, how it would be made, how the user would hold it, which part of the brief it meets, what to change next. Annotation shows detailed development and the conceptual thinking behind the design, and it is how a designer **justifies** decisions.

**System and schematic diagrams**

- A **system (block) diagram** shows a product as boxes for **input → process → output**, with arrows for the flow of signals, energy or materials. It explains how a system works without showing its components.
- A **schematic diagram** uses **standard symbols** to show how components are connected, e.g. a circuit diagram with symbols for a battery, resistor, LED and switch. It shows connections, not the physical layout. Flowcharts for a program, or a pneumatic circuit, are also schematic.

**Exploded diagrams** show a product with its parts **separated along the direction in which they fit together**, often with thin lines showing the path each part moves along. They show **constructional detail** and the **order of assembly**: how a flat-pack shelf, a torch or a pen goes together. Furniture instructions are exploded diagrams.

**Working drawings: third angle orthographic projection**

A **working drawing** gives the maker everything needed to produce the part: accurate views, dimensions, scale, and often materials and tolerances. In the UK the standard is **third angle orthographic projection**: several 2D views of the object, each looking straight at one face, arranged in a fixed pattern.

- The **front view** (front elevation) is drawn first and shows the most detail.
- The **plan** (view from above) goes **directly above** the front view.
- The **right-hand side view** (end elevation) goes **to the right** of the front view; a left-hand view would go to the left.
- The views line up: the plan is the same width as the front view; the side view is the same height. Thin **projection lines** carry sizes across.

The memory rule for third angle: **each view is placed on the side it is seen from**. (First angle, used in much of Europe, does the opposite: plan below, right-hand view on the left.)

**The third angle symbol** is drawn in the corner of the sheet. It shows a truncated cone (a cone with its tip cut off) on its side as a trapezium, with the end view of the cone, two concentric circles, **to its right**. The cone narrows towards the circles. In the first angle symbol the circles are on the left.

**Line types (British Standard conventions)**

| Line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Thick continuous** | visible outlines and edges |
| **Thin continuous** | dimension lines, projection lines, hatching on sections, leader lines |
| **Thin dashed (short dashes)** | **hidden detail**: edges behind a surface, such as a hole you can't see from that view |
| **Thin chain (long dash, dot)** | **centre lines** of holes, cylinders and symmetrical parts |

**Dimensioning rules**

- All sizes are in **millimetres**; "mm" is not written after each number (the title block states it).
- **Dimension lines** are thin, with **arrowheads touching** thin **projection lines**. Leave a small gap between the object and the start of each projection line.
- Numbers sit **above** (or in a gap in the middle of) the dimension line, **readable from the bottom or right** of the sheet.
- Use **Ø** before a diameter and **R** before a radius; e.g. Ø12 is a hole 12 mm across, R5 is a curved corner of radius 5 mm.
- Don't repeat dimensions or dimension to hidden lines; put the overall sizes outside the smaller ones.

**Scale.** A scale is written as drawing size : real size.

| Scale | Meaning | 60 mm real length is drawn as |
|---|---|---|
| **1:1** | full size | 60 mm |
| **1:2** | half size | 30 mm |
| **1:5** | one fifth size | 12 mm |
| **2:1** | twice full size (small parts) | 120 mm |

Dimensions written on a scaled drawing are always the **real** sizes, not the measured drawing sizes.

**Reading an object from its views (text version of the drawing question).** Imagine a rectangular block 80 wide, 40 deep and 30 high, with a vertical hole of Ø10 drilled right through it from top to bottom in the centre. In the **plan** the hole is seen as a **circle** (thick line) with centre lines crossing. In the **front view** the hole can't be seen, so it appears as **two thin dashed vertical lines** 10 apart, with a vertical **centre line** between them. The same dashed lines appear in the side view. If a step or slot is cut into the top at the front, the front view shows its true shape, while the plan shows it as extra lines across the top face.

**Other ways to communicate**

- **Audio and visual recordings**: recording interviews with a client or users, filming users trying a model, or photographing each stage of development. They provide evidence and let the designer revisit exactly what was said.
- **Mathematical modelling**: using calculations, formulae, graphs and spreadsheets to predict how a design will perform before making it, e.g. calculating material quantities and costs, the gear ratio for a required speed, the resistor value for an LED, or the load a shelf will carry.
- **Computer-based tools**: **CAD** 2D and 3D modelling (accurate, easy to edit, renders realistic images, produces files for CAM), simulation and virtual testing (stress analysis, circuit simulation), pattern-cutting software, presentation and spreadsheet software.
- **Modelling with materials**: making physical trial versions: **card models** (quick, cheap, easy to cut and change, ideal for packaging nets and product forms), foam models (styrofoam for hand-held forms), a **toile** (a trial garment in cheap fabric such as calico to check fit and pattern before cutting expensive fabric), and **breadboarding** (building a circuit on a solderless breadboard to test and change it before making a PCB).

::: terms
- **Isometric drawing**: a 3D drawing with vertical lines vertical and horizontal edges drawn at 30° to the horizontal.
- **Perspective drawing**: a realistic 3D drawing in which parallel lines meet at vanishing points on a horizon line.
- **Third angle orthographic projection**: a working drawing of 2D views with the plan above the front view and the right-hand view to its right.
- **Working drawing**: an accurate, dimensioned drawing to scale that gives a maker the information needed to produce a part.
- **Exploded diagram**: a drawing with parts separated along the lines they assemble on, showing construction and assembly order.
- **Schematic diagram**: a diagram that uses standard symbols to show how components are connected, such as a circuit diagram.
- **System diagram**: a block diagram showing the input, process and output parts of a system.
- **Hidden detail line**: a thin dashed line showing an edge that cannot be seen from that view.
- **Centre line**: a thin chain line of long dashes and dots marking the centre of a hole or symmetrical part.
- **Scale**: the ratio of drawing size to real size, e.g. 1:2 means half size.
- **Toile**: a trial version of a garment made in cheap fabric to test the pattern and fit.
- **Breadboard**: a reusable board for building and testing a circuit without soldering.
- **Annotation**: notes on a drawing that explain and justify design decisions.
:::

::: method
1. **Match the technique to its purpose**: ideas (sketch), presentation (isometric, perspective, rendering), manufacture (orthographic working drawing), assembly (exploded), circuits (schematic), function (system diagram), testing (models).
2. **For orthographic questions, place views by the rule**: plan above the front view, right-hand view on the right, everything lined up.
3. **Name the line type** for each feature: outline thick, hidden dashed, centre chain, dimensions thin.
4. **For scale questions**, write the ratio as drawing : real, and divide or multiply; dimensions stay as real sizes.
5. **For "explain the purpose" questions**, say what it shows **and who it helps** (maker, client, user).
6. **For modelling questions**, name the material and the specific thing the model tests (fit, size, function, appearance).
:::

::: example Third angle layout
Describe where the plan and right-hand side view are placed in a third angle orthographic drawing. (2)

The plan (view from above) is placed directly above the front view (1). The right-hand side view is placed to the right of the front view, level with it (1).
:::

::: example Hidden detail
A wooden block has a Ø8 hole drilled vertically through its centre from top to bottom. Describe how the hole appears in the plan and in the front view. (4)

In the plan the hole is seen directly, so it is drawn as a circle 8 mm across in a thick continuous line (1), with two thin chain centre lines crossing at its centre (1). In the front view the hole is inside the block and can't be seen, so it is shown as two thin dashed vertical lines 8 mm apart running the full height (1), with a thin chain centre line midway between them (1).
:::

::: example Scale
A table is 1,200 mm long. It is drawn at a scale of 1:20. Calculate the length of the table on the drawing. (2)

$1200 \div 20$ (1) $= 60$ mm (1).
:::

::: example Exploded diagram purpose
Explain the purpose of an exploded diagram. (2)

It shows every part of a product separated along the direction in which it fits (1), so the reader can see how the parts go together and the order of assembly, e.g. in flat-pack furniture instructions (1).
:::

::: example Choosing a technique
Explain why a designer would produce a toile before making a final garment. (3)

A toile is a trial version made in cheap fabric such as calico (1). It lets the designer check the fit on the client or a mannequin and test the pattern pieces and construction (1), so alterations are made to the pattern before cutting expensive fabric, saving money and waste (1).
:::

Any two: accurate; easy to edit and copy; realistic rendered images for clients; can be rotated and viewed from any angle; files can be emailed/shared; links directly to CAM; virtual testing.

30 degrees (1).

---
Thin dashed line (1).

::: q
Describe the third angle projection symbol. (2)
---
The side view of a truncated cone (trapezium) with its end view of two concentric circles (1) placed to the right of it, the cone narrowing towards the circles (1).
:::

::: q
A part 15 mm long is drawn at a scale of 4:1. Calculate its length on the drawing. (1)
---
$15 \times 4 = 60$ mm (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a system diagram and a schematic diagram. (2)
---
A system diagram shows the input, process and output blocks and how the system works overall (1); a schematic diagram shows the actual components as standard symbols and how they are connected (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why breadboarding a circuit is useful before making a PCB. (3)
---
Components push in without soldering (1), so the circuit can be tested to check it works (1) and components or values changed easily before committing to an etched or milled PCB, avoiding waste (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Plan above, right view to the right** in third angle. Swapping them is first angle and scores zero.
- Isometric uses **30°**, not 45° (that's oblique).
- Hidden edges are **dashed**, centre lines are **chain**. Mixing them loses the drawing marks.
- Dimensions on a scaled drawing are **real sizes**. Don't write the measured drawing size.
- A scale of **1:2 is half size**; **2:1 is twice size**.
- "Explain the purpose" needs **what it shows and why that helps**.
- **Toile** is for textiles, **breadboard** for circuits, **card modelling** for forms and packaging: match the model to the material area.
:::

Explain how mathematical modelling could be used when developing a design for a wall shelf. (3)
---
Calculations predict performance before making (1), e.g. the load the shelf and brackets can carry, the size of screws, the quantity and cost of timber, or deflection under load (1), so the design can be changed on paper rather than by making and breaking prototypes (1).

::: test
Explain how different modelling techniques could be used to develop a design for a hand-held electronic torch. (6)
---
5–6: detailed explanation of several appropriate techniques and what each tests, with a clear link to improving the design. 3–4: good explanation of two techniques. 1–2: basic list. Indicative: freehand sketches with annotation for early ideas; foam or card models to test the grip size and shape in the hand with users (anthropometrics); breadboard to test the circuit, LED brightness and resistor values before a PCB; CAD 3D model to check fit of batteries and PCB inside the case and render for clients, then 3D print a test casing; mathematical modelling for battery life and resistor value; video recording of users trying models; iterative improvement between stages.
:::

::: test
Compare isometric drawing and third angle orthographic projection as ways of communicating a design to a manufacturer. (4)
---
Isometric shows the product in 3D at 30°, so its overall form is easy to understand (1), but it distorts some angles and circles, and hidden detail and dimensions are hard to show (1). Orthographic shows each face as a true 2D view with dimensions, hidden detail and centre lines (1), so it gives the manufacturer precise information to make the part, though it is harder for non-experts to visualise (1).
:::
