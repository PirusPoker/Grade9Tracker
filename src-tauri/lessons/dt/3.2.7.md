## What it is

The same product can be made in very different ways depending on **how many** are needed. **Scale of production** is the volume a product is made in, and it drives almost every other decision: the processes, the tooling, the labour skill, the materials and components, and the **unit cost**. AQA names four scales. For each you need what it is, why it suits some products, the processes it uses (the commercial processes in 3.2.8), and its advantages and drawbacks, with **real product examples**.

### The four scales

| Scale | What it is | Typical processes and equipment | Advantages | Disadvantages | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Prototype (one-off)** | a **single** product, either **bespoke** (made to one client's needs) or a **prototype** to test a design before production | hand tools and general-purpose machines; CNC cutting and **3D printing**; highly **skilled** makers | exactly meets the client's needs; high quality and unique; **no expensive tooling**; very flexible; a prototype finds problems before money is spent on tooling | **high unit cost**, as it is **labour intensive**; slow; hard to make identical copies | a wedding dress, a made-to-measure suit, a fitted kitchen, a sculpture or stage prop, an architectural model, a pre-production prototype of a new kettle |
| **Batch** | a **set quantity** of identical products made together; the line is then reset to make a different batch; batches may be repeated | **jigs, templates, patterns, moulds and formers**; CNC programs reused; semi-skilled workers; e.g. die cutting, offset lithography, routing, casting | cheaper per unit than one-off because set-up is shared; **flexible**, so it can change design, size or colour between batches; responds to demand | **downtime** while machines are reset between batches; stock must be stored; less efficient than mass production | printed leaflets for an event, a seasonal clothing range in several sizes, school uniform, limited-edition furniture, a bakery's daily products, restaurant menus |
| **Mass** | **large numbers** of identical products made over a long period, often on a **production line** | dedicated machinery, **automation and robots**; expensive **tooling** (injection moulds, press tools); pick-and-place and flow soldering; low-skilled or automated labour; standard components | **very low unit cost**, because the huge tooling cost is spread over many units; consistent quality; fast | **very high set-up and tooling cost**; **inflexible**, since changing the design means new tooling; repetitive work; risk if demand falls | cars, kettles, phones, injection-moulded chairs, ballpoint pens, washing machines, flat-pack furniture ranges |
| **Continuous** | production that runs **24 hours a day, non-stop**, often for years, making one product or material | highly **automated**, dedicated plant; few workers who monitor and maintain; e.g. extrusion, paper machines, rolling mills | the **lowest unit cost**; enormous output; consistent product | **extremely expensive** plant; stopping and restarting is costly and wasteful, so demand must be constant; no flexibility | paper and board on reels, sheet steel, polymer extrusions (pipes, film), oil refining, float glass, polymer granules |

### Why the method changes with volume

- **Tooling and set-up costs** are **fixed**: an injection mould can cost tens of thousands of pounds, whether you make 10 parts or 10 million. Spread over a few units they are ruinous. Spread over millions they are pennies. So only high volumes justify dedicated tooling.
- **Labour**: one-off work needs highly skilled (and well paid) craftspeople. Batch uses semi-skilled workers helped by jigs and templates. Mass and continuous production are largely **automated**, with workers who supervise, load and inspect.
- **Flexibility**: the more dedicated the equipment, the harder it is to change the product. Batch production is the balance point, fairly cheap yet adaptable.
- **Consistency**: automated processes repeat exactly, so quality control focuses on measuring samples. One-offs rely on the maker's skill.
- **Materials and components**: at large scale, materials are bought in bulk and in forms suited to automated machines, such as polymer granules, paper on reels and components on reels for pick-and-place. At prototype scale, the designer uses readily available stock forms and easy-to-work materials.

### Unit cost

$\text{Unit cost} = \frac{\text{fixed (set-up and tooling) costs} + (\text{variable cost per unit} \times \text{number made})}{\text{number made}}$

As the number made rises, the fixed cost per unit falls, so the unit cost falls towards the variable cost. To compare two methods, work out the **total cost** of each, or find the **crossover quantity** where the costs are equal. Above that quantity, the method with the higher tooling cost and lower running cost becomes cheaper.

### Across the material categories

| Category | Prototype / one-off | Batch | Mass | Continuous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | hand-made card model or wedding invitation | offset-litho print run of leaflets; die-cut packaging for a product launch | cereal boxes, printed magazines | paper and board made on reels |
| **Timbers** | bespoke oak dining table | a run of CNC-routed chairs using jigs | flat-pack furniture ranges | *(rare: timber is converted in sawmills, but not strictly continuous)* |
| **Metals** | hand-forged gate | sand-cast or milled parts for a small machine run | cans, car body panels | rolled sheet steel |
| **Polymers** | 3D-printed prototype | vacuum-formed trays for a local business | injection-moulded chairs, bottle caps | extruded pipes, window-frame sections, film |
| **Textiles** | made-to-measure suit or wedding dress | seasonal fashion range in sizes | plain T-shirts, socks | yarn spinning and fabric weaving on large mills |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | breadboard or single hand-soldered prototype PCB | a run of PCBs for a specialist device | phone and appliance circuit boards made by pick-and-place and flow soldering | *(components such as wire are made continuously)* |

::: terms
- **Scale of production**: the number of products made, which determines the methods, tooling and labour used.
- **Prototype (one-off) production**: making a single product, either bespoke for one client or as a test model before manufacture.
- **Bespoke**: made to the specific requirements of one individual client.
- **Batch production**: making a set quantity of identical products together, then resetting to make a different batch.
- **Mass production**: making large numbers of identical products over a long period, usually on an automated production line.
- **Continuous production**: non-stop, 24-hour production of a single product or material, because stopping is too costly.
- **Tooling**: the dedicated moulds, dies, press tools and jigs made to produce one particular product.
- **Downtime**: time when machines are not producing, e.g. while they are reset between batches.
- **Unit cost**: the total cost of production divided by the number of products made.
- **Fixed costs**: costs such as tooling and set-up that do not change with the number of products made.
:::

::: method
1. **Define** the scale in one sentence (a single item; a set quantity; large numbers over a long time; non-stop 24/7).
2. **Name a real product** for each scale you discuss. It's compulsory for the top marks in the 8-marker.
3. **Link** the scale to its processes, tooling and labour (jigs and templates for batch; injection moulds and robots for mass).
4. **Explain the advantages and disadvantages**, tying them to **unit cost**, **flexibility**, **quality** and **set-up cost**.
5. **Evaluate**: say which scale suits which situation, and why. "Mass production only pays if demand is high and stable, because…"
6. For calculations: unit cost = (fixed + variable × n) ÷ n. To compare methods, set the totals equal, or compare totals at the given quantity.
:::

::: example Name the scale
State the most likely scale of production for (a) a made-to-measure wedding dress, (b) the PET bottles for a major soft-drink brand. (2)

(a) Prototype/one-off (bespoke) production (1). (b) Mass production (1).
:::

::: example Explain: batch production
Explain why batch production suits a company making school blazers. (3)

Blazers are needed in a set quantity for each school, in a range of sizes and colours (1). Batch production lets the company cut a batch of one size or colour using patterns and templates, then change to the next batch (1), so it can respond to each school's order without the huge tooling cost or inflexibility of mass production, while the unit cost stays far lower than making each blazer one-off (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: unit cost
An injection mould costs £24 000. The material and running cost is £0.60 per part. Calculate the unit cost for (a) 10 000 parts, (b) 100 000 parts. (4)

(a) $(24\,000 + 0.60 \times 10\,000) \div 10\,000$ (1) $= 30\,000 \div 10\,000 = £3.00$ (1)
(b) $(24\,000 + 0.60 \times 100\,000) \div 100\,000$ (1) $= 84\,000 \div 100\,000 = £0.84$ (1)
:::

::: example Calculate: crossover
The same part could be 3D printed with no tooling at £4.50 each. Using the mould costs above, calculate the smallest number of parts for which injection moulding is cheaper. (3)

Set the totals equal: $24\,000 + 0.60n = 4.50n$ (1)
$24\,000 = 3.90n$, so $n = 6153.8$ (1)
Injection moulding is cheaper from **6154 parts** upwards (1)
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate: extended
Analyse and evaluate the different scales of production a manufacturer could use for a new range of chairs. Give examples in your answer. (8)

A prototype chair would be made first, perhaps 3D printed at scale and then hand-built full size, so the designer can test comfort and strength before committing to tooling. This is essential to avoid expensive mistakes, but the unit cost is very high, so one-off production only suits bespoke pieces such as a commissioned oak chair for a client. Batch production would suit a small furniture maker selling a limited run of beech chairs. Jigs and CNC programs make each batch accurate, and the design or finish can change between batches to follow demand, although machines stand idle while they are reset and stock has to be stored. Mass production suits a café or classroom polypropylene chair. The injection mould is very expensive, but spread over hundreds of thousands of chairs the unit cost becomes very low and every chair is identical. The risk is inflexibility: if the design falls out of fashion, the tooling cost is wasted. Continuous production is not suitable for chairs, as it is used for materials made non-stop, such as the polypropylene granules or the paper for the catalogue. Overall, the best scale depends on demand. A new design should be prototyped, then launched in batches to test the market, and moved to mass production only once sales are high and stable enough to justify the tooling. (Level 4: 7–8 marks)
:::

::: q
Give one reason why continuous production runs 24 hours a day. (1)
---
Stopping and restarting the plant is very expensive or wasteful (e.g. reheating furnaces, restarting a paper machine), so it is cheaper to keep it running.
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of batch production. (2)
---
Machines must be stopped and reset between batches (1), causing downtime when nothing is produced, which raises costs (1). Also accept: stock of each batch must be stored, tying up money and space.
:::

::: q
Explain why a prototype is made before a product is mass produced. (3)
---
To test the design's function, size, ergonomics and appearance with users (1); problems can be found and fixed (1) before very expensive tooling is made, which would be costly to change later (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why mass-produced products have a low unit cost. (2)
---
The high tooling and set-up costs are spread over a very large number of products (1), and automation and bulk-bought materials reduce labour and material cost per unit (1).
:::

::: q
A printing company charges a £150 set-up cost per batch, plus £2.20 per item. Calculate the unit cost for a batch of 200 items. (3)
---
$150 + (2.20 \times 200) = 150 + 440 = £590$ (1)(1); $590 \div 200 = £2.95$ (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a bespoke kitchen is made by one-off production. (2)
---
Each kitchen must fit a particular room and the client's needs, so it is unique (1); there is no point making tooling for one design, so skilled workers use general-purpose tools (1).
:::

::: watch
- **No product examples in the 8-mark answer caps it at 6.** Name a real product for every scale.
- Discussing only **one scale** does not answer "the different scales".
- **Continuous ≠ mass.** Continuous runs non-stop 24/7 because stopping is costly, typically for materials such as paper, steel sheet and extrusions.
- **Batch** means a set quantity, then a reset. Mention **jigs, templates and downtime**.
- "Cheap" isn't enough; say **why**: tooling cost spread over many units.
- Unit cost: add the fixed cost **once**, then divide by the number made.
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of prototype (one-off) production. (4)
---
Advantage: the product exactly meets the client's needs, or is high quality and unique (1), because a skilled maker can adapt each detail (1). Disadvantage: high unit cost (1), because it is labour intensive and slow, using skilled workers' time for a single item (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why batch production is suitable for a clothing company producing a seasonal range. (3)
---
The range is needed in a set quantity for one season, in several sizes and colours (1); patterns and templates allow each batch to be cut accurately and repeated if it sells well (1); the line can then switch to the next style or size, giving flexibility that mass production lacks, with a lower unit cost than one-off (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why automation and robots are used in mass production. (3)
---
They work continuously and fast without breaks, increasing output (1); they repeat operations exactly, giving consistent quality with fewer errors (1); they reduce labour costs per unit, although the equipment is expensive to buy (1).
:::

::: test
A company can produce a component by method A, with a set-up cost of £8000 and £1.50 per unit, or method B, with a set-up cost of £500 and £4.00 per unit. Calculate the total cost of each method for 2000 units, and state which is cheaper. (4)
---
A: $8000 + 1.50 \times 2000 = 8000 + 3000 = £11\,000$ (1)(1); B: $500 + 4.00 \times 2000 = 500 + 8000 = £8500$ (1); method B is cheaper at 2000 units (1).
:::

::: test
Using the figures in the previous question, calculate the number of units at which the two methods cost the same. (2)
---
$8000 + 1.5n = 500 + 4n$, so $7500 = 2.5n$ (1); $n = 3000$ units (1).
:::

::: test
A continuous production line makes 1250 metres of polymer pipe per hour. Calculate how many metres it makes in one week of non-stop running. (2)
---
$1250 \times 24 = 30\,000$ m per day (1); $\times 7 = 210\,000$ m per week (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a manufacturer might move a product from batch production to mass production. (3)
---
Demand has grown large and stable (1), so the high cost of dedicated tooling and automation can be justified (1); spreading these fixed costs over many more units lowers the unit cost, allowing a lower price or more profit (1).
:::
