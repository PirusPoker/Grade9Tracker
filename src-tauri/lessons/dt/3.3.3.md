## What it is

Designers learn from other designers. The spec asks you to **investigate, analyse and evaluate** the work of past and present designers and companies **to inform your own designing**. You must know **at least two designers** from AQA's list of 28 and **at least two companies** from its list of 8. The exam may ask you to choose one and describe their work, or to explain why studying others helps.

**Why study the work of others?**

- to find **inspiration**: styles, forms, colours, materials, techniques you can adapt;
- to see how others **solved similar problems**, and what worked and failed;
- to understand how design responds to its **time**: new materials, technology, social change;
- to learn **successful business approaches**: branding, manufacturing, marketing;
- to avoid **copying**: knowing what exists helps you respect intellectual property and aim for something new;
- to develop your own **design identity** by questioning and building on what you see.

"Inform" means influence, not copy: take a principle (Beck's simplification, Dyson's iteration, Rams's "less but better") and apply it to your problem.

**Designers: profiles to learn**

Learn two of these well, ideally the ones your teacher chose. For each, know **who, when, style, two named works, materials or techniques, and influence**.

| Designer | Field and period | Key works and features | Influence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Marcel Breuer** | Furniture and architecture, Bauhaus, 1920s–30s | **Wassily chair** (B3) and the cantilevered **Cesca chair**; bent **tubular steel**, reportedly inspired by bicycle handlebars; canvas or leather seats; cane in the Cesca | Showed industrial materials could make light, mass-producible modern furniture; cantilever chairs are still common |
| **Philippe Starck** | Product and interiors, French, 1980s to now | **Juicy Salif** lemon squeezer for Alessi (cast aluminium, spider-like legs); **Louis Ghost** chair for Kartell (clear moulded polycarbonate) | Playful, sculptural everyday objects; "democratic design" of stylish mass-produced items; the lemon squeezer is more talking point than efficient tool |

**Companies: all eight**

| Company | What it is known for | Approach to learn |
|---|---|---|
| **Alessi** | Italian homeware: kettles, kitchen tools, tableware in stainless steel and colourful plastic | Works with famous outside designers (Starck, Michael Graves, Aldo Rossi, Sottsass) to make playful, sculptural everyday objects; design as a brand; small runs tested before mass production |
| **Apple** | Computers, phones, tablets, software | Minimal, carefully finished products (long led by Jony Ive): the translucent coloured iMac (1998), iPod, iPhone (2007), **unibody** machined aluminium cases; the product, software and services designed as one **ecosystem**; strong branding and user-centred interfaces |
| **Braun** | German electrical products: radios, shavers, calculators, clocks | Under **Dieter Rams**, functional minimalism: neat grids, few controls, neutral colours; his "**less but better**" and ten principles of good design influenced Apple and many others |
| **Dyson** | Vacuum cleaners, hand dryers, fans, hair dryers | Founded by James Dyson; **bagless cyclonic** vacuum cleaner developed through thousands of prototypes; heavy investment in research and engineering; visible "engineered" look with see-through bins; iterative design and patents |
| **Gap** | American clothing retailer | Casual basics (denim, khakis, T-shirts) sold worldwide in large volumes; simple, classic styles; a global supply chain that brings ethical sourcing and sustainability pressures |
| **Primark** | Low-price clothing retailer (Penneys in Ireland) | **Fast fashion** at very low prices: huge volumes, simple designs, low margins, little advertising; criticised over supplier factory conditions (it was one of the brands linked to the 2013 Rana Plaza factory collapse in Bangladesh) and has since promoted a sustainable cotton programme |
| **Under Armour** | American sportswear | Began with **moisture-wicking** synthetic compression shirts that keep athletes dry; performance fabrics for hot and cold conditions; technical textiles and endorsement by athletes |
| **Zara** | Spanish fast-fashion retailer (Inditex group) | Very **fast design-to-shop** time of a few weeks; small batches with frequent new lines; much production kept close to Spain; responds quickly to what sells; **just in time** thinking applied to fashion |

**Evaluating work, not just describing it.** Top answers judge: Breuer's tubular steel was light and strong and suited mass production, but felt cold and industrial to some. Beck's map is easy to read, but distorts distances. Primark makes fashion affordable, but raises concerns about waste and labour. Starck's squeezer is iconic, but juice runs down the legs and acid can mark the finish.

::: terms
- **Design movement**: a group of designers sharing a style and set of ideas at a period in history, e.g. Bauhaus, Art Nouveau, Arts and Crafts, De Stijl, Memphis.
- **Bauhaus**: an influential German design school of the 1920s–30s that combined art, craft and industrial production.
- **Arts and Crafts**: a Victorian movement, led by William Morris, valuing handcraft and natural forms over cheap machine-made goods.
- **Art Nouveau**: a style around 1900 based on flowing, natural and plant-like forms.
- **De Stijl**: a Dutch movement using straight lines, flat planes and primary colours with black and white.
- **Memphis Group**: a 1980s Italian group, founded by Ettore Sottsass, known for bold colour, pattern and playful forms.
- **Fast fashion**: rapid, low-cost production of clothing that follows current trends, as practised by Zara and Primark.
- **Minimalism**: a design approach using the fewest elements, simple forms and little decoration, e.g. Braun under Dieter Rams.
- **Iconic design**: a product so recognisable and influential that it represents its period or company.
- **Intellectual property**: legal ownership of ideas and designs through patents, registered designs, copyright and trademarks.
:::

::: method
1. **Choose the designer or company you know best**, and name them in the first line.
2. **Anchor with facts**: period or movement, field, and **two named products**.
3. **Describe the features**: form, materials, colour, technology, manufacturing method.
4. **Explain the influence**: what changed because of them, and who they influenced.
5. **Evaluate**: a strength and a limitation, with a reason.
6. For "how does researching others help" questions, give **reasons plus an example** of applying a principle, never "copy their design".
:::

::: example Why research others
Describe how researching the work of other designers and companies can help with design work. (4)

It gives inspiration: a designer can adapt a principle, such as Harry Beck simplifying a complex map into straight lines at set angles, to make their own product clearer (1). It shows how others solved similar problems with materials and technology, so the designer avoids repeating mistakes (1). Studying successful companies such as Dyson shows how iterative testing and engineering lead to innovative products (1). Knowing what exists helps the designer avoid infringing others' intellectual property and find a gap in the market (1).
:::

::: example A designer
Choose one designer from: Marcel Breuer, Harry Beck, Mary Quant. Describe their work and explain their influence on design. (6)

Harry Beck designed the London Underground map in the early 1930s (1). Earlier maps followed the real geography, so central stations were crowded and outer lines stretched out (1). Beck, who had drawn electrical circuit diagrams, treated the network like a circuit: lines are only horizontal, vertical or at 45°, stations are evenly spaced and each line has its own colour (1). The map is not to scale, because passengers only need to know the order of stations and where to change, so clarity matters more than distance (1). It was popular with passengers, and its principles were adopted by transport maps around the world, making Beck's approach a model for information design (1). A limitation is that it distorts real distances, so some journeys look longer than they are and passengers may take the Tube when walking would be quicker (1).
:::

::: example A company
Explain the design approach of Dyson. (4)

Dyson solves an engineering problem with existing products, such as bagged vacuum cleaners losing suction as the bag fills (1). It develops solutions through iterative design: many prototypes are built, tested and improved, which led to the bagless cyclonic cleaner (1). It invests heavily in research and protects its technology with patents (1). Its products have a visible "engineered" aesthetic, such as a see-through bin that shows the technology working and becomes part of the brand (1).
:::

::: example Fast fashion
Compare the approaches of Zara and Primark. (4)

Both sell trend-led clothing aimed at a mass market (1). Zara's strength is speed: new designs reach shops within weeks in small batches, often made close to Spain, so lines change often and customers visit repeatedly (1). Primark's strength is very low price: large volumes of simple designs, low margins and little advertising (1). Both face criticism over waste and working conditions in the supply chain; Primark was linked to the Rana Plaza collapse and has since promoted a sustainable cotton programme (1).
:::

::: q
Name the design movement Marcel Breuer is associated with. (1)
---
The Bauhaus (1).

::: q
Give two features of Harry Beck's London Underground map. (2)
---
Any two: lines only horizontal, vertical or at 45°; evenly spaced stations; not to geographic scale; each line a different colour; based on a circuit diagram.
:::

::: q
Explain one way Dieter Rams's work at Braun influenced later product design. (2)
---
His minimal "less but better" style, with few controls and simple forms (1), influenced companies such as Apple, whose products share clean surfaces and simple interfaces (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why Alessi works with well-known outside designers. (2)
---
Famous designers give products a distinctive, sculptural style and a name customers recognise (1), so the products stand out, sell as design objects and command higher prices (1).
:::

::: q
Describe one innovation introduced by Under Armour. (2)
---
Moisture-wicking synthetic compression shirts (1) that draw sweat away from the skin so athletes stay dry and cool (1).
:::

::: q
Evaluate Philippe Starck's Juicy Salif lemon squeezer as a product. (4)
---
3–4: balanced evaluation with judgement, e.g. iconic sculptural form in cast aluminium makes it a talking point and a design object, widely recognised; but as a squeezer it is less practical, juice runs down the legs, pips aren't caught, acid can mark it; it succeeds as an expressive object rather than a tool. 1–2: basic description with little judgement.
:::

::: watch
- **Name specific works and features.** "He designed nice chairs" earns nothing; "tubular steel cantilever Cesca chair" does.
- Know **two designers and two companies** in depth; one will not do if a question asks you to compare.
- Keep facts to what you are sure of: movement, decade, two works. Invented dates lose credibility.
- "Inform your designing" means **apply a principle**, never copy.
- **Evaluate**: give a strength and a weakness, not just praise.
:::
