## What it is

Every designer needs an overview of the main **material categories** so they can choose the right one for a product. This lesson covers the first two groups in 3.1.6.1: **papers and boards**, and **natural and manufactured timbers**. You need to know each named material, what it is like and what it is used for. The exam asks you to classify materials (which is a hardwood, which is a board), name a use, and justify a choice with a property, from 1-mark multiple choice up to a 4-mark "outline two reasons" and longer justified choices. Section B may then ask you to go deeper into one category.

### Papers and boards

Papers and boards are made from **cellulose fibres**, mostly from wood pulp (often softwood) or recycled paper. They are sold by **weight** in **grams per square metre (gsm)** and boards also by **thickness** in **microns** (µm; 1 micron is one thousandth of a millimetre). As a rough guide, paper is below about 200 gsm; heavier material is called card or board.

**Papers**

| Paper | Key characteristics | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Bleed proof paper** | smooth, fairly thin; stops marker-pen ink spreading (bleeding) sideways or soaking through | presentation drawings and design sketches rendered with spirit or alcohol markers |
| **Cartridge paper** | good-quality, heavier, slightly textured, usually creamy white; takes pencil, ink, pastel and paint well | sketching, drawing, painting, mounting work |
| **Grid paper** | printed with a faint grid of squares or an isometric grid | orthographic and isometric drawings, graphs, drawing nets, planning layouts accurately |
| **Layout paper** | thin, lightweight, semi-translucent, cheap | quick design ideas, developing sketches by tracing over earlier ones |
| **Tracing paper** | translucent, smooth, fairly tough | copying and overlaying drawings, transferring images, overlays showing changes |

**Boards**

| Board | Key characteristics | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Corrugated card** | a fluted (wavy) layer glued between flat liner sheets; light but strong and rigid for its weight; cushions impact; good insulator; easily recycled | delivery boxes and packaging, removal boxes, protective packaging, point-of-sale displays |
| **Duplex board** | two layers with different finishes: a smooth, white, printable outer face and a grey or brown (often recycled) inner side; cheaper than solid white board | food packaging such as cereal boxes and frozen-food cartons |
| **Foil lined board** | board with a thin aluminium foil layer on one face; the foil reflects heat, keeping food hot, and forms a barrier to moisture, grease and air | takeaway food container lids, ready-meal sleeves, drinks cartons, cosmetic packaging |
| **Foam core board** | a layer of polystyrene foam sandwiched between two thin sheets of card or paper; very light, rigid and easy to cut with a craft knife; dents easily | architectural and product models, mounting photographs and artwork, exhibition displays |
| **Ink jet card** | card with a coated surface designed to absorb ink-jet ink quickly without smudging or spreading, giving sharp, high-quality prints | printed presentations, photographs, invitations, greetings cards |
| **Solid white board** | high-quality bleached board, white all the way through; excellent surface for printing and embossing; relatively expensive | premium packaging for cosmetics, perfume and gifts; book covers |

### Natural timbers

Natural timber is cut from trees. The labels **hardwood** and **softwood** describe the **type of tree**, not how hard the wood is.

- **Hardwoods** come from **broad-leaved** trees, which grow slowly and usually shed their leaves in winter (in temperate climates). Because they grow slowly they are usually more expensive, often denser and more durable, with attractive grain.
- **Softwoods** come from **coniferous** trees, with needles and cones, which are mostly evergreen and grow quickly, often in managed plantations. They are usually cheaper, lighter and easier to obtain in long, straight lengths, and usually contain knots.

**Balsa is a hardwood**, even though it is the softest, lightest timber you will meet. **Larch is a softwood**, even though it is a conifer that sheds its needles in winter.

| Hardwood | Characteristics | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Ash** | pale cream to light brown, straight grain; tough, flexible and shock-resistant; can be steam-bent | tool handles, sports equipment, ladders, bent-wood furniture |
| **Beech** | pale pinkish-brown, fine close grain; hard, tough, finishes well; does not taint food | children's toys, kitchen utensils, chairs, workbenches, mallets |
| **Mahogany** | reddish-brown, attractive grain; fairly easy to work, fairly durable; tropical and expensive, with concerns about sustainable sourcing | high-quality furniture, veneers, panelling, boat interiors |
| **Oak** | light brown, open grain; hard, tough, very durable; contains tannic acid, which corrodes steel fixings (use brass or stainless steel) | quality furniture, flooring, beams, doors, barrels |
| **Balsa** | very pale, very light and soft; extremely low density; easy to cut with a craft knife | model-making, model aircraft, lightweight cores in surfboards and some composites |

| Softwood | Characteristics | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Larch** | pale reddish-brown; tough and relatively durable for a softwood; resinous and resists rot and water well | outdoor cladding, fencing, decking, boat building |
| **Pine** | pale yellow with brown grain lines and knots; light, easy to work, cheap and widely available | roof joists and general construction, budget furniture, mouldings and skirting |
| **Spruce** | creamy white, fairly fine grain; light and fairly strong; not durable outdoors unless treated | indoor construction timber, packaging crates, musical instrument soundboards |

### Manufactured boards

Manufactured boards are **human-made sheets** produced from wood veneers, particles or fibres glued together with resin. They come in **large, flat, stable sheets** (commonly 2440 × 1220 mm), wider than any board cut from a tree; they do not warp or split like natural timber; they have a consistent quality with no knots; and they use up low-grade timber and waste wood. Their edges need covering, and most are damaged by water unless a moisture-resistant grade is used.

| Board | How it is made | Properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MDF** (medium density fibreboard) | fine wood fibres mixed with resin and pressed under heat | very smooth, no grain, easy to cut, shape and paint; stable; heavy; swells if it gets wet; fine dust is a health hazard | flat-pack furniture, kitchen unit doors, shelving, display stands, models |
| **Plywood** | thin layers of wood veneer (plies) glued with the grain of each layer at 90° to the next, usually an odd number of layers | strong in all directions and resists splitting; stable; can be bent into curves; exterior and marine grades resist water | furniture, construction, boat building, skateboards, drawer bottoms and backs |
| **Chipboard** | chips and particles of wood mixed with resin and pressed into sheets | cheap; fairly rigid but weaker than MDF or ply; edges crumble; swells badly when wet; usually faced with melamine or veneer | kitchen worktops (laminated), flooring panels, flat-pack furniture carcasses |

### Maths and science: gsm, microns and density

**gsm.** An A4 sheet is 210 mm × 297 mm, so its area is $0.210 \times 0.297 = 0.06237$ m². An 80 gsm sheet therefore weighs $80 \times 0.06237 \approx 5.0$ g, and a ream of 500 sheets weighs about $500 \times 5.0 = 2500$ g $= 2.5$ kg.

**Microns.** 1 µm $= 0.001$ mm. A 400 micron board is $400 \times 0.001 = 0.4$ mm thick; a stack of 50 sheets is $50 \times 0.4 = 20$ mm high.

**Density** $=$ mass $\div$ volume. A balsa block 100 × 50 × 20 mm has a volume of $100\,000$ mm³ $= 100$ cm³. If its mass is 15 g, its density is $15 \div 100 = 0.15$ g/cm³. An oak block of the same size with a mass of 70 g has a density of $0.70$ g/cm³: oak is over four and a half times as dense. (Sample figures; real timber varies with moisture and growth.)

::: terms
- **Grams per square metre (gsm)**: the weight of one square metre of paper or board, used to describe how heavy it is.
- **Micron**: one thousandth of a millimetre, used to measure the thickness of board.
- **Bleed proof paper**: smooth paper that stops marker ink spreading or soaking through, used for rendered drawings.
- **Duplex board**: board with a smooth white printing surface on one side and a grey or brown recycled side on the other.
- **Foil lined board**: board with a thin aluminium foil layer that keeps heat in and resists moisture and grease.
- **Foam core board**: a polystyrene foam centre between two thin card faces, making a light, rigid sheet for models and mounting.
- **Hardwood**: timber from a broad-leaved tree, usually slow-growing, which may be hard or, like balsa, very soft.
- **Softwood**: timber from a coniferous tree with needles and cones, usually fast-growing and cheaper.
- **Manufactured board**: a sheet material made from wood veneers, particles or fibres bonded with resin, such as MDF, plywood or chipboard.
- **MDF**: medium density fibreboard; fine wood fibres and resin pressed into a smooth, stable, grainless sheet.
- **Plywood**: layers of veneer glued with the grain of each layer at 90 degrees to the next, giving strength in all directions.
- **Chipboard**: a cheap board made from wood chips and resin, usually covered with a veneer or melamine laminate.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify first**: paper, board, hardwood, softwood or manufactured board. Remember that hardwood and softwood describe the tree.
2. **Name a specific material**, not "wood" or "card": "beech", "foil lined board".
3. **Justify with a property linked to the product's function**: "beech is hard and has a close grain, so a toy will not splinter and can be sanded smooth and safe for a child".
4. **For "outline two reasons"**, give two different properties, each with its benefit to the product or user.
5. **For comparisons**, use the same criteria for both materials (cost, strength, stability, finish, water resistance) and say which is better for the purpose.
6. **For gsm, micron and density sums**, write the formula, convert units first, and give the unit in the answer.
:::

::: example Outline: foil lined board
Outline two reasons why foil lined board is used for the lids of takeaway food containers. (4)

The aluminium foil reflects heat back towards the food (1), so the food stays hot for longer during delivery (1). The foil forms a barrier to moisture and grease (1), so the board does not go soggy or weak, and the lid can still be printed on its card side (1).
:::

::: example Justify: timber for a child's toy
Name a suitable timber for a child's wooden stacking toy and justify your choice. (3)

Beech (1). It is hard and tough with a close grain, so it does not splinter or dent easily when dropped (1), and it can be sanded to a very smooth finish that is safe for a child to handle and does not taint (1).
:::

::: example Compare: MDF and plywood
Compare MDF and plywood for making a flat-pack bookcase. (4)

MDF is smoother with no grain, so it is easy to paint or laminate for a good finish, and it is usually cheaper (1)(1). Plywood is stronger for its weight and holds screws better, especially near edges, so the shelves are less likely to sag or the joints to pull out (1)(1). MDF is often chosen for cost and finish; plywood is better where strength matters.
:::

::: example Calculate: weight of paper
A designer orders 2000 sheets of A4 cartridge paper at 150 gsm. One A4 sheet has an area of 0.06237 m². Calculate the total mass of the order in kilograms, to one decimal place. (3)

Mass of one sheet $= 150 \times 0.06237 = 9.3555$ g (1). Total $= 2000 \times 9.3555 = 18\,711$ g (1) $= 18.7$ kg (1).
:::

::: example Justify: extended material choice
A designer is making a set of garden planters that will stand outdoors all year. Choose a suitable natural timber and justify your choice. (6)

Larch is a suitable choice (1). It is a naturally durable softwood that contains resin, so it resists rot and water better than most softwoods (1), which is essential because the planters will be in contact with wet soil and rain all year (1). It is tough for a softwood, so it withstands knocks from garden tools and the weight of soil (1). As a softwood from fast-growing, managed plantations it is cheaper and more sustainable than a durable hardwood such as oak (1), and it is available in long, straight lengths that are easy to cut and join for a simple box design (1).
Level 3 (5–6): a suitable named timber with several properties clearly linked to the use, environment and cost. Level 2 (3–4): a suitable timber with some justified properties. Level 1 (1–2): a timber named with simple, weakly linked properties.
:::

::: q
Give one use for each of these papers: layout paper and tracing paper. (2)
---
Layout paper: quick design sketches or developing ideas by tracing over earlier sketches (1). Tracing paper: copying or overlaying drawings, transferring images (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why corrugated card is used for delivery boxes. (3)
---
The fluted layer makes it rigid and strong for its weight (1), so it protects the contents from knocks and crushing (1) while keeping the box light, which cuts delivery costs; it is also easily recycled (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why balsa is classed as a hardwood even though it is very soft. (2)
---
Hardwood and softwood describe the type of tree, not the hardness of the timber (1); balsa comes from a broad-leaved tree, so it is a hardwood (1).
:::

::: q
Give two advantages of manufactured boards compared with natural timber. (2)
---
Any two: available in large, wide sheets; stable, so they do not warp or split; consistent quality with no knots; use waste or low-grade timber; cheaper; easy to veneer or laminate.
:::

::: q
Explain why oak furniture is often assembled with brass screws rather than steel screws. (2)
---
Oak contains tannic acid (1), which reacts with and corrodes steel, causing black stains and weakening the joint; brass does not react (1).
:::

::: q
A board is 350 microns thick. Calculate the height of a stack of 120 sheets in millimetres. (2)
---
One sheet $= 350 \times 0.001 = 0.35$ mm (1). Stack $= 120 \times 0.35 = 42$ mm (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Hardwood and softwood describe the tree**, not the hardness. Balsa is a hardwood; larch is a softwood.
- **MDF, plywood and chipboard are manufactured boards**, not natural timbers. Do not list them as softwoods.
- Name the **specific** material: "board" or "wood" earns nothing where "foil lined board" or "beech" would.
- A property alone is half an answer: say **what it does for the product** or user.
- Duplex board has **two different faces**; do not confuse it with corrugated card.
- In gsm sums, convert the sheet area to **square metres** before multiplying.
:::

Explain why duplex board is used for cereal boxes. (3)

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using pine for a child's bedroom furniture. (4)
---
Advantage: pine is cheap, widely available and easy to cut and join (1), so the furniture is affordable and quick to make; it also has an attractive light colour (1). Disadvantage: it is relatively soft (1), so it dents and scratches easily with rough use; knots can loosen or weep resin (1).

::: test
Explain why MDF is often used for kitchen cupboard doors that will be painted. (3)
---
MDF has a very smooth surface with no grain or knots (1), so paint gives an even, high-quality finish (1); it is stable and does not warp, and can be machined into decorative profiles on a CNC router (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why chipboard kitchen worktops are covered with a laminate. (3)
---
Chipboard swells and falls apart if it gets wet (1); the laminate seals it from water and spills in a kitchen (1), and gives a hard, hygienic, easy-to-clean and attractive surface over a cheap core (1).
:::

::: test
A package design uses 5000 sheets of board, each with an area of 0.12 m², at 300 gsm. Calculate the total mass of board in kilograms. (3)
---
Mass of one sheet $= 300 \times 0.12 = 36$ g (1). Total $= 5000 \times 36 = 180\,000$ g (1) $= 180$ kg (1).
:::

::: test
A block of timber measures 200 mm × 50 mm × 40 mm and has a mass of 280 g. Calculate its density in g/cm³. (3)
---
Volume $= 200 \times 50 \times 40 = 400\,000$ mm³ (1) $= 400$ cm³ (1). Density $= 280 \div 400 = 0.7$ g/cm³ (1).
:::

::: test
Compare plywood and chipboard for the base of a heavy toolbox. (4)
---
Plywood is stronger in all directions because of its cross-grained layers (1), so it will not sag or crack under heavy tools, and it holds screws well (1). Chipboard is cheaper (1) but weaker, its edges crumble and screws pull out, and it swells if it gets damp, so plywood is the better choice for a heavy toolbox (1).
:::

Oil finishes are food-safe. Unsuitable choices (e.g. MDF, balsa, pine with resinous knots) limited to Level 1.
