## What it is

A designer chooses a material because of its **properties**: what it is like and how it behaves. The spec splits them into **physical properties** (what the material is, measured without changing its shape: absorbency, density, fusibility, electrical and thermal conductivity) and **working properties** (how it responds to forces and to being worked: strength, hardness, toughness, malleability, ductility and elasticity). You must know them **in relation to the main material categories** (papers and boards, timbers, metals, polymers, textiles), not every individual material. Exam questions ask for precise definitions (often multiple choice), "describe" questions worth 2 marks per property, and short "explain why this property suits this product" answers. Density calculations are a common maths question.

### Physical properties

| Property | Meaning | Across the categories |
|---|---|---|
| **Absorbency** (resistance to moisture) | how readily a material soaks up moisture, liquid or water vapour; the opposite is being water resistant | papers and boards are very absorbent unless coated; **timber** absorbs moisture from the air and swells or shrinks; **natural fibres** (cotton, wool) are absorbent, which makes them comfortable; **metals** and most **polymers** do not absorb water; **synthetic fibres** have low absorbency, so they dry quickly |
| **Density** | mass per unit volume: how heavy a material is for its size | **metals** are generally dense (steel, copper) though aluminium is fairly light; **timbers** and **polymers** are low density, many floating on water; **papers, boards and textiles** are light; balsa and foams are very low density |
| **Fusibility** | the ability of a material to melt (turn to liquid) when heated and fuse or join, and so be cast or welded | **metals** are fusible: they can be cast, welded and soldered; **thermoforming polymers** and **synthetic fibres** melt and can be moulded or heat-sealed; **thermosetting polymers, timber, paper and natural fibres** are not fusible: they char or burn instead |
| **Electrical conductivity** | how well a material allows electricity to pass through it | **metals** are good conductors (copper, aluminium); **polymers, timber, paper and textiles** are **insulators**, although conductive fabrics and graphene-based materials are exceptions |
| **Thermal conductivity** | how well a material allows heat to pass through it | **metals** are good thermal conductors (pan bases, heat sinks); **timbers, polymers, foams and textiles** are poor conductors, so they are used as insulators (pan handles, oven gloves, trapping air in wool) |

### Working properties

| Property | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength** | the ability of a material to withstand a force **without breaking or permanently bending**. Types: **tensile** (pulling), **compressive** (squashing), **bending**, **shear** (sliding forces, as in scissors) and **torsion** (twisting) | steel cable in tension; cast iron and concrete in compression; timber strong along the grain but weaker across it; polyamide in ropes |
| **Hardness** | the ability to resist **scratching, wear, abrasion and indentation** (denting); a harder material can cut or scratch a softer one | high carbon steel chisels, melamine worktop laminates, acrylic compared with HIPS; glass is hard |
| **Toughness** | the ability to **absorb energy and resist breaking** when hit (impact), without shattering; the opposite is **brittle** | polypropylene, mild steel, ash for tool handles, polyamide; glass, acrylic and cast iron are brittle |
| **Malleability** | the ability to be **permanently shaped** by hammering, pressing or rolling **without cracking** | aluminium pressed into cans, copper beaten into bowls, lead and gold sheet; card being folded and creased |
| **Ductility** | the ability to be **drawn or stretched out** into a thin length (wire) without breaking | copper drawn into electrical wire, aluminium wire, mild steel |
| **Elasticity** | the ability to **stretch or bend under a force and return to its original shape** when the force is removed | elastane in sportswear, rubber bands, spring steel, polypropylene hinges |

**Hardness is not toughness.** Glass is hard (difficult to scratch) but not tough (it shatters when dropped). Rubber is tough and elastic but not hard. A good answer never mixes them up.

**Elastic and plastic.** An elastic material returns to its shape only up to its **elastic limit**. Stretch it further and it deforms **permanently** (plastic deformation). Malleable and ductile materials are useful because they deform plastically without cracking.

**Physical versus working.** Physical properties describe the material itself (a copper wire conducts whether or not you work it). Working properties describe its response to forces and processing (how it bends, stretches, resists breaking).

### Linking properties to products

| Product | Key property | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical wiring | electrical conductivity and ductility (copper) | carries current with little loss; can be drawn into thin, flexible wire |
| Saucepan | thermal conductivity (aluminium or copper base) with a poorly conducting handle | heats food quickly; handle stays cool |
| Plug casing | electrical insulation and heat resistance (UF) | protects user from shock; does not soften when warm |
| Hammer handle | toughness (ash, or glass-filled polymer) | absorbs repeated impacts without breaking |
| Chisel | hardness (hardened tool steel) | cutting edge resists wear and cuts softer timber |
| Swimwear | elasticity and low absorbency (elastane, polyamide) | stretches and recovers; does not become heavy with water |
| Towel | absorbency (cotton) | soaks up water from the skin |
| Drinks can | malleability and low density (aluminium) | pressed into shape without cracking; light to transport |

### Maths and science: density and volume

$$\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}} \qquad \rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

Units: g/cm³ or kg/m³. To convert, **1 g/cm³ $=$ 1000 kg/m³**. Typical values: water 1.0 g/cm³; aluminium about 2.7 g/cm³; steel about 7.8 g/cm³; copper about 8.9 g/cm³. A material with a density below 1 g/cm³ floats on water.

**Volume conversions.** 1 cm³ $= 1000$ mm³, and 1 m³ $= 10^9$ mm³. Large volumes in mm³ are easier to handle in **standard form**: a block 120 × 150 × 170 mm has a volume of $120 \times 150 \times 170 = 3\,060\,000$ mm³ $= 3.06 \times 10^6$ mm³ $= 3060$ cm³.

**Worked example.** An aluminium block measures 100 mm × 50 mm × 20 mm. Volume $= 100 \times 50 \times 20 = 100\,000$ mm³ $= 100$ cm³. With density 2.7 g/cm³, mass $= 2.7 \times 100 = 270$ g. The same block in steel would weigh $7.8 \times 100 = 780$ g, nearly three times as much, which is why aluminium is chosen for lightweight products.

::: terms
- **Physical property**: a property of the material itself, such as density or conductivity, measured without shaping or loading it.
- **Working property**: how a material behaves when forces are applied or it is worked, such as strength or malleability.
- **Absorbency**: how readily a material soaks up moisture or liquid; the opposite of being water resistant.
- **Density**: the mass of a material per unit volume, found by dividing mass by volume.
- **Fusibility**: the ability of a material to melt when heated and to fuse, so it can be cast, welded or moulded.
- **Electrical conductivity**: how well a material allows electricity to pass through it.
- **Thermal conductivity**: how well a material allows heat to pass through it.
- **Strength**: the ability to withstand a force, such as tension, compression, bending, shear or torsion, without breaking.
- **Hardness**: the ability to resist scratching, wear, abrasion and denting.
- **Toughness**: the ability to absorb impact energy without breaking or shattering; the opposite of brittle.
- **Malleability**: the ability to be permanently shaped by hammering, pressing or rolling without cracking.
- **Ductility**: the ability to be drawn or stretched out into a thin wire without breaking.
- **Elasticity**: the ability to stretch or bend under a force and return to the original shape when the force is removed.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely**, using the key verb: hardness **resists scratching and denting**; toughness **resists breaking on impact**; ductility **drawn into wire**; malleability **shaped by hammering or pressing**; elasticity **returns to shape**.
2. **For 2-mark "describe" questions**, give the definition plus an example, or two separate correct points (e.g. stretches under force **and** returns to shape).
3. **For "explain why this material"**, name the property, then say what it does for the product or user.
4. **Talk about categories** when the question does ("metals are fusible, so they can be cast"), and specific materials when it asks for a product.
5. **For density sums**, convert all lengths to the same unit, find the volume, then divide mass by volume, and give units. Use standard form for very large numbers.
:::

::: example Describe: hardness and strength
Describe briefly the two material properties hardness and strength. (4)

Hardness: the ability of a material to resist scratching, wear and denting (1); a hard material, such as tool steel, can cut or scratch a softer one (1).
Strength: the ability of a material to withstand a force without breaking or permanently bending (1); this may be a pulling (tensile), squashing (compressive), bending, shearing or twisting force (1).
:::

::: example Describe: elastic material
Describe the characteristics of a material with elastic properties. (2)

It stretches or changes shape when a force is applied (1) and returns to its original shape and size when the force is removed (1).
:::

::: example Explain: copper wiring
Explain why copper is used for electrical wiring. (2)

Copper is an excellent electrical conductor, so current flows with very little energy lost (1); it is ductile, so it can be drawn into long, thin, flexible wires (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: density
A block of polymer measures 80 mm × 50 mm × 25 mm and has a mass of 95 g. Calculate its density in g/cm³ and state whether it would float in water (density 1.0 g/cm³). (4)

Volume $= 80 \times 50 \times 25 = 100\,000$ mm³ (1) $= 100$ cm³ (1). Density $= 95 \div 100 = 0.95$ g/cm³ (1). It would float, because 0.95 is less than 1.0 g/cm³ (1).
:::

::: example Compare: hardness and toughness
Explain the difference between hardness and toughness, using a material example for each. (4)

Hardness is resistance to scratching, wear and denting (1): for example, glass is hard and difficult to scratch (1). Toughness is the ability to absorb an impact without breaking (1): polypropylene is tough, so a PP container survives being dropped, whereas glass, although hard, shatters because it is brittle (1).
:::

::: q
State what is meant by a fusible material. (1)
---
A material that melts (turns to liquid) when heated and can be joined or cast, e.g. metals and thermoforming polymers.
:::

::: q
Explain why wool is warm to wear, using its thermal properties. (2)
---
Wool fibres are crimped and trap air (1); air and wool are poor thermal conductors, so body heat is not lost easily (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between malleability and ductility. (2)
---
Malleability is the ability to be shaped by hammering, pressing or rolling without cracking (1); ductility is the ability to be drawn or stretched out into a thin wire (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why timber used outdoors needs protecting. Refer to a physical property. (2)
---
Timber is absorbent, so it takes in rain and moisture (1), which makes it swell, warp and rot unless it is sealed or treated (1).
:::

::: q
Name the type of strength needed by a rope used to tow a car. (1)
---
Tensile strength.
:::

::: q
A steel bar has a volume of 250 cm³. The density of steel is 7.8 g/cm³. Calculate its mass in kilograms. (2)
---
Mass $= 7.8 \times 250 = 1950$ g (1) $= 1.95$ kg (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Hard is not tough.** Hardness resists scratching and denting; toughness resists breaking on impact. Glass is hard but brittle.
- **Ductile means drawn into wire; malleable means hammered or pressed into shape.** Do not swap them.
- **Elastic** means it **returns** to shape. Saying only "it stretches" gets one mark at most.
- **Dense** means high mass for its volume (a compact structure), not "strong" or "floats".
- **Fusible** means it melts and can be joined or cast, not that it burns.
- In density sums, **convert mm³ to cm³ by dividing by 1000**, and always give units.
:::

::: test
Describe the characteristics of a material with elastic properties. (2)
---
It stretches, bends or changes shape under a force (1) and returns to its original shape or length when the force is removed (1). Full 2 marks for a single detailed statement covering both.
:::

::: test
Describe what is meant by toughness. (2)
---
The ability of a material to absorb energy or withstand an impact (1) without breaking, cracking or shattering, e.g. polypropylene or mild steel (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why aluminium is used for drinks cans, referring to its working properties. (2)
---
It is malleable, so thin sheet can be pressed and drawn into the can shape without cracking (1); it is also light and corrosion resistant, so cans are cheap to transport and do not react with the drink (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the base of a saucepan is made from a metal and the handle from a thermosetting polymer. (4)
---
Metal is a good thermal conductor (1), so heat passes quickly from the hob to cook the food (1). The thermosetting polymer is a poor thermal conductor (1), so the handle stays cool enough to hold, and it does not soften when hot (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why cotton is used for towels, and why synthetic fibres such as polyester are used for sportswear that must dry quickly. (4)
---
Cotton is highly absorbent (1), so it soaks up water from the skin (1). Polyester has low absorbency (1), so sweat passes through or evaporates rather than being held in the fibres, and the garment dries quickly and stays light (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a hammer handle needs to be tough and the hammer head needs to be hard. (4)
---
The handle must be tough (1) so that it absorbs the repeated shocks of striking without cracking or snapping (1). The head must be hard (1) so that its striking face does not dent, chip or wear when it hits nails (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what is meant by fusibility and why it is useful when manufacturing metal products. (3)
---
Fusibility is the ability to melt when heated and fuse (1). Molten metal can be poured into moulds to cast complex shapes (1), and metal parts can be joined permanently by welding, brazing or soldering (1).
:::

::: test
A copper block measures 50 mm × 40 mm × 10 mm. The density of copper is 8.9 g/cm³. Calculate the mass of the block in grams. (3)
---
Volume $= 50 \times 40 \times 10 = 20\,000$ mm³ (1) $= 20$ cm³ (1). Mass $= 8.9 \times 20 = 178$ g (1).
:::

::: test
A material has a density of 2.7 g/cm³. Convert this to kg/m³ and write the answer in standard form. (2)
---
$2.7 \times 1000 = 2700$ kg/m³ (1) $= 2.7 \times 10^3$ kg/m³ (1).
:::

::: test
A designer is choosing a material for a child's outdoor play slide. Explain which physical and working properties the material needs and why. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): several relevant physical and working properties, each clearly explained in relation to the slide, its user and its environment.
Level 2 (3–4): some relevant properties with some explanation.
Level 1 (1–2): properties listed with little or no explanation.
Indicative content: low absorbency / water resistant, so it does not rot, swell or become slippery-soft in rain; poor thermal conductivity, so the surface does not become dangerously hot in sun or very cold in winter (a reason to prefer polymers such as HDPE over metal); toughness, so it absorbs impacts and does not crack or shatter into sharp pieces; hardness, so the surface resists scratching and wear from shoes and stays smooth; strength (bending and compressive), so it supports the weight of children without deforming; low density, so it is light to transport and install; fusibility of thermoforming polymers allows rotational or blow moulding into one seamless piece; UV stability desirable. Credit any suitable named material (e.g. HDPE or rotationally moulded polyethylene) with justified properties.
:::
