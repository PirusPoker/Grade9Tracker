## What it is

A **prototype** is a working, full-size (or near full-size) version of a product or system, made to test and demonstrate a design before it is manufactured. In the spec, "prototype" can mean a **product** or a **system**. Designers develop prototypes in response to the **client's wants and needs**, then **evaluate** them and improve them.

This topic is behind the biggest single question in Section C: a picture of a product plus a short **specification**, then "**Evaluate** the product in terms of (a) … (b) … (c) …", worth **4 + 4 + 4 = 12 marks**. The headings are drawn from the spec list below (user needs and wants, innovation, function, aesthetics, marketability) or from nearby topics (anthropometrics and ergonomics, sustainability). In the app the picture is replaced by a written description.

**What a successful prototype must do**

| Criterion | What it means | What to look for in a product |
|---|---|---|
| **Satisfy the requirements of the brief** | It solves the problem stated in the brief and meets each specification point | go through the specification point by point: is each one met, and how well? |
| **Respond to client wants and needs** | Needs are essential; wants are desirable extras | a need met (holds 2 litres, safe for children) versus a want (choice of colours, app control) |
| **Demonstrate innovation** | Something new or improved: a new material, technology, feature, form or way of using it | a feature rivals don't have, a new combination of functions, smart or modern materials, connectivity |
| **Be functional** | It works as intended, reliably and safely, in its real environment | does it do the job, is it stable, strong, easy to operate, durable, safe? |
| **Consider aesthetics** | Appearance, colour, form, texture, finish, style, and how it suits the target market | does the look appeal to the intended user and fit where it will be used? |
| **Be potentially marketable** | It could be sold profitably: a clear target market, a competitive price, a selling point, and it can be manufactured at scale | USP, price against rivals, brand, cost to make, packaging, sustainability credentials |

**Evaluating a prototype**

"Evaluate" means **make judgements**: weigh strengths against weaknesses, using evidence, and reach a view. Three skills in the spec:

- **Reflect critically and respond to feedback.** Test the prototype against the specification and with the client or users (trial use, questionnaires, focus group, expert opinion). Record results honestly, often in a table: spec point, test, result, met or not met, action. Critical reflection means admitting what did **not** work, and why.
- **Suggest modifications through inception and manufacture.** Improvements can be proposed at any stage, from the original idea (inception) to making at scale. A good modification names the **change**, the **reason** (the evidence), and the **effect**: "Increase the base diameter from 80 to 110 mm, because two of five users knocked it over in testing, so it will be more stable." At manufacture stage, modifications might be changing a material or process for batch or mass production (e.g. a hand-made timber part becomes injection-moulded polypropylene).
- **Assess fitness for purpose.** Is it fit for its intended purpose, for its intended users, in its intended context? A product can be beautifully made but not fit for purpose if it fails the key need.

**Presenting data (maths link).** Evaluations often tabulate user feedback: ratings out of 5, a tally of yes/no, a bar chart comparing the prototype with a rival, or an average score. You may need to calculate a mean score or percentage and use it as evidence.

**How to write a 4-mark evaluation part**

The mark scheme for each 4-mark part is levelled:

- **3–4 marks**: detailed evaluation: several specific features, each linked to the heading with a reason, including judgement (how well, strengths and weaknesses).
- **1–2 marks**: basic analysis: one or two simple points, mostly description, little judgement.

So for each part aim for **three or four linked points**, each in the form **feature → effect on the user → judgement**. Use evidence from the picture/description and specification, and include at least one **limitation**. Don't repeat the same point under all three headings: needs and wants, ergonomics and innovation each need different features.

**A model structure**

"The [feature] means [effect for the user], which [meets / partly meets] the need for [spec point]. However, [limitation], so [judgement]."

Example, a child's scooter, on "ergonomics": "The handlebar height adjusts across three positions, so it suits children of different heights as they grow; the soft rubber grips are shaped for small hands and reduce slipping; the low deck makes it easy to push off. However, the brake is at the back and needs a firm press, which the lightest riders may struggle with, so it is well suited to most users but not the youngest."

::: terms
- **Prototype**: a working version of a product or system made to test and demonstrate a design before manufacture.
- **Client**: the person or organisation the product is designed for, whose needs and wants set the brief.
- **Needs**: features a product must have for it to work for the user.
- **Wants**: features the user would like but that are not essential.
- **Innovation**: introducing something new or improved, such as a feature, material, technology or form.
- **Functionality**: how well a product works and does the job it is intended for.
- **Aesthetics**: how a product appeals to the senses, especially its form, colour, texture and finish.
- **Marketability**: how likely a product is to sell profitably to its target market.
- **Fit for purpose**: meeting the needs of the intended user in the intended situation.
- **Modification**: a change to a design to improve it, based on evidence from testing or feedback.
- **Evaluation**: judging the strengths and weaknesses of a product against criteria such as the specification.
- **USP**: unique selling point, a feature that makes a product different from its rivals.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the specification and the product description** first, and underline features that relate to each heading.
2. **For each 4-mark part, make three or four points**, all about that heading only.
3. **Write each point as feature → effect → judgement**: what it is, what it does for the user, how well.
4. **Include a limitation or an improvement** in each part; evaluation needs weaknesses as well as strengths.
5. **Use the specification's words** to show you are measuring against it.
6. **For modification questions**, give the change, the evidence for it, and the benefit.
7. **Finish each part with a short judgement** ("Overall it largely meets…").
:::

::: example Needs and wants
A cordless kettle has a 1.7 litre capacity, a 360° base, a window showing the water level, an auto switch-off, a cool-touch plastic body and a choice of four colours. Evaluate the kettle in terms of satisfying the needs and wants of the user. (4)

The auto switch-off and cool-touch body meet the essential need for safety, because the kettle turns off when boiled and won't burn the user's hand (1). The 1.7 litre capacity boils enough for a family, while the water window lets the user fill only what they need, saving energy and time (1). The 360° base means it can be lifted and replaced from any angle, which suits left- and right-handed users (1). The four colours meet a want rather than a need, letting customers match their kitchen; however, a plastic body may look less premium than stainless steel and could stain over time, so it meets needs very well and wants reasonably well (1).
:::

::: example Innovation
A smart backpack has a built-in USB charging port with a pocket for a power bank, a hidden anti-theft zip against the back panel, reflective strips and a water-resistant fabric made from recycled bottles. Evaluate the innovation in the design. (4)

The integrated charging port is an innovative convenience: users charge a phone while walking without opening the bag, though the feature relies on the user supplying a power bank (1). The anti-theft zip against the back is a new take on security, because thieves can't open it while it's worn, which suits commuters on busy transport (1). Recycled-bottle fabric is innovative in sustainability and a strong selling point, while still being water resistant (1). The reflective strips are useful for safety but not new, as many bags have them, so overall the bag is innovative through combining features rather than through any one breakthrough (1).
:::

::: example Suggesting modifications
A prototype bedside lamp was tested by five users. Three said the switch was hard to find in the dark, and two knocked it over. Suggest two modifications and justify each one. (4)

Move the switch to the base and use a touch-sensitive or illuminated switch (1), because users could not find it in the dark, so it would be easier to operate when half asleep (1). Widen and weight the base, for example with a steel plate inside (1), because two of five users knocked it over, so a lower centre of gravity would make it more stable and safer (1).
:::

::: example Fit for purpose
Explain what is meant by a prototype being fit for purpose. (2)

It meets the needs of the intended user in the situation where it will be used (1): it works reliably, safely and as the specification requires, not just looking good (1).
:::

::: example Presenting feedback
Six users rated a prototype chair for comfort out of 10: 7, 8, 6, 9, 7, 5. The specification requires a mean comfort score of at least 7. Calculate the mean and state whether the specification is met. (3)

$7 + 8 + 6 + 9 + 7 + 5 = 42$ (1). $42 \div 6 = 7$ (1). The mean is 7, so the specification is just met (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways a designer could gather feedback on a prototype. (2)
---
Any two: user trial/testing; questionnaire; interview with the client; focus group; expert opinion; testing against the specification.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a need and a want. (2)
---
A need is essential for the product to do its job (1); a want is desirable but not essential, e.g. a choice of colour (1).
:::

::: q
Give three features that make a product potentially marketable. (3)
---
Any three: clear target market; competitive price; unique selling point; appealing aesthetics; can be manufactured at a profit; strong brand; meets user needs; sustainability credentials.
:::

::: q
Explain why a designer should test a prototype against the specification. (3)
---
The specification sets measurable criteria from the research (1); testing each point shows objectively which are met and which are not (1), so modifications target the real weaknesses before manufacture (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why a designer might change a material when a prototype moves into mass production. (2)
---
A material suited to one-off making may be too slow or costly at scale (1), e.g. a hand-shaped timber part is changed to injection-moulded polypropylene for fast, identical, cheap production (1).
:::

::: q
A picnic cool bag has a padded shoulder strap, foil-lined insulation, a wipe-clean lining, a front zip pocket and a folding design. Evaluate the cool bag in terms of its function. (4)
---
3–4: detailed evaluation of several features with judgement, e.g. foil-lined insulation keeps food cold for a trip; wipe-clean lining is hygienic after spills; padded strap spreads weight while carrying; folds flat for storage; but no ice pack or rigid sides so contents can be crushed and it won't stay cold all day. 1–2: basic points, little judgement.
:::

::: watch
- **Evaluate means judge**: describing features without saying how well they work caps you at 2 of 4.
- Keep each part **on its heading**. Innovation needs **new or improved** features; ergonomics needs **fit, comfort and ease of use**.
- Use the **specification** given. Examiners reward points linked to its bullet points.
- Include a **weakness or limitation** in every part.
- Modifications need **change + evidence + benefit**. "Make it better" earns nothing.
- Don't invent features the description doesn't show; infer sensibly from what is given.
:::

---

::: test
Eight users scored a prototype backpack's comfort out of 5: 4, 5, 3, 4, 2, 5, 4, 5. Calculate the mean score and the percentage of users who gave 4 or more. (4)
---
Total $= 32$ (1); mean $= 32 \div 8 = 4$ (1). Users giving 4 or more $= 6$ (1); $6 \div 8 \times 100 = 75\%$ (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a designer should respond to negative feedback on a prototype. (3)
---
Reflect critically and identify which specification points or user needs were not met (1); find the cause through further testing (1); suggest and make modifications, then re-test in another iteration (1).
:::

::: test
A prototype garden bird feeder cracked after one frost and squirrels could reach the seed. Suggest two modifications and justify them. (4)
---
Change to a frost-resistant material such as UV-stabilised polycarbonate or powder-coated steel (1), because the prototype cracked in cold weather, so it will last outdoors (1). Add a squirrel guard or a spring-loaded perch that closes under heavy weight (1), because squirrels reached the seed, so only birds can feed (1).
:::

::: test
Study this product and specification. A children's electric toothbrush has a rechargeable battery with a charging stand, a two-minute timer that plays a tune, a small round brush head, a chunky rubber-grip handle, a range of cartoon designs, and an app that rewards brushing. Specification: suitable for ages 4–10; safe and hygienic; encourages good brushing habits; easy to hold; appealing to children. Evaluate the toothbrush in terms of: (a) satisfying the needs and wants of the user (4); (b) anthropometrics and ergonomics (4); (c) innovation in the design (4). (12)
---
Each part levelled: 3–4 detailed evaluation with judgement; 1–2 basic points, little evaluation.
(a) Indicative: two-minute timer meets the need to brush for the recommended time; small head suits children's mouths; cartoon designs meet the want to appeal; rechargeable so no battery changes; but relies on parents to supervise and the app needs a phone.
(b) Indicative: chunky grip suits small hands with less developed grip (5th–95th percentile of ages 4–10); rubber stops slipping when wet; small head sized to child mouths; weight of motor may tire youngest users; stand at a height children can reach.
(c) Indicative: app rewards and tune turn brushing into a game, new motivation; timer is common in adult brushes so less new; combination of app and physical product is innovative; judgement on how new overall.
:::

::: test
Study this product and specification. A folding camping chair has a powder-coated steel tube frame, a polyester fabric seat, a cup holder in the armrest, a carry bag with a shoulder strap and folds into a narrow bundle. It weighs 4 kg and holds users up to 120 kg. Specification: portable; strong and stable; weatherproof; comfortable for long periods; affordable. Evaluate the chair in terms of: (a) function (4); (b) aesthetics (4); (c) marketability (4). (12)
---
Each part levelled: 3–4 detailed evaluation with judgement; 1–2 basic points, little evaluation.
(a) Indicative: folds small into a bag so portable; steel frame strong, 120 kg limit covers most adults; powder coating resists rust; polyester is weatherproof and quick drying; but 4 kg is heavy to carry far and the flat seat may be uncomfortable for long periods.
(b) Indicative: simple functional form; colour choice of fabric; matching bag; industrial look of steel tube; may look plain; colour may fade in sun.
(c) Indicative: large camping and festival market; cheap materials and simple processes keep price low; cup holder is a selling point but common; many rival products so needs a price or brand advantage; judgement.
:::

::: test
Explain how a prototype can demonstrate innovation, and why this matters when trying to sell a new product. (6)
---
5–6: detailed explanation of several ways of showing innovation, linked clearly to marketability, with examples. 3–4: good explanation, some linking. 1–2: basic points. Indicative: new technology (connectivity, sensors), new or smart/modern materials, new combination of functions, new form or way of using, sustainable approach; gives a USP that distinguishes it from rivals; attracts early adopters; can justify a higher price; may be patented to protect it; creates media interest; but innovation must still meet user needs and be affordable to make.
:::
