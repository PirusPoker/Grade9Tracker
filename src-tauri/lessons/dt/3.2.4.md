## What it is

Every material starts as something dug, drilled, grown or farmed: a **primary source**. It has to be processed into a **workable form**, such as a reel of paper, a seasoned plank, an aluminium sheet, polymer granules or a cone of yarn, before a designer can use it. The spec lists five categories for this principle: **papers and boards, timbers, metals, polymers and textiles**. There is no electronics row here. You need the primary source and the **main stages in order** for each, plus the basics of a **life cycle assessment**.

### Summary of sources

| Category | Primary source | Main conversion stages |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | **cellulose fibres** from wood (mostly softwoods such as spruce and pine) and grasses (e.g. bamboo, straw, esparto); recycled waste paper | felling, debarking, chipping → **pulping** → cleaning (and bleaching) → mixing to a watery slurry with additives → spreading on a moving mesh to drain → pressing → drying on heated rollers → **calendering** (smoothing) → reels |
| **Timbers** | trees: **hardwoods** from broad-leaved (mostly deciduous) trees; **softwoods** from coniferous trees | felling → **conversion** (sawing logs into planks) → **seasoning** (drying) → planing to stock sizes; waste and low-grade wood → **manufactured boards** |
| **Metals** | **ores** mined from the Earth's crust, e.g. iron ore (haematite), **bauxite** (aluminium), copper ores | mining → crushing and concentrating → **extraction** (blast furnace or electrolysis) → **refining** to purity, and alloying → casting into ingots → rolling, extruding or drawing into stock forms |
| **Polymers** | mainly **crude oil** (also natural gas; a few from plants, e.g. PLA from corn starch) | drilling → **refining** by **fractional distillation** → **cracking** long molecules into short monomers → **polymerisation** → additives → granules, powder, sheet or film |
| **Textiles** | **animal** (wool, silk), **vegetable** (cotton, linen) and **chemical** sources: synthetics from oil (polyester, polyamide, elastane) and regenerated fibres from wood cellulose (viscose) | harvesting or producing the fibre → cleaning and **processing** (ginning, scouring, carding, combing, or extruding filaments) → **spinning** into yarn → weaving or knitting into fabric |

### Papers and boards

Wood is made of **cellulose fibres** held together by a natural glue called **lignin**. Trees are felled, debarked and chipped. **Mechanical pulping** grinds the chips. It is cheap and uses almost all the wood, but the lignin stays in, so the paper is weaker and yellows in light, as newsprint does. **Chemical pulping** cooks the chips with chemicals to dissolve the lignin, leaving stronger, purer fibres for quality paper and board. The pulp may be **bleached** white, then mixed with lots of water and additives: fillers such as china clay for smoothness, **sizing** to control absorbency, and dyes. On the paper machine the slurry is spread onto a moving wire mesh. Water drains away and the fibres mat together into a web, which is pressed between felt-covered rollers, dried over heated cylinders, **calendered** between polished rollers to a smooth finish, and wound onto reels. **Board** is thicker and heavier, often made from several layers (plies) bonded together. **Recycled** paper is re-pulped and de-inked, but the fibres get shorter each time, so paper can only be recycled a limited number of times and some new fibre is usually added.

### Timbers

After **felling**, logs go to a sawmill for **conversion**, which means cutting the log into usable planks and boards:
- **Through-and-through (plain) sawing**: parallel cuts right through the log. It is quick and cheap with little waste, but the wide boards are prone to **cupping** (warping across the width).
- **Quarter sawing**: the log is quartered, then cut roughly along the radius. The boards are more stable and show attractive figure, but there is more waste and more labour, so it costs more.

Freshly felled ("green") timber can be more than half water by mass. **Seasoning** dries it to a moisture content suited to where it will be used. Unseasoned timber shrinks, warps, splits and is prone to fungal rot as it dries in use.
- **Air seasoning**: planks are stacked with thin spacers (**stickers**) between them, under a roof, so air circulates. It is cheap and uses no fuel, but it is slow, taking months or years, and it cannot dry below the moisture level of the outdoor air. It suits outdoor timber.
- **Kiln seasoning**: planks are dried in a heated chamber with controlled heat, steam and airflow. It takes days or weeks, reaches the lower moisture content needed for centrally heated interiors, and kills insects and fungi. It costs more because it uses energy.

$\text{Moisture content (\%)} = \frac{\text{wet mass} - \text{dry mass}}{\text{dry mass}} \times 100$

Note that you divide by the **dry** mass.

**Manufactured boards** make good use of low-grade timber and waste:
- **Plywood**: thin veneers are peeled from a rotating log, then glued with the grain of each layer at 90° to the next (an odd number of layers) and pressed.
- **MDF**: wood is broken into fine fibres, mixed with resin adhesive, then pressed and heated into sheets.
- **Chipboard**: wood chips are bonded with resin under heat and pressure.

### Metals

Metals are found as **ores**, rock containing metal compounds (usually oxides). Ore is mined by open-cast or underground mining, then crushed and concentrated. **Extraction** releases the metal:
- **Iron**: iron ore, coke (carbon) and limestone are heated in a **blast furnace**. Carbon removes the oxygen from the iron oxide, and molten iron collects at the bottom. The limestone removes impurities as slag.
- **Steel**: this iron has too much carbon, so oxygen is blown through it to burn off the excess, giving steel. Steel is also made by melting scrap in an **electric arc furnace**.
- **Aluminium**: bauxite is refined into aluminium oxide (alumina), then split by **electrolysis**, which uses enormous amounts of electricity. That is why recycling aluminium saves so much energy.
- **Copper**: ores are concentrated and smelted, then purified by electrolysis.

**Refining** removes impurities to reach the purity needed. **Alloying** elements are added at this stage, for example chromium for stainless steel. The metal is cast into ingots or billets, then **rolled** into sheet, **extruded** into sections and tube, or **drawn** into wire and rod.

### Polymers

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons of different chain lengths. At the refinery it is heated and fed into a **fractional distillation** column. The column is hot at the bottom and cooler towards the top. Vapours rise and **condense at different heights** according to their boiling points, separating the oil into **fractions**: gases at the top, then naphtha, kerosene and diesel, down to heavy oils and bitumen at the bottom. There is too much of the long-chain fractions and not enough short ones, so these are **cracked**: heated with a catalyst to break long molecules into shorter, more useful ones, including **monomers** such as ethene and propene. **Polymerisation** joins thousands of monomers into long chains. Ethene becomes polyethylene, and propene becomes polypropylene. Additives are mixed in (pigments, stabilisers, plasticisers, flame retardants), and the polymer is supplied as **granules, powder, sheet, film or rod**.

### Textiles

- **Animal**: **wool** is sheared from sheep, then **scoured** (washed to remove grease, dirt and lanolin) and **carded** (brushed so the fibres lie in the same direction). **Silk** comes from silkworm cocoons, which are soaked so the long continuous filament can be unwound (reeled).
- **Vegetable**: **cotton** is the fluffy fibre around the seeds in the cotton boll. It is picked, then **ginned** to separate fibre from seed, cleaned, carded and combed. **Linen** comes from the stem of the flax plant.
- **Chemical**: **synthetic** fibres (polyester, polyamide/nylon, elastane) are made from oil-based polymers. **Regenerated** fibres (viscose) are made from wood cellulose dissolved in chemicals. Either way the liquid is forced through a **spinneret** (a plate with tiny holes) to form continuous **filaments**, which are cooled or set, then stretched to align the molecules. Filaments can be cut into short **staple** fibres to blend with natural ones.
- **Spinning**: short staple fibres are drawn out into a thin strand and **twisted**. The twist locks the fibres together and gives the yarn strength. Several yarns may be twisted together into a stronger **plied** yarn. Yarn is then **woven** or **knitted** into fabric.

### Life cycle assessment (LCA)

An **LCA** measures a product's environmental impact at every stage, "**cradle to grave**", so that materials and designs can be compared fairly. At each stage it measures energy and water use, emissions (especially CO₂), and waste.

| Stage | What is assessed | Example: an aluminium drinks can |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Raw material extraction | mining, drilling, felling or farming; land, energy, pollution | bauxite mining; habitat loss |
| 2 Processing and manufacture | energy and waste in converting and making | electrolysis (very high electricity); rolling sheet; forming the can |
| 3 Distribution | transport and packaging | lorries to filling plants and shops |
| 4 Use | energy, water or consumables during use; lifespan | little impact; chilled in a fridge |
| 5 End of life | reuse, **recycling**, incineration or landfill | recycled into new cans using a fraction of the energy of new metal |

An LCA can reveal surprises. A heavier reusable product may beat a light disposable one only if it is reused many times. Its results depend on the data and assumptions used, so two LCAs of the same product can disagree. Recycling "**closes the loop**" by feeding stage 5 back into stage 2, avoiding stage 1.

::: terms
- **Primary source**: the raw, natural origin of a material, such as a tree, an ore, crude oil or a cotton plant.
- **Cellulose fibres**: the natural plant fibres, from wood and grasses, that mat together to form paper and board.
- **Pulping**: breaking wood chips down, mechanically or chemically, into separate fibres to make paper.
- **Conversion**: sawing a felled log into usable planks and boards, e.g. through-and-through or quarter sawing.
- **Seasoning**: drying timber in air or a kiln to reduce its moisture content, so it is stable and resists rot.
- **Ore**: rock containing enough of a metal compound to make extracting the metal worthwhile.
- **Extraction**: releasing a metal from its ore, e.g. in a blast furnace or by electrolysis.
- **Refining**: removing impurities from a material, such as a metal or crude oil, to purify or separate it.
- **Fractional distillation**: separating crude oil into fractions by boiling point in a tall column that is hottest at the bottom.
- **Cracking**: breaking long hydrocarbon molecules into shorter, more useful ones using heat and a catalyst.
- **Spinning**: drawing out and twisting fibres together to make a continuous, strong yarn.
- **Life cycle assessment**: measuring the environmental impact of a product at every stage, from raw material to disposal.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the primary source precisely**: "bauxite ore", "crude oil", "the cotton boll", "softwood trees". Not "the ground" or "nature".
2. **Give the stages in the right order**, one step per mark in "describe" questions, using the technical word for each stage: pulping, conversion, seasoning, extraction, refining, fractional distillation, cracking, carding, spinning.
3. **Say what each stage achieves**: seasoning *reduces moisture so the timber doesn't warp*; cracking *makes short monomers*.
4. **Don't mix up similar stages**: conversion (cutting) against seasoning (drying); extraction against refining; distillation (separating) against cracking (breaking).
5. For **moisture content**, subtract the dry mass from the wet mass, then **divide by the dry mass** and multiply by 100.
6. For **LCA or life cycle** answers, go through all five stages with specific detail for your material, and give an environmental impact and an improvement at each.
:::

::: example Explain: seasoning
Explain why timber is seasoned before it is used to make furniture. (2)

Seasoning reduces the moisture content of the timber (1), so it will not shrink, warp or split as it dries out in a warm room, and it is less likely to rot or suffer fungal attack (1).
:::

::: example Describe: paper making
Describe the main stages in converting trees into paper. (4)

Trees are felled, debarked and cut into chips (1). The chips are pulped, mechanically by grinding or chemically by cooking, to separate the cellulose fibres, and the pulp may be bleached (1). The pulp is mixed with water and additives into a slurry and spread onto a moving mesh, where the water drains and the fibres mat together (1). The wet web is pressed between rollers, dried over heated cylinders and calendered smooth before being wound onto reels (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: moisture content
A sample of freshly sawn oak has a mass of 150 g. After drying in an oven its mass is 125 g. Calculate its moisture content. (3)

Water lost $= 150 - 125 = 25$ g (1)
$\frac{25}{125}$ (1) $\times 100 = 20\%$ (1)
:::

::: example Describe: polymers from crude oil
Describe how polymers are produced from crude oil. (4)

Crude oil is drilled and transported to a refinery (1), where it is heated and separated by fractional distillation: vapours rise up a column and condense at different heights according to boiling point (1). Long-chain fractions are cracked, using heat and a catalyst, into shorter molecules, including monomers such as ethene (1). The monomers are joined into long chains by polymerisation, e.g. ethene into polyethylene, then additives are mixed in and the polymer is formed into granules (1).
:::

::: example Describe: life cycle (extended)
Describe the life cycle of a cotton T-shirt. (6)

Raw material: cotton is grown on farms, often needing large amounts of water, fertiliser and pesticide. It is picked, ginned to remove the seeds, cleaned and carded. Processing and manufacture: the fibres are spun into yarn, knitted into jersey fabric, then bleached and dyed, which uses water, energy and chemicals that can pollute rivers. The fabric is cut and sewn into T-shirts, often in factories abroad. Distribution: finished garments are shipped and trucked to warehouses and shops, adding transport emissions. Use: over its life the T-shirt is washed and tumble dried many times, which can use more energy than making it. End of life: it may be reused through charity shops, recycled into wiping cloths or insulation, or landfilled, where cotton biodegrades but gives off methane. Improvements include organic cotton, washing at lower temperatures, and take-back schemes. (Level 3: 5–6 marks)
:::

::: q
Name the primary source of aluminium. (1)
---
Bauxite (ore).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between conversion and seasoning of timber. (2)
---
Conversion is sawing the log into planks or boards (1); seasoning is drying those planks to reduce their moisture content (1).
:::

::: q
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of kiln seasoning compared with air seasoning. (2)
---
Advantage: much faster, or reaches a lower moisture content, or kills insects and fungi (1). Disadvantage: costs more because it uses energy and equipment, or can cause splits if dried too fast (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what happens to crude oil in a fractional distillation column. (3)
---
The oil is heated so most of it vaporises (1); the vapour rises up a column that is hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top (1); fractions condense at different heights according to their boiling points and are drawn off separately (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how synthetic fibres such as polyester are made into yarn. (3)
---
The polymer is melted and forced through a spinneret with tiny holes (1) to form continuous filaments, which are cooled and stretched to align the molecules (1); the filaments are twisted into yarn, or cut into staple fibres and spun (1).
:::

::: q
A piece of pine weighs 230 g. After oven drying it weighs 200 g. Calculate its moisture content. (2)
---
$(230 - 200) \div 200$ (1) $\times 100 = 15\%$ (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Conversion** is cutting; **seasoning** is drying. Most students write one when asked for the other.
- **Extraction** gets the metal out of the ore; **refining** purifies it. **Distillation** separates crude oil; **cracking** breaks molecules.
- Moisture content divides by the **dry** mass, not the wet mass.
- Name the **precise source**: bauxite, crude oil, the cotton boll, silkworm cocoons, softwood trees.
- "Describe the stages" earns **one mark per correct stage in order**, so don't skip the obvious first step (felling, mining, drilling, shearing, harvesting).
- There is no electronics category for this principle; if a question offers a list, it will be from the five material areas.
:::

Describe the stages in converting iron ore into steel. (4)
---

Describe how cotton is processed from the plant into yarn. (4)
---
Cotton bolls are picked from the plant (1); the fibres are ginned to separate them from the seeds, then cleaned (1); the fibres are carded (and combed) so they lie parallel (1); they are drawn out and twisted (spun) into yarn (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one environmental impact of extracting aluminium from bauxite. (3)
---
Either route, 1 mark each for the impact, its effect and a developed consequence. Mining: bauxite is usually strip mined (1), clearing forest and topsoil and destroying habitats (1), and the toxic red mud waste can pollute land and water (1). Energy: electrolysis uses enormous amounts of electricity (1); if this comes from fossil fuels it releases a large amount of CO₂ (1), adding to global warming (1).

::: test
A sample of green timber has a mass of 84 g. After oven drying, its mass is 70 g. Calculate its moisture content. Show your working. (3)
---
$84 - 70 = 14$ g of water (1); $14 \div 70 = 0.2$ (1); $\times 100 = 20\%$ (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage of making manufactured boards such as MDF and chipboard. (2)
---
They use waste and low-grade timber such as offcuts, chips and sawdust (1), so more of each tree is used and fewer high-quality trees need to be felled; they also come in large, stable sheets that do not warp (1).

Choose one material from: cartridge paper · oak · aluminium · high density polyethylene (HDPE) · wool. (a) Name the primary source of your chosen material. (b) Describe the main stages in converting it into a workable form. (1 + 4) (5)

::: test
Describe the stages of a life cycle assessment. (4)
---
Raw material extraction: the impact of mining, drilling, felling or farming (1); processing and manufacture: energy, emissions and waste in making the product (1); distribution and use: transport, and energy or resources used during its life (1); end of life: reuse, recycling, incineration or landfill (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one reason why paper cannot be recycled indefinitely. (2)
---
Each time paper is re-pulped, the cellulose fibres are broken and become shorter (1), so after several cycles they are too short to bond into strong paper, and new fibre must be added (1).
:::

Choose one material category from: papers and boards · timbers · metals · polymers · textiles. Describe the life cycle of a product made from a material in your chosen category. (6)
