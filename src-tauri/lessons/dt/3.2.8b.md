## What it is

Industry makes products that **work and fit every time**. That depends on three things:
- **tolerances**: the acceptable range of sizes a part may be made to;
- **commercial processes**: the industrial methods the spec names for each material category;
- **quality control (QC)**: **measurable** checks made **during manufacture** to keep parts within tolerance.

### Tolerance: manufacturing to minimum and maximum sizes

No process is perfectly accurate, so drawings give a **nominal** (target) size plus a **tolerance**: the permitted variation.
- $50 \pm 0.5$ mm means the part is acceptable anywhere from **49.5 mm (minimum)** to **50.5 mm (maximum)**.
- A tolerance can be one-sided: $20^{+0.2}_{-0.0}$ mm allows 20.0 mm to 20.2 mm, so a peg can be slightly bigger but never smaller.
- Electronic components use **percentage** tolerances. A 470 Ω resistor at ±5% can be anywhere from $470 \times 0.95 = 446.5$ Ω to $470 \times 1.05 = 493.5$ Ω.

**Why tolerances matter:**
- Parts from different machines, batches or suppliers must **fit together and be interchangeable**, like a lid on a box, a bolt in a hole, or a KD fitting in a pre-drilled panel.
- The product must **function**: a loose bearing rattles, and a tight one seizes.
- They set a clear, **measurable standard** for accepting or **rejecting** parts.
- **Tighter tolerances cost more**, because they need more precise machines, slower work and more inspection. So designers set tolerances only as tight as function needs: tight for an engine piston, loose for a garden planter.

### Commercial processes

| Category | Process | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | **Offset lithography** | based on **oil and water not mixing**. A metal **printing plate** carries the image: image areas attract the oily **ink**, and non-image areas are dampened with water, which repels it. The inked image is transferred (**offset**) from the plate cylinder onto a rubber **blanket cylinder**, then onto the paper as it passes the impression cylinder. Full colour uses four units, **CMYK** (cyan, magenta, yellow, black), one plate each. Fast, high quality and cheap per copy on long runs; making the plates makes short runs costly. Used for magazines, leaflets and packaging. |
| | **Die cutting** | a **steel-rule die** (sharp **cutting rules** and blunt **creasing rules** set in a plywood base, with rubber strips to eject the card) is pressed onto printed sheets, cutting and creasing many nets in one stroke. The waste is then stripped away. Used for cartons and point-of-sale displays. |
| **Timbers** | **Routing** | a **router** spins a cutter at very high speed to cut profiles, grooves, rebates, pockets and decorative edges. A **CNC router** moves the cutter in x, y and z from CAD data, cutting flat-pack panels and holes accurately and repeatably. |
| | **Turning** | on a **wood lathe** the work rotates while the turner shapes it with gouges, skews and parting tools on a tool rest. **Spindle turning** between centres makes chair legs and handles; **faceplate turning** makes bowls. In industry, CNC or copying lathes turn identical parts. |
| **Metals** | **Milling** | a rotating multi-tooth cutter removes metal from work fed past it on the table, making flat faces, slots, steps and pockets. **CNC milling machines** work to very fine tolerances. |
| | **Casting** | molten metal is poured or forced into a mould. **Sand casting** uses a sand mould made from a pattern and suits low volumes and large parts. **Die casting** injects molten aluminium or zinc alloy under pressure into reusable **steel dies** for high volumes and fine detail, as in engine parts and door handles. |
| **Polymers** | **Injection moulding** | granules are melted by a heated barrel and screw, then forced under pressure into a closed mould, cooled and ejected (see 3.2.8a). Complex, accurate parts are made in large numbers. |
| | **Extrusion** | molten polymer is forced continuously through a shaped **die**, cooled in a water bath and cut to length, giving pipes, rods, window sections and cable coatings. |
| **Textiles** | **Weaving** | lengthwise **warp** yarns are held under tension on a loom, and crosswise **weft** yarns pass over and under them. **Plain weave** goes over one, under one. The edges form a **selvedge** that doesn't fray. Industrial looms fire the weft across by rapier or air jet. |
| | **Dyeing** | colour is applied at the fibre, yarn, fabric (piece) or garment stage. Material is immersed in a dye bath with fixing agents (**mordants**) so the colour is **colourfast**. Some dyes, such as indigo, are fixed by **oxidation** in air. |
| | **Printing** | **screen printing** (flat-bed or rotary; one screen per colour; ink forced through the mesh with a squeegee), **digital inkjet**, and **heat transfer / sublimation** printing onto polyester. |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | **Pick-and-place assembly** | an automated machine takes **surface-mount** components from reels using vacuum nozzles and places them precisely on **solder paste** on the PCB pads, following CAD coordinates at thousands of parts per hour. |
| | **Flow soldering** | in **wave (flow) soldering** the PCB passes over a flowing wave of molten solder, which solders all the through-hole joints in one pass. Surface-mount boards are usually **reflow** soldered: the board is heated in an oven so the solder paste melts. |

### Quality control: measurable checks during manufacture

**Quality control** means inspecting and testing products **at stages during manufacture** against the specification, using **measurable** criteria, so faults are caught early and faulty parts are rejected before more money is spent on them. **Quality assurance** is the wider system of procedures, training and standards that makes sure quality is built in throughout.

| Category | QC check the spec names | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | **registration marks** | cross-hair marks printed outside the trimmed area by **every colour plate**. If the plates are aligned, the marks sit exactly on top of each other as one crisp black cross. If one colour is out, the marks separate and the image looks blurred or has coloured edges. **Colour bars** check ink density, and **crop marks** show where to trim. |
| **Timbers** | **go/no-go fixture** (gauge) | a gauge cut to the **maximum and minimum** sizes. The part must fit in the **"go"** side and must **not** fit in the **"no-go"** side, e.g. a tenon or dowel thickness. It gives a fast pass/fail check with no measuring or reading of numbers. |
| **Metals** | **depth stop** | set on a pillar drill or milling machine so the tool **cannot go deeper** than the set depth, so every hole or slot is the same depth. |
| **Polymers** | **correct laser settings** | the **power**, **speed**, frequency and **focus** must suit the polymer and its thickness. Wrong settings give incomplete cuts, melted or burnt edges and a wider **kerf**, so parts come out the wrong size. Test cuts are made, and proven settings are recorded and reused. |
| **Textiles** | **checking a repeating print against the original sample** | the pattern repeat is measured and compared with the approved sample for **size, alignment and colour** (under standard lighting), so every length of fabric matches. |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | **UV exposure, developing and etching times** in PCB manufacture | each stage is **timed**, and the chemicals are kept at the right temperature. Under-exposure or under-developing leaves resist where it isn't wanted, so copper bridges between tracks. Over-exposure, over-developing or over-etching thins or breaks tracks. Test strips set the right times. |

Other measurable QC tools include **vernier callipers**, micrometers, feeler gauges and templates, and **sampling**: checking, say, one item in fifty and adjusting the machine if a sample is drifting towards the tolerance limit.

::: terms
- **Tolerance**: the permitted variation from a nominal size, given as a maximum and minimum acceptable measurement.
- **Nominal size**: the target size shown on a drawing, before the tolerance is applied.
- **Quality control**: measurable checks and inspections carried out during manufacture to find and reject faulty parts.
- **Quality assurance**: the overall system of procedures and standards that makes sure quality is built into every stage.
- **Offset lithography**: a printing process where oil-based ink on a plate is transferred to a rubber blanket and then onto paper.
- **Registration marks**: printed cross-hair marks used to check that each colour plate is exactly aligned.
- **Go/no-go gauge**: a fixture with maximum and minimum limits that checks whether a part is within tolerance without measuring it.
- **Depth stop**: an adjustable stop on a drill or mill that prevents the tool cutting deeper than a set depth.
- **Routing**: cutting profiles, grooves and edges with a high-speed rotating cutter, often CNC controlled.
- **Warp and weft**: the lengthwise yarns held on the loom (warp) and the crosswise yarns woven through them (weft).
- **Pick and place**: an automated machine that places surface-mount components onto a PCB from CAD coordinates.
- **Flow soldering**: soldering all the joints on a circuit board at once by passing it over a wave of molten solder.
:::

::: method
1. **Tolerance calculations**: max = nominal + upper limit; min = nominal − lower limit. For a percentage, multiply the nominal by (1 ± %). State both values with units.
2. **Reject questions**: compare each measurement with the range, and say which are outside it. Values **equal** to a limit are acceptable.
3. **Describe a commercial process**: give the key equipment and stages in order (plate, ink and water, blanket, paper; hopper, screw, mould, ejector pins; warp, weft, loom).
4. **QC questions**: name the check, say **what it measures**, **when** it is done, and **what happens** if a part fails (adjust the machine, reject the part).
5. In the **8-mark** question, cover tolerance *and* QC checks across categories, with product examples, and evaluate the balance of cost against precision.
:::

::: example Calculate: tolerance limits
A shelf peg hole is specified as 8 mm ± 0.2 mm. State the maximum and minimum acceptable diameters. (2)

Maximum $= 8 + 0.2 = 8.2$ mm (1). Minimum $= 8 - 0.2 = 7.8$ mm (1).
:::

::: example Which parts are rejected?
A batch of spacers is specified as 25 mm ± 0.1 mm. Five spacers measure 25.08, 24.87, 25.10, 25.13 and 24.95 mm. Identify which spacers are rejected. (2)

Acceptable range: 24.9 mm to 25.1 mm (1). Rejected: **24.87 mm and 25.13 mm** (1). (25.10 mm is on the limit, so it is accepted.)
:::

::: example Describe: offset lithography
Describe how offset lithography prints an image onto paper. (4)

The image is put onto a metal printing plate wrapped around a cylinder (1). Rollers apply water and then oily ink. The ink sticks only to the image areas, because the damp non-image areas repel it (1). The inked image is transferred (offset) from the plate onto a rubber blanket cylinder (1). The blanket presses the image onto the paper as it passes between the blanket and impression cylinders, with one unit for each CMYK colour (1).
:::

::: example Explain: go/no-go
Explain how a go/no-go gauge is used to check the thickness of tenons in a batch of chairs. (3)

The gauge has two slots: one at the maximum permitted thickness (go) and one at the minimum (no-go) (1). Each tenon must slide into the go slot but must not enter the no-go slot (1). This shows instantly whether the tenon is within tolerance without measuring it, so it is fast and consistent, and tenons that fail are rejected or re-cut before assembly (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate: extended
Analyse and evaluate the importance of tolerances and quality control in the commercial manufacture of products. Give examples. (8)

Tolerances matter because mass-produced parts must fit together without adjustment. The holes for cam-lock fittings in a flat-pack wardrobe are CNC routed to a tight tolerance, so any customer can assemble panels from different batches. If the tolerance were loose, fittings would not line up and the product would fail, damaging the brand. Tighter tolerances cost more, though, needing precise CNC machines and more inspection. So a designer sets them only as tight as function needs: very tight for a die-cast engine part, looser for a printed leaflet's trim. Quality control catches faults during production, before money is wasted adding value to a faulty part. On a printed cereal box, registration marks show immediately if a colour plate has drifted, so the press can be adjusted before thousands of blurred boxes are printed. In PCB making, controlled UV exposure and etching times prevent broken or bridged tracks, which would make the whole device fail. Measurable checks such as go/no-go gauges and depth stops are fast and objective, so even low-skilled workers make consistent decisions. However, QC only finds faults after they happen. Quality assurance, with trained staff and maintained machines, prevents them. Overall, tolerances and QC are essential for customer satisfaction and cost control, but the level of precision should match the product's function and price. (Level 4: 7–8 marks)
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a tolerance on a dimension. (2)
---
The permitted variation from the nominal (target) size (1), given as a maximum and minimum measurement within which the part is still acceptable (1).
:::

::: q
A 1 kΩ resistor has a tolerance of ±5%. Calculate its maximum and minimum resistance. (2)
---
Maximum $= 1000 \times 1.05 = 1050$ Ω (1); minimum $= 1000 \times 0.95 = 950$ Ω (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the purpose of registration marks in printing. (2)
---
Each colour plate prints the marks in the same place (1), so checking that they line up exactly shows whether the colours are aligned; if not, the press is adjusted to avoid blurred or offset images (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a depth stop is used when drilling a batch of blind holes in metal parts. (2)
---
It stops the drill at a set depth every time (1), so all holes are the same depth, within tolerance, without measuring each one (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how a PCB is assembled using pick-and-place machines. (3)
---
Solder paste is applied to the PCB pads, e.g. through a stencil (1); the machine picks surface-mount components from reels with vacuum nozzles (1) and places them in exact positions from CAD coordinates, then the board is reflow soldered (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why correct laser settings are a quality control check for cutting acrylic. (3)
---
Power and speed must suit the acrylic's thickness (1); too little power or too much speed leaves incomplete cuts, while too much power melts or burns the edges and widens the kerf (1), so the parts would be the wrong size and fail tolerance checks; proven settings are tested and recorded (1).
:::

::: watch
- A value **on** a tolerance limit is **acceptable**. Only values beyond it are rejected.
- Give **both** the max and min, with units. For ± percentages, multiply, don't add the percentage as a number.
- **QC must be measurable**. "Look at it to see if it is good" is not quality control; name the check and what it measures.
- Match the spec's check to the category: registration marks (paper), go/no-go (timber), depth stop (metal), laser settings (polymer), repeat print (textiles), UV/develop/etch times (PCBs).
- Offset lithography depends on **oil and water not mixing**, and the **blanket** transfer step. Both are often missed.
- **Weaving** is warp and weft; don't confuse it with knitting (interlocking loops).
:::

::: test
Explain why designers do not specify very tight tolerances on every dimension. (2)
---
Tighter tolerances need more precise machines, slower processing and more inspection, so they cost more and cause more rejects (1); many dimensions don't affect function or fit, so a looser tolerance is acceptable and keeps the unit cost down (1).

::: test
Describe the process of weaving a plain-weave fabric. (3)
---
Warp yarns are stretched lengthwise on a loom under tension (1); weft yarns are passed across, alternately over one and under one warp yarn (1); each weft row is pushed tight against the last, and alternate rows reverse the pattern, forming a firm fabric with selvedge edges (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how flow (wave) soldering is used in the commercial manufacture of circuit boards. (3)
---
Components are inserted into the PCB, and flux is applied (1); the board is carried on a conveyor over a wave of molten solder flowing from a bath (1); the solder wets all the pads and leads at once, soldering every joint in one pass quickly and consistently (1).
:::

::: test
Choose one material category from: papers and boards · timbers · metals · polymers · textiles · electronic and mechanical systems. (a) Name one commercial process used in your category. (b) Describe how quality control is carried out during manufacture in your category. (1 + 3) (4)

:::

::: test
Explain the difference between quality control and quality assurance. (2)
---
Quality control is the inspection and measuring of products or parts during manufacture to find and reject faults (1); quality assurance is the overall system of procedures, training and standards that makes sure quality is achieved at every stage (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why PCB etching time must be controlled. (2)
---
Too short, and unwanted copper remains, so tracks can be bridged, causing short circuits (1); too long, and the etchant eats under the resist, making tracks thin or broken, so the circuit fails (1).
:::
