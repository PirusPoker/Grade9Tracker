## What it is

Designers do not work in a vacuum. Environmental, social and economic **challenges** shape what materials can be used, how products are made, and what customers will buy. The spec names three: **deforestation**, a possible **increase in carbon dioxide levels leading to potential global warming**, and **the need for fair trade**. For each, you must explain how it creates **constraints** (limits on what the designer can do) and **opportunities** (new markets, new materials, better products).

The three pillars:

| Pillar | Question the designer asks | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Environmental** | What does this product do to the planet across its life? | materials extracted, energy used, pollution, waste, end of life |
| **Social** | How does it affect people: workers, users, communities? | pay and conditions in factories, health and safety, culture, fair trade |
| **Economic** | Is it affordable to make and to buy, and does it support fair livelihoods? | cost of sustainable materials, prices, jobs, local economies |

**Deforestation**

Deforestation is the permanent clearing of forests, for timber, paper pulp, farming (cattle, palm oil, soya), mining and building. Its effects:

- fewer trees absorb carbon dioxide, and burning or rotting cleared trees releases stored carbon, adding to global warming;
- loss of **habitats** and **biodiversity**;
- **soil erosion** and flooding, because roots no longer hold soil and absorb rain;
- damage to the lives of **indigenous people** who depend on the forest.

**Constraints for designers:** hardwoods from tropical rainforests (e.g. mahogany, teak) may be unethical or restricted; paper, board and timber should come from certified sources, which may cost more; customers may reject products seen as harming forests.

**Opportunities for designers:**

- specify **FSC-certified** (Forest Stewardship Council) or **PEFC** timber, paper and board, from forests that are replanted and managed responsibly, and put the logo on the product as a selling point;
- use **fast-growing** or renewable alternatives: bamboo, softwoods from managed plantations;
- use **manufactured boards** (MDF, chipboard, plywood) that use timber waste, thinnings and small trees, so more of each tree is used;
- use **recycled** paper and board, and design packaging with less material;
- design for **disassembly and repair**, so timber products last longer.

**Carbon dioxide and potential global warming**

Burning **fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas) for electricity, industry and transport releases carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide is a **greenhouse gas**: it traps heat in the atmosphere, and rising levels are linked to global warming, with effects such as more extreme weather, rising sea levels and damage to farming. Almost every stage of a product's life uses energy and releases carbon dioxide: extracting raw materials, processing them (steel and aluminium making are very energy-hungry), manufacturing, **transport** (the "miles" a product travels), use (electrical products), and disposal (landfill and incineration).

The total greenhouse gas released by a product is its **carbon footprint**. How designers respond:

| Stage | What the designer can do |
|---|---|
| Materials | use fewer materials; choose **recycled** materials (recycled aluminium needs far less energy than making it from ore); choose low-energy and renewable materials such as FSC timber; source materials locally |
| Manufacture | choose efficient processes; factories powered by **renewable energy**; lean manufacturing to cut waste |
| Transport | make products closer to the market; flat-pack designs so more fit on each lorry; lightweight products that use less fuel |
| Use | design energy-efficient electrical products (LED lighting, low standby power); design for long life so fewer replacements are made |
| End of life | design for **disassembly**, repair, upgrade and **recycling**; avoid mixed materials that can't be separated; apply the **six Rs** |

**Constraints:** legislation and targets on emissions and energy labelling; the cost of greener materials and processes; customer expectations. **Opportunities:** a growing market of customers who choose low-carbon products; new products such as solar chargers, e-bikes and insulation; brand reputation; lower energy bills for the manufacturer.

**The need for fair trade**

Many raw materials and products (cotton, cocoa, coffee, tea, bananas, gold) are produced by farmers and workers in developing countries who have little bargaining power. World prices can fall below what it costs them to produce, and workers may face low pay, long hours, unsafe conditions and child labour.

**Fair trade** is a movement and certification scheme that aims to give these producers a better deal. Products carrying the **FAIRTRADE Mark** have been certified to meet standards. Producers receive:

- a **minimum price** that covers the cost of sustainable production, even if world prices fall;
- an extra **Fairtrade Premium**, which the community decides how to spend (schools, clean water, better farming equipment);
- safer working conditions, no forced or child labour, and the right to organise;
- **environmental** standards, e.g. limits on harmful pesticides.

**How fair trade affects design and making:**

- the designer must **source** certified materials, such as Fairtrade cotton for a T-shirt, which usually **costs more**, so the selling price rises or the profit margin shrinks (a constraint);
- supply may be **limited** in type or quantity;
- it gives a **marketing opportunity**: many customers will pay more for ethically made goods, and the logo on the swing tag or packaging tells them;
- it protects the company's **reputation**; firms found using exploited labour face boycotts and bad publicity;
- it supports **social sustainability**: the product's social footprint is smaller.

**Linking the challenges.** Deforestation and global warming are linked (fewer trees, more carbon dioxide). Fair trade links to both, since certified farming often includes environmental standards. In a Section C answer, always move from the **issue** to the **design decision**: "Because of deforestation, the designer specifies FSC-certified birch plywood, which costs slightly more but lets the product carry the FSC logo."

::: terms
- **Deforestation**: the permanent clearing of forests for timber, farming, mining or building.
- **Carbon footprint**: the total greenhouse gas, mainly carbon dioxide, released across a product's life.
- **Greenhouse gas**: a gas such as carbon dioxide that traps heat in the atmosphere and contributes to global warming.
- **Global warming**: the gradual rise in the Earth's average temperature linked to rising greenhouse gas levels.
- **FSC**: the Forest Stewardship Council, which certifies timber and paper from responsibly managed, replanted forests.
- **Fair trade**: a trading system that gives producers in developing countries a fair minimum price, a premium and decent working conditions.
- **Fairtrade Premium**: extra money paid on top of the price, spent by the producers' community on projects of their choice.
- **Constraint**: a factor that limits the choices open to a designer.
- **Opportunity**: a factor that opens a new market, material or way of designing.
- **Social footprint**: the impact a product has on the people involved in making, using and disposing of it.
- **Fossil fuels**: coal, oil and gas, finite fuels whose burning releases carbon dioxide.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the issue and its cause in one clause** ("burning fossil fuels in manufacture and transport releases carbon dioxide").
2. **Say what it means for the designer**: a constraint, an opportunity, or both.
3. **Give a specific design decision**: a named material (FSC plywood, recycled PET, Fairtrade cotton), process or feature (flat-pack, design for disassembly).
4. **Add the consequence**: cost, sales, reputation, footprint.
5. **For 3-mark "explain how" questions**, give one well-developed chain or three linked points. For 6-mark discuss questions, cover both constraints and opportunities, and conclude.
:::

::: example Carbon dioxide and design
Explain how increasing carbon dioxide levels in the atmosphere impact on the design and manufacture of products. (3)

Designers try to reduce a product's carbon footprint, so they use fewer materials and specify recycled materials such as recycled aluminium, which needs much less energy to produce than new metal (1). Manufacturers power factories with renewable energy and source materials locally, cutting the carbon dioxide released by processing and transport (1). Products are designed to last longer and be repaired, upgraded or recycled, so fewer new products must be made; electrical products are designed to use less energy in use (1).
:::

::: example Fair trade and design
Explain how the need for fair trade influences the design of a range of cotton T-shirts. (3)

The designer specifies Fairtrade-certified cotton, so the farmers who grow it receive a guaranteed minimum price and a premium for their community (1). This raises the cost of the fabric, so the designer may simplify the design or accept a higher retail price (1). The FAIRTRADE Mark can be printed on the swing tag as a selling point for ethically minded customers, improving the brand's reputation (1).
:::

::: example Deforestation opportunities
Give two ways a designer of wooden garden furniture can reduce the impact of deforestation. (2)

Specify FSC-certified timber from managed forests that are replanted (1). Use a fast-growing renewable alternative such as bamboo, or recycled/reclaimed timber (1).
:::

::: example Discuss both challenges
Discuss how deforestation and global warming influence the choice of materials for a new range of children's toys. (6)

Deforestation means tropical hardwoods would be unethical and harm the brand, so the designer would choose FSC-certified beech or birch plywood; this costs more than uncertified timber, but the logo reassures parents who care about the environment, so it becomes a selling point (2). Global warming pushes the designer to reduce the carbon footprint: timber stores carbon and needs less energy to process than new polymers, and recycled polymers or bio-based plastics could replace virgin ABS for parts that need colour or detail; sourcing materials locally and making the toys flat-packed would cut transport emissions (2). There is a trade-off: toys must be durable and safe, and some low-impact materials are less tough or less bright than ABS, so the designer might use certified timber for the main body with water-based non-toxic finishes, and design the toy to be handed down rather than replaced; overall the challenges constrain material choice but open a market for sustainable toys (2).
:::

::: watch
- Describing the issue is not enough. **Every point must end in a design or manufacturing decision.**
- Global warming is linked to **carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels**, not to the ozone hole.
- Name real responses: **FSC or PEFC**, **Fairtrade Mark**, **recycled aluminium**, **design for disassembly**, **renewable energy**.
- Fair trade is about **producers and workers** getting a fair price and conditions, not about low prices for customers.
- In "opportunities and constraints" questions, give **both**.
:::
