## What it is

Every design starts with **investigation**: finding out who the product is for, what they need and want, and what already exists. Section C of the paper tests whether you know the techniques, why a designer uses each one, and how the findings turn into a **design brief** and a **specification**.

**Primary and secondary data**

| Type | What it is | Examples | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary data** | Information you collect yourself, first hand, for this project | questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, measuring users, testing and taking apart products, observing people using a product | specific to your client and problem; up to date; you control the questions | slow and costly to collect; small samples can mislead |
| **Secondary data** | Information someone else has already collected for another purpose | books, websites, published anthropometric tables, market reports, government statistics, magazines, manufacturers' data sheets | quick and cheap; large samples; expert data such as body measurements | may be out of date, biased or not quite about your users |

A good investigation uses **both**: secondary data for the big picture (the size of the market, standard body sizes, material data) and primary data for the particular client.

**The investigation techniques named in the spec**

- **Market research**: finding out about the potential market before designing. It answers: who are the users (age, gender, income, lifestyle), how big is the market, what do competitors sell and at what price, and what gap is there? It uses questionnaires and surveys (often online), sales data and shop visits. **Quantitative** results (numbers, ticks, ratings) are easy to chart; **qualitative** results (opinions in people's own words) explain *why*.
- **Interviews**: a one-to-one conversation with the client, a user or an expert. Open questions ("What annoys you about your current lunch box?") draw out detail a questionnaire misses, and the designer can follow up on an answer. An interview can be recorded (audio or video) so nothing is lost. The weakness is that it is one person's view.
- **Human factors, including ergonomics**: how people physically and mentally interact with a product. **Ergonomics** is designing a product so it is comfortable, safe and efficient to use: the grip of a handle, the height of a desk, the force needed on a button, the readability of a display. It draws on anthropometric data (sizes), but also on movement, the senses (colour, sound, texture) and the mind (is the control obvious?).
- **Focus groups**: a small group of target users (often 6–10) discuss a product, an idea or a prototype, led by a researcher. The purpose is to get **detailed opinions and reactions** from real users before money is spent on manufacture. People spark ideas off each other, and the designer sees what the group agrees on. Drawbacks: one loud member can sway the group, and people may say what they think the researcher wants to hear.
- **Product analysis and evaluation**: studying existing products in detail, often by disassembly. The mnemonic **ACCESS FM** gives the headings: Aesthetics, Cost, Customer, Environment, Size, Safety, Function, Materials (and manufacture). Designers analyse existing products to find **good features to learn from**, **faults to avoid**, how they are made and from what, the **price range** the new product must compete in, and what is already protected by patents or registered designs so they don't infringe it.
- **Anthropometric data and percentiles**: see below.

**Anthropometrics and percentiles**

**Anthropometrics** is the study of human body measurements: height, arm reach, hand length, grip diameter, seated knee height and so on. Tables of these measurements are published for different ages, sexes and populations. **Ergonomics** is *using* that data (and more) to make the product fit people in use. Keep the two words separate: anthropometrics = the numbers; ergonomics = the fit and comfort.

People vary, so data is given as **percentiles**. The **5th percentile** is the value that 5% of people are smaller than; the **95th percentile** is the value that 95% of people are smaller than. Designers usually design for the **5th to the 95th percentile**, which includes the middle **90%** of the target group. The very smallest and largest 10% are excluded because designing for everyone would make a product awkward or expensive.

Which end matters depends on the dimension:

| Design problem | Which percentile limits it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Door height, bed length, clearance under a desk | **95th** (large users) | if the largest users fit, everyone smaller fits too |
| Reach to a shelf, pedal reach, distance to a control | **5th** (small users) | if the smallest can reach, taller people can too |
| Seat height, handlebar height | a range, so make it **adjustable** | an adjustable part covers 5th to 95th |

Worked idea: if the 5th percentile hand length of adults is 165 mm and the 95th is 205 mm, a handle designed to fit hands from 165 to 205 mm long suits 90% of adults.

**The design brief**

A **design brief** is a short statement of the problem, written after the first investigation. It says:

- the **problem or need** (the context and the situation);
- **who the client and users are**;
- **what is to be designed** in broad terms (not the solution itself);
- any main **constraints** (budget, size, where it will be used).

For example: "Commuters who cycle to work need a way to carry a laptop safely and dry on a bicycle. Design a product that attaches to a standard bike, keeps a 15-inch laptop dry in heavy rain and can be removed quickly."

**Why a designer alters the brief.** The first brief is rarely final. After research the designer may find that:

- the client's real need is different from what they first asked for;
- a similar product already exists, so the brief must target a gap;
- the budget, materials or manufacturing method make the original aim impossible;
- users rated something else as more important;
- new technology or a regulation opens or closes an option.

Modifying the brief keeps the project aimed at the real need. Designers agree changes with the client so the final product still satisfies them.

**The design specification**

A **design specification** is a list of measurable criteria the product must meet. Each point should be **specific and testable**, and **justified** with a reason from the research. Weak: "It must be comfortable." Strong: "The handle must be 30–35 mm in diameter, **because** this suits the grip of 5th to 95th percentile adult hands." The same ACCESS FM headings organise it. The specification is also what the final prototype is **evaluated against**.

A **manufacturing specification** is written later, once the design is chosen. It tells a maker exactly how to produce it: working drawings with dimensions and **tolerances**, the materials and components (stock sizes), the order of making, the equipment, quality control checks and finishes. Another person should be able to make an identical product from it.

**Consider your own and others' needs, wants and interests.** A **need** is essential (the product must hold 2 litres); a **want** is desired but not essential (users would like it in a choice of colours). Interests include the client's style, values and budget, and those of other stakeholders such as retailers or people who live near where it is used.

**Maths links.** You may analyse questionnaire results as frequency tables, bar charts or percentages ("18 of 45 people chose blue: $18 \div 45 \times 100 = 40\%$"), compare products on a performance chart with scores, or read a percentile range.

::: terms
- **Primary data**: information a designer collects first hand for their own project, such as interviews, surveys or measurements.
- **Secondary data**: information already collected by someone else, such as books, websites, published body-size tables or market reports.
- **Market research**: finding out about users, competitors, prices and gaps in the market before designing a product.
- **Focus group**: a small group of target users who discuss a product or idea with a researcher to give detailed opinions.
- **Product analysis**: studying an existing product, often by taking it apart, to learn about its function, materials, manufacture and faults.
- **Anthropometrics**: the study and data of human body measurements, used to size products.
- **Ergonomics**: designing a product so it is comfortable, safe and efficient for people to use.
- **Percentile**: the value below which a stated percentage of a population falls, e.g. 95% of people are smaller than the 95th percentile.
- **Design brief**: a short statement of the problem, the client and users, and what is to be designed.
- **Design specification**: a list of specific, measurable and justified criteria the product must meet.
- **Manufacturing specification**: detailed instructions, drawings, materials, tolerances and quality checks that let someone else make the product.
- **Client**: the person or organisation a product is designed for, who may be different from the end user.
:::

::: method
1. **Spot the command word.** "Give" wants a point; "explain" wants point plus reason; "evaluate" wants a judgement about how well the product does it.
2. **Name the technique precisely** (focus group, interview, product analysis, anthropometric data) and say what it finds out that others don't.
3. **Link to the design.** Every point ends with the effect on the product: "…so the designer can size the grip to fit 90% of users."
4. **For anthropometrics questions, name body measurements and a percentile range**, and say which end matters (reach = 5th, clearance = 95th, adjustable = both).
5. **For specification points, make them measurable and give a reason**: number, unit, "because…".
6. **For 6-mark explain questions, give three developed points** or two very developed ones, with an example product.
:::

::: example Purpose of a focus group
Explain the purpose of using a focus group when developing a product. (3)

A focus group gathers a small number of people from the target market to discuss the idea or prototype (1). Because they are the actual intended users, their opinions show what they like, dislike and would pay for, which a designer's own judgement might miss (1). The designer can then modify the design before the expensive stage of manufacture, reducing the risk that the product fails to sell (1).
:::

::: example Percentiles for a product
A designer is setting the height of a wall-mounted light switch in a public building. Explain which percentile data the designer should use. (3)

The critical dimension is upward reach, since everyone must be able to press the switch (1). The designer should use the 5th percentile reach, so the shortest users in the design range can reach it (1); anyone taller will also reach it, so the switch works for at least 95% of users, including children and wheelchair users if their data is included (1).
:::

::: example Specification points
A designer is producing a child's lunch box. Write two specification points and justify each one. (4)

The lunch box must have a hinged lid that opens with a force a 5-year-old can apply with one hand (1), because children must open it themselves at lunch without help from staff (1). It must be made from food-safe polymer such as polypropylene and be dishwasher safe up to 65 °C (1), because it will hold food every day and parents want it cleaned quickly and hygienically (1).
:::

::: example Why investigate existing products
Explain why designers should investigate existing products before starting to design new products. (6)

Investigating existing products shows the designer which features users like and which faults cause complaints, so the new design can keep good features and avoid building in known problems; for example, analysing rival kettles may show that small handles are uncomfortable for large hands (2). It shows the materials, components and manufacturing methods used, helping the designer judge what is achievable at the target cost, and it reveals the price range the new product must compete in (2). It also shows what is already protected by patents or registered designs, so the designer avoids infringing another company's intellectual property, and it reveals gaps in the market where no current product meets the users' needs (2).
:::

::: example Survey maths
In a survey of 60 teenagers, 27 said they would buy a phone stand made from bamboo, 21 chose recycled polymer and the rest chose aluminium. Calculate the percentage who chose aluminium. (2)

$60 - 27 - 21 = 12$ chose aluminium (1). $12 \div 60 \times 100 = 20\%$ (1).
:::

::: q
Give two differences between primary and secondary data. (2)
---
Primary data is collected by the designer; secondary data was collected by someone else (1). Primary data is specific to the project and up to date; secondary may be dated or not about the exact users (1). Accept cost/time difference.
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of interviewing a client rather than using a questionnaire. (2)
---
The designer can ask open follow-up questions (1) so they learn the reasons behind answers and details a tick-box questionnaire would miss (1).
:::

::: q
State the percentage of the population a product designed for the 5th to 95th percentile will suit. (1)
---
90% of the target population (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between anthropometrics and ergonomics. (2)
---
Anthropometrics is the data of human body measurements (1); ergonomics is using that data and other human factors to make a product comfortable, safe and efficient to use (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why a designer might modify a design brief after carrying out research. (4)
---
Research shows the client's real need is different from the first brief (1), so the brief is changed to aim at the right problem (1). A similar product already exists (1), so the brief is changed to target a gap in the market (1). Accept budget, materials or technology making the brief impossible, developed.
:::

::: q
Describe what a manufacturing specification contains and why it is needed. (4)
---
Working drawings with dimensions and tolerances (1); materials and components with stock sizes (1); the sequence of making, equipment and quality control checks (1). It is needed so someone else can manufacture identical products to the required quality (1).
:::

::: q
Evaluate the use of anthropometrics and ergonomics in the design of an office chair with adjustable seat height, adjustable armrests and a curved backrest. (4)
---
3–4: detailed evaluation, e.g. adjustable seat height covers 5th–95th percentile lower-leg length so feet rest flat and thighs are level, reducing strain; adjustable armrests suit different elbow heights so shoulders stay relaxed; the curved backrest supports the lower spine for long periods of sitting; but a fixed seat depth may not suit the smallest or largest users. 1–2: basic points, e.g. "it is adjustable so it fits people", with little judgement.
:::

::: watch
- **Anthropometrics is data, ergonomics is fit.** Swapping them loses marks in "evaluate the anthropometrics and ergonomics" questions.
- The 5th–95th range covers **90%**, not 95% or 100%.
- Pick the right end: **reach uses the 5th**, **clearance uses the 95th**, a range means **make it adjustable**.
- Specification points need a **number or testable criterion and a reason**. "It must look nice" earns nothing.
- "Explain the purpose of a focus group" needs the **why**: opinions of real users, before manufacture, to reduce risk. Describing the group alone gets 1 mark.
- Don't confuse the **brief** (the problem) with the **specification** (measurable criteria).
:::

::: test

Evaluate a bicycle helmet in terms of its use of anthropometrics and ergonomics. The helmet has a dial at the back to tighten it, adjustable chin straps and foam pads, and is sold in three sizes. (4)

::: test
Discuss the value of combining primary and secondary data when investigating the needs of elderly users for a kitchen tap. (6)
---
5–6: a balanced discussion of both types applied to elderly users, with a conclusion. 3–4: good points about both, some application. 1–2: basic points about one type.
