## What it is

Every product needs energy to be made, and many need energy to be used. Designers must understand **how electricity is generated**, the arguments for and against each source, and **how energy is stored**, because this shapes the choice of power system for a product: mains, battery, solar, or wind-up. The spec covers **fossil fuels** (coal, gas, oil), **nuclear power**, **renewable energy** (wind, solar, tidal, hydro-electric, biomass) and **energy storage** (kinetic pumped storage, alkaline and rechargeable batteries). Expect multiple choice (which source is finite or renewable), 3–4 mark "describe" and "explain" questions, and longer evaluations of energy sources.

### The basic idea: turbines and generators

Most power stations work the same way. Something makes a **turbine** spin; the turbine turns a **generator**, where coils of wire rotate in a magnetic field and produce electricity; the electricity goes through transformers into the **National Grid**. What differs is what spins the turbine: steam (from burning fuel or from nuclear fission), moving air, or moving water. Solar photovoltaic panels are the exception: they turn light straight into electricity with no moving parts.

### Fossil fuels: coal, gas and oil

Coal, natural gas and oil formed over millions of years from the remains of plants and sea creatures. They are **finite** (non-renewable): once burned, they are gone.

**How power is generated:** the fuel is burned in a furnace to release heat energy → the heat boils water in a boiler to make high-pressure steam → the steam drives a turbine → the turbine turns a generator → electricity. Modern gas stations often burn the gas in a gas turbine directly, then use the hot exhaust to make steam for a second turbine, which makes them more efficient.

| For fossil fuels | Against fossil fuels |
|---|---|
| reliable: can generate at any time, whatever the weather | finite: supplies will run out and prices rise as they become scarcer |
| output can be increased quickly to meet peaks in demand (especially gas) | burning releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that causes global warming |
| existing power stations and distribution network already built | coal and oil release sulfur dioxide, which causes acid rain, and smoke particles |
| a lot of energy from a small amount of fuel | mining and drilling damage landscapes; oil spills pollute seas |
| | many countries depend on imports, so supply and price can be affected by world events |

### Nuclear power

**How it is generated:** nuclear fuel (usually uranium, formed into fuel rods) is placed in a **reactor**. The nuclei of uranium atoms are split in a controlled chain reaction called **nuclear fission**, releasing a huge amount of heat. **Control rods** absorb neutrons to control the rate of the reaction. The heat is carried away by a coolant and used to boil water into steam → turbine → generator → electricity.

| For nuclear power | Against nuclear power |
|---|---|
| produces no carbon dioxide while generating, so it helps cut global warming | radioactive waste stays dangerous for thousands of years and must be stored securely |
| a tiny amount of fuel gives a huge amount of energy | an accident can release radiation, making large areas uninhabitable and harming health |
| reliable, steady "baseload" supply day and night, whatever the weather | very expensive and slow to build, and very expensive to decommission at the end of its life |
| reduces dependence on imported fossil fuels | uranium is finite and must be mined |
| | possible target for terrorism; public fear and opposition to new sites |

### Renewable energy

Renewable sources are **non-finite**: they are replaced naturally and will not run out.

| Source | How it generates power | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wind** | wind turns large blades on a turbine; the rotor turns a generator in the nacelle at the top of the tower | no fuel cost; no carbon dioxide while running; can be offshore | only works when the wind blows (not too little, not too much); some think turbines spoil the landscape; noise; can harm birds |
| **Solar** | photovoltaic (PV) cells made from silicon convert light directly into electricity; solar thermal panels heat water | no moving parts; can be fitted to roofs and small products; low running costs | no output at night and less on cloudy days or in winter; large farms take up land; panels are costly to make |
| **Tidal** | the rise and fall of the tides drives water through turbines, either in a **barrage** across an estuary or in underwater turbines in tidal streams | tides are completely predictable, twice a day | very expensive to build; few suitable sites; barrages can damage estuary habitats and affect shipping |
| **Hydro-electric** | a dam holds back a river to form a reservoir; water flows down through turbines at the base of the dam | reliable; can be started quickly to meet demand; no fuel cost | flooding valleys destroys habitats and can displace people; needs suitable mountainous sites; very high build cost |
| **Biomass** | organic material (wood pellets, crop waste, animal waste, energy crops) is burned to make steam, or fermented to make biogas that is then burned | uses waste; reliable, controllable output; can use existing power station designs | releases carbon dioxide when burned; land for energy crops may compete with food; transport of fuel |

**Biomass and carbon.** Biomass is described as **carbon neutral** over its life: the plants absorbed carbon dioxide as they grew, roughly equal to what is released when they are burned, so long as new plants are grown to replace them. It is **not** carbon-free at the moment it is burned.

**The main argument for renewables** is that they do not run out and produce little or no carbon dioxide while operating. **The main argument against** is that many (wind, solar) are **intermittent**: their output depends on the weather and time of day, so they need back-up or **energy storage**.

### Energy storage

**Kinetic pumped storage system.** Two reservoirs at different heights are connected by pipes through reversible pump-turbines.
1. When demand is low (e.g. at night) or there is surplus electricity from wind, cheap electricity is used to **pump water uphill** to the upper reservoir. The energy is stored as gravitational potential energy.
2. When demand suddenly rises (e.g. early evening), the water is **released downhill** through the turbines, generating electricity within seconds or minutes.
3. It returns less energy than it used for pumping, because of losses, but it gives a very fast response and balances supply with demand.

**Batteries** store chemical energy and release it as electrical energy.

| | Alkaline batteries | Rechargeable batteries |
|---|---|---|
| Type | single-use (primary) cells | can be recharged many times (secondary cells), e.g. nickel-metal hydride (NiMH) and lithium-ion (Li-ion) |
| Cost | cheap to buy | more expensive to buy and need a charger, but cheaper over many uses |
| Best for | low-drain products used occasionally: remote controls, clocks, smoke alarms | high-drain products used often: phones, laptops, power tools, cordless vacuums, electric vehicles |
| Environment | thrown away after one use, creating a lot of waste; must be recycled, not binned | far fewer batteries thrown away; still contain metals that must be recycled |
| Other | long shelf life; standard sizes widely available | Li-ion is light and holds a lot of energy for its weight; gradually loses capacity; can overheat or catch fire if damaged |

Product designers choose the power source to suit the product: a phone needs a light, rechargeable Li-ion battery built in; a smoke alarm needs a long-life battery that is easy to replace; a garden light can use a small solar cell to charge a rechargeable battery during the day.

### Maths link: battery life and energy data

**Battery capacity** is given in milliamp-hours (mAh). The time a battery lasts is

$$\text{battery life (h)} = \frac{\text{capacity (mAh)}}{\text{current drawn (mA)}}$$

A torch drawing 150 mA from a 2400 mAh battery lasts $2400 \div 150 = 16$ hours.

**Reading energy data.** In one sample week, a small island's electricity came from: gas 120 GWh, wind 90 GWh, solar 30 GWh, biomass 60 GWh. Total $= 300$ GWh. Renewable share $= (90 + 30 + 60) \div 300 \times 100 = 60\%$. (Biomass counts as renewable.)

::: terms
- **Finite energy source**: a source that will run out because it cannot be replaced once used, such as coal, oil, gas and uranium.
- **Renewable energy source**: a source that is naturally replaced and will not run out, such as wind, solar, tidal, hydro-electric and biomass.
- **Turbine**: a set of blades turned by steam, air or water, which drives a generator.
- **Generator**: a machine in which rotating coils and magnets convert movement into electricity.
- **Nuclear fission**: splitting the nuclei of uranium atoms in a reactor, releasing heat used to make steam.
- **Biomass**: organic material such as wood, crop waste or animal waste burned or fermented to generate energy.
- **Carbon neutral**: releasing no more carbon dioxide overall than is absorbed, e.g. biomass regrown to replace what is burned.
- **Intermittent**: describes a source whose output varies with the weather or time of day, such as wind and solar power.
- **Kinetic pumped storage**: storing energy by pumping water uphill when demand is low and releasing it through turbines when demand is high.
- **Alkaline battery**: a cheap, single-use battery suited to low-drain products; thrown away once flat.
- **Rechargeable battery**: a battery, such as lithium-ion or NiMH, that can be recharged and reused many times.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify the source first**: finite or renewable. Uranium is finite; biomass is renewable.
2. **For "describe how" questions**, give the sequence in order: energy source → heat or movement → turbine → generator → electricity. Each correct stage is a mark.
3. **For "explain why people oppose/support"**, give a reason and its consequence (waste stays radioactive **so** it must be stored securely for thousands of years).
4. **For "evaluate" questions**, give arguments for and against, compare with another source, and finish with a judgement (e.g. renewables need storage or back-up).
5. **For battery questions**, match the battery to the product's use: low-drain and occasional → alkaline; high-drain and frequent → rechargeable.
6. **For calculations**, write the formula, substitute with units and round as asked.
:::

::: example Describe: power from coal
Describe how electricity is generated in a coal-fired power station. (4)

Coal is burned in a furnace to release heat (1). The heat boils water to make high-pressure steam (1). The steam turns a turbine (1), which drives a generator to produce electricity (1).
:::

::: example Explain: opposition to nuclear power
Explain why some people are opposed to the use of nuclear power. (4)

Nuclear waste stays radioactive for thousands of years (1), so it must be stored securely at great cost and could harm future generations if it leaks (1). An accident could release radiation over a wide area (1), making land uninhabitable and damaging people's health for many years, as past disasters have shown (1).
:::

::: example Describe: pumped storage
Describe how a kinetic pumped storage system works. (4)

When demand for electricity is low, surplus electricity is used to pump water from a lower reservoir up to a higher one (1), storing the energy (1). When demand rises, the water is released back down through turbines (1), which drive generators and supply electricity to the grid within seconds or minutes (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: battery life
A child's toy draws a current of 250 mA. It uses a rechargeable battery pack with a capacity of 2000 mAh. Calculate how long the toy will run on a full charge. Give your answer in hours and minutes. (2)

Battery life $= 2000 \div 250 = 8$ hours (1), which is 8 hours 0 minutes (1).
:::

::: example Explain: rechargeable batteries in a product
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using rechargeable batteries in a wireless games controller. (4)

Advantage: the batteries can be recharged hundreds of times (1), so the user does not keep buying new batteries, which saves money and reduces waste (1). Disadvantage: the controller cannot be used when the battery is flat until it is recharged (1), and rechargeable batteries cost more to buy and need a charger, raising the price of the product (1).
:::

::: q
Name two renewable energy sources. (2)
---
Any two: wind; solar; tidal; hydro-electric; biomass (also wave, geothermal).
:::

::: q
Explain why biomass is described as carbon neutral. (2)
---
The plants absorb carbon dioxide from the air as they grow (1), roughly balancing the carbon dioxide released when they are burned, as long as new plants are grown to replace them (1).
:::

::: q
Give two arguments for the use of fossil fuels to generate electricity. (2)
---
Any two: reliable at any time of day and in any weather; output can be increased quickly to meet demand; power stations and infrastructure already exist; a lot of energy from a small amount of fuel.
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of wind power. (2)
---
It is intermittent (1): turbines generate nothing when the wind is too weak or too strong, so back-up supplies or storage are needed (1). (Or: visual impact on the landscape, noise, harm to birds.)
:::

::: q
Describe how a hydro-electric power station generates electricity. (3)
---
A dam holds back a river, creating a reservoir of water (1). Water is released and flows down through pipes to turbines, making them spin (1). The turbines turn generators, which produce electricity (1).
:::

::: q
A remote control draws 20 mA. Its alkaline batteries have a capacity of 2400 mAh. Calculate how many days the batteries would last if the remote was used continuously. (3)
---
Battery life $= 2400 \div 20 = 120$ hours (1)(1). $120 \div 24 = 5$ days (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Uranium is finite**, so nuclear power is **not renewable**, even though it is low-carbon.
- **Biomass is renewable** but releases carbon dioxide when burned. Say "carbon neutral over its life", not "no emissions".
- In "how is power generated" answers, keep the **order**: heat → steam → turbine → generator. Missing the generator loses a mark.
- Pumped storage **stores** energy; it is not a source of energy and it returns less than it takes.
- Solar PV panels have **no turbine**: light is converted directly into electricity.
- Match batteries to use: rechargeable for **high-drain, frequent** use; alkaline for **low-drain, occasional** use.
:::

::: test
A portable speaker uses a 4400 mAh rechargeable battery. At full volume it draws 550 mA. Calculate how long it will play at full volume. (2)
---
$4400 \div 550$ (1) $= 8$ hours (1).
:::

::: test
Table data for one week of electricity generation in a town: gas 75 GWh, wind 50 GWh, solar 25 GWh, nuclear 100 GWh. Calculate the percentage of the electricity that came from renewable sources. (3)
---
Total $= 75 + 50 + 25 + 100 = 250$ GWh (1). Renewable $= 50 + 25 = 75$ GWh (1). Percentage $= 75 \div 250 \times 100 = 30\%$ (1). Nuclear is not renewable.
:::

::: test
Explain why a designer might choose a small solar panel with a rechargeable battery to power a garden light. (3)
---
The solar panel charges the battery from daylight during the day (1), and the battery stores the energy so the light can work at night when there is no sunlight (1); no mains cable or replacement batteries are needed, so it is cheap to run and can be placed anywhere in the garden (1).
:::

Level 2 (3–4): some developed points, may focus on one side or one source.
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements.
Indicative content. For: will not run out; little or no carbon dioxide while generating, helping reduce global warming; no fuel costs once built; reduces dependence on imported fuels; tides predictable; hydro and biomass controllable.
