## What it is

Before making a prototype, the designer must choose **materials** (timber, metal, polymer, board, fabric) and **components** (screws, hinges, zips, LEDs, batteries). The spec asks you to select them by three factors: **functional need**, **cost** and **availability**. The 2025 paper asked students to explain why each factor matters, 2 marks each for 6 marks, so learn each one as a separate developed point. You also need **SI units**, **commercially available stock forms**, and the **composition of important alloys**.

**The three selection factors**

| Factor | Questions the designer asks | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional need** | Is it strong, stiff, tough, hard or flexible enough? Waterproof, heat resistant, food safe, conductive or insulating? Right weight? Can it be joined and finished as needed? Does it suit the look and feel required? | If the material or component can't do the job, the prototype fails its specification or is unsafe, whatever it costs |
| **Cost** | Price per unit, per metre or per sheet; waste produced; cost of processing (tools, time, energy); cost of the finish needed | The product must be made within budget and sell at a competitive price; a prototype or mock-up often uses cheaper stand-in materials; expensive materials that need special tools add hidden costs |
| **Availability** | Is it in stock with suppliers? In the stock form and size needed? How long is delivery? Is supply sustainable and reliable for future production? | Waiting for materials wastes time and misses deadlines; unusual sizes need extra cutting and waste; a product can't go into production if a component is discontinued or scarce |

The three factors trade off against each other. Titanium is light, strong and corrosion-proof (function) but expensive (cost) and harder to buy in small stock sizes (availability), so a bicycle frame prototype might use aluminium alloy tube instead. A good answer names the trade-off and justifies the choice.

**Other considerations** often added: aesthetics (colour, texture, grain), environmental impact (recycled, FSC-certified, recyclable), ease of working with available equipment, ethical sourcing, and the scale of production planned.

**Matching materials to function: examples across the categories**

| Category | Typical prototype choice | Why (function) |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | corrugated card for a packaging mock-up; foam board for an architectural model | cheap, light, easy to cut and score; corrugated is stiff for its weight |
| **Timbers** | pine for a frame; birch plywood for a shelf panel; MDF for a painted part | pine is cheap, easy to work; plywood is stable and strong in all directions; MDF has a smooth surface for paint |
| **Metals** | aluminium sheet for a lightweight casing; mild steel for a welded frame; brass for a decorative fitting | aluminium is light and corrosion resistant; mild steel is strong, cheap and weldable; brass resists corrosion and polishes well |
| **Polymers** | acrylic for a laser-cut display; HIPS for a vacuum-formed tray; PLA for a 3D-printed part | acrylic cuts cleanly and polishes; HIPS forms easily when heated; PLA prints at low temperature |
| **Textiles** | cotton calico for a toile; ripstop nylon for an outdoor bag; polyester fleece for warmth | calico is cheap for testing; nylon is strong and water resistant; fleece is warm and light |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | a microcontroller board, LEDs with resistors, a DC motor with gearbox, a slide switch | chosen for voltage, current, power, speed, torque and size |

**Selecting components** follows the same logic: a hinge sized for the door weight; a zip long enough and strong enough; a resistor of the right value and power rating; a battery giving the voltage and life needed; screws of the right length and type for the material (wood screws, self-tapping screws for plastic, machine screws with nuts for metal). Standard components are cheaper and easier to get than custom-made parts.

**SI units and stock forms**

Measure and specify in **SI units** (or units derived from them): lengths in **millimetres** (mm) and metres, mass in grams and kilograms, force in newtons, electrical values in volts, amps and ohms. Paper and card are specified by weight per area in **gsm** (grams per square metre) and card thickness in **microns**.

**Stock forms** are the standard shapes and sizes materials are sold in. Designing around them saves cutting, time, waste and money.

| Material | Common stock forms |
|---|---|
| Paper and board | sheets in A sizes (A4 = 210 × 297 mm, A3 = 297 × 420 mm), rolls; weight in gsm; board thickness in microns |
| Natural timber | planks and boards, square and rectangular sections, **dowel**, mouldings; often sold planed all round (PAR) |
| Manufactured board | standard sheets **2440 × 1220 mm**, in thicknesses such as 3, 6, 9, 12, 18 mm |
| Metals | sheet, plate, **bar** (flat, square, round, hexagonal), **tube** (round, square), angle, channel, wire |
| Polymers | sheet, rod, tube, film, powder and granules (for moulding), filament (for 3D printing) |
| Textiles | fabric sold off the roll by the metre in set widths; yarn; interfacing; fastenings such as zips and buttons |
| Components | screws, nuts, bolts, hinges; resistors in preferred value ranges; LEDs, switches, batteries in standard sizes |

**Composition of important alloys**

An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with at least one other element, made to improve properties such as strength, hardness, corrosion resistance or melting point.

| Alloy | Made from | Improved property and typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Brass** | copper and zinc | corrosion resistant, casts and machines well, attractive gold colour: door furniture, plumbing fittings, instruments |
| **Bronze** | copper and tin | hard, corrosion resistant: bearings, statues, marine fittings |
| **Stainless steel** | iron, carbon, chromium (commonly around 18%) and nickel (commonly around 8%) | very corrosion resistant, hygienic: cutlery, sinks, surgical tools |
| **Duralumin** | aluminium with a few per cent copper and small amounts of manganese and magnesium | much stronger than pure aluminium but still light: aircraft parts |
| **Pewter** | mainly tin, with small amounts of antimony and copper | low melting point, easy to cast in school: jewellery, ornaments |
| **Steels** (mild, high carbon) | iron with carbon (more carbon = harder but more brittle) | mild steel for frames; high carbon steel for tools |
| **Solder** (lead-free) | mainly tin, with small amounts of copper or silver | low melting point for joining electronic components |

When a question asks you to select an alloy, give the property that matches the function: stainless steel for a kitchen utensil (hygienic, won't rust), duralumin for a lightweight load-bearing part, brass for a decorative fitting that won't tarnish badly and machines easily.

::: terms
- **Functional need**: what a material or component must do for the product to work, such as strength, flexibility or waterproofing.
- **Availability**: whether a material or component can be obtained quickly, reliably and in the form and size needed.
- **Stock form**: a standard shape and size in which a material is sold, such as sheet, bar, tube or dowel.
- **Standard component**: a mass-produced, ready-made part such as a screw, hinge, zip or resistor.
- **SI units**: the international system of units, e.g. metres, kilograms, newtons, volts and amps.
- **gsm**: grams per square metre, the unit used to specify the weight of paper and card.
- **Alloy**: a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, made to improve its properties.
- **Brass**: an alloy of copper and zinc that resists corrosion and machines well.
- **Stainless steel**: an alloy of iron, carbon, chromium and nickel that is highly resistant to rusting.
- **Duralumin**: an aluminium alloy with copper, manganese and magnesium, strong for its weight.
- **Mock-up**: an early model made from cheaper materials to test size, form or appearance.
:::

::: method
1. **Treat each factor separately** when a question lists them: function, then cost, then availability.
2. **For each factor, point + reason**: what the designer checks, and what happens if they ignore it.
3. **For a justified choice**, name the **specific** material or component (not "metal" but "aluminium alloy sheet"), then give two properties linked to the product's function.
4. **Mention a trade-off** where one exists: the ideal material may cost too much or be hard to get.
5. **Use correct units and stock forms** ("18 mm birch plywood sheet", "Ø6 mm aluminium rod").
6. **For alloys, give the elements and the improved property.**
:::

::: example Three factors
Prior to making, materials and components are selected for product function, cost and availability. Explain why each factor needs to be considered before choosing a material or component. (6)

Product function: the material must have the properties the product needs, such as strength, stiffness, water resistance or food safety (1), otherwise the prototype won't work properly or could be unsafe for the user (1). Cost: the material must fit the budget for the prototype and later production (1); expensive materials, or ones that need special tools or finishes, raise the final price and may make the product uncompetitive, so cheaper stand-ins are often used for mock-ups (1). Availability: the material must be in stock in a suitable stock form and size (1), because waiting for deliveries wastes time and can miss deadlines, and non-standard sizes need extra cutting and create waste (1).
:::

::: example Choosing an alloy
Name a suitable alloy for a set of kitchen tongs and justify your choice. (3)

Stainless steel (1). It contains chromium, which makes it highly resistant to rust from water and food acids (1), and it is hygienic, hard-wearing and easy to clean, so it is safe for contact with food (1).
:::

::: example Justified choice
A student is making a prototype outdoor bird box. Name a suitable material and justify it using function, cost and availability. (4)

Exterior-grade plywood (1). Function: it is stable and strong in all directions and resists moisture better than MDF, so it won't warp outside (1). Cost: it is cheaper than a naturally durable hardwood such as oak (1). Availability: it is sold in standard 2440 × 1220 mm sheets in suitable thicknesses such as 12 mm, so it is easy to buy and parts can be nested to reduce waste (1).
:::

::: example Stock forms
Name a suitable stock form for each: (a) the legs of a small stool made from pine; (b) a lightweight frame made from aluminium; (c) the axle of a toy car. (3)

(a) Square-section planed timber (PAR) (1). (b) Aluminium tube, round or square (1). (c) Round rod or dowel, e.g. steel rod or hardwood dowel (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a designer might use a cheaper material for a mock-up than for the final product. (2)
---
The mock-up only tests size, form or appearance (1), so using cheap, easy-to-work materials like card or foam saves money and time if it has to be changed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why designers use standard components. (2)
---
They are mass-produced so they are cheap and easy to buy in stock (1), and they are reliable and replaceable, saving the time of designing and making custom parts (1).
:::

::: q
Name the unit used to specify the weight of paper and card. (1)
---
gsm, grams per square metre (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why duralumin rather than pure aluminium is used for aircraft parts. (2)
---
Alloying with copper and small amounts of other metals makes it much stronger (1) while keeping aluminium's low density, so parts are strong but light (1).
:::

::: q
A designer needs 6 m of aluminium tube. It costs £4.20 per metre, or £22.00 for a 6 m length. Calculate the saving from buying the 6 m length. (2)
---
$6 \times 4.20 = £25.20$ (1). $25.20 - 22.00 = £3.20$ saving (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Answer each listed factor separately.** One combined sentence usually scores only once.
- Every factor needs a **reason or consequence**: "cost, because it must be affordable" is thin; say what happens to the price or budget.
- **Name specific materials and stock forms**, with sizes and units.
- Don't confuse **brass** (copper + zinc) with **bronze** (copper + tin).
- Availability is not only "in the shops": include **stock form, size, lead time and reliable future supply**.
:::

A 2440 × 1220 mm sheet of 12 mm plywood costs £38.00. Calculate the cost per square metre, to the nearest penny. (3)

::: test
Choose one of the following prototypes and justify a suitable material and one component for it: (a) a wall-mounted key holder; (b) a waterproof phone pouch; (c) an LED night light. (4)
---
Material named (1) with a justified property linked to function (1); component named (1) with justification (1). E.g. (a) 9 mm birch plywood: stable, strong, easy to laser cut; brass cup hooks: strong, corrosion resistant. (b) TPU-coated nylon or PVC: waterproof, flexible; waterproof zip or roll-top buckle: seals out water. ::: test
Discuss how a designer balances function, cost and availability when choosing materials for a prototype bicycle mudguard. (6)
---
5–6: detailed discussion of all three factors with specific materials, trade-offs and a justified choice. 3–4: good points, some trade-off. 1–2: basic points. Indicative: function: light, flexible, tough, water and UV resistant, won't rust, easy to fit; options: polypropylene (tough, flexible, cheap), aluminium sheet (light, rust resistant but dents), stainless steel (strong but heavier, costlier), carbon fibre (light but expensive, hard to source/work). Cost: polymer sheet cheap; carbon fibre costly. Availability: polypropylene and aluminium sheet in standard stock; standard stays, clips and bolts. Conclusion, e.g. polypropylene sheet, heat-bent, with stainless fittings.
:::
