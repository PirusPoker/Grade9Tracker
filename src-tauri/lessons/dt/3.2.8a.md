## What it is

To make high-quality products, and especially to make **many identical** ones, designers use **production aids**. These are simple devices that speed up marking out and making and keep every part the same. They also choose from a toolkit of **processes**, which AQA sorts into three families:
- **wastage**: removing material;
- **addition**: adding or joining material;
- **deforming and reforming**: changing a material's shape without removing any, either by bending or pressing it (deforming) or by melting and moulding it (reforming).

Exam questions ask you to explain aids, to **classify** processes, and to **describe the stages** of the big industrial processes: vacuum forming, injection moulding, blow moulding, extrusion and die cutting.

### Production aids

| Aid | What it is | How it is used | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Measurement and reference points** | a **datum**: a fixed edge, face, line or point from which **all** measurements are taken | measuring every dimension from one datum stops errors adding up (cumulative error); CNC machines work from a datum (origin) using **coordinates** | hole centres marked from one planed edge of a board; a laser cutter's origin; registration marks on a print |
| **Template** | a durable shape (card, MDF, acrylic, metal) that is **drawn or cut round** | marks out the same shape many times, quickly and identically, with no measuring | a curved chair-back template drawn round on pine; a card net template |
| **Jig** | a device that **holds the work and/or guides the tool**, so an operation happens in exactly the same place every time | removes the need to mark out each part; parts are identical and interchangeable; often safer, since hands are away from the tool | a drilling jig that locates holes in the same place on every bracket; a bending jig (former) for acrylic; a sawing jig for repeated angles |
| **Pattern** | (textiles) paper or card pieces of a garment, with **seam allowance**, grain line and notches; (casting) a **replica** of the part used to form the mould cavity | textile patterns are pinned to fabric, or lay plans are nested, and cut round; casting patterns are packed in sand, then removed | a dress pattern; a wooden pattern for a sand-cast aluminium bracket |

**Why use them?** They give **speed**, because there is no repeated measuring. They give **accuracy and consistency**, because every part matches, so parts are interchangeable and assembly is easy. They **de-skill** the task, so lower-skilled workers produce good work. They cut **waste** and rejects. A jig or template takes time and money to make, so it pays off in **batch** or mass production, not for a single one-off.

**Maths link: scale and datums.** A drawing at scale 1:5 shows every length at a fifth of full size, so a 36 mm line on the drawing is $36 \times 5 = 180$ mm on the product. A drawing at 2:1 is twice full size, so divide by 2. Coordinates from a datum, such as (40, 25), mean 40 mm along the x-axis and 25 mm up the y-axis from the datum corner.

### Aids and processes across the categories

| Category | Typical production aids | Wastage · addition · deforming examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | net templates; registration marks as reference points; die-cutting forme | die cutting, perforation · printing, laminating, bonding · creasing, folding |
| **Timbers** | templates for curves; drilling jigs for KD fittings; datum face and edge | sawing, turning, drilling, milling (routing) · bonding with PVA, laminating · steam bending, laminated bending over a former |
| **Metals** | bending jigs; drilling jigs; casting patterns; scribed datum edge | sawing, drilling, turning, milling, shearing · brazing, welding · pressing, bending, folding, casting, extrusion |
| **Polymers** | bending formers; vacuum-forming formers; laser cutter datum | laser cutting, drilling · 3D printing, solvent bonding, hot-air welding · vacuum forming, drape forming, line bending, injection and blow moulding, extrusion |
| **Textiles** | paper patterns with seam allowance and notches; lay plans | cutting, die cutting (press knives) · sewing, batik, printing, bonding with fusible web · pressing, pleating |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | PCB drilling jigs; soldering jigs; a datum for pick-and-place coordinates | drilling, cutting · soldering, 3D-printed cases · bending component leads, vacuum-formed or injection-moulded casings |

### Wastage: removing material

| Process | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Die cutting** | a **steel-rule die** (sharp cutting rules, with blunt creasing rules, set in a board) is pressed through sheet material in one stroke | card nets for packaging; felt and fabric shapes; gaskets |
| **Perforation** | rows of small cuts or holes | tear-off tickets, stamps, packaging tear strips |
| **Turning** | work rotates on a **lathe** while a cutting tool removes material | round parts: pins, handles, table legs, bowls |
| **Sawing** | a toothed blade cuts a narrow kerf | cutting stock to length and shape |
| **Milling** | a rotating multi-tooth cutter removes material from work fed past it | flat faces, slots, pockets; CNC mould tools |
| **Drilling** | a rotating twist bit cuts a round hole | holes for fixings and components |
| **Cutting and shearing** | blades (knife, scissors, laser) cut; in **shearing**, two blades pass each other and slice the material without swarf | tin snips and guillotines on sheet metal; laser cutting acrylic, card and ply |

### Addition: adding or joining material

| Process | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Brazing** | a brass filler melts into a joint in steel; the parts themselves don't melt | bike frames, small steel assemblies |
| **Welding** | the parent material melts and fuses (MIG, TIG, arc; hot-air or ultrasonic for polymers) | car bodies, steel frames, HDPE tanks |
| **Lamination** | thin layers bonded together, flat or over a former | plywood, bent laminated chair frames, laminated card and menus |
| **Soldering** | a low-melting-point alloy joins components to a PCB | circuit boards, wiring |
| **3D printing** | material is added **layer by layer** from CAD data, e.g. molten PLA filament or resin | prototypes, complex one-off parts, custom fittings |
| **Batik** | hot **wax** is applied to fabric with a tjanting or brush; the fabric is dyed; the wax **resists** the dye; the wax is then removed by ironing between paper | decorative textile patterns |
| **Sewing** | thread stitched through layers of fabric | all textile products |
| **Bonding** | adhesives join parts, e.g. **PVA** for wood, **epoxy** resin for mixed materials, **solvent cement** for acrylic, **contact adhesive**, fusible web for fabric | furniture joints, acrylic boxes, card models |
| **Printing** | ink is added to a surface: offset lithography, screen printing, digital printing | packaging, T-shirts, labels |

### Deforming and reforming: changing shape without removing material

| Process | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Vacuum forming** | a thermoforming sheet (usually **HIPS**) is clamped and **heated** until soft; the platen raises the **former** into it; a **vacuum** pump removes the air so atmospheric pressure pushes the sheet tight over the former; it cools and is released and trimmed. The former needs **draft angles**, rounded corners, no undercuts, and vent holes in deep recesses | blister packs, trays, chocolate box inserts, masks |
| **Creasing** | a line is pressed into card so it folds neatly | packaging nets |
| **Pressing** | sheet metal is forced between shaped **male and female dies** under great pressure; fabric is pressed with heat and steam; laminates are pressed in moulds | car body panels, steel sinks, pressed-plywood seats |
| **Drape forming** | heated acrylic sheet is laid over a former and **drapes** into a smooth curve under its own weight, or is gently pressed | curved acrylic displays, light shades |
| **Bending** | sheet or strip is bent: metal in a folding machine; polymers along a line on a strip heater; timber by steaming or laminating | brackets, phone stands, chair backs |
| **Folding** | card, sheet metal or fabric is folded along a line | boxes, metal enclosures, hems |
| **Blow moulding** | a hot tube of polymer (**parison**) is extruded; the mould closes round it; **compressed air** is blown in, pushing the polymer onto the cold mould walls; it cools and is ejected | HDPE milk bottles, PET drinks bottles, watering cans |
| **Casting** | liquid material (molten metal or resin) is poured into a **mould** and solidifies: sand casting, die casting, resin casting | engine parts, jewellery, resin paperweights |
| **Injection moulding** | polymer **granules** feed from a **hopper**; a rotating **screw** inside a heated barrel melts them; the screw then acts as a **ram**, forcing molten polymer at high pressure into a closed, cooled **mould**; it is held under pressure, cools and hardens; the mould opens and **ejector pins** push the part out | bottle caps, chair shells, toy bricks, phone cases |
| **Extrusion** | a screw forces molten polymer (or hot aluminium, pushed by a ram) **continuously** through a **die** shaped like the cross-section; it is cooled (e.g. in a water bath) and cut to length | pipes, window-frame sections, curtain track, rods and tube |

::: terms
- **Production aid**: a device such as a jig, template, pattern or datum that makes manufacture faster, more accurate and consistent.
- **Datum**: a fixed edge, surface, line or point from which all measurements are taken, to avoid cumulative error.
- **Template**: a durable shape that is drawn or cut round to mark out identical parts quickly.
- **Jig**: a device that holds the work and/or guides the tool so an operation is repeated in exactly the same position.
- **Pattern**: paper pieces used to cut textile parts, or a replica of a part used to make a casting mould.
- **Wastage**: shaping by removing material, e.g. sawing, drilling, milling, turning, die cutting or shearing.
- **Addition**: shaping or joining by adding material, e.g. welding, soldering, sewing, bonding or 3D printing.
- **Deforming and reforming**: changing a material's shape by bending or pressing it, or by melting and moulding it, without removing material.
- **Die cutting**: pressing a steel-rule die through sheet material to cut and crease a shape in one stroke.
- **Injection moulding**: forcing molten polymer under pressure into a closed mould, where it cools into a solid part.
- **Blow moulding**: inflating a hot polymer tube (parison) inside a mould with compressed air to make hollow products.
- **Extrusion**: forcing softened material continuously through a shaped die to make long parts of constant cross-section.
- **Draft angle**: a slight taper on the sides of a mould or former so the formed part can be released.
:::

::: method
1. For production aids, say **what it is**, **how it is used**, and the **benefit** (speed, accuracy, consistency, less skill), with an example.
2. **Don't mix up aids**: a template is drawn round; a jig holds and guides; a pattern is for fabric or casting; a datum is the reference for measuring.
3. To **classify** processes, ask: is material removed (wastage), added or joined (addition), or reshaped (deforming and reforming)?
4. To **describe a process**, give the stages in order, with the key technical words: hopper, screw, heater, mould, ejector pins; clamp, heat, platen, vacuum; parison, air, mould.
5. Add **design requirements** where relevant: draft angles, rounded corners, no undercuts, uniform wall thickness.
6. Link to **scale**: aids and expensive tooling pay off in batch and mass production.
:::

::: example Explain: jigs
Explain why a manufacturer uses a drilling jig when producing a batch of identical brackets. (3)

The jig holds each bracket in the same position and guides the drill bit to the correct place (1), so the holes don't need to be marked out on every part, which saves time (1). Every bracket has holes in exactly the same position, so the parts are consistent and interchangeable and fit during assembly, with fewer rejects (1).
:::

::: example Classify processes
Classify each process as wastage, addition, or deforming and reforming: (a) milling, (b) soldering, (c) blow moulding. (3)

(a) Wastage (1). (b) Addition (1). (c) Deforming and reforming (1).
:::

::: example Describe: vacuum forming
Describe the stages of vacuum forming a HIPS tray. (5)

A former is made with draft angles and rounded edges, and placed on the platen, which is lowered (1). A sheet of HIPS is clamped in the frame above it (1). The heater is moved over the sheet until the sheet is soft and flexible (1). The platen is raised so the former pushes into the soft sheet, and the vacuum pump is switched on, drawing out the air so the sheet is pulled tightly over the former (1). The sheet is left to cool and harden, then the platen is lowered, the moulding removed, and the excess trimmed (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: scale drawing
A drawing of a toy car is at a scale of 1:4. The wheelbase on the drawing measures 32 mm. Calculate the real wheelbase. (2)

Scale 1:4 means the real length is 4 times the drawn length (1).
$32 \times 4 = 128$ mm (1)
:::

::: example Explain: extended
Explain how production aids improve efficiency and accuracy in the batch production of products. Use examples. (6)

In batch production the same part must be made many times, so any time spent measuring and marking out is repeated for every item. A template, such as an MDF template of a curved chair back, lets a worker draw round the shape in seconds, and every chair back is identical. A jig goes further: a drilling jig for a flat-pack side panel clamps the panel against stops and guides the drill, so the holes for the cam-lock fittings are always in the same place. Assembly then works first time, and fewer panels are rejected. In textiles, a set of paper patterns with seam allowances and notches, laid out in a nested lay plan, lets many layers of fabric be cut at once to identical shapes while wasting little fabric. Measuring every hole and cut from a single datum edge, or programming a CNC router from a datum origin, avoids cumulative error, so parts made weeks apart still match. Together these aids reduce the skill needed, speed up production, cut waste and make parts interchangeable. Their cost is repaid across the batch, but they would not be worthwhile for a single one-off. (Level 3: 5–6 marks)
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a jig and a template. (2)
---
A template is a shape that is drawn or cut round to mark out identical parts (1); a jig holds the work and/or guides the tool so the operation is done in the same place each time, without marking out (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why measurements are taken from a datum. (2)
---
All measurements are taken from one fixed reference edge or point (1), so small errors do not add up (accumulate) along the part, and positions stay accurate (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the batik process. (3)
---
Hot wax is applied to the fabric in a pattern with a tjanting or brush (1); the fabric is dyed, and the waxed areas resist the dye (1); the wax is removed, e.g. by ironing between paper, leaving the undyed pattern (1).
:::

::: q
Give two features a vacuum-forming former should have. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): draft angle (tapered sides); rounded corners and edges; no undercuts; vent holes in deep recesses; smooth surface.
:::

::: q
Describe how a hollow HDPE milk bottle is blow moulded. (4)
---
A hot tube of HDPE (parison) is extruded (1); the two halves of the mould close round it, sealing the bottom (1); compressed air is blown in, forcing the polymer outwards against the mould walls (1); it cools and hardens in the shape of the mould, then the mould opens and the bottle is ejected and trimmed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a textile pattern includes a seam allowance. (2)
---
Extra fabric is needed beyond the stitching line to sew the seam (1); without it the finished piece would be too small and the seam could fray apart (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Jig ≠ template.** A jig *holds and guides*; a template is *drawn round*. Describing one as the other loses the mark.
- **Classifying**: casting, moulding, vacuum forming and bending are *deforming and reforming*; soldering, welding, sewing, bonding and 3D printing are *addition*; die cutting, milling and drilling are *wastage*.
- Process descriptions need the **key parts in order**: hopper → screw and heaters → mould → cooling → ejector pins.
- Injection moulding makes **solid** parts; blow moulding makes **hollow** ones; extrusion makes **constant cross-section** lengths.
- Aids and tooling suit **batch and mass** production. Say why: their cost is spread over many parts.
:::

::: test
Describe the stages of injection moulding. (5)
---
Polymer granules are fed from a hopper into a barrel (1); heaters and the rotating screw melt the polymer (1); the screw moves forward as a ram, forcing molten polymer under high pressure into a closed mould (1); pressure is held while the polymer cools and hardens in the water-cooled mould (1); the mould opens and ejector pins push the product out (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a vacuum-forming former must have a draft angle. (2)
---
The sloping sides let the formed sheet slide off the former once cooled (1); without it the plastic would grip the vertical sides and the moulding or former could be damaged when it is removed (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of injection moulding. (4)
---
Advantage: very fast cycles and high accuracy, producing complex, identical parts with a good finish (1), so the unit cost is very low in mass production (1). Disadvantage: the mould is very expensive to make (1), so it is only economic for very large quantities, and design changes are costly (1).
:::

::: test
A CNC router's datum is the bottom-left corner of a board. A hole is programmed at coordinates (150, 80) and a second hole at (390, 80). Calculate the distance between the hole centres. (2)

Both holes have the same y-coordinate, so the distance is the difference in x (1); $390 - 150 = 240$ mm (1).
