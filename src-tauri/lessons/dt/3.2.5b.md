## What it is

This topic is the practical "how do you actually make it?" side of the specialist principles. Materials are shaped and formed in three broad ways:
- **cutting**: removing material with a blade, saw, drill or cutter, including laser and CNC cutting;
- **abrasion**: wearing material away by rubbing, as in sanding and filing;
- **addition**: joining or building material on, as in soldering, brazing, welding, sewing, quilting, casting and 3D printing.

The spec names particular processes for each category. Exam questions say "**Using notes and/or sketches, describe how to…**" and give **one mark per correct, specific stage**, so you need the **tools**, the **sequence**, and the reason for key steps (accuracy, safety).

### The processes for each category

| Category | Processes the spec names |
|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | cut, crease, score, fold, perforate |
| **Timbers** | cut, drill, chisel, sand, plane |
| **Metals** | cut, drill, turn, mill, cast, braze, weld |
| **Polymers** | cut, drill, cast, deform, print, weld |
| **Textiles** | sew, pleat, gather, quilt, pipe |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | cut, drill, solder |

### Papers and boards
- **Cut**: use a **craft knife** against a **safety rule** on a **cutting mat**, keeping fingers behind the guard. Take several light strokes rather than one heavy one. Use scissors for curves, a **rotary trimmer** or guillotine for straight edges, and a **laser cutter** or CNC card cutter for accurate, repeated nets.
- **Score**: cut **part way through** the top layer of thick card or board along the fold line, then fold **away** from the cut. This gives a crisp fold in board too thick to fold neatly otherwise.
- **Crease**: **press** a line into the card without cutting it, using a creasing tool, bone folder or the back of a blade against a rule. The fold is neat and the surface isn't broken. Industrial dies use blunt **creasing rules**.
- **Fold**: fold along the crease or score line against a straight edge for accuracy, and fold tabs before gluing.
- **Perforate**: make a line of small cuts or holes so a part tears off neatly, as on tickets, tear strips and dispenser openings. Use a perforating wheel or cutter, or a die in industry.

### Timbers
- **Cut**: mark out with a try square and marking knife or pencil, clamp the work, and cut on the **waste side** of the line. Use a **tenon saw** for straight cuts, a **coping saw** or scroll saw for curves, a bandsaw for larger work, and a laser cutter for thin plywood or MDF.
- **Drill**: mark the centre with a bradawl, clamp the work, and use a **pillar drill** with a twist bit, or a flat or **Forstner bit** for large flat-bottomed holes. Put scrap wood underneath to stop breakout. Drill a pilot hole and **countersink** for screws.
- **Chisel**: use a **bevel-edged chisel** and mallet to remove waste from joints such as housings and halving joints. Clamp the work and keep both hands behind the cutting edge. Chop down on the line, then pare the waste, working in from both sides.
- **Sand**: use **glasspaper** wrapped round a **cork sanding block**, working through the grades from coarse to fine and sanding **with the grain**. Disc and belt sanders shape and smooth edges quickly.
- **Plane**: use a smoothing or jack plane, set for a fine cut, to bring timber to size and leave a smooth, flat surface. Plane with the grain, and plane end grain from both edges towards the centre so the corners don't split. A planer-thicknesser does this by machine.

### Metals
- **Cut**: mark with an engineer's square and **scriber** after coating the metal with marking blue. Use a **hacksaw** (or junior hacksaw) with the work held in a vice, **tin snips** or bench shears for thin sheet, and a guillotine for long straight cuts.
- **Drill**: **centre punch** the hole position so the bit doesn't wander, hold the work in a **machine vice** on a pillar drill, and use HSS bits at the right speed with cutting fluid.
- **Turn**: on a **centre lathe** the **work rotates** in a chuck while a **single-point tool** moves along or across it. Operations include facing (a flat end), parallel turning (a diameter), taper turning, drilling on the axis and knurling (a grip pattern).
- **Mill**: on a **milling machine** a **multi-toothed cutter rotates** while the work, clamped to the moving table, is fed past it. Milling makes flat faces, slots, steps and pockets, and is often CNC controlled.
- **Cast**: in **sand casting**, a pattern is packed in casting sand in a two-part moulding box (the drag and cope). Runner and riser holes are made and the pattern removed. Molten metal, usually an aluminium alloy, is poured in and left to cool. The casting is then broken out and **fettled** (the runner and rough edges are cut and filed off).
- **Braze**: clean the joint, apply **flux**, and heat the steel parts to bright red heat with a brazing torch. Melt a **brass brazing rod** (spelter) into the joint. The filler melts well below the melting point of steel, so the parts are joined but not melted.
- **Weld**: the **parent metals themselves are melted** and fuse together, usually with a filler rod. This is done by **MIG**, TIG, arc or oxy-acetylene welding. It gives the strongest permanent joint.

**Science, the melting-point ladder.** Soft **soldering** works at roughly 200 °C, with a tin-based alloy that melts low. **Brazing** works at roughly 900 °C, with a brass filler. **Welding** melts the steel itself, well over 1400 °C. The higher the temperature, the stronger the joint, but the more energy it needs and the more distortion it causes.

### Polymers
- **Cut**: leave the protective film on the acrylic. Use a coping saw or scroll saw for curves, a hacksaw for straight cuts, or a **laser cutter** for accurate, polished edges. File, then use wet-and-dry paper and polish to finish the edges.
- **Drill**: support the work on scrap wood, and use a sharp bit at **low speed** with light pressure so the acrylic doesn't crack or melt.
- **Cast**: mix liquid **polyester or epoxy resin** with a hardener (catalyst) and pour it into a mould, such as a silicone mould. It sets by a chemical reaction. This is used for encapsulating objects, jewellery and small parts.
- **Deform**: thermoforming polymers soften when heated. In **line bending**, the acrylic is heated along a line on a **strip heater**, bent over a former or jig to the correct angle, and held until it cools. **Vacuum forming** and **drape forming** shape heated sheet over a former.
- **Print**: **3D printing** (FDM) builds a part in layers by extruding molten filament such as PLA from a heated nozzle following CAD data. Graphics can also be screen printed or pad printed onto polymer surfaces.
- **Weld**: **hot-air welding** melts the edges of PP or HDPE together with a filler rod of the same polymer. **Ultrasonic welding** uses vibration to melt the joint line. **Solvent welding** (e.g. PVC pipes) dissolves the surfaces so they fuse.

### Textiles
- **Sew**: for a **plain seam**, place the fabric right sides together, pin and tack, then machine stitch at the **seam allowance** (commonly 15 mm). Neaten the raw edges with an **overlocker** or zigzag stitch, and press the seam open.
- **Pleat**: fold the fabric and press it into regular folds, such as knife, box or inverted pleats, then stitch across the top to hold them. Pleats control fullness and give shape and movement to skirts and curtains.
- **Gather**: sew two parallel rows of long, loose stitches, then pull the bobbin threads so the fabric bunches up evenly to fit a shorter piece, such as a waistband or sleeve head. Then stitch it in place.
- **Quilt**: sandwich **wadding** between a top fabric and a backing, then stitch through all layers in lines or patterns. This traps air for **warmth** and adds padding and decoration, as in duvets, jackets and bags.
- **Pipe**: cover a cord with a **bias-cut** strip of fabric and insert it into a seam. **Piping** defines and strengthens edges and adds decoration on cushions, upholstery and bags.

### Electronic and mechanical systems
- **Cut**: cut PCB board with a guillotine or hacksaw, strip wire with **wire strippers**, and trim component legs after soldering with **side cutters**.
- **Drill**: drill component holes, typically about 1 mm, through the copper pads with a small **PCB drill** stand, keeping the bit square to the board.
- **Solder**: fit the component and bend the legs slightly to hold it. Heat the **pad and the leg together** with a clean, tinned iron for a couple of seconds, then feed **solder** onto the joint, not the iron, so it flows round the leg into a small shiny cone. Remove the solder, then the iron. Work in a ventilated area or use fume extraction, and use lead-free solder.

::: terms
- **Wastage**: shaping by removing material, e.g. sawing, drilling, turning, milling or cutting.
- **Abrasion**: wearing material away by rubbing with an abrasive, such as glasspaper, files or a disc sander.
- **Addition**: shaping or joining by adding material, e.g. soldering, brazing, welding, sewing or 3D printing.
- **Scoring**: cutting part way through thick card or board so it folds cleanly along the line.
- **Creasing**: pressing a line into card without cutting it, so it folds neatly without breaking the surface.
- **Perforation**: a line of small cuts or holes that lets part of a product be torn off cleanly.
- **Turning**: shaping on a lathe, where the work rotates and a single-point cutting tool removes material.
- **Milling**: shaping with a rotating multi-toothed cutter while the work is fed past it on a moving table.
- **Brazing**: joining metals with a brass filler that melts at a lower temperature than the parts being joined.
- **Welding**: joining metals or polymers by melting the parent material so the parts fuse together.
- **Soldering**: joining components and wires with a low-melting-point alloy, making an electrical and mechanical joint.
- **Gathering**: pulling loose rows of stitches to bunch fabric evenly so it fits a shorter piece.
- **Piping**: a cord covered in bias-cut fabric and sewn into a seam to define and strengthen an edge.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the process and the specific tools or machines**: coping saw, pillar drill, centre punch, strip heater, overlocker, soldering iron.
2. **Start with marking out and holding the work**: rule, try square, scriber, centre punch; clamp, vice or machine vice.
3. **Give the stages in order**, each specific enough to earn its own mark (one mark per correct stage in "describe" questions).
4. **Add accuracy points**: cut on the waste side, pilot holes, jigs and formers, seam allowances.
5. **Add safety points** where they fit: goggles, guards, clamping, ventilation, a safety rule.
6. **Finish the process**: file and sand edges, neaten seams, trim the legs, fettle the casting.
7. Sketches can earn marks, but label them. An unlabelled sketch earns little.
:::

::: example Explain: scoring
Explain why thick card is scored before it is folded. (2)

Scoring cuts part way through the top layer along the fold line (1), so the board bends cleanly exactly where intended instead of cracking or buckling unevenly, giving a crisp, accurate edge (1).
:::

::: example Describe: line bending acrylic
Using notes and/or sketches, describe how to bend a strip of acrylic to 90° to make a phone stand. (4)

Mark the bend line on the protective film with a marker, and check the length on each side (1). Place the acrylic on a strip heater with the line directly over the heating element, and turn it over regularly until it softens along the line (1). Bend it over a wooden former or jig set at 90°, wearing heat-resistant gloves (1). Hold it against the former until it cools and hardens, then check the angle with a try square (1).
:::

::: example Describe: soldering
Describe how to solder a resistor onto a printed circuit board. (4)

Bend the legs and push the resistor through the correct holes, bending the legs out slightly on the copper side to hold it (1). Clean and tin the soldering iron, then hold its tip against both the copper pad and the leg for a couple of seconds to heat them (1). Feed solder onto the joint, not the iron, so it flows round the leg and forms a small shiny cone (1). Remove the solder, then the iron, let the joint cool without moving it, and trim the excess leg with side cutters (1).
:::

::: example Compare: brazing and welding
Explain the difference between brazing and welding steel. (2)

In brazing, a brass filler rod melts and bonds the steel parts together, but the steel itself does not melt (1). In welding, the edges of the steel parts are melted and fuse together, usually with a filler of the same metal, giving a stronger joint (1).
:::

::: example Describe: extended making sequence
Describe how you would make the pine sides of a small shelf unit with a housing joint to hold the shelf. Include tools, the sequence and safety. (6)

Measure and mark the length with a steel rule and try square, using a marking knife to cut the fibres so the sawn edge will be clean. Mark the housing's width by holding the actual shelf against the side, so the joint fits exactly, and set a marking gauge to the depth. Clamp the side to the bench and cut down both housing lines to the gauge line with a tenon saw, sawing on the waste side of each line. Remove the waste with a bevel-edged chisel and mallet, working in from both edges so the far side doesn't split, and keep both hands behind the cutting edge. Finish the bottom of the housing level with the chisel or a router plane, and test the fit with the shelf. Cut the ends to length with the tenon saw, then plane the edges straight and square with a smoothing plane. Finally, sand all faces with glasspaper on a cork block, working with the grain from coarse to fine grades before assembly. Throughout, wear goggles when chiselling, keep the workpiece clamped, and keep tools sharp so less force is needed. (Level 3: 5–6 marks)
:::

::: q
Name a tool used to cut a curve in 6 mm plywood. (1)
---
Coping saw (or scroll saw, jigsaw, bandsaw, laser cutter).
:::

::: q
Explain why a centre punch is used before drilling metal. (2)
---
It makes a small dent at the centre of the hole (1), so the drill bit starts exactly in position and does not wander across the surface (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the difference between turning and milling. (2)
---
In turning the work rotates and a single-point tool moves along it (on a lathe) (1); in milling the multi-toothed cutter rotates and the work is fed past it on a moving table (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the purpose of perforating card. (2)
---
A line of small cuts or holes weakens the card along a line (1) so part of it can be torn off neatly and easily, e.g. a ticket stub or a tear-open strip on packaging (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how to gather fabric to fit a waistband. (3)
---
Sew two parallel rows of long, loose stitches along the edge within the seam allowance (1); pull the bobbin threads to bunch the fabric until it is the length of the waistband, spreading the gathers evenly (1); pin and stitch the gathered edge to the waistband, then remove the gathering threads (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why quilting is used in a winter jacket. (2)
---
Wadding is held between layers of fabric by stitching (1); the wadding traps air, which insulates the wearer and keeps them warm, and the stitching stops the wadding moving about (1).
:::

::: watch
- Name **specific tools**: "a coping saw", "a pillar drill", "tin snips". "A saw" or "a machine" earns nothing.
- **Score ≠ crease.** Scoring cuts part way through; creasing presses a line without cutting.
- **Brazing** melts the filler only; **welding** melts the parent metal. **Soldering** is the lowest-temperature joining method.
- In turning the **work** spins; in milling the **cutter** spins.
- "Describe how to…" is **one mark per correct stage in order**, so give each stage its own sentence, starting with marking out and holding the work.
- Include **safety and accuracy** points, but don't let a list of PPE replace the actual process steps.
:::

::: test
Describe the difference between scoring and creasing card. (2)
---

::: test
Explain why acrylic should be drilled at a low speed with a backing board underneath. (2)
---
A high speed causes friction heat that can melt the acrylic or make the bit grab and crack it (1); a backing board supports the underside so the acrylic does not chip or shatter as the bit breaks through (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a brazed joint does not melt the steel parts being joined. (2)
---
The brass brazing filler has a lower melting point than steel (1), so the joint is heated only enough to melt the filler, which flows into the joint and bonds the parts without melting them (1).
:::

::: test
Using notes and/or sketches, describe how to sand cast a small aluminium part. (5)
---
1 mark per correct stage, in order: a pattern is placed in the drag half of the moulding box and casting sand is rammed firmly round it (1); the box is turned over, the cope is added, and sand is rammed round sprue and riser pins (1); the pattern and pins are removed, leaving a cavity with a runner and riser (1); molten aluminium alloy is poured steadily into the runner until it appears at the riser, with full PPE (apron, gauntlets, face shield) (1); it is left to cool, broken out of the sand, and fettled by cutting off the runner and riser and filing rough edges (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how to make a plain seam and neaten it. (4)
---
Place the fabric pieces right sides together and pin/tack along the seam line (1); machine stitch at the seam allowance, e.g. 15 mm, reversing at each end to secure it (1); remove the pins and tacking and press the seam open (1); neaten the raw edges with an overlocker or zigzag stitch to stop fraying (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the purpose of pleats in a skirt. (2)
---
Pleats fold extra fabric into a narrower waist (1), controlling fullness so the skirt fits at the waist but can open out for movement and a decorative shape (1).
:::

::: test
Choose one material category from: papers and boards · timbers · metals · polymers · textiles · electronic and mechanical systems. Using notes and/or sketches, describe how to carry out one shaping or forming process from your category. Name the process and the tools used. (6)
---
Process and tools named for the category (1); up to 4 marks for correct, specific stages in a logical order (1 each); 1 mark for a relevant accuracy or safety point. Examples: card, scoring and folding a net (knife, safety rule, mat, bone folder). Timber, cutting a halving joint (try square, tenon saw, chisel) or planing to size. ::: test
Explain why soldering is used for electronic circuits instead of brazing or welding. (3)
---
Soldering works at a much lower temperature (about 200 °C) (1), so it does not damage heat-sensitive components or lift the copper tracks from the board (1); solder conducts electricity, making a reliable electrical as well as mechanical joint that can be desoldered for repair (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage of using a laser cutter rather than a coping saw to cut shapes from 3 mm acrylic. (2)
---
The laser cutter follows CAD data exactly (1), so shapes are accurate, identical when repeated, and the edges are melted smooth and polished, needing no filing (1).
:::

Describe how you would make a part for a product in a material of your choice. Include the tools and equipment, the sequence of processes, and how accuracy and safety are ensured. (6)

Levels:

3–4: A mostly logical sequence with some named tools and some accuracy or safety points; some stages vague or missing.
1–2: Simple, generic steps with little sequence or specific detail.
