## What it is

No one can cut, shape or form a material to an **exact** size every time. Saws wander, tools wear, materials shrink and swell, and measuring has limits. A **tolerance** is the **permitted variation** from the stated size: the range of sizes that is still acceptable. The spec asks you to **work accurately using tolerances**, know how materials are **cut, shaped and formed to designated tolerances**, and explain **why tolerances are applied** during making.

**Writing and calculating tolerances**

A tolerance is usually written as a **plus or minus** value after the nominal (intended) size:

- **40 mm ± 0.5 mm** means any size from **39.5 mm** (lower limit) to **40.5 mm** (upper limit) is acceptable.
- The **upper limit** = nominal + tolerance; the **lower limit** = nominal − tolerance.
- The **total tolerance range** = upper − lower = twice the ± value (here 1 mm).

Tolerances can also be **unequal**, e.g. 25 +0.2 / −0.0 mm, where the part may be slightly bigger but never smaller (used when a part must never be too small, such as a shaft that must fill a bearing).

Tolerances may be given as a **percentage**, especially for electronic components:

- A **470 Ω resistor with ±5%** tolerance: 5% of 470 is $0.05 \times 470 = 23.5$ Ω, so the real value can be anything from $470 - 23.5 = 446.5$ Ω to $470 + 23.5 = 493.5$ Ω.
- The tolerance of a resistor is shown by its **last colour band**: **gold = ±5%**, **silver = ±10%**, **brown = ±1%**.

**Why tolerances are applied**

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Parts must fit together** | a lid must fit a box, a peg its hole, an axle its bearing, a PCB its case; each part's tolerance controls whether the assembly works |
| **Interchangeability** | in batch and mass production any part must fit any matching part; replacement parts must fit without adjustment |
| **Function and safety** | a component outside tolerance may not work, may be loose, may fail under load, or may overheat (e.g. the wrong resistance) |
| **Quality control** | tolerances give a clear pass/fail standard to check against, so faulty parts are rejected before assembly |
| **Cost** | tighter tolerances need more accurate machines, more checks and slower work, so they cost more; designers set tolerances **only as tight as needed** |
| **Communication** | the working drawing or manufacturing specification tells every maker the same acceptable range |

**Types of fit (useful for developed answers)**

- **Clearance fit**: the hole is always a little larger than the shaft, so the parts slide or turn (a wheel on an axle).
- **Interference fit**: the shaft is slightly larger than the hole, so the parts must be pressed together and stay put (a bearing pressed into a housing, a peg in a tight hole).

Choosing the tolerances on each part decides which fit you get.

**Cutting, shaping and forming to designated tolerances, by material area**

| Material area | How accuracy and tolerance are controlled | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | accurate marking out; steel rule and craft knife on a cutting mat; die-cutting in industry; **registration marks** so colours line up in printing; crop marks show trim lines; creasing lines for clean folds | a net must fold so tabs meet edges; misregistered printing looks blurred |
| **Timbers** | marking gauge, try square and marking knife; cut on the **waste side** of the line and plane or sand to the line; jigs and CNC routers for repeatable parts | a joint cut too loose is weak; too tight splits the timber; timber moves with moisture so allow for it |
| **Metals** | scriber and engineer's square with marking blue; centre punch before drilling; files to the line; lathes and CNC milling to fractions of a millimetre; measure with a **vernier calliper** or **micrometer** | a shaft turned to size to fit a bearing |
| **Polymers** | laser cutting (allowing for the **kerf**, the width burnt away); CNC; moulds made oversize to allow for **shrinkage** as polymers cool | acrylic parts slotted together need slot widths to match the actual sheet thickness |
| **Textiles** | **seam allowance** added to pattern pieces (commonly 15 mm); accurate cutting and pinning; notches to match pieces; machine guides for even stitching | seam allowance too small frays or pulls apart; inaccurate seams change the garment's size and fit |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | components chosen with suitable tolerances (e.g. ±1% resistors where precision matters); PCB tracks and holes to fit component legs; gears must mesh at the right centre distance | wrong resistor value changes LED current or timing; misaligned gears jam or slip |

**Quality control checks.** Parts are checked against their tolerances with measuring tools (rules, callipers, micrometers, multimeters for resistance) or with **go/no-go gauges**: a gauge with one end that must fit (go) and one that must not (no-go). If both conditions are satisfied, the part is within tolerance, which is much faster than measuring every part.

**Seam allowance as a tolerance problem.** A pattern piece for a panel whose finished width is 200 mm needs 15 mm seam allowance on each seamed edge, so it is cut $200 + 15 + 15 = 230$ mm wide. If the seams are sewn inaccurately (say at 12 mm instead of 15 mm), the finished panel will be 6 mm wider than planned, which is why sewing machines have guide marks on the plate.

::: terms
- **Tolerance**: the acceptable amount by which a measurement can vary from its stated size, written as ± a value.
- **Nominal size**: the intended or stated size of a part before any tolerance is applied.
- **Upper limit**: the largest acceptable size, found by adding the tolerance to the nominal size.
- **Lower limit**: the smallest acceptable size, found by subtracting the tolerance from the nominal size.
- **Interchangeability**: the ability of any part to fit any matching part, because both are made within tolerance.
- **Clearance fit**: a fit where the hole is always larger than the shaft so parts can move.
- **Interference fit**: a fit where the shaft is slightly larger than the hole so parts are pressed and held tightly.
- **Seam allowance**: the extra fabric added beyond the stitching line on a pattern piece, commonly 15 mm.
- **Kerf**: the width of material removed by a cutting tool, such as a saw blade or laser beam.
- **Go/no-go gauge**: a quality control tool that checks quickly whether a part is within its tolerance limits.
- **Registration marks**: printed marks used to line up each colour accurately in printing.
:::

::: method
1. **Define tolerance** as the **acceptable variation** from a stated size, written ± a value.
2. **For limits**, show both calculations: nominal + tolerance and nominal − tolerance, with units.
3. **For percentage tolerances**, find the percentage of the nominal value first, then add and subtract.
4. **For "why" questions**, link tolerance to **fit, interchangeability, function, quality control or cost**, and develop each point.
5. **Name the tool or method** that achieves the tolerance for the material (vernier calliper, jig, CNC, seam guide).
:::

Explain what is meant by the term "tolerance" when measuring a component. (2)

A tolerance is the acceptable amount a measurement can vary from its stated size (1), given as an upper and lower limit, e.g. 50 mm ± 0.5 mm means anything from 49.5 mm to 50.5 mm is acceptable (1).

::: example Limits
A slot is specified as 12 mm ± 0.2 mm. Calculate the largest and smallest acceptable widths. (2)

Largest: $12 + 0.2 = 12.2$ mm (1). Smallest: $12 - 0.2 = 11.8$ mm (1).
:::

::: example Resistor tolerance
A 1 kΩ resistor has a tolerance of ±10%. Calculate the range of possible resistance values. (3)

$10\%$ of 1000 Ω $= 100$ Ω (1). Lowest $= 1000 - 100 = 900$ Ω (1). Highest $= 1000 + 100 = 1100$ Ω (1).
:::

::: example Why tolerances matter
Explain why tolerances are important when parts made by different people or machines must fit together. (4)

Every part varies slightly in size, so a tolerance sets the range within which each part must be made (1). If each part is within tolerance, any part will fit any matching part, for example any lid will fit any box in a batch (1). This interchangeability means parts can be assembled quickly without adjusting each one, and replacement parts will fit (1). Parts outside tolerance are rejected at quality control, so products don't fail, rattle or jam when used (1).
:::

::: example Seam allowance
A bag panel must be 320 mm by 250 mm when finished. A 15 mm seam allowance is added to all four edges. Calculate the size of the cut fabric piece. (2)

$320 + 2 \times 15 = 350$ mm (1). $250 + 2 \times 15 = 280$ mm, so the piece is 350 mm by 280 mm (1).
:::

Explain why a laser cutter's kerf must be considered when designing slot-together parts. (2)
---
The beam burns away a small width of material (1), so slots come out slightly wider and parts slightly smaller, and joints may be loose unless the drawing is adjusted (1).

::: q
A 220 Ω resistor has a tolerance of ±5%. A student measures it at 228 Ω. Is it within tolerance? Show your working. (3)
---
$0.05 \times 220 = 11$ Ω (1). Range $209$ to $231$ Ω (1). 228 Ω is within tolerance (1).
:::

::: watch
- A tolerance gives a **range**: always state **both limits**.
- Percentage tolerances: find the percentage **of the nominal value** first; don't add 5 to the value.
- Define tolerance as **acceptable variation**, not "how accurate you are".
- "Why tolerances" answers need **consequences**: parts not fitting, loose or jammed assemblies, failure, rejects, cost.
- Seam allowance is added **to each seamed edge**, so a width grows by twice the allowance.
:::

::: test
Describe how a timber joint can be cut to a close tolerance using hand tools. (4)
---
Mark out with a try square, marking gauge and marking knife for fine, accurate lines (1). Cut on the waste side of the line with a tenon saw (1). Pare or plane back to the line with a chisel or plane (1). Test the fit and adjust gradually; check with a square and rule (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a manufacturer of injection-moulded polymer parts ensures they are made to tolerance. (3)
---
The mould is made slightly oversize to allow for shrinkage as the polymer cools (1); temperature, pressure and cooling time are controlled consistently by the machine (1); samples are checked with gauges or callipers at quality control and parts outside tolerance rejected (1).
:::
