## What it is

New materials let designers make products that are lighter, stronger, more responsive or more comfortable than before. The spec sorts them into four groups: **modern materials**, **smart materials**, **composite materials** and **technical textiles**. The exam tests the definitions (a favourite multiple-choice question), named examples with products, and "explain why this material is used" questions. You may also be asked to pull figures out of a technical data table.

### Modern materials

A **modern material** is one that has been **developed through the invention of new or improved processes**, or **altered to perform a particular function**. It does not have to react to anything; it is simply new, or newly engineered.

**Developed through new or improved processes**

| Material | What it is | Key properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Graphene** | a sheet of carbon one atom thick, arranged in a honeycomb (hexagonal) pattern | extremely strong for its weight, very light, flexible, almost transparent, conducts electricity and heat extremely well | added to composites for sports equipment; research into flexible screens, faster-charging batteries, sensors and conductive inks |
| **Metal foams** | a metal, often aluminium, with a large proportion of gas-filled pores, like a solid sponge | very light, stiff, absorbs impact energy and sound, still conducts heat | crash-energy absorbers in cars, lightweight structural panels, heat exchangers, sound insulation |
| **Titanium** | a metal that has become more widely used as processing has improved, although it is still costly to extract | high strength-to-weight ratio, excellent corrosion resistance, biocompatible (the body does not reject it) | aircraft parts, hip and dental implants, high-end bike frames, watch cases, glasses frames |

**Altered to perform a particular function**

| Material | What has been altered | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Coated metals** | a thin layer is applied to a metal to add a property: zinc (galvanising) for corrosion resistance, PTFE for non-stick, chrome for a hard shiny finish, polymer coatings for grip and insulation, titanium nitride for hardness | galvanised steel fencing and buckets, non-stick pans, taps, dipped tool handles, drill bits |
| **Liquid crystal displays (LCDs)** | liquid crystals change alignment when a small voltage is applied, blocking or letting through polarised light to form images | calculators, digital watches, thermostats, screens on appliances and many monitors |
| **Nanomaterials** | materials engineered at the nanoscale (1–100 nanometres; a nanometre is a billionth of a metre), where they behave differently from the bulk material | silver nanoparticles in antibacterial socks and fabrics; self-cleaning glass; water-repellent coatings; carbon nanotubes in sports equipment; sunscreens |

### Smart materials

A **smart material** has one or more properties that **change significantly, in a controlled way, in response to an external stimulus**, such as stress, temperature, moisture, light or pH, and usually **change back** when the stimulus is removed.

| Smart material | Stimulus | What happens | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shape memory alloy (SMA)**, e.g. nitinol (nickel and titanium) | temperature (and stress) | can be deformed, then returns to its original "remembered" shape when heated above a set temperature; some types spring back after bending | glasses frames that spring back into shape, medical stents that open inside a blood vessel, fire sprinkler triggers, small actuators in robotics |
| **Thermochromic pigment** | temperature | changes colour at a set temperature | kettles that change colour when hot, baby spoons and bath toys that warn when food or water is too hot, battery testers, forehead thermometers, novelty mugs |
| **Photochromic pigment** | ultraviolet light | changes colour or darkens in UV light, returns when out of it | lenses that darken outdoors, UV-warning beads and wristbands, T-shirt prints that appear in sunlight |

Other stimuli in the spec: **moisture** (materials that swell or change colour when wet, e.g. nappy wetness indicators) and **pH** (dyes that change colour with acidity, e.g. freshness indicators on food packaging).

**Smart versus modern:** a titanium frame is modern but not smart, because it does not react. An SMA frame is smart, because heat makes it change shape.

### Composite materials

A **composite** is made by **combining two or more different materials** to create an enhanced material with better properties than either part alone. Usually strong **reinforcing fibres** are embedded in a **matrix** (often a resin) that holds them in place, shares the load between them and protects them.

| Composite | Made from | Properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GRP** (glass reinforced plastic) | glass fibres in a polyester resin | strong, light, tough, does not rot or corrode, can be moulded into large complex shapes, fairly cheap | boat hulls, canoes, car body panels, garden ponds, helmets |
| **CRP** (carbon fibre reinforced plastic) | carbon fibres in an epoxy resin | very high strength-to-weight ratio, very stiff, corrosion resistant; expensive and time-consuming to make | racing bike frames, Formula 1 car bodies, aircraft parts, tennis rackets, high-end sports equipment |

Each part brings something: glass or carbon fibres are strong in tension but snap or fray on their own; the resin is weak but holds them together and transfers the load. Other familiar composites include reinforced concrete and plywood.

### Technical textiles

**Technical textiles** are fabrics engineered for performance rather than looks, often by spinning special fibres or adding treatments.

| Textile | How it works | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Conductive fabrics** | fibres coated with, or spun with, metals such as silver or copper so the fabric conducts electricity | touchscreen-friendly glove fingertips, heated clothing, wearable electronics and sensors in sportswear |
| **Fire-resistant fabrics** | inherently flame-resistant fibres (such as aramids) or fabrics treated with flame retardants; they resist ignition and do not melt or drip | firefighters' suits, racing drivers' overalls, furnishing fabrics, oven gloves |
| **Kevlar** | an aramid fibre with extremely long, aligned molecular chains; much stronger than steel for its weight, and resists cutting and abrasion | body armour, cut-resistant gloves, helmets, reinforcement in tyres, ropes and sports equipment |
| **Microfibres** | extremely fine synthetic fibres (finer than silk), usually polyester or polyamide | soft, light, breathable, quick-drying fabrics; sportswear, cleaning cloths, outdoor clothing |
| **Microencapsulation** | microscopic capsules containing a substance (fragrance, moisturiser, insect repellent, antibacterial agent) are attached to fibres and burst gradually with wear and friction | scented or moisturising garments, anti-odour socks, insect-repellent outdoor clothing |

### Maths link: reading a technical data table

Designers compare materials using data sheets. **Strength-to-weight ratio** (specific strength) is found by dividing strength by density: the higher the figure, the more strength you get for each unit of mass. The figures below are rounded, typical sample values.

| Material | Tensile strength (MPa) | Density (g/cm³) | Strength ÷ density |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low carbon steel | 400 | 7.8 | $400 \div 7.8 \approx 51$ |
| Aluminium alloy | 300 | 2.7 | $300 \div 2.7 \approx 111$ |
| Titanium alloy | 900 | 4.5 | $900 \div 4.5 = 200$ |
| CRP | 1500 | 1.6 | $1500 \div 1.6 \approx 938$ |

CRP gives about $938 \div 51 \approx 18$ times the strength per unit mass of low carbon steel, which is why it is chosen for racing bikes despite its cost.

::: terms
- **Modern material**: a material developed through new or improved processes, or altered to perform a particular function.
- **Graphene**: a sheet of carbon one atom thick, extremely strong and light and an excellent conductor of electricity and heat.
- **Metal foam**: a metal, often aluminium, containing many gas-filled pores, making it very light and good at absorbing impact.
- **Nanomaterial**: a material engineered at a scale of 1 to 100 nanometres, where it gains new properties.
- **Smart material**: a material with a property that changes significantly and in a controlled way in response to a stimulus, then changes back.
- **Shape memory alloy**: a metal alloy, such as nitinol, that returns to a remembered shape when heated.
- **Thermochromic pigment**: a pigment that changes colour when its temperature changes.
- **Photochromic pigment**: a pigment that changes colour when exposed to ultraviolet light.
- **Composite material**: two or more different materials combined to make an enhanced material with better properties.
- **Technical textile**: a fabric engineered for performance, such as fire resistance, conductivity or strength, rather than appearance.
- **Microencapsulation**: attaching microscopic capsules of a substance to fibres so it is released gradually with wear.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the group** first: modern, smart, composite or technical textile. Use the exact definition for that group.
2. **For "explain why this material is used"**, name the property, then link it to the product's function or user: "CRP has a high strength-to-weight ratio, so the frame is light enough to accelerate quickly but strong enough to take the rider's weight."
3. **For smart materials**, always name the **stimulus** and the **response**, and say that it reverses.
4. **For composites**, name both parts (fibre and matrix) and say what each contributes.
5. **For data-table questions**, pick out the right column, show the calculation and compare the numbers in words.
:::

::: example Define: smart material
Explain what is meant by a smart material. (2)

A material whose properties change significantly in response to an external stimulus such as temperature, light, stress or moisture (1), and change back when the stimulus is removed, e.g. thermochromic pigment changing colour when heated (1).
:::

::: example Explain: thermochromic product
Give one product that uses thermochromic pigment and explain why it is used. (3)

A baby's feeding spoon (1). The pigment changes colour if the food is too hot (1), warning the parent before the baby is fed, so the baby is not scalded (1).
:::

::: example Explain: CRP bike frame
Explain why carbon fibre reinforced plastic (CRP) is used for a racing bike frame. (4)

CRP has a very high strength-to-weight ratio (1), so the frame is light, allowing the rider to accelerate and climb faster, yet strong enough to support the rider over rough surfaces (1). It is very stiff (1), so less of the rider's pedalling energy is lost in the frame flexing, and it can be moulded into aerodynamic shapes (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: comparing materials
Using the sample data (aluminium alloy: strength 300 MPa, density 2.7 g/cm³; titanium alloy: strength 900 MPa, density 4.5 g/cm³), calculate the strength-to-weight ratio of each and state which gives more strength per unit mass. (3)

Aluminium: $300 \div 2.7 \approx 111$ (1). Titanium: $900 \div 4.5 = 200$ (1). Titanium gives more strength per unit mass (1).
:::

::: example Extended: new materials improving products
Explain how developments in new materials have improved products. (6)

Composites such as CRP have a much higher strength-to-weight ratio than metals (1), so racing bikes, aircraft and sports equipment are lighter and faster while staying strong (1). Smart materials react to their surroundings (1): thermochromic pigments on kettles or baby spoons warn users of heat, improving safety, and SMA glasses frames spring back after being bent, so they last longer (1). Technical textiles such as Kevlar are extremely strong and cut resistant (1), so body armour and protective gloves protect users while remaining light and flexible; microencapsulated fabrics add benefits such as insect repellent or fragrance that last through many washes (1).
Level 3 (5–6): several developed explanations across different material groups with products. Level 2 (3–4): some explained points. Level 1 (1–2): simple statements or a list.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a modern material and a smart material. (2)
---
A modern material has been developed through new or improved processes or altered to perform a function, but does not react to its environment (1); a smart material changes a property in response to a stimulus such as heat or light, and changes back (1).
:::

::: q
Name two modern materials that have been developed through new or improved processes. (2)
---
Any two: graphene; metal foams; titanium.
:::

::: q
Give one use of photochromic pigment and explain why it is used. (2)
---
Lenses in glasses or sunglasses that darken outdoors (1): they darken in UV light to protect the eyes and clear indoors, so the wearer needs only one pair (1). Also accept UV-warning beads or wristbands.
:::

::: q
Explain how a shape memory alloy could be used in a product. (3)
---
Glasses frames made from SMA (1) can be bent or twisted (1) and return to their original shape, so they are hard to break and stay the right fit (1). Or: a medical stent inserted small that expands to its remembered shape at body temperature to hold a blood vessel open.
:::

::: q
Describe what a composite material is, using GRP as an example. (3)
---
Two or more different materials combined to make an enhanced material (1). GRP is glass fibres embedded in a polyester resin (1); the fibres give strength and the resin holds them and gives shape, so it is strong, light and does not rot, e.g. for boat hulls (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why microfibres are used in sportswear. (3)
---
Microfibres are extremely fine synthetic fibres (1) that make a light, soft and breathable fabric (1) which dries quickly and draws sweat away from the skin, keeping the athlete cool and comfortable (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Smart materials respond and reverse.** A composite does not respond to anything, so do not call GRP smart.
- **Thermochromic = temperature; photochromic = light (UV).** Swapping them is a common multiple-choice mistake.
- **Graphene is a modern material**, not a composite or a smart material.
- Name **both parts** of a composite: glass fibres **and** polyester resin; carbon fibres **and** epoxy resin.
- "Strong and light" alone is weak. Link the property to what the user gains: faster, safer, longer-lasting.
- In data questions, divide **strength by density**, not the other way round, and state which material wins.
:::

::: test
Explain why titanium is used for hip replacement joints. (3)
---
It is biocompatible, so the body does not reject it (1). It has excellent corrosion resistance, so it does not break down in body fluids (1). It has a high strength-to-weight ratio, so it can carry the body's weight while being lighter than steel (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why Kevlar is used in protective gloves for workers handling sharp sheet metal. (3)
---
Kevlar is an aramid fibre with very high tensile strength and cut resistance (1), so blades and sharp edges cannot easily cut through it, protecting the hands (1). It is light and flexible, so workers can still grip and move their fingers easily (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how microencapsulation is used in textiles. (3)
---
Microscopic capsules containing a substance such as fragrance, moisturiser, insect repellent or an antibacterial agent (1) are attached to the fibres or fabric (1); they break open gradually through wear and friction, releasing the substance over a long period (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one benefit of using a conductive fabric in a pair of winter gloves. (2)
---
Conductive threads in the fingertips carry the small electrical charge a touchscreen needs (1), so the user can operate a phone without taking the gloves off and getting cold hands (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using CRP rather than aluminium for a car body panel. (4)
---
Advantage: CRP has a higher strength-to-weight ratio (1), so the car is lighter, accelerating faster and using less fuel (1). Disadvantage: CRP is much more expensive and slower to manufacture (1), so the car costs more; it is also difficult to repair and recycle (1).
:::

::: test
Sample data: material X has a tensile strength of 1200 MPa and a density of 1.5 g/cm³; material Y has a tensile strength of 500 MPa and a density of 7.9 g/cm³. Calculate the strength-to-weight ratio of each and state which is better for an aircraft part. (4)
---
X: $1200 \div 1.5 = 800$ (1). Y: $500 \div 7.9 \approx 63$ (1). X has more than twelve times the strength per unit mass (1), so X is better for an aircraft part, where low mass saves fuel (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a fire-resistant fabric is used for a firefighter's suit. (3)
---
It resists catching fire and does not melt or drip (1), so it protects the firefighter's skin from flames and burning material (1); it also slows heat transfer, giving the firefighter more time to escape a dangerous situation (1).
:::
