## What it is

Designers don't buy "some wood" or "some metal". They buy **stock forms**, the standard shapes and sizes manufacturers supply, and **standard components** such as screws, hinges, zips and resistors. Knowing what is available lets a designer **work out exactly how much to order**, design parts to fit standard sizes, and keep waste and cost down. This is the most maths-heavy specialist topic. Expect 3–6 mark calculations on area, quantities, rounding and cost.

### Why standard stock and components?
- **Cheaper**: they are mass-produced in huge volumes.
- **Easy to get quickly**: many suppliers keep them in stock, so there are short lead times.
- **Consistent quality and size**: parts fit together, and machines and tools are set up for them.
- **Easy to calculate and order**: known sizes make quantity and cost planning simple.
- **Standard components save making time**: nobody makes their own screws or zips. They are tested, interchangeable and easy to replace when repairing.

### Stock forms and components by category

| **Electronic and mechanical components** | individual parts, packs, reels (for pick-and-place machines) | **quantity**, **voltage** and **current rating** (e.g. a switch rated 12 V, 3 A) | **E12 resistor series**, **DIL** (dual in-line) **IC packages**, **microcontrollers** (e.g. **PIC**) |

### Paper sizes and weights
- **A0 has an area of 1 m²** (841 × 1189 mm). Each size down is **half the area** of the one before, made by halving the longer side: **A1** 594 × 841, **A2** 420 × 594, **A3** 297 × 420, **A4** 210 × 297, **A5** 148 × 210 mm.
- So A4 is $\frac{1}{16}$ m². Since **gsm is the mass of 1 m²**, one sheet of 80 gsm A4 weighs $80 \div 16 = 5$ g.
- Heavier papers and boards have a higher gsm. **Board** is usually specified by thickness in **microns** (1000 µm = 1 mm) as well as by weight.

### Electronic components
- **E12 series**: resistors are made in 12 "preferred values" per decade: **10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82**, and those × 10, × 100, and so on, e.g. 220 Ω, 4.7 kΩ, 56 kΩ. The steps are spaced so that, with a **±10%** tolerance, the ranges of neighbouring values roughly meet. A calculated value is rounded to a **nearby preferred value**. For a current-limiting resistor, round **up** so the current stays safely below the limit.
- **DIL (dual in-line) IC packages**: integrated circuits with **two parallel rows of pins** at a standard 2.54 mm (0.1 inch) spacing, e.g. an 8-pin 555 timer. They plug into **IC holders**, so the chip isn't overheated during soldering and can be replaced.
- **Microcontrollers (PIC)**: programmable ICs, also in DIL packages, chosen by their number of input/output pins, memory and cost. One chip replaces many separate components.
- Batteries, switches, motors and lamps are chosen by **voltage and current rating**. A component run above its rating overheats or fails.

### Calculation toolkit
- **Convert units first.** 1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm; 1 m² = 1 000 000 mm²; 1 m² = 10 000 cm².
- **Area** = length × width; **volume** = length × width × thickness.
- **How many pieces from a length or sheet?** Divide, and **round down**, because you can't use part of a piece. Remember to allow for the **saw kerf** (the width of the cut) if the question gives it.
- **How many sheets, lengths or tiles to buy?** Divide, and **round up**, because you can't buy part of a sheet.
- **For sheets**, fit pieces along each side separately, (sheet length ÷ part length, rounded down) × (sheet width ÷ part width, rounded down). This is usually more realistic than dividing the areas.
- **Add a waste allowance** if asked: × 1.1 for 10% extra.
- **Cost** = quantity bought × unit price. Write £ with two decimal places.

::: terms
- **Stock form**: a standard shape and size in which a material is supplied, such as sheet, rod, tube, plank or roll.
- **Standard component**: a mass-produced, bought-in part made to standard sizes, such as a screw, hinge, zip or resistor.
- **gsm**: grams per square metre, the unit for the weight of paper, board and fabric.
- **Micron**: one thousandth of a millimetre (µm), used for the thickness of board and films.
- **Gauge**: a measure of the thickness of sheet material or film, used for polymers and thin metals.
- **Ply**: the number of layers bonded into a board, or the number of strands twisted together in a yarn.
- **Standard moulding**: timber machined to a standard profile, such as dowel, quadrant or beading.
- **KD fitting**: a knock-down fitting that lets furniture be assembled and taken apart with simple tools.
- **E12 series**: the set of twelve preferred resistor values per decade, e.g. 10, 12, 15 … 82, used with ±10% tolerance.
- **DIL package**: a dual in-line IC housing with two parallel rows of pins at a standard spacing.
- **Saw kerf**: the width of material removed by a saw cut, which must be allowed for when cutting several pieces.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the units** and convert everything to the same unit **before** multiplying or dividing.
2. **Decide the rounding direction**: pieces you can cut → round **down**; sheets, packs or tiles to buy → round **up**.
3. For sheets, check **how many fit along each side**, rather than just dividing the areas.
4. Add seam allowances, kerfs or a waste percentage **before** dividing, if the question gives them.
5. **Show each line of working**, with units. Method marks are available even if the final answer is wrong.
6. For theory questions, name a **specific stock form** ("18 mm MDF sheet", "25 mm diameter aluminium rod", "3 mm acrylic sheet") and explain **benefits to both designer and manufacturer**: cost, availability, consistency, speed.
:::

::: example Calculate: pieces from a length
A 2.4 m length of dowel is cut into pieces 150 mm long. Each saw cut wastes 3 mm. Calculate how many complete pieces can be cut. (3)

Length in mm $= 2400$ mm (1)
Each piece uses $150 + 3 = 153$ mm (1)
$2400 \div 153 = 15.7$, so **15** complete pieces (round down) (1)
:::

::: example Calculate: tiles needed
A floor has an area of 12.6 m². It is covered with square tiles of 0.3 m × 0.3 m. An extra 10% is ordered for waste and breakages. Calculate the number of tiles to order. (4)

Area of one tile $= 0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09$ m² (1)
Tiles to cover the floor $= 12.6 \div 0.09 = 140$ (1)
With 10% extra: $140 \times 1.1 = 154$ (1)
Order **154** tiles (1)
:::

::: example Calculate: panels from a sheet
MDF sheets measure 2440 mm × 1220 mm and cost £32.00 each. A manufacturer needs 50 panels, each 600 mm × 400 mm. Calculate the cost of the MDF. (4)

Along 2440 mm: $2440 \div 600 = 4.07$, so 4 panels; along 1220 mm: $1220 \div 400 = 3.05$, so 3 panels (1)
Panels per sheet $= 4 \times 3 = 12$ (1)
Sheets needed $= 50 \div 12 = 4.17$, so **5** sheets (round up) (1)
Cost $= 5 \times £32.00 = £160.00$ (1)
:::

::: example Calculate: E12 resistor
An LED needs a series resistor. The supply is 9 V, the LED drops 2 V and the current must not exceed 0.02 A. Using $R = \frac{V}{I}$, calculate the resistor value, and choose the most suitable E12 value from: 330 Ω, 390 Ω, 470 Ω. (3)

Voltage across the resistor $= 9 - 2 = 7$ V (1)
$R = 7 \div 0.02 = 350$ Ω (1)
Choose **390 Ω**: the next preferred value above 350 Ω, which keeps the current below 0.02 A (330 Ω would let too much current flow) (1)
:::

::: example Outline: benefits of stock forms
Outline the benefits of using standard stock forms for designers and manufacturers. (4)

Stock forms are produced in large quantities, so they are cheaper to buy than custom sizes (1). They are widely available from many suppliers with short lead times, so production isn't delayed (1). Their sizes are consistent and known, so designers can plan parts to fit them and calculate quantities, reducing waste (1). Machines, tools and fittings are designed around standard sizes, so parts fit together without extra processing (1).
:::

:::

---
Grams per square metre (1); the weight (mass per unit area) of paper, board or fabric (1).

Give two standard components used in textile products. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): zip; press stud; Velcro (hook and loop); button; eyelet; hook and eye.

::: q
Calculate the area of an A3 sheet (297 mm × 420 mm) in m², to 3 decimal places. (2)
---
$297 \times 420 = 124\,740$ mm² (1); $\div 1\,000\,000 = 0.125$ m² (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the mass of a pack of 500 sheets of 80 gsm A4 paper, in kg. (3)
---
One A4 sheet $= \frac{1}{16}$ m², so its mass $= 80 \div 16 = 5$ g (1); $500 \times 5 = 2500$ g (1) $= 2.5$ kg (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit of using a DIL IC holder rather than soldering the IC directly to the board. (2)
---
The IC is not heated by the soldering iron, so it cannot be damaged by heat (1); it can be removed and replaced easily if it fails or needs reprogramming (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Mixing mm and m** is the commonest error. Convert first, and remember that 1 m² = 1 000 000 mm², not 1000.
- **Round in the right direction**: pieces cut → down; sheets or tiles bought → up. Say why.
- A **specific stock form** means material + form + size: "25 mm diameter aluminium rod", not "metal".
- Fit parts **along each side** of a sheet; dividing the areas overestimates how many will fit.
- E12 values: know the list (10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82), and pick the **next value up** for a current-limiting resistor.
- Show working with units. The final mark often needs the answer to the precision asked, e.g. the nearest whole number or penny.
:::

Describe two advantages to a manufacturer of using standard components. (4)
---
They are mass-produced, so they are cheap (1), which keeps the product's unit cost down (1). ::: test
A patio has an area of 22.5 m². It is to be laid with paving slabs measuring 0.5 m × 0.6 m. Calculate how many slabs are needed. (3)
---
Area of one slab $= 0.5 \times 0.6 = 0.3$ m² (1); $22.5 \div 0.3$ (1) $= 75$ slabs (1).
:::

::: test
A workshop cuts 45 mm lengths from a 1 m aluminium rod. Ignoring waste from cutting, calculate how many complete lengths can be cut, and the length of the offcut left over. (3)
---
$1000 \div 45 = 22.2$ (1), so 22 complete lengths (1); $22 \times 45 = 990$ mm, so the offcut is 10 mm (1).
:::

::: test
A designer makes 6 cushion covers. Each cover needs two square panels of 45 cm × 45 cm, plus a 1.5 cm seam allowance on every edge. The fabric is 150 cm wide and costs £8.50 per metre, sold in whole metres. Calculate the cost of the fabric. (6)
---
Cut size $= 45 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 48$ cm square (1); panels across the width $= 150 \div 48 = 3.1$, so 3 (1); panels needed $= 6 \times 2 = 12$, so rows $= 12 \div 3 = 4$ (1); length $= 4 \times 48 = 192$ cm (1); buy whole metres, so 2 m (1); cost $= 2 \times £8.50 = £17.00$ (1).
:::

::: test
Acrylic sheets measure 1000 mm × 600 mm and cost £18.40 each. A school needs 70 name plates, each 150 mm × 100 mm. Calculate the number of sheets needed and the total cost. (5)
---
Along 1000 mm: $1000 \div 150 = 6.67$, so 6; along 600 mm: $600 \div 100 = 6$ (1); plates per sheet $= 6 \times 6 = 36$ (1); sheets $= 70 \div 36 = 1.94$ (1), so 2 sheets (1); cost $= 2 \times £18.40 = £36.80$ (1). Also accept the plates turned through 90° ($10 \times 4 = 40$ per sheet), which also gives 2 sheets and £36.80.
:::

::: test
Explain why a card manufacturer states board thickness in microns and weight in gsm. (2)
---
gsm gives the mass per square metre, which indicates weight and stiffness, and allows postage and cost to be calculated (1); microns give the actual thickness, which matters for creasing, die cutting and fitting into machines or packaging (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a resistor calculated as 350 Ω would be replaced by a 390 Ω resistor from the E12 series. (2)
---
350 Ω is not a preferred value made in the E12 series (1); 390 Ω is the next value up, so the current stays at or below the safe limit for the component (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why pieces cut from a length are rounded down, but sheets to be bought are rounded up. (2)
---
A part of a piece cannot be used as a complete part, so only whole pieces count (round down) (1); you cannot buy part of a sheet, and buying fewer would not provide enough material (round up) (1).
:::
