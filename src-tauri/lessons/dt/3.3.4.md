## What it is

A **design strategy** is a deliberate way of working that helps a designer generate imaginative ideas and move from a blank page to a good solution. The spec names five: **collaboration**, **user-centred design**, **a systems approach**, **iterative design** and **avoiding design fixation**. It also asks how you **explore and develop** ideas through an iterative process of **sketching, modelling, testing and evaluating** to improve the outcome.

**The five strategies**

| Strategy | What it is | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collaboration** | Working with other people: other designers, engineers, marketers, manufacturers, the client and users | more ideas and viewpoints; shared expertise (a textile designer and an electronics engineer on a heated jacket); problems spotted earlier; workload shared | disagreements; slower decisions; one strong voice can dominate; needs good communication, often online for teams in different places |
| **User-centred design** | Putting the needs, wants and limits of the user at the centre of every decision, and involving users throughout | the product fits real users, so it is easier to use and more likely to sell; fewer costly changes late on | time and cost of research and testing with users; users may not know what they want from a new technology |
| **Systems approach** | Breaking a design problem into **inputs, processes and outputs**, often as a block diagram, and designing each subsystem | a complex product becomes manageable; subsystems can be designed, tested and replaced separately; common in electronics and also in manufacturing | can lead to a technical focus that overlooks aesthetics or users; subsystems must still work together |
| **Iterative design** | A repeated cycle of **design → model/prototype → test → evaluate → improve**, going round until the design meets the specification | problems found early and cheaply; each version better than the last; the user can test each version | can be slow and costly if many iterations; needs a clear end point |
| **Avoiding design fixation** | Deliberately not locking on to the first idea or on an existing product | produces more original, innovative ideas | needs discipline and time |

**User-centred design in practice.** Ask: who are the users, what tasks will they do, where and how? Observe them, interview them, give them models to try, and change the design from their feedback. Designing a remote control for older users, for example, might mean fewer, bigger buttons with high-contrast labels, a shape that won't slip from a weaker grip, and a test group of older people at each stage.

**A systems approach in practice.** An automatic night light breaks down as: **input** (light-dependent resistor senses darkness) → **process** (a circuit or microcontroller compares the light level with a set value) → **output** (LEDs switch on). The designer can choose and test each block, and swap the input for a motion sensor without redesigning the rest. The same thinking applies to making: materials in → processes → finished product out, with feedback from quality control.

**Iterative design in practice.** Dyson's bagless vacuum cleaner is the classic example of thousands of prototype iterations. At school scale:

1. **Sketch** several ideas quickly, annotate them, pick the most promising.
2. **Model** it: a card model, a foam block, a toile for a garment, a breadboarded circuit, or a CAD model.
3. **Test** it: against the specification, with the user, for size, strength, comfort or function.
4. **Evaluate**: what worked, what failed, and why?
5. **Improve**: change the design and go round again.

Each loop costs a little, but finding a problem at the card-model stage is far cheaper than finding it after tooling up for mass production. **Measurement and marking out** matter here: models must be accurate enough that test results mean something, so parts are measured and marked from the drawings, often at scale.

**Design fixation** is when a designer becomes stuck on one idea, often the first idea or an existing product they know, and can't see alternatives. Symptoms: all your ideas look like slight variations of the same thing, or like the product on the shop shelf. Ways to avoid it:

- **brainstorming / mind mapping** with no judging at first, to get a quantity of ideas;
- **collaboration**, since other people bring different ideas;
- **random input** or **morphological analysis**: combine unrelated words, shapes or functions;
- looking at **nature** (biomimicry) or other fields for inspiration;
- **SCAMPER** prompts: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse;
- setting a rule such as "ten different ideas before choosing one";
- **taking a break** from the problem, or starting again from the user's need instead of from an existing product;
- changing the **material, scale or context** of the idea.

**How the strategies fit together.** A strong design process mixes them: a user-centred brief, a team that collaborates, a systems breakdown of what the product must do, iterative modelling and testing, and techniques to avoid fixation during idea generation.

::: terms
- **Design strategy**: a planned method a designer uses to generate and develop creative ideas.
- **Collaboration**: working with others, such as designers, engineers, clients and users, to share ideas and expertise.
- **User-centred design**: designing with the user's needs, wants and abilities at the centre of every decision, involving users throughout.
- **Systems approach**: breaking a design into inputs, processes and outputs so each subsystem can be designed and tested.
- **Iterative design**: a repeated cycle of modelling, testing, evaluating and improving a design until it meets the specification.
- **Design fixation**: becoming stuck on one idea or an existing product, which blocks creative alternatives.
- **Modelling**: making a trial version of a design in card, foam, fabric, a breadboard or CAD to test it.
- **Biomimicry**: taking ideas for design from forms and systems found in nature.
- **Brainstorming**: generating many ideas quickly without judging them, to widen the options.
- **Block diagram**: a diagram of labelled boxes showing the input, process and output parts of a system.
:::

::: method
1. **Define** the strategy in one clear sentence.
2. **Give a benefit and explain it** with "so that…", linked to a better product.
3. **Use an example**: a named product, a school project or a company (Dyson iteration, a night light as a system).
4. **For fixation**, say what it is, why it is a problem, and give **two or more named techniques** to avoid it.
5. **For iterative design**, describe the **loop** (sketch, model, test, evaluate, improve) and why each loop helps.
6. **For 6-mark questions**, develop three points with examples and finish with a judgement.
:::

::: example Design fixation
Explain what is meant by avoiding design fixation. Give examples to support your answer. (4)

Design fixation is when a designer gets stuck on one idea, often the first idea or an existing product, and can't think of alternatives (1). Avoiding it means using techniques that widen thinking, so the final design is more original (1). For example, brainstorming many ideas before judging any of them, or working with others who bring different viewpoints (1). A designer of a phone stand might look at forms in nature, or use SCAMPER to combine the stand with a speaker, rather than redrawing the stand they already own (1).
:::

::: example Collaboration
Explain two benefits of collaboration when designing a product. (4)

People from different specialisms bring expertise the designer lacks (1), for example an electronics engineer helping a textile designer build a heated jacket so the circuit is safe and washable (1). A team generates more ideas and spots problems earlier (1), so fewer costly changes are needed later in manufacture (1).
:::

::: example Systems approach
Describe how a systems approach could be used to design an automatic plant-watering device. (4)

The designer breaks the device into input, process and output blocks (1). Input: a moisture sensor in the soil detects when it is dry (1). Process: a microcontroller compares the reading with a set level and decides when to water (1). Output: a small pump switches on for a set time; each block can be tested separately and improved without redesigning the others (1).
:::

::: example Iterative design
Explain how iterative design leads to a better prototype. (6)

Iterative design is a repeated cycle in which an idea is sketched, modelled, tested, evaluated and improved, then tested again (2). Early models are cheap, such as card or foam, so faults like a handle that is too small or a lid that won't close are found before expensive materials or tooling are used; each loop fixes problems found in the last, so the design steadily improves (2). Testing with the user at each stage means the prototype responds to real feedback rather than the designer's assumptions; Dyson built thousands of prototypes before the bagless cleaner worked, showing that repeated testing leads to an innovative, reliable final design (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why a modelling stage is used before a final prototype is made. (3)
---
Models are quick and cheap to make in card, foam or fabric (1); they let the designer test size, fit and function with users (1), so faults are corrected before expensive materials and time are spent on the final prototype (1).

::: q
Explain one benefit and one drawback of user-centred design. (4)
---
Benefit: product fits users' real needs (1), so it is easier to use and more likely to sell (1). Drawback: research and repeated user testing take time and money (1), raising development costs and delaying launch (1).
:::

::: watch
- Iterative design is a **loop of testing and improving**, not just "making several designs".
- Fixation needs **what it is plus named techniques**. Saying "don't get fixated" is not a technique.
- A systems approach means **input, process, output**. Name them with an example.
- User-centred design involves users **throughout**, not only in the first survey.
- Collaboration answers should name **who** collaborates and **what they add**.
:::
