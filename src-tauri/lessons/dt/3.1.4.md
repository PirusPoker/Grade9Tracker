## What it is

A **systems approach** means thinking of a product as a chain of blocks rather than as a tangle of components. Every electronic system has an **input** (something that senses or is pressed), a **process** (something that decides what to do) and an **output** (something that does it). Designers use this to plan products, choose components and add features cheaply with **programmable microcontrollers**. The exam asks you to complete block diagrams, identify inputs and outputs, explain how a sensor works in a product, and explain why a microcontroller is used.

### The block diagram

$$\text{INPUT} \rightarrow \text{PROCESS} \rightarrow \text{OUTPUT}$$

| Product | Input | Process | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Night light | light sensor | microcontroller decides "is it dark?" | lamp (LED) |
| Fridge door alarm | switch (door open) | microcontroller times 60 seconds | buzzer |
| Digital thermometer | temperature sensor | microcontroller converts the reading | display |
| Laptop | keyboard switches, touchpad, microphone | microprocessor | screen, speakers |
| Doorbell with tune | push switch | microcontroller plays a stored tune | speaker |
| Greenhouse monitor | temperature sensor, moisture sensor | microcontroller compares readings with set limits | buzzer, lamp, motor to open a vent |

The **signal** passes along the arrows. Some systems also have **feedback**: the output changes the input, e.g. a heater warms the room until the temperature sensor reports that the set temperature is reached, and then the heater switches off.

### Inputs

Inputs turn a change in the surroundings, or a user's action, into an electrical signal. Sensors give an **analogue** signal (a value that varies smoothly); switches give a **digital** signal (on or off).

| Input | How it works | Typical products |
|---|---|---|
| **Light sensor** (light dependent resistor, LDR, or photodiode) | an LDR's resistance **falls as light increases** and rises in the dark, so the voltage it gives changes with light level | night lights, street lights, automatic garden lights, camera exposure, burglar alarms that detect a beam being broken |
| **Temperature sensor** (thermistor) | a common thermistor's resistance **falls as temperature rises** | thermostats, digital thermometers, fire alarms, kettles, greenhouse and fridge monitors |
| **Pressure sensor** | detects force or pressure on a pad or in a fluid and changes its output accordingly | pressure mats in burglar alarms, weighing scales, tyre-pressure monitors, touch-sensitive controls, bed sensors for elderly users |
| **Switches** | open or close a circuit when operated: push-to-make, push-to-break, toggle, slide, rocker, microswitch, reed switch (operated by a magnet), tilt switch | keyboards, doorbells, door alarms (reed switch), fridge lights, games controllers, tilt alarms |

### Processes: programmed microcontrollers

A **microcontroller** is a small computer on a single chip. It contains a processor, memory and input/output pins, and runs a program written on a computer (as code or a flowchart) and downloaded to it. In a product it acts as the **process** block in three main ways:

- **Counter**: counts events, such as button presses, people passing a sensor, revolutions of a wheel or items on a conveyor, and acts when a total is reached (e.g. a turnstile that stops at a set number of visitors, a pedometer counting steps).
- **Timer**: measures or creates time delays (e.g. an electric toothbrush that buzzes every 30 seconds, a hand dryer that runs for a set time, an egg timer, a light that stays on for two minutes after a switch is pressed).
- **Decision maker**: compares inputs with set values and chooses an action using "if … then … else" logic (e.g. **if** the soil moisture is below a limit **then** switch on the pump; **if** it is dark **and** motion is detected **then** switch on the security light).

**Why use a microcontroller rather than a fixed ("hard-wired") circuit?**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| one chip replaces many separate components, so the circuit is **smaller**, simpler and more reliable | needs programming skills and software |
| can be **reprogrammed** to change or add functions without changing the circuit board | needs a stable power supply |
| the same board can be used in several products, cutting costs in mass production | software bugs can cause faults |
| can handle complex behaviour: timing, counting, several inputs, tunes, displays | for a very simple task a microcontroller can be more than is needed |
| can be updated to fix problems or customise the product for different users | |

This is how programmable components "provide functionality" and let products be **enhanced and customised**: a washing machine offers many programmes from one control board; a toy can play different tunes; a thermostat can be set to a user's routine.

### Outputs

| Output | What it does | Typical products |
|---|---|---|
| **Buzzer** | produces a single, simple tone or beep; cheap and loud | alarms, timers, microwave "finished" beeps, fridge door alarms, reversing sensors |
| **Speaker** | converts an electrical signal into sound of varying pitch and volume; can play tunes, speech and music | doorbells with tunes, talking toys, phones, audio devices, voice prompts on cash machines |
| **Lamp** | produces light as an indicator or illumination; filament lamps are cheap but hot and inefficient; LEDs are efficient, long-lasting and come in many colours | night lights, torches, status lights on chargers, warning lights, displays |

**Choosing an output:** a buzzer when a simple warning is enough and cost matters; a speaker when the sound must vary or carry information (speech for a visually impaired user); a lamp when the signal must be seen, or silent (e.g. for a deaf user), or when light is the product's purpose.

### Maths link: counting, timing and technical data

- An anemometer (wind-speed sensor) gives one pulse per revolution. A microcontroller counts 540 pulses in 60 seconds: $540 \div 60 = 9$ revolutions per second.
- A lamp is programmed to flash on for 0.25 s and off for 0.25 s. One flash takes $0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5$ s, so it flashes $60 \div 0.5 = 120$ times a minute.
- An electric toothbrush runs for 2 minutes and buzzes at the end of every 30 seconds: $120 \div 30 = 4$ buzzes.
- **Technical specifications**: an LED's data sheet may say it draws 20 mA. Ten LEDs draw $10 \times 20 = 200$ mA, which tells the designer what battery and power supply are needed.

::: terms
- **System**: a set of parts that work together, shown as input, process and output blocks.
- **Block diagram**: a diagram showing a system as input, process and output boxes joined by arrows showing the signal.
- **Input**: the part of a system that senses a change or a user action and turns it into an electrical signal.
- **Light dependent resistor (LDR)**: a light sensor whose resistance falls as the light level increases.
- **Thermistor**: a temperature sensor whose resistance changes with temperature, usually falling as it gets warmer.
- **Microcontroller**: a small programmable computer on one chip that processes inputs and controls outputs.
- **Counter**: a process that counts events, such as button presses or pulses, and acts when a set number is reached.
- **Timer**: a process that creates or measures a time delay before or during an action.
- **Decision making**: a process that compares an input with a set value and chooses an action, using if-then logic.
- **Output**: the part of a system that produces the result, such as sound, light or movement.
- **Feedback**: information from the output fed back to the input so the system can adjust itself.
:::

::: method
1. **Sort every component into its block.** Sensors and switches are inputs; the microcontroller is the process; buzzers, speakers, lamps and motors are outputs.
2. **For "complete the diagram"**, write one specific component in each box that suits the product (not just "sensor": say "temperature sensor").
3. **For "describe how it works"**, follow the signal: the input detects → the microcontroller compares or counts or times → the output responds.
4. **For "why a microcontroller"**, give a feature and its benefit: reprogrammable **so** new functions can be added without redesigning the circuit.
5. **For choosing an output**, link it to the user and the situation (loud buzzer for a warning; speaker for speech; lamp for a silent or visual signal).
6. **For timer or counter maths**, write the sum in steps and include the units.
:::

::: example Complete: block diagram
A bath thermometer warns a parent when the water is too hot. Complete a system block diagram for it by naming one component for each block. (3)

Input: temperature sensor (thermistor) (1). Process: programmed microcontroller comparing the temperature with a set limit (1). Output: buzzer or flashing lamp (LED) (1).
:::

::: example Describe: night light
Describe how a night light uses a light sensor to work automatically. (3)

The light sensor (LDR) detects the light level; its resistance rises as it gets dark (1). The microcontroller reads the signal and decides that it is below the set darkness level (1). It then switches on the lamp, and switches it off again when the light level rises in the morning (1).
:::

::: example Explain: microcontroller vs fixed circuit
Explain why a designer would use a programmable microcontroller rather than a fixed circuit in a child's toy. (3)

One microcontroller replaces many separate components (1), so the circuit board is smaller and cheaper to manufacture in large numbers (1). It can be reprogrammed to play different sounds or add games, so new versions of the toy can be made without redesigning the circuit (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: counting pulses
A cycle computer has a sensor that gives one pulse every time the wheel turns. The wheel travels 2.1 m per turn. The microcontroller counts 300 pulses in one minute. Calculate the distance travelled in that minute and the speed in m/s. (3)

Distance $= 300 \times 2.1 = 630$ m (1). Speed $= 630 \div 60$ (1) $= 10.5$ m/s (1).
:::

::: example Extended: programmable greenhouse monitor
Explain how a programmable system could improve a small greenhouse. (6)

A temperature sensor and a moisture sensor act as inputs (1), continuously measuring the conditions the plants need (1). A microcontroller compares the readings with limits set in its program, so it makes decisions without the gardener being there (1): if the soil is too dry it switches on a pump, and if the temperature is too high it opens a vent using a motor (1). It can use timers to water at set times and count how often watering happens, and a buzzer or lamp warns the owner of a fault such as frost (1). Because the program can be changed, the limits can be customised for different plants or seasons, so crops grow better, less water is wasted and the gardener saves time (1).
Level 3 (5–6): correct inputs, process and outputs with developed explanation of the benefits. Level 2 (3–4): some correct components, partly explained. Level 1 (1–2): simple points.
:::

::: q
State the job of each of the three blocks in a system. (3)
---
Input: senses a change or user action and turns it into a signal (1). Process: decides what to do with the signal, e.g. a microcontroller counting, timing or comparing (1). Output: produces the result, such as sound or light (1).
:::

::: q
Give two types of switch. (2)
---
Any two: push-to-make; push-to-break; toggle; slide; rocker; microswitch; reed switch; tilt switch; membrane (keypad) switch.
:::

::: q
Explain why a speaker rather than a buzzer would be used in a talking clock for a visually impaired user. (2)
---
A buzzer can only make a simple tone (1), whereas a speaker can reproduce speech, so it can tell the user the time out loud (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how a microcontroller could be used as a timer in a hand dryer. (3)
---
A sensor or switch detects hands under the dryer (input) (1). The microcontroller switches on the fan and heater and starts a timer (1); after the set time, e.g. 30 seconds, or when hands are removed, it switches the output off, saving energy (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one use of a pressure sensor in a product for elderly people. (2)
---
A pressure mat beside a bed detects when the person gets up in the night (1), so the system can switch on a light or alert a carer, reducing the risk of falls (1).
:::

::: q
An LED flashes on for 0.2 s and off for 0.3 s repeatedly. Calculate how many times it flashes in one minute. (2)
---
One cycle $= 0.2 + 0.3 = 0.5$ s (1). $60 \div 0.5 = 120$ flashes (1).
:::

::: watch
- The **microcontroller is the process**, never an input or output.
- Be **specific** in block diagrams: "temperature sensor", not "sensor"; "buzzer", not "noise".
- An LDR's resistance **falls** as light **increases**. Getting this backwards spoils a "how it works" answer.
- "Counter, timer, decision maker" are the three process roles in the spec. Use those words.
- When explaining the benefit of a microcontroller, "it is programmable" alone is one mark; add **what that allows** (new functions, customisation, fewer components).
- Match outputs to users: a lamp for a deaf user, a speaker with speech for a blind user.
:::

::: test
Explain why an LED rather than a filament lamp would be chosen for a battery-powered night light. (2)
---
An LED uses much less current for the same light (1), so the batteries last much longer; it also lasts far longer and stays cool, so it is safe for a child's room (1).

::: test
Explain how a microcontroller acts as a decision maker in an automatic security light. (3)
---
It reads inputs from a light sensor and a motion (or pressure) sensor (1). It compares them with set conditions in its program, e.g. if it is dark and movement is detected (1), then it switches the lamp on for a set time, otherwise it keeps it off to save energy (1).
:::

::: test
A turnstile at a gallery counts visitors. It must stop letting people in when 250 visitors are inside. 187 have entered and 42 have left. Calculate how many more visitors can be let in. (2)
---
Inside now $= 187 - 42 = 145$ (1). Places left $= 250 - 145 = 105$ visitors (1).
:::

::: test
An egg timer uses a microcontroller. It counts down 4 minutes and beeps once every 30 seconds during the countdown, including at the end. Calculate how many beeps it makes. (2)
---
4 minutes $= 240$ s (1). $240 \div 30 = 8$ beeps (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a speaker, a buzzer or a lamp might be chosen as the output of a smoke alarm for different users. (4)
---
A buzzer gives a loud, cheap, simple warning tone (1), suitable for most homes because it is easy to hear and uses little power (1). A speaker could give a spoken message such as "Fire, leave the building" (1), helping children or confused elderly people to react correctly; a bright flashing lamp is needed for users who are deaf or hard of hearing, who cannot hear an alarm sound (1).
:::
