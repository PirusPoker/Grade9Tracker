## What it is

**Material management** means using materials efficiently: planning how parts are cut from stock so that as little as possible is wasted, allowing for the material lost to cutting or needed for joints and seams, and marking out accurately so parts are right first time. It is the most **maths-heavy** topic in Section C: expect area, waste percentage, quantity, cost, time and rate calculations worth 2–6 marks.

**Why minimise waste?**

- **Cost**: material is paid for whether it becomes product or offcut; less waste lowers the cost per product.
- **Environment**: less material extracted, processed and transported, and less sent to landfill; smaller carbon footprint.
- **Efficiency**: fewer sheets handled, less cutting time, less storage.
- Offcuts can sometimes be reused for small parts, recycled or sold, but avoiding waste is better than dealing with it.

**Nesting and tessellation**

**Nesting** is arranging the shapes to be cut as closely together as possible on a sheet or roll, so the least material is left over. **Tessellation** is when shapes fit together with **no gaps at all**, such as rectangles, squares, triangles and hexagons. Circles and irregular shapes can't tessellate perfectly, so some waste is unavoidable, but staggering rows (like bricks) or rotating shapes helps.

Good nesting practice:

- place parts **edge to edge**, sharing a cut line where the process allows (a laser cutter can cut a shared edge once);
- **rotate** parts to try different orientations, and check which gives more parts;
- place large parts first, then fill gaps with small parts;
- start from a **straight, square edge** of the stock (a datum edge);
- in textiles, follow the **grain line** of the fabric: pattern pieces are placed parallel to the selvedge, which limits rotation, and **lay plans** are drawn to plan the layout;
- in industry, **CAD nesting software** calculates the best layout automatically for laser cutters, CNC routers and fabric cutters.

**How many parts fit? (The key method)**

Divide each sheet dimension by the matching part dimension and **round down**, since you can't use part of a part. Multiply the two whole numbers. Then **try the other orientation**.

Example: parts 90 × 70 mm from a 600 × 400 mm sheet.

- Orientation 1: $600 \div 90 = 6.67 \to 6$ and $400 \div 70 = 5.71 \to 5$, giving $6 \times 5 = 30$ parts.
- Orientation 2 (parts rotated): $600 \div 70 = 8.57 \to 8$ and $400 \div 90 = 4.44 \to 4$, giving $8 \times 4 = 32$ parts.

Rotating gives 2 more parts per sheet. **Number of sheets needed** is the opposite: **round up**. For 100 parts at 32 per sheet, $100 \div 32 = 3.125$, so **4 sheets** are needed.

**Waste percentage**

$$\text{waste \%} = \frac{\text{area of sheet} - \text{area used}}{\text{area of sheet}} \times 100$$

For 32 parts of 90 × 70 mm: area used $= 32 \times 6300 = 201{,}600$ mm². Sheet area $= 600 \times 400 = 240{,}000$ mm². Waste $= 38{,}400$ mm², which is $38{,}400 \div 240{,}000 \times 100 = 16\%$.

**Units and standard form.** Keep units consistent: convert everything to mm or everything to m before calculating. $1 \text{ m}^2 = 1{,}000{,}000 \text{ mm}^2$, so 240,000 mm² is 0.24 m². Large or small values may be written in **standard form**: 240,000 mm² $= 2.4 \times 10^{5}$ mm², and a volume of 0.000125 m³ $= 1.25 \times 10^{-4}$ m³. **Surface area** (e.g. the net of a box, to find how much board or fabric is needed) and **volume** (e.g. a block of timber or the material to cast) are both examinable.

**Material removed by cutting, and material added for joining**

Plan for material that **disappears** or is **consumed**:

| Allowance | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Kerf** | the width removed by a saw blade or laser beam | cutting eight 40 mm strips from a board with a 3 mm saw kerf uses $8 \times 40 + 7 \times 3 = 341$ mm, not 320 mm |
| **Planing and finishing allowance** | extra left on sawn timber to plane or sand back to the line | cut 2–3 mm oversize, then plane to size |
| **Seam allowance** | extra fabric beyond the stitching line, commonly 15 mm | a 200 mm finished panel is cut 230 mm wide |
| **Hem allowance** | extra fabric folded up at an edge | a 30 mm hem on a skirt |
| **Joint overlap** | extra length where parts overlap, glue or fold | a lap joint, glue tabs on a card net, overlap for a riveted metal seam |
| **Turning or machining allowance** | extra diameter or length to be removed on a lathe | turn a Ø25 bar down to Ø22 |

**Marking out: accuracy and quality**

Accurate **measurement and marking out** is what turns a good drawing into an accurate prototype. Mistakes at this stage waste material, time and money, and inaccurate parts don't fit. Marking out tools by material:

| Material | Marking out tools |
|---|---|
| Papers and boards | pencil, steel rule, set square, compasses, templates, cutting mat |
| Timbers | pencil, **marking knife**, try square, **marking gauge**, sliding bevel for angles |
| Metals | **scriber**, engineer's square, **odd-leg callipers**, dividers, **centre punch**, marking blue on bright metal |
| Polymers | felt-tip or chinagraph pencil on the protective film, scriber, steel rule |
| Textiles | tailor's chalk, pins, **patterns**, tracing wheel, tape measure |

**Angular measures**: a protractor or sliding bevel sets angles; tessellating shapes such as hexagons have fixed angles (the interior angle of a regular hexagon is 120°).

**Data points, coordinates and datums**

A **datum** is a fixed reference from which all measurements are taken, so errors don't accumulate. It can be a **point** (a corner), a **line** (a straight, planed edge) or a **surface** (a flat face). In timber, the **face side and face edge** are marked and all measurements start from them. On a drawing, holes are dimensioned from the datum edges, e.g. a hole at **coordinates (40, 25)** is 40 mm along from the left datum edge and 25 mm up from the bottom datum edge. CNC machines work the same way: every tool position is a coordinate from a set **origin** (datum point).

Measuring each hole from the **previous** hole lets small errors add up (chain dimensioning); measuring every hole from the **datum** keeps each one accurate.

**Templates, jigs and patterns** transfer the same marking or cutting to many parts quickly and accurately:

- **Template**: a shape to draw or cut around, made from card, MDF or acrylic, e.g. the outline of a phone holder.
- **Jig**: a device that holds the work and/or guides the tool so each part is identical, e.g. a drilling jig that positions holes in the same place on every part.
- **Pattern**: the textile version of a template (paper pattern pieces), or in casting, the shape used to make the mould.

::: terms
- **Nesting**: arranging parts closely on a sheet of material so that as little as possible is wasted.
- **Tessellation**: shapes fitting together without any gaps, such as squares, triangles or hexagons.
- **Lay plan**: a plan showing how textile pattern pieces are placed on fabric, following the grain, to reduce waste.
- **Kerf**: the width of material removed by a saw blade or laser beam as it cuts.
- **Seam allowance**: extra fabric beyond the stitching line on a pattern piece, commonly 15 mm.
- **Datum**: a fixed point, line or surface from which all measurements are taken.
- **Coordinates**: a pair of distances from a datum that locate a point, such as the centre of a hole.
- **Template**: a shape used to mark out or cut the same outline repeatedly and accurately.
- **Jig**: a device that holds the work or guides a tool so that repeated parts are identical.
- **Pattern**: a template for textile pieces, or the shape used to make a mould in casting.
- **Standard form**: writing a number as a value between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of 10.
- **Marking out**: transferring measurements and shapes from a drawing onto material before cutting.
:::

::: method
1. **Write the formula or plan in words first** (parts across × parts down; waste = sheet − used).
2. **Convert to consistent units** before calculating: all mm or all m.
3. **Parts that fit: round down. Sheets needed: round up.** Always try both orientations.
4. **Show every step** with units; calculation marks are for method as well as the answer.
5. **Give the answer to the precision asked**: nearest whole number, 1 decimal place, or nearest penny.
6. **For explain questions**, link efficient cutting to **cost, environment and accuracy**, and name the tool or aid (datum, jig, template).
:::

::: example Parts per sheet
Rectangular parts 120 mm × 80 mm are to be cut from a sheet of acrylic 500 mm × 300 mm. Calculate the greatest number of parts that can be cut. (3)

Orientation 1: $500 \div 120 = 4.17 \to 4$; $300 \div 80 = 3.75 \to 3$; $4 \times 3 = 12$ parts (1). Orientation 2: $500 \div 80 = 6.25 \to 6$; $300 \div 120 = 2.5 \to 2$; $6 \times 2 = 12$ parts (1). The greatest number is 12 parts (1).
:::

::: example Waste percentage
Twelve parts of 120 mm × 80 mm are cut from a 500 mm × 300 mm sheet. Calculate the percentage of the sheet wasted, to 1 decimal place. (3)

Area used $= 12 \times 120 \times 80 = 115{,}200$ mm² (1). Sheet $= 150{,}000$ mm²; waste $= 34{,}800$ mm² (1). $34{,}800 \div 150{,}000 \times 100 = 23.2\%$ (1).
:::

::: example Sewing time
A seam is 1,200 mm long. A sewing machine makes 800 stitches per minute, and each stitch is 2.5 mm long. Calculate how many seconds it takes to sew the seam, then the time in minutes to sew 40 seams. (6)

Stitches needed $= 1200 \div 2.5 = 480$ (1). Time $= 480 \div 800 = 0.6$ minutes (1). $0.6 \times 60 = 36$ seconds (1). For 40 seams: $40 \times 36 = 1440$ seconds (1). $1440 \div 60$ (1) $= 24$ minutes (1).
:::

::: example Why allow for waste
Explain why allowances for waste must be included when planning the materials for a prototype. (3)

Some material is removed by the cutting process itself, such as the kerf of a saw or laser, so parts cut to exact size with no allowance end up undersize (1). Extra material is needed for joints, seams and hems, e.g. 15 mm seam allowance or glue tabs on a net, or the product can't be assembled (1). Allowing a little extra for planing, sanding or trimming to the line means the finished part is accurate and the correct amount of material is ordered (1).
:::

::: example Datums
Explain why holes on a part should be measured from a datum edge rather than from one another. (2)

Measuring each hole from the previous one lets small errors add up along the part (1); measuring all holes from one datum keeps every hole independently accurate, so parts fit and match each other (1).
:::

::: q
A block of timber is 250 mm × 60 mm × 40 mm. Calculate its volume in mm³ and write it in standard form. (2)
---
$250 \times 60 \times 40 = 600{,}000$ mm³ (1) $= 6 \times 10^{5}$ mm³ (1).
:::

::: q
Discs of Ø50 mm are cut in a square grid from a 300 mm × 200 mm sheet. Calculate the number of discs and the percentage waste to 1 decimal place. (4)
---
$300 \div 50 = 6$, $200 \div 50 = 4$, 24 discs (1). Disc area $= \pi \times 25^2 = 1963.5$ mm² (1). Used $= 24 \times 1963.5 = 47{,}124$ mm²; sheet $= 60{,}000$ mm² (1). Waste $= 12{,}876 \div 60{,}000 \times 100 = 21.5\%$ (1).
:::

::: q
A hole's centre is given as coordinates (35, 20) from a datum at the bottom-left corner. Describe how to mark it out on a piece of mild steel. (3)
---
Coat with marking blue; from the left datum edge measure 35 mm and scribe a line with an engineer's square or odd-leg callipers (1); from the bottom datum edge measure 20 mm and scribe a line (1); centre punch where they cross ready for drilling (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Round down for parts, round up for sheets.** Rounding 6.67 parts to 7 is the commonest lost mark.
- Always **try both orientations**; the rotated layout often fits more.
- **Mixing mm and m** gives answers a million times out. Convert first.
- Waste % divides by the **sheet area**, not the used area.
- Kerf is lost at **each cut**: n strips need n − 1 cuts between them (plus any trim).
- Show working: most calculation marks are for method.
- In explain questions, say what the aid **does**: a jig holds or guides; a template is drawn round; a datum is a fixed reference.
:::

A seam is 1,000 mm long. A machine sews 1,100 stitches per minute at 2.5 mm per stitch. Calculate the time to sew the seam in seconds, to the nearest whole second. (4)
---

::: test
Explain why accurate measurement and marking out are important when making a quality prototype. (3)
---
Parts must match the working drawing so they fit together and function (1); errors at marking out waste material, time and money because parts must be remade (1); accuracy gives a high-quality finish and makes the prototype a true test of the design (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how using a datum edge and coordinates improves accuracy when marking out several holes on a panel. (4)
---
A datum is a single straight, square reference edge or point (1). Each hole is measured independently from it using its coordinates, e.g. (40, 25) (1), so errors don't accumulate as they would measuring hole to hole (1). The same datum can be used by CNC machines and for every panel, so all panels match and are interchangeable (1).
:::

::: test
A company cuts 70 tessellating triangular panels from each 1,220 mm × 610 mm sheet. Each triangle has a base of 150 mm and a height of 120 mm. Calculate the percentage of the sheet wasted, to 1 decimal place. (5)
---
Triangle area $= \frac{1}{2} \times 150 \times 120 = 9{,}000$ mm² (1). Used $= 70 \times 9{,}000 = 630{,}000$ mm² (1). Sheet $= 1220 \times 610 = 744{,}200$ mm² (1). Waste $= 114{,}200$ mm² (1). $114{,}200 \div 744{,}200 \times 100 = 15.3\%$ (1).
:::
