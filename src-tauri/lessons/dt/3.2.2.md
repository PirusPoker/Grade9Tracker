## What it is

Every product is pushed, pulled, twisted or loaded in use. A designer must know **which forces act on each part**, and how to make a material **resist** them, or **work with** them by flexing on purpose. The exam asks you to name the force, then to explain how a material has been **reinforced, stiffened or made more flexible**.

### The five forces and stresses

| Force | What happens | Everyday examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Tension** | a pulling force that tries to **stretch** a part or pull it apart | a tent guy rope, a guitar string, a bag strap, a zip being pulled, a bike brake cable |
| **Compression** | a pushing force that tries to **squash** or shorten a part | chair and table legs, the walls of a stacked cardboard box, a car jack |
| **Bending** | a load across a part makes it curve: one face goes into **compression**, the opposite face into **tension** | a shelf holding books, a diving board, a ruler flexed between the hands |
| **Torsion** | a **twisting** force applied about a part's long axis | a screwdriver shaft, a drill bit, a door handle, wringing out a wet cloth |
| **Shear** | two forces act in **opposite directions**, slightly offset, so one layer tries to **slide** past the next | scissors cutting paper, a bolt or rivet holding two plates that are pulled apart sideways, a punch through sheet |

**Stress** is how concentrated the force is: the force divided by the area it acts on, $\text{stress} = \frac{F}{A}$, measured in N/mm². A thin section carrying the same force has a higher stress than a thick one, so it is more likely to fail. This is why a designer thickens a part, or spreads a load over a wider area, where stress is high.

**Changing the magnitude and direction of forces.** A force acting at a distance from a pivot creates a **moment** (force × distance). So a long shelf carrying a load at its end puts a large bending moment on its bracket. Designers redirect forces so they act along a member rather than across it:
- **Triangulation**: a diagonal brace turns bending into simple tension and compression, which most materials resist far better.
- **Tubes and I-sections**: these put material far from the centre, where bending stress is greatest, so a light section resists bending well.
- **Corrugation or ribs**: these add depth to thin sheet without adding much weight.

### Reinforcing, stiffening or making more flexible

The spec names five methods. Know what each does *and* which material it suits.

- **Lamination**: bonding thin layers together. With the grain or fibres alternating, as in plywood, the layers support each other, so the sheet is strong in every direction and resists splitting and warping. Laminating card with a polymer film stiffens it and makes it water resistant. **Laminated timber** bent over a former before the glue sets keeps its curve, as in chair frames and glulam beams.
- **Bending**: a flat sheet is floppy, but bending it into a curve, angle or channel gives it depth. Bending a **lip** or **flange** along the edge of sheet metal stops it flexing, and a curved polymer seat shell is far stiffer than a flat sheet of the same thickness.
- **Folding**: folding card or sheet into a 3D form (a box, a tube, a triangular prism) makes a rigid structure from a weak sheet. Each fold acts like a beam edge. Folded **corners and flaps** add layers exactly where the load is.
- **Webbing**: thin **ribs (webs)** moulded on the inside of a polymer product stiffen it without thick walls, so the moulding stays light and cools evenly. In textiles, **webbing** is a strong, tightly woven narrow strap used where tension is high: backpack straps, seat belts, lifting slings.
- **Fabric interfacing**: an extra layer sewn or fused (ironed on with heat-activated adhesive) to the back of a fabric. It stiffens and supports collars, cuffs, waistbands, button stands and bag panels so they keep their shape and resist stretching.

**Making more flexible** is sometimes the goal: a **living hinge** in polypropylene flexes thousands of times; **elastane** gives sportswear stretch; **kerf cuts** (saw cuts part way through) let plywood or MDF bend round a curve; and **bias-cut** fabric drapes and stretches more than fabric cut on the straight grain.

### Across the material categories

| Category | Typical force problem | How it is reinforced, stiffened or made flexible |
|---|---|---|
| **Papers and boards** | a box squashed in a stack (compression); a card stand bending | **corrugated** board (fluted layer between liners); **folding** into boxes and triangular tubes; **laminating** layers or a polymer film; extra folded flaps at corners |
| **Timbers** | a shelf bending; a chair joint racking | **laminating** (plywood, glulam, bent laminated chair frames); **triangulation** and corner blocks; rails and braces; kerf cutting to make a board bend |
| **Metals** | thin sheet flexing; a frame twisting | **bending** or folding a lip/flange; **corrugated** sheet roofing; ribs pressed into car panels; **tube** instead of solid bar (stiff and light); gussets at joints; welded triangulated frames |
| **Polymers** | a thin moulding flexing or cracking | **webbing/ribs** on mouldings; **bending** a sheet into a curved or channel section (e.g. line bending acrylic); fillets at corners to stop stress cracking; **living hinge** in PP for deliberate flexibility |
| **Textiles** | straps and seams in tension; collars losing shape | **interfacing** in collars, cuffs, waistbands; **webbing** straps; **quilting** and layered, bonded fabrics; reinforced seams (double stitching, bar tacks); elastane for stretch |
| **Electronic and mechanical systems** | shafts in **torsion**; gear teeth and pins in **shear**; springs in tension or compression | stiff chassis and brackets with gussets; PCBs held on stand-offs so they don't flex and crack solder joints; shafts sized to carry the torque; tubular frames in vehicles and appliances |

::: terms
- **Tension**: a pulling force that tries to stretch a material or pull it apart.
- **Compression**: a pushing force that tries to squash or shorten a material.
- **Bending**: a load across a part that makes it curve, putting one face in compression and the other in tension.
- **Torsion**: a twisting force applied about the long axis of a part, such as a screwdriver shaft.
- **Shear**: two opposite forces, slightly offset, that make one layer of a material slide past the next.
- **Stress**: how concentrated a force is, found by dividing the force by the area it acts on (N/mm²).
- **Lamination**: bonding thin layers together to make a stronger, stiffer or more stable material.
- **Webbing**: thin moulded ribs that stiffen a polymer product, or a strong woven strap used in textiles.
- **Fabric interfacing**: an extra layer sewn or fused to the back of fabric to stiffen and support it.
- **Triangulation**: adding diagonal members so a frame forms triangles, which cannot change shape without changing length.
- **Corrugation**: forming a sheet into ridges and grooves to make it much stiffer for its weight.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the force precisely**: tension, compression, bending, torsion or shear. If a part bends, say which face is in tension and which in compression.
2. **Say where it acts** on the product: "the strap is in tension when the bag is lifted".
3. **Name a specific method** to resist it, and the **material**: "a lip folded along the edge of the aluminium sheet".
4. **Explain how the method works**: it adds depth, spreads the load, alternates the grain, or redirects the force along a member.
5. **State the benefit**: stiffer for little extra weight or cost, less likely to fail, keeps its shape.
6. In extended answers, cover **several forces and several categories**, with a named product for each.
:::

::: example Name the force
Name the force acting on (a) the rope of a child's swing when in use, (b) the shank of a screwdriver as a screw is driven in. (2)

(a) Tension (1). (b) Torsion (1).
:::

::: example Explain: stiffening card
Explain two ways a designer could stiffen a card point-of-sale display stand. (4)

Use corrugated board rather than single-layer card (1): the fluted middle layer holds the two liners apart, giving the sheet depth so it resists bending and crushing (1). Fold the card into triangular or box-section uprights (1): a folded 3D section is far more rigid than a flat sheet because each fold acts as a stiff edge (1). (Also accept laminating extra layers at stress points.)
:::

::: example Describe: lamination in timber
Describe how lamination makes plywood stronger than a solid board of the same thickness. (3)

Plywood is made from thin veneers glued together (1) with the grain of each layer at 90° to the next (1). Because timber is weak across the grain, the alternating layers support each other, so the sheet is strong in both directions, resists splitting and is much less likely to warp (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: stress
A steel tie rod has a cross-sectional area of 20 mm². It carries a tensile force of 3000 N. Calculate the stress in the rod. (2)

$\text{stress} = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{3000}{20}$ (1) $= 150$ N/mm² (1).
:::

::: example Explain: extended
Explain how designers make products resist the forces acting on them. Use examples from at least two material categories. (6)

A bookshelf is in bending when loaded, so the top of each shelf is in compression and the underside in tension. A designer can use plywood, whose laminated layers with alternating grain resist splitting and sagging better than solid pine of the same thickness, and add a batten under the front edge to give the shelf more depth (2). In metal products such as a car door, the thin steel panel would flex, so ribs are pressed into it and its edges are folded into a lip; bending the sheet gives it depth, making it much stiffer without adding weight or cost (2). In polymers, a moulded storage crate uses webbing ribs under the base and on the walls, so it can carry heavy loads and survive being stacked (compression) while the walls stay thin enough to mould quickly and cheaply (2). (Level 3: 5–6 marks. Answers from textiles, such as interfacing in a shirt collar or webbing straps on a rucksack, are equally valid.)
:::

::: q
Name the force acting on the legs of a stool when someone sits on it. (1)
---
Compression.
:::

::: q
Describe what is meant by shear force. Give an example. (2)
---
Two forces acting in opposite directions, slightly offset, so one part of the material slides past the other (1); e.g. scissors cutting card, or a rivet loaded sideways between two plates (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a shelf under load experiences both tension and compression. (2)
---
The load makes the shelf bend (1); the top surface is squashed (compression) while the bottom surface is stretched (tension) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why fabric interfacing is used in the collar of a shirt. (2)
---
It adds a stiffening layer to the back of the fabric (1), so the collar keeps its shape and stands up rather than going limp or creasing (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why ribs (webbing) are moulded on the inside of a polymer product rather than making the whole wall thicker. (3)
---
Ribs add stiffness where it is needed (1) while walls stay thin, so less polymer is used and the product is lighter and cheaper (1); thin, even walls also cool faster and more evenly in the mould, so there are fewer sink marks or distortion and the cycle time is shorter (1).
:::

::: q
A bracket carries a force of 1200 N over a contact area of 80 mm². Calculate the stress. (2)
---
$1200 \div 80$ (1) $= 15$ N/mm² (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Torsion is twisting; shear is sliding.** Don't describe damage ("it snaps"). Describe the force.
- **Bending is not a separate magic force**: say the top is in compression and the bottom in tension (or the reverse for a cantilever).
- Name a **specific method and material**: "fold a lip on the aluminium sheet" beats "make it stronger".
- **Webbing** means two things: ribs on polymer mouldings, and woven straps in textiles. Make clear which you mean.
- Explain **how** the method works (depth, alternating grain, triangles, spreading the load), not just that it does.
- Stress is force ÷ **area**; keep units (N, mm², N/mm²).
:::

::: test
Which one of the following forces is a twisting force? A tension · B torsion · C compression · D shear (1)
---
B (torsion)
:::

::: test
When a shelf bends under a load of books, which of these statements is correct? A the whole shelf is in tension · B the top surface is in compression and the bottom in tension · C the whole shelf is in shear · D the top surface is in tension and the bottom in compression (1)
---
B (top in compression, bottom in tension)
:::

::: test
Which one of the following is an example of lamination? A pleating a skirt · B plywood made from veneers glued at 90° · C folding a card box · D pressing ribs into a car panel (1)
---
B (plywood veneers glued at 90°)
:::

::: test
Name the force acting on each of the following: (a) a seat belt during sudden braking, (b) a key being turned in a lock. (2)
---
(a) Tension (1). (b) Torsion (1).
:::

::: test
Describe the difference between tension and compression. (2)
---
Tension is a pulling force that tries to stretch a material (1); compression is a pushing force that tries to squash or shorten it (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how corrugated card resists compression better than a single sheet of card of the same weight. (3)
---
A fluted (wavy) layer is glued between two flat liners (1); the flutes hold the liners apart, giving the board depth and acting like many small columns (1), so it resists crushing and bending when boxes are stacked, while using little extra material (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a manufacturer uses steel tube rather than solid steel bar for a bicycle frame. (3)
---
Bending and torsion stresses are greatest at the outside of a section (1); a tube puts the material there, so it resists bending and twisting almost as well as a solid bar (1), while being much lighter and using less metal, so it is cheaper and easier to ride (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one way sheet metal can be stiffened without increasing its thickness. (2)
---
Fold or bend a lip/flange along the edge, or press ribs or corrugations into it (1); this gives the sheet depth, so it resists bending and flexing (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why webbing is used for the shoulder straps of a rucksack. (2)
---
Straps are in tension when the rucksack is carried (1); webbing is tightly woven from strong fibres such as nylon or polyester, so it resists stretching and tearing under load (1).
:::

::: test
A designer wants to make a curved panel for a speaker cabinet from 9 mm MDF. Explain how the MDF could be made flexible enough to bend round the curve. (2)
---
Cut a series of kerf cuts (saw cuts part way through) across the back of the board (1); this removes material so the remaining face can bend round the curve without cracking (1). Also accept using flexible MDF or laminating thin layers over a former.
:::

::: test
A polymer hook carries a load of 450 N. The load acts on an area of 15 mm². Calculate the stress in the hook. (2)
---
$450 \div 15$ (1) $= 30$ N/mm² (1).
:::

::: test
Choose one material category from: papers and boards · timbers · metals · polymers · textiles · electronic and mechanical systems. Name a product from your category and explain two ways it has been reinforced or stiffened to resist the forces acting on it. (5)
---
Named product in the chosen category (1). For each of two methods: the method named (1) and how it resists the force (1). E.g. papers and boards: a shipping box, corrugated board to resist compression; folded double walls/corners. Timbers: a chair, laminated plywood seat; triangulated rails/corner blocks. Metals: a filing cabinet, folded lips on panels; pressed ribs. Polymers: a storage crate, webbing ribs; curved/channel sections. Textiles: a jacket, interfacing in collar and cuffs; webbing or double-stitched seams at stress points. Systems: a washing machine, a stiff chassis with gussets; PCB on stand-offs so solder joints are not flexed.
:::

::: test
Explain the purpose of triangulation in a timber or metal frame. (2)
---
A triangle cannot change shape unless one side changes length (1), so diagonal braces stop the frame racking (distorting) and turn bending loads into tension and compression along the members (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a designer can make a product resist the forces acting on it. Use examples from at least two material categories. (6)
---
Levels:
5–6: Detailed explanation of several forces and appropriate reinforcing or stiffening methods, clearly linked to named products in at least two categories; accurate use of terms.
3–4: Some explanation of methods linked to forces, with some product examples; may be limited to one category or lack detail on how the method works.
1–2: Simple points, e.g. naming forces or methods with little explanation.
Indicative content: identify forces (tension, compression, bending, torsion, shear) acting on parts; lamination (plywood, glulam, laminated card); bending or folding sheet to add depth (lips on metal, folded card boxes); corrugation (card, roofing sheet); webbing ribs on polymer mouldings; webbing straps and interfacing in textiles; triangulation, gussets and tubes in frames; choosing tougher materials or thicker sections where stress is high (stress = force ÷ area); making parts deliberately flexible (living hinge, elastane, kerf bending).
:::
