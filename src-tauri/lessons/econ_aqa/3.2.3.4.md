## What it is

An **externality** is a cost or benefit that falls on a **third party** — someone who is not the buyer or seller (3.1.6.2). Because buyers and sellers ignore these effects, a free market produces **too much** of goods with **negative externalities** (pollution, congestion, noise) and **too little** of goods with **positive externalities** (education, vaccination, insulation). This topic is about the **government policies designed to correct them**: what each tool does, how it changes behaviour, and how to judge whether it will work in a given case.

This is a **microeconomic** topic: the answer is a **market tool** aimed at a particular good or activity — a tax, a subsidy, a regulation, a campaign. Answers about interest rates or general growth policies do not answer an externalities question.

### Policies to reduce negative externalities

| Policy | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Indirect taxes** | a tax on the good raises producers' costs → supply shifts **left** → price rises → quantity bought falls; the tax makes buyers and sellers pay something towards the external cost ("the polluter pays"); raises revenue | fuel duty, tobacco and alcohol duties, a levy on high-sugar soft drinks, landfill tax |
| **Regulation and legislation** | laws that ban or limit an activity, or set minimum standards; firms or people who break them are punished | emission limits for vehicles and factories, bans on smoking indoors, age limits on alcohol, banning certain single-use plastics |
| **Fines** | a financial penalty for causing harm, so the activity costs the person or firm more than it gains them | fines for dropping litter, fly-tipping, or a firm polluting a river |
| **Tradable pollution permits** | the government caps total emissions and issues permits; firms that cut emissions cheaply can **sell** spare permits; heavy polluters must **buy** them → pollution is cut where it is cheapest; the cap can be tightened over time | emissions trading schemes for power stations and heavy industry |
| **Information and advertising campaigns** | tell people about the harm, so they choose to consume less — demand shifts **left** | health warnings on cigarette packets, campaigns on drink-driving, food labelling |
| **Minimum prices** | a legal floor on the price, so cheap, harmful products cost more | a minimum price per unit of alcohol, used in Scotland |
| **Charges on use** | a price for using something that causes congestion or pollution | congestion charges for driving into city centres; a charge on single-use carrier bags |

### Policies to encourage positive externalities

| Policy | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Subsidies** | the government pays part of the cost of producing (or buying) the good → producers' costs fall → supply shifts **right** → price falls → more is bought | grants for home insulation and heat pumps, subsidised bus and rail services, subsidies for electric vehicles, funding apprenticeships |
| **State provision** | the government provides the good itself, free or cheaply, paid for by taxes | state schools, NHS healthcare and vaccinations, public parks and libraries |
| **Information and advertising campaigns** | tell people about the benefits, so demand shifts **right** | campaigns encouraging vaccination, healthy eating, exercise or recycling |
| **Regulation** | make consumption compulsory or required | compulsory education to age 16 (and education or training to 18 in England); some vaccination requirements in other countries |

### The diagrams

A **tax** shifts **supply left** (see 3.1.6.1). A **subsidy** does the reverse. For example, a subsidy for installing loft insulation lowers installers' costs, so supply shifts **right** from S1 to S2; the price falls from P1 to P2 and the number of homes insulated rises from Q1 to Q2 — closer to the amount that is best for society once the external benefits (less energy use, lower emissions, warmer homes and fewer health problems) are counted.

### Evaluating the policies

| Policy | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **Indirect tax** | uses the price mechanism — people choose how to respond; raises revenue that can fund health care or clean-up; the polluter pays | if demand is **price inelastic** (e.g. cigarettes, petrol for commuters), consumption falls only a little; **regressive** — takes a larger share of poorer people's income; hard to set the tax equal to the external cost, which is difficult to **value**; may encourage smuggling or buying abroad |
| **Regulation / ban** | certain and simple — directly stops very harmful activity; easy to understand | costly to **enforce** (inspectors, police); may create **black markets**; gives no incentive to do better than the limit; may raise firms' costs and prices; raises no revenue |
| **Fines** | punish those who cause the harm | only work if offenders are caught — need monitoring; fine may be too small to change behaviour of large firms |
| **Tradable permits** | sets a firm limit on total pollution; cuts pollution where it is cheapest; firms have an incentive to invest in cleaner technology to sell permits | complex and costly to monitor; if too many permits are issued the price is low and little changes; firms may move production abroad |
| **Information** | cheap; changes attitudes and can have lasting effects; does not hit the poor | slow; many people ignore it, especially if the product is addictive; hard to measure success |
| **Subsidy** | lowers prices and increases use of beneficial goods; helps lower-income households afford them | costs the government money — **opportunity cost** (less for other spending) or higher taxes; firms may keep the subsidy as profit rather than cutting prices; hard to remove once started; may be claimed by people who would have bought anyway |
| **State provision** | ensures everyone has access whatever their income | very expensive; may be inefficient without a profit motive; quality can suffer if demand outruns funding (waiting lists) |

Two general cautions:
- **Government failure** — intervention can make things worse if the tax or subsidy is set at the wrong level, if it has unintended consequences (smuggling, black markets) or if it costs more than the harm it prevents.
- **Valuing the externality** — no one knows exactly how much harm one tonne of carbon or one litre of alcohol does, so policies are only ever approximate.

The best policy depends on the **context**: how harmful the activity is (very dangerous → regulation), how **price elastic** demand is (elastic → tax works well), whether people understand the harm (no → information), how much the government can afford (subsidies and provision cost money) and who would bear the burden.

::: terms
- **Externality**: a cost or benefit of an economic activity that falls on a third party not involved in the transaction.
- **Negative externality**: an external cost imposed on third parties, such as pollution or congestion.
- **Positive externality**: an external benefit enjoyed by third parties, such as reduced spread of disease from vaccination.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending on goods and services, which raises producers' costs and the price paid by consumers.
- **Subsidy**: a payment by the government to producers or consumers that lowers the cost and price of a good.
- **Regulation**: rules set by law that ban or limit an activity or set minimum standards that must be met.
- **Tradable pollution permit**: a licence to emit a set amount of pollution that firms can buy and sell, within a total cap set by the government.
- **State provision**: when the government supplies a good or service directly, usually free at the point of use and paid for by taxes.
- **Information provision**: government campaigns, warnings or labelling that inform consumers about the harms or benefits of a product.
- **Government failure**: when government intervention leads to a worse allocation of resources than before.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the externality** in the context: negative (over-consumed) or positive (under-consumed), and who the third parties are.
2. **Pick a market tool** that fits: tax/regulation/fines/permits/information/minimum price for negative; subsidy/state provision/information for positive. Never answer with interest rates or growth policy.
3. **Chain the effect**: tool → costs or information → supply or demand shifts → price → quantity → less harm or more benefit to third parties.
4. **Diagram**: tax shifts supply left; subsidy shifts supply right; information shifts demand. Label P1, P2, Q1, Q2.
5. **Use the data**: percentage changes in quantity, revenue raised, cost of a subsidy (subsidy per unit × units).
6. **Evaluate**: price elasticity, enforcement, cost to government, regressive effects, valuation, government failure — then judge which tool suits this case best.
:::

::: example State — policies for negative externalities
State two policies a government could use to reduce negative externalities. (2)

Any two (1 each): indirect taxes; regulation/legislation/bans; fines; tradable pollution permits; information/advertising campaigns; minimum prices; congestion or usage charges.
:::

::: example Calculate — the cost of a subsidy
Invented data: the government pays installers a subsidy of £450 for every home fitted with loft insulation. Before the subsidy 24,000 homes a year were insulated; with it, 38,000. Calculate the annual cost of the subsidy and the percentage increase in homes insulated. (3)

Cost = £450 × 38,000 = **£17,100,000** (£17.1 million) (1). Increase = 38,000 − 24,000 = 14,000 (1); 14,000 ÷ 24,000 × 100 = **58.3%** (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a subsidy for a positive externality
Explain how a subsidy could increase the number of people using public transport. (2)

The subsidy lowers bus operators' costs, so supply increases and fares fall (1); cheaper fares encourage more people to switch from cars to buses, increasing use of public transport and reducing congestion and pollution for third parties (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a weakness of a tax
Explain one reason why a tax on petrol may not greatly reduce car use. (2)

Demand for petrol is price inelastic because many people have no good alternative to driving, e.g. in rural areas (1), so the price rise causes only a small fall in quantity demanded and pollution falls only a little — mostly the tax just raises revenue (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — choosing a policy for river pollution
Invented data: chemical works along the River Tay-on-Esk (fictional) discharge waste that has cut fish numbers by 40% in five years. Clean-up costs the local council £3 million a year. Analyse how regulation and fines could reduce this negative externality. (6)

The government could set legal limits on the waste each works may discharge, with regular inspections (2). Firms exceeding the limit would face fines larger than the cost of treating their waste, so it becomes cheaper to install filtering equipment than to pollute — the external cost is reduced and fish numbers can recover; the council's £3 million clean-up bill would fall (2). However, regulation only works if it is enforced — inspectors cost money, and if fines are small or rarely imposed, firms may treat them as a cost of doing business; tighter limits may also raise firms' costs and prices (2).
:::

::: q
Which of the following policies is designed to increase consumption of a good with positive externalities? A — a fine; B — a subsidy; C — an indirect tax; D — a ban. (1)
---
B — a subsidy.
:::

::: q
State two policies a government could use to encourage the consumption of goods with positive externalities. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): subsidies; state provision/free provision; information/advertising campaigns; regulation making it compulsory (e.g. education).
:::

::: q
Explain how tradable pollution permits can reduce pollution. (2)
---
The government caps total emissions and issues a limited number of permits, so total pollution cannot exceed the cap (1); firms that cut emissions can sell spare permits, giving all firms an incentive to invest in cleaner technology — and the cap can be lowered over time (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of a ban as a way of reducing a negative externality. (2)
---
A ban must be enforced, which is costly (1); people may still buy the product illegally, creating a black market with no quality control and no tax revenue (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: a 25p tax is placed on each disposable vape sold. Sales fall from 1.2 million to 0.9 million a month. Calculate the monthly tax revenue after the tax and the percentage fall in sales. (2)
---
Revenue = 0.9 million × £0.25 = £225,000 a month (1); fall = 0.3 ÷ 1.2 × 100 = 25% (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse why an information campaign alone may not solve the market failure caused by smoking. (6)
---
Indicative content: campaigns aim to shift demand left by informing people of the health risks (1); but nicotine is addictive, so many smokers continue even when they know the risks (1); demand is price inelastic and habit-based, so behaviour changes slowly (1); the effects take years and are hard to measure (1); campaigns do not raise revenue to fund treatment or make smokers pay for third-party harms such as passive smoking (1); so they work best combined with taxes and regulation (age limits, indoor bans) (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- An externalities question wants **market tools** — taxes, subsidies, regulation, information. Macro answers (interest rates, "boost growth") scored nothing in 2025.
- Get the **curve** right: a tax shifts **supply left**, a subsidy **supply right**, an information campaign shifts **demand**.
- Say **how** the tool changes behaviour (price up → quantity down), and finish on the **third party** who benefits.
- Evaluation must be specific: **price elasticity** for taxes, **enforcement** for bans and fines, **cost/opportunity cost** for subsidies and state provision, **addiction and time** for information, **regressive** effects on the poor.
- Don't forget **government failure** — black markets, smuggling and policies that cost more than the harm.
- In assess and "Do you think…?" answers, compare **at least two** tools and say which suits the context best.
:::

::: test
Invented data: a city introduces a £2 charge for each private car entering a low-emission zone. Before the charge 50,000 cars entered each day; afterwards 42,000. Calculate the percentage fall in cars entering and the daily revenue from the charge. (3)
---

::: test
Explain one reason why a tax on cigarettes may be regressive. (2)
---
The tax adds the same amount to the price of a packet whatever the buyer's income (1), and smoking is more common among lower-income groups, so they pay a larger proportion of their income in the tax (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage of using regulation rather than a tax to reduce a negative externality. (2)
---
Regulation sets a definite limit or ban (1), so the government can be sure harmful activity falls — whereas with a tax, if demand is price inelastic, people keep consuming and the harm continues (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one disadvantage of subsidising a good that has positive externalities. (2)
---
The subsidy must be paid for by the government (1), so there is an opportunity cost — less money for other services, or higher taxes/borrowing (1). (Or: some subsidy goes to people who would have bought anyway; firms may keep it as profit.)
:::

::: test
Explain two policies a government could use to increase the number of people who are vaccinated. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
State provision: vaccines are provided free by the health service (1), removing the price barrier, especially for low-income households (1), so take-up rises and fewer third parties catch the disease (1).
Information campaign: advertising explains the benefits to the person and to others (1), shifting demand to the right (1), so more people choose to be vaccinated (1).
Also creditworthy: subsidies to pharmacies/GPs; regulation such as requirements for certain jobs or travel — developed.
:::

::: test
Invented data — Table 1: single-use plastic water bottles in Country G

| | Before policy | One year after |
|---|---|---|
| Bottles sold per week (millions) | 30 | 24 |
| Price per bottle (£) | 0.80 | 1.00 |
| Bottles recycled (%) | 45 | 70 |

The government introduced a 20p tax per bottle and a deposit-return scheme that refunds 20p when a bottle is returned.

Using Table 1, analyse how these policies have reduced the negative externalities of plastic bottles. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- The tax raised the price by 20p (25%), so sales fell 6 million a week (20%) — fewer bottles produced and discarded.
- Demand fell by less than price in % terms (20% vs 25%), so demand is fairly price inelastic — the tax also raises revenue: 24 million × 20p = £4.8m a week, which could fund clean-up.
- The deposit gives consumers an incentive to return bottles; recycling rose by 25 percentage points, so less litter and plastic waste in rivers and seas — third parties gain.
- Still 30% not recycled and 24 million bottles sold, so externalities remain; tax is regressive; scheme costs to run.
Levels: 5–6 — developed chains using the table; 3–4 — some development, some data; 1–2 — isolated points.
:::

::: test
Explain one reason why it is difficult to set a tax at the right level to correct a negative externality. (2)
---
It is hard to put a money value on the external cost, e.g. the health and climate damage caused by a tonne of emissions (1), so the tax may be set too low (harm continues) or too high (output falls too much and jobs are lost) — government failure (1).
:::

Invented data — Figure 1: In Country F, surveys show that 40% of adults do too little physical activity, and the health service spends an estimated £1.5 billion a year treating conditions linked to inactivity. The government plans a £20 million national advertising campaign about the benefits of exercise. Gym membership costs an average of £35 a month, and many towns have closed public sports centres in recent years.

Using Figure 1, assess whether an information campaign is the best way to reduce the market failure caused by lack of exercise. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For: lack of exercise has positive externalities forgone — fewer illnesses lowers the £1.5bn health cost borne by taxpayers; many people underestimate the benefits (imperfect information), so a campaign tackles the cause; £20m is cheap compared with £1.5bn — even a small change in behaviour could pay for itself; not regressive; may change habits for the long term.
Against: information does not remove barriers — £35 a month gym fees are unaffordable for some, and sports centres have closed; people may know the benefits but lack time or motivation; effects are slow and hard to measure; alternatives: subsidised or free access to sports centres (state provision), subsidies for gyms or cycling schemes, regulation (more PE in schools), which address cost and access.
Conclusion: e.g. a campaign is a useful and cheap first step but is not the best single policy, because cost and access are major barriers; it should be combined with subsidised local facilities.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis using the figure, justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
