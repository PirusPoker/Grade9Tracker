## What it is

Every government wants the economy to do well — but "doing well" has several meanings, and they do not always fit together. This topic covers the **principal economic objectives** of government (full employment, price stability, economic growth and a satisfactory balance of payments), **other objectives** such as reducing inequality and managing environmental change, how policies for one objective can **harm another**, how pursuing an objective can **hurt particular groups of people**, and the **moral, ethical and sustainability** questions involved. The rest of section 3.2.2 looks at each objective in detail; this lesson is the overview that ties them together.

### The four principal objectives

| Objective | What it means | How it is measured | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Maintaining full employment** | everyone who wants a job and is able to work can find one — in practice, a **low rate of unemployment** (some frictional and seasonal unemployment always exists) | the unemployment rate (Labour Force Survey) and the claimant count (3.2.2.3) | jobs bring incomes and living standards; unemployment wastes resources, costs the government benefits and lost tax, and harms people's wellbeing |
| **Ensuring price stability** | **low and stable inflation** — in the UK, the government sets a target of **2%** a year for the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which the Bank of England aims to meet | the annual percentage change in the CPI (3.2.2.4) | stable prices protect the value of incomes and savings, help firms plan and keep UK goods competitive |
| **Achieving economic growth** | a **steady, sustainable increase in real GDP** — the output of goods and services | the percentage change in real GDP (3.2.2.2) | growth raises incomes and living standards, creates jobs and raises tax revenue for public services |
| **A satisfactory balance of payments** | a **sustainable current account** — avoiding a large, persistent deficit (or surplus) with the rest of the world | the current account balance, often as a % of GDP (3.2.2.5) | a large deficit must be financed by borrowing or selling assets and may show that UK firms are uncompetitive |

### Other government objectives

- **Reducing inequality in the distribution of income** (and wealth) — through progressive taxes, benefits, the minimum wage and free public services (3.2.2.6). A very unequal society may suffer social tensions, poor health and wasted talent.
- **Managing environmental change** — reducing pollution and carbon emissions, protecting resources and habitats, and adapting to a changing climate. This links to **sustainability**: meeting today's needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- Others that governments may add: balanced government finances (a sustainable budget), regional balance (narrowing gaps between regions), improving productivity.

### Conflicts between objectives

**Policies used to achieve one objective can have a negative impact on achieving others.** The main conflicts:

| Conflict | The chain of reasoning |
|---|---|
| **Economic growth vs price stability** | to boost growth the government cuts taxes or interest rates → spending rises → if demand grows faster than the economy's capacity, firms raise prices → **demand-pull inflation** |
| **Low unemployment vs price stability** | when unemployment is very low, firms compete for scarce workers → wages rise → firms pass higher costs on as higher prices → inflation |
| **Economic growth vs the balance of payments** | rising incomes → consumers buy more of everything, including **imports** → imports rise faster than exports → the current account deficit widens |
| **Price stability vs growth and employment** | to cut inflation the Bank of England raises interest rates → borrowing and spending fall → firms sell less and invest less → **slower growth and rising unemployment** |
| **Economic growth vs the environment** | more output → more energy use, emissions, waste and use of finite resources → environmental damage; it can harm future generations |
| **Reducing inequality vs growth** | higher taxes on high earners and businesses may reduce incentives to work, invest and take risks (critics argue), slowing growth |
| **Balance of payments vs growth/employment** | cutting a deficit by reducing spending (higher taxes or interest rates) also reduces demand for domestic goods → slower growth and job losses |

Not every pairing always conflicts. Growth usually **helps** employment (more output needs more workers) and can **raise tax revenue** that funds measures to reduce inequality; **supply-side policies** that raise productivity can deliver growth **without** inflation and improve competitiveness (3.2.3.3). The conflicts are sharpest when policy works mainly by changing total **demand**.

### How pursuing an objective can harm groups of people

Every policy creates winners and losers:

| Policy aimed at… | Groups that may lose |
|---|---|
| lower inflation via **higher interest rates** | mortgage holders and other borrowers (higher repayments); firms with loans; workers who lose jobs as demand falls; first-time house buyers |
| lower inflation via **higher taxes / spending cuts** | taxpayers; people who rely on public services or benefits |
| **economic growth** via more road-building, airports or industry | residents affected by noise and pollution; future generations facing environmental damage |
| a smaller **current account deficit** via less spending | consumers (fewer imports, higher prices); workers in retail |
| **environmental targets** (e.g. carbon taxes, bans on polluting products) | workers and communities in polluting industries; lower-income households if energy and fuel prices rise (often regressive) |
| **reducing inequality** via higher taxes on the rich | high earners and some business owners |

Savers, on the other hand, gain from higher interest rates, and people out of work gain from growth — so judging any policy means asking **who** gains and **who** loses.

### Moral, ethical and sustainability considerations

Choosing between objectives is not only technical — it involves **values**:
- Is it fair to accept higher unemployment (hurting some families badly) to bring inflation down for everyone?
- Should today's growth be pursued if it damages the environment that future generations inherit (**sustainability**)?
- How much inequality is acceptable as the price of incentives?
- Should the poorest be protected from policies such as carbon or fuel taxes?

Different people and political parties weigh these trade-offs differently, which is why the choice of objectives and their priority changes over time.

::: terms
- **Economic objective**: a target that the government aims to achieve for the economy as a whole.
- **Full employment**: a situation where everyone who wants to work and is able to can find a job — in practice, low unemployment.
- **Price stability**: low and stable inflation; the UK government's target is 2% CPI inflation.
- **Economic growth**: an increase in the output of goods and services in an economy (real GDP) over time.
- **Balance of payments**: the record of a country's financial transactions with the rest of the world, including the current account.
- **Current account deficit**: when money flowing out for imports and other current payments exceeds money flowing in.
- **Inequality**: unequal distribution of income or wealth between individuals and households.
- **Sustainability**: meeting the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
- **Conflict of objectives**: when a policy that helps achieve one objective makes another harder to achieve.
- **Trade-off**: giving up some of one objective in order to achieve more of another.
:::

::: method
1. **State objectives precisely**: full employment (low unemployment), price stability (low, stable inflation — 2% CPI target), economic growth (rising real GDP), balance of payments (sustainable current account). Never give business objectives such as profit.
2. **Other objectives**: reducing inequality; managing environmental change/sustainability.
3. **Explain a conflict as a chain**: the policy → what it does to spending or costs → why that worsens the second objective. Naming two objectives is not enough.
4. **Groups of people**: identify who loses (borrowers, taxpayers, workers in a sector, future generations) and who gains.
5. **Use data**: compare figures with targets (e.g. inflation 4.5% is 2.5 percentage points above the 2% target); calculate changes where possible.
6. **15-mark**: weigh the objective in the question against the others it conflicts with, consider moral and sustainability issues, and reach a supported judgement.
:::

::: example State — the principal objectives
State two of the government's principal economic objectives. (2)

Any two (1 each): maintaining full employment/low unemployment; price stability/low and stable inflation; economic growth; a sustainable balance of payments on current account.
:::

::: example Explain — growth and the current account
Explain how a policy to increase economic growth could conflict with the balance of payments objective. (2)

Policies such as tax cuts raise households' disposable incomes, so they spend more, including on imported goods such as cars and electronics (1); imports rise faster than exports, so the current account deficit widens (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — comparing data with a target
Invented data: CPI inflation in a country is 5.1% and the government's target is 2%. Last year inflation was 6.8%. Calculate how far inflation is above target, and the change in the inflation rate since last year. (2)

5.1 − 2 = 3.1 percentage points above target (1). Inflation has fallen by 6.8 − 5.1 = 1.7 percentage points — prices are still rising, but more slowly (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a group harmed by a policy
Explain how raising interest rates to reduce inflation may negatively affect one group of people. (2)

Households with variable-rate or newly renewed mortgages face higher monthly repayments (1), so they have less disposable income for other spending and some may struggle to keep up payments (1). (Or: workers who lose jobs as demand falls; firms with loans.)
:::

::: example Analyse — unemployment and other objectives
Invented data — Item B: unemployment in Country K is 7%; inflation is 2.1%; the current account deficit is 1% of GDP. The government plans a large rise in spending on infrastructure to create jobs. Analyse why reducing unemployment may conflict with other government objectives. (6)

Extra government spending creates jobs directly in construction and indirectly as newly employed workers spend their wages, so unemployment falls from 7% (2). But higher spending raises total demand; if demand grows faster than the economy's capacity, firms raise prices, and as unemployment falls firms compete for workers and wages rise, adding cost-push pressure — inflation, currently close to target at 2.1%, could rise above 2% (2). Higher incomes also increase spending on imports, so the current account deficit of 1% of GDP may widen; however, because inflation starts close to target and unemployment is high, there is spare capacity, so the conflict may be small at first (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse how reducing inflation might conflict with achieving economic growth. (6)
---
Indicative content: to reduce inflation the Bank of England raises interest rates (1); borrowing becomes more expensive and saving more attractive (1), so consumer spending on credit and mortgage holders' spending fall (1); firms face higher borrowing costs and lower demand, so they invest less (1); total demand falls, so output grows more slowly or falls (1), which may also raise unemployment — the conflict depends on how much rates rise and on whether inflation is demand-pull or cost-push (1).

::: watch
- These are **government (macroeconomic)** objectives — profit, market share and survival are business objectives and score 0 here.
- A conflict needs the **full chain**: the policy, what it does to spending or costs, and why that harms the other objective.
- Price stability means **low and stable** inflation (2% target), not zero inflation and not falling prices.
- The balance of payments objective is about the **current account**, not the government's budget.
- When asked about **groups of people**, name a specific group (e.g. mortgage holders, workers in coal mining) and the effect on them.
- 15-markers need a judgement weighing the objectives — and moral and sustainability points can lift an answer.
:::

::: test
Invented data — Item B: In Country R, inflation has risen to 6% and the central bank is considering raising interest rates from 3% to 5%. Unemployment is 4%, and 60% of households own their home with a mortgage. Many small firms rely on bank loans.

Using Item B, analyse how pursuing the objective of price stability could negatively affect groups of people in Country R. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):

- Mortgage holders — 60% of households — face higher repayments, so less disposable income; some may fall into arrears.
- Small firms relying on bank loans pay more interest, cutting profits and investment; some may close.
- Lower spending reduces demand, so firms cut jobs — unemployment may rise from 4%, hurting workers and their families.

::: test
Assess whether economic growth should be the government's most important economic objective. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For: growth raises incomes and living standards; it creates jobs, helping full employment; higher tax revenue funds health, education and policies to reduce inequality; it makes government debt easier to manage.
Against: growth can cause inflation if demand outpaces capacity; it can widen the current account deficit; it can damage the environment and is unsustainable if it uses up resources; benefits may go mainly to the better-off, increasing inequality; high inflation or unemployment may cause more immediate harm.
Conclusion: e.g. growth is important because it supports other objectives, but it should not be pursued at any cost — sustainable, non-inflationary growth (e.g. through productivity) is the priority; the most important objective depends on current conditions (e.g. inflation may need priority when it is far above target).
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Item A — Growth or green? (invented)

Country V's economy grew by only 0.5% last year, and unemployment has risen to 6.2%. The government is considering two plans. Plan 1 would approve a large new coal mine and a third runway at its main airport, which supporters say would create 15,000 jobs and add 0.8% to GDP. Plan 2 would spend the same money on home insulation, offshore wind farms and electric bus fleets, which would create about 11,000 jobs and cut national carbon emissions by an estimated 4%. Environmental groups warn that Plan 1 would increase emissions and local air pollution. Business groups say Country V cannot afford to put the environment ahead of jobs while growth is so weak.

| | Plan 1 (mine and runway) | Plan 2 (green investment) |
|---|---|---|
| Jobs created | 15,000 | 11,000 |
| Estimated addition to GDP (%) | 0.8 | 0.5 |
| Change in national carbon emissions (%) | +3 | −4 |

Do you think the government of Country V should give protecting the environment priority over economic growth? Use Item A and your own economic knowledge to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 5, AO3 10. Indicative content:
Use of data: Plan 1 creates 4,000 more jobs (36% more than Plan 2) and adds 0.3 percentage points more to GDP; but raises emissions by 3% versus a 4% cut — a 7-point difference in emissions; unemployment 6.2% and growth 0.5% are weak.
For prioritising the environment (Plan 2): negative externalities of coal and flights — air pollution, health costs, climate change — fall on third parties and future generations (sustainability, a moral issue); Plan 2 still creates 11,000 jobs and adds 0.5% to GDP, so the trade-off is small; green investment may build new industries with long-term growth and export potential; insulation lowers household energy bills, helping low-income households; coal is a declining industry, so jobs may not last (future structural unemployment); international commitments and reputation.
For prioritising growth (Plan 1): with unemployment at 6.2% and growth at 0.5%, jobs and incomes are urgent — unemployment harms families, raises benefit spending and lowers tax revenue; more growth brings tax revenue that could fund environmental measures later; a runway supports trade and tourism; business confidence.
Evaluation: the conflict is real but not total — Plan 2 delivers most of the jobs; the costs of Plan 1 fall partly on future generations; the answer depends on the time horizon, on how long-lasting each plan's jobs are, and on whether some environmental harm could be reduced through regulation; a mix may be possible.
Conclusion: a supported view, e.g. the government should prioritise the environment through Plan 2, because it sacrifices only 4,000 jobs and 0.3% of GDP while avoiding a 3% rise in emissions and building sustainable long-term growth.
Levels: 13–15 — sustained, balanced evaluation using the Item's data, developed chains on both sides and a fully supported judgement; 10–12 — well-developed analysis with good use of the Item and a reasoned judgement, some imbalance; 7–9 — some developed analysis of both sides with some use of the Item, partly supported judgement; 4–6 — limited analysis, mainly one-sided or descriptive, little use of data; 1–3 — isolated points, assertion.
:::
