## What it is

Economists group producers into three **sectors** according to the stage of production they carry out. This topic covers the meaning of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors, their relative sizes in the UK and how these have changed, and the difference between a **good** and a **service**.

### The three sectors

| Sector | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | extracts or harvests **natural resources** — raw materials straight from land and sea | farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying, oil and gas extraction |
| **Secondary** | **manufactures and builds** — turns raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods | car factories, food processing, steel making, brewing, clothing factories, construction, power generation |
| **Tertiary** | provides **services** to consumers and firms | retail, banking and finance, health, education, transport, tourism, hairdressing, entertainment, IT services |

One product often passes through all three: a wheat farmer (primary) sells to a flour mill and bakery (secondary), and the bread is sold by a supermarket and delivered by a haulier (tertiary).

**Classify by the activity, not the product.** A firm that *grows* apples is primary; one that *presses* them into juice is secondary; one that *sells* the juice in a café is tertiary. A big company can have parts in every sector — an oil company drills (primary), refines (secondary) and runs petrol stations (tertiary).

### Relative sizes in the UK

The UK is a **service-based economy**.

- **Tertiary**: by far the largest sector — roughly four-fifths of both output and employment. Financial and professional services, retail, health, education and the creative industries are major parts of it.
- **Secondary**: much smaller than it once was. Manufacturing employed a large share of the workforce in the mid-twentieth century; its share of both employment and output has fallen a long way since. Construction remains a significant part of the sector.
- **Primary**: very small — only a few per cent of output and employment, mostly agriculture, fishing and North Sea oil and gas.

**Why the balance changed.**

| Cause | Effect on the sectors |
|---|---|
| **Rising incomes** | as people get richer they spend a growing share on services — holidays, eating out, entertainment, health — so the tertiary sector grows |
| **Competition from abroad** | countries with lower labour costs produce many manufactured goods (clothing, steel, electronics) more cheaply, so UK factories closed — **deindustrialisation** |
| **Technology and automation** | machines replaced workers in factories and on farms, so output can stay high with far fewer jobs |
| **Exhaustion of resources** | many UK coal mines closed as reserves became expensive to extract and cheaper imports were available; North Sea oil and gas output has passed its peak |
| **Globalisation and the internet** | UK firms sell services (finance, law, software, education) to the whole world |

Note that a sector's **share** can fall even if its output rises: UK manufacturing still produces a large amount, but services have grown much faster.

### Consequences of the changing sizes

- **Structural unemployment**: when mines, shipyards and factories closed, workers' skills no longer matched the jobs available, causing long-term unemployment in former industrial towns.
- **Regional inequality**: service jobs grew most in big cities, especially London and the South East, while some areas in the North, the Midlands, Wales and Scotland lost their main employers.
- **Need for retraining and education**: service jobs often need different skills (IT, communication), so workers must retrain.
- **Changes in the type of work**: more part-time, flexible and self-employed work in services; fewer unionised factory jobs.
- **Trade**: the UK imports more manufactured goods and exports more services.
- **Positives**: services are often less polluting; many service jobs are higher-paid, skilled work; growth in sectors in which the UK is internationally strong.

### Goods and services

| | **Goods** | **Services** |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **tangible** — physical, can be touched | **intangible** — an activity, cannot be touched |
| Storage | can usually be stored and sold later | cannot be stored; produced and consumed at the same time |
| Ownership | the buyer owns the item | the buyer receives the benefit but owns nothing physical |
| Examples | a loaf, a laptop, a car | a haircut, a train journey, insurance, a lesson |

Goods are produced mainly by the primary and secondary sectors; services by the tertiary sector. Many purchases combine both — a restaurant meal includes food (a good) and cooking and serving (a service).

### Calculation skills

Exam questions often give sector data and ask for a **percentage change** or a **share**:

- **Percentage change** = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. If an invented sector's share of employment fell from 34% to 9%: (9 − 34) ÷ 34 × 100 = −73.5% (to one decimal place). Note this is the percentage change *in the share*, not a fall of 25 "per cent" — that is 25 **percentage points**.
- **Share** = sector value ÷ total × 100.

::: terms
- **Primary sector**: the part of the economy that extracts or harvests natural resources, such as farming, fishing and mining.
- **Secondary sector**: the part of the economy that manufactures goods and constructs buildings from raw materials.
- **Tertiary sector**: the part of the economy that provides services, such as retail, finance, health and tourism.
- **Good**: a tangible product that can be touched, stored and owned.
- **Service**: an intangible activity carried out for a customer that cannot be stored.
- **Deindustrialisation**: the long-term decline in the share of manufacturing in an economy's output and employment.
- **Structural unemployment**: long-term unemployment caused by a decline in an industry, so workers' skills no longer match the jobs available.
- **Raw materials**: natural resources from the primary sector used as inputs by other firms.
- **Percentage point**: the simple difference between two percentages, such as a fall from 30% to 20%.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify by activity**: extracting = primary; making or building = secondary; serving = tertiary. Construction is secondary.
2. **Relative size**: tertiary is the largest in the UK; secondary has declined; primary is small. Use the figures given in the question when there are any.
3. **Calculation**: percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; give the decimal places asked; distinguish percentage change from percentage points.
4. **Consequence questions**: name the change (e.g. decline of manufacturing) → its impact (factory closures, structural unemployment) → who is affected (regions, workers with specific skills).
5. **Goods vs services**: tangible vs intangible, with one example of each.
:::

::: example Classify — three firms
State which sector each of these invented firms belongs to: Oak Valley Farm, which grows potatoes; Crispy Co, which makes crisps; Snackstop, which runs a chain of newsagents. (3)

Oak Valley Farm — primary (1). Crispy Co — secondary (1). Snackstop — tertiary (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — percentage change from a chart
Invented data: in 1960 a country's secondary sector employed 8.5 million workers; in 2020 it employed 3.1 million. Calculate the percentage change. Give your answer to one decimal place. (2)

(3.1 − 8.5) ÷ 8.5 × 100 (1) = −63.5% (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a consequence
Explain one possible consequence of the decline of the secondary sector in the UK. (2)

Factories closed in towns that depended on manufacturing (1), so workers whose skills were in factory work faced long-term structural unemployment and those regions became poorer (1).
:::

::: example Explain — goods versus services
Explain the difference between a good and a service, using examples from a cinema. (2)

A good is tangible, such as the popcorn and drinks sold at the cinema, which the customer owns and can take away (1). A service is intangible, such as showing the film, which the customer experiences but cannot keep or store (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — why the tertiary sector grew
Analyse reasons for the growth in the size of the tertiary sector in the UK. (6)

As incomes have risen, consumers have spent a growing share on services such as holidays, restaurants and leisure, because basic needs for goods are already met; the higher demand has drawn resources into service industries (2). Manufacturing moved to countries with lower labour costs, so the share of secondary output fell, and workers moved into services where UK firms remained competitive (2). The UK has strengths in finance, law, education and creative industries which it can export worldwide, helped by the internet and the English language; this international demand expanded the sector further, so its share of output and jobs grew (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse the possible effects on a town of the closure of its car factory. (6)
---
The factory's workers lose their jobs (1); many have skills specific to car making, so they may face structural unemployment (1). Their incomes fall, so they spend less in local shops and services (1), which may lose sales and cut jobs too — a knock-on effect (1). Suppliers of components also lose orders (1). Young people may leave the town to find work, and the government may pay more in benefits and collect less tax, though retraining or new service firms could absorb some workers (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- The **secondary sector is manufacturing and construction** — not shops or offices.
- Classify by **activity**: growing = primary, processing = secondary, selling/serving = tertiary.
- **Percentage change** uses the *old* value as the base; keep the minus sign for a fall and give the decimal places asked.
- A fall from 30% to 10% is **20 percentage points** but a **66.7% fall** — read which the question wants.
- Consequences of sector change should be **specific** — structural unemployment, regional decline — not just "people lose jobs".
:::

::: test
Explain two possible benefits to the UK of having a large tertiary sector. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Benefit 1 — exports of services: UK firms in finance, law and education are competitive internationally (1), so they sell services to overseas customers (1), bringing in money that helps the current account and supports high-paid jobs (1).
Benefit 2 — less pollution: service industries generally use fewer raw materials and emit less than heavy industry (1), so a shift towards services can reduce environmental damage (1) while still raising output and incomes (1).
Also creditworthy: many skilled, well-paid jobs; flexible work for people who want part-time hours; growth in sectors where demand rises as incomes rise.
:::

::: test
Invented data for Northtown: in 1980 its steelworks employed 12,000 people; in 2000 the works closed. Between 2000 and 2020 the number of call-centre and retail jobs rose by 7,500. Using this data, analyse the effects of the change in sectors on Northtown. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- 12,000 secondary-sector jobs lost; only 7,500 service jobs replaced them — a net shortfall of 4,500 jobs.
- Steelworkers' skills did not match call-centre or retail work, so many faced structural unemployment, especially older workers.
- Lower local incomes reduced spending, hitting local businesses (knock-on effect); some people left the town.
- Service jobs may be lower-paid or part-time compared with skilled steel jobs, so incomes may remain lower.
- Positive: new jobs were created; less pollution; younger workers may gain transferable skills.
Levels: 5–6 — developed chains using the figures; 3–4 — some analysis with some data; 1–2 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Assess whether the decline of the secondary sector has been good for the UK economy. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Good: resources moved to services where the UK is competitive internationally (finance, creative industries), raising output and incomes; consumers gained from cheaper imported manufactured goods; less heavy industry means less pollution; many service jobs are skilled and well paid.
Bad: structural unemployment and lasting poverty in former industrial regions; regional inequality between London/South East and other areas; the UK imports more goods, which affects the trade balance; manufacturing jobs offered skilled, well-paid work to people without degrees; dependence on financial services can make the economy vulnerable.
Conclusion: e.g. overall beneficial for national output because it reflected comparative strengths and consumer demand, but the costs were concentrated on particular regions and workers and were made worse by too little retraining — the judgement depends on whose welfare is being considered and the timescale.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, well-developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported conclusion; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
