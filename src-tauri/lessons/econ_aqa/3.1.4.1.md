## What it is

Producers need to know what they are aiming for and whether they are achieving it. This topic covers **business objectives**, the types of **cost** and **revenue**, how to calculate **profit**, how firms can raise profit, why **higher prices give producers an incentive to expand production**, and how producers' motives can **conflict with ethical and moral interests**.

### Business objectives

A **business objective** is an aim or target that a firm works towards.

| Objective | Meaning | Why a firm might choose it |
|---|---|---|
| **Profit (maximisation)** | making the largest possible difference between revenue and costs | rewards owners and shareholders; funds investment; the main aim of most private firms |
| **Sales growth** | increasing the volume or value of sales | bigger firms gain economies of scale and recognition; managers' pay may be linked to size |
| **Increasing market share** | raising the firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales | more power over prices; stronger brand; harder for rivals to compete |
| **Survival** | staying in business | the main aim for new firms, or for any firm in a recession or facing a strong new competitor |
| **Other aims** | ethical or social goals, customer satisfaction, quality, environmental targets | a social enterprise, a co-operative or a firm whose customers value ethics |

Objectives can conflict: cutting prices to gain market share reduces profit per unit in the short run; growing fast may mean borrowing heavily and risking survival.

Be careful: low inflation, economic growth and low unemployment are **government (macroeconomic) objectives**, not business objectives.

### Costs

| Cost | Meaning | Examples | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed costs (FC)** | costs that **do not change with output** in the short run — paid even if nothing is produced | rent, insurance, managers' salaries, loan interest, business rates | — |
| **Variable costs (VC)** | costs that **change directly with output** | raw materials, packaging, energy used by machines, hourly wages of production workers | VC = variable cost per unit × output |
| **Total cost (TC)** | all costs at a given level of output | — | **TC = FC + VC** |
| **Average cost (AC)** | the cost per unit produced (unit cost) | — | **AC = TC ÷ output** |
| Average fixed cost / average variable cost | fixed or variable cost per unit | — | AFC = FC ÷ output; AVC = VC ÷ output; AC = AFC + AVC |

**Why average cost usually falls as output rises at first:** fixed costs are spread over more units. A bakery paying £600 a week rent has a fixed cost of £6 per loaf at 100 loaves, but £0.60 per loaf at 1,000 loaves.

### Revenue

| Revenue | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| **Total revenue (TR)** | all the money received from selling output | **TR = price × quantity sold** |
| **Average revenue (AR)** | revenue per unit sold | **AR = TR ÷ quantity** — when every unit is sold at the same price, AR **equals the price** |

### Profit

**Profit = total revenue − total cost.** If costs are greater than revenue, the result is a **loss**.

Invented data for a small candle maker selling every candle at £20, with fixed costs of £200 a week:

| Output (candles per week) | Fixed cost (£) | Variable cost (£) | Total cost (£) | Average cost (£) | Total revenue (£) | Profit (£) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 200 | 0 | 200 | — | 0 | −200 |
| 10 | 200 | 100 | 300 | 30.00 | 200 | −100 |
| 20 | 200 | 180 | 380 | 19.00 | 400 | 20 |
| 30 | 200 | 270 | 470 | 15.67 | 600 | 130 |
| 40 | 200 | 400 | 600 | 15.00 | 800 | 200 |
| 50 | 200 | 550 | 750 | 15.00 | 1,000 | 250 |

Notice: the firm makes a loss at low output because fixed costs must be paid regardless; average cost falls as the fixed cost is spread; profit rises once price is above average cost. **Profit per unit = price − average cost** (at 40 candles: £20 − £15 = £5, × 40 = £200).

Working backwards is common in exams: **TR = profit + TC**, and **price = TR ÷ quantity**. For example, if profit is £300 and total cost is £900 on 60 units, TR = £1,200 and price = £20.

### How firms can increase profit

Since profit = TR − TC, a firm can:

1. **Reduce average costs** — find cheaper suppliers, buy in bulk, use more efficient machinery, raise productivity (3.1.4.2), cut waste, gain economies of scale (3.1.4.3).
2. **Increase revenue** — raise the price (works if demand is price inelastic — see 3.1.3.5), sell more through advertising or new markets, cut price if demand is elastic, improve quality, launch new products.

Each has risks: cheaper materials may lower quality and lose customers; a price rise may lose sales if demand is elastic; advertising costs money.

### Higher prices, higher profits and the incentive to expand

If the market price rises while costs per unit stay similar, **each unit earns more profit**. This gives existing producers an **incentive to expand production**, and attracts new firms into the market. This is the reason the **supply curve slopes upwards** (3.1.3.2): the higher the price, the more profitable it is to supply, so the quantity supplied extends. It is also how the price mechanism moves resources towards goods consumers want more of (3.1.2.1).

### Moral and ethical considerations

Producers' motives — especially maximising profit — may **conflict with ethical and moral interests**:

| Profit-driven action | Ethical concern |
|---|---|
| paying workers very low wages or using poor working conditions (including in overseas supply chains) | exploitation of workers; unsafe factories |
| **forcing suppliers to sell at very low prices** | farmers and small suppliers squeezed out of business |
| cutting costs by polluting or disposing of waste cheaply | harm to the environment and to local communities |
| selling unhealthy or addictive products, or aggressive marketing to children | harm to consumers' health and wellbeing |
| avoiding tax through complex arrangements | less money for public services |
| exploiting market power to charge high prices | consumers pay more; poorer households suffer |

**Acting ethically** — paying fair prices to suppliers (e.g. Fairtrade schemes), paying a living wage, reducing emissions, sourcing responsibly — usually **raises costs** in the short run. But it can also **bring benefits**: a better reputation and brand loyalty, the ability to charge higher prices to customers who value ethics, more motivated staff and lower staff turnover, and less risk of fines, boycotts or bad publicity. Whether ethics and profit conflict therefore depends on customers, competition and the time period.

::: terms
- **Business objective**: an aim or target that a firm works towards, such as profit, sales growth or market share.
- **Market share**: a firm's sales as a percentage of the total sales in its market.
- **Fixed costs**: costs that do not change with the level of output, such as rent and insurance.
- **Variable costs**: costs that change directly with the level of output, such as raw materials.
- **Total cost**: fixed costs plus variable costs at a given level of output.
- **Average cost**: total cost divided by the number of units produced — the cost per unit.
- **Total revenue**: the money a firm receives from sales, calculated as price × quantity sold.
- **Average revenue**: total revenue divided by quantity sold, which equals the price when all units sell at one price.
- **Profit**: total revenue minus total cost.
- **Loss**: when total cost is greater than total revenue.
- **Ethical considerations**: issues of right and wrong in how a firm treats workers, suppliers, customers, communities and the environment.
:::

::: method
1. **Objectives**: give *business* aims (profit, sales growth, market share, survival) — never macroeconomic ones — and say why this firm would choose it.
2. **Calculations**: write the formula, show the working, give units (£) and the decimal places asked: TC = FC + VC; AC = TC ÷ Q; TR = P × Q; AR = TR ÷ Q; profit = TR − TC.
3. **Read tables carefully**: fixed cost is the total cost at zero output; work backwards with TR = profit + TC and price = TR ÷ Q.
4. **Raising profit**: separate cost-side and revenue-side methods, and link to PED or productivity where relevant.
5. **Ethics questions**: show the conflict (the ethical action raises costs or lowers revenue) and the possible benefits (reputation, loyalty, premium price), then judge for this firm.
6. **15-markers**: use the Item's data, build both sides with full chains, and finish with a supported judgement that says what it depends on.
:::

::: example Calculate — costs and profit
Invented data: a phone-repair shop has fixed costs of £1,500 a month. Each repair has a variable cost of £12 and is sold for £45. In one month it carries out 120 repairs. Calculate its total cost, average cost and profit. (4)

VC = £12 × 120 = £1,440; TC = £1,500 + £1,440 = £2,940 (1). AC = £2,940 ÷ 120 = £24.50 (1). TR = £45 × 120 = £5,400 (1). Profit = £5,400 − £2,940 = £2,460 (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — working backwards
Invented data: a firm makes a profit of £1,600 when it sells 400 units. Its total cost is £4,400. Calculate the price per unit. (2)

TR = profit + TC = £1,600 + £4,400 = £6,000 (1). Price = £6,000 ÷ 400 = £15 (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a business objective
Explain why a new café might have survival as its main objective. (2)

In its first year a new café has few regular customers and must still pay fixed costs such as rent, so it may make losses (1). Staying open long enough to build a customer base matters more than profit at first, so it may set low prices just to cover costs (1).
:::

::: example Explain — the incentive to expand
Explain why a rise in the market price of a product gives producers an incentive to expand production. (2)

If the price rises while the cost of each unit stays the same, each unit sold earns more profit (1). Producers therefore want to make and sell more, and new firms are attracted into the market, so quantity supplied increases (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — ethics versus profit
Analyse how a chocolate maker's decision to pay cocoa farmers a higher, guaranteed price could affect its profit. (6)

Paying farmers more raises the cost of cocoa, a variable cost, so the average cost of each bar rises; if the selling price is unchanged, profit per bar and total profit fall (2). However, the firm can promote its chocolate as ethically sourced, which may attract customers who value fair treatment of farmers and build brand loyalty, so demand becomes more price inelastic and it can charge a higher price without losing many sales, raising revenue (2). A guaranteed price may also make supply more reliable, because farmers can afford to invest in their crops, reducing the risk of shortages; overall the effect on profit depends on whether the extra revenue from ethically minded customers outweighs the higher cocoa cost (2).
:::

::: q
Invented data: a firm sells 2,500 units at £8 each. Its fixed costs are £6,000 and its variable cost is £3.20 per unit. Calculate its profit. (3)
---
TR = 2,500 × £8 = £20,000 (1); TC = £6,000 + (2,500 × £3.20 = £8,000) = £14,000 (1); profit = £6,000 (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way a firm could increase its profit by reducing its average costs. (2)
---
Buy raw materials in bulk at a discount (1), so the cost of each unit falls and, at the same price, profit per unit rises (1). (Or: more efficient machinery, training to raise productivity, cheaper suppliers — developed.)
:::

::: q
Analyse why a supermarket's aim to maximise profit might conflict with the interests of its suppliers. (6)
---
A large supermarket buys huge quantities, giving it power over suppliers (1); to cut costs and raise profit it may force suppliers such as farmers to accept very low prices (1). Suppliers' revenue falls and some may make losses or go out of business (1). It may also delay payments to improve its cash flow, harming small suppliers (1). In the long run, squeezing suppliers could reduce quality or supply reliability, and bad publicity could cost the supermarket customers (1), so the conflict may not benefit the supermarket either (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- Business objectives are a **firm's** aims — "low inflation" or "economic growth" are government objectives and score nothing.
- **Fixed cost is the total cost at zero output**; it does not change with output.
- **AC = TC ÷ output**; **TR = P × Q**; **profit = TR − TC** — write the formula and show working for the method mark.
- Average revenue **equals price** when all units sell at one price — useful for working backwards.
- "Increase profit" answers should say whether they work on **costs** or **revenue**, and how.
- Ethics answers need **both** the cost of acting ethically and the possible benefits — a one-sided answer cannot reach the top level.
:::

::: test
Using an example, assess whether a firm can increase its profit by raising its price. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Yes: a higher price raises revenue per unit; if demand is price inelastic (few substitutes, brand loyalty, necessity) quantity falls by a smaller percentage, so total revenue rises; fewer units sold may also reduce variable costs, so profit rises on both counts — e.g. a well-known brand of headache tablets or a popular branded phone.
No: if demand is price elastic (many substitutes, e.g. one brand of crisps), sales fall by a larger percentage, so revenue falls; lower output may raise average cost because fixed costs are spread over fewer units; rivals may not follow the price rise, so the firm loses market share; customers may see the firm as exploitative, damaging reputation.
Conclusion: e.g. raising price increases profit only when demand is inelastic and rivals do not undercut; the effect depends on PED, the share of fixed costs and the time period (more substitutes emerge in the long run).
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced analysis linking price, PED, revenue and costs with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Item A — Fast fashion (invented)

GoTrend Ltd sells low-priced clothing online and in 300 stores. Its objective is to grow sales and market share. Last year it sold 40 million items at an average price of £12, with total costs of £396 million. Its clothes are made in overseas factories where many workers earn very low wages. A campaign group has criticised GoTrend for poor factory safety and for the amount of clothing that ends up in landfill. GoTrend says that its low prices allow families on low incomes to buy new clothes, and that paying higher wages would mean raising its prices. A rival, EthiWear, pays a living wage in its supply chain and uses recycled materials; its prices are about 40% higher than GoTrend's, but its sales have grown each year.

Do you think GoTrend should put ethical considerations before profit? Use Item A and your own economic knowledge to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 5, AO3 10. Indicative content:
GoTrend's profit: TR = 40m × £12 = £480m; profit = £480m − £396m = £84m (credit use of data).
For putting ethics first: low wages and unsafe factories exploit workers — a clear moral conflict with the profit motive; landfill waste harms the environment (wider costs); campaign groups and bad publicity can damage the brand, leading to boycotts and lost sales; EthiWear's growing sales show some consumers will pay a premium for ethical clothing — ethics can create brand loyalty and less price-elastic demand; better conditions can improve quality and supply reliability; the firm may face future regulation or fines anyway.
For putting profit first (or balancing): GoTrend's customers are price-sensitive — demand for low-cost fashion is likely price elastic with many substitutes, so raising prices could sharply cut sales and market share, conflicting with its growth objective; low-income families benefit from cheap clothing; higher costs could cut its £84m profit and threaten jobs in the UK and in supplier factories (which may still pay more than local alternatives); shareholders expect returns; ethical change may be gradual.
Evaluation: depends on how many of GoTrend's customers value ethics; on whether it can absorb part of the cost (it makes £84m profit — about £2.10 per item); on competitors' actions; on time — ethics may cost profit now but protect it in the long run; a middle course (better safety standards, recycling schemes) may cut the risks at modest cost.
Conclusion: a supported view, e.g. GoTrend should improve safety and wages because the moral case is strong and the long-run threat to its brand is real, while keeping prices low by accepting a smaller profit margin rather than raising prices sharply.
Levels: 13–15 — sustained, balanced evaluation using the Item's data, developed chains on both sides and a fully supported judgement; 10–12 — well-developed analysis with good use of the Item and a reasoned judgement, some imbalance; 7–9 — some developed analysis of both sides with some use of the Item, partly supported judgement; 4–6 — limited analysis, mainly one-sided or descriptive, little use of data; 1–3 — isolated points, assertion.
:::
