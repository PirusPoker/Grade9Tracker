## What it is

Economics starts with a simple observation: people want things, and somebody has to make them. **Economic activity** is everything people do to produce, sell and buy goods and services. This topic gives you the vocabulary every later topic depends on — needs and wants, the purpose of economic activity, the three key decisions and the three main economic groups.

### Needs and wants

| | **Needs** | **Wants** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | things essential for survival | things desired that make life more enjoyable but are not essential |
| Examples | food, drinking water, shelter, warmth, basic clothing | a holiday, a games console, a takeaway, designer trainers, a second car |
| What happens without them | health suffers; in the end a person cannot survive | life is less comfortable, but survival is not threatened |

A useful test: *could a person survive without it?* If yes, it is a want. Many products mix the two — you **need** food, but a restaurant meal is a **want**; you **need** clothing, but a branded coat is a **want**. In a multiple-choice question, the option that is a luxury or leisure item is the want.

**Needs and wants change over time.**

- **Rising incomes** turn yesterday's luxuries into today's normal expectations. Central heating, a fridge and a car were once wants for most households; many families now treat them as essentials.
- **Technology** creates wants that did not exist — streaming services, smartphones, electric cars — and changes how needs are met (ready meals and food delivery rather than cooking from scratch).
- **Social change and expectations**: access to the internet was a luxury; now schoolwork, banking and job applications assume it, so many people regard it as close to a need in a modern economy.
- **Individual circumstances**: a need for a frail older person (heating, medicine) or for a worker in a remote village (a car to get to work) may be a want for someone else.
- **Wants are unlimited.** As soon as one want is satisfied another appears — this is why scarcity never goes away (see 3.1.1.3).

### The central purpose of economic activity

The **central purpose of economic activity is to produce goods and services that satisfy people's needs and wants.** Farms, factories, shops, hospitals and schools all exist for this reason. **Goods** are physical products you can touch (bread, phones); **services** are intangible activities done for you (a haircut, a bus journey, a lesson). Producing them uses scarce resources, so every economy must decide how to use those resources.

### The three key economic decisions

Every economy, from a village to the UK, must answer three questions:

| Decision | The question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **What to produce?** | which goods and services, and how much of each | more houses or more hospitals? more electric cars or more petrol cars? |
| **How to produce?** | which methods and combination of resources | lots of workers (labour-intensive) or lots of machines (capital-intensive)? in the UK or abroad? |
| **Who is to benefit?** (for whom) | who gets the goods and services produced | those who can pay the market price, or everyone according to need (as with NHS treatment)? |

In a **market**, prices answer these questions: firms produce what consumers will pay for, use the cheapest method, and the output goes to those willing and able to buy. The **government** answers some of them directly — for example, it decides that state school places go to every child, free at the point of use.

### The main economic groups

The spec names three: **consumers**, **producers** and **government**.

| Group | Who they are | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | individuals and households who buy and use goods and services | spend income on goods and services; also supply their labour to producers and pay taxes |
| **Producers** | firms and other organisations that make goods and provide services | hire factors of production, pay wages, sell output, pay taxes, aim for profit (or other objectives) |
| **Government** | central and local government and its agencies | collects taxes, spends on public services and benefits, makes laws and regulates markets, and is itself a producer and a large employer |

### How the groups interact

The groups depend on each other in a continuous flow:

- **Consumers ↔ producers**: consumers buy goods and services and pay money to producers; producers employ consumers as workers and pay them wages, which gives consumers income to spend.
- **Producers ↔ government**: firms pay taxes (corporation tax, business rates, VAT collected from customers); government buys from firms (school buildings, medicines, defence equipment), regulates them (health and safety, minimum wage, competition law) and may give grants or subsidies.
- **Consumers ↔ government**: households pay income tax, National Insurance and VAT; the government provides services (the NHS, schools, roads), pays benefits and pensions, and passes laws that protect consumers.

A change in one group's behaviour spreads to the others. If the government raises income tax, consumers have less to spend, producers sell less and may cut jobs, and the government may then collect less VAT. Seeing these links is exactly what "explain the interaction" questions reward.

::: terms
- **Need**: something essential for survival, such as food, water, shelter, warmth and clothing.
- **Want**: something desired that is not essential for survival, such as a holiday or a games console.
- **Economic activity**: the production, distribution and consumption of goods and services.
- **Good**: a tangible (physical) product that can be touched, stored and owned, such as a car or a loaf.
- **Service**: an intangible activity performed for someone, such as a haircut, insurance or teaching.
- **Key economic decisions**: the three choices every economy must make — what to produce, how to produce it and who benefits from it.
- **Consumer**: an individual or household that buys and uses goods and services to satisfy needs and wants.
- **Producer**: a firm or organisation that uses factors of production to make goods or provide services.
- **Government**: the authority that taxes, spends on public services and benefits, and regulates economic activity.
- **Economic groups**: consumers, producers and government — the three main decision-makers in an economy.
:::

::: method
1. **Read which concept is being tested**: need/want, purpose, the three decisions, or the groups and their interactions.
2. **Need versus want**: apply the survival test and give a reason, not just a label — "a holiday is a want because a person can survive without it".
3. **Change over time**: name the driver (rising income, technology, social expectations) and show the result — a former want becomes regarded as a need, or a new want appears.
4. **Key decisions**: use the exact three — what, how, who benefits — and illustrate each with the context in the question.
5. **Interactions**: name both groups and the direction of the flow (money, labour, taxes, goods, services, laws), then add a consequence for one of them — that second step is the second mark.
6. **Assess questions**: give the case for one group making the decision, the case for another, and a conclusion that depends on the type of good (essential and needed by all, or a want that consumers value differently).
:::

::: example Explain — a changing want
Explain how a want can come to be seen as a need over time. (2)

As incomes rise and technology spreads, goods that were once luxuries become part of normal life — internet access is an example (1). Because schoolwork, banking and job applications now assume people are online, many households now regard it as essential rather than optional (1).
:::

::: example Explain — an interaction
Explain one way in which the government interacts with producers. (2)

The government collects taxes such as corporation tax from producers' profits (1), which reduces the money firms have to reinvest but funds public services such as roads that firms use to move their goods (1).
:::

::: example Apply — the key decisions
A small island economy must decide how to use its land. Using this example, explain the three key economic decisions it faces. (3)

What to produce: whether to use land for growing food for local people or for building hotels for tourists (1). How to produce: whether to farm with many workers and simple tools or with tractors and fewer workers (1). Who benefits: whether the hotels' profits and the food go to local residents or to overseas owners and visitors (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a change rippling through the groups
A government cuts the rate of income tax. Analyse how this could affect consumers and producers. (6)

Consumers keep more of their gross pay, so their disposable income rises and they can satisfy more wants — for example, spending more on meals out and holidays (2). Producers of these goods and services see demand rise, so their revenue increases; to meet the extra sales they may hire more workers, which pays wages to more consumers and raises incomes further (2). However, the government collects less income tax, so unless it borrows it may cut spending on public services or the contracts it gives firms; producers that sell to the government, such as construction firms building schools, could lose sales, so the overall effect on producers depends on which sector they are in (2).
:::

::: example Assess — who should decide
Assess whether consumers or the government should decide how much healthcare is produced. (9)

For consumers deciding through the market: consumers know their own wants best, and firms responding to what people will pay should produce the treatments people value most, efficiently, because they compete for customers (2). Prices would ration care and give producers an incentive to innovate (1).
For the government deciding: healthcare is a need, not a want, and illness often strikes those least able to pay; if only those who can afford it are treated, the "who benefits" decision leaves poorer people without essential care (2). Treating infectious disease also protects everyone else, which consumers ignore when they buy for themselves, so the market would produce too little (1).
Judgement: the government should decide the basic level of healthcare because it is a need with wider benefits and the market would leave many unable to access it; consumers could still decide on extras, such as private rooms or cosmetic treatment, which are wants. How much depends on how much tax the public is willing to pay (3).
:::

::: q
Explain why a car might be a need for one person but a want for another. (2)
---
For someone living in a remote village with no public transport, a car may be essential to get to work, buy food or reach a doctor (1); for someone in a city with frequent buses and trains it is a convenience they could live without, so it is a want (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how an increase in consumers' incomes could affect producers and the government. (6)
---
Producers: consumers can afford more wants, so demand rises for items such as holidays, restaurant meals and new cars (1); producers' revenue and profits rise, and they may expand and employ more workers (1), which raises incomes further (1).
Government: higher spending raises VAT receipts and higher incomes raise income tax receipts (1); the government may need to pay fewer benefits as employment rises (1), giving it more to spend on public services or the option of cutting taxes (1).
Award up to 6 for developed chains covering both groups; max 4 if only one group.
:::

::: watch
- A **want is not a need** just because people like it a lot. Holidays, phones and branded clothes are wants; give the survival reason.
- Say **how** needs and wants change — name the cause (income, technology, social expectations), not just "over time things change".
- The key decisions are **what, how and who benefits** — not "when" or "where to sell".
- **Government is an economic group in its own right** — it produces, employs and spends, as well as taxing and regulating.
- "Explain one interaction" needs **both groups named** and a consequence; a list of what one group does is only 1 mark.
- On 9-markers, a judgement that simply repeats one side scores low — say what the answer **depends on**.
:::

::: test
Fizz & Co is a drinks producer. The government introduces a tax on sugary drinks and spends the money on school sports. Analyse how this decision affects the interactions between the three main economic groups. (6)
---
Indicative content:
- Government → producers: the tax raises Fizz & Co's costs; it may raise prices or reformulate drinks with less sugar to avoid the tax.
- Producers → consumers: higher prices mean consumers buy fewer sugary drinks or switch to low-sugar products; producers' sales and possibly employment fall.
- Consumers → government: the government collects tax revenue from drinks consumers still buy, and spends it on school sports, benefiting children and their families.
- Government affects "what is produced": resources move away from sugary drinks towards healthier products and sports facilities.
Levels: 5–6 — two or more developed chains linking at least two pairs of groups, applied to the case; 3–4 — at least one developed chain, some application; 1–2 — isolated points or description.
:::

::: test
Assess whether rising incomes make it easier or harder for an economy to satisfy people's needs and wants. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Easier: higher incomes let households afford more goods and services, so more needs and wants are met; firms respond to higher demand by producing more; the government collects more tax and can fund health, education and benefits for those whose needs are unmet.
Harder: wants are unlimited, so as incomes rise new wants appear and people never feel satisfied; items once regarded as wants (cars, internet) come to be treated as needs, so expectations rise; more consumption uses more scarce resources, and rising demand can push up prices; higher incomes may not be shared equally, so some people's needs remain unmet.
Conclusion: e.g. rising incomes make it easier to satisfy needs, especially through public services, but cannot remove the basic problem because wants grow with income — the answer depends on whether we judge by needs (largely met) or wants (never fully met), and on how evenly income is distributed.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, well-developed analysis of both sides with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some developed analysis, may be one-sided, conclusion partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points or assertions, little or no judgement.
:::
