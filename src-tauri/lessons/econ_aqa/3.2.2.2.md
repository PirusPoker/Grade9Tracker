## What it is

Economic growth is the objective most often in the news: "the economy grew by 0.3% last quarter", "the UK is in recession". This topic covers **what economic growth is** and why it matters, the difference between **GDP, real GDP and GDP per capita** with the **simple calculations** you need, the **causes, costs and benefits** of growth, and the **government policies** used to achieve it.

### What is economic growth?

**Economic growth is an increase in the output of goods and services in an economy over time** — measured as the percentage increase in **real GDP**, usually per year or per quarter.

Its significance:
- **Higher incomes and living standards** — more goods and services are available per person.
- **More jobs** — firms producing more need more workers, so unemployment tends to fall.
- **Higher tax revenue** — without raising tax rates, as incomes, spending and profits rise; this pays for public services such as health and education, or reduces borrowing.
- **Business confidence** — firms expect rising sales, so they invest more.

When output **falls**, growth is negative. A **recession** is commonly defined as **two consecutive quarters (six months) of negative economic growth**.

### GDP, real GDP and GDP per capita

| Measure | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **GDP** (Gross Domestic Product) | the **total value of all goods and services produced** in a country in a year, measured at **current prices** (also called nominal GDP) | the size of the economy in money terms |
| **Real GDP** | GDP **adjusted for inflation** — measured at constant prices, so it shows changes in the actual **quantity** of output | the correct measure of economic growth |
| **GDP per capita** | **GDP ÷ population** — the average output (and income) per person | comparing living standards between countries or over time |

**Why adjust for inflation?** If prices rise by 5% and the quantity of output does not change, nominal GDP rises by 5% — but the economy has not grown at all. Real GDP removes the effect of rising prices.

**Why divide by population?** A country's GDP can rise simply because its population grows. If GDP grows by 2% but the population grows by 3%, each person, on average, has **less** output than before.

### The calculations

**1. Growth rate** = (new real GDP − old real GDP) ÷ old real GDP × 100.
Invented: real GDP rises from £2,150bn to £2,193bn → (2,193 − 2,150) ÷ 2,150 × 100 = **2.0%**.

**2. GDP per capita** = GDP ÷ population.
Invented: GDP £2,400bn, population 60 million → £2,400,000,000,000 ÷ 60,000,000 = **£40,000 per person**. Tip: £bn ÷ millions = thousands of pounds (2,400 ÷ 60 = 40 → £40,000).

**3. Approximate real growth** = nominal GDP growth − inflation.
Invented: nominal GDP grows by 6.5% while inflation is 4.0% → real growth ≈ **2.5%**.

**4. Using a price index to find real GDP** (extension): real GDP = nominal GDP × (base-year index ÷ current index). Invented: nominal GDP £550bn, price index 110 (base = 100) → real GDP = 550 × 100 ÷ 110 = **£500bn** in base-year prices.

### Causes of economic growth

Growth happens when the economy can produce more (**supply** grows) and when spending rises to buy that output (**demand** grows).

| Cause | How it raises output |
|---|---|
| **Investment** in capital — machinery, factories, infrastructure | more and better capital lets each worker produce more |
| **New technology and innovation** | new methods and products raise productivity (e.g. automation, digital services) |
| **Education and training** | a more skilled workforce is more productive (better quality labour) |
| **More workers** — population growth, net migration, more people choosing to work | more labour available to produce output |
| **Higher productivity** generally | more output per worker from the same resources (3.1.4.2) |
| **Discovery of natural resources** | more land/resources available (e.g. new energy sources) |
| **Rising total demand** — consumer spending, investment, government spending, exports | firms produce more to meet extra spending, especially when there is spare capacity |

### Costs and benefits of economic growth

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| higher incomes and living standards | **pollution and environmental damage** — more production and transport, more emissions and waste |
| more jobs, lower unemployment | **depletion of finite resources** (oil, minerals), unsustainable for future generations |
| higher tax revenue for public services without raising tax rates | **inequality** — gains may go mainly to the better-off or to certain regions |
| less spending needed on unemployment benefits | **inflation** if demand grows faster than capacity |
| higher profits, more investment and innovation | a worse **current account** as rising incomes suck in imports |
| more money available to tackle poverty and the environment | stress, longer working hours, congestion; output does not measure wellbeing or leisure |

### Government policies to achieve growth

| Type | Policies | How they work |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal policy** (3.2.3.1) | higher government spending (e.g. infrastructure), lower taxes | more spending and disposable income → higher demand → firms expand output and hire workers |
| **Monetary policy** (3.2.3.2) | lower interest rates (set by the Bank of England) | cheaper borrowing → more consumer spending and business investment |
| **Supply-side policies** (3.2.3.3) | education and training, lower taxes on business profits, infrastructure, deregulation | raise the economy's capacity and productivity → long-run growth without inflation |

Demand-side policies (fiscal and monetary) work faster but risk inflation and a worse current account; supply-side policies take longer but deliver **sustainable** growth.

::: terms
- **Economic growth**: an increase in the output of goods and services in an economy over time, measured by the change in real GDP.
- **GDP**: Gross Domestic Product — the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
- **Real GDP**: GDP adjusted to remove the effects of inflation, measuring the actual volume of output.
- **Nominal GDP**: GDP measured at current prices, without adjusting for inflation.
- **GDP per capita**: GDP divided by the population — the average output or income per person.
- **Recession**: a period of falling output, commonly defined as two consecutive quarters of negative economic growth.
- **Living standards**: the level of material wellbeing of people, often measured by real GDP per capita.
- **Investment**: spending by firms on capital goods such as machinery, buildings and technology.
- **Sustainable growth**: growth that can continue in the long run without causing inflation or harming future generations.
- **Productive capacity**: the maximum output an economy can produce with its existing resources.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely**: growth = increase in real GDP (output) over time — not "more money" or "higher prices".
2. **Know the three measures**: GDP (total output), real GDP (adjusted for inflation), GDP per capita (÷ population).
3. **Calculations**: growth rate = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; per capita = GDP ÷ population (watch billions and millions); real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. Show working and give the decimal places asked.
4. **Read charts carefully**: a smaller positive growth rate means output is still rising, only more slowly; only a negative rate means output falls.
5. **Causes, costs, benefits**: link each to output, jobs, incomes or the environment with a full chain.
6. **Policies / assess**: name the policy type, explain how it raises output, then evaluate — time lags, inflation, cost, sustainability — and judge.
:::

Invented data: a country's real GDP rose from £1,840 billion to £1,876.8 billion. Calculate the rate of economic growth to one decimal place. (2)

(1,876.8 − 1,840) ÷ 1,840 × 100 (1) = 2.0% (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — GDP per capita
Invented data: Country T has GDP of £640 billion and a population of 16 million. Calculate GDP per capita. (2)

£640,000,000,000 ÷ 16,000,000 (1) = £40,000 per person (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — real growth
Invented data: a country's nominal GDP grew by 7.2% in a year when inflation was 4.8%. Calculate the approximate real growth rate and explain what it shows. (2)

Real growth ≈ 7.2 − 4.8 = 2.4% (1). Most of the rise in GDP was due to higher prices; the actual quantity of output grew by only about 2.4% (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a cost of growth
Explain one cost of economic growth. (2)

More production and transport use more fossil fuels (1), increasing pollution and carbon emissions that harm people's health and the environment, now and for future generations (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — GDP versus GDP per capita
Invented data: Country A's GDP grew by 3% and its population by 4%; Country B's GDP grew by 1.5% and its population by 0.5%. Analyse which country's living standards are more likely to have improved. (6)

Country A has the faster GDP growth, 3% against 1.5%, so its total output grew more quickly (2). But its population grew by 4%, faster than its output, so GDP per capita fell by roughly 1% — on average each person has less output and income than before, so living standards are likely to have fallen (2). Country B's output grew three times as fast as its population, so GDP per capita rose by about 1%, suggesting higher average living standards; however, GDP per capita is an average and says nothing about how income is shared, the environment, or working hours, so the conclusion is about averages only (2).
:::

::: watch
- Growth is an increase in **real** output — rising nominal GDP may just be inflation.
- A **fall in the growth rate** (e.g. 2% to 1%) does not mean output fell; it means output rose more slowly.
- GDP per capita: check units — **billions ÷ millions = thousands**. Many marks are lost on powers of ten.
- When comparing living standards, use **per capita** figures, not total GDP.
- Costs of growth should be specific: pollution, resource depletion, inequality, inflation, imports — not just "it's bad".
- Policies must be **government** policies (fiscal, monetary, supply-side), with the chain to higher output.
:::
