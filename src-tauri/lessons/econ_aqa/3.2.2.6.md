## What it is

Reducing inequality is one of the government's "other" objectives (3.2.2.1). This topic covers the **distribution of income** in the UK, the difference between **income and wealth inequality**, **how inequality occurs**, its **consequences**, and how **taxation and government spending** can **redistribute** income and wealth — with the side effects those policies bring.

### Income and wealth

| | **Income** | **Wealth** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | a **flow** of money received over a period of time (per week, month or year) | a **stock** of assets owned at a point in time |
| Examples | wages and salaries; profits from running a business; rent from property; interest and dividends from savings and shares; pensions; state benefits | houses and land; savings in bank accounts; shares; private pension funds; businesses; valuables |
| Link | wealth can **generate income** (rent, interest, dividends), and income that is saved **builds wealth** | |

The **distribution of income** describes how a country's total income is shared out between households. In the UK, income is distributed **unequally**: the richest fifth of households receive a much larger share of total income than the poorest fifth. **Wealth is distributed far more unequally than income** — many households own little beyond their possessions, while the wealthiest tenth own a large share of all property, shares and pension savings. Wealth builds over a lifetime and passes between generations through **inheritance**, so gaps can widen over time.

### How inequality is shown

Inequality is usually shown by dividing households into five equal groups, **quintiles** (or ten **deciles**), ranked from poorest to richest, and comparing their shares of total income or their average incomes.

Invented example: the poorest 20% of households receive 7% of total income, and the richest 20% receive 43%. The richest fifth receive 43 ÷ 7 = about **6 times** the share of the poorest fifth. If incomes were perfectly equal, every quintile would receive 20%.

Statisticians also follow how taxes and benefits change incomes, step by step:

| Stage | What is added or removed |
|---|---|
| **Original income** | income from employment, self-employment, investments and private pensions, before the state gets involved |
| **Gross income** | original income **+ cash benefits** (state pension, Universal Credit and so on) |
| **Disposable income** | gross income **− direct taxes** (income tax, National Insurance, council tax) |
| **Final income** | disposable income **− indirect taxes** (VAT, excise duties) **+ benefits in kind** (the value of free services such as the NHS and state schools) |

Invented example: the poorest quintile's average original income is £8,000 and the richest quintile's is £95,000 — a ratio of 95,000 ÷ 8,000 = **11.9 to 1**. After taxes and benefits, final incomes are £17,000 and £72,000 — a ratio of 72,000 ÷ 17,000 = **4.2 to 1**. Taxes and spending have greatly **reduced** inequality, but not removed it.

(An extension: the **Gini coefficient** summarises inequality in one number from 0, perfect equality, to 1, where one household has everything. A higher figure means more inequality.)

### How inequality occurs: causes

| Cause | How it creates inequality |
|---|---|
| **Differences in skills, education and qualifications** | workers with scarce, valuable skills (surgeons, software engineers) earn far more than those with few qualifications — the **wage differentials** of 3.1.5.4 |
| **Unemployment and economic inactivity** | households with no one in work depend on benefits, which are much lower than most wages; long-term illness, disability and caring responsibilities limit earnings |
| **Ownership of wealth** | owners of property, shares and savings receive rent, dividends and interest on top of earnings; when house and share prices rise, owners grow richer while non-owners fall behind |
| **Inheritance** | wealth passed down generations gives some people assets they did not earn |
| **Age** | young people and many pensioners have lower incomes than people in mid-career; wealth peaks around retirement |
| **Region** | wages and job opportunities vary between regions and between cities and rural or former industrial areas |
| **Discrimination** | unequal treatment by gender, ethnicity or disability can mean lower pay or fewer promotions |
| **Bargaining power** | workers in strong trade unions or with rare skills can win higher pay; low-paid, part-time or zero-hours workers have little power |
| **Technology and globalisation** | demand for highly skilled workers rises, while some low-skilled jobs are automated or moved abroad |
| **Government choices** | less progressive taxes or lower benefits widen the gap after taxes and benefits; more progressive ones narrow it |

### Consequences of inequality

| Negative consequences | Possible positive consequences (arguments made by some economists) |
|---|---|
| **Relative poverty**: the poorest cannot afford what most people take for granted | pay differences give **incentives** to study, train, work hard, take risks and start businesses |
| **Health and education gaps**: poorer health and shorter lives; children in poor households may achieve less | high earners and wealthy households **save more**, providing funds that banks lend for **investment** |
| **Lower social mobility**: talent is wasted if people cannot afford training or housing near good jobs | profits reward enterprise, encouraging new firms and innovation |
| **Social tensions** and possibly higher crime; a sense of unfairness | |
| **Weaker spending**: poorer households spend almost all of their income, so a more equal distribution can support demand | |
| **Higher government spending** on benefits and the health effects of poverty | |

Whether inequality is "too high" is partly a **value judgement** — economists can measure it, but people disagree about how much is fair.

### Redistribution through taxation and government spending

**Redistribution** means the government moving income or wealth from richer to poorer households.

| Tool | How it redistributes | Possible consequences |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive income tax** (3.2.1.2) | higher earners pay a larger proportion of their income | may reduce incentives to work extra hours or take promotion; may encourage tax avoidance or skilled workers moving abroad |
| **Taxes on wealth**: inheritance tax, capital gains tax, higher council tax bands, a possible annual wealth tax | tax the stock of wealth or gains from it, which income taxes miss | hard to value assets; wealthy people may move assets abroad; may discourage saving and investment |
| **Means-tested benefits** (e.g. Universal Credit, help with housing costs) | paid only to households below an income level, so money is targeted at those in need | stigma and complexity mean some eligible people do not claim; withdrawal of benefits as earnings rise can reduce the incentive to work more |
| **Universal benefits** (e.g. the state pension) | paid regardless of income, but form a larger share of a poor household's income | expensive, because the better-off receive them too |
| **Free public services** (NHS, state education, free school meals) | benefits in kind are worth far more, as a share of income, to poorer households | large cost to taxpayers; quality varies |
| **National Minimum and National Living Wage** (a law rather than tax or spending, but often used alongside them) | raises the pay of the lowest-paid workers | firms' costs rise, which may lead to fewer jobs or higher prices; does not help households with no one in work |

Every redistribution policy has an **opportunity cost**: money spent on benefits cannot be spent on other services, and higher taxes may reduce incentives. The government has to balance **fairness (equity)** against **incentives (efficiency)**.

::: terms
- **Income**: a flow of money received over a period of time, such as wages, profits, rent, interest or benefits.
- **Wealth**: a stock of assets owned at a point in time, such as property, savings, shares and pension funds.
- **Distribution of income**: the way a country's total income is shared out between households or individuals.
- **Income inequality**: unequal shares of total income received by different households.
- **Wealth inequality**: unequal ownership of assets between households; usually greater than income inequality.
- **Redistribution**: government action to move income or wealth from richer to poorer households, through taxes and spending.
- **Means-tested benefit**: a benefit paid only to people whose income or savings are below a set level.
- **Universal benefit**: a benefit paid to everyone in a group regardless of their income, such as the state pension.
- **Benefits in kind**: free or subsidised services provided by the government, such as healthcare and education.
- **Relative poverty**: having an income well below the average for the society, so a household cannot afford a normal standard of living.
- **Disposable income**: income after direct taxes have been taken off and cash benefits added.
:::

::: method
1. **Define carefully**: income is a flow (per period); wealth is a stock (owned at a point in time). Wealth is more unequal than income.
2. **Use the data**: compare quintile shares or average incomes; calculate ratios (top ÷ bottom) or percentage changes, and say whether the gap is widening or narrowing.
3. **Causes**: link each cause to income — skills → wage differentials; no work → benefits only; owning assets → rent, interest and rising house prices; inheritance.
4. **Redistribution**: say whether the tool is a **tax** or **spending**, how it shifts money from rich to poor, and its side effect (incentives, cost, avoidance, jobs).
5. **Evaluate**: weigh fairness against incentives and cost; consider whether the tool reaches the poorest (e.g. a minimum wage misses workless households).
:::

::: example Explain — income and wealth
Explain the difference between income and wealth. (2)

Income is a flow of money received over a period of time, such as a monthly salary (1); wealth is a stock of assets owned at a point in time, such as a house or savings (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — a ratio of incomes
Invented data: the average final income of the richest quintile is £76,500 and of the poorest quintile is £18,000. Calculate how many times greater the richest quintile's income is. (2)

76,500 ÷ 18,000 (1) = 4.25 times (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a cause of inequality
Explain one cause of inequality in the distribution of income. (2)

Workers with high levels of skills and qualifications are in short supply and high demand, so they earn much higher wages (1), while workers with few qualifications compete for low-paid jobs, widening the gap between incomes (1).
:::

::: example Explain — redistribution through spending
Explain one way in which government spending can redistribute income. (2)

Means-tested benefits such as Universal Credit are paid only to low-income households (1), so money raised mainly through taxes on higher earners is transferred to the poorest, narrowing the gap in disposable income (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — wealth inequality
Invented data: in Country H, average house prices rose by 40% over ten years while average wages rose by 15%. Analyse how this could have affected the distribution of wealth in Country H. (6)

Homeowners' wealth rose sharply: a house worth £200,000 would now be worth £280,000, a gain of £80,000 — far more than most people could save from wages that rose by only 15% (2). People who do not own a home — younger people and lower-income renters — gained nothing from the price rise, and house prices have risen much faster than their pay, so it is harder for them to save a deposit and start building wealth (2). Wealth therefore becomes more concentrated among older, already wealthier owners, and through inheritance the gap may pass to the next generation — though inheritance tax and capital gains tax on second homes could limit part of the effect (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a progressive income tax and free public services together reduce income inequality. (6)
---
Indicative content: higher earners pay a larger proportion of income in tax (1), so the gap in disposable income is smaller than in original income (1); the revenue funds services such as the NHS and schools (1); these are free at the point of use and worth the same in pounds to every household (1), so they are a larger share of a poor household's income (1); final incomes are therefore much more equal — though indirect taxes partly offset the effect (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Income is a flow; wealth is a stock.** A tax on income does not reach the inequality in property and savings.
- Wealth is **more unequal** than income — remember this when reading data.
- Say **whether a tool is a tax or spending**, and how it moves money from richer to poorer.
- A minimum wage helps low-paid **workers**, not households where **no one works** — the poorest are often in the second group.
- Evaluation needs the **side effects**: incentives to work, tax avoidance, cost to taxpayers, effects on jobs.
- When comparing quintiles, use **ratios or percentage-point differences** from the data rather than vague words like "a lot more".
:::

::: test
Explain one reason why a rise in the national minimum wage may not reduce inequality between households. (2)
---
It only raises the pay of people in work (1), so the poorest households, often with no one in work (unemployed, retired, long-term sick), gain nothing; and firms may cut jobs or hours in response (1).

::: test
Explain two causes of wealth inequality. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Inheritance: wealthy families pass property, savings and shares to their children (1), so some people start adult life with large assets they did not earn (1), and this gap can carry on across generations (1).
Rising asset prices: when house and share prices rise faster than wages (1), people who already own assets grow richer (1), while renters and those without savings fall further behind (1).
Also creditworthy: high incomes allow saving, which builds wealth; age (wealth builds over a working life); returns on wealth (rent, dividends) are reinvested.
:::

::: test
Invented data — Table C: shares of total income received by each quintile in Country F.

| Quintile | 2015 (%) | 2025 (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Poorest 20% | 8 | 6 |
| Second 20% | 13 | 12 |
| Middle 20% | 17 | 16 |
| Fourth 20% | 23 | 23 |
| Richest 20% | 39 | 43 |

During this period Country F cut its top rate of income tax, froze most working-age benefits, and saw strong growth in high-skilled technology jobs.

Using Table C, analyse the reasons for the change in the distribution of income in Country F. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- The richest quintile's share rose by 4 percentage points (39% → 43%) while the poorest fell by 2 (8% → 6%); the ratio of top to bottom share rose from about 4.9 to about 7.2 — inequality has increased.
- Cutting the top rate makes the tax system less progressive, so high earners keep more of their income.
- Freezing benefits means their real value falls with inflation; the poorest rely most on benefits, so their share falls.
- High-skilled technology jobs raise demand for scarce skills, so wages of skilled workers rise faster (wage differentials); the low-skilled do not benefit.
- The middle quintiles changed little — the change is concentrated at the top and bottom.
Levels: 5–6 — developed analysis linking at least two causes to the data; 3–4 — some analysis with some use of data; 1–2 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Explain one possible disadvantage of using higher taxes to redistribute income. (2)
---
Higher taxes reduce the reward for extra work, training or enterprise (1), so some people may work fewer hours, avoid tax or move abroad, reducing tax revenue and economic growth (1).
:::

::: test
Read Figure 1 (invented).

**Figure 1 — A pay rise for the lowest paid?**

The government of Country G is considering raising the national minimum wage by 12% next year, much faster than average pay growth of 4%. Supporters say this would lift the incomes of 2 million low-paid workers, many of them part-time women in retail, hospitality and care. Some small firms warn that they would have to cut hours or staff, or raise prices. About a third of the poorest fifth of households in Country G have no one in work; many of these are pensioners or people who are long-term sick. An alternative plan would spend the same amount on raising means-tested benefits.

Using Figure 1, assess whether a large rise in the minimum wage is the best way for Country G to reduce income inequality. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For: raises pay of 2 million low-paid workers directly, narrowing wage differentials; 12% is three times average pay growth of 4%, so the gap closes quickly; costs the government little (paid by firms), unlike benefits funded by taxes; rewards work, keeping incentives to work; helps groups often low-paid (part-time women).
Against: does nothing for the third of the poorest households with no one in work (pensioners, long-term sick); small firms may cut hours or jobs, so some low-paid workers end up worse off; higher prices in retail and hospitality hit low-income consumers; some minimum-wage earners live in middle-income households (second earners), so it is poorly targeted.
Alternative: means-tested benefits target low-income households directly, including workless ones; but they cost taxpayers and may weaken incentives to work more.
Conclusion: e.g. a minimum wage rise helps the working poor but is not the best single tool; combining a moderate rise with targeted means-tested benefits would reach more of the poorest. Depends on how sensitive employment is to wage costs.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis using Figure 1 with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, may be one-sided, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
