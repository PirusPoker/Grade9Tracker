## What it is

Not every market works in the same way. The market for haircuts in a large town has dozens of independent barbers; the market for train travel on a particular route may have just one operator. This topic introduces **market structure** — the features of a market that affect how firms behave — and the **factors used to tell market structures apart**: the **number of producers**, the **degree of product differentiation** and the **ease of entry** into the market. It also covers **market share** and **concentration**, which are the usual calculations on this topic.

### A range of market structures

**Market structure** describes the characteristics of a market that shape the way firms compete. There is a **range** (a spectrum) of structures, from highly competitive at one end to monopoly at the other:

Real markets do not always fit neatly into one box — they sit somewhere along the range. Markets dominated by a few producers are often called **concentrated** (or non-competitive) markets.

### The factors that distinguish market structures

| Factor | What to look for | Competitive end | Concentrated end |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of producers** | how many firms supply the market, and how big they are | many small firms, none large enough to influence the price | a few large firms (oligopoly) or one firm (monopoly) |
| **Degree of product differentiation** | how far each firm's product is different from rivals' — by brand, design, quality, features or service | products are similar or identical (homogeneous), so buyers switch easily | products are strongly branded and differentiated, or there are no close substitutes at all |
| **Ease of entry (barriers to entry)** | how easy it is for a new firm to start up and compete | **low** barriers — little capital needed, no licences, easy to set up | **high** barriers — huge start-up costs, economies of scale, patents, licences, strong brands, control of supplies |
| Market share / concentration | how much of the market's sales the biggest firms have | the largest firms have small shares | the largest firms have most or all of the market |

**Barriers to entry** are anything that makes it difficult or expensive for a new firm to enter a market. Examples: very high start-up costs (building a steelworks or a mobile network), economies of scale enjoyed by existing firms (3.1.4.3), legal protection such as **patents** and licences, strong brand loyalty, and control of key raw materials or distribution. Ease of **exit** matters too: if a firm can leave without losing much (no huge sunk costs), more firms are willing to try entering.

### Comparing the main structures

| | Competitive market | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of firms | many | a few large firms dominate | one firm (or one dominant firm) |
| Products | similar, little differentiation | often highly differentiated by branding | unique — no close substitutes |
| Barriers to entry | low | high | very high |
| Control over price | very little — firms are **price takers** | considerable, but firms watch rivals' reactions | high — the firm is a **price maker** |
| Typical examples | market stalls, window cleaners, hairdressers, takeaway food in a city | supermarkets, mobile networks, banking, energy supply | a water company in its region, a patented medicine, a single rail operator on a route |

Monopoly and oligopoly are studied in more depth in 3.1.5.3; competitive markets in 3.1.5.2.

### Market share and concentration

**Market share = a firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100.**

Sales can be measured by value (£ revenue) or by volume (units). Market shares in a market always add up to 100%.

A **concentration ratio** is the **combined market share of the largest few firms**. A *four-firm concentration ratio* adds up the shares of the four biggest firms. A high ratio (e.g. above 60–70%) indicates an oligopoly; a very low ratio indicates a competitive market.

Invented data — the market for pet insurance in a country, annual sales £800 million:

| Firm | Sales (£m) | Market share |
|---|---|---|
| Paws Ltd | 240 | 240 ÷ 800 × 100 = **30%** |
| PetSure | 200 | **25%** |
| Tailcover | 120 | **15%** |
| Furguard | 80 | **10%** |
| 25 other firms | 160 | **20%** |

Four-firm concentration ratio = 30 + 25 + 15 + 10 = **80%**. With four firms holding 80% of sales and the rest split among 25 small firms, this market is best described as an **oligopoly**.

### Why structure matters to consumers and producers

The structure affects prices, choice, quality and profit:

- In **competitive** markets consumers usually enjoy lower prices and more choice; producers face pressure to be efficient and earn lower profits.
- In **concentrated** markets firms have more power to set prices; profits tend to be higher; consumers may face higher prices and less choice, although large firms may gain economies of scale and fund innovation.

So when you identify the structure, you can predict how producers are likely to behave and how consumers are likely to be affected.

::: terms
- **Market structure**: the characteristics of a market, such as the number of firms and barriers to entry, that affect how firms compete.
- **Competitive market**: a market with many firms selling similar products, with low barriers to entry.
- **Concentrated market**: a market dominated by a small number of large firms.
- **Oligopoly**: a market dominated by a few large firms.
- **Monopoly**: a market with a single supplier, or one firm that dominates the market.
- **Product differentiation**: making a product different from rivals' through branding, design, quality or features.
- **Barriers to entry**: obstacles that make it difficult or costly for new firms to enter a market.
- **Market share**: a firm's sales as a percentage of the total sales in its market.
- **Concentration ratio**: the combined market share of the largest few firms in a market.
- **Price taker**: a firm with so little market power that it must accept the market price.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the structure from the evidence**: count the producers (and their size), judge how differentiated the products are, and look for barriers to entry.
2. **Get the barrier direction right**: competitive = **low** barriers; oligopoly and monopoly = **high** barriers.
3. **Calculations**: market share = firm's sales ÷ total sales × 100; concentration ratio = sum of the biggest firms' shares. Show working and give the units (%).
4. **"State two factors"**: give two *different* distinguishing factors — number of firms, product differentiation, ease of entry, market share.
5. **Explain / analyse**: link the feature to behaviour — e.g. high barriers → no new rivals → firms can keep prices high.
6. **Assess**: weigh several factors against each other and judge which best describes how competitive the market really is.
:::

::: example Calculate — market share
Invented data: total sales in a town's takeaway pizza market are £2.4 million a year. Pizza Planet's sales are £540,000. Calculate Pizza Planet's market share. (2)

£540,000 ÷ £2,400,000 × 100 (1) = 22.5% (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — concentration ratio
Invented data: the five largest firms in the cinema market have market shares of 28%, 22%, 19%, 9% and 6%. Calculate the three-firm concentration ratio and state what it suggests about the market. (2)

28 + 22 + 19 = 69% (1). Over two-thirds of sales go to just three firms, which suggests an oligopoly (1).
:::

::: example Identify — a market structure
A market has thousands of small sellers offering almost identical products, and anyone can start selling with very little money. Identify the market structure and give one reason. (2)

A competitive market (1), because there are many small firms and barriers to entry are low, so no firm can control the price (1).
:::

::: example Explain — product differentiation
Explain how product differentiation can make a market less competitive. (2)

Strong branding, design or unique features make consumers see a firm's product as different from rivals' and become loyal to it (1), so they are less likely to switch when it raises its price, giving the firm more control over price (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — classifying a market from data
Invented data: the UK market for a type of games console. Three firms share 97% of sales; each sells a console that only runs its own games; developing a new console costs hundreds of millions of pounds. Analyse which market structure best describes this market. (6)

With three firms holding 97% of sales, the market is highly concentrated — the three-firm concentration ratio of 97% is far above the level that indicates an oligopoly (2). Products are strongly differentiated because each console only runs its own games, so consumers who own one are locked in and buyers' loyalty reduces price competition between the three (2). The huge development cost is a high barrier to entry, so new firms are unlikely to appear and the three can keep their dominant shares; the market is therefore an oligopoly rather than competitive — and not a monopoly, because three firms still compete (2).
:::

::: q
Explain one difference between a competitive market and an oligopoly. (2)
---
A competitive market has many small firms, whereas an oligopoly is dominated by a few large firms (1); so in a competitive market no firm can influence the price, while in an oligopoly firms have some control over price (1). (Or: low vs high barriers; similar vs differentiated products — developed.)
:::

::: q
Analyse how high barriers to entry affect the behaviour of firms already in a market. (6)
---
Indicative content: existing firms know new rivals cannot easily enter (1), so they face less competitive pressure (1); they can set higher prices without losing customers to new firms (1), earning higher profits that persist in the long run (1); they may become less efficient or innovative because there is no threat (1); they may spend on advertising/branding to raise barriers further, or compete with each other through non-price competition (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Barriers to entry are LOW in a competitive market and HIGH in a concentrated one** — the June 2025 examiners named swapping these as a common error.
- "Many firms" and "few firms" are the **same factor** (number of producers) — a "state two" answer needs two different factors.
- Market share uses the **total market's** sales as the denominator, not the biggest firm's.
- A concentration ratio **adds** the shares of the largest firms — say how many firms (e.g. "three-firm ratio").
- A monopoly is one firm or one dominant firm; an oligopoly is a **few** firms — do not call a market with four big firms a monopoly.
- Use the evidence in the question: name the number of firms, the product type and the barrier you can see.
:::

::: test
The table shows invented data for two markets in a UK region.

| | Market A: window cleaning | Market B: home broadband |
|---|---|---|
| Number of firms | about 150 | 4 |
| Largest firm's market share | 3% | 38% |
| Four-firm concentration ratio | 9% | 100% |
| Typical start-up cost | under £2,000 | hundreds of millions of pounds |
| Products | very similar service | branded packages with different speeds and extras |

Using the table, analyse the differences between the market structures of Market A and Market B. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- Market A has about 150 firms and a four-firm ratio of only 9% — a competitive market; the largest firm has just 3%.
- Market B has 4 firms holding 100% — an oligopoly; the largest has 38%.
- Start-up costs: under £2,000 in A (low barriers, easy entry) versus hundreds of millions in B (very high barriers).
- Products: similar in A, so window cleaners are price takers and compete on price; differentiated packages in B, so firms compete through branding and extras and have more control over price.
- Implication: consumers in A likely enjoy lower prices relative to cost; firms in B are likely to earn higher profits.
Levels: 5–6 — developed comparison using the data across several factors; 3–4 — some analysis using some data; 1–2 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Explain one reason why a market with low barriers to entry is likely to have many producers. (2)
---
It is cheap and easy to start a business in the market (1), so whenever existing firms make good profits, new firms enter and the number of producers grows (1).
:::

::: test
Assess whether the number of firms in a market is the best way to judge how competitive the market is. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For: more firms usually means more choice for consumers and more rivals to switch to; no firm can set the price; a market with one or two firms is clearly not competitive.
Against: market share matters — many firms may exist but two may hold most of the sales (concentration ratio is more informative); barriers to entry matter — even a market with few firms can be competitive if new firms could easily enter, and many firms behind high barriers may collude; product differentiation and brand loyalty can give firms power even with many rivals; information — consumers who cannot compare prices may not switch.
Conclusion: e.g. the number of firms is a useful starting point but not the best single measure; the concentration ratio together with the height of barriers to entry gives a better picture. It depends on the market — e.g. many small cafés plus one dominant chain.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
