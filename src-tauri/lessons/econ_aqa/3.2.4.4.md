## What it is

**Globalisation** is the process by which the world's economies become **increasingly connected and dependent on each other** — through trade in goods and services, flows of money and investment, movement of people, and the spread of technology and ideas. This topic covers the **main features** of globalisation, the **factors that have caused it to grow** (especially **new technology** and **multinational companies**), its **benefits and drawbacks** for producers, workers and consumers in **developed countries such as the UK** and in **less developed countries**, and the **moral, ethical and sustainability** questions raised when UK firms trade with the rest of the world.

### Main features of globalisation

| Feature | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **More international trade** | a growing share of what countries produce is exported; products are made from parts sourced from many countries (global **supply chains**) |
| **Growth of multinational companies (MNCs)** | firms with operations — factories, offices, shops — in many countries, often with sales larger than the output of some whole countries |
| **Flows of capital (investment)** | firms and investors put money into factories, firms and property abroad (**foreign direct investment**) |
| **Movement of labour** | more people move between countries to work and study |
| **Spread of technology and culture** | the same brands, apps, films and ideas used worldwide |

A smartphone, for example, may be designed in one country, use minerals mined in another, have components made in several more, be assembled in yet another and be sold everywhere.

### Factors that have contributed to globalisation

| Factor | How it has helped |
|---|---|
| **New technology — communications** | the internet, email, video calls and cloud computing let firms manage operations across continents, sell online worldwide and buy services (call centres, software, design) from anywhere |
| **Cheaper, faster transport** | **containerisation** (standard shipping containers moved between ships, trains and lorries), larger ships and cheaper air freight have cut the cost of moving goods around the world |
| **Multinational companies** | MNCs set up production wherever costs are lowest or markets largest, spreading investment, technology and brands across borders |
| **Trade liberalisation** | falling tariffs and quotas through international agreements, trading blocs and free-trade agreements (3.2.4.3) |
| **Freer movement of capital** | it is easier for money to move between countries to fund investment |
| **Growth of emerging economies** | large countries opening up to trade, providing low-cost production and huge new markets of consumers |

### Why multinational companies locate abroad

MNCs set up overseas to: use **cheaper labour** and land; get closer to **raw materials** or to **customers**; get round tariffs by producing inside a market; benefit from **lower taxes** or government incentives; and grow into new markets. They can bring jobs, investment, skills and technology to host countries — but may also send profits home, pay low wages, and have great bargaining power over governments.

### Benefits and drawbacks for developed countries such as the UK

| Group | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | **lower prices** for goods made where costs are lower (clothes, electronics, toys); **more choice** from around the world | concerns about the conditions under which cheap goods are made; dependence on long supply chains that can be disrupted |
| **Producers (firms)** | **bigger markets** to sell to; **economies of scale**; **cheaper inputs** and the option to locate production where costs are lowest; access to global finance and talent | **more competition** from low-cost foreign producers, cutting sales and profits; some firms close; vulnerable to disruption in global supply chains |
| **Workers** | jobs in exporting and high-skill service industries (finance, design, research, education); jobs created by foreign MNCs investing in the UK | **job losses** when production moves abroad (**offshoring**) or when firms cannot compete with imports, especially in manufacturing; downward pressure on wages for lower-skilled work; **structural unemployment** in particular regions |

### Benefits and drawbacks for less developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Workers** | new **jobs** in factories and services, often paying more than farming; **training** and new skills | **low wages**, long hours and **poor working conditions**; sometimes child labour; jobs may leave when costs rise and MNCs move on |
| **Producers** | access to world markets; investment and **technology transfer** from MNCs; growth of local suppliers | local firms may be unable to compete with powerful MNCs and close; **dependency** on a few export products or a few foreign firms |
| **Consumers** | wider choice of goods; rising incomes as the economy grows | traditional local products may be pushed out; benefits may go mainly to a richer minority |
| **The economy** | **economic growth**, higher incomes, tax revenue, foreign currency from exports; many people lifted out of poverty | **profits sent abroad** to MNCs' home countries; **environmental damage** (pollution, deforestation) where rules are weak; exploitation of natural resources; greater inequality |

### Moral, ethical and sustainability considerations

When UK producers buy from or produce in other countries, they face choices that are not only about cost:
- **Working conditions** — are the workers in the supply chain paid fairly and kept safe? Is child or forced labour involved? UK firms may audit their suppliers, and large UK firms must report on what they do to tackle forced labour in their supply chains.
- **Fair trade** — schemes that guarantee producers in poorer countries a minimum price and extra money for community projects; consumers pay a little more.
- **Environmental impact** — transporting goods around the world adds **carbon emissions**; production may move to countries with weaker environmental rules (pollution and deforestation).
- **Sustainability** — using up finite resources or damaging ecosystems for cheap goods today harms future generations.
- **Tax** — MNCs may arrange their affairs to pay tax where it is lowest, reducing revenue in the countries where they actually operate.
- **Consumer pressure** — consumers and campaigners increasingly expect ethical sourcing; firms that are exposed for poor practices can suffer damage to their reputation and sales.

Acting ethically usually **raises costs** — higher wages, auditing, cleaner production, shorter supply chains — so firms face a trade-off between profit and responsibility, though a good reputation can also bring customers.

::: terms
- **Globalisation**: the growing integration and interdependence of the world's economies through trade, investment, migration and technology.
- **Multinational company**: a business that has its headquarters in one country and operations, such as factories or offices, in other countries.
- **Supply chain**: the network of firms, often in different countries, that produce the parts of a product and bring it to the consumer.
- **Offshoring**: moving part of a firm's production or services to another country, usually to cut costs.
- **Foreign direct investment**: investment by a firm in one country in productive assets, such as factories, in another country.
- **Containerisation**: the use of standard-sized shipping containers that can be moved between ships, trains and lorries, cutting transport costs.
- **Less developed country**: a country with relatively low income per head, and often low levels of industry and infrastructure.
- **Technology transfer**: the spread of skills, knowledge and production methods from one country to another, often through MNCs.
- **Fair trade**: a scheme that guarantees producers in poorer countries a fair minimum price and better working conditions.
- **Sustainability**: meeting the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the group and the country type**: producers, workers or consumers — in the UK (developed) or in less developed countries. Keep the answer about that group.
2. **Causes**: technology (internet, communications), transport (containerisation), MNCs, trade liberalisation, capital flows — chain each to more trade or investment.
3. **Benefit/drawback chain**: globalisation → what changes (cheaper imports, offshoring, MNC investment) → effect on the group.
4. **Moral, ethical and sustainability issues**: wages and conditions, child labour, emissions, environmental damage, tax, fair trade — and the cost to firms of acting responsibly.
5. **Use the data**: percentage changes in jobs, wages, exports or emissions.
6. **Evaluate**: who gains and who loses; short run versus long run; whether governments regulate MNCs; then judge.
:::

::: example Define — multinational company
Define the term "multinational company". (2)

A business that is based/headquartered in one country (1) but has operations such as factories or offices in more than one country (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a cause of globalisation
Explain one way in which new technology has contributed to globalisation. (2)

The internet and video communication let firms manage factories, offices and suppliers in other countries cheaply and instantly (1), so firms can spread production across the world and sell to customers anywhere online, increasing international trade (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a drawback for UK producers
Explain one drawback of globalisation for producers in developed countries. (2)

Producers face more competition from firms in countries with much lower labour costs (1), so they lose sales and profits and some are forced to close or move production abroad (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — wages and costs
Invented data: a UK clothing firm pays £11.50 an hour to machinists in the UK. A factory abroad charges the equivalent of £2.30 an hour. Each garment takes 0.8 hours to sew. Calculate the labour cost saving per garment from moving production abroad. (2)

UK cost = 0.8 × 11.50 = £9.20; overseas cost = 0.8 × 2.30 = £1.84 (1). Saving = **£7.36 per garment** (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — an MNC in a less developed country
Invented data: Medira Foods (fictional), a UK-based multinational, opens a fruit-processing plant in Country W, a less developed country. It employs 2,400 local workers at about twice the local farm wage. Most profits are sent back to the UK. Local campaigners say river pollution near the plant has increased. Analyse the benefits and drawbacks of this investment for Country W. (6)

The plant creates 2,400 jobs at twice the farm wage, so workers' incomes rise; they spend more locally, creating further jobs and raising tax revenue (2). Local farmers gain a large buyer for their fruit, and workers gain skills and the firm may bring technology the country lacks (2). But because most profits go back to the UK, less of the income stays in Country W to fund development; and the river pollution is a negative externality for local people and farmers — a cost the firm does not pay, especially if environmental rules are weak (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Apply to the group named**: a drawback "for producers in developed countries" is **cheaper foreign competition**, not low wages abroad.
- Keep developed and less developed countries separate — the same change (e.g. offshoring) is a loss of jobs in one and a gain in the other.
- Causes need a **mechanism**: "technology" alone is not enough — say how it cuts costs or makes trade easier.
- MNCs bring jobs, investment and technology **and** may send profits home, pay low wages and pollute — use both sides.
- Moral, ethical and sustainability points need a specific issue (working conditions, emissions, child labour, fair trade) and who is affected.
- Use any data — calculate changes rather than restating figures.
:::
