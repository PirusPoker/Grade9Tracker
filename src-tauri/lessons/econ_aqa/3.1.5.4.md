## What it is

Labour is a factor of production, and like any other resource it is bought and sold in a market. This topic covers the **role and operation of the labour market**, how **wages are determined by the demand for and supply of labour**, why there are **wage differentials** within and between occupations, and the difference between **gross and net pay**, including how to **calculate** them.

### The labour market

The **labour market** is a **factor market** (3.1.2.1): workers **supply** their labour, and employers **demand** it. The price of labour is the **wage** (or salary).

**Demand for labour** comes from employers. It is a **derived demand** — firms do not want workers for their own sake, but for the goods and services they help to produce. If demand for a product rises, firms need more workers to make or deliver it, so demand for that labour rises too. Demand for labour also depends on:
- the **wage** — at a higher wage, firms employ fewer workers (a movement along the demand curve), so it slopes downwards;
- workers' **productivity** — more productive workers are worth employing at a higher wage;
- the **cost of machines** that could replace workers.

**Supply of labour** is the number of workers willing and able to work in a job at each wage. It slopes upwards: higher wages attract more people. Supply also depends on:
- the **skills, qualifications and training** needed — the longer and harder the training, the fewer people can do the job;
- **working conditions** and how **attractive** the job is (danger, hours, stress, status, flexibility);
- the size of the **population** and **migration**;
- **non-wage benefits** such as pensions, holidays and job security.

Other shifts to recognise:

| Change | Which curve moves | Effect on wage and employment |
|---|---|---|
| more demand for the product the workers make | demand for labour **right** | wage ↑, employment ↑ |
| a machine replaces many workers' tasks | demand for labour **left** | wage ↓, employment ↓ |
| more people train for the job / inward migration of qualified workers | supply of labour **right** | wage ↓, employment ↑ |
| the job becomes more dangerous or stressful; longer training required | supply of labour **left** | wage ↑, employment ↓ |

### Wage differentials

A **wage differential** is a difference in pay between workers. Differentials exist **between occupations** (doctors earn more than waiters) and **within occupations** (one teacher earns more than another).

| Reason | Why it affects pay |
|---|---|
| **Skills, qualifications and training** | few people have the skills of a surgeon or an engineer, so supply is low relative to demand → high wage; jobs anyone can do quickly have a large supply → lower wage |
| **Productivity / value to the employer** | workers who generate a lot of revenue (a top salesperson, a star footballer) are in high demand |
| **Demand for the product** | workers in growing industries (e.g. software) are in high demand; derived demand |
| **Danger, unsociable hours, unpleasant conditions** | firms must pay more to attract enough workers (e.g. oil-rig workers, night shifts) |
| **Experience and seniority** (*within* occupations) | experienced workers are more productive; many pay scales rise with years of service |
| **Region** (*within* occupations) | London and the South East usually pay more because of higher demand and living costs |
| **Trade unions** | strong unions can bargain for higher wages for their members |
| **Government** | the **National Minimum Wage / National Living Wage** sets a legal floor; public-sector pay is set by government |
| **Discrimination** | unfair treatment by gender, ethnicity, age or disability can lead to unequal pay, although it is illegal |
| **Non-wage benefits / job satisfaction** | some workers accept lower pay for a rewarding or secure job (e.g. charity work, some public-sector roles) |

So a doctor earns more than a waiter mainly because of **years of training** (low supply) and the **high value** of the work, while waiting needs little training, so supply is large.

### Gross and net pay

- **Gross pay** is the total pay earned **before** any deductions.
- **Net pay** ("take-home pay") is what the worker actually receives **after** deductions.

**Net pay = gross pay − deductions.** The main deductions in the UK are:

| Deduction | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Income tax** | a tax on income above a tax-free allowance, paid at rates that rise with income (3.2.1.2) |
| **National Insurance contributions** | paid on earnings above a threshold; help to fund the state pension and some benefits |
| **Pension contributions** | money the worker pays into a workplace pension scheme |
| *Student loan repayments* | paid by graduates once earnings exceed a threshold (an extra) |

Things that are **not** deductions from pay: VAT (a tax on spending), council tax (paid separately), maternity pay or overtime (both are additions to pay).

**Calculating gross pay**: hourly wage × hours worked (+ overtime, bonuses, commission). Example: 37.5 hours × £14 per hour = **£525 a week** gross.

**Calculating net pay** (invented, simplified rates — real thresholds and rates change):
A worker earns a gross salary of **£30,000** a year. Rules: no income tax on the first £12,000, then 20%; National Insurance of 8% on earnings above £12,000; pension contribution of 5% of gross pay.

| Step | Working | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Taxable income | £30,000 − £12,000 | £18,000 |
| Income tax | 20% × £18,000 | £3,600 |
| National Insurance | 8% × £18,000 | £1,440 |
| Pension | 5% × £30,000 | £1,500 |
| Total deductions | £3,600 + £1,440 + £1,500 | £6,540 |
| **Net pay** | £30,000 − £6,540 | **£23,460 a year** (£1,955 a month) |

::: terms
- **Labour market**: the factor market in which workers supply labour and employers demand it, determining wages.
- **Derived demand**: demand for a factor of production that comes from the demand for the product it helps make.
- **Supply of labour**: the number of workers willing and able to work in a job at each wage rate.
- **Wage differential**: a difference in pay between workers in different occupations or within the same occupation.
- **Gross pay**: a worker's total pay before any deductions are taken off.
- **Net pay**: a worker's take-home pay after deductions such as income tax and National Insurance.
- **Income tax**: a direct tax on income above a tax-free allowance.
- **National Insurance**: a deduction from earnings above a threshold that helps to fund the state pension and some benefits.
- **National Minimum Wage**: the lowest hourly wage employers are legally allowed to pay, set by the government.
- **Trade union**: an organisation of workers that bargains with employers over pay and conditions.
:::

::: method
1. **Diagram axes**: wage on the vertical axis, quantity of labour on the horizontal; label D (demand for labour) and S (supply of labour).
2. **Which curve moves?** A change in demand for the *product* moves the **demand for labour** (derived demand). Changes in training, attractiveness, population or migration move the **supply of labour**.
3. **Draw and label** (3 marks): original curves and equilibrium W1 Q1 (1); correct shift in the right direction (1); new equilibrium W2 Q2 labelled (1).
4. **Wage differentials**: compare supply (training, skills, attractiveness) and demand (productivity, demand for the product) for the two jobs; add unions, region, minimum wage, discrimination if relevant.
5. **Gross/net pay**: gross = hours × rate (+ extras); net = gross − income tax − NI − pension. Show each step and give £.
6. **Read the group in the question**: "for an individual worker" means effects on that worker, not on the firm.
:::

::: example Calculate — gross and net pay
Invented data: Sam works 40 hours a week at £12.50 an hour. Each week £48 income tax, £26 National Insurance and £20 pension contributions are deducted. Calculate Sam's weekly gross and net pay. (3)

Gross pay = 40 × £12.50 = £500 (1). Deductions = £48 + £26 + £20 = £94 (1). Net pay = £500 − £94 = £406 a week (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a wage differential between occupations
Explain one reason why airline pilots are paid more than shop assistants. (2)

Becoming a pilot requires years of expensive training and a licence, so few people can supply this labour (1); with a low supply relative to airlines' demand, the equilibrium wage is high, whereas many people can do shop work after little training, so supply is high and the wage lower (1).
:::

::: example Draw and label — derived demand
Draw and label a diagram to show the effect of a large rise in demand for electric cars on the labour market for car-battery engineers. (3)

Model answer (described): axes labelled Wage and Quantity of labour, with demand D1 and supply S crossing at W1 Q1 (1). Because demand for engineers is derived from demand for electric cars, the demand for labour shifts **right** to D2 (1). The new equilibrium shows a higher wage W2 and more engineers employed Q2 (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a wage differential within an occupation
Explain one reason why two nurses in the same hospital may be paid different amounts. (2)

One nurse may have many more years of experience and a senior role (1); pay scales rise with experience and responsibility because more experienced nurses are more productive and valuable to the hospital (1). (Or: specialist qualifications, night shifts — developed.)
:::

::: example Analyse — pay differences from data
Invented data — average weekly gross pay: software developer £900; electrician £720; care worker £480. Training: degree or long course; 3–4 year apprenticeship; a few weeks' induction. Analyse possible reasons for the differences in pay. (6)

Software developers need a degree or long training, so the supply of qualified workers is limited, while demand is high and rising because the digital products they make are in growing demand — derived demand pushes their wage up to £900, almost double the care worker's (2). Electricians train for 3–4 years, which limits supply, and their work involves some danger, so firms must pay more to attract workers; but the training is shorter than a degree, so supply is larger than for developers and pay is £180 a week lower (2). Care work needs only a few weeks' training, so many people can supply it, keeping the wage close to the legal minimum even though demand is high from an ageing population; the care provider's own funding limits may also restrict what it can pay (2).
:::

::: q
Invented data: Priya earns a gross salary of £2,600 a month. Deductions are income tax £290, National Insurance £115 and a pension contribution of 5% of gross pay. Calculate her monthly net pay. (3)
---
Pension = 5% × £2,600 = £130 (1); total deductions = £290 + £115 + £130 = £535 (1); net pay = £2,600 − £535 = £2,065 (1).

::: q
Explain why the demand for labour is a derived demand. (2)
---
Firms do not want workers for their own sake but for the goods and services they produce (1), so the demand for workers depends on/rises and falls with the demand for the product (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a large increase in the number of people training to be lorry drivers could affect the wages of lorry drivers. (6)
---
Indicative content: more trained drivers increases the supply of labour (1), shifting the supply curve to the right (1). At the old wage there would be more drivers than jobs (excess supply of labour) (1), so firms can fill vacancies more easily and wage growth slows or wages fall (1); employment of drivers rises (1). The size of the effect depends on demand for lorry drivers — if demand is also rising (e.g. more online shopping), wages may not fall (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Derived demand**: a rise in demand for a product shifts the **demand for labour** right — the classic exam error is shifting supply (examiners named this in 2025).
- Put the **wage** on the vertical axis and **quantity of labour** on the horizontal — and label the curves as demand for and supply of labour.
- VAT, council tax and maternity pay are **not** deductions from gross pay.
- Net pay = gross **minus** deductions; check whether the question asks for weekly, monthly or annual pay and keep units the same.
- Answer about the **worker** when the question says "for an individual worker" — answers about the firm score nothing.
- Wage differentials need the chain: factor → supply or demand of that labour → higher or lower wage.
:::

::: test
Explain two reasons why footballers in a top league are paid more than nurses. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Supply: very few people have the exceptional talent to play at the top level (1), so the supply of such players is extremely low (1), pushing their equilibrium wage very high (1).
Demand/revenue: top clubs earn huge revenue from broadcasting rights, tickets and sponsorship (1); a star player brings in more fans and success, so his value to the club is very high (1) and clubs compete for players, bidding wages up (1).
Also creditworthy: nurses' pay is largely set by government budgets/pay scales; many people train as nurses relative to top footballers; short careers for footballers; non-wage job satisfaction for nurses.
:::
