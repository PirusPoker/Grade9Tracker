## What it is

Why do big supermarkets, car makers and airlines keep getting bigger? Part of the answer is **economies of scale**. This topic covers what economies of scale are, the **five types named in the specification** (managerial, purchasing, financial, technical and risk-bearing), how they affect **business behaviour**, the **costs and benefits of growth** for a business, and **diseconomies of scale** — why average costs can start to rise when a firm grows too big.

### The meaning of economies of scale

**Economies of scale are the fall in average (unit) cost that happens as a firm increases its scale of production.** As the firm grows, total cost rises — it is using more resources — but output rises **faster**, so the cost of each unit falls.

Invented example: a juice maker produces 10,000 bottles a month at a total cost of £5,000, so average cost = £5,000 ÷ 10,000 = **£0.50**. After building a bigger plant it produces 40,000 bottles for £14,000: average cost = £14,000 ÷ 40,000 = **£0.35**. Total cost almost trebled, but average cost fell by (0.35 − 0.50) ÷ 0.50 × 100 = **−30%**.

Keep the distinction clear: economies of scale lower **average** cost, not total cost.

### The types of economies of scale

| Type | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Purchasing** (bulk buying) | large firms buy raw materials and stock in huge quantities and negotiate **discounts** from suppliers | a national chain of bakeries pays less per sack of flour than a single local bakery |
| **Technical** | large firms can afford **bigger, more advanced and more efficient machinery and production methods**; some equipment only pays off at high output; bigger units (a larger ship or lorry) cost less per unit carried | a large car maker uses robotic assembly lines; a container ship carrying twice as many containers does not need twice the crew or fuel |
| **Managerial** | large firms can employ **specialist managers** (finance, marketing, HR, logistics) who each focus on one area and make better decisions | a small firm's owner does everything; a big firm has an expert accountant and an expert buyer |
| **Financial** | large firms can **borrow more cheaply and easily** — banks see them as less risky, so offer lower interest rates; they can also raise money by selling shares | a big retailer borrows at a lower rate than a corner shop |
| **Risk-bearing** | large firms can **diversify** — sell a range of products or sell in many markets/countries — so a fall in demand for one is offset by others | a drinks firm making soft drinks, juices and bottled water; a chain trading in several countries |
| *Marketing* (an extra) | the cost of advertising is spread over far more units; national campaigns become affordable | a TV advert costs the same whether it supports 10 shops or 1,000 |

The examiner identified economies-of-scale multiple-choice questions as among the hardest on the paper. The usual confusion is between **risk-bearing** (spreading risk across products or markets) and **purchasing** (discounts for buying in bulk), and between **technical** (better machinery and methods) and **managerial** (specialist people). Match the *reason* the cost falls to the type.

### Diseconomies of scale

If a firm grows too large, **average costs can start to rise** again. These are **diseconomies of scale**:

| Cause | Why average cost rises |
|---|---|
| **Poor communication** | messages pass through many layers of management, get delayed or distorted; mistakes are made and time is wasted |
| **Coordination and control problems** | it is hard to keep many sites, departments and thousands of workers working towards the same aims; duplication and waste occur |
| **Low motivation** | workers in a huge organisation feel like a small cog, unrecognised and remote from decision-makers; effort falls, absence and staff turnover rise, so productivity falls |
| **Slow decision-making** | many layers and committees mean the firm is slow to react to changes in the market |

### Implications for business behaviour

Because economies of scale lower average costs, they shape how firms act:

- **An incentive to grow** — firms expand by opening new sites or building bigger factories (internal growth), or by **mergers and takeovers** with other firms (external growth), to reach the lowest average cost.
- **Lower prices** — a firm with lower unit costs can undercut smaller rivals and win market share, or keep prices and earn **higher profit margins**.
- **Barriers to entry** — new, small firms cannot match the low costs of established giants, so markets such as supermarkets, car-making and airlines become dominated by a few large firms (3.1.5.3).
- **Pressure to keep growing** — firms may chase size for its own sake, even into the range where diseconomies set in.

### Costs and benefits of growth for a business

| Benefits of growth | Costs of growth |
|---|---|
| economies of scale → lower average cost | diseconomies of scale → rising average cost if growth goes too far |
| higher total revenue and profit | high cost of expansion; may need heavy borrowing (interest, risk) |
| bigger market share and more power over prices and suppliers | loss of the personal touch and flexibility that customers valued |
| a recognised brand; easier to attract finance and skilled staff | management stretched; communication and quality problems |
| diversification spreads risk | takeovers may fail if the firms' cultures clash; the competition authorities may block a merger |
| survival — less likely to be driven out by rivals | may attract criticism for exploiting market power |

Growth benefits a business **up to a point**: the right scale depends on the type of product, the size of the market and how well the firm is managed.

::: terms
- **Economies of scale**: the fall in average cost that occurs as a firm increases its scale of production.
- **Diseconomies of scale**: the rise in average cost that occurs when a firm grows too large and becomes less efficient.
- **Purchasing economies**: lower unit costs from buying inputs in bulk at a discount.
- **Technical economies**: lower unit costs from using larger, more advanced machinery and production methods.
- **Managerial economies**: lower unit costs from employing specialist managers for each area of the business.
- **Financial economies**: lower unit costs because large firms can borrow more easily and at lower interest rates.
- **Risk-bearing economies**: the ability of a large firm to spread risk by selling many products or in many markets.
- **Average cost**: total cost divided by output — the cost of producing one unit.
- **Merger**: when two firms agree to join together to form one larger business.
- **Takeover**: when one firm buys a controlling share in another firm.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely**: economies of scale lower **average/unit** cost as the **scale** of production rises — never just "costs fall".
2. **Name the type from the reason**: bulk discount → purchasing; better machinery → technical; specialist managers → managerial; cheaper borrowing → financial; spreading products or markets → risk-bearing.
3. **"Explain one economy of scale"** (2 marks): name the type (1) + why it lowers the cost of each unit for *this* firm (1).
4. **Calculations**: AC = TC ÷ output at each scale; percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; say clearly whether AC rose or fell.
5. **Diseconomies**: give the cause (communication, coordination, motivation) and the chain to higher average cost.
6. **Assess / 15-mark**: weigh economies against diseconomies and the costs of growth, use the Item's data, and conclude what the right scale depends on.
:::

::: example Calculate — average cost at two outputs
Invented data: a printing firm produces 50,000 leaflets at a total cost of £4,000. After buying a large digital press it produces 200,000 leaflets at a total cost of £10,000. Calculate the change in average cost per leaflet. (3)

AC before = £4,000 ÷ 50,000 = £0.08 (1). AC after = £10,000 ÷ 200,000 = £0.05 (1). Average cost falls by £0.03 per leaflet, or 37.5% (1).
:::

::: example Identify — the type of economy of scale
A large toy manufacturer sells in twenty countries, so a fall in sales in one country has little effect on its overall revenue. State the type of economy of scale this shows. (1)

Risk-bearing economies of scale (1).
:::

::: example Explain — an economy of scale for a hotel chain
Explain one economy of scale a national hotel chain may benefit from. (2)

Purchasing economies: the chain buys bedding, toiletries and food for hundreds of hotels at once and negotiates bulk discounts from suppliers (1), so the cost of each guest night is lower than for a single independent hotel (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a diseconomy of scale
Explain one diseconomy of scale a firm might experience as it grows. (2)

As the firm adds more layers of management, instructions take longer to pass from senior managers to workers and are sometimes misunderstood (1), so mistakes and delays increase, wasting resources and raising the average cost of each unit (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — the effect of economies of scale on a market
Analyse how economies of scale might affect competition in the market for budget flights. (6)

Large airlines can buy many aircraft at once at a discount and spread the fixed cost of booking systems and advertising over millions of passengers, so their average cost per seat is far lower than a small airline's (2). They can therefore set fares that a new, small airline cannot match without making a loss, which acts as a barrier to entry; over time the market may become dominated by a few large airlines (2). In the short run passengers gain from low fares, but with fewer competitors the large airlines could later raise fares or cut service quality, so the long-run effect on consumers depends on whether rivals can still enter the market (2).
:::

---
A — a lower interest rate on its loans.

Define the term "economies of scale". (2)
---
A fall in average/unit cost (1) as a firm increases its scale of production/output (1).
:::

::: q
State two types of economy of scale. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): purchasing/bulk buying; technical; managerial; financial; risk-bearing; marketing.
:::

::: q
Invented data: at an output of 2,000 units a firm's total cost is £16,000; at 5,000 units it is £30,000. Calculate the average cost at each output and state whether the firm is experiencing economies or diseconomies of scale. (3)
---
£16,000 ÷ 2,000 = £8 (1); £30,000 ÷ 5,000 = £6 (1); average cost falls, so economies of scale (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why a firm's average costs may rise as it becomes very large. (2)
---
Workers feel remote from decision-makers and unvalued, so motivation falls (1); absence and staff turnover rise and productivity falls, raising the cost of each unit (1). (Or: communication or coordination problems — developed.)
:::

::: q
Analyse how economies of scale could act as a barrier to entry into a market. (6)
---
Established large firms have low average costs from bulk buying, specialist machinery and spreading fixed costs (1–2). A new firm starts small, so its average cost is higher (1); to compete on price it would make a loss, and to charge more it would lose customers (1). Banks see it as risky, so its finance is dearer (1). It may therefore not enter, leaving the market dominated by a few firms who can keep prices above cost (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- Economies of scale lower **average** cost — "total costs fall" is wrong, since total cost rises as a firm grows.
- **Risk-bearing** means spreading risk across products or markets; **purchasing** means bulk discounts. These are the two types examiners see muddled most often.
- **Technical** economies are about machinery and methods; **managerial** economies are about specialist people.
- A diseconomy needs a cause **and** the effect on average cost — "the firm gets too big" is not enough.
- Apply to the firm in the question: a "supermarket" answer about bulk buying needs to say what it buys and why its unit cost falls.
- 15-markers on growth must weigh **both** economies and diseconomies or other costs of growth, and reach a judgement.
:::

Invented data for a chain of gyms:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of gyms | 10 | 25 | 60 |

::: test
Explain one way in which economies of scale could benefit consumers. (2)
---
A large firm's average cost falls, e.g. through bulk buying (1), so it can charge lower prices / offer better products funded by its savings, which consumers gain from if competition forces it to pass the savings on (1).

::: test
Using an example, assess whether economies of scale always benefit consumers. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Benefit: lower average costs can be passed on as lower prices (e.g. large supermarkets or budget airlines); large firms can fund research and new products; risk-bearing economies make supply more reliable; a wider range of products.
Do not always benefit: firms may keep cost savings as higher profit rather than cut prices; economies of scale create barriers to entry, so a few large firms dominate and may raise prices or reduce choice; local independent shops may close; diseconomies may set in and service quality may fall in huge firms; bulk-buying power may squeeze suppliers, lowering quality.
Conclusion: e.g. consumers benefit when the market stays competitive so savings are passed on; where economies of scale lead to dominance, the benefits are weaker. It depends on the level of competition and on regulation.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Item A — BrewCo's expansion (invented)

BrewCo is a UK chain of coffee shops. Over two years it grew from 80 to 200 cafés, partly by taking over a smaller rival. It now buys its coffee beans, milk and cups centrally in large quantities, and has hired specialist managers for property, marketing and staff training. It also borrowed £40 million to fund the expansion.

| | Year 1 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Cafés | 80 | 200 |
| Cups sold (millions) | 12 | 32 |
| Total cost (£m) | 30 | 72 |
| Average price per cup (£) | 3.20 | 3.20 |
| Staff turnover (% leaving per year) | 20 | 35 |
| Customer satisfaction score (%) | 82 | 74 |

Do you think growth always benefits a business such as BrewCo? Use Item A and your own economic knowledge to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 5, AO3 10. Indicative content:
Use of data: AC Year 1 = £30m ÷ 12m = £2.50 per cup; Year 3 = £72m ÷ 32m = £2.25 (a 10% fall). TR Year 1 = £38.4m, profit £8.4m; Year 3 TR = £102.4m, profit £30.4m.
Benefits of growth: purchasing economies from central bulk buying; managerial economies from specialist managers; marketing costs spread over 200 cafés; lower AC and much higher total profit and profit per cup (£0.70 → £0.95); a bigger brand and market share; the takeover removed a competitor; more cafés spread risk across locations.
Costs/risks of growth: staff turnover up from 20% to 35% — a sign of low motivation (a diseconomy), raising recruitment and training costs; customer satisfaction down from 82% to 74% — quality and the "local" feel may be suffering, which could reduce demand and loyalty in the long run; £40m of borrowing brings interest costs and risk if interest rates rise or sales fall; integrating a takeover is hard; further growth could push AC up.
Evaluation: so far the benefits outweigh the costs (AC down, profit up), but the warning signs suggest diseconomies may be starting; whether growth keeps benefiting BrewCo depends on how well it manages staff and quality, the cost of its debt and how much competition it faces.
Conclusion: a supported view, e.g. growth has benefited BrewCo but does not *always* benefit a business — beyond a certain size the falls in motivation and quality could outweigh the cost savings.
Levels: 13–15 — sustained, balanced evaluation using the Item's data, developed chains on both sides and a fully supported judgement; 10–12 — well-developed analysis with good use of the Item and a reasoned judgement, some imbalance; 7–9 — some developed analysis of both sides with some use of the Item, partly supported judgement; 4–6 — limited analysis, mainly one-sided or descriptive, little use of data; 1–3 — isolated points, assertion.
