## What it is

Scarce resources have to be shared out somehow. In most of the UK economy, that job is done by **markets**. This topic covers what a market is, how markets allocate resources through prices, and the difference between **factor markets** and **product markets**.

### What a market is

A **market is any opportunity for buyers and sellers to interact in order to establish a price.** The key words are *interact* and *price* — a market is not defined by a place.

| Type of market | How buyers and sellers interact |
|---|---|
| a street market or high-street shop | face to face |
| an online marketplace or retailer's website | through a website or app, often never meeting |
| an auction (in a room or online) | buyers bid against each other until a price is reached |
| the stock market or the foreign-exchange market | electronically, through dealers and trading systems, worldwide |
| the job market | through adverts, applications and interviews; the "price" is the wage |

So online retailing *is* a market: buyers and sellers interact through a website and a price is agreed when the buyer pays. A market can be **local** (a town's window cleaners), **national** (UK car insurance) or **global** (crude oil, smartphones).

### How markets allocate scarce resources

Recall the three key decisions (3.1.1.1): *what* to produce, *how* to produce, and *who benefits*. In a market economy, **prices** answer them — this is often called the **price mechanism**. Prices do three jobs:

| Function of price | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Signalling** | a rising price tells producers consumers want more; a falling price tells them to produce less | rising prices for electric bikes signal growing demand |
| **Incentive** | a higher price makes producing the good more profitable, encouraging firms to supply more and new firms to enter | firms switch factory space from petrol scooters to e-bikes |
| **Rationing** | when a good is scarce, a higher price limits it to those willing and able to pay the most | concert tickets go to the fans who pay the highest prices |

**The chain of reallocation.** Suppose consumers' tastes switch from fizzy drinks towards bottled water:

1. Demand for bottled water rises; its price rises.
2. Water producers make more profit, so they expand and new firms enter (**incentive**).
3. They need more workers, bottling machines and spring sites, so they bid for these **factors of production** — offering higher wages and rents.
4. Demand for fizzy drinks falls; its price and profits fall, so producers cut output and release workers and machinery.
5. Resources **move from fizzy drinks to bottled water** — exactly where consumers now want them. Nobody planned it; prices coordinated millions of decisions.

This is how the market answers the three questions:

- **What to produce?** Whatever consumers will pay for at a price that covers costs.
- **How to produce?** The cheapest combination of factors, because firms want to keep costs down and stay competitive.
- **Who benefits?** Those who are willing and able to pay — which means people with higher incomes get more. That is why markets sometimes produce outcomes society judges unfair, and why the government intervenes (market failure is covered in 3.1.6).

### Factor markets and product markets

| | **Factor markets** | **Product markets** |
|---|---|---|
| What is traded | **factors of production** — land, labour, capital, enterprise | **goods and services** — the final output that consumers buy |
| Buyers | firms (producers) | consumers (and other firms) |
| Sellers | households, landowners, lenders | firms (producers) |
| Price | wages, rent, interest (rewards to factors) | the price of the good or service |
| Examples | the labour market for nurses; the market for office space; the market for business loans | the market for pizza; the market for haircuts; the market for cinema tickets |

The two markets are **linked**. Households earn incomes by selling their labour (and other factors) in factor markets, and spend that income buying goods and services in product markets. Firms do the reverse: they buy factors to produce output, then sell that output in product markets. This creates a circular flow of money and resources.

**The examiner's warning.** The difference is **not** "goods versus services". A haircut is a *service* but it is sold in a **product** market. A factor market trades the *inputs* used to produce goods and services; a product market trades the *outputs* — both goods and services.

**Derived demand.** Demand in factor markets comes from demand in product markets. Firms want bakers only because consumers want bread, so the demand for bakers is a **derived demand**. If demand for groceries rises, supermarkets need more shelf-stackers and delivery drivers: demand in the grocery workers' labour market shifts right, raising wages and employment. Factor markets therefore follow product markets.

::: terms
- **Market**: any opportunity for buyers and sellers to interact in order to establish a price.
- **Price mechanism**: the way prices, set by demand and supply, allocate scarce resources in a market economy.
- **Allocation of resources**: how scarce factors of production are shared out between different uses.
- **Signalling function**: the role of price changes in telling producers and consumers about changes in demand or supply.
- **Incentive function**: the role of higher prices and profits in encouraging firms to produce more.
- **Rationing function**: the role of a higher price in limiting a scarce good to those willing and able to pay most.
- **Factor market**: a market in which factors of production, such as labour or land, are bought and sold.
- **Product market**: a market in which finished goods and services are bought and sold.
- **Labour market**: the factor market in which workers supply labour and firms demand it, setting the wage.
- **Derived demand**: demand for a factor of production that comes from demand for the product it helps make.
:::

::: method
1. **Define the market** using both ideas: buyers and sellers *interact* to *establish a price* — a place is not required.
2. **Factor vs product**: say what is traded in each (inputs vs outputs), give an example of each, and avoid "goods versus services".
3. **Allocation questions**: write the chain — change in demand → price changes (signal) → profit changes (incentive) → firms expand/contract → factors move.
4. **Name the factor markets affected** when a product market changes (derived demand): workers, land, machinery.
5. **Evaluate** by asking who benefits: markets respond to those able to pay, so outcomes may be efficient but not fair.
:::

::: example Explain — an online market
Explain why online retailing is an example of a market. (2)

A market is any opportunity for buyers and sellers to interact to establish a price, and it need not be a physical place (1). On a retailer's website, sellers display goods at a price and buyers agree to it by paying online, so a price is established through their interaction (1).
:::

::: example Explain — factor versus product
Explain one difference between a product market and a factor market. (2)

In a product market, finished goods and services such as smartphones or haircuts are bought, mainly by consumers (1); in a factor market, the inputs used to make them, such as labour, are bought by firms — for example, the market for software engineers (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — reallocation after a change in demand
Demand for plant-based foods has risen sharply. Analyse how the market reallocates resources towards plant-based food production. (6)

Higher demand pushes up the price of plant-based foods, signalling to producers that consumers want more (2). Higher prices raise the profit from making them, giving existing firms an incentive to expand and new firms to enter; they buy more factory space and machinery and hire more food technologists, bidding up wages in those factor markets (2). Meanwhile, if demand for some meat products falls, their prices and profits fall, so those producers cut output and release land, workers and equipment, which move into the expanding plant-based sector — resources follow consumer demand without any central plan (2).
:::

::: example Draw and label — derived demand
Draw and label the effect of an increase in demand for home deliveries on the labour market for delivery drivers. (3)

Model answer (described, as you would draw it): axes labelled wage (W) on the vertical and quantity of labour (Q) on the horizontal; a downward-sloping demand for labour curve D1 and an upward-sloping supply of labour curve S, crossing at W1 and Q1 (1). The demand for labour curve shifts right to D2, because the demand for drivers is derived from the demand for deliveries (1). The new equilibrium is labelled at a higher wage W2 and higher quantity of drivers Q2 (1).
:::

::: example Assess — markets and fairness
Assess whether markets allocate scarce resources well. (9)

For: prices respond quickly to changes in what consumers want, so resources move towards the goods people value most without anyone having to plan it (2). Competition and the profit incentive push firms to use the cheapest methods, so scarce resources are not wasted (1).
Against: the market answers "who benefits" by willingness and ability to pay, so people on low incomes may be unable to buy necessities such as housing (2). Markets ignore effects on third parties — a factory that pollutes does not pay for the damage — and may provide too little of goods such as education (1).
Judgement: markets allocate most everyday goods well, such as clothing and food, because consumers know their own wants; but for necessities and goods with wider effects, government intervention is needed. How well markets work depends on competition and on how evenly income is shared (3).
:::

---

::: q
Using an example, analyse how a fall in demand for petrol cars could affect factor markets. (6)
---
Lower demand cuts the price and profit of petrol cars (1), so manufacturers reduce output of them (1). Demand for workers making petrol engines falls — a derived demand — so wages and employment in that labour market fall (1); land and factory space used for engine plants may be sold or reused (1). If demand for electric cars rises at the same time, resources move into battery production, raising wages for workers with those skills (1); workers with old skills may need retraining, or face unemployment (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains linking product and factor markets; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- A market is defined by **interaction to set a price**, not by a building.
- Factor vs product is **inputs vs outputs** — never "services vs goods".
- When a product market changes, **demand for the factor shifts**, not supply (derived demand) — a common diagram error.
- Show the **chain** in allocation questions: price → signal → profit/incentive → resources move.
- Remember markets allocate to those **able to pay** — use this for balance in assess questions.
:::

::: test
Assess whether the price mechanism is the best way to allocate tickets for a major sporting event. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For the price mechanism: price rations scarce tickets to those who value them most (willing to pay most); higher prices raise revenue for the organisers, which can fund facilities or the sport; prices adjust to demand, reducing queues and wasted time; touting is reduced if the official price already reflects demand.
Against: tickets go to the richest, not the most loyal or local fans; high prices may leave empty seats if set wrongly; public anger and damage to the sport's reputation; events may have social value (young people, community) that price ignores.
Alternatives: ballots, loyalty schemes, capped prices with ID checks, a share of tickets set aside cheaply.
Conclusion: e.g. a mixed approach — most tickets sold at market prices to raise revenue, with a share kept for ballots and loyal fans at lower prices; the right answer depends on the organiser's aims (revenue vs fairness).
Levels: L3 7–9 — well-developed, balanced analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some developed analysis, partly supported judgement; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
