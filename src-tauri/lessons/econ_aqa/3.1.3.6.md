## What it is

When the price of a product rises, producers want to sell more — but can they? A t-shirt printer can run an extra shift tomorrow; an apple grower must wait years for new trees to fruit; a city cannot build new flats overnight. **Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures how responsive the quantity supplied is to a change in price. This topic covers why price changes do not always cause equivalent changes in supply, how to calculate PES, the difference between **price elastic** and **price inelastic** supply, the factors that affect PES, and its implications for **producers and consumers**.

### Measuring PES

$$\text{PES} = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity supplied}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

with each percentage change calculated as **(new − old) ÷ old × 100**.

**Worked example** (invented): the price of a punnet of blueberries rises from £2.00 to £2.30, and a farm's weekly supply rises from 400 to 440 punnets.

- % change in price = (2.30 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 × 100 = **+15%**
- % change in quantity supplied = (440 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = **+10%**
- PES = 10 ÷ 15 = **0.67** (to two decimal places)

Because price and quantity supplied move in the **same** direction (the supply curve slopes upwards), PES is normally **positive**.

Sometimes the question gives the percentage change in price and the two quantities. Invented example: price rises by 20% and quantity supplied rises from 250 to 300 units. % change in quantity = 50 ÷ 250 × 100 = 20%, so PES = 20 ÷ 20 = **1**.

### Elastic and inelastic supply

| PES | Name | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **greater than 1** | **price elastic** | quantity supplied changes by a **bigger** percentage than price | mass-produced goods with spare factory capacity; digital downloads |
| **less than 1** | **price inelastic** | quantity supplied changes by a **smaller** percentage than price | housing, farm crops in the short run, seats at a stadium |
| exactly 1 | unitary elasticity | same percentage change | — |
| 0 | perfectly inelastic | quantity supplied cannot change at all | (extension) tickets for one concert in a fixed venue; an original painting |

On a diagram, **inelastic supply is steep** (a large price change, a small quantity change); **elastic supply is flat**.

### Rearranging the formula

**% change in quantity supplied = PES × % change in price.** Invented example: PES for bicycles is 1.5 and the price rises by 8%, so quantity supplied rises by 1.5 × 8% = 12%. A firm supplying 5,000 bicycles a year would supply 5,000 × 1.12 = **5,600**.

### Factors that affect PES

Every factor comes down to one question: **how easily and quickly can producers change their output?**

| Factor | Supply more **elastic** when… | Supply more **inelastic** when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Time period** | producers have time to hire workers, buy machines, build factories, plant crops | in the very short run, output is fixed by what is already produced; crops need a growing season |
| **Spare capacity** | firms have idle machines, empty floor space or workers who can do overtime | firms are already working at full capacity |
| **Stocks and ability to store** | goods can be stored, so stocks can be released when price rises (tinned food, clothing) | goods are perishable (fresh flowers, fresh fish) or services that cannot be stored (haircuts) |
| **Availability of factors of production and raw materials** | workers, materials and components are easy to obtain | inputs are scarce — skilled workers take years to train; land is limited |
| **Ease of switching production** | firms can easily switch from making one product to another (a clothing factory changing styles) | production needs specialist equipment or skills that cannot be redeployed |
| **Length of the production process** | output can be made quickly (printing, downloads) | production takes a long time (houses, aircraft, wine that must mature) |

**Do not answer with PED factors.** Substitutes, necessity, brand loyalty and share of income affect *demand*. A PES question needs *supply-side* factors — time, spare capacity, stocks, availability of inputs, ease of switching.

### Implications for producers and consumers

**When supply is inelastic:**

- An increase in demand mainly causes a **large rise in price** and only a small rise in quantity. Example: when demand for housing in a city rises, building cannot quickly expand, so house prices and rents rise sharply.
- **Prices are volatile**: shifts in demand (or a bad harvest) cause big price swings, as in many agricultural markets.
- **Producers**: existing producers can gain higher revenue and profit when demand rises, but they cannot capture extra sales; they lose when demand falls and they are stuck with output they cannot cut quickly.
- **Consumers**: pay much higher prices when demand rises; may face shortages, waiting lists or queues (stadium tickets, popular toys before a holiday).

**When supply is elastic:**

- An increase in demand is met mainly by a **rise in quantity**, with only a small price rise.
- **Producers** can respond to demand quickly, capture extra sales and revenue, and take market share from rivals.
- **Consumers** get the goods they want with little rise in price; prices are more stable.

**What producers can do to make supply more elastic:** keep spare capacity, hold stocks, use flexible staff (part-time and agency workers), train workers in several tasks, use flexible machinery, and develop reliable suppliers. These all cost money, so firms weigh the cost against the benefit of responding quickly.

::: terms
- **Price elasticity of supply (PES)**: the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price, measured as % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price.
- **Price elastic supply**: when quantity supplied changes by a larger percentage than price, so PES is greater than 1.
- **Price inelastic supply**: when quantity supplied changes by a smaller percentage than price, so PES is less than 1.
- **Spare capacity**: when a firm could produce more with its existing resources because some are not fully used.
- **Stocks**: goods that have been produced and stored, ready to be sold later.
- **Perishable goods**: goods that cannot be stored for long because they go off or lose their value.
- **Short run**: a period in which producers cannot change all their inputs, so supply is less elastic.
- **Long run**: a period long enough for producers to change all inputs, such as building new factories, so supply is more elastic.
- **Price volatility**: large and frequent changes in price, typical of markets with inelastic supply.
:::

::: method
1. **Write the formula**: PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP — quantity *supplied* on top.
2. **Calculate percentage changes from the original values**; if one percentage is given, calculate only the other.
3. **Divide and interpret**: greater than 1 = elastic, less than 1 = inelastic; PES is positive.
4. **Factor questions**: name a **supply-side** factor, say how it affects producers' ability to change output, and apply it to the product (e.g. potatoes need a growing season).
5. **Implication questions**: link PES to what happens to price and quantity when demand shifts; say who gains and who loses.
6. **Evaluate**: short run versus long run, perishable versus storable, the cost of keeping spare capacity.
:::

::: example Calculate — PES with a given price change
The price of potatoes rises by 40%. A farm's quantity supplied increases from 50 kg to 60 kg per day. Calculate the price elasticity of supply. (2)

% change in quantity supplied = (60 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = 20% (1). PES = 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 (1) — supply is price inelastic.
:::

::: example Explain — a factor affecting PES
Explain one factor affecting the price elasticity of supply of potatoes. (2)

Potatoes take several months to grow, so farmers cannot quickly plant and harvest more when the price rises (1). In the short run the quantity supplied can rise only a little, so supply is price inelastic (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — the new quantity
The PES for a factory's garden chairs is 2.2. The price rises by 5% and the factory currently supplies 3,000 chairs a month. Calculate the new quantity supplied. (2)

% change in quantity supplied = 2.2 × 5% = 11% (1). New quantity = 3,000 × 1.11 = 3,330 chairs (1).
:::

::: example Explain — implications for consumers
Explain why consumers pay much higher prices for a product with inelastic supply when demand for it rises. (2)

Producers cannot quickly increase output, so when demand rises there is excess demand that cannot be met by extra supply (1). The price is bid up a long way, so the rise in demand is met mainly by a higher price rather than a larger quantity (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — housing
Analyse why house prices in a city may rise sharply when demand for housing increases. (6)

The supply of housing is price inelastic, especially in the short run, because building new homes takes years — finding land, getting planning permission and construction (2). In cities, land is scarce and limited by planning rules, and the construction industry may not have enough skilled workers, so even in the long run supply cannot expand easily (2). When demand rises — through population growth, more jobs or lower interest rates — the demand curve shifts right along a steep supply curve, so the equilibrium price rises by a large amount while the number of homes rises only a little; buyers and renters pay much more, while existing owners gain (2).
:::

::: q
State the formula for price elasticity of supply. (1)
---
PES = percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price.
:::

::: q
The PES for a product is 0.4. Which of the following is correct? A — supply is price elastic; B — supply is price inelastic; C — demand is price inelastic; D — supply is unitary. (1)
---
B — supply is price inelastic.
:::

::: q
Invented data: the price of a scarf rises from £20 to £22 and quantity supplied rises from 1,000 to 1,250. Calculate the PES. (2)
---
%ΔP = 10%; %ΔQs = 25% (1). PES = 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5 (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the supply of fresh flowers is likely to be price inelastic. (2)
---
Flowers are perishable and cannot be stored (1), so growers cannot release stocks when the price rises and quantity supplied changes by a smaller percentage than price (1). (Or: growing time — developed.)
:::

::: q
Explain how spare capacity affects the PES of a product. (2)
---
If a firm has unused machines or workers (1), it can increase output quickly when the price rises, so supply is price elastic (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the implications for a toy manufacturer of having price inelastic supply in the weeks before a holiday season. (6)
---
Demand for toys rises sharply before the holiday (1), but if the factory is at full capacity it cannot increase output quickly (1), so it cannot meet all orders and loses potential sales to rivals (1). The shortage pushes the price up (1), so the firm earns more per toy on the units it does sell (1); to benefit more, it could build up stocks earlier in the year or hire temporary workers, making supply more elastic (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- A **PES** question needs **supply** factors — spare capacity, time, stocks, availability of inputs. Substitutes or brand loyalty (PED factors) score nothing.
- Quantity **supplied** on top, price on the bottom; use percentage changes from the original values.
- PES is **positive**; greater than 1 = elastic.
- When the price change is given as a percentage, you only need to calculate the **quantity** percentage change.
- Implications: inelastic supply means **demand shifts mainly change price**; elastic supply means they mainly change quantity.
:::

::: test
Explain why supply usually becomes more price elastic over time. (3)
---
In the short run firms are limited by their existing factories, machines and workers (1); over a longer period they can hire and train more workers, buy machinery and build new capacity, and new firms can enter (1), so a price rise leads to a larger percentage increase in quantity supplied (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one implication for consumers of price inelastic supply of concert tickets. (3)
---
The number of seats at a venue is fixed, so supply cannot increase when demand rises (1); for a popular act there is large excess demand at the face-value price (1), so many fans cannot get tickets or must pay much higher prices on resale sites (1).
:::

::: test
Explain two ways in which a manufacturer could make the supply of its product more price elastic. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Hold stocks: the firm stores finished goods in a warehouse (1), so when the price rises it can release stock immediately (1), raising quantity supplied quickly without waiting to produce more (1).
Keep spare capacity / flexible labour: it keeps some machines idle or uses part-time and agency workers (1), so it can run extra shifts when demand rises (1), increasing output by a larger percentage than the price rise (1).
Also creditworthy: multi-skilled workers; flexible machinery; reliable suppliers with short delivery times. Each has a cost (storage, idle machines) that could be noted.
:::

::: test
Assess whether the government can reduce rising rents in a city by making the supply of housing more price elastic. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Yes: rents rise sharply because housing supply is inelastic — demand increases are met mainly by higher prices; if supply became more elastic (faster planning permission, releasing public land, training more construction workers, allowing conversions of empty offices), more homes would be built when rents rise, so the rise in demand would be met more by quantity and less by price; rents would rise more slowly.
Limits: even with reforms, building takes years, so supply remains inelastic in the short run; land in cities is physically limited; construction costs and shortages of skilled workers limit how fast output can grow; developers may build expensive homes that do not reduce rents for low-income renters; local residents may oppose building; demand may keep rising faster than supply (population growth, low interest rates).
Conclusion: e.g. making supply more elastic is the right long-term approach because it tackles the cause of high rents, but it cannot help quickly; short-term support (e.g. housing benefit) may also be needed; success depends on how much land is available and how strongly demand grows.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis using PES with a justified conclusion considering time; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
