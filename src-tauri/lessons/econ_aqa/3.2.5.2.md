## What it is

The **financial sector** is the part of the economy made up of institutions that look after money, lend it, move it and help people manage risk. It links **savers**, who have money they do not need yet, with **borrowers** — households, firms and the government — who want to spend more than they have now. This topic covers the **main agents in the financial sector** (the **Bank of England**, **commercial banks** and **building societies**, plus other institutions), the **role of the Bank of England** in influencing interest rates and keeping the financial system stable, and the **role of high street banks** in serving savers and borrowers and helping to fund investment.

### The main agents in the financial sector

| Institution | What it is | Main roles |
|---|---|---|
| **The Bank of England** | the UK's **central bank**, owned by the state and independent in setting interest rates | sets Bank Rate; keeps the financial system stable; regulates banks; banker to the government and to the banks; issues banknotes (in England and Wales) |
| **Commercial (high street) banks** | public limited companies owned by **shareholders**, run to make a **profit** | accept deposits; lend to households and firms; provide payment services; help firms with finance |
| **Building societies** | **mutual** organisations owned by their **members** — the people who save with them and borrow mortgages from them — not by shareholders | specialise in **savings accounts** and **mortgages**; many also offer current accounts; profits are kept in the society or returned to members through better rates |
| Other institutions (supporting knowledge) | **insurance companies**, **pension funds**, the **stock market** (where shares are bought and sold), investment firms, credit unions | protect against risk; invest people's savings for retirement; let firms raise finance by selling shares |

The difference between a bank and a building society is mainly **ownership**: banks are owned by shareholders and pay them dividends; building societies are owned by their members. Because they do not have to pay shareholders, building societies can sometimes offer slightly better savings and mortgage rates.

### The role of the Bank of England

**1. Influencing interest rates.** The Bank of England's **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** sets **Bank Rate** to meet the government's **2% CPI inflation target** (3.2.3.2). Changes in Bank Rate pass through to the interest rates that high street banks and building societies charge borrowers and pay savers, and so influence spending, saving, borrowing and investment across the economy (3.2.1.1).

**2. Ensuring the stability of the financial system.** A failing bank can cause panic: if savers fear for their money, they all try to withdraw it at once (a **bank run**), which can bring down even a healthy bank and spread to others, cutting off lending to the whole economy. The Bank of England works to prevent this by:
- **regulating and supervising banks and building societies** — checking that they hold enough of their own capital and easily available funds to absorb losses, and that they do not take excessive risks;
- acting as **lender of last resort** — lending to banks that are short of cash in an emergency, so that a temporary shortage does not turn into collapse;
- watching for **risks to the whole system**, such as a rapid rise in risky lending or house prices, and acting to reduce them;
- making sure that savers' deposits are protected up to a limit by a **deposit guarantee scheme**, so that ordinary savers do not need to panic.

**3. Other roles.**
- **Banker to the government** — holds the government's accounts and helps manage its borrowing.
- **Banker to the banks** — commercial banks hold accounts at the Bank of England and use them to settle payments with each other.
- **Issuing banknotes** — it prints and issues banknotes in England and Wales (coins are made by the Royal Mint).
- It also manages the UK's reserves of foreign currency for the government.

What the Bank of England does **not** do: it does not offer current accounts, savings accounts or mortgages to ordinary households and firms. That is the job of the high street banks and building societies.

### The role of high street banks

| Role | How it works | Who benefits |
|---|---|---|
| **A safe place for savers** | households and firms deposit money in current and savings accounts; it is kept safe and, in savings accounts, earns **interest** | savers — security, interest, easy access |
| **Lending to borrowers** | banks lend to households (mortgages, personal loans, overdrafts, credit cards) and firms (business loans, overdrafts) and charge interest | households can buy homes and cars before saving the full price; firms can fund start-up costs, stock and expansion |
| **Funding investment** | banks **channel savings** from millions of savers to firms that want to invest in machinery, premises and new products — linking those with spare funds to those who can use them productively | firms; the economy, because investment raises productive capacity and growth |
| **Payment services** | debit cards, bank transfers, direct debits, standing orders, online and mobile banking | everyone — faster, safer payments without cash |
| **Other services** | currency exchange, advice, insurance, help with international trade | households and firms |

**How banks make a profit**: they pay savers a **lower** interest rate than they charge borrowers. The difference between the two rates is their **interest margin**. For example (invented figures), a bank paying savers 3% and charging borrowers 7% earns a margin of **4 percentage points**: on £100 million of loans funded by £100 million of deposits it receives £7 million in interest and pays out £3 million, leaving £4 million to cover its costs and make a profit. Banks also earn fees and charges.

Banks keep only a **small fraction** of deposits available as cash, because on most days only a few savers withdraw money. They lend out the rest. This is why confidence and regulation matter: if everyone tried to withdraw at once, no bank could pay them all immediately.

### Why the financial sector matters to the economy

- **Saving and investment**: it turns savings into investment, which raises productivity and growth.
- **Consumer spending**: loans and mortgages let households spread big costs over time.
- **Payments**: the economy depends on a fast, safe payment system.
- **Jobs and exports**: finance is a major UK industry, especially in London, and **financial services are among the UK's largest exports** (3.2.4.1).
- **Risks**: when the financial sector gets into trouble — as in the global financial crisis of 2007–08 — lending dries up, firms cannot borrow, and the whole economy can fall into recession. Governments may have to rescue banks with taxpayers' money. This is why the Bank of England's stability role is so important.

::: terms
- **Financial sector**: the banks, building societies, insurance companies and other institutions that provide financial services such as saving, lending and payments.
- **Central bank**: the bank that manages a country's currency, interest rates and financial stability — in the UK, the Bank of England.
- **Commercial bank**: a profit-making bank owned by shareholders that accepts deposits, makes loans and provides payment services.
- **Building society**: a mutual financial institution owned by its members that specialises in savings and mortgages.
- **Mutual organisation**: a business owned by its customers (members) rather than by shareholders.
- **Lender of last resort**: the role of the central bank in lending to banks that face a shortage of funds in an emergency.
- **Bank run**: when many savers try to withdraw their deposits at the same time because they fear the bank will fail.
- **Interest margin**: the difference between the interest rate a bank charges borrowers and the rate it pays savers.
- **Financial stability**: a situation in which banks and financial markets work smoothly and are able to withstand shocks.
- **Mortgage**: a long-term loan to buy property, secured on that property.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the agent** precisely: Bank of England (central bank), commercial/high street bank (shareholder-owned, profit), building society (mutual, member-owned, savings and mortgages).
2. **Match roles to the right agent**: interest rates, the 2% target, stability, lender of last resort, regulation, banknotes → **Bank of England**; deposits, loans, payment services → **high street banks and building societies**.
3. **Explain = role + why it matters**: e.g. channelling savings to firms → investment → productive capacity → growth.
4. **Calculations**: interest earned on savings (exclude the original sum), interest on loans, interest margin in percentage points, bank profit from lending minus interest paid.
5. **Stability**: explain the chain of a bank run and how regulation and lender-of-last-resort support prevent it.
6. **Evaluate** the importance of the sector: benefits (investment, payments, jobs, exports) versus risks (financial crises, over-lending, costs to taxpayers).
:::

::: example Calculate — a bank's interest margin
Invented data: Ashby Bank (fictional) has £600 million in savers' deposits, on which it pays 2.75% interest a year. It lends £600 million to borrowers at an average of 6.5%. Calculate the bank's interest margin and its net interest income for the year. (3)

Margin = 6.5 − 2.75 = **3.75 percentage points** (1). Interest received = 600m × 6.5% = £39 million; interest paid = 600m × 2.75% = £16.5 million (1). Net interest income = 39 − 16.5 = **£22.5 million** (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — banks and investment
Invented data — Table 1: Country K

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Bank lending to firms (£bn) | 120 | 96 |
| Business investment growth (%) | 3.2 | −1.8 |
| Small firms refused a bank loan (%) | 12 | 31 |

Using Table 1, analyse how the role of banks in funding investment affects the economy of Country K. (6)

Banks channel savers' deposits into loans that firms use to buy machinery and expand; in Year 2 bank lending to firms fell by £24bn (20%) (2). With less finance available — 31% of small firms were refused loans, up from 12% — firms had to cancel or delay projects, and investment growth fell from 3.2% to −1.8% (2). Lower investment reduces demand now, so output and jobs in industries making capital goods suffer, and reduces productive capacity in future, slowing long-run growth; small firms, which rely more on bank loans than large firms that can sell shares, are hit hardest (2).

::: watch
- **Keeping inflation at target is the Bank of England's job, not a high street bank's** — a common MCQ distractor.
- A **building society** is owned by its **members**, not shareholders; a **commercial bank** is owned by shareholders and aims for profit.
- The Bank of England does **not** offer accounts or mortgages to the public.
- "Ensuring stability" needs a mechanism — regulation, lender of last resort, deposit protection — and what it prevents (bank runs, lending drying up).
- In interest calculations, interest earned is **only the interest**, not the deposit plus interest; give margins in **percentage points**.
- Link banks to the wider economy: savings → loans → investment → growth.
