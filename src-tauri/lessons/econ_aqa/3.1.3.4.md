## What it is

Markets do not exist in isolation. A change in the price of coffee affects tea sellers; a fall in the price of games consoles affects game publishers; a boom in house building affects bricklayers. This topic covers **complementary** and **substitute** goods, and how changes in **demand, supply and price in one market** spread to **related markets**.

### Complements and substitutes

| | **Complements** (complementary goods) | **Substitutes** (substitute goods) |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | goods **used together**, so demand for one is linked to demand for the other | goods that are **alternatives** to each other, satisfying the same want |
| Examples | printers and ink cartridges; cars and petrol; games consoles and games; phones and phone cases; tennis rackets and tennis balls; fish and chips | tea and coffee; buses and trains; butter and margarine; different brands of cola; streaming services and cinema trips |
| If the price of one **rises**… | demand for the other **falls** (shifts left) | demand for the other **rises** (shifts right) |
| If the price of one **falls**… | demand for the other **rises** (shifts right) | demand for the other **falls** (shifts left) |

Why? A rise in the price of a good causes a **contraction** of demand for it (fewer bought). If fewer printers are bought, fewer ink cartridges are needed — demand for ink shifts left. If coffee becomes dearer, some coffee drinkers switch to tea — demand for tea shifts right.

**Strength of the link.** Close substitutes (two brands of the same bottled water) react strongly to each other's prices; weak substitutes (a cinema trip and a meal out) react only a little. Strong complements (a razor and its blades) are tightly linked; weak ones (bread and butter — many people eat bread without butter) less so.

### Chains between two markets

Always trace the chain **one step at a time**: what happens in market A → what happens to the *quantity* bought in market A → the effect on demand (or supply) in market B → the new price and quantity in market B.

**Example: a poor coffee harvest.**

1. Coffee market: supply shifts left → price of coffee **rises**, quantity of coffee bought **falls**.
2. Tea market (substitute): consumers switch to tea → demand for tea **shifts right** → price and quantity of tea **rise**.
3. Coffee-filter market (complement): fewer coffees made → demand for filters **shifts left** → price and quantity of filters **fall**.

Notice the different shifts: in the coffee market the **supply** curve moved; in the tea market the **demand** curve moved. The change in the related market comes through *consumers' choices*, so it is normally a **demand** shift.

### Does the cause in market A matter? Yes.

A higher price in market A has opposite effects on complements depending on **why** it rose:

- If the coffee price rose because **supply fell**, *fewer* coffees are bought → demand for complements (milk, filters, takeaway cups) **falls**.
- If the coffee price rose because **demand rose** (a new café culture), *more* coffees are bought despite the higher price → demand for complements **rises**.

So follow the **quantity** in market A, not just the price. This is the kind of reasoning that lifts an answer into the top level.

### Derived demand and supply-side links

- **Derived demand**: demand for a factor or input comes from demand for the final product. A rise in demand for new homes raises demand for bricks, timber, bricklayers and electricians. A fall in demand for petrol cars reduces demand for engine-parts workers. (This links product markets to factor markets — see 3.1.2.1 and the labour market in 3.1.5.4.)
- **Input prices and supply**: a change in the price of an input shifts **supply** in the market that uses it. Dearer cocoa raises chocolate makers' costs → supply of chocolate shifts left. Cheaper batteries → supply of electric cars shifts right → cheaper electric cars → demand for petrol cars (a substitute) falls and demand for home chargers (a complement) rises.

### Why intermarket relationships matter

- **Firms** watch rivals' prices (substitutes) and the prices of complements — a games publisher benefits when console prices fall; some firms sell the main product cheaply and profit on the complement (cheap printers, expensive ink).
- **Governments** must allow for knock-on effects: a tax on petrol may raise demand for public transport and electric cars; a subsidy for bus fares may reduce demand for car parking.
- **Workers** in related industries are affected by changes they cannot control.

::: terms
- **Complementary goods**: goods that are used together, so a fall in the price of one raises demand for the other.
- **Substitute goods**: goods that are alternatives to each other, so a rise in the price of one raises demand for the other.
- **Intermarket relationship**: a link between two markets through which a change in one affects demand or supply in the other.
- **Related market**: a market for a substitute, complement, input or product connected to the original market.
- **Derived demand**: demand for a factor or input that comes from demand for the final product it helps to make.
- **Close substitute**: an alternative that consumers regard as very similar, so they switch easily when relative prices change.
- **Knock-on effect**: the effect that a change in one market has on another market.
- **Input**: a resource or component used in producing a good, such as cocoa in chocolate.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the relationship**: substitute, complement, input or derived demand.
2. **Trace the chain step by step**: change in market A → price and **quantity** in A → effect on demand (or supply) in market B → shift direction → new price and quantity in B.
3. **Use the right curve in market B**: a consumer switch or a change in the quantity of a complement shifts **demand**; a change in an input's price shifts **supply**.
4. **Draw and label** the related market: axes, original curves, the correct shift, new equilibrium.
5. **Evaluate**: how close is the substitute or complement? How big was the change in A? Is the effect short-run or long-run?
:::

::: example Explain — a complement
Explain the likely effect of a fall in the price of games consoles on the market for video games. (2)

Lower console prices cause an extension of demand, so more people own consoles (1). Games are a complement, so demand for video games increases — the demand curve shifts right, raising their price and the quantity sold (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a substitute
Explain the likely effect of a rise in train fares on the market for coach travel. (2)

Coaches are a substitute for trains, so when train fares rise some passengers switch to coaches (1). Demand for coach travel shifts right, so the price and number of coach journeys rise (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — a related market
Invented data: when the price of a brand of printer fell from £80 to £60, sales of its ink cartridges rose from 40,000 to 46,000 a month at an unchanged price of £18. Calculate the percentage change in the printer's price and the change in monthly revenue from ink cartridges. (3)

Printer price change = (60 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = −25% (1). Ink revenue before = £18 × 40,000 = £720,000; after = £18 × 46,000 = £828,000 (1). Change = +£108,000 a month (1).
:::

::: example Draw and label — a related market
Draw and label the effect of a large rise in petrol prices on the market for electric bicycles. (3)

Model answer (described): axes labelled Price and Quantity, with D1 and S crossing at P1, Q1 for electric bicycles (1). Electric bicycles are a substitute for driving, so demand shifts right to D2 (1). New equilibrium at higher price P2 and higher quantity Q2 (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — knock-on effects of a poor harvest
Analyse the possible knock-on effects of a poor potato harvest on other markets. (6)

In the potato market, supply shifts left, so the price of potatoes rises and fewer are bought (1). Rice and pasta are substitutes, so some consumers switch to them; demand for rice and pasta shifts right, raising their prices and quantities sold (2). Crisp and chip makers use potatoes as an input, so their costs rise and the supply of crisps and frozen chips shifts left, raising prices and reducing quantities (2). Demand for farm workers to harvest potatoes, a derived demand, may fall because there is less to pick. The size of each effect depends on how bad the harvest is and how close the substitutes are (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how an increase in demand for new houses affects the market for bricklayers. (3)
---
Demand for bricklayers is a derived demand (1); more houses being built means builders need more bricklayers, so demand for bricklayers shifts right (1), raising their wages and the number employed (1).

::: q
Analyse the effect of a fall in the cost of producing batteries on the markets for electric cars and petrol cars. (6)
---
Batteries are an input to electric cars, so lower battery costs shift the supply of electric cars to the right (1), lowering their price and raising the quantity sold (1). Petrol cars are a substitute, so cheaper electric cars draw buyers away (1) and demand for petrol cars shifts left (1), lowering their price and quantity (1). Related markets follow: demand for home chargers (complement to electric cars) rises; demand for petrol falls (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains in both markets with correct curves; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- A rise in a good's price **raises** demand for its **substitute** but **lowers** demand for its **complement**.
- **Trace one step at a time** and name the curve that shifts in each market.
- In the related market it is usually **demand** that shifts — unless the link is through an input's cost (then **supply**).
- Follow the **quantity** in the first market: if its price rose because demand rose, demand for complements rises too.
- Give **examples** of complements and substitutes that are clearly linked — avoid weak pairs.
:::

Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- Electric-car price fell by £5,000 (14.7%); sales rose by 60,000 (40%) — extension of demand (possibly a supply increase from cheaper production).
- Home chargers are a complement: installations rose by 50,000 (45.5%) — demand for chargers shifted right as more people own electric cars.

::: test
Assess whether a fall in the price of games consoles will benefit firms that make video games. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Benefits: consoles and games are complements; cheaper consoles cause an extension of demand for consoles, so more households own one; demand for games shifts right, raising the price and quantity of games sold and the publishers' revenue and profits; a larger installed base of consoles brings long-term sales of new titles and in-game purchases.
Limits: why did the console price fall? If because demand for consoles collapsed (consumers moving to phone or PC gaming), fewer consoles are in use and demand for console games may fall; the benefit depends on how strongly the goods are linked — households may buy a console but few games, or download free-to-play games; competition among publishers may mean individual firms gain little; console makers may charge publishers higher licence fees to make up for lower console prices.
Conclusion: e.g. publishers are likely to benefit if the price cut is a strategy to sell more consoles (supply-side or pricing decision), because more consoles mean more potential buyers of games; but not if the fall reflects weak demand for consoles — the cause of the price change is decisive.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis of the intermarket chain with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some developed analysis, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
