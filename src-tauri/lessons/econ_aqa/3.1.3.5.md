## What it is

The law of demand says that when price rises, quantity demanded falls. But **by how much?** A 10% rise in the price of petrol barely changes how much people buy; a 10% rise in the price of one brand of crisps sends many shoppers to a rival brand. **Price elasticity of demand (PED)** measures this responsiveness. This topic covers why price changes do not always cause equivalent changes in demand, how to calculate PED, the difference between **price elastic** and **price inelastic** demand, the factors that affect PED, and its implications for **producers and consumers**.

### Measuring PED

$$\text{PED} = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

and every percentage change is calculated as **(new − old) ÷ old × 100**.

**Worked example** (invented): a cinema raises its ticket price from £10 to £12, and weekly ticket sales fall from 5,000 to 4,500.

- % change in price = (12 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = **+20%**
- % change in quantity demanded = (4,500 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **−10%**
- PED = −10 ÷ 20 = **−0.5**

Because price and quantity move in opposite directions, PED is **negative**. Read its **size**, ignoring the minus sign.

### Elastic and inelastic demand

| PED (ignoring the sign) | Name | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **greater than 1** | **price elastic** | quantity demanded changes by a **bigger** percentage than price | a particular brand of crisps, foreign holidays |
| **less than 1** | **price inelastic** | quantity demanded changes by a **smaller** percentage than price | petrol, electricity, salt, cigarettes |
| exactly 1 | unitary elasticity | quantity changes by the same percentage as price | — |
| 0 | perfectly inelastic | quantity does not change at all | (extension) a life-saving medicine with no alternative |

So −0.5 means inelastic: a 20% price rise cut sales by only 10%. A PED of −2.5 would mean elastic: each 1% price rise cuts sales by 2.5%.

On a diagram, an **inelastic** demand curve is **steep** (a big price change, a small quantity change) and an **elastic** demand curve is **flat** (a small price change, a big quantity change) — when drawn on the same scales.

### Rearranging the formula

If you know PED and the percentage change in price:

**% change in quantity demanded = PED × % change in price**

Invented example: PED for a gym's membership is −1.4. It cuts its price by 5%. % change in quantity = −1.4 × −5% = **+7%**. If it had 2,000 members, it would expect 2,000 × 1.07 = **2,140** members.

### Factors that affect PED

| Factor | More **elastic** when… | More **inelastic** when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Number and closeness of substitutes** | there are many close substitutes (one brand of cola) | there are few or none (tap water, petrol in the short run) |
| **Necessity or luxury** | the good is a luxury that can be done without | the good is a necessity (bread, heating, medicines) |
| **Proportion of income spent on it** | it takes a large share of income (cars, furniture) | it takes a tiny share (salt, matches, a pint of milk) |
| **Habit and addiction** | — | consumers are addicted or habitual buyers (cigarettes, coffee) |
| **Time period** | consumers have time to find alternatives (switching to an electric car over years) | in the short run, before habits or equipment can change |
| **Brand loyalty** | — | loyal customers keep buying despite price rises (a favourite phone brand) |
| **How broadly the good is defined** | narrowly defined (one supermarket's own-brand bread) | broadly defined (bread in general, food in general) |

The **substitutes** factor is the most important: it is the reason why the other factors work. Luxuries tend to be elastic because the money can be spent on many alternatives; brand loyalty makes demand inelastic because loyal buyers do not regard rivals as close substitutes.

### Implications for producers: pricing and total revenue

Total revenue = price × quantity. When price changes, revenue moves in the direction of whichever changes by the **larger percentage**.

| Demand is… | If the firm **raises** price, revenue… | If the firm **cuts** price, revenue… |
|---|---|---|
| **inelastic** | **rises** (quantity falls by a smaller %) | falls |
| **elastic** | falls | **rises** (quantity rises by a bigger %) |

From the cinema example: revenue before = £10 × 5,000 = £50,000; after = £12 × 4,500 = £54,000 — revenue **rose by £4,000** because demand is inelastic.

What this means for producers:

- **Pricing**: raise prices for inelastic products to increase revenue; cut prices for elastic products to win sales and revenue. (Profit also depends on costs — selling more units costs more to produce.)
- **Making demand less elastic**: advertising and branding build loyalty and reduce the number of goods consumers see as close substitutes, letting firms raise prices without losing many sales.
- **Passing on cost increases or taxes**: when demand is inelastic, a firm can pass higher costs (or an indirect tax) on to consumers through higher prices without losing many sales; when demand is elastic, the firm must absorb more of the cost itself.
- **Different prices for different customers**: firms charge more where demand is inelastic (peak-time train tickets for commuters) and less where it is elastic (off-peak tickets for leisure travellers, student discounts).

### Implications for consumers

- When demand is **inelastic**, consumers have little choice but to keep buying, so **price rises hit them hard** — a rise in energy or fuel prices takes a bigger share of income, especially for low-income households.
- When demand is **elastic**, consumers can **switch** to substitutes and protect themselves from price rises.
- The **government** knows this: it places taxes on goods with inelastic demand (fuel, tobacco, alcohol) because sales fall only a little, so tax revenue is high — but this means consumers, not producers, bear most of the tax. Taxes on inelastic goods do little to cut consumption, which matters if the aim is to reduce harm.

::: terms
- **Price elasticity of demand (PED)**: the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price, measured as % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.
- **Price elastic demand**: when quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than price, so PED is greater than 1 (ignoring the sign).
- **Price inelastic demand**: when quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than price, so PED is less than 1 (ignoring the sign).
- **Unitary elasticity**: when quantity demanded changes by exactly the same percentage as price, so PED equals 1.
- **Percentage change**: (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100.
- **Total revenue**: price multiplied by quantity sold; for consumers, the same amount is their total spending.
- **Substitute**: an alternative good that satisfies the same want — the main factor making demand elastic.
- **Brand loyalty**: consumers' tendency to keep buying a particular brand even when its price rises.
- **Necessity**: a good that consumers regard as essential, so demand for it tends to be price inelastic.
:::

::: method
1. **Write the formula** first, quantity on top: PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP.
2. **Calculate both percentage changes** from the **original** values — never divide raw changes in units by raw changes in pounds.
3. **Divide**, keep the sign, and round as asked (usually two decimal places).
4. **Interpret**: "−0.4, so demand is price inelastic because the value is less than 1 when the sign is ignored."
5. **Rearranged questions**: %ΔQd = PED × %ΔP; then apply the % to the original quantity to find the new quantity.
6. **Revenue questions**: work out P × Q before and after, then the change; link to elasticity.
7. **Factor questions**: name the factor, say whether it makes demand elastic or inelastic, explain why (usually through substitutes), apply to the product.
:::

::: example Calculate — PED
Invented data: a bakery raises the price of a sandwich from £4.00 to £4.40 and daily sales fall from 300 to 240. Calculate the price elasticity of demand. (3)

% change in price = (4.40 − 4.00) ÷ 4.00 × 100 = +10% (1). % change in quantity = (240 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = −20% (1). PED = −20 ÷ 10 = −2 (1) — demand is price elastic.
:::

::: example Calculate — the new quantity
The PED for a train company's off-peak tickets is −1.6. It cuts the price by 15%. It currently sells 8,500 off-peak tickets a day. Calculate the new number of tickets sold. (2)

% change in quantity = −1.6 × −15% = +24% (1). New quantity = 8,500 × 1.24 = 10,540 tickets (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a factor
Explain one factor that makes the demand for cigarettes price inelastic. (2)

Nicotine is addictive, so smokers feel they must keep buying cigarettes even when the price rises (1). As a result, quantity demanded falls by a much smaller percentage than the price rises (1).
:::

::: example Calculate and interpret — revenue
Invented data: a phone-case maker with price elastic demand (PED −1.8) is considering cutting its price from £15 to £12. It currently sells 2,000 cases a week. Calculate the change in total revenue. (3)

% change in price = (12 − 15) ÷ 15 × 100 = −20%; % change in quantity = −1.8 × −20% = +36%, so new quantity = 2,000 × 1.36 = 2,720 (1). Revenue before = £15 × 2,000 = £30,000; after = £12 × 2,720 = £32,640 (1). Revenue rises by £2,640, as expected when demand is elastic and price is cut (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — implications for a firm
Analyse the implications for a supermarket of the price elasticity of demand for its own-brand bread being elastic while demand for bread in general is inelastic. (6)

Bread in general is a necessity with no close substitute, so total demand for bread changes little when all prices rise — demand is inelastic (1). But each supermarket's own-brand loaf has many close substitutes — rival supermarkets' bread and branded loaves — so if this supermarket raised its price alone, shoppers would switch and its sales would fall by a larger percentage, cutting revenue (2). The supermarket should therefore avoid raising its bread price above rivals' and may even cut it, using cheap bread to attract shoppers who then buy other items (2). If costs rise for every baker at once, all prices rise together, and because demand for bread overall is inelastic, sales will fall only a little (1).
:::

State the formula for price elasticity of demand. (1)
---
PED = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price.
:::

::: q
The PED for a product is −0.3. Which of the following is correct? A — demand is price elastic; B — demand is price inelastic; C — the good is a complement; D — demand is unitary. (1)
---
B — demand is price inelastic.
:::

::: q
Invented data: the price of a magazine rises from £5.00 to £5.50 and weekly sales fall from 20,000 to 19,000. Calculate the PED. (2)
---
%ΔP = +10%; %ΔQ = −5% (1). PED = −5 ÷ 10 = −0.5 (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why demand for a luxury holiday is likely to be price elastic. (2)
---
It is a luxury / takes a large share of income, and there are many alternative holidays and destinations (substitutes) (1), so when its price rises many consumers choose a cheaper alternative and quantity demanded falls by a larger percentage (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a firm selling a product with price inelastic demand might raise its price. (2)
---
Quantity demanded falls by a smaller percentage than the price rises (1), so total revenue increases (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse why the government places high taxes on petrol. (6)
---
Demand for petrol is price inelastic because there are few substitutes for most drivers in the short run (1) and it is a necessity for travel to work (1). A tax raises the price, but quantity falls only slightly (1), so the government collects large tax revenue (1). Most of the tax is passed on to consumers because demand is inelastic (1); however, because consumption falls little, the tax is less effective at reducing pollution and it hits low-income drivers hardest (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains using PED; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Quantity on top, price on the bottom** — reversing the formula is the most common error.
- Use **percentage** changes, calculated from the **original** values — raw numbers score nothing.
- Interpret the **size** of PED: below 1 = inelastic, above 1 = elastic. −0.2 is inelastic, not "negative so falling".
- In rearranged questions, apply the % change to the **original** quantity to find the new one.
- Revenue follows **the bigger percentage change** — raise prices if inelastic, cut if elastic.
- Factor answers must say **why** the factor matters (through substitutes, necessity, habit), not just name it.
- Do not mix up PED with PES — a question on supply wants supply factors.
:::

::: test
Explain two reasons why a firm would want to know the price elasticity of demand for its product. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Pricing and revenue: if demand is inelastic, raising the price increases total revenue (1); if elastic, cutting the price increases revenue (1); knowing PED lets the firm choose the price change that raises revenue rather than reducing it (1).
Passing on cost increases: when costs (e.g. energy) or a tax rise (1), the firm can pass them on through higher prices without losing many sales if demand is inelastic (1); if demand is elastic it may have to absorb the cost and accept lower profit (1).
Also creditworthy: forecasting sales after a price change; setting different prices for different groups; deciding whether advertising to build brand loyalty is worthwhile.
:::

::: test
A theatre has seen its ticket sales fall. Assess whether it should raise its ticket prices to increase its revenue. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For raising prices: if demand is price inelastic — e.g. for a hit show with loyal audiences or no nearby alternative — quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises, so revenue rises; regular theatregoers may be loyal; the theatre may need more revenue per seat to cover fixed costs.
Against: sales are already falling, which suggests demand may be weak or elastic — there are many substitutes (cinema, streaming, other venues); a theatre ticket is a luxury taking a noticeable share of income; if demand is elastic, a price rise reduces revenue further; empty seats also cut spending on refreshments and programmes (complements).
Alternatives: price discrimination (higher prices at popular weekend shows, cheaper midweek and student tickets); better marketing; changing the programme.
Conclusion: e.g. raising prices across the board is risky because falling sales and many substitutes suggest elastic demand; a better approach is to raise prices only where demand is inelastic (premium seats, popular shows) and cut them where it is elastic. The decision depends on accurate PED estimates for each type of ticket.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced analysis using PED and revenue with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some developed analysis, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
