## What it is

**Monetary policy** is the use of **interest rates** — and, more broadly, the **supply of money and credit** — to influence the economy. In the UK it is run by the **Bank of England**, not by the government directly. This topic covers **what monetary policy is**, how it is used to meet the objective of **controlling inflation**, and how it can help — or hinder — the **other objectives** of growth, employment and the balance of payments. It builds on 3.2.1.1 (how interest rates affect saving, borrowing, spending and investment) and 3.2.2.4 (inflation).

### Who runs monetary policy in the UK

- The **government** sets the inflation target: **2% a year**, measured by the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**.
- The Bank of England's **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** — nine members — meets regularly through the year and votes on the level of **Bank Rate**, the interest rate the Bank of England pays on money that commercial banks hold with it.
- The Bank of England has been **independent** in setting interest rates since 1997. The idea is that decisions are made on economic evidence rather than political timing (a government might be tempted to cut rates before an election).

**Bank Rate** matters because it feeds through to the rates that high street banks and building societies charge on loans and mortgages and pay on savings. When Bank Rate rises, mortgage rates, loan rates and savings rates generally rise too.

### The main tools

| Tool | What it is | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Changing Bank Rate** | the MPC raises or lowers the base interest rate | the main tool — changes the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving across the economy |
| **Money supply and credit** | measures that change how much money and lending there is in the economy | more money/easier credit → more spending; less → less spending |
| **Quantitative easing (QE)** (extension only) | the Bank of England creates new money to buy financial assets such as government bonds, pushing longer-term interest rates down | used when Bank Rate is already very low and the economy still needs a boost |

At GCSE, focus on **interest rates**; QE is useful background but is not required.

### How monetary policy controls inflation

When inflation is **above** the 2% target — especially **demand-pull** inflation caused by too much spending — the MPC **raises Bank Rate**. This is **contractionary (tight) monetary policy**.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 | Bank Rate rises → banks raise their rates on loans, credit cards and mortgages, and on savings |
| 2 | **Borrowing** becomes more expensive → households borrow less for cars, holidays and home improvements; firms borrow less to **invest** |
| 3 | **Saving** becomes more rewarding → people save more and spend less of their income |
| 4 | Households with **variable-rate mortgages** pay more each month → less **disposable income** to spend on other things |
| 5 | **Exchange rate effect**: higher UK rates attract savings from abroad → demand for pounds rises → the pound **appreciates** → imports become cheaper and UK exports dearer |
| 6 | Total demand grows more slowly → firms find it harder to raise prices → **inflation falls** towards 2%; cheaper imports also lower costs and prices directly |

When inflation is **below** target and the economy is weak, the MPC **cuts Bank Rate** — **expansionary (loose) monetary policy** — reversing every step: borrowing is cheaper, saving less attractive, mortgage payments fall, spending and investment rise.

### How monetary policy affects the other objectives

| Objective | Lower interest rates | Higher interest rates |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic growth** | cheaper borrowing → more consumer spending and business investment → output rises; investment also adds to future capacity | spending and investment fall → growth slows, possibly a recession |
| **Employment** | firms producing more need more workers → unemployment falls | lower demand → firms cut jobs → unemployment rises |
| **Price stability** | risk of rising demand-pull inflation if demand outstrips capacity | inflation falls |
| **Balance of payments** | pound tends to **depreciate** → exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer → current account may improve (although higher incomes also suck in imports) | pound tends to **appreciate** → exports dearer, imports cheaper → current account may worsen |
| **Distribution of income** | borrowers gain; savers (often older people) lose interest income | savers gain; borrowers, especially those with large mortgages, lose |

So monetary policy creates a clear **conflict**: raising rates to cut inflation tends to **slow growth and raise unemployment** in the short run. The MPC has to judge how far to go.

### Limits of monetary policy

- **Time lags** — a change in Bank Rate is thought to take **many months, often a year or more**, to have its full effect on inflation, because mortgage deals are often fixed for several years and firms' investment plans take time to change. The MPC must act on **forecasts**, which can be wrong.
- **Cost-push inflation** — if inflation comes from higher energy, food or import prices, higher rates do little to the cause; they mainly reduce demand and jobs. Monetary policy works best on **demand-pull** inflation.
- **Confidence** — in a deep downturn, firms and households may not borrow even when rates are very low, because they are worried about the future. Rates cannot usefully fall much below zero.
- **A blunt tool** — one Bank Rate applies to the whole UK, even if some regions or industries are booming and others are struggling.
- **Uneven effects** — the burden falls heavily on borrowers (mortgage holders, firms with loans), while savers gain.
- **Global factors** — UK inflation is also driven by world prices and exchange rates, which the MPC cannot control.

### Monetary or fiscal?

| | Monetary policy | Fiscal policy (3.2.3.1) |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Bank of England (MPC) | Government (the Treasury) |
| Tools | interest rates, money supply | government spending, taxation |
| Speed of decision | can change at any MPC meeting | usually at an annual Budget |
| Main target | 2% CPI inflation | growth, employment, redistribution, public services |

::: terms
- **Monetary policy**: the use of interest rates and the supply of money and credit to influence the level of economic activity.
- **Bank Rate**: the base interest rate set by the Bank of England, which influences the rates charged and paid by other banks.
- **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**: the committee at the Bank of England that votes on Bank Rate to meet the inflation target.
- **Inflation target**: the rate of inflation the government asks the Bank of England to achieve — 2% CPI in the UK.
- **Contractionary monetary policy**: raising interest rates (or reducing money supply) to reduce spending and inflation.
- **Expansionary monetary policy**: lowering interest rates (or increasing money supply) to boost spending, growth and jobs.
- **Variable-rate mortgage**: a home loan whose interest rate changes when Bank Rate changes.
- **Appreciation**: a rise in the value of a currency against other currencies.
- **Quantitative easing**: the central bank creating new money to buy financial assets, to lower long-term interest rates and boost spending.
- **Time lag**: the delay between a policy change and its full effect on the economy.
:::

::: method
1. **Define it properly**: interest rates (and money supply/credit), set by the **Bank of England's MPC** — not the government, and not taxes or spending.
2. **Direction first**: inflation too high → raise rates; economy weak/inflation below target → cut rates.
3. **Chain the effect**: rate change → cost of borrowing / reward for saving / mortgage payments / exchange rate → spending and investment → demand → prices, output and jobs. Each link earns marks.
4. **Name the group**: borrowers, savers, mortgage holders, firms with loans, exporters — say how each is affected.
5. **Use the data**: compare inflation with the 2% target in percentage points; show rate changes in percentage points.
6. **Evaluate**: time lags, cost-push vs demand-pull, confidence, conflicts with growth/unemployment, uneven effects; then make a judgement.
:::

::: example Analyse — using a table of rates
Invented data — Table 1

| | Q1 | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bank Rate (%) | 1.0 | 2.0 | 3.0 | 3.5 |
| CPI inflation (%) | 7.0 | 7.6 | 7.2 | 6.5 |
| Business investment growth (%) | 2.1 | 1.4 | 0.2 | −0.6 |

Using Table 1, analyse how the rise in Bank Rate may have affected the economy. (6)

Bank Rate rose by 2.5 percentage points over the year; this raised the cost of borrowing, so firms cut back on investment — investment growth fell from 2.1% to −0.6% (2). Higher rates also raise mortgage payments and reward saving, so consumer spending slows; lower demand reduces firms' ability to raise prices, and inflation fell from a peak of 7.6% to 6.5% (2). But inflation is still far above 2%, showing the **time lag** before rate rises take full effect — and falling investment means slower growth now and lower productive capacity later, a conflict between price stability and growth (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why the Bank of England, rather than the government, sets interest rates in the UK. (2)
---
The Bank of England is independent so decisions are based on economic evidence about meeting the 2% target (1) rather than political pressure, e.g. a government cutting rates before an election, which could lead to higher inflation (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way in which a rise in UK interest rates could affect the exchange rate and inflation. (2)
---
Higher UK rates attract savings/investment from abroad, increasing demand for pounds so the pound appreciates (1); imports become cheaper in pounds, reducing firms' costs and the price of imported goods, so inflation falls (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why monetary policy may be ineffective in reducing cost-push inflation. (2)
---
Cost-push inflation is caused by rising costs such as energy or imported raw material prices (1); higher interest rates reduce demand but do not lower these costs, so prices may keep rising while output and jobs fall (1).
:::

Invented data: Bank Rate is raised from 3.25% to 4.00%. By how many percentage points has it risen, and by what percentage? Give the percentage to one decimal place. (2)

0.75 percentage points (1); 0.75 ÷ 3.25 × 100 = 23.1% (1).

::: q
Analyse how a cut in interest rates could help to reduce unemployment. (6)
---
Indicative content: cheaper borrowing (1) encourages consumers to borrow for cars, furniture and home improvements (1); lower mortgage payments raise disposable income (1); saving is less rewarding, so households spend more (1); firms invest more as loans are cheaper and demand is rising (1); higher demand means firms increase output and need more workers, so cyclical unemployment falls — though the effect is delayed by time lags and weak if confidence is low (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Monetary = interest rates and money supply, set by the Bank of England**. Tax and spending changes are fiscal policy — mixing them up loses the mark immediately.
- Get the **direction** right: to **cut** inflation, rates go **up**.
- The target is **2% CPI** set by the government; the MPC meets it — the MPC does not set the target.
- Write changes in rates as **percentage points**, not per cent (3% to 4% is a 1 percentage point rise but a 33.3% increase).
- Don't stop at "people spend less" — say **why** (borrowing dearer, saving more rewarding, mortgage payments higher) and finish on the **objective**.
- Higher rates make the pound **stronger**, which makes imports **cheaper** — this lowers inflation (a common MCQ trap).
- Evaluation: time lags, cost-push vs demand-pull, conflict with growth and jobs, winners and losers. Judgements need to rest on these, not on "it depends".
:::

::: test
Invented data — Item B: In Country N, the central bank has a 2% inflation target. Inflation is 1.0% and real GDP growth is 0.3%. Unemployment has risen from 4.8% to 5.6% in a year. Bank Rate is 2.5%. Household debt is high, and a survey shows that business confidence has fallen sharply.

Using Item B, analyse whether the central bank should cut Bank Rate. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- Inflation (1.0%) is 1 percentage point below target, so there is room to cut without risking inflation.
- Growth of 0.3% is weak and unemployment has risen 0.8 percentage points — a cut lowers the cost of borrowing, encouraging spending and investment, raising output and jobs.
- Lower mortgage payments raise disposable income; a weaker currency may help exporters.
- But business confidence has fallen sharply — firms may not invest even if borrowing is cheaper; high household debt may make people reluctant to borrow more; time lags mean the effect is delayed; Bank Rate at 2.5% leaves limited room for repeated cuts.
- Judgement: a cut seems justified because both inflation and growth point the same way, but it may need help from fiscal policy.
Levels: 5–6 — developed, two-sided analysis using the Item; 3–4 — some development and use of data; 1–2 — isolated points.

::: test
Invented data — Figure 1: In Country H inflation has risen from 2.2% to 8.5% in eighteen months, mainly because world oil and gas prices have doubled. Unemployment is low at 3.8%. Wages are rising by around 6% a year as workers try to keep up with prices. The central bank is considering raising Bank Rate from 1% to 4%.

Using Figure 1, assess whether raising interest rates is the best way to reduce inflation in Country H. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For: inflation of 8.5% is 6.5 points above a 2% target; higher rates reduce borrowing and spending, cutting demand; with unemployment low at 3.8% and wages rising 6%, there is a risk of a wage-price spiral, which higher rates can help stop by cooling the labour market; a stronger currency makes imported energy cheaper; acting firmly keeps expectations of future inflation down.
Against: the main cause is cost-push (oil and gas prices doubled), which higher rates cannot reverse; a 3-point rise is large — mortgage holders and firms with loans are hit hard, growth slows and unemployment rises; time lags mean the effect comes after energy prices may have fallen anyway; alternatives — temporary support with energy bills, supply-side measures to cut energy dependence.
Conclusion: e.g. some rise in rates is justified to stop high inflation feeding into wages and expectations, but rates alone cannot fix inflation caused by world energy prices, so a smaller, gradual rise is better than a rapid 3-point jump.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis using the figure, justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported conclusion; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Item A — Rates on hold? (invented)

Country E's central bank must keep CPI inflation at 2%. Over the last two years it raised its main interest rate from 0.5% to 5%. Inflation has fallen from 9% to 3.4%, but the economy has stalled: real GDP grew by just 0.1% last year and unemployment has risen to 5.2%. Many households with mortgages are coming to the end of cheap fixed-rate deals and will soon face much higher payments. Savers' groups say that higher rates have finally rewarded people who put money aside. Some economists warn that cutting rates too soon could let inflation rise again.

| | Two years ago | Last year | This year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interest rate (%) | 0.5 | 3.0 | 5.0 |
| CPI inflation (%) | 9.0 | 6.1 | 3.4 |
| Real GDP growth (%) | 2.8 | 0.9 | 0.1 |
| Unemployment (%) | 3.9 | 4.3 | 5.2 |

Do you think Country E's central bank should now cut interest rates? Use Item A and your own economic knowledge to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 5, AO3 10. Indicative content:
Use of data: rates up 4.5 percentage points; inflation down 5.6 points but still 1.4 points above target; growth down from 2.8% to 0.1%; unemployment up 1.3 points.
For cutting: the economy is close to recession — lower rates make borrowing cheaper, raising consumption and investment, output and jobs; rising unemployment (5.2%) harms families and costs the government benefits and lost tax; time lags mean earlier rises are still feeding through, so inflation may keep falling even after a cut — waiting risks overshooting into recession; mortgage holders coming off fixed deals face a large rise in payments, cutting spending further; lower rates weaken the currency, helping exporters.
Against cutting: inflation is still above the 2% target — cutting too soon could let it rise again and damage the credibility of the target; low expectations of inflation are hard won; savers have gained after years of low rates; if remaining inflation is cost-push, cuts won't harm it much but neither will holding rates; the stall may be partly due to other factors (weak world demand), which rate cuts won't fix.
Evaluation: depends on whether inflation is still falling and on forecasts; size and timing — a small, gradual cut is different from a return to 0.5%; groups affected differ (borrowers vs savers); fiscal policy could support growth instead.
Conclusion: a supported view, e.g. a small cut is justified now, because inflation is close to target and still falling while unemployment is rising, so the risk of recession now outweighs the risk of inflation returning — but further cuts should depend on inflation continuing to fall.
Levels: 13–15 — sustained, balanced evaluation using the Item's data, developed chains on both sides, fully supported judgement; 10–12 — well-developed analysis with good use of the Item and a reasoned judgement, some imbalance; 7–9 — some developed analysis of both sides, some use of the Item, partly supported judgement; 4–6 — limited, mainly one-sided or descriptive, little data; 1–3 — isolated points, assertion.
