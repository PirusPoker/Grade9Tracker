## What it is

A street with six coffee shops, a town with twenty hairdressers, an online marketplace with hundreds of phone-case sellers: these are **competitive markets**. This topic covers what a competitive market is, **how producers operate** in one, the impact of competition on **price and choice**, its economic impact on **consumers, producers and workers**, and **why profits are likely to be lower** in a competitive market than in one dominated by a small number of producers.

### What is a competitive market?

A **competitive market** is one in which many firms compete to sell similar products, so no single firm can control the market price. Its main characteristics:

| Characteristic | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Many producers** | lots of firms, each with a small market share |
| **Similar products** | little product differentiation, so buyers see the firms' products as close substitutes |
| **Low barriers to entry and exit** | new firms can start up cheaply and easily; firms can leave without huge losses |
| **Good information** | consumers can easily compare prices and quality (price-comparison websites, reviews, shops side by side) |
| **Firms are price takers** | if one firm charges more than its rivals, customers switch; the firm has to accept roughly the market price |

The more strongly a market shows these features, the more competitive it is. In reality, few markets are *perfectly* competitive, but many are close — fruit and vegetable stalls, taxis in a big city, plumbers, cafés, takeaways.

### How producers operate in a competitive market

Because customers can switch easily, firms must work hard to win and keep them:

- **Keep costs low** — efficient methods, cheap suppliers, controlling waste, so they can charge low prices and still cover their costs.
- **Compete on price** — offers, discounts, loyalty schemes, matching rivals' prices.
- **Compete on quality and service** — friendlier staff, faster delivery, longer opening hours, better reviews.
- **Innovate and differentiate where they can** — a new menu item, a unique design, a local brand — to make their product stand out and reduce price competition a little.
- **React quickly** to changes in tastes, because a rival will if they do not.

### The impact on price and choice

Competition generally means **lower prices** — because firms undercut each other and new firms enter whenever profits look attractive — and **more choice** — because many firms offer different varieties, locations and levels of service.

### The economic impact of competition

| Group | Benefits of competition | Drawbacks of competition |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | lower prices; more choice; better quality and service; new products as firms innovate | firms cutting costs may cut corners on quality or safety; some small firms close, so a favourite local supplier may disappear; too much choice can be confusing |
| **Producers** | pressure to become efficient; rewards for good firms that innovate; easy to start a business | low profit margins; constant pressure on prices; risk of making losses and closing; little money left over to invest or research |
| **Workers** | firms compete for good staff, so skilled, productive workers can be rewarded; many employers mean more choice of job and easier to move | pressure on firms to keep wages low to keep costs down; job insecurity, as firms may close or cut staff; pressure to work harder or more flexibly (e.g. zero-hours contracts) |

Notice that the same feature can help one group and harm another: low prices help consumers but squeeze producers' profits and workers' wages.

### Why profits are lower in a competitive market

Profits are likely to be **lower** in a competitive market than in a market dominated by a few large producers, because:

1. **Firms cannot raise prices** — if one firm charges more than its rivals, customers switch to the many close substitutes, so price is kept close to cost.
2. **Low barriers to entry** — if firms earn high profits, new firms are attracted in; supply increases and the price falls, competing the extra profit away (the diagram above).
3. **Spending to compete** — firms must spend on offers, advertising and service just to keep their customers, which adds to costs.
4. **Small scale** — each firm is small, so it cannot gain the economies of scale that lower the costs of large dominant firms.

In a market dominated by a few producers, by contrast, high barriers stop new firms entering, and the large firms have the power to set higher prices, so high profits can last.

::: terms
- **Competitive market**: a market with many firms selling similar products, where no single firm can control the price.
- **Price taker**: a firm that has to accept the market price because customers would switch if it charged more.
- **New entrant**: a firm that starts selling in a market for the first time.
- **Barriers to entry**: obstacles that make it difficult or costly for new firms to enter a market.
- **Profit margin**: the profit made on each unit sold, as an amount or a percentage of the price.
- **Innovation**: developing new products or new ways of producing and selling them.
- **Non-price competition**: competing through quality, service, branding or features rather than lower prices.
- **Consumer choice**: the range of products, sellers and options available to buyers.
- **Efficiency**: producing output at the lowest possible cost without wasting resources.
:::

::: method
1. **Characteristics**: many firms, similar products, low barriers to entry and exit, good information, price takers — use different features for each point.
2. **How producers operate**: keep costs low, compete on price, quality and service, innovate or differentiate.
3. **Impact questions**: check which group the question names — consumers, producers or workers — and answer only about that group.
4. **Why profits are lower**: customers switch to rivals, so price stays close to cost; and high profits attract new entrants, increasing supply and lowering price.
5. **Diagrams**: new entrants shift **supply** right → equilibrium price falls, quantity rises; label axes, original and new curves, and both equilibria.
6. **Explain two / assess**: develop each chain to its effect on the group, then weigh the benefits against the drawbacks before judging.
:::

::: example Explain — impact on consumer choice
Explain one impact of a competitive market on consumer choice. (2)

There are many firms each trying to win customers by offering something slightly different — a different location, menu or service (1), so consumers can pick the product that best suits their tastes and budget, and switch if they are unhappy (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why profits are lower
Explain why profits are likely to be lower in a competitive market than in one dominated by a few firms. (2)

Barriers to entry are low, so if firms earn high profits new firms enter the market (1); supply increases and the price falls, competing the profit away, whereas a dominant firm protected by barriers can keep prices high (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — the effect of competition on revenue
Invented data: a market trader sells 400 phone cases a week at £8. After two rival stalls open, she cuts her price to £6.50 and sells 440 a week. Calculate the change in her weekly revenue. (2)

Before: 400 × £8 = £3,200; after: 440 × £6.50 = £2,860 (1). Revenue falls by £340 a week (1).
:::

::: example Explain — impact on workers
Explain one way competition between firms could harm workers. (2)

Firms competing on price must keep their costs low, and wages are a large cost (1), so they may hold down pay, cut hours or use insecure contracts, leaving workers with lower or less reliable incomes (1).
:::

::: example Explain two — new entrants and consumers
Explain two ways new firms entering a market can affect consumers. (6)

Lower prices: more firms increase supply in the market (1), and each new firm tries to win customers by undercutting existing firms (1), so the market price falls and consumers can buy more for the same money (1).
More choice and quality: new entrants often bring new products, styles or services to stand out (1); existing firms must improve their quality and service to keep customers (1), so consumers get a wider range of better products — although, if too many firms enter and some close, the choice may later shrink (1).
:::

::: q
Which of the following is a characteristic of a competitive market? A — high barriers to entry; B — a small number of very large firms; C — many firms selling similar products; D — firms that are price makers. (1)
---
C — many firms selling similar products.
:::

::: q
State two ways in which a competitive market differs from a non-competitive market. (2)
---
Any two different features (1 each): many firms rather than few/one; low rather than high barriers to entry; similar rather than differentiated/unique products; firms are price takers rather than price makers; lower rather than higher profits; good information/easy switching.
:::

::: q
Explain how producers may operate in a competitive market. (2)
---
Because consumers can easily switch to rivals, producers keep their costs as low as possible (1) so that they can match or undercut rivals' prices and still make a profit (1). (Or: compete on quality/service; innovate; offers and loyalty schemes — developed.)
:::

::: q
Invented data: in a competitive market for takeaway coffee in a town, the average price falls from £3.20 to £2.80 after new cafés open. Calculate the percentage change in the average price. (2)
---
(2.80 − 3.20) ÷ 3.20 × 100 (1) = −12.5%, a fall of 12.5% (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit of competition for producers. (2)
---
Competition forces producers to look for cheaper, better ways of working (1), so efficient firms with lower costs survive and grow, and the firm stays able to compete in the long run (1). (Or: easy to enter and start a business — developed.)
:::

::: q
Analyse the impact of increased competition on workers in an industry. (6)
---
Indicative content: firms compete on price, so they try to cut costs (1); wages are a major cost, so pay may be held down or hours cut (1); less efficient firms close, causing job losses and insecurity (1). But firms also compete for skilled, productive workers (1), so good workers may be offered higher pay or training (1); more employers mean workers have more choice of job and can move to better ones (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains, ideally both sides; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- Answer about the **group named** — a question about workers answered with consumer benefits scores nothing (the June 2025 report noted answers drifting to the wrong group).
- In a competitive market barriers to entry are **low**; do not call them high.
- "Many firms" and "not few firms" are one point — a "state two" answer needs two **different** features.
- New firms entering shift the **supply** curve right, not demand.
- "Profits are lower" needs a reason: switching to rivals and/or new entrants increasing supply.
- Competition is not all good: assess answers should include drawbacks such as low wages, closures and cost-cutting on quality.
:::

::: test
Explain two ways in which competition can affect producers. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Lower profits: customers switch to cheaper rivals (1), so a firm cannot raise its price above the market level (1); its profit margin is small and it may make losses and close (1).
Pressure to be efficient: to survive, firms must cut waste and costs (1), e.g. by finding cheaper suppliers or new technology (1); efficient firms can then grow and win market share from rivals (1).

| Average delivery fee (£) | 3.50 | 2.10 |

::: test
Explain one way a firm in a competitive market could try to increase its profit. (2)
---
It could cut its average costs, e.g. by finding cheaper suppliers or using its staff more efficiently (1), because it cannot raise its price without losing customers to rivals, so lower costs are the main way to widen its margin (1). (Or: differentiate its product to build loyalty — developed.)
:::

::: test
Assess whether consumers always benefit from competitive markets. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Benefit: lower prices as firms undercut rivals and new firms enter; more choice of products and sellers; firms must improve quality and service to keep customers; innovation; good information helps consumers find the best deal.
Not always: cost-cutting may reduce quality or safety; small competitive firms cannot gain economies of scale, so prices may be higher than a large efficient firm could charge; low profits mean little money for research and new products; many firms closing creates uncertainty (e.g. lost deposits, poor after-sales service); too much choice can confuse consumers; workers (who are also consumers) may face lower pay.
Conclusion: e.g. consumers usually benefit, especially on price and choice, but not always — in industries with high fixed costs or a need for heavy research, larger firms may serve consumers better. It depends on the type of product and on whether regulation protects quality.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
