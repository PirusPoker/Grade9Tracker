## What it is

**Fiscal policy** is the use of **government spending** and **taxation** to influence the economy. In the UK, the Chancellor of the Exchequer sets out the main tax and spending plans in the **Budget**. This topic covers how fiscal policy changes the **level of income and spending** in the economy, how it can be used to hit the **government's objectives** (3.2.2.1), and what is meant by a **balanced budget**, a **budget deficit** and a **budget surplus** — including what happens when a government runs each one.

### The two tools

| Tool | Examples | Links |
|---|---|---|
| **Government spending** | the NHS, schools, defence, roads and railways, police, state pensions and benefits, public-sector wages | 3.2.1.2 covers the main areas of spending |
| **Taxation** | **direct taxes** on income and wealth (income tax, National Insurance, corporation tax); **indirect taxes** on spending (VAT, excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco) | 3.2.1.2 covers direct/indirect and progressive/regressive taxes |

Fiscal policy is set by the **government** (the Treasury). Do not confuse it with **monetary policy** (3.2.3.2), which is about interest rates and is set by the **Bank of England**.

### How fiscal policy affects income and spending

Spending in the economy comes from households (consumption), firms (investment), the government, and foreigners (exports minus imports). Fiscal policy changes this total directly and indirectly.

**Expansionary (or "loose") fiscal policy** — the government **spends more and/or taxes less**:
- higher government spending on, say, new hospitals creates orders for construction firms and jobs for builders, nurses and suppliers;
- a cut in **income tax** raises households' **disposable income**, so they spend more in shops, restaurants and on holidays;
- a cut in **corporation tax** leaves firms with more profit after tax to invest in new machinery;
- a cut in **VAT** lowers prices, so the same income buys more.

The extra spending becomes someone else's income. The builder who is hired spends his wages at the local garage, whose owner then spends more, and so on — so the final rise in total income is **bigger than the first injection** (this knock-on is often called the **multiplier effect**). Output rises, firms need more workers, and unemployment falls.

**Contractionary (or "tight") fiscal policy** — the government **spends less and/or taxes more**. Disposable income falls, households and firms spend less, total demand grows more slowly or falls, and this reduces inflationary pressure — but it can also slow growth and raise unemployment.

| | Expansionary | Contractionary |
|---|---|---|
| Government spending | ↑ | ↓ |
| Taxes | ↓ | ↑ |
| Total spending (demand) | ↑ | ↓ |
| Output and jobs | ↑ | ↓ (or grow more slowly) |
| Inflation | may rise | falls |
| Budget balance | moves towards deficit | moves towards surplus |

### Using fiscal policy to achieve objectives

| Objective | Fiscal policy that could help | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic growth** | more spending on infrastructure, education; lower taxes | raises demand now; better roads, broadband and skills also raise **productive capacity** in the long run |
| **Full employment** | higher spending, tax cuts, spending targeted at regions with high unemployment | more demand → firms produce more → need more workers |
| **Price stability** | higher taxes, lower spending | less disposable income → less spending → less **demand-pull** inflation; a cut in VAT can lower measured prices briefly |
| **Balance of payments** | higher taxes to cut spending on imports | lower incomes → fewer imports; but also fewer domestic sales |
| **Reducing inequality** | progressive income tax; benefits, free public services | takes a larger share from high earners and transfers income to the poorest (3.2.2.6) |
| **Environment** | taxes on pollution (fuel duty, landfill tax); subsidies for green energy | changes the relative prices of polluting and clean goods (3.2.3.4) |

### Limits of fiscal policy

- **Time lags** — new schools or railways take years to plan and build, so the boost may arrive after the problem has passed.
- **Conflicts** — expansionary policy that cuts unemployment may cause inflation and widen the current account deficit (more spending on imports).
- **Borrowing and debt** — tax cuts and spending rises usually increase the deficit, so the government must borrow more and pay more interest.
- **Leakages** — some of a tax cut is saved or spent on imports rather than on UK output, so the effect on UK jobs is smaller than hoped.
- **Incentives** — very high tax rates may reduce the incentive to work, invest or start a business (see 3.2.3.3).
- **Political choices** — tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular, so governments may delay them.

### The government budget

The **government budget** compares the government's **revenue** (mainly taxes) with its **spending** over a year.

| Position | Meaning | Calculation result |
|---|---|---|
| **Balanced budget** | spending = revenue | revenue − spending = 0 |
| **Budget deficit** | spending > revenue — the gap is filled by **borrowing** (e.g. selling government bonds) | negative |
| **Budget surplus** | revenue > spending — the extra can be used to **repay debt** | positive |

The **national debt** is the **total** the government owes — the build-up of all past deficits minus surpluses. A deficit is a flow over **one year**; the debt is a **stock** at a point in time. Every year of deficit adds to the debt. The UK has run a budget deficit in most years in recent decades.

**Budget balance as a percentage of GDP** — because a £50 billion deficit means very different things for a large and a small economy, the balance is usually given relative to GDP:

> budget balance as % of GDP = (revenue − spending) ÷ GDP × 100

Worked example (invented data): revenue £920 billion, spending £1,010 billion, GDP £2,600 billion.
- Balance = 920 − 1,010 = **−£90 billion** (a deficit)
- As % of GDP = 90 ÷ 2,600 × 100 = 3.4615… = **3.5% of GDP** (to 1 decimal place)

Divide by **GDP**, not by spending or revenue, and round only at the end.

### Consequences of a deficit and a surplus

| | **Budget deficit** | **Budget surplus** |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on the economy now | injects more spending than it withdraws — supports demand, growth and jobs (useful in a recession) | withdraws more in tax than it spends — reduces demand, helping to control inflation |
| Debt | adds to the national debt | can be used to pay debt down |
| Interest | more debt means more **debt interest** — money that cannot be spent on schools or hospitals; if lenders lose confidence, the interest the government must pay can rise | less debt means lower interest payments in future, freeing money for services or tax cuts |
| Future generations | may face higher taxes or spending cuts to repay the debt — a question of fairness between generations | inherit less debt |
| Other risks | **crowding out**: heavy government borrowing may push up interest rates and reduce private investment; may add to inflation if the economy is near full capacity | taxes are higher or services poorer than they could be; may slow growth or deepen a downturn; public services and investment may be squeezed |
| When it may be sensible | in a **recession**, or to fund investment (e.g. infrastructure) that raises future growth and tax revenue | in a boom, when demand is strong and inflation is rising; to rebuild room for borrowing in the next downturn |

A deficit is not automatically "bad": borrowing to build a railway that lasts fifty years is different from borrowing to pay day-to-day costs. What matters is its **size** (as a % of GDP), **how long** it lasts and **what it pays for**.

Deficits also change automatically with the economy: in a recession tax revenue falls (fewer people working, lower profits, less spending) while benefit spending rises, so the deficit widens even without any new policy. In a boom the reverse happens.

::: terms
- **Fiscal policy**: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the level of economic activity.
- **Expansionary fiscal policy**: higher government spending and/or lower taxes, which increase total demand in the economy.
- **Contractionary fiscal policy**: lower government spending and/or higher taxes, which reduce total demand in the economy.
- **Government budget**: the comparison of the government's planned revenue with its planned spending over a year.
- **Balanced budget**: when government spending equals government revenue over the year.
- **Budget deficit**: when government spending is greater than revenue in a year, so the gap must be borrowed.
- **Budget surplus**: when government revenue is greater than spending in a year.
- **National debt**: the total amount the government owes, built up from past budget deficits.
- **Disposable income**: income left after direct taxes have been paid and benefits received, available to spend or save.
- **Multiplier effect**: the knock-on rise in total income when an injection of spending becomes other people's income and is spent again.
- **Crowding out**: when heavy government borrowing raises interest rates and reduces private sector investment.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the tool precisely**: say which tax or which area of spending changes, and in which direction. Fiscal = government spending and taxes; interest rates are monetary policy.
2. **Build the chain**: policy → disposable income / orders for firms → spending (demand) → output → jobs / prices. Each arrow is a mark in "explain" and "analyse" answers.
3. **Link to the objective named in the question** — growth, unemployment, inflation, inequality — and finish the chain on that objective.
4. **Budget calculations**: balance = revenue − spending; negative = deficit. As % of GDP: divide by GDP, × 100, round to the places asked, and say "deficit" or "surplus".
5. **Deficit vs debt**: a deficit is one year's gap; the debt is the total owed.
6. **Evaluate**: time lags, extra borrowing and interest, imports, inflation if near capacity, incentives, and whether the problem is demand-side or supply-side.
:::

::: example State — the tools of fiscal policy
State two instruments of fiscal policy. (2)

Any two (1 each): government spending; direct taxes such as income tax/corporation tax; indirect taxes such as VAT/excise duty; benefits and transfer payments.
:::

::: example Calculate — budget balance as a percentage of GDP
Invented data: in Country T, government revenue is £410 billion, government spending is £452 billion and GDP is £1,750 billion. Calculate the budget balance as a percentage of GDP. Give your answer to one decimal place. (2)

Balance = 410 − 452 = −£42 billion, i.e. a deficit of £42 billion (1). 42 ÷ 1,750 × 100 = 2.4, so a **deficit of 2.4% of GDP** (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a consequence of a surplus
Explain one consequence of a government operating a budget surplus rather than a budget deficit. (2)

With a surplus the government does not need to borrow and can use the extra revenue to repay part of the national debt (1), so its future interest payments fall, leaving more money for public services or tax cuts later (1). (Or: taxes are higher/spending lower than they would otherwise be, so demand and growth may be slower.)
:::

::: example Explain — fiscal policy and unemployment
Explain how a reduction in income tax could reduce unemployment. (2)

A cut in income tax raises households' disposable income, so they spend more on goods and services (1); firms see higher demand, increase output and need more workers, so unemployment falls (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — government spending and unemployment
Invented data — Table 1: Country P

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployment rate (%) | 7.8 | 7.9 |
| Government spending (£bn) | 300 | 340 |
| Budget deficit (% of GDP) | 2.0 | 4.1 |

The government raised spending in Year 2 to build new railways and hire more teachers. Using Table 1, analyse how an increase in government spending might reduce unemployment. (6)

Spending rose by £40 billion (13.3%), which creates jobs **directly** — construction workers on the railways and new teachers are hired (2). Those workers spend their wages in local shops and businesses, so demand rises in other industries too, which then take on staff — the multiplier effect means total income rises by more than £40 billion (2). Unemployment has not yet fallen (7.8% to 7.9%), probably because of **time lags**: railways take years to plan before hiring begins; and the deficit has more than doubled to 4.1% of GDP, so the extra borrowing may be hard to sustain if jobs do not follow (2).
:::

::: q
Explain one way in which fiscal policy could be used to reduce inflation. (2)
---
The government could raise income tax / cut its own spending (1), which reduces disposable income/total demand, so firms are less able to raise prices and demand-pull inflation falls (1).

::: q
Analyse the possible effects of a large cut in corporation tax on the economy. (6)
---
Indicative content: firms keep more of their profit after tax (1), so they have more funds and more incentive to invest in machinery, technology and new premises (1); investment adds to demand now, creating jobs in industries that build and supply capital goods (1), and raises productive capacity and productivity later, supporting long-run growth (1); the UK may attract more foreign firms (1); but tax revenue falls, widening the budget deficit unless spending is cut, and firms may pay out profits as dividends rather than invest (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Fiscal is tax and government spending**; interest-rate answers belong to monetary policy and score nothing here.
- The budget balance is **revenue minus spending**. Divide by **GDP** — not by spending — and round to the decimal places asked (examiners saw both mistakes).
- Always say **deficit or surplus** after a budget calculation; a bare number loses the mark.
- Don't confuse the **budget deficit** (government finances) with a **current account deficit** (trade with the rest of the world).
- Deficit = one year; debt = the total owed. They are not the same.
- In "explain how" answers, go beyond "the government spends more" — show who gets the money, what they do with it and how that reaches the objective.
- In evaluation, use time lags, the cost of extra borrowing, leakages into imports and the risk of inflation rather than "it costs a lot".
:::
