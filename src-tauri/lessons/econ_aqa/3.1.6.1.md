## What it is

The price mechanism usually moves scarce resources towards the goods consumers want most (3.1.2.1). But sometimes markets get it wrong: they produce **too much** of some goods, **too little** of others, or leave some people unable to get what they need. This topic covers **market failure as a misallocation of resources**, the **costs** of that misallocation, and the **methods of government intervention** used to correct it — together with how to **evaluate** whether intervention works. It is regular territory for the 9-mark and 15-mark questions on Paper 1.

### The meaning of market failure

**Market failure is the inability of the market system to allocate resources efficiently.** Left to itself, the market produces the "wrong" quantity of some goods and services from society's point of view:

- **too much** of goods that harm others or the user — e.g. pollution-heavy production, sugary drinks, single-use plastics;
- **too little** of goods that benefit society — e.g. vaccinations, education, training, public transport;
- **none at all** of some goods that people cannot be charged for individually (e.g. street lighting, flood defences, national defence) — no private firm can make a profit from them;
- goods priced too high, or rationed unfairly, because a firm has **monopoly power** (3.1.5.3).

### Why markets misallocate resources

| Source of failure | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Negative externalities** (3.1.6.2) | buyers and sellers ignore costs that fall on third parties, so the good is **over-produced/over-consumed** | a factory's smoke, traffic congestion |
| **Positive externalities** | buyers ignore benefits to others, so the good is **under-consumed** | vaccinations protect others; education raises everyone's productivity |
| **Imperfect information** | consumers do not know the true costs or benefits | underestimating the health risks of smoking or the benefits of a pension |
| **Goods the market will not provide** | no one can be excluded from using them, so people would not pay | street lights, lighthouses |
| **Monopoly power** | dominant firms restrict output and raise prices | a firm with a patent on a vital medicine |
| **Inequality** | the market rations by ability to pay, so the poor may go without essentials | private healthcare only for those who can afford it |

### The costs of a misallocation of resources

When resources are misallocated, society is worse off than it could be:

- **Harm to third parties** — health damage from air pollution, noise for local residents, congestion delays for other road users.
- **Waste of scarce resources** — producing things whose true cost to society is greater than their benefit; resources could have been used elsewhere (opportunity cost).
- **Under-provision of beneficial goods** — too few people vaccinated, too little training, so society's health and productivity are lower than they could be.
- **Costs to government** — the NHS treats illnesses linked to smoking, alcohol and obesity; councils clear litter; these costs are paid from taxation.
- **Environmental damage** — climate change, loss of habitats, depletion of resources, much of it falling on future generations.
- **Inequality of access and exploitation** — high prices and poor service when a monopolist faces no competition; essentials that the poorest cannot afford.

### Methods of government intervention

| Method | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Indirect taxes** | a tax on the good raises firms' costs, so the supply curve shifts **left**; price rises and the quantity bought falls; raises revenue | tobacco, alcohol, fuel, sugary drinks, landfill |
| **Subsidies** | a payment to producers lowers their costs, so supply shifts **right**; price falls and quantity rises | public transport, renewable energy, apprenticeships |
| **Regulation and legislation** | laws that ban, limit or set standards | smoking bans in workplaces, emission limits, age limits on sales, minimum standards for food safety |
| **Fines and penalties** | punishment for breaking the rules, which raises the cost of harmful behaviour | fines for littering, fly-tipping or polluting rivers |
| **State provision** | the government provides the good itself, free or subsidised, funded by taxes | state schools, NHS healthcare, street lighting, flood defences |
| **Information provision** | campaigns, labelling and education so consumers make better choices | health warnings on cigarette packs, food traffic-light labels, anti-drink-driving adverts |
| *Tradable pollution permits* (extension) | firms receive permits to pollute up to a limit; cleaner firms can sell spare permits | carbon emissions schemes |
| **Competition policy** | investigating mergers, fining firms that abuse dominance, capping prices | regulated water and energy prices |

### Evaluating government intervention

Intervention does not always work, and it has costs of its own:

- **Price elasticity of demand** (3.1.3.5) — a tax on a good with **price inelastic** demand (cigarettes, fuel) raises lots of revenue but cuts consumption only a little; regulation may work better.
- **Regressive effects** — taxes on goods such as tobacco or fuel take a bigger share of poorer people's income.
- **Cost and opportunity cost** — subsidies and state provision must be paid for from taxes or borrowing; the money cannot be spent elsewhere.
- **Enforcement** — bans and regulations need inspectors and police; they may encourage **illegal markets** (e.g. smuggled cigarettes).
- **Valuing the harm** — it is hard to know how big a tax or subsidy should be.
- **Information campaigns** are cheap and respect choice, but people may ignore them, and changes in behaviour take time.
- **Government failure** — intervention can make things worse: unintended consequences, poor information, political pressure, red tape that raises firms' costs.

The best method depends on the good: how harmful it is, how price elastic demand is, how easy rules are to enforce, and who bears the cost.

::: terms
- **Market failure**: the inability of the market system to allocate resources efficiently.
- **Misallocation of resources**: when the market produces too much or too little of a good compared with what is best for society.
- **Government intervention**: action by the government to change the outcome of a market.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending on goods and services, which raises producers' costs and the price.
- **Subsidy**: a payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs and encourages output.
- **Regulation**: rules or laws that control the behaviour of producers or consumers, such as bans and standards.
- **State provision**: the government supplying a good or service directly, funded by taxation.
- **Information provision**: government campaigns and labelling that help consumers make better-informed choices.
- **Imperfect information**: when buyers or sellers do not know the true costs or benefits of a decision.
- **Government failure**: when government intervention leads to a worse allocation of resources than before.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely**: market failure = the market fails to allocate resources efficiently — too much, too little or none of a good.
2. **Identify the failure**: over-consumption of a harmful good, under-consumption of a beneficial one, monopoly power, imperfect information.
3. **Costs of misallocation**: name who bears them — third parties, the government (e.g. the NHS), the environment, future generations.
4. **Methods**: give the tool and how it changes behaviour — tax → supply left → price up → quantity down; subsidy → supply right → price down → quantity up.
5. **Evaluate the tool**: PED, regressive effects, cost to government, enforcement, illegal markets, government failure — and compare with an alternative tool.
6. **9- and 15-markers**: use the figure or Item, argue both sides, and finish with a judgement on which method is best *for this good*. Keep it microeconomic — macro policies (interest rates, fiscal stimulus) do not correct market failure.
:::

::: example Define — market failure
Define the term "market failure". (2)

When the market system fails to allocate resources efficiently (1), so that too much or too little of a good or service is produced or consumed (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a cost of misallocation
Explain one cost of the over-consumption of alcohol. (2)

Excessive drinking leads to illness and accidents that must be treated by the NHS (1), so the government spends tax revenue on treatment that could have been used for other services — an opportunity cost to society (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — the effect of a tax on price
Invented data: a soft drink costs £1.20 before a tax of 24p per bottle is introduced. Producers pass on two-thirds of the tax to consumers. Calculate the new price and the percentage rise in price. (3)

Two-thirds of 24p = 16p passed on (1). New price = £1.20 + £0.16 = £1.36 (1). Percentage rise = 0.16 ÷ 1.20 × 100 = 13.3% (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a subsidy
Explain how a subsidy could correct the under-use of public transport. (2)

A subsidy paid to bus companies lowers their costs, so supply shifts right and fares fall (1); cheaper fares encourage more people to use buses instead of cars, raising use towards the level that benefits society (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — choosing a method
Invented data: in a town, litter in parks costs the council £400,000 a year to clear; 60% of it is takeaway packaging. Analyse how the council could use fines and information campaigns to reduce litter. (6)

Fines raise the cost of dropping litter for the individual, so people weigh a possible penalty against the small convenience of dropping it; if enforcement officers are visible in parks, the expected cost is high enough to change behaviour and the council's £400,000 clean-up bill falls (2). However, fines need officers to catch offenders, which costs money, and many litterers will not be caught, so the deterrent may be weak (2). An information campaign — signs, school visits and work with takeaways, which account for 60% of the litter — is cheaper and could change habits in the long term, especially if takeaways print reminders on packaging; but people may ignore messages, so combining both methods may cut litter more than either alone (2).
:::

::: q
Which of the following is an example of market failure? A — the price of strawberries falls in summer; B — a firm makes a loss and closes; C — a factory's pollution damages the health of local residents; D — consumers switch from tea to coffee. (1)
---
C — pollution harming local residents (a negative externality).
:::

::: q
State two methods of government intervention to correct market failure. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): indirect taxes; subsidies; regulation/legislation/bans; fines; state provision; information provision/campaigns; tradable permits; competition policy/price caps.
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why the market may under-provide a good such as street lighting. (2)
---
Once lights are installed, everyone in the street benefits whether or not they pay (1), so people would not pay voluntarily and private firms cannot make a profit, so the market provides too little or none (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: a tax raises the price of a pack of cigarettes from £12.00 to £13.20, and the quantity bought falls from 10,000 to 9,600 packs a week in a town. Calculate the price elasticity of demand. (3)
---
% change in price = 1.20 ÷ 12 × 100 = +10% (1); % change in quantity = −400 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = −4% (1); PED = −4 ÷ 10 = −0.4 (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of using an indirect tax to reduce consumption of a harmful good. (2)
---
If demand is price inelastic, for example because the good is addictive (1), the price rise cuts consumption only slightly, so the harm continues and consumers simply pay more (1). (Or: regressive; encourages smuggling — developed.)
:::

::: q
Analyse how state provision of education corrects a misallocation of resources. (6)
---
Indicative content: education benefits society as well as the student — a skilled workforce, higher productivity and tax revenue (1); if people had to pay, many would buy too little because they ignore the wider benefits or cannot afford it (1), so education would be under-consumed (1). The government provides free schools funded by taxes (1), so every child receives education regardless of income, moving consumption towards the level that benefits society (1); the cost is the opportunity cost of the tax revenue and possible inefficiency without competition (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- Market failure is about the **allocation of resources** (too much or too little of a good) — not a firm going bust or prices rising.
- A tax shifts **supply left** (costs rise); a subsidy shifts **supply right**. Do not shift demand for either.
- Evaluate the tool, not just describe it: a tax on a price **inelastic** good raises revenue but changes behaviour very little.
- Keep market-failure answers **microeconomic** — the June 2025 examiners saw answers drift into interest rates and macro policies, which earn nothing.
- Mention who bears the costs of misallocation (third parties, the NHS, the environment).
- 9- and 15-markers need a **judgement** on which method works best for the specific good, not a list of methods.
:::

::: test
Assess whether an indirect tax is the best way to reduce the number of short car journeys in towns. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For a tax (e.g. on fuel or a local charge): raises the cost of driving, so some drivers switch to walking, cycling or buses; drivers pay for the congestion and pollution they cause; raises revenue that could fund alternatives; leaves people free to choose.
Against: demand for car travel is often price inelastic (habit, lack of alternatives), so journeys fall only slightly; fuel taxes hit all journeys, including essential rural ones; regressive — a bigger burden on lower-income drivers; alternatives may be better: subsidised buses, safe cycle lanes (positive alternatives), regulation (car-free zones near schools), information campaigns about health benefits of walking.
Conclusion: e.g. a tax works only if good alternatives exist; combining a modest charge with subsidised public transport and better cycle routes is likely to be more effective and fairer than a tax alone. It depends on PED and on the availability of substitutes.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::

Sales of high-caffeine energy drinks in Country Z have doubled in five years. Doctors report that many teenagers drink several cans a day, which is linked to poor sleep, anxiety and tooth decay; health services in Country Z estimate the related treatment costs at £120 million a year. A can costs about £1.50, and producers spend heavily on advertising aimed at young people. The government is considering three options: (1) a tax of 50p per can; (2) a ban on selling energy drinks to under-16s; (3) a national information campaign in schools costing £15 million a year.

| | Five years ago | Today |
|---|---|---|
| Cans sold per year (millions) | 300 | 600 |
| Share of sales to under-18s (%) | 15 | 30 |
| Estimated price elasticity of demand among teenagers | — | −0.4 |

Do you think government intervention is the best way to correct the misallocation of resources in the market for energy drinks in Country Z? Use Item A and your own economic knowledge to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 5, AO3 10. Indicative content:
Use of data: sales doubled (+100%); under-18 sales rose from 45m to 180m cans (four-fold); a 50p tax is a 33% rise on £1.50, so with PED −0.4 teen consumption would fall by only about 13%; the £120m treatment cost is a cost to society; the campaign costs £15m a year.
Why there is misallocation: over-consumption — teenagers and their parents may have imperfect information about the health effects; heavy advertising aimed at the young; health costs fall on the health service (third parties/taxpayers); the market ignores these costs.
For intervention: the market will not correct itself while advertising drives demand; a ban on under-16 sales targets the group most at risk directly and does not rely on price; a tax raises revenue (e.g. 50p on hundreds of millions of cans) that could fund the campaign and treatment; information addresses the root cause of imperfect information and may change habits for life.

Conclusion: a supported view, e.g. intervention is justified because the market is clearly over-supplying energy drinks to teenagers and creating large costs for others, but the most effective intervention is regulation (age ban) supported by information rather than the tax alone.
