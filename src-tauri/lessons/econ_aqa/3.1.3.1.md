## What it is

Demand is half of the most important diagram in GCSE Economics. This topic covers what demand means, the factors that influence it, how to **construct an individual demand curve from consumer data**, and the difference between a **movement along** the demand curve and a **shift** of it.

### What demand means

**Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price in a given time period.** Each part of the definition matters:

- **willing and able** — wanting a sports car is not demand unless you can pay for it (this is called *effective* demand);
- **at a given price** — demand is always linked to a particular price;
- **in a given time period** — "300 coffees" means nothing until you say per day, per week or per year.

**Individual demand** is one consumer's demand; **market demand** is the total of all consumers' demand at each price — add up every individual's quantity at each price.

### The law of demand and the demand curve

As the price of a good **rises, the quantity demanded falls**; as the price falls, the quantity demanded rises (other things being equal). Reasons:

- at a lower price, the good is **more affordable** — consumers' income stretches further;
- at a lower price, the good becomes **better value compared with substitutes**, so people switch towards it;
- new buyers who could not afford it before now enter the market.

So the demand curve **slopes downwards from left to right**, with **price on the vertical axis** and **quantity on the horizontal axis**.

### Factors that influence demand

**The good's own price** causes a **movement along** the curve. **Every other factor** changes how much is demanded *at every price*, so it **shifts** the whole curve.

| Non-price factor | Shifts demand **right** (increase) when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Income** | incomes rise (for most goods) | higher wages → more meals out and holidays |
| **Price of a substitute** | the substitute's price **rises** | dearer coffee → more demand for tea |
| **Price of a complement** | the complement's price **falls** | cheaper games consoles → more demand for games |
| **Tastes and fashion** | the good becomes more fashionable or popular | a health trend → more demand for running shoes |
| **Advertising** | successful advertising or promotion | an influencer campaign for a snack brand |
| **Population** | the number of potential buyers grows, or its age structure changes | more older people → more demand for care homes |
| **Seasons and weather** | the time of year or weather favours the good | hot weather → more demand for ice cream |
| **Expectations** | consumers expect the price to rise soon | buying fuel before an announced price rise |
| **Interest rates and credit** | borrowing becomes cheaper or easier | lower interest rates → more demand for cars and houses bought on credit |

The opposite change shifts demand **left** (a decrease): falling incomes, a cheaper substitute, a dearer complement, a health scare, an ageing population for products bought by the young.

(Strictly, for a few **inferior** goods — such as the cheapest own-brand products — demand falls when incomes rise, as people trade up. You do not need this term, but it can earn credit if used correctly.)

| | **Movement along** the curve | **Shift** of the curve |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | a change in the good's **own price** | a change in **any other factor** |
| Words to use | **extension** (price falls, more demanded) / **contraction** (price rises, less demanded) | **increase** in demand (shift right) / **decrease** in demand (shift left) |
| On the diagram | from one point to another on the **same** curve | a **new** curve, D1 to D2 |
| At the same price | — | more (or less) is demanded than before |

Precise vocabulary earns marks: "demand increased" means the curve shifted right; "quantity demanded increased" (an extension) means price fell and we moved along the curve.

::: terms
- **Demand**: the quantity of a good or service consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price in a given time period.
- **Effective demand**: a want for a good that is backed by the ability to pay for it.
- **Individual demand**: the quantity one consumer is willing and able to buy at each price.
- **Market demand**: the total quantity all consumers are willing and able to buy at each price.
- **Demand curve**: a graph showing the quantity demanded at each price, sloping downwards from left to right.
- **Extension of demand**: a rise in quantity demanded caused by a fall in the good's own price — a movement down the curve.
- **Contraction of demand**: a fall in quantity demanded caused by a rise in the good's own price — a movement up the curve.
- **Increase in demand**: a rightward shift of the demand curve caused by a change in a non-price factor.
- **Decrease in demand**: a leftward shift of the demand curve caused by a change in a non-price factor.
- **Non-price factors**: influences on demand other than the good's own price, such as income, tastes and related goods' prices.
:::

::: method
1. **Ask: is it the good's own price?** Yes → movement along (extension/contraction). No → shift (increase/decrease).
2. **Name the factor and the direction**: "incomes rise, so consumers can afford more at every price — demand shifts right".
3. **Plotting**: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal, both labelled with units; even scales from zero; plot points accurately; label the curve D.
4. **Draw and label a shift**: axes labelled (P, Q); original curve D1 and new curve D2 in the right direction; show price and quantities if asked.
5. **Explain one reason for a shift** (2 marks): factor (1) + why it changes how much consumers are willing and able to buy at each price (1).
6. **Analyse/assess**: link several factors to the context and judge which has the biggest effect.
:::

::: example Explain — a rightward shift
Explain one reason why the demand curve for electric bicycles might shift to the right. (2)

Petrol prices could rise, making driving more expensive (1), so more commuters are willing to buy an electric bicycle as a cheaper substitute at every price, increasing demand (1).
:::

::: example Identify — movement or shift
A cinema cuts its ticket price from £12 to £8 on Tuesdays, and more tickets are sold. State whether this is a movement along or a shift of the demand curve, and explain why. (2)

It is a movement along the demand curve — an extension of demand (1) — because it is caused by a change in the price of the cinema tickets themselves, not a non-price factor (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — market demand from individual demand
Invented data: at a price of £2 a slice, Ali demands 3 slices of cake a week, Beth 1 and Chen 4. At £1.50 a slice, Ali demands 4, Beth 2 and Chen 6. Calculate the change in market demand (for these three consumers) when the price falls from £2 to £1.50, and state the percentage change. (3)

Market demand at £2 = 3 + 1 + 4 = 8 slices; at £1.50 = 4 + 2 + 6 = 12 slices (1). Change = +4 slices (1). Percentage change = 4 ÷ 8 × 100 = 50% (1).
:::

::: example Draw and label — a decrease in demand
Draw and label a diagram to show the effect on the demand for sugary drinks of a successful government health campaign. (3)

Model answer (described): vertical axis labelled Price, horizontal axis labelled Quantity (1). An original downward-sloping demand curve D1 (1). A new demand curve D2 drawn to the **left** of D1, showing that at every price fewer sugary drinks are demanded, with Q2 less than Q1 at the same price (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — several factors
Using the data, analyse why demand for gym memberships in an invented town may have increased: average incomes rose by 4%; a new housing estate added 3,000 residents; a national campaign promoted exercise; the price of home fitness equipment rose by 15%. (6)

Higher incomes mean households can afford more non-essential services, so at every membership price more people are willing and able to join — demand shifts right (2). The new estate increases the number of potential customers in the town, so population growth adds to demand, and the exercise campaign makes fitness more fashionable, changing tastes in favour of gyms (2). Home fitness equipment is a substitute; its 15% price rise makes training at home dearer, so some consumers switch to the gym. Of these, the substitute's large price rise and the campaign may have the biggest effects, because a 4% income rise is modest (2).
:::

---
Any two (1 each): higher incomes; advertising; a change in tastes; a rise in the price of substitutes (e.g. staycations); a fall in the price of complements (e.g. flights); lower interest rates/easier credit; population growth.

::: q
Explain the difference between an increase in demand and an extension of demand. (2)
---
An increase in demand is a shift of the whole curve to the right, caused by a non-price factor such as higher income (1); an extension is a movement down the existing curve, caused by a fall in the good's own price (1).
:::

Invented data — Jake's weekly demand for bus journeys: £3.00 → 4; £2.50 → 6; £2.00 → 8; £1.50 → 11. Describe how you would draw his demand curve, and state the quantity demanded at £2.50. (3)

::: q
Analyse how an ageing population could affect the demand for different goods and services. (6)
---
More older people means demand for products they buy rises: care homes, healthcare, mobility aids, cruises (1), so these demand curves shift right (1) and producers in those markets may raise prices and expand (1). Products mainly bought by young people — e.g. school uniforms, nightclubs — may face lower demand as their share of the population falls (1), shifting demand left (1); firms may need to change products or advertising to target older customers (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Only the good's own price causes a movement**; every other factor shifts the curve.
- State the **direction** of the shift — "demand changes" scores nothing.
- Use the right words: extension/contraction for movements; increase/decrease for shifts.
- Substitutes and complements move **opposite ways**: a dearer substitute raises demand; a dearer complement lowers it.
- Demand needs **willingness and ability** to pay, at a given price, in a given time period — a definition missing the time period may lose a mark.
- Diagrams: **price on the vertical axis**, curves and axes labelled.
:::

---
