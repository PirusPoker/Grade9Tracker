## What it is

**International trade** is the buying and selling of goods and services across national borders. **Exports** are goods and services produced in one country and sold to buyers abroad; **imports** are goods and services bought from abroad. This topic covers **why trade matters** to economies, the **main types of goods and services the UK exports and imports**, the **advantages of trade**, and the **consequences of global interdependence** — the fact that the UK economy now depends heavily on what happens in other countries.

### Why countries trade

No country can efficiently produce everything its people want. Countries trade because:

| Reason | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Access to goods and resources a country cannot produce** | some raw materials, foods and fuels exist only in certain places or need a particular climate | the UK cannot grow bananas, coffee or cocoa commercially; it has little of some metals and minerals |
| **Some goods are produced more cheaply elsewhere** | other countries may have cheaper labour, better land or climate, or more experience in an industry | clothing and electronics assembled where labour costs are lower; fruit grown in warmer climates |
| **Specialisation** | each country concentrates on what it produces relatively well and trades for the rest — just as workers specialise (3.1.2.3), countries can too; total world output rises | the UK specialises in financial, business and creative services; other countries specialise in manufacturing or farming |
| **Larger markets for firms** | exporting lets firms sell to millions more customers | a UK games studio or university selling worldwide |
| **Economies of scale** | selling to a bigger market lets firms produce on a larger scale, lowering average costs (3.1.4.3) | an aircraft-engine maker spreading huge research costs over world sales |
| **Competition** | imports compete with domestic firms, pushing them to cut costs, improve quality and innovate | UK car makers competing with imported cars |
| **More choice for consumers** | consumers can buy varieties and brands from all over the world, all year round | fresh strawberries in winter; foreign cars, films and holidays |

### The UK's exports and imports

Keep the details general — the exact figures change every year — but know the pattern:

| | Main types |
|---|---|
| **UK exports of services** | **financial services** (banking, insurance), **business and professional services** (law, accounting, consultancy), **education** (overseas students at UK universities), **tourism** (foreign visitors spending in the UK), **creative industries** (film, music, games, publishing), telecoms and computer services |
| **UK exports of goods** | cars and other vehicles, machinery and engines (including aircraft engines and parts), **pharmaceuticals/medicines**, chemicals, precious metals, crude oil and fuels, food and drink such as whisky |
| **UK imports of goods** | machinery and electrical goods, cars, **fuels** (oil and gas), **food**, clothing and footwear, consumer electronics, raw materials and components for UK manufacturers |
| **UK imports of services** | travel and tourism (UK residents' holidays abroad), transport, business services bought from abroad |

Key points to remember:
- **Services are the UK's biggest export**, making up roughly half of all UK exports. The UK usually runs a **surplus in trade in services** and a **deficit in trade in goods** (3.2.2.5).
- The UK **imports a large share of its food** and much of its energy, which makes it sensitive to world food and energy prices.
- The **EU** taken together is the UK's largest trading partner, and the **United States** is its largest single-country partner; China is also a major source of imports.
- A tourist visiting the UK is a UK **export** of a service (money flows in); a UK family's holiday abroad is an **import** (money flows out).

### The advantages of trade

| For | Advantages |
|---|---|
| **Consumers** | **lower prices** (goods made where it is cheapest; more competition); **more choice and variety**; access to goods the UK cannot produce at all; better quality as firms compete |
| **Producers (firms)** | **bigger markets** and more sales; **economies of scale**; access to cheaper or better **raw materials and components**; new ideas and technology from abroad |
| **Workers** | jobs in **export industries** (e.g. finance, cars, aerospace, universities); often relatively well-paid jobs |
| **The economy as a whole** | higher output and **economic growth** through specialisation; more efficient use of resources; tax revenue from exporting firms; access to foreign investment |

### Global interdependence and its consequences for the UK

**Global interdependence** means that countries rely on each other for goods, services, markets, finance and investment. Because of trade, an event in one country quickly affects others. For the UK this has consequences, both good and bad:

| Consequence | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Exposure to shocks abroad** | a recession in a major trading partner reduces demand for UK exports, so UK output and jobs fall |
| **Supply-chain disruption** | many UK products use imported components; if a factory, port or shipping route abroad is disrupted, UK firms may run short, production stops and prices rise |
| **Imported inflation** | a rise in world energy or food prices feeds straight into UK prices because the UK imports much of both (cost-push inflation, 3.2.2.4) |
| **Job losses in import-competing industries** | cheaper imports can force UK firms that cannot compete to cut jobs or close, causing **structural unemployment** in particular regions (as happened to parts of UK textiles, steel and shipbuilding) |
| **Dependence on others for essentials** | relying on imports for energy, food or key medicines can be a risk if relations with a supplier break down |
| **Shared benefits** | growth abroad raises demand for UK exports; cooperation and trade agreements; access to the best technology and ideas from around the world |

Interdependence is therefore a **trade-off**: the UK gains cheaper goods, wider choice and larger markets, but loses some control over its own economy.

::: terms
- **International trade**: the exchange of goods and services between countries.
- **Exports**: goods and services produced in one country and sold to buyers in other countries, bringing money into the country.
- **Imports**: goods and services bought from other countries, sending money out of the country.
- **Specialisation**: when a country concentrates on producing the goods and services it produces relatively well, and trades for the rest.
- **Global interdependence**: the way countries depend on each other for goods, services, markets and investment, so events in one affect others.
- **Supply chain**: the sequence of firms and countries involved in producing a good, from raw materials to the final product.
- **Economies of scale**: falls in average cost as a firm increases its scale of production.
- **Import-competing industry**: a domestic industry that faces competition from imported goods.
- **Trading partner**: a country that another country exports to and/or imports from.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the group** in the question — consumers, producers, workers, the economy — and keep the whole answer about that group.
2. **Give a specific benefit or cost** (lower prices, larger market, economies of scale, imported inflation, job losses) and chain it: trade → what changes → effect on the group.
3. **UK trade facts**: services are the largest export; the UK imports much of its food and energy; the EU is the largest partner as a bloc.
4. **Calculations**: shares of total trade (part ÷ total × 100), percentage changes in exports or imports, trade balances (exports − imports).
5. **Interdependence**: show how an event abroad travels into the UK economy — through export demand, supply chains or import prices.
6. **Evaluate**: gains are spread widely (all consumers) but losses are concentrated (particular workers and regions); dependence on imports is a risk; judge the overall effect.
:::

::: example State — reasons for trade
State two reasons why countries trade with each other. (2)

Any two (1 each): to obtain goods/resources they cannot produce themselves; goods can be produced more cheaply abroad; to specialise; to reach bigger markets/sell more; to gain economies of scale; more choice for consumers; more competition.
:::

::: example Explain — benefit to consumers
Explain one benefit of international trade to UK consumers. (2)

Imports from countries that can produce goods more cheaply, together with competition from foreign firms (1), mean UK consumers pay lower prices and so their incomes buy more goods (1). (Or: more choice and variety, e.g. fruit all year round.)
:::

::: example Calculate — share of exports
Invented data: Country U's exports total £820 billion, of which £394 billion are services. Calculate the percentage of exports that are services, to one decimal place. (2)

394 ÷ 820 × 100 (1) = **48.0%** (1).
:::

::: example Explain — interdependence
Explain one way in which a recession in the UK's major trading partners could affect the UK economy. (2)

Incomes abroad fall, so foreign consumers and firms buy fewer UK goods and services (1); UK exports fall, so UK firms cut output and jobs — slower growth and higher unemployment in the UK (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — dependence on imported energy
Invented data — Table 1: Country K

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Share of energy that is imported (%) | 55 | 55 |
| World gas price (index) | 100 | 240 |
| CPI inflation (%) | 2.1 | 7.8 |

Using Table 1, analyse how global interdependence affected Country K in Year 2. (6)

Country K imports over half its energy (55%), so it depends on world energy markets (2). When the world gas price index rose by 140%, the cost of imported energy rose sharply; households paid more for heating and firms faced higher costs for power and transport, which they passed on as higher prices — **imported, cost-push inflation** — CPI inflation rose by 5.7 percentage points to 7.8% (2). Higher energy bills leave households less to spend on other goods, so UK output and jobs may suffer too; a country producing more of its own energy would have been less exposed, although even domestic producers usually sell at world prices (2).
:::
