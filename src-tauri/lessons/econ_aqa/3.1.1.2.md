## What it is

Nothing can be produced out of nothing. Every good or service is made by combining **economic resources** — the inputs used in production. Economists group these resources into four **factors of production**, and each factor earns its own **reward** when it is used.

### What makes something an economic resource

An **economic resource** is anything that can be used to produce goods and services. Resources are **scarce**: there is only a limited amount of land, a limited number of workers and hours in the day, a limited stock of machinery, and only so many people able and willing to run businesses. Because a resource used for one thing cannot be used for another at the same time, its use has an opportunity cost (3.1.1.3). A resource is "economic" because it has alternative uses and is limited — fresh air in a park is not normally treated as an economic resource, but a plot of building land is.

### The four factors and their rewards

| Factor | What it is | Examples | Reward |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Land** | natural resources — everything provided by nature, not just the ground | farmland, building plots, oil, gas, minerals, fish stocks, forests, rivers, wind and sunshine | **rent** |
| **Labour** | human effort, both physical and mental, used in production | a nurse, a coder, a bricklayer, a teacher, a delivery driver | **wages** (and salaries) |
| **Capital** | man-made goods used to produce other goods and services | machinery, tools, factories, offices, delivery vans, computers, ovens | **interest** |
| **Enterprise** | the skill of organising the other three factors and taking the risk of running a business | the entrepreneur who starts a café, a firm's owner who decides what to make | **profit** |

Memory aid: **L**and, **L**abour, **C**apital, **E**nterprise → **R**ent, **W**ages, **I**nterest, **P**rofit.

### Capital is not money

This is the most common mistake on the topic. In economics, **capital means man-made physical aids to production** — the oven in a bakery, the tractor on a farm, the laptop of a web designer. Money is not itself a factor of production: you cannot bake bread with a bank balance. Money is used to *buy* capital; the reward for lending money to buy capital is **interest**. So when a question asks for an example of capital, give a machine, tool, vehicle or building — never "money", "savings" or "a loan".

Equally, **income, profit and rent are rewards, not factors**. A multiple-choice question that lists "profit" or "rent" alongside "machinery" is testing whether you know which one is the factor.

### Factors in a real business

Take an invented bakery, *Rise & Shine Ltd*:

| Factor | At Rise & Shine |
|---|---|
| Land | the plot the shop stands on; the wheat grown on farms that becomes its flour; the water in the dough; the gas for the ovens |
| Labour | bakers, shop assistants, the delivery driver, the person doing the accounts |
| Capital | ovens, mixers, the shop building, display counters, the delivery van, the till |
| Enterprise | the owner who raised the money, chose the location, decided the product range and stands to gain profit or suffer a loss |

The same item can play different roles: the flour the bakery buys is a raw material (the product of land and labour at a mill), while the mill's grinding machinery is the mill's capital.

### Quantity and quality of factors

Output depends on **how much** of each factor is available and **how good** it is.

- **Quantity**: more workers, more land under cultivation, more machines — e.g. migration adds to the labour force.
- **Quality**: a better-trained workforce, more fertile land or more modern machinery produces more from the same quantity. Education and training improve **labour**; investment in new technology improves **capital**.
- Factors are often **combined in different proportions**. A labour-intensive business (a hairdresser, a care home) uses a lot of labour relative to capital; a capital-intensive one (an oil refinery, an automated warehouse) uses a lot of machinery relative to workers. This is the "how to produce" decision from 3.1.1.1.

### Why the rewards matter

Rewards are the **incentive** that brings factors into use. Workers supply labour because they are paid wages; landowners rent out land because they receive rent; savers lend money for capital because they earn interest; entrepreneurs accept risk because they hope to make a profit. If a reward rises — for example, higher wages in one occupation — more of that factor tends to move there. This is how markets allocate resources (3.1.2.1), and the market for labour is studied in detail in 3.1.5.4.

::: terms
- **Economic resource**: any input that can be used to produce goods and services; it is scarce and has alternative uses.
- **Factors of production**: the four groups of economic resources — land, labour, capital and enterprise.
- **Land**: all natural resources used in production, including the ground, minerals, oil, fish and forests.
- **Labour**: the physical and mental effort of people used in producing goods and services.
- **Capital**: man-made goods used to produce other goods and services, such as machinery, tools and buildings.
- **Enterprise**: organising the other factors of production and taking the risk of running a business.
- **Entrepreneur**: a person who uses enterprise, starting or running a business in the hope of profit.
- **Rent**: the reward to the owner of land for its use in production.
- **Wages**: the reward paid to labour for the work it does.
- **Interest**: the reward for capital, paid to those who provide the funds to buy it.
- **Profit**: the reward for enterprise — what is left from revenue after all costs are paid.
- **Labour-intensive**: a production method that uses a high proportion of labour compared with capital.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the factor** from its definition: from nature = land; human effort = labour; man-made aid to production = capital; organising and risk-taking = enterprise.
2. **Give a specific example from the business in the question** — "the delivery van" not "machines in general".
3. **Pair each factor with its reward** if asked: rent, wages, interest, profit — in that order.
4. **Never offer money, income, profit or rent as a factor**; they are means of payment or rewards.
5. **For "explain" questions**, add a consequence: why the factor matters to output or costs, or how its quantity or quality changes production.
6. **For evaluation**, compare two factors (e.g. more labour versus more capital) using cost, productivity and flexibility, then judge for this business.
:::

::: example Identify — factors in a business
Identify one example of each of the four factors of production used by a fishing business. (4)

Land: the fish in the sea (1). Labour: the crew who work on the boat (1). Capital: the fishing boat and its nets (1). Enterprise: the owner who organises the business and risks their money on it (1).
:::

::: example Explain — capital versus money
Explain why money is not counted as capital in economics. (2)

Capital is a man-made good, such as a machine or a building, that is used to produce other goods and services (1). Money cannot itself produce anything — it is only used to buy capital, so the reward for providing it is interest rather than money being a factor itself (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a reward as an incentive
Explain why the reward to enterprise is profit. (2)

An entrepreneur organises the other factors and risks their own money, which could be lost if the business fails (1). Profit — revenue left after paying rent, wages and interest — is the return that compensates them for that risk and gives them the incentive to start the business (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — shares of the rewards
An invented garden centre receives £400,000 in revenue in a year. It pays £60,000 rent for its site, £180,000 in wages, £20,000 interest on a loan used to buy greenhouses, and £90,000 for plants and other supplies. Calculate the reward to enterprise and state it as a percentage of revenue. (3)

Profit = £400,000 − (£60,000 + £180,000 + £20,000 + £90,000) = £400,000 − £350,000 = £50,000 (1)(1). As a percentage of revenue: £50,000 ÷ £400,000 × 100 = 12.5% (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — changing the mix of factors
A supermarket replaces some checkout staff with self-service tills. Analyse how this changes the use of the factors of production. (6)

The supermarket uses more capital — the self-service machines — and less labour, so its production becomes more capital-intensive (2). It now pays more interest (or loan repayments) on the money used to buy the machines, but less in wages; if the machines serve customers at lower cost per transaction, profit, the reward to enterprise, should rise (2). The remaining labour may need different skills — staff to help customers with machines and fix faults — so the quality of labour required changes, while some workers lose their jobs and must find work elsewhere, perhaps after retraining (2).
:::

::: q
Explain why labour is an economic resource. (2)
---
Labour can be used to produce goods and services (1), and the number of workers and hours is limited, so labour used in one job cannot be used in another at the same time — it is scarce (1).

::: q
Analyse how improving the quality of labour could affect a car manufacturer. (6)
---
Training workers raises their skills (1), so each worker can produce more cars or make fewer mistakes, raising output per worker (1); this lowers the cost per car, so the firm could cut prices or increase profit (1). Skilled workers can operate more advanced machinery (1), allowing the firm to use new technology and improve quality (1), which may increase sales; however, training costs money and trained workers may be poached by rivals or demand higher wages (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains applied to car making; 3–4 some development; 1–2 simple points.
:::

- **Land includes all natural resources** — fish, oil, water, wind — not just the ground.
- Give **examples from the business in the question**; generic lists score less in applied questions.

::: test
The table below shows invented data on two ways an apple-juice firm could produce 10,000 bottles a week.

| | Method X | Method Y |
|---|---|---|
| Workers employed | 20 | 6 |
| Machines used | 2 | 8 |
| Weekly wage bill (£) | 9,000 | 3,000 |
| Weekly interest and running cost of machines (£) | 1,000 | 5,500 |

Using the table, analyse which method makes better use of the factors of production for the firm. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- Method X is labour-intensive (20 workers, 2 machines); Method Y is capital-intensive (6 workers, 8 machines).
- Cost of labour plus capital: X = £10,000; Y = £8,500 — Y is £1,500 cheaper a week for the same output, so profit (the reward to enterprise) is higher.
- Y needs fewer workers, so the firm saves on wages but depends on machines; a breakdown could halt production; staff may need more technical skills.
- X is more flexible — workers can switch tasks, and output can be cut by reducing hours — and avoids large borrowing to buy machines.
- Judgement linked to data: e.g. Y, because it is cheaper, if demand is steady and the machines are reliable.
Levels: 5–6 — developed reasoning using the figures (e.g. the £1,500 saving) and a clear link to rewards/costs; 3–4 — some analysis with limited use of data; 1–2 — simple statements.
:::

::: test
Assess whether labour or capital is the more important factor of production for a business such as a hospital. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Labour more important: healthcare is a service delivered by people — doctors, nurses, carers; skills, judgement and care cannot be fully replaced by machines; quality of labour (training) largely determines the quality of treatment; staff shortages close wards even when equipment is available.
Capital more important: scanners, operating theatres, beds and IT systems make diagnosis and surgery possible; new technology can raise the number of patients treated per doctor; without buildings and equipment, skilled staff cannot work.
Other factors: land (site) and enterprise (management organising staff and equipment) also matter.
Conclusion: e.g. the two are complementary — each is useless without the other — but for a hospital labour is the binding constraint because skilled staff take years to train while equipment can be bought; it depends on the department (radiology more capital-intensive than nursing care).
Levels: L3 7–9 — well-developed two-sided analysis applied to a hospital with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some developed analysis, may be one-sided, partly supported conclusion; L1 1–3 — simple points, little application.
:::
