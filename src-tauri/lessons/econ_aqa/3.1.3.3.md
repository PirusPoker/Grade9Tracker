## What it is

Put demand (3.1.3.1) and supply (3.1.3.2) on the same diagram and you can explain the price of almost anything. This topic covers how **equilibrium price** is set, why **excess demand** and **excess supply** push the price towards equilibrium, how shifts change the equilibrium, how the model applies to **real-world markets**, and how to show a producer's **revenue** on the diagram.

### Equilibrium

The **equilibrium price** is the price at which **quantity demanded equals quantity supplied**. On the diagram it is where the demand and supply curves cross. At that price every buyer willing to pay it can buy, and every seller willing to sell at it can sell — there is no pressure for the price to change. The quantity traded is the **equilibrium quantity**.

### Excess supply and excess demand

| | **Excess supply** (a surplus) | **Excess demand** (a shortage) |
|---|---|---|
| When | the price is **above** equilibrium (P1) | the price is **below** equilibrium (P2) |
| What happens | quantity supplied > quantity demanded; unsold stock builds up | quantity demanded > quantity supplied; queues, waiting lists, sold-out signs |
| How it is removed | sellers **cut prices** to clear stock → demand extends, supply contracts | sellers find they can **raise prices** (or buyers bid more) → demand contracts, supply extends |
| Result | price falls to Pe | price rises to Pe |

Size of the gap = quantity demanded − quantity supplied at that price. For example, with invented data, if at £8 consumers demand 500 and firms supply 800, there is excess supply of 300 units.

### Changes in equilibrium

When a non-price factor shifts a curve, a new equilibrium forms. The four basic cases:

| Change | Shift | Effect on equilibrium price | Effect on equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Increase in demand (e.g. incomes rise) | D shifts right | **rises** | **rises** |
| Decrease in demand (e.g. a health scare) | D shifts left | **falls** | **falls** |
| Increase in supply (e.g. new technology) | S shifts right | **falls** | **rises** |
| Decrease in supply (e.g. dearer raw materials) | S shifts left | **rises** | **falls** |

**The process matters, not just the result.** After demand increases, at the old price there is now excess demand; this pushes the price up, which causes an **extension of supply** (a movement along S) until a new equilibrium is reached. Shifts of one curve cause movements along the other.

**Both curves shifting.** If demand and supply both increase, quantity definitely rises, but the price could rise, fall or stay the same — it depends on which shift is larger. If demand rises while supply falls, the price definitely rises but the change in quantity depends on the sizes of the shifts. Say so: "the overall effect on price depends on the relative size of the shifts".

### Applying demand and supply to real-world markets

| Market | Typical story |
|---|---|
| **Housing** | population growth and low interest rates increase demand; planning limits and land shortages restrict supply → high and rising prices |
| **Oil** | supply cut by conflict or producer decisions → price rises; a global slowdown reduces demand → price falls |
| **Concert tickets** | fixed supply (seats); huge demand for a popular act → excess demand at the face value → resale at higher prices |
| **Replica football shirts** | demand jumps when a team wins a trophy or a new kit launches; falls at the end of the season → discounting |
| **Labour** | demand for a type of worker rises (derived demand) → wages rise; more workers trained → supply rises → wages fall |
| **Agricultural products** | a poor harvest shifts supply left → price rises; a bumper harvest shifts supply right → price falls |

### Revenue on the demand and supply diagram

A producer's (or the whole market's) **total revenue = price × quantity**. On the diagram it is the **rectangle** whose height is the price and whose width is the quantity — from the origin up to the equilibrium price and across to the equilibrium quantity. Revenue earned by producers is the same as total consumer spending.

When demand rises (D1 to D2), both price and quantity rise, so revenue rises — the rectangle grows in both directions. When supply rises, price falls but quantity rises, so revenue could rise or fall: that depends on the **price elasticity of demand** (3.1.3.5).

**Worked calculation** (invented): equilibrium is £12 and 3,000 units, so revenue = £12 × 3,000 = £36,000. After an increase in demand, equilibrium is £14 and 3,400 units: revenue = £47,600. Change = +£11,600, a rise of 11,600 ÷ 36,000 × 100 = 32.2%.

::: terms
- **Equilibrium price**: the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied.
- **Equilibrium quantity**: the quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price.
- **Excess demand**: when quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied at a given price, creating a shortage.
- **Excess supply**: when quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded at a given price, creating a surplus.
- **Shortage**: another name for excess demand — buyers cannot get all they want at the current price.
- **Surplus**: another name for excess supply — firms cannot sell all they produce at the current price.
- **Total revenue**: price multiplied by quantity sold, shown as a rectangle on a demand and supply diagram.
- **Market clearing**: when the price adjusts so that everything supplied is bought, leaving no shortage or surplus.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the cause** and decide: demand or supply? Which direction?
2. **Shift the right curve** and find the new equilibrium; state what happens to price **and** quantity.
3. **Explain the process**: at the old price there is now excess demand/supply → price rises/falls → extension/contraction along the other curve → new equilibrium.
4. **Draw and label** (3 marks): labelled axes and original curves with equilibrium P1, Q1; the correct curve shifted in the correct direction; new equilibrium P2, Q2 labelled.
5. **Revenue**: price × quantity before and after; give the change in £ (and % if asked); on a diagram, shade or describe the rectangle.
6. **Two shifts or assess questions**: weigh which shift is bigger; say "it depends on" and justify.
:::

::: example Explain — a fall in equilibrium price
Explain one possible reason for a decrease in the equilibrium price of laptops. (2)

New production technology could lower the cost of making each laptop, so manufacturers supply more at every price and the supply curve shifts to the right (1). At the old price there is now excess supply, so firms cut prices until a new, lower equilibrium price is reached (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — equilibrium and excess demand
Invented data for a market for phone cases:

| Price (£) | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded | 900 | 750 | 600 | 450 | 300 |
| Quantity supplied | 300 | 450 | 600 | 750 | 900 |

State the equilibrium price, calculate the excess demand at £6, and calculate total revenue at equilibrium. (4)

Equilibrium price = £8, where quantity demanded = quantity supplied = 600 (1). At £6, excess demand = 750 − 450 (1) = 300 units (1). Revenue = £8 × 600 = £4,800 (1).
:::

::: example Draw and label — a supply decrease
Draw and label a diagram to show the effect of a poor coffee harvest on the market for coffee. (3)

Model answer (described): axes labelled Price and Quantity, with D and S1 crossing at P1, Q1 (1). Supply shifts to the left, S1 to S2, because a poor harvest means less coffee can be supplied at every price (1). New equilibrium where S2 crosses D, at a higher price P2 and lower quantity Q2 (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — change in revenue
Invented data: the equilibrium price of a theme-park ticket is £40 and 25,000 tickets are sold a month. A new rival park opens and the equilibrium becomes £36 and 21,000 tickets. Calculate the change in total revenue. (2)

Before: £40 × 25,000 = £1,000,000; after: £36 × 21,000 = £756,000 (1). Change = −£244,000 (a fall of £244,000) (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — two simultaneous changes
Analyse the effect on the market for new homes of a rise in population and new government rules that make it quicker to get planning permission. (6)

A rising population increases the number of households needing homes, so demand shifts right; at the old price there is excess demand, pushing prices up (2). Faster planning permission lets builders start more projects and lowers delays and costs, so supply shifts right, which on its own would push prices down (2). Equilibrium quantity rises because both shifts increase it, but the effect on price depends on which shift is larger; since houses take years to build, supply responds slowly, so in the short run the demand shift is likely to dominate and prices still rise (2).
:::

::: q
Define the term "equilibrium price". (2)
---
The price at which the quantity demanded (1) equals the quantity supplied (1).
:::

::: q
Which of the following will cause the equilibrium price of a good to rise and the equilibrium quantity to fall? A — an increase in demand; B — a decrease in demand; C — an increase in supply; D — a decrease in supply. (1)
---
D — a decrease in supply.
:::

::: q
Explain why a price above the equilibrium price is likely to fall. (2)
---
At that price quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded — excess supply (1); firms are left with unsold stock so they cut prices until demand equals supply (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: at a price of £15, quantity demanded is 2,200 and quantity supplied is 1,600. State whether there is excess demand or excess supply, calculate its size, and state the likely direction of price change. (3)
---
Excess demand (1) of 2,200 − 1,600 = 600 units (1); the price is likely to rise (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why tickets for a popular concert are often resold at prices far above their face value. (3)
---
The number of seats is fixed, so supply cannot increase (1); at the face-value price demand is far greater than supply — excess demand (1); fans who missed out are willing to pay more, so resellers can charge prices closer to (or above) the price that would clear the market (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the effect on the market for umbrellas of a forecast of a very wet summer and a rise in the cost of the fabric used to make them. (6)
---
The forecast increases demand — D shifts right (1) — which on its own raises price and quantity (1). Dearer fabric raises costs, so supply shifts left (1), which on its own raises price and lowers quantity (1). Both changes raise the equilibrium price, so price definitely rises (1); the effect on quantity depends on which shift is larger (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains with correct shifts and a conclusion on price and quantity; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Say which curve shifts and in which direction before stating the price change** — under half of candidates managed this on a recent paper.
- A shift of one curve causes a **movement along the other** — do not shift both unless both factors change.
- Excess demand is **below** equilibrium; excess supply is **above** it.
- Revenue = **price × quantity** — the rectangle, not the area under the curve.
- With two shifts, one of price or quantity is **uncertain** — say what it depends on.
- Diagram marks: labelled axes, labelled curves, original **and** new equilibrium marked.
:::

::: test
The table shows invented data for the market for petrol in a country.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| World crude oil price (£ per barrel) | 60 | 78 |
| Electric cars on the road (millions) | 0.8 | 1.3 |
| Average petrol price (pence per litre) | 140 | 158 |

Using the table, analyse the reasons for the changes in the petrol market. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- Crude oil price rose by 30%, raising the cost of producing petrol → supply shifts left → higher price, lower quantity.
- Electric cars rose by 0.5 million (62.5%) — a substitute for petrol cars, so demand for petrol shifts left → lower price and quantity.
- Price rose by 18p (12.9%) while quantity fell by 2bn litres (6.7%): price rose, so the supply decrease must have outweighed the demand decrease; both shifts reduce quantity.

::: test
Using a demand and supply diagram, assess whether a large rise in the number of students in a university town will lead to higher rents for student housing. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Rents likely to rise: more students increase demand for rented rooms — demand shifts right; supply of housing is slow to respond (planning, building time), so in the short run the main effect is a higher equilibrium rent with only a small rise in quantity; excess demand at the old rent; landlords can raise rents.
Rents may not rise much: the university or private developers may build new halls, shifting supply right; landlords may convert family homes into student lets; students may live further away or at home and commute (substitutes); if many students arrive but some landlords leave the market, the result could differ.
Diagram: D1 to D2 with S; rent rises from R1 to R2; possible S1 to S2 shift later.
Conclusion: e.g. rents will rise in the short run because supply is fixed while demand rises immediately; in the long run the size of the rise depends on how quickly new accommodation is built.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis using correct shifts and a justified conclusion considering time; L2 4–6 — some correct analysis, may be one-sided; L1 1–3 — simple points or incorrect shifts.
