## What it is

Economics exists because of one fact: **we cannot have everything we want.** This topic explains why choices are unavoidable, how consumers, producers and the government make them, and how every choice carries a cost — the **opportunity cost**.

### The basic economic problem

| Side of the problem | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Scarce (finite) resources** | there is only a limited amount of land, labour, capital and enterprise at any time |
| **Unlimited wants** | people always want more goods and services; when one want is met, another appears |

Put together, **scarce resources and unlimited wants** mean that not every want can be satisfied, so **choices must be made** about how to use resources. This is the **basic economic problem**, and it applies to everyone — a student with £20, a firm with one factory, a government with a fixed budget, and even the richest country in the world.

Be precise in multiple-choice questions: the basic economic problem is *scarce* resources and *unlimited* wants. Options that say "limited wants" or "renewable resources" are distractors.

**Scarcity is not the same as poverty.** A wealthy person still faces scarcity of time — they cannot be in two places at once — and a rich country must still choose between spending on defence or on schools.

### How and why choices are made

Each economic group faces the basic problem and makes choices to get the greatest benefit from its limited resources.

| Group | Limited resource | Typical choice | What they hope to maximise |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | income and time | which goods and services to buy; whether to spend or save; whether to work more hours or have more leisure | satisfaction (welfare) |
| **Producers** | money, workers, machines, space | what to produce, which machines to buy, whether to expand | profit (or other objectives — see 3.1.4.1) |
| **Government** | tax revenue and what it can borrow | how much to spend on health, education, defence, benefits; which taxes to raise | the welfare of society as a whole |

### Weighing up costs and benefits

A rational decision-maker compares the **costs** of a choice with its **benefits** and chooses the option with the greatest **net benefit** (benefits minus costs).

- **Costs** include the money price, time, effort, risk and — most importantly — what must be given up.
- **Benefits** include the satisfaction, income, profit or social gain the choice brings.

A simple cost–benefit table (invented figures) for a student choosing how to spend a Saturday:

| Option | Benefits | Costs | Net benefit (rough score) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Work an 8-hour shift | £96 wages; work experience | loses a day of revision and seeing friends | high |
| Revise for mocks | better grades; less stress later | no wages; less free time | high |
| Day out with friends | enjoyment | £30 spent; no wages; no revision | medium |

Real choices are rarely this tidy — benefits may come in the future (grades), be uncertain (a business investment) or fall on other people (a new hospital) — but the principle is the same: **compare what you gain with what you give up**.

### Opportunity cost

**Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone** when a choice is made. Three key ideas:

1. It is **one** alternative — the *next best* one — not everything else you could have done.
2. It is measured by what is **given up**, not by the money spent. Money is just the way of buying; what the money could otherwise have bought is the cost.
3. **Every choice has one**, even "free" things. Time spent watching a "free" film could have been spent working or revising.

| Decision-maker | Choice | Opportunity cost (next best alternative) |
|---|---|---|
| Student | stays on at sixth form | the wages from a full-time job or apprenticeship |
| Consumer | spends £60 on concert tickets | the trainers they would otherwise have bought |
| Firm | uses its factory to make bikes | the electric scooters it could have made instead |
| Firm | buys a new machine for £50,000 | the extra staff it could have hired, or interest on the money saved |
| Government | spends £1bn on a new hospital | the schools or roads that £1bn could have built (or a tax cut) |

**Opportunity cost for government** is particularly important because the money comes from taxpayers. Spending more on one public service means spending less on another, raising taxes or borrowing more — and each of those has its own cost.

### Why opportunity cost matters

- It makes the **true cost** of a choice visible. A council that allows a field to be used as a car park "for free" gives up the rent, the housing or the park it could have been.
- It helps decision-makers **rank** options: if the benefit of the chosen option is less than the value of what is given up, the choice is a poor one.
- It explains why **resources move** between uses: when the reward from one use rises (e.g. higher wages elsewhere), the opportunity cost of staying in the current use rises too.

::: terms
- **Basic economic problem**: scarce resources and unlimited wants, which together mean choices must be made.
- **Scarcity**: the situation in which resources are limited compared with the wants they could satisfy.
- **Finite resources**: resources that exist in limited quantities at any time.
- **Unlimited wants**: the idea that people's desire for goods and services can never be fully satisfied.
- **Choice**: deciding how to use scarce resources between competing alternatives.
- **Opportunity cost**: the value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
- **Cost–benefit analysis**: comparing the costs and benefits of an option to decide whether it is worthwhile.
- **Net benefit**: the total benefits of a choice minus its total costs.
- **Trade-off**: having less of one thing in order to have more of another.
- **Rational decision**: a choice made by weighing up costs and benefits to get the greatest net benefit.
:::

::: method
1. **Basic economic problem**: always state both halves — scarce/finite resources **and** unlimited wants — then "so choices must be made".
2. **Opportunity cost**: name the decision-maker, the choice and the **single next best alternative** given up — be specific ("the new science block", not "other things").
3. **Do not equate cost with price**: say what the money (or time, or land) *could otherwise have been used for*.
4. **Cost–benefit questions**: list the main benefits and costs, use the numbers given to compare them, and include the opportunity cost.
5. **Analyse/assess**: show the chain — choice → what is gained → what is given up → who is affected — then weigh which option has the greater net benefit and what it depends on.
:::

::: example Define and apply
Explain the opportunity cost to a government of building a new hospital. (2)

The government has a limited budget, so the money spent on the hospital cannot be spent elsewhere (1). The opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up — for example, the new secondary schools the same money could have built (1).
:::

::: example Explain — the basic problem for a firm
Explain how the basic economic problem affects a small furniture maker. (2)

The firm's resources — its workshop space, its four workers and its machines — are limited, while customers want many different products (1). It therefore has to choose what to make, e.g. tables rather than wardrobes, and give up the alternative (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — opportunity cost in money
Kai earns £11.50 an hour in a part-time job. He takes an unpaid 4-hour Saturday off to attend a revision course costing £25. Calculate the total cost of attending the course, including the opportunity cost of his time. (2)

Lost wages = 4 × £11.50 = £46 (1). Total cost = £46 + £25 = £71 (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a firm's investment choice
An invented bakery has £30,000 to invest. Option A: a new oven that would cut costs by £8,000 a year. Option B: a delivery van expected to add £12,000 a year to revenue, with running costs of £5,000 a year. Analyse the choice the bakery faces. (6)

The bakery's funds are scarce, so it cannot do both; whichever it chooses, the other is its opportunity cost (1). Option A raises profit by £8,000 a year by lowering costs, and the gain is fairly certain because it depends on the bakery's own production (2). Option B adds £12,000 − £5,000 = £7,000 a year to profit, slightly less than A, and depends on customers actually ordering deliveries, so it is riskier (2). On these figures A has the greater net benefit, so the opportunity cost of choosing B would be £1,000 a year more than it gains — unless the van opens a new market that could grow beyond the forecast (1).
:::

::: example Assess — a government spending choice
Assess whether the government should spend an extra £2bn on healthcare rather than education. (9)

For healthcare: an ageing population means rising demand for treatment, and long waiting lists leave people unable to work, which reduces output and tax revenue (2). Health spending has immediate, visible benefits — lives saved and pain reduced — that voters value (1).
For education: better-educated workers are more productive and earn more, so education raises future output and tax revenue that could fund healthcare later (2). Many benefits are long-term and spread across society, so they may be undervalued in a short-term decision (1).
Judgement: the opportunity cost of each is the other, so the choice depends on which has the greater net benefit. If waiting lists are keeping many workers out of the labour force, healthcare may give the larger return now; but if the aim is long-term growth, education is likely to give the greater benefit over time. A government might split the money, accepting a smaller gain in each (3).
:::

::: q
A council owns a field. It could sell it for housing for £3m, or turn it into a park that costs £200,000 to set up. Explain the opportunity cost to the council of creating the park. (3)
---
The council gives up the next best alternative (1): the £3m it would have received from selling the field, which could have been spent on other services (1); in addition, the £200,000 set-up cost could have been used elsewhere, e.g. on road repairs (1). Accept the housing that would have been built as the alternative forgone.
:::

::: q
Analyse how the basic economic problem affects the decisions of consumers. (6)
---
Consumers have limited income and time (1) but want many goods and services (1), so they must choose what to buy (1). They weigh the benefit (satisfaction) of each item against its price and what else the money could buy (1); buying one item means forgoing the next best alternative — the opportunity cost (1); when income rises or prices fall, they can satisfy more wants, but never all of them (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

- Make the alternative **specific to the context** (the schools that could have been built), not "other things".
- Opportunity cost applies to **time and land**, not just money; "free" choices still have one.
- In assess questions, compare **net benefits** and say what the decision depends on.

::: test
Explain two ways in which the basic economic problem affects a producer such as a farmer. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3: 3 = a full, applied explanation; 2 = partly developed; 1 = a relevant point only.
Way 1 — choosing what to produce: the farmer's land is limited (1), so using a field for wheat means it cannot be used for potatoes or grazing sheep (1); the farmer must compare the expected profit of each and the crop not grown is the opportunity cost (1).
Way 2 — choosing how to produce: the farmer's funds are limited (1), so spending on a new tractor means less to spend on hiring seasonal workers (1); the farmer must weigh the cost savings of the machine against the flexibility of extra labour (1).
Also creditworthy: limited time at harvest forcing priorities; limited budget for fertiliser versus seed; choosing whether to expand or keep savings.

::: test
The table shows invented data on two projects a city council could fund with £10m.

| | Project P: new bus lanes | Project Q: new sports centre |
|---|---|---|
| Cost (£m) | 10 | 10 |
| Estimated yearly benefit to residents (£m) | 1.6 | 1.1 |
| Yearly running costs (£m) | 0.3 | 0.4 |
| Number of residents benefiting each week | 40,000 | 6,000 |

Using the table, analyse the choice facing the council. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- Scarcity: the council has £10m and cannot fund both; each project's opportunity cost is the other.
- Net yearly benefit: P = 1.6 − 0.3 = £1.3m; Q = 1.1 − 0.4 = £0.7m — P gives nearly twice the net benefit.
- P reaches 40,000 residents a week against 6,000, so its benefits are spread more widely.
- Q may bring benefits not in the table — health, fewer days off sick, young people's activities — and P may have costs not shown (disruption to car drivers, lost parking).
- Choosing Q would mean forgoing £0.6m a year of net benefit.
Levels: 5–6 — developed analysis using calculated figures and opportunity cost; 3–4 — some analysis using data; 1–2 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Assess whether opportunity cost is a useful idea when individuals make choices. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Useful: makes the true cost of a decision visible beyond the money price (e.g. lost wages from staying in education); encourages people to compare options and choose the one with the greatest net benefit; applies to time as well as money, so it helps with decisions such as part-time work versus revision; helps avoid wasting scarce income.
Limitations: the next best alternative is often hard to identify or value (satisfaction is subjective); many decisions are made by habit or impulse, not careful comparison; information about future benefits is uncertain (will a degree raise earnings?); some choices involve values that cannot be priced, e.g. caring for a relative.
Conclusion: e.g. opportunity cost is most useful for large, planned decisions (education, buying a car) where the alternatives are clear; less useful for everyday small purchases. It remains the right way to think about cost even when the exact value is hard to measure.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, may be one-sided, conclusion partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points or assertion.
:::
