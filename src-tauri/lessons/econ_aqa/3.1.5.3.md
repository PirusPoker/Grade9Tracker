## What it is

At the other end of the range from competitive markets are **non-competitive** (concentrated) markets, dominated by one firm or a handful. This topic covers what a non-competitive market is, **how producers operate** in one, the meaning of **monopoly** and **oligopoly**, their impact on **price and choice**, and the **causes and consequences of monopolistic and oligopolistic power**. It is one of the most common topics for the 15-mark final question on Paper 1.

### What is a non-competitive market?

A **non-competitive market** is one dominated by one or a few large producers, protected by **high barriers to entry**, so the firms have significant power over the price and the quantity supplied. Its main characteristics:

| Characteristic | What it means |
|---|---|
| **One or a few dominant firms** | the largest firms hold most of the market share (a high concentration ratio) |
| **High barriers to entry** | new firms find it hard or very costly to enter, so the dominant firms are protected |
| **Differentiated or unique products** | strong brands, or no close substitutes, so customers do not switch easily |
| **Price makers** | the dominant firms can set prices (within limits) rather than having to accept a market price |
| **Imperfect information** | consumers may find it hard to compare prices or to switch (complex tariffs, contracts, switching costs) |

### Monopoly

**A monopoly is a market dominated by a single firm.**

- A **pure monopoly** is one firm with **100%** of the market — the only supplier, with no close substitutes (e.g. the only water company in a region, the only firm allowed to sell a patented medicine).
- In practice, and in UK competition law, a firm can be treated as having **monopoly power** with a much smaller share; a firm with **25% or more** of a market is commonly used as the working definition, and any firm with a **dominant position** has monopoly power.

### Oligopoly

**An oligopoly is a market dominated by a few large firms** — for example, supermarkets, mobile networks, banking or car making, where a handful of firms hold most of the sales.

Key features:
- **Interdependence** — each firm must consider how its rivals will react. If one cuts its price, the others may follow, starting a **price war** that hurts all their profits.
- Because price wars are risky, oligopolists often prefer **non-price competition**: heavy advertising, branding, loyalty cards, special offers, new features, better service.
- Firms may be tempted to **collude** — secretly agree to fix prices or share out the market, behaving like a single monopoly. Price-fixing agreements (cartels) are **illegal** in the UK, and the competition authorities can fine firms that are caught.

### How producers operate in a non-competitive market

| Behaviour | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Price setting** | dominant firms are price makers: they can raise the price, because customers have few alternatives |
| **Restricting output** | by supplying less, a monopolist can keep the price higher |
| **Product differentiation and advertising** | branding builds loyalty and makes demand more **price inelastic**, so price rises lose few customers |
| **Building barriers** | heavy advertising, exclusive deals with suppliers or retailers, buying up small rivals, owning patents |
| **Collusion / avoiding price wars** | oligopolists may follow a price leader or (illegally) agree prices |
| **Spending profits on research** | high, lasting profits can fund new products and technology |

### Causes of monopoly and oligopoly power

The power of dominant firms comes mainly from **barriers to entry**:

- **Economies of scale** — large firms have far lower average costs, so a small new firm cannot compete on price (3.1.4.3).
- **High start-up costs** — building a national network, a factory or an airline needs huge capital, which also makes the venture risky.
- **Legal barriers** — patents, copyright and licences give a firm the sole right to make or sell something for a period.
- **Control of supply or distribution** — owning the key raw material, the pipes or the shelf space.
- **Brand loyalty and advertising** — years of marketing make customers reluctant to try a newcomer.
- **Mergers and takeovers** — firms join together or buy rivals, reducing the number of competitors.
- **Imperfect information and switching costs** — if consumers cannot easily compare or switch, firms keep customers without competing hard.
- **Natural monopoly** — in some industries (water pipes, rail track) it would be wasteful to have more than one network.

### Consequences of monopoly and oligopoly power

| Possible costs (mainly to consumers) | Possible benefits |
|---|---|
| **higher prices** — the firm can raise prices above the competitive level | **economies of scale** can lower average costs, which *may* be passed on as lower prices |
| **less choice** — few or no alternative suppliers | **high profits fund research and innovation** — new medicines, faster networks, new technology |
| **lower quality and poorer service** — no fear of losing customers | **reliable supply** and large-scale investment in infrastructure |
| **restricted output** — less produced than consumers would buy at a competitive price | can compete with large foreign firms in world markets |
| **inefficiency** — lack of competitive pressure means costs may not be kept down | profits create tax revenue and returns for pension funds that own shares |
| **exploitation of suppliers and workers** where the firm is the main buyer | stable firms provide secure jobs |
| **supernormal (abnormally high) profits** that persist because new firms cannot enter | |

Whether monopoly power is good or bad for consumers depends on whether the cost savings and innovation are **passed on**, and on whether the firm is **regulated** — the government can investigate mergers, fine firms that abuse a dominant position and cap prices in industries such as water and energy.

::: terms
- **Non-competitive market**: a market dominated by one or a few firms, protected by high barriers to entry.
- **Monopoly**: a market dominated by a single firm; a pure monopoly has 100% of the market.
- **Monopoly power**: the ability of a dominant firm to influence price and output, usually from holding a large market share.
- **Oligopoly**: a market dominated by a few large firms whose decisions are interdependent.
- **Price maker**: a firm with enough market power to set its own price.
- **Collusion**: firms agreeing to fix prices or share markets rather than compete — illegal in the UK.
- **Price war**: a series of price cuts by rival firms, each trying to undercut the others.
- **Non-price competition**: competing through branding, advertising, quality, service and features rather than price.
- **Patent**: a legal right giving the inventor the sole right to make and sell an invention for a period of time.
- **Merger**: when two firms join together to form one larger business.
- **Natural monopoly**: an industry where one supplier can serve the market at lower cost than several competing networks.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions**: monopoly = market dominated by a single firm (100% pure, 25%+ or a dominant position in practice); oligopoly = a few large firms dominate.
2. **How producers operate**: price makers, restricted output, branding and advertising, non-price competition, collusion, building barriers.
3. **Causes**: always link to **barriers to entry** — economies of scale, start-up costs, patents, brand loyalty, mergers, control of supply.
4. **Market-share calculations**: share = firm's sales ÷ market total × 100; add shares for a concentration ratio; then say what it shows (e.g. above 25% = monopoly power).
5. **Consequences**: say who is affected — consumers, producers, workers, suppliers — and give both the costs and the possible benefits.
6. **15-mark**: answer for the group named (usually consumers), use the Item's numbers, weigh benefits against costs, and judge with "it depends on…" (regulation, whether savings are passed on, threat of entry).
:::

::: example Define — monopoly
Define the term "monopoly". (2)

A market dominated by a single firm (1) — e.g. one firm with 100% of the market, or in UK law a firm with 25% or more / a dominant position (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — market share and monopoly power
Invented data: total annual sales of cat food in a country are £1.2 billion. Purrfect Foods sells £372 million. Calculate Purrfect's market share and state whether it would be considered to have monopoly power under the working definition. (3)

£372m ÷ £1,200m (1) × 100 = 31% (1). Yes — 31% is above the 25% working definition, so it has monopoly power (1).
:::

::: example Explain — interdependence in oligopoly
Explain why firms in an oligopoly often avoid competing on price. (2)

Each firm knows that if it cuts its price, its few large rivals are likely to cut theirs too (1), starting a price war that lowers all their revenues and profits without any firm gaining market share, so they compete through advertising and branding instead (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a cause of monopoly power
Explain how a patent can give a firm monopoly power. (2)

A patent gives the firm the sole legal right to make and sell its invention for a number of years (1), so no rival can produce the same product, creating a barrier to entry that lets the firm set a high price (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — why a market may not be competitive
Invented data: three firms supply 92% of a country's mobile phone contracts; building a national network costs several billion pounds; customers sign two-year contracts. Analyse why this market may not be competitive. (6)

Three firms hold 92% of sales, a very high concentration ratio, so the market is an oligopoly; each firm's decisions affect the others, and they may prefer to avoid price wars, keeping prices higher than in a competitive market (2). The cost of building a national network is a huge barrier to entry, so new firms are unlikely to appear even if the three earn high profits; the threat of entry, which keeps prices down in competitive markets, is missing (2). Two-year contracts make switching slow and costly, so consumers cannot easily move to a cheaper rival; demand for each firm becomes less price elastic, giving the firms more power to raise prices without losing many customers (2).
:::

:::

::: q
State two barriers to entry that can create monopoly power. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): economies of scale; high start-up costs; patents/copyright; licences; brand loyalty/advertising; control of raw materials or distribution; mergers/takeovers.
:::

::: q
Invented data: in a market worth £800 million, the four largest firms have sales of £240m, £160m, £120m and £96m. Calculate the four-firm concentration ratio. (3)
---
Combined sales = £616m (1); £616m ÷ £800m × 100 (1) = 77% (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way a monopoly may harm consumers. (2)
---
With no close substitutes, consumers cannot switch to a rival (1), so the monopolist can charge a higher price than in a competitive market / reduce quality without losing customers (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a merger between two large firms could affect consumers. (6)
---
Indicative content: the merged firm has a larger market share and fewer rivals (1), so it gains more power to raise prices (1) and consumers have less choice (1). However, the merged firm may gain economies of scale — bulk buying, shared head-office costs (1) — lowering average cost (1), which could be passed on as lower prices or used to fund new products, depending on whether competition or regulation forces it to share the savings (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains with both sides; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- A "define monopoly" answer needs **one firm dominating** — "few firms" describes oligopoly and scores 0.
- The working definition is **25% or more** of a market, but a pure monopoly is 100% — either is credited if stated clearly.
- Barriers to entry are **high** in non-competitive markets — the June 2025 examiners saw the direction muddled.
- 15-markers about **consumers** must stay on consumers — answers that drifted to firms' profits or government tax revenue were weaker in 2025.
- Give **both** costs and benefits of monopoly power (economies of scale, innovation) to reach the top levels.
- Collusion is a feature of **oligopoly**, and it is illegal in the UK — do not suggest firms can legally fix prices.
:::

::: test

---
High barriers to entry stop new firms entering when profits are high (1), so the dominant firms do not face extra supply pushing the price down and can keep prices above their costs over the long run (1).

::: test

The large firm gains economies of scale, so its average costs are lower (1), which may allow it to charge lower prices than many small firms could / fund research into better products (1).

::: test

---
Large firms have much lower average costs than small ones (1), so smaller firms cannot match their prices and are driven out or taken over, leaving a few large firms to dominate (1).

::: test
Explain two ways producers operate in a non-competitive market. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Price setting: dominant firms face few close substitutes (1), so they act as price makers and set prices above the competitive level (1), earning high profits that new firms cannot compete away (1).
Non-price competition: in an oligopoly firms fear price wars (1), so they compete through advertising, branding and loyalty schemes (1), which builds brand loyalty, makes demand more price inelastic and raises barriers to entry (1).

::: test
The table shows invented data on the market for national parcel delivery in a country.

| | 2020 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Number of national firms | 7 | 3 |
| Largest firm's market share (%) | 22 | 41 |
| Average price of a standard parcel (£) | 3.40 | 4.25 |
| Average profit margin (%) | 6 | 14 |

Using the table, analyse why this market has become less competitive and the effect on consumers. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- The number of national firms fell from 7 to 3 — likely through mergers/takeovers or smaller firms closing; the market is now an oligopoly.
- The largest firm's share rose from 22% to 41% — above the 25% working definition, so it now has monopoly power.
- Price rose by £0.85, a 25% increase — fewer rivals to switch to, so firms can act as price makers.
- Profit margin more than doubled from 6% to 14% — prices have risen faster than costs, suggesting market power rather than rising costs.
- Barriers to entry (national networks of depots and vans, economies of scale) stop new firms entering to compete the profits away; consumers face higher prices and less choice, though larger firms may invest in faster tracking/delivery.
Levels: 5–6 — developed analysis using several pieces of data; 3–4 — some analysis and data; 1–2 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Assess whether oligopoly is bad for consumers. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Bad: few firms and high barriers allow prices above the competitive level; firms may collude or follow a price leader; interdependence means firms avoid price competition, so prices stay high; less choice; heavy advertising adds to costs that consumers pay for.
Not necessarily bad: oligopolists compete fiercely through non-price competition — quality, service, new features, loyalty rewards; occasional price wars benefit consumers; economies of scale may lower costs and prices (e.g. supermarkets); high profits fund innovation; competition authorities can fine colluding firms.
Conclusion: e.g. oligopoly is not always bad — the outcome depends on whether the firms compete or collude, how high barriers are, and how well the market is regulated.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Item A — TicketMax (invented)

TicketMax sells tickets for concerts, comedy and sports events in a country. Over ten years it bought several smaller ticket firms and now handles about 85% of all live-event tickets sold online. Most large venues have signed long contracts that allow only TicketMax to sell their tickets. Its booking fee has risen from 8% to 15% of the ticket price over five years, and some customers complain that its website crashes when popular events go on sale. TicketMax says it has spent heavily on a secure app that has almost eliminated fake tickets, that it can handle millions of buyers at once, and that its size lets it offer artists a single national system.

| | Five years ago | Today |
|---|---|---|
| TicketMax market share (%) | 52 | 85 |
| Average booking fee (% of ticket price) | 8 | 15 |
| Number of rival online ticket firms | 9 | 3 |

Do you think the benefits to consumers of TicketMax's monopoly power are greater than the disadvantages? Use Item A and your own economic knowledge to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 5, AO3 10. Indicative content:
Use of data: market share up from 52% to 85% — well above 25%, near-pure monopoly; booking fee nearly doubled (up 7 percentage points, an 87.5% rise); rivals fell from 9 to 3 through takeovers.
Disadvantages to consumers: higher fees — consumers have no real alternative because venues sign exclusive contracts (a barrier to entry), so demand for TicketMax is price inelastic; less choice of seller; poorer service (crashes) because there is little pressure to improve; takeovers removed competitors, so the power is likely to persist; consumers may face extra charges hidden until checkout (imperfect information).
Benefits to consumers: the secure app has almost eliminated fake tickets — a real gain in safety and trust; economies of scale and one national system can handle millions of buyers and may keep the cost per ticket processed low; profits fund investment in technology; one familiar platform is convenient.
Evaluation: the benefits depend on whether savings are passed on — the rising fee suggests they are not; fraud protection could be provided by competing firms too; the exclusive venue contracts are the key barrier — regulation (banning exclusivity, capping fees) could keep the benefits and remove the costs; the judgement may differ for different consumers (regular gig-goers vs occasional buyers).
Conclusion: a supported view, e.g. the disadvantages are greater, because the fee has almost doubled, rivals have disappeared and service problems remain, while the main benefit (secure tickets) does not require a monopoly.
Levels: 13–15 — sustained, balanced evaluation focused on consumers, using the Item's data, developed chains on both sides and a fully supported judgement; 10–12 — well-developed analysis with good use of the Item and a reasoned judgement, some imbalance; 7–9 — some developed analysis of both sides with some use of the Item, partly supported judgement; 4–6 — limited analysis, mainly one-sided or descriptive, little use of data; 1–3 — isolated points, assertion.
:::
