## What it is

An **exchange rate** is the **price of one currency in terms of another** — for example, £1 = €1.15 or £1 = 1.25 US dollars. It matters because almost every international transaction involves swapping one currency for another: a UK firm buying German machinery needs euros; an American tourist in London needs pounds. This topic covers how exchange rates are **determined by the demand for and supply of a currency**, how to show changes on a **diagram**, and the **effects of a rising or falling exchange rate on consumers and producers**.

The UK has a **floating exchange rate**: the value of the pound is set by the market — by buyers and sellers in the foreign exchange market — not fixed by the government.

### Demand for and supply of pounds

Think of the pound as a product traded in a market. Its "price" is how many euros (or US dollars, yen…) one pound buys.

| **Demand for pounds** comes from people who want to **buy** pounds | **Supply of pounds** comes from people who want to **sell** pounds (to get foreign currency) |
|---|---|
| foreign buyers of **UK exports** of goods and services (they must pay UK firms in pounds) | UK buyers of **imports** (they swap pounds for euros, dollars, etc.) |
| **foreign tourists** visiting the UK | **UK tourists** going abroad |
| foreign firms and people **investing in the UK** — building factories, buying UK firms or property | UK firms and people **investing abroad** |
| foreigners **saving in UK banks** — attracted when **UK interest rates are higher** than elsewhere | UK savers moving money abroad — attracted when **interest rates abroad are higher** |
| **speculators** who expect the pound to rise | speculators who expect the pound to fall |

**Equilibrium**: the exchange rate settles where the quantity of pounds demanded equals the quantity supplied.

### Showing a change on a diagram

The diagram has **price of £ (e.g. € per £)** on the vertical axis and **quantity of pounds** on the horizontal axis. Demand slopes down and supply slopes up, as in any market.

Example — **UK consumers buy more imports from the eurozone**. To pay for them they must sell pounds to buy euros, so the **supply of pounds shifts right** from S1 to S2. At the old rate there is now an excess supply of pounds, so the price of the pound **falls** from ER1 to ER2 — the pound **depreciates** — and the quantity of pounds traded rises from Q1 to Q2.

Learn which curve moves:

| Change | Curve that shifts | Effect on the pound |
|---|---|---|
| foreigners buy more UK exports / more tourists visit the UK | **demand** for £ **right** | **appreciates** (rises) |
| UK interest rates rise relative to other countries | **demand** for £ **right** (foreign savings flow in); supply may shift left as UK savers keep money at home | **appreciates** |
| more foreign firms invest in the UK | **demand** for £ **right** | **appreciates** |
| UK residents buy more imports / take more holidays abroad | **supply** of £ **right** | **depreciates** (falls) |
| UK interest rates fall relative to other countries | **demand** for £ **left** | **depreciates** |
| foreigners lose confidence in the UK economy and pull investment out | **demand** for £ **left**, supply right as they sell pounds | **depreciates** |

**Appreciation** = the pound buys **more** foreign currency (£1 = €1.10 → £1 = €1.20). **Depreciation** = the pound buys **less** (£1 = €1.20 → £1 = €1.10).

### Converting prices

To find the price in the other currency:
- pounds → euros: **multiply** by the rate. A £20,000 UK car at £1 = €1.15 costs 20,000 × 1.15 = **€23,000**.
- euros → pounds: **divide** by the rate. A €600 German bike at £1 = €1.20 costs 600 ÷ 1.20 = **£500**.

If the pound appreciates to £1 = €1.25: the UK car now costs 20,000 × 1.25 = €25,000 in Europe (dearer), and the €600 bike costs 600 ÷ 1.25 = £480 in the UK (cheaper).

### Effects of a change in the exchange rate

A handy memory aid: **SPICED** — a **S**trong **P**ound makes **I**mports **C**heaper and **E**xports **D**earer. A weak pound does the reverse.

| | **Pound appreciates (stronger)** | **Pound depreciates (weaker)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | imports and **holidays abroad are cheaper** — more choice and higher real incomes | imports and foreign holidays are **dearer**; the cost of living rises |
| **UK exporters** | UK goods cost more in foreign currency → **less competitive**, sales and profits fall, possible job losses | UK goods are cheaper abroad → **more competitive**, sales rise, more jobs |
| **UK firms that import raw materials or components** | costs fall → can cut prices or raise profit | costs rise → higher prices or lower profit |
| **UK firms competing with imports** | foreign rivals become cheaper → harder to compete | foreign rivals become dearer → easier to compete at home |
| **UK tourism industry** | fewer foreign visitors (the UK is dearer for them) | more foreign visitors |
| **Inflation** | cheaper imports and lower costs → **lower inflation** | dearer imports and higher costs → **higher (imported, cost-push) inflation** |
| **Current account** | exports fall, imports rise → likely to **worsen** | exports rise, imports fall → likely to **improve** |
| **Growth and jobs** | lower export demand may slow growth | higher export demand may boost growth |

How large the effects are depends on things such as **price elasticity of demand** for exports and imports (if demand for UK exports is price inelastic, a stronger pound loses few sales), how much of a firm's inputs are imported, and **how long** the change lasts — firms often fix prices or buy currency in advance, so effects take time.

::: terms
- **Exchange rate**: the price of one currency expressed in terms of another currency.
- **Floating exchange rate**: an exchange rate determined by the demand for and supply of the currency in the market.
- **Appreciation**: a rise in the value of a currency, so it buys more of other currencies.
- **Depreciation**: a fall in the value of a currency, so it buys less of other currencies.
- **Foreign exchange market**: the market in which currencies are bought and sold.
- **Demand for pounds**: the quantity of pounds wanted by people abroad to buy UK exports, visit the UK, invest or save in the UK.
- **Supply of pounds**: the quantity of pounds offered by UK residents to buy foreign currency for imports, travel or investment abroad.
- **Competitiveness**: the ability of a country's firms to sell their goods and services at attractive prices and quality compared with foreign rivals.
- **Imported inflation**: a rise in the general price level caused by higher prices of imported goods and raw materials.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify who is buying or selling pounds**: foreigners buying UK things → **demand** for £; UK residents buying foreign things → **supply** of £.
2. **Shift the right curve** and say which way, then state the new rate: demand right or supply left → £ up; demand left or supply right → £ down.
3. **Draw and label** (3 marks): axes (price of £ in another currency; quantity of £), original D and S with equilibrium ER1/Q1, the correct shift (D2 or S2), new equilibrium ER2/Q2.
4. **Conversions**: £ → foreign currency multiply by the rate; foreign currency → £ divide by it.
5. **Effects**: use SPICED and apply to the group asked about (consumers, exporters, importers, workers); chain to inflation, the current account and jobs.
6. **Evaluate**: price elasticity of demand for exports and imports, imported inputs, how long the change lasts, other factors (quality, brand).
:::

::: example Calculate — converting a price
Invented data: a UK-made bicycle sells for £640. The exchange rate changes from £1 = €1.10 to £1 = €1.25. Calculate the change in the bicycle's price in euros. (2)

Before: 640 × 1.10 = €704; after: 640 × 1.25 = €800 (1). The price rises by **€96** (1).
:::

::: example Explain — exchange rate and inflation
Explain how an appreciation of the pound could reduce inflation in the UK. (2)

A stronger pound buys more foreign currency, so imported goods, fuel and raw materials cost fewer pounds (1); lower import prices and lower costs for UK firms reduce the prices consumers pay, so CPI inflation falls (1).
:::

::: example Draw — more UK imports from the eurozone
Draw a diagram to show the effect on the euro price of the pound of an increase in UK imports from the eurozone. Label the original and new exchange rates. (3)

(Sketch on paper.) Axes: price of £ in euros (vertical), quantity of pounds (horizontal); downward-sloping demand for £ and upward-sloping supply of £ crossing at ER1, Q1 (1). Supply of pounds shifts **right** from S1 to S2, because UK importers sell pounds to buy euros (1). New equilibrium at a **lower** exchange rate ER2 and higher quantity Q2 (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a weaker pound and exporters
Explain one way a depreciation of the pound could benefit UK exporters. (2)

A weaker pound means each pound costs foreign buyers less of their currency, so UK goods become cheaper abroad (1); demand for UK exports rises, so exporters sell more and their revenue and profits increase (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — the exchange rate and a manufacturer
Invented data — Table 1: Fenwick Turbines (fictional UK firm)

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| £1 = € | 1.30 | 1.12 |
| Share of sales exported to the eurozone (%) | 60 | 60 |
| Share of components imported from the eurozone (%) | 35 | 35 |

Using Table 1, analyse how the change in the exchange rate may affect Fenwick Turbines. (6)

The pound has depreciated from €1.30 to €1.12, a fall of about 13.8% (2). Fenwick's turbines are now cheaper for eurozone buyers, so with 60% of sales exported there, demand and revenue should rise — especially if demand is price elastic — and the firm may expand output and hire workers (2). But 35% of its components come from the eurozone and now cost more in pounds, raising its costs; this may squeeze profit margins or force it to raise prices, offsetting part of the gain — so the overall effect is probably positive but smaller than the 13.8% fall suggests (2).
:::

Explain one reason why a rise in UK interest rates may cause the pound to appreciate. (2)
---

::: q
Explain one way a depreciation of the pound could harm UK consumers. (2)
---
Imports such as food, fuel and electronics cost more pounds (1), so consumers pay higher prices and their real income/cost of living worsens; foreign holidays are dearer too (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how an appreciation of the pound could affect the UK's current account. (6)
---
Indicative content: a stronger pound makes UK exports dearer in foreign currencies (1), so foreign demand for UK goods and services falls (1); imports become cheaper in pounds (1), so UK consumers and firms buy more imports (1); exports fall and imports rise, so the current account deficit widens (1); the size of the effect depends on price elasticity of demand for exports and imports, and on non-price factors such as quality (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **More UK imports means more pounds SUPPLIED** (to buy foreign currency) — the supply curve shifts **right** and the pound **falls**. Shifting demand here is the classic error.
- Foreign buyers of UK exports, tourists and investors **demand** pounds; UK buyers of imports and UK tourists **supply** them.
- A **strong pound makes imports cheaper and lowers inflation** — the hardest MCQ on the 2025 paper turned on this.
- On a draw-and-label question, label the axes (price of £ in another currency; quantity of £), both curves, the shift and **both** exchange rates.
- In conversions, multiply going from pounds to foreign currency and divide going the other way — check the answer makes sense.
- Write about the group named: "exporters" and "consumers" are affected in opposite ways.
:::

Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):

::: test
Explain one reason why a fall in the exchange rate may not increase a country's exports by much. (2)
---
If demand for its exports is price inelastic — e.g. they are specialised or bought for their quality (1) — a lower price in foreign currency leads to only a small rise in the quantity bought (1). (Or: exporters rely on imported inputs whose costs rise; buyers' incomes abroad are falling.)
:::
