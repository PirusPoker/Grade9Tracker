## What it is

Every time a UK firm sells to a customer abroad, a UK household buys an imported product, or money flows in or out as profits, wages or aid, it is recorded in the **balance of payments**. This topic covers the **balance of trade** and the **current account**, how to do **simple calculations with current account figures**, what a **deficit** or **surplus** means and why it matters, the **reasons** for a deficit or surplus, and the **government policies** that can influence it.

### The balance of payments and the current account

The **balance of payments** is a record of all the financial transactions between one country and the rest of the world over a period, usually a year. It has several parts; at GCSE the one that matters is the **current account**, which records trade and income flows.

| Component of the current account | What it records | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Trade in goods** | exports and imports of physical, tangible products | UK cars and medicines sold abroad (exports); German machinery or Spanish fruit bought by UK buyers (imports) |
| **Trade in services** | exports and imports of intangible products | a US firm buying insurance or legal advice from a London firm, or a tourist from abroad spending in the UK (exports); a UK family's holiday in Greece, or a UK firm using an overseas call centre (imports) |
| **Primary income** | income earned from owning assets abroad or working abroad | profits, dividends and interest from UK-owned firms and investments overseas (in); profits sent home by foreign-owned firms operating in the UK (out); wages of people working across borders |
| **Secondary income** | transfers — money sent with nothing given in return | UK overseas aid (out); money sent home by migrant workers, called remittances (out or in); payments to international organisations (out); pensions paid to UK pensioners living abroad (out) |

Two balances are used constantly:
- **Balance of trade** = **exports of goods and services − imports of goods and services**. It is sometimes split into the balance of trade in goods and the balance of trade in services.
- **Current account balance** = balance of trade in goods + balance of trade in services + primary income balance + secondary income balance.

A **current account deficit** means more money flows **out** of the country through these items than flows **in**. A **current account surplus** means more flows **in** than out.

**Exports bring money in; imports send money out.** A UK resident buying a holiday in Portugal is an **import** of a service, even though the person travels out — the money leaves the UK.

The UK's current account has been in **deficit for most of the past few decades**. The UK usually has a large **deficit in trade in goods** — it imports more manufactured goods, fuel and food than it exports — partly offset by a **surplus in trade in services**, where its financial, business, legal and creative services sell strongly abroad.

**Do not confuse the current account with the government budget.** The current account is about a **country's** transactions with the rest of the world. The budget balance (3.2.3.1) is about the **government's** own revenue and spending.

### Calculations with current account figures

Worked example (invented data, £ billion):

| Item | £bn |
|---|---|
| Exports of goods | 320 |
| Imports of goods | 410 |
| Exports of services | 290 |
| Imports of services | 170 |
| Primary income balance | −15 |
| Secondary income balance | −22 |

- Trade in goods balance = 320 − 410 = **−90** (a deficit)
- Trade in services balance = 290 − 170 = **+120** (a surplus)
- Balance of trade = −90 + 120 = **+30** (a surplus)
- Current account = 30 − 15 − 22 = **−7**, a **deficit of £7 billion**

If GDP is £1,400 billion, the deficit is 7 ÷ 1,400 × 100 = **0.5% of GDP**. Economists usually compare a deficit with GDP, because a £7 billion deficit is tiny for a large economy but serious for a small one.

Watch the signs: a balance that is already given as negative is **added** as a negative number, not subtracted again. And always say whether the answer is a **surplus or a deficit**.

### Why a deficit or surplus matters

| | **Current account deficit** | **Current account surplus** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | money out > money in | money in > money out |
| How it is paid for | the country must **borrow from abroad** or **sell assets** (shares, property, firms) to foreigners, or attract foreign investment | the country builds up **savings and assets abroad**, or lends to other countries |
| Possible problems | rising debts to foreigners; foreign ownership of more UK assets, so more profits flow out in future; it may show UK firms are **uncompetitive**; spending "leaks" abroad, so fewer jobs in UK exporting and import-competing industries; pressure for the pound to fall | some countries' surpluses are other countries' deficits — this can cause trade tensions; consumers may be consuming less than the country produces; the currency may rise, hurting exporters later |
| Why it may not matter | a **small** deficit (as a % of GDP) is easy to finance; it may reflect strong consumer spending or imports of machinery that raise future productivity; foreign investment brings jobs and technology | a surplus adds to demand and jobs at home |

A **persistent, large** deficit is a bigger concern than a small, temporary one. That is why the government's objective is described as a **satisfactory** or **sustainable** current account, not a surplus at all costs.

### Reasons for a current account deficit (or surplus)

| Reason | How it leads to a deficit | The opposite → surplus |
|---|---|---|
| **Low productivity** | lower output per worker → higher costs per unit → UK prices less competitive than foreign rivals' → fewer exports, more imports | high productivity keeps unit costs and prices low |
| **Higher inflation than trading partners** | UK goods become relatively dearer abroad and imports relatively cheaper at home | lower inflation than rivals |
| **A strong exchange rate** (3.2.4.2) | a high pound makes UK exports dearer for foreigners and imports cheaper for UK buyers | a weak currency boosts exports |
| **Rapid economic growth at home** | rising incomes mean consumers buy more of everything, including imports | a recession at home cuts spending on imports |
| **Recession in trading partners** | foreign buyers have less income, so they buy fewer UK exports | strong growth abroad lifts exports |
| **Poor non-price competitiveness** | weaker quality, design, reliability, after-sales service or marketing | strong brands and reputation |
| **Structure of the economy** | the decline of UK manufacturing (deindustrialisation) means many goods are now imported; few natural resources of some kinds | a country rich in oil, gas or minerals, or with a strong manufacturing base |

**Productivity is not the same as production.** Low *productivity* means low output **per worker or per hour**, which raises unit costs; a country can produce a lot in total and still have low productivity.

### Government policies to influence the current account

| Policy | How it could reduce a deficit | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Supply-side policies** (3.2.3.3): training, investment in infrastructure, support for research, lower taxes on business investment | raise **productivity** and quality, so UK firms compete better on price and non-price factors → more exports, fewer imports | slow to work; expensive; results uncertain |
| **Reducing total demand** — higher taxes, lower government spending (fiscal) or higher interest rates (monetary) | lower incomes and spending → fewer imports | also cuts spending on UK goods → slower growth, higher unemployment (a conflict of objectives); higher interest rates may strengthen the pound, which hurts exports |
| **A fall in the exchange rate** | exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home | imports of raw materials and energy cost more → cost-push inflation; the UK's floating pound is set by the market, so the government cannot simply choose its value |
| **Protectionism** — tariffs, quotas | makes imports dearer or limits them | retaliation against UK exports; higher prices for consumers; may break trade agreements; protects inefficient firms |
| **Export promotion** — trade missions, export finance and advice, new trade agreements | helps firms find and win overseas customers | limited scale; trade agreements also open the UK market to more imports |

::: terms
- **Balance of payments**: a record of all financial transactions between a country and the rest of the world over a period of time.
- **Current account**: the part of the balance of payments recording trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income.
- **Exports**: goods and services produced in one country and sold to buyers in other countries, bringing money in.
- **Imports**: goods and services bought from other countries, sending money out.
- **Balance of trade**: the value of exports of goods and services minus the value of imports of goods and services.
- **Trade in services**: exports and imports of intangible products such as tourism, banking, insurance and transport.
- **Primary income**: income flows from owning assets abroad or working abroad, such as profits, dividends, interest and wages.
- **Secondary income**: transfers between countries with nothing given in return, such as overseas aid and remittances.
- **Current account deficit**: when money flowing out on the current account is greater than money flowing in.
- **Current account surplus**: when money flowing in on the current account is greater than money flowing out.
- **Competitiveness**: the ability of a country's firms to sell their goods and services against foreign rivals, on price or quality.
:::

::: method
1. **Know the four components**: trade in goods, trade in services, primary income, secondary income. Exports = money in; imports = money out.
2. **Calculate step by step**: goods balance, services balance, then add the income balances, keeping minus signs. State surplus or deficit; give % of GDP if asked (balance ÷ GDP × 100).
3. **Reasons**: give the chain — e.g. low productivity → high unit costs → uncompetitive prices → fewer exports and more imports → deficit. Productivity ≠ production.
4. **Significance**: ask how large the deficit is relative to GDP, how long it has lasted and how it is financed.
5. **Policies**: explain how the policy changes exports or imports, then the cost — slower growth, inflation, retaliation or time lags.
6. **Never** mix up the current account with the government's budget balance.
:::

::: example State — components of the current account
State two components of the current account of the balance of payments. (2)

Any two (1 each): trade in goods; trade in services; primary income; secondary income.
:::

::: example Calculate — the current account balance
Invented data (£bn): exports of goods 180; imports of goods 230; exports of services 140; imports of services 85; primary income balance +6; secondary income balance −14. Calculate the current account balance and state whether it is a surplus or a deficit. (3)

Goods −50 and services +55, so the balance of trade = +5 (1); current account = 5 + 6 − 14 = −3 (1); a deficit of £3 billion (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — as a percentage of GDP
Invented data: Country L has a current account deficit of £27 billion and GDP of £900 billion. Calculate the deficit as a percentage of GDP. (2)

27 ÷ 900 × 100 (1) = 3% of GDP (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a reason for a deficit
Explain one reason why a country may have a current account deficit. (2)

If its workers' productivity is lower than in rival countries, its firms' costs per unit are higher (1), so its goods are less price-competitive — exports fall and buyers switch to cheaper imports, widening the deficit (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — trade in goods and services
Invented data — Country M (£bn): trade in goods balance −60 last year and −75 this year; trade in services balance +48 last year and +50 this year; primary and secondary income combined −10 in both years. Analyse the change in Country M's current account. (6)

The current account deficit has widened from £22bn (−60 + 48 − 10) to £35bn (−75 + 50 − 10), a rise of £13bn, almost entirely because the goods deficit grew by £15bn while services improved by only £2bn (2). A wider goods deficit suggests that M's manufacturers are losing competitiveness — perhaps higher inflation or a strong currency is making their exports dearer — or that rising incomes at home are sucking in more imported goods (2). Its services surplus offsets much of the goods deficit, so M's strengths lie in services; whether the deficit is a problem depends on its size relative to GDP and how it is financed — if it reflects imports of machinery that raise productivity, it may correct itself (2).
:::

::: q
Explain one way in which a current account deficit may be financed. (2)
---
The country can borrow from abroad (1), or sell assets such as shares, property or companies to foreign buyers, so money flows in to match the money flowing out on the current account (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a current account deficit and a budget deficit. (2)
---
A current account deficit is when a country's payments abroad for imports and other current items exceed its receipts from exports and income (1); a budget deficit is when the government's spending exceeds its revenue from taxes (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a fall in the value of the pound could affect the UK current account. (6)
---
Indicative content: a weaker pound makes UK exports cheaper in foreign currencies (1), so foreign demand for UK goods and services rises (1); imports become more expensive in pounds (1), so UK buyers switch to domestic products (1) → the deficit narrows (1); but if demand for exports and imports is price inelastic, or imported raw materials raise UK costs and inflation, the improvement may be small or slow (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Exports = money in; imports = money out.** A UK tourist abroad is a UK **import** of services.
- The current account is about the **country's** trade and income with the rest of the world — not the government's budget.
- In calculations, keep track of **minus signs** and always state **surplus or deficit**.
- **Productivity** (output per worker) is not **production** (total output). Low productivity raises unit costs.
- A deficit is not automatically bad — consider its **size relative to GDP**, how long it lasts and what is being imported.
- Policies to cut a deficit usually **conflict** with other objectives — say which, and why.
:::
