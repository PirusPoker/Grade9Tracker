## What it is

Supply is the producers' side of the market. This topic covers what supply means, why the supply curve slopes upwards, the factors that influence supply, how to **construct an individual firm's supply curve from production data**, and the difference between a **movement along** the supply curve and a **shift** of it.

### What supply means

**Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at a given price in a given time period.** As with demand, every part counts: *willing* (it must be worth their while), *able* (they must have the capacity to produce it), *at a given price* and *in a given time period*.

**Individual supply** is one firm's supply; **market supply** is the total supplied by all firms at each price — add the firms' quantities together at each price.

### Why the supply curve slopes upwards

As the **price rises, the quantity supplied rises** (other things being equal):

- **Profit incentive**: a higher price makes each unit more profitable, so firms are willing to produce and sell more (3.1.4.1 links higher prices to the incentive to expand).
- **Covering higher costs**: producing extra output often costs more per unit — overtime pay, using older machines, buying extra materials at short notice — and a higher price makes this worthwhile.
- **New producers**: at a higher price, firms that could not cover their costs before can now enter the market, and existing firms switch resources from less profitable products.

So the supply curve **slopes upwards from left to right**, with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.

### Factors that influence supply

The good's **own price** causes a **movement along** the supply curve. **Every other factor** changes how much firms will supply *at every price*, so it **shifts** the curve.

| Non-price factor | Shifts supply **right** (increase) when… | Shifts supply **left** (decrease) when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Costs of production** — wages, raw materials, energy, rent | costs fall | costs rise (e.g. dearer flour for a bakery) |
| **Technology and productivity** | better machines or methods raise output per worker | machinery breaks down; productivity falls |
| **Indirect taxes** (e.g. VAT, duty) | a tax is cut | a tax is introduced or raised — it adds to each unit's cost |
| **Subsidies** | a subsidy is given — it lowers each unit's cost | a subsidy is removed |
| **Number of firms** | new firms enter the market | firms close or leave |
| **Weather and natural events** (mainly agriculture) | a good harvest | drought, floods, disease, a poor harvest |
| **Expectations and the prices of other products** | — | firms switch to a more profitable alternative, or hold stock back expecting higher prices later |
| **Government regulation** | rules are relaxed | stricter safety or environmental rules raise costs |

Remember the key chain: **higher costs → each unit is less profitable at every price → firms supply less at every price → supply shifts left**.

| | **Movement along** the curve | **Shift** of the curve |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | a change in the good's **own price** | a change in a **non-price factor** |
| Words | **extension** (price rises, more supplied) / **contraction** (price falls, less supplied) | **increase** in supply (right) / **decrease** in supply (left) |
| Diagram | along the same curve | a new curve, S1 to S2 |

A leftward shift can also be read **vertically**: at every quantity, firms now need a *higher* price to supply it — which is why a tax or a cost rise moves supply "up and to the left".

::: terms
- **Supply**: the quantity of a good or service producers are willing and able to sell at a given price in a given time period.
- **Individual supply**: the quantity one firm is willing and able to sell at each price.
- **Market supply**: the total quantity all firms are willing and able to sell at each price.
- **Supply curve**: a graph showing the quantity supplied at each price, sloping upwards from left to right.
- **Extension of supply**: a rise in quantity supplied caused by a rise in the good's own price.
- **Contraction of supply**: a fall in quantity supplied caused by a fall in the good's own price.
- **Increase in supply**: a rightward shift of the supply curve caused by a non-price factor, such as lower costs.
- **Decrease in supply**: a leftward shift of the supply curve caused by a non-price factor, such as higher costs.
- **Costs of production**: the payments a firm makes for its inputs, such as wages, raw materials and energy.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending, such as VAT or fuel duty, which raises firms' costs per unit.
- **Subsidy**: a payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs per unit.
:::

::: method
1. **Ask: is it the good's own price?** Yes → movement (extension/contraction). No → shift (increase/decrease).
2. **Check it is a supply factor, not a demand factor** — costs, technology, taxes, subsidies, number of firms, weather. Income and tastes are demand factors.
3. **Write the chain**: factor → effect on costs or ability to produce → firms supply more/less at every price → shift right/left.
4. **Plotting**: price vertical, quantity horizontal, both labelled with units; even scales; label S.
5. **Draw and label**: axes; original S1; S2 in the correct direction; show the new quantity at the same price (or the new equilibrium if demand is drawn).
6. **Analyse/assess**: weigh several supply factors using the data; say which has the biggest effect and why.
:::

::: example Explain — a decrease in supply
Explain one factor that could decrease the supply of wheat. (2)

A drought or very wet summer could damage crops (1), so farmers can harvest less wheat at every price, shifting the supply curve to the left (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why supply slopes upwards
Explain why the quantity supplied of a good usually rises as its price rises. (2)

A higher price makes each unit more profitable to produce (1), so existing firms have an incentive to produce more and new firms are attracted into the market (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — market supply
Invented data: at a price of £20, Firm A supplies 300 units, Firm B 450 and Firm C 250. At £25, A supplies 380, B 520 and C 300. Calculate the percentage change in market supply when the price rises from £20 to £25. (3)

Market supply at £20 = 300 + 450 + 250 = 1,000; at £25 = 380 + 520 + 300 = 1,200 (1). Change = 200 units (1). Percentage change = 200 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 20% (1).
:::

::: example Draw and label — a subsidy
Draw and label a diagram to show the effect of a government subsidy on the supply of solar panels. (3)

Model answer (described): axes labelled Price and Quantity (1); an upward-sloping original supply curve S1 (1); a new supply curve S2 drawn to the **right** of S1, because the subsidy lowers producers' costs per unit so they supply more at every price (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — supply factors in data
Invented data on the UK market for bread over one year: the price of wheat rose 18%; energy prices for bakeries rose 25%; two large bakeries installed faster automated ovens; one regional bakery closed. Analyse the likely overall effect on the supply of bread. (6)

The 18% rise in wheat and the 25% rise in energy costs raise the cost of every loaf, making bread less profitable at each price, so bakeries supply less — the supply curve shifts left (2). The closure of a regional bakery reduces the number of firms, shifting market supply further left (1). Against this, faster automated ovens raise productivity and lower the cost per loaf for two large bakeries, shifting supply to the right (2). Overall, supply is likely to decrease, because cost rises affect every bakery whereas the new ovens help only two firms; the size of the shift depends on how much of total output those two firms produce (1).
:::

State two factors that could cause the supply of a good to decrease. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): higher costs of production (wages, raw materials, energy); a new or higher indirect tax; removal of a subsidy; firms leaving the market; bad weather/natural disaster; stricter regulation.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between an extension of supply and an increase in supply. (2)
---
An extension is a movement up the existing supply curve caused by a rise in the good's own price (1); an increase is a shift of the whole curve to the right caused by a non-price factor such as lower costs (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data — a printing firm supplies 200 T-shirts a week at £6, 300 at £8, 400 at £10 and 500 at £12. Calculate the percentage change in quantity supplied when the price rises from £8 to £10. (2)
---
(400 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 (1) = 33.3% (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how an increase in the minimum wage could affect the supply of restaurant meals. (6)
---
Restaurants employ many low-paid workers, so a higher minimum wage raises their labour costs (1). Each meal becomes more expensive to produce and less profitable at every price (1), so restaurants supply fewer meals at each price — supply shifts left (1). Some restaurants may close, reducing the number of firms and shifting supply further left (1). Others may reduce costs by using ordering apps or fewer staff, limiting the shift (1); the size of the effect depends on the share of wages in total costs (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **A rise in the cost of raw materials shifts SUPPLY left** — students often shift demand instead and lose every diagram mark.
- Income, tastes and advertising are **demand** factors; do not offer them for supply.
- A **tax** shifts supply left; a **subsidy** shifts it right.
- Use extension/contraction for movements and increase/decrease for shifts.
- Supply slopes **upwards** — label it S, and label both axes.
- Data questions: work out which factors push supply right and which push it left, then judge the **net** effect.
:::

::: test
Assess whether a rise in energy prices is likely to have a large effect on the supply of goods in an economy. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Large effect: energy is used in almost all production — heating, lighting, running machinery, transport — so costs rise for nearly every firm; energy-intensive industries (steel, glass, chemicals, bakeries) face a large rise in cost per unit and may cut output or close, shifting supply left; transport costs feed through to supply chains; some firms may stop producing altogether.
Smaller effect: for many service firms energy is a small share of costs (e.g. accountancy, software); firms may have fixed-price energy contracts in the short run; some can switch to more efficient equipment or renewable sources; government support schemes may cap prices; firms may absorb some of the cost by accepting lower profit rather than cutting output.
Conclusion: e.g. the effect is large for energy-intensive manufacturing and agriculture but smaller for services; it depends on energy's share of total costs, how long the price rise lasts and whether firms can substitute or receive support.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis using supply concepts with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported conclusion; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
