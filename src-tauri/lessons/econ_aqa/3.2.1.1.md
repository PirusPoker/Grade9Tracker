## What it is

Interest rates are the first piece of the national economy you study on Paper 2, and they come back again and again — in monetary policy, inflation, growth and exchange rates. This topic covers **what an interest rate is**, the **factors that make different interest rates higher or lower**, how changes in interest rates affect **consumers' decisions to save, borrow or spend**, how they affect **producers' decisions to save, borrow or invest**, and **how to calculate interest on savings**.

### What is an interest rate?

**An interest rate is the price of borrowing money and the reward for saving it, expressed as a percentage of the amount borrowed or saved, usually per year.**

- A **borrower** pays interest to the lender on top of repaying the amount borrowed (the **principal**).
- A **saver** receives interest from the bank or building society for letting it use their money.

If you borrow £1,000 for a year at 8%, you repay £1,000 plus £80 interest. If you save £1,000 for a year at 3%, you receive £30 interest.

In the UK, the **Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee** sets the **Bank Rate** — the base rate that influences all the other interest rates in the economy (3.2.3.2). When Bank Rate rises, banks usually raise the rates they charge borrowers and pay savers.

### Why are there so many different interest rates?

At any one time there are hundreds of different rates. They differ because of:

| Factor | Effect on the rate | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Bank Rate** | most rates move up and down with it | a rise in Bank Rate pushes up mortgage, loan and savings rates |
| **Risk of the loan** | the riskier the loan, the higher the rate — lenders need compensation for the chance of not being repaid | an unsecured personal loan or credit card costs more than a mortgage |
| **Security (collateral)** | a **secured** loan is backed by an asset the lender can take if the borrower does not repay | a mortgage is secured on the house, so its rate is lower |
| **Borrower's credit history** | people or firms with a record of missed payments are charged more, or refused | a firm with a strong track record borrows more cheaply |
| **Length of the loan or saving** | longer fixed-term savings usually pay more because the saver gives up access to the money; longer loans may cost more because there is more time for things to go wrong | a two-year fixed-rate savings bond vs an instant-access account |
| **Amount** | very small loans can carry higher rates because the admin costs are spread over less money | a short-term "payday" loan |
| **Competition between lenders** | more competition pushes borrowing rates down and savings rates up | online banks offering better savings rates to win customers |
| **Saving versus borrowing** | banks charge borrowers more than they pay savers — the gap (the margin) covers costs and gives the bank its profit | a bank pays savers 3% and charges borrowers 7% |

**Why are personal loans more expensive than mortgages?** This is a classic question that the examiners found poorly answered. A mortgage is **secured** on the property — if the borrower does not repay, the lender can repossess and sell the house to recover the money. An unsecured personal loan has **no asset backing it**, so the lender faces a greater **risk** of losing its money and charges a higher interest rate to compensate.

### How interest-rate changes affect consumers

| A **rise** in interest rates means… | Consumer response |
|---|---|
| **saving** becomes more rewarding | some households save more and spend less |
| **borrowing** becomes more expensive — loans, credit cards, car finance | fewer people borrow to buy cars, holidays, furniture; spending on big-ticket items falls |
| **mortgage repayments** rise for people on variable or newly fixed mortgages | less **disposable income** left to spend on other goods and services |
| homes become harder to afford for first-time buyers | demand for houses falls |
| savers who rely on interest (often pensioners) receive more income | they may spend a little more |

A **fall** in interest rates has the opposite effects: saving is less attractive, borrowing is cheaper, mortgage payments fall, and consumer spending tends to rise.

Overall, **higher interest rates usually reduce consumer spending; lower rates usually increase it.** But the effect differs between groups: borrowers lose and savers gain when rates rise.

### How interest-rate changes affect producers

| A **rise** in interest rates means… | Producer response |
|---|---|
| the **cost of borrowing for investment** rises — new machinery, buildings, expansion | fewer investment projects are profitable, so firms invest less |
| firms with existing variable-rate loans or overdrafts pay more interest | higher costs, lower profit; less money to invest |
| money kept in the bank (e.g. **retained profit**) earns more | firms may prefer to **save** profits rather than invest them |
| consumers spend less, especially on goods bought on credit | lower demand and sales for firms selling cars, furniture, houses, holidays |
| the pound may rise in value as foreign savers buy pounds (3.2.4.2) | exporters' goods become more expensive abroad |

When interest rates fall, borrowing to invest is cheaper, saving retained profit earns less, and consumer demand rises — so investment usually increases.

### Calculating interest on savings

**Annual interest = amount saved × interest rate ÷ 100.**

Example: £2,400 saved for one year at 3.5%: £2,400 × 3.5 ÷ 100 = **£84**.

**Part of a year** — scale the annual interest by the fraction of the year:
£2,400 at 3.5% for **9 months**: £84 × 9/12 = **£63**.

**Total in the account** = principal + interest = £2,400 + £84 = £2,484. But if a question asks **"how much interest"**, the answer is **only the interest** (£84), not the total. Adding back the principal was the commonest error in June 2025.

**Compound interest (extension)** — if interest is left in the account, next year's interest is earned on the larger balance:
£5,000 at 4% for 2 years: Year 1 interest = £200 → balance £5,200. Year 2 interest = £5,200 × 4% = £208 → balance £5,408. Total interest = **£408** (compared with £400 simple interest). The shortcut is £5,000 × 1.04 × 1.04 = £5,408.

**Interest rates and inflation**: if a saver earns 3% interest but prices rise by 5%, the real value of the savings falls — the money buys less than before, even though the balance has grown.

::: terms
- **Interest rate**: the price of borrowing money and the reward for saving, shown as a percentage per year.
- **Bank Rate**: the base interest rate set by the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee, which influences other rates.
- **Principal**: the original sum of money saved or borrowed, on which interest is calculated.
- **Saving**: income that is not spent, often kept in a bank or building society to earn interest.
- **Borrowing**: receiving money from a lender that must be repaid later with interest.
- **Mortgage**: a long-term loan to buy property, secured on that property.
- **Secured loan**: a loan backed by an asset that the lender can take if the borrower fails to repay.
- **Unsecured loan**: a loan with no asset backing it, so it carries more risk for the lender.
- **Investment**: spending by firms on capital goods such as machinery and buildings to increase future production.
- **Disposable income**: the income a household has left to spend or save after taxes and essential payments.
- **Compound interest**: interest earned on both the original principal and the interest already added.
:::

::: method
1. **Define** an interest rate as the price of borrowing / reward for saving, as a percentage.
2. **Different rates**: link the rate to **risk** (secured vs unsecured, credit history), length, amount, competition and the bank's margin.
3. **Consumers**: follow the chain for save, borrow **and** spend — rate ↑ → saving more attractive / borrowing dearer / mortgage payments up → less disposable income → spending falls.
4. **Producers**: rate ↑ → borrowing for investment dearer → fewer profitable projects; retained profit earns more if saved; consumer demand falls.
5. **Calculations**: interest = principal × rate ÷ 100; part year × months/12; "how much interest" = the interest only. Show working and give £ to 2 decimal places where needed.
6. **15-mark**: use the Item's numbers, consider different groups (savers, borrowers, firms), weigh short and long term, and judge.
:::

::: example Calculate — interest for part of a year
Invented data: Aisha saves £3,600 in an account paying 4.2% a year. She withdraws all her money after 8 months. Calculate how much interest she earns. (2)

Annual interest = £3,600 × 4.2 ÷ 100 = £151.20 (1). For 8 months = £151.20 × 8/12 = £100.80 (1).
:::

::: example Explain — loan rates and mortgage rates
Explain why the interest rate on a personal loan is usually higher than the interest rate on a mortgage. (2)

A mortgage is secured on the house, so if the borrower cannot repay, the lender can sell the property to get its money back (1); a personal loan is unsecured, so the lender faces a greater risk of losing money and charges a higher rate to compensate (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a rise in rates and consumers
Explain one effect of a rise in interest rates on consumers. (2)

People on variable-rate mortgages face higher monthly repayments (1), leaving them with less disposable income to spend on other goods and services, so their consumer spending falls (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a fall in rates and producers
Explain one way a fall in interest rates could affect a firm's decision to invest. (2)

Borrowing to buy new machinery becomes cheaper, so the interest cost of the investment falls (1); more projects become profitable, so the firm is more likely to borrow and invest (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — compound interest
Invented data: £8,000 is saved for 2 years at 5% a year, with the interest added to the account at the end of each year. Calculate the total interest earned. (3)

Year 1 interest = £8,000 × 5% = £400, balance £8,400 (1). Year 2 interest = £8,400 × 5% = £420 (1). Total interest = £400 + £420 = £820 (1).
:::

::: test
Assess whether a rise in interest rates always reduces investment by firms. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Reduces investment: borrowing costs rise, so fewer projects are profitable; firms with retained profit may prefer to earn the higher return from saving; consumer spending falls, so firms expect lower sales and need less capacity; firms with existing debts have less profit left to invest.
Not always: firms using retained profit or share capital rather than loans are less affected; if demand is strong (e.g. a booming market or new technology) the expected return may still exceed the higher interest cost; business confidence and expectations may matter more than a small change in rates; fixed-rate loans delay the effect; investment needed to meet regulations or replace broken machinery goes ahead anyway; small rises have small effects.
Conclusion: e.g. higher rates usually reduce investment, especially for firms that rely on borrowing and sell goods bought on credit, but not always — the effect depends on the size of the rise, how firms finance investment, and business confidence.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
