## What it is

**Money** is anything that is **generally accepted as a means of payment** for goods and services and for settling debts. Without it, people would have to **barter** — swap goods directly — which is slow and awkward. This topic covers the **four functions of money** (medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account and means of deferred payment) and why money is **defined as more than the banknotes and coins in circulation**.

### Why we need money: the problem with barter

In a **barter** economy, a farmer who wants shoes must find a shoemaker who wants eggs — at the same time and in the right quantities. This **double coincidence of wants** rarely happens. Barter also has other problems:
- there is no common measure of value — how many eggs is a pair of shoes worth? Every good would need a price in terms of every other good;
- many goods cannot be divided (half a cow) or stored (eggs go off);
- it is hard to save or to lend for the future.

Money solves each of these problems. Each solution is one of its **functions**.

### The four functions of money

| Function | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Medium of exchange** | money is accepted in exchange for goods and services, so buyers and sellers no longer need a double coincidence of wants | paying £3.50 for a sandwich by card; an employer paying wages, which workers then spend; a firm paying a supplier |
| **Store of value** | money can be kept and used later to buy things — it holds its purchasing power over time (as long as inflation is low) | putting part of your wages in a savings account to buy a car next year; a firm keeping cash reserves |
| **Unit of account** | money provides a common measure of value, so the prices of different goods can be compared and costs, revenues and profits can be recorded | a price label saying £1.20; comparing a £25 game with a £60 pair of trainers; a firm's accounts showing profit of £40,000; GDP measured in pounds |
| **Means of deferred payment** | money allows payment to be made at a later date, so goods can be bought now and paid for later, and debts can be agreed in money terms | a mortgage repaid over 25 years; paying for a phone in monthly instalments; a firm buying stock on 30-day credit |

**Matching function to example** — look at what the money is **doing**:
- money **changing hands now** for a good → **medium of exchange**;
- money being **kept for later** → **store of value**;
- money being used to **measure, price, compare or record** → **unit of account**;
- a **debt** or payment **agreed now but made later** → **means of deferred payment**.

The same purchase can involve several functions: a shop **labels** a coat at £80 (unit of account); a customer **pays** for it (medium of exchange) with money she had **saved** (store of value); or she buys it on **credit** and repays next month (deferred payment).

### Inflation and the functions of money

Money only does its job well if its value is **stable**. When **inflation** is high, money loses purchasing power quickly:
- it becomes a **poor store of value** — £1,000 saved buys less next year;
- it is a less reliable **unit of account**, as prices change constantly;
- lenders lose out on **deferred payments**, because they are repaid in money worth less (so they charge higher interest);
- in extreme cases (**hyperinflation**) people may stop accepting the currency at all, using foreign currency or barter instead — money stops being a **medium of exchange**.

This links to the price stability objective (3.2.2.4) and to interest on savings: the **real** return on savings is roughly the **interest rate minus the inflation rate**. Savings earning 3% when inflation is 5% lose about 2% of their purchasing power a year.

### Characteristics of good money (supporting knowledge)

To carry out its functions, money should be: **acceptable** (people trust it and take it), **durable** (doesn't wear out quickly), **portable** (easy to carry), **divisible** (can be split into small amounts — pence), **scarce/limited in supply** (so it keeps its value) and **hard to forge**. Gold, shells and cigarettes have all been used as money in the past; today money is mostly digital.

### Money is more than notes and coins

**Notes and coins** (cash) are only a **small part** of the money in the UK economy — just a few per cent of the total. Most money exists as **bank deposits**: the balances in people's and firms' **current accounts** and **savings accounts**, held as electronic records at banks and building societies.

Why bank deposits count as money:
- they are **generally accepted** for payments — you can pay with a debit card, contactless phone payment, bank transfer or direct debit, and the money moves from one account to another without any cash changing hands;
- they can be **stored** and earn interest;
- they are recorded and priced in **pounds**.

Most payments in the UK are now made by card or bank transfer rather than cash. Much of this money is **created by banks when they lend**: when a bank grants a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit, which the borrower can then spend — adding to the money in the economy.

So economists measure the **money supply** in different ways:
- **narrow money** — notes and coins plus money that can be spent instantly, such as current-account balances;
- **broad money** — narrow money plus savings deposits and other accounts that can be turned into spending money fairly easily.

Things that are **not** money:
- a **credit card** itself is not money — it is a way of **borrowing**; the debt must be paid later with money (so it uses money as a means of deferred payment);
- a **debit card** is not money either — it is a way of **transferring** the money in a bank account.

::: terms
- **Money**: anything generally accepted as a means of payment for goods and services and for settling debts.
- **Barter**: the direct exchange of one good or service for another without using money.
- **Double coincidence of wants**: the need, under barter, for each trader to want exactly what the other is offering.
- **Medium of exchange**: the function of money as something accepted in payment for goods and services.
- **Store of value**: the function of money as a way of holding purchasing power to spend in the future.
- **Unit of account**: the function of money as a common measure of value for pricing, comparing and recording.
- **Means of deferred payment**: the function of money that allows debts to be agreed now and paid at a later date.
- **Bank deposits**: money held in current and savings accounts at banks and building societies, which can be used for payments.
- **Money supply**: the total amount of money in an economy, including notes, coins and bank deposits.
- **Purchasing power**: the quantity of goods and services that a given amount of money can buy.
:::

::: method
1. **Learn the four functions by name** and a clear example of each — medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account, means of deferred payment.
2. **Matching questions**: ask what the money is doing in the example — paying now, keeping for later, measuring/pricing, or settling a debt later.
3. **Explain = function + why it matters**: e.g. medium of exchange removes the need for a double coincidence of wants, so trade and specialisation are easier.
4. **"More than notes and coins"**: bank deposits in current and savings accounts are accepted for payment by card and transfer; they are most of the money supply; banks create deposits when they lend.
5. **Calculations**: percentage shares of the money supply; interest on savings; real return = interest rate − inflation rate.
6. **Evaluate**: inflation weakens money's functions; cards are not money themselves but ways of moving or borrowing it.
:::

::: example Calculate — share of the money supply
Invented data: in Country Q, notes and coins in circulation total £85 billion, current-account deposits £1,100 billion and savings deposits £1,315 billion. Calculate notes and coins as a percentage of the total money supply, to one decimal place. (2)

Total = 85 + 1,100 + 1,315 = £2,500 billion (1). 85 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **3.4%** (1).

::: example Explain — money more than notes and coins
Explain why money can be defined as more than the notes and coins in circulation. (2)

Balances in bank current and savings accounts are generally accepted as payment through debit cards, contactless and bank transfers (1), and these deposits make up most of the money in the economy — far more than cash — so they count as money too (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — high inflation and money
Invented data — Table 1: Country Z

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inflation (%) | 4 | 35 | 210 |
| Interest rate on savings (%) | 3 | 12 | 40 |
| Share of shops accepting foreign currency (%) | 5 | 20 | 65 |

Using Table 1, analyse how rising inflation affected the functions of money in Country Z. (6)

As inflation rose from 4% to 210%, money lost purchasing power rapidly, so it became a poor **store of value**: in Year 3 savings earned 40% while prices rose 210%, so £100 saved grew to £140 while goods that had cost £100 cost £310 — the savings lost more than half their purchasing power (140 ÷ 310 ≈ 0.45) (2). Prices changed constantly, so money was a weak **unit of account** — shops had to keep relabelling, and lenders would be repaid in money worth far less, making **deferred payment** risky; they charged higher interest (3% to 40%) to compensate (2). The share of shops accepting foreign currency rose from 5% to 65%, showing that people were losing trust in the currency as a **medium of exchange** and switching to a more stable money (2).
:::

---

The government says this would cut crime and tax evasion and save banks £300 million a year in handling cash. ---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
