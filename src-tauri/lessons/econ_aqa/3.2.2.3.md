## What it is

Full employment is one of the government's principal objectives (3.2.2.1). This topic covers how **employment and unemployment are measured**, including the unemployment-rate calculation that costs many candidates marks; the main **types and causes** of unemployment — **structural, seasonal, frictional and cyclical**; the **consequences** of unemployment for different groups in the economy; and the **government policies** used to reduce it.

### Who counts as unemployed?

The **working-age population** can be split into three groups:

| Group | Who is in it |
|---|---|
| **Employed** | people in paid work, full-time or part-time, including the self-employed |
| **Unemployed** | people **without a job who are actively looking for work and able to start** |
| **Economically inactive** | people without a job who are **not looking for work or not available** — full-time students, retired people, carers looking after family, the long-term sick or disabled, people who have given up looking |

The **economically active** population (also called the **labour force**) = **employed + unemployed**. The economically inactive are **not** part of the labour force.

### Measuring unemployment

**The unemployment rate = number unemployed ÷ economically active (employed + unemployed) × 100.**

Using the invented figures above: 1.5m ÷ (30m + 1.5m) × 100 = 1.5 ÷ 31.5 × 100 = **4.8%**.

The common mistake is to divide by the whole working-age population (1.5 ÷ 40 × 100 = 3.75%) — that is **wrong**, because students, retired people and carers are not in the labour force. The examiners report this error every year.

The UK uses two measures:

| Measure | How it works | Strengths and weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **Labour Force Survey (LFS) / ILO measure** | a regular survey of households asks whether people are without a job, have looked for work recently and could start soon | internationally comparable and includes people not entitled to benefits; but based on a sample, so subject to error |
| **Claimant count** | counts the number of people **claiming unemployment-related benefits** | cheap and quick, from official records; but **misses** unemployed people who are not eligible or choose not to claim, and changes whenever benefit rules change |

The two measures give different totals. The claimant count misses unemployed people who are not eligible or do not claim, which tends to make it **lower**; but because it depends on benefit rules, a change in those rules can push it up or down even if the real number of unemployed has not changed.

The **employment rate** = employed ÷ working-age population × 100 (here 30 ÷ 40 = 75%) — a different statistic, so check which one the question asks for.

### Types and causes of unemployment

| Type | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | a long-term **decline in an industry** leaves workers whose skills or location no longer match the jobs available (**skills mismatch** or **geographical mismatch**); caused by changes in demand, new technology or foreign competition | coal miners after pit closures; shop workers as shopping moves online; workers replaced by automation |
| **Seasonal** | demand for some workers **rises and falls with the time of year** | fruit pickers, ski instructors, seaside hotel staff in winter, extra retail staff after Christmas |
| **Frictional** | people **between jobs** — the short time spent searching for a new job after leaving one, or after finishing education | a graduate applying for jobs; a worker who has resigned and is attending interviews |
| **Cyclical** (demand-deficient) | a **fall in total demand** in the economy during a downturn or recession; firms sell less, so they need fewer workers | widespread job losses across many industries in a recession |

Structural and cyclical unemployment tend to be the most serious: structural because it can last for years and hit whole communities; cyclical because it affects many industries at once. Frictional and seasonal unemployment are usually short-term — some frictional unemployment exists even when the economy is at "full employment".

### Consequences of unemployment

| Group | Consequences |
|---|---|
| **Unemployed individuals and their families** | loss of income and lower living standards; debt; stress, anxiety and poorer mental and physical health; **loss of skills** the longer they are out of work, making it harder to get a job; loss of confidence |
| **Firms** | lower sales, because unemployed people spend less (especially for firms selling non-essentials); but **easier recruitment** — a larger pool of applicants and less pressure to raise wages |
| **Government** | **lower tax revenue** (less income tax, NICs and VAT) and **higher spending on benefits**; so a larger budget deficit or less money for other services — an **opportunity cost** |
| **The economy** | **lost output** — unemployed workers are wasted resources, so GDP is lower than it could be; a fall in spending can cause further job losses |
| **Local communities** | in areas where a major employer closes: rising poverty, empty shops, young people moving away, more crime and social problems |

### Government policies to reduce unemployment

The right policy depends on the **type** of unemployment:

| Type | Suitable policies |
|---|---|
| **Cyclical** | **expansionary fiscal policy** — more government spending (e.g. on infrastructure) or tax cuts to raise demand; **expansionary monetary policy** — lower interest rates to encourage spending and investment |
| **Structural** | **supply-side policies** — retraining and education for new skills, apprenticeships, help to relocate, subsidies or tax incentives for firms that set up in areas of high unemployment |
| **Frictional** | better information about vacancies (job centres, online job matching), careers advice, reducing the time spent between jobs |
| **Seasonal** | encouraging year-round tourism or work, helping workers find complementary jobs in the off-season |

Each policy has drawbacks: more spending raises government borrowing and can cause inflation; lower interest rates hurt savers and work slowly; retraining takes time, costs money and may not match the jobs actually available.

::: terms
- **Unemployment**: the state of people of working age who are without a job, actively looking for work and able to start.
- **Unemployment rate**: the number unemployed divided by the economically active population, times 100.
- **Economically active**: people of working age who are either employed or unemployed — the labour force.
- **Economically inactive**: people of working age not in work and not looking for work, such as students, retired people and carers.
- **Claimant count**: a measure of unemployment that counts people claiming unemployment-related benefits.
- **Structural unemployment**: long-term unemployment caused by the decline of an industry, leaving a mismatch of skills or location.
- **Seasonal unemployment**: unemployment caused by falls in demand for workers at certain times of the year.
- **Frictional unemployment**: short-term unemployment of people moving between jobs.
- **Cyclical unemployment**: unemployment caused by a fall in total demand during an economic downturn or recession.
- **Retraining**: providing new skills to workers so they can move into different jobs or industries.
:::

::: method
1. **Unemployment rate**: unemployed ÷ (employed + unemployed) × 100. Never divide by the working-age population. Show the working; give the decimal places asked.
2. **Types**: match the cause to the type — industry decline → structural; time of year → seasonal; between jobs → frictional; recession / low demand → cyclical.
3. **Define a type** (2 marks): what it is (1) + its cause or an example (1).
4. **Consequences**: name the group the question asks about and give a developed chain for that group only.
5. **Policies**: match the policy to the type (fiscal/monetary for cyclical, supply-side for structural) and explain the chain to more jobs.
6. **Explain two / 15-mark**: evaluate cost, time lags, inflation risk and whether the policy fits the type of unemployment in the data.
:::

::: example Calculate — the unemployment rate
Invented data: in a region, 2.28 million people are employed, 120,000 are unemployed and 600,000 are economically inactive. Calculate the unemployment rate. (3)

Economically active = 2,280,000 + 120,000 = 2,400,000 (1). Rate = 120,000 ÷ 2,400,000 (1) × 100 = 5% (1).
:::

::: example Define — seasonal unemployment
Define the term "seasonal unemployment". (2)

Unemployment that occurs at certain times of the year (1) because demand for some workers falls when their industry is out of season, e.g. seaside hotel staff in winter (1).
:::

::: example Explain — structural unemployment
Explain one cause of structural unemployment. (2)

New technology such as self-service checkouts and online shopping reduces the demand for shop workers (1); workers whose skills were suited to retail may not have the skills needed for the jobs being created, so they stay unemployed for a long time (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a consequence for government
Explain one consequence of rising unemployment for the government. (2)

The government receives less income tax and VAT because unemployed people earn and spend less, while it pays out more in benefits (1), so its budget deficit rises or it must cut spending on other services such as education (1).
:::

::: example Explain two — policies to reduce unemployment
Explain two policies the government could use to reduce unemployment. (6)

Lower interest rates (monetary policy): borrowing becomes cheaper, so consumers spend more and firms invest more (1); higher demand means firms need to produce more (1), so they hire more workers, reducing cyclical unemployment (1).
Retraining schemes (supply-side): the government funds courses in skills that employers need, such as engineering or care (1); workers from declining industries can then apply for new jobs (1), reducing structural unemployment, though it takes time and money (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why the claimant count may be lower than the Labour Force Survey measure of unemployment. (2)
---
Some unemployed people are not entitled to benefits (e.g. because a partner earns too much) or choose not to claim (1), so they are counted by the survey but not by the claimant count (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how cyclical unemployment can affect firms. (6)
---
Indicative content: in a recession many people lose their jobs (1), so their incomes and spending fall (1); firms, especially those selling non-essentials, face lower sales and profits (1), so they may cut output and lay off more workers, deepening the downturn (1). However, firms find it easier to recruit and face less pressure to raise wages (1), which may help firms that are still expanding; firms selling cheap essentials may gain customers (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ economically active** (employed + unemployed) — dividing by the working-age population cost most of the marks in 2025.
- Students, retired people and carers are **economically inactive**, not unemployed.
- Do not confuse **structural** (industry decline, long-term) with **cyclical** (recession, many industries) — use the cause in the data to decide.
- **Seasonal** needs the time-of-year link; **frictional** is short-term, between jobs.
- Consequences must match the group: "for government" means tax revenue and benefit spending, not lost income for the worker.
- Policies should fit the type — lower interest rates do little for miners whose skills no longer match the available jobs.
:::

::: test
Assess whether retraining schemes are the best way to reduce unemployment. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For: they tackle structural unemployment directly by matching workers' skills to available jobs; they raise productivity and long-run growth; they do not add to inflation; they help long-term unemployed people regain confidence and skills.
Against: they take time to work and cost money (opportunity cost); they do little for cyclical unemployment — if there are no jobs because demand is low, new skills do not create vacancies; schemes may train people for the wrong jobs; workers may still be unable to move to where jobs are; older workers may be reluctant to retrain. Alternatives: fiscal or monetary policy to raise demand; relocation grants; incentives for firms to invest in high-unemployment areas.
Conclusion: e.g. retraining is the best policy for structural unemployment but not for cyclical, where raising demand is more effective; the best approach depends on the type of unemployment and usually combines policies.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Item A — Unemployment in Country W (invented)

Unemployment in Country W has risen over the last year. Part of the rise is linked to a slowdown in the economy: shops, restaurants and builders report fewer customers and have cut staff across the country. But in the north-east region, unemployment has been high for years because the steel and shipbuilding industries that once employed thousands have closed, and many former workers lack the skills needed for the new technology and healthcare jobs in the region's cities. The government already has a budget deficit, and inflation is 2.3%, close to its 2% target. Opposition politicians are calling for a large increase in government spending on infrastructure to create jobs.

| | Last year | This year |
|---|---|---|
| National unemployment rate (%) | 4.1 | 5.6 |
| North-east unemployment rate (%) | 7.8 | 8.4 |
| Real GDP growth (%) | 1.2 | −0.3 |
| Government budget deficit (% of GDP) | 3.0 | 4.2 |

Do you think the government of Country W should increase its spending to reduce unemployment? Use Item A and your own economic knowledge to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 5, AO3 10. Indicative content:
Use of data: national unemployment up 1.5 percentage points (to 5.6%); north-east up only 0.6 points but at a much higher level (8.4%); GDP fell 0.3% — the economy is shrinking; deficit up from 3.0% to 4.2% of GDP; inflation 2.3% close to target.
For more spending: the national rise is largely cyclical — falling demand (GDP −0.3%) caused firms to cut staff across many industries; government spending on infrastructure raises demand directly (construction jobs) and indirectly as workers spend wages, reversing cyclical unemployment; with inflation near target and output falling, there is spare capacity, so the risk of demand-pull inflation is small; unemployment itself lowers tax revenue and raises benefit spending, so cutting it may partly pay for itself; benefits to individuals and communities (health, skills).
Against / limits: the deficit is already 4.2% of GDP and rising — more spending means more borrowing and interest costs (opportunity cost, burden on future taxpayers); time lags — infrastructure projects take time to start; in the north-east the problem is structural — skills mismatch — so general spending may create jobs that former steel workers cannot fill; supply-side policies (retraining in technology and healthcare skills, help to relocate or commute to the cities) would target it better; lower interest rates (monetary policy) could boost demand without raising the deficit.
Evaluation: the decision depends on the mix of cyclical and structural unemployment — spending can tackle the national cyclical rise but not the north-east's long-term problem; targeting spending (e.g. infrastructure in the north-east plus retraining) could address both; the risk to government finances depends on how long the downturn lasts.
Conclusion: a supported view, e.g. a temporary, targeted increase in spending is justified because most of the rise is cyclical and inflation is close to target, but it should be combined with retraining for the north-east rather than relying on spending alone.
Levels: 13–15 — sustained, balanced evaluation using the Item's data, developed chains on both sides and a fully supported judgement; 10–12 — well-developed analysis with good use of the Item and a reasoned judgement, some imbalance; 7–9 — some developed analysis of both sides with some use of the Item, partly supported judgement; 4–6 — limited analysis, mainly one-sided or descriptive, little use of data; 1–3 — isolated points, assertion.
:::
