## What it is

Two words that sound alike are tested every year because candidates mix them up. This topic covers the difference between **production** and **productivity**, the **factors that influence productivity**, and the **benefits of increased productivity** for firms, workers, consumers and the whole economy.

### Production versus productivity

**Production** is the **total output** of goods and services made by a firm, an industry or a country in a time period — for example, 5,000 loaves a week.

**Productivity** is **output per unit of input** — how efficiently inputs are turned into output. The most common measure is **labour productivity**:

**Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers** (or ÷ number of hours worked)

| | Production | Productivity |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | how **much** is made in total | how **efficiently** it is made |
| Example | a factory makes 12,000 phone cases a week | each worker makes 400 cases a week |
| How it rises | hire more workers, add more machines, open a second site — or make each input more efficient | each worker, machine or hour produces **more** output |
| Can it rise while the other falls? | yes — hiring many untrained workers can raise production while output per worker falls | yes — a firm can cut staff and output yet make more per worker |

The key test: **more output is not higher productivity unless output per worker (or per hour) has risen.** If a firm doubles its workforce and doubles output, production has doubled but productivity is unchanged.

Productivity can also be measured for other inputs: output per machine, output per hectare of farmland (land productivity) or output per pound of capital invested. At GCSE, labour productivity is the one you will calculate.

### Calculating productivity and its change

Invented data for a bakery:

| | Before training | After training |
|---|---|---|
| Workers | 8 | 8 |
| Loaves per week | 2,400 | 2,760 |
| Labour productivity (loaves per worker per week) | 2,400 ÷ 8 = **300** | 2,760 ÷ 8 = **345** |

**Percentage change in productivity = (new − old) ÷ old × 100** = (345 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = **15%**.

Per hour is often more accurate than per worker, because some workers are part-time. If 10 workers each work 35 hours (350 hours) and produce 7,000 units, productivity is 7,000 ÷ 350 = **20 units per worker-hour**.

Watch a trap: when a firm hires extra workers, total output may rise but **output per worker may fall** — for instance, if the new workers are untrained or there are not enough machines for them all.

### Factors that influence productivity

| Factor | How it raises output per worker |
|---|---|
| **Education and training** | skilled workers work faster, make fewer mistakes and can use more advanced equipment |
| **Capital investment and technology** | better machinery, computers, software and automation let each worker produce far more (a delivery driver with route-planning software completes more drops per shift) |
| **Specialisation and the division of labour** | each worker repeats one task and becomes quicker at it (3.1.2.3) |
| **Motivation** | well-paid, well-treated, recognised workers put in more effort; bonus or piece-rate schemes reward output |
| **Management and organisation** | good planning, efficient layouts, fewer bottlenecks, clear targets, less waste of time and materials |
| **Working conditions** | safe, comfortable, well-lit workplaces reduce sickness, absence and accidents |
| **Quality of other factors** | fertile land, reliable raw materials and good infrastructure (fast broadband, roads) help each worker produce more |

Productivity grows most when these work together — for example, new machines are only productive if workers are trained to use them.

### Why productivity matters: the benefits

**For the firm**
- **Lower average (unit) costs**: if each worker makes more in the same paid hours, the wage cost per unit falls.
- **Higher profit** at the same price, or the ability to **cut prices** and stay competitive against rivals, at home and abroad.
- More output from the same resources, so the firm can meet extra demand without expanding.

**For workers**
- Firms can afford **higher wages** because each worker generates more revenue; skilled, productive workers are in demand.
- Possibly better job security if the firm becomes more competitive.
- But: if productivity rises through automation, some jobs may be lost (a cost to set against the benefits).

**For consumers**
- **Lower prices**, better quality and more choice as firms compete.

**For the economy**
- Higher productivity is the main source of long-run **economic growth** (3.2.2.2): more output from the same resources raises living standards.
- UK firms become more **internationally competitive**, helping exports and the current account (3.2.2.5).
- Growth without inflation, because output can rise without extra pressure on scarce resources.

### Links to costs and supply

Higher productivity lowers average cost (3.1.4.1), which makes each unit more profitable and shifts the **supply curve to the right** (3.1.3.2). That is why productivity improvements usually mean lower prices and higher quantities in the market.

::: terms
- **Production**: the total output of goods and services made by a firm, industry or economy in a time period.
- **Productivity**: the output produced per unit of input, such as per worker or per hour worked.
- **Labour productivity**: total output divided by the number of workers or hours worked.
- **Capital investment**: spending on machinery, equipment, buildings and technology used to produce goods and services.
- **Training**: teaching workers new skills or improving existing ones so they can work more effectively.
- **Automation**: using machines or computer systems to do tasks previously done by people.
- **Motivation**: the willingness of workers to put effort into their work, affected by pay, conditions and recognition.
- **Unit cost**: the average cost of producing one unit of output, falling when productivity rises.
- **Competitiveness**: the ability of a firm or country to sell its products against rivals on price or quality.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the question word**: "production" means total output; "productivity" means output per worker or per hour — never treat them as the same.
2. **Calculations**: productivity = output ÷ workers (or hours). Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Show the working and give units ("units per worker per week") and the decimal places asked.
3. **"Explain one factor"** (2 marks): name the factor (1), then the chain to more output per worker (1) — e.g. training → faster, fewer errors → more output per hour.
4. **Benefits**: say *who* benefits (firm, worker, consumer, economy) and how — usually via lower average cost.
5. **Analyse from a table**: calculate productivity for each period, then link the change to the factors in the data and to costs, prices and profit.
6. **Assess**: weigh up the factor in the question against alternatives, consider cost, time and side effects (job losses), and reach a judgement that says what it depends on.
:::

::: example Calculate — labour productivity
Invented data: a warehouse employs 25 workers who pick 30,000 orders a week. After new scanning technology is installed, the same 25 workers pick 34,500 orders a week. Calculate the percentage change in labour productivity. (3)

Productivity before = 30,000 ÷ 25 = 1,200 orders per worker per week (1). After = 34,500 ÷ 25 = 1,380 (1). Change = (1,380 − 1,200) ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 15% (1).
:::

::: example Distinguish — production and productivity
Invented data: a farm hires 4 extra pickers, raising its workforce from 10 to 14, and its strawberry output rises from 5,000 kg to 6,300 kg a week. Explain whether production and productivity have increased. (3)

Production has increased, because total output rose by 1,300 kg a week (1). Productivity was 5,000 ÷ 10 = 500 kg per worker and is now 6,300 ÷ 14 = 450 kg per worker (1), so productivity has fallen even though production rose — perhaps the new pickers are less experienced (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a factor that raises productivity
Explain one factor that could increase labour productivity in a hospital. (2)

Investment in new technology such as electronic patient records (1) reduces the time nurses and doctors spend on paperwork, so each member of staff can treat more patients per shift (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a benefit to workers
Explain one benefit of increased productivity for workers. (2)

Each worker produces more output, so generates more revenue for the firm (1); the firm can therefore afford to pay higher wages without raising its costs per unit (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — productivity and the firm
Invented data: a call centre introduced a bonus scheme and new software. Calls handled per worker per day rose from 60 to 75, and the wage cost per call fell from £2.00 to £1.70. Analyse how the rise in productivity may benefit the call centre. (6)

Productivity rose by (75 − 60) ÷ 60 × 100 = 25%, so each worker handles a quarter more calls in the same paid day (2). Because wages are paid per day, the cost of each call falls — the data show it fell from £2.00 to £1.70, a 15% fall — so the call centre's average cost falls and, if it charges clients the same fee per call, its profit per call rises (2). Lower costs also let it offer lower prices to win contracts from rival call centres, or handle more calls without hiring more staff, so revenue can grow while costs rise slowly; the bonus scheme adds to costs, but the data suggest the savings more than cover it (2).
:::

Invented data: a garage's 6 mechanics service 168 cars a week. Calculate the labour productivity per mechanic per week. (2)
---
168 ÷ 6 (1) = 28 cars per mechanic per week (1).

::: q
State two factors that influence labour productivity. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): training/education; investment in capital/technology; specialisation/division of labour; motivation/pay; management/organisation; working conditions.
:::

::: q
Explain one way that better management could increase productivity. (2)
---
Managers could reorganise the production line to remove bottlenecks / plan work so that no time is wasted (1), so each worker produces more output in each hour (1). (Or: clear targets, matching workers to tasks they are best at — developed.)
:::

::: q
Analyse how higher productivity in UK manufacturing could benefit the UK economy. (6)
---
Indicative content: higher productivity lowers unit costs (1), so UK manufacturers can cut prices and become more internationally competitive (1); exports rise and the current account improves (1); more output from the same resources means economic growth (1), so incomes and tax revenue rise (1); firms can afford higher wages, raising living standards, and growth need not cause inflation because capacity has risen (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Production ≠ productivity**. More output is higher productivity only if output **per worker or per hour** has risen — examiners say this is confused every year.
- Productivity calculations divide **output by input**, not the other way round.
- When a question gives hours, use **output per hour**, not per worker.
- Show the percentage-change formula: (new − old) ÷ **old** × 100.
- A factor answer needs the **chain** to more output per worker, not just the factor's name ("training" alone earns 1 mark at most).
- Benefits must match the group in the question — "benefits for workers" answered with "higher profits" scores nothing.
:::

::: test
Invented data:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Output (units per week) | 9,000 | 11,700 |
| Workers | 30 | 36 |

Calculate the percentage change in labour productivity between Year 1 and Year 2. (3)
---
Year 1 = 9,000 ÷ 30 = 300 units per worker (1); Year 2 = 11,700 ÷ 36 = 325 (1); (325 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = 8.3% (1). (Allow 8.33%.)

::: test
The table shows invented data for an online retailer's packing department.

| | Before new system | After new system |
|---|---|---|
| Packers | 40 | 40 |
| Parcels packed per day | 12,000 | 15,000 |
| Average wage per packer per day (£) | 96 | 100 |
| Errors (wrong items sent) per 1,000 parcels | 8 | 3 |

Using the table, analyse how the new system may benefit the retailer. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- Productivity rose from 300 to 375 parcels per packer per day, a 25% rise.
- Wage cost per parcel: £96 ÷ 300 = £0.32 before; £100 ÷ 375 ≈ £0.27 after — average cost falls even though wages rose by about 4%, so profit per parcel rises.
- More parcels from the same staff means the retailer can meet higher demand (e.g. peak seasons) without hiring.
- Errors fell from 8 to 3 per 1,000 — fewer returns and refunds, lower costs, better customer satisfaction and repeat custom.
- Lower costs can be passed on as lower prices or faster delivery, helping it compete with rivals; the cost of the system must be set against the savings.
Levels: 5–6 — developed analysis with accurate use of the data; 3–4 — some analysis and some use of data; 1–2 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Assess whether investing in new technology is the best way for a manufacturing firm to increase its productivity. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For technology: machines and automation can multiply output per worker far more than effort alone; they work long hours with consistent quality; fewer errors and less waste; average cost falls sharply, helping the firm compete with overseas rivals.
Against / alternatives: high upfront cost — the firm may need to borrow, and the payback takes time; technology only raises productivity if workers are trained to use it; it may cause redundancies, lowering morale among remaining workers; training, better management and motivation (bonuses, better conditions) can be cheaper and quicker; for small firms or service-based tasks, organisation and skills may matter more.
Conclusion: e.g. technology gives the largest long-run gains in manufacturing, but it works best combined with training; for a firm short of finance, improving organisation and motivation may be the better first step. The answer depends on the firm's finance, the nature of its products and the skills of its workforce.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported conclusion; L1 1–3 — simple, descriptive points.
:::
