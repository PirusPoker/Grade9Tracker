## What it is

**Free trade** is international trade with **no barriers** — no taxes on imports, no limits on quantities and no extra rules designed to keep foreign goods out. In practice, most countries use some barriers, and groups of countries sign **free-trade agreements** to remove or reduce them between themselves. This topic covers the **arguments for and against free trade**, and the **significance and benefits of free-trade agreements** such as the **European Union (EU)**, including what has changed since the UK left it.

### Barriers to trade (what free trade removes)

| Barrier | What it is | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Tariff** | a tax on imported goods, often a percentage of their value | raises the price of imports, so consumers switch to domestic goods; raises revenue for the government |
| **Quota** | a legal limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported | restricts supply of imports, so their price rises |
| **Regulations and "red tape"** | product standards, paperwork, customs checks, licences that foreign firms must meet | adds cost and delay for foreign firms; sometimes used deliberately to protect domestic producers |
| **Subsidies to domestic firms** | government payments that lower domestic firms' costs | domestic goods become cheaper than imports |
| **Embargo** | a complete ban on trade with a country or in a product | stops that trade entirely |

Using barriers to shield domestic firms from foreign competition is called **protectionism**.

Example (invented): a bicycle made abroad costs £300 when it arrives in the UK. With a **15% tariff**, the importer pays £300 × 0.15 = £45 in tax, so the bike costs **£345** before any profit margin — making UK-made bikes at £330 more competitive.

### Arguments for free trade

| Argument | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Lower prices** | without tariffs, imports are cheaper, and competition from foreign firms forces domestic firms to keep prices down — consumers' real incomes rise |
| **More choice** | consumers can buy goods and brands from all over the world |
| **Specialisation and efficiency** | each country focuses on what it produces relatively well; resources are used more efficiently and total world output rises |
| **Larger markets for exporters** | firms can sell more abroad without facing tariffs, gaining **economies of scale** |
| **Competition and innovation** | domestic firms must become more efficient and innovative to survive, raising productivity |
| **Cheaper inputs for firms** | firms can buy raw materials and components at world prices, lowering their costs |
| **Growth and jobs in exporting industries** | more exports mean more output and employment in competitive sectors |
| **Good relations** | trade links can strengthen cooperation between countries |

### Arguments against free trade (for some protection)

| Argument | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Job losses in uncompetitive industries** | domestic firms that cannot match cheaper imports cut jobs or close → **structural unemployment**, often concentrated in particular towns |
| **Infant industries** | a new industry may need temporary protection until it grows large enough to gain economies of scale and compete |
| **Strategic industries** | a country may want to keep some production at home — food, energy, steel, defence equipment, medicines — so it is not dependent on others in a crisis |
| **Dumping** | foreign firms may sell goods abroad below their cost of production (perhaps with government subsidies) to drive out competitors; tariffs can stop this unfair competition |
| **Standards** | imports may be cheap because they are made with lower labour, safety, animal-welfare or environmental standards; free trade may put pressure on domestic standards |
| **Balance of payments** | a flood of imports could worsen a current account deficit |
| **Tariff revenue** | some governments rely on tariffs for revenue |

**Evaluating protection**: tariffs raise prices for consumers, reduce choice, can protect inefficient firms indefinitely, and invite **retaliation** — if one country puts tariffs on another's goods, the other may respond with its own, hurting exporters in both. So protection may save jobs in one industry while costing jobs in others.

### Free-trade agreements

A **free-trade agreement (FTA)** is a deal between two or more countries to **remove or reduce tariffs, quotas and other barriers** on trade between them. Each country still sets its own barriers on trade with countries outside the agreement. Modern agreements often also cover services, investment, product standards and how disputes will be settled.

Related, deeper forms of integration:
- **Customs union** — a free-trade area where members also agree a **common external tariff** on imports from outside, so goods can move between members without customs checks on tariffs.
- **Single market** — goes further, removing most other barriers, with **common rules and standards** and the free movement of **goods, services, capital (money) and people** between members.

### The significance of the European Union

The **EU** is a group of European countries (27 since the UK left) that form a **single market** and a **customs union**. The UK was a member from 1973 until it left on **31 January 2020**; it stayed in the EU single market and customs union for a transition period to the end of 2020.

While the UK was a member:
- UK firms could sell to hundreds of millions of consumers across the EU with **no tariffs, no quotas and no customs checks**, and with **one set of product rules**;
- **free movement of people** let UK firms recruit workers from other EU countries and UK citizens work across the EU;
- UK service firms, especially in finance, could sell across the EU relatively easily;
- the EU negotiated trade deals with other countries on behalf of all members, including the UK.

Since **1 January 2021**, trade between the UK and EU has been covered by the **UK–EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement**. Goods that meet its rules generally cross **without tariffs or quotas**, but the UK is no longer in the single market or customs union, so there are **customs declarations, checks and extra paperwork**, and services face more barriers. These extra **non-tariff costs** have made trade with the EU more difficult for some firms, especially small ones. The EU is still the UK's largest trading partner overall.

Outside the EU the UK makes its **own trade agreements**. Many of them carried over ("rolled over") deals the EU already had with other countries; the UK has also signed new ones, for example with Australia and New Zealand, and has joined a large trade agreement among Pacific-region countries (the CPTPP).

### Benefits and impacts of free-trade agreements

| Group | Benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | lower prices on imports as tariffs are removed; more choice | — (though they may worry about lower standards on some imports) |
| **Exporting firms** | tariff-free access to bigger markets → more sales, economies of scale | must meet the agreement's rules (e.g. rules of origin paperwork) |
| **Import-competing firms** | cheaper imported inputs | **more competition** from foreign firms → lost sales, lower profit |
| **Workers** | more jobs in exporting industries | job losses in industries that can't compete (e.g. some farming if cheaper food imports arrive) |
| **The economy** | more trade, efficiency and growth; foreign investment attracted by market access | adjustment costs; possible pressure on standards |

The key point: an FTA **helps exporters and consumers** but also **exposes some domestic producers to more competition**. Who gains and who loses depends on the industry.

::: terms
- **Free trade**: international trade without barriers such as tariffs, quotas or excessive regulations.
- **Tariff**: a tax on imported goods, which raises their price in the domestic market.
- **Quota**: a legal limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported over a period.
- **Protectionism**: the use of trade barriers to protect domestic producers from foreign competition.
- **Free-trade agreement**: a deal between countries to remove or reduce barriers to trade between them.
- **Customs union**: a group of countries with free trade between them and a common external tariff on imports from outside.
- **Single market**: an area with free movement of goods, services, capital and people and common rules and standards.
- **Dumping**: selling goods in a foreign market at a price below their cost of production.
- **Infant industry**: a new industry that may need temporary protection until it is large enough to compete internationally.
- **Retaliation**: when a country responds to another's trade barriers by imposing its own.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely**: free trade = no tariffs, quotas or red tape; an FTA = agreement to cut those barriers between members.
2. **Arguments for**: lower prices, choice, specialisation, bigger markets, economies of scale, competition. **Against**: job losses, infant and strategic industries, dumping, standards.
3. **Apply to the group** in the question — consumers, exporters, import-competing firms, workers — and show both sides for top-level analysis.
4. **Calculations**: tariff = rate × import price; new price = price + tariff; percentage changes in trade.
5. **EU**: single market and customs union, UK left in 2020, Trade and Cooperation Agreement since 2021 — tariff-free for most goods but more paperwork and checks.
6. **Judge**: do the gains (widespread, to consumers and exporters) outweigh the losses (concentrated, to some workers and firms)? What would reduce the losses — retraining, transition periods?
:::

::: example Analyse — a new free-trade agreement
Invented data — Table 1: effect of a new UK free-trade agreement with Country Z after three years

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| UK exports to Z (£bn) | 4.0 | 5.2 |
| UK imports from Z (£bn) | 3.5 | 4.9 |
| Average UK tariff on Z's beef (%) | 20 | 0 |

Using Table 1, analyse the impact of this free-trade agreement on the UK. (6)

UK exports to Z rose by 30% (£1.2bn) as tariffs were removed, so UK exporting firms gained sales and could expand output and jobs (2). Imports from Z rose by 40% (£1.4bn); removing the 20% tariff on Z's beef makes it cheaper for UK consumers, increasing choice and real incomes (2). But UK beef farmers now face cheaper competition and may lose sales or leave farming, and since imports rose by more than exports the UK's trade balance with Z worsened by £0.2bn — the agreement benefits exporters and consumers while some producers lose (2).

- Apply to the group asked about: workers in exporting industries gain; workers in import-competing industries may lose.
