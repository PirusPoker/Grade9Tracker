## What it is

The UK government is the biggest single spender in the economy. Where does its money come from, and where does it go? This topic covers the **main sources of UK government revenue**, the **main areas of UK government spending**, the difference between **direct and indirect taxation**, and why some taxes are **progressive** while others are **regressive**.

### Where the money comes from: government revenue

Most government revenue comes from **taxation**. The main sources are:

| Source | What it is | Paid by |
|---|---|---|
| **Income tax** | a tax on earnings, pensions and other income above a tax-free personal allowance; the rate rises in bands as income rises | individuals |
| **National Insurance contributions (NICs)** | paid on earnings above a threshold; linked to the state pension and some benefits | employees, employers and the self-employed |
| **VAT (Value Added Tax)** | a tax added to the price of most goods and services; the standard rate is 20%, with some items (e.g. most food, children's clothes) at zero | consumers, collected by firms |
| **Corporation tax** | a tax on companies' profits | companies |
| **Excise duties** | taxes on specific goods: fuel, alcohol, tobacco | consumers, collected by producers and importers |
| **Council tax** | a local tax based on the value band of a home | households, paid to local councils |
| **Business rates** | a local tax on non-domestic property such as shops, offices and factories | firms |
| Other | stamp duty on property purchases, inheritance tax, capital gains tax, air passenger duty and others | various |

**Income tax** is the largest single source of revenue, followed by **National Insurance** and **VAT**. Together these three raise more than half of all tax revenue.

When spending is greater than revenue, the government must **borrow** the difference — usually by selling government bonds. This borrowing is a **budget deficit** (3.2.3.1), and the interest on past borrowing becomes a spending item in its own right.

### Where the money goes: government spending

| Area of spending | What it pays for |
|---|---|
| **Social protection** (welfare) | the **state pension** (the biggest single item), Universal Credit and other benefits for people who are unemployed, on low incomes, disabled or caring |
| **Health** | the NHS: hospitals, GPs, nurses, medicines |
| **Education** | schools, colleges, support for universities and training |
| **Debt interest** | interest payments on the national debt |
| **Defence** | the armed forces and equipment |
| **Public order and safety** | police, courts, prisons, fire services |
| **Transport** | roads, railways, local transport |
| Others | housing and environment, industry and agriculture, overseas aid, local services |

**Social protection** is the largest area of spending, and **health** is the second largest; together they take a large share of the total. Spending changes with the state of the economy: in a recession, spending on benefits rises automatically as unemployment rises, while tax revenue falls.

Government spending has an **opportunity cost**: money spent on one area cannot be spent on another, or used to cut taxes.

### Direct and indirect taxes

| | **Direct tax** | **Indirect tax** |
|---|---|---|
| What it taxes | **income and wealth** — taken directly from what people and firms earn or own | **spending** — added to the price of goods and services |
| Who pays it | the person or firm earning the income pays it to the government (often taken through PAYE) | collected by the seller from the consumer and passed to the government |
| Examples | income tax, National Insurance, corporation tax, inheritance tax, capital gains tax | VAT, excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, air passenger duty |
| Can people avoid it? | only by earning less | partly, by changing what they buy |

### Progressive, proportional and regressive taxes

These terms describe how the **proportion (percentage) of income** paid in tax changes as income rises:

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | higher earners pay a **larger proportion** of their income in tax | income tax — a tax-free allowance, then higher rates for higher bands of income |
| **Proportional** | everyone pays the **same proportion** of their income | a flat-rate tax, e.g. a single 15% rate on all income |
| **Regressive** | lower earners pay a **larger proportion** of their income in tax | VAT and excise duties — a poorer household spends a bigger share of its income, so the tax takes a bigger share of its income |

**Regressive does not mean the poor pay more pounds.** A rich person buying a new car pays more VAT in pounds than a poor person buying groceries — but as a **percentage of income**, the poor person pays more.

Worked example (invented): two households each pay VAT on their spending.

| | Household A | Household B |
|---|---|---|
| Annual income | £18,000 | £90,000 |
| Spending subject to VAT | £9,000 | £27,000 |
| VAT at 20% | £1,800 | £5,400 |
| VAT as % of income | 1,800 ÷ 18,000 = **10%** | 5,400 ÷ 90,000 = **6%** |

B pays three times as much VAT in pounds, but A pays a larger share of income — so VAT is **regressive**.

**Calculating income tax with bands** (invented, simplified bands):

| Band | Taxable income | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Allowance | first £12,000 | 0% |
| Basic | £12,001 – £50,000 | 20% |
| Higher | over £50,000 | 40% |

A person earning £70,000: 0% on £12,000 = £0; 20% on £38,000 = £7,600; 40% on £20,000 = £8,000. Total tax = **£15,600**, which is 15,600 ÷ 70,000 = **22.3%** of income. A person earning £25,000 pays 20% on £13,000 = £2,600, which is **10.4%** of income. The proportion rises with income — the tax is **progressive**.

### Pie charts of revenue and spending

Data on government finances often appear as pie charts. **Angle = share (%) × 360 ÷ 100.** If health takes 20% of spending, its slice is 20 × 3.6 = **72°**. To find a percentage from an angle: angle ÷ 360 × 100.

::: terms
- **Government revenue**: the money the government receives, mainly from taxation.
- **Government spending**: money spent by the government on public services, benefits, debt interest and investment.
- **Direct tax**: a tax on income or wealth, paid directly to the government by the person or firm that earns it.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending, added to the price of goods and services and collected by the seller.
- **Income tax**: a direct tax on individuals' income above a tax-free personal allowance.
- **VAT**: Value Added Tax — an indirect tax added to the price of most goods and services.
- **Excise duty**: an indirect tax on specific goods such as fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
- **Corporation tax**: a direct tax on the profits of companies.
- **Progressive tax**: a tax that takes a larger proportion of income from people with higher incomes.
- **Regressive tax**: a tax that takes a larger proportion of income from people with lower incomes.
- **Proportional tax**: a tax that takes the same proportion of income from everyone.
:::

::: method
1. **Sources and spending**: name UK examples precisely — income tax, NICs, VAT, corporation tax, excise duties, council tax; social protection (pensions, benefits), health, education, debt interest, defence.
2. **Direct vs indirect**: direct = on income or wealth; indirect = on spending. Give one example of each.
3. **Progressive vs regressive**: always talk about the **proportion (percentage) of income**, not the amount in pounds.
4. **Tax calculations**: work band by band; add the bands; then divide tax by income × 100 to find the average rate.
5. **Pie charts**: angle = % × 3.6; % = angle ÷ 3.6. Check the total is 360° or 100%.
6. **Assess**: consider fairness, revenue raised, incentives to work and invest, and effects on different income groups before judging.
:::

::: example Explain — regressive taxation
Explain what is meant by regressive taxation. (2)

A regressive tax takes a larger proportion of income from people on low incomes than from people on high incomes (1); for example, VAT is regressive because poorer households spend a bigger share of their income on taxed goods, so VAT is a bigger percentage of their income (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — income tax with bands
Invented bands: 0% on the first £12,000; 20% on £12,001–£50,000; 40% above £50,000. Calculate the income tax paid by someone earning £58,000, and the percentage of their income paid in tax. (3)

20% × £38,000 = £7,600 (1); 40% × £8,000 = £3,200; total = £10,800 (1). £10,800 ÷ £58,000 × 100 = 18.6% (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — pie-chart angles
Invented data: a government's spending is £1,200 billion, of which health is £216 billion. Calculate health's percentage share of spending and the angle of its slice on a pie chart. (2)

£216bn ÷ £1,200bn × 100 = 18% (1). Angle = 18 × 3.6 = 64.8° (1).
:::

::: example Explain — direct and indirect
Explain the difference between a direct tax and an indirect tax. (2)

A direct tax is taken from income or wealth, such as income tax paid on wages (1); an indirect tax is placed on spending and added to the price of goods and services, such as VAT (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a shift from direct to indirect tax
Analyse how replacing some income tax with a higher rate of VAT could affect households on different incomes. (6)

Income tax is progressive, so cutting it gives higher earners the biggest gains in pounds and as a share of their income — someone paying higher-rate tax saves much more than someone earning just above the allowance, and people below the allowance gain nothing (2). VAT is regressive, because poorer households spend almost all of their income, much of it on taxed goods, so a higher VAT rate takes a bigger percentage of their income than of a rich household's, which saves a larger share (2). The overall effect is to shift the tax burden from high-income to low-income households, widening income inequality — although zero-rated essentials such as most food would limit the harm to the poorest, and lower income tax may encourage some people to work more (2).
:::

::: q
Analyse why income tax is considered a progressive tax. (6)
---
Indicative content: there is a tax-free allowance, so the lowest earners pay nothing (1); higher bands of income are taxed at higher rates (1); so as income rises, the average percentage of income paid in tax rises (1) — e.g. a worked example using bands (1). Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- **Regressive = a larger percentage of a low income** — not "the poor pay more pounds". The rich usually pay more in pounds even for a regressive tax.
- **VAT is indirect and regressive**; **income tax is direct and progressive**. Do not swap them.
- National Insurance and corporation tax are **direct** taxes.
- Do not confuse government **revenue** with government **spending**, or the government budget with the balance of trade.
- In band calculations, tax each slice of income at its own rate — do not apply the top rate to the whole income.
- Pie-chart angles: % × 3.6. Show the working so a slip still earns the method mark.
:::

::: test
Assess whether the government should raise more of its revenue from indirect taxes rather than income tax. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
For: lower income tax increases incentives to work, train and set up businesses; indirect taxes on harmful goods (tobacco, alcohol, fuel) discourage consumption and correct negative externalities; indirect taxes are harder to evade and are paid by everyone who spends, including tourists; people can partly choose how much tax they pay by changing what they buy.
Against: indirect taxes are regressive — they take a bigger share of poorer households' incomes and would widen inequality; higher prices may push up inflation; revenue from excise duties falls if they succeed in changing behaviour (e.g. fewer smokers); lower spending could hurt shops and jobs; income tax is progressive and fairer by ability to pay.
Conclusion: e.g. a moderate shift towards taxes on harmful goods may be justified, but relying more on VAT would hurt low-income households; the answer depends on whether the government prioritises incentives and efficiency or equality, and on whether benefits are adjusted to protect the poorest.
Levels: L3 7–9 — balanced, developed analysis with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — some development, partly supported; L1 1–3 — simple points.
:::
