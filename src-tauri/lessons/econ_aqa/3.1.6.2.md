## What it is

Many economic decisions affect people who had no say in them. A factory's smoke drifts over a housing estate; a neighbour's beautiful garden cheers up the whole street. These spill-over effects are **externalities**, and they are the most common cause of market failure (3.1.6.1). This topic covers **externalities as the difference between social and private costs or benefits**, the difference between **positive and negative externalities** and how to **identify** them, and how **both production and consumption** can lead to negative externalities.

### Private, external and social costs

When a firm produces or a consumer consumes, there are **private** costs and benefits — those that fall on the people making the decision — and sometimes **external** costs and benefits — those that fall on **third parties**, people outside the transaction who are not paid or compensated.

| Term | Meaning | Example: a cement works |
|---|---|---|
| **Private cost** | the cost paid by the producer or consumer making the decision | wages, fuel, raw materials, machinery the firm pays for |
| **External cost** | a cost imposed on a third party that the decision-maker does not pay for | dust and breathing problems for local residents; heavy lorries damaging roads |
| **Social cost** | the full cost to society | **social cost = private cost + external cost** |

| Term | Meaning | Example: a person getting a flu vaccination |
|---|---|---|
| **Private benefit** | the benefit to the person making the decision | they are less likely to fall ill |
| **External benefit** | a benefit enjoyed by a third party who does not pay for it | family, colleagues and the wider public are less likely to catch flu; fewer hospital admissions |
| **Social benefit** | the full benefit to society | **social benefit = private benefit + external benefit** |

**An externality is the difference between the social cost (or benefit) and the private cost (or benefit).** In other words:

- **external cost = social cost − private cost**
- **external benefit = social benefit − private benefit**

Invented example: producing one tonne of a chemical costs the firm £400 (private cost). Pollution of a nearby river costs fishing businesses and the water company £150 per tonne (external cost). Social cost = £400 + £150 = **£550 per tonne**. The firm decides how much to produce by looking only at its £400 cost, so it ignores more than a quarter of the true cost to society.

### Negative and positive externalities

| | **Negative externality** | **Positive externality** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | a harmful spill-over: an **external cost** on third parties | a beneficial spill-over: an **external benefit** to third parties |
| Relationship | social cost **>** private cost | social benefit **>** private benefit |
| Result in a free market | the good is **over-produced / over-consumed**, because decision-makers ignore the harm to others | the good is **under-produced / under-consumed**, because decision-makers ignore the benefit to others |
| Examples | air and water pollution, noise, traffic congestion, litter, passive smoking, carbon emissions, antisocial behaviour linked to alcohol | education and training, vaccinations, public transport (fewer cars on the roads), well-kept gardens and restored buildings, research and development, a new railway station that raises local businesses' trade |

To **identify** an externality in an exam question, ask three things:
1. **Who** is affected? It must be a **third party** — not the buyer or the seller.
2. **Do they pay or get paid** for it? If not, it is external.
3. **Is the effect harmful or beneficial?** That decides negative or positive.

A higher price paid by the buyer, or lower profit for the firm, is **not** an externality — it falls on the people in the transaction.

### Production and consumption can both cause negative externalities

| | **Negative externality of production** | **Negative externality of consumption** |
|---|---|---|
| Where it comes from | the process of **making** a good or service | the act of **using** a good or service |
| Examples | factory smoke and chemical waste; noise from a quarry; lorries congesting roads; farming run-off polluting rivers; carbon emissions from power stations | second-hand smoke from cigarettes; noise from a late-night party; drink-related violence and policing costs; litter from takeaway packaging; congestion and exhaust fumes from people driving cars |

Some goods create negative externalities at **both** stages — for example, a car's production uses energy and creates emissions, and driving it causes congestion and fumes.

Positive externalities, likewise, can come from production (a firm trains workers who later move to other firms, taking their skills with them; a company's research leads to discoveries others use) and from consumption (education, vaccinations, using a bike instead of a car).

### Why externalities matter

Because the market price reflects only **private** costs and benefits:
- goods with **negative externalities** are too cheap from society's point of view, so too much is produced and consumed;
- goods with **positive externalities** are under-valued by individuals, so too little is bought.

This is a **misallocation of resources** — a market failure — which is why governments use taxes, regulation, subsidies and information to correct it (3.1.6.1, 3.2.3.4).

::: terms
- **Externality**: the difference between the social and private costs or benefits of an activity — a spill-over effect on a third party.
- **Third party**: a person or group affected by a transaction who is not the buyer or the seller.
- **Private cost**: the cost of an activity paid by the producer or consumer who carries it out.
- **External cost**: a cost imposed on a third party that is not paid for by the decision-maker.
- **Social cost**: the total cost of an activity to society — private cost plus external cost.
- **Private benefit**: the benefit received by the producer or consumer who carries out an activity.
- **External benefit**: a benefit received by a third party who does not pay for it.
- **Social benefit**: the total benefit of an activity to society — private benefit plus external benefit.
- **Negative externality**: a harmful spill-over effect where social cost is greater than private cost.
- **Positive externality**: a beneficial spill-over effect where social benefit is greater than private benefit.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions**: externality = social cost/benefit − private cost/benefit; social = private + external.
2. **Identify**: the effect must fall on a **third party** who does not pay or get paid; harmful = negative, beneficial = positive.
3. **Production or consumption?** Ask whether the spill-over comes from *making* the good or from *using* it.
4. **Calculations**: social cost = private + external; external = social − private; show working with £ and units (per tonne, per year).
5. **Consequence**: negative → over-production/over-consumption; positive → under-production/under-consumption.
6. **9-mark "Using Figure…, assess whether the negative externalities outweigh the positive"**: list and develop both sides from the figure, weigh size, who is affected and how long it lasts, then judge.
:::

::: example Explain — private and external cost
Explain the difference between a private cost and an external cost. (2)

A private cost is paid by the person or firm making the decision, such as a factory's spending on raw materials (1); an external cost falls on a third party who is not involved in the transaction and is not compensated, such as residents breathing the factory's fumes (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — social cost
Invented data: an airline's private cost of a flight is £18,000. The external costs of noise and emissions are estimated at £4,500. Calculate the social cost of the flight and the external cost as a percentage of the social cost. (3)

Social cost = £18,000 + £4,500 = £22,500 (1). External cost as a percentage = £4,500 ÷ £22,500 (1) × 100 = 20% (1).
:::

::: example Identify — consumption externality
A neighbour plays loud music late at night. State whether this is an externality of production or consumption, and whether it is positive or negative. (2)

It is an externality of consumption, because it comes from using a good (the music system) rather than producing it (1). It is negative, because it harms third parties — the neighbours lose sleep — without compensation (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a positive externality
Explain one positive externality of people cycling to work instead of driving. (2)

Each cyclist removes a car from the road, reducing congestion and exhaust fumes (1), so other road users enjoy quicker journeys and residents breathe cleaner air without paying for it (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — over-consumption
Analyse why a free market is likely to lead to over-consumption of single-use plastic bags. (6)

When a shopper takes a plastic bag, they weigh only their private cost — often a few pence or nothing — against the private benefit of convenience (2). They ignore the external costs: discarded bags litter streets, harm wildlife and pollute seas, and councils must pay to clear them, so the social cost of each bag is much higher than the private cost (2). Because the price shoppers face is below the true cost to society, they use far more bags than would be best for society — the market over-allocates resources to plastic bags, which is why charges or bans can reduce their use (2).
:::

::: q
Which of the following is a negative externality of production? A — a fall in a firm's profit; B — smoke from a power station affecting local residents; C — a rise in the price of electricity; D — workers at the power station being paid overtime. (1)
---
B — smoke from a power station affecting local residents.
:::

::: q
Define the term "externality". (2)
---
The difference between social and private costs/benefits (1) — a cost or benefit to a third party not involved in the activity (1).
:::

::: q
State two examples of positive externalities. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): education; vaccinations; public transport use; training by firms; research and development; well-kept gardens/restored buildings; cycling instead of driving.
:::

::: q
Invented data: the social benefit of a training course is £5,200 per worker and the private benefit to the worker is £3,800. Calculate the external benefit. (2)
---
£5,200 − £3,800 (1) = £1,400 per worker (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one negative externality of consumption caused by alcohol. (2)
---
Some heavy drinkers become violent or disorderly (1), so police, hospitals and members of the public bear costs — injuries, extra policing paid from taxes — that the drinker does not pay (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse why goods with positive externalities tend to be under-consumed in a free market. (6)
---
Indicative content: consumers decide using their private benefit only (1); they ignore the benefits to third parties, e.g. others not catching a disease after a vaccination (1); so the value they place on the good is below the social benefit (1); at the market price fewer people buy it than is best for society (1) — resources are under-allocated (1); e.g. some people skip a flu jab because the private benefit seems small, increasing the spread of illness and NHS costs (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- An externality falls on a **third party** — higher prices for the buyer or lower profits for the firm are private effects, not externalities.
- **Social cost = private cost + external cost** (and the same for benefits) — make sure you add rather than subtract.
- Negative externality → **over**-production/consumption; positive → **under**-production/consumption. Do not reverse them.
- Say whether the externality comes from **production** or **consumption** when asked — both can be negative.
- In "assess whether the negative externalities are greater than the positive" questions (the June 2025 style), use the figure's evidence for **both** sides and reach a judgement.
- Avoid vague answers like "it is bad for the environment" — say **who** is harmed and how.
:::

::: test
Invented data: a new bypass costs a council £30 million to build. Its private benefit (time saved by drivers who use it) is valued at £24 million, and the external benefits (quieter, safer streets for town-centre residents and businesses) at £11 million. Calculate the social benefit and state whether the social benefit is greater than the cost. (3)
---
Social benefit = £24m + £11m = £35m (1). £35m is greater than the £30m cost (1), by £5m — so the bypass is worthwhile to society even though the private benefit alone (£24m) is below the cost (1).

::: test
Explain one reason why a good with negative externalities is over-produced in a free market. (2)
---
Producers decide output using only their private costs and ignore the external costs on third parties (1), so the price is lower than the true social cost and more is produced and consumed than is best for society (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one positive externality of a firm training its workers. (2)
---
Trained workers who later move to another firm take their skills with them (1), so other firms gain more productive workers without paying for the training (1). (Or: higher productivity raises tax revenue/growth — developed.)
:::

::: test
Explain two negative externalities that could result from building a large out-of-town shopping centre. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Congestion: thousands of shoppers drive to the centre (1), causing traffic jams on nearby roads (1), so local residents and other road users lose time and face more pollution without compensation (1).
Effects on the town centre: shoppers switch away from high-street shops (1), so some close, leaving empty units (1), which makes the town centre less attractive for remaining businesses and residents (1).
Also creditworthy: noise/light pollution for nearby residents; loss of green space/wildlife habitat; litter; carbon emissions from car journeys.
:::

::: test
Invented data — estimated annual externalities of a city's international airport:

| Externality | Estimated value (£m per year) |
|---|---|
| Noise affecting nearby homes and schools | 85 |
| Air pollution and carbon emissions | 140 |
| Extra trade for local hotels, restaurants and taxis | 120 |
| Jobs and skills for local people in firms that supply the airport | 70 |

Using the table, analyse the externalities created by the airport. (6)
---
Indicative content (AO2 2, AO3 4):
- Negative externalities: noise (£85m) and pollution/emissions (£140m) — total £225m — fall on residents, schools and future generations who do not use the airport.
- Positive externalities: extra trade for local businesses (£120m) and jobs/skills in supplier firms (£70m) — total £190m — benefits to third parties outside the airport's own transactions.
- Net: negative externalities exceed positive by £35m a year; the largest single item is pollution/emissions.
- Different groups gain and lose — nearby residents bear the noise while hotels gain trade.
- Values are estimates and hard to measure (e.g. health effects, long-run climate damage).
Levels: 5–6 — developed analysis identifying both types with accurate data and some judgement; 3–4 — some analysis with some data; 1–2 — simple points.
:::

::: test
Invented data: a dairy farm's private cost of producing 1,000 litres of milk is £320. Run-off from the farm causes £45 of damage to a river for every 1,000 litres. Calculate the social cost per 1,000 litres and the external cost as a percentage of the private cost. (2)
---
Social cost = £320 + £45 = £365 (1); £45 ÷ £320 × 100 = 14.1% (1).
:::

::: test
Item — a summer music festival (invented)

A three-day music festival attracts 60,000 visitors to a small rural town each year. A council report on the festival says: local residents complain about traffic jams, late-night noise and litter left in fields and lanes; the council spends £250,000 on extra cleaning and road management, part of which the organiser repays. Local pubs, shops and campsites report their busiest week of the year, and the festival funds a youth music project in the town. Some residents say the festival has put the town "on the map", attracting tourists all year round.

Using the council report, assess whether the negative externalities of the festival are greater than the positive externalities. (9)
---
AO2 3, AO3 6. Indicative content:
Negative externalities: traffic congestion delays residents and businesses; late-night noise disturbs sleep; litter harms farmland and wildlife; the council's £250,000 costs fall on taxpayers (if not fully repaid); possible crime/antisocial behaviour.
Positive externalities: extra trade for pubs, shops and campsites that are not part of the festival's own transactions; jobs; the funded youth project; the town's higher profile brings year-round tourism.
Evaluation: the negatives are concentrated in a few days and fall mainly on residents; the positives may last longer (year-round tourism, youth project); the organiser repays part of the council's costs, reducing the external cost; values are hard to measure; different groups gain and lose — business owners versus residents near the site.
Conclusion: e.g. the positive externalities are probably greater because they last all year and spread widely, provided the organiser covers the clean-up costs; for residents living next to the site the negatives may dominate.
Levels: L3 7–9 — developed two-sided analysis using the figure with a justified conclusion; L2 4–6 — partly developed or one-sided, partly supported conclusion; L1 1–3 — descriptive points, assertion.
:::
