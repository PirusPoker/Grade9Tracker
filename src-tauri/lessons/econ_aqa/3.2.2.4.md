## What it is

**Price stability** is one of the government's principal objectives (3.2.2.1). This topic covers what **inflation** and the **rate of inflation** mean, how inflation is measured with the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, how to do **simple calculations with CPI figures**, the two main causes (**cost-push** and **demand-pull**), and the **consequences of inflation for different groups** — savers, borrowers, workers, people on fixed incomes, firms and the government. It finishes with the policies used to manage inflation, which are taught in full in 3.2.3.

### Inflation, deflation and disinflation

**Inflation** is a **sustained rise in the general price level** — the average level of prices across the economy, not the price of one product. When there is inflation, each pound buys fewer goods and services: the **purchasing power** (value) of money falls.

The **rate of inflation** is the **percentage change in the price level over a year**. "Inflation is 4%" means that, on average, prices are 4% higher than twelve months earlier.

| Term | Meaning | Example (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| **Inflation** | the general price level is rising | the rate is +3% |
| **Disinflation** | the rate of inflation is **falling**, but prices are still rising | the rate falls from 8% to 3% — prices are still going up, just more slowly |
| **Deflation** | the general price level is **falling**; the inflation rate is negative | the rate is −1% |

This is the commonest data mistake on the paper: **a falling inflation rate does not mean falling prices**. Only a negative rate means prices are lower than a year ago.

The UK government sets the Bank of England a target of **2% CPI inflation**. Price stability means **low and stable** inflation — not zero, and not falling prices.

### Measuring inflation: the Consumer Price Index

The UK's headline measure of inflation is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, published every month by the Office for National Statistics (ONS). It is built in stages:

1. **A basket of goods and services.** Statisticians choose several hundred items that represent what typical households buy — food, clothing, energy, transport, rent, leisure, phones and so on. The basket is reviewed every year: new items are added as spending habits change, and items people no longer buy are removed.
2. **Prices are collected** each month for every item, from many shops and websites across the country.
3. **Weights.** Each category is weighted by its **share of household spending** (found from a large spending survey). A 10% rise in petrol prices matters more to the index than a 10% rise in the price of birthday cards, because households spend far more on petrol.
4. **An index number.** The weighted average price is turned into an index, with a **base year = 100**. If the index is 112 two years later, prices have risen by 12% since the base year.

Other measures exist — for example **CPIH**, which adds owner-occupiers' housing costs — but CPI is the one used for the 2% target.

**Limitations of the CPI:** it measures an *average* household's spending, so a pensioner, a student or a low-income family may face a different personal inflation rate; quality improvements are hard to capture (a new phone may cost more but do more); new products enter the basket with a delay.

### Calculations with CPI figures

| What you need | Formula |
|---|---|
| Rate of inflation between two years | (new index − old index) ÷ old index × 100 |
| Price change since the base year | index − 100 = % change since the base year |
| Price in a later year | old price × new index ÷ old index |
| Weighted average inflation | multiply each category's inflation by its weight (as a decimal or share) and add them up |
| Approximate real change | money (nominal) change − inflation rate |

Worked examples (invented data):
- CPI rises from **125.0 to 131.5**. Inflation = (131.5 − 125.0) ÷ 125.0 × 100 = 6.5 ÷ 125 × 100 = **5.2%**.
- An index of **108** means prices are **8% higher than the base year**. But a move from 108 to 116 is **not** 8% inflation: 8 ÷ 108 × 100 = **7.4%**. Always divide by the **earlier** index, not by 100.
- A bicycle costs **£400** in a year when CPI is 100. If its price rises in line with the index to **115**, it costs 400 × 115 ÷ 100 = **£460**.
- **Weighted index:** food (weight 20%) prices rise 10%, transport (30%) rise 5%, housing (50%) rise 2%. Inflation = 0.2 × 10 + 0.3 × 5 + 0.5 × 2 = 2 + 1.5 + 1 = **4.5%**. Food rose fastest, but housing's large weight pulls the average down.
- **Real wages:** pay rises by 3% while inflation is 5%. **Real** pay falls by about **2%** — workers can buy less than a year ago even though they earn more pounds.

### Causes of inflation

| | **Cost-push inflation** | **Demand-pull inflation** |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | firms' **costs of production rise**, and they pass them on to consumers as higher prices | **total demand** in the economy **grows faster than the economy's ability to supply** goods and services |
| Typical triggers | higher **wages** (especially if pay rises faster than productivity); dearer **raw materials and energy** (e.g. oil, gas, wheat); a **fall in the exchange rate** making imported inputs dearer; higher **indirect taxes** such as VAT or fuel duty; supply shocks (poor harvests, war, shipping disruption) | **lower interest rates** (cheaper borrowing, less reward for saving); **tax cuts** or higher government spending; rising consumer and business **confidence**; strong **export** demand; rapid growth in credit and the money supply |
| What happens | prices rise even if demand is weak — output may fall at the same time | firms cannot raise output fast enough, so shortages appear and prices are bid up |
| Often linked with | rising unemployment and slow growth | fast growth and low unemployment |

The two can feed each other in a **wage–price spiral**: prices rise → workers ask for higher wages to protect their living standards → firms' costs rise → firms raise prices again. **Expectations** matter: if everyone expects high inflation next year, they bargain for higher pay and set higher prices, which makes high inflation happen.

### Consequences of inflation for different groups

| Group | How inflation affects them |
|---|---|
| **Savers** | if the interest rate on savings is **below** inflation, the **real value** of savings falls (a 3% interest rate with 5% inflation is a **real return of about −2%**) — this discourages saving |
| **Borrowers** | the **real value of debt falls**: wages and prices rise but the amount owed does not, so a fixed loan becomes easier to repay — unless interest rates are raised to fight inflation, which raises repayments on variable-rate loans and mortgages |
| **People on fixed or slowly rising incomes** (some pensioners, students, benefit claimants if uprating lags behind) | their purchasing power falls; they can afford less |
| **Workers** | **real wages** fall unless pay rises at least as fast as prices; stronger unions and workers with scarce skills are more able to win pay rises |
| **Low-income households** | often hit hardest when inflation is driven by **food and energy**, because these take a bigger share of their spending |
| **Firms** | **uncertainty** makes planning and investment harder; **menu costs** (changing prices, catalogues, websites) and time spent negotiating pay; rising input costs squeeze profits; UK goods become **less competitive** abroad if UK inflation is higher than in other countries, hurting exporters; firms with stock or assets may gain as their value rises |
| **Government** | spending on index-linked pensions and benefits rises; public-sector workers want higher pay; but **tax revenue rises** too (VAT on higher prices; more income tax as wages rise, especially if tax thresholds are frozen — **fiscal drag**); the real value of government debt falls |
| **The economy** | the current account may worsen as exports become dearer; investment and growth may slow; high inflation can redistribute wealth unfairly from savers to borrowers |

**Deflation** is not automatically good news either: consumers may **delay purchases** expecting lower prices, firms cut output and jobs, and the real value of debts **rises**.

### Government policies to manage inflation

- **Monetary policy (3.2.3.2):** the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee raises **Bank Rate** to reduce borrowing and spending, cutting demand-pull inflation; a higher rate may also strengthen the pound, making imports cheaper.
- **Fiscal policy (3.2.3.1):** higher taxes or lower government spending reduce total demand.
- **Supply-side policies (3.2.3.3):** raising productivity increases the economy's capacity, so demand can grow without pushing up prices; this is slow but tackles cost pressures.

Policies that work by **reducing demand** are better suited to demand-pull inflation. Against cost-push inflation they can bring prices down only by squeezing spending and jobs, which is why the type of inflation matters when judging policy.

::: terms
- **Inflation**: a sustained rise in the general level of prices in an economy over time.
- **Rate of inflation**: the percentage change in the general price level over a twelve-month period.
- **Deflation**: a sustained fall in the general price level, shown by a negative inflation rate.
- **Disinflation**: a fall in the rate of inflation while prices are still rising, only more slowly.
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**: the UK's main measure of inflation, tracking the weighted price of a basket of consumer goods and services.
- **Basket of goods**: the representative selection of goods and services whose prices are recorded to calculate the CPI.
- **Weights**: the importance given to each category in the CPI, based on its share of household spending.
- **Base year**: the year given an index value of 100, against which later price levels are compared.
- **Cost-push inflation**: inflation caused by rising costs of production, such as wages, energy or imported materials, being passed on in prices.
- **Demand-pull inflation**: inflation caused by total demand in the economy growing faster than the economy's ability to supply.
- **Real wages**: wages adjusted for inflation, showing how much the pay can actually buy.
- **Purchasing power**: the quantity of goods and services that a sum of money can buy.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely**: inflation is a sustained rise in the *general* price level; the rate is the % change over a year.
2. **Read data carefully**: a lower but positive rate is disinflation — prices are still rising. Only a negative rate is deflation.
3. **Calculate**: (new index − old index) ÷ old index × 100. Divide by the earlier index, show working, round as asked and add the % sign.
4. **Causes**: name the type, then give the chain — cost-push: costs rise → firms pass them on; demand-pull: spending grows faster than capacity → prices bid up.
5. **Consequences**: always name the group (savers, borrowers, pensioners, workers, exporters, government) and compare interest or pay rises with the inflation rate.
6. **Analyse and evaluate**: use the figures in the item; ask whether the effect depends on the size of inflation, whether it is expected, and whether incomes keep pace; then judge which group suffers most.
:::

::: example Calculate — inflation from index numbers
Invented data: the CPI was 136.0 in Year 1 and 142.8 in Year 2. Calculate the rate of inflation between Year 1 and Year 2. (2)

(142.8 − 136.0) ÷ 136.0 × 100 (1) = 6.8 ÷ 136 × 100 = 5% (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — a weighted index
Invented data: in a simple price index, food has a weight of 25% and its prices rose 8%; energy has a weight of 15% and its prices rose 20%; all other items have a weight of 60% and their prices rose 2%. Calculate the overall rate of inflation. (2)

0.25 × 8 + 0.15 × 20 + 0.60 × 2 = 2 + 3 + 1.2 (1) = 6.2% (1).
:::

::: example Explain — cost-push inflation
Explain one cause of cost-push inflation. (2)

A rise in the world price of oil and gas raises firms' energy and transport costs (1); to protect their profit margins firms pass the higher costs on to consumers as higher prices, so the general price level rises (1).
:::

::: example Explain — inflation and savers
Explain one consequence of inflation for savers. (2)

If the interest rate on savings is lower than the rate of inflation, the real value of savings falls (1) — e.g. 2% interest with 6% inflation means savers can buy about 4% less with their money after a year, which discourages saving (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — who loses from inflation
Invented data: in Country P inflation has risen to 7%. Average pay has risen by 4%, the state pension by 7%, and the best savings account pays 3%. Analyse how the rise in inflation affects different groups in Country P. (6)

Workers are worse off in real terms: pay up 4% against prices up 7% means real wages have fallen by about 3%, so households can buy less and may cut back on non-essential spending, which also hits retailers (2). Savers lose too: a 3% return against 7% inflation is a real return of about −4%, so the purchasing power of their savings falls and the incentive to save weakens (2). Pensioners are protected this year, because the pension has risen in line with prices, and borrowers with fixed-rate loans gain as the real value of their debt falls — but if the central bank raises interest rates to fight inflation, borrowers on variable rates will face higher repayments, so who loses most depends on the policy response (2).
:::

---

::: q
Explain why the CPI gives a weight to each category of spending. (2)
---
Households spend different amounts on different items (1), so a price change in a large category such as housing or food should affect the measured inflation rate more than the same change in a small category (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one cause of demand-pull inflation. (2)
---
A cut in interest rates makes borrowing cheaper and saving less attractive (1), so consumer and business spending rise faster than firms can increase output, and prices are bid up (1). (Or: tax cuts; higher government spending; strong export demand; rising confidence — developed.)
:::

::: q
Analyse how high inflation could affect UK exporters. (6)
---
Indicative content: if UK inflation is higher than in other countries (1), UK goods become relatively more expensive abroad (1), so overseas demand for UK exports falls (1); exporters lose sales and market share to foreign rivals (1); rising wage and input costs squeeze profit margins (1); but if the pound falls at the same time, competitiveness may be restored / exporters with strong brands or unique products may be less affected (1).
Levels: 5–6 developed chains; 3–4 some development; 1–2 isolated points.
:::

::: watch
- A **falling inflation rate** (e.g. 9% → 4%) means prices are still **rising**, just more slowly — that is disinflation, not deflation.
- Inflation is a rise in the **general** price level — a rise in the price of one product is not inflation.
- In CPI calculations divide by the **earlier** index (the starting point), not by 100 or by the later figure, and show your working.
- **Cost-push** starts with costs (wages, energy, imports, taxes); **demand-pull** starts with spending. Do not label a rise in oil prices as demand-pull.
- Consequences must be for the **group named** in the question — households, firms, savers or the government — and should compare pay or interest rates with the inflation rate.
- Borrowers can gain from inflation (the real value of debt falls) but lose when interest rates rise to fight it — say which effect you mean.
:::

::: test
Explain two consequences of high inflation for firms. (6)
---
Marked 3 + 3.
Rising costs: suppliers and workers demand more money (1), so firms' costs of production rise (1); if they cannot pass the costs on in full, profit margins are squeezed (1).
Uncertainty: when prices change rapidly firms find it hard to predict future costs and revenue (1), so they may postpone investment in new machinery or premises (1), which slows their growth and productivity (1).
Also creditworthy: menu costs; loss of international competitiveness and export sales; time spent on pay negotiations; falling real demand as consumers' real incomes fall.
