## What it is

**Fortnite** (launched 2017) is the Section B set product for the **video games industry** and its **audiences**. Your centre studies the **Fortnite website** (the homepage and at least two other pages); extracts of gameplay may be used only to illustrate industry and audience points. As with the rest of Section B, the game is evidence for issues (business models, convergence, regulation, targeting and why people play), not a text to analyse for connotations.

### The product and the company

- **Epic Games**, an American developer founded by **Tim Sweeney** in 1991, makes Fortnite. Epic also created the **Unreal Engine**, the software used to build many other games and films, and licenses it to other developers. It runs its own **Epic Games Store**.
- **Ownership and investment**: the Chinese tech conglomerate **Tencent** bought a large minority stake (around 40 per cent) in 2012. Later investors include Sony and the family company behind LEGO (2022) and **Disney**, which invested heavily in 2024 to build a Disney world connected to Fortnite. These are **global** partners with money, platforms and famous intellectual property.
- **The modes**: **Battle Royale**, the famous mode in which up to 100 players drop onto an island and the last player or team standing wins as a storm shrinks the map; **Save the World**, the original paid co-operative mode; and **Creative**, where players build their own islands. Since 2023 creators can use professional tools to publish their own games inside Fortnite, and Epic has added its own games such as LEGO Fortnite, a racing game and a music game. Fortnite is increasingly a **platform** of many games rather than one game.
- **Genre**: a hybrid of a **shooter** (battle royale) and a **building** game, with a bright, cartoon-like style and little realistic violence.

### The business model: free-to-play and games as a service

Battle Royale is **free-to-play**: anyone can download it. Epic makes money through **Games as a Service (GaaS)**, a continuous stream of new content and purchases after the download:
- **V-Bucks**, the in-game currency bought with real money (or earned slowly in play);
- the **Item Shop**, which sells **cosmetics**: **skins** (character outfits), **emotes** (dances), gliders and pickaxes. These are **purely cosmetic** and give no advantage in play, which keeps the game feeling fair;
- the **Battle Pass**, bought each **season**, which unlocks rewards as players progress, so they keep logging in for weeks;
- **Fortnite Crew**, a monthly **subscription** that bundles V-Bucks, the Battle Pass and exclusive skins;
- **limited-time items**: skins that rotate out of the shop create scarcity and **fear of missing out**, pushing players to buy now;
- **microtransactions**: many small payments add up to billions;
- **collaborations**: brands and film studios pay or partner to appear (see below), and creators earn a share of revenue for popular islands.

**Seasons** work like TV series. Each brings a new map, story events and a new pass, and it ends with a **live event** that everyone attends at the same time. This keeps a huge audience returning, which is the core of GaaS.

### Convergence and cross-media collaborations

**Technological convergence**: Fortnite runs on PC, PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo Switch and mobile, with **cross-platform play** and progress that follows the player's account, so friends on different devices can play together. (A long legal dispute with Apple over in-app payments kept it off the iPhone App Store for several years, which shows how much power platform owners hold.)

**Industrial convergence and synergy**:
- **brand and film collaborations**: Marvel, Star Wars, DC, anime series, sports leagues and football clubs appear as skins, maps and events, which promotes both the partner and Fortnite;
- **music**: in-game **virtual concerts** by major artists attracted millions of simultaneous players, and a music game lets players perform hit songs;
- **streaming and video**: Fortnite is one of the most watched games on **Twitch** and **YouTube**, and streamers and content creators act as free marketing;
- **esports**: tournaments with large cash prizes, such as the 2019 Fortnite World Cup won by a teenager, turn top players into celebrities;
- **the website**: news on the current season, the Battle Pass and Item Shop, competitive events, downloads for each platform, creator tools, merchandise and a **parents' guide** with information on controls and spending.

### Regulation

- **PEGI** (Pan European Game Information) gives age ratings across Europe. In the UK the **Video Standards Council Rating Board** applies PEGI ratings, which are legally enforceable for physical sales. Fortnite is rated **PEGI 12** for frequent mild violence and carries the "**in-game purchases**" descriptor.
- **Challenges for regulation from new digital technology**:
  - **online play and voice or text chat** with strangers, which an age rating on game content does not cover;
  - **spending**: microtransactions and pressure to buy limited items, especially among children. In the USA, Epic agreed to pay more than 500 million US dollars in 2022 to settle claims about children's privacy and designs that led to unwanted purchases;
  - **addiction and screen-time** worries, which led to moral panics in the press;
  - **constantly updated content**: a live game changes every season, so a rating given at launch is only a guide;
  - **user-generated islands** made by millions of creators are hard to check;
  - **global** services cross national regulators.
- **Responses**: parental controls on spending, chat and play time; restricted accounts for younger children; clear descriptions of what each purchase contains (no random paid loot boxes in Battle Royale); age guidance on creators' islands.

### Audiences

- **Broad but youth-skewed**: huge numbers of **teenagers and young adults**, mostly male, but also younger children (below the PEGI 12 rating, often with parents' permission), families, and older players. The cartoon style and the building make it accessible to casual players, not just hardcore gamers.
- **How Epic targets them**: free entry removes the cost barrier; the bright, non-gory style reassures parents; collaborations with films, footballers and musicians pull in their fans; seasons and live events create excitement; social media, Twitch and YouTube reach young audiences where they already are; cross-platform play lets everyone join their friends.
- **Categorising**: demographics (age, gender) and psychographics (competitive players, social players, creative builders, collectors of skins, fans of a brand).

**Uses and Gratifications**:
- **Entertainment and diversion**: fast, colourful, competitive play and **escapism** into a fantasy island.
- **Social interaction**: playing in squads with friends, talking over voice chat, watching streams together, and sharing clips and emotes (Fortnite is sometimes described as a social space as much as a game).
- **Personal identity**: skins let players express themselves or show status; buying a rare skin signals being a real fan; players identify with streamers as role models. This links to the **actual and desired self**: players present the self they want others to see.
- **Surveillance / information**: watching streamers to learn tactics, and following news of the next season.

**Active and participatory audiences**: players **create** islands and games, **produce** videos and streams, invent dances, and join tournaments. Audiences also interpret the game differently: parents may see risk and spending, players see friendship and creativity, and the press has at times framed it as a moral panic.

::: terms
- **Free-to-play**: a game that costs nothing to download and makes money from purchases inside the game.
- **Games as a Service**: a business model in which a game is constantly updated with new content and earns continuous revenue after launch.
- **Microtransaction**: a small in-game payment, such as buying V-Bucks to spend on a skin or emote.
- **V-Bucks**: Fortnite's in-game currency, bought with real money or earned through play.
- **Battle Pass**: a seasonal purchase that unlocks rewards as the player progresses, encouraging regular play.
- **Cosmetic item**: an item such as a skin or emote that changes appearance but gives no gameplay advantage.
- **Cross-platform play**: players on different devices (console, PC, mobile) playing together in the same game.
- **PEGI**: Pan European Game Information, the age-rating system for games used in the UK and Europe.
- **Esports**: organised competitive gaming watched by large audiences online and in arenas.
- **Participatory culture**: audiences creating and sharing their own content, such as islands, videos, streams and dances.
- **Moral panic**: exaggerated public fear that a media product is harming society, often focused on young people.
:::

::: example Briefly explain: making money from a free game
Briefly explain how Fortnite makes money even though it is free to play. (4)

Fortnite uses a Games as a Service model: the download is free, which builds a huge audience, and money is made from purchases inside the game (1). Players buy V-Bucks with real money to spend in the Item Shop on cosmetic skins and emotes, which are only on sale for a limited time, creating pressure to buy now (1). ::: example Briefly explain: the website

News of the current season, live events and collaborations creates excitement and gives players reasons to return and log in (1). Pages for the Battle Pass, the Item Shop and V-Bucks turn the site into a shop window that encourages spending (1). Download links for every platform (console, PC and mobile) reflect cross-platform play and make joining easy for anyone (1). ::: example Extended: interaction and U&G
Explain how audiences interact with video games. Refer to the Uses and Gratifications theory and Fortnite to support your points. (12)

Fortnite shows that game audiences are highly active: they don't just play, they buy, create, stream and socialise, and Blumler and Katz's theory explains the needs this satisfies (1).

The most obvious interaction is **social**. Players team up in squads with friends across PlayStation, Xbox, PC and phones because of cross-platform play, and they talk over voice chat, so for many young people the game is a place to hang out, gratifying the need for **social interaction** (2). Live events and concerts at the end of a season bring millions of players together at the same moment, which makes the game feel like a shared cultural event (1).

Players also interact through **personal identity**. Buying skins and emotes with V-Bucks lets them present an **ideal or desired self** and show status. Collaborations let a fan appear as a favourite superhero or footballer, and randomly assigned default skins and a wide range of outfits let players choose characters of different genders and ethnicities (2).

**Entertainment and diversion** come from fast, colourful, low-gore battles and building, a form of **escapism** that suits a PEGI 12 audience (1). For **surveillance**, players watch Twitch and YouTube streamers to learn tactics and follow news of the next season on the website and social media (1).

Interaction is increasingly **participatory**. Players build and publish their own islands in Creative, stream their games and invent dances, so audiences become producers and help market the game for free (2). This interactivity also raises contextual concerns, such as moral panics about addiction and spending by children, which led to parental controls and legal action in the USA, showing that different audiences (players and parents) interpret the same game very differently (2).

This would reach the top band (10–12): U&G applied to specific features throughout, with industry, representation and context drawn together.
:::

::: example Briefly explain: regulation challenges
Briefly explain the challenges of regulating online video games such as Fortnite. (4)

A PEGI rating judges the game's content, but online chat with strangers and players' own creations are not covered by it, so younger players may meet unsuitable content or contact (1). Microtransactions and limited-time items encourage spending, and children may buy without understanding the real cost; in the USA Epic paid a large settlement over purchase designs and children's privacy (1). Because Fortnite updates every season and millions of players publish their own islands, content changes constantly and is hard to check (1). As a global service it crosses national borders, so no single regulator controls it, and much relies on parental controls and Epic's own rules (1).
:::
