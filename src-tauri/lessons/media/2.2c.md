## What it is

**The Sweeney, Series 1, Episode 1, "Ringer" (1975, ITV, certificate 15)** is the **historical** product for the crime drama option. Your centre chooses a **ten-minute extract** (at least ten minutes) from the episode. The exam's TV extract always comes from the contemporary episode (Trigger Point), so The Sweeney is used mainly in **Question 2** (industries, audiences or contexts), e.g. *"How do crime dramas reflect their historical contexts? Refer to The Sweeney"*, and as a **comparison** to show how the genre has changed. It must still be known across the whole framework.

### The product

- First shown on **ITV** on **2 January 1975 at 9pm**. It ran until 1978 and led to two feature films (1977 and 2012).
- Created by Ian Kennedy Martin as a spin-off from a one-off 1974 TV play, *Regan*, whose success led to the series.
- It follows two detectives in the Metropolitan Police's **Flying Squad**, the unit that tackled armed robbery. The title comes from **Cockney rhyming slang**: "Sweeney Todd" for Flying Squad.
- **John Thaw** played Detective Inspector **Jack Regan**; **Dennis Waterman** played Detective Sergeant **George Carter**. Their boss is **DCI Haskins**. The criminals in "Ringer" include the gang boss **Kemble**, his sidekick **Brooker**, and the lower-ranking **Billy** and **Stupid Hawes**. The main female characters are **Jenny**, who is threatened by the gang, and **Edi**.

### Media language

**Genre: an action-led police procedural.** The Sweeney blends **police procedure** with **action** (car chases, fights and armed raids), which had mostly been seen in cinema until then. It **repeats** conventions of earlier British police series and **varies** them with grit, violence and a less reassuring view of the police. It also borrowed from **American** crime imports and influenced later dramas.

| Convention | In The Sweeney |
|---|---|
| **Narrative** | a crime to be investigated and solved; a **linear** structure (Todorov's disruption → resolution); each episode a self-contained case with a new title, with the characters' relationships carrying over |
| **Binary oppositions** | good against evil, police against criminals, and detectives against their own superiors |
| **Character types** | a hierarchy of boss (Haskins), detective (Regan) and sidekick (Carter), mirrored by the villains' hierarchy (Kemble, Brooker, Billy, Hawes) |
| **Settings** | **real London locations**: waste ground in the pre-title sequence, the police station, Jenny's flat, streets; an unglamorous view of the capital |
| **Iconography** | guns (armed robbery), the Flying Squad's **cars** (chases and action), Kemble's luxurious house, leather chair and pipe (power and class) |
| **Costume** | Haskins's formal suit and overcoat (senior rank), Regan and Carter's slightly shabby plain clothes (working detectives), the villains' distinct look |

**Propp's roles**: Regan and Carter are **heroes**, solving the crime and protecting the public, but Regan is also an **anti-hero**: he drinks, smokes, bends rules and challenges authority, though he is not corrupt (a villain says of the Squad that you can't buy them). **Carter** is his loyal **helper**. **Jenny** is the **princess** or damsel in distress. The criminals are the **villains**, each with a sidekick.

**Technical codes and technology**:
- shot on **16mm film on location** by a small crew. This gave a **grainy, washed-out** look and **gritty realism**, a contrast with studio-bound police dramas of the time;
- mostly **continuity editing**, and some scenes clearly use a single camera, which reflects the simpler technology of the 1970s;
- **longer takes** than in modern dramas, asking the audience to concentrate as in serious drama;
- **sound**: sparing use of music; **silence** during surveillance (innovative and realistic), then music cutting in as the story moves on; an **iconic theme tune** that became part of the brand;
- **accents** mark **class and hierarchy**: Cockney villains against Haskins's received pronunciation (RP), and a joke about a highbrow play that the other officers don't understand.

### Representation

- **Masculinity**: Regan is tough, powerful and willing to use violence and threats. He is introduced in the morning in a woman's flowered dressing gown in Jenny's flat, which suggests casual relationships and relaxed morals. His slightly shabby look suggests a man who doesn't care about appearances, a stereotype of men of the time. Carter is more stable and humorous, the loyal "buddy". Both drink, smoke, fight and break rules, a **macho** 1970s workplace culture presented as exciting.
- **Women** get little screen time. **Jenny** is a **victim**: at first we hear rather than see her, she is threatened by the gang, and she needs Regan's protection. In the final scene she sits on the floor while he sits above her, a **subservient** position. **Edi** is feistier and streetwise but still **long-suffering**, left in the dark by her husband's return to crime. The only **policewoman** appears in a **caring** role, looking after Jenny.
- **Ethnicity**: the police and the criminals are **white**, reflecting how most TV drama of the time left out ethnic minorities (**under-representation**).
- **Class**: Kemble's pipe, refined accent and luxurious home separate him from his working-class gang; the RP-speaking boss is set against the Cockney detectives and villains.
- **The police**: shown as effective but rough, and willing to bend the law, a less reassuring image than earlier series.

### Industry

- Made by **Euston Films**, the film-making arm of **Thames Television**. Thames held the **ITV franchise** for London on weekdays (1968–1992); ITV was then a network of regional companies.
- **Commercially funded**: ITV was the UK's only commercial TV network in 1975, funded by **advertising**. High ratings meant valuable advertising income, so The Sweeney was important to Thames and ITV. (Trigger Point is also advertising-funded ITV drama, but it reaches its audience through ITVX streaming as well.)
- **High production values for TV of the time**: film stock, location shooting and a tight shooting schedule for each episode.
- **Regulation**: shown **after the 9pm watershed** because of its violence and mature themes. Swearing and references to sex were restrained even so, reflecting the standards of the day. The DVDs are rated **15**.
- **Afterlife**: repeats on digital channels, remastered DVD box sets and streaming bring it to new audiences and generate further income.

### Audiences

- **Mass audience**: in 1975 there were only **three channels** (BBC1, BBC2 and ITV), so popular programmes reached huge shared audiences. At its peak The Sweeney drew around **19 million** viewers.
- **Appeal**: a more **realistic** picture of the police, which matched stories in the press; **action** (chases, shoot-outs) usually seen only at the cinema; the **humour** and loyalty between Regan and Carter; exciting male lives aimed particularly at a **male** audience of the time.
- **Uses and Gratifications**: **entertainment and diversion** (a thrilling escape into a fictional London underworld); **information** (a glimpse of police procedure and gangland, echoing real gangsters such as the Kray twins, whom the episode mentions); **personal identity** (men of the time identifying with tough heroes); **social interaction** (talking about the previous night's episode at work).
- **Changing responses over time**: 1970s audiences saw exciting, realistic heroes; modern viewers may read the sexism, the violence against suspects and the all-white cast **critically**, or enjoy the show as **retro**, nostalgic entertainment. Older fans may rewatch it on digital channels or box sets.

### Contexts

- **Historical and political**: in the early 1970s the real **Flying Squad** and CID were hit by **corruption** scandals (bribery and links with gangsters). A new Metropolitan Police commissioner, Robert Mark, was brought in from 1972 to clean up the force, and many officers were prosecuted or left. The programme was made before the **Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984**, which set rules on how police could treat suspects, so Regan's threats and violence to extract information reflect a time with **fewer limits** on police methods.
- **Social and cultural**: a **patriarchal** society and male-dominated workplaces with few women in senior police ranks; a **macho** culture of drinking, smoking and violence; social hierarchy and class; an almost entirely white mainstream television. Cultural influences included press crime stories and American police dramas. The show itself became part of popular culture, quoted and referenced in songs and later TV.
- **Compared with Trigger Point**: from an all-male, all-white Flying Squad with women as victims, to a female expert lead and a diverse team; from rule-bending violence to professional specialists and technology; from single stories for a mass audience on three channels, to a serial with cliffhangers premiered on ITV and ITVX for a fragmented, on-demand audience. Both are commercial ITV dramas shown after the watershed.

::: terms
- **Police procedural**: a crime drama sub-genre that follows police officers investigating crimes, showing their methods and teamwork.
- **Flying Squad**: the Metropolitan Police unit that dealt with armed robbery, known in rhyming slang as "the Sweeney".
- **Anti-hero**: a protagonist who lacks some traditional heroic qualities, such as Regan's rule-breaking, drinking and violence.
- **Binary opposition**: a pair of opposites that structures a narrative, such as police against criminals.
- **16mm film**: a lightweight film stock that allowed location shooting and gave The Sweeney its grainy, realistic look.
- **Location filming**: shooting in real places rather than on studio sets, which creates realism.
- **Received pronunciation**: the accent traditionally associated with the educated upper and middle classes, used by Haskins to signal rank.
- **ITV franchise**: the licence to broadcast on the ITV network in a region, held by companies such as Thames Television in London.
- **Patriarchy**: a social system in which men hold most power and women are treated as subordinate.
- **Gritty realism**: a style showing a rough, unglamorous version of reality, through real locations, grainy images and violence.
:::

::: example Q2 historical contexts
How do crime dramas reflect their historical contexts? Refer to The Sweeney to support your points. (10)

The Sweeney is shaped by 1970s Britain in its view of the police, its representations and its production (1). In the early 1970s the real Flying Squad was hit by **corruption** scandals, and a new commissioner set out to clean up the Met. The Sweeney reflects this less trusting mood: Regan bends rules and defies Haskins, although the programme insists that he and Carter are honest (2). It was made before the **Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984**, so Regan's threats and rough treatment of suspects to get information reflect a time with few limits on police methods (2). The **macho 1970s workplace** is shown in the drinking, smoking and violence of an all-male squad, while women appear as victims, like Jenny, or in caring roles, like the only policewoman, reflecting gender inequality at the time (2). The all-white cast reflects the under-representation of ethnic minorities in 1970s TV (1). Its production reflects the period too: shot on **16mm film on location** by Euston Films for **ITV**, at 9pm, to a mass audience of up to 19 million when there were only three channels (2).
:::

::: example Comparing with Trigger Point
Explain how the representation of gender in crime drama has changed. Refer to The Sweeney and Trigger Point. (10)

Gender in crime drama has moved from male dominance to a female-led, more varied picture, although some conventions remain (1). In The Sweeney, power belongs to men: Regan, Carter, Haskins and the criminals are all men, and toughness, drinking and violence define masculinity (2). Women are marginal. Jenny is a victim threatened by the gang and protected by Regan, and she is placed physically below him in the final scene, while the only policewoman is there to comfort her, reflecting 1970s patriarchy (2). In Trigger Point the hero is a woman, Lana Washington, an expert who leads the team and speaks with authority, and in the rescue of Thom the roles are **reversed**, with a frightened man saved by a composed woman (2). Masculinity is also less one-dimensional: Thom's fear shows vulnerability, and John Hudson's resistance to female authority is framed as a flaw (2). The change reflects feminism, more women in male-dominated jobs and audiences who expect diverse leads, although both dramas still centre a driven, troubled protagonist (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why The Sweeney was scheduled at 9pm. (3)
---
It contained violence, threats and mature themes (1); the 9pm watershed meant content unsuitable for children could only be shown after that time (1); the late slot also targeted an adult, especially male, audience and suited advertisers wanting to reach it (1).
