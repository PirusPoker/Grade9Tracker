## What it is

**Media language** is the set of choices a producer uses to make meaning: every colour, font, camera angle, sound and word in a product. Media Studies treats a product as a **text** that has been built, element by element, to make an audience think and feel something. Your job in the exam is to name an element, say what it suggests and explain why the producer chose it. This topic is tested on both papers: Component 1 Question 1 (15 marks on a print set product) and Component 2 Questions 1 and 3 (on the TV episode and the music videos).

### The four kinds of code

| Code | What it covers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual codes** | Everything you can see that is not text: colour, costume, make-up, hair, gesture, facial expression, props, setting, lighting | A red outfit on a cover star; a gun as a prop on a spy poster; a hospital waiting room in an advert |
| **Written codes** | The words and how they look: headline, cover line, tagline, slogan, caption, typography (font, size, case, colour) | A pun headline on a tabloid; a serif masthead that suggests tradition and class |
| **Technical codes** | How the product is made: shot type, camera angle and movement, framing, focus, lighting set-up, editing, layout, photo manipulation | A low-angle long shot that makes a footballer look powerful; a fast-cut chase |
| **Audio codes** | Everything you hear: dialogue, music, sound effects, silence, voiceover; **diegetic** (in the story world) or **non-diegetic** (added for the audience) | A tense non-diegetic string drone before a reveal; a siren (diegetic) in a crime drama |

Print products use visual, written and technical codes. Audio-visual products add audio codes and editing. **Mise-en-scène** ("everything placed in the frame") is a useful umbrella for setting, props, costume, make-up, lighting, and the positioning of people.

### Semiotics: denotation, connotation, anchorage

**Semiotics** is the study of signs, and Roland Barthes gives you the core tools.
- A **sign** is anything that carries meaning: an image, a word, a colour.
- **Denotation** is what the sign literally shows: *a woman in a red headscarf looking at the camera*.
- **Connotation** is the associations it carries for an audience: *warmth, confidence, faith, celebration*. Connotations depend on culture, so the same colour can mean different things to different audiences.
- **Anchorage** is the way written text fixes (anchors) the meaning of an image, closing down other readings. A caption under a cover star tells you which connotations to take.
- **Enigma codes** raise questions the audience wants answered (Who is holding the gun? What happened next?). **Action codes** signal that something is about to happen (a raised fist, a car at speed). Both push the audience to buy, read or keep watching.
- **Myth**: Barthes' idea that connotations repeated over and over come to seem natural, like common sense. The idea that a spy hero must be a white man in a suit felt "natural" for decades only because it was repeated.

The exam rewards the move from denotation to connotation to **purpose**: *what* is shown → what it *suggests* → *why* the producer wants the audience to read it that way.

### Selection, combination and exclusion

Every product is the result of choices.
- **Selection**: what the producer chose to include: this photo, this word, this star.
- **Combination**: how elements are put together so they affect each other. A soft smile combined with a tough cover line makes a different meaning from either alone.
- **Exclusion**: what is left out. A newspaper that pictures a fire but not the engineers fixing it is making a choice; so is a film poster that leaves off the actors' names.

These choices **create narratives**, **portray reality** (a constructed version of it), **construct a point of view** (whose side we are on) and **convey messages and values** (what the product thinks matters).

### Layout and design conventions in print

- **Rule of thirds** and the **Z-rule**: readers' eyes travel top-left → top-right → diagonally → bottom-left → bottom-right, so producers place the masthead top-left and a price, date or call to action bottom-right.
- **Central framing** gives dominance; **size** signals importance; **colour palettes** create a **house style** and link elements.
- **Typography**: serif fonts connote tradition, authority and class; sans-serif fonts connote modernity, clarity and accessibility; capitals connote urgency or power; italics connote elegance or a personal voice.

### Media language in audio-visual products

| Area | Key terms and typical meanings |
|---|---|
| **Shots** | Extreme long shot/establishing shot (setting, isolation); long shot (whole body, action); mid-shot (interaction); close-up (emotion); extreme close-up (detail, tension) |
| **Angles** | Low angle (power); high angle (vulnerability); eye level (equality); canted/Dutch angle (unease) |
| **Movement** | Pan, tilt, tracking/dolly, handheld (realism, panic), crane or drone shot (scale), zoom |
| **Editing** | Continuity editing (invisible, realistic); shot–reverse shot (conversation); cross-cutting (two actions at once, building tension); pace of cutting (fast = excitement); match on action; montage; fades and wipes; slow motion |
| **Sound** | Diegetic dialogue and effects; non-diegetic score, voiceover, laughter track; sound bridge; silence for shock |

**Worked audio-visual reading.** Imagine a crime drama opening: an aerial drone shot of a city at dawn (establishing, scale, a whole community at risk); cut to a handheld close-up of an officer's face as a radio crackles (diegetic, urgency, realism); cross-cutting between the officer and a suspect's hands on a bag, with a rising non-diegetic drone (tension, enigma: what is in the bag?); then a sudden silence and a jump cut (shock). Each technical choice builds genre expectations and holds the audience.

### Genre: codes and conventions that change

A **genre** is a type of product recognised by shared **conventions**: typical characters, settings, iconography, narratives, technical codes and audience pleasures. A spy film poster has a hero with a gun, glamorous locations and a franchise logo; a sitcom has a familiar setting, stock characters and a circular story.

Genre theory (associated with Steve Neale) gives you four ideas the spec names:
- **Repetition and variation**: genres repeat familiar conventions (pleasure, recognition, easy marketing) but must vary them to stay fresh. A new Bond poster repeats the 007 logo but varies who holds the gun.
- **The dynamic nature of genre**: conventions change over time as society, technology and audiences change. Conventions "become established" by repetition across many products until they feel like a style.
- **Hybridity**: mixing genres, such as a mockumentary sitcom, or a music video that combines performance and narrative.
- **Intertextuality**: one product referring to another. It rewards audiences who spot it, borrows meanings (a pun headline on a famous sitcom title borrows its comedy of incompetence) and builds brand continuity (a franchise logo repeated across decades).

**Technology and media products.** What a product can look like depends on how it is made. A 1970s film poster is illustrated partly because painted collages were the standard way to show many scenes at once; a 2020s poster is a digital photographic montage with colour grading. Location filming on lightweight cameras allowed 1970s crime drama to leave the studio; digital editing lets music videos cut to the beat. Always ask what the technology *allowed* and what it *meant*.

### Narrative

- **Propp's character roles**: from folk tales, Vladimir Propp identified recurring roles: the **hero** (on a quest), the **villain** (opposes the hero), the **donor** (gives the hero something useful), the **helper**, the **princess** (the reward or the person to be saved, often passive), her **father**, the **dispatcher** (sends the hero off) and the **false hero**. Use them on posters and adverts as well as on TV: a Bond poster shows hero, villain and "princess" figures before you have seen a frame of film.
- **Equilibrium** (Todorov): a story moves from equilibrium → disruption → recognition → attempt to repair → a new equilibrium. Sitcoms often return to the old equilibrium; crime dramas end with a new one, often uneasy.
- **Enigma and action codes** (Barthes) drive narrative on the page as well as on screen: a cover line that promises a secret is a narrative hook.
- Print products **imply** narrative: a single image can suggest before, during and after.

::: terms
- **Denotation**: the literal, surface meaning of a sign, what is actually shown or said.
- **Connotation**: the associations and ideas a sign suggests to an audience, which depend on culture and context.
- **Anchorage**: the way written text, such as a caption or cover line, fixes the preferred meaning of an image.
- **Enigma code**: a question or mystery set up in a product that makes the audience want to find out more.
- **Mise-en-scène**: everything placed in the frame, including setting, props, costume, make-up, lighting and positioning.
- **Diegetic sound**: sound that exists in the story world and that the characters could hear, such as dialogue or a siren.
- **Non-diegetic sound**: sound added for the audience only, such as a musical score, voiceover or laughter track.
- **Convention**: a typical, expected feature of a genre or media form that audiences recognise.
- **Hybridity**: the mixing of two or more genres or forms in one product.
- **Intertextuality**: a reference in one media product to another product, which borrows or plays with its meanings.
- **House style**: the consistent design of a brand (fonts, colours, layout) that makes it instantly recognisable.
- **Propp's roles**: a set of recurring character functions, such as hero, villain, helper and princess, found in narratives.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the question's aspect** (layout and design, images, narrative, written language, camerawork, sound). Stay on that aspect; mention others only as they combine with it.
2. **Pick three or four specific elements** of the product. Be precise: "the silver italic caption beneath her name", not "the writing".
3. **Denote briefly, then connote.** One short clause of what is there, then what it suggests. Use a term every time (low angle, serif, anchorage, non-diegetic).
4. **Explain the purpose**: why this choice, for this audience, genre and brand. This is what lifts an answer from 5–6 to 9–10.
5. **Link to theory where it sharpens a point**: Barthes (connotation, anchorage, enigma), genre (repetition and variation, intertextuality), Propp's roles, equilibrium.
6. **For a 10-mark part**, develop each point through combination (how two elements work together) and, where relevant, context. For a 5-mark part, two or three well-developed points are enough.
:::

::: example Denotation to purpose
Explain how colour creates meaning on a magazine front cover you have studied. (4)

On the Vogue (July 2021) cover, Malala Yousafzai is shown against a warm red background, wearing a red headscarf and outfit (1). Red connotes celebration, confidence and strength, and in her culture it is associated with joyful occasions such as weddings (1). The limited palette of red, silver-grey, black and white looks sophisticated and uncluttered, which matches Vogue's high-end brand identity for an ABC1 audience (1). The silver-grey of the masthead is repeated in the caption that labels her a survivor and activist, visually linking her to the Vogue brand and inviting readers to see her as a Vogue role model (1).
:::

::: example Anchorage
What is anchorage? Give an example from a print set product. (2)

Anchorage is when written text fixes the preferred meaning of an image and limits other readings (1). For example, on the GQ (August 2019) cover the "Guardian Angel" cover line anchors the black wings on Raheem Sterling's back, so they read as protection and moral goodness rather than, say, fantasy costume (1).
:::

::: example Genre and intertextuality
Briefly explain how film posters use intertextuality. Refer to a set product to support your points. (4)

Intertextuality is when one product refers to another, borrowing its meanings and rewarding audiences who notice (1). The No Time to Die poster repeats the 007 gun logo, used across the franchise for decades, which acts as an intertextual link that brands the film instantly and gives long-standing fans a sense of familiarity and nostalgia (1). Its montage design, with many characters layered together, echoes earlier Bond posters, repeating a convention while varying it through a photographic, colour-graded style (1). The Aston Martin DB5 on the poster refers back to the car's first appearance in the 1960s films, connecting Daniel Craig's final film to the franchise's history and appealing to a core audience of older fans (1).
:::

::: example An audio-visual sequence
Explore how editing and sound create meaning in a tense scene from the television episode you have studied. (10)

Model answer (a general plan to adapt to your episode): the scene opens with a slow establishing shot and quiet diegetic sound, creating a false sense of equilibrium (1). As the threat appears, the pace of editing quickens: shots shorten, and cross-cutting between the investigator and the threat builds suspense because the audience sees both sides and anticipates the collision (2). Close-ups on hands and faces are inserted between longer shots, focusing attention on small details and creating enigma codes (1). A non-diegetic score of low, repeated notes rises under the action, telling the audience how to feel and following the conventions of the crime genre (2). At the climax the music cuts to silence, and a single diegetic sound (a phone, a footstep) becomes shocking by contrast (2). Continuity editing keeps the audience oriented, so the tension comes from pace rather than confusion, and the sequence ends on a close-up reaction shot, a cliffhanger that holds the audience for the next scene (2).

Band: 9–10. Each technique is named, its effect explained, and linked to genre and audience.
:::

::: example Propp on a poster
Briefly explain how Propp's character roles can be applied to a film poster you have studied. (4)

Propp identified recurring character functions such as hero, villain and princess (1). On The Man with the Golden Gun (1974) poster, Bond is the dominant central figure holding a gun, constructing him as the hero on a quest (1). The golden gun in extreme close-up, pointed at him by an unseen hand, stands for the villain, and because we cannot see the villain's face it also works as an enigma code (1). The bikini-clad women around Bond fit the passive "princess" role as rewards for the hero, though one woman in a martial-arts pose complicates this by looking more active (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by "repetition and variation" in genre. (3)
---
Genres repeat familiar conventions so audiences recognise them and get expected pleasures (1); they also vary conventions to keep products fresh and appeal to changing audiences (1); e.g. a Bond poster repeats the 007 logo but varies the representation of women by giving a female agent a gun (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a low-angle shot creates meaning. Use an example. (3)
---
The camera looks up at the subject (1), which makes them appear powerful, dominant or heroic (1); e.g. Raheem Sterling on the GQ cover is shot from a low angle, constructing him as an admired protector figure (1).
:::

::: q
What is the difference between diegetic and non-diegetic sound? Give an example of each. (4)
---
Diegetic sound comes from within the story world and characters could hear it (1), e.g. dialogue, a siren, a slammed door (1). Non-diegetic sound is added for the audience only (1), e.g. a musical score, voiceover or laughter track (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how selection and exclusion shape the meaning of a newspaper front page. (4)
---
Editors select which story leads and which image dominates, signalling what the paper thinks matters most (1); e.g. a tabloid choosing a dramatic fire image makes an event feel like a disaster (1). Excluding stories or viewpoints narrows the version of reality offered (1), so the page constructs a point of view that fits its audience and politics (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Describing is not analysing.** "There is a woman in red" gets almost nothing; say what red suggests and why the producer chose it.
- **Stay on the aspect asked.** In a "layout and design" part, write about placement, size, composition and typography, not a list of connotations of the image.
- **Use terms precisely**: masthead, cover line, anchorage, long shot, serif, non-diegetic. Vague words like "the writing" or "the picture" keep you in the lower bands.
- **Theory is a tool, not a paragraph.** One accurate use of Barthes or Propp inside a point beats a memorised definition.
- **Purpose and audience** are what separate 7–8 from 9–10: always finish the point with why.
:::
