## What it is

Taylor Swift's ***The Man*** (2020) is the alternative to *Good as Hell* for your **first** contemporary music video. It is studied for **all five areas**, with her official website, **taylorswift.com**, and her social media. In the exam it appears in **Q3 (20 marks)** alongside your second video (*Superheroes* or *Intentions*), and her website can be the subject of **Q4 (10 marks)**: in 2025 Q4 asked how users interact with the Lizzo or Taylor Swift website.

**Background.**
- *The Man* is from Swift's 2019 album ***Lover***, released on **Republic Records** (part of **Universal Music Group**), which she joined in 2018 after leaving Big Machine Records. The video premiered on her YouTube channel in **February 2020**.
- Swift **wrote and directed** it. It won the MTV Video Music Award for **Best Direction** in 2020, the first time a solo female director had won that category.
- Swift plays **"the Man"**, her male alter ego, using **prosthetics and a muscle suit** that took hours to apply each day. His voice is performed by **Dwayne Johnson**, a star known for hyper-masculine roles.
- The song imagines how differently she would be treated if she were a man. The video turns that into **satire**: exaggerated scenes of male privilege and the **double standards** women face.

### What happens

| Scene | What we see | The double standard behind it |
|---|---|---|
| **Subway** | The Man sprawls across seats (**manspreading**), flicks ash at a woman; graffiti names Swift's earlier albums; a poster punning on "boss" and her old label boss's first name, with "greedy" scrawled beside it; posters of hyper-masculine action films | Men take up space and ignore others without consequence |
| **Office** | He tosses a crumpled paper and three women scramble to catch it | Women made to compete for male approval, like catching a bouquet |
| **Yacht** | In a gold shirt, holding champagne, centred and raised above women in identical yellow bikinis looking up at him | Wealthy men flaunting women as accessories are envied, not judged |
| **Park** | He is applauded by mothers for a few seconds with his daughter | Fathers praised for what mothers do every day unnoticed |
| **Tennis** | He smashes his racket (a nod to tennis star John McEnroe's famous tantrums); after a time jump he marries a much younger woman and shoves cake in her face | Male anger seen as passion; older men with young wives admired |
| **The reveal** | Swift, as director, calls "cut" and tells the actor to be "sexier" and "more likeable"; behind-the-scenes shots show the transformation; credits: written and directed by Taylor Swift | The notes women routinely get, now given by a woman to a man; she is the real author |

Cameos include TikTok star **Loren Gray**, whose eye-roll at the Man's tantrum invites the audience to share the disapproval, and Swift's father as a line judge.

### Media language

- **Challenges conventions.** There is **no performance** of the song, **no lip-sync** by Swift and **no direct address**. It is almost entirely a **narrative** video, which is unusual for a pop star whose image is the product. The reveal turns the whole video into a performance of a *character*.
- **Linear narrative** through a "typical day": everyday settings (subway, office, park) show everyday sexism; luxurious settings (yacht, tennis club) show **wealth and privilege**.
- **Propp.** The Man is the **false hero**: he takes credit and applause throughout. Swift is unmasked as the **true hero** when she is revealed as writer and director.
- **Camerawork.** The Man is **centred** and tracked by the camera, often surrounded by supporting characters, connoting his assumed importance. On the yacht a **long shot** places him higher than the women around him, signifying dominance and their subservience. **Reaction shots** of minor characters (the eye-roll, a disapproving crew member) cue the audience's response.
- **Costume and colour.** The **business suit** connotes power and status; the **gold designer shirt** on the yacht, wealth and excess; the women's **identical yellow bikinis** strip them of individuality, a visual joke about objectification.
- **Gesture codes.** Manspreading, ash-flicking and racket-smashing all show disregard for others.
- **Intertextuality.** References to her own albums (graffiti), to John McEnroe, to action-film posters, to her 2020 Netflix documentary *Miss Americana* (a poster for a male version of it), and to her dispute over her master recordings. Fans enjoy decoding these "Easter eggs", which rewards close viewing and repeat watching.

### Representation

- **Men.** Represented as **arrogant, entitled and self-obsessed**, but through **exaggeration and satire**, not realism. The video represents **male privilege**: how the same behaviour earns men praise and women criticism.
- **Women.** Mostly **secondary, supporting characters** (admirers, assistants, a young bride), which mirrors the problem the video criticises. Until the end, women are defined by their relationship to the Man. A feminist reading would note the **objectification** in the yacht and wedding scenes, used deliberately to expose it.
- **Swift's self-representation.** The reveal constructs her as a **powerful creative author**, a woman in control of her image, her work and the camera, rebutting the idea that female pop stars are products shaped by men. This is the video's key representation.
- **Theory.** **Feminist approaches**: the video exposes the **male gaze** and patriarchal double standards. Reception: the **preferred reading** is feminist empowerment; an **oppositional** reading might find the male stereotype crude or outdated.
- **Ethnicity and class.** A mostly white, wealthy world. You could argue that the video's feminism focuses on privileged, successful women, which is a limit on its representation.

### Industry

- **Ownership and control.** Swift's early albums were made with **Big Machine Records**; its founder, **Scott Borchetta**, and later other owners held the **master recordings**. After a public dispute she **re-recorded** her first six albums as "Taylor's Version" to own new masters, and in 2025 she announced she had bought back her original masters. The subway poster alludes to this dispute. It is a clear case study of **artist control versus label ownership**.
- **Label**: Republic Records / Universal, a **major** with global distribution.
- **Convergence**: YouTube premiere; streaming playlists (added for International Women's Day 2020); the Netflix documentary *Miss Americana*; behind-the-scenes "making of" video; huge world tours (the **Eras Tour** became the highest-grossing tour of all time) and a concert film.
- **taylorswift.com** (credit any version): home page led by the **current era**, with its own colours and imagery; "listen now" links to streaming and stores; **collectible vinyl and CD variants** (different colours and covers) that encourage fans to buy several copies; merchandise; tour dates and tickets; microsites for tours and films; links to social media. The 2025 mark scheme cited an Eras Tour microsite with a "How to Watch" page and *The Tortured Poets Department* release.

### Audiences

- **Swifties**: a huge, loyal, global, mostly young-adult and female fandom that grew up with her.
- **Participatory fandom**: fans decode clues, make theories, share edits and use hashtags (#TheMan); Swift posted a **teaser** two days before the premiere and answered **fan questions** live before it; she thanked the cameo actors publicly on social media.
- **Uses and gratifications**: *entertainment* (satire, spectacle); *personal identity* (women recognising the double standards; fans identifying with Swift); *social interaction* (theories, sharing); *information* (raising awareness of gender inequality).
- The song was quoted by a UK government minister in a House of Commons debate on International Women's Day 2020, an unusual crossover from pop into politics.

### Contexts

- **Social and political**: the **#MeToo** movement (from 2017), campaigns against harassment and for women to be believed; wider debates on the **gender pay gap** and male privilege.
- **Industry context**: the **under-representation of women** as directors and producers in the music and film industries; Swift's fight over her masters.
- **Personal context**: *Miss Americana* showed her deciding to speak out politically; the video continues that public self-representation.

::: terms
- **Alter ego**: a second, invented identity, like Swift's male character "the Man".
- **Satire**: using exaggeration and humour to criticise people, behaviour or society.
- **Double standard**: a rule or judgement applied differently to different groups, such as praising men for what women are criticised for.
- **Male privilege**: the social advantages men receive because of their gender.
- **Manspreading**: men sitting with legs wide apart on public transport, taking up more space than needed.
- **False hero**: Propp's character who claims the hero's credit without deserving it.
- **Master recordings**: the original recordings of songs, whose owner controls how they are used and earns from them.
- **Easter egg**: a hidden reference or clue placed in a product for fans to find.
- **#MeToo**: a movement from 2017 in which women shared experiences of sexual harassment and abuse.
- **Prosthetics**: artificial make-up pieces used to change a performer's appearance.
:::

4. **Link to context** (#MeToo, the masters dispute, women directors) in most paragraphs.

::: example Satire and double standards
Explore how The Man uses media language to criticise gender double standards. (10)

The whole video is built on an alter ego: Swift, in prosthetics, plays a man so that ordinary male behaviour can be viewed from outside (1). In the subway scene the Man is centred in the frame, manspreading and flicking ash on a woman, gesture codes that show his disregard for others (1); because he suffers no consequence, the scene exposes how men are allowed to take up space (1). On the yacht a long shot places him above a row of women in identical yellow bikinis looking up at him, connoting dominance and objectification (1), while his gold shirt and champagne signify privilege (1). In the park, mothers applaud him for a few seconds with his child, exposing how fathers are praised for what mothers do unnoticed (1). Reaction shots, like Loren Gray's eye-roll, cue the audience to disapprove (1). The final reveal breaks the illusion: Swift, as director, tells the actor to be "more likeable", the kind of note women receive (1). Revealing her as writer and director makes the Man a Proppian false hero and Swift the true hero (1), a point sharpened by the #MeToo context and the under-representation of female directors (1).
:::

::: example Challenging conventions
How far does The Man challenge the conventions of music videos? (8)

It challenges them strongly. Swift never performs the song, lip-syncs or addresses the camera, which breaks the most basic convention of a star video (2). It is almost pure narrative, a satirical "day in the life" (2). Yet some conventions remain: the star is still at the centre of every shot, only disguised; costume, glamorous settings and intertextual Easter eggs build her brand; and the behind-the-scenes reveal ultimately restores Swift as the star (2). So it challenges the form's surface while reinforcing the star-centred purpose of the music video, which is to promote the artist (2).
:::

::: example Industry: ownership
Explain how Taylor Swift's dispute over her master recordings is reflected in The Man. (4)

Her early albums' masters were owned by her former label, so she did not control their use (1). In the subway scene a poster punning on "boss" and the first name of her former label's founder, with "greedy" scrawled beside it, alludes to this (1). The video therefore links gender inequality to her own fight for control in the industry (1), and the reveal of Swift as director reinforces the message that she owns her work (1).
:::
