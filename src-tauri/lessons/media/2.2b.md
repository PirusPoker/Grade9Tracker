## What it is

**Trigger Point, Series 2, Episode 1 (2024, ITV, certificate 15)** is the contemporary set episode for the **crime drama option** (for exams from 2027). It is studied in depth across **all areas** of the framework: media language, representation, industries, audiences and contexts. It is paired with a ten-minute extract from **The Sweeney (1975)**, and the spec highlights two contrasts: Trigger Point's **gender and ethnic diversity** against The Sweeney's all-male world, and the fact that **both are commercially funded ITV dramas**, although Trigger Point's **simultaneous premiere on ITV and ITVX** reflects the growth of streaming.

**The premise**: **Lana Washington**, played by **Vicky McClure**, is an ex-military bomb-disposal expert (an "**Expo**") and a veteran of the war in Afghanistan. She now works in a Metropolitan Police bomb-disposal team dealing with terrorist attacks in London. Key characters named in the official factsheet include her colleague **Danny**, the officer **Hassan**, **Thom**, **Commander Francis** and **John Hudson**. The exam shows a **three-minute extract** from this episode, so know its key sequences well. Where you're unsure of a detail, describe what you see in the extract rather than relying on memory.

### Media language

**Genre**: Trigger Point is a **crime thriller** and a **police drama** with many recognisable conventions:
- **characters**: a troubled, dedicated protagonist (Lana), a trusted sidekick (Danny), anonymous terrorists as villains, and senior officers with their own agendas;
- **settings**: the **urban** city, the police station and control room, and **dangerous locations** such as the power station;
- **iconography**: uniforms, **protective bomb suits** and specialist equipment, screens, drones, phones and high-tech devices, **explosions** and dead bodies;
- **sound**: **diegetic** sirens, detonations, radio talk and technical **jargon** for realism, with a **non-diegetic** score that builds tension.

**Variation** (the dynamic nature of genre): a **female protagonist** in a genre and job historically dominated by men (one senior officer remarks on how unusual it is to see a woman in the role), and a focus on a **bomb-disposal unit** rather than detectives investigating a murder. The genre is **repeated** enough to be recognisable and **varied** enough to feel new.

**Narrative**:
- **Suspense** and **action codes**: Lana works to defuse devices **against the clock**, and the audience knows the danger before the characters do.
- **Enigma codes** (Barthes): who are the terrorists, and what do they want?
- **Cliffhanger**: the episode ends on a hook to bring viewers back for the next instalment, which matters for both weekly broadcast and streaming.
- **Propp's roles** (as applied in the official factsheet): **Lana = hero**; **Danny = helper**; the **terrorists = villains**; **Hassan = dispatcher**; **Thom = princess**, the one who is saved; **Commander Francis = false hero**, who interferes and has questionable motives.
- **Todorov**: an equilibrium of routine work is disrupted by an attack, and the investigation and defusing attempts follow. The serial form delays any new equilibrium across the series.

**Technical codes to look for in the extract** (and how to write about them):
- **close-ups** of faces and hands during defusing: concentration, fear and the tiny margin for error;
- **point-of-view** or over-the-shoulder shots: we share Lana's view and her tension;
- **handheld** camera and **fast cutting** in action: chaos and urgency; **slow, lingering** shots in quiet moments build dread;
- **cross-cutting** between locations or characters: parallel action and rising stakes;
- **low-key lighting** and a cold palette: danger and a grim, realistic tone;
- **sound**: a pulsing score, heavy breathing inside the suit, sudden **silence** before a possible explosion.

### Representation

**Gender**:
- **Lana** is constructed as **highly effective, determined and calm under pressure**. Her costume and equipment signal her status in the team, and her voice is clear, firm and authoritative.
- **Role reversal**: in the rescue of **Thom** from a room rigged with pressure plates, Lana shows composure, skill and bravery, while Thom's words and body language convey fear and dependence. This **reverses** the stereotype of the male hero saving a vulnerable woman.
- **Masculinity** is more varied than in older crime drama: Thom's panic shows **male vulnerability**, and John Hudson's arrogant resistance to female authority is presented negatively.

**Ethnicity**:
- The episode addresses the **under-representation** and **misrepresentation** of ethnic minorities in TV drama.
- **Hassan**, a British Asian Muslim, is a highly competent officer **fighting** terrorism. This challenges the damaging stereotype of the **Muslim terrorist**.
- **Danny**, a Black man, is a trusted, valued colleague, which challenges negative stereotypes of Black men in crime drama.
- Some **white** characters are shown less sympathetically: Hudson has expressed **racist** views and Lana distrusts him.

**Why it matters**: these representations reflect changing gender roles after several **waves of feminism**, a more **multicultural** society, and audiences who expect **diverse, progressive** representation. Some viewers will read them as positive progress; others may see the diversity as a deliberate selling point. Readings depend on the audience's own experience and values.

### Industry

- **Production**: **HTM Television**, a UK company jointly owned by **Jed Mercurio** (creator of the hit police drama Line of Duty) and **Hat Trick Productions**, an independent producer. Mercurio is executive producer. Series 1 was written by a **new writer**, Daniel Brierley, supported by an industry bursary. This shows ITV backing new talent while **reducing risk** with established names such as Mercurio and McClure.
- **ITV plc** is a **conglomerate** and a **vertically integrated** "producer, broadcaster and streamer": **ITV Studios** makes and sells content worldwide, and **Media & Entertainment** runs the ITV channels and **ITVX**. Owning production, broadcasting and streaming lets ITV control content and earn from it at every stage, including global sales.
- **Funding**: a **commercial** model. **Advertising** in breaks on ITV and on the free streaming service **ITVX** pays for it, and ITVX also has **sponsorship** (the factsheet notes a pizza chain's branding on the service). **ITVX Premium** is a paid, **ad-free** tier. The drama was also sold to **third-party** streaming (Amazon Prime Video) and released on **DVD**.
- **Distribution and convergence**: a **simultaneous premiere on ITV and ITVX** reflects the growth of streaming and lets ITV reach younger viewers who don't watch linear TV, while keeping its older broadcast audience.
- **Marketing**: a mix of traditional and new methods. For series 1 a **countdown clock** was projected onto London buildings (**guerrilla marketing**), and before series 2 an **immersive escape room** created buzz. There were also trailers and social media.
- **Regulation**: **Ofcom** regulates ITV. With a **15** rating for violence, gore and intense scenes, it is broadcast **after the 9pm watershed**. ITVX shows content warnings (terrorism, peril, strong language) and offers **parental controls**.

### Audiences

- **ITV's audience** tends to be **older** and more **C2DE** than streamers' audiences. The 15 rating targets adults and young adults.
- **Appeals**: familiar crime-thriller pleasures; **Vicky McClure's fanbase** from Line of Duty and other roles; the **Mercurio** brand; a strong female lead for viewers who value **equality**; diverse characters that widen the appeal.
- The set episode drew a **large** audience for contemporary TV (over six million viewers in the UK, according to the factsheet).
- **Technology**: older viewers are more likely to watch the **linear** broadcast; younger viewers are more likely to watch on **ITVX** or other streamers, on phones and laptops, **when and where they choose**.
- **Uses and Gratifications**: **entertainment and diversion** (suspense, action, lives unlike our own); **information** about terrorism, radicalisation and gender inequality; **social interaction** (discussing episodes with family and friends, or posting on X and Instagram); **personal identity** (comparing your own experiences with the characters').
- **Different responses**: viewers respond differently to the romance subplot and the racist officer depending on their own backgrounds and views.

### Contexts

- **Social and cultural**: women increasingly visible in traditionally male professions; changing ideas of masculinity; a more multicultural Britain than the almost all-white casts of earlier crime dramas.
- **Historical and political**: anxiety about **terrorism** and **radicalisation** in contemporary Britain; a veteran of the war in Afghanistan as the lead; more sophisticated, technology-led policing; the growth of **streaming**.
- **Compared with The Sweeney (1975)**: a macho, all-white male Flying Squad with women as victims, against a female expert lead with an ethnically diverse team; violent, rule-bending detectives against professional specialists; single stories on 16mm film for a mass audience of a few channels, against a serial built for streaming and a fragmented audience. **Both are commercial ITV dramas funded by advertising and shown after the watershed.**

::: terms
- **Crime thriller**: a crime drama that stresses suspense, danger and action, often around a threat that must be stopped in time.
- **Expo**: a bomb-disposal (explosives) operative, Lana Washington's role in Trigger Point.
- **Protagonist**: the main character who drives the narrative; in Trigger Point, Lana Washington.
- **Enigma code**: a narrative device that raises a question the audience wants answered, such as the terrorists' identity.
- **Action code**: a device that creates suspense about what will happen next, such as a countdown or a chase.
- **Cliffhanger**: an unresolved ending that encourages the audience to watch the next episode.
- **Role reversal**: swapping the expected positions of social groups, such as a woman rescuing a frightened man.
- **Vertical integration**: one company owning production, broadcasting and streaming, as ITV plc does.
- **Simultaneous premiere**: releasing an episode on linear TV and a streaming service at the same moment.
- **Guerrilla marketing**: unconventional, attention-grabbing promotion in public spaces, such as a projected countdown clock.
- **Under-representation**: a social group appearing in the media less often than its presence in society would suggest.
:::

::: example Q1(a) media language: camerawork
Explore how camerawork creates meaning in the scene in which Lana rescues Thom from the room rigged with pressure plates. (10)

**Close-ups** on Lana's face as she assesses the room show a controlled, focused expression, which constructs her as the calm, expert **hero** and invites the audience to trust her (2). Contrasting close-ups on Thom's face and body show fear and tension, so the camera emphasises his **vulnerability** and reverses the usual gender roles of the rescue scene (2). Where the camera frames the **floor and the pressure plates**, the tiny margin for error becomes visible to the audience, creating **suspense** because we see the danger precisely (2). **Point-of-view** or over-the-shoulder shots from Lana's position place us in her role, so we share her tension as she decides each move (2). Any **two-shot** of the pair frames their relationship as protector and protected, and wider shots of the room remind us of the stakes. Together the camerawork builds tension and constructs Lana as the skilled, authoritative lead of a variation on the crime thriller (2).

::: example Q1(b) representation judgement
How stereotypical are the representations of gender in the set episode of Trigger Point? (10)

The episode is largely **counter-stereotypical** in gender, though it keeps some familiar elements (1). Lana subverts the stereotype of women in crime drama as victims, love interests or support staff: she leads a bomb-disposal team, her costume and equipment signal expertise, and her firm, clear voice gives her authority (2). The rescue of Thom **reverses** the Proppian pattern in which a male hero saves a female "princess": here he is the one who panics and she stays composed (2). Masculinity is also less stereotypical, since Thom's fear shows male vulnerability rarely seen in older police dramas such as The Sweeney (2). Some stereotypes remain: Lana is still the troubled, driven protagonist common to the genre, and John Hudson's resistance to female authority shows sexism in the workplace, although the episode presents his attitude as wrong (2). Overall, the representations **challenge** gender stereotypes far more than they reinforce them, reflecting feminist progress and audiences' expectations of strong female leads (1).
:::

::: example Q2 industry
Explain how Trigger Point reflects the television industry today. (10)

Trigger Point shows a commercial broadcaster adapting to streaming (1). It was made by **HTM Television**, co-owned by Jed Mercurio and Hat Trick Productions, so ITV used an established creator's company and a star from Line of Duty, Vicky McClure, to reduce the risk of a new drama (2). **ITV plc** is a vertically integrated producer, broadcaster and streamer, whose ITV Studios arm can make and sell programmes worldwide while its channels and **ITVX** show them, so it earns from every stage (2). The drama is funded commercially by **advertising** in breaks on ITV and ITVX, plus **sponsorship** on ITVX and an ad-free **Premium** subscription tier (2). Its **simultaneous premiere on ITV and ITVX** and its later availability on Amazon Prime Video and DVD reflect convergence and the growth of on-demand viewing, reaching younger audiences as well as ITV's older linear viewers (2). Ofcom regulation, a 15 rating and a post-watershed slot, with content warnings and parental controls on ITVX, show how regulation now covers both broadcast and streaming (1).
:::

::: example Q2 contexts
How does Trigger Point reflect the time in which it was made? (10)

Trigger Point reflects 2020s Britain in its issues, its representations and its distribution (1). Its narrative of terrorist bombings in London and its themes of **radicalisation** reflect contemporary anxieties about terrorism, and Lana's background as an **Afghanistan** veteran links the story to recent wars (2). A female expert leading a bomb-disposal team reflects **changing gender roles** and the visibility of women in male-dominated jobs after several waves of feminism, while Thom's fear reflects more open ideas about male vulnerability (2). The ethnically diverse team, including Hassan as a Muslim officer fighting terrorism, reflects **multicultural** Britain and a push against the misrepresentation of Muslims (2). Drones, screens and high-tech devices mirror today's technology-led policing (1). Finally, the **simultaneous ITV and ITVX premiere** reflects the growth of streaming and viewers' demand to watch when they choose (2).
:::
