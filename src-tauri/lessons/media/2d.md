## What it is

**Audiences** is about who media products are for, how organisations find and target them, how people use and interpret media, and why. It is tested in Component 1 Section B Question 4 (a stepped question: recall of 1–2 marks, a "Briefly explain" of 4–5 marks, then a synoptic 12-mark question that often names Uses and Gratifications) and in Component 2 Questions 2 and 4 (10 marks on TV or music audiences). Question 4(c) rewards **synoptic** answers that draw in industry, representation, media language and context, not audiences alone.

### Mass and niche audiences

- A **mass audience** is a large, varied audience: a Bond film, The Sun's website, Fortnite. Mass products spread high costs over many buyers and suit advertisers who want reach.
- A **niche (specialised) audience** is small and specific: a specialist hobby magazine, a radio programme for older listeners, a fan community. Niche products attract advertisers who want a particular, often wealthy or committed, group.
- Most organisations now target **both**: a mass brand with niche content on different platforms (GQ's print issue for affluent men plus a free website and social media), or a niche programme made widely available as a podcast.

### Targeting and marketing

Organisations target audiences through **marketing**: advertising, trailers, posters, websites, social media, influencers, promotions and giveaways, release timing and scheduling. Every choice rests on **assumptions** about the audience: that Sun readers want celebrity and sport, that Vogue readers aspire to luxury and purpose, that young people will find a health service online. Assumptions can be wrong or can exclude people, and audiences may resist them.

### Categorising audiences

**Demographics** divide audiences by measurable facts: age, gender, ethnicity, income, region, and **social grade**. The UK social grades (used in media and advertising research) are:

| Grade | Occupation of the chief income earner | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Higher managerial, administrative, professional | Upper middle class |
| **B** | Intermediate managerial, administrative, professional | Middle class |
| **C1** | Supervisory, clerical, junior managerial | Lower middle class |
| **C2** | Skilled manual workers | Skilled working class |
| **D** | Semi-skilled and unskilled manual workers | Working class |
| **E** | State pensioners, casual and lowest-grade workers, unemployed | Lowest income |

Media often group these as **ABC1** (higher-income, professional audiences, e.g. Vogue, GQ, The Guardian) and **C2DE** (lower-income, working-class audiences, e.g. The Sun's core print readership).

**Psychographics** divide audiences by attitudes, values, lifestyles and motivations. A widely used set of types (from the advertising agency Young & Rubicam) includes the **mainstreamer** (wants security, trusts big brands), the **aspirer** (wants status and image), the **succeeder** (already successful, wants control), the **reformer** (socially conscious, values ideas over status), the **explorer** (wants new experiences) and the **struggler**. The Guardian's audience is often described as reformers; Vogue appeals to aspirers and succeeders.

### Technology: reaching, measuring and consuming

- **Reaching**: social media, search engines, apps, streaming recommendations and targeted online advertising let organisations reach precise groups, often younger ones who no longer buy print or watch live TV.
- **Identifying and measuring**: industry bodies measure audiences (BARB for TV, RAJAR for radio, PAMCo for newsreading, ABC for circulation); online, organisations use cookies, log-ins, analytics, likes, shares and time spent. Data lets them sell advertising and tailor content.
- **Consumption and usage**: people consume on demand, on mobile, across platforms, and often while doing other things; they binge-watch, listen to podcasts, stream, and share.

### Active and passive audiences

- **Passive audience** ideas (sometimes called the **hypodermic needle** or effects model) assume audiences absorb media messages uncritically, so the media directly shape attitudes and behaviour. This underlies worries about violent games or newspapers influencing votes.
- **Active audience** ideas assume people choose, use and interpret media for their own purposes and can question or reject messages. Most media theory now treats audiences as active, although heavy exposure to repeated messages may still have effects.
- **Audience response and interpretation**: Stuart Hall's **reception** idea is a useful frame. A producer encodes a **preferred reading**; audiences may accept it (**preferred/dominant**), partly accept it (**negotiated**) or reject it (**oppositional**). Interpretations differ because of **social differences** (class, age, gender, ethnicity, politics) and **individual differences** (personal experience, taste).
- **Change over time**: responses change as society changes. The Quality Street advert's romance was probably read as charming in 1956 and is now often read as sexist; a 1970s crime drama's attitudes can feel shocking to a modern audience.

### Blumler and Katz: Uses and Gratifications

Blumler and Katz argued that audiences are **active** and choose media to satisfy needs. The four main needs:

| Need | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Information / surveillance** | Learning about the world, keeping up to date, finding advice | Reading a news story; a lifestyle magazine's advice; checking game updates |
| **Personal identity** | Seeing your values reflected, finding role models, exploring who you are or want to be | Identifying with a cover star; choosing a Fortnite skin; a newspaper that shares your politics |
| **Social interaction / integration** | Having something to talk about, connecting with others, belonging | Discussing a story; playing with friends online; joining a fan community |
| **Entertainment / diversion** | Escape, enjoyment, relaxation, excitement | A celebrity story; the thrill of a battle royale; a sitcom after school |

**The trap**: listing the four needs earns little. Each need must be tied to a **specific feature** of the set product and to a **specific audience**.

### Identity, significance and everyday life

- **Actual and desired self**: people use media to express who they are (actual self) and who they would like to be (desired self). A GQ reader buys into the stylish, successful man; a gamer's skin shows the self they want to perform.
- **Social, cultural and political significance**: media address themes and issues (politics, racism, health, gender), fulfil needs and desires, and serve **everyday functions**: keeping informed on the commute, relaxing in the evening, being part of shared national moments.

::: terms
- **Mass audience**: a large, varied audience that a product aims to reach as widely as possible.
- **Niche audience**: a small, specialised audience with particular interests or characteristics.
- **Demographics**: categorising audiences by measurable facts such as age, gender, income, ethnicity and social grade.
- **Psychographics**: categorising audiences by attitudes, values, lifestyles and motivations.
- **ABC1**: the higher social grades, professional and managerial audiences with more disposable income.
- **C2DE**: the lower social grades, skilled and unskilled manual workers and those on low or no income.
- **Active audience**: the idea that audiences choose, use and interpret media for their own purposes rather than simply absorbing it.
- **Passive audience**: the idea that audiences absorb media messages uncritically and are directly influenced by them.
- **Uses and Gratifications**: Blumler and Katz's theory that audiences actively use media to meet needs for information, identity, social interaction and entertainment.
- **Preferred reading**: the meaning the producer intends the audience to take from a product.
- **Oppositional reading**: an interpretation that rejects the producer's intended meaning, often because of the audience's values.
- **Desired self**: the identity a person would like to have, which media use can help them explore or express.
:::

::: method
1. **Recall [1–2]**: name a specific audience with a demographic or psychographic label ("C2DE men aged 35–64", "teenage gamers"), not "everyone".
2. **Briefly explain [4–5]**: make two or three developed points, each linking a targeting method or need to an audience, with a set-product example if asked.
3. **The 12-mark question**: open with a sentence showing you understand the idea (e.g. audiences are active and seek gratifications). Then, for each point, say *which audience*, *which feature of the set product*, and *which need or effect*.
4. **Go synoptic**: bring in industry (ownership, funding, technology), representation, and context. Q4(c) says it rewards drawing different areas together.
5. **Show difference**: note that audiences interpret the same product differently, and why.
6. **Conclude** briefly: why the product still attracts its audience, or how audiences are changing.
:::

::: example Recall
Identify two audiences for The Sun newspaper. (2)

C2DE working-class readers, especially men aged about 35–64 who buy the print edition (1). Younger readers under 35 who read The Sun free online and on social media (1).
:::

::: example Psychographics
What is meant by a psychographic audience category? Give an example. (2)

A way of grouping audiences by their values, attitudes, lifestyles and motivations rather than by measurable facts (1). For example, an "aspirer" is motivated by status and image, and would be targeted by a luxury fashion magazine (1).
:::

::: example Briefly explain: targeting
Briefly explain how magazines target audiences. Refer to GQ to support your points. (4)

GQ targets ABC1 men aged roughly 20 to 44 with disposable income and an interest in style (1). Its cover star, a successful celebrity sportsman in high-fashion clothes, offers an aspirational role model for men who want to be admired (1). Cover lines giving style advice (how to wear a certain suit, why a belt bag is acceptable) assume readers care about image and consumer products, which also attracts luxury advertisers (1). Serious cover lines on politics broaden the appeal to readers who see themselves as intelligent and informed, fitting GQ's brand as a men's magazine with brains (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark Uses and Gratifications
Explain why audiences play video games. Refer to Fortnite and the Uses and Gratifications theory to support your points. (12)

Blumler and Katz argued that audiences are active and choose media to meet needs; Fortnite, a free-to-play online game from Epic Games, meets several at once for a large, youth-skewed global audience (1). **Entertainment and diversion**: the battle royale mode offers fast, competitive action and the thrill of being the last player standing, while regular new seasons and live in-game events keep the experience fresh (2). **Social interaction**: players team up in squads with friends across different devices thanks to cross-platform play, and talk about matches at school or online, so the game becomes a shared space, almost a place to hang out (2). **Personal identity**: players choose skins, emotes and cosmetics, often collaborations with film franchises, musicians and sports stars, to express who they are or want to be, their desired self (2). **Information/surveillance** is less central but players follow patch notes, streamers and news of upcoming events to stay informed (1). The free-to-play funding model matters here: because there is no upfront cost the audience is huge, and Epic earns money when players buy V-Bucks to spend on the identity and status items they value (2). Audiences are also participatory: they build in Creative mode and stream gameplay on platforms like Twitch and YouTube, turning play into production (1). However, parents may read the game differently, worrying about spending and screen time, showing how interpretations vary with age and role (1).

Band: 10–12. Each need tied to a specific feature; synoptic links to industry and contexts.
:::

::: example Active or passive?
Briefly explain the difference between active and passive audience theories. (4)

Passive theories, such as the hypodermic needle idea, suggest audiences absorb media messages without questioning them, so media directly shape their views and behaviour (1). This is why people have worried that violent games or tabloid headlines change what audiences think and do (1). Active theories suggest audiences choose media to satisfy their own needs and interpret it according to their own experiences, beliefs and values (1). Blumler and Katz's Uses and Gratifications and Hall's idea of preferred, negotiated and oppositional readings both assume an active audience that can accept or reject what a product intends (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways technology helps media organisations identify audiences. (2)
---
Any two: cookies and online tracking; analytics (clicks, time spent); log-ins and accounts; social media engagement data; measurement bodies' panels such as BARB and RAJAR (1 each).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by "actual and desired self". (2)
---
Actual self is who a person is now; desired self is who they would like to be (1); media use lets people express or explore both, e.g. a reader aspiring to the lifestyle in GQ (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why audience interpretations of a product may change over time. (3)
---
Social values change, e.g. attitudes to gender and ethnicity (1); so later audiences may read a product oppositionally (1); e.g. the 1956 Quality Street advert's romance may now be read as reinforcing patriarchal stereotypes (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Name a specific audience.** "Everyone" or "people who like news" earns nothing; use demographics and psychographics.
- **Don't just list the four gratifications.** Each needs a concrete feature of the set product that meets it.
- **Q4(c) is synoptic.** The top band draws in industry (ownership, funding, technology), representation and context, not audiences alone.
- **Balance** helps: show that some audiences read the product differently.
- **Use the right social grades**: ABC1 is higher, C2DE is lower. Don't swap them.
:::
