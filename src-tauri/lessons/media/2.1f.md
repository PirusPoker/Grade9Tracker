## What it is

In Section B you study **The Sun** as an example of the **newspaper industry** and its **audiences**. Your centre chooses **one complete print edition** plus the **website homepage and at least one other page**. This is **not textual analysis**: you won't be asked what a headline connotes. You use the paper as evidence for industry and audience issues such as ownership, funding, convergence, regulation, targeting and why people read.

### Ownership and control

The Sun is a British daily tabloid ("**red top**"), owned by **News UK**, which is part of **News Corp**, the global company controlled by the Murdoch family (Rupert Murdoch bought The Sun in 1969 and handed the chair of News Corp to his son Lachlan in 2023).

| Level | What it owns (examples) | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **News Corp** (global) | newspapers in the UK, USA and Australia (e.g. The Wall Street Journal, New York Post), the book publisher HarperCollins | a **conglomerate**: many media companies under one owner, so money, content and promotion can be shared |
| **News UK** (UK) | The Sun and The Sun on Sunday, The Times and The Sunday Times, radio stations such as talkSPORT and Times Radio | **diversification** across newspapers and radio lets it **cross-promote**, e.g. Sun sport stories and talkSPORT |
| **The Sun** | print edition, thesun.co.uk, The Sun app, social channels, promotions such as Sun Savers | one brand spread across many platforms |

**Why conglomerate ownership matters**: a big owner can absorb losses as print sales fall, invest in digital technology, share journalists and stories, and sell advertising across several titles. The risk is **concentration of power**: a single owner controls several influential papers, which raises questions about **political influence** and the range of views the public hears.

### Funding: a commercial model under pressure

The Sun is **commercially funded**: it must make a profit.
- **Cover price** of the print edition.
- **Advertising** in print and online, including **advertorials** (paid promotions written to look like articles).
- **Promotions and partnerships**: token-collecting offers (Sun Savers, Sun Holidays, theme-park tickets) that make readers buy the paper several days running, and brand partners who gain publicity.
- **Diversification** into other businesses carrying the Sun brand, such as online gaming sites.
- **Digital subscriptions**: the website is free, but readers can pay for an ad-free version or a digital copy of the print edition in the app.

**The decline of print.** Newspapers were once hugely profitable, but the internet moved readers online and advertising to Google and Facebook. The Sun's print sales have fallen from several million a day in its peak years to roughly a million or so, and it stopped publishing its circulation figures in 2020. Its answer has been **scale online**. It tried a **paywall** in 2013 but dropped it in 2015, because a mass-market tabloid earns more by attracting huge free audiences for advertisers than by charging a few subscribers.

### Convergence and technology

**Convergence** is the coming together of media platforms and technologies. The Sun is now a multi-platform news brand:
- the **website** thesun.co.uk (free, updated constantly, with video, live blogs and comments);
- the **app** on iOS and Android, including a digital edition of the paper;
- **social media**: Facebook, X, Instagram, TikTok and Snapchat, each with content shaped for its users;
- **news aggregators** such as Apple News, which send a large share of its online traffic;
- **cross-media links** within News UK (radio, other titles).

Technology lets The Sun **measure** its audience precisely (clicks, shares, time on page), **target** adverts using data, and publish **breaking news** instantly. Citizen journalists' smartphone footage feeds stories. The challenge is that clicks bring in far less advertising money per reader than print once did.

### Regulation

Newspapers are **self-regulated**. Most national papers, including The Sun, belong to **IPSO** (the Independent Press Standards Organisation, set up in 2014). IPSO enforces the **Editors' Code of Practice** (accuracy, privacy, harassment, discrimination, children and so on), investigates complaints and can order corrections to be published prominently.

**The context: phone hacking.** In 2011 News Corp's Sunday paper, the News of the World, was closed after revelations that journalists had hacked phones, including a murdered schoolgirl's. The **Leveson Inquiry** (2011–12) examined press ethics and recommended an independent regulator with legal backing. Most big newspapers refused that system and set up IPSO instead. Critics say IPSO is still too close to the industry it regulates. The Sun on Sunday launched in 2012 to fill the gap left by the News of the World.

**Challenges from digital technology**:
- stories spread on social media before they can be checked, and corrections rarely reach as many people;
- online comments and user posts are hard to police;
- global platforms and bloggers sit outside IPSO altogether;
- the pressure for clicks encourages sensational "clickbait" headlines;
- print and online versions of a story can differ, and online stories can be edited silently.

### Political stance and influence

The Sun is **right-leaning** and **populist** in tone, but it backs the party it thinks will win or that suits its owner. It supported Margaret Thatcher's Conservatives, then famously claimed credit for the 1992 Conservative win; it switched to Tony Blair's New Labour in 1997, back to the Conservatives in 2010, campaigned for Leave in the 2016 EU referendum, and backed Labour in 2024. Political parties have long courted The Sun because of its **mass, working-class readership**. Today its influence is debated: print readers are fewer, but its stories still set the agenda across social media and broadcast news.

### Audiences

| Who | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Social grade** | mainly **C2DE** (skilled manual, semi-skilled and unskilled workers, and people on low incomes) |
| **Gender** | slightly **male**-skewed in print and online |
| **Age** | print readers are mostly **middle-aged and older** (roughly 35–64); **online readers are much younger**, with the majority under 35 |
| **Reach** | a print readership of a couple of million a day, but a far larger audience online across its website, app and social channels |
| **Psychographics** | readers who want entertainment, sport and celebrity; "strugglers" and "mainstreamers" who want a quick, easy read and value for money |

**How The Sun targets them**: short, simple copy with a low reading age and bold images; **puns** and humour; **celebrity** and soaps; **sport**, especially football, with a big back-page section; **giveaways** and money-saving offers that reward loyalty; a **patriotic, populist** voice that speaks "for" ordinary people; and on social media, short video and shareable stories.

**Uses and Gratifications (Blumler and Katz)** explains why people actively choose it:
- **Entertainment / diversion**: gossip, puzzles, puns and sport as a break from routine.
- **Information / surveillance**: headline news, sport results, TV listings, a quick overview of the day.
- **Personal identity**: readers who share its patriotic, plain-speaking values see themselves in it (or define themselves against it).
- **Social interaction**: football and celebrity stories to talk about at work; sharing and commenting online.

**Active audiences and different readings.** Audiences are not passive: readers can accept its politics (**preferred reading**), take the sport and ignore the politics (**negotiated**), or reject it (**oppositional**). The clearest example is Merseyside, where many people have refused to buy The Sun for decades because of its false coverage of Liverpool fans after the 1989 Hillsborough disaster. Responses also change over time: the topless "Page 3" photographs, once a fixture, were dropped from print in 2015 as attitudes to sexism shifted.

::: terms
- **Conglomerate**: a large company that owns many different media businesses, such as News Corp owning newspapers, radio and book publishing.
- **Diversification**: a media company expanding into different products or markets to spread risk and find new income.
- **Convergence**: the coming together of platforms and technologies, so one brand is read in print, on a website, in an app and on social media.
- **Circulation**: the number of copies of a newspaper sold or distributed; readership is higher because each copy is read by several people.
- **Paywall**: a system that makes readers pay to see online content; The Sun tried one from 2013 to 2015.
- **Advertorial**: paid-for promotional content written in the style of a newspaper article.
- **IPSO**: the Independent Press Standards Organisation, which handles complaints against most UK newspapers under the Editors' Code.
- **Self-regulation**: a system in which an industry sets and polices its own standards rather than being controlled by law.
- **Leveson Inquiry**: the 2011–12 public inquiry into press ethics after the phone-hacking scandal.
- **C2DE**: social grades covering skilled manual, semi-skilled and unskilled workers and those on low or no income.
- **Uses and Gratifications**: Blumler and Katz's theory that audiences actively choose media to meet needs such as entertainment, information, identity and social interaction.
:::

::: method
1. **Recall questions [1–2]**: give the fact and nothing else, e.g. "IPSO", "News UK", "C2DE readers; younger online readers".
2. **"Briefly explain" [4–5]**: make two or three developed points, each with a term and an example (ownership, funding, convergence or targeting). You don't have to refer to The Sun unless asked, but a Sun example lifts the answer.
3. **Extended [10–12]**: open with a one-line answer to the question, then four or five paragraphs, each linking an industry or audience **concept** to a specific **Sun example** (the print edition, the homepage, a promotion, a social channel).
4. **Name the theory where asked** (Uses and Gratifications, active and passive audiences) and apply each need to a real Sun feature.
5. **For the synoptic Q4(c)**, pull in other areas of the framework: contexts (political stance, the decline of print), industry (ownership, funding) and representation (who appears and how).
6. **Never analyse connotations of headlines** for their own sake. Always turn a feature into an industry or audience point.
:::

::: example Briefly explain: conglomerate ownership
Briefly explain one advantage for The Sun of being owned by a conglomerate. (4)

The Sun is owned by News UK, part of News Corp, a conglomerate with newspapers, radio stations and book publishing (1). This gives it financial security as print sales fall: losses can be covered by other parts of the business while it invests in its website and app (1). It can also cross-promote across the group, for example sharing sport stories with News UK's radio station talkSPORT, which reaches the same mainly male sports audience (1). Sharing resources such as printing, advertising sales teams and technology cuts costs, so the paper stays competitive in a shrinking market (1).
:::

::: example Briefly explain: newspaper websites
Briefly explain how newspaper websites attract audiences. (4)

Newspaper websites are usually free, like thesun.co.uk, which removes the cost barrier and attracts a mass audience that advertisers will pay to reach (1). They update constantly with breaking news, live blogs and video, so readers return several times a day rather than once (1). Stories are designed to be shared on social media and via aggregators such as Apple News, which brings in new, younger readers who would never buy the print edition (1). Interactive features such as comments, polls and personalised recommendations make readers feel involved and keep them on the site longer (1).
:::

::: example Extended: why people read print newspapers
Explain why audiences read print newspapers. Refer to The Sun and the Uses and Gratifications theory to support your points. (12)

Although most news is now read online, a loyal, mostly older C2DE audience still buys The Sun in print, and Uses and Gratifications explains why: readers actively choose it to meet particular needs (1).

The strongest need is **entertainment and diversion**. The print edition mixes celebrity stories, TV and soap gossip, puzzles and a large sport section, and the puns and humour make it an easy, enjoyable read on a commute or tea break (2). For **information and surveillance**, the front page and short news stories give a quick summary of the day's events at a low reading age, which suits readers who want the headlines without long analysis (2).

**Personal identity** matters too. The Sun speaks in a patriotic, populist voice "for" ordinary working people, so readers who share those values find their opinions confirmed; this links to its political context as a right-leaning paper that often attacks politicians it dislikes (2). **Social interaction** is gratified by football coverage and celebrity stories that readers talk about with friends and colleagues. For older readers the physical paper is also a daily **habit and ritual** (1).

Print offers extra rewards that the free website doesn't: token-collecting promotions such as Sun Savers and theme-park ticket offers reward buying the paper day after day, which is attractive to lower-income readers looking for value (2). Finally, audiences are active, not passive. Some readers buy it only for the sport and ignore its politics (a negotiated reading), while others reject it entirely, as on Merseyside after its Hillsborough coverage, which shows that people's reasons for reading are shaped by their own experiences and values (2).

This answer would reach the top band (10–12): accurate knowledge, the theory applied throughout to specific Sun features, and links to context and industry.
:::

::: example Extended: convergence
Explain how newspapers use convergence to reach audiences. Refer to The Sun to support your points. (10)

Convergence means one media brand works across several platforms, and The Sun uses it to reach far more people than its falling print sales (1). The free website carries all the print content plus breaking news and video, so it reaches a mass audience around the clock and earns advertising income (2). The app offers a digital edition of the paper and push notifications, keeping loyal readers engaged on their phones (2). Social media accounts on Facebook, X, Instagram, TikTok and Snapchat adapt content to each platform, e.g. short videos for younger users, which explains why most online readers are under 35 while most print readers are older (2). Supplying stories to aggregators such as Apple News brings a large share of visitors who never go to the homepage (1). Within News UK, stories and brands are cross-promoted with sister titles and radio stations (1). The data collected across platforms lets The Sun see what readers click and sell targeted advertising, which is how a free newspaper funds itself (1).
