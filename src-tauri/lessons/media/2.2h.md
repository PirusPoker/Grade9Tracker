## What it is

You must study the **official websites of both contemporary artists** you chose (lizzomusic.com *or* taylorswift.com, **and** stormzy.com *or* justinbiebermusic.com) and the role of **social and participatory media** around them, referring to **at least two platforms**. Websites are studied for **all five areas** of the framework. They are examined mainly in **Q4 (10 marks)**, e.g. "Explain how users interact with music websites" (2025) or "How do music websites promote artists to a global audience?" (2023).

**Websites change constantly.** The spec says so, and the mark schemes credit **any version** you have studied. So learn **how to analyse** a site and keep **two or three up-to-date examples** from each of your artists' sites. Visit them regularly during the course and note what is on the home page.

### The three elements the spec names

1. **The home page design, including its use of images and topical material.** What greets you first: usually a large **hero image** or video of the current album or single, with the artist styled to match that era's **visual identity** (colour palette, fonts, costume). Topical material means whatever is being promoted *now*: a new release, a tour, a film, a special-edition product.
2. **Links to other content, including audio-visual material and music videos.** "Listen now" or "Watch" buttons that send users to streaming services (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music, Amazon Music), embedded or linked videos on the official YouTube channel, and sometimes **microsites** for a tour or album.
3. **Interactive links, including to social and participatory media.** Icons linking to Instagram, TikTok, X, Facebook, YouTube and Snapchat; mailing-list or fan-club sign-ups; the online **store**; **tour dates** with ticket links; sometimes fan-generated content, polls or competitions.

### Analysing a website: media language and representation

| Look at | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Layout and navigation** | Is it simple and uncluttered? What is placed at the top, and what is one click away? A clear navigation bar (Music, Tour, Store, Videos) shows the site's priorities: **selling and streaming** |
| **Images** | How is the artist posed, dressed and lit? Direct gaze? Does the image match the album artwork? What does it connote about the **star persona**? |
| **Colour, typography, logos** | Is there a consistent **brand identity** across the site, album, merchandise and social media? |
| **Language** | Imperatives such as "Listen now", "Pre-order", "Shop" or "Sign up"; direct address to fans; exclusive offers |
| **Representation and self-representation** | The site is controlled by the artist and their team, so it shows the image they **choose**. Is the artist represented as glamorous, relatable, political, caring? Which social groups appear? |

### Industry: what the website is for

- **A shop window and a sales platform.** It sells merchandise (clothing, accessories, signed or collectible vinyl in multiple colour variants that fans are encouraged to collect), tickets, and sometimes the artist's other brands (for example Lizzo's shapewear brand, Yitty).
- **A hub for convergence.** One release is pushed across platforms (the video on YouTube, the song on streaming, clips on social media), and the website links them all. This **synergy** maximises reach and revenue for the artist and the label.
- **Data collection.** Mailing-list sign-ups, accounts, cookies and purchases give the artist's team **data** about fans (location, age, spending), which is used to target marketing, plan tours and decide where to advertise. Social media analytics show which posts get the most engagement.
- **Global reach.** A website is accessible anywhere; stores ship internationally or have regional versions; tour pages list dates across continents; links go to globally available streaming services.
- **Control.** Unlike press coverage, the site is **owned media**: the artist's team controls the message. Brand managers often run the artist's online presence, and posts are scheduled across platforms at once.

### Social and participatory media

- **Social media** lets artists speak directly to fans, bypassing traditional gatekeepers such as music journalists, radio and TV. Typical uses: **teasers** before a release, countdowns, behind-the-scenes clips, live streams, Q&As, personal posts (pets, family, everyday life) that make the star seem **relatable**, and statements on political and social issues.
- **Participatory media** means platforms where **audiences create and share content**, not just consume it. Examples:
  - **TikTok** dance challenges and trends set to a song, which can turn a track into a hit (Lizzo's popularity grew hugely through TikTok and Instagram).
  - **Covers, remixes, reaction videos, fan edits and lyric videos** on YouTube and TikTok.
  - **Fan communities** and hashtags (#Swifties; "Beliebers"), fan accounts that decode clues and hidden references ("Easter eggs") in videos.
  - Fans' comments, duets and stitches spreading the release for free.
- **Platform comparison.** Instagram: polished images and Stories; TikTok: short, participatory video and the algorithmic "For You" page; X: instant news and conversation; YouTube: long-form video, premieres with live chat; Facebook: older audiences and events.
- **Why it matters to the industry**: word-of-mouth marketing at almost no cost, feedback on what fans want, and a sense of community that keeps fans loyal and spending.

### Audiences

- **Active audiences.** Users choose what to click, share, comment and create. The 2025 mark scheme focused on **interactivity**: listening, watching, buying, booking tickets, joining the mailing list, following on social media.
- **Uses and gratifications**: *information* (tour dates, release news); *entertainment* (videos, behind-the-scenes clips); *personal identity* (wearing merchandise, identifying with the star's values, such as body positivity or feminism); *social interaction* (fan communities, sharing, commenting, joining trends).
- **Identity: actual and desired self.** Fans use artists' media to express who they are and who they would like to be, for example by buying the look or joining a movement the artist supports.
- **Different readings.** A loyal fan may see a personal post as authentic; a critical viewer may see it as calculated marketing.

### Contexts

- **Technological**: smartphones, streaming and social media changed how fans discover and follow artists. Before the internet, communities formed offline, through fan clubs and music magazines.
- **Social and political**: artists' sites and feeds carry messages on issues (Black Lives Matter, #MeToo, voting, charity), which shape their representation.
- **Economic**: with streaming paying little per play, **direct-to-fan sales** of merchandise, special editions and tickets matter more than ever.

### Regulation

Websites and social platforms are hard to regulate: content is global and user-generated, and platforms enforce their own community guidelines. Age restrictions on social media and online-safety law (in the UK, the Online Safety Act) try to protect young users, but enforcement is difficult. This is an example of the spec's "challenges for regulation" presented by digital technologies.

::: terms
- **Home page**: the first page of a website, designed to grab attention and show what is being promoted now.
- **Hero image**: the large, dominant image at the top of a home page, usually showing the artist's current release.
- **Navigation bar**: the menu of links across a website, showing its main sections such as Music, Tour and Store.
- **Interactivity**: the ways users can act on a website or platform: clicking, buying, commenting, sharing and creating.
- **Participatory media**: platforms where audiences create and share their own content, not only consume it.
- **Social media**: online platforms, such as Instagram, TikTok and X, used to share content and communicate with followers.
- **Convergence**: different media platforms and technologies coming together, so one release is spread across many.
- **Owned media**: channels an artist or company controls directly, such as its own website and accounts.
- **Microsite**: a smaller, separate website linked from the main site, dedicated to one tour, album or campaign.
- **Brand identity**: the consistent look, style and values that make an artist recognisable across all products.
- **Data analytics**: collecting and analysing information about users to target marketing and measure engagement.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the site and a dated example**: "When I studied taylorswift.com, the home page promoted…". The mark scheme accepts any version.
2. **Use the spec's three elements** as a structure: home page and images, links to audio-visual content, interactive and social links.
3. For **each point**, say what the user can **do** or **see**, then what it achieves for the **artist or industry** (promotion, sales, loyalty, data, global reach), and what **need** it meets for the **audience** (Uses and Gratifications).
4. Mention **at least two social or participatory platforms** and give a concrete example of fans **creating** content.
5. Use terminology: convergence, synergy, interactivity, star persona, brand identity, global audience, active audience.
6. Aim for **four or five developed points** in a 10-mark answer; description of the page with no explanation stays in the middle band.
:::

::: example Users interacting with a music website
Explain how users interact with music websites. Refer to the official Taylor Swift website to support your response. (10)

When I studied taylorswift.com, the home page was dominated by a large image promoting her latest album, with a "Listen now" button (1). Users could click to choose a streaming service or store, so the site acts as a gateway that converts interest into streams and sales (1). A navigation bar let users select Tour, where they could search dates by location and click through to buy tickets, meeting an information need (1). Through the Store, users could buy clothing and several colour variants of the album on vinyl, which encourages collecting and lets fans express their personal identity as "Swifties" (1). Links to videos took users to her YouTube channel, where they can watch, like and comment (1). Icons linked to Instagram, TikTok and X, where users interact directly: commenting, sharing, and hunting for the hidden clues Swift is known for planting, which fans discuss in online communities (1). This participatory culture means fans spread the promotion themselves (1). A mailing-list sign-up offers exclusive news in return for users' data, which her team uses to target future campaigns (1). Overall, users are active: they seek information, entertainment, identity and social interaction (Uses and Gratifications) (1), and the site turns each of these into promotion and revenue for the artist and Universal/Republic (1).
:::

::: example Participatory media
Explain, with an example, how participatory media differs from simply following an artist on social media. (4)

Following means consuming the artist's posts, perhaps liking them (1). Participatory media means fans produce content themselves (1), for example posting TikTok dances, covers or fan edits using the artist's song (1). This spreads the song to new audiences for free and can turn it into a hit, so fans become part of the marketing (1).
:::

::: example Global audience
How do music websites promote artists to a global audience? (6)

The website can be reached from anywhere, and its links go to streaming platforms available in most countries, so a new release can be heard worldwide on the day it comes out (2). Tour pages list dates across several continents with local ticket links, and stores ship internationally, turning global fans into customers (2). Links to social media let fans in any country follow, share and create content, so the artist's reach grows through online communities without national boundaries (2).
:::
