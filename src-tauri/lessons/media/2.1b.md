## What it is

Component 1 Section A sets two James Bond film posters, almost fifty years apart: **The Man with the Golden Gun (1974)**, Roger Moore's second Bond film, and **No Time to Die (2021)**, Daniel Craig's fifth and final. Studied side by side, they show how a genre and a franchise keep their core conventions while their representations of gender and ethnicity change with society. They are tested on **media language**, **representation** and **contexts**: Question 1 (15 marks, e.g. narrative [5] + images [10]); Question 2(a) (5 marks, usually historical context); Question 2(b) (25 marks, comparing a set poster with an **unseen** poster, e.g. a 2015 spy comedy poster was used in 2024). No Time to Die is also a Section B film-industry product, but that is a separate topic: here you analyse the poster.

### Film poster conventions

| Convention | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Title** (often as a logo in a distinctive font) | Identifies and brands the film |
| **Central image** of the star(s) | Star appeal; signals protagonist |
| **Iconography** (props, costume, settings) | Signals genre quickly: guns, cars, explosions, exotic locations |
| **Star billing** (actors' names) | Draws fans of the stars |
| **Tagline** | A short phrase hinting at narrative or tone |
| **Credit block** | Small print listing director, producers, companies; industry information |
| **Release date, websites, social media** | Tells audiences when and where; drives online engagement |
| **Franchise branding** | The 007 gun logo; links to earlier films |

A poster must communicate genre, narrative and appeal in seconds, so it relies on familiar conventions and stereotypes. The spy/action genre's conventions include a skilled male hero, a villain with a plan, glamorous women, weapons, gadgets, fast vehicles, explosions and exotic locations.

### The Man with the Golden Gun (1974): media language

- **Illustrated collage**: the poster is painted, not photographic. Before digital montage, illustration was the common way to fit many scenes into one image, and it gives a sense of fantasy and excess. The audience is expected to "read" the poster, working through the images to piece together the plot.
- **Central image**: a mid-shot of Bond, in a smart suit, holding a gun across his body. Dominance of size and position marks him as the protagonist, Propp's **hero**; the suit connotes professionalism and sophistication, the gun danger and action (an iconic part of Bond's "uniform").
- **Star name**: Roger Moore's name sits directly above his image, reinforcing the star link (Moore was already famous from TV and from his first Bond film).
- **The golden gun**: in the foreground at the bottom, an extreme close-up of a golden gun pointed at Bond, being loaded by a hand with a bullet marked for 007. Gold connotes wealth and status; the hidden shooter is an **enigma code** (who wants Bond dead?) and Propp's **villain**. (In the film this is the assassin Scaramanga.)
- **Women**: two women in bikinis flank Bond, slim, made-up, with long flowing hair; one seems to point Bond out to the gunman while the other stretches out an arm as if protecting him: an enigma about allies and enemies. A third woman, in a martial-arts uniform and fighting pose, is darker-haired and appears to be from a different ethnic group.
- **Action iconography**: explosions, enemies attacking, a power plant in the lower left and an energy beam aimed at Bond: codes of the action/thriller genre.
- **Title and credits**: the title appears near the bottom with Ian Fleming's name (the films were based on his novels); the credit block is small so it does not distract.

### The Man with the Golden Gun: representation and context

- **Masculinity**: Bond is the ideal man of the franchise: charming, suave, intelligent, strong, armed, heterosexual, desired by women. The message: this is what a man should aspire to be.
- **Women**: two are sexualised, their bodies on display for the audience, a clear case of the **male gaze**; they are decorative "princess" figures and rewards for the hero. A feminist reading says they are reduced to bodies to be looked at. The martial-arts woman is more active, but her difference is framed as **exotic**, the ethnic "other", echoing older stereotypes of minority ethnic characters as exotic or dangerous.
- **Historical context**: made by EON Productions and distributed by United Artists. Martial-arts films were booming in the early 1970s (Bruce Lee's success), so the film includes kung fu scenes and was filmed largely in Asian locations. The 1973 oil crisis gave the plot its energy theme, reflected in the power-plant imagery. The poster also appeared during the growth of the women's liberation movement, which makes its representation of women look dated even for its time.

### No Time to Die (2021): media language

- **Photographic montage**: separate images of characters and scenes are layered together, echoing the montage tradition of earlier Bond posters (intertextuality and nostalgia) but in a polished digital style with colour grading.
- **Bond**: the dominant image is a mid-shot of Daniel Craig, smartly dressed, side-on, his care-worn, thoughtful expression lit to show his age, framing the other characters. Unusually, he holds no weapon in the main image. Smaller action shots show him on a motorbike and driving an Aston Martin DB5 (the model first used in the 1960s films): he is on a quest (Propp's hero), and the vehicles promise chases and excitement.
- **Supporting characters**: Nomi (Lashana Lynch), a new 00 agent, in military costume with an earpiece and a gun, calm and focused; Paloma (Ana de Armas), a CIA agent in a glamorous, revealing navy evening dress; Madeleine Swann (Léa Seydoux), pictured twice; Q (Ben Whishaw); and the villain Safin (Rami Malek), placed centrally and large, looking at the audience. Direct address from several characters personalises the poster.
- **Villains**: Safin's facial scarring, and a second figure in a parka wearing a Japanese Noh mask and carrying a gun: death, danger and a hidden identity (an **enigma code**, Propp's villain).
- **Guns**: three guns appear, relatively small or partly seen and not being fired, consistent with advertising rules against glamorising violence.
- **Colour and settings**: warm daylight scenes (Italy) contrast with cold neon night scenes (a bar in Cuba), exotic locations expected in the genre; blue tones in the worn, aged 007 logo at the top (hinting at an older Bond) link to the villain's suit, the bar and Paloma's dress.
- **Typography**: the capitalised title at the bottom (in a bold geometric font) connotes power; a gold 007 logo connotes luxury and exclusivity. The title is ambiguous: too busy to die, or a bad time to die, hinting at significant deaths.
- **What is missing**: no actors' names (the producers assume stars are recognised), no reference to Ian Fleming (the story is not based on one of his novels); release date, social media and company logos are small at the bottom.

### No Time to Die: representation and context

- **Masculinity**: Bond still connotes skill, intelligence and strength, and frames the whole design; but he is older, more thoughtful and vulnerable, reflecting modern ideas of masculinity. The two largest figures, hero and villain, are both men: the franchise remains male-centred.
- **Women**: all three women are lit and made up as conventionally beautiful, as expected of a blockbuster, but two hold guns with confident, relaxed postures: they have **agency** and are not damsels in distress. Nomi's costume and earpiece mark her as central to the action. Paloma's gown, though, recalls the "Bond girl" tradition, so the change is partial. The producers brought in Phoebe Waller-Bridge, one of very few women to write on a Bond film, to develop the female characters.
- **Ethnicity**: Bond posters have rarely shown people of colour, usually as villains or love interests. A black British woman as 007's successor is a significant moment in the franchise; her casting drew racist online abuse from a minority, and Lynch spoke about Black women's experience of being mis- and under-represented. Safin, played by an American actor of Egyptian heritage, with disfigurement make-up, continues the criticised convention of villains marked by physical difference and "otherness".
- **Age**: lighting emphasises Bond's age, constructing wisdom and experience, while most other characters are younger.
- **Context**: #OscarsSoWhite (2016), #MeToo (from 2017) and the 2020 Black Lives Matter protests raised audience expectations about diversity. Release was delayed from April 2020 by a change of director and the Covid-19 pandemic.

### Comparing the posters

| | 1974 | 2021 |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Illustrated collage | Photographic digital montage |
| Bond | Armed, central, confident | Unarmed in main image, older, thoughtful, still central |
| Women | Bikinis, decorative, male gaze; one exoticised fighter | Armed agents with agency; one glamorous gown; all conventionally beautiful |
| Ethnicity | A single exoticised woman | A black female 00 agent; villain of Egyptian heritage; still a mostly white cast |
| Villain | Hidden hand, golden gun (enigma) | Scarred Safin and a masked figure (enigma) |
| Constant | 007 branding, guns, vehicles, exotic locations, hero-villain narrative |

::: terms
- **Film poster**: a print marketing product promoting a film's release through images, title and key information.
- **Credit block**: the small block of text listing the film's makers, companies and stars.
- **Iconography**: the familiar objects, costumes and settings that signal a genre, such as guns and fast cars in spy films.
- **Montage**: a design in which several separate images are layered or combined into one composition.
- **Franchise**: a series of related products built on the same characters or brand, such as the Bond films.
- **Star billing**: the placing of actors' names on a poster to attract their fans.
- **Agency**: a character's power to act and make choices rather than only be acted upon.
- **The other**: a group represented as different, strange or exotic compared with the dominant group.
- **Bond girl**: the franchise's traditional glamorous female character, often a love interest with little narrative importance.
- **Colour grading**: digital adjustment of the colours in an image to create a mood or consistent look.
:::

::: method
1. **Question 1 aspects**: *narrative* (Propp's roles, enigmas, what story the images imply), *images* (shots, costume, props, expression, colour), *layout and design* (composition, size, typography, placement), *written language* (title, names, ambiguity).
2. For each point: element → connotation → purpose for the genre and audience. Use genre terms (iconography, conventions, franchise) and at least one theorist where it helps.
3. **Question 2(a) historical context**: tie two or three facts about the period (illustration, kung fu craze, oil crisis, permissive era; or #MeToo, Black Lives Matter, the pandemic) to specific poster choices.
4. **Question 2(b)**: compare the set poster with the unseen in every paragraph: choices (costume, pose, weapons, position, gaze), similarities, differences, and a judgement about how far.
5. **Judge the 1974 poster in its context first**, then say how modern audiences might read it.
:::

::: example Narrative (5)
Explore how The Man with the Golden Gun (1974) poster uses narrative to communicate meanings. (5)

Bond is the central figure holding a gun, so he is constructed as the hero on a quest, following Propp's roles (1). In the foreground, an unseen hand loads a golden gun pointed at him with a bullet marked for 007, making the villain's goal clear: to kill Bond (1). Because we see only the hand, this is an enigma code that makes audiences want to see the film to find out who the assassin is (1). The women around Bond create a further enigma: one appears to point him out to the gunman while another seems to shield him, suggesting he has female allies and enemies who look alike (1). Explosions, attackers and a power plant imply a chain of action scenes, and the collage lets the poster suggest many narrative moments at once, promising a spectacular, action-packed story (1).
:::

::: example Images (10)
Explore how the No Time to Die (2021) poster uses images to communicate meanings. (10)

The dominant mid-shot of Daniel Craig, smartly dressed and side-on, marks Bond as the protagonist, and because his image frames the others he seems to hold the whole story together (2). His expression is thoughtful and care-worn, and the lighting shows his age, which creates a more reflective, vulnerable hero suited to Craig's final film (1). Unusually he holds no weapon, while the action insets on a motorbike and in an Aston Martin DB5 keep him skilled and on a quest; the DB5 is also an intertextual nod to the franchise's past for loyal fans (2). Nomi, in military costume with an earpiece and a gun, looks calm and in control, representing a woman as an active agent, while Paloma's revealing evening dress recalls the older "Bond girl" image, so the poster mixes change and tradition (2). The scarred villain Safin, placed centrally and looking at the audience, and a masked figure with a gun, connote danger and set up enigmas about identity (2). Contrasting warm daylight in Italy with cold neon night-time Cuba signals the glamorous global locations that audiences expect from the genre (1).

Band: 9–10.
:::

::: example Historical context (5)
Explain how historical contexts influence film posters. Refer to No Time to Die (2021) to support your points. (5)

By 2021, #MeToo (from 2017) had made audiences more critical of how women were represented, so the poster shows women as armed agents rather than only decorative love interests (1). The Black Lives Matter protests of 2020 and earlier campaigns such as #OscarsSoWhite increased pressure for diversity, reflected in the prominence of Lashana Lynch as a black female 00 agent (1). Changing ideas of masculinity are reflected in an older, thoughtful Bond who holds no gun in the main image (1). Digital photography and montage produce a polished, colour-graded look, and the poster includes social media details, reflecting how films are now marketed online as well as in print (1). The film's release was delayed from April 2020 by the Covid-19 pandemic, so the long-running campaign had to keep the brand visible over a much longer period (1).
:::

::: example Comparison paragraph with an unseen poster
The unseen poster (invented for practice):

> **Deadlight**, a 2019 action thriller. A photographic poster: a woman in her forties in a dark tactical jacket stands in the centre in a low-angle long shot, holding a pistol pointed at the ground, her face bruised. Behind her, a city skyline burns at night in orange and black. A younger man in a suit lies injured at her feet. The tagline reads "She was never the backup." Her name appears above the title in large white capitals.

Compare how the No Time to Die and Deadlight posters represent women. Write one developed paragraph. (5)

Both posters select women who hold guns and adopt confident postures, giving them agency within the action genre (1). However, in No Time to Die the women are secondary to Bond and the male villain, who are larger and central, whereas in Deadlight the woman is the protagonist: centred, shot from a low angle, with her name in large capitals above the title (1). Deadlight's bruised face and tactical jacket construct toughness rather than glamour, while No Time to Die still lights all its women as conventionally beautiful and dresses one in a revealing gown (1). The Deadlight tagline directly challenges the stereotype of women as helpers or "backup", reversing Propp's roles by placing an injured man at her feet (1). So both reflect changing expectations after #MeToo, but Deadlight challenges gender conventions more fully, because a franchise poster must protect a male hero at its centre (1).
:::
