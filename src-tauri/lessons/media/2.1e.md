## What it is

This lesson is about **exam technique** for Component 1 Section A, and in particular the two parts of Question 2. Question 1 (media language, 15 marks) is taught in the set-product lessons. Here you learn how to read an **unseen product** under time pressure, how to write the 5-mark **context question**, and how to build the 25-mark **comparison**, which is the biggest single question in the whole GCSE.

**How Section A works**

| Question | Marks | What it tests | Suggested time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Study time | 0 | Read the set products and the unseen resource. Annotate. | 10 min |
| **Q1** | 15 (often 5 + 10, or 5 + 5 + 5) | **Media language** in one set product, e.g. "layout and design", "images", "narrative", "genre" | 15 min |
| **Q2(a)** | 5 | **Contexts** (historical, social and cultural, or political) and how they influence one form, with reference to a set product | 7–8 min |
| **Q2(b)** | 25 | **Representation**: compare a set product with an **unseen** product in the same form | 25–30 min |

The paper advises about **55 minutes on Section A, including 10 minutes to study the print resources**, and 35 minutes on Section B. Q1 and Q2 are always on **different forms**: if Q1 is on the adverts, Q2 might be on the magazine covers. You are given clean copies of the set products, so you don't need to memorise every detail. You do need to know the **contexts**, because they are not printed on the page.

**The four Section A forms and their set products (from 2028)**: magazine covers (Vogue, July 2021; GQ, August 2019); film posters (The Man with the Golden Gun, 1974; No Time to Die, 2021); newspaper front pages (The Guardian, 6 May 2025; The Sun, 22 March 2025); print adverts (Quality Street, 1956; NHS 111 "Help Us Help You", 2023). The unseen product is always in the **same form** as the set product it is paired with. Past papers have paired Vogue with a women's magazine cover, The Man with the Golden Gun with a spy-comedy poster, and GQ with a later GQ cover.

### Reading an unseen product in the study time

You have about ten minutes for everything, so spend four or five of them on the unseen product with a fixed routine:

1. **Form, date and purpose.** What is it, when was it made, who is it for? A 2020s cover will reflect different contexts from a 1970s poster.
2. **Dominant image.** Who is shown, and how? Shot size, angle, pose, gesture, gaze (direct or indirect address), costume, props and setting.
3. **Written codes.** Masthead or title, cover lines, tagline, headline and caption. Which words **anchor** the image?
4. **Colour, typography and layout.** What do the palette and the fonts connote? Where does the eye go first?
5. **The representation in the question.** If the question is about gender, circle everything that constructs gender. Then draw arrows to the **matching** features on the set product: a similarity, a difference, or a feature one has and the other lacks.

End the study time with a **one-line judgement**, e.g. "similar surface, different values", or "both challenge stereotypes, but in different ways". That line becomes your introduction.

### The 5-mark context question, Q2(a)

The question looks like this: *Explain how [historical / social and cultural / political] contexts influence [form]. Refer to [set product] to support your points.* It is marked on **knowledge and understanding of contexts** (AO1) in five one-mark levels. The top mark needs **accurate, detailed** knowledge of the context and **highly appropriate** reference to the set product.

The formula for each point is **context → specific feature of the product → what it means**. Aim for **three developed points** in about eight minutes. Write no introduction and no conclusion.

| Context type | What it means | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical** | how the product reflects the **time** it was made: its technology, events, values and genre conventions of that era | What was happening then? What could or couldn't producers do? What was normal? |
| **Social and cultural** | how it reflects the **society and culture**: attitudes to gender, ethnicity, age and faith; cultural influences such as other genres, styles and stars | Which social debates or cultural trends does it respond to? |
| **Political** | how it reflects **politics and power**: the government of the day, the owner's political stance, regulation, campaigns | Whose side is it on? Who funds or owns it? |

**A quick context bank for the set products** (learn these; they are not printed on the page):
- **Vogue (2021)**: the editor Edward Enninful pushed for more diverse covers; a Muslim woman in a headscarf was rare on glossy covers; post-lockdown lifestyle cover lines; Malala's story as an activist for girls' education.
- **GQ (2019)**: black men were under-represented on glossy covers; Raheem Sterling spoke out against racism in football and in press coverage; ideas of the "metrosexual" and body-focused masculinity.
- **The Man with the Golden Gun (1974)**: hand-drawn artwork, because illustration was normal before digital design; the 1973 energy crisis; the kung fu film craze; women as glamorous objects of the gaze; Roger Moore as an established star.
- **No Time to Die (2021)**: Daniel Craig's final Bond; a release delayed by the pandemic; #MeToo and changing expectations of female characters; Nomi as a black female 00 agent.
- **The Guardian (6 May 2025)**: a Labour government after bad local-election results; the winter fuel payment cut; the VE Day 80th anniversary; the Scott Trust and the paper's liberal-left stance.
- **The Sun (22 March 2025)**: the substation fire that shut Heathrow; a right-leaning paper implying that the government mismanaged infrastructure; a Fawlty Towers pun that suits older readers; the media's new focus on women over 50.
- **Quality Street (1956)**: the end of rationing in 1954 and the post-war consumer boom; patriarchal 1950s gender roles; luxury chocolates as an affordable treat.
- **NHS 111 (2023)**: pressure on A&E after Covid; a government-funded public-information campaign; greater diversity and accessibility in health advertising, and fathers as carers.

### The 25-mark comparison, Q2(b)

The question is always a version of *Compare the representation of [gender / ethnicity / men / women / age / an event] in [set product] and [unseen product]*, with three bullets: the **choices the producers have made**, **how far** the representation is **similar**, and **how far** it is **different**. It is marked on AO2: analysis, comparison, use of the theoretical framework, and **judgements and conclusions**. It is also an **extended response**, so a "sustained line of reasoning which is coherent … and logically structured" is rewarded.

What separates the bands:
- **21–25**: detailed analysis of **both** products, engaging with **complex** aspects of representation; detailed comparisons throughout; confident use of theory and terminology; well-reasoned judgements about **how far**.
- **11–15**: obvious aspects, some description, and comparisons that may lean on one product.
- **1–10**: description, one product at a time, no real judgement.

**The structure that works**: a short introduction with your judgement, then **three or four comparative paragraphs**, each built on **one aspect** of representation (not one product), then a conclusion that answers "how far".

| Paragraph | Build it around | Compare |
|---|---|---|
| Intro | your overall judgement | both products and their contexts in two sentences |
| 1 | the **dominant image**: pose, gaze, shot, costume | how each constructs the group |
| 2 | **written codes**: cover lines, taglines, headlines, anchorage | the values each attaches to the group |
| 3 | **colour, typography and layout**; the position of the group on the page | power and status |
| 4 | **context and purpose**: audience, time, producer | *why* the choices differ |
| Conclusion | answer **how far**, with a reason | similar in X, different in Y, and which matters more |

**Use theory to sharpen a point, not to decorate it.** Representations are **selected, constructed and mediated**: say what the producer *chose* to show and what they left out. **Stereotypes** help audiences read a product quickly; say whether each product reinforces or subverts one. **Feminist approaches** such as the **male gaze** fit the older products and the sexualised images. **Under-representation** fits the questions on ethnicity. Link each idea to a specific feature in the same sentence.

**Comparative language keeps both products in every paragraph**: *whereas*, *similarly*, *in contrast*, *both … but*, *this is reinforced in / undermined in*, *more … than*.

::: terms
- **Unseen product**: a media product you have not studied, given in the exam to compare with a set product in the same form.
- **Context**: the historical, social and cultural, or political circumstances that shape how a product is made and what it means.
- **Historical context**: how a product reflects the time it was made, including its technology, events, values and the genre conventions of the period.
- **Political context**: how a product reflects politics and power, including the owner's viewpoint, government, campaigns and regulation.
- **Selection**: the producer's choice of what to include in a representation, and therefore what to leave out.
- **Construction**: the way media language (image, colour, font, words) is put together to build a particular version of a person or group.
- **Mediation**: the process by which a representation passes through a producer's values and purposes before it reaches the audience.
- **Anchorage**: written text that fixes the preferred meaning of an image, such as a cover line or caption.
- **Stereotype**: a simplified, widely recognised image of a group that lets audiences read a product quickly.
- **Male gaze**: a feminist idea that many products frame women as objects to be looked at from a heterosexual male point of view.
- **Judgement**: a reasoned conclusion about how far two representations are similar or different, supported by analysis.
:::

::: method
1. **Study time (10 min).** Annotate the unseen product with the five-step routine, then draw arrows to matching features on the set product. Write a one-line judgement.
2. **Q2(a) (5 marks, about 8 min).** Make three points, each in the form context → specific feature of the set product → meaning. Name the context type used in the question, and use no introduction.
3. **Q2(b) plan (3 min).** List three or four aspects, such as image, written codes, colour and layout, and context. Note one similarity and one difference for each.
4. **Q2(b) introduction.** In two sentences, name both products and their contexts and state your judgement about how far they are similar.
5. **Comparative paragraphs.** Each covers one aspect and **both** products: point, evidence from product A, evidence from product B, analysis of the meaning, and a theory or context link.
6. **Keep answering "how far".** End each paragraph with a mini-judgement, e.g. "so the similarity is only on the surface".
7. **Conclusion.** Weigh the similarities against the differences, give a reason tied to purpose or context, and don't introduce new evidence.
:::

::: example Q2(a): historical context and film posters
Explain how historical contexts influence film posters. Refer to The Man with the Golden Gun poster to support your points. (5)

In 1974 film posters were usually **hand-illustrated**, and The Man with the Golden Gun is a busy painted collage rather than a single photograph. The artwork lets the designers pack in explosions, fights and several characters at once, so the poster sells the film as non-stop action (1). The film came out soon after the **1973 energy crisis**, and the poster's power-plant imagery and energy beam reflect public anxiety about oil and new power sources (1). Kung fu films were hugely popular at the time, so a woman in a martial-arts pose and the Asian settings tie Bond to that **cultural trend** to widen the film's appeal (1). The women in bikinis around Bond reflect 1970s attitudes in which women in action films were often decorative objects of the **male gaze**, although the 1970s were also a time of growing campaigns for women's equality (1). Roger Moore's name is placed prominently because he was already a household name from television and his previous Bond film, which shows how stars were used to sell films before the internet and trailers online (1).
:::

::: example Q2(a): political context and newspapers
Explain how political contexts influence newspapers. Refer to The Sun front page (22 March 2025) to support your points. (5)

The Sun is owned by News UK, part of Rupert Murdoch's News Corp, and has a **right-leaning** political stance. Its front pages often frame events to criticise governments it opposes (1). Here the Heathrow shutdown after a substation fire is headlined with a pun on the sitcom Fawlty Towers. This connotes incompetence, and in 2025 it implies that the **Labour government** had let Britain's infrastructure decline (1). The bullet-point sub-headings about a "humiliated" Britain use **populist**, nationalistic language that turns a technical failure into a story about national pride, a political framing typical of the tabloid press (1). Emotive words in the copy, such as "fiasco", encourage outrage rather than calm explanation, which suits a paper that sees itself as speaking up for ordinary readers against the establishment (1). By contrast, a liberal paper such as The Guardian reported the government more cautiously the same spring, which shows that a paper's **political viewpoint** shapes both its choice of story and its language (1).
:::

::: example A full plan for Q2(b)
Compare the representation of men in GQ (August 2019) and the unseen cover below. (25)

> **Unseen: *IRON*, a men's fitness magazine cover (invented for practice, 2024).** A white man in his thirties stands shirtless in a mid-shot, shot from a slightly low angle, arms folded, with an unsmiling expression and direct gaze. The background is dark grey; the masthead is in heavy red capitals behind his head. Cover lines: "BUILD A BIGGER CHEST IN 6 WEEKS", "The 5am club: discipline wins", "Beat stress: the fighter's mindset". The palette is red, black and white, and the font is bold and sans serif throughout.

Plan (about 3 minutes):
- **Judgement**: both construct an idealised, muscular masculinity, but GQ adds moral purpose, style and ethnicity, while *IRON* narrows masculinity to strength and discipline.
- **Image**: both show a shirtless, muscular body and use direct address, and both place the cover star in the centre as a hero. Sterling's angel wings and open pose connote protection, while the folded arms in *IRON* connote toughness and control. Theory: construction of the hyper-masculine ideal.
- **Written codes**: GQ's "Guardian Angel" anchors Sterling as someone with a social mission against racism, and the fashion cover lines show men who care about style. The *IRON* cover lines use imperatives and numbers ("6 weeks") and present masculinity as self-discipline and fighting. Both reinforce aspiration, but through different values.
- **Colour and type**: GQ's gold connotes luxury and success, while the heavy red capitals of *IRON* connote aggression and energy. Both brand masculinity with power, one refined and one physical.
- **Ethnicity and context**: GQ's black cover star challenges the under-representation of black men on glossy covers and reflects Sterling's anti-racism campaign. *IRON* shows a white man and makes no comment on ethnicity, which is typical of the genre, so the difference is one of selection.
- **Conclusion**: they are similar in the body ideal, different in the purpose of masculinity. GQ's lifestyle audience (ABC1, 20–44) is offered a broader identity than the niche fitness reader.

This plan would support a band 5 answer (21–25), because every paragraph compares both covers and ends in a judgement.
:::

::: example Two model paragraphs for Q2(b)
Write an introduction and one comparative paragraph for: Compare the representation of women in No Time to Die and the unseen poster below. (25)

> **Unseen: *NIGHTFALL*, an action-thriller poster (invented for practice, 2023).** A woman in a black combat jacket stands in the centre in a long shot, holding a pistol at her side, with city lights behind her. Two men in suits stand smaller and blurred in the background. The tagline reads "She was never on their side." The title is in cold silver capitals; the palette is dark blue and silver.

**Introduction.** Both posters belong to the spy-action genre, which has traditionally placed a man at the centre and women at the edges, so both are shaped by a 2020s context in which audiences expect more active female characters (1). However, No Time to Die still keeps Bond as the dominant image and gives its women a supporting place, whereas *NIGHTFALL* makes a woman the hero. So they share a modern attitude, but they differ in how far they are willing to challenge the genre's gender hierarchy (1).

**Comparative paragraph.** The clearest similarity is that women in both posters are given **agency** through the same genre iconography. In No Time to Die, Nomi wears a military costume and an earpiece and holds a gun, which constructs her as a working agent rather than a "Bond girl" (1). The woman in *NIGHTFALL* is similarly coded as dangerous by her combat jacket and pistol (1). The difference lies in **selection and framing**. Nomi is one figure in a montage, placed below and around a much larger image of Bond, so the poster still positions the audience to see the story through him, while Paloma's evening dress keeps a trace of the older glamour stereotype (1). *NIGHTFALL* reverses this hierarchy: the men are small and blurred, and the tagline anchors the woman as the one with hidden knowledge and power, casting her as the Proppian hero rather than a helper (1). So both posters challenge the passive female stereotype, but No Time to Die does so **within** a male-centred franchise that needs to protect its brand, whereas *NIGHTFALL* makes the challenge its selling point (1).

Marked as part of a whole answer, paragraphs like these keep the response in band 5, because both products appear in the same analysis and the paragraph ends with a judgement.
:::

::: watch
- The 25-mark answer that analyses one product and then the other never gets past the middle band. Compare in every paragraph.
- **Q2(a)** is marked on **context**. Describing the product without naming what was happening at the time (or in society, or in politics) caps it at 2.
- Answer the **context type in the question**: a "political" question answered with general history loses marks.
- Don't **describe** the unseen product. Every detail you mention must be followed by what it connotes about the group in the question.
- "How far" needs a **judgement**. Write "more similar than different because…" or the reverse, and give a reason.
- Theory named without a specific feature attached ("this links to Mulvey") earns almost nothing.
- Keep to the **representation named** (gender, ethnicity, age). Media-language points only count if they build that representation.
- Watch the clock: Q2(a) is worth 5 marks, so it should get about 8 minutes, not 15.
:::

Indicative content. Similarities: both covers construct successful, aspirational men to admire; both link masculinity to consumption and style (the fashion cover lines in GQ, the watch guide in *The Gent*); both use the Proppian hero or role model. Differences: GQ uses Sterling's young, muscular, shirtless body, low angle, wings and direct address to construct physical power and moral mission, and it represents a black man, challenging under-representation; *The Gent* constructs mature, gentle, intellectual masculinity through the tailored suit, glasses, seated pose and averted gaze, so power comes from experience rather than the body; the "kindness" cover line challenges hyper-masculinity, whereas GQ keeps a hyper-masculine body alongside a caring message. Colour: gold for luxury and success against muted, traditional colours for heritage. Contexts: age (a 20–44 ABC1 target against an older reader), celebrity culture and anti-racism in 2019, and a 2020s debate about positive masculinity. Judgement, e.g.: similar in constructing aspirational, consumerist masculinity, but different in the source of that power (body and activism against age and gentleness), so more different in the values offered.
