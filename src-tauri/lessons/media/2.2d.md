## What it is

*Man Like Mobeen*, Series 1, Episode 2, **"Wifey Riddim"**, is one of the two contemporary sitcom options for Component 2, Section A. If you study it, you also study a ten-minute extract from the *Friends* pilot (lesson 2.2f), so you can show how the sitcom has changed. The set episode is studied for **all five areas**: media language, representation, industries, audiences and contexts. In the exam you watch a **three-minute extract twice**, taken from the **BBC iPlayer** version, so learn the episode scene by scene.

**The product.** The series was made by **Tiger Aspect** (part of Endemol Shine UK, inside a global media conglomerate) for **BBC Three**, and launched on **iPlayer** in late 2017, while BBC Three was an online-only channel. It is written by **Guz Khan**, who plays Mobeen, with Andy Milligan. The character began in short YouTube videos Khan made while working as a teacher, then a BBC comedy short and a pilot, before a full series was commissioned. Series 1 was later shown on **BBC One**, and the series reached **Netflix**. The show won awards for its writing and its cast.

**The situation.** Mobeen, 28, lives in **Small Heath, Birmingham**. His mother has gone back to Pakistan, so he is bringing up his teenage sister **Aqsa**. He is a care worker, a practising Muslim and an ex-"roadman" with a past as a teenage drug dealer that the community keeps mentioning. His friends are **Eight** (the dim one) and **Nate** (a black British friend who is not Muslim). The older men **Uncle Habib** and **Uncle Shady** act as his advisers.

### Media language

- **Genre: repetition and variation (Neale).** It repeats the sitcom's core: a fixed situation (brother raising sister), a home setting, a small recurring cast, stock types (the joker, the stroppy teenager, the grumpy elder), a **circular narrative** and verbal and visual humour. It varies the genre with **location filming** in real Birmingham streets, a **single-camera** style with no studio audience or laughter track, and a **popular-music soundtrack** spanning decades.
- **Narrative (Todorov).** Equilibrium (a normal morning) → disruption (Aqsa's suspension) → attempts to repair (find Mobeen a wife) → failure → return to the start. Sitcoms rarely reach a *new* equilibrium; the reassurance is that nothing important changes.
- **Narrative (Propp).** Mobeen is the **hero**, but a flawed **anti-hero** who fails in his quest; Aqsa is partly the **princess** he protects, yet she is really the boss. The uncles are **dispatchers and mentors** (they send him on the quest), Eight and Nate are **helpers**, and Brother Ahmed is the **villain** blocking him.
- **Camerawork.** Establishing high-angle shots of rooftops and a **low-angle pan** of the mosque give a strong sense of place and faith; Mobeen is often **centred**; close-ups and extreme close-ups capture reactions; **two-shots** show friendship; over-the-shoulder shots draw us into conversations (Khadijah's interview); a handheld, documentary feel in street scenes adds realism.
- **Editing.** Split screen, the wipe, time-lapse and jump cuts are **playful and self-referential**, closer to online video than to the studio sitcom.
- **Sound.** Diegetic street noise for realism; the **ticking clock and silence** in Brother Ahmed's front room turn awkwardness into comedy; the retro soundtrack widens appeal.
- **Mise-en-scène and costume.** Mobeen's **kurta, baseball cap and Maharishi coat** signify a man between cultures; Aqsa's uniform and western casual clothes mark her age; the uncles' traditional dress marks age and faith; the spotless white carpet is a prop that sets up physical comedy.

### Representation

- **Ethnicity and faith.** The programme challenges the **under-representation and misrepresentation** of British Muslims, who in news and drama are often linked to extremism. Here a working-class Muslim community is ordinary, funny and supportive. It is **self-representation**: a British Muslim writer-performer from the Midlands. Jokes play *on* stereotypes (arranged marriage, the "terrorist" joke an uncle makes, a carpet that is "almost racistly white") to expose and defuse them. Nate's friendship suggests **racial harmony**; the factsheet links this to Gilroy's idea of everyday "conviviality".
- **Masculinity.** Mobeen mixes traditional and non-traditional traits. He is the protective big brother who warns Aqsa off boys, and an ex-roadman with street credibility. He is also **maternal**: a carer by job and at home, he hugs his friends and makes smoothies. The uncles tease him as "domesticated". Humour comes from the gap between the macho image and the caring reality.
- **Femininity.** **Aqsa** is strong-minded, suspended for fighting and in charge at home, which challenges the passive-girl stereotype, though she also fits the stroppy teenager type. **Khadijah** arrives with prepared questions about Mobeen's views on women, a progressive, self-determining young Muslim woman; yet she is still presented by her father as a daughter to be married. **Older Muslim women are absent**: the men trade outside the mosque while the women are assumed to be at home, which is arguably stereotypical under-representation.
- **Age.** The uncles are traditional, deadpan and meddling, but protective; the older generation gives advice, the younger generation ignores it.
- **Family and place.** The "family" is non-traditional: brother, sister and two friends on a sofa. Birmingham, rarely shown positively on TV, is represented as diverse and close-knit, which Khan has said was his aim.

### Industry

- **Public service broadcasting.** The BBC is funded by the **licence fee** and has a remit to **inform, educate and entertain**, which lets it take risks on new voices and on programming for young and diverse audiences that advertisers might not pay for. It is governed by its **Royal Charter**, overseen by the BBC Board, and regulated by **Ofcom**.
- **Convergence and distribution.** Online-first on iPlayer, then **linear TV** on BBC One (reaching an older audience), then **Netflix** (global reach); box-set promotion encourages **binge-viewing**. BBC Three itself returned to linear TV in 2022.
- **Production.** An independent company (Tiger Aspect) making a commission for the BBC; later series trained working-class trainees from Birmingham through a diversity scheme.
- **Regulation.** Self-regulation through scheduling, the watershed and content warnings; Ofcom's Broadcasting Code.

### Audiences

- **BBC Three's remit** targets roughly **16–34-year-olds**, diverse and UK-wide, and actively looks beyond the South East. Most of Mobeen's audience is under 35, but it has **crossover appeal** because faith is the setting, not the whole joke.
- **Marketing**: interviews on BBC **Asian Network**, iPlayer trailers, a box-set promotion with the tagline "Roadman or Role Model?", an image of Mobeen with a picture of Small Heath superimposed on him (regional identity), a BBC Bitesize careers feature, and social media discussion (fans even asked where to buy the coat).
- **Uses and gratifications**: *entertainment* (slapstick, dialogue); *information* (an insight into a community some viewers do not know); *personal identity* (young British Asians see themselves, or anyone who has sat through an awkward family visit); *social interaction* (sharing clips and talking on social media while watching).

### Contexts

- **Social and cultural**: growing expectations of diversity on screen; a contrast with the all-white *Friends* and with older, more one-dimensional British Asian sitcom characters. Topical subjects (arranged marriage, Islamophobia, policing, drugs) come from contemporary Britain. References to 1980s films and the retro soundtrack reflect a **nostalgia** culture.
- **Industry context**: the shift from linear schedules to streaming, and the BBC's need to reach young audiences who watch online.

::: terms
- **Sitcom**: situation comedy; a genre built on a fixed situation, recurring characters and settings, and a circular narrative.
- **Circular narrative**: a structure that returns the characters to their original situation at the end of each episode.
- **Verisimilitude**: the appearance of being real or believable, built here through location filming and diegetic sound.
- **Single-camera production**: filming shot by shot with one camera and editing afterwards, as in film, with no studio audience.
- **Split screen**: an editing technique showing two images side by side in the same frame at once.
- **Self-representation**: a social group representing itself, such as a British Muslim writer creating British Muslim characters.
- **Under-representation**: a social group appearing in the media far less often than it exists in society.
- **Public service broadcaster**: a broadcaster with a remit to inform, educate and entertain, such as the licence-fee funded BBC.
- **Licence fee**: the annual charge paid by UK households that watch live TV or use iPlayer, which funds the BBC.
- **Linear television**: programmes broadcast to a fixed schedule on a channel, as opposed to on-demand viewing.
- **Crossover appeal**: appeal that reaches beyond a product's core target audience to a wider mainstream audience.
:::

2. **Pick the exact code the question names** (camerawork, editing, sound, costume, setting or characters) and give **three or four precise examples**, each with a technical term.
3. **Say what each one means**: denotation, then connotation, then the effect on the audience (usually comedy, realism or alignment with Mobeen).
4. For **"how typical"** or **"how stereotypical"** questions, argue both ways for each example (typical of sitcom *because*…; varied *because*…), then give an overall judgement.
5. **Link to context or industry in one sentence** where it strengthens the point: BBC Three's young, diverse remit; self-representation by Guz Khan; the contrast with *Friends*.
6. For the 10-mark industry, audience or context question, make **four or five developed points**, each tied to a specific detail of *Man Like Mobeen* (iPlayer, BBC One, Netflix, Asian Network, the licence fee).

::: example Camerawork in the mosque scene
Explore how camerawork creates meaning in the scene outside the mosque. (10)

The scene opens with high-angle establishing shots over the rooftops of Small Heath, which fix the sitcom in a real, working-class Birmingham neighbourhood and give the programme the strong sense of place that its location filming is built on (1). A low-angle pan then shows the mosque framed in the centre, connoting that faith sits at the heart of this community (1). The market stalls are shown in wider shots full of people, so the audience reads the area as busy, close-knit and self-sufficient rather than as the troubled place such areas often are in the news (1). When the uncles talk to Mobeen, medium shots and two-shots keep them together in the frame, showing their role as advisers and the respect between generations (1). Close-ups catch Uncle Shady's deadpan expression, so the comedy comes from the reaction as much as the line (1). Mobeen is usually placed centrally, reminding us he is the protagonist whose quest (finding a wife to mother Aqsa) begins here (1). The slightly handheld feel of the street footage gives a documentary realism unlike the fixed cameras of a studio sitcom such as Friends (1). Overall the camerawork represents a British Muslim community warmly and realistically (1), which fits the programme's aim of challenging misrepresentation (1) and BBC Three's remit to show diverse, young Britain beyond London (1).
:::

::: example How typical are the characters?
How typical of the sitcom genre are the characters in Man Like Mobeen? (10)

Many characters are recognisable sitcom types, which helps the audience understand the comedy quickly. Eight is the classic "dumb" friend whose misunderstandings of words produce one-liners (2), and Aqsa fits the truculent teenager who answers back (1). The uncles fill the role of meddling elders whose deadpan criticism of Mobeen is a stock source of humour (1). However, these types are varied. Aqsa is also the real head of the household, suspended for fighting, which turns the older-brother-in-charge situation upside down (2). Mobeen himself is less typical: an ex-roadman who is also nurturing, emotional and domestic, so the joke is the gap between image and reality rather than a simple stereotype (2). Khadijah, arriving with a list of questions for her suitor, subverts the passive bride of an arranged-marriage story (1). My judgement is that the character *types* are conventional, which suits a mainstream BBC sitcom, but the way they are filled with British Muslim, working-class experience makes them more original than the all-white, middle-class types in older sitcoms such as Friends (1).
:::

::: example Industry: digital technologies
Explain how digital technologies have changed the way audiences watch Man Like Mobeen. (6)

It was first released on BBC iPlayer while BBC Three was online only, so audiences streamed it on demand, on phones and laptops, rather than at a scheduled time (2). The whole series was offered as a box set, which encouraged binge-viewing of several episodes in one sitting (2). It later moved to Netflix, so viewers outside the UK could find it through a subscription service's recommendations, giving a BBC comedy a global audience it would not have reached through linear broadcasting (2).
:::

::: example Representation of masculinity
Explain how Mobeen challenges stereotypes of masculinity. Refer to two examples. (4)

He is shown in a caring, maternal role, bringing up his sister and working as a care worker (1), which reverses the usual sitcom pattern of the mother running the home while the father is useless (1). He is openly affectionate, greeting his friends with hugs (1), so male friendship is shown as warm and emotional rather than competitive or cool (1).
