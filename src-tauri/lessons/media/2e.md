## What it is

Every media product is made at a particular time, in a particular society, under particular political conditions, and it carries the marks of all three. **Media contexts** asks how those conditions shape what a product says and how it is made, sold and received. The spec names three: **historical**, **social and cultural**, and **political**. Context is tested directly in Component 1 Question 2(a) (5 marks: "Explain how [historical / social and cultural / political] contexts influence [form]. Refer to [set product]") and in Component 2 Questions 2 and 4 (10 marks, e.g. "How do music videos reflect the time in which they were made?"). It also lifts answers everywhere else: a representation point explained through context is worth more than one that isn't.

### The golden rule

Context is not a history lesson. A mark comes from linking **a fact about the time** to **a specific choice in the product**, and explaining **why** the one produced the other. "In the 1950s rationing ended" earns little on its own; "Sweets came off rationing in 1953, so a lavish tin of chocolates offered as a romantic prize sold the new pleasure of affordable luxury" earns the mark.

### Historical context

How a product reflects the time it was made through:
- its **media language and genre conventions** (illustrated vs photographic posters; hand-drawn adverts; the style of a 1980s music video);
- its **representations, themes, values, messages and viewpoints** (gender roles, attitudes to ethnicity);
- its **production, distribution, marketing, regulation, circulation and consumption** (print-only vs cross-platform; cinema release vs streaming; one TV channel vs on demand).

| Product | Time | How the time shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Quality Street advert** | 1956 | Post-war Britain: rationing of sweets ended in 1953 and of all goods in 1954, so a consumer boom began and luxury became possible again. Hand-drawn artwork and a rich colour palette reflect the print style of the period. Patriarchal gender roles: the man in evening dress as provider, women as romantic and decorative. |
| **The Man with the Golden Gun poster** | 1974 | Illustrated collage posters were standard. The martial-arts craze (the success of Bruce Lee's films) led to kung fu scenes and Asian locations. The 1973 oil crisis is hinted at by a power plant and energy beam. The permissive era and the male gaze: bikini-clad women, even as the women's liberation movement was growing. |
| **No Time to Die poster** | 2021 | Digital photographic montage. #MeToo (from 2017) and Black Lives Matter protests (2020) changed audience expectations, so women are armed agents and a black woman is a 00 agent. Release delayed by the Covid-19 pandemic; marketed through websites and social media. |

### Social and cultural context

How a product reflects the society and culture in which it was made through:
- **representations, themes, values, messages and viewpoints**;
- **cultural influences**: genres, styles, technologies and the work of other producers (the Bond franchise's own history; a sitcom referenced in a headline; the editor who changes a magazine's direction);
- **production, distribution, marketing, regulation, circulation and consumption**;
- **how audience responses change over time**.

Key set-product examples:
- **Vogue (July 2021)**: black and Asian models were long under-represented on glossy covers. Edward Enninful, appointed editor in 2017 as the first black (and first male) editor of British Vogue, championed diversity of ethnicity, age, size and faith. Choosing Malala, a Muslim woman in a headscarf, reflects and pushes that change.
- **GQ (August 2019)**: changing ideas of masculinity (the grooming-conscious "metrosexual", the body-focused man), and anti-racism in football: Sterling had publicly challenged racist treatment of black players in the press, so he is framed as a moral leader.
- **NHS 111 (2023)**: post-Covid pressure on A&E and emergency services; fathers now far more involved in childcare than in earlier generations; growing representation of deaf and disabled people; public-health aims to reach under-served, global-majority audiences.
- **Newspapers (2025)**: the reappraisal of women over 50 in the media; national rituals such as the VE Day anniversary; football as the dominant national sport.
- **Responses change**: many readers now see the 1956 Quality Street advert and the 1974 Bond poster as sexist, where original audiences probably saw romance and glamour.

### Political context

How a product reflects the politics of its time through:
- its **representations, themes, values, messages and viewpoints**;
- its **ownership and political viewpoint**, production, distribution, marketing, **regulation**, circulation and consumption.

Key examples:
- **The Sun (22 March 2025)**: owned by News UK, part of Rupert Murdoch's News Corp; right-leaning and populist. Under a Labour government, its pun headline on the Heathrow power failure implies incompetence in running national infrastructure.
- **The Guardian (6 May 2025)**: owned by the Scott Trust, liberal-left. Its lead story reports the Labour government reconsidering its winter fuel payment cut after local-election losses, using measured language ("rethink") rather than mockery.
- **Vogue (July 2021)**: published as Western troops prepared to leave Afghanistan and the Taliban regained control, making Malala's "activist" label politically charged.
- **Regulation**: IPSO for the press, the BBFC for films, the ASA for adverts, Ofcom for broadcasting all reflect political decisions about how media should be controlled.

### Writing a context answer (Q2(a), 5 marks)

In about eight minutes: two or three linked points, each **context → product choice → effect/meaning**, using the named type of context and the named set product. Don't drift into media language analysis for its own sake, and don't write general history.

::: terms
- **Historical context**: the conditions of the time a product was made, and how they shape its language, representations, production and reception.
- **Social and cultural context**: the values, attitudes, customs and cultural influences of the society in which a product was made.
- **Political context**: the political situation, ownership and viewpoints that shape a product's messages and regulation.
- **Post-war consumerism**: the growth of spending on goods and luxuries in Britain after rationing ended in the early 1950s.
- **Patriarchal values**: attitudes that treat male authority and traditional male and female roles as normal.
- **Permissive society**: the more liberal attitudes to sex, censorship and behaviour associated with the 1960s and 1970s.
- **#MeToo**: a movement from 2017 against sexual harassment and abuse, which increased scrutiny of how women are represented.
- **Political stance**: the political position a media product or organisation takes, often reflecting its ownership and audience.
- **Zeitgeist**: the spirit or mood of a particular time, which products may reflect or try to shape.
:::

::: method
1. **Underline the context type** (historical, social and cultural, political) and the form and product named. Answer that type only.
2. **Choose two or three facts about the time** that you know are accurate. Keep them brief.
3. **Link each fact to a specific choice** in the product: an image, a costume, a headline, a cover star, a layout convention, a production method.
4. **Explain the effect**: what the choice means, and why it made sense to the audience of the time.
5. **Where it helps, add change over time**: how audiences would read it differently now.
6. **For a 10-mark question**, develop three or four linked points across media language, representation and industry, with a short concluding judgement.
:::

::: example Historical context: 5 marks
Explain how historical contexts influence film posters. Refer to The Man with the Golden Gun (1974) poster to support your points. (5)

The poster is a hand-illustrated collage, which was the standard way to show many scenes at once before photographic digital montage; it lets the designers pack in action, explosions, enemies and women around Bond (1). Martial-arts films were hugely popular in the early 1970s, especially after Bruce Lee's success, so the poster includes a woman in a karate pose and fighting figures, and the film was shot largely in Asia to cash in on the trend (1). The 1973 oil crisis is reflected in the power-plant iconography and the energy beam aimed at Bond, making the villain's threat feel topical (1). Bond himself embodies the ideal 1970s action man, smart, confident and armed, while the bikini-clad women reflect a permissive era in which the male gaze was a normal selling point (1). A modern audience, shaped by #MeToo and feminist criticism, is more likely to read these women as objectified, which shows how the poster reflects the values of its time (1).
:::

::: example Social and cultural context: 5 marks
Explain how social and cultural contexts influence magazines. Refer to the Vogue (July 2021) front cover. (5)

For decades, glossy magazines rarely put women of colour on their covers, often claiming without evidence that it would hurt sales (1). When Edward Enninful became editor of British Vogue in 2017, the first black editor of the magazine, he set out to widen who appeared on its covers, including different ethnicities, ages, sizes and faiths (1). Choosing Malala Yousafzai, a Pakistani Muslim woman, and showing her in a red headscarf central to her Pashtun identity, reflects that cultural shift and challenges the stereotype that women who wear headscarves are oppressed (1). The caption calling her a survivor and activist reflects a culture in which fashion magazines present themselves as having a purpose, not only selling clothes (1). The issue also appeared as the Taliban regained control of Afghanistan, giving her activism for girls' education a sharp relevance and helping Vogue reach readers beyond its traditional fashion audience (1).
:::

::: example Political context: 5 marks
Explain how political contexts influence newspapers. Refer to The Sun (22 March 2025) front page. (5)

The Sun is owned by News UK, part of Rupert Murdoch's News Corp, and has a right-leaning, populist political stance (1). In 2025 the UK had a Labour government, which The Sun generally opposes (1). Its pun headline on the Heathrow shutdown borrows the name of a famous sitcom about a hopeless hotel owner, implying that Britain's power infrastructure, and by extension the government responsible for it, is being run incompetently (1). Its sub-headings suggest the country has been humiliated, framing a technical failure as a national scandal, a patriotic angle that fits its readers' values (1). Emotive words throughout the story encourage outrage rather than calm explanation, showing how a paper's politics shape the way an event is represented (1).
:::

::: example 10-mark context: music video
How do music videos reflect the time in which they were made? Refer to the music video from the 1980s or 1990s you have studied. (10)

Model plan (adapt to Rio or Waterfalls): open by linking the video to its industry moment: MTV launched in 1981 and made the music video essential marketing, so record labels invested large budgets in glossy, filmic videos (2). Explain two representation points and the social values they reflect: for Rio, the 1980s culture of wealth and consumerism and the stylish, playful New Romantic look, with women mainly objects of the male gaze; for Waterfalls, the mid-1990s HIV/AIDS crisis and concern about drugs and violence in US cities, with the women as confident moral voices (4). Explain one media language point tied to the technology of the time: location filming on a yacht in the Caribbean for Rio; early CGI water effects for Waterfalls (2). Conclude on how audiences' responses have changed since (2).

Band: 9–10 when every point links a fact about the time to a specific choice in the video.
:::

Explain how audience responses to a product can change over time. Use a set product. (3)
---
Social values change (1); e.g. a 1974 audience may have enjoyed the glamour of the bikini-clad women on the Golden Gun poster (1), while modern audiences influenced by feminism and #MeToo often read them as objectified (1).

::: q
Explain how the Covid-19 pandemic influenced No Time to Die. (2)
---
Its release was delayed from April 2020 to autumn 2021 (1), extending the marketing campaign and making it a major event in cinemas' recovery; it later became available on streaming (1).

::: watch
- **Context must be linked to the product.** Two lines of history followed by two lines of analysis, unconnected, stays in the lower bands.
- **Answer the context type asked.** A political context question about Vogue needs Afghanistan and politics, not only fashion.
- **Keep facts safe and short.** A wrong date costs credibility; a general "early 1950s" is better than an invented figure.
- **Don't judge the past only by today's standards**: explain what the product meant to its original audience first.
- **5 marks means about eight minutes.** Two or three developed points, not an essay.
:::
