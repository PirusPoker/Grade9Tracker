## What it is

Stormzy's ***Superheroes*** (2020) is one of the two options for your **second** contemporary music video (the other is Justin Bieber's *Intentions*). The spec sets the **video** for **media language, representation and contexts**. His official website, **stormzy.com**, and his social media are studied for **all five areas**, so industry and audience questions about Stormzy come through the website. In the exam the video appears in **Q3 (20 marks)** alongside your first video (*Good as Hell* or *The Man*). The song contains mild expletives; the focus is the **visuals**.

**Background.**
- The track is from Stormzy's second album, ***Heavy Is the Head*** (December 2019). The video premiered on **9 September 2020**, when it was **streamed into school classrooms** as pupils returned after the Covid-19 closures. Stormzy recorded a message for pupils telling them they were superheroes and that the sky was the limit.
- It is a **2D animated video**, directed by British filmmaker **Taz Tron Delix** and made by **2veinte**, an animation studio in **Buenos Aires, Argentina**, in about eight weeks. To make that possible, simple backgrounds were used after the establishing shots, keeping the focus on the characters.
- It is dedicated to the actor **Chadwick Boseman** (star of *Black Panther*), who died in August 2020, with an end card calling him a superhero forever.

### What happens

Stormzy appears to a young black boy who lacks confidence, and takes him on a **journey** across the city, showing him other children and young people who have discovered their "superpowers": a student solving maths problems with seemingly telekinetic control of pens; a young musician drawn with **six arms** to show his many talents; a girl playing football in a **Marcus Rashford** shirt; a girl cooking and washing up while caring for her grandmother; a girl anxious in the mirror who gains confidence from a picture of the rapper **Little Simz**; a father doing his daughter's hair; protesters with placards and masks at a **Black Lives Matter** demonstration. Stormzy reads **Malorie Blackman's *Noughts and Crosses*** and hands it to police officers. When the boy tries to fly alone and falls, Stormzy rescues him. At the end Stormzy places the **world in the boy's hands** and leaves.

### Media language

- **Challenges conventions.** There is **no performance**: Stormzy does not sing to camera or perform the song. The video is entirely **narrative**, with the lyrics narrating the story, and it is **animated**, which is rare for a major rap video.
- **Why animation?** It allows **fantasy and superpowers** (flying, lightning, teleporting, extra arms, glowing golden bones) that would be costly or impossible in live action. It connects with the **young target audience**, gives a feeling of timeless fable, and lets Stormzy appear as a guide or spirit rather than as a celebrity.
- **Narrative (Propp).** Stormzy is the **mentor/donor** and **hero** who rescues the boy when he falls; the boy is a hero on a **hero's journey** from doubt to confidence; the "young black kings and queens" he meets are further heroes and helpers. Todorov: a lack of confidence → journey → a new equilibrium in which the world is in his hands.
- **Colour and lighting.** **Soft pastel** shades and a **pink sky** connote hope and optimism; **gold** highlights on the bones of Stormzy and the boy when they turn translucent connote **inner strength and courage**; **lightning, sunlight and stars** connote energy and superpower.
- **Sound.** The opening **birdsong** over a wide shot of a UK cityscape connotes hope and a new beginning.
- **Camerawork.** A **low-angle shot** makes Stormzy heroic when he first meets the boy, who looks up at him from the corner of the frame, positioning us to see Stormzy as a **role model**. Wide establishing shots set the urban scene.
- **Costume.** Stormzy's **all-black outfit** can connote black empowerment; the boy's **tracksuit** was chosen to echo the dress in Stormzy's own earlier videos, adding **relatability** for young viewers.
- **Iconography and symbolism.** Placards and **raised fists** (black power); the **globe** placed in the boy's hands (the future belongs to him); *Noughts and Crosses* (education and resistance to racism).
- **Intertextuality.** Flying with the boy recalls **Buzz and Woody** in *Toy Story*; the maths student recalls **Neo** in *The Matrix*; the superhero framing and dedication recall *Black Panther*. Real people (Rashford, Little Simz, rapper Dave) are woven in, adding authenticity.

### Representation

- **Young black British people.** Represented as **talented, intelligent, creative and powerful**, the "superheroes" of the title. This directly counters **negative stereotypes** in news media that link young black people, especially boys, to gangs and crime, and addresses their **under-representation** in positions of achievement.
- **Stormzy.** A **positive black male role model**: caring, patient, protective and educated (he reads a novel). This is his **self-representation** as a mentor rather than a tough grime MC.
- **Masculinity.** Challenges stereotypes: black fathers are shown as **nurturing** (the father doing his daughter's hair); boys are shown excelling at maths and music.
- **Femininity.** The **footballer** challenges gender stereotypes about sport; the **mirror scene** addresses **body confidence** and the value of role models such as Little Simz; the girl **cooking and caring** for her grandmother arguably reinforces a **domestic stereotype** for girls, but the video reframes caring as heroic. You can argue both ways.
- **Family.** Different family types, such as a girl living with her grandmother and a father raising his daughter, show that not everyone lives in a "typical" family.
- **Police and race.** Handing *Noughts and Crosses* to the police and the protest scene represent **racial profiling** and **stop and search**, positioning **education** as the route to change.
- **Reception.** The **preferred reading** is empowerment and hope for young black audiences; some might see the focus on exceptional talent as putting pressure on children to be extraordinary.

### Contexts

- **Black Lives Matter (2020).** After the murder of George Floyd in the US, protests spread to the UK; Stormzy took part in London. The masked protesters also place the video in the **Covid-19 pandemic**.
- **Marcus Rashford's free school meals campaign (2020)** pressured the UK government to reverse a decision during the pandemic, making him a hero to many young people.
- **Education and activism.** Stormzy has funded **scholarships for black British students at Cambridge University** (from 2018), launched the **#Merky Books** publishing imprint, and pledged **£10 million over ten years** to organisations fighting racial inequality in the UK (2020). He is known for political statements, including at his **Glastonbury headline set** in 2019.
- **Culture.** Stormzy helped bring **grime** into the mainstream; *Noughts and Crosses* had just been adapted by the BBC, with Stormzy in a small role.

### stormzy.com, social media, industry and audiences

- **Industry.** His first album was released **independently**; *Heavy Is the Head* was released through **Atlantic Records** (Warner) with his own **#Merky** brand, which extends into books, a festival and charitable foundations. He was the first grime artist to headline Glastonbury. **Convergence**: the website links to YouTube, streaming, tours and merchandise, and the *Superheroes* video sits on its videos page.
- **Website (credit any version).** A home page led by the **current album era** and its artwork; links to stream and buy; videos; tour dates; merchandise; social links; content on #Merky initiatives that shows his **social responsibility**. His brand is built on **authenticity**, faith and community.
- **Social media.** Instagram, X and YouTube; personal posts and political views (Black Lives Matter, voting). His followers are split roughly evenly between male and female.
- **Audiences.** His core audience is **millennial and Gen Z grime and rap fans**, but *Superheroes* targeted **younger black British children** (Generation Alpha) through animation and the schools premiere. Uses and gratifications: *personal identity* (seeing themselves as heroes), *information/education*, *entertainment*; older fans enjoyed spotting a sample of a song known as a children's TV theme at the end of the track and shared their reactions online.

::: terms
- **Animation**: images drawn or generated frame by frame to create movement, here 2D hand-drawn style.
- **Hero's journey**: a narrative pattern in which a hero leaves the ordinary world, is guided, faces challenges and returns changed.
- **Mentor**: a guiding character who helps the hero; Stormzy's role in Superheroes.
- **Role model**: a person whose behaviour and success others, especially the young, look up to and copy.
- **Racial profiling**: targeting people for suspicion, such as police stop and search, because of their ethnicity.
- **Black Lives Matter**: a movement against racism and police violence towards black people, with major protests in 2020.
- **Intertextuality**: a reference within one media product to another media product or cultural text.
- **Grime**: a British music genre that grew out of London in the early 2000s, related to hip hop and electronic music.
- **Generation Alpha**: children born from the early 2010s onwards, the younger audience targeted by Superheroes.
:::

::: example Why animation?
Explain why animation suits the message of Superheroes. (6)

The video's message is that ordinary black children have superpowers, and animation can show these literally (flying, lightning, a musician with six arms), so the metaphor becomes visible and memorable (2). Its target audience was schoolchildren, and animation is familiar and appealing to that age group, which is why it could premiere in classrooms (2). Animation also gives the video a fable-like, timeless quality, and lets Stormzy appear as a magical guide rather than as a celebrity performing, which puts the focus on the children (2).
:::

::: example Representation of young black people
How far does Superheroes challenge stereotypes of young black people? (10)

It challenges them strongly. Young black characters are shown excelling in maths, music, football and caring, so they are defined by talent and intelligence rather than by the crime and gang stereotypes common in news coverage (3). The boy's journey from shyness to confidence, guided by a caring black male mentor, gives a positive model of black masculinity (2). The mirror scene and the picture of Little Simz address black girls' body confidence and the need for visible role models (2). The protest and the police scenes link this to Black Lives Matter and racial profiling in 2020, framing education as the route to change (2). One limit is that the girl cooking and washing up reinforces a domestic role for girls, though the video presents her caring as heroic; overall it challenges stereotypes far more than it reinforces them (1).
:::
