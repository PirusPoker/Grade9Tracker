## What it is

**Desert Island Discs** is the Section B radio set product (from 2027). The spec asks you to know it as an **evolving** product: its **historical significance** as a **talk radio** programme, how it has **changed in response to industry and audience demands**, and what it shows about the contemporary radio industry and radio audiences. Your centre chooses **one complete episode**, plus the programme's **website homepage and at least one other page**. As with all of Section B, you use it as an example of industry and audience issues, not for textual analysis.

### The programme and its history

- **Created by Roy Plomley** and first broadcast by the BBC in **1942**, during the Second World War. Plomley presented it for more than 40 years, until his death in 1985.
- Later presenters: **Michael Parkinson**, **Sue Lawley**, **Kirsty Young**, and since **2018 Lauren Laverne**. Each host has brought a different interviewing style, but the format has hardly changed. Keeping the familiar format is itself an industry decision about protecting a **brand**.
- **The format**: each week a guest, the **castaway**, imagines being stranded on a desert island. They choose **eight recordings** to take, and the choices are woven into a conversation about their life. They also choose a **book** (the Bible or another religious or philosophical text and the complete works of Shakespeare are already there) and a **luxury** item, and they pick the one record they would save above all. The theme music with seagull sounds has opened the show for decades.
- It is broadcast on **BBC Radio 4**, the BBC's speech network, on Sunday mornings, with a repeat later in the week. It is one of the longest-running radio programmes in the world.

**Why it matters as talk radio.** Talk radio is speech-led radio: interviews, discussion, phone-ins and documentaries. Desert Island Discs is a hybrid: an **in-depth interview** built around **music as memory**. The music breaks the conversation into chapters, so each record opens up a period or emotion in the guest's life. That mix of intimacy and music has made it the model for many celebrity interview podcasts.

### How it has evolved

| Pressure | How the programme responded |
|---|---|
| **New technology** | moved from live radio only to repeats, **BBC iPlayer Radio** and then **BBC Sounds** (launched 2018), downloads and a **podcast** feed; an online **archive** of past episodes |
| **Music rights** | full episodes with complete tracks are on Radio 4 and BBC Sounds; podcast versions have often used **shortened music**, because rights for full tracks in downloadable podcasts are costly |
| **Younger audiences** | on-demand listening, a presenter known from BBC 6 Music and TV, short clips and quotes on social media, castaways from pop music, sport, comedy and online culture |
| **Diversity and representation** | a wider range of castaways by gender, ethnicity, class and profession than in the early decades, which reflects the BBC's duty to represent the whole UK |
| **Competition** | the podcast boom, streaming and commercial radio mean the BBC has to make its heritage brands discoverable on apps, smart speakers and search |

### The radio industry: three funding models

| Model | Who | How it's funded | What it must do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public service broadcasting (PSB)** | the **BBC** (Radio 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Live, 6 Music, the Asian Network, local stations) | the **licence fee**, not advertising | fulfil a **public service remit**: to **inform, educate and entertain**, and serve all audiences, including minorities |
| **Commercial** | e.g. Global (Capital, Heart, LBC, Classic FM) and Bauer (Kiss, Magic, Absolute) | **advertising**, sponsorship, promotions | attract large audiences that advertisers want, often through a music format with a strong brand; often owned by large groups |
| **Community / not-for-profit** | small local stations, e.g. hospital, student or community stations | grants, donations, charities, limited local advertising | serve a specific local or specialist audience; licensed by Ofcom |

The **BBC** is set up by a **Royal Charter**, which sets out its **mission** (to inform, educate and entertain) and its **public purposes**, such as impartial news, reflecting the UK's diverse communities and supporting creativity. Desert Island Discs meets that remit because it **informs** (life stories, history, science, public service), **educates** (music and culture from across genres and eras) and **entertains** (celebrity, humour, emotion). It carries **no adverts**, because it is licence-fee funded.

**Regulation**: **Ofcom** regulates UK radio and television, including the BBC. It enforces the **Broadcasting Code** (harm and offence, accuracy, impartiality, fairness and privacy) and checks that the BBC delivers its public purposes. Commercial and community stations need an Ofcom **licence**. The challenge from digital technology is that podcasts and global audio platforms fall largely outside broadcast regulation.

### Distribution, convergence and promotion

The BBC uses **convergence** to take one programme to audiences across platforms:
- **live broadcast** on Radio 4 via FM, **DAB** digital radio, TV set-top boxes and online streams;
- a **repeat** later in the week, so listeners can catch it at another time;
- **BBC Sounds**, the BBC's own app and website, for live, catch-up, download and personalised recommendations;
- a **podcast** feed on other podcast apps, reaching people who never tune in to Radio 4;
- **smart speakers**, where listeners ask for the programme by name;
- the **programme website**: the homepage, episode pages with castaways' choices, a searchable **archive** of castaways, and links to BBC Sounds;
- **social media**: short clips, quotes and castaway announcements that are easy to share.

Technology also lets the BBC **measure** listening: **RAJAR** surveys measure live radio audiences, and BBC Sounds records on-demand plays and downloads. That data helps it see who listens and when.

### Audiences

- **Core audience**: Radio 4's audience is **older** and mainly **ABC1**, educated, middle-class listeners who value speech radio, culture and current affairs.
- **Mass and niche**: it reaches a large national audience, but talk radio built around long interviews is a more **specialised** taste than pop radio.
- **New audiences**: podcast and BBC Sounds listeners are younger. Popular castaways from music, sport, comedy and online culture draw fans who might never listen to Radio 4 live.
- **Global**: the podcast and BBC Sounds reach listeners abroad, extending the BBC brand.

**Uses and Gratifications**:
- **Entertainment / diversion**: famous voices, music and emotional life stories, often as company while cooking or driving.
- **Information and education**: learning about people's lives, their work and music you might not know.
- **Personal identity**: listeners compare their own choices with the castaways' ("what would my eight be?") and hear people like themselves, or people they aspire to be.
- **Social interaction**: sharing clips, discussing a castaway's surprising choice, recommending an episode.

Audiences are **active**. They choose when and how to listen, skip or save episodes, and interpret castaways differently depending on their own experiences, generation and taste in music.

::: terms
- **Talk radio**: speech-led radio built around interviews, discussion and documentary rather than continuous music.
- **Castaway**: the guest on Desert Island Discs, who chooses eight records, a book and a luxury for an imaginary desert island.
- **Public service broadcasting**: broadcasting that serves the public rather than profit, with a remit to inform, educate and entertain.
- **Licence fee**: the charge paid by UK households that watch live TV or use BBC iPlayer, which funds the BBC's services, including its radio.
- **Royal Charter**: the constitutional document that establishes the BBC and sets out its mission and public purposes.
- **Ofcom**: the Office of Communications, the UK regulator for radio, television and the BBC.
- **Commercial radio**: radio funded mainly by advertising and sponsorship, such as Capital and Heart.
- **Community radio**: small not-for-profit stations serving a local or specialist community, licensed by Ofcom.
- **BBC Sounds**: the BBC's app and website for live radio, catch-up, downloads and podcasts.
- **Podcast**: an audio programme that can be downloaded or streamed on demand and subscribed to.
- **RAJAR**: the body that measures radio audiences in the UK.
:::

::: example Briefly explain: PSB against commercial
Briefly explain the difference between a public service radio broadcaster and a commercial radio broadcaster. (4)

A public service broadcaster such as the BBC is funded by the licence fee, so it carries no adverts and does not need to make a profit (1). It has a remit set by its Royal Charter to inform, educate and entertain and to serve all audiences, which is why Radio 4 can run a long, speech-based programme like Desert Island Discs for a specialised audience (1). Commercial stations such as Heart or Capital are funded by advertising and sponsorship, so they must attract large audiences with a clear demographic that advertisers want (1). As a result they usually rely on popular music formats and strong branding, and they are often owned by large groups such as Global or Bauer, whose aim is profit (1).
:::

::: example Extended: platforms
Explain how the BBC uses different platforms to distribute radio programmes. Refer to Desert Island Discs to support your points. (10)

The BBC uses convergence so that one programme reaches audiences on many platforms, and Desert Island Discs, first heard live in 1942, is now available in more ways than ever (1).

It is still broadcast live on Radio 4 via FM, DAB and online streams, which suits its loyal older core audience who listen at a set time on Sunday mornings, and a **repeat** later in the week catches people who missed it (2). **BBC Sounds** lets listeners stream or download episodes whenever they like and recommends other programmes, which keeps them inside the BBC's own platform and gives the BBC data on what is played (2).

The **podcast** version takes the programme to people who never tune in to Radio 4, including younger listeners and audiences abroad, although podcast versions have often shortened the music because of rights costs (2). The **website** works as a shop window and an archive: listeners can search decades of castaways and their choices, which turns the programme's history into a resource that keeps attracting new listeners (1). **Social media** clips and castaway announcements promote each new episode, and smart speakers let people ask for it by name (1). Together these platforms show a public service broadcaster adapting a heritage brand to on-demand listening, so the licence fee still delivers value to all audiences (1).

This would reach the top band (9–10): accurate knowledge of distribution, several specific references to the set product, and confident terminology.
:::

::: example Extended: audiences
Explain why audiences listen to talk radio programmes. Refer to Desert Island Discs and the Uses and Gratifications theory. (12)

Talk radio survives in an age of streaming because it gives listeners something music playlists cannot, and Blumler and Katz's Uses and Gratifications theory shows that audiences actively choose Desert Island Discs to meet particular needs (1).

**Entertainment and diversion**: famous castaways tell funny and moving stories, and the music breaks the interview into chapters, so it is easy company while driving or cooking (2). **Information and education**: listeners learn about people's lives, careers and the history they lived through, and they discover music from different genres and eras, which fits the BBC's remit to inform and educate (2).

**Personal identity**: the format invites listeners to imagine their own eight records, book and luxury, so they reflect on their own memories and values. When castaways come from backgrounds like theirs, listeners also feel represented, and the BBC has widened the range of castaways over time for this reason (2). **Social interaction**: surprising choices and emotional moments are shared as clips and discussed online and at home (1).

Different audiences gratify different needs. The older, ABC1 Radio 4 audience values thoughtful conversation and routine, while younger podcast listeners choose episodes by guest and listen on demand (2). Audiences are active and interpret castaways differently according to their own generation and taste, and responses change over time as audiences hear archive episodes with new ears (2).

This would reach the top band (10–12): the theory applied to specific features, with industry (the BBC's remit, platforms), representation (the diversity of castaways) and context (the move to on-demand listening) drawn in.
