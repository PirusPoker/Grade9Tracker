## What it is

TLC's ***Waterfalls*** (1995) is the alternative to *Rio* for the **music video from the past**. It is the more serious of the two: a big-budget video that uses **performance, narrative and special effects** to warn about **drug-related violence** and **HIV/AIDS**. It is most likely to appear in **Q4 (10 marks)**, e.g. "How do music videos reflect the time in which they were made? Refer to Rio or Waterfalls" (2024), and it gives you a strong comparison for Q3 on how videos represent social issues.

**The group and the song.**
- **TLC** were an American **R&B/hip-hop** girl group: **Tionne "T-Boz" Watkins**, **Lisa "Left Eye" Lopes** and **Rozonda "Chilli" Thomas**. They fused smooth R&B singing with rap and had a "tomboy", streetwise image, with baggy clothes and outspoken interviews. They were known for promoting **safe sex**, sometimes wearing condoms as accessories.
- *Waterfalls* is from their second album, ***CrazySexyCool*** (1994), released on **LaFace Records** (distributed by **Arista**). It reached **number one in the US** and in many other countries and became their signature song. Many consider it the first number-one hit to address HIV/AIDS in its lyrics.
- The video was directed by **F. Gary Gray** and won **Video of the Year** at the 1995 MTV Video Music Awards.
- Left Eye died in a car accident in 2002; the other two continued as a duo.

### What happens

- The video opens as if **falling from the sky** towards the earth. TLC appear as **figures made of water** rising from the sea, then perform on a surface of water in front of **waterfalls**, lakes and forest. A rushing **flying montage** of natural and city landscapes bookends each section.
- **Narrative 1 (verse 1).** In an inner-city neighbourhood of brownstone houses and street corners, a **mother** watches anxiously from a window as her **teenage son** is drawn into drug dealing to make easy money. She appears as a **ghostly figure** trying to stop him. The deal goes wrong and he is shot. He then appears as a **ghost** his mother cannot see. Drugs are never shown directly: a **brown paper bag** stands in for them. Before the end he makes a "call me" gesture to her, showing he still cares.
- **Narrative 2 (verse 2).** A wealthy young couple in a glamorous bedroom (silk, tangled sheets, large mirrors) decide not to use protection. The man **falls ill**: his skin deteriorates, his face is over-exposed and his eyes glow, and he **does not recognise his reflection**. A **montage of photos** of the woman's previous partners hints at how the virus was passed on. At the end the couple's **picture frames fade to white**, signifying their deaths.
- Performance sections by TLC are **intercut** throughout, so the group keeps returning to comment on the stories.

### Media language

- **Hybrid of all three types**: **performance** (TLC singing and dancing in sync, close-ups, **direct address**, cutting to the rhythm), **narrative** (two cautionary tales illustrating the verses), and **concept** (water spirits, flying montages, CGI).
- **Illustrating the lyrics.** Several images **literally illustrate** the words, such as the mother at the window and the man who doesn't recognise his own reflection, so the message cannot be missed.
- **Special effects and CGI.** The liquid figures were ground-breaking for a music video in 1995 and are an **intertextual** echo of the liquid-metal T-1000 in *Terminator 2* (1991). Ghost effects for mother and son, and horror-style effects for the man's illness, make the video **expressionistic**: showing emotions and fears rather than realistic events. The disease looks almost like a **curse**.
- **Symbolism and connotation.** The **brown paper bag** (drugs), the **fading photos** (death), the **montage of ex-partners** (how HIV spreads) and **water** (purity, nature, the song's warning about chasing waterfalls: wanting more than is safe).
- **Mise-en-scène.** Narrative 1 uses **urban iconography** (brownstones, stoops, street corners, baggy tracksuits, caps and dark glasses) that connote danger and link intertextually to 1990s **"hood" films** such as *Boyz n the Hood* and *New Jack City*. Narrative 2 uses the iconography of **romance** (attractive, semi-clothed actors, silk, mirrors), which turns into **horror**.
- **Colour.** The natural blues and greens of the performance sections connote calm and safety, in contrast with the dark, gritty streets and the harsh, bleached effects of illness.
- **Structure.** Performance sections **frame** the narratives, so TLC act as **narrators or guardian spirits** watching over the stories and delivering the moral.

### Representation

- **TLC.** Represented as almost **supernatural**, wise and moral: guardian figures rising from water. Their image mixes **traditionally feminine** codes (make-up emphasising eyes and lips, colourful cropped tops showing their midriffs) with **masculine or tomboy** codes (loose, combat-style trousers that hide their legs, unlike many videos that focus on women's legs). They are **not objectified** in the usual way; their authority comes from their message. This fits their image of **female solidarity and empowerment**.
- **Young black men.** The son is not a stereotypical gangster: he is **sympathetic**, trying to make money for his family. However, the gang members who kill him reinforce **stereotypes** of violent young black men through props, costume and gesture. You can argue both ways.
- **Women and mothers.** The mother is **wise, loving and powerless**; the woman in narrative 2 is shown as sexually active and careless, which some critics might see as placing blame on women, though both partners are shown as irresponsible.
- **Age.** Young people as **impulsive**, ignoring warnings; parents as protective but unable to save them. This is fairly stereotypical.
- **Social issues.** Handled **sympathetically**, not preachily: TLC wanted to send a message without lecturing. Close-ups of the ill man encourage **empathy rather than condemnation**, which was important at a time when people with HIV faced stigma.

### Industry

- **Label and budget.** LaFace/Arista invested in an expensive, effects-heavy video because **MTV** was still the key route to a mass audience in the mid-1990s; a spectacular video was the main **marketing** tool for the single and the album.
- **Director.** F. Gary Gray went on to direct feature films, showing the overlap between music-video and film industries.
- **Rewards.** Heavy MTV rotation, major awards, and the song's chart success; *CrazySexyCool* became one of the best-selling albums by a girl group.

### Audiences

- **Then:** young R&B and hip-hop fans, and a wide pop audience via MTV. The video carried a **public-health message** to young people at risk.
- **Now:** watched on YouTube; its message still resonates, and it is often cited among the great videos of the 1990s.
- **Uses and gratifications:** *information/education* (warnings about drugs and unsafe sex); *entertainment* (spectacle, special effects, performance); *personal identity* (young women identifying with an outspoken, independent group); *emotional release* through the tragic stories.

### Contexts: how Waterfalls reflects its time

- **HIV/AIDS in the 1990s.** Before effective treatments became widely available in the later 1990s, HIV was usually fatal. High-profile cases (the basketball star Magic Johnson announced he was HIV-positive in 1991) and public campaigns (in the UK, the 1986 "Don't die of ignorance" campaign) put **safe sex** in the news. The video's ending is a clear safe-sex warning.
- **Drug-related violence** in US cities in the late 1980s and 1990s, and media coverage of gangs, influenced narrative 1.
- **Music culture.** The **R&B/hip-hop hybrid** was rising; many hip-hop videos glamorised crime and wealth, and many R&B videos focused on romance. *Waterfalls* uses both sets of conventions but gives them a **cautionary** twist; it sits within **socially conscious** hip-hop.
- **Girl groups and empowerment.** TLC were among the biggest girl groups of the decade, part of a wider 1990s wave of **female solidarity** in pop (compare the Spice Girls).
- **Technology.** Early **CGI** became affordable enough for high-end music videos; *Terminator 2* had just shown what it could do.

::: terms
- **Hybrid music video**: a video combining performance, narrative and concept elements.
- **CGI**: computer-generated imagery, digital special effects such as TLC's liquid water figures.
- **Expressionism**: an artistic style that shows inner emotions and fears in exaggerated, non-realistic ways.
- **Cautionary tale**: a story that warns the audience of the consequences of dangerous behaviour.
- **Symbolism**: using an object or image to stand for a bigger idea, such as the paper bag for drugs.
- **Illustration**: when a video's images show the literal meaning of the lyrics.
- **Socially conscious music**: songs that address real social problems rather than glamorising a lifestyle.
- **Stigma**: strong social disapproval attached to a condition or group, such as people with HIV in the 1990s.
- **R&B**: rhythm and blues, a soulful popular music genre, here blended with hip-hop.
:::

How do music videos reflect the time in which they were made? Refer to Waterfalls (TLC, 1995) to support your points. (10)

Waterfalls reflects the mid-1990s HIV/AIDS crisis, when the virus was usually fatal and safe-sex campaigns were prominent (1). Its second narrative shows a couple choosing not to use protection; the man's skin deteriorates and his eyes glow, and at the end their photos fade to white, a clear warning to young viewers to practise safe sex (1). The montage of the woman's previous partners shows how the virus spreads without naming it, handling a stigmatised subject with symbolism (1), and close-ups of the ill man invite empathy rather than blame, which mattered when people with HIV faced prejudice (1). The first narrative reflects the drug-related violence in US cities that was widely covered in the news: a son drawn into dealing is shot despite his mother's efforts (1). Its brownstones, street corners and baggy clothing echo early-1990s "hood" films such as Boyz n the Hood (1). The group itself reflects the rise of the R&B/hip-hop hybrid and of confident girl groups: TLC mix feminine make-up with baggy, tomboy trousers and act as moral guardians rather than objects (1). The liquid, CGI versions of TLC echo Terminator 2's T-1000 and show how early digital effects were entering big-budget music videos (1). Its budget, awards and success reflect MTV's dominance as the route to a mass audience in 1995 (1). Unlike many hip-hop videos of the time, which glamorised crime, Waterfalls is cautionary, placing it within socially conscious music of the decade (1).
