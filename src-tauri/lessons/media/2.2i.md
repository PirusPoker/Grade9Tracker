## What it is

Lizzo's ***Good as Hell*** video (2019) is one of the two options for your **first** contemporary music video (the other is Taylor Swift's *The Man*). It is studied for **all five areas**, together with Lizzo's official website, **lizzomusic.com**, and her social media. In the exam it appears in **Q3 (20 marks)**, paired with your second video (*Superheroes* or *Intentions*), and her website can be the subject of **Q4 (10 marks)**. The song contains mild expletives; the spec's focus is the **visuals**, so you do not need to quote the lyrics.

**Background.**
- The song was written by Lizzo with producer Ricky Reed and first released in **2016** on the *Coconut Oil* EP through **Atlantic Records** (part of Warner Music Group). An earlier video showed Lizzo and friends at a salon and tied in with the film *Barbershop: The Next Cut*.
- It became a **sleeper hit**: after Lizzo's breakthrough in 2019 (the album *Cuz I Love You*, her performance at the MTV Video Music Awards) the song re-entered the charts and reached the US top three and the UK top ten.
- The set video, the **second official video**, premiered on YouTube in **December 2019**. It was directed by **Alan Ferguson** and filmed at **Southern University** in Baton Rouge, Louisiana, a **historically Black college and university (HBCU)**, featuring its marching band (the "Human Jukebox") and dance team (the "Fabulous Dancing Dolls"). Lizzo played flute and piccolo in a marching band herself.

### What happens

Three young women are introduced **before Lizzo appears**, each with a problem to overcome:
- **Nicole**, who would rather be playing music, is told off in a chemistry class;
- a student whose boyfriend has let her down (he appears on her phone screen and later in the crowd);
- a **drummer** who feels shut out by the boys' camaraderie in the percussion section.

Other students struggle too, such as a saxophonist making mistakes while a male teacher shakes his head, and are helped by female friends. Lizzo appears conducting practice in sports kit, riding a custom **"Lizzo" bus** in her own version of the band uniform, and dancing with the Dolls. The video builds to the **homecoming football game**, where the band performs in full blue-and-gold uniform. It closes with a montage of the young women **smiling in close-up**, an on-screen **thank you**, and an "out-take" panning shot with **diegetic** cheering after practice.

### Media language

- **Hybrid form.** Mainly **performance** (Lizzo singing and dancing; the band playing and marching), with **three narrative strands** woven in. It uses conventions, then varies them.
- **Narrative (Propp).** Each young woman is a **hero** on a journey from doubt to confidence; other women and Lizzo are **helpers/donors**; the cheating boyfriend and the dismissive male teacher act as **villains** or obstacles. **Todorov**: disruption (failure, heartbreak, exclusion) → resolution (joyful performance at homecoming).
- **Colour palette.** It starts with **navy practice tracksuits** and becomes richer until the bright **blue and gold** of homecoming, so the look of the video grows in confidence as the characters do. **Gold** (Lizzo's costume, the Dolls, the band) connotes **glamour, success and celebration**.
- **Costume.** Lizzo moves between the conductor's sports kit, her own band uniform and a dance costume. She wears what the students wear, signifying **unity and support**, while her gold outfit marks her **star persona**.
- **Camerawork.** Lizzo is often **centred** and addresses the camera directly, building intimacy with a mainly female audience; **wide shots** of the band in formation show **teamwork**; **close-ups** of the young women track their emotions.
- **Editing and sound.** Cuts to the beat of the drums and brass; a triumphant, celebratory rhythm; **diegetic sound** at the end breaks the convention of a video ending with the song, adding realism and inclusion.
- **Iconography.** Brass instruments, drums, the stadium and the bus: the iconography of **music rather than sport**. Lizzo focuses on the band and the dancers, not the football players.
- **Documentary touches.** The on-screen thank you and the out-take ending give a sense of authenticity and gratitude to a real community.

### Representation

- **Lizzo herself: body positivity.** A **plus-size black woman** presented as **confident, glamorous and in charge**. This challenges the music-video convention of slim, conventionally attractive women, and makes her a **role model** for audiences who have experienced body-shaming. It is **self-representation** that matches her public persona.
- **Young women.** Shown as supportive of each other, talented and determined. That is stereotypical in one sense (women as nurturing) but positive. They are **not objectified** or shot for a **male gaze**; their value comes from skill and confidence.
- **Feminist vs post-feminist readings.** A feminist critic might argue that the Dolls' costumes and the song's references to hair and nails uphold **beauty ideals**. A **post-feminist** reading says these are tools of **self-confidence**, chosen for women's own pleasure rather than to please men.
- **Men.** Mostly marginal or negative: the neglectful boyfriend, the male teacher shaking his head. But group shots of men and women playing together show **harmony through a shared goal**. A male viewer might produce an **oppositional reading** that men are misrepresented.
- **Ethnicity.** An **all-black cast** at an HBCU represents young black Americans as **educated, talented, disciplined and successful**, countering stereotypes of crime or failure and the **under-representation** of black academic achievement.
- **Age.** Young people as capable and ambitious, supported by a slightly older role model.

### Industry

- **Label**: Atlantic Records (Warner). The **re-release** strategy shows how labels revive a song when an artist's profile rises.
- **Convergence and star-building**: TV performances (the MTV VMAs, the BBC Radio 1 Live Lounge, US talk shows), **magazine covers** (including *Vogue*), a reality competition series on Amazon (*Watch Out for the Big Grrrls*, which won an Emmy in 2022), songs used in films and adverts (**synchronisation**), and her shapewear brand **Yitty** (launched 2022). Each platform promotes the others.
- **lizzomusic.com**: a **home page** dominated by the current release and a styled image of Lizzo (bold colours, confident poses matching the album era); links to stream or buy on multiple platforms; video links to her YouTube channel; tour dates and tickets; a **store** selling merchandise; links to social media. Keep your own dated notes, since the site changes.
- **Social media**: **TikTok** (a huge following; personal, funny and vulnerable videos, especially during the 2020 lockdown) and **Instagram**. She posts about body image, racism, LGBTQ+ Pride and politics. Fans' dance and lip-sync trends helped make her songs hits.

### Audiences

- **Target**: mainly **young women** (her largest group is roughly 18–24), plus LGBTQ+ fans and anyone who identifies with messages of self-love.
- **Uses and gratifications**: *entertainment* (spectacle, dance, feel-good energy); *personal identity* (seeing a body-confident role model; identifying with the three heroes); *social interaction* (sharing, TikTok trends); *escapism*.
- **Reception**: the **preferred reading** is empowerment and joy; a **negotiated** reading might admire the message but question the beauty ideals; an **oppositional** reading might reject the negative portrayal of men.
- Lizzo dedicated her 2022 Emmy to "the big grrrls", naming the audience that identifies with her.

### Contexts

- **Social**: the **body-positivity** movement and online debates about **body-shaming**; **female empowerment** and feminism in late-2010s pop; **Black empowerment** and the cultural importance of **HBCUs** (Southern University was founded in 1880).
- **Cultural**: the HBCU **marching band and homecoming** tradition, which Lizzo knew personally; homecoming as a moment of community pride.
- **Industry**: the streaming era, in which a three-year-old song could become a hit through social media and a TV performance.

::: terms
- **Body positivity**: a movement promoting acceptance of all body shapes and sizes and challenging narrow beauty ideals.
- **HBCU**: a historically Black college or university in the US, founded to educate Black students.
- **Sleeper hit**: a song that sells modestly at first but becomes a major hit later.
- **Colour palette**: the range of colours chosen for a product, which carries connotations and mood.
- **Star persona**: the public image of an artist, constructed through videos, styling, interviews and social media.
- **Post-feminism**: a view that women can choose traditionally feminine things, like make-up, as empowering rather than oppressive.
- **Preferred reading**: the interpretation of a product that its producers intend the audience to take.
- **Oppositional reading**: an interpretation that rejects the producers' intended message.
- **Self-representation**: a person or group constructing their own image in the media.
- **Homecoming**: a US college tradition in which former students return for a football game and celebrations.
:::

::: example Media language: colour and costume
Explore how colour and costume create meaning in Good as Hell. (8)

The video starts with students in plain navy practice tracksuits, which connotes routine, effort and the young women's lack of confidence (2). As the narrative moves towards homecoming the palette becomes brighter until the band performs in blue and gold, so the look of the video grows in confidence alongside the characters (2). Gold appears on Lizzo's costume, the Dolls and the band, connoting success, glamour and celebration, and linking the star with the students (2). Lizzo wears versions of the students' outfits (conductor's kit, band uniform, dance costume), signifying that she is one of them and supports them, a role model rather than a distant star (2).
:::

::: example Representation of women
How far does Good as Hell challenge stereotypical representations of women? (10)

It strongly challenges the music-video convention of objectifying women for a male gaze. The camera focuses on the young women's talent and emotions, not their bodies, and Lizzo, a plus-size black woman, is framed as a confident, glamorous star (3). This reflects the late-2010s body-positivity movement and offers identification to young women who do not see themselves in mainstream beauty images (2). The three heroes overcome failure, heartbreak and exclusion through skill and friendship, representing women as capable and determined (2). However, the idea of women as emotionally supportive of each other is itself a gender stereotype, although a positive one, and a feminist reading might say that the Dolls' costumes and focus on appearance still uphold beauty ideals (2). Overall, the video largely challenges stereotypes, because its message is that confidence comes from within and from other women rather than from male approval (1).
:::
