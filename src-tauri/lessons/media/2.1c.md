## What it is

For exams from 2028, Component 1 Section A sets two newspaper front pages: **The Guardian (6 May 2025)** and **The Sun (22 March 2025)**. They show two very different kinds of British newspaper, a liberal "quality" paper and a right-leaning "red top" tabloid, making choices about the news for different audiences. They are tested on **media language**, **representation** and **contexts**: Question 1 (15 marks, often three parts of 5: layout and design, images, written language); Question 2(a) (5 marks, often political context); Question 2(b) (25 marks, comparing a set front page with an **unseen** front page, e.g. on gender, age or the representation of an event). The Sun is also studied in Section B for industry and audiences; that is a separate topic.

### Front page conventions

| Convention | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Masthead** | The paper's name in its fixed design: instant brand recognition |
| **Skyline / puff** | A strip above or beside the masthead promoting other content, supplements or offers |
| **Headline** | The lead story's title: informs, intrigues, reveals the paper's angle |
| **Strapline / sub-headings** | Extra lines expanding the headline |
| **Dominant image** and **caption** | Draws the eye; the caption anchors it |
| **Byline** | The journalist's name: credibility, a sense of "hard news" |
| **Body copy** | The opening of the story, continued inside |
| **Off-leads** | Secondary stories, often with smaller images |
| **Price, date, web address, social media** | Practical information and convergence with digital platforms |

**Quality vs tabloid conventions.** Quality papers (historically broadsheet in size) favour hard news, politics and economics, formal language, several stories, and restrained design. **Red tops** (tabloids with red mastheads, like The Sun) favour one dramatic lead, huge headlines with puns, emotive and colloquial language, celebrity and sport, bold colour and giveaways. The Guardian now prints in a compact tabloid-size format for cost reasons, but it keeps quality conventions; "tabloid" in Media Studies means a style of journalism as well as a size.

**News values** explain why stories are chosen: timeliness, proximity (closeness to the audience), scale, negativity, elite people and nations, celebrity, surprise, human interest, and fit with the paper's agenda and audience.

### The Guardian (6 May 2025): media language

- **Dominant image**: a wide shot of the Prince and Princess of Wales with their three children on the Buckingham Palace balcony during the VE Day 80th anniversary commemorations, all leaning to watch the military flypast. Prince William wears RAF uniform; the Princess wears a purple coat and hat. Formal dress signals tradition and respect for the occasion; the caption explains the moment. The choice of an unposed, off-guard moment, rather than a formal pose, suggests a relaxed, relatable view of the monarchy and national unity.
- **Headline**: the lead is an **exclusive** reporting that No 10 is set to rethink the winter fuel payment cut after local-election losses alarmed Labour MPs. This is classic quality-paper hard news, combining two issues that matter to its audience. "Exclusive" suggests privileged access and credibility. "No 10" is a compact shorthand for the government. "Rethink" is a soft, measured verb (compare "U-turn"), fitting a paper broadly sympathetic to a centre-left government; "alarm" still adds drama.
- **Off-leads**: President Trump's threatened tariffs on foreign-made films (a risk to the UK film industry and jobs); the actor Blake Lively, in a head shot with a black blazer and direct gaze, and her legal dispute, described with a "lightning rod" metaphor; the footballer Trent Alexander-Arnold leaving Liverpool for Real Madrid, in his Liverpool kit; and Macmillan Cancer Support cutting local welfare services, with the verb "axe" siding with the communities who lose out.
- **Layout and house style**: a structured grid with several stories, a distinctive deep sapphire blue in its branding, consistent across print, web and app, the paper's own serif titling font for the masthead and headlines, a highly legible body font, red used for news labels and yellow used sparingly for emphasis. The **skyline** promotes the G2 features section and Sport, lighter content for browsers at the newsstand.

### The Guardian: representation and context

- **Gender**: women appear in positions of professional power: the lead story's byline names two female political journalists; Blake Lively is presented as a confident Hollywood professional under attack rather than as a victim; the Princess of Wales is shown in both public and maternal roles. Men appear in power too (the Prime Minister, the Mayor of London and the US President feature in stories, suggesting political life is still male-dominated), and in uniforms of profession (RAF, Liverpool FC). Prince William is shown equally as royal and as father. Relative gender balance fits the paper's liberal, feminist values.
- **Age**: a spread from the royal children (born in the 2010s) to Millennials and Gen Z, with VE Day as a moment of **intergenerational remembrance**; the children on the balcony echo the first VE Day, when the young future Queen Elizabeth II appeared there, representing continuity and the monarchy's future. Pensioners are implicitly represented through the winter fuel story, as a group harmed by policy.
- **Political context**: owned by the **Scott Trust**, which reinvests profits in journalism rather than paying shareholders; liberal-left, independent of any party. In 2025 Labour was in government, had cut the winter fuel payment for most pensioners in 2024, and had lost many council seats in the May 2025 local elections. The paper reports pressure on the government seriously but without mockery.
- **Audience**: overwhelmingly ABC1, educated, socially conscious, liberal, with a large global online readership; psychographically "reformers".

### The Sun (22 March 2025): media language

- **Headline**: a pun, "Faulty Powers", referring to the 1970s sitcom Fawlty Towers (celebrating its 50th anniversary in 2025) about an inept, bad-tempered hotelier. Some letters are deliberately wonky, echoing the sitcom's famously broken hotel sign and the chaos of the event. The **intertextual** pun implies incompetence: in the national grid and, by implication, in the Labour government's management of infrastructure.
- **Story**: a fire at an electrical substation in Hayes that closed Heathrow, Europe's busiest airport, for about 16 hours and disrupted hundreds of thousands of passengers.
- **Dominant image**: a tightly cropped, dramatic night-time photograph of the fire, filling most of the page; reds, oranges and yellows connote danger and destruction. A cropped inset of an information board announcing Heathrow closed adds proximity and a sense that reporters were on the spot.
- **Sub-headings**: bullet-point lines above the image summarise the story and work as **enigma codes**: how the fire started, how Britain was humiliated, why the back-up failed.
- **Body copy** begins on the right-hand third with a byline, signalling hard news; the language is emotive and colloquial (words like "fiasco", "mayhem", "blaze"), typical of tabloids and suited to a low reading age.
- **Masthead**: bold white block capitals on red: the **red top**, associated with sensational, celebrity-led, simplified journalism.
- **Off-leads**: the TV presenter and former soap actor Kym Marsh in a red, floor-length strapless gown, with a line about the "Fab at 50" club (soft news, glamour, broadening appeal; the dress may recall the cartoon screen siren Jessica Rabbit); England footballers Myles Lewis-Skelly and Harry Kane celebrating an England 2 Albania 0 win, with the pun "Myles Better".
- **Promotion**: a theme-park offer (two Alton Towers tickets "on us") placed in the masthead area with the park's logo: **synergy** between brands, and an incentive to buy the print edition rather than read free online.

### The Sun: representation and context

- **Gender**: largely stereotypical. Kym Marsh is represented through glamour and appearance: an archive red-carpet image from years earlier, suggesting women are valued as objects of visual pleasure (the **male gaze**). The footballers are dynamic, successful and strong, traditional masculinity for a mostly male readership. Both are presented as stars through first-name familiarity and puns.
- **Age**: the "Fab at 50" line seems to celebrate midlife women, reflecting a wider media reappraisal of women over 50; but using an image from when she was much younger implies that looking 50 is undesirable, reinforcing **ageless beauty** ideals. The 18-year-old debut scorer and the veteran captain are represented alike: sport flattens age into achievement.
- **Event**: the fire is represented as a national and even global disaster through hyperbole and emotive language; The Sun positions itself as exposing failure and national humiliation, a **populist** framing that turns a complex infrastructure failure into an emotional drama.
- **Political context**: owned by **News UK**, part of Rupert Murdoch's **News Corp**; right-leaning and populist; critical of the Labour government. Big infrastructure is political because government builds or regulates it.
- **Audience**: mass-market, mainly **C2DE**, majority male, print readers mostly middle-aged and older; online readers much younger.

::: terms
- **Masthead**: the newspaper's title at the top of the front page, in a fixed design that brands the paper.
- **Red top**: a tabloid newspaper with a red masthead, associated with sensational, celebrity-led, simplified journalism.
- **Broadsheet**: historically a large-format quality paper; in Media Studies it names a serious, formal style of journalism.
- **Skyline**: a strip at the top of a front page promoting other content, sections or offers.
- **Off-lead**: a secondary story on the front page, placed below or beside the lead.
- **Byline**: the line naming the journalist who wrote a story, adding credibility.
- **News values**: the criteria editors use to decide which stories are newsworthy, such as timeliness and proximity.
- **Hard news**: serious, timely news about politics, the economy and major events.
- **Soft news**: lighter human-interest, celebrity and lifestyle stories.
- **Emotive language**: words chosen to provoke strong feelings such as outrage or sympathy.
- **Scott Trust**: the not-for-profit trust that owns The Guardian and reinvests profits in its journalism.
- **Populism**: presenting issues as the ordinary people against an incompetent or out-of-touch elite.
:::

::: method
1. **Question 1**: for each part stay on the aspect: *layout and design* (placement, grid, size, colour, typography, skyline); *images* (shot, framing, composition, colour, expression, caption); *written language* (headline wording, puns, verbs, emotive vs formal register, sub-headings, bylines).
2. For each point: element → connotation → purpose, linked to the paper's audience and ideology (house style, political stance).
3. **Question 2(a)**: link the political (or historical, social) context to specific choices: ownership → stance → wording or story choice.
4. **Question 2(b)**: compare the set front page and the unseen in every paragraph: selection of stories and images; construction through language and image; mediation by the paper's values. Judge how far similar and different.
5. **Use off-leads and puffs as evidence**: they reveal audience and values as much as the lead.
:::

::: example Written language (5)
Explore how The Sun (22 March 2025) front page uses written language to communicate meanings. (5)

The headline "Faulty Powers" is a pun on the sitcom Fawlty Towers, an intertextual reference that older readers will know, which borrows the sitcom's comedy of incompetence to mock the failure of Heathrow's power supply (1). The wonky letters make the words look broken, visually echoing the chaos (1). The bullet-point sub-headings summarise the story quickly and act as enigma codes, hinting at how the fire started and why the back-up failed, so readers must buy the paper for answers (1). Emotive, colloquial words in the body copy such as "fiasco" and "mayhem" provoke outrage and suit a paper written for a low reading age (1). The claim that Britain was humiliated turns a technical failure into a patriotic grievance, reflecting The Sun's populist, right-leaning viewpoint and its opposition to the Labour government (1).
:::

::: example Images (5)
Explore how The Guardian (6 May 2025) front page uses images to communicate meanings. (5)

The dominant image is a wide shot of the Prince and Princess of Wales and their children on the palace balcony, placing the VE Day anniversary at the heart of the page as a moment of national unity (1). William's RAF uniform and Catherine's formal purple coat connote tradition, duty and royalty, respecting the solemnity of the occasion (1). The family are all leaning the same way, watching the flypast, and the caption anchors this, so the photo feels spontaneous and relatable rather than stiffly posed, suiting The Guardian's relaxed, not deferential, attitude to the monarchy (1). The children on the balcony represent the monarchy's future and link generations, echoing the young future Queen at the 1945 celebrations (1). The smaller head shot of Blake Lively in a black blazer, looking directly at the camera, connotes professionalism and confidence, so she reads as strong rather than as a victim (1).
:::

::: example Political context (5)
Explain how political contexts influence newspapers. Refer to The Guardian (6 May 2025) to support your points. (5)

The Guardian is owned by the Scott Trust, which reinvests its profits in journalism rather than paying shareholders, so it can keep an independent, liberal-left stance (1). In May 2025 Labour was in government, having restricted the winter fuel payment for most pensioners and then lost many council seats in the local elections (1). The lead exclusive reports that No 10 will rethink the cut, a hard-news political story chosen because it matters to the paper's politically engaged ABC1 readers (1). The verb "rethink" is gentler than the "U-turn" a hostile paper might choose, reflecting shared values with a centre-left government, although "alarm" shows it is still willing to report political pressure (1). Off-leads on US film tariffs and a charity axing services show concern for jobs and vulnerable people, typical of its liberal agenda (1).
:::

::: example Comparison paragraph with an unseen front page
The unseen front page (invented for practice):

> **The Daily Courier**, a mid-market paper. Masthead in navy serif capitals. Lead story: a teenage climate activist addressing a crowd in the rain, in a mid-shot, fist raised. Headline: "Our future, our fight". Off-lead: a photo of three smiling women in their sixties in wetsuits on a beach, with the line "Never too old to take the plunge". A skyline promotes a free gardening guide.

Compare the representation of age in The Sun (22 March 2025) and The Daily Courier. Write one developed paragraph. (5)

Both front pages select a woman in midlife or later as soft news, suggesting both papers want to appeal to older readers (1). However, The Sun represents a woman near 50 through a glamorous archive image from years earlier, implying that looking older is undesirable, whereas the Courier shows women in their sixties as they are, active and joyful in wetsuits, a countertype to stereotypes of older women as frail or invisible (1). The Courier's lead represents youth as politically engaged and powerful through a raised fist and the collective "our", while The Sun represents youth only through an 18-year-old footballer, whose age is flattened into sporting success (1). So the Courier constructs age as a source of energy at both ends of life, while The Sun links worth to appearance for women and performance for men, reflecting its tabloid conventions (1). Overall the representations of age are more different than similar, though both papers use celebration of midlife to attract older readers who buy print (1).
:::
