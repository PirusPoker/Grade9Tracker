## What it is

Component 2, **Section B (30 marks)** is about **music**: two contemporary music videos, one music video from the past, and the artists' websites and social media. This lesson covers the common ground that every option route needs: what a music video is, its **types and conventions**, and how the **music industry** uses videos to make money and reach audiences. ### What a music video is for

A music video is two things at once:
1. **A media product in its own right**, watched for pleasure, shared and streamed (YouTube views count towards chart positions and earn advertising income).
2. **A marketing tool** that promotes the single, the album, the tour and the artist's **star image**. The spec stresses that the music industry is **interdependent** with other media: videos premiere on YouTube, are trailed on social media and are promoted on TV, in magazines and on websites.

### Types of music video

| Type | What it is | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Performance** | The artist or band sings and plays, on a stage, in a studio or on location | Cheaper and quicker; feels **authentic**; showcases the star | Can be repetitive; relies on the artist already being popular |
| **Narrative** | Tells a story, often illustrating or amplifying the lyrics, with characters (sometimes played by the artist) | Engages viewers emotionally; room to address issues | More expensive and time-consuming |
| **Concept** | Built around an idea, mood, or striking visual style rather than a clear story | Creative, memorable, can become iconic | Costly; risk of confusing the audience |

Most videos are **hybrids**. *Waterfalls* blends performance, narrative and concept; *Good as Hell* is mainly performance with three narrative strands; *The Man* and *Superheroes* are almost pure narrative with **no performance of the song**, which is itself a variation of the form.

### Conventions of the form

- **The star at the centre**: frequent close-ups of the artist, centred framing, the artist introduced early, and costume and styling that build the **star persona**.
- **Lip-sync and direct address**: the artist sings to the camera, building intimacy with the viewer.
- **Editing to the beat**: cuts timed to the rhythm; fast montage; slow motion for emphasis.
- **Links between visuals and lyrics**: images that **illustrate** the words (literally), **amplify** them (add meaning), or **disjuncture** (deliberately contrast with them).
- **Genre codes of the music**: hip hop and R&B iconography (urban settings, dance crews, cars, jewellery), pop glamour, rock live performance.
- **Intertextuality**: references to films, other videos, celebrities and the artist's own earlier work.
- **Representation conventions**: historically, female artists and dancers are often **objectified** for a **male gaze**; many contemporary videos work against this.
- **Motifs** repeated across a video or across an artist's career (colours, symbols, dance moves).

The mark schemes credit **Neale's repetition and variation**, **Propp's character roles**, **Todorov's narrative**, and **Goodwin's ideas about music video** (star image, relationships between visuals and lyrics, intertextuality, voyeurism). You do not need a theory to reach the top band, but naming one accurately helps.

### A short history

- **1981:** **MTV** launched in the US, a channel playing videos 24 hours a day. Videos became essential marketing, and big budgets, film directors and exotic locations followed. *Rio* (1982) is a textbook example.
- **1990s:** videos became mini-films with special effects and serious themes; MTV was the main route to audiences. *Waterfalls* (1995) used early CGI and social-issue narratives.
- **2005 onwards:** **YouTube** made videos free and on demand worldwide. **VEVO**, a platform backed by major labels, channelled official videos and advertising revenue. Views are now counted by charts.
- **2010s–2020s:** streaming (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music) dominates; artists premiere videos online, tease them on **Instagram and TikTok**, and fans react, remix and share them instantly.

### The music industry

- **Record labels.** Three **major labels** dominate: **Universal Music Group**, **Sony Music** and **Warner Music Group**. They own many smaller **imprints**. The set artists illustrate this: Lizzo on **Atlantic** (Warner); Swift on **Republic** (Universal); Bieber on **Def Jam** (Universal); Stormzy began independently, then released *Heavy Is the Head* through **Atlantic**, with his own **#Merky** brand; Duran Duran on **EMI**; TLC on **LaFace/Arista**.
- **Independent vs major.** Independents give artists more control; majors offer money, marketing and global distribution. Stars increasingly want **control over their own work**: Taylor Swift re-recorded her early albums after losing control of the master recordings.
- **How artists and labels make money**: streaming royalties (tiny per stream, so volume matters); **YouTube advertising** on video views; downloads and physical sales (vinyl has revived, often in collectible variants); **live touring** (now the biggest earner for most stars); **merchandise**; **brand deals and sponsorship**; **synchronisation** (songs licensed for films, adverts, games). Many modern contracts are "360" deals, in which the label shares income from all these streams.
- **Convergence and synergy.** One release is pushed across platforms at once: video on YouTube, song on streaming, clips on TikTok, interviews on TV and podcasts, merchandise on the website, tour on sale. Each promotes the other.
- **Global reach.** Online distribution lets a video reach audiences everywhere on the day of release. English-language pop dominates, but the market is global.
- **Regulation.** Videos on TV are regulated (Ofcom in the UK; the watershed; edited "clean" versions), but online platforms are harder to regulate. The BBFC has age-rated some online music videos in the UK, but most rely on the platform's own community guidelines and age gates, which is a challenge for regulators.

### Audiences

- Videos target audiences through the artist's **star image**, genre, and platform: TikTok for younger fans, YouTube for the widest reach.
- **Uses and gratifications**: *entertainment* (spectacle, dance); *personal identity* (fans identify with the star or with characters in the narrative, as in body-positive or empowering videos); *social interaction* (sharing, reaction videos, fan theories about hidden references); *information* (videos about issues).
- **Active audiences** interpret videos differently: a feminist, a fan and a critic may read the same video very differently depending on their experiences.

### Contexts

Videos reflect their time. *Rio* reflects early-MTV glamour and 1980s consumerism; *Waterfalls* reflects the mid-1990s HIV/AIDS crisis and violence linked to drugs; the 2019–2020 videos reflect **body positivity**, **#MeToo** and feminism, **Black Lives Matter** and **social responsibility** by celebrities.

::: terms
- **Performance video**: a music video showing the artist singing and playing, on stage, in a studio or on location.
- **Narrative video**: a music video that tells a story with characters, often linked to the lyrics.
- **Concept video**: a music video built around an idea, mood or visual style rather than a clear story.
- **Star persona**: the public image of an artist, constructed through videos, styling, interviews and social media.
- **Direct address**: the artist looking and singing into the camera, speaking straight to the viewer.
- **Intertextuality**: a reference within one media product to another media product or cultural text.
- **Major label**: one of the three largest record companies: Universal, Sony and Warner.
- **Synergy**: two or more media products or companies promoting each other so the combined effect is greater.
- **Streaming**: listening to or watching media online without downloading a permanent copy.
- **Male gaze**: a feminist term for media that positions the viewer as a heterosexual man looking at women as objects.
- **Synchronisation**: licensing a song for use in a film, advert, TV programme or game.
:::

::: method
**Q3 (20 marks):**
1. **Decode the question**: is it media language (conventions, narrative, camerawork) or representation (gender, ethnicity, issues)? What judgement does "how far" require?
2. **Plan a line of argument** with a clear answer ("largely challenges…, although…").
3. Write about **both videos**, ideally alternating or comparing. **One video caps you at half marks.**
4. For each point: **specific moment → technical term → meaning → context → judgement**.
5. Include **both sides** (conventional and unconventional; stereotypical and challenging).
6. **Conclude** by answering the question directly, with a reason.

**Q4 (10 marks):** four or five developed points, each tied to a precise feature of the named website or the past video, and to industry, audience or context knowledge.

::: example How far do music videos challenge conventions?
How far do these two music videos challenge the conventions of music videos? (Using Good as Hell and Superheroes.) (20)

Both videos use some familiar conventions, but Superheroes challenges them far more than Good as Hell. Good as Hell is largely a performance video: Lizzo sings in direct address, often centred in the frame, and her gold costume constructs the glamorous star persona typical of pop (3). The marching band's formation shots are cut to the rhythm, another conventional feature (2). It varies the form by introducing three young women before Lizzo appears and giving each a narrative strand: a student in trouble in class, one after a break-up, and one excluded by the drummers. They act as Proppian heroes helped by other women and by Lizzo (3). Its most unconventional aspect is representation: a plus-size black woman as a confident star and an all-black university cast, rather than slim dancers shot for a male gaze, which reflects the 2019 body-positivity movement (3). Superheroes breaks the most basic convention: Stormzy does not perform the song at all. It is a 2D animated narrative in which he guides a boy on a hero's journey (3). Animation allows fantastical imagery, such as flying, lightning and telekinesis, and intertextual nods to Toy Story and The Matrix, which live action on a limited budget could not (2). Its purpose also differs, since it premiered in schools in September 2020 to inspire young black pupils, linked to Black Lives Matter (2). In conclusion, Good as Hell mainly works within the conventions and challenges representation, while Superheroes challenges the form itself (2).

This would sit in the top band (17–20): both videos, specific examples, terminology, contexts and a sustained judgement.
:::

::: example Music videos as marketing
Explain how music videos are used to promote artists. (6)

A video premiered on YouTube earns advertising income and views that count towards charts, while promoting the single (2). It builds the artist's star persona, as in Lizzo's gold costume or Swift's reveal as director, which keeps fans loyal across albums, tours and merchandise (2). Clips and teasers are cut for Instagram and TikTok before release, creating anticipation and encouraging fans to share, so the video promotes itself through the audience (2).
:::

::: example Industry: how labels make money
Briefly explain two ways the music industry makes money from an artist beyond record sales. (4)

Live touring: tickets for large tours bring in far more than streaming, and the label may take a share through a 360 deal (2). Merchandise sold through the artist's website and at concerts, such as clothing and collectible vinyl, turns fan loyalty into income (2).
:::
