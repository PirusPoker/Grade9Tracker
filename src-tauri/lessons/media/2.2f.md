## What it is

*Friends*, Season 1, Episode 1 (the 1994 pilot), is the **past** sitcom product for the sitcom option. Your school chooses a **ten-minute extract**, and you study it **alongside** *Man Like Mobeen* or *Modern Family* to show **how the genre has changed over time**. It is never the exam extract in Q1; it appears in **Q2 (10 marks)**, for example "How do sitcoms reflect their historical contexts? Refer to Friends", or in questions about how audiences' viewing has changed.

**The product.** Created by **David Crane and Marta Kauffman**, produced by **Warner Bros.**, and first broadcast on **NBC** in the US in September 1994. In the UK it was shown first on **Channel 4** (from 1995), later on **E4** and other channels, and now streams. It ran for **ten seasons** and became one of the most popular and profitable TV shows ever. The pilot introduces six friends in their twenties living in Manhattan.

### What happens in the pilot

- The friends are drinking coffee at **Central Perk** (the equilibrium).
- **Rachel** bursts in wearing a **wedding dress**, having left her fiancé at the altar. She has never had a job, has lived on her father's money, and in a phone call tells her father she will not go back. Monica takes her in.
- **Ross** is miserable because his marriage has ended; his ex-wife has left him for a woman.
- **Monica** goes on a date with Paul, "the wine guy", who lies to get her into bed; she gets her own back by stamping on his watch.
- The men fail comically to assemble furniture; Joey tries to chat Rachel up on her wedding day.
- Rachel cuts up her credit cards as a first step to independence and gets a job at the café; Ross asks whether he might ask her out some time.

### Media language: a typical sitcom

The spec describes *Friends* as using **sitcom conventions in a typical way**. That makes it the benchmark against which your contemporary episode's variations show up.

| Convention | In the Friends pilot |
|---|---|
| **Limited, familiar settings** | Central Perk and Monica's apartment (plus Joey and Chandler's across the hall). They became iconic, part of the brand. Monica's warm, cluttered flat versus the men's sparse one tells us about the characters |
| **Studio, multi-camera production** | Shot on sets in front of a **live studio audience**, several cameras recording at once; consistent medium shots of the group on the sofa |
| **Laughter track** | The audience's laughter is heard; actors pause for it; it tells viewers at home when to laugh |
| **Ensemble cast of types** | Joey the womaniser and joker; Chandler the sarcastic one; Ross the sensitive, serious one; Monica the organised, maternal one; Phoebe the eccentric free spirit; Rachel the spoilt girl learning independence |
| **Circular narrative (Todorov)** | Coffee-shop equilibrium → disruption (Rachel and Ross arrive upset) → some new equilibrium (Rachel's job, Ross's hope). As a pilot it also sets up long arcs, such as Ross and Rachel |
| **Verbal and visual humour** | One-liners, comic timing, running jokes; Rachel's dramatic gestures; Phoebe miming clearing Ross's aura; the furniture scene |
| **Iconography and costume** | Rachel's wedding dress (innocence, a life she is escaping); Joey's leather jacket (an intertextual nod to James Dean's rebel cool); Phoebe's bunches, earrings and frayed denim (free spirit) |
| **Sound** | The theme song (by the Rembrandts) became central to the brand and sums up the idea of friends as family; music bridges between scenes |
| **Establishing shots** | Exteriors of the café and apartment block as quick narrative shortcuts |

**Genre development.** The "single-com" about young adults living as a **pseudo-family**, rather than a real family or workplace, was relatively fresh in 1994 and suited its audience.

### Representation

- **Age: Generation X "twixters".** The friends sit between adolescence and adulthood: jobs, dating and friendship matter, while bills and children are far away. The show constructs a carefree, idealised version of young adult life in New York (Monica's large flat on a modest salary requires a suspension of disbelief).
- **Masculinity.** Ross is the emotional **"new man"**, openly hurt and close to his sister, which contrasted with older sitcom men. Joey is the confident alpha male who treats women as conquests, while Chandler hides insecurity behind sarcasm. None of the men can build the furniture, a gentle dig at masculine competence.
- **Femininity.** Mixed. Rachel starts as naive, spoilt and dependent, but her refusal to marry a man she does not love and her decision to earn her own living show **independence**, even if she hesitates over the credit cards. Monica is independent and matriarchal but is fooled by Paul, then asserts control. Phoebe is independent and childlike. The show both **reinforces and challenges** stereotypes, reflecting 1990s gender debates.
- **Sexuality.** Ross's ex-wife leaving him for a woman is part of the plot, but it is mainly a source of jokes at Ross's expense. The series later showed a same-sex wedding, yet LGBTQ+ characters were often punchlines, reflecting mainstream attitudes of the time.
- **Ethnicity.** All six leads are **white and middle-class** in one of the world's most diverse cities. The spec calls this a **conservative** representation of US diversity: minority groups are **under-represented**, which was typical of mainstream US TV at the time. Use this as a context point, not just a criticism.

### Industry

- **Commercial US network TV.** NBC is funded by **advertising**; a hit sitcom commands very high prices for ad slots, especially for landmark episodes. Warner Bros. owns the show and profits from selling it on.
- **A UK terrestrial broadcaster buying an American series.** Channel 4 is a **publisher-broadcaster** (it commissions or buys programmes rather than making them), funded by advertising but with a **public service remit**. A popular import like *Friends* brought in advertising revenue that helped **cross-fund** more niche, loss-making programmes. It moved to E4 because it fitted that channel's 16–34 target audience.
- **Longevity and licensing.** *Friends* has been sold to broadcasters worldwide and licensed to streaming services for very large sums, showing the long-term value of a back catalogue. **Merchandise** (clothing, homeware, games) with the recognisable logo adds income.
- **Regulation.** Channel 4 is regulated by **Ofcom** and self-regulates through scheduling and the watershed; streaming services have raised new regulatory challenges.

### Audiences

- **Then:** aimed at **Generation X** twenty-somethings; scheduled weekly, so audiences watched at the same time and talked about it next day (a **"watercooler"** show). Channel 4 placed it in a **Friday-night comedy** zone for 16–25-year-olds. It reached very large US audiences weekly.
- **Now:** **binge-watched** on streaming services; discovered by viewers born long after it first aired; watched with **nostalgia** by original fans; shared as clip compilations on YouTube and discussed on social media.
- **Uses and gratifications:** *entertainment/escapism* (a carefree New York life; the laughter track invites us in); *social interaction* (watercooler talk then, social media now); *personal identity* (friendship and relationship worries are still relatable); *information* (an insight into 1990s popular culture).

### Contexts

- **Historical and social:** 1990s America, pre-9/11, an optimistic version of the **American Dream** (Rachel arrives to make her own way); young people leaving home and **delaying marriage and children**; the "new man" and second-wave/third-wave feminist ideas of independent women.
- **Cultural:** influenced fashion (the "Rachel" haircut) and slang; the lack of diversity reflects the **mainstream media's under-representation** of minorities.
- **Technological:** no catch-up TV or streaming; you had to watch at broadcast time. Multi-camera studio production was the industry standard.

### Using Friends to show change

| | Friends (1994) | Man Like Mobeen | Modern Family |
|---|---|---|---|
| Production | Multi-camera studio, live audience, laughter track | Single-camera, location, no laughter track | Single-camera mockumentary, no laughter track |
| Ethnicity | All-white leads | British Muslim, South Asian and black characters; self-representation | More diverse, still mainly white and affluent |
| Family | Friends as pseudo-family | Brother raising sister, friends as family | Three family types, including a gay couple |
| Distribution | Weekly broadcast (NBC; Channel 4 in UK) | iPlayer first, then BBC One and Netflix | ABC, then Netflix, then Disney+ |

::: terms
- **Multi-camera production**: filming a scene with several cameras at once on a studio set, usually in front of a live audience.
- **Laughter track**: recorded or live audience laughter heard in a programme, signalling to viewers when to laugh.
- **Ensemble cast**: a group of main characters of similar importance rather than a single lead.
- **Pseudo-family**: a group of friends who function like a family, providing support and belonging.
- **Generation X**: people born roughly between the mid-1960s and 1980, the original target audience for Friends.
- **Publisher-broadcaster**: a broadcaster, such as Channel 4, that commissions or buys programmes rather than making them itself.
- **Cross-funding**: using profit from popular programmes to pay for less commercial ones.
- **Watercooler television**: a programme so widely watched at the same time that people discuss it together the next day.
- **Under-representation**: a social group appearing in the media far less often than it exists in society.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the question's focus**: historical contexts, audiences' consumption, industry, or representation. Friends questions are almost always **Q2 (10 marks)**.
2. **Name the context precisely** (1990s Generation X, new-man masculinity, under-representation of ethnic minorities, pre-streaming scheduled viewing).
3. **Link each context to a specific detail** of the extract: Rachel's wedding dress and job, Ross's divorce, the all-white group, the studio set and laughter track, the watercooler slot.
4. Where the question allows, **compare with your contemporary episode** to show change over time. One sentence of contrast per point is enough.
5. Aim for **four or five developed points**; accuracy and specific links move you into the top band.
:::

::: example Historical contexts
How do sitcoms reflect their historical contexts? Refer to Friends to support your points. (10)

Friends reflects early-1990s America and its "Generation X" twenty-somethings, who delayed marriage and children and made friends their family (1). The pilot centres on six young adults whose main concerns are dating, jobs and each other, and Rachel's escape from her wedding shows a young woman choosing independence over a conventional settled life (1). This carefree, optimistic New York, before 9/11 and with few money worries, reflects the confident mood of the time and a version of the American Dream (1). Gender reflects 1990s debates: Ross is an emotional "new man", openly upset about his divorce (1), while Joey's womanising shows more traditional attitudes still in place (1). The all-white cast in a diverse city reflects the under-representation of ethnic minorities in mainstream US television of the period (1), and jokes about Ross's ex-wife's relationship with a woman show that gay people were often punchlines rather than central characters (1). Production reflects its time too: a multi-camera studio shoot with a live audience and laughter track was the industry standard (1), and broadcast at a fixed time on NBC and Channel 4 made it a shared "watercooler" moment before catch-up TV existed (1). By contrast, my contemporary episode is shot on location without a laugh track and streamed on demand, which shows how much the genre has changed (1).
:::

::: example Audiences then and now
Explain how audiences' consumption of Friends has changed since it was first broadcast. (6)

In the 1990s it was watched at a fixed weekly time on NBC, or on Channel 4's Friday-night comedy slot in the UK, so viewers shared the experience and discussed it the next day (2). Now whole seasons can be streamed and binge-watched on demand, on phones and laptops as well as TVs (2). New audiences who were not born when it aired discover it through streaming and clip compilations on YouTube and social media, while original fans rewatch it for nostalgia (2).
:::

::: example Industry
Explain why Channel 4 bought an American sitcom like Friends. (4)

Channel 4 is a publisher-broadcaster that buys or commissions programmes rather than making them (1). A popular US import attracted large young audiences that advertisers want (1), and the advertising revenue could cross-fund Channel 4's less commercial, public service programming (1). It also fitted Channel 4's reputation for introducing new American shows to 16–25-year-old viewers (1).
:::

---

::: q
Explain why the lack of ethnic diversity in Friends is a point about context. (3)
---
All six leads are white and middle-class, although 1990s New York was very diverse (1). This reflects the under-representation of ethnic minorities in mainstream US TV at the time (1), and it contrasts with the more diverse representations in contemporary sitcoms, showing change over time (1).

He is openly emotional and hurt by the end of his marriage (1), an example of the 1990s "new man", unlike the tough or bumbling men of older sitcoms (1).

::: watch
- Friends is the **past** product, so its questions are about **context and change**. Always link the point to 1994.
- Don't just say "it isn't diverse". Explain **why** that reflects its time, and set it against your contemporary episode.
- Know the production model: **multi-camera, studio, live audience, laughter track**. It is the clearest contrast with Mobeen and Modern Family.
- Channel 4 is a **commercial public service broadcaster** and a **publisher-broadcaster**, not the BBC.
- Refer to specific moments from **your school's extract** (Rachel's arrival, the phone call, Ross's divorce, the furniture), not general knowledge of the whole series.
:::
