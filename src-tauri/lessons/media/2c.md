## What it is

**Media industries** is about who makes media, who owns it, who pays for it, who controls it, and how all of that shapes the product. It is tested in Component 1 Section B Question 3 (a stepped question on one of newspapers, radio, film or video games: short recall of 1–2 marks, a "Briefly explain" of 4–5 marks, then a 10- or 12-mark explanation referring to a set product) and in Component 2 Questions 2 and 4 (10 marks on television or music). Section B is **not** textual analysis: use the set products as examples of industry issues, not as images to decode.

### Who produces media

- **Large organisations** that own the products they make: News UK owns The Sun; Condé Nast owns Vogue and GQ; the BBC makes and broadcasts Desert Island Discs; Epic Games owns Fortnite. Owning the product (the **intellectual property**) means the organisation keeps the profits from every version and platform.
- **Independent producers, individuals and groups**: independent TV companies make programmes for broadcasters; small studios make games; artists, YouTubers and podcasters produce content themselves using cheap digital tools.

**Production processes, personnel and technologies** shape the final product. A Bond film involves a production company, a director, writers, a large crew, stars, locations and post-production effects, and a budget of hundreds of millions, so it must attract a global mass audience to recover its costs. A newspaper page is shaped by the editor's agenda, journalists, photographers, designers and advertising sales teams. **When and where** a product is made matters too: a 1970s poster was painted by an illustrator; a 2021 poster is a digital montage built by a specialist design agency.

### Ownership and control

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Conglomerate** | A very large company that owns many businesses, often across different media and other industries | News Corp (newspapers, publishing, other media); Comcast owns NBCUniversal, which includes Universal Pictures |
| **Diversification** | A company expanding into new, different areas of business to spread risk and find new income | A newspaper group running betting brands, holidays, radio stations or events |
| **Vertical integration** | One company owning different stages of the same industry: production, distribution and exhibition/broadcast | ITV makes programmes (ITV Studios), broadcasts them (ITV channels) and streams them (ITVX) |
| **Horizontal integration** | A company buying competitors at the same stage of the industry | A publisher buying another magazine publisher |
| **Synergy** | Two or more products or companies working together so each promotes the other | A film's theme song by a star singer; a newspaper giving away theme-park tickets |

**Why ownership matters**: owners set the priorities (profit for shareholders, or public service, or journalism), can influence political viewpoint (a newspaper's stance often reflects its owner's), and control what gets made. Concentration of ownership means fewer voices.

**Advantages of vertical integration**: costs are kept in-house (no fees to other companies); control over distribution and release dates (avoid clashing with rivals); guaranteed outlets for products; ability to promote across the company's own platforms; profits from every stage stay in one company.

### Convergence

**Convergence** means different media technologies and platforms coming together, so the same content moves across them, and one device (the smartphone) delivers text, audio, video and games.
- **Technological convergence**: a phone as newspaper, radio, TV and games console.
- **Industry convergence**: companies working across platforms: a newspaper with a website, app, podcasts and TikTok; a radio programme available live, on BBC Sounds and as a podcast; a film franchise with a website, trailers on YouTube, social media and a streaming release.
- **Across national settings**: products made in one country for audiences around the world, co-produced by companies in several countries, and distributed globally online.

Convergence helps organisations reach new (often younger) audiences, gather data and create more ways to earn money, but it also means competing with free content and global tech platforms for attention and advertising.

### Funding models

| Model | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial** | Makes money for owners and shareholders through advertising, sales, subscriptions, sponsorship, merchandise, in-app purchases | The Sun (cover price, advertising, promotions); Fortnite (free to play, funded by in-game purchases); ITV (advertising) |
| **Government funded / public** | Funded by public money, with a duty to serve the public | The BBC, funded mainly by the **licence fee** that households pay to watch live TV or use iPlayer, set by the government; NHS 111 campaign paid for by the government and NHS England |
| **Not-for-profit** | Any profit is reinvested in the product rather than paid to shareholders | The Guardian, owned by the Scott Trust, which reinvests profits in journalism; Channel 4, publicly owned but funded by advertising |

**Public service broadcasting (PSB)**: broadcasters with a duty to inform, educate and entertain, to serve all audiences (including minorities and the nations and regions) and to be impartial in news. The BBC is the main PSB; ITV, Channel 4 and Channel 5 have some public service obligations too. Funding affects content: the BBC can make programmes for small audiences and carries no adverts; commercial broadcasters must attract audiences advertisers want.

### A global commercial industry

Most media operate as **global businesses**. Hollywood exports films worldwide; Fortnite is played across the globe; music streams everywhere at once. Organisations reach both **mass audiences** (a Bond film, The Sun's website) and **niche or specialised audiences** (a specialist magazine, a radio programme for older listeners), often both at once through different platforms. Global reach spreads high costs over huge audiences, but also means products are shaped to travel: familiar genres, stars, spectacle, less dialogue-dependent humour.

### Regulation

**Regulation** means rules and bodies that control what media can make and show, to protect audiences (especially children), ensure fairness and accuracy, and uphold standards.

| Regulator | What it regulates | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Ofcom** | TV, radio (including the BBC), video-on-demand services, and increasingly online safety | Set up by government; enforces a Broadcasting Code (harm, offence, impartiality, the 9pm watershed); can fine broadcasters or take licences away |
| **IPSO** (Independent Press Standards Organisation) | Most UK newspapers and magazines, including The Sun | Self-regulation: the industry funds it; handles complaints under the Editors' Code (accuracy, privacy, harassment); can require corrections. Not all papers belong: The Guardian chose not to join and has its own readers' editor |
| **BBFC** (British Board of Film Classification) | Films and videos | Age ratings U, PG, 12A/12, 15, 18, R18; may require cuts. No Time to Die was 12A in cinemas |
| **PEGI** (Pan European Game Information) | Video games | Age ratings 3, 7, 12, 16, 18 plus content descriptors; in the UK enforced by the Video Standards Council |
| **ASA** (Advertising Standards Authority) | Adverts in all media | Self-regulation of advertising under codes that ads must be legal, decent, honest and truthful |

**Challenges from digital technologies**: content crosses borders instantly, so UK rules are hard to enforce on companies based abroad; the sheer volume of user-generated content cannot be checked before it is posted; age ratings are easy to ignore online; in-game purchases, online chat and loot-box style mechanics raise new concerns that older rules did not anticipate; news spreads through social media where press regulators have little reach; misinformation is fast and hard to correct. New laws (such as the Online Safety Act 2023, which gives Ofcom powers over online platforms) try to catch up.

::: terms
- **Conglomerate**: a very large company that owns many different businesses, often across several media and other industries.
- **Diversification**: when a media company expands into new and different areas of business to spread risk and increase income.
- **Vertical integration**: when one company owns different stages of production, distribution and exhibition in the same industry.
- **Horizontal integration**: when a company owns or buys other companies operating at the same stage of the industry.
- **Convergence**: the coming together of media technologies, platforms and industries, so content moves across them.
- **Synergy**: when two products or companies work together so that each promotes the other for mutual benefit.
- **Licence fee**: the annual fee UK households pay to watch live TV or use BBC iPlayer, which funds the BBC.
- **Public service broadcasting**: broadcasting with a duty to inform, educate and entertain all audiences rather than only to make profit.
- **Not-for-profit**: a funding model in which any profit is reinvested in the product rather than paid to shareholders.
- **Regulation**: the rules and bodies that control media content and practice to protect audiences and uphold standards.
- **Self-regulation**: when an industry sets up and funds its own regulator, as the press does with IPSO.
- **Intellectual property**: ownership of creative content such as characters, brands and franchises, which can be licensed and sold.
:::

::: method
1. **Recall questions [1–2]**: answer precisely and briefly: one name, one term, two items. Give the full name if you can (BBFC / British Board of Film Classification).
2. **"Briefly explain" [4–5]**: define the concept in one sentence, then give two or three developed points (advantage, effect, example). A set product is welcome but not always required.
3. **"Explain how/why ... Refer to [set product]" [10–12]**: open with a short definition; then three or four paragraphs, each making an industry point and backing it with a specific detail of the set product (owner, platform, funding, regulator, marketing).
4. **Name organisations**: owner, producer, distributor, regulator. Vague answers ("the company") stay in the lower bands.
5. **Explain the effect**: always say how the industry factor shapes the product or reaches the audience.
6. **Don't analyse images** in Section B. Mention the product's content only to show an industry point.
:::

::: example Recall
Name the organisation that regulates newspapers such as The Sun. (1)

IPSO, the Independent Press Standards Organisation (1).
:::

::: example Briefly explain: funding
Briefly explain the difference between public service and commercial broadcasting. (4)

Public service broadcasters, such as the BBC, have a duty to inform, educate and entertain all audiences, and the BBC is funded mainly by the licence fee rather than advertising (1). This means it can make programmes for small or specialist audiences and must be impartial in its news (1). Commercial broadcasters, such as commercial radio stations or Sky, are funded mainly by advertising, sponsorship and subscriptions, so they must attract the large or valuable audiences that advertisers want to reach (1). Their aim is profit for owners and shareholders, which can make them more cautious and ratings-driven, although some (like ITV) also carry public service obligations (1).
:::

::: example Briefly explain: vertical integration
Briefly explain the advantages of vertical integration for the television industry. (4)

Vertical integration is when one company controls production, distribution and broadcast (1). ITV makes programmes through ITV Studios, broadcasts them on its channels and streams them on ITVX, so it keeps the profits from each stage rather than paying other companies (1). It controls scheduling and release: it can premiere a drama on its channel and streaming service at the same time and promote it across all its own platforms (1). It also owns programmes it can sell abroad and reuse, spreading its costs and giving a long-term income (1).
:::

::: example Explain with a set product
Explain how convergence helps media organisations reach audiences. Refer to The Sun to support your points. (12)

Convergence is the coming together of media platforms and technologies so the same content reaches audiences in many ways (1). The Sun began as a print tabloid, but falling print sales and the rise of free online news forced it to move across platforms (1). Its website is free to read and funded by advertising, which lets it reach a far larger online audience than the print edition, and a much younger one: most online readers are under 35 while print readers are mostly middle-aged (2). A paid app offers a digital edition of the print paper, bringing print loyalty onto phones and tablets (1). The Sun also distributes content through Apple News and social media such as TikTok, Instagram, Snapchat and X, tailoring different content to each platform's audience and bringing readers back to its site, where clicks earn advertising income (2). Convergence supports its owner, News UK, part of Rupert Murdoch's News Corp, a conglomerate that can share content and promotion across its businesses (1). Promotions such as theme-park giveaways link print and online through codes, encouraging repeat purchase (1). However, convergence also means competing with tech platforms for advertising, which is why The Sun has diversified into other commercial ventures (1). Overall, convergence allows The Sun to remain one of the UK's most-read news brands despite the decline of print (2).

Band: 10–12. Clear definition, detailed set-product evidence, the effect on audiences explained.
:::
