## What it is

Component 1 Section A sets two print adverts from very different worlds: the **Quality Street advert (1956)**, a hand-drawn commercial advert for a luxury tin of chocolates and toffees, and the **NHS 111 "Help Us Help You" advert (2023)**, a photographic public-information poster for a government health service. Together they show how advertising persuades, how representations of gender and family reflect their times, and how purpose (selling vs informing) shapes every choice. They are tested on **media language**, **representation** and **contexts**: Question 1 (15 marks; in 2025 it was Quality Street, layout and design [5] + images [10]); Question 2(a) (5 marks, e.g. historical or social context); Question 2(b) (25 marks, comparing a set advert with an **unseen** advert).

### Print advert conventions

| Convention | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Brand name and logo** | Identifies the brand; brand recognition |
| **Product shot / pack shot** | Shows the product clearly so it can be recognised in shops |
| **Main image** | Creates an idea, mood or story around the product |
| **Slogan or tagline** | A memorable phrase used across the campaign |
| **Body copy** | Persuasive or informative text |
| **Call to action** | Tells the audience what to do (buy, call, visit, download) |
| **Small print / information** | Prices, sizes, how to access a service |
| **Colour palette and typography** | Brand identity, mood and audience appeal |

**Commercial** adverts sell products for profit, often by associating them with desires (romance, luxury, status). **Public-information** or **public-service** adverts change behaviour for the public good, usually by making the audience identify with ordinary people and giving a clear instruction.

### Quality Street (1956): media language

- **Hand-drawn artwork** in a rich palette of primary and secondary colours. The illustrated style, like a painting or animation, was typical of the period and makes the scene feel luxurious and idealised, reflecting the new consumer culture.
- **Triangular composition**: a centrally framed man in formal evening dress sits with the product (the tin) in his lap; two women lean in towards him, one on each side, forming a triangle that leads the eye to the product at its centre. Central framing gives the product and the man dominance.
- **The women's dresses** echo the colourful twisted wrappers of the sweets, visually linking the women to the product.
- **Narrative dilemma**: on one level the man must choose between two women; on another, the women are choosing the chocolates. A romantic mini-story with a playful twist.
- **Gold frame** behind the characters, like a halo or an old-master painting, containing **Major Quality and Miss Sweetly**, Regency-era characters who had been part of the brand since the 1930s. This is **intertextuality** within the brand: tradition, heritage, elegance, and continuity with earlier advertising.
- **Typography**: the brand name runs across the bottom third in a bold purple serif font, drawing the eye; purple and serif lettering connote richness, quality and royalty.
- **Product shots**: close-up images of individual sweets in their wrappers in the foreground (a pack shot), their colours linked to the women's dresses; smaller print gives product information.
- **Persuasive language**: alliteration, emotive language and superlatives in the copy suggest luxury and indulgence and address a reasonably educated audience.

### Quality Street: representation and context

- **Men**: the man is dominant, centrally framed, in control of the product, dressed as a successful, wealthy gentleman: the **provider**, the Proppian hero with the "prize" in his lap.
- **Women**: two stereotypes: a supposed irresistible desire for chocolate, and subservience to a dominant man; they are defined by romance and appetite. A feminist reading links this to the **male gaze** and to patriarchal values in which a woman's success lies in attracting a man. An alternative reading sees the women as using romance to manipulate him and get the sweets, which hints at women's growing independence.
- **Class**: formal dress, the gold frame and the sophisticated style connote upper-middle-class elegance, an aspiration for ordinary families; the product unites different classes (the Regency figures, the gentleman, the women) around one shared pleasure.
- **Historical context**: Britain was emerging from post-war austerity. Rationing of sweets ended in 1953 and all rationing in 1954, so the mid-1950s saw a consumer boom in which luxuries became affordable. Commercial television began in 1955, and advertising grew. Gender roles were strongly patriarchal: the male breadwinner and the housewife ideal. Many 1950s adverts showed women as subservient, caring for the man of the house. The product, a family assortment, was marketed as an affordable luxury for working families.
- **Audience**: families and young-to-middle-aged adults with growing spending power; women as the likely shoppers, men as the imagined providers.

### NHS 111 (2023): media language

- **Producers and purpose**: part of the national "Help Us Help You" campaign created for NHS England by the agency M&C Saatchi and distributed by the government's Department of Health and Social Care. It is a **public-health** campaign with no commercial aim: to get people with urgent but not life-threatening needs to use NHS 111 (by phone, online or through the NHS App) instead of calling 999 or going to A&E.
- **Main image**: a long shot of a father sitting on his sick daughter's bed. Neither is famous: ordinary models let ordinary adults identify with the scenario. His concerned expression, protective arm and posture connote care; her position in bed, stripy pyjamas and look towards him suggest illness and dependence. He holds a tablet, showing the digital routes to help.
- **Setting**: the bed (wooden frame, colourful bedding, fairy lights, a rosette) is placed inside an NHS Urgent Treatment Centre waiting area, with a reception desk, leaflet rack, wipe-clean seating, a clock and signage; background staff wear masks behind screens. The clinical grey setting makes the homely bed look brighter. We are not meant to believe the bed is really in the clinic: the combination shows that using 111 at home is a way into the NHS's services.
- **Narrative**: the father is the Proppian **hero** on a quest to find help for his daughter; the NHS, through the tablet, is the **helper**.
- **Written codes**: the tagline "Get to the help you need", framed around the father's head, uses the imperative and direct address (a call to action). Simple, concise body copy in a clear sans-serif font makes the message accessible to people with low literacy.
- **Branding**: the HM Government logo top-left and the NHS logo top-right signal an official, trustworthy, non-commercial message. NHS blue runs through the logo, text and signage, and a muted, soft palette connotes calm and reliability.
- **Warning triangle** icon in the bottom-right corner echoes road warning signs: urgency and attention.
- **Technology**: the campaign ran on posters but also digital displays, social media and search engines, and the tablet in the image reinforces the online and app routes aimed at younger adults and parents.

### NHS 111: representation and context

- **Gender and family**: a father as the nurturing, proactive carer, deciding how to get help, challenges the stereotype that mothers are always the primary carers and that men are not emotionally nurturing. This reflects real social change: fathers now spend far more time on childcare than in the 1970s, with more dual-income and single-parent households. The daughter is constructed through stereotypically girlish codes: pink, plaited pigtails.
- **Ethnicity**: a Black family. The campaign aimed to reach **global majority** audiences in the UK, and Black men have been under-represented in health campaigns; representation matters because groups who don't see themselves addressed may not act on the message.
- **Disability**: the girl wears a hearing aid, reflecting growing representation of deaf people in mainstream media; the campaign also produced British Sign Language versions and promoted an interpreter service.
- **Context**: post-Covid pressure on A&E and emergency services; masks and screens reflect ongoing infection control; the NHS's push to channel people to the right service; the digital shift in accessing healthcare.
- **Audience**: all adults, but especially under-35s and parents of children aged 5 to 12.

### Comparing the two

| | Quality Street (1956) | NHS 111 (2023) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Commercial: sell chocolates | Public information: change behaviour |
| Style | Hand-drawn, rich colours, fantasy | Photographic, muted, realistic |
| Man | Wealthy provider in control | Caring father seeking help |
| Female | Romantic, decorative, desiring | Child, dependent, cared for; disabled and visible |
| Persuasion | Desire, romance, luxury | Identification, reassurance, instruction |

::: terms
- **Pack shot**: an image of the product or its packaging so the audience can recognise it in shops.
- **Slogan**: a short, memorable phrase associated with a brand or campaign.
- **Call to action**: a direct instruction telling the audience what to do, such as call, visit or buy.
- **Body copy**: the main block of text in an advert giving persuasive or practical information.
- **Public information campaign**: a non-commercial campaign, often government-funded, that aims to inform people or change their behaviour.
- **Brand identity**: the recognisable image of a brand, built through logos, colours, characters and style.
- **Triangular composition**: an arrangement of figures in a triangle that leads the eye to a central point.
- **Verisimilitude**: the appearance of being real or true to life, created through setting, props and costume.
- **Global majority**: a term for people of Black, Asian, mixed and other ethnic backgrounds who form the majority of the world's population.
- **Austerity**: a period of economic hardship and restricted spending, such as rationing in post-war Britain.
- **Provider**: the traditional role of the man as the family's breadwinner and source of material goods.
:::

::: method
1. **Question 1 aspects**: *layout and design* (composition, placement, framing, typography, colour, balance of image and text); *images* (characters, costume, gesture, gaze, props, setting, colour, product shot); *written language* (brand name, slogan, copy, imperative, alliteration).
2. For each point: element → connotation → persuasive purpose for the target audience at the time.
3. **Name the purpose**: selling a luxury (Quality Street) vs informing and instructing (NHS 111). Purpose explains nearly every choice.
4. **Question 2(a)**: link 1950s post-war consumerism and gender roles, or post-Covid NHS pressure and changing families, to specific choices.
5. **Question 2(b)**: compare set and unseen in every paragraph: who is shown and how, which stereotypes are reinforced or challenged, why (purpose, audience, context), and how far similar and different.
:::

::: example Layout and design (5)
Explore how the Quality Street (1956) advert uses layout and design to communicate meanings. (5)

The advert uses a triangular composition: the man sits centrally with the tin in his lap and a woman leans in from each side, so every line leads the eye to the product at the centre (1). Central framing makes the man and the product dominant, positioning him as the provider in control of the "prize" (1). The gold frame behind them acts like a halo and holds the brand's Regency characters, Major Quality and Miss Sweetly, linking the modern scene to the brand's heritage (1). The brand name runs across the bottom third in bold purple serif lettering, which stands out and connotes richness and quality, so the name is the last thing the eye settles on (1). Close-ups of the wrapped sweets in the foreground, coloured like the women's dresses, act as a pack shot, making the product recognisable in shops while tying the whole design together (1).
:::

::: example Images (10)
Explore how the NHS 111 (2023) advert uses images to communicate meanings. (10)

The long shot shows a father sitting on his daughter's bed, and because the models are ordinary rather than famous, parents can easily identify with the scene (1). His concerned expression and protective arm connote care and responsibility, while the girl's position in bed and her look towards him suggest illness and dependence, creating a simple narrative in which he is the Proppian hero seeking help (2). The tablet in his hand is the helper, showing the digital routes to NHS 111 and targeting younger parents who use apps and online services (1). Familiar details, slippers, a grey top and jeans, her stripy pyjamas, fairy lights and a rosette, create verisimilitude and a homely warmth (1). The bed is placed inside an Urgent Treatment Centre waiting area with reception, seating, signage and masked staff behind screens; the grey clinical setting makes the bedroom colours brighter and symbolises that using 111 at home opens the door to NHS services (2). Representing a Black father as carer, and a girl with a hearing aid, targets under-served audiences and challenges stereotypes of fathers and of disability (2). The muted palette and NHS blue connote calm and trust, reinforcing the reassuring public-service message (1).

Band: 9–10.
:::

::: example Historical context (5)
Explain how historical contexts influence print adverts. Refer to the Quality Street (1956) advert to support your points. (5)

After the Second World War Britain went through years of austerity, and sweets were rationed until 1953, with all rationing ending in 1954 (1). By 1956 a consumer boom was beginning, so an advert that presents a tin of chocolates as a luxurious romantic prize sold the new pleasure of affordable indulgence to working families (1). Commercial television had begun in 1955 and advertising was expanding, and the rich, hand-drawn colour artwork reflects the persuasive, aspirational style of the period (1). Gender roles were strongly patriarchal: the man in evening dress, in control of the product, reflects the male breadwinner, while the women's romantic, subservient poses reflect expectations that women should seek a husband (1). The Regency characters in the gold frame connote tradition and heritage, reassuring audiences at a time of change that the brand stood for quality and continuity (1).
:::

::: example Comparison paragraph with an unseen advert
The unseen advert (invented for practice):

> **SparkClean** washing powder (invented for practice), a 1960s print advert. A smiling housewife in an apron holds up a gleaming white shirt in a bright kitchen while her husband, in a suit and hat, heads out of the door with a briefcase. The slogan says "He'll never know how hard you worked". A large box of the powder sits in the corner.

Compare the representation of men in the Quality Street advert and the SparkClean advert. Write one developed paragraph. (5)

Both adverts select a man in formal dress, suit or evening wear, to represent the successful provider, reflecting post-war patriarchal values (1). In Quality Street he is centrally framed and in control of the product, the focus of the women's attention, whereas in SparkClean he is leaving the frame, a background figure whose approval the woman's work is for (1). So Quality Street places the man at the centre of desire and choice, while SparkClean makes him the absent judge of domestic labour, though in both his needs define the woman's role (1). The SparkClean slogan makes his ignorance of housework a virtue, reinforcing the separation of male public work and female domestic work more directly than Quality Street's playful romance (1). Overall the two represent men very similarly as dominant providers, differing mainly in whether the man is the centre of the narrative or its reason (1).
