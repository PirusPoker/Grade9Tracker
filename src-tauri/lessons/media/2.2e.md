## What it is

*Modern Family*, Season 8, Episode 2, **"A Stereotypical Day"**, is the other contemporary sitcom option for Component 2, Section A. It is paired with the *Friends* extract (lesson 2.2f) and studied for **all five areas**. In the exam you watch a **three-minute extract twice** and answer Q1(a) and (b) on it, so know every strand and scene.

**The product.** An American **mockumentary** sitcom created by Christopher Lloyd and Steven Levitan, co-produced with **20th Century Fox Television** and first shown on **ABC** in the US. ABC and the distributor are now owned by **Disney**. It ran for 11 seasons (2009–2020), won the Emmy for Outstanding Comedy Series five times, and follows **three branches of one extended family** in Los Angeles, all linked to the patriarch **Jay Pritchett**. The set episode first aired in the US in 2016 (the spec lists it as 2017).

**The families.**
- **Jay** (older, wealthy, traditional), his Colombian wife **Gloria**, and her teenage son **Manny**.
- **Claire** (Jay's daughter, now boss of the family firm) and **Phil** (an estate agent), with their children **Hayley**, **Alex** (home ill from Caltech) and **Luke**.
- **Mitchell** (Jay's son) and **Cam**, a gay couple, and their adopted daughter **Lily**.

The title is the joke. The episode deliberately **plays with stereotypes**, then shows characters caught out by their own assumptions.

### The three strands

| Strand | Disruption | Complications | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dunphys** | Alex is ill at home; Phil was locked in a room at a house he was selling and claims to be traumatised | Hayley (who has secretly lost her job), Luke (copying Alex's work) and Phil all lean on Alex; Phil strokes her like a **therapy dog**; Claire is busy being the boss at work | Alex realises she has been used and takes the semester off; the family is exposed |
| **Jay, Gloria, Manny** | New black neighbours move in just as Jay installs **security cameras** | Jay worries they will think he is a racist; he brings in his black friend **Miles** to prove otherwise and embarrasses himself; Manny adopts a **Mao suit** and communism to impress a girl | Jay visits the neighbour; the CCTV footage reveals Manny eating cakes from the bin |
| **Cam, Mitchell, Lily** | Lily calls her friend **Tom**, a transgender child, a "weirdo" | Cam and Mitchell assume prejudice and try to correct her; Lily says she hates the mural they painted for her as a baby | Jay points out their own assumptions; they face up to their prejudice; reconciliation over the mural |

Each strand follows **Todorov**: a disruption, complications, and a **new equilibrium** in which a character learns something. The ending is more like Todorov's model than the pure return-to-start of older sitcoms, though the family set-up itself never changes.

### Media language

- **Hybrid genre: the mockumentary.** Comedy plus documentary conventions: characters speak to an unseen interviewer in **talking-head "confessionals"** on the sofa, the camera is handheld and sometimes "catches" events, and zooms and quick pans imitate a documentary crew. Neale's **repetition and variation**: it repeats the home setting, ensemble cast, stock types and comic misunderstandings, but varies the form. Mockumentary was a 2000s trend (for example *The Office*, *Parks and Recreation*).
- **Single-camera production** edited like film, and **no laughter track**, so the audience decides what is funny. This contrasts directly with the multi-camera, live-audience studio model of *Friends*.
- **Sofa interviews** give a **privileged spectator position**: Hayley confesses she has lost her job and Luke admits cheating before the others know, so dramatic irony drives the comedy.
- **Medium shots and close-ups** catch reactions (Phil's pained face); **two-shots** show alliances (Hayley and Luke on the sofa together); **long shots** give room for slapstick (Phil falling as he tries to escape).
- **Non-linear elements**: **flashbacks** show events characters recall in interviews, unusual for sitcom.
- **Sound**: a short upbeat signature tune is almost the only non-diegetic sound; wordplay (Cam's invented word about his gender-awareness) and running jokes carry the humour.
- **Mise-en-scène**: establishing shots of large LA homes signify **affluence**; Claire's business suit versus Phil's casual clothes marks their roles; Alex in her dressing gown signals suffering; Manny's Mao suit is a visual joke; Lily's mural is a prop that carries emotion.
- **Tight structure**: about 22 minutes, three strands intercut, each resolved.

### Representation

- **Family.** The core idea is that the "modern" family is **plural**: a blended family with a large age gap, a traditional nuclear family, and a same-sex couple with an adopted child. This challenges the single white nuclear family of earlier American sitcoms.
- **Gender.** **Claire** is the powerful boss and the stricter parent (she calls herself the "bad cop"), while **Phil** is the child-like, soft "good cop" whose absence goes unnoticed, a reversal of traditional roles used for comedy. **Alex** is the geek girl studying STEM at Caltech, a progressive type, but the humour shows the family exploiting her competence. **Gloria**, struggling with the TV remote, reinforces a tired stereotype of women and technology, and Jay's treatment of her can seem patronising. **Luke** is the lazy teenage boy; **Hayley** the more image-conscious elder sister.
- **Sexuality.** Cam and Mitchell are a positive, central gay couple, married within the series. That is a major change from *Friends*, where gay characters were often the joke. In this episode the comedy turns on *them* making assumptions.
- **Gender identity.** Casting a transgender child actor as Tom was significant for its time; the storyline makes the adults' anxiety, not the child, the source of humour.
- **Ethnicity.** More diverse than *Friends*, but still mostly white and affluent. Gloria is written with **Latina stereotypes** (temper, accent jokes); Jay's use of **Miles** as a "token" black friend is the target of the satire; Jay's fear that the neighbours will see him as a "racist old man" exposes how stereotypes of black people as a threat operate; Lily is Vietnamese-American. Jay also complains about stereotypes of old white men, which lets the audience laugh at his blind spots.
- **Age.** Jay as the older patriarch whose traditional views are gently mocked, yet who gives the episode's wisest advice to Cam and Mitchell.

### Industry

- **Commercial, conglomerate television.** ABC is a commercial network funded by **advertising**. Production, distribution and broadcast all sit inside **Disney**, an example of **vertical integration** and **conglomerate ownership** that supports high production values.
- **Product placement** added revenue: brands competed to appear, and Apple products feature heavily (one later episode was shot on Apple devices; in this episode Alex's laptop matters to the plot).
- **Global licensing and streaming.** Sold to channels worldwide, licensed to **Netflix** (seasons became available in many countries), then withdrawn so it could be exclusive to Disney's own service, **Disney+**, a clear case of a conglomerate using its back catalogue to build its own platform. Also available to buy digitally.
- **Regulation.** US network standards; in the UK, streaming services are not governed by the watershed in the same way as broadcast TV, which is a challenge for regulation.

### Audiences

- A **broad family audience** and, for years, one of the top US scripted shows among **18–49-year-olds**, the group advertisers value most. Some critics argue its appeal is strongest with white, middle-class viewers, as most characters are affluent and white.
- **Marketing**: trailers on ABC and YouTube, official pages linked to Facebook, X and Instagram, banner ads on social media and streaming platforms, and free publicity from fans discussing episodes.
- **Uses and gratifications**: *entertainment* (slapstick and wordplay); *information* (changing family structures, attitudes to LGBTQ+ people and ethnicity); *personal identity* (such a wide cast lets many viewers see themselves); *social interaction* (hashtag discussion, for instance around Cam and Mitchell's wedding).
- **Streaming** enables binge-viewing and new audiences long after first broadcast.

### Contexts

- **Social**: greater acceptance of same-sex marriage and adoption in the 2010s; growing visibility of transgender people; debates about casual racism and "political correctness", the very thing the episode's title jokes about.
- **Cultural and technological**: technology is woven into plots (laptops, CCTV), reflecting a digital age; the mockumentary trend in TV comedy.
- **Compared with Friends (1994)**: *Friends* shows an all-white group in a studio; *Modern Family* shows diverse family types on location in a documentary style. Both reflect their times.

::: terms
- **Mockumentary**: a hybrid genre that uses documentary conventions, such as interviews and handheld camera, for fictional comedy.
- **Talking head**: a shot of a character speaking directly to an unseen interviewer, used on the sofa in Modern Family.
- **Direct address**: a character speaking to the camera and audience, breaking the fourth wall.
- **Hybridity**: the mixing of conventions from two or more genres in one product.
- **Multi-stranded narrative**: a narrative with several storylines that are intercut and resolved together.
- **Ensemble cast**: a large group of main characters of roughly equal importance, rather than one lead.
- **Vertical integration**: one company owning different stages of the process, such as production, distribution and broadcast.
- **Product placement**: payment by a brand to have its products appear within a programme.
- **Licensing**: selling another company the right to show a programme for a set period, for example to a streaming service.
- **Token character**: a minority character included mainly to make a product or a character appear diverse.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify which strand** the extract comes from (Dunphy, Jay/Gloria, Cam/Mitchell) and what has just happened.
2. For **media language** questions, use the mockumentary conventions: talking heads, direct address, handheld camera, zooms, flashbacks, no laughter track. Give the shot, what it shows, and what it means.
3. For **representation**, take one group at a time (gender, sexuality, ethnicity, age, family) and say whether each is **reinforced or subverted**. Remember that the episode often sets up a stereotype in order to **undercut** it.
4. For **"how typical / how stereotypical"**, weigh both sides and finish with a clear judgement. Compare briefly with *Friends* to show how the genre has changed.
5. For the **10-mark Q2**, use industry facts (ABC, Disney, advertising, product placement, Netflix then Disney+), audience facts (18–49, social media, streaming) or context, and tie each to the programme.
:::

::: example Mockumentary conventions
Explore how the use of the mockumentary form creates meaning in Modern Family. (10)

The talking-head interviews on the sofa are the most distinctive convention (1). When Phil confesses to the camera that he is traumatised after being locked in a room, the medium shot shows both his exaggerated expression and the familiar sofa (1), so the audience laughs at the gap between how serious he thinks it is and how trivial it looks (1). These confessionals give a privileged spectator position (1): Hayley admits she has lost her job before her family knows, so we enjoy dramatic irony in later scenes (1). Handheld camerawork and quick pans imitate a documentary crew catching events as they happen, which creates realism and makes the characters seem like real people (1). Flashbacks show what characters describe, a non-linear element unusual for sitcom (1). Without a laughter track, the audience is not told when to laugh, which suits a subtler, observational comedy (1). This hybrid of documentary and sitcom varies the genre, in Neale's terms (1), and contrasts sharply with the studio-audience model of Friends, showing how the sitcom developed in the 2000s (1).
:::

::: example Stereotypes of ethnicity
How stereotypical are the representations of ethnicity in Modern Family? (10)

Some representations clearly reinforce stereotypes. Gloria is written as a fiery Latina whose accent is a source of jokes, and her struggle with the remote adds a gender stereotype on top (2). The show is also dominated by affluent white characters, so its diversity has limits (1). However, the episode is built around exposing stereotypes. Jay's panic that his new black neighbours will think his cameras are aimed at them shows how the stereotype of black people as a threat works, and makes Jay the butt of the joke (2). Bringing in Miles as a token friend is satirised when Jay embarrasses himself, so the "token" convention is the target, not simply repeated (2). Lily, a Vietnamese-American adopted by a gay couple, is a normal, stroppy child whose ethnicity is not a joke (1). Overall I think the episode is knowingly stereotypical: it uses familiar types for quick comedy but frames the white characters' assumptions as the problem (1), which makes it more progressive than the all-white Friends, though Gloria's representation remains dated (1).
:::

::: example Industry: streaming
Explain how streaming has affected the distribution of Modern Family. (4)

After first broadcast on ABC, it was licensed to Netflix, making it available on demand in many countries and to viewers who had never seen it on TV (2). It was later withdrawn so that Disney, which owns ABC and the programme's distributor, could make it exclusive to Disney+, using a popular back catalogue to attract subscribers to its own platform (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Mockumentary** is the key term. Use it, and explain what the talking heads **do** for the comedy, not just that they exist.
- Don't claim the whole show is "diverse" and stop. It is **mostly white and wealthy**, and Gloria is stereotyped. Balance earns the top band.
- The title is a joke about stereotypes, so explain how the episode **sets up a stereotype and then undercuts it**.
- Keep the three strands straight: **Alex / Phil** (Dunphys), **Jay / neighbours / Miles / Manny**, **Cam and Mitchell / Lily / Tom**.
- Industry: ABC is **commercial** (advertising), unlike the BBC. Disney's **vertical integration** is a precise, creditable point.
:::
