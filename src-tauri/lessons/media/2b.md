## What it is

The media do not show us the world; they **re-present** it. Every photo is framed, every headline worded, every character cast and costumed. What reaches the audience is a **version of reality** built by producers with purposes of their own: to sell, to persuade, to inform, to entertain. Representation asks *who or what* is shown, *how*, *by whom*, *why*, and *who is missing*. It is assessed in Component 1 Question 2(b) (a 25-mark comparison of a set print product with an unseen one) and in Component 2 Questions 1 and 3 (the TV extract and the music videos).

### Selection, construction, mediation

These three processes, named in the spec, are the backbone of every representation answer (Stuart Hall's work on representation is the usual theoretical reference).

| Process | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Selection** | Choosing what to include and exclude: which person, which photo, which story, which angle | The Sun selects an archive red-carpet photo of a TV presenter for a story about turning 50 |
| **Construction** | Building the representation from media language: costume, pose, lighting, shot, words | Raheem Sterling is constructed as a protector through black wings, a low angle and the "Guardian Angel" cover line |
| **Mediation** | The representation passes through the producer's values, the organisation's purpose and the medium's conventions before it reaches us | The Guardian's liberal values mediate how it words a story about a Labour government ("rethink" rather than a harsher "U-turn") |

The key idea: representations are never neutral, even when they look natural. A photo *feels* like reality; that is what makes its choices powerful.

### Purpose shapes representation

The same group or event is represented differently depending on the producer's purpose.
- A **commercial** product (a chocolate advert, a glossy magazine) represents people in ways that sell: aspirational, glamorous, desirable.
- A **public-information** product (NHS 111) represents people so the target audience identifies and acts: ordinary, relatable, diverse.
- A **newspaper** represents events to fit its political stance and audience: a tabloid dramatises a power cut as national humiliation; a liberal paper foregrounds its effect on vulnerable people.

### Stereotypes

A **stereotype** is a simplified, generalised representation of a group, repeated until it seems natural.
- **How they become established**: through repetition across many products over time, often reflecting the views of those with power to make media.
- **Functions and uses**: they let audiences **read a product quickly** (a poster has seconds to communicate), they make comedy work (sitcoms play with them), and they can reinforce a dominant ideology. Producers also use them deliberately in order to **subvert** or **challenge** them.
- **How they vary over time**: the glamorous, passive "Bond girl" of the 1970s gives way to a female 00 agent in combat gear by 2021, as social attitudes change.
- **Countertypes** are representations that deliberately go against a stereotype, such as a nurturing father as the carer in a health advert.
- **Ethnicity**: Manuel Alvarado's idea that minority ethnic groups have often been represented as *exotic*, *dangerous*, *humorous* or *pitied* is a useful checklist, for example when discussing the dark-haired woman in a martial-arts pose on the 1974 Bond poster, framed as the exotic "other".

### Under-representation and misrepresentation

- **Under-representation**: a group appears less often than its share of the population, or not in positions of power. Black and Asian models were long rarely chosen for glossy magazine covers, justified by unevidenced claims that they hurt sales.
- **Misrepresentation**: a group is shown inaccurately or negatively, e.g. villains marked by facial scarring (a criticised Hollywood habit), or Muslims shown mainly in connection with terrorism.
- **Why it happens**: who owns and staffs media organisations; assumptions about what the (often mainstream, white, male) audience wants; reliance on stereotypes for speed; commercial caution. **Why it matters**: audiences take their sense of what is "normal" partly from media, so absence and distortion shape attitudes and self-esteem.

### Viewpoints, messages and values; self-representation

Representations carry **viewpoints** (whose side are we on?), **messages** (what are we told?) and **values** (what is treated as good?). These are reinforced when many products repeat them: the idea that success means wealth, or that women should look ageless. **Self-representation** is when people represent themselves: an artist on social media, Malala's own words inside Vogue about her headscarf, a creator who writes and stars in their own sitcom. It can challenge stereotypes because the group controls the construction.

### Social, cultural and political significance; context

Representations matter beyond the product. A Muslim woman in a headscarf on the cover of Vogue was significant because it challenged decades of under-representation and the stereotype that such women are oppressed. A father caring for a sick child in a government campaign normalises changing family roles. Always link a representation to the **context** it reflects: the 1950s patriarchal home, the 1970s permissive era, the #MeToo movement, Black Lives Matter protests in 2020, post-Covid pressure on the NHS.

### Gender: feminist approaches

- **The male gaze** (Laura Mulvey): mainstream media often position the audience to look at women from a heterosexual male point of view, turning women into objects "to be looked at" while men act. Look for camera angles and poses that display women's bodies, and for men who drive the narrative.
- Feminist approaches more broadly ask whether women are shown as **active or passive**, as **subjects or objects**, and whether representations reinforce **patriarchy** (male power). Liesbet van Zoonen argued that gender representations are constructed and change with culture; bell hooks stressed that gender and ethnicity must be read together. Use names lightly: the idea matters more than the citation.
- **Masculinity** is a representation too: the tough, emotionally closed action hero; the **metrosexual** man who cares about grooming; the body-focused man with a gym-built physique. GQ and the Bond posters are the key set examples.

### Audience interpretation

The same representation can be read differently. Factors include the audience's **own experiences** (a parent sees the NHS 111 father differently from a teenager), **beliefs and values** (a Guardian reader and a Sun reader read the same political story differently), **age, gender, ethnicity and culture**, and **the time** of reading: a 1956 audience likely saw the Quality Street advert as charming romance; many modern readers see patriarchal stereotypes.

::: terms
- **Re-presentation**: the idea that the media construct a version of reality rather than simply showing the world as it is.
- **Selection**: the producer's choice of what to include in and exclude from a representation.
- **Construction**: building a representation from elements of media language such as costume, pose, framing and words.
- **Mediation**: the way a representation is shaped by the producer's values, purpose and medium before it reaches the audience.
- **Stereotype**: a simplified, generalised representation of a social group, established through repetition.
- **Countertype**: a representation that deliberately contradicts a familiar stereotype.
- **Under-representation**: when a social group appears in the media far less than its presence in society, or rarely in powerful roles.
- **Misrepresentation**: an inaccurate, distorted or negative representation of a group, event or issue.
- **Self-representation**: when individuals or groups construct their own representation, e.g. on social media.
- **Male gaze**: Mulvey's idea that media position the audience to view women from a heterosexual male perspective, as objects to be looked at.
- **Patriarchy**: a social system in which men hold most power and male authority is treated as normal.
- **Ideology**: a set of values and beliefs, often those of powerful groups, that a representation carries and can make seem natural.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the group or issue precisely** (women, men, ethnicity, age, an event) and the product(s).
2. **Show the construction**: pick specific elements of media language (costume, pose, shot, colour, cover lines, headline wording) and say what each suggests about the group.
3. **Explain the producer's choice**: selection, construction and mediation; the purpose and target audience; the organisation's values.
4. **Judge against stereotypes**: does it reinforce, vary or challenge one? Use a feminist or ethnicity perspective where it sharpens the point.
5. **Link to context**: what in the time and society explains this representation, and how might audiences read it differently?
6. **For comparisons and judgements**, keep both products in every paragraph, say how far they are similar and different, and end with a reasoned conclusion.
:::

::: example Define and apply
What is meant by "mediation"? Give an example from a set product. (2)

Mediation is the way a representation is shaped by the producer's values, purpose and medium before it reaches the audience (1). For example, The Sun's right-wing stance mediates its Heathrow story, using words like "humiliated" and a pun implying incompetence, so the power cut is framed as a failure of the government's management of infrastructure (1).
:::

::: example Stereotypes and speed
Briefly explain why film posters use stereotypes. Refer to a set product. (4)

A poster must communicate genre, character and appeal in a few seconds, and stereotypes let audiences read it quickly because they draw on familiar ideas (1). On The Man with the Golden Gun poster, Bond in a suit with a gun is instantly recognisable as the suave, heroic masculine spy (1), while the bikini-clad women are stereotyped as glamorous and sexualised, signalling to the 1970s target audience the pleasures the film offers (1). Stereotypes therefore sell the film efficiently, although they reinforce a patriarchal ideology in which men act and women are looked at (1).
:::

::: example Under-representation
Explain why some social groups are under-represented in the media. Refer to a set product. (5)

Under-representation happens when a group appears much less than its presence in society, or rarely in powerful roles (1). Historically, glossy magazine editors assumed that cover stars of colour would reduce sales, so black and Asian people were rarely selected, and the assumption was never properly tested (1). This reflects who controlled the industry: mostly white editors and owners making choices for an audience they imagined as white (1). The Vogue July 2021 cover shows how this can change: under Edward Enninful, the magazine's first black editor, it chose Malala Yousafzai, a Pakistani Muslim woman in a headscarf, as its cover star (1). Its caption and warm, celebratory colours present her as a legend and role model, significant because audiences who rarely see themselves on such covers gain visibility, while the magazine reaches a more diverse audience (1).
:::

::: example Feminist reading
Use a feminist approach to explain the representation of women in a print set product. (6)

A feminist approach asks whether women are shown as active subjects or passive objects, and whose interests the representation serves (1). In the 1956 Quality Street advert, the man is centrally framed holding the tin, so he controls the "prize", while the two women lean in to him, their dresses echoing the colourful wrappers (2). This links women to the sweets themselves: they are decorative, consumable and defined by their desire for chocolate and a man's attention, which fits Mulvey's idea of women as objects to be looked at (1). The representation reflects 1950s patriarchal gender roles, in which men were providers and women were expected to seek romance and marriage (1). A modern audience may take an oppositional reading, seeing the women as cleverly using romance to get the chocolates, which shows how interpretations change over time (1).
:::

::: example How stereotypical?
How stereotypical are the representations of men in a set product you have studied? (10)

In GQ (August 2019), Raheem Sterling's representation is partly stereotypical: shot in a low-angle long shot, topless and muscular, with a wide stance, he embodies the traditional ideal of the strong, dominant, physically powerful man (2). The body-focused image fits the idea of men defined by their physiques, even though he is a footballer rather than a bodybuilder (1). Thick silver jewellery and a watch connote wealth, reinforcing the capitalist message that a successful man is a rich one (1). However, the black angel wings, cross tattoo and the cover line naming him a "Guardian Angel" who saved football from itself construct him as a moral, protective figure, which goes beyond the tough-man stereotype (2). A tattoo of a baby adds fatherhood to his identity, a more caring side of masculinity (1). As a black British man on a glossy cover, he also counters the under-representation of black men in this form, and his anti-racism stance gives the representation political significance (2). Judgement: the visual codes are largely stereotypical of hyper-masculinity, but the written codes and context challenge stereotypes of black footballers, so the representation is mixed rather than simply stereotypical (1).

Band: 9–10. Balanced judgement, precise evidence, context used to explain.
:::

---

:::

::: q
What is the difference between under-representation and misrepresentation? (2)
---
Under-representation: a group appears rarely or seldom in powerful roles (1); misrepresentation: a group is shown inaccurately or negatively (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what "self-representation" means and why it matters. (3)
---
When people construct their own representation, e.g. on social media or by writing and starring in their own show (1); it gives the group control over how it is seen (1), so it can challenge stereotypes created by others (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how audiences' own experiences can affect how they read a representation. (3)
---
Audiences bring their identity, beliefs and experiences to a product (1); e.g. a Black father may identify strongly with the NHS 111 advert, while someone who distrusts government may read it as propaganda (1); the time of reading matters too: the Quality Street advert is read differently now than in 1956 (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how representations can change over time. Use two set products. (4)
---
Representations reflect changing social attitudes (1). Women on the 1974 Bond poster are mainly passive and sexualised (1), while the 2021 poster gives women guns and active roles, including a black female 00 agent (1), reflecting movements for gender and racial equality such as #MeToo and Black Lives Matter (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"It's stereotypical" is not analysis.** Name the stereotype, show which media language builds it, and say whose interests it serves.
- **Don't forget the producer.** Every representation point should say why this was chosen: purpose, audience, organisation, context.
- **Compare throughout.** In the 25-mark question, answers that write about one product then the other stay in the middle band; set them side by side in each paragraph.
- **Judge historical products in context.** Explain what a 1950s or 1970s audience expected before critiquing with modern values.
- **Theory should do work**: "Mulvey's male gaze explains why the camera..." beats a memorised definition.
- **Include the written codes.** Headlines, captions and cover lines anchor representations; many answers only discuss the picture.
:::
