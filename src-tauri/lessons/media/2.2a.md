## What it is

Component 2 Section A is a detailed study of **one television genre**: **crime drama** or **sitcom**. Each option pairs a **complete contemporary episode** with a **ten-minute extract from the past**, so that you can see how the genre has **changed over time** and how it reflects its contexts. This lesson covers what every student needs whichever option they take: the **conventions** of both genres, **genre theory**, the **TV industry**, TV **audiences**, and the **exam format**.

### The Component 2 exam, Section A

| Part | Marks | What happens | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading time | 0 | **2 minutes** to read Q1(a) and (b) before the extract | 2 min |
| Viewing | 0 | a **~3-minute extract** from your set episode, shown **twice**, with **6 minutes** for notes in between; you write on a **notes sheet** | about 15 min |
| **Q1(a)** | 10 | **Analyse**: e.g. "Explore how camerawork / sound / editing / costume creates meaning in this extract", or "Explore the representations of gender in this extract" | 12–14 min |
| **Q1(b)** | 10 | **Judge**: e.g. "How typical of the genre are the characters / locations in this extract?" or "How stereotypical are the representations of ethnicity?" | 12–14 min |
| **Q2** | 10 | **Industries, audiences or contexts**, often using the past extract: "How do crime dramas or sitcoms reflect their historical contexts? Refer to The Sweeney or Friends" | 10–12 min |

Both parts of Q1 test **either** media language **or** representation, and they are marked on **analysis** (and in (b) on **judgements and conclusions**) in five bands: **9–10, 7–8, 5–6, 3–4, 1–2**. Generalised points that don't come from the extract aren't credited, although you may draw on your knowledge of the whole episode. The paper suggests about **50 minutes on Section A**, including the viewing, and 40 minutes on Section B (music).

**Notes-sheet tactic**: on the first viewing, jot quick labels for the aspect in Q1(a) (shot types, sound cues or costume) with a timestamp or scene word. In the 6-minute gap, add what each one *means*. On the second viewing, collect evidence for Q1(b).

### Crime drama conventions

| Element | Typical conventions |
|---|---|
| **Characters** | a detective or investigator (often flawed, a **maverick**), a partner or sidekick, a boss, victims, villains or suspects, forensic or technical experts |
| **Settings** | the **urban city**, police station or HQ, crime scene, interrogation room, dangerous or run-down locations |
| **Iconography** | badges and warrant cards, police tape, uniforms and protective kit, guns, police cars and sirens, screens, phones and forensic equipment |
| **Narrative** | a crime **disrupts** order, an investigation follows, and a resolution restores it (**Todorov**); **enigmas** (whodunnit, why), red herrings, **action codes** (chases, fights, countdowns), multi-strand plots and **cliffhangers** in serials |
| **Propp's roles** | **hero** (the detective), **villain** (the criminal), **helper** (the partner), **dispatcher** (the boss who sends the hero), **princess** (the victim to be saved), **false hero** |
| **Themes** | justice, law and order, good against evil, morality and corruption, the pressure of the job |
| **Technical codes** | low-key lighting and a cold blue-grey palette; handheld camera and fast editing in action; close-ups on reactions and clues; a tense non-diegetic score; diegetic sirens, radio chatter and procedural jargon for realism |

**How it has changed since the 1970s**: early British police series in the 1950s–60s presented a **reassuring**, community image of the police. The 1970s brought **gritty action** on location (**The Sweeney**): tough, rule-bending detectives in a macho, all-male world. From the 1990s, female leads and forensic detail became more common, and recent dramas feature **flawed, complex** heroes, **gender and ethnic diversity**, **serial** storylines built for **binge-watching**, and contemporary issues such as **terrorism**, radicalisation, corruption and technology (e.g. **Trigger Point**).

### Sitcom conventions

| Element | Typical conventions |
|---|---|
| **Situation** | a fixed **home** or **workplace** (the flat, the café, the office, the family house) that traps characters together |
| **Characters** | **stock characters** and **stereotypes** that the audience recognises instantly (the loser, the know-all, the romantic, the parent figure); often an **ensemble** cast |
| **Narrative** | a **circular** structure: a small problem or scheme disrupts the **status quo**, events escalate, then everything returns to normal by the end, so the next episode can start fresh; A and B plots; around 22–30 minutes |
| **Comedy** | **verbal** (one-liners, wordplay, catchphrases, sarcasm) and **visual** (slapstick, reaction shots), running gags, misunderstandings, **irony** |
| **Production** | the **multi-camera** studio sitcom shot in front of a live audience, with a **laughter track** (e.g. Friends), against the **single-camera** sitcom shot like a film, often on location with no laugh track; the **mockumentary** hybrid with **talking-head** interviews (e.g. Modern Family) |
| **Themes** | family, friendship, love, work, class, and identity and belonging |

**How it has changed since the 1990s**: the classic US studio sitcom (Friends) was multi-camera with a live audience and a mainly white, middle-class cast. Since the 2000s, **single-camera** and **mockumentary** styles have grown, and sitcoms more often play knowingly with **stereotypes**, include more **diverse** families and communities, and are made or distributed **online first** (Man Like Mobeen began on the online-only BBC Three).

### Genre theory

- **Repetition and variation** (associated with Steve Neale): a genre product repeats enough conventions to be recognisable, and varies some to stay fresh. Top answers show **both**: "typical in X, but it varies the genre by Y".
- **Genres are dynamic**: they change over time to reflect **social and cultural contexts**, technology and industry.
- **Hybridity**: genres mix, as in a crime thriller or a comedy mockumentary.
- **Why genre matters to the industry**: it reduces **financial risk**, makes products easy to **market**, and gives audiences **expected pleasures**.

### The TV industry

**Public service broadcasters (PSBs)** must meet a public-service remit (quality news, UK-made programmes, diversity, programmes for all audiences), and in return get prominent positions on TV guides and access to free-to-air spectrum.

| Broadcaster | Funding | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **BBC** | **licence fee**; no adverts; commercial arm BBC Studios sells programmes abroad | Royal Charter; mission to **inform, educate and entertain**; BBC One, BBC Three, iPlayer |
| **ITV** | **advertising** and sponsorship; subscription (ITVX Premium); ITV Studios sells programmes worldwide | commercial PSB; a **vertically integrated** producer, broadcaster and streamer |
| **Channel 4** | **advertising**, but publicly owned and not-for-profit | commissions from independent companies; remit to be innovative, diverse and to appeal to young audiences |
| **Channel 5** | advertising | commercial PSB owned by a US conglomerate |

**Commercial subscription and streaming**: Sky (subscription and advertising), **Netflix**, **Amazon Prime Video** and **Disney+** (subscription, some with cheaper **ad-supported tiers**). They are global, **non-linear** services that invest heavily in original content and **license** back catalogues.

**Key industry ideas**:
- **Linear TV** is scheduled broadcasting at set times; **non-linear** or on-demand viewing is when the viewer chooses (iPlayer, ITVX, Netflix).
- **Independent production companies** make many programmes that broadcasters **commission** (e.g. HTM Television for ITV, Tiger Aspect for the BBC).
- **Global sales and licensing**: programmes and **formats** are sold abroad, and streaming rights are licensed to other platforms (e.g. US sitcoms licensed to Netflix).
- **Scheduling**: prime time (evening) for the biggest audiences; the **9pm watershed**, before which content must be suitable for children; placing a new show next to a popular one.

**Regulation**: **Ofcom** regulates UK broadcasters, including the BBC, through the **Broadcasting Code**: protecting under-18s (the watershed), harm and offence, due accuracy and impartiality, fairness and privacy. It can fine broadcasters or require apologies. **Streaming is the challenge**: global services based abroad were lightly regulated in the UK. The **Media Act 2024** gives Ofcom powers to set standards for the biggest streamers, but content still crosses borders and is watched at any time, so the watershed means less; age ratings and parental PINs on apps do more of the work.

### TV audiences

- **Targeting**: channel brand (BBC Three and E4 for young audiences, ITV's broad family audience), scheduling, casting of stars, trailers, social media campaigns, and genre pleasures.
- **Measuring**: **Barb** measures viewing across TV sets and devices; streamers use their own **data** on what users watch.
- **Changing consumption through digital technology**:
  - **time-shifting** and catch-up (watching later on iPlayer or ITVX);
  - **binge-watching** whole series at once, with box sets, full-series drops and autoplay;
  - **mobile viewing** on phones and tablets, anywhere;
  - **second-screening**: using a phone while watching and live-posting reactions;
  - **fragmentation**: more channels and platforms, so smaller audiences for each show and fewer shared "event" viewings;
  - **global audiences** watching the same show at the same time on streaming services;
  - **older shows** finding new fans on streaming (e.g. 1970s and 1990s series).
- **Uses and Gratifications**: entertainment (escapism, suspense, laughter), information (insight into policing or different communities), personal identity (seeing people like yourself, or comparing yourself), social interaction (discussing episodes, memes, fan communities).

::: terms
- **Genre conventions**: the familiar elements (characters, settings, iconography, narrative, technical codes) that audiences expect in a genre.
- **Repetition and variation**: the idea that genre products repeat familiar conventions and vary some of them to stay fresh.
- **Hybrid genre**: a product that combines conventions from two or more genres, such as a comedy mockumentary.
- **Stock character**: a familiar character type that audiences recognise instantly, such as the maverick detective or the sitcom loser.
- **Circular narrative**: a structure that returns to the status quo at the end of an episode, typical of sitcoms.
- **Multi-camera**: a studio production method using several cameras at once, often in front of a live audience.
- **Public service broadcaster**: a broadcaster with a legal remit to serve the public, such as the BBC, ITV and Channel 4.
- **Linear television**: programmes broadcast at scheduled times on channels, as opposed to on-demand viewing.
- **Watershed**: 9pm, the time before which UK broadcasters must avoid content unsuitable for children.
- **Binge-watching**: watching several episodes of a series in one sitting, encouraged by streaming and box sets.
- **Fragmentation**: the splitting of audiences across many channels and platforms, so each programme's audience is smaller.
- **Ofcom**: the UK regulator for television, radio and, increasingly, the largest streaming services.
:::

::: method
1. **Q1(a) "Explore how [aspect] creates meaning"**: make four or five specific examples from the extract. For each, give the technical term, what it shows, and what it **means** (character, narrative, genre, representation). Use no introduction.
2. **Q1(b) "How typical…?"**: name a convention, show how the extract **repeats** it, then show where it **varies** it, and finish with a judgement ("mostly typical, but it updates the genre by…"). Explain **why** a convention is used, e.g. so audiences read the character instantly.
3. **Q2 contexts or industry**: link each context or industry point to a **specific feature** of the named product (a character, scene, production detail or way of watching).
4. **Use genre theory** (repetition and variation, dynamic genres, hybridity) and **Propp** or **Todorov** where they help a judgement.
5. **Compare past and present** in Q2 and contexts answers: the 1970s or 1990s extract against the contemporary episode shows how genres change.
6. **Timing**: about 12 minutes per 10-mark answer. Leave time for Section B.
:::

::: example Recall: conventions
Identify two conventions of the crime drama genre. (2)

A flawed or maverick detective as the hero (1); an urban setting such as a police station or crime scene (1).
:::

::: example Recall: sitcom
Give two differences between a multi-camera and a single-camera sitcom. (2)

A multi-camera sitcom is usually filmed in a studio in front of a live audience, with a laughter track, while a single-camera sitcom is shot like a film and usually has no laughter track (1). Multi-camera sitcoms use fixed studio sets and staged performances, while single-camera ones often use real locations and more varied shots, such as handheld or documentary-style camerawork (1).
:::

::: example Q2: digital technologies
Explain how digital technologies change the ways audiences consume television. Refer to the television programmes you have studied. (10)

Digital technology has turned television from a scheduled, shared, living-room experience into something audiences choose and control (1). When programmes such as The Sweeney or Friends were first shown, audiences watched them live at a fixed time on a handful of channels, so episodes were shared national events (2). Now broadcaster streaming services such as BBC iPlayer and ITVX, and global platforms such as Netflix and Amazon Prime Video, let viewers **time-shift** and watch whole series in one sitting. ITV premiered Trigger Point on ITV and ITVX at the same time, for example, and whole series of sitcoms sit on streaming services to be **binge-watched** (2). Smartphones and tablets allow **mobile** viewing on the move, which suits younger viewers, who are less likely to watch linear TV (1). Social media creates **second-screen** viewing: fans post reactions, memes and theories while they watch, gratifying the need for **social interaction** and turning a programme into an online conversation (2). Streaming also brings old shows to new generations and global audiences, but the huge choice has **fragmented** audiences, so fewer programmes reach the vast shared audiences of the 1970s (2).

This would reach the top band (9–10): detailed, accurate knowledge, with examples from both periods and theory applied.
:::

::: example Q1(b)-style judgement on genre
How typical of the crime drama genre is the contemporary episode you have studied? (10)

For Trigger Point: the episode is highly **typical** in most of its conventions but **varies** the genre in its central character and focus (1). Its settings are conventional: the urban London location, the command centre and dangerous sites such as the power station signal the genre at once and create realism (2). Its iconography of protective suits, bomb-disposal equipment, screens and explosions is expected, although it is specific to bomb disposal rather than a standard murder squad, which is a **variation** within the genre (2). The narrative repeats key conventions: suspense as Lana works against the clock, **enigmas** about who the terrorists are and why, and a **cliffhanger** to keep viewers watching, and Propp's roles fit neatly, with Lana as hero, Danny as helper and the terrorists as villains (2). The biggest variation is **gender**: a female expert leads a male-dominated unit and rescues a man, which reverses the stereotype of the male hero saving a female "princess" (2). Overall it is **typical in structure and style but updated** for a contemporary audience that expects diverse, complex leads, which shows that genres are dynamic (1).
:::

::: q
State two ways public service broadcasters are funded in the UK. (2)
---
Any two, 1 each: the licence fee (BBC); advertising (ITV, Channel 4, Channel 5); sponsorship; subscription (e.g. ITVX Premium); sales of programmes abroad (BBC Studios, ITV Studios).

::: q
Explain what is meant by the 9pm watershed. (2)
---
The time before which broadcasters must not show material unsuitable for children (1); programmes with strong violence, language or adult themes, such as many crime dramas, are scheduled after 9pm (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why sitcoms usually have a circular narrative. (3)
---
The situation returns to the status quo at the end of each episode (1), so episodes can be watched in any order or dropped into a schedule and still make sense (1); audiences get familiar pleasures, and the characters' flaws can generate new comedy each week without permanent change (1).
:::

::: q
Briefly explain how crime drama has changed since the 1970s. (4)
---
Four one-mark levels. Indicative: from all-male, macho detectives (The Sweeney) to female and ethnically diverse leads (Trigger Point); from single-episode stories to serial narratives with cliffhangers designed for binge-watching; more complex, flawed heroes; contemporary issues such as terrorism and technology; production on film on location to digital, high-budget cinematic style; audiences watching on demand on streaming services.
:::

::: q
Explain why genre is important to the television industry. (4)
---
Two marks each for two developed points. Indicative: it reduces financial risk because audiences know what to expect; it makes marketing easy (trailers can signal genre pleasures instantly); it builds loyal audiences for long-running series; it helps channels schedule and target audiences; genres can be varied to stand out while staying familiar.
:::

::: watch
- Genre answers list conventions. The marks are for explaining why a convention is used and how it is varied.
- Q1 must use **specific moments from the extract**. General points about the genre or the whole series earn little.
- "How typical…?" needs a **judgement**: typical in what, varied in what, and overall how far.
- Know the funding models precisely: the BBC has **no adverts**; ITV and Channel 4 are **advertising-funded**; Channel 4 is **publicly owned** but commercially funded.
- In Q2, answer the context or industry question; don't slip back into analysing the extract.
- Keep to about 12 minutes per 10-mark question, so Section B (music, 30 marks) gets its full 40 minutes.
:::
