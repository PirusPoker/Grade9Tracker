## What it is

Spectators are part of what makes sport special — but crowds can also intimidate, disrupt and, at worst, turn violent. This topic covers the **positive and negative influences of spectators**, the **reasons hooliganism occurs**, and the **strategies** used to combat it — which you must be able to **evaluate**, not just list.

### How spectators influence events

| Positive influences | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| **Creation of atmosphere** | noise, singing and colour make the event exciting for everyone — performers, fans at the ground and TV viewers; a great atmosphere attracts more spectators and sponsors |
| **Home-field advantage** (for the home team or individual) | a supportive crowd raises the home performers' motivation and arousal, gives them confidence, and can intimidate visiting performers; home performers also know the venue and do not have to travel |

| Negative influences | Why it harms |
|---|---|
| **Negative effect on performance through increased pressure** | a hostile or demanding crowd can raise a performer's arousal and anxiety beyond the optimum, so they "choke" — missing a penalty, a putt or a free throw; the expectation of the home crowd can weigh on home performers too |
| **Potential for crowd trouble / hooliganism** | fights, throwing objects, pitch invasions, abuse — danger to fans, performers and officials |
| **Safety costs / concerns** | clubs and police must pay for stewards, policing, CCTV and segregation; fans may stay away because they fear for their safety |
| **Negative effect on participation numbers among younger performers** | young people and families who see or fear crowd trouble or abusive behaviour may be put off attending or taking up the sport; parents may not want their children involved |

Crowd influence links to arousal (3.2.1.5a): a crowd **raises arousal**, which helps a gross-skill performer who needs high arousal but can push a fine-skill performer into over-arousal. An **extrovert** may thrive on a big crowd; an **introvert** may find it harder.

### Hooliganism

**Hooliganism** is **violent, disorderly or antisocial behaviour by spectators**, especially at football matches: fighting, vandalism, throwing missiles, racist abuse, pitch invasions.

**Reasons why hooliganism occurs:**

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Rivalries** | long-standing local, regional or national rivalries (derbies) create hostility between groups of fans |
| **Hype** | media and social media build up a match as a "battle" or a grudge match, raising tension and expectations before a ball is kicked |
| **Fuelled by alcohol / drugs** | reduce inhibitions and self-control, making violence more likely |
| **Gang culture** | organised groups ("firms") attach themselves to a club and arrange violence; belonging to the group gives identity, and individuals act differently within a crowd |
| **Frustration** | anger at an official's decision, a poor result or a team's performance spills over into aggression |
| **Display of masculinity** | some young men see fighting as a way to prove toughness and status in front of their peers |

### Strategies to combat hooliganism — and how effective they are

The spec expects you to **evaluate** strategies: say how each works, then weigh its **effectiveness** against its **drawbacks** (for example, the **high costs of security versus the safety of spectators**).

| Strategy | How it helps | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Early kick-offs** | less time for fans to drink alcohol before the match | inconvenient for travelling fans and TV schedules; fans may simply drink earlier or at home; clashes can still happen after the game |
| **All-seater stadia** | fans are in allocated seats, so crowds are easier to control and monitor; no surging terraces; troublemakers are identifiable by seat number | expensive to build and convert; lower capacity; some fans feel it spoils the atmosphere; does not affect trouble outside the ground |
| **Segregation of fans** | home and away supporters kept apart in the ground and on the way in and out, reducing direct contact and confrontation | needs extra stewards and police; fans can still clash away from the ground or in town centres; can make away fans feel penned in |
| **Improved security** (police, stewards, **CCTV**, searches) | deters trouble; troublemakers can be identified, removed and prosecuted | **very expensive** — the cost falls on clubs and taxpayers; heavy policing can feel intimidating; smaller clubs may not afford it |
| **Alcohol restrictions** (no alcohol in view of the pitch, dry trains and coaches, pubs closed near the ground) | reduces drunkenness, one of the main triggers | fans may drink before arriving; hard to enforce away from the ground; loss of income for clubs and pubs |
| **Travel restrictions / banning orders** | known hooligans are banned from attending matches and may have to surrender passports during tournaments abroad; stops repeat offenders | only works once someone has offended; needs identification and enforcement; offenders may cause trouble elsewhere |
| **Education / promotional activity / campaigns and high-profile endorsements** | campaigns (e.g. against racism and violence), fan-education schemes and messages from star players change attitudes over time, especially among young fans | slow to work; the most committed hooligans may ignore them; hard to measure success |

**A balanced verdict:** no single measure works alone. Physical measures (all-seater stadia, segregation, CCTV) have made **grounds** much safer, but they are **costly** and shift some trouble **outside** the stadium. **Banning orders** and **alcohol restrictions** target the main causes and known offenders. **Education** tackles attitudes and is the best long-term answer but works slowly. A combination has been most effective.

::: terms
- **Spectator**: a person who watches a sporting event, at the venue or through the media.
- **Atmosphere**: the mood and excitement created by spectators at an event through noise, singing and support.
- **Home-field advantage**: the benefit to the home team or performer from a supportive crowd and familiar surroundings.
- **Hooliganism**: violent, disorderly or antisocial behaviour by spectators, especially at football matches.
- **Rivalry**: long-standing competition and hostility between two teams or groups of fans.
- **Hype**: excessive build-up of an event by the media, raising tension and expectations.
- **Segregation**: keeping home and away supporters apart inside and around a venue.
- **All-seater stadium**: a ground where every spectator has an allocated seat rather than standing terraces.
- **Banning order**: a legal order preventing a known troublemaker from attending matches, sometimes with travel restrictions.
- **Gang culture**: organised groups of fans who identify with each other and plan disorder.
:::

::: method
1. **Spectator effects**: name the effect and explain the mechanism — atmosphere, home advantage (confidence, motivation, intimidation), pressure (over-arousal, choking), safety, youth participation.
2. **Reasons for hooliganism**: use the spec list — rivalries, hype, alcohol/drugs, gang culture, frustration, masculinity — each with a short explanation.
3. **Strategies**: say **how** the strategy reduces trouble.
4. **Evaluate**: weigh each strategy — effectiveness vs cost, inconvenience, and whether it simply moves the problem.
5. **Conclude**: which is most effective and why, usually "a combination".
:::

Explain how home-field advantage can help a performer. (2)

::: example Pressure on performers
Explain how spectators could have a negative effect on a basketball player taking a free throw. (3)

A hostile or noisy crowd increases the pressure on the player (1). This can raise their arousal and anxiety above the optimal level for a fine skill (1), so muscle tension increases and concentration drops, making them more likely to miss (1).

::: example Discuss two strategies
Discuss the effectiveness of segregation and improved security in reducing hooliganism at football matches. (4)

Segregation keeps rival fans apart inside and around the ground, so there is less chance of direct confrontation (1); but fans can still meet and fight away from the stadium, in town centres or at stations (1). Improved security such as CCTV, stewards and police deters trouble and allows offenders to be identified and removed (1); however, it is very expensive for clubs and taxpayers, and heavy policing can make ordinary fans feel intimidated (1).
:::

::: example Extended — evaluate strategies
Evaluate the strategies used to combat hooliganism at football matches. (9)

Hooliganism has several causes — rivalries, hype, alcohol, gang culture, frustration and a display of masculinity — so strategies target different causes. Alcohol restrictions, such as dry travel and no drinking in view of the pitch, and early kick-offs reduce drunkenness, a major trigger; but determined fans drink before arriving or at home, and early kick-offs are inconvenient for travelling supporters. All-seater stadia and segregation have made grounds far safer: every fan has a seat, crowds are controlled, and rival fans are kept apart. Their drawbacks are high building costs, lower capacities and the fact that trouble may simply move outside the ground. Improved security, including CCTV, stewards and police, deters trouble and helps identify offenders, but it is very expensive — the classic trade-off of high security costs against the safety of spectators. Banning orders and travel restrictions stop known hooligans attending or travelling abroad, which directly targets gang culture, though they only work after an offence and depend on enforcement. Education campaigns and endorsements by high-profile players change attitudes, especially among young fans, but they work slowly and the most committed hooligans may ignore them. Overall, physical measures and banning orders give the quickest, most visible improvement inside grounds, but a combination with education is needed to tackle the causes in the long term. (Level 3.)
:::

::: watch
- **Evaluating** a strategy means weighing it — e.g. extra security improves safety **but** is expensive — not listing more strategies.
- Give the **reason** a strategy works: segregation works because rival fans cannot meet, not just "it keeps fans safe".
- Home-field advantage needs a **mechanism** (confidence, motivation, intimidation of opponents).
- Link crowd pressure to **arousal**: over-arousal, anxiety, choking.
- Don't forget the **negative effect on young people's participation** — an easy mark often missed.
- Use the spec's reasons for hooliganism; "they are bad people" earns nothing.
