## What it is

Every training session and competition should begin with a **warm-up** and end with a **cool-down**. You need to know the **parts** of each, the **benefits** of each, and how to **justify** the right activities for a particular sport. The questions are very common. Marks are lost through vague wording ("do some stretches") and one well-known trap: a cool-down lowers heart rate **gradually**.

### The warm-up

A warm-up prepares the body and mind for exercise. Its parts, in a sensible order:

| Part | What it involves | Example (footballer) |
|---|---|---|
| **Gradual pulse raiser** | light, whole-body activity that slowly raises heart rate, breathing rate and body temperature | 5 minutes of easy jogging round the pitch, building pace |
| **Stretching** | stretching the muscles that will be used. Dynamic (moving) stretches suit a warm-up, and static holds can be included | leg swings, walking lunges, hamstring and groin stretches |
| **Skill-based practices / familiarisation** | practising the movements and skills of the sport at increasing intensity; getting used to the surface, equipment and conditions | passing in pairs, dribbling through cones, shooting at the goalkeeper, a few short sprints |
| **Mental preparation** | focusing, rehearsing, controlling arousal and nerves | thinking through set-piece roles; imagery of a first touch |

Together these **increase the amount of oxygen delivered to the working muscles**. Heart rate and breathing rise, and blood is redistributed towards the muscles.

**Benefits of warming up**

| Benefit | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| **Raises body (and muscle) temperature** | warm muscles are more elastic and contract faster and more forcefully |
| **Increases range of movement** | muscles, tendons and ligaments are more pliable, and synovial fluid becomes less viscous, so joints move more freely |
| **Gradual increase of effort to full pace** | the body moves from rest to full intensity in steps, not suddenly |
| **Psychological preparation** | the performer focuses, feels confident and gets arousal to the right level |
| **Practises movement skills through the whole range of movement** | rehearses the exact actions of the sport, improving coordination at the start |
| **Injury prevention** | warm, elastic muscles are less likely to strain or tear |

### The cool-down

A cool-down helps the body return to its resting state. Its parts:

| Part | What it involves | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Maintaining elevated breathing and heart rate** | continuing light exercise so they stay raised for a time | a walk or light jog after the final whistle |
| **Gradual reduction in intensity** | easing down from the jog to a walk, lowering heart and breathing rate **gradually** | jog → brisk walk → walk over 5–10 minutes |
| **Stretching** | stretching the muscles used, usually static holds | holding quadriceps, hamstring and calf stretches |

**Benefits of cooling down**

| Benefit | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| **Allows the body to recover** | heart rate, breathing and temperature come down **gradually** to resting levels |
| **Removal of lactic acid, CO2 and waste products** | keeping blood flow and breathing elevated flushes waste out of the muscles faster, helping repay the oxygen debt |
| **Prevents (delayed onset) muscle soreness / DOMS** | removing lactic acid and stretching reduces the soreness and stiffness felt 24–48 hours later |

A cool-down that says "lowers heart rate" without **gradually** does not score, because stopping dead also lowers heart rate. Stopping suddenly can let blood **pool** in the legs, which can cause dizziness. The light activity keeps the muscles pumping blood back to the heart. "Reduces the risk of injury" is **not** credited as a cool-down benefit. It is a warm-up benefit.

### Matching warm-ups and cool-downs to the activity

Warm-ups must be **specific** to the activity:
- **Sprinter:** a jog, then dynamic drills (high knees, heel flicks, strides), then block starts and build-up sprints at increasing speed. Hamstrings and hip flexors are stretched thoroughly, because maximal-speed running stretches them hard.
- **Gymnast:** a pulse raiser, then extensive flexibility work (splits, bridges, shoulder stretches), then easier versions of the routine's elements on the apparatus. Mental rehearsal of the routine.
- **Swimmer:** easy swimming in the pool, drills, then some race-pace efforts. Shoulder stretches.
- **Tennis player:** a light jog or skipping, dynamic stretches for shoulders and legs, then groundstrokes, volleys and serves in the knock-up.
- **Rugby player:** jog, dynamic stretches, then passing drills, contact rehearsal at low intensity, then sprints.
- **Chess or darts:** there is little physical demand, so a pulse raiser is barely needed. Mental preparation and skill familiarisation (practice throws) matter most. Not every part needs the same weight in every activity.

Cool-downs are similar across sports: easy movement in the same activity (a slow swim, a gentle jog), then static stretches of the muscles that did the most work.

::: terms
- **Warm-up**: activities before exercise that prepare the body and mind, including a pulse raiser, stretching, skill practice and mental preparation.
- **Pulse raiser**: light, gradually increasing whole-body exercise that raises heart rate, breathing rate and temperature.
- **Familiarisation**: getting used to the skills, surface, equipment and conditions of the activity during the warm-up.
- **Mental preparation**: focusing and rehearsing the performance and controlling arousal before starting.
- **Cool-down**: light activity and stretching after exercise that gradually returns the body to its resting state.
- **Lactic acid**: a waste product of anaerobic respiration that builds up in the muscles and causes fatigue.
- **DOMS**: delayed onset muscle soreness — pain and stiffness felt in the muscles a day or two after exercise.
- **Range of movement**: how far a joint can move; a warm-up increases it by making muscles and connective tissue more pliable.
- **Blood pooling**: blood collecting in the veins of the legs when exercise stops suddenly, which can cause dizziness.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the parts precisely:** warm-up = pulse raiser, stretching, skill-based practice/familiarisation, mental preparation. Cool-down = elevated breathing/heart rate (walk, jog), **gradual** reduction in intensity, stretching.
2. **Give a benefit with its reason:** "raises muscle temperature **so** muscles are more elastic and less likely to tear".
3. **For the cool-down, always write "gradually"** when talking about heart rate, breathing or temperature.
4. **Apply to the named activity:** say what the performer actually does in each part (the drills, the muscles stretched, the skills rehearsed).
5. **For justify or 6/9-mark questions,** give each part, an activity-specific example, and why it matters for that performer's performance or safety. Where it fits, judge which parts matter most for that activity.
:::

::: example Outline — cool-down reasons
Outline three reasons why a cool-down is important. (3)

It lowers heart rate and breathing rate gradually, allowing the body to recover (1).

It helps remove lactic acid, carbon dioxide and other waste products from the muscles (1).

It helps prevent delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) (1).
:::

::: example Explain — two warm-up benefits for a sprinter
Explain two benefits of a warm-up for a 100 m sprinter. (4)

It raises muscle temperature (1), so the muscles are more elastic and can contract more forcefully and quickly, giving a faster drive out of the blocks (1).

It helps prevent injury (1). Warm, pliable hamstrings are less likely to tear when they are stretched hard at maximum speed (1).
:::

::: example Describe a specific warm-up activity
Describe a skill-based practice that a basketball player could include in a warm-up and explain why it is included. (3)

Lay-up drills from both sides of the basket, followed by passing and dribbling at increasing speed (1).

This rehearses the exact movements of the game through their full range of movement (1).

It also familiarises the player with the court and the ball, so their coordination and accuracy are sharp from the first minute (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why "gradually" matters
Explain why the intensity of a cool-down should be reduced gradually. (2)

Light activity keeps blood flowing and breathing elevated, so lactic acid and waste products continue to be removed from the muscles (1).

Stopping suddenly can cause blood to pool in the legs, leading to dizziness, and slows recovery (1).
:::

::: example Justify — 9-mark warm-up and cool-down
Justify the warm-up and cool-down a gymnast should complete for a floor routine competition. (9)

(AO1) A warm-up includes a gradual pulse raiser, stretching, skill-based practice and mental preparation. A cool-down includes light activity to keep breathing and heart rate raised, a gradual reduction in intensity, and stretching.

(AO2) The gymnast jogs around the floor area and does skipping and star jumps to raise her pulse. She then does thorough stretches of the hamstrings, hips, shoulders and spine, including splits and bridges. Next she practises cartwheels, round-offs and her tumbling lines at increasing difficulty, and finally mentally rehearses the routine. Afterwards she walks and jogs lightly, then holds static stretches of her legs and shoulders.

(AO3) Stretching is the most important warm-up part for a gymnast. The routine demands extreme range of movement, so a greater range lets her reach full splits and a deep bridge, gaining execution marks and reducing the risk of tearing a muscle on landing. The pulse raiser raises muscle temperature, which makes that stretching safer and more effective, so it must come first. Skill practice lets her feel the spring of the floor and rehearse take-offs, which matters because small timing errors in tumbling are costly. Mental preparation calms nerves and focuses her on the sequence in front of judges. After the routine, the cool-down lowers her heart rate gradually and removes lactic acid from the explosive tumbles, reducing DOMS. That matters if she competes again the next day. Overall, a warm-up with a strong emphasis on flexibility and skill rehearsal, and a short cool-down with stretching, is fully justified for her.
:::

Give an example of a pulse raiser suitable for a swimmer and explain why it is suitable. (2)
---

:::

::: watch
- A cool-down lowers heart rate, breathing and temperature **gradually**. Without "gradually" the mark is lost.
- "Reduces the risk of injury" is a **warm-up** benefit. It is not credited as a cool-down benefit.
- Each benefit needs a **reason**: temperature → elasticity; range of movement → pliable tissues; waste removal → less DOMS.
- "Do some stretches" is too vague. Name **which** muscles and why they matter in **that** activity.
- Don't confuse DOMS (soreness **a day or two later**) with the fatigue felt immediately after exercise.
- Skill-based practice should use the **actual skills** of the sport.
:::

Justify the parts of a warm-up a 100 m sprinter should complete before a race. (6)

AO2 (2 marks): application — jog/easy strides to raise pulse; dynamic stretches and drills (high knees, heel flicks) for hamstrings, hip flexors and calves; block starts and build-up sprints at increasing speed; mental rehearsal/focus on reacting to the gun.

::: test
Evaluate the importance of a warm-up and a cool-down for a professional footballer who plays two matches a week. (9)
---
AO1 (2 marks): parts and benefits of warm-up (pulse raiser, stretching, skill practice, mental preparation; temperature, range of movement, gradual increase to full pace, psychological preparation, injury prevention) and cool-down (elevated HR/breathing, gradual reduction, stretching; recovery, lactic acid/waste removal, prevents DOMS).
AO2 (2 marks): application — pre-match jog, dynamic stretches, passing and shooting drills, short sprints, focus on tactics; post-match light jog and static stretches of hamstrings, quadriceps, calves; possibly on the pitch or in the gym.
AO3 (5 marks): evaluation — warm-up is vital because football involves sudden sprints, turns and kicks from the first minute, so cold muscles risk hamstring and groin strains that could rule him out for weeks; skill familiarisation adjusts to the pitch surface and ball, improving early touches; mental preparation sharpens focus. With two matches a week, the cool-down is arguably just as important: removing lactic acid and reducing DOMS speeds recovery so he can train and play again within three or four days; without it, stiffness may reduce performance in the next match and raise injury risk. Limitations — a warm-up cannot prevent all injuries (e.g. contact injuries from tackles); a very long warm-up could cause fatigue; cool-downs are sometimes skipped after matches in practice. Judgement: both are essential; the warm-up protects performance on the day, and the cool-down protects performance across the week.
Levels: 7–9 — accurate, detailed knowledge; clear application to the footballer's schedule; thorough evaluation with a well-reasoned judgement. 4–6 — knowledge evident but uneven (e.g. warm-up much more developed); some application; evaluation present but one-sided or undeveloped. 1–3 — limited knowledge; little application; little or no evaluation. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
