## What it is

Why do people take part in physical activity, exercise and sport — and what does it do for them? This topic links participation to **health**, **well-being** and **fitness**, split into **physical**, **mental (emotional)** and **social** benefits, and asks how exercise can be matched to the **different needs** of different people. It connects to Paper 1's 3.1.3.1 (health and fitness definitions) and to the next topic, the consequences of *not* being active.

### The key ideas

- **Health** is a state of complete **physical, mental and social well-being**, not merely the absence of disease or illness. It has three parts — which is why this topic is organised in three.
- **Well-being** is how well a person feels and functions — being comfortable, healthy and happy physically, mentally and socially.
- **Fitness** is the **ability to meet the demands of the environment** — to cope with the physical demands of daily life, work and sport without undue fatigue.
- **Physical activity, exercise and sport** are the means: regular participation improves all three kinds of health and well-being as well as fitness.

### Physical health and well-being

| Benefit | How participation brings it about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Improves heart function** | regular aerobic exercise strengthens and enlarges the heart (cardiac hypertrophy), increasing stroke volume and lowering resting heart rate, so the heart works more efficiently | a jogger's resting heart rate falls over months of training |
| **Improves efficiency of the body systems** | the respiratory system takes in and uses oxygen better; the muscular and skeletal systems become stronger; circulation improves | stronger bones from weight-bearing exercise; better lung function |
| **Reduces the risk of some illness** | lowers the risk of conditions such as heart disease, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, some cancers and osteoporosis | regular walkers are less likely to develop type 2 diabetes |
| **Able to do everyday tasks** | strength, flexibility and stamina make ordinary activities easy — climbing stairs, carrying shopping, playing with children | an older adult who stays active can still garden and lift grandchildren |
| **To avoid obesity** | exercise uses energy (calories), helping balance energy intake and output so excess fat is not stored | a teenager who plays sport most days maintains a healthy weight |

### Mental (emotional) health and well-being

| Benefit | How participation brings it about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduces stress / tension** | exercise gives a break from worries, uses up the body's stress response, and lets people "switch off" | a student goes for a run after revising to clear their head |
| **Release of feel-good hormones (serotonin)** | exercise triggers the release of hormones such as **serotonin** that improve mood and give a sense of well-being | feeling happier and more positive after a swim |
| **Able to control emotions** | sport teaches performers to manage frustration, anger and nerves, and to channel emotions positively | a young footballer learns to accept a referee's decision without losing their temper |

Participation can also build **confidence and self-esteem** (achieving goals, improving skill, a better body image), which is part of good mental well-being.

### Social health and well-being

| Benefit | How participation brings it about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Opportunities to socialise / make friends** | clubs, classes and teams bring people together regularly | joining a running club in a new town; a parkrun each weekend |
| **Cooperation** | working with others towards a shared aim, following rules and respecting others | doubles partners coordinating their positions in tennis |
| **Teamwork** | team sports require communication, trust and shared roles | a netball team working set plays together |
| **Essential human needs (food, shelter, clothing)** | being fit and healthy helps a person work and earn, so they can provide these basic needs for themselves and their family | a fit, healthy adult can hold down a job and support their household |

### Fitness

| Benefit | How participation brings it about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Improves fitness** | training improves the components of fitness — cardiovascular endurance, strength, flexibility and so on | a beginner can run 5 km after a ten-week programme |
| **Reduces the chances of injury** | stronger muscles, tendons and ligaments and better flexibility protect joints | a hockey player with good flexibility is less likely to strain a hamstring |
| **Can aid the physical ability to work** | fitness helps people cope with physically demanding jobs — **on your feet all day**, **manual labour** — without excessive tiredness | a nurse on a 12-hour shift; a builder carrying materials |

### Reasons for participating

People take part for a mix of these reasons: to **improve health** and **avoid illness**, to **lose weight** or look good, to **relieve stress**, to **meet friends**, for **enjoyment**, to **compete** and win, to **improve fitness for work** or everyday life, or because a doctor or family member encouraged them.

### Exercise can suit the needs of different people

Exercise works best when it fits the person. Match the activity to their **age**, **fitness**, **health**, **ability or disability**, and **lifestyle**:

| Person | Needs | Suitable activity and why |
|---|---|---|
| **Older adult** | maintain mobility, balance and bone strength; low impact on joints; social contact | walking, swimming, aqua aerobics, yoga, walking football — low impact, social, maintains flexibility and balance |
| **Busy working parent** | limited time; convenience | short high-intensity sessions, home workouts, cycling to work — fit into a tight schedule |
| **Teenager** | fun, friends, developing skills and fitness | team sports, clubs, dance — social benefits plus physical fitness |
| **Someone overweight** | lose weight safely without strain on joints | swimming, cycling, brisk walking — low impact, burns calories |
| **Disabled person** | adapted, accessible activity | wheelchair basketball, adapted swimming, seated exercise — inclusive, builds fitness and social contact |
| **Someone with a stressful job** | stress relief | running, yoga, a team sport after work — releases serotonin, a break from pressure |

::: terms
- **Health**: a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease or illness.
- **Well-being**: how well a person feels and functions physically, mentally and socially.
- **Fitness**: the ability to meet the demands of the environment, including daily life, work and sport.
- **Physical health**: the healthy working of the body and its systems, free from illness.
- **Mental (emotional) health**: a state of well-being in which a person copes with stress and controls their emotions.
- **Social health**: having friends, support and positive relationships, and having basic needs such as food, shelter and clothing met.
- **Serotonin**: a feel-good hormone released during exercise that improves mood.
- **Cooperation**: working together with others towards a shared aim, following rules and respecting each other.
- **Sedentary**: sitting or lying down for long periods with little physical activity.
:::

::: method
1. **Sort every benefit** into physical, mental, social or fitness — the heading the question asks for is the only one that scores.
2. **Explain how** participation causes the benefit (e.g. "aerobic exercise strengthens the heart, so resting heart rate falls").
3. **Apply to the named person** — their age, job, lifestyle or activity.
4. **For "suit the needs" questions**, name an activity, then justify it with the person's needs (low impact, time, social, adapted).
5. **In 6/9-mark answers**, cover all three kinds of health, analyse links between them (a team sport gives fitness *and* friends *and* reduced stress), and evaluate which matters most for this person.
:::

::: example Mental benefits
State two mental health benefits of taking part in physical activity. (2)

Reduces stress/tension (1). Releases feel-good hormones such as serotonin (1).
:::

::: example Social benefit
Explain one social benefit of joining a rowing club. (2)

Rowing in a crew means working closely with others to keep time and coordinate the stroke (1), so the rower develops cooperation and teamwork and makes friends at the club (1).
:::

::: example Fitness for work
Explain how being fit can help a postal worker at work. (2)

A postal worker walks long distances and carries heavy bags, so good cardiovascular and muscular endurance means they can cope with being on their feet all day (1) without becoming overly tired, and good strength reduces the chance of injury when lifting (1).
:::

::: example Suiting different needs
Suggest a suitable physical activity for a 70-year-old who wants to stay healthy, and justify your choice. (3)

Aqua aerobics (1). The water supports body weight, so it is low impact and places little stress on the joints (1); it maintains flexibility, heart function and the ability to do everyday tasks, and a class gives the chance to socialise and make friends (1).
:::

::: example Extended — analyse team sport for a teenager
Analyse how taking part in team sport can improve the health and well-being of a teenager. (6)

Physically, regular football training improves heart function — the heart gets stronger and resting heart rate falls — and the energy used helps the teenager avoid obesity, lowering the risk of illness such as type 2 diabetes later in life. Mentally, exercise releases serotonin, so the teenager feels happier, and training gives a break from exam stress; learning to cope with a bad decision or a loss teaches them to control their emotions. Socially, being in a squad brings friends outside school and develops cooperation and teamwork, as players must communicate and trust each other in set plays. These benefits reinforce one another: friendships keep the teenager attending training, which sustains the physical and mental benefits, and improved fitness also reduces the chance of injury. Team sport therefore improves all three aspects of health at a stage when habits for adult life are being formed. (Level 3.)
:::

- **Explain how** — "it's good for your heart" is weaker than "strengthens the heart so resting heart rate falls".

- When asked to **suit the needs** of a person, the justification must refer to **their** needs (age, joints, time, disability).
