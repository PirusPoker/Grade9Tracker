## What it is

The **heart** is a muscular pump made of two pumps side by side. The **right side** receives deoxygenated blood from the body and pumps it to the lungs. The **left side** receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it to the body. How much blood it pumps each minute, the **cardiac output**, decides how much oxygen reaches the working muscles.

### Structure of the heart

- **Atria** (left and right): the two upper chambers, which **receive** blood coming into the heart.
- **Ventricles** (left and right): the two lower chambers, which **pump** blood out of the heart. The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pumps blood all round the body.

| Blood vessel | Connects | Blood carried |
|---|---|---|
| **Vena cava** | body → **right atrium** | deoxygenated |
| **Pulmonary artery** | **right ventricle** → lungs | **deoxygenated** |
| **Pulmonary vein** | lungs → **left atrium** | **oxygenated** |
| **Aorta** | **left ventricle** → body | oxygenated |

Arteries carry blood **away** from the heart and veins carry it **towards** the heart. That is why the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood: it is leaving the heart for the lungs. It is the most commonly lost mark in this topic.

### The pathway of blood

1. **Deoxygenated** blood from the body enters the **right atrium** through the **vena cava**.
2. It passes into the **right ventricle**.
3. The right ventricle pumps it through the **pulmonary artery** to the **lungs**.
4. **Gaseous exchange** happens: the blood picks up oxygen and gives up carbon dioxide, so it becomes **oxygenated**.
5. The **pulmonary vein** carries the oxygenated blood back to the **left atrium**.
6. It passes into the **left ventricle**.
7. The left ventricle ejects it into the **aorta**, which carries it to the **body**.

It is a loop, so a question can start anywhere, for example "starting at the right ventricle" or "starting at the lungs". Carry on round until you reach the point where you started, or the end the question asks for.

### The cardiac cycle: diastole and systole

- **Diastole** is the **filling** phase. The heart muscle relaxes, and blood flows into the atria and then into the ventricles.
- **Systole** is the **ejection** phase. The heart muscle contracts. The atria contract first to push blood into the ventricles; then the ventricles contract to eject blood into the pulmonary artery and aorta.

**Valves** keep the blood moving one way. They **open because of pressure** (blood pushing on them from the side that is contracting) and **close to prevent backflow**. You do not need the valve names.

Cardiac cycle **starting at the right ventricle**: the right ventricle contracts (systole) and ejects blood into the pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium fills (diastole) → the left atrium contracts and the left ventricle fills → the left ventricle contracts (systole), ejecting blood into the aorta → body → vena cava → the right atrium fills (diastole) → the right atrium contracts and the right ventricle fills.

### Cardiac output, stroke volume and heart rate

| Term | Definition | Typical units |
|---|---|---|
| **Heart rate (HR)** | the number of times the heart beats per minute | bpm |
| **Stroke volume (SV)** | the volume of blood pumped out of the heart (left ventricle) **in each beat** | ml |
| **Cardiac output (Q)** | the volume of blood pumped out of the heart **per minute** | litres/min (or ml/min) |

**Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate**, or Q = SV × HR.

**Worked calculations** (the numbers are illustrative):
- At rest: SV = 70 ml, HR = 72 bpm. Q = 70 × 72 = 5,040 ml/min = **5.04 l/min**.
- During exercise: SV = 110 ml, HR = 150 bpm. Q = 110 × 150 = 16,500 ml/min = **16.5 l/min**.
- Rearranged: if Q = 20 l/min and HR = 160 bpm, SV = Q ÷ HR = 20,000 ÷ 160 = **125 ml**.
- If Q = 5 l/min and SV = 100 ml, HR = Q ÷ SV = 5,000 ÷ 100 = **50 bpm**.

Convert litres to millilitres (× 1,000) before dividing, and always show the working.

During exercise both **HR and SV rise**, so **cardiac output rises**: more blood, and therefore more oxygen, reaches the working muscles and more carbon dioxide is removed. A trained athlete's heart has a larger stroke volume (see 3.1.1.4), so they can deliver the same resting cardiac output with a lower heart rate.

### Interpreting heart rate graphs

Exam graphs show heart rate (bpm) against time. In words, this is a typical graph for a runner doing a steady 5-minute run:

| Time (min) | −2 | −1 | 0 (start) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 (stop) | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| HR (bpm) | 70 | 85 | 95 | 130 | 150 | 152 | 152 | 152 | 120 | 95 | 80 |

What to read from it:
- **Anticipatory rise** (−2 to 0 min): heart rate rises *before* exercise starts, because the hormone **adrenaline** is released in anticipation, preparing the body by sending more blood to the muscles.
- **Rapid rise** (0–2 min): exercise starts, the muscles demand more oxygen, and heart rate climbs quickly.
- **Plateau / steady state** (2–5 min): at a constant intensity, heart rate levels off because oxygen supply meets demand.
- **Recovery** (5–10 min): heart rate falls quickly at first, then more slowly, back towards resting as oxygen debt is repaid.

**Changes in intensity** show up directly. In an interval session, heart rate climbs during each hard effort and falls in each rest, giving a jagged "saw-tooth" line. A higher intensity gives a higher plateau.

::: terms
- **Atrium**: an upper chamber of the heart that receives blood coming into the heart.
- **Ventricle**: a lower chamber of the heart that pumps blood out to the lungs or the body.
- **Vena cava**: the vein that returns deoxygenated blood from the body to the right atrium.
- **Pulmonary artery**: the artery that carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs.
- **Pulmonary vein**: the vein that carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium.
- **Aorta**: the artery that carries oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the body.
- **Diastole**: the phase of the cardiac cycle when the heart relaxes and the chambers fill with blood.
- **Systole**: the phase of the cardiac cycle when the heart contracts and blood is ejected from the chambers.
- **Heart rate**: the number of times the heart beats per minute (bpm).
- **Stroke volume**: the volume of blood pumped out of the heart in each beat.
- **Cardiac output**: the volume of blood pumped out of the heart per minute; stroke volume multiplied by heart rate.
- **Anticipatory rise**: an increase in heart rate before exercise starts, caused by the release of adrenaline.
:::

::: method
1. **Pathway or cycle**: start exactly where the question says, name every chamber *and* vessel in order, and say where the blood becomes oxygenated (at the lungs). Include diastole (filling) and systole (ejection) if the question says "cardiac cycle".
2. **Calculations**: write the formula, substitute with units, convert litres ↔ ml, then give the answer with units. Rearrange as SV = Q ÷ HR or HR = Q ÷ SV.
3. **Explain a change in cardiac output**: HR and/or SV up means more blood per minute, which means more oxygen to the muscles and more carbon dioxide removed.
4. **Graphs**: quote values and times from the graph. Name the section (anticipatory rise, steady state, recovery) and explain *why* it happens.
5. **Extended answers**: knowledge of structure, cycle and Q (AO1), applied to the named performer's event (AO2), then the effect on oxygen supply and performance (AO3).
:::

::: example Pathway from a given chamber
Describe the pathway of blood through the heart, starting at the right atrium and ending in the aorta. (4)

Deoxygenated blood passes from the right atrium into the right ventricle (1), which pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs (1). Gaseous exchange oxygenates it, and the pulmonary vein carries it back to the left atrium (1). It passes into the left ventricle, which ejects it into the aorta (1).
:::

::: example Calculation
A swimmer has a stroke volume of 120 ml and a heart rate of 160 bpm during a race. Calculate her cardiac output. Show your working. (2)

Q = SV × HR = 120 × 160 (1) = 19,200 ml/min = 19.2 l/min (1).
:::

::: example Rearranging
A trained cyclist has a resting cardiac output of 5.4 l/min and a resting heart rate of 54 bpm. Calculate his resting stroke volume. (2)

SV = Q ÷ HR = 5,400 ÷ 54 (1) = 100 ml (1).
:::

::: example Anticipatory rise
Explain why a sprinter's heart rate rises while she waits on the start line. (2)

Adrenaline is released in anticipation of the race (1), which increases heart rate so that more blood and oxygen are already flowing to the muscles when the race starts (1).
:::

::: example Graph interpretation
Using the heart rate table in the lesson, describe and explain what happens to the runner's heart rate between 2 and 5 minutes and between 5 and 10 minutes. (4)

Between 2 and 5 minutes it levels off at around 150–152 bpm, a plateau or steady state (1), because the runner is working at a constant intensity and oxygen supply now meets demand (1). From 5 to 10 minutes it falls from 152 to 80 bpm, quickly at first then more slowly (1), as exercise has stopped and the body recovers, repaying the oxygen debt (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Pulmonary artery = deoxygenated; pulmonary vein = oxygenated.** Arteries go away from the heart, whatever the blood.
- Start the pathway **exactly where the question says**. Starting at the vena cava when asked to start at the right ventricle costs marks.
- Do not skip the **lungs** between the pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein. That is where the blood is oxygenated.
- Diastole = **filling**, systole = **ejection**. Swapping them loses both marks.
- In calculations, **convert units** (18 l = 18,000 ml) and give units in the answer. A bare number may not score.
- The anticipatory rise is caused by **adrenaline**, before exercise. It is not the rise at the start of exercise.
:::

::: test
A table shows a netball player's heart rate: 72 bpm ten minutes before the match, 95 bpm as she walks on court, 165 bpm after five minutes of play, 150 bpm during a time-out and 170 bpm when play restarts. Using the data, describe and explain the changes in her heart rate. (4)
---
Heart rate rises from 72 to 95 bpm before play starts (1) — an anticipatory rise caused by adrenaline (1); it rises to 165 bpm during play because the muscles need more oxygen / cardiac output must increase (1); it falls to 150 bpm during the time-out as intensity drops, then rises to 170 bpm as intensity rises again when play restarts (1).

Valves open due to pressure when a chamber contracts, letting blood through (1), and close to prevent backflow, so all the blood ejected moves forward to the lungs/body (1).
