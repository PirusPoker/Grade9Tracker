## What it is

Paper 2 opens with sports psychology, and the first building blocks are three ideas: what a **skill** is, how skills are **classified**, and what kind of **goal** a performer is chasing. They reappear across the paper — classification decides the arousal level a skill needs (3.2.1.5a), the guidance a beginner gets (3.2.1.4) and how the information processing model plays out (3.2.1.3) — so get the definitions exact.

### Skill and ability

A **skill** is a **learned** action or behaviour, carried out with the intention of producing a set result **consistently**, with the least possible time and energy. A skilled performer makes it look easy: a netball shooter who scores from the same spot almost every time, with no wasted movement.

An **ability** is an **inherited**, fairly stable trait that a person is born with and that sets their **potential** to learn skills. Examples: balance, coordination, reaction time, flexibility, spatial awareness.

| | Skill | Ability |
|---|---|---|
| Where it comes from | **learned** through practice and coaching | **inherited** — you are born with it |
| Does it change? | improves with practice | fairly stable, sets the ceiling |
| Example | a tennis serve, a lay-up, a tumble turn | coordination, balance, reaction time |
| Relationship | skills are **built from** abilities | abilities **underpin** many skills |

The link matters in answers: a gymnast's cartwheel (skill) depends on balance and coordination (abilities). Someone with good natural balance will learn it faster, but nobody is born able to cartwheel.

### The four classification continua

Skills are placed on a **continuum** — a line between two extremes — rather than in a box, because most skills have some of each. The exam asks you to **choose** a position and **justify** it by pointing to what happens in the example.

| Continuum | One end | Other end | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Basic ↔ complex** | **Basic**: simple, few decisions, little concentration, learned quickly (running, a forward roll) | **Complex**: many decisions and sub-routines, high concentration and coordination, takes long to learn (a somersault with a twist, a pole vault, a tennis serve) | How much thinking and coordination does it need? |
| **Open ↔ closed** | **Open**: done in a changing, unpredictable environment; performer must adapt to opponents, team-mates, ball, weather (a pass in football, a tackle in rugby) | **Closed**: done in a stable, predictable environment; the same every time, not affected by others (a free throw, a golf tee shot, a vault) | Does the environment change while the skill is performed? |
| **Self-paced ↔ externally paced** | **Self-paced**: the performer decides when to start and how fast to go (a serve, a javelin throw, a penalty flick) | **Externally paced**: the start and speed are set by something outside the performer — an opponent, the ball, a starting gun (receiving a serve, a sprint start, blocking a spike) | Who controls when it starts and how fast? |
| **Gross ↔ fine** | **Gross**: large muscle groups, big powerful movements, little precision (a shot put, a rugby tackle, a sprint) | **Fine**: small muscle groups, small precise movements, high hand–eye coordination (a snooker shot, a dart throw, spin bowling release, a putt) | How big are the movements and muscles involved? |

**Justifying a classification** — the mark comes from the reason, and the reason must be tied to the example:

- **Closed**: "A basketball free throw is closed because the environment is stable: nobody can move or block the shooter, and the basket and distance are the same every time."
- **Open**: "Passing in hockey is open because the performer must adjust to the positions of team-mates and defenders, which keep changing."
- **Self-paced**: "A high jump is self-paced because the athlete decides when to start the run-up and how fast to approach."
- **Externally paced**: "Returning a badminton smash is externally paced because the opponent's shot decides when and how fast the player must react."
- **Fine**: "A snooker cue action is fine because it uses small muscles in the fingers and wrist to make a precise contact."
- **Complex**: "A tumble turn is complex because the swimmer must judge the wall, time the rotation, push off and streamline — several sub-routines with a lot of coordination."

**Watch the context**: the same action can shift along a continuum. A penalty in football is fairly closed (stable positions at the moment of the kick), but the keeper's dive to save it is externally paced (the kicker decides when). Classify the exact action named in the question.

### Types of goal

| Goal | What it is | Example | Controllable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Performance goal** | a target about **your own performance** — improving on what you did before, **with no comparison to others** | "Improve my 1500 m time from 5:10 to 5:00"; "raise my first-serve percentage to 65%"; "make 8 out of 10 free throws" | yes — depends only on the performer |
| **Outcome goal** | a target about the **result** — **winning**, finishing position, beating an opponent | "Win the county final"; "finish in the top three"; "beat St Mary's" | no — depends on opponents, officials, luck |

An **appropriate target** is one that fits the performer. A club runner might set a performance goal of a personal best; a professional team might set an outcome goal of promotion. Using and evaluating these goals — why outcome goals are usually avoided, especially by beginners — is the next topic, 3.2.1.2.

::: terms
- **Skill**: a learned action carried out to bring about a set result consistently, with the least time and energy.
- **Ability**: an inherited, stable trait a person is born with that sets their potential to learn skills.
- **Continuum**: a line between two extremes on which a skill is placed, because most skills share features of both ends.
- **Basic skill**: a simple skill needing few decisions and little concentration, learned quickly, such as running.
- **Complex skill**: a skill with many decisions and sub-routines that needs high concentration and coordination, such as a twisting somersault.
- **Open skill**: a skill performed in a changing environment, where the performer must adapt to others, the ball or conditions.
- **Closed skill**: a skill performed in a stable, predictable environment that is not affected by others, done the same way each time.
- **Self-paced skill**: a skill where the performer decides when to start it and how fast to perform it.
- **Externally paced skill**: a skill whose start and speed are controlled by something outside the performer, such as an opponent or a gun.
- **Gross skill**: a skill using large muscle groups in big, powerful movements with little precision.
- **Fine skill**: a skill using small muscle groups in small, precise movements with high accuracy.
- **Performance goal**: a target about improving one's own performance, with no comparison to other people.
- **Outcome goal**: a target about the result of a competition, such as winning or finishing position.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely.** Skill = *learned*, consistent, efficient; ability = *inherited*, stable, underpins skills. Never define a skill with the word "skill".
2. **Name the classification the question asks for** (only the continuum named), then state where the example sits.
3. **Justify with the example's own detail**: the environment (open/closed), who controls timing (self/externally paced), the size of muscles and precision (gross/fine), the number of decisions and sub-routines (basic/complex).
4. **Classify the exact action**, not the sport — "the pass", "the serve", "the tackle".
5. **For goals**, state whether the target is about personal performance (performance goal) or the result (outcome goal), and make the example concrete: a number, a time, a position.
6. **In 6- and 9-mark answers**, do all three things: know the term (AO1), apply it to the performer (AO2), then explain what the classification *means* for how the skill is performed or practised (AO3).
:::

::: example Define and give an example
Define ability and give a sporting example. (2)

An ability is an inherited, stable trait that a person is born with, which determines their potential to learn skills (1). For example, good reaction time helps a sprinter respond quickly to the starting gun (1).
:::

::: example Classify and justify — open/closed
Classify a hockey player dribbling past a defender on the open–closed continuum. Justify your answer. (3)

The dribble is an open skill (1). The environment keeps changing: the defender moves to close down and tackle, so the player must keep adjusting direction and speed (1). Team-mates and other opponents are moving too, so the player cannot perform the dribble the same way twice and must make decisions during it (1).
:::

::: example Classify and justify — two continua
A golfer is taking a tee shot. Using the self-paced/externally paced and gross/fine continua, classify the tee shot and justify each classification. (4)

Self-paced (1): the golfer decides when to begin the swing and how quickly to perform it; no opponent or signal controls the timing (1). It sits towards the gross end but is not fully gross (1): the swing uses large muscles of the legs, trunk and shoulders to generate power, although the grip and clubface control need some precision from the hands and wrists (1).
:::

::: example Performance and outcome goals
Rafe is a county-level 400 m runner. Give an example of a performance goal and an outcome goal for him. (2)

Performance goal: to lower his personal best from 51.8 seconds to 51.2 seconds by the end of the season (1). Outcome goal: to win the 400 m at the county championships (1).
:::

::: example Extended — justify classifications
Justify the classification of three skills used by a netball player. (6)

A shooter's shot at goal is closed and fine. Although the defender stands a metre away, the post, ring and distance do not change, and the shooter repeats the same technique each time, so it is towards the closed end; it relies on small muscles of the wrist and fingers for accuracy, so it is fine. A chest pass to a team-mate cutting into space is open, and sits near the middle of the gross–fine continuum: the thrower must judge a moving receiver and moving defenders, and the power comes from the arms and shoulders while the release needs accuracy. Intercepting a pass is open and externally paced: the thrower decides when and where the ball goes, so the defender must read the flight and react, and the positions of every player keep changing. Because the interception is open and externally paced, a coach would practise it in game-like drills with varied passes, whereas the shot, being closed, can be grooved by repeated practice from set spots. (Level 3: accurate classifications, each applied to a netball action and justified with the effect on how the skill is performed or trained.)
:::

---
A learned action/behaviour carried out to bring about a predetermined result consistently, with the least time and energy (1). Do not accept an answer that uses "skill" to define itself or describes an inherited trait.
:::

::: q
Give one sporting example of a basic skill. Justify your choice. (3)
---
Example, e.g. jogging during a warm-up / a chest pass (1). Justification: few decisions need to be made (1); little concentration or coordination is required / it is learned quickly and can be done by beginners (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a sprint start is externally paced. (2)
---
The start is controlled by the starting gun/starter, not the athlete (1); the sprinter must react to the signal, so they cannot choose when to begin (1).
:::

::: q
Classify a snooker shot on the gross–fine continuum. Justify your answer. (3)
---
Fine (1). Uses small muscle groups in the fingers, wrist and forearm (1); needs very precise, controlled movement and hand–eye coordination to strike the cue ball accurately (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between skill and ability. (2)
---
A skill is learned through practice, whereas an ability is inherited/born with (1); abilities (e.g. balance) underpin skills (e.g. a cartwheel), so abilities set the potential for learning skills (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a performance goal might be more useful than an outcome goal for a club swimmer. (3)
---
A performance goal depends only on the swimmer's own time/technique (1), so the swimmer is in control of whether it is reached (1); an outcome goal such as winning depends on how fast the other swimmers are, so the swimmer could improve and still "fail", which can demotivate (1).
:::

::: watch
- A classification mark needs a **justification tied to the example** — "it is closed because it's always the same" is weaker than naming what is stable (the ball is still, nobody can interfere).
- Classify the **action**, not the sport: "football" cannot be classified; "a free kick" or "a slide tackle" can.
- **Open** is about the environment changing; **externally paced** is about who controls the timing. Students often swap them.
- **Ability is inherited; skill is learned.** Calling ability "how good you are at something" scores nothing.
- A performance goal has **no social comparison** — "beat my rival's time" is an outcome-style target, not a performance goal.
- Give a **specific sporting example** ("a tennis serve"), not a vague one ("in tennis").
:::
