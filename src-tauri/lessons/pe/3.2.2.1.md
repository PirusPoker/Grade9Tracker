## What it is

**Engagement patterns** are the trends in **who takes part** in physical activity and sport — how often, in which activities, and how many drop out. Participation differs between **social groups**, and the exam wants you to explain **why**, by making a **justified link** between a **factor** and a **group**. A factor on its own scores nothing; a factor linked to a group, with the effect on participation, scores.

### The five social groups

| Group | Typical pattern (in general terms) |
|---|---|
| **Gender** | women and girls have traditionally taken part less than men and boys, and teenage girls are a group where drop-out is common; participation differs by activity (more women in fitness classes, more men in team contact sports) |
| **Race / religion / culture** | some ethnic minority and religious groups are under-represented in certain sports and over-represented in others; participation can be shaped by tradition, dress, timing and experiences of discrimination |
| **Age** | participation tends to fall with age, and changes type — from team sport in youth towards lower-impact activities such as walking, swimming and golf later |
| **Family / friends / peers** | people take part more if their family is active and their friends play; peer pressure can push in either direction |
| **Disability** | disabled people take part less than non-disabled people, often because of access, provision and attitudes rather than the impairment itself |

### The twelve factors — and how each links to groups

| Factor | How it affects participation | Groups it links to most clearly |
|---|---|---|
| **Attitudes** | the views of the person and the people around them about whether an activity is "for them" | girls told a sport "isn't for girls"; older people who think exercise is for the young; negative attitudes to disabled people |
| **Role models** | seeing someone like yourself succeed inspires you to try; few role models means fewer people start | women's sport with little coverage has fewer visible role models; ethnic minority groups under-represented in a sport; Paralympians inspiring disabled people |
| **Accessibility** (to facilities, clubs, activities) | whether you can physically get to and use a facility or join a club | disabled people need ramps, lifts, accessible changing rooms and adapted equipment; older people need nearby, low-impact sessions; rural areas have fewer clubs |
| **Media coverage** | what is shown on TV, online and in the press shapes what people want to try and who they see doing it | less coverage of women's and disability sport means fewer role models; big events can cause a short-term rise in participation |
| **Sexism / stereotyping** | fixed ideas about who "should" do an activity (e.g. that boys do not dance or girls do not play rugby) put people off | gender (both directions); stereotypes about ethnic groups being "suited" to certain sports; assumptions about what disabled or older people can do |
| **Culture / religion / religious festivals** | beliefs, customs and festivals may affect dress, mixed-sex activity, the day or time people can take part | some Muslim women need single-sex sessions or modest kit; fasting in Ramadan reduces energy; activity on a holy day may be avoided |
| **Family commitments** | caring for children or relatives, or household duties, leaves less time and energy | parents of young children, especially mothers; young carers; people caring for elderly relatives |
| **Available leisure time** | the free time left after work, school and commitments | working-age adults in full-time jobs have less; retired people may have more; students revising for exams have less |
| **Familiarity** | people take part in what they know — activities their family did, that their school offered, or that they have seen | children of active parents; ethnic groups with strong traditions in particular sports (e.g. cricket); older people returning to a sport played when young |
| **Education** | what schools teach and how well they teach it; knowing the health benefits of activity | school PE shapes lifelong habits; people who understand the health benefits are more likely to exercise; a poor PE experience can put girls off |
| **Socio-economic factors / disposable income** | the money left after essentials determines whether you can afford fees, kit, travel and memberships | lower-income families; expensive sports (golf, sailing, skiing, equestrian) vs cheap ones (running, football); disabled people may face extra costs for adapted equipment |
| **Adaptability / inclusiveness** | whether an activity can be changed so everyone can take part, and whether people are made to feel welcome | disabled people (wheelchair basketball, sitting volleyball, guide runners); older people (walking football, walking netball); beginner-friendly and women-only sessions |

### Making a justified link

The mark scheme rewards **factor + group + effect**:

- Weak: "Media coverage affects participation." (no group, no effect)
- Better: "Women's sport gets less media coverage." (group, but no effect on participation)
- Full: "Women's sport gets less media coverage than men's, so girls see fewer female role models, which means fewer are inspired to take up the sport." (factor → group → effect)

**Factors can increase as well as reduce participation.** Walking football is an **adaptation** that **increases** participation among older men; a club offering free taster sessions reduces the **disposable income** barrier; high-profile female role models raise girls' interest.

### Reading participation data

Paper 2 can ask you to read a table or chart of participation by group. Use the figures:

*Fictional survey: percentage of each age group doing at least 150 minutes of activity a week*

| Age group | 16–24 | 25–34 | 35–54 | 55–74 | 75+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Men (%) | 72 | 66 | 61 | 53 | 34 |
| Women (%) | 62 | 60 | 58 | 52 | 28 |

What it shows: participation **falls with age** for both men and women — for men from 72% at 16–24 to 34% at 75+. **Men take part more than women at every age**, but the gap is largest at 16–24 (10 percentage points) and narrows in middle age (1 point at 55–74). Explanations: older people may have health problems, less confidence and fewer suitable activities; the gender gap among young adults may reflect stereotyping, fewer role models and less media coverage of women's sport.

::: terms
- **Engagement patterns**: trends in who takes part in physical activity and sport, how often and in which activities.
- **Social group**: a group of people who share a characteristic, such as gender, age, ethnicity or disability.
- **Role model**: a person others look up to and want to copy, who can inspire them to take part in an activity.
- **Accessibility**: how easy it is to reach and use facilities, clubs and activities, including for disabled people.
- **Stereotyping**: a fixed, oversimplified belief about what a group of people can or should do.
- **Sexism**: prejudice or discrimination against people because of their sex, usually against women.
- **Disposable income**: the money a person has left to spend after paying for essentials such as housing and food.
- **Available leisure time**: the free time a person has after work, study and commitments.
- **Familiarity**: how well a person knows an activity, often because family or school introduced them to it.
- **Adaptability**: the extent to which an activity can be changed so that a particular group can take part.
- **Inclusiveness**: making sure activities are open and welcoming so everyone can take part.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the group** the question names (gender, age, race/religion/culture, family/friends/peers, disability).
2. **Choose factors that genuinely fit that group** — accessibility for disability, family commitments and leisure time for parents, culture/religion for faith groups.
3. **Write each point as factor → group → effect on participation**: "Because… so… which means more/fewer…".
4. **Show both directions** where you can: a factor can be a barrier or, when improved, increase participation.
5. **With data**, quote figures, name the trend, then explain it with a factor.
6. **In extended answers**, analyse several factors in depth and judge which has the biggest effect for this group.
:::

::: example Role models
Explain how role models can increase participation in sport among ethnic minority groups. (2)

If young people from an ethnic minority group see successful performers from their own background in a sport, they are more likely to believe the sport is "for them" and be inspired to try it (1). Without such role models, they may feel the sport belongs to other groups and not take part (1).
:::

::: example Family commitments
Explain how family commitments may affect the participation of adults aged 25–40. (2)

Many adults in this age group are raising young children, so they have less leisure time and energy after work and childcare (1). As a result they may give up club sport, or choose flexible activities such as home workouts or running that fit around the family (1).
:::

::: example Using data
Using the fictional survey above, describe the trend in participation for women and suggest one reason for it. (3)

Participation among women falls as age increases (1), from 62% at 16–24 to 28% at 75+ (1). One reason: older women may have health problems or reduced mobility, and there may be fewer suitable, accessible activities for their age group (1).
:::

::: example Disability — accessibility and adaptability
Explain two ways a leisure centre could increase participation among disabled people. (4)

Improve accessibility, e.g. ramps, lifts, hoists into the pool and accessible changing rooms (1), so wheelchair users can physically get into and use the facility (1). Offer adapted, inclusive sessions such as wheelchair basketball or sitting volleyball with trained staff (1), so disabled people can take part on equal terms and feel welcome (1).
:::

::: example Extended — analyse factors for a group
Analyse the factors that may affect the participation of girls aged 13–16 in sport. (6)

Stereotyping and attitudes are important: if girls are told, or see, that sports such as football or rugby are for boys, they may feel they do not belong and choose not to take part. Media coverage links to this, because women's sport receives less coverage than men's, so girls see fewer female role models and are less inspired. Education matters: if school PE offers only traditional games and a girl has a poor experience — feeling watched or judged in kit — she may drop out as soon as PE is no longer compulsory. Familiarity and family also shape choices: a girl whose parents and friends are active is more likely to keep playing, while peer pressure can pull her away if her friends see sport as uncool. Finally, disposable income and accessibility decide whether she can afford club fees and get to training. Where schools and clubs offer girls-only sessions, a wider range of activities and visible female role models, these factors can be reversed and participation rises. (Level 3.)
:::

::: q
State two factors that could affect the participation of older adults in sport. (2)
---
Any two: attitudes (e.g. belief exercise is for the young); accessibility; available leisure time; adaptability (e.g. walking football); role models; familiarity; disposable income; health/mobility accepted where linked to accessibility.
:::

::: q
Explain how disposable income can affect participation. (2)
---
People with low disposable income may not be able to afford membership fees, kit, equipment or travel (1), so they are less likely to take part, especially in expensive activities such as golf or skiing (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how religious festivals could affect participation in sport. (2)
---
During a festival such as Ramadan, people may be fasting, so have less energy (1); training or matches may clash with worship/festival activities, so participation falls at that time (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how familiarity may affect a young person's choice of sport. (2)
---
Young people tend to take up activities they know, e.g. those their parents played or their school offered (1), so they are more likely to join a club in that activity than try an unfamiliar one (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how media coverage could affect participation in disability sport. (2)
---
Coverage of events such as the Paralympics shows disabled athletes succeeding, providing role models (1), which can inspire disabled people to try sport and change attitudes about what they can do (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how adaptability can increase the participation of older people. (2)
---
Activities can be modified to reduce impact/speed, e.g. walking football or walking netball (1), so older people with reduced mobility or fitness can take part safely and enjoy the sport (1).
:::

::: watch
- Every point needs a **link to the group** — "media coverage" alone scores nothing; say *whose* participation it affects and *how*.
- Use the **spec's factor names** (attitudes, role models, accessibility, media coverage, sexism/stereotyping, culture/religion, family commitments, leisure time, familiarity, education, disposable income, adaptability/inclusiveness).
- **Don't write about individuals' fitness** or health when the question is about social factors, unless you link it to a factor such as accessibility.
- With data, **quote numbers** and describe the **trend** before explaining it.
- Factors can **increase** participation too — use this to show balance.
- Avoid sweeping claims about a group; write "may" and "some" — tendencies, not certainties.
:::
