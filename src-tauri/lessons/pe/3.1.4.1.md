## What it is

PE uses data all the time: fitness test scores, heart rates, participation figures, a coach's notes on technique. **Use of data** is examined on **both papers**, usually as short questions attached to a table or graph. This first part is about **how data is collected** and the difference between its two types: **quantitative** and **qualitative**.

### Quantitative data

**Quantitative data deals with numbers.** It is anything that can be counted or measured and written as a figure:
- a 30 m sprint time of **4.3 seconds**
- a **Multi-Stage Fitness Test** result of level 10, shuttle 2
- a resting heart rate of **58 bpm**
- **64%** of Year 10 students taking part in an after-school club
- the **number of passes** completed in a netball quarter

**Methods for collecting quantitative data:**
- **Questionnaires**: a written set of questions, often closed ("How many hours of exercise do you do each week? 0–1 / 2–3 / 4–5 / 6+"), answered by each person on paper or online.
- **Surveys**: collecting the same information from a large number of people, often using a questionnaire, to find overall figures and patterns, e.g. a national survey of how many adults are active for 150 minutes a week.

Fitness testing and measurement (stopwatches, heart-rate monitors, tally charts of passes) also produce quantitative data.

**Strengths:** easy to **compare** (with norms, national averages, other groups or earlier results), easy to present as **tables and graphs**, quick to collect from **large numbers**, and more **objective**. **Weaknesses:** it tells you *what* and *how much*, but not *why*. A survey may show that teenage girls' participation falls, but not the reasons.

### Qualitative data

**Qualitative data deals with descriptions.** It is about qualities, opinions, feelings and reasons, written in words rather than numbers:
- "I felt sick in the last two levels of the bleep test."
- A coach's note: "her follow-through is short and she is falling away from the ball."
- "I stopped playing football because the club moved training to Sunday mornings."
- A judge's comment that a dive was "clean, with a tight tuck".

**Methods for collecting qualitative data:**
- **Interviews**: asking a person questions face to face (or by phone or video) and recording their answers. The interviewer can **ask follow-up questions** to find out more.
- **Observations**: watching a performance or behaviour and describing what happens, e.g. a coach analysing a player's technique, or a teacher watching how students behave in a lesson.

**Strengths:** gives **depth, detail and reasons**. It explains *why* something happens and captures feelings and opinions that numbers miss. **Weaknesses:** harder and slower to collect, so **smaller samples**; harder to compare and present; can be **subjective**, because it depends on the person observing or interviewing.

### Telling them apart

Ask one question: **is the answer a number or a description?**

| Data | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sit and Reach score of 14 cm | quantitative | a measured number |
| "My hamstrings felt tight" | qualitative | a description of a feeling |
| 72% of a club's members said they were satisfied | quantitative | a number/percentage |
| "The coaches are friendly and encouraging" | qualitative | an opinion in words |
| Heart rate recorded every minute | quantitative | measured numbers |
| A coach's written notes on a tennis serve | qualitative | a description of technique |

**Watch out:** a questionnaire *can* collect both types. A closed question with tick boxes gives quantitative data. An open question ("Why do you enjoy swimming?") gives qualitative data. The method does not decide the type. The **form of the answer** does.

Quantitative fitness data can be compared with **national averages** (see 3.1.3.2b). Researchers often use **both** types together: a survey shows how many people have stopped playing, and interviews then find out why.

::: terms
- **Data**: information collected about performance, fitness or participation that can be analysed.
- **Quantitative data**: data that deals with numbers; it can be counted or measured.
- **Qualitative data**: data that deals with descriptions, such as opinions, feelings and reasons.
- **Questionnaire**: a set of written questions answered by each respondent, used mainly to collect quantitative data.
- **Survey**: collecting the same information from a large number of people to find overall patterns.
- **Interview**: asking a person questions directly and recording their answers, with the chance to ask follow-up questions.
- **Observation**: watching a performance or behaviour and describing what happens.
- **Closed question**: a question with fixed answers to choose from, giving data that can be counted.
- **Open question**: a question the respondent answers in their own words, giving descriptive data.
- **Subjective**: based on personal opinion or judgement rather than measurement.
:::

::: method
1. **Define with the key word:** quantitative = **numbers**; qualitative = **descriptions**.
2. **Identify the type** by asking: is the answer a number or a description? Justify with that reason.
3. **Match the method:** quantitative → questionnaires and surveys; qualitative → interviews and observations.
4. **For "explain an advantage",** link the method to the purpose: large numbers and easy comparison (questionnaire/survey) or depth and reasons (interview/observation).
5. **For evaluate questions,** give strengths and weaknesses of each method **for the stated purpose**, then judge which is better (or suggest using both).
:::

::: example Identify and justify
A student's resting heart rate is recorded as 64 bpm. State whether this is qualitative or quantitative data. Justify your answer. (2)

Quantitative (1).

It is numerical data: a heart rate measured in beats per minute (1).
:::

::: example Methods
State one method of collecting qualitative data and one method of collecting quantitative data. (2)

Qualitative: an interview or an observation (1).

Quantitative: a questionnaire or a survey (1).
:::

::: example Explain an advantage of interviews
A sports centre wants to find out why teenagers stop attending its sessions. Explain one advantage of using interviews rather than a questionnaire. (3)

Interviews collect qualitative data: descriptions of the teenagers' reasons and feelings (1).

The interviewer can ask follow-up questions, e.g. "What would make you come back?" (1).

This gives detailed reasons that a tick-box questionnaire would miss, so the centre can change the right thing, such as session times or cost (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — 6-mark
A school PE department wants to find out how active Year 11 students are and why some do little exercise. Evaluate the use of questionnaires and interviews for this purpose. (6)

(AO1) A questionnaire is a written set of questions that mainly gives quantitative data (numbers). An interview is a conversation that gives qualitative data (descriptions).

(AO2) A questionnaire could ask all 180 Year 11 students how many hours of exercise they do each week and which activities. Interviews could be held with a small group of students who do the least.

(AO3) The questionnaire is quick to give to the whole year group, and the numbers are easy to put into a bar chart and compare with national guidelines. That answers "how active" well. However, students may exaggerate, and tick-box answers cannot explain why some are inactive. Interviews give detailed reasons, such as cost, confidence or dislike of team sports, and allow follow-up questions. But they are slow, so only a few students can be interviewed, and the answers are harder to compare. The best approach is to use both: the questionnaire to measure activity levels across the year, then interviews to explain the reasons behind the low figures.
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of using observation to collect data on a performer's technique. (2)
---
It is subjective — it depends on the opinion/expertise of the observer (1), so two coaches may describe the same performance differently, reducing reliability (1). Or: time-consuming, so few performers can be observed (1+1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a questionnaire can produce both qualitative and quantitative data. (2)
---
Closed questions with fixed options produce answers that can be counted — quantitative (1); open questions answered in the respondent's own words produce descriptions — qualitative (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Quantitative = numbers; qualitative = descriptions.** Use those words in definitions.
- When justifying the type, refer to the **form of the answer** ("it is a number measured in seconds"), not to the method.
- Questionnaires and surveys → **quantitative**; interviews and observations → **qualitative**, as the spec lists them.
- "Qualitative means quality, quantitative means quantity" is a memory aid, not a definition.
- Advantages must be **linked to the purpose** in the question: numbers to compare, or reasons to explain.
:::

::: test
A coach collects two sets of data on her squad: their 30 m sprint times and their written comments about how the training felt. Explain why using both types of data is useful. (3)
---
Sprint times are quantitative and show objectively whether speed has improved and by how much (1); the comments are qualitative and explain how players experienced the training, e.g. whether it was too hard or boring (1); together the coach knows both whether the programme works and why, so she can adjust it (e.g. change intensity or add variety) (1).
:::

::: test
A local authority wants to find out why fewer women than men use its leisure centres. Evaluate the use of surveys and interviews to collect this data. (6)
---
AO1 (1 mark): surveys collect quantitative (numerical) data from large numbers; interviews collect qualitative (descriptive) data, with follow-up questions.
AO2 (2 marks): a survey of leisure-centre users/residents could record numbers of women and men using facilities, times and activities; interviews with a sample of women could explore reasons, e.g. childcare, cost, confidence, session times, lack of women-only sessions.
AO3 (3 marks): the survey is quick, reaches many people and gives figures that can be compared by sex and age and shown in graphs, but it cannot explain reasons and fixed answers may miss the real barriers; interviews give detailed reasons and allow follow-up, so the authority can plan targeted changes, but they are slow, costly, involve few people and answers are harder to compare and may be subjective; judgement, e.g. use both — survey to measure the gap, interviews to explain it.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate knowledge, clearly applied to the context, balanced evaluation with a conclusion; 3–4 — knowledge evident, some application, evaluation one-sided or undeveloped; 1–2 — limited knowledge, little application or evaluation; 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
