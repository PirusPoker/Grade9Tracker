## What it is

A coach helps a performer improve in two ways: **guidance** (help given *before* or *during* a skill, to show or tell the performer how to do it) and **feedback** (information *during* or *after* the skill about how it went). The exam wants you to know every type, give an example of **how it is delivered**, and — most importantly — **choose and justify** which types suit a **beginner** and which suit an **elite performer**.

### The four types of guidance

| Type | What it is | How it is given | Best for | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Visual** (seeing) | the performer **sees** how the skill should look | a **demonstration** by the coach or a skilled player; video; photos, posters, diagrams | **beginners** especially, but useful at all levels | gives a clear mental picture of the whole skill; quick; beginners copy what they see | the demonstration must be accurate; a complex skill may be too fast or detailed to take in; does not tell the performer how the movement *feels* |
| **Verbal** (hearing) | the performer is **told** how to perform | instructions, coaching points, questions; often alongside a demonstration | **elite performers** (and beginners if kept short and simple) | can be given during performance (a shout from the touchline); precise technical detail for experienced players; easy to combine with visual | beginners may not understand technical terms; too much information causes **overload**; hard to describe how a movement feels; less effective on its own |
| **Manual** (physical assistance) | the coach **physically moves or supports** the performer's body through the movement | holding a trampolinist's waist during a seat drop; guiding a golfer's arms through the swing; supporting a gymnast's back in a back walkover | **beginners**, and anyone learning a risky skill | builds **confidence** and **safety**; lets the performer **feel** the correct movement; reduces fear | performer may become **reliant** on the support; the feel is not quite the same as doing it alone; close physical contact needs care and consent |
| **Mechanical** (objects or aids) | **equipment** supports or shapes the movement | swimming **floats** or armbands; a **harness** (twisting belt) in trampolining or gymnastics; a **ball machine**; stabilisers; a kicking tee | **beginners** learning risky skills; also elite performers practising dangerous moves | **safety**; confidence; allows practice of a skill that could not yet be done unaided; frees the coach to watch | performer can become **reliant** on the aid; the feel differs from the real skill; equipment can be expensive |

**Choosing for the performer:**
- **Beginner** — **visual** first (a demonstration gives a clear picture), with **manual** or **mechanical** for safety and confidence on risky skills, and **short, simple verbal** cues. Long technical verbal guidance overloads a beginner.
- **Elite performer** — mostly **verbal** (detailed technical coaching, tactical instructions) and **visual** in the form of **video analysis**. Mechanical guidance still appears for dangerous new skills (a harness for a new twisting dismount).

### The types of feedback

Feedback comes in three pairs:

| Pair | Type | What it is | Example | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Positive / negative** | **Positive** | what was done **well**; praise | "Great follow-through on that shot" | **beginners** — builds confidence and motivation |
| | **Negative** | what was done **wrong** and needs correcting | "Your elbow dropped, so the ball went left" | **elite performers** — they can take criticism and use it to refine technique; beginners may be demotivated by too much |
| **Knowledge of results / knowledge of performance** | **Knowledge of results (KR)** | information about the **outcome** — did it work? | the time, the score, whether the ball went in, the distance jumped | **beginners** — simple and easy to understand; often obvious to them |
| | **Knowledge of performance (KP)** | information about the **quality of the movement / technique** — how it was done | "Your take-off foot was too far from the board"; video analysis of a stroke | **elite performers** — they can understand and act on technical detail |
| **Intrinsic / extrinsic** | **Intrinsic** | from **within** the performer — the **feel** of the movement (kinaesthetic sense) | a tennis player feels a sweet contact on the racket | **elite performers** — they know what a correct movement feels like |
| | **Extrinsic** | from **outside** — coach, team-mate, video, crowd, scoreboard | a coach's comment after a routine | **beginners** — they cannot yet tell a good movement from a bad one by feel |

**The pattern to remember:**
- **Beginner**: **positive**, **extrinsic**, **knowledge of results** (with simple KP) — builds confidence, and they cannot yet judge their own technique.
- **Elite**: **negative** (constructive), **intrinsic**, **knowledge of performance** — they can feel errors, want detailed correction and are motivated enough not to be put off by criticism.

A well-coached session usually mixes them: a "feedback sandwich" of positive, then a correction, then positive again works well for young learners.

::: terms
- **Guidance**: help given to a performer to show or tell them how to perform a skill correctly.
- **Visual guidance**: guidance the performer sees, such as a demonstration, video or diagram.
- **Verbal guidance**: guidance the performer hears, such as spoken instructions or coaching points.
- **Manual guidance**: the coach physically moves or supports the performer's body through a movement.
- **Mechanical guidance**: the use of objects or aids, such as floats or a harness, to support or shape a movement.
- **Positive feedback**: information about what was done well, often in the form of praise.
- **Negative feedback**: information about what was done wrong, so that errors can be corrected.
- **Knowledge of results**: feedback about the outcome of a performance, such as a score, time or distance.
- **Knowledge of performance**: feedback about the quality of the technique or movement, not the outcome.
- **Intrinsic feedback**: feedback from within the performer, from the feel of the movement.
- **Extrinsic feedback**: feedback from an outside source, such as a coach, team-mate, video or scoreboard.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the type** from the scenario: seeing = visual; hearing = verbal; coach's hands = manual; equipment = mechanical. For feedback, ask: praise or correction? outcome or technique? from within or from outside?
2. **Say how it is given** with a concrete example ("a demonstration of the lay-up").
3. **Name the performer's level** and justify the choice for them: beginners need clear pictures, safety, confidence and simple information; elite performers can use detail and feel.
4. **Evaluate**: one strength and one limitation of the type, then whether it suits this performer.
5. **In 6/9-mark answers**, compare beginner and elite for both guidance and feedback, and reach a judgement.
:::

::: example Identify feedback
After a long jump, a coach tells the athlete, "5.42 metres — ten centimetres further than last week." Identify the type of feedback. (1)

Knowledge of results (1). (Also accept extrinsic or positive feedback.)
:::

::: example Manual guidance
Explain how manual guidance could be used to help a beginner trampolinist learn a seat drop. (2)

The coach stands beside the trampolinist and physically supports their waist and back as they drop into the seated position (1). This gives the beginner confidence and safety, and lets them feel the correct body shape (1).
:::

::: example Verbal guidance for beginners
Explain one reason why verbal guidance on its own can be unsuitable for a beginner. (2)

Beginners may not understand technical terms, so the instruction means little to them (1); too much spoken information at once can overload them, so they forget the key points (1).
:::

::: example Choose and justify guidance
Identify two types of guidance a coach could use to teach a beginner swimmer front crawl. Justify each choice. (4)

Visual guidance (1) — the coach demonstrates the arm action on the poolside, giving the beginner a clear picture of what the stroke should look like to copy (1). Mechanical guidance (1) — floats or a pull-buoy support the beginner so they stay afloat and feel safe, allowing them to focus on one part of the stroke at a time (1).
:::

::: example Extended — beginner vs elite
Evaluate the use of guidance and feedback for a beginner gymnast compared with an elite gymnast. (9)

Guidance: the beginner learning a handstand needs visual guidance — a demonstration shows the whole shape clearly — and manual guidance, with the coach holding their legs, because this is safe, builds confidence and lets them feel the straight body position; the risk is that they come to rely on the support, so it must be withdrawn gradually. Verbal guidance should be brief ("squeeze your tummy"), as long technical instruction would overload them. The elite gymnast can use detailed verbal guidance and video analysis because they understand technical language; a harness (mechanical guidance) remains useful for learning a dangerous new twisting element safely. Feedback: the beginner benefits most from positive, extrinsic feedback and knowledge of results ("you held it for three seconds") because praise motivates and they cannot yet feel their own errors. The elite gymnast uses intrinsic feedback — they can feel a bent arm — plus negative feedback and knowledge of performance from the coach and video, which pinpoint small faults that cost tenths of a mark. Judgement: the same four types exist for both, but the balance shifts — visual, manual and positive extrinsic feedback suit the beginner, while verbal, intrinsic and knowledge-of-performance feedback suit the elite gymnast. A coach who used negative, technical feedback with a beginner would risk demotivating them. (Level 3: detailed knowledge, applied to both gymnasts, with a reasoned judgement.)
:::

::: q
Give one example of mechanical guidance in sport. (1)
---
Floats/armbands in swimming / a harness or twisting belt in trampolining or gymnastics / a ball machine in tennis / stabilisers (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of mechanical guidance. (2)
---
The performer may become reliant on the aid (1), so they struggle to perform the skill when it is removed / the movement feels different without it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between knowledge of results and knowledge of performance. (2)
---
Knowledge of results is about the outcome, e.g. time, score, whether it went in (1); knowledge of performance is about the quality of the technique/how the movement was performed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why an elite performer can make use of intrinsic feedback. (2)
---
They have a well-learned, grooved technique and know what a correct movement feels like (1), so they can sense errors themselves and correct them without a coach (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why positive feedback is suitable for a beginner. (2)
---
It builds confidence/motivation (1), so the beginner keeps practising rather than dropping out / is not put off by errors (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Always say who the performer is**: beginners — visual, manual/mechanical, positive, extrinsic, KR; elite — verbal, video, intrinsic, negative, KP.
- Give **how it is delivered**: "visual guidance via a demonstration" earns more than "visual guidance".
- **Manual** = the coach's hands on the performer; **mechanical** = equipment. Don't swap them.
- **KR is about the outcome, KP is about the technique** — "the ball went in" is KR even if the coach says it.
- "Evaluate" needs **a strength and a limitation** — e.g. manual guidance builds confidence *but* can create reliance.
- Negative feedback is not "being rude" — it is **information about errors** so they can be corrected.
:::
