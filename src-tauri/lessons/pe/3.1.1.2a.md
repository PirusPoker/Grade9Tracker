## What it is

Working muscles need a constant supply of **oxygen** and need their **carbon dioxide** removed. The **cardio-respiratory system** does both jobs. The respiratory system brings air into the lungs, gas exchange loads oxygen into the blood, and the blood vessels carry it to the muscles and redirect it to where it is needed most during exercise.

### The pathway of air

Air travels in this order (and only these structures are required):

**mouth / nose → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli**

- The **trachea** (windpipe) is a tube held open by rings of cartilage.
- It splits into two **bronchi**, one into each lung.
- Each bronchus branches into many smaller **bronchioles**.
- The bronchioles end in millions of tiny air sacs, the **alveoli**, where **gaseous exchange** happens.

A labelled figure of the lungs often asks for the trachea, a bronchus and an alveolus. Examiners report that students mix up **bronchi** (the two large branches) with **bronchioles** (the many small ones).

### Gaseous exchange at the alveoli

Gases move by **diffusion**: from an area of **high concentration** to an area of **low concentration**.
- Air in the alveoli has a **high concentration of oxygen**; blood arriving in the capillaries from the body has a low one. Oxygen **diffuses from the alveoli into the blood**.
- Blood arriving has a **high concentration of carbon dioxide**; the alveoli have a low one. Carbon dioxide **diffuses from the blood into the alveoli** and is breathed out.

Features of the alveoli that make this fast and efficient:

| Feature | Why it helps gas exchange |
|---|---|
| **Large surface area** (millions of alveoli) | more gas can diffuse at the same time |
| **Moist, thin walls, one cell thick** | gases dissolve in the moisture and pass through easily |
| **Short diffusion pathway** | the gases have only a tiny distance to travel between air and blood, so diffusion is quick |
| **Lots of capillaries** surrounding each alveolus | a large area of blood is in contact with the air |
| **Large blood supply** | blood keeps flowing past, carrying oxygen away and bringing carbon dioxide, so the concentration difference is maintained |
| **High to low concentration** | the difference in concentration drives the movement of each gas |

**Carrying the oxygen.** Oxygen combines with **haemoglobin** in the **red blood cells** to form **oxyhaemoglobin**, which is carried to the working muscles. At the muscles, where oxygen concentration is low, the oxygen is released. Haemoglobin can also carry **carbon dioxide** back to the lungs.

### Blood vessels: structure and function

| | **Arteries** | **Capillaries** | **Veins** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction | carry blood **away from** the heart | link arteries to veins, **at the tissues and alveoli** | carry blood **back to** the heart |
| Blood | oxygenated (except the **pulmonary artery**) | exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide | deoxygenated (except the **pulmonary vein**) |
| Wall | **thick, muscular and elastic** | **one cell thick** | **thin**, less muscle |
| Size / lumen | smaller internal diameter for their wall | tiny: red blood cells pass in single file | **large** internal diameter |
| Valves | none | none | **valves** stop blood flowing backwards |
| Pressure | **high** | low | **low** |

**How structure fits function:**
- **Arteries** take the full force of each heartbeat. The thick, elastic, muscular walls withstand and maintain **high pressure**, and the muscle lets them change their diameter.
- **Capillaries** have walls only one cell thick and a huge total surface area, so **gas exchange** can happen quickly between blood and muscle, or blood and alveoli. Blood flows slowly through them, giving time for diffusion.
- **Veins** carry blood at **low pressure**, often uphill from the legs. The large lumen reduces resistance, and **valves** stop the blood flowing backwards. Squeezing by the skeletal muscles around them helps push the blood back to the heart.

The vessels at the heart are named in 3.1.1.2b: the **aorta** (artery to the body), **pulmonary artery** (to the lungs), **pulmonary vein** (from the lungs) and **vena cava** (vein from the body).

### Redistribution of blood during exercise

At rest, a large share of blood goes to the digestive system and other organs. During exercise the working muscles need far more oxygen, so blood is **redistributed** (the **vascular shunt**):
- **Vasodilation**: the blood vessels supplying the **working muscles widen**, so more blood, and therefore more oxygen and glucose, reaches them and more carbon dioxide and lactic acid is removed.
- **Vasoconstriction**: the blood vessels supplying **non-essential areas**, especially the **digestive system**, **narrow**, so less blood goes there.

Vessels near the skin also dilate to lose heat, which is why skin looks red during exercise. Blood flow to the brain stays roughly the same. The result is more oxygen delivered where the energy is being used, so the performer can work harder for longer before fatigue. It is also a reason not to eat a large meal just before exercise: digestion and the muscles compete for blood.

::: terms
- **Trachea**: the windpipe, carrying air from the mouth and nose to the bronchi.
- **Bronchi**: the two large airways branching from the trachea, one into each lung.
- **Bronchioles**: the many small airways branching from the bronchi that lead to the alveoli.
- **Alveoli**: tiny air sacs at the end of the bronchioles where gaseous exchange takes place.
- **Diffusion**: the movement of a gas from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration.
- **Haemoglobin**: the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen (and some carbon dioxide).
- **Oxyhaemoglobin**: haemoglobin combined with oxygen, formed at the lungs and carried to the muscles.
- **Artery**: a thick-walled, elastic blood vessel carrying blood away from the heart under high pressure.
- **Capillary**: a blood vessel with a wall one cell thick where gas exchange happens between blood and tissue.
- **Vein**: a thin-walled blood vessel with a large lumen and valves, carrying blood back to the heart at low pressure.
- **Vasodilation**: the widening of blood vessels to increase blood flow, e.g. to the working muscles.
- **Vasoconstriction**: the narrowing of blood vessels to reduce blood flow, e.g. to the digestive system.
:::

::: method
1. **Pathway questions**: write the full chain in order and do not skip bronchioles: mouth/nose, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli.
2. **Gas exchange**: name the feature, then explain the benefit (more gas at once, shorter distance, faster diffusion). Always say which gas moves which way, and why: high to low concentration.
3. **Compare vessels**: use matching points for both vessels (wall thickness, lumen, valves, pressure, direction). "Arteries have thick walls, whereas veins have thin walls" earns more than two separate descriptions.
4. **Redistribution**: use both technical terms and say *where*: vasodilation to the working muscles, vasoconstriction to the digestive system. Then give the effect on performance.
5. **Extended answers**: knowledge of the process (AO1), applied to the named event (AO2), then a chain of analysis: more blood, so more oxygen, so more aerobic energy, so a faster pace held for longer with less fatigue (AO3).
:::

::: example Features of the alveoli
Explain two features of the alveoli that help gaseous exchange. (4)

The walls are only one cell thick (1), giving a short diffusion pathway, so oxygen passes into the blood quickly (1). There are lots of capillaries around each alveolus (1), so a large volume of blood is in contact with the air and more oxygen can diffuse into the blood at once (1).
:::

::: example Compare vessels
Compare the structure of an artery and a vein. (3)

Arteries have thick, muscular, elastic walls, whereas veins have thinner walls (1). Veins have a larger internal diameter (lumen) than arteries (1). Veins have valves to prevent backflow, whereas arteries do not (1).
:::

::: example Oxygen transport
Describe how oxygen is transported from the alveoli to the working muscles. (3)

Oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the blood in the capillaries (1). It combines with haemoglobin in the red blood cells to form oxyhaemoglobin (1), which is carried in the arteries to the muscles, where the oxygen is released (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — redistribution
Analyse how the redistribution of blood during exercise helps a 1500 m swimmer to perform. (6)

As the race starts, the blood vessels supplying the swimmer's working muscles in the shoulders, arms and legs vasodilate, widening so more blood flows to them, while the vessels to the digestive system vasoconstrict, narrowing so less blood goes to non-essential organs (AO1/AO2). Because more blood reaches the deltoids, latissimus dorsi and quadriceps, more oxygen and glucose are delivered, so more energy can be released aerobically (AO3). The swimmer can therefore hold a fast stroke rate for the whole race, which lasts many minutes, rather than slowing as the muscles tire (AO3). The extra blood flow also removes carbon dioxide and heat more quickly, delaying fatigue in the final lengths, when the race is often won or lost (AO3).

Level 3 (5–6): both terms are used correctly, applied to the swimmer, and linked in a clear chain to performance.
:::

::: watch
- **Bronchi ≠ bronchioles.** Bronchi are the two big branches; bronchioles are the many small ones.
- "Gases are exchanged" is too vague. Say **oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out**, from **high to low concentration**.
- Each alveolus feature needs its **benefit**: thin walls, *so* a short distance, *so* faster diffusion.
- Redistribution answers need both **vasodilation and vasoconstriction**, and **where** each happens. "Blood is pumped to the muscles" is level 1 at best.
- The pulmonary artery carries **deoxygenated** blood and the pulmonary vein **oxygenated** blood, the reverse of the usual rule.
- In a compare question, write matching points with "whereas". Two separate descriptions may not earn the comparison.
:::

::: test
Analyse how the redistribution of blood improves the performance of a road cyclist during a long race. (6)
---
AO1 (1): knowledge that blood is redistributed by vasodilation (widening of vessels to the working muscles) and vasoconstriction (narrowing of vessels to non-essential organs such as the digestive system).
AO2 (2): application: vessels supplying the quadriceps, hamstrings, gluteals and gastrocnemius dilate as the cyclist pedals; vessels to the stomach and intestines constrict; skin vessels dilate for heat loss on a long climb.
AO3 (3): analysis: more blood to the legs delivers more oxygen and glucose so energy is released aerobically for hours, allowing a high cadence and speed to be held; more carbon dioxide/lactic acid and heat removed so fatigue is delayed on climbs and in the final sprint; less blood wasted on digestion, although this limits eating large amounts during the race; conclusion on the overall effect.
Levels: 5–6 — detailed, accurate knowledge with both terms; clear application; thorough analysis linked to performance. 3–4 — some knowledge, possibly only one term; some application; analysis undeveloped. 1–2 — simple statements, e.g. "blood goes to the muscles", little application. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
Evaluate how the structures of the respiratory system and blood vessels allow a marathon runner to keep supplying oxygen to the working muscles throughout a race. (9)
---
AO1 (2): pathway of air (mouth/nose, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli); alveoli features (large surface area, thin moist walls, short diffusion pathway, many capillaries, large blood supply, high-to-low concentration); oxyhaemoglobin; artery, capillary and vein structure; vasodilation and vasoconstriction.
AO2 (2): application to the marathon: deeper, faster breathing brings more air to the alveoli; the large surface area and many capillaries load oxygen onto haemoglobin quickly; arteries carry it at high pressure to the leg muscles; capillaries in the muscles release it; veins with valves return deoxygenated blood from the legs; shunting of blood to the legs away from the digestive system.
AO3 (5): evaluation: gas exchange at the alveoli is efficient enough that oxygen supply is rarely limited at the lungs, so for a runner the limiting stage is more often delivery by the blood and heart; redistribution is highly effective for delivering oxygen but reduces digestion, making it harder to take on energy drinks or gels late in the race; valves and the muscle pump help return blood, supporting the next heartbeat; over 2–3 hours, keeping the concentration gradient high depends on a large, continuous blood supply; judgement on which structure is most important to sustaining pace and delaying fatigue.
Levels: 7–9 — accurate and detailed knowledge; consistently applied to the marathon runner; thorough evaluation with well-reasoned conclusions; correct terminology throughout. 4–6 — knowledge evident but uneven; some application; evaluation limited or one-sided. 1–3 — limited knowledge; little application; little or no evaluation. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
