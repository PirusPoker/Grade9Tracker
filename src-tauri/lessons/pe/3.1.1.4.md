## What it is

Exercise changes the body on three timescales, and the exam uses them precisely:
- **Immediate effects**: what happens **during** exercise.
- **Short-term effects**: what the performer feels **up to 36 hours after** exercise.
- **Long-term effects**: the adaptations that come from **months and years** of regular exercise and training.

Putting an effect on the wrong timescale scores nothing. Learn the three lists as separate sets.

### Immediate effects (during exercise)

| Effect | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| **Hot, sweaty, red skin** | energy release produces heat; sweating cools the body as sweat evaporates; vessels near the skin widen (vasodilation) to lose heat, so the skin looks red |
| **Increase in depth and frequency of breathing** | the muscles need more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide, so the performer breathes deeper (bigger tidal volume) and faster |
| **Increased heart rate** | cardiac output must rise to deliver more oxygenated blood to the working muscles and remove carbon dioxide |

### Short-term effects (up to 36 hours after exercise)

| Effect | Why it happens / what it means |
|---|---|
| **Tiredness / fatigue** | energy stores are used up and muscles are tired; performance in the next session may be lower until recovery |
| **Light-headedness** | blood pressure can drop and less blood reaches the brain when hard exercise stops suddenly, especially without a cool down; dehydration adds to it |
| **Nausea** | feeling sick after very intense exercise, linked to blood having been shunted away from the digestive system, dehydration and lactic acid build-up |
| **Aching / DOMS / cramp** | **DOMS** (delayed onset muscle soreness) is pain and stiffness that comes on hours later, often the next day, after hard or unfamiliar exercise; **cramp** is a sudden, painful, involuntary contraction of a muscle, often linked to fatigue or dehydration |

These effects can be reduced by a good cool down, rehydration and refuelling (see 3.1.1.3).

### Long-term effects (months and years of exercising)

Long-term effects are **adaptations**: the body changes structure to cope better with the training it is given. The spec wants them explained through the **components of fitness**, so link each one to a component and to performance.

| Long-term effect | What changes | Component improved | Effect on performance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Body shape may change** | less body fat and/or more muscle; e.g. a sprinter becomes more muscular, a distance runner leaner | body composition | less weight to carry, or more power, depending on the sport |
| **Build muscle strength** | muscles get bigger and stronger (muscle **hypertrophy**) through resistance training | strength | a rugby forward can drive harder in the scrum; a thrower throws further |
| **Improve muscular endurance** | muscles can contract repeatedly for longer without tiring | muscular endurance | a rower keeps a powerful stroke through the whole race |
| **Improve speed** | faster, more powerful muscle contractions | speed | a winger beats a defender to the ball |
| **Improve suppleness** | a greater range of movement at joints | flexibility | a gymnast reaches a full split; a hurdler clears the hurdle more efficiently |
| **Build cardiovascular endurance** | the heart and lungs supply oxygen more efficiently | cardiovascular endurance | a midfielder keeps working hard for the full 90 minutes |
| **Improve stamina** | the body can keep going for longer before it tires | cardiovascular endurance and muscular endurance | the performer can train harder and perform at a higher level for longer |
| **Increase in the size of the heart (hypertrophy)** | the heart muscle gets bigger and stronger, so it pumps more blood each beat: **stroke volume increases** | cardiovascular endurance | more oxygen reaches the muscles per beat, at rest and in exercise |
| **Lower resting heart rate (bradycardia)** | because stroke volume is larger, the heart needs fewer beats to deliver the same cardiac output at rest; a resting heart rate below 60 bpm is commonly called bradycardia | cardiovascular endurance | the heart is more efficient, has more room to rise in exercise, and recovers faster after effort |

**The heart adaptations link together.** Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate. Regular aerobic training causes **hypertrophy** of the heart. A bigger, stronger heart pumps a **larger stroke volume**. At rest the body needs about the same cardiac output as before, so **resting heart rate falls**. That lower resting rate is **bradycardia**. During exercise the trained heart can reach a much higher cardiac output, so more oxygen reaches the working muscles and the performer can work harder for longer before fatigue.

**Data questions.** A common exam table shows resting heart rate falling over a training programme. For example: week 0, 72 bpm; week 4, 68 bpm; week 8, 63 bpm; week 12, 58 bpm. Describe the trend with the numbers (it fell by 14 bpm over 12 weeks). Name the effect (bradycardia by week 12, below 60). Explain the cause (hypertrophy of the heart, so a larger stroke volume).

::: terms
- **Immediate effect**: a change that happens during exercise, such as increased heart rate or red, sweaty skin.
- **Short-term effect**: an effect felt up to 36 hours after exercise, such as fatigue, nausea or DOMS.
- **Long-term effect**: an adaptation resulting from months and years of regular exercise, such as a lower resting heart rate.
- **DOMS**: delayed onset muscle soreness; pain and stiffness felt in the muscles hours or days after exercise.
- **Cramp**: a sudden, painful, involuntary contraction of a muscle.
- **Nausea**: a feeling of sickness that can follow very intense exercise.
- **Hypertrophy**: an increase in the size of a muscle; in the heart it means a bigger, stronger heart muscle.
- **Bradycardia**: a lower resting heart rate, commonly below 60 bpm, caused by long-term training.
- **Stamina**: the ability to keep working for a long time before tiring.
- **Suppleness**: flexibility; the range of movement possible at a joint.
:::

::: method
1. **Check the timescale in the question**: *during* = immediate; *up to 36 hours after* = short-term; *months and years* = long-term. Only use effects from that list.
2. **State** the effect in the spec's words, then **explain** it with the reason (more oxygen needed, heat loss, energy stores used, muscle damage).
3. **Long-term effects**: link each one to a **component of fitness** and then to **performance** in the named activity. Effect, then component, then "so the performer can…".
4. **Heart questions**: follow the chain from hypertrophy to larger stroke volume to lower resting heart rate (bradycardia), using Q = SV × HR.
5. **Data**: quote numbers and the change over time, name the effect, then explain its cause.
6. **9-mark answers**: several long-term effects (AO1), each applied to the named performer (AO2), then analysis of *how much* each improves performance and which matters most (AO3).
:::

::: example Immediate effects
State two immediate effects of exercise. (2)

Any two: hot/sweaty/red skin (1); increased depth and frequency of breathing (1); increased heart rate (1).
:::

::: example Short-term effect explained
Explain one short-term effect of exercise on a performer who has played in a hard hockey match. (2)

Aching/DOMS in the leg muscles the day after the match (1), which means the player may be stiff and unable to train at full intensity until the soreness passes (1).
:::

::: example Bradycardia from data
A table shows a rower's resting heart rate: January 70 bpm, April 64 bpm, July 57 bpm. Identify the long-term effect shown and explain why it has happened. (3)

Resting heart rate has fallen by 13 bpm to below 60 bpm: bradycardia (1). Regular training has caused hypertrophy of the heart (1), so stroke volume is larger and fewer beats are needed to pump the same volume of blood at rest (1).
:::

::: example Long-term effect to performance
Explain how an improvement in suppleness could benefit a high jumper. (2)

A greater range of movement at the hip and spine (1) lets the jumper arch over the bar more easily, clearing a greater height without knocking it off (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — 9 marks
Analyse how the long-term effects of regular training improve the performance of a club rower in a 2,000 m race. (9)

Months of training cause hypertrophy of the heart, so it becomes larger and stronger (AO1). Its stroke volume increases, so at rest the rower's heart needs fewer beats, giving bradycardia, and in the race cardiac output can rise much higher (AO2). More oxygen reaches the leg, back and arm muscles each minute, so more energy is released aerobically and less lactic acid builds up; the rower can hold a higher stroke rate through the middle 1,000 m, where races are often lost (AO3). Improved cardiovascular endurance and stamina mean the rower is less fatigued in the final 500 m, so they still have energy for a sprint finish (AO3). Training also builds muscle strength and muscular endurance in the quadriceps, gluteals and latissimus dorsi (AO1/AO2); each stroke is more powerful and the rower can repeat it hundreds of times without the power fading, so the boat travels further per stroke (AO3). Body shape may change, with less fat and more muscle, so there is less dead weight in the boat relative to power (AO3). Of these, the heart adaptations and muscular endurance are probably the most important, because a 2,000 m race lasts several minutes and is mainly aerobic; strength matters most at the start, but the race is won by whoever can keep producing power to the end (AO3).

Level 3 (7–9): accurate knowledge of several long-term effects, each applied to the rower, with a thorough analysis of their impact and a reasoned conclusion.
:::

---
Heat is produced when muscles release energy (1); blood vessels near the skin widen/more blood flows to the skin to lose heat (1).
:::

::: q
Define bradycardia. (1)
---
A lower resting heart rate (commonly below 60 bpm) as a result of long-term training (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why breathing becomes deeper and more frequent during exercise. (2)
---
The working muscles need more oxygen / produce more carbon dioxide (1); deeper, faster breathing takes more oxygen into the lungs and removes carbon dioxide (1).

State two long-term effects of exercise on the heart. (2)
---
Increase in size of the heart / hypertrophy (1); lower resting heart rate / bradycardia (1). Accept increased stroke volume.

Explain how improved muscular endurance could benefit a swimmer in a 1500 m race. (2)
---
The arm and leg muscles can contract repeatedly without tiring (1), so the swimmer keeps a strong, efficient stroke to the end of the race (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a performer might feel light-headed straight after a very hard sprint session. (2)
---
Blood pressure falls / less blood reaches the brain when the exercise stops suddenly (1), especially if there is no cool down or the performer is dehydrated (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Timescales are fixed.** DOMS, nausea and fatigue are **short-term**; a lower resting heart rate is **long-term**; increased heart rate is **immediate**. Mixing them scores nothing.
- "Heart rate increases" is immediate. "**Resting** heart rate decreases" is long-term. Read which one the question means.
- **Hypertrophy of the heart** causes the lower resting heart rate. Link them through **stroke volume**.
- Link each long-term effect to a **component of fitness** and to a **specific moment** of the performance, not just "they perform better".
- In the June 2025 extended question, answers that described the training method instead of the **long-term effects** gained little. Answer the question set.
- Quote **numbers** from a data table when you describe a trend.
:::

::: test
Analyse how the short-term effects of exercise could affect a badminton player competing in a two-day tournament. (6)
---
AO1 (1): knowledge of short-term effects: tiredness/fatigue, light-headedness, nausea, aching/DOMS/cramp, up to 36 hours after exercise.
AO2 (2): application: after day one's matches of repeated lunges and jumps, the player may have DOMS in the quadriceps and calves on day two; fatigue from several matches; cramp late in a long match; light-headedness or nausea after a very intense match if they stop suddenly.
AO3 (3): analysis: DOMS and fatigue reduce speed to the shuttle and jump height, so smashes and retrieves suffer on day two; cramp could force the player to stop or lose a game; nausea/light-headedness affect concentration and decision-making; because the tournament falls within the 36-hour window, recovery (cool down, rehydration, carbohydrates, massage) becomes decisive; judgement on which effect is most damaging.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate knowledge; clearly applied to the two-day tournament; thorough analysis of the impact on performance. 3–4 — knowledge evident but uneven; some application; analysis undeveloped. 1–2 — limited knowledge; effects listed with little application or analysis. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
