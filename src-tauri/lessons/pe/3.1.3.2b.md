## What it is

Fitness tests measure the **components of fitness** (3.1.3.2a). You need to know **why** performers test, the **limitations** of testing, **how each of eleven tests is carried out**, how the scores are **recorded**, and how to **draw conclusions** by comparing scores with norms. Every year the paper includes at least one test question. It is often a norm table to read, or "evaluate this test for this performer".

### Reasons for fitness testing

- to **identify strengths and weaknesses** in a performance, or to judge the success of a training programme
- to **monitor improvement** (retest after a block of training)
- to show a **starting level** of fitness (a baseline)
- to **inform training requirements**, so a weak component can be targeted
- to **compare** against the norms of the group or **national averages**
- to **motivate** the performer and help **set goals**
- to provide **variety** in a training programme (breaks tedium)

### Limitations of fitness testing

- Tests are often **not sport-specific** and are too general. The Sit and Reach tests hamstring and lower-back flexibility only, which tells a swimmer little about shoulder flexibility.
- They **do not replicate the movements** of the activity. A rower's endurance is not measured by running shuttles.
- They **do not replicate competitive conditions**: no opponents, no crowd, no pressure, no fatigue from the rest of the game.
- Many use **indirect or sub-maximal measurement**, so they are **inaccurate**. The MSFT only *predicts* aerobic capacity.
- Some need the performer to be **highly motivated**. A maximal test such as the MSFT depends on how hard the person is willing to push.
- Some have **questionable reliability**. Results vary with the tester, the surface, the weather or the time of day.
- They must follow the **correct procedure** every time to be **valid**. Changing the start, the surface or the timing makes results meaningless to compare.

### The eleven tests

| Component | Test | Equipment / set-up | Procedure (tasks and rules) | Score recorded in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Agility** | **Illinois Agility Test** | flat, non-slip surface; 8 cones marking a course about 10 m long and 5 m wide, with 4 cones down the centre; stopwatch | start **lying face down** with head at the start line; on "go", get up and run the course as fast as possible, **weaving in and out** of the centre cones up and back, without knocking cones over, to the finish | **seconds** (lower = better) |
| **Balance** | **Stork Stand Test** | flat surface; stopwatch | stand on the preferred leg, place the other foot against the inside of the standing knee, **hands on hips**; raise the heel to balance on the ball of the foot; time starts when the heel lifts and **stops when the heel touches the floor, the foot leaves the knee or the hands leave the hips** | **seconds** (higher = better) |
| **Cardiovascular endurance** | **Multi-Stage Fitness Test** (bleep test) | flat 20 m course marked by cones or lines; audio recording of bleeps | run 20 m shuttles, reaching the line **in time with each bleep**; the bleeps get **faster at each level**; continue until you can no longer reach the line before the bleep (usually after a warning) | **level and shuttle** reached; can be used to predict VO2 max |
| **Coordination** | **Wall Toss Test** | a smooth wall; tennis ball; tape line **2 m** from the wall; stopwatch | throw the ball underarm with one hand against the wall and **catch it with the other hand**, then throw back with that hand; repeat for **30 seconds** | **number** of successful catches |
| **Flexibility** | **Sit and Reach Test** | sit and reach box with a ruler scale | sit with **legs straight** and shoes off, feet flat against the box; reach forward slowly with both hands along the scale as far as possible and **hold** the position briefly; knees must stay straight, no jerking or bouncing | **centimetres** reached (higher = better) |
| **Muscular endurance** | **Sit-Up Bleep Test** | mat; audio recording of bleeps; partner | lie on the mat with knees bent and feet flat; sit up and lie back **in time with the bleeps**, which get quicker; stop when you can no longer keep pace or technique breaks down | **number** of sit-ups (or level) |
| **Power** | **Vertical Jump Test** | wall; chalk or a jump board; tape measure | stand side-on to the wall and reach up with the nearer hand to mark the **standing reach**; from standing (no run-up), **jump as high as possible** and mark the wall at the highest point; measure the difference | **centimetres** (best of a few attempts) |
| **Reaction time** | **Ruler Drop Test** | metre ruler; partner | partner holds the ruler vertically with the **zero mark between the performer's thumb and finger**; partner drops it **without warning**; performer catches it as quickly as possible; read the mark level with the top of the thumb | **centimetres** (lower = faster); averaged over several attempts |
| **Maximal strength** | **One Rep Max Test** | free weights or a machine (e.g. bench press); **spotter** | warm up; lift a weight you can manage once with correct technique; rest and increase the weight; repeat until you can only lift it once | **kilograms** (heaviest single lift) |
| **Speed** | **30 Metre Sprint Test** | flat 30 m track marked by cones; stopwatch | from a standing start, sprint **flat out** over 30 m; timer starts on "go" and stops as the runner crosses the finish; full recovery between attempts | **seconds** (lower = better) |
| **Strength** | **Handgrip Dynamometer Test** | handgrip dynamometer | hold the dynamometer in the dominant hand, arm by the side; **squeeze as hard as possible** for a few seconds; best of several attempts with rest between | **kilograms** (kg) |

### Collecting data and drawing conclusions

Every test score here is **quantitative** data: it is a **number** (seconds, levels, centimetres, repetitions, kilograms). **Qualitative** data is **descriptive**, such as how the performer felt during the test or a coach's observation of their technique. Quantitative scores can be **compared with national averages or norms** to rate the performer.

An illustrative norm table for the Sit and Reach (16–19 year olds; figures invented for practice):

| Rating | Male (cm) | Female (cm) |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | above 16 | above 20 |
| Good | 12 to 16 | 16 to 20 |
| Average | 8 to 11 | 11 to 15 |
| Below average | 4 to 7 | 7 to 10 |
| Poor | below 4 | below 7 |

For the Ruler Drop, 30 m sprint and Illinois test the scale runs the other way: a **lower** score is better.

**Drawing a conclusion:** a 17-year-old girl who reaches 17 cm is rated **Good** for flexibility. Compare her with the norm, then say what it means for training: flexibility is a relative strength, so her programme can target a weaker component.

**Is the test relevant?** Always ask whether the test's movement, stimulus and demand match what the performer actually does in their sport. The worked examples below show how.

::: terms
- **Fitness test**: a set procedure used to measure one component of fitness so it can be compared over time or against norms.
- **Norms**: typical scores for a population, grouped by age and sex, used to rate an individual's result.
- **National average**: the typical score for people of the same age and sex across the country.
- **Baseline**: a starting level of fitness measured before training begins.
- **Validity**: whether a test measures what it claims to measure, including using the correct procedure.
- **Reliability**: whether a test gives consistent results when repeated in the same conditions.
- **Sub-maximal test**: a test in which the performer does not work to their limit, so the result is a prediction rather than a direct measure.
- **Quantitative data**: numerical data, such as a time in seconds or a level on the MSFT.
- **Qualitative data**: descriptive data about opinions, feelings or observed quality rather than numbers.
- **Maximal test**: a test that requires the performer to work to exhaustion or their absolute limit, such as the MSFT or one rep max.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the test for the component**, and learn the pairs: Illinois–agility, Stork–balance, MSFT–cardiovascular endurance, Wall Toss–coordination, Sit and Reach–flexibility, Sit-Up Bleep–muscular endurance, Vertical Jump–power, Ruler Drop–reaction time, One Rep Max–maximal strength, 30 m Sprint–speed, Handgrip–strength.
2. **To describe a test,** give the set-up (equipment, distances), what the performer does, the rules, and what is measured with its **unit**. One mark usually goes to each.
3. **For a norm table,** find the right row and column (age, sex), check which direction is better (lower times and distances are better for sprint, Illinois and Ruler Drop), and quote the rating.
4. **To evaluate a test for a performer,** give strengths (it measures a component the performer needs, it is cheap and easy) **and** limitations (not sport-specific, wrong movement or stimulus, no competitive conditions, needs motivation), each **applied to that performer**, then give a judgement and suggest a better test if relevant.
5. **For reasons and limitations questions,** use the spec lists but always tie the point to the named performer.
:::

::: example Describe a test
Describe how to carry out the Illinois Agility Test. (3)

Set up cones to mark a course 10 m long by 5 m wide, with four cones in a line down the middle (1).

The performer starts lying face down at the start. On "go" they get up and run the course as fast as possible, weaving in and out of the centre cones without knocking any over (1).

The time from "go" to crossing the finish line is recorded in seconds with a stopwatch and compared with norms (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate a test for a performer
Evaluate the use of the Illinois Agility Test for a 100 m sprinter. (4)

For: the test starts from a stationary position and includes flat-out acceleration over 10 m lengths, which is relevant to the sprinter's drive phase out of the blocks (1). It is cheap, quick and easy to repeat, so progress can be monitored (1).

Against: the sprinter runs in a straight line and never changes direction, so most of the test's weaving does not replicate the 100 m (1).

The start is from lying down on the command "go", not a crouch start reacting to a gun. So the 30 m Sprint Test would be a more relevant measure of the speed the sprinter needs (1).
:::

::: example Reasons for testing
Explain two reasons why a coach might carry out fitness tests on a squad before the season. (4)

To show a starting level of fitness (1), so that later tests can be compared against it to monitor whether pre-season training has worked (1).

To identify strengths and weaknesses (1), so the coach can inform each player's training, e.g. extra interval work for a player with a low MSFT score (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate fitness testing — 9-mark
A county hockey team uses the Multi-Stage Fitness Test, the Sit and Reach Test and the 30 Metre Sprint Test during pre-season. Evaluate the use of fitness testing for the team. (9)

(AO1) Fitness testing gives a baseline, identifies strengths and weaknesses, monitors improvement and motivates. Its limitations include not being sport-specific, not replicating competitive conditions and some tests needing high motivation. The MSFT measures cardiovascular endurance, the Sit and Reach measures flexibility and the 30 m Sprint measures speed.

(AO2) Hockey players run repeatedly for a whole match, so the MSFT is relevant: its repeated shuttles with turns are similar to running up and down a pitch. The 30 m sprint matches the short bursts players make to reach a loose ball or close down an opponent. The Sit and Reach tests hamstring and lower-back flexibility, which matters for the low, bent-over stance used when dribbling.

(AO3) The scores can be compared with national averages and with each player's later results. A rising MSFT level shows pre-season training is working, which motivates, and a slow sprint time tells the coach to add speed work for that player. However, no test uses a stick and ball or match pressure, so a player who is quick in a straight line may still be slow when dribbling. The MSFT is maximal, so a poorly motivated player may stop early and give a misleading score. Agility is not tested at all, so the Illinois test could be added. Overall, testing is valuable for a baseline and for monitoring, provided it is used alongside match observation.
:::

::: q
Describe how to carry out the Ruler Drop Test. (3)
---
A partner holds a metre ruler vertically with the zero mark between the performer's open thumb and index finger (1); the partner drops the ruler without warning and the performer catches it as quickly as possible (1); the distance in cm at the top of the thumb is recorded, averaged over several attempts; a lower score = quicker reaction (1).

::: q
Give two limitations of fitness testing. (2)
---
Any two: not sport-specific/too general (1); do not replicate movements of the activity (1); do not replicate competitive conditions (1); indirect/sub-maximal, so inaccurate (1); some need motivation (1); questionable reliability (1); must follow correct procedure to be valid (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the Multi-Stage Fitness Test may give an inaccurate result for some performers. (2)
---
It is a maximal test that relies on the performer pushing until they can go no further (1); a performer who lacks motivation or gives up early will score below their true level / it only predicts VO2 max rather than measuring it directly (1).

::: watch
- Match the **test to the component** exactly: Handgrip = strength, **One Rep Max = maximal strength**, Vertical Jump = **power**, Sit-Up Bleep = **muscular endurance**.
- Describing the **wrong test** scores zero. Examiners regularly see the MSFT or a T-test written for the Illinois.
- Give the **unit** of the score: seconds, level, cm, number, kg.
- **Lower is better** for the 30 m sprint, Illinois and Ruler Drop. Read norm tables carefully, including age and sex.
- "Evaluate this test for this performer" needs **both sides** and must be **applied**: what the performer actually does in their sport.
- A suggested alternative test only earns credit when you say **why** it suits that performer better.
:::

::: test
Describe how to carry out the Stork Stand Test. (3)
---
Stand on one (preferred) leg with the other foot against the inside of the standing knee and hands on hips (1); raise the heel and balance on the ball of the foot, starting the stopwatch (1); stop the watch when the heel touches the ground, the foot moves from the knee or the hands leave the hips; the time in seconds is recorded (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how to carry out the Wall Toss Test. (3)
---
Stand a set distance (2 m) from a wall (1); throw a tennis ball underarm with one hand against the wall and catch it with the opposite hand, then repeat back the other way (1); count the number of successful catches in 30 seconds (1).
:::

::: test
George, aged 16, completes 38 sit-ups in the Sit-Up Bleep Test, which is rated Good against national norms for his age. Explain what his coach might conclude and do next. (2)
---
George's muscular endurance is above average/good compared with national norms, so it is a relative strength (1); the coach may keep it at a maintenance level and focus training on a weaker component, or set a goal of reaching Excellent and retest later (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the Sit and Reach Test may not be a valid test of flexibility for a swimmer. (3)
---
The test only measures flexibility of the hamstrings and lower back (1); a swimmer relies mainly on shoulder (and ankle) flexibility for a long, efficient stroke (1); so a good or poor score does not show the flexibility that affects swimming performance / it is not sport-specific (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how to carry out the One Rep Max Test and state one safety precaution. (4)
---
Warm up (1); choose an exercise, e.g. bench press, and lift a weight that can be lifted once with correct technique (1); after rest, increase the weight and repeat until only one lift is possible; the heaviest weight lifted once in kg is the score (1). Safety: use a spotter / correct lifting technique / proper warm-up (1).
:::

::: test
Evaluate the use of the Ruler Drop Test for a 100 m sprinter. (4)
---
For (up to 2): it measures reaction time, which is important for a fast start from the blocks (1); it is quick, cheap and needs only a ruler, so it can be repeated to monitor progress (1). Against (up to 2): the sprinter reacts to a sound (the gun), whereas the test uses a visual stimulus (1); the response is a hand catch, not a whole-body drive from the blocks, so it does not replicate the movement (1); results depend on the dropper and can be unreliable (1). Max 3 for one side only; max 4.
:::

::: test
Justify which two fitness tests would be most relevant for a netball centre. (4)
---
Illinois Agility Test (1) because the centre must dodge and change direction quickly to get free of her marker, which the test's weaving replicates (1). Multi-Stage Fitness Test (1) because the centre covers most of the court for the whole match, so cardiovascular endurance is vital, and the test's repeated shuttles with turns resemble her movement (1). Accept other tests with a valid justification, e.g. Vertical Jump for leaping to intercept.
:::

::: test
Evaluate whether fitness testing is useful for an elite marathon runner. (6)
---
AO1 (1 mark): reasons (baseline, monitor improvement, identify weaknesses, motivate) and limitations (not sport-specific, not competitive conditions, needs motivation, reliability).
AO2 (2 marks): the MSFT measures cardiovascular endurance, the key component for a marathon; retesting through a training block shows whether aerobic fitness is improving before a race; the runner can compare with elite norms.
AO3 (3 marks): useful because the score gives objective evidence to adjust training (e.g. more long runs if progress stalls) and can motivate; but the MSFT involves 20 m shuttles and turns, not steady running on roads, and it is maximal, so a tired or unmotivated runner may score poorly; elite runners may already exceed the norms, so the comparison adds little; race times over set distances may be a more specific test; judgement, e.g. useful as one tool but best combined with sport-specific measures.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate knowledge, applied clearly to the marathon runner, balanced evaluation with a conclusion; 3–4 — knowledge evident, some application, evaluation one-sided or undeveloped; 1–2 — limited knowledge, little application or evaluation; 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
A Premier League football club tests its squad using the 30 Metre Sprint Test, the Vertical Jump Test and the Handgrip Dynamometer Test. Evaluate the club's use of fitness testing. (9)
---
AO1 (2 marks): reasons for testing (strengths/weaknesses, baseline, monitoring, informing training, comparison with norms, motivation, variety); limitations (not sport-specific, don't replicate movements or competitive conditions, indirect/sub-maximal, motivation, reliability, correct procedure for validity); correct components: speed, power, strength.
AO2 (2 marks): application — the 30 m sprint matches sprints to reach through balls or track back; the vertical jump relates to jumping to head the ball at corners; handgrip strength has little relevance to outfield players, though it may matter to a goalkeeper catching the ball.
AO3 (5 marks): evaluation — results give objective data to compare players with each other and with previous tests, so coaches can target training (e.g. plyometrics for a centre-back with a low jump score) and judge whether a returning injured player is match-fit; competition between players may motivate. However, the tests do not replicate match conditions — no ball, no opponents, no fatigue from 90 minutes; the sprint is in a straight line whereas footballers often turn and change direction, so agility (Illinois) might be more relevant; no test of cardiovascular endurance is included despite players covering a large distance in a match; handgrip is largely irrelevant for outfield players; players who are not motivated may not give maximal effort. Judgement: useful for monitoring specific components, but the battery should be made more sport-specific (e.g. add the MSFT and Illinois, drop handgrip for outfield players) and combined with match data.
Levels: 7–9 — accurate, detailed knowledge; clear application to footballers; thorough, balanced evaluation with a well-reasoned judgement; correct terminology. 4–6 — knowledge evident but uneven; some application; evaluation present but one-sided or not developed. 1–3 — limited knowledge; little application; little or no evaluation. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
