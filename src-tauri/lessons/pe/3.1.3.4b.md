## What it is

This topic covers two ways of planning training around the performer's circumstances. **High altitude training** is a specific technique for improving aerobic fitness. You must be able to explain how it works and **evaluate** it for different performers. The **training seasons** divide a sporting year into three phases with different aims, and you must apply and justify them for different sports.

### High altitude training

**High altitude training** is a form of **aerobic training** carried out at **high altitude**, well above sea level, typically at mountain training camps (often quoted as above about 2,000 m).

How it works:
1. At high altitude there is **less oxygen in the air** the performer breathes. The air is "thinner".
2. So less oxygen reaches the blood, and the body's **oxygen-carrying capacity is reduced**.
3. The body **compensates by making more red blood cells** (containing more **haemoglobin**) to carry oxygen.
4. When the performer returns to sea level, the extra red blood cells mean **more oxygen can be delivered to the working muscles**. This improves **cardiovascular endurance** and aerobic performance, and lets the athlete work longer before fatigue.

You do **not** need to calculate training intensities for altitude.

**Evaluating altitude training**

| Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|
| more red blood cells and haemoglobin, so **greater oxygen-carrying capacity** | **expensive**: travel, accommodation and time away |
| improved **cardiovascular endurance / aerobic performance** on return | **altitude sickness**: headaches, nausea, breathlessness, disturbed sleep |
| can delay fatigue, so the athlete keeps a higher pace for longer | **hard to train at the usual intensity** because less oxygen is available, so some fitness can be lost (detraining) |
| a **legal**, natural way to gain an aerobic advantage | the benefits are **lost within a few weeks** of returning to sea level (reversibility), so timing before competition is critical |
| a change of environment can add variety and focus | time away from family and home can be psychologically difficult |
| | **little benefit for anaerobic / power performers** |

**Who benefits?** Altitude training suits **endurance (aerobic)** performers: marathon and 10,000 m runners, road cyclists, triathletes, cross-country skiers, rowers. It is of **little use** to a **sprinter, weightlifter, shot putter or high jumper**. Their events last seconds and are **anaerobic**, so extra oxygen-carrying capacity makes little difference. The harder training conditions could even reduce the intensity of their speed and power work. Claiming altitude helps a sprinter, without qualification, is marked as a misunderstanding.

### The training seasons

| Season | Also called | Aims (what it entails) | Benefit to the performer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-season** | **preparation** | build **general / aerobic fitness** first, then the **specific fitness needs** of the sport (speed, power, agility) | arrives at the first match fit enough to perform and less likely to be injured |
| **Competition season** | **peak / playing** season | **maintain fitness levels** and **work on specific skills** and tactics; avoid heavy training that causes fatigue before matches | performs at peak through the season without burning out |
| **Post-season** | **transition** | **rest** and recovery (including from injury) plus **light aerobic training** to **maintain a level of general fitness** | recovers mentally and physically, while avoiding full reversibility before the next pre-season |

**Applying the seasons.** Match them to the calendar of the sport:
- **Football (Premier League):** pre-season in summer, from around July; competition season roughly August to May; post-season in the early summer. In pre-season a player does long runs and circuits first, then sprints and small-sided games.
- **Cricket (county, UK):** the competition season is the summer. Pre-season is in late winter and spring (nets, fitness work). Post-season is autumn.
- **A marathon runner** may plan several phases around one or two target races a year. After a spring marathon, a transition period of rest and easy running comes before building up again.

**Why not train flat out all year?** Peaking every week is impossible. The body needs recovery, and a mental break prevents **tedium** and burnout. But total rest in the transition phase causes **reversibility**, which is why light aerobic work is kept.

::: terms
- **High altitude training**: aerobic training carried out at high altitude, where there is less oxygen in the air, so the body adapts by producing more red blood cells.
- **Oxygen-carrying capacity**: the amount of oxygen the blood can transport, which depends on the number of red blood cells and the haemoglobin they contain.
- **Red blood cells**: blood cells containing haemoglobin, which carries oxygen from the lungs to the working muscles.
- **Altitude sickness**: illness caused by the reduced oxygen at high altitude, with symptoms such as headaches, nausea and breathlessness.
- **Pre-season (preparation)**: the phase before competition, used to build general/aerobic fitness and then the specific fitness needs of the sport.
- **Competition season (peak/playing)**: the phase when matches or events take place, used to maintain fitness and work on specific skills.
- **Post-season (transition)**: the phase after competition, used for rest, recovery and light aerobic training to maintain general fitness.
:::

::: method
1. **For altitude, give the chain:** high altitude → less oxygen in the air → reduced oxygen-carrying capacity → body makes more red blood cells → more oxygen to the muscles on return → better aerobic performance.
2. **Match it to the performer:** is the event aerobic (benefit) or anaerobic (little benefit)? Say why.
3. **Evaluate both sides:** benefits (red blood cells, endurance, legal) and limitations (cost, sickness, reduced training intensity, benefits lost after return), each applied, then give a judgement.
4. **For seasons, name all three with their alternative names** and give each one's aim.
5. **Apply to the named sport:** say what training the performer would actually do in that season, and justify it with the benefit (fit for the first match, maintained through the season, recovered but not detrained).
:::

::: example Explain — how altitude training works
Explain how high altitude training can improve performance. (3)

At high altitude there is less oxygen in the air, so the body's oxygen-carrying capacity is reduced (1).

The body compensates by producing more red blood cells to carry oxygen (1).

On returning to sea level, more oxygen can be delivered to the working muscles, so the performer's cardiovascular endurance improves and they can work harder for longer (1).
:::

::: example Name the seasons
Name the three training seasons. (3)

Pre-season / preparation (1).

Competition / peak / playing season (1).

Post-season / transition (1).

::: example Justify — post-season training
Justify the training a hockey player should do in the post-season. (3)

She should rest to recover from the physical and mental demands of the season and let any injuries heal (1).

She should also do light aerobic training, such as easy jogging, swimming or cycling a few times a week (1).

This maintains a level of general fitness, so reversibility does not undo all her fitness before the next pre-season, and she starts that phase from a higher base (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — altitude for a runner
Evaluate the use of altitude training for a 10,000 m runner. (4)

For: the 10,000 m is an aerobic event, so the extra red blood cells produced at altitude increase oxygen delivery to the muscles and should improve his race pace and delay fatigue (1). It is a legal way to gain this advantage (1).

Against: he may suffer altitude sickness, and with less oxygen he cannot train at his normal intensity, so some fitness may be lost (1).

The benefit fades within a few weeks of returning, so the camp must be timed carefully before a key race. It is also expensive and takes him away from home (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — 9-mark
Evaluate the use of high altitude training for a triathlete and for a 100 m sprinter. (9)

(AO1) Altitude training is aerobic training at high altitude. There is less oxygen in the air, which reduces oxygen-carrying capacity, and the body compensates by producing more red blood cells. Limitations include altitude sickness, reduced training intensity, cost, and the benefits being lost after return.

(AO2) The triathlete races for up to two hours or more, swimming, cycling and running aerobically, so a few weeks at a mountain training camp could raise her red blood cell count before a key race. The sprinter's race lasts about 10 seconds and is anaerobic, relying on power and speed.

(AO3) For the triathlete, the extra oxygen delivered to the working muscles on return should let her hold a higher pace in all three disciplines and delay fatigue. That is a significant advantage in a long aerobic event, gained legally. However, she may struggle with altitude sickness, and training quality may drop because she cannot reach her usual intensity. The gains fade within weeks, so timing is critical, and the cost may be too high for an amateur. For the sprinter, the benefit is very small. More red blood cells do not help an anaerobic event where oxygen delivery is not the limiting factor, and the thinner air may reduce the quality of his speed and power sessions. The cost and time away are hard to justify. Overall, altitude training is highly appropriate for the triathlete if it is well timed and affordable, but largely inappropriate for the sprinter.
:::
