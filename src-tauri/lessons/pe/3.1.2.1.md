## What it is

Every movement at a joint is a **lever system**. A lever has three parts:
- **Fulcrum (F)**: the pivot point, which is the **joint**.
- **Effort (E)**: the force applied, from the **muscle** pulling on the bone via its tendon.
- **Load / resistance (L)**: the weight being moved: the body part itself plus anything it carries, such as a ball, a dumbbell or the whole body.

The bone is the lever arm. Which of the three sits **in the middle** decides the **class** of lever, and the class decides whether the lever is built for **force** or for **speed and range of movement**.

### The three classes

A memory aid: **"F-L-E, 1-2-3"**. The letter in the middle gives the class: **F**ulcrum in the middle is **1st** class, **L**oad in the middle is **2nd** class, **E**ffort in the middle is **3rd** class.

| Class | In the middle | Linear drawing (left to right) | Sporting example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First class** | **fulcrum** | L — F — E | **neck extension** when heading a football (the joint at the top of the spine is the fulcrum, the weight of the head is the load, the neck muscles at the back pull as the effort); **extension of the elbow**, as in a throw-in or pressing a weight overhead (triceps effort behind the elbow) |
| **Second class** | **load** | F — L — E | **plantar flexion at the ankle** when rising on to the toes or taking off in a jump (ball of the foot is the fulcrum, body weight through the ankle is the load, the gastrocnemius pulls up on the heel as the effort) |
| **Third class** | **effort** | F — E — L | **flexion of the elbow** in a biceps curl (elbow = fulcrum, biceps attach just below the elbow = effort, weight in the hand = load); **flexion and extension of the knee**, as in kicking (knee = fulcrum, hamstrings or quadriceps attach just below the knee = effort, lower leg and foot = load) |

Most levers in the body are **third class**.

**Drawing a linear lever.** The exam asks for a simple straight-line diagram, not a drawing of the body. Draw a horizontal line (the lever) with:
- a **triangle** under it for the **fulcrum**;
- an **arrow** for the **effort**, pointing in the direction the muscle pulls;
- an **arrow** (or a block) for the **load**, usually pointing **down** because it is a weight.

For the **second class** ankle, the effort arrow must point **up** (the calf pulls the heel up). In June 2025 many students drew it pointing down and lost the mark. In a **third class** elbow flexion, the effort arrow also points **up**, near the fulcrum, and the load arrow points **down** at the far end. In a **first class** lever such as the neck, the load and effort are on opposite sides of the fulcrum, and both arrows point down.

### Effort arm, load arm and mechanical advantage

- **Effort arm**: the distance from the **fulcrum to the effort**.
- **Load (resistance) arm**: the distance from the **fulcrum to the load**.

**Mechanical advantage (MA) = effort arm ÷ load (resistance) arm.**

| Class | Arms | MA | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Second class** | effort arm **longer** than load arm | **greater than 1**: a **high** mechanical advantage | can move a **large load with a small effort**, e.g. lifting the whole body weight on to the toes to jump or sprint |
| **Third class** | effort arm **shorter** than load arm | **less than 1**: a **low** mechanical advantage (mechanical disadvantage) | needs a large effort to move the load, **but** gives a **large range of movement and speed** at the end of the lever, e.g. the fast-moving foot in a kick or hand in a throw |
| **First class** | depends where the fulcrum sits | above or below 1 | if the fulcrum is closer to the load, MA is above 1; if closer to the effort, below 1 |

Worked examples (illustrative measurements):
- Ankle rising on the toes: effort arm 12 cm, load arm 4 cm. MA = 12 ÷ 4 = **3**, so the calf can lift the body efficiently.
- Elbow in a biceps curl: effort arm 4 cm, load arm 32 cm. MA = 4 ÷ 32 = **0.125**. The biceps must pull hard, but a small shortening of the muscle moves the hand a long way, quickly.

That trade-off is the key interpretation point: **second class is built for force; third class is built for speed and range**.

### Movement analysis: joint actions in sporting movements

The movements were introduced in 3.1.1.1a. Here you apply them to **phases** of a movement: **preparation** (getting ready), **execution** (the action) and recovery.

| Action | Preparation / lowering | Execution / driving |
|---|---|---|
| **Push-up** (elbow) | flexion as the body lowers | extension as the body is pushed up |
| **Football throw-in** (elbow) | flexion as the ball is taken behind the head | extension as the ball is thrown |
| **Running**: drive (stance) leg | — | hip **extension**, knee **extension**, ankle **plantar flexion** to push off |
| **Running**: recovery leg | hip **flexion**, knee **flexion**, ankle **dorsiflexion** as the leg swings through | — |
| **Kicking** (kicking leg) | hip **extension**, knee **flexion** as the leg is drawn back | hip **flexion**, knee **extension**, ankle **plantar flexion** (toes pointed) as the foot strikes the ball |
| **Standing vertical jump** | hip **flexion**, knee **flexion**, ankle **dorsiflexion** in the crouch | hip **extension**, knee **extension**, ankle **plantar flexion** at take-off |
| **Basic squat** | hip **flexion**, knee **flexion**, ankle **dorsiflexion** going down | hip **extension**, knee **extension**, ankle **plantar flexion** coming up |
| **Cricket bowling** (shoulder) | — | **circumduction** of the shoulder as the straight arm swings in a full circle to release the ball |

Linking back to levers:
- Knee extension in a kick or jump is **third class**, giving the foot speed.
- Ankle plantar flexion at take-off is **second class**, lifting body weight efficiently.
- Elbow extension in a throw-in or push-up is **first class**.
- Elbow flexion is **third class**.

::: terms
- **Lever**: a rigid bar (a bone) that turns about a pivot (a joint) to move a load.
- **Fulcrum**: the pivot point of a lever; in the body it is the joint.
- **Effort**: the force applied to move the lever; in the body it comes from a muscle contracting.
- **Load (resistance)**: the weight moved by the lever, such as a body part, the body or an object held.
- **First class lever**: a lever with the fulcrum between the load and the effort, such as neck extension.
- **Second class lever**: a lever with the load between the fulcrum and the effort, such as plantar flexion at the ankle.
- **Third class lever**: a lever with the effort between the fulcrum and the load, such as elbow flexion.
- **Effort arm**: the distance from the fulcrum to the point where the effort is applied.
- **Load arm**: the distance from the fulcrum to the load (also called the resistance arm).
- **Mechanical advantage**: effort arm divided by load arm; a value above 1 means a large load can be moved with little effort.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the joint and movement** (e.g. ankle, plantar flexion).
2. **Place F, L and E**: joint = fulcrum; where the muscle pulls = effort; the weight moved = load. See which is in the middle: F = 1st, L = 2nd, E = 3rd.
3. **Describe or draw the linear lever**: a straight line, a triangle for the fulcrum, an arrow for effort in the direction of the muscle's pull (up for the ankle and elbow flexion), and the load arrow down. Label effort arm and load arm when asked.
4. **Mechanical advantage**: MA = effort arm ÷ load arm. Say whether it is high (>1, force) or low (<1, speed and range) and what that means for the action.
5. **Movement analysis**: split the skill into preparation and execution, and give the movement at each joint named: hip, knee, ankle, elbow or shoulder.
6. **Extended answers**: joint actions and lever classes (AO1), applied to phases of the named skill (AO2), then analysis of how the lever's force or speed advantage affects the outcome (AO3).
:::

::: example Identify the lever
A basketball player rises on to her toes to jump for a rebound. Identify the class of lever operating at the ankle. (1)

Second class lever (1).
:::

::: example Describe the drawing
Describe a fully labelled linear diagram of the lever operating at the ankle when the basketball player takes off. (3)

A straight line with the fulcrum (triangle) at one end, representing the ball of the foot (1). The load, body weight, is in the middle, with an arrow pointing down (1). The effort, the gastrocnemius, is at the other end with an arrow pointing up (1).
:::

::: example Mechanical advantage
Explain what is meant by mechanical advantage and why the ankle lever has a high mechanical advantage. (3)

Mechanical advantage is effort arm ÷ load arm (1). At the ankle the effort arm (from the ball of the foot to the heel) is longer than the load arm (from the ball of the foot to the ankle) (1), so the MA is greater than 1 and the calf can lift the whole body weight with a relatively small effort (1).
:::

::: example Movement analysis — kick
Identify the movement at the hip and knee of the kicking leg in the preparation phase and in the execution phase of a penalty kick. (4)

Preparation: hip extension (1) and knee flexion (1). Execution: hip flexion (1) and knee extension (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — levers in a jump
Analyse how the lever systems at the knee and ankle help a volleyball player jump to spike the ball. (6)

At the ankle, plantar flexion at take-off is a second class lever, with the load (body weight) between the fulcrum (ball of the foot) and the effort (gastrocnemius) (AO1/AO2). Its effort arm is longer than its load arm, giving a high mechanical advantage, so the calf can lift the player's whole body weight with relatively little effort; this lets the player drive powerfully off the floor on every jump without tiring as quickly (AO3). At the knee, extension is a third class lever, with the quadriceps' effort between the knee and the load of the lower leg (AO2). It has a low mechanical advantage, needing a large effort, but it gives a wide range of movement and high speed, so the legs straighten very quickly and produce the fast take-off velocity that gets the hand above the net (AO3). Together, the ankle gives efficient force and the knee gives speed, so the player reaches a greater height and hits the ball on a steeper downward angle (AO3).

Level 3 (5–6): correct lever classes and positions, applied to the take-off, with clear analysis of MA and the effect on the spike.
:::

::: watch
- **"F-L-E, 1-2-3"**: the middle letter gives the class. Second class has the **load** in the middle.
- In a second class ankle drawing the **effort arrow points up**. Drawing it down lost a mark for many in June 2025.
- A diagram must be **labelled**: fulcrum, load and effort, plus the effort arm and load arm when mechanical advantage is asked. Unlabelled arms cannot score the MA mark.
- If you get the lever class wrong, the follow-up drawing marks are lost too. Check where the muscle pulls before you commit.
- **MA = effort arm ÷ load arm**, not the other way up.
- Movement analysis needs the **phase**: knee flexion in the preparation of a kick, knee extension in execution.
- The movement at the ankle when pushing off is **plantar flexion**, not "extension".
:::

::: test
Analyse the joint movements and lever systems used by a footballer when striking the ball in a long pass. (6)
---
AO1 (1): knowledge of movements (flexion/extension, plantar flexion) and lever classes (third class at the knee, second class at the ankle of the standing leg).
AO2 (2): application: preparation — hip extension and knee flexion of the kicking leg; execution — hip flexion and knee extension (quadriceps effort, third class), with the ankle plantar flexed to strike with the laces; standing leg ankle on the ball of the foot / plantar flexion.
AO3 (3): analysis: the third class knee lever has a low MA but gives a large range and high speed at the foot, so more force transfers to the ball and it travels further; greater knee flexion in preparation increases the range for the execution; a pointed, locked ankle gives a firm contact surface for accuracy; the stable standing leg keeps balance so the strike is controlled; judgement on the most important action.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate movements and lever classes; clearly applied to both phases; thorough analysis of the effect on the pass. 3–4 — mostly accurate knowledge; some application; limited analysis. 1–2 — limited knowledge, e.g. movements named with little link to the phases or performance. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
Analyse how joint movements, lever systems and mechanical advantage contribute to a high jumper's take-off. (9)
---
AO1 (2): knowledge of the three lever classes (F-L-E), effort/load arms, MA = effort arm ÷ load arm, high vs low MA; movements: flexion/extension, plantar/dorsiflexion.
AO2 (2): application: approach run — drive leg hip extension, knee extension, plantar flexion; take-off preparation — hip and knee flexion, dorsiflexion as the take-off foot plants; execution — rapid hip extension, knee extension (third class, quadriceps effort) and plantar flexion (second class, gastrocnemius effort, fulcrum at the ball of the foot); arms and free leg swing upward (shoulder flexion, hip flexion).
AO3 (5): analysis: second class ankle lever has a long effort arm and high MA, so the calf lifts the whole body weight efficiently in the final push; the third class knee lever has low MA but high speed and range, so the leg straightens quickly for greater vertical velocity; flexing the knee in the plant stores and then releases force, turning horizontal speed into height; the combination of force (ankle) and speed (knee, hip) determines take-off height; weaknesses, e.g. the third class lever needs strong quadriceps because of its mechanical disadvantage; a reasoned conclusion on which contributes most.
Levels: 7–9 — accurate, detailed knowledge; consistently applied to the phases of the take-off; thorough analysis of MA and its effect with a well-reasoned conclusion; correct terminology. 4–6 — knowledge evident but uneven; some application; analysis present but undeveloped. 1–3 — limited knowledge; little application; little or no analysis. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
