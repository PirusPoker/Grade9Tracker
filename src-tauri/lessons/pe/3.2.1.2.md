## What it is

**Goal setting** is choosing targets that give training a purpose. Done well, it raises motivation, focuses effort, helps a performer control anxiety, and makes progress visible. This topic builds on the two goal types from 3.2.1.1 and adds the checklist that makes a target work: **SMART**.

### Using and evaluating performance and outcome goals

| | Performance goal | Outcome goal |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | the performer's own technique, time, score or statistic | the result — winning, a ranking, a medal |
| Control | **fully within the performer's control** | depends on **factors outside their control**: opponents, team-mates, officials, weather, luck |
| Strengths | progress is measurable and can be achieved even in defeat; keeps motivation high; reduces anxiety because the performer only has to worry about their own performance | can be highly motivating for an elite performer or team; gives a clear end point (a title, a medal) |
| Weaknesses | may feel less exciting than winning | a performer can improve and still "fail"; failure demotivates; the pressure to win raises anxiety |

**The key points to make in an evaluation:**
- **The two can be combined.** A sensible plan pairs an outcome goal ("reach the county final") with the performance goals that make it more likely ("80% first-serve accuracy", "fewer than three unforced errors a set").
- **Outcome goals are generally best avoided**, because they rely on things the performer cannot control — above all, **other performers**. A swimmer who knocks two seconds off a personal best but finishes fourth has done everything right and still missed an outcome goal.
- **Beginners in particular should avoid outcome goals.** They are unlikely to win against more experienced performers, so winning is an **unrealistic** goal; repeated **failure demotivates** them and may make them drop out. Performance goals let a beginner see steady improvement ("I can now serve over the net 7 times out of 10"), which builds confidence.
- **Elite performers** can use outcome goals more readily, because winning is realistic for them and they are more resilient to a setback — but even they rely on performance goals day to day.

### SMART targets

AQA's version of SMART — learn these exact words:

| Letter | Stands for | What it means | Example for a club 800 m runner |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | **Specific** | clear and precise, tied to one part of performance — not "get better" | improve my 800 m time |
| **M** | **Measurable** | progress can be measured with a number, so you can tell if it has been met | from 2:18 to 2:14 |
| **A** | **Accepted** | agreed by the performer and the coach, so the performer owns it and commits to it | agreed with my coach at our review |
| **R** | **Realistic** | challenging but possible given the performer's ability, time and resources | four seconds is a sensible gain for one season at my level |
| **T** | **Time-bound** | has a deadline or a review date | by the county championships in June |

Put together: *"To lower my 800 m time from 2:18 to 2:14 by the county championships in June, agreed with my coach, through two interval sessions a week."*

**Why each part improves or optimises performance:**
- **Specific** — the performer knows exactly what to work on, so training is focused rather than general.
- **Measurable** — the performer can see improvement, which motivates; the coach can check whether training is working and adjust it.
- **Accepted** — a goal imposed by a coach may be ignored; one the performer has agreed to is one they will work for. Commitment and motivation rise.
- **Realistic** — too easy and the performer is bored and does not improve; too hard and failure demotivates and raises anxiety. A realistic goal keeps effort and confidence high.
- **Time-bound** — a deadline creates urgency and stops the performer putting off training; it also sets a point to review and set the next goal.

A beginner's SMART target is still SMART — just smaller. *"Swim 25 m front crawl without stopping by the end of the six-week course, agreed with my teacher"* is specific, measurable, accepted, realistic and time-bound.

::: terms
- **Goal setting**: choosing targets for a performer to work towards, to raise motivation, focus training and improve performance.
- **Performance goal**: a target about improving one's own performance, such as a time or technique, with no comparison to others.
- **Outcome goal**: a target about the result of a competition, such as winning, which depends partly on other people.
- **SMART**: a checklist for goal setting: specific, measurable, accepted, realistic and time-bound.
- **Specific**: a goal that is clear and precise about exactly which part of performance is to improve.
- **Measurable**: a goal whose progress can be measured with a figure, so it is clear whether it has been reached.
- **Accepted**: a goal agreed by both the performer and the coach, so that the performer is committed to it.
- **Realistic**: a goal that is challenging but possible for the performer, given their ability and resources.
- **Time-bound**: a goal with a set deadline or review date by which it should be met.
:::

::: method
1. **Recall the letters exactly**: specific, measurable, **accepted**, realistic, time-bound. "Achievable" is not AQA's word.
2. **When asked to set a SMART target**, write one sentence containing a skill or event, a figure, a deadline, and who agreed it — then say why it is realistic for *this* performer.
3. **When asked to explain SMART**, give each letter's meaning and *how* it improves performance (motivation, focus, confidence, review).
4. **For performance vs outcome questions**, use the control argument: outcome goals depend on others, so can lead to failure and demotivation.
5. **Always name the level of the performer** — beginners avoid outcome goals; elite performers may combine both.
6. **In evaluate questions**, give strengths and limitations of each goal type, then a judgement (usually: combine them, with performance goals as the basis).
:::

::: example Explain — beginners and outcome goals
Explain why a beginner should avoid setting outcome goals. (2)

A beginner is unlikely to win against more experienced performers, so an outcome goal such as winning is unrealistic (1); repeatedly failing to reach it would demotivate them and could lead them to give up the activity (1).
:::

::: example Apply SMART to a performer
Ffion is a netball goal shooter who scores 60% of her shots. Using the SMART principle, set a suitable target for her and explain how it would improve her performance. (4)

Target: "Raise my shooting accuracy from 60% to 70% of attempts over the next eight weeks, as agreed with my coach" (1). It is specific and measurable — it names shooting accuracy and a percentage, so Ffion knows exactly what to practise and can record her success rate in every match to see progress, which motivates her (1). It is accepted, so she is committed to the extra shooting practice (1). A 10% rise is realistic for a player already at 60%, and the eight-week deadline gives her urgency and a date to review and set a new target (1).
:::

::: example Compare goals for two performers
Explain why an Olympic champion rower might use outcome goals but a beginner rower should not. (4)

For the Olympic champion, winning is a realistic target because they are among the best in the world (1), and an outcome goal such as retaining the title can drive them through years of hard training (1). A beginner has little chance of winning races against experienced crews, so an outcome goal is unrealistic (1); failing to win would demotivate them, whereas a performance goal such as improving their 2 km time lets them see progress they control (1).
:::

::: example Extended — analyse SMART for a named performer
Kai is a 15-year-old club swimmer who wants to be selected for the regional squad. Analyse how SMART targets could help Kai improve his performance. (6)

A specific target such as improving his 100 m backstroke turn tells Kai exactly what to work on, so his sessions focus on the part of the race costing him time rather than general swimming. Making it measurable — cutting his time from 1:12 to 1:09 — means his coach can time him each week; seeing seconds come off keeps him motivated, and if progress stalls the coach knows to change the training. Because the target is accepted by both Kai and his coach, Kai owns it and is more likely to attend early-morning sessions. It must be realistic: three seconds is achievable for a developing 15-year-old, whereas ten seconds would lead to failure, demotivation and anxiety. Finally, making it time-bound — before the regional trials in March — gives urgency and a clear review point. Together these features raise his motivation and focus his training, which gives him the best chance of the selection he wants. (Level 3: each letter known, applied to Kai and analysed for its effect on his performance.)
:::

::: q
Explain why a target should be accepted by the performer. (2)
---
If the performer has agreed to the target they feel ownership/commitment (1), so they are more motivated to train towards it than if it were imposed by the coach (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why outcome goals are generally avoided. (2)
---
They depend on factors the performer cannot control, e.g. how well opponents perform / officials / weather (1); so a performer can perform well and still fail, which is demotivating (1).
:::

::: q
Give one reason why a target should be realistic. (2)
---
If it is too hard the performer will fail, lose motivation/confidence (1); if it is too easy there is no challenge and performance will not improve (1).
:::

::: q
Set a SMART target for a beginner golfer. (3)
---
One sentence containing: a specific, measurable aim, e.g. "hit 6 out of 10 drives onto the fairway" (1); a deadline, e.g. "by the end of the 8-week course" (1); agreement with coach and a realistic level for a beginner (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how combining performance and outcome goals could help a football team. (3)
---
The outcome goal (e.g. win the league) gives a long-term target that motivates the team (1); performance goals (e.g. complete 80% of passes, concede fewer than one goal a game) are within the players' control and can be measured each week (1); meeting the performance goals makes the outcome more likely and maintains motivation even after a defeat (1).
:::

::: watch
- AQA's **A = accepted**. "Achievable" or "agreed" on its own as the answer to "what does A stand for" risks the mark — write **accepted**.
- A SMART target **must contain a number and a deadline** — "get better at shooting this season" is neither measurable nor specific.
- When the question names a performer, **use their details** (their event, their current score) — generic SMART definitions earn AO1 only.
- "Explain how SMART improves performance" needs the **effect** (motivation, focus, confidence, review) — not just what the letters mean.
- The argument against outcome goals is **lack of control** over other performers, not "they are harder".
- Beginners: outcome goals are **unrealistic** and failure **demotivates** — make both points.
:::
