## What it is

There are seven **types of training** in the spec. For each you need to **describe** it, know its **advantages and disadvantages** (the effects on the body), and **select and justify** the right one for a performer's **aerobic or anaerobic** needs. Types of training are among the most common 6- and 9-mark topics on Paper 1: "evaluate continuous training for…", "evaluate interval training for team games".

### The seven methods

**Circuit training.** A series of **stations**, each with a different exercise, done one after another with set work and rest periods. The design must consider the **space** and **equipment** available, the **number of stations**, and the **work:rest ratio**. The **content and demand can be changed** to target different components. Skill stations (dribbling, passing) make it sport-specific. Press-ups and step-ups build muscular endurance. Short, intense stations with long rests build power and speed. Long work periods with short rests build aerobic fitness.

**Continuous training.** Sustained exercise at a **constant rate (steady state)** with **no rest**, working **aerobically** for **at least 20 minutes**, e.g. running, swimming, rowing, cycling. It typically works in the aerobic training zone (60–80% of max heart rate). It is fully appropriate for marathon runners and other endurance performers.

**Fartlek training.** "Speed play". Continuous exercise with **varying speed**, **terrain** and **work:recovery ratios**: jog, then sprint to a lamp post, walk, stride uphill, jog again. It works both **aerobic and anaerobic** systems, so it suits games players whose sport changes pace.

**Interval training / HIIT.** **Periods of hard exercise** interspersed with **periods of rest or low-intensity exercise**, e.g. 8 × 200 m with 90 seconds' walk recovery. Changing the work intensity, work time and rest makes it aerobic or anaerobic. **High intensity interval training (HIIT)** uses very hard, short efforts (e.g. 30 seconds all-out, 30 seconds easy).

**Static stretching.** A stretch is taken to the end of the range and **held still (isometric) for up to 30 seconds**, using **correct technique**. Over-stretching should be avoided. It improves **flexibility**.

**Weight training.** A form of resistance training using free weights or machines. The **choice of weight and exercise depends on the aim** (see 3.1.3.4a):
- **strength/power**: **high weight, low reps** (above 70% of one rep max, about 3 sets of 4–8)
- **muscular endurance**: **low weight, high reps** (below 70% of one rep max, about 3 sets of 12–15)

**Safe practice** matters: correct lifting technique (straight back, lift with the legs), a warm-up, and **spotters** for heavy lifts such as the bench press.

**Plyometric training.** Explosive jumping and bounding exercises, e.g. **bounding**, **depth jumping** (dropping off a box and immediately jumping up), hopping and box jumps, to increase **power**. The physiology: when landing, the muscle (e.g. the quadriceps) lengthens under tension in an **eccentric contraction**, which stretches it and stores energy like a spring. This is followed immediately by a **larger, more forceful concentric contraction** as the performer jumps.

### Every method must consider

- the **training purpose**: which component and which energy system is being targeted
- **training thresholds, targets and zones**: working in the right heart-rate zone (aerobic 60–80%, anaerobic 80–90% of max) or at the right % of one rep max
- **rest and recovery**: between repetitions, and between sessions, so the body can adapt and avoid injury

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Method | Advantages (effects on the body) | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Circuit** | can target almost any component, aerobic or anaerobic; varied, so less tedium; can include skills so it is sport-specific; suits groups | takes time and space to set up; needs equipment; hard to check everyone's technique in a big group; general stations may not be specific |
| **Continuous** | improves **cardiovascular endurance** (stronger heart, lower resting heart rate, more capillaries); little or no equipment; easy to do alone; burns fat | can be **tedious**; does not improve speed or anaerobic fitness; not specific to stop-start games; repetitive impact can cause overuse injuries |
| **Fartlek** | works **aerobic and anaerobic** systems; changes of pace mimic games; varied terrain reduces tedium; flexible, little equipment | hard for a coach to monitor; relies on self-motivation, so hard parts can be skipped; needs varied terrain |
| **Interval / HIIT** | can be aerobic or anaerobic by changing work and rest; improves speed and **tolerance of lactic acid**; easy to measure and progress; HIIT is time-efficient; suits games | very demanding, so needs high motivation; high intensity raises injury risk; can be tedious on a track |
| **Static stretching** | increases **range of movement** at a joint; controlled and relatively safe; no equipment; can be done anywhere | gains are slow; only improves flexibility; risk of injury if over-stretched or bounced |
| **Weight** | builds **strength or muscular endurance** of specific muscles; muscle **hypertrophy**; stronger bones and tendons; progress is easy to measure (kg) | needs equipment or a gym (cost); injury risk with poor technique; needs spotters for heavy lifts |
| **Plyometric** | develops **power** for jumping and sprinting sports; sport-specific movements; little equipment | high-impact stress on joints and tendons; injury risk; can cause muscle soreness (DOMS); needs a base of strength and good technique |

### Selecting a method

Match the method to the **energy demand** and the **component**:
- **Aerobic, long duration** (marathon, triathlon, long-distance swimming): **continuous**, with some fartlek for variety.
- **Anaerobic, short and explosive** (100 m, long jump, volleyball spike): **interval/HIIT**, **plyometrics**, **weights** for strength and power.
- **Intermittent team games** (football, hockey, netball): **fartlek**, **interval/HIIT** and **circuits** with skill stations. They mix aerobic and anaerobic work and changes of pace.
- **Flexibility-dependent** activities (gymnastics, hurdles, dance): **static stretching** alongside the main method.

::: terms
- **Circuit training**: a series of exercise stations completed in order with set work and rest periods, adaptable to target different components.
- **Continuous training**: sustained exercise at a constant (steady-state) rate without rest, working aerobically for at least 20 minutes.
- **Fartlek training**: continuous exercise with changes of speed, terrain and work:recovery ratio, working aerobic and anaerobic systems.
- **Interval training**: periods of hard exercise interspersed with periods of rest or low-intensity exercise.
- **HIIT**: high intensity interval training — very hard, short work periods alternating with short rest or easy recovery.
- **Static stretching**: stretching a muscle to the end of its range and holding it still (isometric) for up to 30 seconds.
- **Weight training**: using free weights or machines as resistance; heavy/low reps for strength, light/high reps for muscular endurance.
- **Plyometric training**: explosive jumping or bounding exercises in which an eccentric contraction is followed by a larger concentric contraction, to increase power.
- **Work:rest ratio**: the length of the work period compared with the rest period, e.g. 1:2.
- **Spotter**: a person who stands by a lifter to help and prevent injury if the weight cannot be controlled.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the performer's demand:** aerobic or anaerobic, and which components matter most (from the sport and the position).
2. **Describe the method accurately:** e.g. continuous = steady state, no rest, at least 20 minutes, aerobic; plyometrics = eccentric then larger concentric contraction.
3. **Apply it:** give a session this performer would actually do, with distances, times, work:rest or % of one rep max.
4. **Weigh it up:** give advantages (effects on the body, specificity, motivation) **and** disadvantages (not specific, injury, tedium, equipment), each applied to the performer.
5. **Conclude:** is it appropriate, fully or partly? Suggest a better or complementary method if the question allows, with a reason.
:::

::: example Describe — fartlek
Describe fartlek training. (2)

Continuous training in which the performer varies the speed and/or terrain (1).

It mixes periods of hard work with recovery, e.g. jog, sprint, walk, run uphill, so it works both the aerobic and anaerobic systems (1).
:::

::: example Explain — plyometrics
Explain how plyometric training improves power. (3)

Plyometrics uses explosive exercises such as bounding or depth jumps (1).

On landing, the muscles lengthen under tension in an eccentric contraction, which stretches them and stores energy (1).

This is followed immediately by a larger, more forceful concentric contraction as the performer jumps, training the muscles to produce more force quickly, so power (strength × speed) increases (1).
:::

::: example Circuit design
Explain how a circuit could be changed so that it improves muscular endurance rather than power. (3)

Use exercises that work the same muscle groups repeatedly, such as press-ups, sit-ups and step-ups, at a lower resistance (1).

Make the work periods longer, e.g. 60 seconds instead of 15 (1).

Use shorter rests between stations, e.g. a 2:1 work:rest ratio, so the muscles must keep contracting without fully recovering (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — continuous training for a games player
Evaluate the use of continuous training for a hockey player. (4)

For: it improves cardiovascular endurance, e.g. through a stronger heart and a lower resting heart rate, which helps the player keep working for the whole match and recover between sprints (1). It is easy to organise and needs no equipment (1).

Against: hockey is stop-start, with sprints and changes of direction, but continuous training is steady-paced, so it does not improve speed, agility or anaerobic fitness (1).

It can be tedious. Fartlek or interval training would be more specific because they mix pace changes and recovery, like a match (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — 9-mark
Evaluate the use of interval training and continuous training for a 1500 m runner. (9)

(AO1) Interval training is periods of hard work separated by rest or low-intensity recovery. Continuous training is steady-state exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes, working aerobically.

(AO2) A 1500 m race lasts about four minutes and is mainly aerobic, with an anaerobic sprint finish. The runner could do 6 × 400 m at race pace with 90 seconds' recovery (interval), and a 40-minute steady run at 70% of max heart rate (continuous).

(AO3) Continuous training builds the aerobic base: a stronger heart, more capillaries and a lower resting heart rate let the runner hold a fast pace through the first three laps without building up lactic acid. It is also low-risk and easy to do. But on its own it does not develop the speed or lactic acid tolerance needed for the final 300 m, and weekly long runs can become tedious. Interval training is more specific. Working at race pace develops both systems, and the recovery periods allow a greater volume of fast running than a single hard effort. It also improves the kick at the end of the race, and changing the reps and rest makes progressive overload easy to measure. However, it is very demanding and raises the injury risk if overused, so it needs rest days. Overall, interval training is the more important method for a 1500 m runner because it matches the race's pace and mixed energy demand. Continuous training remains valuable as the aerobic foundation, especially in pre-season, so the best programme uses both.
:::
