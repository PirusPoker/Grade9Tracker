## What it is

The **skeleton** is the body's rigid framework. On its own it cannot move, but working **with the muscular system** it lets a performer run, jump, throw and tackle. Paper 1 tests three things here: naming the bones at each major joint, explaining the skeleton's functions *in sport*, and knowing how synovial joints are built to move freely without injury.

### Bones you must identify

Learn the bones joint by joint, because questions describe a joint ("the bones at the performer's elbow") or give a labelled figure of one region.

| Location | Bones |
|---|---|
| Head and neck | **cranium**, **vertebrae** |
| Shoulder | **scapula**, **humerus** |
| Chest | **ribs**, **sternum** |
| Elbow | **humerus**, **radius**, **ulna** |
| Hip | **pelvis**, **femur** |
| Knee | **femur**, **tibia** (the **patella** sits in front of the knee joint) |
| Ankle | **tibia**, **fibula**, **talus** |

Examiners do not credit "skull" for cranium or "hip bone" for pelvis. The humerus, femur and tibia each appear at two joints, so learn the table as a chain down the arm and the leg.

### How the skeleton gives a framework for movement

- **Movement happens at joints.** Where two or more bones meet, the skeleton can bend, straighten or rotate.
- **Shape and type of bone decide how much movement.** **Long bones** (femur, humerus, tibia) act as levers for **gross movement**, such as kicking or throwing a javelin. **Short bones** (the talus, the small bones of the wrist and foot) allow **finer, controlled movement**, such as small adjustments of the foot on a balance beam.
- **Flat bones protect vital organs**: the cranium, ribs, sternum, scapula and pelvis.
- **Different joint types allow different movements.** A hinge joint moves in one direction only; a ball and socket joint moves in almost every direction.
- **The skeleton provides attachment points for muscles.** Muscles are joined to bones by **tendons**. When a muscle contracts it **pulls** on the bone, and the bone moves at the joint.

### The six functions of the skeleton, applied to sport

The spec wants every function *applied to performance*, so learn an example with each one.

| Function | What it means | Sporting application |
|---|---|---|
| **Support** | holds the body upright and keeps organs in place | a gymnast holds a still handstand; a rugby prop stays upright and stable in the scrum |
| **Protection of vital organs** (by flat bones) | a hard casing around delicate organs | the **cranium** protects the brain when heading a football; the **ribs** and **sternum** protect the heart and lungs in a rugby tackle or a punch to the body in boxing |
| **Movement** | bones are levers moved by muscles at joints | the femur and tibia swing at the hip and knee to kick a ball |
| **Structural shape and points for attachment** | gives the body its shape and gives muscles somewhere to attach via tendons | a tall frame and long levers help a basketball player or high jumper; the tendon of the quadriceps pulls on the tibia to extend the knee |
| **Mineral storage** | bones store **calcium** and **phosphorus**, released when the body needs them | strong, dense bones resist fractures in contact sports; calcium is also needed for muscle contraction |
| **Blood cell production** | **bone marrow** in long bones such as the femur makes blood cells | **red blood cells** carry oxygen to working muscles (vital for an endurance runner); **white blood cells** fight infection so the athlete can keep training; **platelets** help blood clot after a cut |

A mark scheme wants **function + application**. "Protection" alone is one mark at most; "the ribs protect the heart and lungs when a rugby player is tackled" earns the application too.

### Synovial joints and injury prevention

The shoulder, elbow, hip, knee and ankle are all **synovial joints**, also called freely movable joints. Each has a set of structures, and the exam asks you to identify each one *and* explain how it helps prevent injury.

| Structure | Where / what | How it helps prevent injury |
|---|---|---|
| **Joint capsule** | a tough, fibrous sac that encloses the whole joint | surrounds and holds the joint together, adding stability |
| **Synovial membrane** | the lining on the inside of the capsule | **secretes synovial fluid** into the joint |
| **Synovial fluid** | fluid filling the joint cavity | **lubricates** the joint, reducing friction between the bones so they move smoothly without wear |
| **Cartilage** | smooth tissue covering the ends of the bones | acts as a **shock absorber** and stops the bones rubbing against each other |
| **Ligaments** | tough bands joining **bone to bone** outside the joint | hold the joint in place and limit unwanted movement, preventing **dislocation** |
| **Bursae** | small fluid-filled sacs between tendon and bone | reduce friction where tendons rub over bone, cushioning the joint |

Keep ligaments and tendons apart: **ligaments join bone to bone; tendons join muscle to bone.**

### Joint types and the movements they allow

| Joint | Type | Movements (spec) |
|---|---|---|
| Elbow | **hinge** | flexion, extension |
| Knee | **hinge** | flexion, extension |
| Ankle | **hinge** | plantar flexion, dorsiflexion |
| Hip | **ball and socket** | flexion, extension |
| Shoulder | **ball and socket** | flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation, circumduction |

A **hinge joint** lets the bones move in one direction and back, like a door. A **ball and socket joint** has a rounded head of one bone (the head of the humerus or femur) sitting in a cup of another (the scapula or pelvis), so it moves in many directions and can rotate. The trade-off: the shoulder has the greatest range of movement in the body but is less stable, so it depends heavily on ligaments and the rotator cuff muscles.

The movements themselves:
- **Flexion**: bending at a joint, which decreases the angle between the bones.
- **Extension**: straightening at a joint, which increases the angle.
- **Abduction**: moving a limb **away from** the midline of the body.
- **Adduction**: moving a limb **towards** the midline.
- **Rotation**: the limb turns around its own long axis.
- **Circumduction**: the limb moves in a circle, combining flexion, extension, abduction and adduction; only a ball and socket joint can do it.
- **Plantar flexion**: pointing the toes, moving the foot away from the shin.
- **Dorsiflexion**: pulling the toes up towards the shin.

Here you only need to link each *movement* to its *joint type*. Applying them to phases of a sporting action (the knee in a kick, the elbow in a throw-in) is taught in movement analysis, 3.1.2.1.

::: terms
- **Cranium**: the flat bone case of the head that protects the brain; examiners do not accept "skull".
- **Flat bone**: a broad, flat bone such as the cranium, ribs, sternum or pelvis whose main role is protecting vital organs.
- **Long bone**: a bone longer than it is wide, such as the femur or humerus, that acts as a lever for gross movement.
- **Synovial joint**: a freely movable joint with a capsule, a membrane and fluid-filled cavity, such as the knee or shoulder.
- **Synovial fluid**: the lubricating fluid inside a synovial joint that reduces friction between the bones.
- **Cartilage**: smooth tissue covering the ends of bones at a joint that absorbs shock and stops bones rubbing.
- **Ligament**: a tough band of tissue that joins bone to bone and keeps a joint stable.
- **Bursa**: a small fluid-filled sac between a tendon and a bone that reduces friction at a joint.
- **Hinge joint**: a joint allowing movement in one direction and back only, such as the elbow, knee and ankle.
- **Ball and socket joint**: a joint where a rounded head sits in a cup, allowing movement in many directions, such as the hip and shoulder.
- **Circumduction**: circular movement of a limb at a ball and socket joint combining flexion, extension, abduction and adduction.
- **Abduction**: movement of a limb away from the midline of the body.
:::

::: method
1. **Bones questions**: find the joint first, then give the bones from the table in anatomical names only (cranium, pelvis, never skull or hip bone). One bone per label; do not hedge with two.
2. **Function questions**: name the function, then apply it with a specific sporting moment and the bone involved: "the ribs protect the lungs when a rugby player is tackled".
3. **Synovial joint questions**: name the structure, say where or what it is, then say *how* it prevents injury (reduces friction, absorbs shock, stops dislocation, adds stability).
4. **Joint type questions**: hinge for elbow, knee and ankle; ball and socket for hip and shoulder. Answer "synovial" only if the question asks for the class of joint, never for the type.
5. **Movement questions**: match the movement to a joint that allows it; only the shoulder does abduction, adduction, rotation and circumduction in this spec.
6. **Extended answers (6 or 9)**: give accurate knowledge (AO1), apply every point to the named performer (AO2), then analyse *why* it matters for their performance, ending with a judgement about which function or structure matters most (AO3).
:::

::: example Synovial structures
Explain how two structures of a synovial joint help to prevent injury to a netball player's knee when she lands. (4)

Cartilage covers the ends of the femur and tibia (1) and absorbs the shock of landing, so the bones do not grind against each other (1). Synovial fluid lubricates the joint (1), reducing friction as the knee bends on landing so the surfaces do not wear (1).
:::

::: example Functions for a performer
Explain how blood cell production helps an 800 m runner. (2)

Bone marrow in long bones produces red blood cells (1), which carry more oxygen to the working leg muscles so the runner can keep a fast pace for longer before tiring (1).
:::

::: example Justify — extended
Justify the importance of the functions of the skeleton for a rugby player. (6)

The skeleton protects vital organs: the ribs and sternum shield the heart and lungs when the player is tackled or rucks, and the cranium protects the brain in collisions; without it a single tackle could cause a serious injury and end the game (AO1/AO2). It provides support, so a prop can stay upright and stable while the scrum pushes against him; if he collapsed, his team would lose the scrum and possession (AO2/AO3). It allows movement, because muscles pull on long bones like the femur to sprint and change direction, which is needed to break through defenders (AO2). Blood cell production matters too: red blood cells carry oxygen for repeated sprints, although in a collision sport protection is arguably the most important function because it keeps the player safe to play at all (AO3).

A level 3 answer (5–6) because each function is applied to a rugby moment and the answer reaches a reasoned judgement.
:::

::: watch
- **Cranium, not skull**; **pelvis, not hip bone**. Unscientific names score nothing.
- The **patella** is not at the knee joint itself; it sits in front of it. The bones of the knee are the femur and tibia.
- **Ligament = bone to bone; tendon = muscle to bone.** Swapping them loses the mark every time.
- "Synovial" is not a joint **type** in this spec. Give hinge or ball and socket.
- A function without a sporting application usually earns only the knowledge mark. Name the moment: tackle, header, scrum, sprint.
- Blood cell production and mineral storage are functions too. They are the ones students forget when asked for four.
- Circumduction, rotation and abduction/adduction happen only at the **ball and socket** joints in this spec.
:::

::: test
Analyse how the structures of a synovial joint help a basketball player to perform repeated jumps and landings during a match. (6)
---
AO1 (1): accurate knowledge of synovial structures: cartilage, synovial fluid, synovial membrane, joint capsule, ligaments, bursae.
AO2 (2): applied to the jumps and landings at the knee and ankle: cartilage cushions the femur and tibia on landing; fluid lets the knee bend and straighten smoothly for the next jump; ligaments hold the knee and ankle steady when landing on one foot.
AO3 (3): analysis of the effect on performance: less friction and wear means less pain and fatigue in the joint, so the player can jump as high in the final quarter as the first; shock absorption reduces the risk of injury, so they stay on court; stability lets them land and immediately change direction, gaining a rebound or getting back in defence; judgement, e.g. cartilage and ligaments matter most because landing forces are greatest.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate, detailed knowledge; clearly applied to the basketball player; thorough analysis of how each structure affects performance, with correct terminology. 3–4 — knowledge evident but with some inaccuracy or one part developed more than another; some application; analysis present but not developed. 1–2 — limited knowledge, a list of structures with little application or analysis. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
Evaluate the importance of the skeleton's functions for an elite gymnast performing a floor routine. (9)
---
AO1 (2): knowledge of the functions: support, protection, movement, structural shape and attachment, mineral storage, blood cell production; joint types (ball and socket shoulder, hinge knee).
AO2 (2): application to the routine: support in a still handstand or balance; movement at the shoulder (ball and socket, circumduction when circling the arms in a dance element) and knee (hinge, extension on take-off for a tumble); attachment points for the quadriceps and deltoid to generate power; cranium protecting the brain in a fall; short bones of the foot allowing fine control on landing.
AO3 (5): evaluation: movement and support are most important because the routine is judged on skill, control and holding balances — without a rigid, supported frame the gymnast cannot hold a handstand, which loses marks; attachment points allow powerful muscles to pull on long bones for height in tumbles, raising difficulty scores; protection matters less in a non-contact event but becomes vital when a landing goes wrong; blood cell production and mineral storage are less immediate (a routine is short and largely anaerobic, so oxygen delivery is less decisive) but strong, dense bones reduce stress fractures from repeated landings in training; a justified conclusion ranking the functions.
Levels: 7–9 — accurate and detailed knowledge; application to the gymnast is appropriate and consistent; evaluation is thorough and reaches a well-reasoned judgement; technical language used correctly throughout. 4–6 — knowledge evident but uneven; some application; evaluation present but one-sided or not fully developed. 1–3 — limited knowledge; little application; little or no evaluation. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
