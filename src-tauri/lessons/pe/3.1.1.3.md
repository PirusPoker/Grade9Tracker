## What it is

Muscles need **energy** to contract, and they release it from **glucose**. How they do it depends on whether enough **oxygen** is reaching them. When the heart and lungs can keep up, energy is released **aerobically**. When the demand is too great, the muscles work **anaerobically** and build up an **oxygen debt** that has to be repaid afterwards. Recovery methods help the body pay it off and get ready to perform again.

### Aerobic and anaerobic exercise

| | **Aerobic exercise** | **Anaerobic exercise** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | exercise **in the presence of oxygen** | exercise **in the absence of enough oxygen** |
| Word summary | **glucose + oxygen → energy + carbon dioxide + water** | **glucose → energy + lactic acid** |
| Intensity | low to moderate | high: maximal or near-maximal |
| Duration | long, continuous | short bursts |
| By-products | carbon dioxide (breathed out) and water (sweat, breath, urine): easily removed | **lactic acid**, which builds up in the muscles and causes fatigue and pain |
| Examples | marathon, long-distance cycling, a steady 30-minute swim | 100 m sprint, shot put, a vault, a weightlifter's clean and jerk |

Note the wording: anaerobic is **not** "without oxygen". It is "without **enough** oxygen". The body is always taking in some oxygen; it just cannot supply it fast enough for the work being done.

### Deciding and justifying: aerobic or anaerobic?

To **justify** a choice, use the activity's **intensity** and **duration**:
- **Marathon** is **aerobic**: it lasts over two hours at a moderate intensity the runner can sustain, so the heart and lungs can supply enough oxygen.
- **100 m sprint** is **anaerobic**: it lasts only seconds at maximum intensity, faster than oxygen can be delivered, so energy comes from glucose without enough oxygen. A full answer ends with the equation: glucose → energy + lactic acid.
- **Games players** (football, hockey, netball, basketball) **switch between both**. Jogging back into position or walking during a stoppage is aerobic; a sprint to the ball, a jump for a rebound or a tackle is anaerobic. Most of the match time is aerobic, but the decisive moments are often anaerobic.

The higher the intensity and the shorter the burst, the more anaerobic the activity. The lower the intensity and the longer it continues, the more aerobic.

### EPOC (oxygen debt)

**EPOC** stands for **excess post-exercise oxygen consumption**. It is the extra oxygen the body takes in **after** vigorous exercise, over and above what it would normally use at rest, to **repay the oxygen debt**.

- During vigorous exercise the muscles respire **anaerobically** and produce **lactic acid**. They build up an **oxygen debt**.
- After exercise, the performer keeps **breathing faster and more deeply** (and heart rate stays raised) even though they have stopped.
- The extra oxygen is used to **remove the lactic acid** and help the body return to its resting state.

That is why a sprinter is still gasping a minute after a 400 m race, while a jogger recovers their breath quickly. The more anaerobic the work, the bigger the oxygen debt and the longer EPOC lasts.

### The recovery process

| Method | What it involves | Reasons for use |
|---|---|---|
| **Cool down** | light exercise such as jogging or walking, then stretching | keeps **breathing rate and heart rate elevated** so blood keeps flowing through the muscles, which helps **remove lactic acid**; brings heart rate and breathing down **gradually**; **stretching** helps the muscles return to their resting length and reduces stiffness |
| **Manipulation of diet** | drinking fluids; eating carbohydrates | **rehydration** replaces the water lost in sweat; **carbohydrates** replace the energy stores used, ready for the next training session or match |
| **Ice baths** | sitting in very cold water for a few minutes | help to **prevent DOMS**: the cold narrows blood vessels, reducing swelling, and as the body warms up fresh blood flows back through the muscles, flushing out waste such as lactic acid |
| **Massage** | a therapist works the muscles | helps to **prevent DOMS**: increases blood flow to the muscles, helps remove lactic acid and relaxes the muscles |

**DOMS (delayed onset muscle soreness)** is pain and stiffness in the muscles that appears some time **after** exercise, often the next day, not straight away. It is most common after unfamiliar or very intense exercise, especially eccentric work such as running downhill or lowering heavy weights.

### Evaluating recovery methods for different activities

The spec asks you to **evaluate** these methods, which means weighing their benefits against their drawbacks and judging their relevance to a named activity.

| Method | Strengths | Limitations | Most relevant to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cool down | free, quick, no equipment, can be done anywhere straight after the event | needs discipline when tired; limited effect on DOMS after very heavy work | almost everyone; essential after anaerobic work such as a 400 m or a hard interval session |
| Diet manipulation | vital to refuel and rehydrate; can be planned around the event | needs knowledge and planning; eating too soon before the next event can cause discomfort | endurance events and tournaments with several matches, such as tennis, where glycogen and fluid must be replaced between matches; hot conditions |
| Ice baths | effective at reducing soreness and swelling | uncomfortable; need a facility and supervision; more common at elite level | contact sports such as rugby, where muscles are damaged and bruised; multi-day events |
| Massage | reduces soreness, relaxes muscles, can help detect small injuries | needs a qualified therapist; costly and time-consuming | elite athletes with support staff; performers training or competing on consecutive days |

The strongest evaluations link the method to **how soon the performer competes again**. A tennis player with two matches in one day needs quick refuelling and rehydration most. A rugby player with a week before the next match benefits from an ice bath and massage to reduce DOMS so they can train properly during the week.

::: terms
- **Aerobic exercise**: exercise in the presence of oxygen, at a low to moderate intensity that can be sustained.
- **Anaerobic exercise**: exercise in the absence of enough oxygen, at a high intensity for a short duration.
- **Lactic acid**: the by-product of anaerobic respiration, which builds up in muscles and causes fatigue and pain.
- **EPOC**: excess post-exercise oxygen consumption; the extra oxygen taken in after exercise to repay the oxygen debt.
- **Oxygen debt**: the amount of oxygen that must be taken in after anaerobic exercise to remove lactic acid and recover.
- **Cool down**: light exercise and stretching after activity that keeps blood flowing to remove lactic acid and lowers heart rate gradually.
- **Rehydration**: replacing the water lost through sweating by drinking fluids after exercise.
- **DOMS**: delayed onset muscle soreness; muscle pain and stiffness felt some time after exercise, often the next day.
- **Ice bath**: sitting in cold water after exercise to reduce swelling and help prevent DOMS.
:::

::: method
1. **Aerobic or anaerobic?** State which, then justify with the **intensity** *and* the **duration** of the named activity, then give the matching word equation for the final mark.
2. **Games players**: show both, with a specific moment of each (jogging back into position = aerobic; sprinting for a through ball = anaerobic).
3. **EPOC**: define it (extra oxygen after exercise to repay the debt), give the cause (anaerobic work producing lactic acid) and the sign (breathing rate stays high after exercise).
4. **Recovery methods**: name the method, give its *reason* (keeps heart rate up to remove lactic acid; rehydrates; refuels; prevents DOMS) and apply it to the performer.
5. **Evaluate**: strengths and limitations of each method, relevance to the named activity and schedule, and a justified judgement on the best one.
6. **9-mark answers**: AO1 knowledge (2), AO2 application to the named performer (2), then AO3 evaluation, the biggest share (5), so spend most words weighing up.
:::

::: example State and justify
State whether the 100 m sprint is an aerobic or anaerobic event. Justify your answer. (4)

Anaerobic (1). It is performed at maximal intensity (1) for a very short duration of around ten seconds (1), so the body cannot supply oxygen fast enough and energy is released from glucose without enough oxygen: glucose → energy + lactic acid (1).
:::

::: example Define EPOC
Define EPOC and explain why a 400 m runner is still breathing heavily several minutes after the race. (3)

EPOC is the extra oxygen consumed after exercise to repay the oxygen debt (1). The 400 m is run largely anaerobically, so lactic acid builds up in the muscles (1); the runner keeps breathing rate high to take in the extra oxygen needed to remove it (1).
:::

::: example Games player
Explain why a hockey player uses both aerobic and anaerobic exercise during a match. (4)

The match lasts over an hour, and much of it is spent jogging into position at a moderate intensity (1), which is aerobic, as oxygen supply can meet demand (1). The player also makes short, maximal sprints to close down an opponent or reach a loose ball (1), which are anaerobic, as oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough and lactic acid is produced (1).
:::

::: example Massage and DOMS
Explain how massage can help a performer recover from vigorous exercise. (3)

Massage increases blood flow to the muscles (1), which helps to remove lactic acid (1) and relaxes the muscles, reducing the chance of delayed onset muscle soreness the next day (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — 9 marks
Evaluate the use of different recovery methods for a tennis player taking part in a tournament. (9)

A cool down, such as gentle jogging and stretching after each match, keeps the player's heart rate and breathing raised so blood keeps flowing through the leg and shoulder muscles and removes lactic acid built up during long anaerobic rallies (AO1/AO2). It is free and quick, so it is the most practical method to use after every match; however, on its own it may not prevent soreness after a very long five-set match (AO3). Manipulating diet matters most in a tournament: tennis players can play on consecutive days, sometimes in heat, so rehydrating and eating carbohydrates straight after a match replaces the fluid lost in sweat and the energy stores used, without which the player would start the next match tired and dehydrated (AO2/AO3). The drawback is that it must be planned; eating a large meal too close to the next match could cause discomfort (AO3). Ice baths and massage both help prevent DOMS, which would otherwise slow the player's movement around the court the next day (AO1/AO2). They are effective, but ice baths are unpleasant and massage needs a qualified therapist, which a professional player will have but a club player may not (AO3). Overall, diet and a cool down are the most important because they are needed after every match and directly affect the next day's performance; ice baths and massage are valuable extras when the schedule is tight and support staff are available (AO3).

Level 3 (7–9): accurate knowledge of all three methods, consistently applied to the tennis tournament, with a balanced evaluation and a justified conclusion.
:::

::: watch
- Anaerobic means **without enough oxygen**, not "without oxygen".
- A justification needs **intensity and duration** of the named activity. "It's a sprint" is a label, not a reason.
- The top mark on an aerobic/anaerobic question often goes to the **word equation**. Learn both exactly.
- DOMS must be **delayed**. Saying it happens straight after exercise loses the definition mark.
- A cool down lowers heart rate and breathing **gradually**. "It lowers heart rate" alone was not credited in June 2025, and nor was "reduces the risk of injury".
- In "evaluate" questions, give **drawbacks** and **relevance** too. An answer that only lists benefits is capped.
:::
