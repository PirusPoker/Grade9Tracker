## What it is

Bones only move because **muscles pull on them**. A muscle is joined to bone by a **tendon**, a tough, non-elastic cord. When the muscle contracts it shortens or tenses and pulls the tendon, which pulls the bone and moves it at the joint. Muscles can only **pull, never push**, so every joint needs muscles on both sides working as **antagonistic pairs**.

### The thirteen muscles and groups

Use the full anatomical name. Examiners do not credit gym slang such as "lats", "pecs", "traps" or "quads" on their own.

| Muscle | Where | Main action (joint) |
|---|---|---|
| **Latissimus dorsi** | large muscle across the back | adduction and extension of the arm (shoulder) |
| **Deltoid** | cap over the top of the shoulder | abduction, flexion and extension of the arm (shoulder) |
| **Rotator cuffs** | small muscles around the shoulder joint | rotation of the arm and holding the humerus in its socket (shoulder) |
| **Pectorals** | front of the chest | adduction and flexion of the arm (shoulder) |
| **Biceps** | front of the upper arm | flexion (elbow) |
| **Triceps** | back of the upper arm | extension (elbow) |
| **Abdominals** | front of the trunk | flexion of the trunk; forced breathing out |
| **Hip flexors** | front of the hip | flexion (hip) |
| **Gluteals** | buttocks | extension (hip) |
| **Hamstring group** | back of the thigh | flexion (knee) |
| **Quadriceps group** | front of the thigh | extension (knee) |
| **Gastrocnemius** | calf, back of the lower leg | plantar flexion (ankle) |
| **Tibialis anterior** | front of the shin | dorsiflexion (ankle) |

You only need the **group** names for the hamstrings and quadriceps, not the individual muscles.

### Antagonistic pairs

In any movement:
- the **agonist** (prime mover) is the muscle that **contracts to cause the movement**;
- the **antagonist** is the muscle that **relaxes (lengthens) to allow the movement** to happen smoothly.

When the movement reverses, the roles swap. A definition that only says "the agonist is the prime mover" scores nothing, because it just repeats the term. Say what the muscle *does*.

| Joint | Bones at the joint | Movement | Agonist | Antagonist |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elbow | humerus, radius, ulna | flexion | biceps | triceps |
| Elbow | | extension | triceps | biceps |
| Knee | femur, tibia | flexion | hamstrings | quadriceps |
| Knee | | extension | quadriceps | hamstrings |
| Hip | pelvis, femur | flexion | hip flexors | gluteals |
| Hip | | extension | gluteals | hip flexors |
| Ankle | tibia, fibula, talus | plantar flexion | gastrocnemius | tibialis anterior |
| Ankle | | dorsiflexion | tibialis anterior | gastrocnemius |
| Shoulder | scapula, humerus | abduction | deltoid | latissimus dorsi |
| Shoulder | | adduction | latissimus dorsi | deltoid |
| Shoulder | | flexion | deltoid / pectorals | latissimus dorsi |
| Shoulder | | extension | latissimus dorsi | deltoid / pectorals |

The **rotator cuffs** turn the arm (rotation) and keep the head of the humerus in the shoulder socket, which matters in throwing and bowling.

### Types of contraction

| Contraction | What happens to the muscle | Movement? | Sporting example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Isometric** | the muscle is under tension but stays the **same length** | none: the joint is held still | the abdominals holding a plank; the deltoid holding the arms out to the side in a gymnastic balance; the quadriceps holding a skier's crouched tuck |
| **Isotonic concentric** | the muscle **shortens** under tension | yes | the biceps in the upward phase of a biceps curl; the quadriceps extending the knee to kick a ball |
| **Isotonic eccentric** | the muscle **lengthens** under tension, controlling the movement | yes, usually a lowering or landing | the biceps as the weight is lowered slowly in a curl; the quadriceps as a performer lowers into a squat or lands from a jump |

The key difference: in **concentric** the muscle shortens as it contracts; in **eccentric** it lengthens while still contracting, working as a brake. Eccentric contractions usually happen when a body part moves **with gravity** in a controlled way, so the muscle that did the lifting is also the one that controls the lowering.

**Worked through, the press-up:**
- Upward phase: elbow **extension**; agonist **triceps**, contracting **concentrically**; antagonist biceps.
- Downward phase: elbow **flexion**, but gravity is doing the work. The **triceps** lengthen under tension to lower the body slowly, which is an **eccentric** contraction. It is not a concentric contraction of the biceps.

**The squat:** going down is knee flexion with the **quadriceps** working eccentrically; driving up is knee extension with the quadriceps working concentrically. The gluteals do the same at the hip.

::: terms
- **Tendon**: a tough, non-elastic cord of tissue that attaches a muscle to a bone.
- **Agonist**: the muscle that contracts to cause a movement at a joint (the prime mover).
- **Antagonist**: the muscle that relaxes and lengthens to allow the agonist to cause a movement.
- **Antagonistic pair**: two muscles on opposite sides of a joint that work together, one contracting as the other relaxes.
- **Isometric contraction**: a muscle contraction where tension develops but the muscle length does not change and no movement occurs.
- **Isotonic contraction**: a muscle contraction where the muscle changes length and causes movement.
- **Concentric contraction**: an isotonic contraction in which the muscle shortens under tension.
- **Eccentric contraction**: an isotonic contraction in which the muscle lengthens under tension, controlling a movement.
- **Rotator cuffs**: a group of small muscles around the shoulder that rotate the arm and hold the humerus in its socket.
- **Hamstring group**: the muscles at the back of the thigh that flex the knee.
- **Quadriceps group**: the muscles at the front of the thigh that extend the knee.
:::

::: method
1. **Find the joint and the movement first** (for example, elbow extension). Everything else follows from that.
2. **Name the agonist** that causes that movement, then the **antagonist** on the opposite side of the joint. Use full names only.
3. **Decide the contraction.** Is the joint still? Then it is isometric. Is the muscle shortening to lift or move against resistance? Concentric. Is it lengthening to lower or land under control? Eccentric, and it is the *same* muscle that lifted.
4. **For "explain how they work antagonistically"**: agonist contracts, antagonist relaxes, the movement happens; then the roles swap to reverse it. Link each step to the named action.
5. **Extended answers**: identify the joints, movements, agonists and contraction types (AO1), apply each to a phase of the named action (AO2), and analyse how correct muscle action affects the quality of the performance, such as power, control or injury risk (AO3).
:::

::: example Name muscles at a joint
Name three muscles that act at the shoulder. (3)

Any three of: deltoid (1), latissimus dorsi (1), rotator cuffs (1), pectorals (1).
:::

::: example Role of the agonist
State the role of the agonist in a movement. (1)

It is the muscle that contracts to cause the movement (1). "The prime mover" alone scores nothing.
:::

::: example Contraction in a biceps curl
A performer lowers a dumbbell slowly from the shoulder to the thigh in a biceps curl. Identify the movement at the elbow and the type of contraction of the biceps. (2)

Extension of the elbow (1). The biceps contract eccentrically, lengthening under tension to control the lowering (1).
:::

::: example Antagonistic action in a press-up
Explain how the biceps and triceps work antagonistically at the elbow during the upward phase of a press-up. (3)

The elbow extends as the performer pushes up (1). The triceps are the agonist and contract concentrically, shortening to straighten the arm (1), while the biceps are the antagonist and relax/lengthen to allow the movement to happen (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — extended
Analyse the muscle action at the knee and hip as a volleyball player squats down and then jumps to block. (6)

Going down, the knee and hip flex under control. The quadriceps and gluteals lengthen under tension, contracting eccentrically as the agonists, while the hamstrings and hip flexors act as antagonists (AO1/AO2). This braking action stores the player's body weight in a controlled way, so they do not collapse and can stop at the depth that gives the most power (AO3). On the jump, the knee extends as the quadriceps contract concentrically with the hamstrings relaxing, and the hip extends as the gluteals contract concentrically with the hip flexors relaxing (AO2). Because both large muscle groups shorten forcefully at the same time, the player drives off the floor quickly and gains height, so the hands get above the net before the attacker hits the ball (AO3). The quick switch from eccentric to concentric is what gives a good block its speed (AO3).

Level 3 (5–6): the muscles, movements and contraction types are accurate and applied to both phases, and each is linked to its effect on the block.
:::

::: watch
- **Prime mover is not a definition of agonist.** Say the agonist *contracts to cause movement*; the antagonist *relaxes to allow it*.
- **Lowering = eccentric of the lifting muscle.** In the down phase of a press-up it is the triceps working eccentrically, not the biceps concentrically.
- A still position (plank, handstand, holding a weight overhead) is **isometric**. No movement means no isotonic contraction.
- Use full names: **latissimus dorsi, pectorals, quadriceps group**, not lats, pecs or quads.
- Hamstrings flex the **knee**; the **hip flexors** flex the hip. Students often swap them.
- If a question asks for a *contraction*, don't answer with a movement (flexion) or a muscle (biceps).
:::

::: test
Analyse the role of the muscles at the elbow and shoulder when a netball player makes a two-handed chest pass. (6)
---
AO1 (1): knowledge of the agonist/antagonist, the muscles named (triceps, biceps, pectorals, deltoid) and concentric contraction.
AO2 (2): application: elbow extension with the triceps as agonist contracting concentrically and the biceps relaxing as antagonist; at the shoulder, the arms move forwards (flexion/horizontal adduction) with the pectorals and deltoid as agonists and the latissimus dorsi as antagonist; the rotator cuffs stabilise the shoulder.
AO3 (3): analysis: forceful concentric contraction of the triceps and pectorals gives a fast, flat pass that is harder to intercept; the antagonists relaxing in time lets the movement be smooth and accurate rather than jerky; stable shoulders give control of direction; conclusion on which muscles matter most for pass speed.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate knowledge; clearly applied to both joints; thorough analysis of the effect on the pass. 3–4 — knowledge evident but uneven; some application; some analysis. 1–2 — limited knowledge; muscles listed with little application or analysis. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
Evaluate how the muscles and types of muscle contraction used during a basic squat affect a weightlifter's performance. (9)
---
AO1 (2): knowledge of muscles (quadriceps, hamstrings, gluteals, hip flexors, gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior, abdominals); agonist/antagonist; concentric, eccentric and isometric contractions.
AO2 (2): application to the squat: down phase — knee and hip flexion with the quadriceps and gluteals contracting eccentrically; bottom position — brief isometric hold; up phase — knee and hip extension with the quadriceps and gluteals contracting concentrically and the hamstrings/hip flexors as antagonists; ankle dorsiflexion as the lifter lowers; abdominals working isometrically to keep the back straight.
AO3 (5): evaluation: the eccentric phase controls the heavy bar so the lifter reaches depth safely without losing balance, but it is also where muscle damage and DOMS are most likely, so training loads must be managed; strong concentric contraction of the large quadriceps and gluteals decides whether the lift is completed; the isometric work of the abdominals protects the spine, so a weak core limits the weight the lifter can safely use even with strong legs; the antagonists relaxing smoothly avoids a jerky movement; judgement on which contraction is most decisive (e.g. concentric strength decides success, but eccentric control and isometric stability are what make it safe and repeatable).
Levels: 7–9 — accurate, detailed knowledge; consistently applied to the phases of the squat; thorough evaluation with a well-reasoned conclusion; correct terminology. 4–6 — knowledge evident but uneven; some application; evaluation present but undeveloped or one-sided. 1–3 — limited knowledge; little application; little or no evaluation. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
