## What it is

This short topic opens the **physical training** section of Paper 1. It asks two things: can you define **health** and **fitness** precisely, and can you explain how the two affect each other? The ideas look simple, but the exam rewards exact definitions and a clear chain of cause and effect.

**Health** is a state of complete **physical, mental and social well-being**, not simply the absence of disease or infirmity. All three parts matter. A person with no illness who is lonely and anxious is not fully healthy. A definition that says only "not being ill" misses most of the idea and does not score.

| Part of health | What it looks like | Example in sport |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | body systems working well, free of illness and injury | a netballer free of injury who can train every session |
| **Mental** (emotional) | coping with stress, controlling emotions, feeling good about yourself | a penalty taker who manages nerves and keeps confidence after a miss |
| **Social** | having friends and support, belonging, being able to interact with others | a club runner who trains with a group and feels part of the team |

**Fitness** is the ability to **meet the demands of the environment**. "Environment" here means whatever a person's life asks of them: a full-time job and walking to work, or a 90-minute hockey match twice a week. Fitness is **relative**. A person may be fit enough for their daily life but not fit enough for a triathlon. Fitness is made up of the **components of fitness** (topic 3.1.3.2a), such as cardiovascular endurance, strength and flexibility.

### How health and fitness affect each other

The spec names two relationships, and exam questions usually ask for one of them with an example.

**1. Poor health can reduce fitness.** Ill health can leave a person **unable to train**, or able to train only at a low intensity. When training stops, the body loses the adaptations it had made (this is **reversibility**, see 3.1.3.3a), so fitness falls.
- A footballer with flu misses two weeks of training. Her cardiovascular endurance drops, so she tires earlier in her first match back.
- A rugby player with a hamstring injury cannot sprint for six weeks. His speed and power fall.
- Mental ill health counts too. A swimmer suffering from depression or anxiety may lose the motivation to train, so her fitness drops.

**2. A person can be unhealthy and still increase fitness.** Someone can be in poor health in one area and still be **able to train**. Because training continues, fitness can go up.
- A cyclist who eats a poor diet high in saturated fat, or who smokes, can still train hard and improve cardiovascular endurance. They are fitter but less healthy.
- An athlete who over-trains to the point of stress and poor sleep may still gain strength, while their **mental** health suffers.
- A player with a mild cold can still do light technique work and keep some fitness.
- A person with a long-term condition such as asthma can often train safely with medical advice and improve fitness while still having the condition.

The two are linked but **not the same**. Exercise usually improves both: it builds fitness and it improves physical health (lower blood pressure, healthy weight), mental health (stress relief, confidence) and social health (friendships through clubs). But being very fit does not make someone healthy, and being healthy does not make someone fit for a particular sport.

### The role exercise plays in both

| Exercise helps… | How |
|---|---|
| **fitness** | training improves the components of fitness, e.g. running improves cardiovascular endurance |
| **physical health** | lowers the risk of heart disease, obesity and type 2 diabetes; stronger bones and muscles |
| **mental health** | releases feel-good hormones (serotonin), relieves stress, raises self-esteem |
| **social health** | team and club activities give friendship, teamwork and a sense of belonging |

The wider benefits of exercise for well-being are taught in detail on Paper 2 (3.2.3.1). On Paper 1 the focus is the health–fitness relationship.

::: terms
- **Health**: a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.
- **Fitness**: the ability to meet (or cope with) the demands of the environment.
- **Physical health**: the body's systems working well, free from illness and injury.
- **Mental health**: emotional well-being; the ability to cope with stress and feel positive about yourself.
- **Social health**: having friends, support and the ability to interact with and belong to a group.
- **Ill health**: being in a poor state of physical, mental or social well-being.
- **Reversibility**: fitness gained through training is lost when training stops or is reduced, for example through illness.
- **Exercise**: a form of physical activity done to maintain or improve health and/or fitness.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely.** Health must mention physical, mental **and** social well-being. Fitness must mention meeting the demands of the environment.
2. **Identify which relationship the question wants:** poor health lowering fitness, or someone being unhealthy but still fit enough to train.
3. **Build the chain:** the health problem → its effect on the ability to train → the effect on fitness → the effect on performance.
4. **Use a specific example** that names the sport, the health problem and the fitness component: "a netballer with glandular fever cannot train for a month, so her cardiovascular endurance falls and she tires in the last quarter".
5. **For 6-mark questions,** cover both directions of the relationship, apply every point to the named performer, and finish with a judgement about how closely the two are linked.
:::

::: example Define — health
Define the term health. (1)

A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity (1).

A common error: "being free from illness" alone scores nothing, because it misses the mental and social parts.
:::

::: example Explain — poor health lowers fitness
Explain, using an example, how ill health can lead to a decrease in fitness. (3)

Ill health, such as a chest infection, means a performer is unable to train, or can only train at a low intensity (1).

Without training, the adaptations the body has made are lost (reversibility), so fitness falls (1).

For example, a 1500 m runner who misses three weeks of training with a chest infection loses cardiovascular endurance, so she cannot keep her usual pace for the final lap when she returns (1).
:::

::: example Explain — unhealthy but fitter
Explain how a person can be unhealthy but still increase their fitness. Use an example in your answer. (3)

Being unhealthy in one area does not always stop someone training (1).

As long as they are able to train, the body still adapts and fitness goes up (1).

For example, a club cyclist who smokes and eats a diet high in saturated fat is not healthy, but because he still rides 100 km every week his cardiovascular endurance improves (1).
:::

::: example Distinguish — the two terms
Using an example, explain the difference between health and fitness. (4)

Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, not just being free of disease (1). Fitness is the ability to meet the demands of the environment (1).

The two can be separate. A marathon runner can be very fit, able to run 42 km at a strong pace (1), yet not be healthy if the pressure of training leaves them anxious and isolated, which is poor mental and social health (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a named performer (6-mark)
Jake is a county squash player. He has been diagnosed with a stress-related illness and is also recovering from a sprained ankle. Analyse how Jake's health may affect his fitness and his performance. (6)

(AO1) Health covers physical, mental and social well-being. Fitness is the ability to meet the demands of the environment, and ill health can reduce it by stopping training.

(AO2) The sprained ankle is a physical health problem. Jake cannot do court sprints or lunges, so his agility and speed will fall through reversibility. The stress-related illness is a mental health problem. He may lack the motivation or energy to train, so his cardiovascular endurance may drop too.

(AO3) Squash needs fast changes of direction and repeated high-intensity rallies, so lower agility will make him slower to reach drop shots and he will tire in long games. That could cost him points late in each game. However, he may still be able to train some areas, such as upper-body strength or low-intensity cycling if the ankle allows. That would keep some fitness, showing that ill health does not always mean a total loss. Overall, because squash depends on the very components the ankle affects, his performance is likely to fall until both problems are treated.
:::

::: q
A student says, "If you are healthy you must be fit." Explain why this statement is not correct. (2)
---
Health and fitness are different: health is physical, mental and social well-being, while fitness is meeting the demands of the environment (1). Someone can be free of illness and happy but not have the fitness for a demanding task, e.g. a healthy office worker who could not complete a 10 km race (1).
:::

::: watch
- Health needs **all three**: physical, mental **and** social well-being. "Not being ill" alone scores nothing.
- Fitness is about **meeting the demands of the environment**, not "being sporty" or "being in shape".
- Say **why** ill health lowers fitness: the person **cannot train**, so adaptations are lost (reversibility).
- Examples must name a **sport, a health problem and a fitness effect**. "A footballer who is ill" is too vague.
- Remember the second relationship: being **unhealthy but still able to train** means fitness can go **up**.
- Mental ill health counts as ill health, and it can reduce fitness by removing the motivation to train.
:::

Emma is a county-level netball player. She has a stress-related illness and a knee injury. Analyse how the relationship between health and fitness could affect Emma's performance. (6)

AO1 (1 mark): health is physical, mental and social well-being; fitness is the ability to meet the demands of the environment; poor health can stop training and lower fitness; people can be unhealthy but still train.
