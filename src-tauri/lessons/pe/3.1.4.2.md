## What it is

Once data has been collected it must be **presented** so it can be read and compared. The spec asks for three skills: presenting data in **tables**, plotting basic **bar charts** and **line graphs**, and **labelling the x and y axes**. In the exam you may be asked to complete a table, name the most suitable graph, give the axis labels, or spot what is wrong with a graph. In this app you describe a graph in words: what goes on each axis, the units, the scale and the points plotted.

### Tables

A good table:
- has a clear **title** saying what the data shows
- has a **heading for every column**, with the **unit** in the heading (e.g. "Time (s)"), not after every number
- puts the thing you chose or controlled (the **category** or the **time point**) in the **first column**, and the measured results in the next columns
- lists the data in a sensible **order**: time order, or rank order
- uses the same number of decimal places throughout a column

**Example: a sprinter's 30 m times over an 8-week training programme**

| Week | 30 m sprint time (s) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 4.62 |
| 2 | 4.55 |
| 4 | 4.49 |
| 6 | 4.44 |
| 8 | 4.41 |

**Example: members of a leisure centre by activity**

| Activity | Number of members |
|---|---|
| Swimming | 420 |
| Gym | 610 |
| Badminton | 150 |
| Fitness classes | 380 |

### Bar charts

Use a **bar chart** when the data falls into separate **categories**, such as sports, age groups, teams, or male and female.
- **x-axis** (horizontal): the **categories** (e.g. "Activity"), each bar labelled.
- **y-axis** (vertical): the **value** being measured, with its **unit** (e.g. "Number of members", "Participation (%)").
- Bars are the **same width** with **gaps between them**, because the categories are separate.
- The y-axis scale goes up in **equal steps** and normally **starts at zero**, so the bar heights can be compared fairly.
- For two groups in each category (e.g. boys and girls), draw pairs of bars side by side and add a **key**.

For the leisure-centre table: x-axis "Activity" with four bars (Swimming, Gym, Badminton, Fitness classes). y-axis "Number of members" from 0 to 700 in steps of 100. The Gym bar reaches 610, the tallest.

### Line graphs

Use a **line graph** to show how something **changes over time**, or how one measurement changes as another changes continuously (e.g. heart rate during a run).
- **x-axis**: the **time** (or the thing that changes steadily), with units, e.g. "Time (minutes)" or "Week".
- **y-axis**: the **measured value**, with units, e.g. "Heart rate (bpm)".
- Plot each point with a small cross (×) at the correct x and y values, then **join the points** with straight lines (or a smooth line).
- The scales must go up in **equal steps**. The y-axis may start above zero if the values are all high (e.g. heart rates from 60 to 180), but the scale must be clearly marked.
- Two lines on the same axes (e.g. two performers) need a **key**.

For the sprint table: x-axis "Week" from 0 to 8 in steps of 2. y-axis "30 m sprint time (s)" from 4.30 to 4.70 in steps of 0.05. Five points joined with a line sloping downwards, showing the times falling.

### Choosing the graph

| Data | Best graph | Why |
|---|---|---|
| participation in five different sports | **bar chart** | separate categories |
| heart rate every minute during a 20-minute run | **line graph** | continuous change over time |
| MSFT scores of boys and girls in each year group | **bar chart** (paired bars, with key) | categories compared between two groups |
| a runner's weekly mileage over a season | **line graph** | change over time |
| percentages of carbohydrate, fat and protein in a diet | **pie chart** (reading only, 3.1.4.3) or bar chart | parts of a whole |

You must be able to **interpret** pie charts (3.1.4.3), but the spec only requires you to **plot** bar charts and line graphs.

### Common errors examiners see

- axes **not labelled**, or labelled without **units**
- the **axes swapped**: time put on the y-axis
- **uneven scales**, e.g. 0, 10, 20, 50, 100
- a **line graph** used for categories (joining "football" to "netball" means nothing)
- bars of different widths, or bars touching when the categories are separate
- **no title** or **no key** when there are two data sets

::: terms
- **Table**: data set out in rows and columns, with a heading and units for each column.
- **Bar chart**: a graph using separate bars of equal width to compare values across different categories.
- **Line graph**: a graph of plotted points joined by a line, used to show how a value changes over time.
- **x-axis**: the horizontal axis; shows the categories on a bar chart or the time on a line graph.
- **y-axis**: the vertical axis; shows the value being measured, with its unit.
- **Scale**: the numbers marked along an axis, which must go up in equal steps.
- **Key**: a guide showing which bar or line represents which group when more than one data set is plotted.
- **Unit**: the measure a value is recorded in, such as seconds, bpm, cm or %, written in the axis label or column heading.
- **Pie chart**: a circle divided into sectors showing each part as a share of the whole (100%).
:::

::: method
1. **Decide the graph:** categories → **bar chart**; change over time → **line graph**.
2. **Label the axes:** x = categories or time; y = the measured value. Each label says **what** is measured and its **unit** in brackets.
3. **Choose equal-step scales** that fit the data (start at zero for bar charts), and plot accurately.
4. **Add a title**, and a **key** if there is more than one data set.
5. **For tables:** a title, a heading with a unit for each column, and data in a logical order.
6. **When asked to justify a choice,** link it to the type of data: "a line graph because heart rate changes continuously over time".
:::

::: example Choose and label a graph
A coach records a player's heart rate every 2 minutes during a 20-minute training session. State the most suitable type of graph to present this data, and state what should be shown on each axis. (3)

A line graph (1).

x-axis: time, in minutes (1).

y-axis: heart rate, in beats per minute (bpm) (1).
:::

::: example Complete a table
A PE teacher records Vertical Jump results: Asha 42 cm, Ben 51 cm, Cara 38 cm, Dev 47 cm. Describe how this data should be presented in a table so that the best performer is easy to see. (3)

Two columns with headings "Student" and "Vertical jump height (cm)", with the unit in the heading (1).

The rows in rank order from highest to lowest: Ben 51, Dev 47, Asha 42, Cara 38 (1).

A title such as "Vertical Jump Test results, Year 10 group" (1).
:::

::: example Spot the errors
A student plots a line graph of the number of students attending five different after-school clubs (football, dance, netball, badminton, rugby). The y-axis is labelled "Students" and goes 0, 5, 10, 20, 40. Identify two errors and explain how to correct them. (4)

A line graph is the wrong type, because the clubs are separate categories (1). A bar chart should be used instead (1).

The y-axis scale is uneven, which distorts the comparison (1). It should go up in equal steps, e.g. 0, 5, 10, 15, 20… (1).
:::

::: example Describe a plotted graph — 6-mark
The table shows the resting heart rate of a 16-year-old swimmer over a 12-week training programme.
| Week | Resting heart rate (bpm) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 74 |
| 3 | 71 |
| 6 | 68 |
| 9 | 66 |
| 12 | 64 |
Describe how the swimmer's coach should present this data as a graph. Justify your choices. (6)

(AO1) A line graph, because it shows change over time, with points plotted and joined.

(AO2) x-axis labelled "Week", with a scale from 0 to 12 in equal steps of 3 (or 1). y-axis labelled "Resting heart rate (bpm)", with an equal-step scale from, for example, 60 to 76 in steps of 2. Plot crosses at (0, 74), (3, 71), (6, 68), (9, 66) and (12, 64) and join them. Title: "Swimmer's resting heart rate over a 12-week programme".

(AO3) A line graph suits this data because heart rate is measured at regular times, and joining the points shows the downward trend clearly. A bar chart would hide it. Starting the y-axis at 60 rather than 0 spreads the points out so the fall of 10 bpm is visible, but the scale must be clearly marked so the size of the change is not exaggerated. Units on both axes mean anyone reading the graph knows what was measured. The coach can then see at a glance that training is working, since a lower resting heart rate is a sign of a stronger heart.
:::

::: q
State the most suitable type of graph to compare the number of students taking part in four different sports. (1)
---
Bar chart (1).
:::

::: q
State two features of a well-presented table. (2)
---
Any two: a title; a heading for each column; units in the headings; data in a logical order; consistent decimal places (1 each).
:::

::: q
A line graph shows a runner's heart rate during a race. Suggest suitable labels for the x-axis and y-axis. (2)
---
x-axis: time (minutes/seconds) (1); y-axis: heart rate (bpm/beats per minute) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the bars on a bar chart should have gaps between them. (1)
---
The categories are separate/discrete, so the gaps show that the bars are not continuous (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a key is needed on a line graph showing the MSFT scores of two athletes over a season. (2)
---
There are two lines on the same axes (1); the key shows which line belongs to which athlete so the data can be read correctly (1).
:::

::: q
A student draws a bar chart of club membership with the y-axis starting at 300 instead of 0. Explain why this could be misleading. (2)
---
Bar heights would no longer be in proportion to the values (1), so small differences look much larger than they really are, e.g. 320 vs 380 looks like a huge gap (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Label both axes** with what is measured **and the unit**. An unlabelled axis loses the mark even if the plotting is perfect.
- **Categories → bar chart; change over time → line graph.** Justify the choice using the type of data.
- Time goes on the **x-axis**.
- Scales must go up in **equal steps**, and bar-chart y-axes should normally start at zero.
- Tables need **headings with units**. Don't write the unit after every number.
- Add a **key** when there is more than one data set.
:::

::: test
State the most suitable type of graph to compare the average Sit and Reach scores of four Year 10 groups. Justify your answer. (2)
---
Bar chart (1); the groups are separate categories, not a change over time (1).
:::

::: test
State what should be included in the label of a graph axis. (2)
---
What is being measured, e.g. heart rate (1); the unit, e.g. bpm (1).
:::

::: test
The table shows a netball player's MSFT level at the start of each month of pre-season.

| Month | MSFT level |
|---|---|
| June | 8.2 |
| July | 9.1 |
| August | 9.8 |

State the most suitable type of graph for this data and what should be shown on each axis. (3)
---
Line graph (1); x-axis: month/time (1); y-axis: MSFT level (1). Accept bar chart for the first mark only if justified as comparing three separate test dates.
:::

::: test
Give two features that should be included when drawing a bar chart. (2)
---
Any two: labelled x-axis (categories) and y-axis (value with unit); equal-width bars with gaps; y-axis scale in equal steps starting at 0; a title; a key if two data sets (1 each).
:::

::: test
Explain why a line graph is not suitable for showing the number of people who play each of five different sports. (2)
---
A line graph shows continuous change, e.g. over time (1); the sports are separate categories, so joining them with a line suggests a trend/connection that does not exist — a bar chart should be used (1).
:::

::: test
A student's table of 30 m sprint times has the column headings "Name" and "Time". Explain one way the table could be improved. (2)
---
Add the unit to the heading, e.g. "Time (s)" (1), so the reader knows the times are in seconds (1). Or: put the rows in rank order (1) so the fastest is easy to see (1); or add a title (1) describing the data (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how you would plot a line graph of a runner's heart rate taken every minute for 10 minutes. (3)
---
x-axis labelled time (minutes) from 0 to 10 in equal steps (1); y-axis labelled heart rate (bpm) with an equal-step scale covering the range of readings (1); plot each reading as a point/cross at the correct time and join the points with a line; add a title (1).
:::

::: test
A coach wants to compare the number of goals scored by her team in home matches and away matches in each of the last four seasons. Describe the most suitable graph and how it should be labelled. (3)
---
A bar chart with paired bars for home and away (1); x-axis labelled season, y-axis labelled number of goals, with a scale starting at 0 in equal steps (1); a key to show which bar is home and which is away, and a title (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the scale on the y-axis of a graph must go up in equal steps. (2)
---
So that the position/height of each point or bar is in proportion to its value (1); uneven steps distort the graph and make comparisons or trends misleading (1).
:::

::: test
A student has recorded the resting heart rate of the same athlete every week for ten weeks. Explain why a line graph is more useful than a table for showing this data to the athlete. (3)
---
A line graph shows the data visually (1); joining the points shows the trend/pattern of change over time clearly, e.g. a steady fall (1); the athlete can see at a glance whether training is working, which may be harder to spot from a list of numbers (1).
:::

::: test
A PE department has recorded the number of Year 10 boys and girls attending five extra-curricular clubs. Explain how the department should present this data in a table and a graph so it can be compared easily. Justify your choices. (6)
---
AO1 (1 mark): tables need a title, headings and units; bar charts suit categories; axes labelled with units; key for two data sets.
AO2 (2 marks): table — columns "Club", "Number of boys", "Number of girls" (possibly "Total"), with the clubs in a logical order and a title; graph — a bar chart with the five clubs on the x-axis, "Number of students" on the y-axis from 0 in equal steps, paired bars for boys and girls with a key, and a title.
AO3 (3 marks): justification — the clubs are separate categories, so a bar chart (not a line graph) is correct; paired bars let boys' and girls' attendance be compared directly within each club; starting at 0 with equal steps keeps the bar heights in proportion so differences are not exaggerated; the table keeps exact figures for calculations while the chart shows patterns at a glance, e.g. which clubs attract few girls, helping the department act.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate knowledge, clear application to the data, well-justified choices; 3–4 — knowledge evident, some application, justification partial; 1–2 — limited knowledge, little application or justification; 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
