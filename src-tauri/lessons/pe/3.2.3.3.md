## What it is

Performers are fuelled by what they eat and drink. This topic covers **energy use** (measured in calories), the reasons for a **balanced diet** and its proportions, the **roles of carbohydrate, fat, protein, vitamins and minerals**, and **hydration** — what **dehydration** does to the body and how much it matters in **different sporting activities**.

### Energy use

- **Energy** is measured in **calories (kcal)** and comes from the **food we eat**.
- The **average adult male** needs about **2,500 kcal per day**; the **average adult female** about **2,000 kcal per day**.
- The amount a person actually needs depends on:

| Factor | Effect on energy needs |
|---|---|
| **Age** | growing teenagers need a lot of energy; needs usually fall in older age as metabolism slows and activity drops |
| **Gender** | males usually need more, as they tend to have more muscle mass and larger bodies |
| **Height** | taller (larger) people need more energy to maintain their bodies |
| **Energy expenditure (exercise)** | the more active a person is, the more energy they use and must replace — an endurance athlete in heavy training may need far more than the average |

**Energy balance**: if energy **in** (food) equals energy **out** (daily living plus exercise), weight stays the same. If more comes in than goes out, the surplus is **stored as fat** and weight rises; if less comes in, weight falls.

### Why a balanced diet?

**No single food contains all the nutrients the body needs**, so a **balanced diet** contains **lots of different types of food** to provide the right **nutrients, vitamins and minerals** in the right amounts. The reasons:

- **Unused energy is stored as fat**, which could cause **obesity** — particularly if the diet is high in **saturated fat**.
- **Suitable energy is available for activity**, so the performer can train and compete at the right intensity.
- **The body needs nutrients for energy, growth and hydration** — to fuel movement, build and repair tissue, and keep the body's systems working.

### The proportions

| Nutrient | Share of a balanced diet |
|---|---|
| **Carbohydrate** | **55–60%** |
| **Fat** | **25–30%** |
| **Protein** | **15–20%** |

### What each nutrient does

| Nutrient | Role | Food sources | Performer example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | the **main and preferred energy source for all types of exercise, of all intensities** | pasta, rice, bread, potatoes, cereals, fruit | a footballer, a marathon runner, a sprinter — everyone relies on carbohydrate; endurance athletes eat extra before a big event to fill their stores |
| **Fat** | also an energy source; it **provides more energy than carbohydrate** (gram for gram) **but only at low intensity** | oils, butter, cheese, nuts, oily fish, meat | used during long, steady, low-intensity exercise such as walking or a slow long-distance ride |
| **Protein** | **growth and repair of muscle tissue** | meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils, tofu | important for power athletes and for recovery after hard training; a weightlifter or rugby player may eat a little more |
| **Vitamins and minerals** | **maintain the efficient working of the body systems and general health** | fruit, vegetables, dairy, whole grains | all performers — needed to stay healthy and train consistently (you do **not** need to learn specific vitamins or minerals) |

**The key contrast**: carbohydrate is the **main fuel for exercise at every intensity**. Fat gives **more energy** but can only be used **at low intensity** — so a sprinter, a squash player or a footballer making repeated sprints relies on carbohydrate, not fat.

### Reading diet data

*A fictional games player's daily diet, by energy source*

| Nutrient | Carbohydrate | Fat | Protein |
|---|---|---|---|
| % of daily energy | 40% | 42% | 18% |

Compared with the recommended proportions, **carbohydrate is too low** (40% vs 55–60%) and **fat is too high** (42% vs 25–30%); **protein is within the range** (15–20%). Consequences: less stored carbohydrate to fuel intense play, so the player may tire in the second half; and unused energy from the excess fat may be stored, risking weight gain. Advice: swap some fatty foods for pasta, rice, bread or fruit.

### Hydration

**Dehydration** is when the body **loses more water than it takes in**, so it does not have enough water to function properly. **Water balance (hydration)** — drinking enough to replace what is lost in sweat, breathing and urine — **prevents dehydration**.

**Effects of dehydration on the body and performance:**

| Effect | What happens | Impact on performance |
|---|---|---|
| **Blood thickening (increased viscosity)**, which **slows blood flow** | less water in the blood plasma makes it thicker | less oxygen reaches the working muscles, so aerobic performance and endurance fall |
| **Increase in heart rate / heart has to work harder / irregular heart rhythm** | the heart must pump harder to push thicker blood round the body | the performer tires sooner; in extreme cases the rhythm becomes irregular — dangerous |
| **Increase in body temperature / overheating** | less sweat can be produced, so the body cannot cool itself | risk of heat exhaustion; performance drops sharply in hot conditions |
| **Slowing of reactions / increased reaction time / poorer decisions** | the brain is affected by lack of water | slower responses, errors of judgement, loss of concentration |
| **Muscle fatigue / cramps** | muscles are less able to work efficiently | loss of power, cramping, possibly having to stop |

**Evaluating the consequences in different activities** — the effect depends on the **duration**, **intensity**, **conditions** and **skills** involved:

| Activity | How much dehydration matters |
|---|---|
| **Marathon or Ironman, especially in heat** | **very serious**: hours of sweating, high body temperature, thick blood and a hard-working heart — performance collapses and health is at real risk |
| **Football or hockey match** | **significant**: 70–90 minutes of intermittent running; later in the game, fatigue, cramps and slower decisions cost goals — players drink at breaks |
| **Tennis match in summer** | **significant**: long matches in heat; cramps and poorer shot selection in the deciding set |
| **100 m sprint** | **minor during the race** (it lasts seconds), though dehydration from earlier could still slow reactions at the start |
| **Snooker, darts, archery** | **little physical effect**, but slower reactions and **poorer decisions and concentration** could still matter over a long session |

::: terms
- **Calorie (kcal)**: the unit used to measure the energy in food.
- **Energy balance**: when the energy taken in from food equals the energy used, so body weight stays the same.
- **Balanced diet**: a diet containing a variety of foods that provides the right amounts of nutrients, vitamins and minerals.
- **Carbohydrate**: the body's main and preferred energy source for exercise of all intensities.
- **Fat**: a nutrient that provides more energy than carbohydrate but can only be used at low intensity.
- **Protein**: a nutrient used for the growth and repair of muscle tissue.
- **Vitamins and minerals**: nutrients that maintain the efficient working of the body systems and general health.
- **Saturated fat**: a type of fat, found in foods such as butter and fatty meat, that is linked to obesity.
- **Hydration**: having enough water in the body; keeping water balance.
- **Dehydration**: when the body loses more water than it takes in, so it cannot function properly.
- **Blood viscosity**: the thickness of the blood; it increases when a person is dehydrated.
:::

::: method
1. **Recall the numbers exactly**: male 2,500 kcal, female 2,000 kcal; diet 55–60% carbohydrate, 25–30% fat, 15–20% protein.
2. **Give the factors** that change energy needs: age, gender, height, energy expenditure.

4. **Diet data**: compare each figure to the recommended range, say too high/too low/within, then give the effect on performance.
5. **Dehydration**: name the effect, the mechanism, and the impact on this performer.
6. **Evaluate across activities**: longer, more intense and hotter = more serious; precision sports suffer mainly through reactions and decisions.
:::

::: example Extended — evaluate dehydration
Evaluate the consequences of dehydration for performers in different sporting activities. (9)

Dehydration occurs when the body loses more water than it takes in. It thickens the blood, so blood flow slows and less oxygen reaches the muscles; the heart has to work harder and heart rate rises, sometimes irregularly; body temperature rises because less sweat can be produced; reactions slow and decisions worsen; and muscles fatigue and cramp. For a marathon runner in hot weather these effects are severe: over two or more hours the runner sweats heavily, and thick blood and overheating would force them to slow down dramatically and could cause heat exhaustion, so dehydration could end the race and endanger health. A footballer is also badly affected in the last part of a match: cramps and fatigue reduce sprinting, and slower decision-making leads to mistakes at crucial moments. For a 100 m sprinter the race is too short for dehydration during it to matter much, though arriving dehydrated could slow reaction to the gun. For a snooker player there is little physical demand, but over a long session slower reactions and poorer concentration and decisions could cost frames. Overall, dehydration matters most in long, intense activities in hot conditions, where it harms both performance and health, and least in short events; but no performer is unaffected, because decision-making suffers in every activity. (Level 3.)
:::
