## What it is

Training only works if it follows a few rules. The **principles of training** explain *why* a programme improves fitness, and the exam expects you to **define** each one and **apply** it to a named performer. AQA groups them under two memory aids: **SPORT** (the key principles) and **FITT** (the principles of **overload**).

### SPORT: the key principles

| Principle | Meaning | Applied example |
|---|---|---|
| **Specificity** | matching training to the **requirements of the activity**: the same muscles, movements, energy system and components of fitness | a 1500 m runner does interval runs at race pace rather than heavy weight training; a swimmer trains mostly in the pool |
| **Progressive overload** | **gradually** increasing the amount of overload (the demand on the body) so that fitness keeps improving, **without** risking injury | a rower adds 5 minutes to her steady-state session every fortnight; a lifter adds 2.5 kg once 3 sets of 8 feel comfortable |
| **Reversibility** | any **adaptation** from training is **lost** when training **stops or is reduced**, for example through injury, illness or a break | a footballer out for eight weeks with a broken ankle loses cardiovascular endurance and muscle mass |
| **Tedium** | the **boredom** that can come from training the same way every time. Programmes need **variety** to keep motivation | a cyclist swaps one indoor turbo session a week for a hill ride or a fartlek run |

**Overload** means making the body work **harder than normal** so it is forced to adapt. **Progressive** means the extra demand is added **gradually**. The body adapts to each new level, then the demand is raised again. Too much, too soon causes injury or overtraining. Too little, and there is no improvement. The mark is often for the word **gradually**.

**Specificity** is not only "the right sport". It includes the **energy system** (aerobic vs anaerobic), the **muscles and movement patterns**, and even the **position**: a rugby prop's training should differ from a winger's.

**Reversibility** works in both directions of a question. It explains why an injured player loses fitness, why an off-season with no training at all is a mistake, and why a performer should keep some light training in the post-season (see 3.1.3.4b).

**Tedium** matters because a bored performer may train less hard, skip sessions or drop out, and then reversibility sets in. Variety (different drills, venues, training partners or methods) keeps motivation high while still meeting specificity.

### FITT: the principles of overload

FITT gives you the four "dials" a coach turns to **apply progressive overload**.

| Principle | Meaning | How to increase overload |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** | **how often** you train (sessions per week) | from 3 to 4 sessions a week |
| **Intensity** | **how hard** you train, e.g. % of max heart rate, weight lifted, speed | run at 75% rather than 65% of max heart rate; lift a heavier weight |
| **Time** | **how long** each session (or each exercise) lasts | lengthen a steady run from 30 to 40 minutes |
| **Type** | **which kind** of training is used. It must suit the aim (specificity) and can be changed to add variety | swap continuous running for interval training to target anaerobic fitness |

Change **one or two dials at a time**, and only a little. A coach who doubles frequency and intensity in the same week invites injury, which breaks the rule of *progressive* overload.

### Applying the principles to a programme

A well-planned programme uses all of them together. Take **Aisha**, a 16-year-old county 800 m runner, over an 8-week block:
- **Specificity:** mixes interval sessions at race pace (anaerobic) with a longer run (aerobic), because the 800 m uses both. She adds plyometric bounding for leg power.
- **Progressive overload (via FITT):** weeks 1–2, 3 sessions of 6 × 200 m. Weeks 3–4, 7 × 200 m (time). Weeks 5–6, a faster target pace (intensity). Week 7, a fourth session (frequency).
- **Reversibility:** if she misses a week with illness, she drops back one level rather than jumping straight in. In the off-season she keeps some light running so her aerobic base is not lost.
- **Tedium:** one session a week moves to the grass or the hills (fartlek), and she sometimes trains with a group.

**How the principles bring improvement:** overload stresses the body; during **rest and recovery** it adapts (for example, a stronger heart and more capillaries, or muscle hypertrophy), so the next time the same work feels easier. That is why rest days are part of the programme, not a break from it.

::: terms
- **Principles of training**: the rules that, if followed, make a training programme effective and safe.
- **Specificity**: matching training to the requirements of an activity — its movements, muscles, energy system and components of fitness.
- **Overload**: making the body work harder than normal so that it adapts and improves.
- **Progressive overload**: gradually increasing the amount of overload so that fitness gains occur without injury.
- **Reversibility**: any adaptation that takes place from training will be lost when training stops or is reduced.
- **Tedium**: the boredom that can occur from training in the same way every time, reduced by adding variety.
- **Frequency**: how often a performer trains, e.g. the number of sessions per week.
- **Intensity**: how hard a performer trains, e.g. the percentage of maximum heart rate or the weight lifted.
- **Time**: how long a performer trains for, e.g. the length of a session.
- **Type**: the kind of training used, which should suit the aim and can be changed to give variety.
- **Adaptation**: a change the body makes in response to training, such as muscle hypertrophy or a lower resting heart rate.
:::

::: method
1. **Define the principle with its key word:** specificity = *matching* to the activity; progressive overload = *gradually* increasing; reversibility = adaptations *lost* when training *stops*; tedium = *boredom*, so add *variety*.
2. **Apply it to the named performer:** say exactly *what they would do*, with numbers where possible ("add 2 km every two weeks", "3 sets of 8 at a heavier weight").
3. **Link to the effect:** explain how this leads to improvement or avoids a problem (injury, lost fitness, drop-out).
4. **For FITT questions,** use each letter as a separate, applied change, and remind the examiner that changes should be small and gradual.
5. **For 6- and 9-mark questions,** define (AO1), apply to the performer's sport and season (AO2), then analyse or evaluate. How does each principle affect performance? What happens if it is ignored? Which matters most? (AO3)
:::

::: example Define — progressive overload
Define progressive overload. (1)

Gradually increasing the amount of overload (the demand of training) so that fitness gains occur, without the risk of injury (1). "Training harder" on its own does not score, because it misses *gradually*.
:::

::: example Apply — specificity
Explain how a sprint swimmer could apply the principle of specificity to her training. (3)

Specificity means matching training to the requirements of the activity (1).

A sprint swimmer's races last well under a minute and are mainly anaerobic, so she should use high-intensity interval sets in the pool at race pace, not long slow swims (1).

Training in the water, using her race stroke, works the same muscles and movement patterns, so the adaptations transfer directly to her races (1).
:::

::: example Explain — reversibility
Explain how reversibility might affect a netball player who breaks her wrist in mid-season. (3)

Reversibility means that adaptations from training are lost when training stops (1).

With a broken wrist she cannot take part in training for several weeks, so her cardiovascular endurance and muscular strength will decline (1).

When she returns she will tire sooner and be slower around the court, so her performance falls until she rebuilds her fitness gradually (1).
:::

::: example FITT — increasing overload
Using the FITT principles, explain how a cyclist could increase the overload in his training. (4)

Frequency: increase the number of rides from three to four per week (1).

Intensity: ride at a higher percentage of his maximum heart rate, or up steeper hills (1).

Time: extend his long ride from 90 to 105 minutes (1).

Type: add an interval session on the turbo trainer to stress the anaerobic system as well (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — 6-mark
Analyse how a netball player could use the principles of SPORT during pre-season training. (6)

(AO1) SPORT stands for specificity, progressive overload, reversibility and tedium.

(AO2) Specificity: she should do short sprints with sharp changes of direction and jumping drills, because netball needs agility, speed and power in bursts, plus some continuous running for the whole match. Progressive overload: she could start with 10 × 20 m shuttle sprints and add two more each week, increasing gradually.

(AO3) Because the drills match netball, the gains in agility and power will show directly in her ability to get free from her marker. Gradual increases avoid the injuries that are common when players return from the summer break too quickly. Reversibility explains why pre-season is needed at all: fitness was lost over the break, so this block rebuilds it, and she must avoid missing sessions. Tedium could reduce her effort over a long pre-season, so varying the sessions (circuits, court drills, small-sided games) keeps her motivated. Specificity and progressive overload drive the improvement; the other two protect it.
:::

::: q
State what each letter of FITT stands for. (4)
---
Frequency (1); intensity (1); time (1); type (1).
:::

::: q
Define tedium and explain how a coach could reduce it. (2)
---
Boredom that may occur from training in the same way every time (1); the coach adds variety, e.g. changing the training method, venue or drills (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why progressive overload must be gradual. (2)
---
If overload increases too quickly the body cannot adapt in time (1), which can cause injury or overtraining / fatigue, stopping training altogether (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a weightlifter could apply progressive overload over six weeks. (3)
---
Start with a weight that allows e.g. 3 sets of 6 reps with good technique (1); once this feels comfortable, increase the weight slightly, e.g. by 2.5 kg, or add a set (1); repeat the small increases every week or two, so the muscles keep adapting and strength rises without injury (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a marathon runner would not follow the same programme as a 100 m sprinter. Refer to specificity. (3)
---
Specificity means training must match the demands of the activity (1); the marathon is aerobic and lasts over two hours, so the runner needs long continuous runs to improve cardiovascular endurance (1); the sprint is anaerobic and lasts about 10 seconds, so the sprinter needs short, maximal sprints and power training — each programme targets a different energy system and component (1).
:::

::: q
A footballer has had a four-week break in the summer. Explain what has happened to his fitness and how he should return to training. (3)
---
Reversibility: fitness/adaptations gained have been partly lost during the break (1); he should restart at a lower intensity than at the end of last season (1) and increase gradually (progressive overload) to avoid injury (1).
:::

::: watch
- Progressive overload needs **gradually**. "Training harder" or "doing more" is not enough.
- Specificity is about the **activity's demands**: energy system, muscles, movements, components. Just "training for your sport" is too vague.
- Reversibility is about **losing adaptations** when training **stops or is reduced**. Say what is lost (e.g. cardiovascular endurance).
- Tedium is **boredom**. The fix is **variety**, but the variety must still be specific.
- In FITT, **time** is the length of a session, **not** the time of day; **type** is the training method.
- Applied answers need **what the performer actually does**, ideally with numbers.
:::

Explain how a basketball player could apply the principle of specificity. (3)
---
Specificity: matching training to the requirements of the activity (1); basketball involves short sprints, jumps and changes of direction (anaerobic, power, agility) (1); so the player uses e.g. plyometric jumps, court sprints and agility drills rather than long slow runs, so gains transfer to rebounding and driving to the basket (1).

::: test
Explain how tedium could affect a performer's training and how it can be avoided. (3)
---
Doing the same training every time causes boredom (1); the performer may lose motivation, train with less effort or miss sessions, so progress slows / reversibility occurs (1); avoid by adding variety, e.g. different methods, venues, partners or drills while keeping training specific (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a coach should not increase frequency, intensity and time all in the same week. (2)
---
The overload would rise too quickly for the body to adapt (1), increasing the risk of injury/overtraining; progressive overload should be gradual (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a long-distance runner could use the FITT principles to plan her training. (4)
---
Frequency: e.g. runs 4–5 times a week, adding a session gradually (1); intensity: works in the aerobic zone (60–80% max HR), raising pace over time (1); time: sessions of at least 20–30 minutes, lengthening the long run progressively (1); type: mainly continuous training, suitable because the event is aerobic, with some fartlek for variety (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why reversibility means a rugby player should not stop training completely in the off-season. (3)
---
Reversibility: fitness/adaptations are lost when training stops (1); if he does no training, e.g. his cardiovascular endurance and strength will decline over the break (1); he would start pre-season from a lower level, needing longer to regain fitness and risking injury, so light training maintains a base (1).
:::

::: test
Analyse how a 400 m hurdler could apply the principles of progressive overload and specificity to improve her performance. (6)
---
AO1 (1 mark): progressive overload — gradually increasing the demand so fitness gains occur without injury; specificity — matching training to the requirements of the activity.
AO2 (2 marks): specificity — she uses interval sessions at race pace over 200–300 m (mainly anaerobic event), hurdle drills to improve flexibility and technique, plyometrics for power; progressive overload — adds reps or reduces recovery time week by week, e.g. 5 × 300 m with 3 minutes' rest to 6 × 300 m, then 2 minutes 30 seconds' rest.
AO3 (3 marks): specific training develops the anaerobic capacity and hurdling technique she needs, so she keeps her stride pattern and speed in the last 100 m when fatigue sets in; gradual increases allow the body to adapt and reduce hamstring or Achilles injuries, which would otherwise cause reversibility; if overload rises too fast she risks injury; if training is not specific, gains (e.g. from long slow runs) will not transfer to her race; conclusion.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate knowledge, clearly applied to the hurdler, developed analysis with a conclusion; 3–4 — knowledge evident, some application, analysis present but uneven; 1–2 — limited knowledge, little application or analysis; 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
Evaluate how effectively a Sunday league footballer who trains once a week can apply the principles of training (SPORT and FITT) to improve his fitness. (9)
---
AO1 (2 marks): accurate knowledge of SPORT (specificity, progressive overload, reversibility, tedium) and FITT (frequency, intensity, time, type).
AO2 (2 marks): application — specificity through small-sided games, sprint and change-of-direction drills; progressive overload by gradually adding repetitions or reducing rest; type of training such as interval training matched to the stop–start nature of football; tedium avoided by varying drills; reversibility if he misses weeks through work or injury.
AO3 (5 marks): evaluation — a frequency of once a week is low, so overload is limited and improvements will be small; there are six days between sessions for fitness gains to reverse, so he may only maintain fitness; he could increase frequency with a home run or circuit, but work and family may prevent this; intensity can be raised in the single session to compensate, but high intensity without a base risks injury; specificity can still be applied well because his one session and match are football-specific; the weekly match itself provides overload; tedium is unlikely if sessions are game-based, which also helps motivation and attendance. Judgement: he can apply specificity and tedium effectively, but low frequency limits progressive overload and makes reversibility a constant problem, so progress will be slow unless he adds another session.
Levels: 7–9 — accurate, detailed knowledge; clear application to the footballer's situation; thorough evaluation with a well-reasoned judgement; correct terminology. 4–6 — knowledge evident but uneven; some application; evaluation present but one-sided or undeveloped. 1–3 — limited knowledge; little application; little or no evaluation. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
