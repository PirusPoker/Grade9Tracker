## What it is

Every time a performer acts — returning a serve, choosing a pass, saving a penalty — they take in information, decide what to do, do it, and find out how it went. The **basic information processing model** sets out those four stages. You must be able to **draw it in boxes**, **explain each stage**, and **apply it to a sporting skill**.

### The model in box format

The model is drawn as four boxes joined by arrows, with a loop back from the end to the start:

**INPUT** → **DECISION MAKING** → **OUTPUT** → **FEEDBACK** → (arrow looping back to) **INPUT**

In the exam, draw four labelled boxes in that order, with arrows pointing forward and one arrow returning from feedback to input. The loop matters: feedback becomes new input for the next attempt.

### What happens at each stage

| Stage | What happens | Key ideas | Example: a cricket batter facing a delivery |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Input** | information is taken in from the **display** (the sporting environment) through the **senses** | **display**; senses — sight, hearing, touch, and **proprioception** (the body's sense of its own position); **selective attention** | the batter watches the bowler's hand and the ball's line, length and speed, hears the fielders, feels the grip |
| **Decision making** | the performer **selects the appropriate response from memory** | **short-term memory** (working memory) holds what is happening now; **long-term memory** stores learned skills and past experiences | the batter recognises a short ball, recalls from long-term memory how a pull shot is played, and decides to pull |
| **Output** | **information is sent to the muscles** to carry out the chosen response | nerve impulses from brain to muscles; the movement happens | the batter moves back and across and swings the bat horizontally |
| **Feedback** | information about the response, received during or after it | **intrinsic** — from within, the feel of the movement; **extrinsic** — from others, e.g. coach, team-mates, crowd, video, scoreboard | the batter feels a clean contact (intrinsic); the coach shouts "well played" and the umpire signals four (extrinsic) |

### Input in more detail

The **display** is everything the performer could sense: the ball, team-mates, opponents, the crowd, the weather, the scoreboard. Far too much to process, so the brain uses **selective attention**:

- **Selective attention** means **focusing on the relevant information and filtering out what is irrelevant**. A goalkeeper facing a penalty attends to the kicker's body position and the ball, and blocks out the crowd's noise and the flags waving behind the goal.
- Why it matters: it **speeds up** decision making (less to process, so **reaction time** is shorter), **reduces errors**, and helps the performer **stay focused** under pressure.
- It improves with experience — an elite player knows what to look for; a beginner tries to watch everything and is overloaded.

### Decision making — the two memories

| Memory | Role | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-term memory (STM)** | the "workspace": holds the information just taken in and compares it with what is stored in long-term memory | limited capacity (only a few items) and very short duration unless rehearsed |
| **Long-term memory (LTM)** | the store of learned skills, tactics and past experiences, from which a response is selected | very large capacity; lasts a long time; built up through **practice and repetition** — a skill practised often becomes stored as a motor programme |

The two work together: incoming information passes into STM; STM searches LTM for a matching past situation; the stored response is retrieved and chosen. This is why **practice** matters — the more often a skill is rehearsed, the more firmly it is stored in LTM and the quicker the correct response can be selected.

### Output and feedback

**Output** is the chosen response being carried out: the brain sends impulses to the muscles, which contract to produce the movement. If the decision was poor, a perfect output still fails — and a good decision can be spoiled by a poor output.

**Feedback** closes the loop. **Intrinsic feedback** comes from the performer's own senses — the feel of a well-struck ball, the sense of balance on landing. **Extrinsic feedback** comes from outside — a coach's comments, a video, the result, a judge's score. Feedback returns as input, so the next attempt can be adjusted. Guidance and the different types of feedback are covered in 3.2.1.4.

### Applying the model to a skill — the pattern

Pick the skill, then walk through the four stages with specific detail:

*A tennis player returning a serve.* **Input**: sees the server's ball toss, racket angle and the ball's spin and speed; uses selective attention to ignore the crowd and focus on the ball. **Decision making**: STM holds the ball's flight; LTM recalls how this serve was returned before; the player decides on a backhand return cross-court. **Output**: impulses to the muscles move the feet and swing the racket. **Feedback**: intrinsic — the contact felt clean; extrinsic — the coach nods and the umpire makes no call.

::: terms
- **Information processing**: the way a performer takes in information, makes a decision, acts on it and receives feedback.
- **Display**: the sporting environment the performer takes information from, including the ball, players, crowd and conditions.
- **Input**: the stage in which information is taken in from the display through the senses.
- **Selective attention**: focusing on relevant information from the display and filtering out irrelevant information.
- **Decision making**: the stage in which the performer selects an appropriate response from memory.
- **Short-term memory**: the working memory that briefly holds current information and compares it with stored experience; limited capacity.
- **Long-term memory**: the large, lasting store of learned skills and past experiences from which responses are selected.
- **Output**: the stage in which information is sent to the muscles to carry out the chosen response.
- **Feedback**: information received about a performance, during or after it, which returns as input.
- **Intrinsic feedback**: feedback from within the performer, such as the feel of the movement.
- **Extrinsic feedback**: feedback from an outside source, such as a coach, team-mate, video or result.
:::

::: method
1. **Draw it**: four labelled boxes — input, decision making, output, feedback — with forward arrows and a loop from feedback back to input.
2. **Explain a stage** by saying what happens *and* using the key idea: input — display, senses, selective attention; decision making — STM and LTM; output — information to muscles; feedback — intrinsic/extrinsic.
3. **Apply to the named skill**: give one concrete detail per stage (what they see, what they decide, what they do, what they learn).
4. **Keep things in the right box**: selective attention = input; memory = decision making; muscles = output.
5. **For "explain the role of…" questions**, give the effect on performance — faster reactions, fewer errors, better decisions next time.
6. **In extended answers**, analyse links between stages: better selective attention gives faster decisions; practice builds LTM; feedback improves the next input.
:::

::: example Selective attention
Explain the role of selective attention for a goalkeeper facing a penalty. (2)

Selective attention lets the goalkeeper focus on the relevant cues — the kicker's approach, hips and the ball — and ignore irrelevant ones such as crowd noise (1). This speeds up the decision about which way to dive, reducing reaction time and making a save more likely (1).
:::

::: example Memory in decision making
Explain the role of long-term memory in the decision-making stage for a rugby fly-half. (3)

Long-term memory stores the skills and tactics the fly-half has practised and past match situations (1). When the fly-half sees the defence moving up quickly, short-term memory holds this and compares it with stored experiences in long-term memory (1), so the fly-half can quickly retrieve and select the right response, such as a chip kick over the defenders (1).
:::

::: example Apply the model
Apply the basic information processing model to a basketball player taking a free throw. (4)

Input: the player sees the basket and judges the distance, feels the ball in their hands, and uses selective attention to block out the crowd (1). Decision making: long-term memory holds the practised shooting action; the player selects this response, adjusting from the previous attempt held in short-term memory (1). Output: information is sent to the muscles of the legs, arms and wrist, which extend to release the ball (1). Feedback: intrinsic — the release felt smooth; extrinsic — team-mates cheer and the score changes on the scoreboard (1).
:::

::: example Extended — analyse the model
Analyse how the information processing model can help a tennis player return a fast serve. (6)

At input, the returner must pick out a few crucial cues from a busy display — the server's ball toss, racket angle and the ball's early flight — while filtering out the crowd and line judges. Good selective attention cuts the amount of information to process, so the returner reacts faster to a ball arriving at high speed. At decision making, short-term memory holds the ball's line and spin and compares them with long-term memory; an experienced player has returned thousands of serves, so a suitable response (a blocked backhand return) is found almost instantly, whereas a beginner would be too slow. At output, impulses reach the muscles, which move the feet and swing the racket; even a correct decision fails if the output is poorly timed. Feedback — the feel of the contact and whether the ball lands in — returns as input, so the player can stand further back or shorten the swing on the next serve. Understanding the model shows that improving selective attention and building long-term memory through practice are the keys to returning fast serves. (Level 3.)
:::

::: watch
- **Selective attention is input**, not decision making; **memory is decision making**; **muscles are output**. Misplacing them is the most common error.
- When drawing the model, include **arrows and the feedback loop** back to input — four unconnected boxes lose marks.
- "Apply the model" needs **the named skill at every stage** — a generic description of the stages earns little.
- Decision making is **selecting a response from memory** — not "thinking about it".
- Output is **information sent to muscles**, not the result of the shot.
- Intrinsic feedback comes **from the performer's own senses** (the feel of the movement); extrinsic comes **from outside** — a coach, team-mate, video, judge or scoreboard.
:::
