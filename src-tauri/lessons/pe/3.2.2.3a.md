## What it is

Sport depends on performers competing fairly. This topic covers the **conduct** expected of performers — from the unwritten courtesies of a sport to deliberate rule-bending — and the most serious breach of fair play: **performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs)** and **blood doping**. You need the categories, what each does, its side effects, **which performers** might be tempted by it, and the **advantages and disadvantages** for the performer and the sport.

### Conduct of performers

| Term | Meaning | Sporting examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Etiquette** | a **convention or unwritten rule** in a sport — not in the rulebook, but expected | kicking the ball out of play when an opponent is injured, and returning it after the throw-in; shaking hands with opponents after a match; a batter walking when clearly out; not celebrating a point won off the net cord in tennis |
| **Sportsmanship** | **conforming to the rules, spirit and etiquette** of a sport — fair play, respect for opponents and officials | helping an opponent up after a tackle; applauding a good shot by an opponent; admitting a touch or a foul the official missed |
| **Gamesmanship** | **bending the rules** or using **dubious methods** to gain an advantage **without actually breaking** the rules | time-wasting when winning (slow substitutions, taking time at throw-ins); sledging in cricket; calling for an injury time-out to break an opponent's rhythm; deliberately delaying a serve |
| **Contract to compete** | the **unwritten agreement** between opponents to **abide by the written and unwritten rules** — to try their best and play fairly | both teams agreeing, simply by turning up, to give full effort, accept officials' decisions and not cheat; broken by doping, match-fixing or deliberate cheating |

Sportsmanship and etiquette **keep** the contract to compete; gamesmanship **stretches** it; cheating (including PEDs) **breaks** it.

### Prohibited substances

| Category | What it does (positive effect) | Side effects | Performers who might use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stimulants** | increase **alertness** and mental arousal; reduce tiredness; can increase competitiveness and aggression | insomnia; anxiety and irritability; aggression; raised heart rate and blood pressure; addiction | sprinters and other short explosive events, boxers, team players needing alertness and reactions, endurance athletes fighting fatigue |
| **Narcotic analgesics** | **painkillers** — mask pain from injury or over-training so the performer can keep training or competing | addiction; can make an injury **worse** because pain is masked; nausea; loss of concentration | any performer carrying an injury: boxers, footballers, rugby players, endurance athletes |
| **Anabolic agents** (e.g. anabolic steroids) | increase **muscle mass** and strength; allow harder training and faster recovery | aggression / mood swings; liver damage; heart disease and high blood pressure; acne; infertility; in women, development of male characteristics (e.g. facial hair, deeper voice) | power and strength athletes: weightlifters, sprinters, throwers, rugby players, bodybuilders |
| **Peptide hormones (EPO)** | EPO stimulates the production of **red blood cells**, increasing the blood's **oxygen-carrying capacity**, so aerobic endurance improves | thickened blood (increased viscosity), blood clots, stroke, heart attack | endurance performers: long-distance cyclists, marathon runners, long-distance swimmers, cross-country skiers, triathletes |
| **Diuretics** | increase urine production so the performer **loses weight** (water) quickly; can also **mask** other drugs by diluting the urine | dehydration; dizziness; muscle cramps; kidney problems | performers in **weight categories** (boxers, judo players, jockeys, weightlifters) who must "make the weight"; also those hiding another drug |

### Prohibited method: blood doping

**How it is done**: blood is **removed** from the performer **a few weeks before** competition and **frozen**. The body replaces the lost blood over the following weeks. **Just before** competition, the stored blood is **re-injected**. The performer now has **more red blood cells** than normal.

**Effect**: a higher **red blood cell count** means the blood can carry **more oxygen** to the working muscles, improving **aerobic endurance** and delaying fatigue. So it benefits **endurance performers** — long-distance cyclists, marathon runners, long-distance swimmers, triathletes, rowers. It would do little for a sprinter or a weightlifter, whose events are mainly anaerobic, or for a snooker player.

**Side effects**: **thickening of the blood (increased viscosity)**; potential **infection** (from needles or stored blood); potential for **heart attack**; **embolism** (a blockage of a blood vessel).

### Drugs subject to restrictions: beta blockers

**Beta blockers** are medicines that are **prescribed by a medical professional** (for example, for heart conditions or high blood pressure), and are **banned in certain precision sports**. They:
- **reduce heart rate, muscle tension and blood pressure**;
- **reduce the effects of adrenaline**;
- **improve fine control and preciseness** — steadier hands and calmer nerves.

**Performers who might use them**: those needing **fine motor control** and **low arousal** — **archers, shooters, snooker players, darts players, golfers** (putting).

**Side effects**: **nausea**, **weakness**, **heart problems** (such as a very slow heart rate).

### Quick match: drug → performer

| Need | Drug or method | Example performer |
|---|---|---|
| alertness, reaction | stimulants | 100 m sprinter |
| mask pain from injury/over-training | narcotic analgesics | boxer with an injured hand |
| muscle mass, power | anabolic agents | weightlifter, shot putter |
| oxygen-carrying capacity | EPO; blood doping | Tour cyclist, marathon runner |
| lose weight quickly | diuretics | jockey, boxer, judo player |
| fine control, calm nerves | beta blockers | archer, shooter, snooker player |

### Why performers take PEDs — and why they shouldn't

| Advantages for the performer | Disadvantages for the performer | Disadvantages for the sport / event |
|---|---|---|
| **increased chance of success**; **fame**; **wealth** (prize money, sponsorship); a "**level playing field**" — belief that rivals are cheating, so they must too | **cheating / immoral** — breaks the contract to compete; **health risks** (side effects above, some fatal); **fines**; **bans**; **reputational damage** — loss of sponsors, titles stripped, public disgrace | damages the sport's **reputation**; loses **credibility** — spectators doubt whether any result is genuine; sponsors and viewers may withdraw; clean athletes are cheated of medals and money |

::: terms
- **Etiquette**: a convention or unwritten rule in a sport that performers are expected to follow.
- **Sportsmanship**: conforming to the rules, spirit and etiquette of a sport; playing fairly and respecting others.
- **Gamesmanship**: bending the rules or using dubious methods to gain an advantage without actually breaking the rules.
- **Contract to compete**: the unwritten agreement between competitors to abide by the written and unwritten rules of a sport.
- **Performance-enhancing drug (PED)**: a substance taken to improve sporting performance, which is prohibited or restricted.
- **Stimulant**: a drug that increases alertness and mental arousal and reduces tiredness.
- **Narcotic analgesic**: a painkilling drug that masks pain from injury or over-training.
- **Anabolic agent**: a drug that increases muscle mass and strength and speeds recovery from training.
- **Peptide hormone (EPO)**: a hormone that increases red blood cell production, raising oxygen-carrying capacity.
- **Diuretic**: a drug that increases urine production to lose weight quickly or to mask other drugs.
- **Blood doping**: removing blood weeks before competition, freezing it and re-injecting it just before, to raise red blood cell count.
- **Beta blocker**: a prescribed drug that lowers heart rate, muscle tension and blood pressure, improving fine control.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions**: give the precise meaning and a specific sporting example — conduct terms are 1+1 mark questions.
2. **PED questions**: name the category, its **positive effect**, a **side effect**, and a **matching performer** — the match must fit the event's demands.
3. **Blood doping**: describe the **three steps** (remove weeks before → freeze → re-inject just before), then the **effect** (more red blood cells → more oxygen) and **who benefits** (endurance).
4. **Beta blockers**: link reduced heart rate/tremor/adrenaline to **fine control**, and to precision sports.
5. **Evaluate**: advantages (success, fame, wealth, level playing field) against disadvantages for performer (cheating, health, fines, bans, reputation) and sport (reputation, credibility); reach a judgement.
:::

Describe how blood doping is carried out. (3)

Blood is removed from the performer a few weeks before the competition (1). It is frozen and stored while the body replaces the lost blood (1). The stored blood is re-injected just before the competition, increasing the red blood cell count (1).

::: example Explain blood doping for an endurance athlete
Explain how blood doping could improve the performance of a long-distance cyclist. (3)

Re-injecting stored blood increases the number of red blood cells (1), so more oxygen can be carried to the working muscles (1). This improves aerobic endurance, so the cyclist can work at a higher intensity for longer before fatiguing (1).
:::

::: example Identify and justify a PED
Identify the PED a pistol shooter might use. Justify your choice. (3)

Beta blockers (1). They reduce heart rate, muscle tension and the effects of adrenaline, so the shooter's hands are steadier (1). This improves fine control and precision, giving a more accurate shot (1).
:::

::: example Extended — evaluate PEDs for two performers
Evaluate the use of PEDs by a sprinter and by a snooker player. (9)

A sprinter needs explosive power and fast reactions, so anabolic agents would increase muscle mass and strength and allow harder training, and stimulants would raise alertness at the start. A snooker player needs calm, fine control, so beta blockers would lower heart rate and muscle tension and reduce the effect of adrenaline, steadying the cue arm under pressure. For both, the advantages are a better chance of winning and the fame and wealth that come with it, and some may argue they are only creating a level playing field if rivals are doping. However, the disadvantages are serious. The sprinter risks anabolic side effects such as liver damage, heart disease and aggression, and stimulants can cause anxiety and addiction; the snooker player risks nausea, weakness and heart problems. Both are cheating and break the contract to compete, and if caught face bans, fines, loss of titles and sponsorship, and lasting reputational damage. Their sports suffer too: repeated doping cases make spectators doubt results, damaging credibility and driving away sponsors. The sprinter probably stands to gain more physically from PEDs than the snooker player, which may make the temptation greater, but for both the health risks and the likelihood of being caught and disgraced outweigh the short-term gain. (Level 3: accurate knowledge, applied to both performers, with a reasoned conclusion.)
:::

::: watch
- **Match the drug to the performer**: EPO and blood doping — endurance; anabolic agents — power; beta blockers — fine control; diuretics — weight categories; narcotic analgesics — injury; stimulants — alertness.
- Blood doping has **three steps and a timeline** — "weeks before" and "just before" both matter.
- **Gamesmanship is not cheating** — it bends but does not break the rules. Diving to win a penalty is cheating, not gamesmanship.
- Beta blockers are **restricted/prescribed**, not simply "illegal"; they **reduce** heart rate — don't say they increase it.
- Evaluate questions need **advantages and disadvantages**, and ideally the **sport's** view as well as the performer's.
- Give **side effects** where asked — "bad for you" is too vague.
