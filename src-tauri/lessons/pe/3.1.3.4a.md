## What it is

Training only improves fitness if it is done at the **right intensity**, and only helps a performer if it doesn't injure them. This topic has three parts: **heart-rate training zones** (with calculations), using **one rep max** in weight training, and the **factors that prevent injury**. It is one of the few places on Paper 1 where you must **calculate**, so show your working every time.

### Training thresholds and zones

The **training threshold** is the level of intensity a performer must reach for training to bring about an improvement in fitness. The **training zone** is the band of intensity between a **lower** and an **upper threshold**. Work below the lower threshold and there is too little overload. Work above the upper threshold and the training moves into a different energy system, or becomes unsafe.

Intensity is set using **heart rate** as a percentage of **maximum heart rate** (max HR):

| Step | Rule |
|---|---|
| 1. Maximum heart rate | **220 − age** (beats per minute, bpm) |
| 2. **Aerobic** training zone | **60% to 80%** of max HR |
| 3. **Anaerobic** training zone | **80% to 90%** of max HR |

**Worked calculation for a 16-year-old:**
- Max HR = 220 − 16 = **204 bpm**
- Aerobic zone: 60% of 204 = 0.6 × 204 = **122.4**; 80% of 204 = 0.8 × 204 = **163.2**. So about **122–163 bpm**.
- Anaerobic zone: 80% = **163.2**; 90% = 0.9 × 204 = **183.6**. So about **163–184 bpm**.

| Age | Max HR (bpm) | Aerobic zone, 60–80% (bpm) | Anaerobic zone, 80–90% (bpm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 205 | 123 – 164 | 164 – 184.5 |
| 16 | 204 | 122.4 – 163.2 | 163.2 – 183.6 |
| 17 | 203 | 121.8 – 162.4 | 162.4 – 182.7 |
| 18 | 202 | 121.2 – 161.6 | 161.6 – 181.8 |
| 25 | 195 | 117 – 156 | 156 – 175.5 |
| 40 | 180 | 108 – 144 | 144 – 162 |

A marathon runner doing a long steady run works in the **aerobic** zone. A 400 m runner doing race-pace intervals works in the **anaerobic** zone. As a performer gets fitter, the same pace produces a **lower** heart rate, so they must run faster to stay in the zone. This is **progressive overload** in action.

**Circuit training:** the fitness aim is set by altering the **time** at each station, the **rest** between stations and the **content** (the exercises). Long work with short rest makes the circuit aerobic or builds muscular endurance. Short, maximal work with long rest makes it anaerobic, for power or speed.

### One rep max (1RM)

**One rep max** is the **maximum weight a person can lift once** with correct technique. It is found by warming up, lifting a weight that can be managed, resting, adding weight and repeating until only one lift is possible (the One Rep Max Test, 3.1.3.2b). The training load is then set as a **percentage** of it:

| Aim | Load | Sets and reps |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength / power** | **high weight, low reps**: **above 70%** of 1RM | about **3 sets of 4–8 reps** |
| **Muscular endurance** | **low weight, high reps**: **below 70%** of 1RM | about **3 sets of 12–15 reps** |

**Worked example:** a rower's 1RM on the leg press is 150 kg. 70% of 150 = 0.7 × 150 = **105 kg**.
- For strength: above 105 kg, e.g. 120 kg, 3 sets of 6 reps.
- For muscular endurance: below 105 kg, e.g. 80 kg, 3 sets of 15 reps.

### Considerations to prevent injury

First principle: the **type and intensity of training should match its purpose**. Aerobic aims mean aerobic-zone work. Pushing a beginner into the anaerobic zone every session invites injury and overtraining.

| Factor | How it prevents injury | Applied example |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm up** | raises muscle temperature and elasticity, increasing range of movement, so muscles and tendons are less likely to tear | a sprinter jogs, stretches and does build-up runs before max-speed work |
| **Avoid overtraining** | the body needs time to adapt; too much weight, volume or frequency causes overuse injuries and fatigue | a lifter uses an appropriate weight rather than one beyond their 1RM |
| **Appropriate clothing and footwear** | grip, cushioning, support and protection | **studded boots** stop a footballer slipping on wet grass; **cushioned running shoes** absorb impact for a road runner; shin pads in hockey |
| **Taping / bracing** | supports a weak or previously injured joint and limits harmful movement | a volleyball player tapes her ankle to prevent it rolling on landing |
| **Hydration** | dehydration causes fatigue, cramp and poor concentration, which lead to mistakes and injury | a tennis player drinks at every change of ends in hot weather |
| **No over-stretching or bouncing** | bouncing (ballistic) or forcing a stretch beyond the range can tear muscle fibres | a gymnast holds a static stretch smoothly rather than bouncing into the splits |
| **Correct technique** | reduces strain on joints, back and ligaments | a weightlifter keeps a straight back and lifts with the legs; a rugby player tackles with the head to the side |
| **Rest between sessions** | allows recovery and repair, so tissues are not overloaded while already damaged | a runner leaves 48 hours between hard interval sessions |

The exam wants the factor **applied to a named sport**. Examiners report that "wearing the right footwear" with no sport named gains nothing.

::: terms
- **Training threshold**: the level of intensity a performer must work at, or above, for training to improve fitness; the zone lies between a lower and an upper threshold.
- **Training zone**: the band of heart rates (as a percentage of maximum heart rate) in which a performer trains to achieve a particular aim.
- **Maximum heart rate**: the highest heart rate a person can reach, estimated as 220 minus age, in beats per minute.
- **Aerobic training zone**: 60% to 80% of maximum heart rate, used to improve cardiovascular endurance.
- **Anaerobic training zone**: 80% to 90% of maximum heart rate, used to improve anaerobic fitness such as speed.
- **One rep max (1RM)**: the maximum weight a person can lift in a single repetition with correct technique.
- **Overtraining**: training too hard or too often without enough rest, leading to fatigue, poor performance and injury.
- **Taping/bracing**: supporting a joint with tape or a brace to limit harmful movement and protect it from injury.
- **Hydration**: having enough water in the body; maintained by drinking before, during and after exercise.
:::

::: method
1. **Heart-rate calculations:** write "Max HR = 220 − age" and the answer; then work out each boundary percentage separately (0.6 ×, 0.8 ×, 0.9 ×) and state the zone as a range **with units (bpm)**. One mark usually goes to each step.
2. **One rep max:** find 70% of the 1RM first, then choose a load **above** it (strength/power, 3 × 4–8) or **below** it (muscular endurance, 3 × 12–15).
3. **Match the zone to the aim:** aerobic (endurance events, long steady runs) or anaerobic (sprints, intervals).
4. **Injury questions:** name the factor, say **how** it prevents injury, and tie it to the **named sport**.
5. **Extended answers:** define (AO1), apply the calculations and injury factors to the performer's training (AO2), and analyse or evaluate how they optimise training and reduce risk (AO3).
:::

::: example Calculate — aerobic zone
Calculate the aerobic training zone for a 20-year-old. Show your working. (3)

Maximum heart rate = 220 − 20 = 200 bpm (1).

60% of 200 = 0.6 × 200 = 120 bpm; 80% of 200 = 0.8 × 200 = 160 bpm (1).

Aerobic training zone = 120 to 160 bpm (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — anaerobic zone
Kira is 15. Calculate her anaerobic training zone. Show your working. (3)

Maximum heart rate = 220 − 15 = 205 bpm (1).

80% of 205 = 164 bpm; 90% of 205 = 184.5 bpm (1).

Anaerobic zone = 164 to 184.5 bpm (about 164–185 bpm) (1).
:::

::: example Using one rep max
A weightlifter has a one rep max of 80 kg on the bench press. He wants to improve his strength. Suggest a suitable load and number of sets and repetitions. Show your working. (3)

70% of 80 kg = 0.7 × 80 = 56 kg (1).

For strength he should lift above 70%, e.g. 64 kg (80% of 1RM) (1).

He should do about 3 sets of 4–8 repetitions (1).
:::

::: example Explain — injury prevention with footwear
Explain how appropriate footwear can help prevent injury. Use a sporting example. (2)

Studded football boots grip wet grass (1).

This stops the player slipping when turning or tackling, which could otherwise cause a sprained ankle or a twisted knee (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — 6-mark injury prevention
Tom is a 17-year-old who plays rugby and lifts weights three times a week. Analyse how Tom could reduce his risk of injury during training. (6)

(AO1) Injury can be prevented by warming up, avoiding overtraining, wearing correct clothing and footwear, taping or bracing, staying hydrated, not over-stretching and using correct technique, with rest between sessions.

(AO2) In the gym, Tom should use a spotter and keep a straight back on squats, and he should set his loads from his one rep max rather than lifting beyond it. On the pitch he should wear studded boots and a gum shield, and tape an ankle he has sprained before.

(AO3) Correct lifting technique reduces strain on his lower back, where heavy squats can cause serious injury, and a spotter means a failed bench press does not trap him. Basing loads on his 1RM (above 70% for strength) gives enough overload without exceeding what his muscles can handle, which helps avoid tears. Because he combines rugby with three weights sessions, overtraining is his biggest risk. Planning rest days, including 48 hours before a match, lets his muscles recover. Otherwise fatigue makes his tackling technique slip, and in a contact sport that is when injuries happen.
:::

Give three factors that should be considered to prevent injury in training. (3)
---

::: test
Explain why a 10,000 m runner should train mainly in the aerobic training zone. (3)
---
The event lasts about half an hour or more and is mainly aerobic (1); training at 60–80% of max HR matches this demand (specificity) (1) and improves cardiovascular endurance, so the runner can keep a fast pace without lactic acid building up (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why rest between training sessions helps to prevent injury. (2)
---
The body needs time to recover and repair muscle tissue after training (1); training again before recovery means working on fatigued/damaged muscles, raising the risk of strains and overuse injuries (1).
:::
