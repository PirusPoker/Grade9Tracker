## What it is

Mental preparation is about getting a performer into the right state to perform. The central idea is **arousal**; the **inverted-U theory** explains how it affects performance; and three **stress management techniques** let a performer bring it under control. The June paper regularly asks you to **draw** the graph, so practise it until it is automatic.

### Arousal

**Arousal** is a **physical and mental (physiological and psychological) state of alertness or readiness**, ranging from deep sleep at one extreme to intense excitement at the other. As arousal rises, heart rate, breathing rate, adrenaline and muscle tension rise, and the performer becomes more alert and focused — up to a point.

### The inverted-U theory

**Drawing it** (you will be asked to sketch the curve and label both axes):
- **y-axis** (vertical): **performance level**, from **low** at the bottom to **high** at the top.
- **x-axis** (horizontal): **arousal level**, from **low** on the left to **high** on the right.
- The curve is an **upside-down U**: it starts low on the left, rises to a peak in the middle, then falls back down on the right — a symmetrical hump.
- Label the peak **optimal arousal** (optimum performance); the left side **under-aroused**; the right side **over-aroused**.

**Describing it** — the relationship between arousal and performance:

| Part of the curve | Arousal | Performance | What the performer is like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Left side | **low** — **under-aroused** | **low** | bored, flat, lacking motivation and concentration; slow to react; not "up for it" |
| Rising section | increasing | improving | becoming alert, focused and energised |
| Peak | **moderate — optimal** | **highest** | the "zone": alert, focused, confident; best decisions and technique |
| Falling section | continuing to rise | getting worse | starting to feel anxious, tense |
| Right side | **high** — **over-aroused** | **low** | anxious, tense muscles, poor decisions, loss of focus, mistakes, possible aggression |

So **both under-arousal and over-arousal cause low performance**; the best performance comes at a moderate, **optimal** level.

A simple set of points to reason with (illustrative, on a 0–10 scale):

| Arousal level | 1 | 3 | 5 | 7 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Performance level | 2 | 6 | 9 | 6 | 2 |

Plot them and you get the inverted U: performance rises from 2 to a peak of 9 at moderate arousal, then falls back to 2.

### Optimal arousal depends on the skill

The peak is not in the same place for every skill. **Link the arousal level to the skill (not the sport)**:

| Skill type | Optimal arousal | Why | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gross** skills — large muscle groups, power, little precision | **high** | extra adrenaline and muscle tension help strength and power, and precision is less important | a **rugby tackle**, a **scrum**, a weightlifter's clean and jerk, a sprint start, a shot put |
| **Fine** skills — small muscles, precise control | **low** | high arousal brings muscle tension and shaking that ruin precision, and narrows concentration | a **golf putt**, a **snooker** pot, a **basketball free throw**, releasing an **arrow**, a darts throw, a rugby **conversion kick** |

The same sport can need both: a rugby player needs high arousal for the tackle, then must calm down for a conversion minutes later. That is why AQA insists on skills, not sports. (Complex skills and beginners also tend to need lower arousal; simple, well-learned skills tolerate more.)

### Stress management techniques

These lower arousal (and anxiety) **before or during** performance. Know **how each is carried out**, with a sporting example.

| Technique | How it is carried out | Example | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deep breathing** | take **slow, deep breaths**, breathing in through the nose to fill the lungs, holding briefly, then breathing out slowly through the mouth; repeat several times; can be done in seconds | a basketball player at the free-throw line takes two slow deep breaths before shooting; a sprinter on the blocks before the "set" call | slows heart rate and relaxes tense muscles; focuses attention on the breath, away from worry |
| **Mental rehearsal / visualisation / imagery** | in a quiet place, with eyes closed, the performer **pictures themselves performing the skill perfectly** — seeing, hearing and feeling it — and the successful outcome; or imagines a calm, relaxing place | a high jumper pictures the run-up, take-off and clearance before the attempt; a gymnast runs through the whole routine in their head; a penalty taker sees the ball hitting the net | rehearses the correct movement so it feels familiar; builds confidence; distracts from anxiety; calms the performer |
| **Positive self-talk** | the performer **replaces negative thoughts with positive statements**, said aloud or in their head — short, first-person, present-tense phrases | a tennis player after a double fault says "next point, watch the ball" instead of "I always choke"; a long-distance runner repeats "strong and steady" | stops negative thinking spiralling; builds confidence and focus; keeps arousal controlled |

Stress management mainly helps the **over-aroused** performer (moving them back left towards the peak). An **under-aroused** performer needs the opposite — a rousing team talk, upbeat music, a vigorous warm-up.

::: terms
- **Arousal**: a physical and mental state of alertness or readiness, ranging from deep sleep to intense excitement.
- **Inverted-U theory**: the theory that performance improves as arousal rises to an optimal level, then worsens as arousal rises further.
- **Optimal arousal**: the moderate level of arousal at which a performer produces their best performance for a skill.
- **Under-aroused**: arousal below the optimal level, so the performer is flat, unfocused and performs poorly.
- **Over-aroused**: arousal above the optimal level, so the performer is anxious and tense and makes mistakes.
- **Stress management**: techniques used before or during a performance to control arousal and anxiety.
- **Deep breathing**: taking slow, deep breaths in and out to lower heart rate and relax muscles before or during a skill.
- **Mental rehearsal**: picturing yourself performing a skill successfully in your mind, also called visualisation or imagery.
- **Positive self-talk**: replacing negative thoughts with positive statements said aloud or in your head to build confidence.
:::

::: method
1. **Define arousal precisely**: a physical and mental state of alertness/readiness.
2. **Drawing the graph**: y = performance level (low to high), x = arousal level (low to high), an upside-down U, peak labelled optimal.
3. **Describing it**: low arousal → low performance; moderate (optimal) → best; high → low performance again. Say *why* at each end (bored vs anxious).
4. **Linking skills to arousal**: gross/power → high; fine/precision → low. Name the **skill**, not the sport.
5. **Stress management**: say **how it is done** (the steps), when, and **why** it lowers arousal — then apply it to the named performer.
6. **In extended answers**, connect the ideas: identify the skill, its optimal arousal, the effect of being off the peak, and the technique that brings the performer back.
:::

::: example Describe the inverted-U
Describe the relationship between arousal and performance shown by the inverted-U theory. (3)

When arousal is low (under-aroused), performance is low because the performer is unmotivated and unfocused (1). As arousal increases to a moderate, optimal level, performance improves to its highest point (1). If arousal rises beyond the optimal level (over-aroused), performance falls again because the performer becomes anxious, tense and makes mistakes (1).
:::

::: example Link arousal to skills
Explain why a rugby fly-half needs a high level of arousal when making a tackle but a lower level when kicking a penalty at goal. (3)

The tackle is a gross skill using large muscle groups to drive into the opponent with maximum power (1), so high arousal — more adrenaline and muscle tension — helps him generate force and commit to the contact (1). The kick at goal is a fine, closed skill needing precise control of the foot and body, so lower arousal avoids muscle tension and anxiety that would pull the kick off line (1).
:::

::: example How a technique is carried out
Explain how a golfer could use deep breathing before a putt. (3)

Standing behind the ball, the golfer takes several slow, deep breaths — in through the nose, filling the lungs, then slowly out through the mouth (1). This lowers heart rate and relaxes the muscles in the arms and shoulders (1), so the golfer is at the low optimal arousal a fine skill needs, and can make a smooth, precise stroke (1).
:::

::: example Extended — analyse arousal control
Chloe is an archer competing in a national final. Analyse how Chloe could use her understanding of arousal and stress management to improve her performance. (6)

Releasing an arrow is a fine skill that relies on small muscles and precise control, so Chloe's optimal arousal is low, at the left of the peak for a gross skill. In a national final the crowd and pressure may push her over-aroused: her heart rate rises, her hands may shake and her muscles tense, so the arrow is released inaccurately and her score falls. Knowing this, Chloe can use deep breathing between arrows — slow breaths in through the nose and out through the mouth — to lower heart rate and relax her bow arm. Before shooting she can use mental rehearsal, picturing a smooth draw, anchor and release with the arrow striking the centre, so the movement feels familiar and her confidence grows. If she misses, positive self-talk ("next one, smooth release") stops negative thoughts raising her anxiety further. Together these keep her near her optimal arousal, so her precision and consistency are maintained under pressure. (Level 3.)
:::

::: watch
- When drawing the graph, **label both axes** fully — "performance level" (y) and "arousal level" (x) — or the marks go.
- **Link arousal to the skill, not the sport**: "rugby needs high arousal" is wrong — the tackle does, the conversion does not.
- **Both ends are low performance**: under-arousal *and* over-arousal. Don't say "more arousal is always better".
- For a technique, describe **how it is carried out** (slow breaths, eyes closed, positive phrase) — naming it alone earns little.
- **Mental rehearsal, visualisation and imagery** are treated as the same technique on this spec.
- Stress management lowers arousal; **don't recommend it to an under-aroused performer**.
:::
