## What it is

The second half of mental preparation covers three things that differ from one performer to the next: how they channel **aggression**, what **personality** type they have, and what **motivates** them. Each links back to arousal (3.2.1.5a): aggression often comes from over-arousal, extroverts seek high arousal, and motivation drives the effort that makes practice work.

### Aggression

In sport, **aggression** is a **deliberate intent to harm or injure** another person, physically or mentally. Many sports channel aggressive behaviour **within the rules** — a legal tackle is still aggressive. The spec splits it in two:

| | **Direct aggression** | **Indirect aggression** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | aggression that involves **physical contact** with another person | aggression that **does not involve physical contact**; it is directed at an **object** (usually the ball or shuttle) to gain an advantage |
| Examples | a **tackle** in rugby; a **punch** in boxing; a shoulder **charge** or **block** in ice hockey; a **hand-off**; a **takedown** in judo | a **smash** in badminton or tennis hit hard at the opponent's body; a fast **bouncer** in cricket aimed to intimidate the batter; hitting a hockey ball hard to force a hurried stop; a **drive** hit hard in squash |
| Usual sports | contact sports: rugby, boxing, judo, American football | non-contact sports: racket sports, cricket, volleyball, most striking games |

**Key point:** direct aggression is defined by **contact**, not by whether it is legal. A fair rugby tackle and a punch thrown in a football brawl are both direct aggression. Indirect aggression is **through an object**.

**How aggression affects performance** (useful in 6-mark "discuss" questions):
- **Positive**: controlled aggression can raise effort and intensity, win physical contests (tackles, rucks), intimidate opponents and gain a psychological edge.
- **Negative**: uncontrolled aggression — often a sign of **over-arousal** — can lead to **fouls, penalties, yellow or red cards and bans**, **injury** to self or others, **loss of concentration** and poor decisions, and letting the team down; it also damages reputation.

### Personality: introverts and extroverts

| | **Introvert** | **Extrovert** |
|---|---|---|
| Characteristics | **shy / quiet**; **thoughtful**; **enjoy being on their own** | **enjoy interaction with others / sociable**, **aroused by others**; **enthusiastic / talkative**; **prone to boredom when isolated** |
| Tend to play | **individual** sports | **team** sports |
| When the activity needs | **concentration / precision (fine skills)**; **low arousal** | a **fast pace**; **low concentration** (little need for sustained precision); **gross skills** |
| Examples | archery, golf, snooker, shooting, long-distance running, gymnastics, darts | rugby, football, hockey, basketball, netball, ice hockey |

**Justifying a match** — join a characteristic to a demand of the activity:
- *Introvert and archery*: an archer spends long periods alone and must concentrate intensely on a precise, fine release at low arousal, which suits a quiet, thoughtful person who is comfortable on their own.
- *Extrovert and basketball*: basketball is a fast-paced team game with constant interaction and gross movements; an extrovert enjoys working with team-mates, is energised by the crowd and would be bored by long periods of solitary practice.

These are **tendencies**, not rules — plenty of successful team players are introverts. Most people sit somewhere between the two.

### Motivation

**Motivation** is the drive to succeed or to do something. It comes from two sources:

| Type | Where it comes from | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic** | **from within** the performer — **pride**, **self-satisfaction**, a sense of **personal achievement**, enjoyment | a runner training because she loves the feeling of improving; a climber proud of completing a hard route |
| **Extrinsic** — **tangible** | from **another source or person**; rewards you can **touch** | **certificates**, **trophies**, **medals**, prize money, badges |
| **Extrinsic** — **intangible** | from **another source or person**; rewards you **cannot touch** | **praise**, **feedback**, **applause**, recognition in the media |

**Evaluating intrinsic and extrinsic motivation:**
- **Intrinsic motivation is generally more effective.** It comes from within, so it does not depend on anyone else, and it is more likely to lead to **continued effort and participation** — people who play for enjoyment keep playing.
- **Overuse of extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation**: if a child is always rewarded with a certificate, they may start playing for the certificate rather than the fun, and stop when the rewards stop.
- **Performers can become reliant on extrinsic rewards** — they lose interest when the trophies, money or praise dry up, or only try hard when a reward is on offer.
- **But extrinsic rewards have value**: they can **attract** beginners to start an activity, they mark achievement, and receiving a medal or praise can **result in feelings of pride and self-satisfaction** — in other words, extrinsic rewards can build intrinsic motivation.
- The best approach is usually a **combination**: use extrinsic rewards (especially praise) sparingly to encourage, while developing enjoyment and personal satisfaction.

::: terms
- **Aggression**: a deliberate intent to harm or injure another person, which can be physical or mental.
- **Direct aggression**: aggression that involves physical contact with another person, such as a tackle in rugby.
- **Indirect aggression**: aggression with no physical contact, directed at an object such as a ball to gain an advantage.
- **Personality**: the individual characteristics that make each person unique and affect how they behave.
- **Introvert**: a personality type that is shy, quiet and thoughtful and enjoys being on their own.
- **Extrovert**: a personality type that is sociable, enthusiastic, talkative, aroused by others and bored when alone.
- **Motivation**: the drive to succeed or the desire to do something.
- **Intrinsic motivation**: motivation from within the performer, for pride, self-satisfaction or personal achievement.
- **Extrinsic motivation**: motivation from another source or person, in the form of tangible or intangible rewards.
- **Tangible reward**: an extrinsic reward that can be touched, such as a trophy, medal or certificate.
- **Intangible reward**: an extrinsic reward that cannot be touched, such as praise, feedback or applause.
:::

::: method
1. **Aggression**: define it (deliberate intent to harm), then decide **contact or no contact** → direct or indirect. Give a named sporting action.
2. **Personality**: name the type, give **two characteristics**, then **link each to a demand of the activity** (team/individual, pace, concentration, fine/gross, arousal).
3. **Motivation**: say **where it comes from** (within = intrinsic; another person = extrinsic), and for extrinsic, whether it is **tangible** or **intangible**.
4. **Evaluate motivation** using the spec's arguments: intrinsic more effective and sustains participation; extrinsic can undermine intrinsic and cause reliance; extrinsic can still create pride.
5. **For "discuss" or "evaluate" questions**, give positives and negatives for the named performer, then a judgement.
:::

::: example Evaluate extrinsic rewards
Evaluate the use of extrinsic rewards to motivate young performers. (4)

Extrinsic rewards such as badges and certificates can encourage children to start and keep attending an activity (1), and receiving them can give a sense of pride that builds intrinsic motivation (1). However, overuse can undermine intrinsic motivation, so the child plays for the reward rather than for enjoyment (1), and they may become reliant on rewards and drop out when they stop (1). Rewards are therefore best used occasionally alongside developing enjoyment.
:::

::: example Extended — discuss aggression
Discuss how aggression can affect the performance of an ice hockey player during a match. (6)

Ice hockey allows legal direct aggression — body checks and physical battles along the boards — so a player who channels aggression within the rules can win possession, protect the puck and intimidate opponents, giving the team a physical and psychological edge. Controlled aggression raises intensity and effort, helping in the gross, high-arousal parts of the game. However, if aggression becomes uncontrolled — often a sign of over-arousal after a bad call or a hard hit — the player may commit illegal checks or fight, earning penalty minutes; the team then plays short-handed and may concede. Over-aroused, aggressive players also lose concentration on their positional role and make poor passing decisions, and they risk injuring themselves or an opponent and receiving a ban. So aggression helps performance only while it is controlled and within the rules; beyond that it harms both the player and the team. (Level 3: accurate knowledge, applied to ice hockey, with a balanced discussion and conclusion.)
:::

- Personality answers need a **characteristic linked to the activity** — "extroverts like team sports" alone rarely scores the justification mark.
