## What it is

The last topic showed what activity does for health; this one shows what happens **without** it. It covers the **consequences of a sedentary lifestyle**, **obesity** and how it limits performance and causes ill health, and the three **somatotypes** (body types) — with the skill of **matching a body type to a sport or position and justifying it**.

### Sedentary lifestyles

- A **lifestyle** is **the way a person lives** — their habits, choices and routine (diet, activity, sleep, work, smoking, alcohol).
- **Sedentary** means **sitting or lying down a lot, with little, irregular or no physical activity**.
- So a **sedentary lifestyle** is a way of living with **little, irregular or no physical activity**.

**Why people become sedentary**: desk-based jobs and study, driving rather than walking, screen time (TV, gaming, phones), lack of time, lack of motivation or confidence, cost or lack of local facilities, illness or injury. Teenagers may drop out of sport when PE stops being compulsory or when exam pressure builds.

**Possible consequences of a sedentary lifestyle** (the spec's list — learn it):

| Consequence | Why inactivity causes it |
|---|---|
| **Weight gain / obesity** | too few calories are used, so energy intake exceeds output and the surplus is stored as fat |
| **Heart disease** | the heart is not strengthened by exercise; fatty deposits can build up in the arteries, narrowing them |
| **Hypertension** (high blood pressure) | narrowed, less elastic arteries and excess weight raise blood pressure |
| **Diabetes** (type 2) | inactivity and excess body fat make the body less able to control blood sugar |
| **Poor sleep** | the body is not physically tired and stress is not relieved, so sleep quality falls |
| **Poor self-esteem** | weight gain, low fitness and missing the social side of sport can lower confidence and body image |
| **Lethargy** | a lack of energy and constant tiredness; low fitness makes everyday tasks feel harder, which reduces activity further |

These feed each other: a sedentary person gains weight, feels lethargic and has low self-esteem, so they are even less likely to exercise — a **vicious circle**.

### Obesity

**Obesity** is having a **very high proportion of body fat** — usually defined as a **body mass index (BMI) of 30 or more**. It is not the same as being **overweight** (heavier than the standard for one's height), since a muscular rugby player can be overweight without being overfat.

**How obesity may affect performance in physical activity and sport:**

| Limits… | Why |
|---|---|
| **stamina / cardiovascular endurance** | the heart and lungs must work harder to move extra body mass; the performer tires quickly and cannot sustain activity |
| **flexibility** | excess fat around joints physically restricts the range of movement |
| **agility** | extra mass is harder to accelerate, stop and change direction quickly, and balance is harder to control |
| **speed / power** | more mass to move relative to muscle, so acceleration and top speed fall; jumping height drops |

Obesity would affect **strength** least, and in a few activities (a sumo wrestler, a front-row forward) extra body mass can even help, because mass makes a performer harder to move.

**Obesity causes ill health:**

| Physical | Mental | Social |
|---|---|---|
| **cancer** (some types); **heart disease / heart attacks**; **diabetes** (type 2); **high cholesterol** | **depression**; **loss of confidence** (low self-esteem, poor body image) | **inability to socialise** (embarrassment, low mobility); **inability to leave home** in severe cases |

Notice the parallel with 3.2.3.1: obesity damages all three kinds of health, just as activity improves all three.

### Somatotypes

A **somatotype** is a way of classifying **body type** by shape and composition. There are three extremes, and most people are a mix.

| Somatotype | Description | Suited to | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Endomorph** | **pear-shaped**: wide hips, narrow shoulders, **a lot of fat** on the body, especially the stomach and hips; rounded | **sumo wrestling**; **prop** in rugby; some throwing and strength events | extra mass gives stability and makes the performer hard to push or move; a low centre of gravity helps in a scrum or bout |
| **Mesomorph** | **wedge-shaped (inverted triangle)**: broad shoulders, narrow hips, **muscular** arms and legs, **little fat** | **sprinting**, **gymnastics**, **boxing**, **swimming**, **rugby backs**, **tennis**, most power and speed sports | high muscle mass gives strength, power and speed; low fat means little dead weight; broad shoulders give upper-body strength |
| **Ectomorph** | **tall and thin**: narrow shoulders and hips, **little muscle or fat**, long limbs | **high jump**, **long-distance running**, **basketball** (height), **netball** (goal shooter), **volleyball** blocking | low body weight is easy to lift and carry over long distances; long levers and height help reach and clear a bar |

**Identifying and justifying**: name the type, describe the relevant features, and **link them to the demands of the sport or position**.
- *A 100 m sprinter — mesomorph.* High muscle mass in the legs gives explosive power out of the blocks, and little body fat means less weight to accelerate.
- *A marathon runner — ectomorph.* A light, thin frame carries little mass over 42 km, so less energy is used and less heat builds up.
- *A rugby prop — endomorph (with mesomorph strength).* Mass and a low centre of gravity give stability in the scrum and make the prop hard to shift.

Positions within a sport can differ: in rugby, props suit endomorphs while wingers suit mesomorphs; in netball, a tall ectomorph suits goal shooter while a mesomorph suits centre. Some events need a mix — a throwing athlete may combine an endomorph's mass with a mesomorph's muscle — so justify whatever you choose from the event's demands.

::: terms
- **Lifestyle**: the way a person lives, including their habits and choices about activity, diet and sleep.
- **Sedentary lifestyle**: a way of living with little, irregular or no physical activity.
- **Hypertension**: high blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.
- **Lethargy**: a lack of energy and a constant feeling of tiredness.
- **Obesity**: having a very high proportion of body fat, usually defined as a BMI of 30 or more.
- **Overweight**: being heavier than the standard weight for one's height, which may be due to muscle rather than fat.
- **Somatotype**: a way of classifying a person's body type according to its shape and composition.
- **Endomorph**: a pear-shaped body type with wide hips, narrow shoulders and a high proportion of fat.
- **Mesomorph**: a muscular, wedge-shaped body type with broad shoulders, narrow hips and little fat.
- **Ectomorph**: a tall, thin body type with narrow shoulders and hips and little muscle or fat.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely**: sedentary lifestyle = little, irregular or no physical activity; obesity = very high body fat (BMI 30+).
2. **Consequences**: use the spec list (weight gain/obesity, heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, poor sleep, poor self-esteem, lethargy) and explain *why* inactivity causes each.
3. **Obesity and performance**: name the component limited (stamina, flexibility, agility, speed/power) and the reason (extra mass, restricted joints).
4. **Obesity and health**: sort into physical, mental and social.
5. **Somatotype questions**: type → features → link to the demands of the sport or **position**.
6. **In evaluate questions**, weigh the effects and reach a judgement (e.g. obesity limits most activities, though mass can help in a few).
:::

::: example Define
Define a sedentary lifestyle. (1)

A way of living in which a person takes little, irregular or no physical activity (1).
:::

::: example Consequences
Explain two consequences of a sedentary lifestyle. (4)

Weight gain/obesity (1): with little activity, fewer calories are used than are eaten, so the extra energy is stored as fat (1). Hypertension (1): lack of exercise and excess weight lead to narrower, less elastic arteries, which raises blood pressure and increases the risk of heart disease (1).
:::

::: example Identify and justify a somatotype
Identify the most suitable somatotype for a high jumper. Justify your choice. (3)

Ectomorph (1). A tall frame with long legs gives a high centre of mass at take-off and long levers to help clear the bar (1). Low body fat and muscle mass mean a light body that is easier to lift over the bar (1).
:::

::: example Obesity and performance
Explain how obesity may limit the performance of a badminton player. (3)

Excess body mass makes it harder to accelerate, stop and change direction, so agility around the court is reduced (1). The heart and lungs must work harder to move the extra mass, so cardiovascular endurance is limited and the player tires in long rallies (1). Fat around the joints restricts flexibility, making it harder to lunge and stretch for a drop shot (1).
:::

::: example Extended — evaluate obesity
Evaluate the effects of obesity on a person's performance in physical activity and on their health. (9)

Obesity means having a very high proportion of body fat. For performance, the extra mass means the heart and lungs must work harder, so stamina and cardiovascular endurance are limited — a person playing five-a-side would tire quickly and struggle to keep up. More mass to move also reduces speed and power, and makes accelerating and changing direction difficult, so agility suffers, while fat around the joints restricts flexibility. Strength is least affected, and in a few activities, such as sumo wrestling or scrummaging, mass can even help stability, but for most activities obesity is a serious limitation. For health, obesity raises the risk of physical conditions — heart disease and heart attacks, type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol and some cancers. It also affects mental health, causing loss of confidence and sometimes depression, and social health, as embarrassment or low mobility can lead to an inability to socialise or even to leave home. These effects reinforce one another: poor performance and low confidence discourage activity, which worsens the obesity. Overall, although mass may help in a handful of specialised roles, obesity damages performance in most activities and harms physical, mental and social health, so its overall effect is strongly negative. (Level 3.)
:::
