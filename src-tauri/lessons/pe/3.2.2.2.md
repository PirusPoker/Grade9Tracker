## What it is

Modern elite sport is a business. **Commercialisation** is the **management and promotion of sport (or anything) for profit** — turning sport into a product that can be sold. It rests on a three-way relationship between **sport, sponsorship and the media**, and it has been accelerated by **technology**. For each, the exam asks about **positive and negative impacts** on five groups: the **performer**, the **sport**, the **official**, the **audience/spectator**, and the **sponsor/company** — and every impact needs a **justification** (why it is good or bad).

### The golden triangle — sport, sponsorship and the media

The three depend on each other, which is why the relationship is called the **golden triangle**:

- **The media need sport**: live sport attracts huge, loyal audiences, so broadcasters pay large sums for **broadcasting rights** to fill schedules and attract subscribers and advertisers.
- **Sport needs the media**: TV money funds the sport; coverage raises its **profile**, attracts new participants and fans, and makes it attractive to sponsors.
- **Sponsors need the media**: a sponsor pays because its **brand is seen** by the audience the media deliver — the bigger the TV audience, the more a sponsorship is worth.
- **Sport needs sponsors**: sponsorship money pays for events, facilities, players' wages and grassroots development.
- **The media benefit from sponsors** through advertising revenue, and sponsors' money makes events bigger and more watchable.

So money flows round the triangle: a sport that is televised attracts sponsors; sponsors' money makes the sport more spectacular; a more spectacular sport attracts more viewers and more media money. A sport with little coverage struggles to find sponsors — and the reverse holds too.

### Sponsorship

**Sponsorship** is when a company **provides money, equipment, clothing or facilities** to a performer, team or event **in return for** publicity, advertising and an association with success — a **commercial arrangement** benefiting both.

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Financial** | a cash payment to an athlete; a company paying a club to have its logo on the shirt; a title sponsor paying to name a league or event |
| **Clothing and equipment, including footwear** | a sportswear brand supplying boots, rackets or kit to a player who wears its logo |
| **Facilities** | a company paying to have a stadium named after it; funding a new training centre |

### The media

**The media** are the means of **communicating information to large numbers of people**.

| Type | How it covers sport |
|---|---|
| **Television** | live broadcasts, highlights, analysis; free-to-air and subscription (pay-per-view) channels |
| **Radio** | live commentary and phone-ins, available in the car or at work |
| **The press** | newspapers and magazines: match reports, interviews, opinion, gossip |
| **The internet** | websites, streaming, live scores and statistics on demand |
| **Social media** | performers, clubs and fans communicate directly; instant clips and comment |

### Impacts of sponsorship and the media

| Group | Positive impacts (and why) | Negative impacts (and why) |
|---|---|---|
| **Performer** | **income** from sponsors lets them train full-time; free **equipment and kit** of high quality; **fame** and a higher profile; media coverage makes them **role models** and opens further deals | may be **dropped** if performance or behaviour slips, causing financial insecurity; sponsor may **dictate** appearances, kit or schedule; **pressure** to win; **invasion of privacy** and media criticism; social media abuse |
| **Sport** | **money** for events, facilities and grassroots; higher **profile** attracts participants and fans; coverage keeps it popular | the sport may **change rules or timings** to suit TV (e.g. kick-off times, breaks for adverts); **over-reliance** on a sponsor; minority sports and women's sport get **little coverage**, so the gap widens; unsuitable sponsors (e.g. gambling, alcohol, fast food) may damage the sport's image |
| **Official** | officials can become **full-time professionals**, raising standards; **replays** help them review and learn; profile raised | **mistakes are replayed** and criticised in front of millions; **pressure** and abuse, including on social media; decisions scrutinised by pundits |
| **Audience / spectator** | can watch **live sport at home** and in more places; expert **analysis** and replays improve understanding; better facilities and spectacle at events | **pay-per-view** or subscriptions make it **expensive**; kick-off times set for TV can be **inconvenient** for fans at the ground; more adverts and breaks; ticket prices may rise |
| **Sponsor / company** | **publicity** and brand exposure to huge audiences; **increased sales**; association with a winning, healthy image improves the company's reputation | **bad publicity** if the performer or team behaves badly or fails (e.g. a drugs ban), damaging the brand; money wasted if the performer is injured or the event gets little coverage |

### Technology

**Technology** in sport includes **officiating aids** (ball-tracking systems, video referees, goal-line technology), **performance analysis** (video, GPS vests, heart-rate monitors), **equipment** (lighter rackets, faster swimsuits, carbon-fibre bikes) and **broadcast technology**. The spec names examples such as Hawk-Eye and the Television Match Official to make you aware of them, but **questions are about technology in general** — answer generically.

| Group | Positive impacts | Negative impacts |
|---|---|---|
| **Performer** | **fairer decisions**, so results reflect performance; **analysis** (GPS, video, heart-rate data) improves training and technique and reduces injury; better **equipment** improves performance; improved medical treatment speeds recovery | **expensive** — not all performers can afford the latest equipment, creating inequality; over-reliance on data; stoppages for reviews break concentration and momentum |
| **Sport** | fairer, more **accurate** outcomes protect the sport's **credibility**; review moments add **drama** that attracts viewers | **expensive** to install, so not available at lower levels (the game differs between levels); **stoppages** slow the game; disputes about how it is used |
| **Official** | helps them make **correct decisions**; reduces pressure and **criticism** for close calls; reviews protect them from blame | can **undermine** their authority and confidence — players and fans may question every call; officials may become reliant on it; errors are still exposed on replay |
| **Audience / spectator** | **replays** and graphics help understanding; decisions shown on big screens add **excitement**; fairer results; can watch on phones and online | **delays** while decisions are reviewed interrupt the flow; the spontaneous moment of celebration can be lost; some fans feel it removes debate |
| **Sponsor / company** | companies can **sponsor the technology** (a branded replay or review screen) for exposure; fairer, more exciting sport attracts bigger audiences for their brand | if the technology fails or causes controversy, the sponsor linked to it may suffer negative publicity; high cost of investing in new equipment |

::: terms
- **Commercialisation**: the management and promotion of sport, or anything, for profit.
- **Golden triangle**: the interdependent relationship between sport, sponsorship and the media.
- **Sponsorship**: a company providing money, equipment, clothing or facilities in return for publicity and advertising.
- **The media**: the ways of communicating information to large numbers of people, such as TV, radio, the press and the internet.
- **Broadcasting rights**: the right, bought by a media company, to show a sporting event.
- **Social media**: websites and apps that let users create and share content and communicate directly.
- **Pay-per-view**: a system in which viewers pay a fee to watch a particular event or channel.
- **Technology in sport**: equipment and systems used to improve performance, analysis, officiating or broadcasting.
- **Role model**: a high-profile performer whom others, especially young people, look up to and copy.
:::

::: method
1. **Check the group** the question names (performer, sport, official, spectator, sponsor) and whether it asks about sponsorship, the media or technology.
2. **State the impact, then justify it** — say *why* it is positive or negative for that group ("…so the performer can train full-time").
3. **Keep technology generic** — "technology gives officials replays" rather than a long account of one system.
4. **For the golden triangle**, show two-way links: each of sport, sponsorship and the media gives something to and gets something from the others.
5. **For evaluate questions**, weigh positives against negatives for the named group or sport and reach a judgement.
:::

Define commercialisation. (1)

::: example Negative impact on a performer
Explain one negative impact of sponsorship on a performer. (2)

A sponsor may withdraw its funding if the performer loses form, gets injured or behaves badly (1), leaving the performer without the income they need to train full-time and creating financial insecurity and pressure to perform (1).
:::

::: example The golden triangle
Explain the relationship between sport, sponsorship and the media, known as the golden triangle. (4)

The relationship is interdependent — known as the golden triangle (1). The media pay sport for broadcasting rights because live sport attracts large audiences (1). Sponsors pay the sport to have their brand shown, but only because the media carry it to those audiences (1). The money from media and sponsors funds the sport, making it more spectacular, which attracts more viewers and so more media and sponsorship money (1).
:::

::: example Technology and spectators
Evaluate the impact of technology on spectators. (4)

Replays on big screens and TV let spectators see exactly what happened and understand decisions (1), and review moments build suspense and excitement (1). However, reviews can cause long delays that break the flow of the game (1), and fans may wait to celebrate a goal in case it is overturned, losing some of the spontaneous atmosphere (1). Overall, most spectators gain from fairer, better-explained decisions, provided reviews are quick.
:::

::: example Extended — evaluate commercialisation
Evaluate the impact of commercialisation on a sport such as professional tennis. (9)

Commercialisation is the management and promotion of sport for profit, based on the golden triangle of sport, sponsorship and the media. For the sport, broadcasting deals and sponsors bring money that funds tournaments, prize money, facilities and junior programmes, and wide TV and internet coverage raises its profile so more people take up tennis. For performers, clothing and equipment deals and prize money let top players train full-time with the best support, and media exposure makes them role models. Spectators can watch matches live on TV and online with replays and expert analysis. However, the benefits are uneven: most sponsorship goes to a handful of star players, while lower-ranked players struggle to cover travel costs; the media favour the biggest names and events, so smaller tournaments and some players get little coverage. Performers face pressure to satisfy sponsors, intrusive media attention and social-media abuse after defeats. Scheduling may suit TV audiences rather than players, and subscription channels make some events expensive to watch. Sponsors gain publicity but risk their image if a player is involved in a scandal. On balance, commercialisation has made professional tennis richer and more visible and has benefited elite performers and spectators; its main costs are inequality between the top and the rest and the pressure it places on players. (Level 3: detailed knowledge, applied to tennis across several groups, with a justified conclusion.)
:::
