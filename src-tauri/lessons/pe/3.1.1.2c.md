## What it is

Air does not get sucked in by the lungs themselves. Breathing works by **changing the volume of the chest (thoracic) cavity**, which changes the **air pressure** inside the lungs. Air always flows **from higher pressure to lower pressure**, so making the chest bigger draws air in and making it smaller pushes air out. The muscles that do this are the **intercostals** (between the ribs) and the **diaphragm** (a dome-shaped sheet of muscle under the lungs), with extra muscles joining in during exercise.

### Mechanics of breathing at rest

| | **Inhaling (inspiration) at rest** | **Exhaling (expiration) at rest** |
|---|---|---|
| Intercostals | **contract** | **relax** |
| Rib cage | moves **up and out** | moves **down and in** |
| Diaphragm | **contracts and flattens** (moves down) | **relaxes and returns to its dome shape** (moves up) |
| Chest cavity volume | **increases** | **decreases** |
| Air pressure in the lungs | **decreases**, lower than the air outside | **increases**, higher than the air outside |
| Air flow | air rushes **in** | air is pushed **out** |

At rest, breathing out is largely **passive**: the muscles simply relax and the lungs recoil.

### Mechanics of breathing during exercise

During exercise the muscles need more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide, so breathing becomes **deeper and faster**.
- **Inhaling during exercise**: as well as the intercostals and diaphragm, the **pectorals** and the **sternocleidomastoid** (a muscle in the neck) contract. They lift the ribs and sternum **higher and further**, so the chest expands more and more air is drawn in.
- **Exhaling during exercise**: breathing out becomes **active**. The **abdominals** contract to pull the rib cage **down more quickly** and push the diaphragm up, so air is **forced out faster**.

The point to make in any answer: bigger changes in chest volume give bigger changes in pressure, so more air moves per breath, and the extra muscles make each breath quicker.

### Lung volumes and the spirometer trace

A **spirometer** records breathing as a trace: a wavy line where each rise is a breath in and each fall is a breath out. Four volumes are named on it:

| Volume | Definition | Rest → exercise |
|---|---|---|
| **Tidal volume (TV)** | the amount of air breathed in or out in a **normal breath** | **increases** |
| **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** | the **extra** air that can be forcibly breathed **in** after a normal breath in | **decreases** |
| **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** | the **extra** air that can be forcibly breathed **out** after a normal breath out | **decreases** (slightly) |
| **Residual volume (RV)** | the air that **remains in the lungs** after a maximal breath out | **stays the same** |

Why: during exercise each breath is much deeper, so tidal volume "eats into" both reserve volumes. The top of each breath reaches nearer the maximum you can breathe in (IRV falls), and the bottom of each breath goes further towards the maximum you can breathe out (ERV falls). Residual volume cannot be breathed out at all; it keeps the alveoli open. It does not change.

**Reading a trace.** On a trace, the small regular waves at rest show tidal volume. One large peak marks the deepest possible breath in, and one deep trough the deepest possible breath out. The gap between the top of a normal wave and the top of the big peak is the IRV. The gap between the bottom of a normal wave and the bottom of the big trough is the ERV. Residual volume sits below the lowest point and is never shown as part of the wave.

Illustrative values for one performer:

| | TV | IRV | ERV | RV | Breaths per minute |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| At rest | 0.5 l | 3.0 l | 1.2 l | 1.2 l | about 12 |
| During exercise | 2.0 l | 1.8 l | 0.9 l | 1.2 l | over 30 |

**Continuing a trace from rest into exercise.** When exercise starts, the waves become **taller** (a bigger tidal volume, so higher peaks and lower troughs) and **closer together** (a faster breathing rate, so more breaths in the same time). The residual volume line stays where it was. If the exam asks you to "continue the trace", that is what you draw; in this app, describe it in those words.

Minute ventilation, the air breathed per minute, is tidal volume × breaths per minute. In the table it rises from 0.5 × 12 = 6 l/min to at least 2.0 × 30 = 60 l/min. That idea helps explain why more oxygen reaches the alveoli.

::: terms
- **Intercostals**: muscles between the ribs that contract to lift the rib cage up and out when breathing in.
- **Diaphragm**: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle below the lungs that contracts and flattens during inhalation.
- **Sternocleidomastoid**: a neck muscle that helps lift the sternum and ribs higher during inhalation in exercise.
- **Thoracic cavity**: the chest cavity containing the lungs, whose volume changes to cause breathing.
- **Spirometer**: a device that measures and records the volumes of air breathed in and out as a trace.
- **Tidal volume**: the amount of air breathed in or out during a normal breath.
- **Inspiratory reserve volume**: the extra volume of air that can be forcibly breathed in after a normal inhalation.
- **Expiratory reserve volume**: the extra volume of air that can be forcibly breathed out after a normal exhalation.
- **Residual volume**: the volume of air left in the lungs after a maximal exhalation, which cannot be breathed out.
:::

::: method
1. **Mechanics questions**: follow the chain in order: muscle action, then rib cage/diaphragm movement, then chest volume, then pressure in the lungs, then air flow. Each link is usually a mark.
2. **During exercise**: add the extra muscles and what they do: pectorals and sternocleidomastoid lift the ribs further (more air in); abdominals pull the ribs down faster (air forced out quicker).
3. **Define a volume**: use "normal breath" for tidal volume, "extra / forcibly after a normal breath" for the reserves, and "left after a maximal breath out" for residual.
4. **Change with exercise**: TV up; IRV and ERV down; RV unchanged. Give the reason: deeper breaths use up the reserves.
5. **Describe or continue a trace**: waves taller (deeper breaths) and closer together (faster rate); RV level unchanged. Quote values if a table is given.
6. **Extended answers**: knowledge (AO1), applied to the named activity (AO2), then the effect of more air per minute on oxygen supply, carbon dioxide removal and performance (AO3).
:::

::: example Role of the diaphragm
Explain the role of the diaphragm during inhalation at rest. (2)

The diaphragm contracts and flattens (1), which increases the volume of the chest cavity so the pressure in the lungs falls and air flows in (1).
:::

::: example Define and apply ERV
Define expiratory reserve volume and state what happens to it during exercise. (2)

The extra amount of air that can be forcibly breathed out after a normal breath out (1). It decreases during exercise (1).
:::

::: example Exhaling during exercise
Explain how exhalation changes when a rower increases her work rate. (3)

At rest exhalation is passive, with the intercostals and diaphragm relaxing (1). During hard exercise the abdominals contract (1), pulling the rib cage down more quickly so air is forced out faster, allowing the next breath to come sooner (1).
:::

::: example Describe a trace change
A spirometer trace shows a performer breathing at rest and then starting to jog. Describe how the trace would change at the start of the jog. (3)

The waves become taller, showing a larger tidal volume (1). The waves become closer together, showing a faster breathing rate (1). The gap to the maximum breath in and out gets smaller, showing that IRV and ERV decrease (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — extended
Analyse how the mechanics of breathing change to meet the demands of a 5,000 m runner. (6)

At rest, inhalation uses the intercostals and diaphragm, and exhalation is passive (AO1). Once the race starts, the pectorals and sternocleidomastoid also contract, lifting the ribs higher so the chest expands more; tidal volume rises from about half a litre towards two litres (AO2). As a result more air, and so more oxygen, reaches the alveoli with each breath, and more oxygen diffuses into the blood for aerobic energy, so the runner can keep a fast pace for a race lasting well over ten minutes (AO3). The abdominals make exhalation active, forcing air out faster, so breathing frequency rises as well as depth (AO2). This removes carbon dioxide quickly, delaying fatigue and breathlessness in the last laps, when the runner may need to kick for the finish (AO3). Because both depth and rate increase, the air breathed per minute rises many times over, which is what allows the runner to keep going (AO3).

Level 3 (5–6): the muscles and chain are accurate, applied to the runner, and linked to oxygen supply and pace.
:::

::: watch
- **ERV is not residual volume.** ERV is the extra you can force out after a normal breath out; RV is what is left after the maximal breath out. In June 2025 this confusion cost many students both marks.
- **TV up, IRV down, ERV down, RV unchanged.** "Everything increases" scores nothing.
- Mechanics answers need **pressure**: volume up, so pressure down, so air in. Missing the pressure link drops a mark.
- The **diaphragm contracts and flattens** to breathe in. Students often say it "moves up".
- Name the **exercise muscles** precisely: pectorals and sternocleidomastoid to breathe in, abdominals to breathe out.
- A trace description needs **both** changes: taller waves (depth) and closer waves (rate).
:::
