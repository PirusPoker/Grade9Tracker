## What it is

Fitness is not one thing. It is made up of separate **components of fitness**, and every sport, and every position within a sport, needs a different mix of them. The exam asks you to **define** each component precisely, give a **sporting example that shows it in action**, and **justify** why a performer does or does not need it.

### The components and their definitions

| Component | Definition (learn the key words) | Example that shows it in action |
|---|---|---|
| **Agility** | the ability to **move and change direction quickly** (at speed) while **maintaining control** | a rugby winger side-stepping past a defender; dodging a marker in netball |
| **Balance** | maintaining the **centre of mass over the base of support** | a gymnast holding a handstand on the beam (static); a surfer staying upright on a moving wave (dynamic) |
| **Cardiovascular endurance** (aerobic power) | the ability of the **heart and lungs** to **supply oxygen to the working muscles** so the whole body can keep exercising for a long time | a marathon runner keeping a steady pace for 42 km |
| **Coordination** | the ability to use **two or more body parts together** smoothly and efficiently | a tennis player tossing the ball with one hand and striking it with the racket in the other |
| **Flexibility** | the **range of movement possible at a joint** | a hurdler lifting the lead leg high over the hurdle; a goalkeeper stretching to tip a shot round the post |
| **Muscular endurance** | the ability of a **muscle or muscle group** to undergo **repeated contractions** without tiring (avoiding fatigue) | a rower's legs and arms pulling stroke after stroke through a 2,000 m race |
| **Power / explosive strength** (anaerobic power) | the product of **strength × speed**; exerting a large force in a short time | a long jumper driving off the take-off board; a basketball player jumping for a rebound |
| **Reaction time** | the **time taken to initiate a response to a stimulus** | a sprinter pushing off the blocks after the gun; a goalkeeper diving for a close-range header |
| **Strength** | the ability to **overcome a resistance** | a rugby prop driving forward in the scrum |
| **Speed** | the maximum rate at which a person can move a distance or perform a movement; **distance ÷ time** | a 100 m sprinter; a footballer running onto a through ball |

### The four types of strength

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximal strength** | the greatest force a muscle can produce in a **single maximal contraction** | lifting the heaviest possible weight once in a powerlifting dead lift |
| **Static strength** | force applied **without movement**. The muscle stays the same length (isometric) | holding a weight above the head after the lift; holding a crucifix on the rings; a rugby scrum at a standstill |
| **Dynamic strength** | force applied **with movement**, often repeatedly or over a period of time | a rower pulling each stroke; a swimmer's arms pulling through the water |
| **Explosive strength** | force applied in **one short, fast burst**. This is the same as **power** | a shot putter releasing the shot; a sprint start |

**Read the stem carefully.** If a weightlifter is described **holding** the bar above the head, that is **static** strength. The lift from the floor to that position used maximal or explosive strength. Examiners report that many students lose all three marks on this kind of question by answering "maximal".

### Linking components to activities

You must be able to **justify** why a component **is or is not** needed. A justification has three parts: the component, **what the performer does** with it, and **how it helps performance** (or why it does not matter).

- **Marathon runner:** cardiovascular endurance and muscular endurance are vital. Reaction time barely matters, because a few hundredths of a second at the start of a two-hour race make no difference. Power and flexibility are needed much less.
- **100 m sprinter:** reaction time (the start), power (drive from the blocks), speed. Cardiovascular endurance matters little, because the race lasts about 10–12 seconds and is anaerobic.
- **Gymnast:** balance (beam, handstands), flexibility (splits, bridges), power (vaults and tumbles), coordination, static strength (holds on the rings).
- **Football goalkeeper:** reaction time and power (diving saves), agility, flexibility (stretching to reach the ball). Cardiovascular endurance is less important than for a midfielder, who may cover 10 km or more in a match.
- **Netball centre:** agility (dodging), cardiovascular endurance (the centre can go anywhere except the shooting circles), speed, coordination (catch-and-pass).
- **Archer:** static strength (holding the drawn bow), balance, coordination. Speed and cardiovascular endurance matter little.
- **Rugby prop:** strength (scrums), power (driving into rucks), muscular endurance (repeated scrums). Flexibility and agility matter less than for a winger.

Different **positions in the same sport** need different mixes. That is why a 9-mark question often names a position rather than just a sport.

::: terms
- **Agility**: the ability to move and change direction quickly while keeping control of the body.
- **Balance**: maintaining the centre of mass over the base of support, when still (static) or moving (dynamic).
- **Cardiovascular endurance**: the ability of the heart and lungs to supply oxygen to the working muscles so the whole body can exercise for long periods.
- **Coordination**: the ability to use two or more body parts together smoothly and efficiently.
- **Flexibility**: the range of movement possible at a joint.
- **Muscular endurance**: the ability of a muscle or muscle group to undergo repeated contractions while avoiding fatigue.
- **Power**: strength multiplied by speed; also called explosive strength or anaerobic power.
- **Reaction time**: the time taken to initiate (begin) a response to a stimulus.
- **Speed**: the maximum rate at which a person can move over a distance or perform a movement (distance divided by time).
- **Maximal strength**: the largest force possible in a single maximal voluntary contraction.
- **Static strength**: force exerted without any change in muscle length, holding a position (isometric).
- **Dynamic strength**: force exerted while the muscles are moving, often repeatedly over a period of time.
:::

::: method
1. **Define with the key words.** Power = strength × speed; agility = change direction quickly with control; reaction time = time to initiate a response to a stimulus. Never define a term using the term itself.
2. **Give an example that shows the component in action.** Name the moment, not just the sport: "diving to save a penalty", not "a goalkeeper".
3. **For "identify the type of strength"**, read the exact moment described: holding = static; one heaviest lift = maximal; a fast burst = explosive; repeated movement = dynamic.
4. **To justify,** link the component → what the performer does → how it improves performance. Where it helps, say why a component is *not* needed as well.
5. **For 6- and 9-mark questions,** define the components (AO1), apply them to the named performer's actual actions (AO2), then analyse or evaluate how much each one matters to the result, including limits, before giving a conclusion (AO3).
:::

::: example Define and give an example
Define reaction time. Give an example of when it is used in a sporting activity. (2)

The time taken to initiate a response to a stimulus (1).

A sprinter pushing off the blocks as soon as the starting gun fires (1). "A goalkeeper in football" alone would not score. The example must show the stimulus and the response.
:::

::: example Identify the type of strength
A weightlifter is holding a barbell above her head, waiting for the judges' signal. Identify the type of strength being used. Justify your answer. (3)

Static strength (1).

The muscles are contracting but not changing length, so this is an isometric contraction, and no movement is taking place (1).

She has to apply force to hold the weight steady against gravity until the signal. If she cannot hold it still, the lift is not counted (1).
:::

::: example Justify — why a component is not needed
Justify why cardiovascular endurance is less important for a shot putter than for a 10,000 m runner. (3)

A shot put lasts only a second or two and is a single explosive action, so the energy comes anaerobically and the heart and lungs do not need to supply oxygen over time (1).

Between throws the athlete has several minutes of rest (1).

The 10,000 m runner must keep a fast pace for around half an hour, so the heart and lungs must deliver oxygen to the working muscles throughout to avoid fatigue. Better cardiovascular endurance directly means a faster time (1).
:::

::: example Explain — power
Explain why power is important to a volleyball player at the net. (2)

Power is strength × speed, so the player can jump explosively (1).

A higher, faster jump lets her spike the ball down over the block or reach high enough to block an opponent's spike (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — 9-mark
Evaluate the importance of agility, balance and reaction time for a football goalkeeper. (9)

(AO1) Agility is the ability to change direction quickly while keeping control. Balance is keeping the centre of mass over the base of support. Reaction time is the time taken to initiate a response to a stimulus.

(AO2) The goalkeeper uses reaction time when a shot is deflected or a close-range header comes in and he must start his dive immediately. He uses agility to shuffle across the goal and change direction when the ball is switched from one wing to the other, or to come off his line to close down a striker. Balance lets him stay set on the balls of his feet as the shot is struck, and land and recover to his feet quickly after a save.

(AO3) Reaction time is arguably the most important of the three. A goalkeeper faces shots travelling at high speed from close range, so a fraction of a second decides whether he gets a hand to the ball; a slow reaction concedes goals directly. Agility matters a great deal in one-on-ones and when the ball moves quickly across the box, because a keeper who cannot reset his position leaves the far post open. It is still used less often than reaction time, because much of his positioning is done at walking pace. Balance underpins both: a keeper caught off balance cannot push off in either direction, so balance helps reaction time and agility turn into effective saves. However, a keeper can partly make up for weaker balance through good positioning and reading of the game. Overall, reaction time has the most direct effect on saves, agility is highly important in open play, and balance is the foundation that makes the other two effective. So all three are important, in that order of priority.
:::

Define flexibility and give a sporting example. (2)
---
The range of movement possible at a joint (1). Example showing it in action, e.g. a gymnast performing the splits on the beam / a hurdler lifting the lead leg over the hurdle (1).
:::

::: q
Define muscular endurance. (1)
---
The ability of a muscle or muscle group to undergo repeated contractions avoiding fatigue (1).
:::

::: q
State the four types of strength. (4)
---
Maximal (1); static (1); dynamic (1); explosive (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why coordination is important to a badminton player. (2)
---
Coordination is using two or more body parts together efficiently (1); e.g. she must move her feet into position while bringing her racket arm through to hit the shuttle cleanly, so she plays accurate shots / fewer mis-hits (1).
:::

::: q
A rower is in the middle of a 2,000 m race. Identify the type of strength he is using and justify your answer. (3)
---
Dynamic strength (1). His muscles are repeatedly contracting and changing length with each stroke (1) over a sustained period of several minutes, so he must apply force repeatedly without fatigue (1).
:::

::: q
Justify why balance is important to a gymnast performing on the beam. (3)
---
Balance is keeping the centre of mass over the base of support (1); the beam is very narrow, so her base of support is small and she must hold positions such as an arabesque or land leaps without wobbling (1); a wobble or fall loses marks from the judges, so good balance gives a higher score (1).
:::

::: watch
- Learn the **key words** of each definition. **Power = strength × speed**. **Agility** needs "change direction" **and** "quickly/at speed" (plus control). "Being quick" is speed, not agility.
- Reaction time is the time to **initiate** a response, not the time to complete the movement.
- Examples must show the component **in action**. "Dribbling around a defender in basketball" scores; "basketball" does not.
- **Read the stem.** Holding a weight still = **static** strength, whatever happened before.
- In justify questions, say **what the performer does** with the component and **how it helps** the result.
- A 9-mark evaluate needs a judgement about which components matter **most** and why, not just a list of definitions.
:::

::: test
Explain why muscular endurance is important to a 400 m swimmer. (2)
---
Her arm and leg muscles must contract repeatedly, stroke after stroke, for the whole race (1); good muscular endurance lets her keep stroke rate and technique without fatigue, so she can hold her pace to the finish (1).

::: test
Justify why speed and power are important for a rugby winger. (4)
---
Speed (distance ÷ time): lets him outrun defenders along the touchline (1) to score tries/reach kicks first (1). Power (strength × speed): gives an explosive acceleration away from a standing start or a hand-off (1), so he breaks tackles or beats the first defender (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why reaction time is more important to a 100 m sprinter than to a marathon runner. (3)
---
The sprinter's race lasts about 10 seconds and can be won by hundredths of a second (1), so a fast reaction to the gun gives a real advantage at the start (1); a marathon lasts over two hours, so a slow reaction at the start makes no meaningful difference to the result (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one reason why a basketball point guard needs agility. (2)
---
Agility is changing direction quickly with control (1); the point guard must dribble past defenders / change direction to lose a marker, creating space to pass or shoot (1).
:::

::: test
Justify the importance of flexibility and coordination for a trampolinist. (6)
---
AO1 (1 mark): flexibility is the range of movement at a joint; coordination is using two or more body parts together smoothly and efficiently.
AO2 (2 marks): flexibility at the hip lets the trampolinist hold straight or piked shapes (e.g. a pike jump with legs straight and hands to toes); coordination lets her time arm swing and leg drive together on take-off and bring arms and legs in together for somersaults and twists.
AO3 (3 marks): judges reward full, clear shapes, so more flexibility gives a tighter pike and higher marks; poor coordination leads to travel across the bed, loss of height and a lower score, or an unsafe landing; coordination is arguably the more important because every skill in a routine depends on it, while flexibility mainly affects the shape marks; conclusion that both are important.
Levels: 5–6 — accurate definitions, clearly applied to trampolining, with developed justification and a conclusion; 3–4 — knowledge evident, some application, justification present but uneven or one component only developed; 1–2 — limited knowledge, little application or justification; 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::

::: test
Evaluate the importance of cardiovascular endurance, speed and agility for a hockey midfielder. (9)
---
AO1 (2 marks): accurate definitions — cardiovascular endurance: the ability of the heart and lungs to supply oxygen to the working muscles for a sustained period; speed: maximum rate of movement / distance ÷ time; agility: moving and changing direction quickly with control.
AO2 (2 marks): application — the midfielder covers a large distance over a 60-minute match, supporting attack and defence; she sprints to close down an opponent or break forward; she changes direction to dodge a tackle or follow the ball when play switches.
AO3 (5 marks): evaluation — cardiovascular endurance underpins everything: without it she fatigues, her decision making and skill drop late in the game, and she cannot get back to defend; it is arguably the most important for a midfield role, more so than for a goalkeeper. Speed wins races to loose balls and lets her break quickly on the counter-attack, but it is used in short bursts and rolling substitutions reduce the demand; a slow but fit player can still be effective through positioning. Agility is vital for beating opponents in tight midfield space and tackling from different angles; lack of agility means she is easily dodged. However, some of these demands depend on tactics and the level of play. Judgement: all three are needed, with cardiovascular endurance the foundation for a midfielder, and speed and agility deciding key moments.
Levels: 7–9 — accurate, detailed knowledge; application to the midfielder is clear and appropriate; evaluation is thorough with a well-reasoned judgement; correct terminology throughout. 4–6 — knowledge evident but more detailed on some components; some application; evaluation present but undeveloped or one-sided. 1–3 — limited knowledge; little application; little or no evaluation. 0 — nothing worthy of credit.
:::
