## What it is

**Sonnet 29** by Elizabeth Barrett Browning is a love poem with a surprising argument. The speaker thinks about her beloved constantly, so much that her thoughts grow over him like wild vines over a tree until she can hardly see the real man. She tells him to shake them off, to come back in person, because when he is with her she does not need to think of him at all: "I am too near thee". **Being with him is better than thinking about him.** That is the paradox the whole sonnet builds towards.

**Line by line**

| Lines | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | She thinks of him; her thoughts grow round him like vines round a tree, until only leaves are visible | "my thoughts do twine and bud / About thee, as wild vines, about a tree" |
| 5–8 | The turn: she will not accept thoughts instead of him, and calls on him to return | "Yet, O my palm-tree"; "I will not have my thoughts instead of thee"; "Rather, instantly / Renew thy presence" |
| 9–11 | He should shake his branches and let the greenery fall | "Rustle thy boughs and set thy trunk all bare"; "burst, shattered, everywhere!" |
| 12–14 | Why: in his presence she feels deep joy and new life, and no longer needs thought | "I do not think of thee—I am too near thee" |

**Voice and speaker.** A first-person female speaker addresses her beloved directly as "thee" (the intimate, old-fashioned "you"). Because the sonnets were written during Barrett's courtship with Robert Browning, readers usually take the speaker as Barrett herself. The voice is passionate and impatient: exclamations, commands and dashes break up the formal sonnet.

**Key language**
- **The vine metaphor**: her thoughts "twine and bud" round him and "Put out broad leaves", until "there's nought to see / Except the straggling green which hides the wood". Love in absence becomes obsessive and smothering, hiding the man it is about. "straggling" makes the thoughts untidy and out of control.
- **The palm tree**: "O my palm-tree" is an exotic, upright, strong tree, a symbol of victory and of masculine strength. The vine clinging to a tree was a traditional image of a dependent wife (a Bible psalm compares a wife to a fruitful vine), but here the speaker asks the tree to *throw the vine off*.
- **Imperatives**: "Renew thy presence", "Rustle thy boughs", "set thy trunk all bare", "let these bands of greenery... Drop". She commands her lover, unusual in a Victorian woman's voice.
- **Violent verbs**: "burst, shattered, everywhere!" The list of three, the exclamation and the hard sounds show the energy of desire breaking free.
- **"bands of greenery which insphere thee"**: "bands" suggests bonds or chains; "insphere" means enclose completely. Her thoughts have imprisoned him.
- **The senses**: "to see and hear thee / And breathe within thy shadow a new air". Presence is physical and sensory, and "new air" suggests rebirth.
- **The paradox**: "I do not think of thee—I am too near thee". The poem began "I think of thee!" and ends "I do not think of thee", a perfect reversal.

**Form and structure**
- **A Petrarchan sonnet**: 14 lines of iambic pentameter, an **octave** rhyming ABBAABBA and a **sestet** rhyming CDCDCD. The sonnet is the traditional form of love poetry, usually written by men about silent women; here the woman speaks.
- **The volta comes early.** The formal turn should come at line 9, but the argument turns at "Yet" in line 5 and decisively at "Rather, instantly" in line 7. Her impatience breaks the form's timing, just as she wants him to break through the vines.
- **Enjambment and dashes**: sentences run over line endings and are broken by dashes and exclamations, so the strict form strains with feeling.
- **Circular structure with a twist**: the first and last lines both concern thinking "of thee", but the meaning flips.
- **Feminine rhymes** in the sestet ("insphere thee", "hear thee", "near thee") end on an unstressed syllable, giving a soft, lingering close on "thee", the person the poem is about.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Elizabeth Barrett (1806–1861)** was already a famous poet, and largely confined to her room by illness, when Robert Browning wrote to her in 1845 admiring her work. Their courtship was mostly in letters and private visits.
- **Her father forbade his children to marry**, so in 1846 they married in secret and left for Italy; her father never forgave her. A poem demanding the beloved's "presence" was written by a woman whose love had to be hidden.
- **Sonnets from the Portuguese** (1850), the sequence this comes from, records the courtship. The title made them seem like translations, disguising how personal they were.
- **Victorian expectations**: women were expected to be passive and modest in love. A woman ordering her lover to "set thy trunk all bare" was bold; the poem claims an active, desiring voice.

**Themes**: love and desire; absence and presence; thought against experience; female voice and independence; obsession.

::: terms
- **Petrarchan sonnet**: a 14-line poem in an octave (ABBAABBA) and a sestet, traditionally about love.
- **Octave**: the first eight lines of a Petrarchan sonnet, usually setting out a problem.
- **Sestet**: the final six lines of a Petrarchan sonnet, usually answering or resolving the octave.
- **Volta**: the turn in a sonnet's argument, here coming early with "Yet" and "Rather, instantly".
- **Extended metaphor**: a comparison developed across the poem, like thoughts as vines round a tree.
- **Paradox**: an apparent contradiction that reveals a truth, like not thinking of him because she is so near him.
- **Imperative**: a command verb, such as "Renew" and "Rustle".
- **Enjambment**: a sentence running over the end of a line, creating urgency and flow.
:::

::: example The vine metaphor
Explain how lines 1–4 present the speaker's thoughts. (4)

Her thoughts "twine and bud" round him "as wild vines, about a tree", a natural image of growth and clinging (1). They "Put out broad leaves" until there is "nought to see / Except the straggling green", so her thoughts cover him completely (1). "straggling" and "wild" suggest they are untidy and out of control, an obsession (1). The metaphor shows that imagining a lover can hide the real person, the problem the rest of the sonnet solves (1).
:::

::: example The early volta
Explain how the sonnet's structure reflects the speaker's feelings. (4)

It is a Petrarchan sonnet, where the turn would normally come at line 9 (1). Here it comes early: "Yet" at line 5 and "Rather, instantly" at line 7 (1). "instantly" itself shows impatience, so the speaker cannot wait for the form's proper moment (1). Enjambment and dashes keep pushing past line endings, so the strict form strains under her desire (1).
:::

::: example The final line
Explain the effect of the final line. (4)

"I do not think of thee—I am too near thee" reverses the opening "I think of thee!" (1). The paradox resolves the argument: thought was only a substitute, and presence makes it unnecessary (1). The dash creates a pause before the explanation, like a breath of relief (1). The feminine rhyme ends softly on "thee", so the poem finishes on the beloved himself rather than on her thoughts about him (1).
:::
