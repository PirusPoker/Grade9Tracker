## What it is

Section B of Component 2 is one **source-based question on your 19th-century novel**, worth **40 marks**, in about **45 minutes**, closed book. It looks like the Section A question, with one big difference: **context (AO3) is assessed**, and **AO1, AO2 and AO3 carry equal weight**. That means roughly 13 marks each for your response, your analysis of methods and your use of context. This lesson teaches the method on A Christmas Carol; it works the same way for Jekyll and Hyde, Silas Marner, The War of the Worlds, Pride and Prejudice or Jane Eyre.

**What the question looks like**

| Part | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| "You should use the extract below and your knowledge of the whole novel to answer this question." | The extract (about a page) is a starting point; the whole novel must be covered. |
| "Write about how Dickens presents ... at different points in the novel." / "Write about [character] and how he is presented at different points in the novel." / "Write about [character] and how he is important to the novel." | The **focus**, and a reminder to **track change** from beginning to end. |
| Bullets: "refer to the extract and the novel as a whole"; "show your understanding of characters and events in the novel"; "refer to the contexts of the novel" | Three checklists, one for each assessment objective. The third bullet is the one students forget. |
| [40] | No separate accuracy marks in Section B (AO4 is in Section A only), but clear writing still helps your AO1 mark. |

Recent focuses for A Christmas Carol have included how Scrooge's thoughts and feelings about Christmas change, how Scrooge is presented at different points, and sadness and suffering. Expect a **character**, a **theme** or a **change**.

**The bands in plain words (40 marks, bands of 8)**

| Band | Marks | AO1 + AO2 + AO3 together |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 33–40 | A sustained, evaluative argument with an overview; extract and wider novel analysed critically; precise references from across the text; methods explored and evaluated; an assured grasp of how contexts shape the novel and its readers. |
| 4 | 25–32 | Thoughtful, coherent and well referenced; methods discussed with increasing analysis; context clearly and relevantly linked to the text. |
| 3 | 17–24 | Straightforward and relevant; references support the points; some comment on methods; some relevant context. |
| 2 | 9–16 | Some focus; general references, often narrative; basic or general context. |
| 1 | 1–8 | Limited focus and reference; little or no context. |

Because the three objectives are weighted equally, a brilliant analysis with **no context** is held back, and so is a history essay with little analysis. The mark goes to the **combination**.

**"At different points".** Plan from the **beginning, the middle and the end**. For A Christmas Carol that means moments from Stave 1, at least two of Staves 2 to 4, and Stave 5. Show **how** the focus develops, and **why** Dickens structures it that way (gradual change, the circular structure, past-present-future as cause-condition-consequence).

**Using the extract.** Choose two or three details to analyse closely (a phrase, an image, a list, a line of dialogue), say where the extract sits in the novel and what has just happened, then move outwards. Examiners accept extract-first, linear or integrated approaches; what they don't reward is ten minutes of line-by-line commentary followed by a rushed list of other events.

**Integrating context (AO3).** Context is not a paragraph of history; it is the **reason** behind a choice Dickens makes. A useful pattern is:

**Dickens [does X with a method] because [context], so that his readers [feel or realise Y].**

Example: "Dickens gives Scrooge the language of the workhouse debate, 'Are there no prisons?', because after the 1834 Poor Law many respectable Victorians thought harsh workhouses were all the poor deserved, so his middle-class readers hear their own opinions sound cruel in a miser's mouth."

**Kinds of context** you can use (all count for AO3):
- **Social and historical**: the Poor Law of 1834 and workhouses; Malthus and "surplus population"; child labour and the 1843 reports on children in mines and factories; ragged schools; the gap between rich and poor in 1840s London.
- **Biographical**: Dickens's childhood in a blacking factory while his father was in a debtors' prison.
- **Religious**: Christian charity, sin and redemption; Christmas as a sacred festival.
- **Literary**: the ghost story told at Christmas; the novella in staves like a carol; the moral or didactic tale; allegory.
- **Readers then and now**: how a wealthy Victorian reader would respond, and how a modern reader might respond to the same moment (for example, debates about poverty today).

**Context bank: moment and matching context**

| Moment | Context that explains it |
|---|---|
| The charity collectors, Stave 1 | The 1834 Poor Law; workhouses as deterrent; Malthus |
| Marley's chain | Christian ideas of sin and judgement; the ghost story genre |
| The lonely schoolboy, Stave 2 | Dickens's own neglected childhood |
| Fezziwig's party | Dickens's view of good employers in an age of factory and office drudgery |
| The Cratchits' dinner and Tiny Tim | Low wages, child mortality and disease among the poor |
| Ignorance and Want | The 1843 reports on child labour; ragged schools; Dickens's belief in education |
| Old Joe's shop and the grave | Victorian fear of a lonely death and an unmourned funeral |
| Stave 5 generosity | Christian redemption; Dickens's hope that readers would act at Christmas |

**Timing (45 minutes)**

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Underline the focus; read the extract; plan an overview and five moments across the staves, each with one method and one context. |
| 5–15 | Introduction and extract analysis. |
| 15–40 | Four paragraphs across the novel, each joining AO1, AO2 and AO3. |
| 40–45 | Conclusion on Dickens's purpose; check you have touched the beginning and the end. |

::: terms
- **AO3**: understanding the relationship between a text and its contexts, worth a third of the marks in Section B.
- **Bolted-on context**: background facts placed in a separate paragraph without linking them to the text, which earns little credit.
- **Integrated context**: context used inside a point to explain why the writer makes a choice and how readers respond.
- **At different points**: the question's signal to track a character or idea from the beginning, through the middle, to the end.
- **Overview**: an opening statement of your argument about the whole novel in relation to the focus.
- **Readers then and now**: the contexts in which a text is received, such as a wealthy Victorian reader compared with a reader today.
- **Literary context**: the genres and forms a writer works within, such as the Christmas ghost story or the moral tale.
- **Writer's purpose**: what the author wanted readers to think, feel or do, the bridge between methods and context.
- **Linear approach**: working through the novel in order and dealing with the extract where it falls.
:::

::: method
1. **Underline the focus and "at different points"**; write a one-line overview that answers the question for the whole novel.
2. **Plan five moments** from the beginning, middle and end, each with a quotation, a method and a matching piece of context.
3. **Open with the overview**, mentioning Dickens's purpose.
4. **Analyse the extract**: place it, then explore two or three details closely, with context woven in.
5. **Move across the novel**: in each paragraph, point, quotation, method, effect, and context that explains the choice (the "because ... so that" pattern).
6. **Track change** with linking phrases: "By Stave 3...", "In contrast to Stave 1...", "By the end...".
7. **Conclude** with Dickens's purpose for his readers then, and a thought about readers now.
:::

::: example Bolted-on to integrated
Improve this context: "In Victorian times there was a lot of poverty and the Poor Law made people go to workhouses. Dickens did not like this." (4)

Weak because it is general and unlinked (1). Integrated version: "When Scrooge asks whether the workhouses are 'still in operation', Dickens gives him the language of the 1834 Poor Law debate (1), in which harsh workhouses were meant to deter the poor from asking for help (1); placing those views in the mouth of a miser invites his wealthy readers to hear how cruel their own opinions sound (1)."
:::

::: example Extract paragraph at two bands
Read this extract from Stave 2, then compare the two paragraphs.

"A small matter," said the Ghost, "to make these silly folks so full of gratitude."
"Small!" echoed Scrooge.
The Spirit signed to him to listen to the two apprentices, who were pouring out their hearts in praise of Fezziwig: and when he had done so, said,
"Why! Is it not? He has spent but a few pounds of your mortal money: three or four, perhaps. Is that so much that he deserves this praise?"
"It isn't that," said Scrooge, heated by the remark, and speaking unconsciously like his former, not his latter, self. "It isn't that, Spirit. He has the power to render us happy or unhappy; to make our service light or burdensome; a pleasure or a toil. Say that his power lies in words and looks; in things so slight and insignificant that it is impossible to add and count 'em up: what then? The happiness he gives, is quite as great as if it cost a fortune."
He felt the Spirit's glance, and stopped.
"What is the matter?" asked the Ghost.
"Nothing particular," said Scrooge.
"Something, I think?" the Ghost insisted.
"No," said Scrooge, "No. I should like to be able to say a word or two to my clerk just now. That's all."

Explain why paragraph B scores more highly than paragraph A on a question about how Dickens presents generosity. (6)

A: "Fezziwig is generous because he throws a party for his workers. Scrooge says the happiness he gives is as great as if it cost a fortune. This shows Scrooge is changing. In Victorian times bosses were often cruel."

B: "Dickens presents generosity here as something measured in kindness rather than money. The Ghost deliberately provokes Scrooge with the language of accounts, a few pounds 'of your mortal money', and Scrooge answers 'unconsciously like his former' self, as if the spirit has unlocked the young man buried under the miser. The balanced pairs 'light or burdensome; a pleasure or a toil' show that an employer's power over workers is emotional as much as financial. Writing in an age when many clerks and factory hands worked long hours in cold rooms for masters who saw them only as costs, Dickens offers Fezziwig as a model to his middle-class readers: generosity costs little. The short, embarrassed reply about his own clerk shows that the lesson has landed, and prepares for Stave 5, when Bob's salary is raised."

A is accurate but general (1), quotes without analysis (1) and bolts on vague context (1). B makes an argument about the focus (1), analyses specific words and the balanced structure (1), and integrates context with purpose and links forward to Stave 5 (1).
:::

::: example An overview for a character question
Write an opening for: "Write about Scrooge and how he is presented at different points in the novel." (4)

Dickens presents Scrooge first as a cold, isolated miser who speaks the language of profit and the workhouse (1), then gradually reawakens him through memory, empathy and fear across the three spirits' visits (1), until in Stave 5 he becomes a generous member of the community (1). Writing for wealthy readers in the hungry 1840s, Dickens uses Scrooge's redemption to show that anyone can change, and that they should (1).
:::

::: example Readers then and now
Explain how readers then and now might respond differently to Tiny Tim. (4)

A Victorian reader would have known that disease and early death were common among poor children (1), so Tiny Tim's "vacant seat" would feel like a real threat and a call to charity (1). A modern reader may find his goodness sentimental (1), but might still connect his family's struggle with debates about child poverty and healthcare today (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Leaving out context** costs up to a third of the marks; it must be there in every section of the answer.
- **Bolted-on context** (a history paragraph) earns far less than one clause linked to a moment.
- **Vague context** ("Victorians were poor") is weak; name the Poor Law, the workhouses, Malthus, Dickens's childhood, the 1843 reports.
- **Line-by-line extract commentary** leaves no time for the whole novel.
- **Narrative**: telling the plot stave by stave instead of building an argument about the focus.
- **Missing the ending**: "at different points" means Stave 5 too.
- **Film details**: write about the novella, not adaptations.
- **Belle was Scrooge's fiancée**, not his wife.
:::

"Spirit," said Scrooge, with an interest he had never felt before, "tell me if Tiny Tim will live."
"I see a vacant seat," replied the Ghost, "in the poor chimney-corner, and a crutch without an owner, carefully preserved. If these shadows remain unaltered by the Future, the child will die."

Scrooge hung his head to hear his own words quoted by the Spirit, and was overcome with penitence and grief.
"Man," said the Ghost, "if man you be in heart, not adamant, forbear that wicked cant until you have discovered What the surplus is, and Where it is. - Band 5 (33–40): sustained, evaluative argument about the Cratchits' presentation and purpose; extract and novel analysed critically with precise references across the staves; methods evaluated; context integrated throughout.
- Band 4 (25–32): thoughtful, coherent and well referenced; methods analysed with growing confidence; context clearly linked.
