## What it is

**A Christmas Carol** (Charles Dickens, 1843) is the most popular text in Component 2 Section B. You answer **one source-based question worth 40 marks** in about **45 minutes**, closed book. An extract of roughly a page is printed; the question asks about a character or theme **at different points in the novel**. Unlike Section A, **AO1, AO2 and AO3 are equally weighted**, so about a third of your mark comes from **context**: the world Dickens wrote in and why he wrote as he did. (If your school studies Jekyll and Hyde or another Section B novel, delete this topic and keep the method topic that follows.)

**Form.** It is a **novella** divided into five **staves**, the verses of a song, so the book presents itself as a carol to be shared every year. It is also a **Christmas ghost story**, a popular Victorian genre, used for a moral purpose: a wealthy reader enjoys being frightened and comes away persuaded. Dickens called it a "Ghostly little book" in his preface and hoped it would haunt readers' houses pleasantly.

**Structure**
- **Circular**: Stave 5 meets the same people as Stave 1 (Fred, the collector, Bob) with the opposite response, so the reader can measure the change.
- **Past, present, future**: cause (how Scrooge became cold), condition (what his coldness costs others now), consequence (his unmourned death). The change is **gradual**, and each spirit is more frightening than the last.
- **Scrooge's words come back to him**: "surplus population" and "Are there no prisons?" are repeated by the Ghost of Christmas Present, turning Stave 1 into a trap.

**Characters**

| Character | Role and presentation |
|---|---|
| **Ebenezer Scrooge** | A rich, miserly moneylender, described through cold and sharp imagery: "Hard and sharp as flint". He stands for the selfish, wealthy class Dickens wanted to persuade. His redemption shows no one is beyond change. |
| **Jacob Marley** | Scrooge's dead partner; a warning of what Scrooge will become. His chain is a **symbol** of a life spent on money. "Mankind was my business" reverses Scrooge's idea of business. |
| **Fred** | Scrooge's cheerful nephew, the voice of Christmas spirit in Stave 1; keeps inviting Scrooge despite rudeness. Shows forgiveness and family. |
| **Bob Cratchit and family** | Poor but loving; Bob works in a cold office for low wages yet toasts Scrooge on Christmas Day. The family's dinner shows happiness without money and the real cost of poverty. |
| **Tiny Tim** | Disabled, gentle, faithful; his possible death makes poverty personal and drives Scrooge's change. |
| **Fezziwig** | Scrooge's old employer: a kind master whose party costs little but brings great joy. A model of how the rich should treat workers. |
| **Belle** | Scrooge's former **fiancée** (not his wife), who releases him because he loves money more. Later seen as a happy mother. |
| **The three spirits** | Teachers rather than terrors: memory, the present world, and the future. The **allegorical** figures of Ignorance and Want belong to the second. |
| **The charity gentlemen** | Show the duty of the rich at Christmas; their return in Stave 5 proves the change. |

**Themes**
- **Poverty and social responsibility**: the rich are responsible for the poor; Ignorance and Want are "Man's" children, everyone's problem.
- **Redemption and change**: a sinner can be transformed through memory, empathy and fear.
- **Christmas spirit and generosity**: Christmas as a time to open "shut-up hearts".
- **Family and community**: the Cratchits and Fred against Scrooge's isolation.
- **Isolation**: Scrooge's loneliness is both his choice and his punishment.
- **Sadness and suffering**: the lonely child, Belle's loss, the Cratchits' grief, the starving children, the death that no one mourns.

**Methods**
- **Narrator's voice**: chatty, humorous, judging, directly addressing the reader ("as I am now to you"), like a storyteller by the fire.
- **Imagery of cold and warmth**: Scrooge's frost and ice against the fires, food and light of Christmas.
- **Lists and accumulation**: "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner!"
- **Contrast**: Scrooge and Fezziwig; Scrooge's office and the Cratchits' home; Stave 1 and Stave 5.
- **Allegory and symbolism**: the chain, Ignorance and Want, the silent pointing hand.
- **Pathetic fallacy**: fog and cold in Stave 1; a bright, clear Christmas morning in Stave 5.

**Context (AO3) that explains Dickens's choices**
- **The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834** made poor relief available mainly in harsh **workhouses**, designed to deter. Scrooge's "Are there no workhouses?" repeats the attitudes of people who supported them.
- **Thomas Malthus** argued that population would outgrow food; some read this as meaning the poor should not be helped. "Surplus population" echoes that language, and Dickens makes the Spirit throw it back.
- **Dickens's own childhood**: when his father was imprisoned for debt, the twelve-year-old Dickens worked in a blacking factory. He knew poverty and child labour from the inside.
- **1843**: reports on children working in mines and factories, and Dickens's visits to schools for the poorest children, made him want to strike a blow for the poor man's child. He chose a story rather than a pamphlet because it would reach more readers and move them.
- **The Victorian Christmas**: the 1840s saw Christmas grow as a family festival; Dickens helped shape the idea of Christmas as a time of charity, feasting and family.
- **Religion**: Christian ideas of charity and redemption run through the book (Tiny Tim's blessing; Fred's "sacred name and origin"), so a Christian reader is reminded of duties they already believe in.

::: terms
- **Novella**: a short novel; Dickens's book is short enough to be read aloud in an evening.
- **Stave**: a verse of a song; the five staves present the book as a carol.
- **Allegory**: a story in which characters stand for ideas, such as the children Ignorance and Want.
- **Redemption**: being saved from sin or wrongdoing; Scrooge's journey across the five staves.
- **Poor Law Amendment Act (1834)**: the law that pushed poor relief into harsh workhouses to discourage people from asking for help.
- **Malthusian**: following Thomas Malthus's argument that population growth outstrips food, used by some to oppose helping the poor.
- **Pathetic fallacy**: weather or setting that reflects mood, such as the fog and cold surrounding Scrooge in Stave 1.
- **Didactic**: written to teach a moral lesson, as Dickens's novella teaches readers to care for the poor.
- **Circular structure**: an ending that returns to the people and situations of the opening, to show change.
- **Intrusive narrator**: a narrator who comments, jokes and speaks directly to the reader.
:::

::: method
1. **Know one key event and two short quotations per stave**, so you can write "at different points" from memory.
2. **Track change**: for any character or theme, ask what it is like at the start, the turning points and the end.
3. **Pair each moment with a method**: imagery, contrast, the narrator, allegory, structure.
4. **Attach a piece of context to the moment it explains**, in the same paragraph: workhouses with the collectors, Malthus with "surplus population", Dickens's childhood with the lonely schoolboy.
5. **Say what Dickens wants his reader to feel or do**: the purpose is the bridge between AO2 and AO3.
6. **Use the circular structure** as evidence of change: Stave 1 against Stave 5.
:::

::: example Context that explains a choice
Explain how context helps a reader understand Scrooge's words to the charity collectors in Stave 1. (6)

Scrooge asks "Are there no prisons?" and "the Union workhouses?" as if they were enough for the poor (1). After the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834, workhouses were deliberately harsh to discourage people from asking for help (1), so his words repeat a real, respectable attitude of the time (1). His idea that the poor should die and "decrease the surplus population" borrows the language of Malthus's theory (1). Dickens makes Scrooge sound like a cold economist so that the reader recognises their own society in him (1), and later has the Ghost of Christmas Present throw the same words back at him over Tiny Tim, making the reader feel their cruelty (1).
:::

::: example Gradual change
Explain how Dickens shows that Scrooge's change in Stave 2 is only a beginning. (4)

Scrooge weeps at the sight of his younger self left alone at school (1) and wishes he had given something to the boy singing a carol at his door (1), the first sign of conscience. After Fezziwig's party he thinks of how he speaks to his own clerk (1). But when he sees Belle he begs the spirit to stop and struggles to extinguish its light, showing he is not yet ready to face the truth (1).
:::

::: example Ignorance and Want
Explain how Dickens uses Ignorance and Want to present his ideas about poverty. (6)

The children are described as "Yellow, meagre, ragged, scowling, wolfish", a list that makes them barely human (1). They are an **allegory**: not real children but figures for society's neglect (1). The Spirit says they are "Man's", so they belong to everyone, not only to their parents (1). He warns that Ignorance carries "Doom" unless the writing is erased, suggesting that uneducated, neglected children threaten the whole of society (1). Dickens had visited schools for the poorest children in 1843 and believed education was the answer (1). By ending Stave 3 with this image and Scrooge's own words, he makes the reader's comfort impossible (1).
:::

::: example Cold and warmth
Explain how Dickens uses imagery of cold in Stave 1. (4)

Scrooge's personality is described as physical cold: "The cold within him froze his old features" and he carries "his own low temperature always about with him" (1). The imagery shows his emotional coldness and isolation (1). Bob's tiny fire and the fog outside reinforce the mood (1), and the contrast with the warmth and food of later staves makes Scrooge's change feel like a thaw (1).
:::

::: example The narrator
Explain one effect of the narrator's voice in the opening of the novel. (3)

The narrator begins chattily, insisting that Marley was dead "to begin with" and joking about the phrase "dead as a door-nail" (1). This creates the feel of a story told aloud by the fire, which suits a Christmas ghost story (1), and draws the reader into a friendly relationship with the storyteller, so we are more willing to accept the moral he wants to teach (1).
:::

::: q
In which year was A Christmas Carol published? (1)
---
In 1843 (it came out in time for Christmas).
:::

::: q
Who is Belle, and why does she leave Scrooge? (2)
---
His former fiancée (1); she releases him because money, "A golden one", has replaced her in his heart (1).
:::

::: q
What does Marley's chain represent? (2)
---
The greed and selfishness of his life, made "link by link" (1); a warning of the heavier chain Scrooge is forging (1).
:::

::: q
Name the three groups Scrooge sees keeping Christmas in remote places with the Ghost of Christmas Present. (3)
---
Miners (1), lighthouse-keepers (1), sailors on a ship (1).
:::

::: q
What does Scrooge do for Bob Cratchit in Stave 5? Give two things. (2)
---
Any two: sends the prize turkey; raises his salary; promises to help his family; becomes a second father to Tiny Tim (1 each).
:::

::: q
Explain what Fezziwig shows Scrooge about being an employer. (3)
---
His party costs little but makes his workers very happy (1); Scrooge says his power lies in words and looks (1); Scrooge then wishes he could speak to his own clerk, showing he sees his own meanness (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Belle was Scrooge's fiancée**, not his wife: examiners report this mistake every year.
- Stick to **the novella, not a film version**: no invented scenes.
- **Context must be in every paragraph**, linked to the moment it explains; a context paragraph at the end is "bolted on".
- **Don't jump from mean to generous**: show the gradual steps across Staves 2, 3 and 4.
- **Quote from across the staves**; answers that only use Stave 1 and Stave 5 miss the turning points.
- Avoid **vague context** ("Victorians were poor"); name the Poor Law, workhouses, Malthus, Dickens's childhood.
:::

::: test
Explain how Dickens's own childhood helps a reader understand the scenes of the young Scrooge. (4)
---
Dickens was sent to work in a blacking factory as a boy when his father was imprisoned for debt (1); he knew loneliness and neglect (1). The image of Scrooge as a solitary child left at school over Christmas draws on that experience (1), making the reader pity the boy and understand that Scrooge's coldness has causes (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how Dickens presents the Cratchit family in Stave 3. (5)
---
Poor: a small goose eked out with apple sauce and potatoes, clothes made over (1). Loving and cheerful: the whole family celebrates the pudding and Bob toasts Scrooge despite everything (1). Tiny Tim's goodness and illness create pathos (1). Dickens uses them to show that the poor are deserving and human, not a "surplus population" (1); his middle-class readers would see a family like their own threatened by low wages (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the circular structure presents Scrooge's change. (4)
---
Stave 5 repeats encounters from Stave 1 (1): the charity collector, now given a large sum with "back-payments" (1); Fred, whose dinner Scrooge now attends (1); Bob, whose salary is raised instead of grudged (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how context helps a reader understand the phrase "decrease the surplus population". (4)
---
It echoes the ideas of Thomas Malthus, that population would outgrow food (1), used by some to argue against helping the poor (1). Dickens puts the phrase in Scrooge's mouth to make the theory sound heartless (1) and has the Ghost repeat it about Tiny Tim, forcing the reader to see a real child behind the statistic (1).
:::

::: test
Why does Dickens make the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come silent? (3)
---
Its silence makes it more frightening, suiting the fear of death (1); Scrooge must interpret the scenes himself, so the lesson becomes his own (1); the future is uncertain and unspoken, which allows him to change it (1).
:::

From Stave 1. Scrooge's nephew has come into the counting-house to wish him a merry Christmas.

"Don't be cross, uncle!" said the nephew.
"What else can I be," returned the uncle, "when I live in such a world of fools as this? Merry Christmas! Out upon merry Christmas! What's Christmas time to you but a time for paying bills without money; a time for finding yourself a year older, but not an hour richer; a time for balancing your books and having every item in 'em through a round dozen of months presented dead against you? If I could work my will," said Scrooge indignantly, "every idiot who goes about with 'Merry Christmas' on his lips, should be boiled with his own pudding, and buried with a stake of holly through his heart. "Uncle!" pleaded the nephew.
"Nephew!" returned the uncle, sternly, "keep Christmas in your own way, and let me keep it in mine."
"Keep it!" repeated Scrooge's nephew. "But you don't keep it."
"Let me leave it alone, then," said Scrooge. "Much good may it do you! Much good it has ever done you!"
"There are many things from which I might have derived good, by which I have not profited, I dare say," returned the nephew: "Christmas among the rest. But I am sure I have always thought of Christmas time, when it has come round – apart from the veneration due to its sacred name and origin, if anything belonging to it can be apart from that – as a good time; a kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time; the only time I know of, in the long calendar of the year, when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely, and to think of people below them as if they really were fellow-passengers to the grave, and not another race of creatures bound on other journeys. And therefore, uncle, though it has never put a scrap of gold or silver in my pocket, I believe that it has done me good, and will do me good; and I say, God bless it!"
The clerk in the Tank involuntarily applauded.

Indicative content (not a checklist):
- Extract: Scrooge sees Christmas only in terms of money (bills, books, "not an hour richer"); violent, comic hyperbole (boiled with his own pudding, a stake of holly); Fred's long, balanced sentence as a counter-argument; "shut-up hearts" and "fellow-passengers to the grave" present Christmas as a time of equality and shared humanity; Fred values it though it brings no "gold or silver"; the clerk's applause.
- Stave 2: Fezziwig's party, joy that costs little; the lonely schoolboy at Christmas; Fan bringing him home.
- Stave 3: the Ghost of Christmas Present surrounded by abundance; the Cratchits' dinner and Tiny Tim's blessing; Christmas kept by miners, lighthouse-keepers and sailors; Fred's party; Ignorance and Want as a warning that Christmas cheer must include the poor.
- Stave 4: a Christmas future without love or mourning for Scrooge; the Cratchits' grief.
- Stave 5: Scrooge's vow kept; the turkey, the donation, Fred's dinner, Bob's raise; "knew how to keep Christmas well".
- Methods: contrast of Scrooge and Fred, the circular structure, imagery of cold and warmth, the narrator's celebratory voice, the carol form.
- Context: the 1840s revival of Christmas as a family festival; Christian ideas of charity and the "sacred name and origin"; Dickens's purpose of persuading wealthy readers at Christmas to help the poor; the Poor Law and attitudes to poverty that Christmas generosity answers.
