## What it is

The second Shakespeare question is an **essay on the play as a whole**, with no extract printed. It is worth **25 marks: 20 for AO1 and AO2** (in five bands of four marks) **plus 5 for AO4**, accuracy in spelling, punctuation, vocabulary and sentence structures. You have **about 40 minutes**, from memory, so the essay is won by planning and by quotations you have learned. As with the extract, **AO3 context is not assessed**: examiners say long passages on James I, witchcraft or Jacobean women "rarely added much".

**The question types.** Eduqas questions are short and plain. Expect one of these forms:

| Form | Recent Macbeth examples | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| **A character "and how Shakespeare presents"** them | Banquo (sample paper) | Moments across the play, what the character shows, how they are built |
| **Change** | "some of the ways Macbeth changes" (2025) | A clear before, turning points, after |
| **A theme or idea** | deception and trickery (2026) | Several characters, several acts, different kinds of the idea |
| **"For which character do you have the most sympathy?"** | 2024 | A choice, a reasoned argument, moments that build or test sympathy |
| **A relationship** | Macbeth and Lady Macbeth | How the balance of power shifts |

**What lifts an essay.** The examiners' reports keep saying the same things:
- **Track across the whole play.** Answers that work through the play chronologically, or follow a character through it, beat answers that treat quotations in isolation. In 2026 the best deception essays ranged from Cawdor in Act 1 to Malcolm's false confession in Act 4 and Macduff's birth in Act 5; weaker ones looked only at the witches.
- **Mine your quotations.** Examiners were "frustrated" by students who had learned good quotations and then not analysed a word of them. Every quotation needs a word, image or method picked out.
- **Name turning points.** Saying *where* a moment sits (the first murder, Fleance's escape, the slaughter of the Macduffs) earns credit for structure under AO2.
- **Argue.** An overview, or thesis, at the start, and a line of argument through the essay, separate Band 5 from Band 3. See characters as constructs Shakespeare uses, not real people.
- **Do not retell.** Narrative answers "struggled to get out of Band 2".
- **Terminology serves analysis.** Showy, obscure terms used wrongly impress no one; a plain term with a sharp effect does.

**A five-minute plan**
1. **Underline the key words** in the question (e.g. "sympathy", "changes", "presents").
2. **Write a one-sentence thesis**: your answer to the question, with a twist ("Macbeth changes from a man horrified by murder into one who feels nothing, but Shakespeare shows that the capacity for violence was there from the start").
3. **Choose four or five moments** from the beginning, middle and end. One per paragraph.
4. **Jot one short quotation and one method** beside each.
5. **Order them** so the argument moves: usually chronological for change, by kind for themes.

**Building a paragraph.** Point in the words of the question → moment placed in the play → short embedded quotation → analysis of a word or method → effect on the audience → link back to the argument. For example: "By Act 3, Shakespeare shows that the crown has brought Macbeth fear rather than peace. Alone after his coronation, he says 'To be thus is nothing, / But to be safely thus'; the stress on 'safely' reveals that kingship now means constant threat, and the audience watches ambition turn into paranoia that will lead him to murder his closest friend."

**Writing about structure.** Structure is about *where* and *in what order*: Macbeth is praised before we meet him; Banquo's murder follows his warning; Lady Macbeth vanishes after Act 3 and reappears broken; the final scenes are short and fast; Macduff, introduced quietly in Act 2, becomes the man who kills Macbeth. "Shakespeare places..." is a good sentence opener.

**Sympathy and "how far" questions.** Choose one character and argue, but show you can see the other side: sympathy that rises and falls is more interesting than sympathy that never moves. For Macbeth: we may pity him for his torment and his wife's pressure, yet his free choice and the murder of children test that sympathy hard; by "Tomorrow, and tomorrow" an audience may feel pity again for the emptiness he has made. Other strong choices: Macduff (the slaughter of his family), Lady Macbeth (her collapse), Banquo (betrayed by his friend).

**AO4: the five easy marks.** Spell the characters' names correctly (Macbeth, Macduff, Banquo, Duncan, Malcolm, Fleance), use capitals for names and titles, punctuate quotations properly, vary sentence openings and lengths, and use precise vocabulary ("tyrannical", "remorse", "manipulates"). Leave two minutes to proofread.

**Timing.** About 5 minutes planning, 32 writing (four or five paragraphs plus a short introduction and conclusion), 3 checking.

::: terms
- **Thesis**: a one-sentence answer to the question that the whole essay argues and develops.
- **Overview**: a sense of the whole play shown at the start and throughout, rewarded in the top band.
- **Turning point**: a moment where the direction of the play or a character changes, such as Fleance's escape.
- **Embedded quotation**: a short quotation built into your own sentence rather than set apart.
- **Tracking**: following a character, relationship or theme through the play in order, showing how it develops.
- **Construct**: a character understood as something the writer has made for a purpose, not a real person.
- **Foreshadowing**: a hint early in the play of something that happens later.
- **AO4**: the 5 marks for accurate spelling and punctuation and a range of vocabulary and sentence structures.
- **Narrative answer**: an essay that retells events instead of analysing how they are presented; it stays in the lower bands.
:::

::: method
1. **Five minutes planning**: underline key words, write a thesis, pick four or five moments from across the play, add a quotation and method to each.
2. **Open with your argument**, not with context or a summary of the plot.
3. **One moment per paragraph**, placed in the play ("In Act 4...").
4. **Embed short quotations** and analyse a word, image or form in each.
5. **Add structure**: where the moment falls, what it foreshadows or echoes, how it turns the play.
6. **Give the audience's response**, and return to the question's key word in every paragraph.
7. **Conclude by answering the question** in a sentence that develops your thesis, then proofread for AO4.
:::

::: example A five-minute plan
Write a plan for: "Write about the witches and how Shakespeare presents their influence in the play." (5)

Thesis: Shakespeare presents the witches as powerful tempters whose words shape what Macbeth imagines, but never as the cause of his crimes, which he chooses (1).
1. Act 1 Scene 1: "Fair is foul" sets a world of inverted values; chant and rhyme (1).
2. Act 1 Scene 3: the prophecies; Macbeth "rapt" while Banquo warns of "instruments of darkness": the same words, two responses (1).
3. Act 1 Scene 7 and Act 2 Scene 1: the witches absent; Lady Macbeth and his own imagination do the work, the dagger as a "dagger of the mind" (1).
4. Act 4 Scene 1 and Act 5: the apparitions' equivocations ("none of woman born", Birnam Wood) give false security; their power is in deception, collapsing when Macduff reveals his birth (1).
:::

::: example A Band 5 introduction
Write an introduction for: "For which character do you have the most sympathy?" (3)

"Although Macbeth commits the play's worst crimes, I have most sympathy for him, because Shakespeare lets the audience inside his mind as no other character, so we watch a man destroy himself knowingly (1). From the soldier praised as 'brave Macbeth' to the king for whom life is 'a tale / Told by an idiot', his tragedy is that he sees exactly what he is losing (1). That sympathy is tested, above all by the murder of Macduff's children, which is why it rises and falls through the play rather than staying fixed (1)."

Why it scores: a clear choice, an overview spanning the play, and a hint of evaluation.
:::

::: example A Band 5 paragraph on guilt
Write a paragraph for: "Write about how Shakespeare presents guilt in the play." (6)

"Shakespeare first presents guilt as something Macbeth cannot control, in the moments after the murder in Act 2 Scene 2 (1). He asks whether 'all great Neptune's ocean' could wash the blood from his hand, a hyperbole that makes his guilt as large as the sea (1). He then answers himself: his hand would rather turn the 'multitudinous seas incarnadine', the long Latinate words swelling the line before the plain 'Making the green one red' lands like a confession (1). Lady Macbeth's reply, 'A little water clears us of this deed', is deliberately small and practical (1), and Shakespeare structures the play so that this line returns against her in Act 5, when she cannot clean a single 'spot' (1). The audience therefore sees guilt as delayed but unavoidable: the character who dismisses it is the one it finally destroys (1)."
:::

::: example Fixing AO4
Correct and improve: "macbeth is very evil he kills duncan and then he kills banquo, and macduffs family, this shows hes a tyrant." (3)

"Macbeth's crimes escalate: he murders Duncan, then Banquo, and finally Macduff's family, whose deaths serve no purpose but terror." Capitals for names and the apostrophe in "Macduff's" fixed (1); the comma splice removed with a colon (1); "very evil" replaced by precise vocabulary ("escalate", "terror") that also adds analysis (1).
:::

::: q
How many marks is the essay worth, and how are they split? (2)
---
25 marks (1): 20 for AO1 and AO2, 5 for AO4 accuracy (1).
:::

::: q
Why is a narrative answer marked low? (2)
---
It retells events without analysing how Shakespeare presents them (1), so it shows little AO2 and no argument; reports say such answers struggle to leave Band 2 (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways to show structure in an essay. (2)
---
Any two: say where a moment comes and why (1); identify turning points (1); show foreshadowing or echoes between acts (1); track change from beginning to end (1). Max 2.
:::

::: q
What should a thesis do? (2)
---
Answer the question directly in a sentence (1) and set up a line of argument the essay develops, ideally with some nuance (1).
:::

::: q
Name four moments from different acts you could use for an essay on deception. (4)
---
Any four from different acts, e.g.: the witches' "Fair is foul" or the first Thane of Cawdor (Act 1) (1); Lady Macbeth's "innocent flower" or the faked shock at the murder (Act 1–2) (1); Macbeth keeping Lady Macbeth "innocent of the knowledge" or hiding behind a smile at the banquet (Act 3) (1); Malcolm's false confession or the apparitions' riddles (Act 4) (1); Birnam Wood and Macduff's birth (Act 5) (1). Max 4.
:::

::: q
How much context should go into the essay? (2)
---
Very little: AO3 is not assessed in Section A (1); a clause that explains an audience reaction is enough, as long paragraphs waste time better spent on analysis (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Plan first.** Students who plan for a few minutes write more focused essays, say the examiners.
- **Range across the play.** Beginning, middle and end, including Acts 4 and 5.
- **Analyse every quotation.** A learned quotation left unanalysed is wasted.
- **Answer the question's key word** (sympathy, change, deception) in every paragraph.
- **No history lessons.** Context is not marked here.
- **Proofread for AO4**: names, capitals, apostrophes, and no abbreviations such as "LM" for Lady Macbeth.
:::

Plan a paragraph on Macduff for an essay on loyalty: give the moment, a quotation and the method you would analyse. (4)
---
Moment placed in the play, e.g. Act 4 Scene 3 when Malcolm tests him (1); quotation, e.g. "O Scotland, Scotland!" or "I must also feel it as a man" (1); method, e.g. repetition, exclamation or the redefinition of "man" (1); effect and link to loyalty, e.g. his despair proves he is loyal to Scotland, not to a king, which convinces Malcolm and the audience (1).

::: test
Explain how you could write about structure in an essay on Lady Macbeth. (4)
---
She dominates Act 1 and the aftermath of the murder (1); after Macbeth shuts her out in Act 3 she disappears from the stage (1); she returns only in Act 5, sleepwalking in prose, then dies offstage (1); Shakespeare's placing of her scenes traces her power rising and collapsing, and the reversal of "A little water" shows it (1).
:::

---
Capitals for her name (1); quotation marks and correct punctuation around "unsex me here" (1); precise vocabulary and a less flat structure, e.g. "When Lady Macbeth demands that the spirits 'unsex me here', she asks to be emptied of the pity her society linked to women" (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why tracking the play chronologically is useful for a question about change. (2)
---
It lets you show a clear before and after with turning points in between (1), which earns credit for structure and keeps the essay from becoming a list of quotations (1).

::: test

---
20 marks AO1 and AO2 equally:
- Band 5 (17–20): a sustained, perceptive argument with an overview; precise references from across the play; perceptive analysis of language, imagery and structure.
- Band 4 (13–16): thoughtful and secure; well-chosen references; methods discussed and increasingly analysed.
- Band 3 (9–12): straightforward and relevant; appropriate references; some comment on methods.
- Band 2 (5–8): some focus; some references, often narrative; simple comments.
- Band 1 (1–4): limited, mainly retelling.

Indicative content:
- Macbeth's conscience before the murder: "If it were done", "Vaulting ambition", the dagger as "a dagger of the mind".
- Immediate guilt: "Macbeth does murder sleep"; "Neptune's ocean"; his inability to say "Amen".
- Guilt made visible: Banquo's ghost at the banquet; "full of scorpions is my mind".
- Guilt that hardens: "in blood / Stepped in so far"; the murder of the Macduffs without hesitation.
- Lady Macbeth's suppressed guilt: "A little water" against the sleepwalking, "Out, damned spot!", her offstage suicide.
- Methods: blood and sleep imagery; hallucination; soliloquy; prose for breakdown; structure of reversal between the two Macbeths.
- Evaluation: guilt destroys one partner through feeling and the other through numbness; neither escapes.
