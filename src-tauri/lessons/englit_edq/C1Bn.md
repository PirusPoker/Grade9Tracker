## What it is

**Remains** by Simon Armitage is a soldier's account of a killing and of what it does to him afterwards. On patrol, he and two others are sent to deal with looters at a bank. One runs off, "probably armed, possibly not", and all three soldiers open fire. The man is torn apart; his body is thrown into a lorry. That should be the "End of story", but it is not: the soldier keeps walking over the bloodstain on patrol, and when he is home on leave the man comes back in flashbacks and dreams that drink and drugs cannot wash away. The poem ends with the dead man's life "in my bloody hands". The killing takes a few stanzas; the **guilt takes the rest**, and that imbalance is the poem's argument about **post-traumatic stress**.

**Stanza by stanza**

| Stanzas | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Sent to stop looters raiding a bank; one runs; the three soldiers all fire | "On another occasion"; "probably armed, possibly not"; "Three of a kind" |
| 3–4 | The shooting in graphic detail; a comrade tosses the man's guts back into his body; the body is carted off | "rips through his life"; "sort of inside out"; "tosses his guts back" |
| 5 | The turn: the story should be over but is not; he walks over the bloodstain week after week; then he is home on leave | "End of story"; "except not really"; "His blood-shadow stays" |
| 6–7 | Flashbacks and dreams; drink and drugs fail; the dead man is lodged in his head | "the drink and the drugs"; "dug in behind enemy lines" |
| 8 (couplet) | The guilt is close and present, the man's life in his hands | "here and now"; "my bloody hands" |

**Voice and speaker.** A soldier speaks in the first person, in a **colloquial, conversational voice**, as if telling the story to a mate or an interviewer: "legs it", "sort of", "I swear", "mates". The casual tone at the start ("On another occasion", as if this were one anecdote among many) becomes horrifying as we realise how much it has cost him. The voice moves from **"we"** (shared action) to **"I"** (sole guilt).

**Key language**
- **"On another occasion"**: an offhand opening, as if this is just one of many incidents. It suggests killing has become routine, and it plunges us into the middle of a conversation.
- **"legs it up the road"**: slang, almost comic; the casual language contrasts with the violence to come.
- **"probably armed, possibly not"**: the key line, repeated later. The doubt is the source of his guilt: he may have killed an unarmed man. The second time it appears it haunts his sleep.
- **"Three of a kind"** **"letting fly"**: card-game language, a poker hand. It shares out responsibility ("all three of us") and treats the shooting like a game.
- **"rips through his life"**: violent, vivid verb "rips"; the bullet goes through a "life", not just a body. "I see" in the present tense shows the memory replaying.
- **"sort of inside out"** and **"tosses his guts back"**: graphic, almost grotesque imagery delivered in casual language. "tosses" makes the body a piece of rubbish; the gap between tone and content is shocking.
- **"carted off"**: the body is dumped like goods, dehumanised.
- **"End of story"** and **"except not really"**: the **volta**. The cliché is undercut at once; the rest of the poem is the story that does not end.
- **"His blood-shadow stays"**: a compound word for the stain on the street, which he walks over "week after week". The man has left a permanent mark, like a ghost.
- **"he bursts again"**: home on leave, a **flashback**. Present tense makes the past happen again.
- **"the drink and the drugs"**: attempts at self-medication fail; he cannot "flush him out".
- **"dug in behind enemy lines"**: military metaphor. The dead man is now the enemy, entrenched inside the soldier's mind, a war he cannot win.
- **"sun-stunned, sand-smothered land"**: alliteration and compound adjectives; the foreign land is far away, but the man is not left there.
- **"his bloody life" in "my bloody hands"**: "bloody" works twice, as literal blood and as a British swear word showing anger and despair. Blood on the hands is an image of guilt (it echoes Lady Macbeth). "here and now" ends the poem in the present, permanently.

**Form and structure**
- **Seven four-line stanzas and a final couplet.** The quatrains are fairly regular but unrhymed; the closing couplet is cut short, like his life, or like the story that has no proper ending.
- **The turn in stanza 5**: the first half is the event, the second half the aftermath. Giving more space to the aftermath shows that the trauma outlasts the act.
- **Enjambment across stanzas** ("I swear // I see"; "I blink // and he") pushes the memory onwards, out of control, as flashbacks are.
- **Shift in tense and place**: from the street abroad to home on leave, and from a past anecdote to the present tense "here and now". The past has become permanent present.
- **Repetition** of "probably armed, possibly not" and of the bloody images shows the memory looping.
- **Pronouns**: "we", "all three of us", "somebody else" spread the blame at first; by the end it is "my head", "my bloody hands". Guilt cannot be shared.
- **Title**: "Remains" means the dead man's body, what remains in the soldier's memory, and the soldier himself, who remains alive and damaged.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Simon Armitage** (born 1963, from West Yorkshire) became **Poet Laureate in 2019**. He often uses everyday, colloquial voices.
- The poem comes from **The Not Dead (2008)**, a collection based on interviews with soldiers who served in recent conflicts, made alongside a television documentary. Remains is based on the testimony of a British soldier who served in **Iraq**; Armitage turns a real account into poetry, keeping its spoken voice.
- **Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)**: flashbacks, nightmares, guilt and the use of drink and drugs to cope are recognised symptoms. The poem shows a mental wound that does not show on the body.
- **Modern warfare**: soldiers often had to make split-second decisions among civilians, where it was unclear who was a threat. "probably armed, possibly not" captures that uncertainty, and the moral weight it leaves.

**Themes**: conflict and its effects; guilt and conscience; memory and trauma; dehumanisation; the individual against the group; the past that will not stay past.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **Disabled** (Owen): a young soldier damaged by war and its aftermath; Owen's wound is physical, Armitage's is psychological.
- **War Photographer** (Duffy): a man haunted at home by a memory from a war zone; witness against participant.
- **Kamikaze** (Garland): the aftermath of conflict for one man; silence and shame against flashbacks and guilt.
- **Decomposition** (Ghose): guilt that comes back to accuse the speaker.
- **Drummer Hodge** (Hardy): a dead body in a foreign land; Hodge is buried "uncoffined", the looter is not left "six-feet-under" but lives in the soldier's mind.

::: terms
- **Colloquial language**: informal, everyday speech, like "legs it" and "sort of".
- **Dramatic monologue**: a poem in the voice of one character speaking, often to a listener.
- **Volta**: a turn in a poem's direction; here "End of story" / "except not really".
- **Enjambment**: a sentence running across a line or stanza break.
- **Flashback**: a sudden, vivid return of a past event in the mind.
- **Metaphor**: a direct comparison, like the dead man "dug in behind enemy lines".
- **Dehumanisation**: treating a person as an object, as in "carted off" and "tosses his guts".
- **Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)**: a condition after trauma, with flashbacks, nightmares and anxiety.
- **Testimony**: a first-hand spoken account, like the soldiers' interviews behind the poem.
:::

::: method
1. **Overview first**: a soldier's account of a killing becomes a study of guilt and trauma that will not end.
2. **Work in two halves**: the event (stanzas 1–4), then the aftermath (5–8), with the turn at "End of story".
3. **Analyse the colloquial voice against the violent imagery**, the repeated doubt, and the blood and war metaphors at the end.
4. **Use structure**: quatrains and the final couplet, the turn, enjambment, tense shift, pronoun shift, the title.
5. **Weave in context**: The Not Dead, a real soldier's testimony from Iraq, PTSD, split-second decisions.
6. **For 7.2**, choose a poem on conflict, guilt, memory or the effects of war and compare throughout.
:::

::: example The colloquial voice
Explain the effect of the colloquial language in the first half of the poem. (4)

Phrases like "legs it", "sort of inside out" and "I swear" make the soldier sound as if he is chatting (1). "On another occasion" suggests killing has become an everyday anecdote (1). The casual tone clashes with graphic content such as "tosses his guts back", which shocks the reader (1). It also sounds like a defence: talking lightly is his way of distancing himself from what he did, which the second half shows has failed (1).
:::

::: example The turn
Explain how the structure of the poem shows the effects of trauma. (4)

"End of story" suggests the event should be finished, but "except not really" immediately undercuts it (1). The second half of the poem is about the aftermath, so the guilt takes up as much space as the killing (1). The setting shifts from the street to home on leave, and the tense to the present, "here and now", showing the past has become permanent (1). Enjambment across stanzas, "I blink // and he", mimics the way flashbacks break in uncontrollably (1).
:::

::: example The final couplet
Explain the effect of "his bloody life" in "my bloody hands". (3)

"bloody" is literal: blood stains his hands, an image of guilt like Lady Macbeth's (1). It is also a swear word, showing the soldier's anger and despair (1). The parallel "his ... my" binds the dead man's life to the soldier's hands for ever, and the short final couplet ends the poem on the guilt (1).
:::
