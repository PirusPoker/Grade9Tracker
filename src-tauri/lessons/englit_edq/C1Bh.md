## What it is

**Decomposition** by Zulfikar Ghose (published 1967) is a short poem about a photograph. The speaker took a picture in Bombay of an old beggar asleep on the pavement. He describes the image closely, remembers how pleased he was with it at the time, and then, in the last lines, feels rebuked by it: he has made **art out of another man's hunger**. The poem criticises the city's indifference, but its sharpest criticism is aimed at the speaker himself.

**Section by section**

| Lines | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1–8 | The photograph: an old man asleep on the pavement, so still that he seems to become part of the stone | "his shadow thrown aside"; "cracks in the stone"; "a fossil man" |
| 9–12 | The background: a crowd distracted by a street entertainer, untroubled by the sleeping man | "quite indifferent"; "this very common sight" |
| 13–16 | **Then**: the speaker's pride in his "composition" and the clever title he gave it | "a good composition"; "glibly called it" |
| 17–20 | **Now**: the man's posture seems to scold him for turning suffering into art | "chides me now"; "art of his hunger" |

**Voice and speaker.** A first-person speaker, an educated outsider with a camera, talks calmly and almost casually ("I have a picture"). The calm is part of the point: for most of the poem he looks at the man as a **subject**, not a person. The confession at the end breaks that detachment. Many readers take the speaker as close to Ghose himself, but you only need to treat him as "the speaker".

**Key language**
- **"his shadow thrown aside"**: the shadow is compared to a blanket he has thrown off. The simile is bitter: the only covering this man has is his own shadow.
- **"cracks in the stone"**: his limbs could be cracks in the pavement, routes for ants and flies. He is turned into **ground**, something insects use and people walk over.
- **"Brain-washed by the sun"**: the heat has emptied him into exhaustion. "Brain-washed" also hints that poverty has stripped him of thought and will.
- **"a fossil man"**: the climax of the stone imagery. A fossil is a dead thing preserved in rock, so he seems already dead, ancient and permanent, part of the city's foundations. This links directly to the title: **decomposition**, the decay of a body.
- **"quite indifferent"** and **"this very common sight"**: the crowd watches a street trickster instead. Poverty is so ordinary that nobody notices it; "common" means both frequent and unremarkable.
- **"a good composition"**: art vocabulary. He judged the picture on how well it was arranged, not on what it showed.
- **"glibly called it"**: "glibly" (smoothly, without thought) is the speaker condemning his younger self. The title he chose, "The Man in the Street", is a **pun**: the phrase usually means an ordinary citizen, but this man literally lives in the street. The joke treats homelessness as wit.
- **"typical ... of India"**: a sweeping generalisation, the outsider's stereotype that reduces a country to one image.
- **"the posture of one weeping"**: the man's head lies as if weeping into a pillow he does not have. The speaker now reads grief in the image he once saw as picturesque.
- **"chides me now"**: the photograph (or the man in it) scolds him. Power shifts: the silent subject now judges the photographer.
- **"art of his hunger"**: the poem ends on "hunger and solitude", the human facts the "composition" ignored. "presumption" names the speaker's arrogance.

**Form and structure**
- **One unbroken stanza of twenty lines.** There is no gap between past and present: the guilt grows straight out of the description, as if he cannot separate them.
- **A turn from then to now.** The first twelve lines describe; "I thought it then" signals the memory of his pride; "chides me now" brings the reversal. The structure mirrors a change of conscience.
- **Mostly unrhymed lines of roughly even length**, with occasional rhyme (the crowd is "quite" indifferent to the common "sight"). The near-regular lines resemble the frame of a photograph: controlled, composed.
- **Enjambment** carries sentences across lines, giving the measured, reflective tone of someone studying a picture.
- **The title's double meaning.** **Decomposition** = decay, the man's body wasting into stone; **de-composition** = the undoing of a "composition", as the speaker takes apart his own artistic view. The poem itself performs that undoing.
- **Irony**: the poem is also a composition about the man. Ghose cannot fully escape the problem he names, and a top answer can notice that.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Zulfikar Ghose** was born in 1935 in **Sialkot**, then part of **British India** (now Pakistan), and moved to **England** as a teenager. He wrote as someone between cultures: of the subcontinent, yet viewing it with an outsider's eye. The speaker's tourist-like judgement ("typical ... of India") fits that position, and his guilt suggests the poet questioning it.
- **Bombay** (now Mumbai) in the decades after independence (1947) had extreme poverty beside wealth; people sleeping on pavements were, as the poem says, a common sight.
- **Photography and ethics**: the camera can record suffering, but it can also turn suffering into an attractive picture for viewers who will never help. The poem asks whether looking is a kind of using.
- **"The man in the street"** was a familiar English phrase for the ordinary voter or citizen, so the pun mixes British idiom with an Indian scene, again the outsider's perspective.

**Themes**: poverty and indifference; guilt and conscience; art and ethics; the observer and the observed; power and powerlessness; change of perspective over time.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **War Photographer** (Duffy): the strongest pair. Both are about photographs of suffering and the guilt of the person behind the camera; Duffy's photographer feels it in the darkroom, Ghose's speaker years later. Both also accuse the indifferent public.
- **Remains** (Armitage): guilt that returns and will not leave; one man killed, one man exploited as an image.
- **Drummer Hodge** (Hardy): a human body absorbed into a landscape; Hodge becomes part of a foreign land, the beggar part of the pavement.
- **The Schoolboy** (Blake): society's treatment of the powerless, and a poet criticising institutions and attitudes.

::: terms
- **Composition**: the arrangement of a picture or artwork; also the act of creating it.
- **Decomposition**: the decay of a dead body or organic matter; in the title also the undoing of a composition.
- **Pun**: a play on words with two meanings, like "The Man in the Street".
- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as", such as the shadow thrown aside like a blanket.
- **Metaphor**: a direct comparison, such as calling the sleeping beggar a fossil.
- **Dehumanisation**: presenting a person as an object or animal, as the stone imagery does.
- **Volta**: a turn in a poem's argument or feeling, here from past pride to present guilt.
- **Enjambment**: a sentence running over the end of a line without punctuation.
- **Irony**: a gap between what is said or expected and what is true.
- **Outsider's gaze**: the view of someone looking at a place or people from outside, often simplifying them.
:::

::: method
1. **Overview first**: a photograph of a sleeping beggar becomes a source of guilt; the poem judges the city's indifference and the speaker's own.
2. **Move through the poem in order**: the stone imagery, the crowd, the "then" of pride, the "now" of rebuke.
3. **Analyse the imagery of stone and decay** and connect it to the title's double meaning.
4. **Use structure**: one stanza, the turn from then to now, the final line ending on hunger and solitude.
5. **Weave in context**: Ghose's position between cultures, poverty in post-independence Bombay, the ethics of photographing suffering.
6. **For 7.2**, choose a poem on guilt, suffering, observation or power, and compare in every paragraph.

::: example The stone imagery
Explain how Ghose presents the beggar through the imagery of stone. (4)

His limbs could be "cracks in the stone", so he blends into the pavement as if he were part of it (1). Insects use him as "routes", which shows how far he has been reduced to an object or surface (1). The metaphor "a fossil man" goes further: a fossil is something dead preserved in rock, so he seems already lifeless and permanent (1). This dehumanising imagery reflects how the city, and at first the speaker, see him, not as a person but as part of the street (1).
:::

::: example The title
Explain the double meaning of the title. (3)

"Decomposition" means decay, which fits the man's body wasting away and turning into stone (1). Read as "de-composition", it means undoing a composition, which is what the speaker does to his own photograph when he stops seeing it as good art (1). The title therefore points to both the man's suffering and the speaker's change of conscience (1).
:::

::: example The turn
Explain how the structure shows the speaker's change of attitude. (4)

The first twelve lines describe the photograph in a detached way (1). "I thought it then" marks a shift to his past pride in "a good composition" (1). "chides me now" brings the reversal: the present-tense guilt overturns his earlier judgement (1). Because the poem is one stanza, the guilt seems to grow out of the description itself, and ending on "hunger and solitude" leaves the man's suffering as the last word (1).
:::

::: example Context woven into analysis
Write two sentences linking "typical ... of India" to context. (2)

"The phrase 'typical ... of India' shows the speaker reducing a whole country to a single image of poverty, the kind of generalisation an outsider makes (1). Ghose, born in British India but living in England, writes from between two cultures, and the poem's guilty ending suggests him questioning that outsider's gaze in himself (1)."
:::
