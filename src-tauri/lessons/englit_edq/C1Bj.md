## What it is

**Blackberry Picking** by Seamus Heaney is a memory of childhood summers on a farm in Northern Ireland. Every late August the children picked blackberries in a frenzy, filling every can and jar they could find, then hoarded them in the cow shed. Every year the berries went mouldy and rotten within days. The adult speaker ends with the lesson he learned, and kept hoping against: **beautiful things do not last**. On the surface it is about fruit; underneath it is about **desire, greed, hope and disappointment**, and the loss of childhood innocence.

**Section by section**

| Part | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| The ripening | After a week of rain and sun the first berry ripens; eating it gives a taste so rich it creates a craving | "a glossy purple clot"; "Like thickened wine"; "summer's blood" |
| The picking | The children set out with milk cans, pea tins and jam-pots, scratched by briars, picking across the fields until the containers are full | "lust for / Picking"; "Like a plate of eyes"; "sticky as Bluebeard's" |
| The rotting | The berries are hoarded in the byre; soon a grey fungus covers them and the juice stinks | "A rat-grey fungus"; "glutting on our cache" |
| The lesson | The child's tears and sense of injustice; the adult's admission that he always knew | "It wasn't fair"; "knew they would not" |

**Voice and speaker.** An adult remembers in the first person, mostly as "we" and "our", so the memory is shared with the other children. One line switches to "You ate that first one", drawing the reader into the experience. At the end the child's voice breaks through ("It wasn't fair"), then the adult's quiet final line looks back at a lifetime of the same hope.

**Key language**
- **"a glossy purple clot"**: the first ripe berry. "Glossy" is attractive, but **"clot"** is a word for blood, an unsettling hint of violence and of something going wrong.
- **"Like thickened wine"**: a sensual simile of richness and intoxication; wine also suggests something adult, perhaps forbidden.
- **"summer's blood was in it"**: the berry holds the life of the season. Blood imagery runs through the first part (clot, blood, stains, "Bluebeard's"), making the picking seem violent and guilty.
- **"lust for / Picking"**: "lust" is a strong, almost sinful word for desire. The enjambment makes the reader wait for the object, as the craving drives them on. Some readers see an adolescent, even sexual, desire beneath it, alongside "flesh" and "sweet".
- **"that hunger"**: the desire is a physical appetite that "Sent us out", as if the children are controlled by it.
- **"Like a plate of eyes"**: the dark berries on top of the full can seem to stare back. It is a strange, slightly sinister image, as if the berries are watching or judging their greed.
- **"sticky as Bluebeard's"**: in the folk tale, Bluebeard murders his wives. The children's juice-stained hands look blood-stained like a murderer's, a comic exaggeration that carries real guilt.
- **"A rat-grey fungus"** and **"glutting on our cache"**: the second part turns to decay. "rat-grey" is dull and disgusting after the rich purple; "glutting" means greedily overeating, so the fungus mirrors the children's own greed. A "cache" is a hidden store, like treasure.
- **"stinking"** and **"turn sour"**: the senses that delighted now repel; sweet becomes sour.
- **"I always felt like crying"** and **"It wasn't fair"**: short, simple, childish sentences after the long sensuous ones. The plain language shows real disappointment.
- **"knew they would not"**: the final line holds two times. Each year he "hoped they'd keep", and yet he **knew** they would rot. Hope and knowledge sit side by side, which is why the line feels so adult.

**Form and structure**
- **Two parts**: a long first section of ripening and picking (anticipation and desire) and a shorter second section of hoarding and rot (disappointment). The imbalance mirrors the experience: weeks of excitement, then a quick collapse.
- **Rhyming couplets**, many of them **half-rhymes** (sun / ripen, sweet / it, byre / fur). The near-misses suggest something slightly wrong, like fruit just starting to turn. Full rhymes stand out: "clot / knot" near the start and "rot / not" at the very end, so the poem's clearest rhyme closes on its bleakest truth.
- **Roughly iambic pentameter**, loose and conversational, like a memory being told.
- **Enjambment and long sentences** in the picking section create momentum and excitement; short, end-stopped sentences in the final lines bring everything to a halt.
- **Cyclical time**: "Each year" shows the whole pattern repeated every summer, which makes the final line a lesson about life rather than one bad week.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Seamus Heaney** (1939–2013) grew up on a farm in **County Derry, Northern Ireland**, the eldest of a large family. Many of his early poems remember rural childhood, farm work and the natural world in physical, sensory detail.
- The poem appeared in his first major collection, **Death of a Naturalist** (1966). That book is full of childhood experiences that end in a shock of disgust or fear, the loss of innocence that comes with growing up. Blackberry Picking fits that pattern exactly.
- Heaney won the **Nobel Prize in Literature in 1995**.
- **Rural life**: the byre, the hayfields and potato-drills, the containers from the kitchen are an authentic farm world. The children's hoarding against waste makes sense in a farming community, yet nature cannot be stored or controlled.
- Readers have found wider meanings: **greed** and its punishment, the passing of **childhood**, the way **adult hopes** (for love, success or permanence) are disappointed. Present these as interpretations, not facts.

**Themes**: childhood and growing up; desire and greed; hope and disappointment; nature and decay; memory; loss of innocence; the passing of time.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud** (Wordsworth): nature and memory. Wordsworth's memory preserves the daffodils forever; Heaney's shows that what he gathered rotted.
- **Origin Story** (Ewing): things that do not last. Ewing's comic book love "doesn't last" but is cherished; Heaney's hoarded berries cannot be kept. Both are about trying to preserve something fragile.
- **The Schoolboy** (Blake): childhood and seasons; Blake also uses fruit and harvest imagery for joy that is lost.
- **Catrin** (Clarke): growing up and the loss of childhood.

::: terms
- **Sensory imagery**: description that appeals to the senses of taste, touch, sight, smell or sound.
- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as", such as "Like thickened wine".
- **Half-rhyme**: words whose sounds nearly but not fully rhyme, like "sun" and "ripen".
- **Rhyming couplet**: a pair of lines that rhyme; the poem is built from couplets.
- **Allusion**: a reference to another story or text, like the folk tale of Bluebeard.
- **Enjambment**: a sentence running across a line break, as in "lust for / Picking".
- **Juxtaposition**: placing contrasting things side by side, like sweet and sour, purple and grey.
- **Loss of innocence**: the move from childhood trust to adult knowledge, often through a shock.
- **Byre**: a cow shed on a farm, where the berries are hoarded.
- **Death of a Naturalist**: Heaney's first major collection (1966), which contains this poem.
:::

::: example The first berry
Explain how Heaney presents the first ripe blackberry. (4)

It is "a glossy purple clot", attractive and shiny but described with a word for clotted blood (1). The simile "Like thickened wine" makes its taste rich and intoxicating (1). "summer's blood was in it" suggests the berry contains the life of the season (1). The blood imagery hints that this pleasure carries violence or guilt, preparing for the decay to come (1).
:::

::: example Greed
Explain how the poem presents the children's greed. (4)

Their desire is "lust for / Picking", a strong, almost sinful word (1). They use every container they can find, picking "until the cans were full" (1). Their hands are "sticky as Bluebeard's", an allusion to a murderer that makes their greed seem guilty (1). The fungus "glutting on our cache" mirrors their own greed back at them, as if punishing it (1).
:::

::: example The final couplet
Explain why the final couplet is effective. (3)

"Each year" shows the disappointment was repeated every summer, turning one memory into a pattern (1). "hoped they'd keep" and "knew they would not" set hope against knowledge in one line, a mature recognition (1). The full rhyme of "rot" and "not" closes the poem firmly after many half-rhymes, making the bleak truth final (1).
:::
