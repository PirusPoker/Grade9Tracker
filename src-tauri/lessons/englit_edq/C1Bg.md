## What it is

**I Shall Return** by Claude McKay is a sonnet of longing for a homeland. The speaker promises, again and again, that he will go back: to laugh and love, to watch the forest fires at noon, to loiter by streams, to hear the fiddles of the village dances. The memories are vivid with colour and music. Only the last line reveals why he needs to return: "To ease my mind of long, long years of pain." McKay was born in Jamaica and wrote in the United States, so the poem is usually read as an exile's longing for the Caribbean island he left.

**Section by section**

| Lines | What he will return to | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Laughter, love and wonder; forest fires at noon sending smoke into the sky | "watch with wonder-eyes"; "blue-black smoke to sapphire skies" |
| 5–8 | Streams and grasses; waters rushing down mountain passes, the stuff of his dreams | "the brown blades of the bending grasses"; "my thousand dreams" |
| 9–12 | Village dances, fiddle and fife, music that stirs the depths of "native life" | "dear delicious tunes"; "Stray melodies of dim remembered runes" |
| 13–14 | The couplet: the promise repeated, and the reason revealed | "To ease my mind of long, long years of pain" |

**Voice and speaker.** A first-person speaker, far from home, speaks with determination and yearning. Everything he describes is remembered or imagined: he is not there. The poem is a **promise** and a **prayer** at once.

**Key language**
- **"I shall return"**: the title phrase is repeated six times, opening each quatrain and the couplet. "shall" is firm and determined, like a vow; but repetition can also suggest he needs to keep reassuring himself, because the return has not happened.
- **"wonder-eyes"**: a compound word that makes him childlike; home restores innocence and amazement.
- **Colour imagery**: "golden noon", "blue-black smoke", "sapphire skies", "brown blades". Rich, jewel-like colours ("golden", "sapphire") make home precious, like treasure.
- **Water and nature**: "streams / That bathe the brown blades", "waters rushing down the mountain passes". The landscape is alive, gentle ("bathe") and powerful ("rushing"). Water suggests renewal and cleansing, which the last line needs.
- **Alliteration and sound**: "brown blades of the bending grasses", "fiddle and fife", "dear delicious tunes", "dim remembered runes". The sounds make the poem musical, like the village music it remembers.
- **"native life"** and **"hidden depths"**: home is where his identity lies, a deeper self that the music "stir[s]".
- **"dim remembered runes"**: "runes" are old songs or mysterious signs; "dim" admits his memories are fading with time and distance, the sadness behind the joy.
- **"long, long years of pain"**: the repetition of "long" slows the line and stretches time. The pain is never explained, which makes it more powerful: homesickness, exile, or the hardship of the life he lives now.

**Form and structure**
- **A sonnet**: 14 lines in mostly iambic pentameter, rhyming **ABAB CDCD EFEF GG** (three quatrains and a couplet, the Shakespearean pattern).
- **Each quatrain is a different memory**: landscape and light; water and grass; music and village life. Together they build a full picture of home through sight, touch and sound.
- **The volta comes in the final couplet.** After twelve lines of joy, the couplet turns to "pain". The sonnet's traditional turn changes how we read everything before it: the beautiful memories are what keep him going in a painful present.
- **Feminine rhymes** in the second quatrain ("grasses / passes") give a soft, flowing ending, like the water they describe.
- **Future tense** throughout: the whole poem looks forward to a return that may or may not come.
- **Using a sonnet**, a traditional European love-poem form, for love of a Caribbean homeland is itself a statement: a poet from a British colony uses an English form to celebrate the place Britain ruled.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Claude McKay** was born in **Jamaica**, then a British colony, and moved to the **United States**, where he wrote much of his poetry.
- **The Harlem Renaissance** of the 1920s was a flowering of Black writing, art and music centred on Harlem in New York. Its writers explored Black identity, African and Caribbean roots, and the racism of American life. McKay was one of its important voices.
- **Migration and exile**: many Caribbean people left for America seeking work and opportunity, and found hardship and discrimination as well. The poem's unexplained "pain" can be read in that light, against the warmth and belonging of home.
- **"native life"**: in a world that treated Black migrants as outsiders, home is where the speaker's identity is whole.

**Themes**: home and belonging; exile and longing; memory; nature and its beauty; identity and roots; pain beneath joy.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud** (Wordsworth): memory of a beloved landscape; Wordsworth can summon his, McKay's is far away.
- **Drummer Hodge** (Hardy): home and a foreign land; Hodge can never return, McKay vows to.
- **Origin Story** (Ewing): identity and roots; family and where we come from.
- **Sonnet 29** (Barrett Browning): another sonnet of longing for what is absent.

::: terms
- **Sonnet**: a 14-line poem in iambic pentameter with a set rhyme scheme, traditionally about love.
- **Quatrain**: a group of four lines; this sonnet has three, each on a different memory.
- **Rhyming couplet**: two rhyming lines; here the final couplet carries the poem's turn.
- **Volta**: the turn in a sonnet, here the shift from joy to "pain" in the last line.
- **Repetition**: repeating a word or phrase for emphasis, like the six uses of "I shall return".
- **Alliteration**: repeated consonant sounds at the start of nearby words, like "brown blades... bending".
- **Exile**: living away from one's homeland, by force or circumstance.
- **Harlem Renaissance**: a 1920s movement of Black writers, artists and musicians centred on Harlem, New York.
:::

::: example The repeated title phrase
Explain the effect of repeating "I shall return". (4)

The phrase appears six times, opening each quatrain and the couplet, so it structures the poem (1). "shall" is determined, like a promise or vow (1). Repetition can also suggest he is reassuring himself, because the return has not yet happened and may never happen (1). It works like a chant or refrain, giving the poem the musical quality of the village tunes it remembers (1).
:::

::: example Colour imagery
Explain how McKay uses colour in lines 3–4. (3)

"golden noon" suggests warmth, brightness and value (1). "blue-black smoke to sapphire skies" contrasts dark smoke with jewel-bright sky (1); the precious words "golden" and "sapphire" make home seem like treasure, valued more highly from far away (1).
:::

::: example The couplet
Explain how the final couplet changes the reader's understanding of the poem. (4)

The couplet repeats the vow, "I shall return, I shall return again", building to a climax (1). The last line reveals the reason, "To ease my mind of long, long years of pain" (1). This volta reframes the joyful memories: they are what sustain him through suffering in his present life (1). The repetition of "long" stretches the line and the time, and the pain is unexplained, which suggests exile and hardship too deep to name (1).
:::
