## What it is

In Section B you write about a printed anthology poem, then **compare it with a second poem that you choose and write about from memory**. The question names a theme ("Choose one other poem from the anthology in which the poet also writes about ..."). You have about a minute to choose, so the choice must already be made in your head. This lesson links all fifteen poems: by **theme**, by **context and period**, and by **form**, with a short recall sheet of quotations. The spec asks you to understand how poets "explore similar themes in different ways" and how poems relate to the **contexts** in which they were written, so every pairing below names a similarity *and* the point of contrast that makes a comparison interesting.

::: terms
- **Comparison**: discussing two poems together, showing similarities and differences in every paragraph.
- **Theme**: a central idea a poem explores, such as love, conflict or memory.
- **Context**: the circumstances of a poem's writing: the poet's life, period, society and events.
- **Romanticism**: a late-18th and early-19th-century movement valuing nature, feeling, imagination and childhood.
- **Victorian era**: Queen Victoria's reign, 1837–1901, marked by strict social and gender codes and Empire.
- **Dramatic monologue**: a poem spoken by one character, revealing their situation and feelings.
- **Sonnet**: a 14-line poem with a set rhyme scheme and a turn, traditionally about love.
- **Free verse**: poetry without regular rhyme or metre.
- **Comparative connective**: a linking word for comparison, such as "whereas", "similarly" or "in contrast".
- **Pairing**: a pre-chosen second poem linked to a first by theme, with a clear point of contrast.
:::

::: method
1. **Underline the theme** in the 7.2 question; it is usually the same theme as the 7.1 question.
2. **Pick from your pairing table** in under a minute: a poem you know well, with quotations you can recall, that offers a clear contrast.
3. **Plan three or four comparative points** that cover content and structure, methods and effects, and contexts (the question's three bullets).
4. **Write each paragraph about both poems**, linked with comparative connectives, with a short quotation from each.
5. **Explain contexts as reasons for difference**: why a Victorian woman and a modern Scottish poet write about love differently.
6. **End with an evaluative overview**: which poem presents the theme more hopefully, bleakly or powerfully, and why.
:::

::: example Choosing a pairing
The 7.1 poem is Disabled and the theme is the effects of war. Name the best poem to compare and explain your choice. (3)

Remains (1). Both show a soldier damaged after a war, so the theme fits exactly (1). There is a clear contrast to explore: Owen's soldier suffers physical loss and social rejection, told in the third person, while Armitage's suffers psychological guilt in a colloquial first-person voice; their First World War and recent-war contexts explain the difference (1).
:::

::: example A comparative topic sentence on love
Write a comparative topic sentence on love in Sonnet 29 and Dusting the Phone. (2)

"Both speakers are overwhelmed by thoughts of an absent lover (1), but whereas Barrett Browning's Petrarchan sonnet resolves its longing in the lover's 'presence', Kay's free verse ends unresolved in 'I don't know what.' (1)"
:::

::: example Comparing contexts
Explain how context helps explain the different presentation of a woman's voice in Cousin Kate and Dusting the Phone. (4)

Rossetti wrote in Victorian England, where a woman seduced by a nobleman was condemned as "fallen" while the man kept his status (1); so her speaker's anger is directed at class and sexual double standards, "Call me an outcast thing" (1). Kay writes in modern Scotland, where the obstacle is not public shame but a lover who will not commit, "don't mention love" (1); so her speaker's pain is private and comic as well as anguished, and she has no clear target for her anger (1).
:::

::: example Grouping by form
Name two sonnets in the anthology and one difference between them. (3)

Sonnet 29 (1) and I Shall Return (1). Sonnet 29 is Petrarchan with a volta after the octave, while I Shall Return uses three quatrains and a couplet with its turn at the end (1).
:::

::: example Contrasting contexts of conflict
Write two sentences comparing the contexts of Drummer Hodge and Kamikaze. (2)

"Hardy wrote during the Boer War, questioning an Empire that sent young countrymen like Hodge to be buried in a land they never understood (1), whereas Garland looks back on the Second World War from the present, questioning a Japanese honour code that treated a survivor as if he had died (1)."
:::

::: watch
- **Choose for contrast, not just similarity.** Two poems that say exactly the same thing give you little to compare.
- **Choose a poem you can quote from memory.** The printed poem is in front of you; the chosen one is not.
- **Keep the comparison in every paragraph**, not poem A then poem B.
- **Compare contexts to explain differences**, not as separate history paragraphs.
- **Learn the pairings in advance**, so the choice takes a minute, not ten.
:::

::: test
Explain how the contexts of Disabled and Remains help explain their different presentations of a soldier's suffering. (5)
---
Owen wrote in 1917 from experience of the First World War, when young men were recruited with patriotic promises (1); his soldier's suffering is physical and social: he lost his legs and is ignored by women and the public (1). Armitage's poem comes from soldiers' testimony from recent wars, gathered for The Not Dead (1); his soldier's suffering is psychological: flashbacks and guilt now understood as PTSD (1); so the move from a visible to an invisible wound reflects changing understandings of war's damage (1).
:::
