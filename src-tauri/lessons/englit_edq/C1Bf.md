## What it is

**Disabled** by Wilfred Owen shows a young soldier who has lost both legs and parts of his arms in the First World War. He sits in a wheelchair in a park at dusk, listening to boys playing, and remembers his life before: the girls, the football, the vanity and pressure that made him join up under age. Now women look past him, a single man thanked him on his return, and he will spend his life in institutions, dependent on others even to be put to bed. The poem's anger is aimed at the war and also at everyone who sent him: recruiters, crowds, girls, and his own young pride.

**Stanza by stanza**

| Section | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| Opening stanza | He sits in a wheelchair at dusk, cold, hearing boys' voices fade | "He sat in a wheeled chair, waiting for dark"; "Legless, sewn short at elbow" |
| Memory of the town | Evenings used to be lively, with lamps and girls; now girls touch him with revulsion | "before he threw away his knees"; "like some queer disease" |
| His face and his blood | An artist once wanted to paint his young face; now he is old, his colour lost in battle | "Poured it down shell-holes till the veins ran dry" |
| Why he joined (the longest stanza) | After football and a drink, flattered and wanting to impress girls, he enlisted; they accepted his lie about his age; he imagined uniforms, salutes and glory | "Someone had said he'd look a god in kilts"; "Smiling they wrote his lie" |
| His return | Fewer cheered him home than cheer a goal; one solemn man thanked him | "not as crowds cheer Goal" |
| His future | Years in institutions, accepting pity; women's eyes pass to whole men | "take whatever pity they may dole" |
| Final couplet | Alone in the cold, waiting for someone to put him to bed | "Why don't they come?" |

**Voice.** A third-person narrator tells the story, but moves close to the man's thoughts (free indirect style): "He wonders why..." and the final questions are his. Owen never names him, so he stands for thousands of wounded men.

**Key language**
- **"wheeled chair"** and **"ghastly suit of grey"**: the hospital uniform is a grim parody of the uniform he wanted; "ghastly" is ghost-like, as if he is already half dead. "waiting for dark" suggests waiting for night and for death.
- **"Legless, sewn short at elbow"**: blunt, clinical description; "sewn" makes his body a garment altered by others.
- **"Voices of boys rang saddening like a hymn"**: the simile links play with a funeral hymn; his youth is being mourned. The boys are "mothered" away by sleep, while he has no one.
- **Past against present**: "About this time Town used to swing so gay" against "Now he will never feel again". The repeated "Now" pulls him back each time from memory.
- **"before he threw away his knees"**: a bitter, casual verb. "threw away" admits his own part in the loss, as if he discarded his body carelessly.
- **"like some queer disease"**: girls now touch him as if he were infectious; the simile shows how disability has made him an object of disgust.
- **Colour and blood**: "He's lost his colour very far from here, / Poured it down shell-holes till the veins ran dry". Losing colour means losing blood, youth and life. "leap of purple spurted from his thigh" is vivid and violent; the earlier "bloodsmear down his leg" from football was a badge of pride, now horribly echoed.
- **"he'd look a god in kilts"**: vanity. He joined to look good, wearing a Scottish kilted uniform; he imagined "jewelled hilts", "smart salutes" and "Esprit de corps", the glamour of soldiering, not the reality.
- **"to please his Meg"** and **"the giddy jilts"**: he joined to impress girls who did not care; "jilts" suggests fickle women who reject lovers.
- **"Smiling they wrote his lie"**: the recruiters knew he was under age and accepted him anyway. "Smiling" makes them complicit.
- **"Some cheered him home, but not as crowds cheer Goal"**: the crowd that celebrated his football gives the wounded soldier less.
- **"take whatever pity they may dole"**: "dole" suggests charity handed out in small amounts; his life is now dependent and diminished.
- **"Why don't they come?"**: repeated, it ends the poem on a helpless, childlike plea.

**Form and structure**
- **Irregular stanzas**: short opening and closing stanzas frame a long central stanza about enlisting. The irregularity reflects a broken body and a mind jumping between past and present.
- **Shifts in time**: present (the park), past (the town, the artist, football, enlisting), back to present and future (institutions, tonight). The juxtaposition makes the loss unbearable.
- **Half-regular rhyme and long lines**: there is rhyme, but irregular and often hidden, as if order is barely holding.
- **Lists**: "jewelled hilts... smart salutes; / And care of arms; and leave; and pay arrears" pile up the boyish fantasies of soldiering, all trivial beside what he loses.
- **The ending**: a two-line stanza with a repeated question. "How cold and late it is!" brings back the dark of the first line. The poem ends without an answer.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Wilfred Owen (1893–1918)** fought as an officer on the Western Front. In 1917 he was treated for shell shock at **Craiglockhart** War Hospital near Edinburgh, where he met the poet Siegfried Sassoon, who encouraged him to write the truth about the war. Disabled was drafted there. Owen was killed in November 1918, a week before the Armistice; most of his poems were published after his death.
- **Recruitment**: in 1914–15 propaganda posters, recruiting at football matches and pressure from women (some handed white feathers to men not in uniform) pushed young men to enlist. Soldiers were meant to be 19 to serve abroad, and many younger boys lied about their age, often with recruiters turning a blind eye: "Smiling they wrote his lie: aged nineteen years."
- **Wounded veterans** returned in huge numbers, many with amputations. Owen shows society's brief gratitude turning into pity and neglect.
- **Owen's purpose**: to expose the gap between the glamour sold to young men and the reality, and to make readers at home feel what they would rather ignore.

**Themes**: the cost of war; youth and loss; masculinity and vanity; society's indifference; isolation; propaganda and the lie of glory.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **Remains** (Armitage): the aftermath of war in a survivor's life; trauma that will not leave.
- **Drummer Hodge** (Hardy): a young, naive soldier destroyed by war he did not understand.
- **Kamikaze** (Garland): a soldier rejected by those at home after he returns.
- **Cousin Kate** (Rossetti): someone admired, used, then cast aside.

::: terms
- **Juxtaposition**: placing contrasting things side by side, like his past and present life.
- **Free indirect style**: third-person narration that slips into a character's own thoughts and words.
- **Propaganda**: persuasive material, such as recruitment posters, designed to make people support the war.
- **Craiglockhart**: the military hospital near Edinburgh where Owen was treated for shell shock and met Sassoon.
- **Irony**: a gap between expectation and reality, like joining to "look a god" and ending "Legless".
- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as", such as "rang saddening like a hymn".
- **Rhetorical question**: a question without an answer, like the closing "Why don't they come?"
- **Esprit de corps**: French for team spirit and loyalty in a group, one of the glamorous ideas he joined for.
:::

::: method
1. **Overview**: a young man's life destroyed by a war he joined for vanity and approval, then ignored by the society that sent him.
2. **Track the time shifts**: present in the park, past in the town and at enlistment, future in institutions, back to tonight.
3. **Analyse the contrasts**: colour and grey, girls then and now, the football crowd and the homecoming.
4. **Use form**: irregular stanzas, the long central stanza, lists, the two-line ending and repeated question.
5. **Weave in context**: recruitment and propaganda, under-age soldiers, Craiglockhart, treatment of the wounded.
6. **For 7.2**, pick a poem on war, its aftermath or rejection and compare throughout.
:::

::: example The opening
Explain how the first stanza presents the soldier's situation. (4)

"He sat in a wheeled chair, waiting for dark" shows him passive and helpless; "waiting for dark" suggests both night and death (1). "ghastly suit of grey" makes him ghost-like and drained of colour (1). "Legless, sewn short at elbow" is blunt and clinical, and "sewn" shows others have altered his body (1). The boys' voices ring "saddening like a hymn", a funeral simile, so his own lost boyhood is being mourned (1).
:::

::: example Why he joined
Explain how Owen presents the soldier's reasons for enlisting. (4)

He joined "after football, when he'd drunk a peg": a casual decision made on impulse and drink (1). "Someone had said he'd look a god in kilts" shows vanity and the desire to be admired (1). He also wanted "to please his Meg" and "the giddy jilts", so pressure from girls played a part (1). His fantasies of "jewelled hilts" and "smart salutes" show that propaganda sold war as glamour, and the irony is brutal: the man who wanted to look like a god is now "Legless" (1).
:::

::: example The homecoming
Explain the effect of "Some cheered him home, but not as crowds cheer Goal." (3)

The comparison sets the war hero against a football goal (1); the crowd that cheered him as a player gives him less as a wounded soldier (1); it exposes society's shallow values and its unwillingness to face the cost of the war it supported (1).
:::

::: example Context woven into analysis
Write a sentence linking "Smiling they wrote his lie: aged nineteen years" to context. (2)

"Because soldiers were supposed to be nineteen to fight abroad, the recruiters who 'Smiling... wrote his lie' knowingly accepted an under-age boy (1), and Owen, who saw such boys at the front and wrote this at Craiglockhart, makes the smile itself an accusation against the system that wanted bodies more than it protected them (1)."
:::
