## What it is

Question **3.2** is the second half of the unseen poetry section and the biggest single question on Component 2's poetry: **25 marks, about 40 minutes**. You read a **second unseen poem** and compare it with the poem you wrote about in 3.1. It is marked on **AO1 and AO2 equally**, and **comparison drives the bands**: an excellent essay on the new poem alone cannot reach the top.

**What the question looks like**

**3.2** Now compare [second poem] by [poet] and [first poem] by [poet]. [25]
You should:
- compare what the poems are about and how they are organised
- compare the ideas the poets may have wanted us to think about
- compare the poets' choice of words, phrases and images and the effects they create
- compare how you respond to the poems.

Notice that the bullets are the same four as 3.1, each with "compare" added, and that the second poem is named **first**. The statement above both poems ("The two poems are about ...") tells you what links them on the surface.

**The bands in plain words (25 marks, bands of 5)**

| Band | Marks | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 21–25 | Comparison that is critical, illuminating and sustained throughout; a wide-ranging discussion of similarities and differences; both poems analysed perceptively with precise references; a convincing personal response to both. |
| 4 | 16–20 | Focused, coherent comparison sustained across the answer; clear discussion of similarities and differences; thoughtful analysis of both poems. |
| 3 | 11–15 | Some valid comparison of similarities and/or differences; relevant references; some explanation of effects in both poems. |
| 2 | 6–10 | General or obvious comparison (both are about...); simple comments; often mostly one poem. |
| 1 | 1–5 | Very limited comparison; basic comments on the poems. |

**The second poem needs a full analysis.** This is the only question in which the second poem is assessed, so explore it in as much depth as you explored the first in 3.1, either as a separate opening section or inside your comparison. Examiners report two common faults: giving one poem far more attention than the other, and simply copying out what was said in 3.1 before adding a few lines on the new poem. Reuse your understanding of poem one, but **rethink it in the light of poem two**.

**Reading the second poem with the first in mind (5 minutes)**
1. Read the statement again: what do the poems share on the surface?
2. Read the new poem twice, as in 3.1: situation, speaker, striking details, shift, ending.
3. Fill a quick **comparison grid** in the margin:

| Aspect | Poem B (new) | Poem A (3.1) |
|---|---|---|
| Situation and speaker | | |
| Mood and tone (does it change?) | | |
| Key images (two each) | | |
| Organisation (stanzas, shift, ending) | | |
| Ideas | | |
| My response | | |

4. Circle the **biggest difference**: that becomes your overview and drives the answer.

**Three ways to organise the answer**

| Structure | How | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Integrated (by aspect)** | Each paragraph takes one aspect (subject, imagery, organisation, ending, ideas, response) and moves between both poems. | Best for sustained comparison; aim for this. |
| **New poem first, then comparison** | A section analysing poem B fully, then several paragraphs comparing B with A. | Valid and used by many strong students; make sure the comparison section is substantial. |
| **All of A, then all of B** | Two separate essays with a link sentence. | Avoid: comparison is not sustained, so the answer is capped. |

**Compare effects, not features.** "Both poems use similes" earns little. "Both poets turn the weather into a character, but where the snow is playful, dressing the bins as judges, the heat is oppressive, softening the tarmac like toffee, so one city is made a game and the other a trap" compares **what the methods do**.

**Comparative connectives**: similarly; likewise; both poets; in the same way; whereas; while; in contrast; however; on the other hand; unlike; although both poems ...; where poem A ..., poem B ...; this is more striking in ...; this is darker in ...

**Comparing responses.** The fourth bullet asks how you respond to both. Make a judgement: which poem moved, unsettled or amused you more, and why? "Although both endings look forward to tomorrow, Heatwave left me more uneasy, because..." Evaluative comparisons like this are characteristic of the top band.

**Timing across the hour**

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| 0–20 | 3.1 on the first poem (previous lesson). |
| 20–25 | Read poem B twice, fill the grid, choose the overview. |
| 25–55 | Overview, then four or five comparative paragraphs (or a section on B, then comparison). |
| 55–60 | Comparative conclusion and response. |

**Model plan.** The two poems below were written for this lesson; "Allotment" is the 3.1 poem from the previous lesson, reprinted here. The two poems are about things we inherit from grandparents.

Allotment

The padlock still remembers his thumb;
it sticks, then gives, the way he did
when we were small and wanted sweets.
Inside, the shed keeps its brown breath:
creosote, twine, a calendar
stopped at a month of broad beans.

His rows have lost their discipline.
Couch grass marches over the onions,
the runner beans have climbed their canes
and kept on climbing, reaching for him
past the tops of the poles, into nothing.

I kneel where his knees wore the soil smooth
and pull one weed, then another,
not because it will make a difference
but because the earth seems to expect it,
and because my hands are his hands, now.

Inheritance

My grandmother's recipes were never written down
in any way a stranger could follow.
"A handful of flour," the card says,
"butter the size of an egg,
bake until it smells right."

Her handful was bigger than mine.
Her eggs came from a neighbour's hens.
The oven she trusted has been scrapped,
and nobody alive knows what "right" smells like.

So every Sunday I fail her a little:
the scones too flat, the pastry tough,
the kitchen full of smoke and guessing.
And every Sunday my daughter
licks the bowl and says it's perfect,
and writes down nothing,
and remembers everything.

- **Overview**: both speakers carry on a dead grandparent's work with their hands; Allotment is quiet and solemn, finding the grandfather in the earth, while Inheritance is wry and funny, admitting failure, and looks forward to a third generation.
- **Subject and organisation**: both have three stanzas that move from objects (padlock and shed; the recipe card) to loss (the overgrown rows; the scrapped oven) to action in the present (weeding; Sunday baking). Allotment ends with the speaker becoming her grandfather; Inheritance ends with her daughter, so its time-scale is longer.
- **Imagery**: personified objects in both (the padlock "remembers"; the card "says"). Allotment's images of growth reaching "into nothing" are sad; Inheritance's comic list of "too flat", "tough", "smoke and guessing" makes failure affectionate.
- **Ideas**: what survives a death. Allotment: habits of the body ("my hands are his hands"). Inheritance: love and memory matter more than exactness; the daughter "remembers everything".
- **Response**: Allotment moved me more deeply; Inheritance made me smile and felt more hopeful, because its ending passes the tradition on.

::: terms
- **Comparison**: discussing two texts together, showing how they are similar and different and why.
- **Integrated comparison**: each paragraph moves between both poems on the same aspect, rather than treating them separately.
- **Comparative connective**: a linking word or phrase, such as whereas or similarly, that signals a comparison.
- **Overview**: a short opening statement of the main similarity and difference between the poems.
- **Aspect**: one area to compare, such as subject, imagery, organisation, mood, ideas or the ending.
- **Evaluative comparison**: a judgement about which poem achieves something more powerfully or affects you more, with reasons.
- **Tone**: the attitude or mood of the speaker's voice, such as solemn, wry, bitter or tender.
- **Sequential answer**: writing about one poem and then the other with little linking, which limits the mark.
- **Comparison grid**: a quick table in the margin listing each aspect for both poems before you write.
:::

::: method
1. **Re-read the statement** and decide what links the poems on the surface.
2. **Read the second poem twice** and annotate it as fully as you did the first.
3. **Fill a quick comparison grid**: situation, mood, two images each, organisation, ideas, response.
4. **Write an overview** naming the main similarity and the key difference.
5. **Write four or five comparative paragraphs**, one aspect each, moving between the poems with connectives and short quotations from both.
6. **Compare effects**, not just features: say what each poet's choice does and why the difference matters.
7. **Finish with an evaluative comparison of your response** to the two poems. Keep to about 40 minutes.
:::

::: example An overview sentence
Write an overview for a comparison of Inheritance and Allotment. (3)

Both poems show a grandchild continuing a dead grandparent's work with their own hands (1), but where Allotment is hushed and solemn, finding the grandfather in the soil itself (1), Inheritance is wry and affectionate, laughing at the speaker's failures and passing the tradition on to a third generation (1).
:::

::: example A Band 2 and a Band 5 paragraph
Compare these two paragraphs on imagery and explain the difference in band. (5)

Band 2: "In Inheritance the poet uses a list, 'the scones too flat, the pastry tough'. This shows she is bad at baking. In Allotment the poet uses personification, 'the padlock still remembers'. Both poems use good imagery."

Band 5: "Both poets give the grandparent's possessions a kind of memory, but to different effect. Allotment's padlock 'remembers his thumb', so the objects themselves seem to grieve, which gives the poem its solemn, almost sacred mood. In Inheritance the card 'says' its vague instructions, and the joke is that it cannot really speak for her: 'nobody alive knows' what 'right' smells like. Where Allotment's personification keeps the grandfather present, Inheritance's shows how much of the grandmother is lost, which makes the speaker's comic list of failures, 'too flat', 'tough', 'smoke and guessing', both funny and quietly sad."

The Band 2 paragraph treats each poem separately (1) and names devices without exploring their effects (1); "both use good imagery" is a vague link (1). The Band 5 paragraph compares the same method in both poems (1) and explores how it creates different moods and ideas, with precise quotations and evaluative judgement (1).
:::

::: example Comparing endings
Compare the endings of Allotment and Inheritance. (4)

Both endings turn from loss to continuation (1). Allotment closes on "my hands are his hands, now": the speaker herself becomes the link, and the final "now" makes it feel like a quiet moment of realisation (1). Inheritance looks a generation further: the daughter "writes down nothing, / and remembers everything", a paradox suggesting that love, not accuracy, is what is passed on (1). Allotment's ending is more personal and solemn; Inheritance's is more hopeful because the tradition is already moving on (1).
:::

::: example Comparing organisation
Compare how the two poems are organised. (4)

Both use three stanzas that move from the grandparent's objects to the speaker acting in the present (1). Allotment uses past and present tense ("wore", "kneel") to bring the grandfather's habits into the speaker's body (1), whereas Inheritance uses the repeated "every Sunday" to show a weekly ritual of trying and failing (1). The repetition in Inheritance builds to a gentle punchline, while Allotment builds to a still, single realisation (1).
:::

::: example Comparing responses
Write two sentences comparing your response to the two poems. (2)

"Allotment affected me more strongly, because the image of the beans 'reaching for him' made his absence physical (1). Inheritance, though, left me happier, as its ending suggests that families pass on love even when they lose the details (1)."
:::

---

::: q
Turn this into a comparison of effects: "Both poems use repetition." (3)
---
Name the repetition in each poem (1); explain what it does in each, e.g. "every Sunday" creates a weekly ritual of failure, while "one weed, then another" shows slow, patient grief (1); judge the difference, e.g. one is comic and affectionate, the other solemn (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Writing about the new poem alone**: without comparison you are capped, however good the analysis.
- **Recycling 3.1**: don't copy out your first answer; rethink poem one in the light of poem two.
- **Lopsided answers**: give both poems real depth; the second poem needs the fuller analysis.
- **"Both use similes"** comparisons: always compare what the methods do.
- **Imposed readings**: stay rooted in the statement and the words of both poems.
- **No response comparison**: the fourth bullet wants a judgement about how each poem affects you.
- **Running out of time**: plan for five minutes and write for thirty-five.
:::

::: test
Read the two poems below. Both were written for this lesson. Both poems are about unusual weather in a town or city.

Heatwave

The town has stopped pretending it can cope.
Tarmac softens like toffee under the buses,
and the river, low and sulking, shows
the shopping trolleys it has hidden all year.

Curtains stay closed at noon. Behind them
we lie like dropped coats, too hot to argue
and arguing anyway, about windows,
about whose turn it was to fill the ice tray.

Next door's radio leaks through the wall,
the same song three times, then a forecast
that promises nothing. A dog pants on a step.
Even the pigeons have given up and sit
in the shade of the war memorial
like old men with nowhere to go.

At nine the sky cools to the colour of a bruise.
Someone waters a dry garden. We open the doors
and nod to the neighbours, but no one speaks.
We are too tired to be kind. Tomorrow, the radio says,
will be hotter.

Snow Day

Overnight the city lost its edges.
Cars became loaves, left out to cool;
the bins wore white wigs like judges
and the traffic lights blinked at nobody,
red, amber, green, to an empty road.

No buses. No school. The news
says stay at home, so everyone comes out:
the man from Number Nine who never speaks
is building a snowman with his daughter,
a woman in a dressing gown is laughing
at the sky, as if it told a joke.

By four the gritters arrive, orange and patient,
salting the streets back into roads.
The snowman slumps. The silence melts.
Tomorrow we will be strangers again,
but for one white day the city
remembered it was a village.

Compare the effect of the weather on the people in the two poems. (4)
---
In Snow Day the weather brings people out and together: the silent neighbour builds a snowman, a woman laughs at the sky (1). In Heatwave it drives people indoors and apart: curtains closed, people "too hot to argue / and arguing anyway" (1). Both end with neighbours, but Snow Day's "village" contrasts with Heatwave's "no one speaks" and "too tired to be kind" (1). One poem presents shared joy, the other shared misery that still isolates (1).
:::

::: test
Compare the similes "like toffee" (Heatwave) and "like judges" (Snow Day). (4)
---
Both are humorous, everyday comparisons that make the transformed town vivid (1). "Like toffee" suggests the town melting and sticky, oppressive and uncomfortable (1); "white wigs like judges" is playful, dressing the bins up as if for a game (1). The tone of one is weary, the other comic and delighted (1).
:::

::: test
Compare the endings of Heatwave and Snow Day. (4)
---
Both look to "tomorrow" (1). Snow Day predicts a return to being "strangers" but celebrates the day the city "remembered it was a village", so it is bittersweet and grateful (1). Heatwave ends on a short line, "will be hotter", promising worse to come with no relief (1). Snow Day leaves a warm memory; Heatwave leaves exhaustion and dread (1).
:::

::: test
Read the two poems below. Both were written for this lesson. Both poems are about journeys on public transport.

Morning Train

Seven-fifteen. We fold ourselves
into the carriage like letters into envelopes,
addressed to offices, to shops, to schools.

Nobody looks up. The windows show
fields we no longer notice, cows
grazing on the far side of our phones.
A man reads the same email four times.
A girl practises a smile for an interview
in the black glass of her screen.

The train is late. The announcer is sorry.
Somebody sighs, and the sigh goes round
the carriage like a rumour, and for a second
we almost catch each other's eyes,
almost laugh, almost speak,

then the doors open on the city
and we pour out onto the platform
and are gone, a hundred separate hurries,
before anyone has said a word.

Night Bus

The last bus home is a lit aquarium
swimming the dark streets.
We sit apart, each in our own glass:
a nurse with her shoes in a bag,
two boys sharing a single earphone,
a man asleep against the window,
his breath printing a small ghost on the pane.

The driver knows the way without looking,
takes the corners like an old song.
Stop after stop, the doors sigh open,
let a little cold in, let one of us go.

Nobody speaks. Nobody needs to.
We are a family for twenty minutes,
held in the same yellow light,
rocking to the same engine,
until my stop, and I step down
into the dark, and the bus swims on
carrying the others home.

Compare how the two poets present the passengers' silence. (5)
---
In both poems nobody talks (1). In Night Bus the silence is comforting: "Nobody speaks. Nobody needs to." suggests a bond without words (1). In Morning Train the silence is a barrier: people are absorbed in phones and the repeated "almost catch ... almost laugh, almost speak" shows connection nearly happening and failing (1). The night poem turns silence into kinship, "a family for twenty minutes", while the morning poem leaves "a hundred separate hurries" (1). Credit a judgement on which feels lonelier and why (1).
:::
