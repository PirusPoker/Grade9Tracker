## What it is

Section B of Component 1 is worth **40 marks** (20% of the GCSE) and has two linked questions on the anthology, numbered **7.1** and **7.2**. You have about **an hour**. Both are marked equally on **AO1** (a personal, informed response with references), **AO2** (language, form and structure, with terminology) and **AO3** (contexts). The exam is **closed book**: the 7.1 poem is printed; the 7.2 poem is not.

| | 7.1 | 7.2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Marks** | 15 | 25 |
| **Time** | about 20 minutes | about 40 minutes (including a few minutes' planning) |
| **The poem** | One named anthology poem, printed on the paper | Your choice of a second anthology poem, written about **from memory** and compared with the 7.1 poem |
| **The task** | How does the poet present [a theme] in the poem? Refer to the contexts of the poem. | Choose another poem on the same theme; compare how the two poets present it |
| **Bullets** | none | compare content and structure; compare how the writers create effects, with terminology; compare the contexts |
| **Bands** | five bands of 3 marks | five bands of 5 marks |

**The 7.1 method (20 minutes, 15 marks)**
1. **Read the question and underline the theme** (two minutes). Every paragraph must be about *that* theme, not the poem in general.
2. **Annotate the printed poem** for the theme: three or four moments spread from beginning to end, a key method in each, and one place where context explains an idea.
3. **Open with an overview** (one or two sentences): what the poem says about the theme, and the turn or shape of the poem.
4. **Track through the poem in order.** Examiners reward coverage of the *whole* poem: an answer that stops halfway leaves marks behind.
5. **In each paragraph**: a point about the theme, a short quotation, analysis of a word, image or structural choice and its effect, and, where it genuinely helps, a context link. Use tentative language for interpretations ("this could suggest").
6. **Finish with the ending**: how the last lines change or complete the presentation of the theme.

**The 7.2 method (40 minutes, 25 marks)**
1. **Choose the second poem in under a minute**, using your pairing table (see the linking lesson). The choice must fit the **theme**: a poem you know well but which is only loosely on the theme will drag your AO1 down however good the analysis.
2. **Plan for three to five minutes**: three or four comparative points, each covering both poems. Use the three bullets as a checklist: content and structure, methods and effects, contexts.
3. **Write comparatively throughout.** Each paragraph should move between the poems with comparative connectives. Avoid writing all of one poem then all of the other with a single linking sentence: that structure caps the answer.
4. **Give both poems equal weight.** You may reuse ideas about the 7.1 poem, but develop them in a new comparative light rather than repeating.
5. **Quote the chosen poem from memory**: short quotations of two to five words that you have learned. Without them you cannot analyse methods, which loses AO2 marks.
6. **Compare contexts as explanations of difference**: why a Victorian woman and a modern Scottish poet present love so differently.
7. **End with an evaluative judgement**: which poem presents the theme more bleakly, hopefully or powerfully, and why.

**Comparative connectives**: similarly; likewise; both poets; in the same way; whereas; while; in contrast; however; on the other hand; unlike; although both ..., ...; where X ..., Y ...

**How the bands differ** (in plain words)
- **Top band**: a sensitive, evaluative overview; precise quotations from across both poems; methods analysed closely with accurate terms; context woven into the argument; comparison sustained and integrated in every paragraph.
- **Upper-middle**: a thoughtful, clear response; well-chosen references; methods explained with growing analysis; context clearly linked; comparison consistent.
- **Middle**: relevant and straightforward; some analysis of methods; some relevant context; some comparison of similarities and differences.
- **Lower**: general points, narrative, feature-spotting without effect, context bolted on or missing, and comparison only at the start or end.

**Model 7.2 plan.** Question: Drummer Hodge was the 7.1 poem on the theme of conflict. Chosen poem: **Remains**.
- Overview: both show what war does to an individual; Hardy's young soldier is a victim absorbed into a foreign land, Armitage's soldier a survivor who cannot escape what he did.
- Point 1 (content and structure): Hardy moves from burial to ignorance to eternal union with the land; Armitage moves from the killing to its endless aftermath, turning at "End of story".
- Point 2 (methods): Hardy's foreign words (kopje, veldt, Karoo) and "Strange stars" show displacement; Armitage's colloquial voice and blood imagery ("my bloody hands") show guilt.
- Point 3 (endings and time): Hodge is united with the land "for ever"; the looter is "here and now". Both endings are permanent, one peaceful, one tormented.
- Point 4 (contexts): Hardy's anti-jingoism during the Boer War, questioning an Empire that sends rural boys to die; Armitage's soldiers' testimony and PTSD from recent wars.
- Judgement: Hardy grieves for a soldier; Armitage shows that surviving can be its own kind of wound.

**Two paragraphs at different bands**

*Middle band:* "In Drummer Hodge the soldier is buried 'Uncoffined', which is sad. In Remains the soldier kills a looter. Hardy uses foreign words like 'veldt' to show it is a foreign place. Armitage uses informal language like 'legs it'. Both poems are about war, and Hardy wrote during the Boer War, and Armitage's poem is about soldiers in Iraq."
*Why it is middle*: relevant points with references, but methods are named rather than analysed, the poems sit side by side rather than being compared, and context is listed, not used.

*Top band:* "Both poets show war leaving a body in a foreign land, but they draw opposite conclusions about where that body ends up. Hodge, thrown in 'Uncoffined', becomes 'portion of that unknown plain', his 'Northern breast and brain' turning into a 'Southern tree'; Hardy's eerie, almost consoling image absorbs the boy into the landscape that killed him, a quiet protest against an Empire that sent a Wessex farm boy to die for land he never understood. Armitage, by contrast, insists that his looter is not 'six-feet-under' in some distant desert but 'dug in behind enemy lines' inside the speaker's head. Where Hardy's victim is lost to the land, Armitage's victim is lodged in the mind of his killer, reflecting a modern understanding of trauma that the soldiers' testimony behind The Not Dead made vivid."
*Why it is top*: a comparative opening, embedded quotations from both poems, analysis of specific images and their effects, and context used to explain the *difference*, all in one argument.

::: terms
- **AO1**: a personal, informed response to the poems, supported by references and quotations.
- **AO2**: analysis of language, form and structure and their effects, using terminology.
- **AO3**: understanding of the contexts of the poems and how they shape ideas.
- **Overview**: a short summary of the poem's whole argument or shape at the start of an answer.
- **Integrated comparison**: comparing both poems within each paragraph rather than one after the other.
- **Comparative connective**: a linking word or phrase that shows similarity or difference.
- **Embedded quotation**: a short quotation built into your own sentence.
- **Tentative language**: phrasing such as "this could suggest" that offers an interpretation without overclaiming.
- **Evaluation**: a judgement about how effective or significant a method or idea is.
:::

::: method
1. **7.1 (20 minutes)**: underline the theme; annotate three or four moments across the poem; open with an overview; track the whole poem in order; analyse methods; weave in context; finish on the ending.
2. **7.2 choice (one minute)**: pick the pairing that best fits the theme and that you can quote.
3. **7.2 plan (three to five minutes)**: three or four comparative points covering content and structure, methods and effects, and contexts.
4. **7.2 writing (about 35 minutes)**: compare in every paragraph, with comparative connectives, short quotations from both poems, and context explaining differences.
5. **Finish with a judgement** and leave a minute to check names and spelling.
:::

::: example An overview for 7.1
Write an overview for a 7.1 answer on how Hardy presents death in Drummer Hodge. (2)

"Hardy presents the death of a young drummer as lonely and anonymous, buried 'Uncoffined' under foreign stars he never understood (1); yet the poem moves from this harsh burial to a strange consolation, as Hodge becomes 'for ever' part of the land, a quiet protest against an Empire that sends ordinary boys to die far from home (1)."
:::

::: example A comparative topic sentence
Write a comparative topic sentence on the theme of childhood for The Schoolboy and Blackberry Picking. (2)

"Both poems present childhood joy that is spoiled (1), but whereas Blake blames the 'cruel eye' of school for caging a child's natural freedom, Heaney finds the spoiling in nature itself, as the hoarded berries rot and teach him that hope does not last (1)."
:::

::: example Integrating context in comparison
Write two sentences comparing how context shapes the presentation of a woman's love in Sonnet 29 and Cousin Kate. (4)

"Barrett Browning's speaker longs for her lover's 'presence' with a joy that her Victorian courtship, conducted in secret against her father's wishes, made all the more intense (1); her controlled Petrarchan sonnet turns that longing into fulfilment (1). Rossetti's speaker, by contrast, has been used and discarded by a lord, and her bitter rhetorical questions expose the Victorian double standard that called her 'an outcast thing' while he kept his status (1); where one woman's love is private and rewarded, the other's is public and punished, showing two sides of the same society (1)."
:::

::: example Choosing well
The 7.1 poem is Catrin and the theme is love. Explain which poem you would choose for 7.2 and why. (3)

Origin Story (1): it is about love between parents and children, so it fits the theme closely and I can quote it from memory ("love is paper", "a good ending") (1); it also gives a clear contrast, a child looking at her parents' love that ended against a mother's love that survives every struggle, and two different contexts, Wales and Chicago (1).

::: watch
- **Fit the theme**: in 7.1 every paragraph must address the named theme; in 7.2 choose a poem that clearly shares it.
- **Cover the whole 7.1 poem**, beginning to end.
- **Learn short quotations for every poem**: in 7.2 you cannot analyse what you cannot quote.
- **Compare in every paragraph**, never poem A then poem B.

::: test
Read these stanzas from Drummer Hodge by Thomas Hardy.

They throw in Drummer Hodge, to rest
Uncoffined -- just as found:
His landmark is a kopje-crest
That breaks the veldt around:
And foreign constellations west
Each night above his mound.

Yet portion of that unknown plain
Will Hodge for ever be;
His homely Northern breast and brain
Grow up some Southern tree,
And strange-eyed constellations reign
His stars eternally.

Drummer Hodge is a poem about death. How does Thomas Hardy present death in the poem? Remember to refer to the contexts of the poem in your answer. (15)
---
AO1, AO2 and AO3 equally.
- Band 5 (13–15): perceptive, evaluative response with an overview; precise references from across the poem; methods analysed closely with accurate terms; context woven in to explain ideas.
- Band 4 (10–12): thoughtful and secure; well-chosen references; methods increasingly analysed; context clearly linked.
- Band 3 (7–9): straightforward and relevant; appropriate references; some comment on methods; relevant context.
- Band 2 (4–6): some focus and references; simple comments; context general or bolted on.

Indicative content:
- A careless, anonymous burial: "They throw in", "Uncoffined", "just as found"; the impersonal "They".
- Displacement: Afrikaans words "kopje", "veldt", "Karoo"; "foreign constellations"; "Strange stars".
- Youth and ignorance: "Young Hodge" "never knew" the land's meaning; "Fresh from his Wessex home".

- Form: three six-line stanzas moving from burial to ignorance to eternity; regular rhyme and ballad-like rhythm; "Hodge" as a name for any rural labourer.
