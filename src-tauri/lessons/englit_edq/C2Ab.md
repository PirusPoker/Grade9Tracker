## What it is

Section A of Component 2 is one **source-based question worth 40 marks**, about **45 minutes**, on your post-1914 prose or drama text. It is worth a fifth of the whole GCSE, so technique matters as much as knowledge. This lesson teaches the method on An Inspector Calls, but it works unchanged for Lord of the Flies, Blood Brothers or any other Section A text.

**What the question looks like**

| Part of the question | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| "You should use the extract below and your knowledge of the whole play to answer this question." | The extract is a **starting point**, not the whole task. |
| "Write about [character] and how he/she is important to the play as a whole." / "Write about the relationship between ..." / "Write about some of the times ... and how this is presented at different points in the play." / "How is ... presented?" | The **focus**. Every paragraph must serve it. |
| Bullets: "refer to the extract and the play as a whole"; "show your understanding of characters and events in the play" | Your checklist: extract **and** wider text, characters **and** events. |
| "5 of this question's marks are allocated for accuracy in spelling, punctuation and the use of vocabulary and sentence structures." | **AO4**: five marks for clean, controlled writing. |

**How the 40 marks work**

- **35 marks for AO1 and AO2, equally weighted**, in five bands of seven. AO1 is your response to the task and your references; AO2 is your analysis of how the writer uses language, form and structure (for a play, that includes stage directions and dramatic methods).
- **5 marks for AO4** in a separate grid: 4–5 for consistent accuracy and effective control of vocabulary and sentence structures, 2–3 for considerable accuracy and general control, 1 for reasonable accuracy where errors do not get in the way of meaning.
- **No AO3.** Context is not assessed in Section A. Examiners welcome it only when it is tied directly to a point about the characters or events; general history earns nothing and wastes time.

**The bands in plain words (35 marks)**

| Band | Marks | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 29–35 | A sustained, evaluative argument with an overview; the extract and the wider play handled critically; precise references from across the text; methods analysed and their effects evaluated; some originality. |
| 4 | 22–28 | A thoughtful, clear response; well-chosen references; methods discussed and increasingly analysed; secure understanding. |
| 3 | 15–21 | Straightforward and relevant; references support the points; some comment on methods and effects; some narrative creeping in. |
| 2 | 8–14 | Some focus; general references; mostly retelling; basic comments on language. |
| 1 | 1–7 | Limited focus; simple comments with little reference. |

**Three valid ways to use the extract.** Examiners accept all three, as long as both the extract and the wider text are covered.

1. **Extract first, then outwards ("exploding the extract")**: one or two paragraphs on the extract, then the rest of the play in order. The safest approach for most students.
2. **Linear**: work through the play from beginning to end and deal with the extract where it falls. Works well when the extract comes late in the play, as it often does.
3. **Integrated**: dip in and out of the extract while ranging across the text. Hardest to control, but the most fluent at the top end.

Whichever you use, **don't over-analyse the extract**. You are not required to analyse every line; choose two or three key words, stage directions or images and explore them, then spend most of the 45 minutes on the play as a whole. Weak answers either live in the extract or ignore it.

**Timing (45 minutes)**

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Underline the focus, read the extract, plan: a thesis and five moments spread across the play (beginning, middle, end). |
| 5–15 | Introduction (two or three sentences with your overview) and the extract paragraph(s). |
| 15–40 | Four or five paragraphs on the wider play, in order of the plot or by idea. |
| 40–45 | Conclusion that answers the question; proofread for AO4. |

Keep the plan short: points and a word or two for each, not pages of quotations. Students who write out every quotation they know before starting often run out of time.

**Writing about a play as a play.** Strong AO2 in drama goes beyond words to how the scene works on stage:
- **stage directions** (tone of voice, movements, pauses, lighting) and what they show about a character's state;
- **entrances and exits** and their timing;
- **dramatic irony** and what the audience knows;
- **structure**: where a moment comes, the chain of revelations, act endings, the cyclical close;
- **the audience's response**: "the audience might feel...", "Priestley makes the audience uncomfortable because...".

**What examiners don't reward.** Labelling every word class; forcing in technical terms (a "tricolon" that isn't really one); counting sentence lengths or punctuation for its own sake; "semantic field" where "words to do with" would do. Simple, accurate terms used to explain an effect beat a long list of labels.

**A paragraph that works (AO1 + AO2 together).** Point that answers the focus; short embedded quotation or precise reference; a method; its effect on the audience; a link to the bigger idea or to Priestley's purpose in a clause. One idea per paragraph, then a sentence that drives the argument on.

**AO4: five marks for care.** Spell names and the author correctly (Priestley, Birling, Sheila, Gerald, Eric, Eva Smith, Goole); capitals for names and the title; apostrophes (Sheila's, the Birlings'); no "alot", "eachother" or "should of"; varied sentence openings; precise vocabulary such as "hypocritical", "complacent", "remorseful", "patriarchal". Two minutes of proofreading is the cheapest grade in the paper.

**Model plan.** Question: "Write about Sheila and how she is important to the play as a whole." Extract: Act One, Sheila's reaction to the photograph and her confession about Milwards.
- **Thesis**: Sheila is the play's proof that people can change; Priestley turns a sheltered girl into the moral voice of the family and the model for his audience.
- **Extract**: her immediate distress and running out; honest confession of jealousy; her promise never to do it again; the Inspector's quieter treatment of her.
- **Act One opening**: childish, playful, delighted with the ring; the barbed hint about Gerald's absence last summer.
- **Act Two**: understands the Inspector's method before anyone else; returns the ring; warns her mother against building a wall between the family and the girl.
- **Act Three**: refuses the hoax as an escape; "Fairy Prince" sarcasm earlier shows her growing judgement; her hesitation over the ring.
- **Conclusion**: her importance is that she does what the older characters cannot, which is exactly what Priestley wants from a 1945 audience.

::: terms
- **Source-based question**: a question with a printed extract that must be answered using both the extract and the whole text.
- **Overview**: a short statement at the start of an answer that gives your argument about the whole text in relation to the question.
- **Exploding the extract**: analysing two or three key details in the extract and then linking each to moments across the wider text.
- **Thesis**: the one-sentence argument your whole essay sets out to prove, repeated and developed in the conclusion.
- **Embedded quotation**: a short quotation built into your own sentence rather than set out on a separate line.
- **AO4**: the five marks for accurate spelling and punctuation and a range of vocabulary and sentence structures.
- **Narrative retelling**: describing what happens instead of analysing how and why the writer presents it; the main reason answers stay in the lower bands.
- **Dramatic method**: any choice a playwright makes for the stage, such as stage directions, entrances, lighting, irony or act structure.
- **Wider text**: everything in the play outside the printed extract, which must carry most of your answer.
:::

::: method
1. **Underline the focus** (character, relationship, theme) and the word "important" or "presented". Write a one-line thesis that answers it.
2. **Read the extract once** for the focus; mark two or three details (a line, a stage direction, an image).
3. **Plan five moments** spread across the beginning, middle and end of the play, each with a method.
4. **Open with an overview**: two or three sentences that answer the question for the whole play.
5. **Use the extract purposefully**: one or two paragraphs, or deal with it where it falls in a linear answer.
6. **Range across the wider text**: in each paragraph, point, reference, method, effect on the audience, link to the writer's purpose.
7. **Conclude** by answering the question directly, then **proofread** names, capitals and apostrophes for AO4.
:::

::: example Opening with an overview
Write an introduction for: "Write about the Inspector and how he is presented in the play as a whole." (4)

Priestley presents the Inspector less as a policeman than as a moral force who invades the Birlings' comfortable dining room (1). From the moment he arrives, the lighting hardens and he takes control of the evening, questioning each character in turn so that their confidence collapses (1). His name, his knowledge and his disappearance suggest he may be something more than human (1), and his final warning makes him the voice of Priestley's argument that we are all responsible for one another (1).
:::

::: example A Band 3 and a Band 5 paragraph
Compare these two paragraphs on Sheila and explain the difference in band. (6)

Band 3 version: "At the start Sheila is happy because she is getting engaged. Then she finds out she got Eva sacked from Milwards because she was jealous. She says she will never do it again. This shows she feels guilty. Later she gives back the ring to Gerald."

Band 5 version: "Priestley first presents Sheila as sheltered and playful, delighted by the ring, yet even here her teasing remark about Gerald's absence last summer hints at a sharper mind beneath. When she confesses that she had Eva dismissed in a jealous temper, her promise to 'never, never do it again' is broken and repetitive, as if she is learning a new language of guilt. Unlike her father, she is changed by the confession rather than defended by it, and by Act Three it is Sheila who explains the Inspector's purpose to her parents. Priestley makes the audience's sympathy follow her because she models the response he wants from them."

The first is accurate but **narrative** and does not analyse a method (1); its comments are general ("this shows she feels guilty") (1). The second has an argument about change across the play (1), analyses the repetition and broken speech of a short quotation (1), uses structure (contrast with Birling; her role in Act Three) (1) and links to Priestley's purpose and the audience in a clause (1).
:::

::: example Using a stage direction
Explain how you could use a stage direction in an answer about Mr Birling. (4)

Choose a direction that shows character, for example the hard, bright lighting that replaces the "pink and intimate" glow when the Inspector arrives (1). Explain what it shows: Birling's cosy world of profit and status is exposed to scrutiny (1). Comment on the effect on the audience: they sense at once that Birling's control is over (1). Tie it to the question: Birling is important because he is the first to be tested, and the lighting signals that his values are on trial (1).
:::

::: example Context in a clause
Rewrite this sentence so that the context earns its place in Section A: "In 1945 there was a general election and Labour won and created the welfare state." (3)

"Birling's confident claim that a man must look after himself alone (1) would have sounded dangerously out of date to Priestley's 1945 audience, who were voting for a fairer, shared society (1), so the Inspector's warning seems to speak directly to them (1)."

The context now explains a dramatic choice instead of standing on its own.
:::

::: example Timing failure
A student spends 25 minutes on the extract and runs out of time after one paragraph on the wider play. Explain the likely effect on the mark and how to fix it. (4)

The answer cannot show knowledge of the play as a whole, which the question and the bullets require (1), so it is unlikely to rise beyond the middle bands however good the extract analysis (1). Fix: plan five moments across the play before writing (1) and limit the extract to about ten minutes, or deal with it where it falls in a linear answer (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Living in the extract.** Examiners say there is no need to analyse it line by line; use a few details, then go wide.
- **Ignoring the extract** is just as costly: the first bullet asks for both.
- **Reshaping a prepared essay**: a learned essay on responsibility forced onto a question about relationships or a single character drifts off the focus.
- **Narrative**: retelling each character's link to Eva instead of analysing the focus keeps answers in Band 2 or 3.
- **Feature spotting**: word-class labels and forced terms add nothing; explain effects.
- **Context paragraphs** earn nothing in Section A.
- **Not reaching the ending**: cover the beginning, middle and end, including the final telephone call.
- **AO4 slips**: "Priestly", "Shiela", missing capitals, "alot", "eachother".
:::

---

::: test
Explain how you would use the final telephone call in an answer about the Inspector. (4)
---
It comes after the hoax has apparently been exposed and the older characters relax (1). The call announces a real death and another inspector, so the Inspector seems to have known the future (1), adding to his mysterious, almost supernatural presentation (1). It makes his message permanent: those who failed to learn must face it again (1).
:::
