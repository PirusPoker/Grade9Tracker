## What it is

**Catrin** by Gillian Clarke is a mother speaking to her daughter. In the first stanza she remembers the day of the birth in a bare hospital room: the labour is their "first / Fierce confrontation", a struggle over the cord that joined them. In the second stanza the daughter is older, with long hair and a defiant look, asking if she can go skating in the dark for one more hour. The same struggle is still going on. The poem's central idea is that **love and conflict are bound together**: the tie that connects mother and child is also the thing they fight over.

**Stanza by stanza**

| Stanza | When | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The birth, remembered | In a hot, white, sterile room the mother watches traffic outside, then labours to give birth; mother and baby fight to become two people | "Fierce confrontation"; "Red rope of love"; "wild, tender circles"; "To be two" |
| 2 | Years later, the present | The struggle was never won or lost; the teenage daughter stands with a "Defiant glare" and asks to stay out skating | "Neither won nor lost"; "From the heart's pool"; "skate / In the dark" |

**Voice and speaker.** The mother speaks in the first person directly to "you, child". The **direct address** makes the poem intimate, like a letter or a private memory spoken aloud. The repeated "I can remember you" shows how vividly the birth stays with her.

**Key language**
- **"hot, white / Room"**: the hospital is clinical and uncomfortable; white suggests sterility and blankness. She watches the traffic "taking / Turn" at the lights, an image of order and permission (stop and go) that quietly foreshadows the later negotiation over going out.
- **"Fierce confrontation"**: the birth is described as a battle, not a tender moment. Clarke is honest about the pain and effort of labour.
- **"Red rope of love"**: the **umbilical cord** as a rope that both of them "Fought over", like a tug-of-war. Red suggests blood, life and passion; "rope" suggests both a lifeline and something to pull against. The alliteration of r sounds ties the words together, like the rope itself.
- **"Environmental blank"** and **"clean squares"**: the cold, institutional language of the room.
- **"wild, tender circles"**: an **oxymoron**. She "wrote all over the walls" with her words (her cries in labour, and perhaps her poetry), colouring the blank room. **Circles** (natural, curved, womb-like, endless) contrast with the hospital's **squares** (rigid and controlled).
- **"To be two"**: the shout of both mother and baby. Birth is the start of separation: each wants to be "ourselves".
- **"Neither won nor lost"**: the struggle did not end at birth; it continues, unresolved, through the child's life.
- **"glass tank clouded with feelings"**: an ambiguous image of the room, a cot or the womb, a space blurred by emotion.
- **"Defiant glare"** and **"straight, strong, long / Brown hair"**: the daughter is now confident and independent. The list of adjectives sounds admiring as well as exasperated; "rosy" keeps her childlike.
- **"From the heart's pool"**: the cord image returns. It now comes from the heart, an emotional bond rather than a physical one, and it is "Tightening about my life", almost a noose: love that also restricts.
- **"skate / In the dark"**: the ending is an ordinary teenage request, but darkness hints at danger and the unknown world the daughter is moving into. We never hear the mother's answer.

**Form and structure**
- **Two stanzas, two moments**: the birth (past tense, "I can remember") and the present ("Still I am fighting"). The structure shows that the conflict of the first stanza repeats in the second.
- **Free verse**: no regular rhyme or metre, like natural speech and the unpredictable feelings of motherhood. Occasional sound echoes (white, lights, tight) knit the first lines together.
- **Heavy enjambment**: sentences break across lines ("our first / Fierce confrontation", "the tight / Red rope"), so ideas strain over the line ends, like the pull of the rope. The **capital letter at the start of every line**, despite the run-on sentences, adds a tension between order and flow.
- **The rope as a structural link**: it appears in both stanzas, tying the birth to the present, just as the cord tied the two bodies.
- **The open ending**: the poem stops on the daughter's question, so the struggle stays unresolved, which is exactly its point.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Gillian Clarke** is a Welsh poet, **National Poet of Wales from 2008 to 2016**. Much of her writing is about family, women's lives, nature and Wales.
- **Catrin** is her own daughter; the name is the Welsh form of Catherine. The poem is personal and autobiographical, which explains its honesty about the less comfortable side of love.
- **Motherhood in poetry**: for centuries most published poets were men, and childbirth was rarely written about from the inside. Clarke writes about labour as an intense physical and emotional experience, a woman's perspective the traditional canon often missed.
- **The hospital setting**: the sterile room reflects modern medicalised birth, and the mother's "wild" words resisting its blankness suggest the human experience pushing against the institution.

**Themes**: parent and child; love and conflict; independence and separation; memory and time; motherhood; growing up.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **Kamikaze** (Garland): family relationships; Clarke's bond survives every conflict, Garland's family cuts the father off.
- **Origin Story** (Ewing): parents and children; how a family begins; love that is imperfect but treasured.
- **The Schoolboy** (Blake): a child who wants freedom, and an appeal to parents; Blake's child begs for it, Catrin demands it.
- **Sonnet 29** (Barrett Browning): love as an image of things entwined (vines round a tree, a rope) and a wish to be separate as well as close.
- **Blackberry Picking** (Heaney): childhood and growing up.

::: terms
- **Direct address**: speaking to someone as "you"; here the mother addresses her daughter.
- **Extended metaphor**: a comparison developed through a poem, like the rope of love.
- **Umbilical cord**: the cord joining a baby to its mother in the womb, cut at birth.
- **Oxymoron**: two contrasting words placed together, like "wild, tender".
- **Free verse**: poetry with no regular rhyme scheme or metre.
- **Enjambment**: a sentence running over a line break without punctuation.
- **Juxtaposition**: placing two contrasting things side by side, like squares and circles.
- **Autobiographical**: drawn from the writer's own life.
- **National Poet of Wales**: an honorary role representing Welsh poetry, held by Clarke 2008–2016.
:::

::: example The rope
Explain how Clarke uses the image of the rope. (4)

In the first stanza the "Red rope of love" is the umbilical cord, the physical tie between mother and baby (1). They "Fought over" it, so the rope is also a tug-of-war, showing love and conflict at once (1). In the second stanza it returns "From the heart's pool", now an emotional bond rather than a physical one (1). "Tightening about my life" suggests the bond can feel restricting, almost like a noose, so the image captures how love both connects and constrains (1).
:::

::: example Squares and circles
Explain the contrast between "clean squares" and "wild, tender circles". (3)

The squares are the sterile, rigid walls of the hospital room (1). The circles are the mother's emotional, physical experience, curved and natural like the womb (1). The oxymoron "wild, tender" shows that the experience of birth is violent and loving at once, and her words colour the blank institutional space (1).
:::

::: example The ending
Explain the effect of ending the poem with the daughter asking to skate in the dark. (3)

It is an everyday teenage request, which brings the poem down to ordinary family life (1). "In the dark" hints at danger and the unknown world the daughter wants to explore (1). The mother's answer is never given, so the struggle for independence stays unresolved, echoing "Neither won nor lost" (1).
:::
