## What it is

**Cousin Kate** by Christina Rossetti (published 1862) is spoken by a young woman from a cottage who was seduced by a "great lord", lived with him as his "plaything", and was then cast off when he married her cousin Kate instead. She is now an outcast; Kate is a lady. The poem is her bitter address to Kate, but it ends with a twist: the speaker has the lord's son, and Kate has given him no heir. It is a protest against a society that ruins a woman for what it forgives in a man.

**Stanza by stanza**

| Stanza | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A contented cottage girl; why did a great lord notice her? | "Why did a great lord find me out": repeated question, blaming him for seeking her out |
| 2 | Lured to his palace, used and discarded | "He wore me like a silken knot, / He changed me like a glove" |
| 3 | Direct address to Kate: he saw Kate, chose her, "cast me by" | "He lifted you from mean estate / To sit with him on high" |
| 4 | Kate is married and called "good and pure"; the speaker is "an outcast thing" | "Even so I sit and howl in dust, / You sit in gold and sing" |
| 5 | Defiance: her love was true, Kate's "writ in sand"; she would have spat at him | "I would have spit into his face" |
| 6 | The twist: she has a son; Kate has none, and the lord would give lands for an heir | "My fair-haired son, my shame, my pride" |

**Voice: a dramatic monologue.** The speaker is a character, not Rossetti, speaking to a silent listener (Kate). We hear only her side, which lets Rossetti give a voice to a kind of woman Victorian society silenced. Her tone moves through **regret** and **shame** (stanzas 1–2), **bitterness** (3–4), **defiance** (5) and something like **triumph** (6).

**Key language**
- **Similes of possession**: "He wore me like a silken knot, / He changed me like a glove". She was an accessory, put on and taken off at will; "changed" suggests swapping one glove for another, as he swaps her for Kate.
- **"His plaything and his love"**: the pairing exposes the lie; to him "love" meant a toy.
- **"shameless shameful"**: she was without shame at the time and full of it now; the near-repetition shows a self-judgement she has absorbed from society.
- **Bird imagery**: she "might have been a dove" (purity, peace) and is instead "an unclean thing". Kate "had the stronger wing": she was the better survivor, not the better person.
- **Dust and gold**: "I sit and howl in dust, / You sit in gold and sing". Parallel lines set poverty and disgrace against wealth and respect; "howl" makes her animal, "sing" makes Kate a contented caged bird.
- **"good and pure"**: repeated, first as the lord's reason for marrying Kate, then as the neighbours' verdict. The repetition becomes ironic: purity here is a label given by power.
- **"bound you with his ring"**: marriage is also a binding; Kate is owned too, only more respectably.
- **"Your love was writ in sand"**: a metaphor for shallow, impermanent love, against her own "true" love.
- **"my shame, my pride"**: the son is both. The paradox shows society's verdict and her own love side by side.
- **"coronet"**: a noble's small crown. Without a legitimate heir, the lord's title and lands have no future; the son he cannot claim is the one thing his power cannot buy.

**Form and structure**
- **Six eight-line stanzas**, with lines that alternate between longer and shorter, and the even lines rhyming on one sound in each stanza. The regular, ballad-like music suits a story of a country girl and makes it sound like a traditional song.
- **Rhetorical questions** open the poem ("Why did a great lord find me out") and return in stanza 4 ("Now which of us has tenderer heart?"): she demands justice from someone who cannot answer.
- **Direct address** ("O Lady Kate, my cousin Kate") makes the poem a confrontation, and the shift from "Lady Kate" to "cousin Kate" in stanza 5 brings Kate down to her level.
- **The turn at "Yet"** in the last stanza reverses the power: the outcast holds the "gift" the wife lacks. The poem ends on "coronet", the symbol of the lord's power, now dependent on her son.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Christina Rossetti (1830–1894)**, a devout Anglican poet, part of the circle of the Pre-Raphaelites (her brother was the painter Dante Gabriel Rossetti). The poem appeared in *Goblin Market and Other Poems* (1862).
- **The "fallen woman"**: Victorian society condemned a woman who had sex outside marriage as "fallen" and often unemployable or unmarriageable, while the man faced little consequence. Rossetti volunteered at a refuge in Highgate for such women, so she knew their stories.
- **The double standard** is the poem's target: the lord seduces, discards and marries; the neighbours call his wife pure and the girl an "outcast thing".
- **Class power**: the lord's wealth ("palace", "lands") lets him pick and drop women of "mean estate". Inheritance passed through legitimate sons, which is why the final lines strike at his deepest need.

**Themes**: love and betrayal; power and class; gender and the double standard; shame and pride; motherhood; jealousy and rivalry.

::: terms
- **Dramatic monologue**: a poem spoken by a character, not the poet, to a silent listener, revealing their situation and feelings.
- **Fallen woman**: a Victorian label for a woman who had sex outside marriage, treated as disgraced and outcast.
- **Double standard**: a rule applied more harshly to one group than another, here women more than men.
- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as", such as "He changed me like a glove".
- **Paradox**: a statement that seems contradictory but holds truth, like "my shame, my pride".
- **Juxtaposition**: placing contrasting things side by side, like dust and gold.
- **Irony**: a gap between what is said and what is meant or true, as in the neighbours calling Kate "good and pure".
- **Ballad**: a traditional song-like narrative poem with regular stanzas and rhyme.
:::

::: example The glove simile
Explain how "He wore me like a silken knot, / He changed me like a glove" presents the lord's treatment of the speaker. (4)

Both similes compare her to clothing accessories, things a rich man wears for decoration (1). "silken knot" suggests luxury and display: she was an ornament, and "knot" can be untied (1). "changed me like a glove" shows she was replaceable, swapped as casually as a glove, which foreshadows his marriage to Kate (1). The verbs "wore" and "changed" give him all the action, so the speaker is an object, reflecting how Victorian class and gender left a poor woman little power (1).
:::

::: example Dust and gold
Explain the effect of "Even so I sit and howl in dust, / You sit in gold and sing". (4)

The two lines are parallel ("I sit... You sit"), so the contrast is stark (1). "dust" suggests poverty, humiliation and mourning, while "gold" suggests wealth and status (1). "howl" makes her grief raw and animal, while "sing" makes Kate a contented bird in a gilded cage (1). The juxtaposition exposes the unfairness: the two women did the same thing in loving the lord, but marriage decides which one society honours (1).
:::

::: example The final stanza
Explain how the ending changes the balance of power. (4)

"Yet I've a gift you have not got" turns the poem with "Yet" (1). The gift is her son, whom she calls "my shame, my pride", a paradox that holds society's judgement and her love together (1). Kate has no child, and the lord "would give lands" for an heir to "wear his coronet" (1). So the outcast holds the one thing the powerful man cannot buy, and the poem ends on his weakness rather than hers (1).
:::

::: q

---
Her cousin Kate (1); the nobleman who seduced the speaker and then married Kate (1).
:::

::: q
What does "an unclean thing, / Who might have been a dove" suggest about how the speaker sees herself? (2)
---
She has absorbed society's judgement that she is impure and dehumanised ("thing") (1); the dove, a symbol of purity and peace, shows what she has lost (1).
:::

::: q
What form is the poem, and why does it suit the subject? (2)
---
A dramatic monologue (1); it gives a voice to a "fallen woman" whom Victorian society silenced, so we hear her side directly (1).
:::

::: q
What does "Your love was writ in sand" suggest? (2)
---
Kate's love is shallow and impermanent, like writing that the tide washes away (1), unlike the speaker's "true" love; it may also suggest Kate married for status (1).
:::

::: q
What would the speaker have done if she had been in Kate's place? (2)
---
She would have refused him (1), spitting in his face rather than taking his hand: defiance and pride (1).
:::

::: q
Why does the lord want the speaker's son? (2)
---
He has no legitimate heir with Kate (1), and needs a son to inherit his title, the "coronet", and lands (1).
