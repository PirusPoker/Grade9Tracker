## What it is

**The Schoolboy** by William Blake is a child's complaint: on a summer morning he wants to be outdoors with the birds, but he has to go to school, where joy dies. In the last two stanzas he turns to his parents with an argument: if you stunt a young plant in spring, how can it bear fruit in summer? The poem first appeared in *Songs of Innocence* (1789) and was later moved into *Songs of Experience*, which tells you how to read it: a child's innocent joy meeting the adult world that crushes it.

**Stanza by stanza**

| Stanza | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Joy: rising early in summer, with birds singing, a huntsman's horn and a skylark | "the sky-lark sings with me": the child is part of nature's choir |
| 2 | The turn: going to school "drives all joy away" | "Under a cruel eye outworn": a tired, harsh teacher; the "little ones" spend the day "In sighing and dismay" |
| 3 | The schoolroom from inside: drooping, anxious, unable to enjoy books | "Nor sit in learnings bower": learning should be a shady garden, but it is "Worn thro' with the dreary shower" |
| 4 | The caged bird: two questions about a creature made for joy being shut in | "How can the bird that is born for joy, / Sit in a cage and sing." |
| 5 | Direct appeal to "father & mother": buds nipped, blossoms blown away | The child becomes a plant stripped "Of their joy in the springing day" |
| 6 | The argument completed: without spring joy there is no summer fruit, no harvest, only winter | "When the blasts of winter appear": a ruined life |

**Voice and speaker.** The speaker is the schoolboy himself, in the first person. His language is simple and musical, like a song (these are *Songs*), with exclamations ("O! what sweet company"). That simplicity is Blake's method: the plainest voice delivers the sharpest criticism, and adults are made to hear it from the child.

**Key images**
- **Birdsong and the cage.** Stanza 1 shows the child singing *with* the skylark; stanza 4 asks how a bird "born for joy" can "Sit in a cage and sing". The school is the cage. "droop his tender wing" makes the child a fledgling that cannot fly.
- **Plants and seasons.** Buds "nip'd", blossoms "blown away", "tender plants" stripped: childhood is spring, and school is a frost that kills growth. The last stanza follows the logic through the year: no spring joy, no "summer fruits", no harvest to "gather", only "the blasts of winter". Education was meant to make children grow; Blake says this kind destroys the growth it claims to produce.
- **Weather and light.** Sunny "summer morn" against "the dreary shower" of the classroom: pathetic fallacy for the child's mood.
- **"cruel eye outworn".** The teacher is reduced to a watching eye: surveillance, not care. "outworn" suggests the teacher is exhausted too, and the system itself is worn out.

**Form and structure**
- **Six five-line stanzas rhyming ABABB.** The fifth line repeats the B rhyme, so each stanza ends with an extra echo, like a sigh or a song's refrain ("company", "dismay", "shower").
- **Short, mostly four-beat lines** give a song-like, childlike rhythm, which makes the misery more poignant.
- **The volta comes at stanza 2**: "But" turns from joy to school. The poem then moves from description (stanzas 1–3) to **argument** (stanzas 4–6), using **rhetorical questions**. The final question runs across two whole stanzas and is never answered: the parents, and the reader, must answer it.
- **Natural cycle as structure.** The poem starts on a summer morning and ends with winter: the whole year is compressed to show a life spoiled at its start.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Blake (1757–1827)**, a London engraver and poet, was a **Romantic**: he valued imagination, nature and the child's natural joy above reason, rules and institutions.
- **Songs of Innocence and of Experience** set two "contrary states of the human soul" side by side. Moving this poem to *Experience* suggests that school is where innocence is first damaged.
- **Education in Blake's time** was often rote learning enforced by harsh discipline; for many poor children there was little schooling at all, only work. Romantic thinkers (Rousseau's *Émile*, 1762) argued that children learn best through freedom and nature, an idea the poem shares.
- **Blake distrusted institutions** (church, state, school) that control people "for their own good". The poem's appeal to parents asks them to protect children from that control.

**Themes**: childhood and innocence; nature and freedom; education and institutions; parents' responsibility; growth and the seasons of life.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **Catrin** (Clarke): parent and child, a child struggling for freedom; Blake's child pleads with parents, Clarke's mother struggles with a child.
- **Blackberry Picking** (Heaney): childhood joy and disappointment; nature that spoils; both end with a sense of loss.
- **I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud** (Wordsworth): Romantic joy in nature, freedom outdoors; Wordsworth's joy lasts in memory, Blake's is destroyed.
- **Decomposition** (Ghose): society's treatment of the powerless.

::: terms
- **Romanticism**: a late 18th- and early 19th-century movement valuing nature, emotion, imagination and the innocence of childhood.
- **Songs of Innocence and of Experience**: Blake's paired collections showing the child's joyful view and the darker adult world.
- **Extended metaphor**: a comparison developed across several lines, like the child as a growing plant in stanzas 5 and 6.
- **Rhetorical question**: a question asked for effect, not to get an answer, used here to make the parents think.
- **Pathetic fallacy**: weather or nature reflecting feeling, like the classroom's "dreary shower".
- **Volta**: the turn in a poem's argument or mood, here the "But" at the start of stanza 2.
- **Refrain-like rhyme**: the repeated B rhyme in each fifth line that echoes like a song.
- **Rote learning**: memorising by repetition without understanding, common in schools of Blake's time.
:::

::: method
1. **Start with an overview**: the poem argues that school destroys the joy and growth it should nourish.
2. **Track the structure**: joy (stanza 1), turn (stanza 2), schoolroom (3), caged bird (4), plant and seasons (5–6).
3. **Analyse the two image patterns** (bird and plant) as Blake's case against the school.
4. **Use form**: song-like stanzas, the extra rhyme line, the unanswered final question.
5. **Weave in context**: Romantic belief in childhood and nature, distrust of institutions, harsh schooling of the time, the move from Innocence to Experience.
6. **For 7.2**, choose a poem on childhood, nature or parents and compare in every paragraph.
:::

::: example The caged bird
Explain how Blake uses the image of the caged bird in stanza 4. (4)

"How can the bird that is born for joy, / Sit in a cage and sing." turns the child into a songbird whose nature is freedom (1). "born for joy" suggests joy is a child's natural, God-given state, not a reward (1). The cage stands for the school, so learning becomes imprisonment (1). The question is rhetorical: a caged bird cannot truly sing, so the child cannot truly learn when shut in, which is Blake's Romantic argument against forced education (1).
:::

::: example The seasonal argument
Explain how the final two stanzas develop the poem's argument. (4)

The child speaks directly to "father & mother", turning complaint into appeal (1). He uses an extended metaphor of plants: if "buds are nip'd" in spring, there will be no "summer fruits" (1). The logic follows the seasons to the "blasts of winter", so a damaged childhood means a barren adult life (1). The question is never answered, leaving the parents, and Blake's readers, responsible for the outcome (1).
:::

::: example Context woven into analysis
Write a sentence that links the "cruel eye outworn" to Blake's context. (2)

"Blake, a Romantic who distrusted institutions, reduces the teacher to a single 'cruel eye' (1), suggesting that the schools of his day watched and disciplined children rather than nurturing them, and 'outworn' implies the whole system is as tired and out of date as the teacher (1)."
:::

::: example A comparative topic sentence
Write a topic sentence comparing The Schoolboy and Blackberry Picking on childhood. (3)

"Both poems present childhood as a time of joy in nature that is spoiled (1), but in The Schoolboy the joy is crushed from outside by adults and their school, while in Blackberry Picking it decays from within, as the children's own greed and nature's rot teach them disappointment (1); Blake's Romantic protest becomes, in Heaney's twentieth-century memory, a quieter lesson about growing up (1)."
:::

Stanzas 1–3 describe joy outdoors and misery in school (1); stanza 4 asks questions using the bird image (1); stanzas 5–6 make an argument to the parents through the plant and seasons metaphor, ending unanswered (1).
