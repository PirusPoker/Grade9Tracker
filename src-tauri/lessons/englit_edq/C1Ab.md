## What it is

The first Shakespeare question on Component 1 prints an extract of about 30–40 lines and always asks the same thing: **look at how the characters speak and behave here, and how you think an audience might respond to this part of the play**, referring closely to the extract. It is worth **15 marks** and you have **about 20 minutes**. The method below works for any of the six set plays; it is shown here on *Macbeth*, which in recent papers is question 2 (2.1 extract, 2.2 essay).

**What the 15 marks reward.** AO1 (your response, with references) and AO2 (language, form and structure) are weighted equally, in five bands of three marks. **AO3 context is not assessed** here, and **AO4 accuracy is not marked** on this question (it is on the essay). Examiners reward three things above all:
- **Coverage**: answers that work through the *whole* extract, to the last lines, do better than ones that stop halfway.
- **Close reading**: individual words, images, verse, rhythm and stage directions, explained for their effect.
- **Audience**: what an audience might feel or think, and why, ideally with more than one possible reaction.

**Stay inside the extract.** The question is about *this part of the play*. A sentence placing the moment (what has just happened, what comes next) helps, and a brief link forward (Lady Macbeth's "A little water" in Act 2 against her hand-washing in Act 5) can be rewarded, but the marks are for analysing the printed lines. Save the whole play for the essay.

**The rubric trap.** Answer **both questions on the one play you studied**. Every year examiners report a minority of students who answer extract questions on several plays. Answers on a play you have not studied are marked as unseen and score very little, and they steal time from your real answers.

**The 20-minute method**

| Minutes | Step | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | **Read and place** | Read the extract once. Note who is on stage, where it falls (act, what just happened) and who has the power. |
| 3–5 | **Find the shape** | Mark where it turns: a change of mood, an entrance, a shift in who speaks most, a final line that changes things. |
| 5–19 | **Write in order** | Work through the extract in sections (or character by character), 3–4 paragraphs, each with short quotations, a method and an audience response. |
| 19–20 | **Reach the end** | Make sure you have dealt with the closing lines: they often hold the scene's sting. |

**What to look for: a toolkit**

| Feature | What to notice | Macbeth example |
|---|---|---|
| **Word choice** | Connotations of single words | "Infirm of purpose!": Lady Macbeth calls him sick and weak |
| **Imagery** | Patterns of blood, darkness, sleep, clothes, animals | "full of scorpions is my mind" |
| **Sentence forms** | Questions, imperatives, exclamations, short and long sentences | Lady Macbeth's commands against Macbeth's questions |
| **Verse and prose** | Who speaks blank verse, rhyme or prose, and when it changes | The sleepwalking scene in prose |
| **Rhythm and metre** | Regular iambic pentameter for control; broken or short lines for panic | Short, jagged lines just after the murder |
| **Shared lines** | One character finishes another's line of verse | Closeness, or one character interrupting and taking control |
| **Stage directions and entrances** | Knocking, exits, a ghost, who is left alone | The knocking at the gate in Act 2 Scene 2 |
| **Dramatic devices** | Aside, soliloquy, dramatic irony, foreshadowing | Banquo's suspicion while Macbeth is "rapt" |
| **Audience response** | Horror, pity, tension, laughter, judgement; a Jacobean audience and a modern one | Fear of the witches then; perhaps fascination now |

**Writing about the audience.** The question asks how an audience *might* respond, so tentative, layered responses score best: "An audience may be horrified by her coldness, yet some might also feel she is the more frightened of the two, holding the plan together because Macbeth cannot." One clause of context can sharpen this ("for an audience who believed in witches"), but a paragraph of history earns nothing.

**Characters as constructs.** Top answers talk about what *Shakespeare* does with a character: "Shakespeare gives Lady Macbeth the imperatives here, so the power has shifted to her." Treat characters as complex, not as one-word labels: the 2026 report praised answers that gave both Macbeths equal attention and saw them as more than villains.

::: terms
- **Extract question**: the first Section A question, on a printed passage, asking how the characters speak and behave and how an audience might respond.
- **Iambic pentameter**: a line of ten syllables in five unstressed-stressed pairs, the regular rhythm of Shakespeare's verse.
- **Blank verse**: unrhymed iambic pentameter, used by most noble characters when in control of themselves.
- **Prose**: ordinary speech without metre, used by lower-status or comic characters and by characters whose minds are breaking.
- **Shared line**: a line of verse split between two speakers, showing closeness, urgency or one character cutting in.
- **Rhyming couplet**: two rhyming lines, often used to close a scene or make a decision sound final.
- **Stage direction**: an instruction in the script about action, entrances, exits or sound effects.
- **Aside**: a remark to the audience that other characters on stage do not hear.
- **Soliloquy**: a speech by a character alone on stage, giving the audience direct access to their thoughts.
- **Rubric**: the exam instructions; here, answer both questions on one play only.
:::

::: method
1. **Place it** in one or two sentences: act and scene if you know them, what has just happened, what is at stake.
2. **Find the shape**: where does the mood, the power or the speaker change? Use that to divide the extract into three or four sections.
3. **Work through in order**, giving each section a paragraph. Quote two to six words at a time and say what the words, images or forms do.
4. **Use the drama**: stage directions, entrances, exits, shared or broken lines, who is silent, who speaks most.
5. **Give audience responses** in every paragraph, and more than one where you can (pity and horror; then and now).
6. **Reach the last lines.** Finish on how the extract ends and how that leaves the audience feeling.
7. **No context paragraphs, no whole-play essay**, and answer only on your own play.
:::

::: example Placing an extract in two sentences
The extract is from Act 1 Scene 3, where the witches have just greeted Macbeth as Thane of Glamis, Thane of Cawdor and future king. Write the opening of your answer. (2)

"This is the first meeting between Macbeth, Banquo and the witches on the heath, moments after the witches have hailed Macbeth with three titles, one of which (king) he can only gain by Duncan's death (1). In the extract the two friends respond very differently, and Shakespeare uses that contrast to show the audience the temptation taking hold of Macbeth before he has said a word about murder (1)."
:::

::: example A top-band paragraph on the same extract
Banquo asks Macbeth "why do you start, and seem to fear / Things that do sound so fair?" Write a paragraph on how Macbeth behaves and how an audience might respond. (6)

"Shakespeare shows Macbeth's reaction through another character's eyes: Banquo notices that he 'start[s]', a sudden physical flinch that suggests the prophecy has touched a thought already in his mind (1). The word 'fear' is striking, because the prophecies are good news; an audience may sense that Macbeth fears what he would have to do to make them true (1). Banquo's phrase 'sound so fair' recalls the witches' 'Fair is foul', so the audience is reminded that these fair words are dangerous (1). Banquo then says Macbeth seems 'rapt', lost in his own thoughts, which stages a silence at the centre of the scene (1). Macbeth's later 'Stay, you imperfect speakers, tell me more' turns him from frightened to hungry, the imperatives showing he wants control of the future (1). A Jacobean audience, who believed witches could tempt the soul, might fear for him; a modern audience might feel the tension of watching ambition wake up (1)."

Why it scores: it follows the extract in order, quotes briefly, names methods (word choice, echo, silence, imperatives) and gives two audience responses.
:::

::: example A middle-band paragraph and how to lift it
A student writes: "In this extract Macbeth is scared of the witches. He says 'tell me more' which shows he wants to know more. The witches are evil and in Shakespeare's time people believed in witches and King James wrote a book about them, so the audience would be scared." Explain what band this paragraph is likely to reach and how to improve it. (4)

It is likely to sit in Band 2 (4–6): it has some focus and a reference, but the comment on "tell me more" repeats the quotation rather than analysing it (1). The context sentence is history, not analysis, and earns no AO3 marks here (1). To improve: analyse the imperative "Stay" and the insult "imperfect speakers", which show Macbeth trying to command supernatural beings (1); and make the audience response specific, e.g. tension because we see him wanting the prophecy, not just fearing it (1).
:::

::: example Using a stage direction
In Act 2 Scene 2 Lady Macbeth exits to return the daggers and a knocking is heard. Explain how Shakespeare uses this moment. (4)

Lady Macbeth's exit leaves Macbeth alone on stage, so his short speech becomes a near-soliloquy in which the audience sees his guilt without her control (1). The stage direction for knocking breaks the silence of the night: Macbeth asks "Whence is that knocking?", showing he is terrified by every sound (1). His question "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" uses hyperbole to show guilt too large to clean (1). The repeated knocking keeps the threat of discovery growing, so the audience's tension rises to the end of the scene (1).
:::

Give two possible audience responses to Lady Macbeth in the sleepwalking scene. (2)

::: watch
- **One play only.** Answer both Section A questions on your own play; extracts from other plays score almost nothing.
- **Cover the whole extract.** Stopping halfway is the commonest reason for a capped mark.
- **Analyse, do not paraphrase.** "He says he wants to know more" repeats; "the imperative 'Stay' shows him trying to command the witches" analyses.
- **No context paragraphs.** One clause at most.
- **Read names carefully.** Examiners report students confusing Lady Macduff with Lady Macbeth.
- **Do not wander into the rest of the play.** A brief link is fine; an essay on Act 5 is not.
:::

::: test
What is a soliloquy? (1)
---
A speech made by a character alone on stage, revealing their thoughts to the audience.
:::

::: test
What is an aside, and how does it differ from a soliloquy? (2)
---
A short remark to the audience that other characters on stage do not hear (1); a soliloquy is spoken alone on stage, while an aside is made with others present (1).
:::

::: test
Name the regular metre of Shakespeare's verse and say what a break in it can show. (2)
---
Iambic pentameter (1); a broken or short line can show panic, shock or loss of control (1).
:::

::: test
Why might Shakespeare give a character prose instead of verse? (2)
---
Prose suits lower-status or comic characters such as the Porter (1), or a mind that has lost control, like Lady Macbeth sleepwalking (1).

::: test
Read these lines from Act 1 Scene 3. The witches have just greeted Macbeth.

BANQUO: Good sir, why do you start, and seem to fear
Things that do sound so fair? I' th' name of truth,
Are ye fantastical, or that indeed
Which outwardly ye show? My noble partner
You greet with present grace and great prediction
Of noble having and of royal hope,
That he seems rapt withal. To me you speak not.

How does Banquo speak and behave here, and how might an audience respond? (5)
---
Award 1 mark per developed point, up to 5: Banquo questions the witches directly and boldly, unlike the silent Macbeth (1); "fantastical" and "outwardly" show he suspects their appearance may deceive, linking to appearance and reality (1); he notices and reports Macbeth's reaction ("start", "fear", "rapt"), guiding the audience to watch Macbeth (1); the balanced, controlled verse suggests a clear head (1); "To me you speak not" shows curiosity and perhaps a touch of envy, so he is tempted too (1); the audience may trust Banquo as a reliable observer and fear for Macbeth (1).
:::

::: test
Read these lines from Act 2 Scene 1. Macbeth is alone, waiting for the signal to kill Duncan.

Is this a dagger which I see before me,
The handle toward my hand? Come, let me clutch thee.
I have thee not, and yet I see thee still.
Art thou not, fatal vision, sensible
To feeling as to sight? or art thou but
A dagger of the mind, a false creation,
Proceeding from the heat-oppressed brain?

Explain how Shakespeare presents Macbeth's state of mind in these lines. (6)
---
Up to 6 for developed points such as: the opening question shows confusion and uncertainty (1); the dagger points "toward my hand", as if inviting him, which suggests temptation or fate (1); the imperative "Come, let me clutch thee" shows desire, while "I have thee not" shows failure, a mind pulled two ways (1); he wonders if it is "A dagger of the mind", so he half-knows it comes from his own guilty imagination (1); "heat-oppressed brain" suggests fever and pressure (1); as a soliloquy it lets the audience see his torment, building tension and perhaps pity before the murder (1); the run of questions slows the action, so the audience waits in dread (1).
:::

::: test
Read these lines from Act 3 Scene 1. Macbeth is now king, alone before he meets the murderers.

To be thus is nothing,
But to be safely thus. Our fears in Banquo
Stick deep, and in his royalty of nature
Reigns that which would be feared.

Upon my head they placed a fruitless crown,
And put a barren sceptre in my gripe,
Thence to be wrenched with an unlineal hand,
No son of mine succeeding.

Explain how Shakespeare presents Macbeth's feelings about being king. (5)
---
Up to 5: "To be thus is nothing" shows the crown has not brought peace (1); "safely" shows his kingship is ruled by fear (1); "royalty of nature" admits Banquo has the natural nobility he lacks (1); "fruitless crown" and "barren sceptre" are images of sterility: he has no heir, so his crime gains nothing lasting (1); "wrenched" is violent, imagining his sceptre torn away (1); the audience may see his envy turning to murderous intent, and dread Banquo's fate (1).
:::

::: test
Read the extract below from Act 4 Scene 3. In England, Ross has come to tell Macduff what has happened to his family.

ROSS: Your castle is surprised; your wife and babes
Savagely slaughtered. To relate the manner,
Were, on the quarry of these murdered deer,
To add the death of you.
MALCOLM: Merciful heaven!
What, man! ne'er pull your hat upon your brows;
Give sorrow words. The grief that does not speak
Whispers the o'er-fraught heart and bids it break.
MACDUFF: My children too?
ROSS: Wife, children, servants, all
That could be found.
MACDUFF: And I must be from thence!
My wife killed too?
ROSS: I have said.
MALCOLM: Be comforted.
Let's make us medicines of our great revenge,
To cure this deadly grief.
MACDUFF: He has no children. All my pretty ones?
Did you say all? O hell-kite! All?
What, all my pretty chickens and their dam
At one fell swoop?
MALCOLM: Dispute it like a man.
MACDUFF: I shall do so;
But I must also feel it as a man:
I cannot but remember such things were,
That were most precious to me. Did heaven look on,
And would not take their part? Sinful Macduff,
They were all struck for thee! naught that I am,
Not for their own demerits, but for mine,
Fell slaughter on their souls. Heaven rest them now!
MALCOLM: Be this the whetstone of your sword: let grief
Convert to anger; blunt not the heart, enrage it.

Look at how the characters speak and behave here. How do you think an audience might respond to this part of the play? Refer closely to details from the extract to support your answer.

(15)
---
AO1 and AO2 equally.
- Band 5 (13–15): perceptive and evaluative; tracks the whole extract to Malcolm's last lines; precise references; close analysis of language, verse, questions and shared lines; several audience responses explored.
- Band 4 (10–12): thoughtful and secure; well-chosen references; methods increasingly analysed; clear audience response.
- Band 3 (7–9): straightforward and relevant; appropriate references; some analysis of methods.
- Band 2 (4–6): some focus and references; simple comments; may retell.
- Band 1 (1–3): limited, general comments.

Indicative content:
- Ross's blunt, brutal news ("Savagely slaughtered") after earlier hesitation; the hunting image of "murdered deer" makes the family helpless prey.
- Malcolm's practical advice ("Give sorrow words") and rhyming couplet; his quick turn to "revenge" may seem cold or wise.
- Macduff's short, repeated questions ("My children too?", "All?") and broken lines show shock; the repetition of "all".
- The bird imagery: "hell-kite", "pretty chickens and their dam", "fell swoop" turn Macbeth into a predator.
- "He has no children": possibly about Malcolm (who cannot understand) or Macbeth (who cannot be paid back in kind); either reading rewarded.
- The debate over manhood: "Dispute it like a man" against "I must also feel it as a man", the shared line and repetition.
- Macduff's guilt ("Sinful Macduff") for leaving them; his religious questioning.
- Malcolm's "whetstone" image turns grief into a weapon, preparing the final battle.
- Audience: deep pity; anger at Macbeth's tyranny; respect for Macduff as a man who feels; perhaps unease at Malcolm's speed.
:::

::: test
Read the extract below from Act 5 Scene 1. A Doctor and a Gentlewoman watch Lady Macbeth walking in her sleep, carrying a candle.

DOCTOR: You see, her eyes are open.
GENTLEWOMAN: Ay, but their sense is shut.
DOCTOR: What is it she does now? Look, how she rubs her hands.
GENTLEWOMAN: It is an accustomed action with her, to seem thus washing her hands: I have known her continue in this a quarter of an hour.
LADY MACBETH: Yet here's a spot.
DOCTOR: Hark! she speaks: I will set down what comes from her, to satisfy my remembrance the more strongly.
LADY MACBETH: Out, damned spot! out, I say! One: two: why, then, 'tis time to do't. Hell is murky! Fie, my lord, fie! a soldier, and afeard? What need we fear who knows it, when none can call our power to account? Yet who would have thought the old man to have had so much blood in him.
DOCTOR: Do you mark that?
LADY MACBETH: The Thane of Fife had a wife: where is she now? What, will these hands ne'er be clean? No more o' that, my lord, no more o' that: you mar all with this starting.
DOCTOR: Go to, go to; you have known what you should not.
GENTLEWOMAN: She has spoke what she should not, I am sure of that: heaven knows what she has known.
LADY MACBETH: Here's the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand. Oh, oh, oh!
DOCTOR: What a sigh is there! The heart is sorely charged.

Look at how the characters speak and behave here. How do you think an audience might respond to this part of the play? Refer closely to details from the extract to support your answer.

(15)
---
AO1 and AO2 equally. Bands as for the extract question: 13–15 perceptive, evaluative, whole extract, precise analysis and varied audience responses; 10–12 thoughtful, well-chosen references, methods increasingly analysed; 7–9 straightforward, some analysis; 4–6 simple comments, some references; 1–3 limited.

Indicative content:
- The watchers frame the scene: their calm, questioning prose and the Doctor writing things down make the audience witnesses too; the paradox of open eyes and shut "sense".
- The repeated hand-washing, an "accustomed action", shows guilt as a compulsion.
- Lady Macbeth's fragmented prose, exclamations and jumps in time replay the murder ("One: two"), her taunts to Macbeth ("a soldier, and afeard?") and later crimes.
- "Hell is murky": she now fears damnation, against her Act 1 call for "thick night".
- "so much blood in him": the horror she suppressed resurfaces.
- "The Thane of Fife had a wife": the nursery-rhyme rhythm and the knowledge of Lady Macduff's murder, which Macbeth kept from her, show she has guessed it.
- Reversal of "A little water": now not even the "perfumes of Arabia" can clean "this little hand"; the senses of smell and sight; the sighs ("Oh, oh, oh!").
- Audience: pity for a broken woman; fear; a sense of justice; awareness that the watchers now know the truth, so danger for the Macbeths.
:::

::: test
In Act 2 Scene 2, Lady Macbeth says "Infirm of purpose! / Give me the daggers." Explain how this presents her and how an audience might respond. (4)
---
"Infirm" means sick or weak, so she treats Macbeth's horror as a feebleness (1); the exclamation and imperative show her taking command (1); she takes on the practical task he cannot face, returning to the bloody scene (1); the audience may be shocked by her coldness, or see that she is holding together a plan that is falling apart (1).
:::
