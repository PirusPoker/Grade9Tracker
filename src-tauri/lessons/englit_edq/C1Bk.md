## What it is

**Kamikaze** by Beatrice Garland tells the story of a Japanese pilot in the Second World War who sets off on a suicide mission and **turns back**. We never hear him speak. His daughter, years later, tells his story to her own children, imagining what he saw from the cockpit: fishing boats, shoals of fish, the sea of his childhood. Those memories seem to draw him home. But when he returns, his wife and the neighbours treat him as if he does not exist, and in time even his children learn to be silent. The poem ends with the daughter's guess that he must have wondered **which way of dying was better**: in the attack, or in the living death of being shunned.

**Stanza by stanza (in sections)**

| Section | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| The departure | He sets off at sunrise with water, a samurai sword and ritual chants in his head, with fuel for one way only | "embarked at sunrise"; "a one-way / journey into history" |
| The view from the plane | Halfway, the daughter imagines, he looked down at fishing boats and shoals of fish shining in the sun | "strung out like bunting"; "the dark shoals of fishes" |
| The memory of childhood | He remembered building pebble cairns on the shore with his brothers, waiting for their father's boat to bring home its catch | "cairns of pearl-grey pebbles"; "yes, grandfather's boat"; "the dark prince" |
| The return and the silence | He came back; his wife never spoke to him again or met his eyes; the neighbours ignored him; the children slowly learned silence too | "my mother never spoke again"; "he no longer existed"; "learned / to be silent" |
| The final thought | Perhaps he wondered which death was better | "the better way to die" |

**Voice and speaker.** The poem has **layers of voice**. A narrator tells us what a daughter told her children ("she thought", "she said"). Most of the flight is the daughter **imagining** her father's thoughts, which is why it is full of "must have". Late in the poem the daughter's own words take over in the first person ("my mother"), so the family's rejection is told by someone who lived through it. The pilot himself never speaks: he is silenced in the poem as he was in his family.

**Key language**
- **"embarked at sunrise"**: the rising sun is the emblem of Japan, so the start of the mission is tied to the nation. "embarked" sounds like the start of a voyage, not a death.
- **samurai sword** and **"powerful incantations"**: tradition, honour and ritual. The "shaven head" filled with chants suggests he has been prepared, almost programmed, for sacrifice.
- **"a one-way / journey into history"**: the enjambment leaves "one-way" hanging; "history" is how propaganda framed the mission, as glory and immortality.
- **"strung out like bunting"**: the boats look like celebration flags, as if welcoming a hero home. The simile is full of life and festivity, the opposite of the mission.
- **"green-blue translucent sea"**: clear, beautiful, peaceful colour; the natural world calls him back to life.
- **"the dark shoals of fishes"**, moving like a flag "waved first one way" and back: the shoal is like a national flag, but its swinging back and forth also mirrors his **indecision**. The fish "flashing silver" turn towards the sun, as he turns towards home.
- **"cairns of pearl-grey pebbles"**: a childhood game with his brothers, testing whose pile could survive the waves. The memory of family and play is what turns him round.
- **"yes, grandfather's boat"**: the daughter interrupts herself to remind her children who this was. The dash-framed aside brings the story into the present and shows three generations linked.
- **"the dark prince, muscular, dangerous"**: the tuna, a powerful catch. It can be read as a symbol of the fighter he was meant to be, or of the danger beneath the beautiful sea.
- **"my mother never spoke again"**: the shift to first person and plain statement. Silence is the family's punishment.
- **"he no longer existed"**: he is socially dead. Surviving has cost him everything a death in the attack would have kept: honour and belonging.
- **"learned / to be silent"**: the children first "chattered and laughed", then were taught silence. Shame is passed on, and the enjambment makes the reader pause on the lesson.
- **"the better way to die"**: the final line equates his return with a slow death. The poem's real conflict is the silence afterwards, not the flight.

**Form and structure**
- **Regular six-line stanzas without rhyme.** The steady blocks give a controlled frame, while the lack of rhyme keeps the voice like spoken storytelling.
- **Long, enjambed sentences** run across stanzas during the flight and the memory, like a flowing daydream that carries him (and us) back to the shore.
- **A turn after the memory**: "And though he came back" moves abruptly from the beautiful past to the cold present. The sea's abundance is followed by silence and absence.
- **Shift in narrative voice**: third person (the narrator), then the daughter's imagined account, then her first-person memory. The shift makes the ending more personal and painful.
- **No direct speech from the pilot**: the poem enacts the silence it describes.
- **Circular framing**: the poem begins with the narrator and ends with "she said", reminding us this is a story handed down, partly guessed, never confirmed.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Kamikaze pilots** flew aircraft loaded with explosives into enemy (mostly American) warships in the final stage of the **Second World War** (1944–45). The word means **"divine wind"**, recalling storms said to have saved Japan from invasion centuries earlier.
- Japanese military culture drew on the **samurai code of honour (bushido)**: dying for the Emperor and the nation was glorious; surrender or failure was deeply shameful. That is why the family's silence is so total: he has shamed them.
- A pilot who returned could be treated as a disgrace. Garland imagines the human cost of that code: a man who chose life is denied it by the people he chose it for.
- The poem is a **modern poem looking back**: written decades later, it invites a reader to question the honour code and to sympathise with the man rather than the mission.

**Themes**: conflict and its aftermath; family and rejection; honour and shame; nature and memory; choice and free will; silence; life and death.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **Disabled** (Owen): a young soldier rejected after war. Owen's soldier is ignored by women and the town; Garland's pilot by his wife and neighbours. Both lose belonging.
- **Remains** (Armitage): the aftermath of conflict lasting long after the event.
- **Catrin** (Clarke): a parent and child, told from the child's side in Kamikaze and the mother's in Catrin; family bonds tested.
- **Drummer Hodge** (Hardy): a young man taken by war; Hodge dies far from home, the pilot returns home and is treated as dead.
- **Cousin Kate** (Rossetti): a person cast out by society for breaking its code.

::: terms
- **Kamikaze**: Japanese for "divine wind"; Second World War suicide missions by pilots flying into enemy ships.
- **Bushido**: the samurai code of honour, loyalty and readiness to die, used in Japanese military culture.
- **Narrative voice**: the person telling a story; here it shifts between a narrator and the daughter.
- **Third-person narration**: telling a story using "he" and "she" rather than "I".
- **Enjambment**: a sentence running across a line or stanza break without punctuation.
- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as", such as "strung out like bunting".
- **Symbolism**: using an object to stand for an idea, like the rising sun for Japan.
- **Ostracism**: being excluded and ignored by a family or community.
- **Juxtaposition**: placing contrasting things side by side, like the lively sea and the silent home.
:::

::: example The natural imagery
Explain how Garland uses natural imagery to show why the pilot turns back. (4)

The fishing boats "strung out like bunting" look like flags of celebration, suggesting welcome and life (1). The "green-blue translucent sea" is calm and beautiful, a peaceful contrast to the mission (1). The fish moving like a flag waved "first one way" and then back mirror his indecision (1). These images lead into memories of the shore and his brothers, suggesting that nature and family remind him of the life he is about to throw away (1).
:::

::: example The silence
Explain how the poem presents the family's response to his return. (4)

The daughter says "my mother never spoke again" in his presence, a total withdrawal (1). The neighbours treated him as if "he no longer existed", so he becomes socially dead (1). The children at first "chattered and laughed" but "learned / to be silent", showing that shame is taught and passed on (1). The silence is a punishment based on the honour code: by surviving he has disgraced them (1).
:::

::: example The narrative voice
Explain the effect of telling the story through the daughter. (3)

Much of the flight is what she "thought" he "must have" seen, so it is imagined, not known (1). This shows the pilot never explained himself, linking to the silence imposed on him (1). The shift to her first person ("my mother") makes the rejection personal, as she was part of it (1).
:::

::: example Context woven into analysis
Write two sentences linking the samurai sword to context. (2)

"The 'samurai sword' carried in the cockpit links the mission to the bushido code, in which death for the Emperor was honourable and failure shameful (1). This explains why his family treats his return as a disgrace, and why Garland, writing much later, invites us to question a code that values a man's death over his life (1)."
:::
