## What it is

**Origin Story** by Eve L. Ewing tells how the speaker's parents met, and what kind of love they had. The first stanza is the story, told like a true anecdote: two young people in **Chicago in the mid-1980s**, a meeting at the Greyhound bus station, a father who drew comic books and a mother who bought one. The second stanza turns the story into an **extended metaphor**: love is like a comic book, fragile paper that can be protected in plastic and kept for decades, or read and reread until it falls apart. Her parents' love was the second kind. It did not last, but it was handled, enjoyed and "memorized", and the poem's final words insist that it **has a good ending**.

**Stanza by stanza**

| Stanza | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "This is true": the parents meet at a Chicago bus station in the mid-eighties. The mother came west at nineteen, cared for a friend's children, played tambourine in a cover band; the father ran away at seventeen, copied radical newspapers and drew comic books for sale. She bought one. | "This is true:"; "thick glass and afro puff"; "sleeveless and soft eye"; "my mother bought one" |
| 2 | Love is like a comic book: fragile, best protected in plastic and boxes so it might survive. Her parents' love was never protected; it was carried, lent, read and reread until it wore out. It did not last, but it ended well. | "like a comic book"; "love is paper"; "never saw polyvinyl"; "memorized, mishandled, worn thin"; "a good ending" |

**Voice and speaker.** A first-person speaker, the child of the couple, tells their story. The voice is **conversational and affectionate**, sometimes wry. She was not there when they met, so the first stanza is the family legend as she has received it; the second is her own reflection, as an adult who understands that love can end and still matter.

**Key language**
- **"This is true:"**: the opening insists on truth, like the start of an anecdote, and also like the claim at the start of a comic's origin story. It invites us to trust the story and hints that it sounds almost too good to be true.
- **"thick glass and afro puff"** and **"sleeveless and soft eye"**: snapshot descriptions in which one or two details stand for a whole person, like a comic-book character drawn in a few lines. "soft eye" suggests the father's gentleness.
- **The parents' backgrounds**: the mother came west on the train at nineteen and played tambourine in a **Chaka Khan** cover band; the father ran away at seventeen and **mimeographed** communist newspapers. They are young, independent, creative and a little rebellious: ordinary people living vivid lives.
- **"comic books / like this one"**: a striking self-reference. "this one" could mean the poem itself, as if the poem were the comic and we were holding it. The title borrows the comic-book term **origin story**, the tale of how a superhero came to be. The speaker's origin is her parents' meeting.
- **"my mother bought one"**: a plain, short line that is the whole romance in four words. The understatement is funny and tender.
- **"like a comic book"** and **"it's fragile"**: the simile launches the extended metaphor. Love, like paper, can tear, fade and wear out.
- **"plastic and cardboard"**, dark rooms and boxes: the ways collectors protect comics. The image suggests that keeping love "intact" means shutting it away, safe but unread.
- **"never meant to last"**: paper and love are not built for permanence; surviving is an achievement.
- **"love is paper."**: a short, flat metaphor stated as fact, the poem's central claim.
- **"never saw polyvinyl"**, never had a backing board: the technical language of comic collecting (plastic bags and cardboard boards). Her parents' love was never protected or preserved.
- **The list of uses**: curled into a back pocket, lent to a friend, read under covers, reread hanging upside-down, **"memorized, mishandled, worn thin"**, staples rusted. Each image is of a comic that was **loved by being used**. "memorized" shows how well they knew it; "mishandled" admits mistakes; "worn thin" shows it was used up.
- **"doesn't last"** and **"a good ending"**: the final line balances loss and value. The love ended, but it was fully lived and enjoyed; and the "good ending" may also be the speaker herself, the story's result.

**Form and structure**
- **Two stanzas: story, then reflection.** The first is narrative and full of details; the second turns to metaphor and meaning. The structure moves from what happened to what it means, like a parable.
- **Free verse** in a **conversational** rhythm, with long lists and short punchy lines ("my mother bought one", "love is paper").
- **Lower-case letters** at the start of sentences ("my mother", "love is", "a love like that") make the poem informal and intimate, like handwriting or comic-book lettering, and stop anything sounding grand.
- **Parallel structure** in the first stanza ("my mother, all ..." / "my father, all ...") presents the parents as two equal characters introduced side by side before they meet.
- **The list in the second stanza** builds to a climax of wear ("staples rusted"), then the turn "but" in the last line reverses the sadness.
- **A bittersweet ending**: "doesn't last" and "a good ending" sit together, so the poem refuses to be either simply sad or simply happy.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Eve L. Ewing** is a writer, poet and sociologist from **Chicago**. She has also written comics, including superhero comics, so the extended metaphor draws on a world she knows from the inside.
- The poem appears in her collection **Electric Arches (2017)**, which mixes poetry, prose and images and explores growing up as a Black girl in Chicago, family and imagination.
- **Chicago in the 1980s**: the Greyhound bus station, a cover band of the Chicago-born singer Chaka Khan, and photocopied political papers locate the parents in a real, working, creative city culture, not a fairy tale. That realism matches "This is true".
- **Comic-book culture**: collectors keep comics in plastic bags with card backing to preserve their value; a "mint" copy is often one that has never been read. Ewing turns this into a question about love: is it better to preserve love untouched or to use it up?

**Themes**: love and relationships; family and origins; things that do not last; memory and storytelling; identity; acceptance of loss.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **Catrin** (Clarke): parents and children; Clarke's mother looks at her child, Ewing's child looks at her parents. Both see love and imperfection together.
- **Blackberry Picking** (Heaney): things that do not last; the children hoard berries that rot, collectors protect comics to keep them. Heaney grieves; Ewing accepts.
- **Sonnet 29** (Barrett Browning): love; a Victorian sonnet of devotion against a modern free-verse poem about love that ends.
- **Dusting the Phone** (Kay): modern love seen through an everyday object, with humour and pain.
- **Cousin Kate** (Rossetti): a relationship that ends; Rossetti's speaker is bitter, Ewing's is grateful.

::: terms
- **Origin story**: in comics, the tale of how a hero came to be; here, how the speaker's family began.
- **Extended metaphor**: a comparison developed through a whole section, like love as a comic book.
- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as", such as love "like a comic book".
- **Free verse**: poetry without regular rhyme or metre.
- **Self-reference**: when a text refers to itself, as in "comic books / like this one".
- **Understatement**: presenting something important in plain, low-key words, like "my mother bought one".
- **Bittersweet**: mixing sadness and happiness, as the final line does.
- **Parallelism**: repeating a sentence pattern, as in the introductions of the mother and father.
- **Electric Arches**: Ewing's 2017 collection of poetry, prose and images, in which the poem appears.
:::

::: method
1. **Overview first**: a child tells how her parents met and compares their love to a well-read comic: it did not last, but it was good.
2. **Work stanza by stanza**: the story of the meeting; then the comic-book metaphor and the parents' "worn" love.
3. **Analyse the extended metaphor**, the snapshot descriptions, the understatement and the final turn.
4. **Use form**: two stanzas (story and reflection), free verse, lower case, the list and the bittersweet last line.
5. **Weave in context**: Ewing as a Chicago writer of poetry and comics; Electric Arches; 1980s Chicago; comic collecting.
6. **For 7.2**, choose a poem on love, family, memory or things that do not last and compare throughout.
:::

::: example The extended metaphor
Explain how Ewing develops the metaphor of love as a comic book. (4)

The simile "like a comic book" introduces love as "fragile", like paper (1). Collectors protect comics with "plastic and cardboard", so love can be preserved, but only by shutting it away (1). Her parents' love "never saw polyvinyl": it was not protected (1). Instead it was "memorized, mishandled, worn thin", like a comic that has been read again and again, so the metaphor shows a love that was used up because it was enjoyed (1).
:::

::: example The first stanza
Explain how the first stanza presents the speaker's parents. (4)

"This is true:" frames their meeting as a real family legend (1). Each is introduced in a parallel snapshot, "thick glass and afro puff" and "sleeveless and soft eye", like characters drawn in a few lines (1). Their backgrounds (running away, a cover band, communist newspapers) make them young, independent and creative (1). "my mother bought one" sums up the start of the romance with tender understatement (1).
:::

::: example The ending
Explain why the final line is not simply sad. (3)

"doesn't last" admits the relationship ended (1). "it has a good ending" turns the line, valuing what the love was rather than how long it lasted (1). The "good ending" could also be the speaker herself, the result of their love, which fits the title "Origin Story" (1).
:::

::: example Context woven into analysis
Write two sentences linking "never saw polyvinyl" to context. (2)

"Polyvinyl bags are what comic collectors use to keep comics in perfect condition, so saying her parents' love never had one means it was never protected (1). Ewing, a Chicago writer who has also written comics, uses the language of a world she knows to suggest that love which is fully used is worth more than love kept safe and untouched (1)."
:::
