## What it is

**Dusting the Phone** by Jackie Kay is a monologue of someone in love, waiting for the beloved to ring. The speaker knows the waiting is pointless and unhealthy, but cannot stop: every silence means disaster, every call is the wrong person, and the phone itself becomes an object to polish and dress up for, as if it could be bribed. The lover seems unwilling to commit ("don't mention love"), and the relationship may be secret. The poem is **funny and painful at once**: the absurd behaviour makes us smile, and the loneliness underneath makes us wince. It ends with an unfinished threat that collapses into "I don't know what."

**Section by section**

| Lines | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | The speaker admits to imagining the worst instead of enjoying being in love; a ringing phone means disaster, a silent one means disaster too | "imagining the worst"; "heralding some disaster. Sirens." |
| The future | Nobody would even know to tell her; the future is a list of fragments: a gloved hand, an empty cup, a marriage, a weekly night in a stranger's sheets | "Nobody knows"; "a long gloved hand"; "An empty cup" |
| The lover's rule | The lover says forget tomorrow and don't mention love; she tries and fails; she harasses the postman, looks for flowers, replays their time together | "don't mention love"; "I assault the postman" |
| Devotion to the phone | She waits on the phone like a servant, polishes it, dresses for it, offers it a tip; it sends hoaxes, wrong numbers and dull callers | "Silver service. I polish it." |
| Collapse | The lover's voice vanishes into her lonely bed; she is trapped and wants them all the time; she has only a photo; her demand breaks off | "lonely cotton sheets"; "I don't know what." |

**Voice and speaker.** A first-person speaker talks directly, half to herself and half to the absent lover ("you say", "damn you"). The voice is **self-aware** (she admits it is "not a good idea") yet unable to act on that knowledge, which is both comic and sad. Neither speaker nor lover is named or gendered (this lesson calls the speaker "she", as most readers do), so the poem can speak for anyone who has waited for a call.

**Key language**
- **"imagining the worst"**: the first line confesses the obsession. She could be remembering "the best"; instead anxiety fills the time love should fill.
- **"heralding some disaster. Sirens."**: "heralding" is grand and formal, like a trumpet announcing doom, for a ringing phone. The one-word sentence **"Sirens."** is repeated, the alarm going off in her head. **Hyperbole** makes the anxiety comic and frightening.
- **"which also means disaster"**: whether the phone rings or not, she loses. The logic traps her.
- **"Nobody knows"**: if something happened to the lover, no one would tell her, because no one knows about them. The relationship is hidden, which deepens her isolation.
- **"a long gloved hand"** and **"An empty cup"**: the future as a string of **fragments** (a gloved hand, an empty cup, a marriage, a full house, one night a week). Some sound glamorous, some lonely, some like an affair. She cannot picture a single clear future.
- **"don't mention love"**: the lover's rule. It suggests the lover wants less than she does: an uneven relationship.
- **"I assault the postman"**: comic exaggeration; she is so desperate for a letter she would attack the messenger.
- **"Silver service. I polish it."**: she is "waiting on the phone", a pun: waiting for it and waiting on it like a waiter at a formal dinner. She polishes and dresses for it, as if it were a person to impress or an idol to worship. This is the poem's title image: **dusting the phone**, a domestic chore turned into devotion.
- **"I'll give it extra"**: she offers the phone a tip, as if it could be bribed to deliver the call.
- **"hoaxes, wrong numbers"** and **"calls from boring people"**: comic anticlimax. The phone seems to taunt her ("Infuriatingly").
- **"lonely cotton sheets"**: the lover's voice disappears into an empty bed; sexual longing and loneliness together.
- **"I am trapped in it"**, followed by more short, blunt sentences of need. She is imprisoned by her own feelings.
- **"only a photo"**: all she has of the lover is an image, a substitute for presence.
- **"Come on, damn you, ring"**: anger at last, an imperative aimed at the lover (or the phone).
- **"I don't know what."**: the threat "Or else" collapses because she has no power. The poem ends deflated and helpless.

**Form and structure**
- **Free verse with long, prose-like lines**: the lines run on like anxious thoughts that never stop. There is no rhyme to contain them.
- **Sentence length shrinks**: early sentences are long and reasoned (she knows it is "not a good idea"); by the end they are fragments ("All the time.", "Or else. What?"). The structure enacts her loss of control.
- **Lists and repetition** ("disaster. Sirens." twice; the list of futures) show her mind circling.
- **Present tense** throughout: we are inside the wait, "This very second".
- **Tone shifts**: rueful humour, then devotion, then irritation, then desperation and anger. The **open ending** with a question ("What?") leaves her, and us, still waiting.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Jackie Kay** (born 1961 in Edinburgh, raised in Glasgow) is a Scottish poet and novelist. She was **Makar, Scotland's national poet, from 2016 to 2021**. Her writing often explores identity, family and love with warmth and humour.
- **Love before mobile phones**: the poem belongs to a world of landlines, letters and photographs. Waiting by the phone at home, hoping for a letter from the postman, was what longing looked like; it explains the speaker's physical attachment to one object.
- **Hidden love**: "Nobody knows" and the lover's refusal to talk about love suggest a secret or unequal relationship. Kay has written openly about love between women; the ungendered "you" lets the poem speak about any love that has to stay hidden, as well as any lover who will not commit.
- **Modern love**: unlike Victorian sonnets of devotion, the poem mixes passion with everyday objects, swearing and humour, presenting love as obsessive, undignified and real.

**Themes**: love and longing; obsession; absence; loneliness; power in relationships; secrecy; anxiety; humour and pain.

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **Sonnet 29** (Barrett Browning): the absence of the beloved and thoughts that grow out of control; Browning's thoughts are vines round a tree, Kay's are disasters and sirens. Victorian sonnet against modern free verse.
- **Cousin Kate** (Rossetti): love and pain in an unequal relationship; both speakers are left waiting or abandoned by a lover with more power.
- **Origin Story** (Ewing): modern love told through everyday objects (a comic book, a phone) and a conversational voice.
- **I Shall Return** (McKay): longing for what is far away.

::: terms
- **Dramatic monologue**: a poem spoken by one character, revealing their thoughts and situation.
- **Hyperbole**: deliberate exaggeration for effect, like assaulting the postman.
- **Pun**: a play on a word with two meanings, like "waiting on the phone".
- **Minor sentence**: a sentence without a main verb, such as "Sirens."
- **Fragmentation**: breaking writing into short, incomplete pieces to show disorder.
- **Free verse**: poetry without regular rhyme or metre.
- **Anticlimax**: a let-down after building expectation, like calls from boring people.
- **Tone**: the attitude or mood of a voice, here comic and anguished at once.
- **Makar**: Scotland's national poet; Kay held the role 2016–2021.
:::

::: example Humour and pain
Explain how Kay makes the poem both funny and painful. (4)

Hyperbole such as "I assault the postman" and the phone as a waiter's "Silver service" makes her behaviour absurd and comic (1). The phone's "hoaxes, wrong numbers" and "calls from boring people" are a comic anticlimax (1). Underneath, she is isolated: "Nobody knows" about the relationship and the lover says "don't mention love" (1). By the end, "I am trapped" and "lonely cotton sheets" show real pain, so the humour is a way of coping with loneliness (1).
:::

::: example The phone
Explain how Kay presents the phone in the poem. (4)

The phone is the centre of her life: ringing or silent, it means "disaster" (1). She treats it like a guest or an idol: "I polish it", she says, and she dresses for it (1). "Silver service" and the pun on "waiting on" make her a servant to the object (1). She even offers it "extra", as if it could be bribed, which shows how powerless she feels: she can control the phone but not the person who might ring (1).
:::

::: example The ending
Explain the effect of the poem's final lines. (3)

The imperative "Come on, damn you, ring" shows anger and desperation for the first time (1). "Or else" begins a threat, but "I don't know what" collapses it (1). The fragmented ending shows she has no power in the relationship and leaves her still waiting (1).
:::
