## What it is

**An Inspector Calls** by J.B. Priestley is the most popular text in Component 2 Section A. You answer **one question worth 40 marks: 35 for AO1 and AO2** (in five bands of 7) **plus 5 for AO4** accuracy. You have about **45 minutes** and the exam is **closed book**: an extract from the play is printed, but the question is always about the **play as a whole**. **AO3 context earns no marks in this section**, so context is a tool for explaining Priestley's choices, never a paragraph of its own. (If your school studies a different post-1914 text, such as Lord of the Flies or Blood Brothers, delete this topic and keep the method topic that follows it.)

**The play in one paragraph.** It is a spring evening in **1912** in the dining room of Arthur Birling, a wealthy manufacturer in the industrial town of **Brumley**. The family is celebrating the engagement of his daughter **Sheila** to **Gerald Croft**, son of a rival, more aristocratic business family. An **Inspector Goole** arrives to say that a young working-class woman, **Eva Smith**, has died in the Infirmary after swallowing disinfectant. Questioning each person in turn, he shows that every one of them helped push her towards her death. After he leaves, the older Birlings and Gerald discover that no such inspector exists and that no girl has died that night, and they relax. Then the telephone rings: a girl has just died, and a police inspector is on his way.

Priestley wrote the play in **1944–45** and it was first performed in **1945**, just after the Second World War. Setting it in 1912 gives the audience a huge advantage over the characters: they know what is coming (the Titanic, two world wars, the strikes and suffering of the next decades), so the Birlings' confidence looks foolish from the first scene.

|---|---|---|---|

He sacked Eva from his works after she helped lead a strike for higher wages. Sheila, in a jealous temper, had her dismissed from Milwards, a department store. Eva then changed her name to **Daisy Renton**. | Gerald gives himself away when he hears the name Daisy Renton. Sheila realises; the Inspector returns with a single questioning word. A cliffhanger. |
| **Two** | Gerald, then Mrs Birling | Gerald kept Daisy as his mistress for some months after rescuing her in the Palace bar, then ended it. Sheila hands back the ring. Mrs Birling, chair of a women's charity, used her influence to refuse help to a pregnant girl who had used the name "Mrs Birling", and blamed the unknown father. | Mrs Birling demands that the young father be punished; the audience works out that it is **Eric**, who walks in at that moment. |
| **Three** | Eric | Eric met her in the same bar, forced his way into her lodgings when drunk, made her pregnant and stole money from his father's office to support her; she refused stolen money. The Inspector's final speech warns of the consequences of not learning. | After the hoax is apparently exposed, the parents and Gerald celebrate, Sheila and Eric do not, and the final telephone call announces a real death and a real inspector. Curtain. |

**The chain structure.** The Inspector insists on dealing with one person and one line of inquiry at a time, so the play is a chain of linked confessions, each in the order of Eva's life. This gives the audience a growing sense that one person's choices hand a victim on to the next. It also lets Priestley turn each family member's confidence into guilt in front of the others.

**The big ideas**

- **Social responsibility versus individualism.** Birling's creed is that a man must look after himself and his own; the Inspector's final speech answers that we do not live alone and that we are responsible for each other. The whole play is built as an argument between these two speeches.
- **Class.** Every step of Eva's decline is caused by someone with more money or status: an employer, a customer, a gentleman, a charity chair. The Birlings treat the working class as labour, entertainment or a problem.
- **Gender.** Women in the play are judged and used by men (Gerald and Eric), and also by other women (Sheila's jealousy, Mrs Birling's judgement). Sheila grows from a girl protected from anything unpleasant into the play's moral voice.
- **Age and the generations.** The older characters refuse to learn; the younger ones, Sheila and Eric, are changed. Priestley places his hope in the young, which mattered to a 1945 audience rebuilding the country.
- **Power and its abuse.** Each character used a small amount of power carelessly; the Inspector reverses the power on stage by interrogating the powerful.
- **Appearance and reality / public and private morality.** A respectable family dinner hides cruelty, an affair, drunkenness and theft.

**Stagecraft (AO2 for a play)**

- **Lighting.** The stage direction asks for light that is "pink and intimate" until the Inspector arrives and then "brighter and harder": the rosy self-satisfaction of the family is replaced by an interrogation lamp.
- **The single set.** The dining room is heavy, comfortable and closed. Nobody escapes it; the truth is forced out in one room in continuous time, which builds pressure.
- **Entrances and exits.** The **doorbell** cuts off Birling at the very moment he dismisses the idea of community. Eric's entrance at the end of Act Two lands just as his mother has condemned the father. Gerald's walk-out and the Inspector's exit are timed for maximum effect.
- **The photograph.** The Inspector shows it to one person at a time, so nobody can be sure all the stories are about the same girl, which sets up the hoax argument in Act Three.
- **Cliffhanger act endings** keep the audience in suspense like a detective thriller.
- **The cyclical ending.** The final telephone call repeats the opening situation: the story will begin again because the older characters learned nothing. The audience is left to ask whether **they** have learned.
- **Dramatic irony** throughout Act One, so the audience distrusts Birling before the Inspector even arrives.
- **Genre.** It looks like a "well-made" drawing-room drama and a detective story, but works as a **morality play**: each character embodies a failing (pride, prejudice, lust, greed) and is judged.

**Context as a tool (not for marks).** Priestley was a socialist who believed in collective responsibility; his wartime radio talks argued for a fairer Britain, and in 1945 the country voted for the welfare state. Use this in a clause to explain why the Inspector speaks as he does, for example: "Priestley, writing for an audience about to rebuild Britain, makes the Inspector's warning sound like prophecy." That is enough: a page of history earns nothing here.

::: terms
- **Dramatic irony**: when the audience knows something the characters do not, such as the fate of the Titanic in Act One.
- **Mouthpiece**: a character who voices the writer's own views; the Inspector carries Priestley's message about shared responsibility.
- **Stage directions**: the playwright's instructions for lighting, movement and tone, which can be analysed as a method like any line of dialogue.
- **Cyclical structure**: an ending that returns to the beginning, here the second inspector, suggesting the lesson must be learned again.
- **Morality play**: an old form of drama in which characters represent virtues and sins and the audience is taught a lesson.
- **Cliffhanger**: an act ending that leaves a question unanswered, creating suspense and pulling the audience into the next act.
- **Capitalism**: an economic system run for private profit, the belief Mr Birling stands for in the play.
- **Socialism**: the belief that society should share wealth and responsibility, the outlook Priestley promotes through the Inspector.
- **Generational divide**: the gap between the older characters, who refuse to change, and the younger ones, who accept their guilt.
- **Well-made play**: a tightly plotted drama of revelations, entrances and act endings; Priestley uses its machinery for a moral purpose.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the question focus first** (a character, a relationship or a theme) and underline it. Every paragraph must be about that focus, not about the play in general.
2. **Place the extract** in a sentence: which act, what has just happened, who is on stage.
3. **Mine the extract for two or three moments**: a line of dialogue and a **stage direction** for each, with a word or two analysed for its effect on the audience.
4. **Move outwards across all three acts**: the beginning, the middle and the ending (including the final telephone call). Choose four or five key moments you know well.
5. **In every paragraph**: point about the focus, short quotation or precise reference, a dramatic method (stage direction, entrance, irony, structure, lighting, the Inspector's control) and its effect.
6. **Say why Priestley does it.** Link the focus to his message in a clause; never write a separate context paragraph.
7. **Conclude with an overview** of how the character or idea is important to the play as a whole, then proofread names (Priestley, Birling, Sheila, Gerald, Eva) for AO4.
:::

::: example Dramatic irony in Act One
Explain how Priestley uses dramatic irony to present Mr Birling in Act One. (6)

Birling tells Gerald and Eric, with complete confidence, that the new liner is "unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable" and that talk of war is nonsense (1). The audience in 1945 knows the Titanic sank on its first voyage in 1912 and that two world wars followed (1), so every prediction makes him look more foolish (1). Because his business judgement is wrong about the world, the audience begins to doubt his judgement about people too (1): when he claims a man must look after himself and his family, we already distrust him (1). Priestley undermines capitalism's spokesman before the Inspector arrives, so the Inspector's view seems right before he has argued it (1).
:::

::: example The doorbell
Explain the dramatic effect of the moment the Inspector arrives. (4)

Birling is lecturing the young men that a man should mind his own business and dismissing "community and all that nonsense" (1). The sharp ring of the doorbell interrupts him (1), as if the world he has just dismissed is knocking at his door (1). Priestley times the entrance so that the Inspector's whole investigation becomes the answer to Birling's speech, and the lighting changes to "brighter and harder" as he enters (1).
:::

::: example Sheila changes
How does Priestley show Sheila changing across the play? (6)

In Act One she is playful and childish, admiring the ring and teasing Gerald about where he was last summer (1). Her reaction to the photograph is immediate distress, and she admits her jealousy honestly, promising she will "never, never do it again" (1). In Act Two she returns the ring and warns her mother against building a wall between the family and the girl (1). In Act Three she sees through her parents' relief, telling them the point is what they did, not whether the Inspector was real (1). Her language moves from excited exclamations to calm, moral judgement (1), so Priestley uses her as the model of the audience he hopes for: young, honest and able to change (1).
:::

::: example The final telephone call
Explain why the ending of the play is effective. (5)

The parents and Gerald have convinced themselves the evening was a hoax and are laughing and relaxing (1). The telephone rings and Birling reports that a girl has died and a police inspector is coming (1). The structure is cyclical: the investigation will begin again (1), which punishes those who refused to learn and suggests the Inspector may have been more than human (1). The audience leaves unsettled and asking whether they would pass the test, which is exactly Priestley's aim (1).
:::

::: example Eva Smith as a symbol
Explain why it matters that Eva Smith never appears on stage. (4)

Because she is never seen, the audience builds her only from other people's accounts, which reveal more about them than about her (1). She can stand for all working-class women rather than one individual (1); her name joins the first woman, Eve, to the commonest surname (1). The Inspector makes the point directly when he speaks of "millions and millions" of Eva Smiths (1).
:::

::: q
In which year is the play set, and when was it first performed? (2)
---
Set in 1912 (1); first performed in 1945 (1).
:::

::: q
Why was Eva Smith sacked from Birling's works? (2)
---
She was one of the leaders of a strike for higher wages (1); Birling sacked the ringleaders to keep wages down (1).
:::

::: q
Who says "Fairy Prince", and what tone does it have? (2)
---
Sheila, about Gerald (1); sarcastic, mocking his view of himself as Daisy's rescuer (1).
:::

::: q
Name two stage directions or staging choices Priestley uses to show the Inspector's power. (2)
---
Any two: the lighting changes from pink and intimate to brighter and harder (1); the doorbell interrupts Birling (1); he is described with massiveness and solidity (1); he controls who sees the photograph (1); he times exits and entrances (1).
:::

::: q
How does the end of Act Two use structure to create tension? (3)
---
Mrs Birling insists the young father must be exposed and punished (1); the audience realises it is Eric before she does (dramatic irony) (1); Eric's entrance at that moment makes her condemn her own son, leaving a cliffhanger (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the ending divides the characters into two groups. (4)
---
Mr and Mrs Birling and Gerald are relieved by the hoax and want to carry on as before (1); Sheila and Eric insist that what they did is unchanged (1). The division follows age, the older generation against the younger (1), showing Priestley's hope lies with the young (1).
:::

::: q
What is the significance of the Inspector's name? (2)
---
Goole sounds like "ghoul", a ghost or spirit (1), hinting that he is supernatural or a conscience rather than a real policeman (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Narrative retelling** of each character's link to Eva is the commonest way to stay in the lower bands. Every reference must prove something about the question focus.
- **No AO3 marks here.** Context is welcome only in a clause that explains Priestley's choices; whole paragraphs on 1912 or 1945 earn nothing.
- Treat it as a **play**: stage directions, entrances, lighting and the audience's response are your strongest AO2 evidence.
- Cover **all three acts and the ending**. Answers that stop at Mrs Birling's interrogation miss Act Three, the hoax and the final call.
- **Spell the names** correctly for AO4: Priestley, Birling, Sheila, Gerald Croft, Eric, Eva Smith, Daisy Renton, Goole.
- **Get the facts straight**: Sheila and Gerald are engaged, not married; Eva was Gerald's mistress, not his fiancée; the Inspector is never proved to be real or unreal.
:::

::: test

---
He reminds the family that they are "members of one body" (1), the play's central idea of shared responsibility (1). He warns that if people do not learn, they will be taught in "fire and blood and anguish" (1), which a 1945 audience would hear as the two world wars (1). The speech answers Birling's individualism from Act One, giving the play its argument structure (1).
:::

::: test

---
The older characters have decided it was all a hoax and celebrate (1). The telephone announces a girl's death and an inspector on his way (1), so the play circles back to its beginning (1). The repetition suggests that those who refused to learn must face the lesson again (1), and leaves the audience to ask whether they would learn it (1).
:::
