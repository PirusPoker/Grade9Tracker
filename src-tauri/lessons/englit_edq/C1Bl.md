## What it is

**War Photographer** by Carol Ann Duffy follows a photographer who has come home to England from a war zone. Alone in his darkroom he develops his films. As one image slowly appears, he remembers the man in it, the cries of the man's wife, and the blood in the dust. He knows his editor will choose only a handful of the pictures for a Sunday colour supplement, where readers will feel a brief prick of tears before getting on with their weekend. The poem ends with him in an aeroplane, looking down at the place where he works and the people who **do not care**. It is about the photographer's trauma, but its final accusation is aimed at **us**, the comfortable readers.

**Stanza by stanza**

| Stanza | What happens | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | In the red light of the darkroom his rolls of film are laid out; the room feels like a church and he like a priest; a list of war zones | "spools of suffering"; "this were a church"; "All flesh is grass" |
| 2 | He has a job to do; chemicals in trays; his hands, steady in the war, now shake; England's "ordinary pain" against fields that explode beneath running children | "a job to do"; "did not tremble then"; "a nightmare heat" |
| 3 | A face appears in the developing tray, a "half-formed ghost"; he remembers the man's wife's cries and asking permission, without words, to take the picture | "a half-formed ghost"; "foreign dust" |
| 4 | A hundred images; the editor will pick five or six; readers briefly weep between the bath and pre-lunch drinks; he looks down from a plane at where he earns his living | "A hundred agonies"; "pre-lunch beers"; "they do not care" |

**Voice and speaker.** The poem is in the **third person** ("he"), so we watch the photographer from outside, almost as if we were looking at a photograph of him. That distance mirrors his own professional detachment, and it lets Duffy step back at the end to judge the readers as well.

**Key language**
- **"spools of suffering"**: alliteration; each roll of film holds real pain. "set out in ordered rows" shows him imposing neatness on chaos.
- **The darkroom as a church**, he **"a priest"** preparing **"a Mass"**: religious imagery. The red light glows like a sanctuary lamp; he treats his work as a sacred duty, honouring the dead as a priest does at a funeral.
- **"Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh."**: three place names as **minor sentences**, a list of conflicts. The full stops make each a separate wound; the alliteration of B and P gives a drumbeat.
- **"All flesh is grass"**: a biblical phrase (from the Book of Isaiah) meaning that human life is brief and fragile. It universalises the deaths, and fits the priest image.
- **"a job to do"**: the sentence is short, blunt and professional. He tells himself it is work so he can bear it.
- **"Solutions slop in trays"**: developing chemicals; the word "solutions" is also a quiet pun: there are no solutions to the wars.
- **"did not tremble then"**: in the war zone he was steady; at home the trauma reaches him. The contrast of "then" and "now" shows delayed shock.
- **"Rural England. Home again"** to **"ordinary pain"**: English problems are trivial, cured by sunshine. The juxtaposition with fields that explode under **"running children"** in **"a nightmare heat"** is brutal: their pain cannot be dispelled.
- **"a half-formed ghost"**: the face developing in the tray is like a ghost rising: the dead man returns as an image and as a memory.
- **"blood stained into foreign dust"**: "foreign" marks the distance between where it happened and where it will be seen; the blood has permanently marked the ground.
- **"A hundred agonies"**, all **"in black and white"**: each photograph is a person's agony; "black and white" also suggests a simple moral truth.
- **"five or six"**: the editor's casual selection shows how little of the truth reaches the public.
- **"eyeballs prick / with tears"**: the sympathy is physical and shallow, only the eyeballs "prick". It is sandwiched between **"the bath and pre-lunch beers"**, the comfortable rituals of an English Sunday.
- **"stares impassively"** and **"they do not care"**: he is numb; the final line's "they" most obviously means the readers, though it could also suggest the wider world. Either way the poem ends on indifference.

**Form and structure**
- **Four regular six-line stanzas**, each ending in a **rhyming couplet** (Mass / grass, feet / heat, must / dust, where / care). The neat pattern mirrors the "ordered rows" of film: the photographer, and the poem, try to impose order on chaos.
- **Lines of uneven length and frequent caesura** ("Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh.") disturb that order, as if the trauma keeps breaking through the control.
- **Present tense** in the darkroom, with **flashbacks** to the war zone, so memories intrude as the image appears.
- **A movement outward**: from the private darkroom (stanzas 1–3) to the newspaper, the reader and the aeroplane (stanza 4). The focus widens from one man's grief to a society's indifference.
- **Circular journey**: he is home, but the final image has him flying back out. The cycle of war, photograph and forgetting will repeat.

**Context, woven into meaning**
- **Carol Ann Duffy** (born 1955 in Glasgow) was **Poet Laureate from 2009 to 2019**, the first woman in the role. The poem appeared in her collection **Standing Female Nude (1985)**.
- Duffy had friends who were war photographers, and she was interested in the conflict at the heart of their work: they must record terrible suffering without intervening, then watch it be consumed as news.
- **Belfast** (the Troubles in Northern Ireland), **Beirut** (the Lebanese civil war) and **Phnom Penh** (Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge) were conflicts of the 1970s and 1980s, the period when the poem was written. Including Belfast reminds a British reader that war was happening inside the UK.
- **Sunday colour supplements** placed war photographs among adverts and lifestyle features. The poem criticises a culture that turns suffering into something to glance at over the weekend.

**Themes**: conflict and its effects; memory and trauma; the observer and guilt; indifference; duty; distance between "here" and "there".

**Best pairings for the comparison question**
- **Decomposition** (Ghose): the strongest pair. Both are about a photograph of suffering and the guilt of the person who took it; both accuse an indifferent public.
- **Remains** (Armitage): a memory of a killing that will not leave a man who was there; trauma back home.
- **Disabled** (Owen): the effects of war seen after the event, and a public that does not care.
- **Kamikaze** (Garland): both include a man looking down from an aircraft; the pilot sees home and turns back, the photographer sees where he works and flies on.

::: terms
- **Third-person narration**: telling about a character as "he" or "she" from outside.
- **Religious imagery**: language drawn from religion, like church, priest and Mass.
- **Alliteration**: repeated consonant sounds at the start of nearby words, like "spools of suffering".
- **Minor sentence**: a sentence without a main verb, like "Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh."
- **Caesura**: a pause in the middle of a line, often made by a full stop.
- **Juxtaposition**: placing contrasting things side by side, like English weather and exploding fields.
- **Rhyming couplet**: two rhyming lines; each stanza here ends with one.
- **Sunday supplement**: a colour magazine sold with a Sunday newspaper.
- **Poet Laureate**: the poet appointed to the Crown; Duffy held the role 2009–2019.
- **Impassive**: showing no emotion.
:::

::: method
1. **Overview first**: a war photographer develops his pictures at home, haunted by what he saw, knowing the public will barely care.
2. **Work stanza by stanza**: the darkroom as church; the contrast of England and war; the ghost in the tray; the editor, the reader and the plane.
3. **Analyse the religious imagery, the juxtapositions and the final indifference.**
4. **Use form**: regular stanzas and couplets as order imposed on chaos, caesura breaking it, the outward movement of the last stanza.
5. **Weave in context**: Duffy and war photographers, the conflicts named, Sunday supplements.
6. **For 7.2**, choose a poem on conflict, guilt, observation or indifference and compare throughout.
:::

::: example The religious imagery
Explain how Duffy uses religious imagery in the first stanza. (4)

The darkroom is described as if it were a church and he is like a priest preparing a Mass (1). The red light glows like a sanctuary lamp, giving the room a solemn, sacred atmosphere (1). This suggests he treats developing the photographs as a ritual honouring the dead, like a funeral service (1). "All flesh is grass", a biblical phrase, reminds the reader how fragile and brief human life is (1).
:::

::: example The contrast of England and the war zone
Explain how Duffy contrasts England with the war zones in the second stanza. (4)

"Rural England" offers only "ordinary pain" that "simple weather can dispel", so problems at home are trivial (1). This is juxtaposed with fields that "don't explode" under the feet of children (1), reminding us that in war even fields are deadly (1). The image of "running children" in "a nightmare heat" makes the suffering innocent and terrifying, sharpening the contrast with comfortable England (1).
:::

::: example The readers
Explain how the final stanza presents the newspaper readers. (4)

Only "five or six" of "A hundred agonies" will be printed, so most of the truth is lost (1). The reader's "eyeballs prick / with tears", a brief physical reaction rather than real feeling (1). The tears come "the bath and pre-lunch beers", placing the sympathy among comfortable weekend routines (1). The final words "they do not care" condemn the public's indifference (1).
:::

::: example Context woven into analysis
