## What it is

Section C of Component 2 is **unseen poetry**: two poems from the 20th or 21st century that you have never read, worth **40 marks** in about **an hour**. This lesson covers the first question, **3.1**: one poem, **15 marks, about 20 minutes**. The next lesson covers the comparison (3.2, 25 marks). Both are marked on **AO1** (your response and references) and **AO2** (language, form and structure) equally. There is **no context** to learn and no AO4.

**What the question looks like.** Above the poems is a short **statement** that tells you what both poems are about on the surface, for example "The two poems are about different times of the year." Then:

**3.1** Write about the poem [title] by [poet] and its effect on you. [15]
You may wish to:
- consider what the poem is about and how it is organised
- consider the ideas the poet may have wanted us to think about
- consider the poet's choice of words, phrases and images and the effects they create
- consider how you respond to the poem.

**What the four bullets ask for**

| Bullet | What to write |
|---|---|
| What it is about and how it is organised | A clear overview: the situation, the speaker, how the poem moves (stanzas, a shift in time or mood, a turn, the ending). |
| The ideas the poet wants us to think about | The deeper meaning, grown out of the surface: what the poem suggests about grief, community, nature, growing up. Use "perhaps" and "could suggest". |
| Words, phrases and images and their effects | The heart of your answer: close analysis of chosen details, what they make you see, hear or feel, and why. |
| How you respond | Your **personal response**: how the poem makes you feel and think, justified by the words. This is what "its effect on you" means. |

**The bands in plain words (15 marks, bands of 3)**

| Band | Marks | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 13–15 | A sensitive, evaluative response with an overview; precise references from across the poem; perceptive analysis of language and structure; a convincing personal response. |
| 4 | 10–12 | A thoughtful, clear response; well-chosen references; methods examined with growing analysis; engaged personal response. |
| 3 | 7–9 | A relevant, straightforward response; references support the points; some explanation of effects. |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some understanding; general references; simple comments, often feature spotting. |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic awareness of what the poem is about; very little reference. |

**Start from the surface: the statement matters.** Examiners report that many students miss the statement about what the poems are about and invent dramatic readings: a poem about swimming becomes a poem about drowning, funerals, depression or the underworld. Those answers pick evidence to fit an idea instead of letting the idea grow from the words, and they score poorly. Read the statement, read the title, and build your interpretation **outwards from the literal**. Deeper readings are welcome when the words support them.

**A reading routine (about 5 minutes of the 20)**
1. Read the **statement and title**. What should this poem be about?
2. **First read, whole poem, no pen**: who is speaking, to whom, where, when, what is happening?
3. **Second read, with pen**: underline 6–8 striking words, phrases or images; mark where the mood, time or focus **shifts**; circle the **ending**.
4. Write a **one-sentence overview**: "The poem describes ... and suggests that ...".
5. Decide your **response**: one or two words for how it makes you feel (moved, unsettled, amused, comforted) and why.

**Language beats labels.** The best answers explore what particular words and images do. Naming a rhyme scheme, counting stanzas or spotting enjambment earns little unless you say what it does for the poem's meaning. "The verb 'marches' makes the weeds sound like an invading army" is worth more than "there is personification in line 8".

**Useful terms, with the effect you might attach**

| Term | An effect you might explain |
|---|---|
| Metaphor / simile | Makes an abstract or ordinary thing vivid by comparison; ask what the comparison suggests. |
| Personification | Gives objects human feelings or actions, often to show the speaker's emotion projected on them. |
| Sensory imagery | Puts the reader in the scene through sound, smell, touch, taste. |
| Verb choice | Shows the kind of movement or energy; often the most precise detail in a line. |
| Repetition | Emphasis, obsession, rhythm, a sense of something that will not go away. |
| Contrast | Sharpens a change of mood, place or time. |
| Enjambment | A line that runs on can create momentum, surprise, or a pause that lands on a key word. |
| Short sentence or line | Finality, shock, quiet emphasis. |
| Shift or turn | The point where the poem changes; often where its idea is revealed. |
| Ending | What the final image leaves you with: the strongest place for personal response. |

**Writing a personal response that earns marks.** "I liked this poem" earns nothing. A response is grounded in a detail and explained: "The final line moved me because the speaker discovers that grief can be practical: by weeding she continues his care, so the loss feels gentler than I expected." Use phrases such as: "This makes me feel...", "I find this image unsettling because...", "As a reader I am left with...", "Perhaps the poet wants us to...", "This could suggest... although it might also...".

**Timing (20 minutes)**

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Statement, title, two readings, annotation, one-sentence overview. |
| 5–18 | Overview paragraph, then three or four paragraphs working through the poem in order, each with a detail, its effect and your response. |
| 18–20 | A final paragraph on the ending and the poem's overall effect on you. |

Don't overrun: question 3.2 is worth 25 marks and needs about 40 minutes. Working **through the poem in order** is the simplest way to cover its organisation and reach the ending.

**Reading practice.** The specification suggests poets whose work is good preparation, including Fleur Adcock, John Agard, Moniza Alvi, Maya Angelou, Simon Armitage, Wendy Cope, Carol Ann Duffy, Seamus Heaney, Ted Hughes, Jackie Kay, Philip Larkin, Roger McGough, Grace Nichols, Owen Sheers and Benjamin Zephaniah. Read a short modern poem a week and practise the routine on it.

**The practice poems in this lesson** were written for this lesson, so you meet them unseen.

::: terms
- **Unseen poem**: a poem you have not studied, printed in the exam for you to analyse on the spot.
- **Statement**: the line above the questions that says what the two poems are about on the surface; use it as your starting point.
- **Overview**: a short summary at the start of your answer of what the poem is about and what it suggests.
- **Personal response**: your own reaction to the poem, how it makes you feel or think, explained with evidence.
- **Tentative language**: phrases such as "perhaps" or "could suggest" that show you are interpreting, not stating facts.
- **Feature spotting**: naming techniques without explaining their effect, which earns very little credit.
- **Shift**: a change in the poem's time, place, mood or focus, often where its meaning deepens.
- **Imagery**: descriptive language that creates a picture or sensation in the reader's mind.
- **Speaker**: the voice of the poem, which may not be the poet.
- **Imposed reading**: an interpretation not supported by the poem's words, often a dramatic theme forced onto it.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the statement and title** first: they tell you the surface subject.
2. **Read the poem twice**: once for the situation, once with a pen for striking words, the shift and the ending.
3. **Write a one-sentence overview** of what the poem is about and what it suggests.
4. **Work through the poem in order**, three or four paragraphs: quote a short detail, explore its effect, say how it makes you feel or think.
5. **Comment on organisation where it matters**: stanzas, the turn, the ending, and what each does.
6. **Use tentative language** for deeper ideas, and keep them rooted in the words.
7. **End on the final lines and the poem's overall effect on you.** Stop at 20 minutes.
:::

::: example A model 3.1 answer (extracts)
The poem below was written for this lesson. The poem is about a speaker visiting her late grandfather's allotment.

Allotment

The padlock still remembers his thumb;
it sticks, then gives, the way he did
when we were small and wanted sweets.
Inside, the shed keeps its brown breath:
creosote, twine, a calendar
stopped at a month of broad beans.

His rows have lost their discipline.
Couch grass marches over the onions,
the runner beans have climbed their canes
and kept on climbing, reaching for him
past the tops of the poles, into nothing.

I kneel where his knees wore the soil smooth
and pull one weed, then another,
not because it will make a difference
but because the earth seems to expect it,
and because my hands are his hands, now.

Write about the poem Allotment and its effect on you. (15)

Overview: The poem follows a speaker into her dead grandfather's allotment, where everything still seems to carry his presence, and moves from noticing his absence to quietly continuing his work, suggesting that we keep the people we love by doing what they did (1).

Paragraph on stanza 1: The opening personifies the padlock, which "remembers his thumb", so that even objects seem to grieve (1). The comparison of the stiff lock to the grandfather, who "sticks, then gives" when children beg for sweets, is affectionate and funny, and made me picture a gruff man who was soft underneath (1). The calendar "stopped" at broad beans is a quiet image of death: time ended for him in the middle of an ordinary month (1).

Paragraph on stanza 2: The garden has "lost their discipline", and the verb "marches" turns the couch grass into an invading army now that its general has gone (1). The runner beans that keep climbing "reaching for him" are the most moving image for me: the plants seem to search for him, and the line runs on and ends "into nothing", so the reader's eye falls into the same emptiness (1).

Paragraph on stanza 3: The shift to the present tense and the first person "I kneel" brings the speaker into the poem's centre (1). The repetition "one weed, then another" is slow and patient. She admits it will not "make a difference", which is honest about grief, yet the final line, "my hands are his hands, now", turns a hopeless task into a kind of inheritance (1).

Response: I found the ending comforting rather than sad, because the poem suggests that love survives in habits and work, not just memories (1).
:::

::: example Grounding an interpretation
A student writes: "The calendar stopped at broad beans shows the speaker is trapped in depression and wants to die." Explain what is wrong and rewrite it. (4)

The reading is imposed: nothing in the poem suggests the speaker wants to die (1), and it ignores the statement, which says the poem is about visiting a late grandfather's allotment (1). Better: "The calendar 'stopped' at broad beans could suggest the moment the grandfather's life ended (1): time stood still for him in the middle of an ordinary growing season, which makes his death feel sudden and unfinished (1)."
:::

::: example Effect, not label
Improve: "In line 8 there is personification and alliteration." (3)

Name the detail precisely: "Couch grass marches over the onions" (1). Explore the effect: "marches" turns the weeds into a disciplined army taking over (1). Link to meaning or response: the order the grandfather kept has been overrun, which made me feel how quickly his absence shows (1).
:::

::: example Organisation
Explain how Allotment is organised and why that suits its ideas. (4)

Three stanzas move from entering (the padlock and shed), to looking (the overgrown rows), to acting (kneeling and weeding) (1). The first two stanzas describe things in the third person, "his" thumb, "his" rows, so the grandfather is present only through objects (1). The third stanza shifts to "I", so the speaker steps into his place (1), matching the idea that grief becomes continuation (1).
:::

::: example A personal response sentence
Write two sentences of personal response to the ending of Allotment. (2)

"I was surprised that the poem ends with hope: by saying 'my hands are his hands', the speaker turns her grief into a way of keeping him alive (1). This made me think about the habits I have picked up from my own family, which is perhaps what the poet wants every reader to notice (1)."
:::

::: watch
- **Ignoring the statement** and inventing a dramatic hidden meaning (death, drowning, mental illness) that the words do not support.
- **Feature spotting**: naming enjambment, caesura or rhyme without saying what they do.
- **No personal response**: the question asks for the poem's effect on you; say how it makes you feel or think, and why.
- **Stopping halfway**: work through to the ending, where the meaning often turns.
- **Overrunning**: more than 20 minutes on 3.1 steals time from the 25-mark comparison.
- **Long quotations**: choose short, precise details and analyse them.
:::

::: test
Read the poem below. It was written for this lesson. The poem is about a city on the morning after heavy snow.

Snow Day

Overnight the city lost its edges.
Cars became loaves, left out to cool;
the bins wore white wigs like judges
and the traffic lights blinked at nobody,
red, amber, green, to an empty road.

No buses. No school. The news
says stay at home, so everyone comes out:
the man from Number Nine who never speaks
is building a snowman with his daughter,
a woman in a dressing gown is laughing
at the sky, as if it told a joke.

By four the gritters arrive, orange and patient,
salting the streets back into roads.
The snowman slumps. The silence melts.
Tomorrow we will be strangers again,
but for one white day the city
remembered it was a village.

Explain the effect of the metaphor "the city lost its edges" in line 1. (2)
---
Snow covers kerbs, walls and corners, softening the city's hard lines (1); it also hints that the usual barriers between people are disappearing, preparing for the community of stanza 2 (1).
:::

::: test
In Snow Day, give one image from stanza 1 that is humorous and explain its effect. (2)
---
E.g. cars as "loaves, left out to cool" or bins in "white wigs like judges" (1); the playful comparisons make the snowy city feel comic and transformed, like a childish game (1).
:::

::: test
In Snow Day, what is surprising about the line "says stay at home, so everyone comes out"? (2)
---
It is a contradiction: people do the opposite of the advice (1), showing the snow creates a holiday mood and draws people together (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the poet presents the man from Number Nine and the woman in the dressing gown. (4)
---
The man "who never speaks" is usually isolated, but now plays with his daughter (1), showing the snow breaking down his reserve (1). The woman laughing in her dressing gown has forgotten normal rules about appearance (1); her laughter "as if it told a joke" personifies the sky as a friend, suggesting shared joy (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the final stanza of Snow Day changes the mood. (4)
---
The gritters arrive and salt the streets "back into roads", restoring ordinary life (1). Short sentences, "The snowman slumps. The silence melts.", sound final and sad (1). The speaker predicts "we will be strangers again" (1), but the last lines celebrate the day when the city "remembered it was a village", so the mood is bittersweet (1).
:::

Read the poem below. It was written for this lesson. The poem is about a journey home on a late-night bus.

Night Bus

The last bus home is a lit aquarium
swimming the dark streets.
We sit apart, each in our own glass:
a nurse with her shoes in a bag,
two boys sharing a single earphone,
a man asleep against the window,
his breath printing a small ghost on the pane.

The driver knows the way without looking,
takes the corners like an old song.
Stop after stop, the doors sigh open,
let a little cold in, let one of us go.

Nobody speaks. Nobody needs to.
We are a family for twenty minutes,
held in the same yellow light,
rocking to the same engine,
until my stop, and I step down
into the dark, and the bus swims on
carrying the others home.

Explain the effect of the metaphor of the bus as "a lit aquarium". (3)
---
The bus is a bright glass box moving through darkness (1); the passengers are like fish, seen but separate (1); the image is calm and slightly dreamlike, setting the quiet mood (1).
