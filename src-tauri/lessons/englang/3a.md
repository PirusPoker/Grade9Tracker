## What it is

The **Spoken Language endorsement** is a compulsory part of GCSE English Language, reported **separately** from your 9–1 grade as **Pass, Merit, Distinction** (or Not Classified). It does not affect the grade, but it appears on your certificate, universities and employers can see it, and — more usefully — it is the one part of the course that is a life skill in its raw form. The assessment is a **presentation** you give to your teacher and (usually) some of your class, on a topic you choose or agree with your teacher, followed by **questions** from the audience (see lesson 3b). It is recorded, and a sample is sent to the exam board.

**What is assessed** (AO7, AO8, AO9):

- **Presenting** — expressing ideas and information clearly and effectively, using **Standard English**, with a **structure** and content that suit the purpose and the audience;
- **Responding** — listening to questions and feedback and answering appropriately, developing your ideas;
- **Standard English** — throughout, in both the presentation and the responses.

**The grades in plain terms.** *Pass*: you speak audibly, use Standard English, the talk is organised and makes sense, and you answer questions. *Merit*: the talk is **well structured** and **engaging**, you use spoken-language features (rhetorical questions, emphasis, pauses) to hold the audience, and your answers to questions are **developed**. *Distinction*: the content is **sophisticated** — ideas are challenging or original, the structure is **inventive**, delivery is confident and controlled, and answers to questions are **perceptive**, building on what was asked rather than just replying. The step from Pass to Merit is mostly *preparation*; from Merit to Distinction is mostly *thinking*.

**Choosing a topic.** The best topics are ones you have a **view** on, not just knowledge of: an argument ("Grades should not be reported on certificates"), a case ("Why everyone should learn to cook one meal properly"), an experience with a point ("What running taught me about failing"). Pure information ("The history of the Roman Empire") is hard to make engaging in five minutes and offers nothing to argue with in the questions. The topic should be one you can talk about for **three to five minutes** without reading, and about which the audience can ask you things you would enjoy answering.

**Structure.** Exactly the shape of a good speech (lesson 2.2b): open by addressing the room with a hook; state what you are going to argue; three points with an example each; deal with the obvious objection; close on a line, not on "that's it". Signpost so a listener can follow — "there are three reasons, and I'll take the surprising one last."

**Delivery.** Stand, look up, speak to the back of the room. Use **cue cards** with key words — never a full script (reading aloud is the fastest route to a Pass). **Pause** after important sentences instead of filling with "um" and "like". Vary pace and volume on purpose: slow down for the key point, drop your voice for the serious one. Use hands for emphasis, then let them rest. If you lose your place, pause, look at the card, carry on — a pause reads as confidence to an audience even when it is panic to you.

::: terms
- **Spoken Language endorsement**: a separately reported part of GCSE English Language, graded Pass, Merit or Distinction.
- **Presentation**: a prepared talk on a topic, given to an audience.
- **Standard English**: the formal variety of English, used correctly in grammar and vocabulary.
- **Cue cards**: brief notes of key points, used as prompts rather than a full script.
- **Delivery**: how the talk is spoken — pace, volume, emphasis, eye contact.
:::

::: method
1. **Choose a topic with a view** in it — something you would argue about with a friend. Write your position in one sentence.
2. **Plan as a speech**: hook, position, three points with examples (one personal anecdote is worth a lot), the objection and your answer, a closing line. Aim for four minutes.
3. **Cue cards**: one card per section, five to eight words each. No sentences. Number them.
4. **Rehearse aloud** at least three times — once to a mirror, once to a person, once recorded on your phone. Listen back for fillers ("um", "like", "basically") and slang, and for the bits where you sped up.
5. **Anticipate five questions** you are likely to be asked, and prepare a developed answer to each (see 3b).
6. On the day: breathe, look up, address the room by name ("Good morning, everyone"), and **end on your line** — then stop and wait for questions.
:::

::: example Choosing between topics
Candidate's shortlist: (a) "The solar system"; (b) "Why school should teach first aid"; (c) "My holiday in Spain".

(a) is information only — hard to structure as an argument, and the questions will test knowledge you might not have. (c) is an experience with no point — unless it becomes "what travelling without my parents taught me", which has one. (b) is a view: it has a hook (a real incident, or a statistic about how few people would know what to do), three reasons, an obvious objection (the timetable is full), and it invites good questions. Choose (b), or (c) with a point.
:::

::: example An opening that earns Merit
Pass-level opening: "Hi. My presentation is about first aid. First aid is important because it saves lives. I'm going to talk about three reasons."

Merit-level opening: "Good morning. Put your hand up if you know what to do if the person next to you stopped breathing. [Pause.] Keep it up if you're sure. [Pause.] That's about the national average, and it is why I think every one of us should leave this school knowing first aid — and why I'm going to argue that it matters more than at least one subject on our timetable." Direct address, an action for the audience, a pause used deliberately, a position stated, a hint of controversy for the questions.
:::

::: example Cue cards, not scripts
Script (what to avoid): "First aid is a set of simple techniques that can be performed by anyone and which, in the critical minutes before professional help arrives, can mean the difference between…" — read aloud, this is flat and the eyes never leave the paper.

Cue card 2: *first minutes — 999 avg 8 min — CPR doubles survival — Uncle Tom story*. The speaker knows what each phrase means and says it in their own words, looking at the audience. The words come out slightly differently each time; that is what makes it sound like speech.
:::

::: example Delivery choices that reach Distinction
The same content can be a Merit or a Distinction depending on control. Distinction speakers: **slow down** on the key sentence and say it once, cleanly; **stop** after it — two full seconds — and let it land; **change register** deliberately — a joke, then a serious turn signalled by a lower, slower voice; **use the room** — refer to something in it, or to someone who asked a question last time; **finish on a line and stop**, without "so yeah, that's basically it". None of these require a better topic; all of them require rehearsal.
:::

::: q
How is the Spoken Language endorsement reported?
---
Separately from the 9–1 grade, as Pass, Merit or Distinction (or Not Classified).
:::

::: q
What three things are assessed?
---
Presenting clearly and effectively in Standard English with suitable structure; responding to questions and feedback; using Standard English throughout.
:::

::: q
What kind of topic works best, and why?
---
One you have a view on — an argument or an experience with a point — because it gives structure, engagement and something to answer questions about.
:::

::: q
Why should you use cue cards rather than a script?
---
Reading a script keeps your eyes down and sounds flat; key-word cards make you speak naturally to the audience.
:::

::: q
What separates a Merit from a Pass?
---
A well-structured, engaging talk using spoken-language features (direct address, rhetorical questions, pauses) and developed answers to questions.
:::

::: q
What separates a Distinction from a Merit?
---
Sophisticated content and ideas, inventive structure, confident controlled delivery, and perceptive answers that build on the questions.
:::

::: q
What should you do instead of saying "um" while thinking?
---
Pause — a silent pause reads as confidence to the audience.
:::

::: q
How should the presentation end?
---
On a prepared closing line, then stop and wait for questions — not "that's basically it".
:::

::: watch
- **A topic with a view**; information-only topics cap you.
- **Speech structure**: hook, position, three points, objection, closing line.
- **Cue cards, five to eight words each**; never a script.
- **Rehearse aloud three times** and listen to a recording for fillers and slang.
- **Standard English throughout** — including the questions afterwards.
- **Pause, don't fill.** End on the line and stop.
:::
