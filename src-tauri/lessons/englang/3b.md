## What it is

After the presentation, the audience — your teacher and classmates — ask **questions**, and how you answer them is assessed as much as the talk itself (AO8: "listen and respond appropriately to spoken language, including to questions and feedback on presentations"). Many students who give a Merit-level talk drop to a Pass in this part because they treat questions as a threat to survive rather than a chance to show thinking. The grade descriptors are explicit: *Pass* — "listens to questions/feedback and provides an appropriate response"; *Merit* — "listens to questions/feedback responding formally and in some detail"; *Distinction* — "listens to questions/feedback, responds perceptively and, if appropriate, elaborates with further ideas and information". The words to hold on to are **detail** (Merit) and **perceptive / elaborates** (Distinction).

**What a good answer looks like.** Not one sentence. The shape is **answer → reason → example or development → link back to your argument**. A question is an invitation to add a paragraph to your talk. "Do you think first aid should be compulsory?" — Pass: "Yes, I do." Merit: "Yes — because the people who need it most are the ones least likely to choose it, so leaving it optional defeats the purpose." Distinction: the Merit answer, plus "…and there is a fairness point too: if it is optional, the pupils who take it are the ones with parents who already think it matters, which is exactly the group that would have learned it at home anyway. So compulsion is the only way it reaches the people it is for. That is actually a stronger argument than the one I made in the talk, so thank you for the question."

**Listening is assessed.** Answer the question that was **asked**, not the one you prepared for. If the question is unclear, ask for it to be repeated or rephrased — "Do you mean whether it should replace a subject, or be added?" — which shows listening and buys thinking time. Repeating the key words of the question back ("So the question is whether it would really change behaviour…") also shows listening and gives you a launch.

**Difficult questions.** You will be asked something you do not know, or something that challenges your argument. Both are opportunities:

- **Don't know**: say so briefly, then reason anyway — "I don't know the exact figure, but I'd expect it to be low, because…". Never bluff a statistic; a made-up number under questioning is easy to catch and undermines everything before it.
- **Challenge**: **concede what is true**, then hold your ground — "That's fair; a two-hour course won't make anyone an expert. But the point isn't expertise, it's the first three minutes, and for those two hours is enough." Conceding shows you have listened and thought; refusing to concede anything sounds rehearsed.
- **Off-topic or hostile**: answer briefly and steer back — "That's a different debate, but on the question of cost…".

**Standard English still applies.** The relaxed, conversational part is where slang and fillers creep back — "yeah, no, like, basically it's…". Pause instead. Formal does not mean stiff; it means complete sentences and ordinary vocabulary.

**Feedback.** Sometimes the teacher gives feedback rather than a question ("Your second point was stronger than your first"). Respond to it: agree or disagree with a reason, and if you agree, say what you would change. "Listening to feedback" is in the descriptor.

::: terms
- **Questions and feedback**: after the presentation, the audience asks questions, which you answer.
- **Developed answer**: a response that gives a point, a reason and an example.
- **Conceding**: agreeing that part of a challenge is fair.
- **Holding your position**: keeping your argument while acknowledging the challenge.
- **Active listening**: showing you have understood the question before answering it.
:::

::: method
1. **Prepare five likely questions** in advance — the obvious objection, a "why this topic" question, a "what would you do about it" question, a "what about [counter-example]" question, and a personal one — and rehearse a four-part answer to each.
2. When a question comes: **look at the person**, let them finish, and **repeat its key words** back if you need a second.
3. Answer in the shape **answer → reason → example → link**. Aim for thirty to sixty seconds, not five.
4. If challenged, **concede the true part first**, then make your case. If you don't know, say so and reason from what you do know.
5. Where you can, **build**: "That connects to something I didn't have time for…" — elaboration is the Distinction word.
6. Stay in **Standard English**; pause rather than fill; finish each answer cleanly and look for the next question.
:::

::: example From one sentence to a developed answer
Question: "Isn't first aid something people should learn outside school?"

One-sentence answer (Pass): "No, because not everyone would."

Developed (Merit): "Some would — but the people who go and learn it themselves are already the type who take it seriously. The whole point is to reach the people who wouldn't, and the only place you can reliably reach everyone is school. It's the same logic we use for teaching everyone to read."

Elaborated (Distinction): the Merit answer, then: "And there's a practical point I didn't make in the talk: it's cheaper in school, because one trainer does thirty people at once and the equipment is already in the PE cupboard. So it's not just fairer to do it in school — it's the only version that scales."
:::

::: example Conceding and holding
Question: "A two-hour course won't make anyone competent. Isn't it a bit of a token gesture?"

"You're right that it won't make anyone competent in the way a paramedic is, and I wouldn't claim it does. But competence isn't the target. The target is the three or four things that matter in the first five minutes — call, check breathing, start compressions, don't stop — and those genuinely can be taught in two hours; the resuscitation council designs the courses around exactly that. So it's less a token gesture than a very narrow one, on purpose." Concede the accurate part; redefine what success means; support with a reason; return to the argument.
:::

::: example Not knowing
Question: "How many people actually die each year because nobody nearby knew CPR?"

Bluff (bad): "About fifty thousand." (Invented; the next question will be "where did you get that?")

Honest and reasoned (good): "I don't have the exact number and I don't want to make one up. What I do know is that survival from a cardiac arrest outside hospital in this country is under one in ten, and it roughly doubles when someone starts CPR before the ambulance arrives — so whatever the absolute figure is, the proportion that's preventable is large. If it matters to you, I can find the number and tell you tomorrow." The honesty is itself a Distinction-level move, and the reasoning does the work the number would have.
:::

::: example Responding to feedback
Teacher: "I thought your anecdote about your uncle was the strongest part, and you moved past it quite quickly."

Weak: "OK, thanks."

Strong: "I think you're right — I cut it because I was worried about time, but it's the bit that makes the argument real rather than abstract. If I did it again I'd open with it instead of the statistic, and let the statistic come after as the 'and this happens a lot' point." Agreement with a reason and a concrete change: listening, reflection, and thinking aloud.
:::

::: watch
- **Prepare five questions** and rehearse developed answers — the questions part is planned, not improvised.
- **Thirty to sixty seconds per answer**: answer, reason, example, link.
- **Concede, then hold.** Refusing to concede anything sounds rehearsed; conceding everything sounds beaten.
- **Never invent a number** under questioning.
- **Elaborate** — add the point you did not have time for. That is the Distinction word.
- **Standard English and no fillers** — the conversational part is where they creep back.
:::
