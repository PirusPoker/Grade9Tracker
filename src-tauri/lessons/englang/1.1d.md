## What it is

Paper 1 Question 4 is worth **20 marks** — half of the reading marks on the paper — and tests **AO4: evaluate texts critically**. You are given a line range covering roughly the second half of the source, and a **statement** by an imagined student: *"A student, having read this section of the text, said: 'The writer makes the reader feel sorry for the old man; he seems completely alone.' To what extent do you agree?"* The question then bullets three things:

- **write about your own impressions** of (the character / the situation);
- **evaluate how the writer has created these impressions**;
- **support your opinions with references to the text**.

**What evaluation means.** Not "is the writer good?" but "**does the text do what the statement says, how does it do it, and how far?**" The statement almost always has **two parts** (here: *sorry for him* and *completely alone*). Top answers deal with both, and usually find that one part is more true than the other, or that it is true *in a particular way*. That is what "to what extent" invites: agree, but with precision — "I agree that…, though the word 'completely' is too strong because…".

**Evaluation versus analysis.** Question 4 *includes* language and structure analysis, but in the service of a judgement. Every paragraph should follow the shape: **claim about the statement → evidence → how the writer's method creates that impression → how far this supports the statement**. A Question-2-style paragraph that analyses a simile beautifully but never returns to the statement is capped at the middle levels.

**The levels.** Level 1 (1–5): simple, limited evaluation; Level 2 (6–10): some evaluative comment, some methods mentioned; Level 3 (11–15): clear, relevant evaluation, clear understanding of methods, a range of references; Level 4 (16–20): **perceptive and detailed** evaluation, **critical** engagement with the statement, judicious references, methods analysed convincingly. The word *critical* means you are weighing the statement, not just agreeing with it.

**Timing.** Twenty to twenty-five minutes. Four or five developed paragraphs plus a one-sentence conclusion.

::: method
1. **Split the statement** into its parts and underline them. Decide, before writing, how far you agree with each — this is your line of argument.
2. **Hunt evidence for each part** in the line range: three or four moments where the writer creates the impression (or complicates it). Mark them.
3. Opening sentence: state your view directly — "I largely agree that…, although…".
4. Each paragraph: **impression → evidence → method (language, structure, dialogue, perspective) → link back to the statement** ("so this strongly supports the idea that…" / "this suggests 'completely' overstates it because…").
5. Include **one paragraph that pushes against** the statement or refines it — evidence that the character is not *completely* alone, or that pity is mixed with something else. This is what "critical" and "to what extent" reward.
6. Conclude in one or two sentences: which part of the statement is most true, and why.
:::

::: example Splitting the statement
Statement: *"The writer makes the storm seem terrifying, and the family seem helpless against it."*

Two parts: (a) the storm is terrifying; (b) the family is helpless. A candidate who only writes about the storm can reach Level 3 at best. Planning: Storm — three moments (the roof, the sound, the darkness). Family — the mother's actions (is she helpless? she nails the shutters; she sings to the baby). Line of argument: fully agree the storm is terrifying; partly disagree that they are *helpless* — the mother's small actions are the writer's way of making the family brave rather than helpless, which actually makes the storm feel worse.
:::

::: example A full evaluative paragraph
Statement part: *the old man seems completely alone.*

"The writer creates a strong impression of isolation through the description of his routine: he 'set two cups on the table, then, remembering, put one back.' The structure of the sentence — the action, the pause, the correction — makes the reader witness the habit of a shared life that no longer exists, so the loneliness is shown rather than stated, which is far more affecting. However, I would not agree that he is *completely* alone: the neighbour's boy 'left the paper on the step, as he did every morning' — the phrase 'as he did every morning' quietly tells us someone has been thinking of him for a long time. The writer wants us to feel his loneliness *and* notice the small kindnesses he cannot see, so the statement is true of how the old man feels but not of his situation."
:::

::: example Methods in an evaluation answer
The bullet says *evaluate how the writer has created these impressions*. Methods to reach for: **dialogue** (what characters say and, more often, don't say); **narrative perspective** (are we inside the character's head, or watching from outside?); **contrast** (a cheerful setting against a sad character); **structure** (a memory placed just before a present-day detail); **language** (a single loaded word). Naming the method and explaining how it produces the *impression the statement describes* is the whole skill. "The writer uses a short sentence" is a method with no evaluation; "The short sentence 'Nobody came.' lands after a long paragraph of preparation, so the reader feels the anticlimax with him — which strongly supports the idea that we are made to pity him" is evaluation.
:::

::: example The "to what extent" conclusion
Weak: "In conclusion, I agree with the statement because the writer uses lots of techniques."

Strong: "Overall, the statement is right about the reader's pity but too strong about isolation: the writer gives the old man a world of small, unnoticed care, and the pity comes precisely from his not seeing it." A conclusion that refines the statement, in one sentence, signals Level 4 thinking.
:::

::: q
How many marks is Paper 1 Question 4 worth and which assessment objective does it test?
---
Twenty marks; AO4 — evaluating texts critically.
:::

::: q
What are the three bullet points under the question?
---
Write about your own impressions; evaluate how the writer created them; support your opinions with references to the text.
:::

::: q
Why should you split the statement before you plan?
---
It almost always has two parts, and top answers address both — often agreeing with one more than the other.
:::

::: q
What is the paragraph shape for an evaluation answer?
---
Impression/claim → evidence → the writer's method that creates it → how far it supports the statement.
:::

::: q
What does "critical" mean in the Level 4 descriptor?
---
Weighing the statement rather than simply agreeing — testing, refining or partly challenging it with evidence.
:::

::: q
Why is a brilliant language-analysis paragraph capped in this question if it never mentions the statement?
---
Because the marks are for evaluation; analysis that does not feed a judgement about the statement is not answering the question.
:::

::: q
Name three writer's methods you might evaluate besides word choice.
---
Any three of: dialogue, narrative perspective, contrast, structure/placement, sentence length, imagery, what is withheld.
:::

::: q
How long should Question 4 take?
---
Twenty to twenty-five minutes — it is half of the reading marks.
:::

::: watch
- Deal with **both halves** of the statement; the second half is usually where the "to what extent" lives.
- **Judgement in every paragraph**: end each one by saying how far the evidence supports the statement.
- Methods must be **named and explained** — but as a means to the judgement, not as the point.
- **One paragraph that refines or challenges** the statement is the difference between Level 3 and Level 4.
- Stay in the **line range** given; evidence from earlier in the source is not credited.
- **Twenty-plus minutes.** If you are running short, this is the question to protect.
:::
