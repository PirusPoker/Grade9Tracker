## What it is

Every Paper 2 writing task names a **form** (what kind of text), an **audience** (who reads it) and a **purpose** (what it is for). The 24 content-and-organisation marks reward writing that is "matched to purpose, audience and form" — at the top band, "convincingly". Getting the form wrong does not score zero, but it caps you: a speech written as an essay, or a letter with no sense of a reader, cannot be *convincing*. The good news is that each form has a small set of **conventions** that signal it, and they take thirty seconds to include.

**The forms that come up:**

| Form | Conventions to include | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| **Article** (newspaper or magazine) | A headline (short, punchy, maybe a pun); optional strapline; a hook opening; subheadings are optional and can look gimmicky — one at most; a strong last line | Confident, public; broadsheet = measured and witty; magazine/school = warmer, more direct |
| **Letter** | Address of recipient not needed — just *Dear Sir/Madam* or *Dear Ms Patel* (formal) / *Dear Editor*; a first paragraph saying why you are writing; paragraphs of argument; *Yours faithfully* (Sir/Madam) or *Yours sincerely* (named) | Formal but personal — you are one person addressing another; polite, firm |
| **Speech** | Open by addressing the audience ("Good morning, Year 11 —"); direct address throughout; rhetorical questions, triples, repetition; signposting ("First… And then there is…"); a closing call to action; no "thank you for listening" — end on the line | Spoken rhythm: shorter sentences, pauses (dashes), the odd one-word paragraph |
| **Essay** | A title or the statement as heading; an introduction stating the position; paragraphs of reasoned argument; counter-argument; a conclusion | Formal, considered, fewer rhetorical flourishes — the most "argue"-heavy form |
| **Leaflet / guide** (rare) | A title, a clear sense of sections, direct instruction | Practical, direct |
| **Blog / web article** (occasional) | Like a magazine article but more personal; a title; direct address; short paragraphs | Informal-leaning but still Standard English |

**Audience decides register.** Register is the level of formality and the vocabulary and sentence style that go with it. A broadsheet newspaper wants Standard English, precise vocabulary, sentences that can be complex, humour that is dry. A speech to your year group can be warmer, funnier, more direct, with shorter sentences — but **still Standard English**: no text-speak, no "gonna", no slang unless deliberately quoted. A letter to your MP or headteacher is formal and respectful even when angry — the anger should be in the argument, not the manners. The commonest register error is writing to adults as if they were friends; the second commonest is writing to teenagers as if reading them a legal document.

**Purpose shapes structure.** *Argue* → reasons in order, counter-argument, conclusion. *Persuade* → strongest emotional appeal early and late, rhetorical devices throughout, a call to action. *Explain / inform* (rarer) → clear sequence, examples, less rhetoric. *Advise* → direct address, imperatives, structured steps. Most tasks are argue/persuade; the structure in lesson 2.2a fits both.

**Signalling without gimmicks.** The examiner wants to see you *know* the form — a headline, a salutation, an address to the audience. They do not want a page of layout: drawn boxes, columns, pictures, "Page 1 of 3", fake web addresses. One headline, one strapline at most, then prose.

::: terms
- **Form**: the type of text — article, letter, speech, leaflet, essay.
- **Audience**: who the text is written for.
- **Purpose**: what the text aims to do — argue, persuade, advise, explain.
- **Register**: the level of formality of the language, chosen to suit audience and purpose.
- **Letter conventions**: address, date, greeting (Dear…) and a matching sign-off (Yours sincerely / faithfully).
- **Speech conventions**: direct address to the audience, signposting and a memorable ending.
- **Article conventions**: a headline, strapline and subheadings.
:::

::: method
1. Read the task and **write F / A / P in the margin** with one word each: *article / broadsheet readers / argue*.
2. Choose the **register** from the audience: formal-witty (broadsheet), warm-direct (school speech or magazine), formal-personal (letter).
3. Put the **form's signals** in place before the first paragraph: headline; *Dear…*; "Good morning, everyone."
4. Write the body using the argument structure from 2.2a. Keep the **audience in the room**: address them, anticipate their objections, refer to what they know.
5. **Close in the form's way**: article — a quotable last line; letter — a request and a sign-off; speech — a call to action.
6. Reread for register slips — slang in a formal piece, stiffness in a speech.
:::

::: example Same argument, three forms
Position: the school day should start later.

**Article (broadsheet):** headline *"Let Them Sleep"*; opens with a statistic about teenage circadian rhythms; measured, wry tone; ends "The evidence has been in for a decade. The alarm clocks have not been told."

**Speech (to the school council):** "Good afternoon. I want to talk about the worst moment of your day, and I already know when it is: 6.45." Direct address, a rhetorical question or two, a triple ("later start, better lessons, fewer detentions"), a call to action: "Vote for the trial. Then go home and sleep."

**Letter (to the headteacher):** "Dear Mrs Okafor, I am writing to ask the school to consider trialling a 9.30 start for Years 10 and 11 next term." Reasons in polite, firm paragraphs; the counter-argument acknowledged ("I understand the difficulty this causes for buses"); "I would be glad to discuss this further. Yours sincerely, …".

Same reasons in all three; the *packaging* changes and so does the sentence rhythm.
:::

::: example Register slips
Speech to Year 11 about volunteering, draft: "Volunteering is like, honestly, so worth it, you guys should just do it, it's not even that bad." — slang, filler, no argument.

Rewrite: "You will hear that volunteering looks good on a form. It does. But that is the worst reason to do it, and I want to give you three better ones." — direct, spoken rhythm, Standard English, an argument beginning. A speech can be warm and funny without being sloppy.

Letter to a councillor, draft: "Hi, I'm writing coz the park is a mess and it's well out of order." Rewrite: "Dear Councillor Reid, I am writing about the condition of Harper Park, which has deteriorated badly over the past year." Same complaint, now in a register the reader will take seriously.
:::

::: example Letter conventions
"Dear Sir or Madam" pairs with "Yours faithfully"; "Dear Mr Ahmed" pairs with "Yours sincerely". The first paragraph says who you are (briefly) and why you are writing. The last paragraph says what you want to happen. Between them, the argument. There is no need for addresses or dates in the exam — they earn nothing and cost time. A letter to a newspaper is "Dear Editor" and can be a little more public in tone, since it will be printed.
:::

::: example Speech conventions
Open by acknowledging the room. Signpost so a listener can follow: "There are three reasons, and the third is the one that matters." Use repetition a listener will hear: "Not because it is easy. Not because it is popular. Because it is right." Use pauses — dashes and one-line paragraphs. End on the strongest line, not on thanks: "Sign up at the office on Friday. Bring a friend. Bring two." Written speeches are still marked for accuracy, so punctuate the rhythm properly rather than leaving it to the reader.
:::

::: q
What three things does every Paper 2 writing task specify?
---
Form, audience and purpose.
:::

::: q
Give two conventions of an article.
---
A headline (and optional strapline), a hook opening, a strong final line — subheadings optional and sparing.
:::

::: q
Which sign-off goes with "Dear Sir or Madam", and which with a named person?
---
"Yours faithfully" for Sir/Madam; "Yours sincerely" for a named recipient.
:::

::: q
Name three features that signal a speech.
---
Addressing the audience at the start, direct address throughout, signposting, repetition/triples, a closing call to action.
:::

::: q
What decides the register of your writing?
---
The audience — formal and precise for broadsheet readers or officials; warmer and more direct for peers, but always Standard English.
:::

::: q
What is the most common register error?
---
Writing to adults or officials as if to friends — slang, filler and casual openings in a formal form.
:::

::: q
Should you draw layout features such as columns and pictures for an article?
---
No — a headline and possibly a strapline are enough; drawn layout wastes time and earns nothing.
:::

::: q
How does purpose change structure?
---
Argue: ordered reasons plus counter-argument; persuade: emotional appeals early and late plus a call to action; explain/advise: clear sequence and direct instruction.
:::

::: watch
- Write **F / A / P** in the margin before you plan; choose register from the audience.
- **Form signals up front**: headline, *Dear…*, or an address to the room — thirty seconds, real marks.
- **Standard English in every form**, including speeches to teenagers.
- **Letters**: faithfully/sincerely pairing; no addresses; say what you want in the last paragraph.
- **Speeches**: signpost, repeat, end on a call to action — not "thank you for listening".
- Same argument fits every form; only the **packaging and rhythm** change.
:::
