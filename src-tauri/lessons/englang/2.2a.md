## What it is

Paper 2 Section B is worth **40 marks** (24 for content and organisation, 16 for technical accuracy — the same split as Paper 1) and asks you to **write to present a viewpoint**. The task gives you a **statement** — *"Homework should be abolished."*, *"Young people today are more concerned with their phones than with the world around them."* — and a **form, audience and purpose**: *"Write an article for a broadsheet newspaper in which you argue your point of view on this statement."* You have **45 minutes**.

**Argue versus persuade.** The task will say *argue*, *persuade*, or *explain your point of view*. In practice the best answers do both: they **argue** — build a case with reasons, evidence and logic, and deal with the other side — and they **persuade** — use rhetoric, anecdote, tone and rhythm to make the reader feel the case. An answer that only persuades sounds like an advert; one that only argues sounds like a textbook. The top band ("convincing, compelling") is a **reasoned argument with a voice**.

**The core: a clear line of argument.** Before writing, decide **what you think** — one sentence — and **three reasons** for it. Then decide the **strongest objection** to your view and how you will answer it. That is the whole plan. Candidates who "see both sides" without ever committing produce fog; the task says *your* point of view. You may hold a nuanced view ("Homework should not be abolished but it should be cut by half and set differently"), and nuance reads as intelligence — but it must still be a *position*.

**Rhetorical tools, used deliberately** (learn these; use four or five, not all of them):

- **Direct address** — "you", "we" — to include the reader in the argument.
- **Rhetorical question** — one that the argument then answers; not five in a row.
- **Triples / rule of three** — "cheaper, faster, fairer".
- **Anecdote** — a specific, plausible, short story (invented is fine) to make the abstract concrete.
- **Statistics and expert opinion** — invented is acceptable in this exam if plausible and not absurd ("a 2019 study found…", "the head of a large secondary school told me…"); use sparingly.
- **Counter-argument and rebuttal** — "Some will say… But…" — the single most valuable move for *argue* tasks.
- **Contrast** — "not X but Y".
- **Emotive language**, controlled — the odd loaded word, not a torrent.
- **Repetition / anaphora** — a phrase repeated at the start of successive sentences, once in the piece.
- **Short sentence after a long one** — for the verdict.
- **Humour and irony** — a voice that can be wry is a voice the reader trusts.

**Tone and register.** Match them to the audience (see 2.2b). For a broadsheet article or a speech to the school: **Standard English**, confident, allowed to be witty, never slangy. Avoid "I think" more than once; state things.

::: terms
- **Viewpoint writing**: writing that presents and argues a personal opinion.
- **Argue**: to present a case, supported by evidence and reasoning, to convince the reader.
- **Persuade**: to make the reader agree or act, often with emotional appeals.
- **Counter-argument**: an opposing view you raise yourself.
- **Rebuttal**: showing why the counter-argument is weak.
- **Hook**: an opening designed to grab the reader's interest.
:::

::: method
1. **Five minutes planning**: your position in one sentence; three reasons, each with an example or piece of evidence; the strongest objection and your reply; an opening hook and a closing line.
2. **Opening paragraph**: hook (anecdote, striking claim, a question you will answer), then state your position clearly by the end of the paragraph.
3. **One reason per paragraph**, in ascending order of strength — save the best for last. Each: topic sentence → development → evidence/example → link back to the position.
4. **Counter-argument paragraph** before your final reason: state the other side fairly in one or two sentences, then dismantle it.
5. **Closing paragraph**: short. Return to the hook; end on a line the reader can quote. No "In conclusion, I have shown that…".
6. **Five minutes checking** for the accuracy killers: comma splices, apostrophes, homophones, capitals, paragraph breaks.
:::

::: example Planning a viewpoint piece
Statement: *"School uniform should be scrapped."* Task: article for a school magazine arguing your view.

Position: keep uniform, but change what it is. Reasons: (1) it removes a daily source of comparison and cost — the anecdote of the pupil with one hoodie; (2) it separates school from home in a way that helps concentration — the "costume" argument; (3) the real problem is *bad* uniform — blazers in July — so the fix is better design, not abolition. Objection: uniform suppresses individuality. Reply: nobody's individuality lives in a jumper; it lives in what they say and make, and uniform frees the morning for that. Hook: the ninety seconds every morning spent deciding what to wear, multiplied by a year. Closing line: "Give us a uniform we can run in, and we will stop arguing about it."
:::

::: example Opening paragraph with a hook
"Every school morning in this country, around three million teenagers spend the first ninety seconds of consciousness worrying about what they look like. That is roughly 75,000 hours of adolescent anxiety a day, and school uniform is the only thing standing between us and doubling it. So before we scrap it — and every year someone proposes scrapping it — it is worth asking what we would be scrapping it *for*."

A statistic (plausible, invented), a wry tone, a direct claim, and a position implied by the end of the paragraph. The reader knows where the piece is going and wants to see it get there.
:::

::: example Counter-argument and rebuttal
"The case against uniform is usually made in one word: individuality. Uniform, we are told, turns young people into identical units and stifles self-expression. It is a serious point and it deserves a serious answer, which is this: nobody's individuality lives in a jumper. It lives in what they argue, make, play and write — and a school that lets pupils spend their energy on those things, rather than on a daily fashion audit, is protecting individuality, not attacking it. The people most damaged by non-uniform days are not the confident; they are the ones who own one hoodie."

The other side stated fairly, then turned. The final sentence lands the anecdote from the plan as a punch.
:::

::: example Closing paragraph
Weak: "In conclusion, I have argued that school uniform should be kept because of cost, concentration and design. I hope you agree with my point of view."

Strong: "So keep the uniform. Make it cheap, make it something we can run in, and stop making us wear a blazer in July. Do that, and the ninety seconds every morning go back to what they should be for: sleep." Short, returns to the hook, ends on a line with rhythm and a joke. The position is restated by *doing* it, not announcing it.
:::

::: q
How many marks is Paper 2 Section B worth and how are they split?
---
Forty: 24 for content and organisation, 16 for technical accuracy.
:::

::: q
What is the difference between arguing and persuading, and which should you do?
---
Arguing builds a reasoned case with evidence and counter-argument; persuading uses rhetoric and feeling. The best answers do both.
:::

::: q
What must you decide before you write?
---
Your position in one sentence, three reasons with examples, the strongest objection and your reply, a hook and a closing line.
:::

::: q
Why is a counter-argument paragraph so valuable?
---
It shows reasoning rather than assertion — stating the other side fairly and dismantling it is what makes an argument convincing.
:::

::: q
May you invent statistics or experts?
---
Yes, if plausible and used sparingly — "a 2019 study found…" is fine; an absurd figure damages credibility.
:::

::: q
In what order should your reasons appear?
---
Ascending strength — the best reason last, just before the close.
:::

::: q
What should a closing paragraph do and avoid?
---
Return to the hook and end on a quotable line; avoid "In conclusion, I have shown…" summaries.
:::

::: q
How many rhetorical devices should you aim to use?
---
Four or five, deliberately placed — not every device once each.
:::

::: watch
- **Commit to a position** — nuanced is fine, fence-sitting is not.
- **Plan the counter-argument**; it is the move that separates arguing from asserting.
- **Hook, then position** in the first paragraph; **hook again** in the last.
- Rhetorical questions **once or twice**; not a paragraph of them.
- **Standard English, confident register**; "I think" at most once.
- **Five minutes checking** — the sixteen accuracy marks are the same on both papers.
:::
