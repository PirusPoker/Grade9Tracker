## What it is

Paper 2 Question 2 is worth **8 marks** and asks you to use **both sources** to **"write a summary of the differences (or similarities) between…"** — the two writers' experiences, the two places, the two people. It tests **AO1**: identify explicit and implicit information and ideas, and **select and synthesise evidence from different texts**. It is the question where candidates most often do the wrong task well: they *compare methods* (that is Question 4) or *analyse language* (Question 3), when Question 2 wants **what** each text says, not **how**.

**The three things a top-band summary does:**

1. **States a difference clearly** — a sentence that names the difference and puts both texts in it: "Both writers describe a school, but for the first writer it is a place of fear whereas for the second it is a refuge."
2. **Supports it with evidence from both texts** — a short quotation or precise paraphrase from Source A and one from Source B for *each* difference.
3. **Infers** — goes beyond the surface fact to what it implies. "The first writer 'counted the days' until leaving, which suggests the school was something to be endured rather than experienced." The word *suggests* (or *implies*, *this shows that*) is where the top marks live: Level 4 (7–8) requires "perceptive synthesis and interpretation", meaning **inferences** about both texts, not just a list of facts.

**What "synthesis" means.** Putting the two texts together in the same sentence or paragraph so that the reader sees the difference *as* a difference. A summary that does all of Source A and then all of Source B is two summaries, not a synthesis, and caps at the middle levels. Every paragraph should hold both texts.

**What to leave out.** No techniques, no "the writer uses…", no comments on effect, no evaluation of which text is better. If you write the word *simile* in Question 2 you have wandered into the wrong question. Also no whole-text comparison of tone or purpose — that is Question 4 territory. Keep to the *subject* named in the question: if it says "differences between the two hotels", stay on the hotels, not on the writers' journeys.

**Timing.** Eight to ten minutes. Three paragraphs, each one difference, each with evidence from both and an inference on each. That is the whole answer.

::: terms
- **Summary**: a concise account of the main points, in your own words.
- **Synthesis**: bringing together information from two sources.
- **Inference**: working out what is implied, rather than stated.
- **Differences / similarities**: Question 2 asks you to summarise differences (or similarities) between the two sources on a given topic.
- **Embedded quotation**: a short quotation built into your own sentence.
:::

::: method
1. Underline the **focus** in the question (the thing you are summarising). Skim both sources with only that focus in mind, marking three or four relevant details in each.
2. Pair the details: which detail in A contrasts most sharply with which in B? Choose **three pairs**.
3. Write each paragraph in the shape: **difference statement (both texts named) → evidence A + inference → evidence B + inference**.
4. Use inference verbs: *suggests, implies, indicates, shows that*. One inference per piece of evidence.
5. Use comparison connectives to hold the texts together: *whereas, while, in contrast, unlike, similarly*.
6. Read your answer back for the banned words: *technique, simile, metaphor, effect, the writer uses*. Delete any paragraph built on them.
:::

::: example A full Question 2 paragraph
Question: *"Use details from both sources. Write a summary of the differences between the two markets."*

"The most obvious difference is size and order. The market in Source A is vast and chaotic — the writer is 'carried along' by the crowd and cannot see where the stalls end — which suggests it is a place where a visitor loses control and simply submits to it. In contrast, the market in Source B is small enough that the writer knows 'every trader by name', implying a settled, local community where the visitor is a participant rather than a stranger. So Source A's market overwhelms while Source B's includes."

Difference stated with both texts; evidence from each; an inference on each; a closing sentence that binds them. Repeat twice with different differences.
:::

::: example Fact versus inference
Text A: *"The children sat in rows, hands folded, until the bell."* Text B: *"The children were everywhere — under the tables, on the windowsill, one of them apparently in the cupboard."*

Fact-only summary (Level 2): "In A the children sit in rows; in B the children are all over the room."

With inference (Level 4): "In A the children sit 'in rows, hands folded', which suggests a school built on obedience and stillness, where the children have learned to wait. In B they are 'everywhere', even 'in the cupboard', implying a school where movement and curiosity are allowed — or where the adults have given up controlling them." The second version reads what the details *mean*; the "or" shows the writer weighing two readings, which is perceptive.
:::

::: example The wrong task
Candidate: "Source A uses a metaphor, 'a river of people', to show the market is busy. Source B uses a list to show variety." Both sentences are about *how* the writers write. On Question 2 they score nothing — the marks are for *what* each market is like. Rewritten: "Source A's market is so crowded that the writer describes it as 'a river of people', suggesting movement she cannot resist; Source B's stalls sell 'cheese, boots, birdseed and secondhand hymn books', which implies a small market serving every need of one village." Same quotations, now used as evidence for content.
:::

::: example Choosing differences that are actually different
The question asks about the two grandmothers. A candidate's three differences: one is old, the other is old; one lives in a house, the other in a flat; one bakes, the other cooks. The first is not a difference; the second and third are trivial. Better: "Grandmother A is described through what she *gives* — food, sweets, time — whereas Grandmother B is described through what she *withholds*, sitting 'silent through every meal'; this suggests the first writer remembers warmth and the second remembers distance." One real difference with inference is worth more than three trivial ones.
:::

::: q
How many marks is Paper 2 Question 2 worth and which assessment objective does it test?
---
Eight marks; AO1 — identifying information and ideas and synthesising evidence from both texts.
:::

::: q
What three things must each paragraph contain?
---
A clear statement of the difference naming both texts; evidence from each source; an inference on each piece of evidence.
:::

::: q
What does "synthesis" mean in this question?
---
Holding both texts together in the same paragraph so the difference is seen as a difference — not Source A then Source B separately.
:::

::: q
Why should you never write "the writer uses a simile" in Question 2?
---
Question 2 is about what the texts say, not how they say it; methods and effects belong to Questions 3 and 4.
:::

::: q
Give three inference verbs.
---
Suggests, implies, indicates (also: shows that, reveals).
:::

::: q
What separates a Level 2 summary from a Level 4 one?
---
Level 2 lists facts; Level 4 interprets them — perceptive inferences about what the details mean in both texts.
:::

::: q
How many differences should you aim to write about?
---
Three, each developed with evidence from both texts.
:::

::: q
Name three connectives that hold two texts together.
---
Whereas, in contrast, while (also: unlike, similarly, both).
:::

::: watch
- **What, not how.** Content and inference only — no techniques, no effects.
- **Both texts in every paragraph**, joined by *whereas / in contrast / while*.
- **Inference on every quotation** — "which suggests…" is the phrase that earns Level 4.
- Stay on the **focus** named in the question; a difference about something else does not count.
- Three real differences, each developed, in **eight to ten minutes**.
- Do not evaluate which text is better — that is not asked.
:::

::: test
You need to refer to both sources.

**Source A** — from a newspaper column, 2024

[1] I got my first job at fourteen, washing up at a café on the seafront for four pounds an hour. I loved it. I loved the steam and the clatter and the radio that played the same six songs all summer, and I loved the envelope of coins on a Saturday night that was mine, entirely mine, to spend on nothing useful at all.

[2] The work was hard in a cheerful sort of way. By eleven the sinks were full; by one the floor was a lake. Pete, the owner, shouted at everyone and meant none of it. When I cut my hand on a broken glass he drove me to the hospital himself and paid me for the afternoon I missed.

[3] Looking back, I learned more in that kitchen than in a year of lessons: how to talk to strangers, how to keep going when you are tired, how money is made one plate at a time. My own daughter, at fifteen, has just started stacking shelves on Sundays. I tell her she is lucky. She tells me her feet hurt. We are both right.

**Source B** — a report on London's child workers, written for this test in the style of 1871

[1] In a narrow court off the Whitechapel Road I found a room, not twelve feet square, in which a mother and four children were engaged in the making of match-boxes. The youngest could not have been more than five years of age. She sat upon an upturned crate, pasting the paper wrappers with a rapidity that would have astonished any lady of leisure, and she did not once raise her eyes to me.

[2] "We gets twopence-farthing the gross," said the mother, "and finds our own paste." A gross is one hundred and forty-four boxes. By working from six in the morning until past ten at night, the family might complete seven gross in a day, and so earn something less than sixteen pence — out of which the rent, the fire and the paste must all be paid.

[3] I asked the eldest boy whether he went to school. He looked at me as if I had asked whether he kept a carriage. There is, I am told, a Ragged School not half a mile distant; but a child at school earns nothing, and in such a household a child who earns nothing is a luxury no one can afford.

The children in both sources work. Use details from both sources to write a summary of the differences between the children's experiences of work. (8)
---
Indicative content:

- the writer in Source A chose to work and loved it; the children in Source B work because the family would starve otherwise
- Source A's wages were hers 'to spend on nothing useful at all'; in Source B every penny goes on rent, fire and paste
- Source A worked Saturdays; the Source B family works from six in the morning until past ten at night
- Pete in Source A cared for her when she was hurt; no one in Source B cares for the children — they are paid by the gross
- Source A's writer learned life skills 'more than in a year of lessons'; the Source B children cannot go to school at all

Mark with the levels:
- Level 4 (7–8): perceptive synthesis of both sources; perceptive inferences about the differences; judicious references to both.
- Level 3 (5–6): clear synthesis; clear inferences; relevant references to both.
- Level 2 (3–4): some attempt to link the sources; some inference; some references.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple, mostly literal comments.
:::
