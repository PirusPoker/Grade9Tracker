## What it is

The examiner forms a view of your writing in the **first three lines** and confirms it in the **last three**. Everything in between is read in the light of that view. So the opening and the ending are worth far more than their length, and both are things you can practise in isolation until they are reliable. The **16 technical accuracy marks** (AO6) are a separate, mechanical business — and they are the most improvable marks on either paper, because the errors that cost them are always the same ones.

**Openings — what the first sentence must do.** Give the reader something *specific* (an object, a sound, an action, a line of speech), in a *voice* (a narrator or describer with an attitude), and imply a *situation* (something is about to happen, or has just happened). What it must **not** do: summarise the scene ("It was a busy market"), introduce the narrator ("My name is…"), state the weather as a full sentence, or promise ("This is the story of the worst day of my life"). Five dependable opening moves:

1. **The odd detail** — "There were two forks on the table and only one of us."
2. **Mid-action** — "She was already running when the alarm started."
3. **A line of dialogue** — "'Don't look down,' he said, which was the first time I thought about looking down."
4. **A short declarative with weight** — "The house had been sold. Nobody had told the dog."
5. **A sensory hit** — "The heat came in through the closed windows like a smell."

**Endings — what the last sentence must do.** Land on an **image**, ideally one that echoes the opening, and **stop**. Do not explain what the piece meant; do not add a moral; do not summarise. If the opening had two forks, the ending can have one fork washed and put away. If the story began with a dog, end with the dog. A cyclical ending signals control, which is exactly what the top band of AO5 ("compelling, convincing, crafted") is looking for. Practical rule: **the last paragraph is short**, three sentences or fewer, and the last sentence is the shortest.

**Technical accuracy — where the sixteen marks go.** Level 4 (13–16) needs: sentence demarcation "consistently secure and consistently accurate" (full stops and capitals in the right places every time), a **wide range of punctuation used with accuracy** (commas, apostrophes, speech marks, colons, semicolons, dashes where they help), a **full range of sentence forms** used for effect, Standard English "consistently", **high accuracy** in spelling including ambitious words, and **extensive and ambitious vocabulary**. The errors that drop candidates to Level 2 or 3 are boring and fixable:

- **Comma splices** — two sentences joined by a comma: "It was late, she went home." Fix: full stop, semicolon, or a conjunction.
- **Apostrophes** — *its/it's*, plurals with apostrophes ("the dog's were barking"), possession without ("the dogs bowl").
- **Homophones** — *their/there/they're*, *where/were/we're*, *to/too*, *affect/effect*, *of* for *have* ("should of").
- **Tense drift** — a past-tense story slipping into present for a paragraph and back.
- **Speech punctuation** — no new line for a new speaker; missing commas before the closing speech mark.
- **Capitals** — missing on *I*, names, and sentence starts; random capitals on ordinary nouns.

Ambitious punctuation is rewarded when it is correct: a **semicolon** between two closely linked complete sentences; a **colon** before an explanation or list; a pair of **dashes** for an interruption. Use each once or twice, correctly, rather than ten times hopefully.

::: method
1. Before the exam, **rehearse five openings and five endings** for common prompts (a deserted place, a storm, a journey, a time things went wrong, an old photograph) so that you are not inventing under pressure.
2. In the exam, write the **final image on your plan** before writing the opening; then choose an opening that the ending can echo.
3. First sentence: **detail + voice + implied situation**. Test it — would you read the second sentence?
4. Last paragraph: **short**, returns to the opening image, ends on the shortest sentence. No moral, no summary.
5. Reserve **five minutes** for a proofreading pass that hunts *only* for the six errors above. Read each sentence backwards from the last one if you tend to skim.
6. Use one semicolon, one colon and one pair of dashes somewhere, **correctly** — and check them in the proofread.
:::

::: example Rewriting an opening
Prompt: a description of a deserted place.

Draft: "The old factory was abandoned and deserted. It was very quiet and there was nobody there. It looked scary."

Rewrite: "Somebody had left a mug on the windowsill of the factory office, and forty years of dust had not moved it." One specific object; an implied story (the sudden leaving); the scale of time; a calm, observant voice that is more unsettling than "scary". The rewrite is one sentence and does more than the three.
:::

::: example Rewriting an ending
Draft ending of a story about a lost dog found: "In the end we found Max and I realised how much he meant to me. I will never leave the gate open again. It was the best day ever."

Rewrite: "Max walked ahead of us all the way home, stopping every few steps to check we were still there. At the gate he waited. I closed it, and then, because he was watching, I checked it." The draft tells us the lesson three times; the rewrite shows the lesson in an action and ends on the shortest sentence. If the story opened with the gate, this is cyclical.
:::

::: example Fixing a comma splice
"The rain had stopped, the street was shining, nobody had come out yet." — three sentences held together by commas. Three fixes, each acceptable: full stops ("The rain had stopped. The street was shining. Nobody had come out yet."); a semicolon where the link is close ("The rain had stopped; the street was shining. Nobody had come out yet."); a conjunction ("The rain had stopped and the street was shining, but nobody had come out yet."). The best version varies the rhythm: "The rain had stopped and the street was shining. Nobody had come out yet." Long, then short.
:::

::: example Ambitious punctuation done correctly
Colon before an explanation: "He knew exactly what the noise was: the gate, again." Semicolon between linked sentences: "The kettle was still warm; whoever had been here had not been gone long." Dashes for interruption: "The letter — she had read it four times — was in her coat pocket." Each is one mark's worth of evidence for "wide range of punctuation used with accuracy". Each, misused, is evidence of the opposite — so if unsure, use a full stop.
:::

::: q
What three things should a first sentence do?
---
Give a specific detail, establish a voice, and imply a situation.
:::

::: q
Name three opening moves you could rely on.
---
Any three of: an odd detail, mid-action, a line of dialogue, a short weighty declarative, a sensory hit.
:::

::: q
What should the final paragraph avoid, and what should it contain?
---
Avoid explanation, moral or summary; contain a short return to the opening image, ending on the shortest sentence.
:::

::: q
What is a comma splice and how do you fix it?
---
Two complete sentences joined only by a comma; fix with a full stop, a semicolon, or a conjunction.
:::

::: q
List four homophone pairs that cost accuracy marks.
---
their/there/they're; where/were; to/too; affect/effect (also "should of" for "should have").
:::

::: q
What does Level 4 technical accuracy require of sentence demarcation?
---
Consistently secure and consistently accurate full stops and capital letters.
:::

::: q
When is a semicolon correct?
---
Between two closely related complete sentences (each could stand alone).
:::

::: q
Why write the ending on your plan before the opening?
---
So the opening can plant the image the ending will return to — a cyclical structure that shows control.
:::

::: watch
- The examiner decides in the **first three lines**; make them a detail with a voice, not a summary.
- **Ending = image, short, echoing the opening.** No moral, no "best day ever".
- The **six errors** — comma splices, apostrophes, homophones, tense drift, speech punctuation, capitals — are the whole difference between 8 and 14 out of 16.
- **Ambitious punctuation once, correctly**, beats often and wrongly.
- **Five minutes' proofreading** is non-negotiable; hunt errors, do not re-read for enjoyment.
- Rehearse openings and endings **before** the exam; they are the most practisable part of the paper.
:::
