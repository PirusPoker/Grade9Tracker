## What it is

Paper 2 Question 1 is worth **4 marks** and takes the form of **eight statements** about a given line range of **Source A**; you must **shade the boxes of the four that are true**. It tests **AO1** (identify explicit and implicit information). It is the only multiple-choice question on either paper and it is designed to be done in five minutes — but under time pressure it is also easy to lose marks on by misreading a statement, shading five boxes, or answering from memory rather than from the lines.

**What the statements are like.** Four are true, four are false. The false ones are not random: they are engineered to catch a skimmer. Typical traps:

- **A detail from outside the line range** — true of the text, false for the question.
- **A near miss** — the text says the writer *considered* leaving; the statement says she *left*.
- **A swapped number, name or time** — the text says twenty minutes; the statement says twelve.
- **An inference that goes too far** — the text says the room was cold; the statement says the heating was broken.
- **A reversal** — the text says he was *not* surprised; the statement says he was surprised.
- **A plausible general fact** that is simply not in the text.

**How marking works.** One mark per correctly shaded true statement, up to four. If you shade **more than four**, the examiner marks only the first four you shaded in order — so extra boxes can only cost you. If you change your mind, **cross the box out clearly** and shade the new one; ambiguous shading scores nothing.

**Reading the lines.** The question tells you which lines — usually a short chunk near the start of Source A. Read those lines **twice**, slowly, before you look at the statements; then check each statement **against the lines**, not against your memory. Put a small tick, cross or question mark beside each statement. If you have more than four ticks, re-check the ticks for near misses and reversals. If you have fewer than four, re-check the crosses for statements you dismissed because they were phrased differently from the text (a correct paraphrase is still true).

::: terms
- **True or false statements**: Paper 2 Question 1 lists eight statements; you shade the four that are true.
- **Explicit information**: information stated directly in the text.
- **Line reference**: the specific lines the statements are based on.
- **Paraphrase**: a statement may be true even when worded differently from the source.
:::

::: method
1. Draw a line under the last line of the range on the source. Read the range twice.
2. Read all **eight** statements before shading anything.
3. For each statement, find the **exact phrase in the text** that confirms or contradicts it. Tick, cross or question mark in the margin.
4. Check every tick for the traps: outside the range? near miss? number or name changed? reversal? inference too far?
5. Shade **exactly four**. If you are stuck between five, the one with the *strongest word* (always, never, completely, all) is most likely the false one.
6. Do not spend more than five minutes. Four marks; move on to Question 2.
:::

::: example Spotting the traps
Lines 1 to 6 of Source A: *"I arrived in the town on a wet Thursday, twenty minutes later than I had promised. The hotel, which the brochure had called 'charming', had one window that did not close and a landlady who did not smile. I considered leaving that first night. I did not."*

Statements: (A) The writer arrived on a Thursday. (B) The writer was twelve minutes late. (C) The brochure called the hotel charming. (D) The hotel's windows would not open. (E) The landlady was unfriendly. (F) The writer left the hotel on the first night. (G) It was raining when the writer arrived. (H) The writer had visited the town before.

True: **A** (explicit), **C** (explicit), **E** (safe inference from "did not smile"), **G** ("a wet Thursday"). False: B (twenty, not twelve — swapped number); D (one window did not *close*, not open — near miss); F (considered leaving; "I did not" — reversal); H (nothing in the text — plausible invention).
:::

::: example Paraphrase is still true
Text: *"The bus was so full that three of us stood the whole way, holding the rail with both hands."*

Statement: "The journey was uncomfortable." Nothing in the text says "uncomfortable" — but a full bus, standing the whole way, gripping with both hands, supports it as a safe one-step inference. **True.** Candidates who cross it because the word is not there lose the mark. The test is whether the lines clearly support the statement, not whether they contain its words.
:::

::: example The "too many ticks" fix
A candidate has ticked five statements. Two of them: "The writer was frightened of the dog" and "The writer was nervous around the dog." The text says she "kept the table between us" and "watched its mouth". Both statements are supported — so the false one is elsewhere. Re-checking the other three ticks: "The dog belonged to the writer's uncle." The text says it belonged to "the man next door". Family relation invented — cross it. Exactly four remain.
:::

::: example Shading discipline
A candidate shades A, C, E, G — then re-reads and thinks F might be true too, and shades it without unshading anything. Five boxes: the examiner marks the first four shaded, which were correct, so no harm done *this time*. But if the candidate had first shaded a wrong one and then added the right one as a fifth, the right one would not count. Rule: **change** a decision by clearly crossing out the old box, never by adding a box.
:::

::: q
How many statements are given in Paper 2 Question 1 and how many are true?
---
Eight statements; four are true.
:::

::: q
What happens if you shade five boxes?
---
Only the first four you shaded are marked — a fifth can never gain a mark and may cost one.
:::

::: q
Name three kinds of false statement the question uses as traps.
---
Any three of: detail from outside the line range; near miss (changed verb); swapped number/name; inference too far; reversal; plausible fact not in the text.
:::

::: q
The text says "he did not look surprised"; the statement says "he was surprised". True or false, and why?
---
False — it is a reversal of what the text says.
:::

::: q
Should you cross a statement because it uses different words from the text?
---
No — an accurate paraphrase or safe one-step inference is still true; check the meaning, not the wording.
:::

::: q
If you have five ticks and must lose one, which statement is the most likely to be false?
---
The one with the strongest absolute word (always, never, completely, all) or the one whose detail you cannot find in the exact lines.
:::

::: q
How should you change your mind about a shaded box?
---
Cross out the old box clearly and shade the new one; never just add another shaded box.
:::

::: q
How long should Question 1 take?
---
About five minutes.
:::

::: watch
- **Read the lines twice before the statements**, and check each statement against the text, not your memory.
- **Exactly four boxes.** Change by crossing out, never by adding.
- Watch for **near misses** (open/close, considered/did), **reversals** and **swapped numbers**.
- A **paraphrase** of the text is still true; do not cross a statement for using different words.
- Anything from **outside the line range** is false for this question, however true of the text.
- Five minutes, then Question 2.
:::
