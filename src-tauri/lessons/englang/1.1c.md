## What it is

Paper 1 Question 3 is worth **8 marks** and asks **"How has the writer structured the text to interest you as a reader?"** — always about the **whole source**, not a line range. It is the question students find hardest, because "structure" sounds vague. It is not vague. Structure means **the order in which the writer shows you things, and the effect of that order**. The question bullets three things:

- **what the writer focuses your attention on at the beginning**;
- **how and why the writer changes this focus as the text develops**;
- **any other structural features that interest you**.

So the core of an answer is a **map of the writer's focus**: it starts *here*, it moves *there*, it ends *here* — and each move does something to the reader. Think of the writer as holding a camera. Where does the camera point first? Does it zoom in from wide to close, or pull back? Does it cut between two things? Does it return to where it began?

**Structural features to look for** (learn this list):

- **Opening** — in the middle of action? a wide setting shot? a single detail? dialogue? a question?
- **Shifts of focus** — from setting to character, from outside to inside, from one character to another, from the present to a memory (flashback) and back.
- **Zoom in / zoom out** — the writer narrows to a small detail or widens to a whole landscape.
- **Change of pace** — long descriptive paragraphs slowing down, short paragraphs or one-line paragraphs speeding up or jolting.
- **Repetition and return** — an image or phrase from the opening coming back at the end, changed (cyclical structure).
- **Withholding** — the writer delays telling you who or what something is, so you read on to find out.
- **Contrast / juxtaposition** — placing two very different things side by side.
- **Ending** — resolution, cliffhanger, twist, a return to the beginning, a widening out.
- **Perspective** — who we see through; a shift from one character's viewpoint to another.
- **Paragraphing and sentence length as structure** — a one-sentence paragraph is a structural choice.

**What NOT to do.** Question 3 is not Question 2. Analysing word choices ("the verb 'crept' suggests…") scores nothing here. If you find yourself explaining a metaphor, stop. The evidence in a structure answer is **what happens where**: "the writer opens with…", "in the third paragraph the focus shifts to…", "the final line returns to…". Short quotations are fine as signposts, but the analysis is about *placement*, not diction.

**Timing.** About ten minutes; three linked paragraphs — beginning, development, ending — is the natural shape.

::: method
1. As you read the whole source, number the paragraphs and write **one word in the margin** for what each is about (setting, girl, memory, storm, girl again…). This is your focus map.
2. Paragraph 1 of your answer: the **opening**. What does the writer show first, and what does that make the reader want or expect?
3. Paragraph 2: the **main shift(s)**. Where does the focus move, how (zoom, cut, flashback, change of perspective), and *why* — what does the reader learn or feel because of the order?
4. Paragraph 3: the **ending**. How does it relate to the beginning? Does it answer, reverse, echo or widen?
5. Add one comment on **pace** — where a short paragraph or a single-sentence paragraph changes the rhythm and what it does.
6. Every point must have an **effect on the reader**: builds tension, creates sympathy, delays an answer, makes us re-read the opening differently.
:::

::: example Mapping a source
A source has six paragraphs: (1) wide description of a frozen lake at dawn; (2) a small figure walking onto the ice; (3) flashback to the figure, a boy, arguing with his father the night before; (4) back to the ice, which creaks; (5) a single line: "He kept walking."; (6) the father at the window, watching, unable to shout.

Structure answer, opening: "The writer begins with a wide, still shot of the frozen lake, establishing a cold, empty setting before any person appears, so that when the 'small figure' enters in paragraph two the reader feels its vulnerability against that scale." Development: "The focus then cuts away from the danger to a flashback of the argument, which withholds the outcome on the ice while explaining why the boy is there; the delay builds tension and creates sympathy, because we now understand his anger." Ending: "The one-line paragraph 'He kept walking' jolts the pace and refuses to resolve the danger, and the final shift of perspective to the father at the window mirrors the opening — a watcher and a small figure — but now with a person's fear in it."
:::

::: example Zoom and withholding
A source opens with a description of a kitchen: the kettle, the calendar, a chair pushed back from the table. Only in paragraph four is a woman mentioned, sitting very still. Only in the last line do we learn she is holding a letter.

"The writer structures the opening as a slow zoom: the reader is shown objects before people — the kettle, the calendar, the pushed-back chair — so that the kitchen feels interrupted before we know why. Withholding the woman until the fourth paragraph, and the letter until the final line, means the objects are re-read in hindsight as evidence of a shock. The structure makes the reader a detective, and the last-line reveal rewards the delay." Notice: no word-level analysis, all placement and effect.
:::

::: example Pace through paragraphing
A long, flowing paragraph describes a runner's steady rhythm over a mile; then: "Then the dog." — a two-word paragraph; then a paragraph of short, fragmented sentences.

"The writer controls pace through paragraph length. The long opening paragraph mirrors the runner's unbroken stride, lulling the reader into the same rhythm. The two-word paragraph 'Then the dog.' breaks that rhythm as abruptly as the dog breaks the run, and the fragmented sentences that follow reproduce panic. The structure makes the reader experience the interruption rather than just being told about it."
:::

::: example What loses marks
Candidate: "At the beginning the writer uses the adjective 'silent' to create a tense atmosphere, and the simile 'like glass' shows how fragile the ice is." Both points are about language, so neither scores on Question 3. The same observations become structural when the point is *placement*: "The writer places the description of silence and fragility *before* introducing the boy, so that the reader is already braced for breakage when he steps onto the ice." Same source, same details, now a structure point.
:::

::: q
How many marks is Paper 1 Question 3 worth and what part of the source does it cover?
---
Eight marks; the whole source, not a line range.
:::

::: q
In one sentence, what does "structure" mean in this question?
---
The order in which the writer shows the reader things, and the effect of that order.
:::

::: q
What are the three bullet points the question gives you?
---
What the writer focuses on at the beginning; how and why the focus changes; any other structural features.
:::

::: q
Name five structural features you could write about.
---
Any of: opening focus, shift of focus, zoom in/out, flashback, change of pace, one-line paragraphs, repetition/cyclical ending, withholding information, contrast, change of perspective, ending type.
:::

::: q
Why does analysing a metaphor score nothing on Question 3?
---
That is language analysis (Question 2); Question 3 rewards comments on placement and order, not word choice.
:::

::: q
What is a "cyclical" structure?
---
The ending returns to an image, place or phrase from the opening, usually changed by what has happened between.
:::

::: q
What quick technique helps you see a text's structure while reading?
---
Number the paragraphs and write one word in the margin for the focus of each — a focus map.
:::

::: q
How can paragraph length be a structural feature?
---
A single short paragraph after long ones changes pace and jolts the reader; long paragraphs slow the reading down.
:::

::: watch
- **Whole text**, beginning → development → ending. Three paragraphs in that order is the safest shape.
- Evidence is **where things are placed**, not which words are used. No metaphors, no connotations.
- Every structural point needs a **reader effect**: tension, sympathy, surprise, re-reading the opening differently.
- Use the words **focus, shift, zoom, withhold, return, pace** — accurate structural terminology.
- Look for the **one-line paragraph** and the **ending's link to the opening**; both are nearly always there.
- Ten minutes. Do not let Question 3 eat Question 4's twenty.
:::
