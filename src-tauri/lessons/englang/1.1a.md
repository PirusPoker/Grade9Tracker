## What it is

Paper 1 Question 1 is the easiest four marks on the paper, and every year people drop some of them. You are given a **line range** (for example "Read again the first part of the source, from lines 1 to 5") and asked to **list four things** about something specific — "List four things about the house", "List four things you learn about the narrator's father". The marks are for **explicit information**: things the text *says*, not things you *work out*. No analysis, no quotations required, no explanation of effect. One mark per correct thing, four things, done.

The question tests **AO1**: identify and interpret explicit and implicit information. In practice the examiner is checking three things about each of your four answers:

1. It comes from **inside the line range**. Anything from line 6 in a "lines 1 to 5" question scores nothing, however true.
2. It is **about the thing named in the question**. If the question is about the house, a fact about the weather scores nothing.
3. It is **true to the text** — a quotation, a paraphrase, or a very safe inference. "The house is old" is fine if the text says the paint is peeling and the roof sags; "the house is haunted" is your imagination.

**What counts as a "thing".** You can quote directly ("the door was painted red"), paraphrase ("its door was red"), or give a short accurate inference ("it had not been looked after"). Each of your four lines must contain a *different* piece of information — "the door was red" and "it had a red door" are the same thing and score one mark between them.

**Timing.** Five minutes at most, including reading the lines. The reading section is an hour; Question 1 is worth 4 of the 40 reading marks, so it deserves about a tenth of the time.

::: method
1. Read the question **twice**. Circle the line range and underline the *thing* you are being asked about (the house, the father, the storm).
2. Draw a line across the source **under the last line of the range** so your eye cannot drift below it.
3. Read only that range and put a small tick beside every fact about the named thing.
4. Write **four short, separate points**, one per line, each a different fact. Quote or paraphrase — either scores.
5. Check each answer against the two killers: is it *in the range*, is it *about the thing*? Replace anything that fails.
6. Do not write a fifth. If one of your four is wrong, a fifth would not be marked anyway — the first four are what count.
:::

::: example A straightforward Question 1
Source, lines 1 to 4: *"The cottage stood alone at the end of the track. Its thatch was black with age and sagged over the windows like a heavy brow. Smoke rose from a single crooked chimney, and a bicycle without a front wheel leaned against the wall."*

**List four things about the cottage.** (4)

1. It stood alone / at the end of a track.
2. Its thatch was black with age.
3. The thatch sagged over the windows.
4. It had one crooked chimney (or: smoke was rising from the chimney).

Note what is *not* used: the bicycle. It is beside the cottage, not a fact about the cottage itself. A cautious candidate might still get a mark for "a bicycle leaned against its wall", but why gamble when there are four safe answers?
:::

::: example The line-range trap
Source, lines 1 to 3: *"My grandmother was small and fierce. She wore the same grey coat every day of the year and kept boiled sweets in its pockets for children she approved of."* Line 4 onward: *"She had been a nurse in the war, and she never spoke about it."*

**List four things about the grandmother from lines 1 to 3.** (4)

Safe answers: she was small; she was fierce; she wore the same grey coat every day; she kept boiled sweets in her pockets (or: she gave sweets to children she approved of).

**Zero-mark answer**: "She had been a nurse in the war." It is true and it is about the grandmother — but it is on line 4. The line range is not a suggestion.
:::

::: example Repeating a fact by accident
A candidate writes, about a garden: "1. It was overgrown. 2. The grass was long. 3. Weeds had taken over the beds. 4. Nobody had looked after it."

Points 1, 2 and 3 are the same idea said three ways — the examiner will give one mark for the group, plus one for point 4 if the text supports it. Two marks out of four for a perfectly accurate answer. Each point must be a **separate** fact: "the grass was long" and "there was a broken bench" and "the gate was rusted" and "it was overgrown" would score four.
:::

::: example Inference: how far is too far?
Text: *"He counted the coins twice, then put half of them back in the jar."*

Acceptable: "He counted the coins twice." "He put half the coins back." "He kept coins in a jar." "He was careful with money" — a small, safe inference the text clearly supports.

Not acceptable: "He was poor." "He was saving up for something." These might be true in the wider story, but nothing in the line says so. Question 1 rewards what is on the page. Keep inferences to the size of a single step.
:::

::: q
How many marks is Paper 1 Question 1 worth, and roughly how long should it take?
---
Four marks; about five minutes.
:::

::: q
What does "explicit information" mean in this question?
---
Facts the text directly states — not things you have to interpret or guess.
:::

::: q
Give the two most common reasons a Question 1 answer scores zero.
---
It comes from outside the given line range, or it is not about the thing named in the question.
:::

::: q
Do you need to quote in Question 1?
---
No — quoting, paraphrasing or a safe one-step inference all score; a quotation is simply the safest option.
:::

::: q
Why would "the room was dark" and "there was no light in the room" score only one mark between them?
---
They are the same piece of information expressed twice; each of the four points must be a different fact.
:::

::: q
The question says lines 1 to 6 and the perfect fact is on line 7. What should you do?
---
Leave it out — it cannot score; find a fourth fact inside lines 1 to 6 instead.
:::

::: q
Is "the man was frightened" acceptable if the text says his hands shook and he kept glancing at the door?
---
Yes — it is a small inference the text clearly supports; a larger leap such as "he was being hunted" would not be.
:::

::: q
What practical step stops your eye drifting below the line range?
---
Draw a line across the source under the last line of the range before you start reading.
:::

::: watch
- **Line range first**: circle it, rule it off, and never use anything below it.
- **The named thing only**: a fact about anything else scores nothing.
- **Four different facts**, one per line — repeats are merged into one mark.
- **No analysis, no effect, no long sentences**: this is the one question where writing more cannot help you.
- Five minutes. Bank the four marks and move on — Question 4 needs the time.
:::
