## What it is

Paper 2 Question 4 is worth **16 marks** and asks you to **compare how the two writers convey their different perspectives (attitudes / feelings) on…** the subject the sources share. It tests **AO3**: compare writers' ideas and perspectives, **as well as how these are conveyed**, across two or more texts. The question bullets three things:

- **compare their different perspectives on** (the subject);
- **compare the methods the writers use** to convey those perspectives;
- **support your response with references to both texts**.

So it is three things at once: **what** each writer thinks or feels (perspective), **how** each conveys it (methods — language, tone, structure, form), and the **comparison** that holds the two together. Question 2 was *what* without *how*; Question 3 was *how* in one text; Question 4 is *what* and *how* across both. It is the reading question with the most marks on Paper 2 and it needs **twenty minutes**.

**Perspective.** A writer's perspective is their **attitude to the subject**, and it is usually more precise than "positive" or "negative": *nostalgic*, *appalled*, *amused and slightly superior*, *reverent*, *sceptical*, *defensive*. Top answers name the perspective precisely and notice where it **shifts** within a text (a writer who begins amused and ends unsettled). They also notice **similarity within difference**: two writers who both admire a place but for opposite reasons.

**Methods.** Anything the writer does on purpose: word choice and imagery; **tone** (and tone is a method — the choice to be ironic, or intimate, or formal); **form** (a letter to a friend allows confession; a newspaper article allows public argument; a diary allows the unedited thought); **structure** (where the writer places the strongest point); **anecdote** versus **statistics**; **direct address**; **sentence forms**. Naming the method and explaining **how it conveys the perspective** is the core move.

**Comparison.** The answer must be **integrated**: both texts in each paragraph, joined by comparative connectives, with the comparison doing real work — "A is angry and B is calm" is a start; "A's anger is aimed at the reader, whereas B's calm is a way of trusting the reader to be angry on his own" is a comparison that says something. Level 4 (13–16) needs "perceptive" comparison, "detailed" understanding of perspectives, and "judicious" references — a **sustained** comparison, not two summaries plus a "however".

**Timing and shape.** Twenty minutes. A one-sentence opening stating both perspectives. Three or four comparative paragraphs, each on **one aspect** (their attitude to the people, to the place, to the past; or their opening, their strongest moment, their ending). No conclusion beyond a sentence.

::: method
1. In the margin of each source, write the writer's perspective in **two or three precise words** and note where it **shifts**, if it does.
2. Choose **three points of comparison** — aspects of the subject, or moments in the texts — where the difference (or similarity) in perspective is clearest.
3. Opening sentence: name both perspectives and the main difference between them.
4. Each paragraph: **comparative statement → Source A: perspective + evidence + method + how the method conveys it → connective → Source B: the same → a sentence that says what the comparison reveals**.
5. Include **tone** and **form** as methods at least once each — they are the methods specific to non-fiction and the easiest to compare.
6. Twenty minutes; if short of time, write the third paragraph as a shorter version of the same shape rather than dropping the comparison.
:::

::: example An integrated comparative paragraph
Question: *"Compare how the writers convey their different perspectives on the city."*

"Both writers are outsiders in the city, but their perspectives divide over what the crowd means. For the 19th-century writer the crowd is a threat: he calls it 'the tide' and describes being 'borne along' by it, and the metaphor of water conveys a perspective in which the individual is helpless and the city has no faces. The tone is formal and slightly appalled, which suits a letter written to reassure a country reader that he is enduring rather than enjoying it. The modern writer, by contrast, is exhilarated by the same anonymity: she writes 'nobody knew me, and nobody needed to', and the short balanced clauses convey relief — the city is freedom from being known. Her tone is intimate and amused, and the article form lets her address readers who share the feeling. The comparison shows that the same fact — a crowd of strangers — is a horror in one perspective and a liberation in the other, and each writer's method (metaphor of water; balanced short clauses) makes the reader feel the perspective rather than just be told it."
:::

::: example Naming perspectives precisely
Weak: "Writer A is negative about school and writer B is positive."

Better: "Writer A looks back at school with a mix of resentment and grudging affection — the resentment in the list of punishments, the affection in the joke he allows himself at the end — whereas Writer B, still a pupil, is anxious and eager to please, a perspective conveyed by how often she reports what teachers *say* rather than what she thinks." Precise perspectives, a shift noticed in A, and a method (reported speech) already tied to B's perspective.
:::

::: example Form and tone as methods
"The letter form matters to how A's perspective reaches us: because he is writing to his sister, he can admit fear — 'I will not pretend I was not afraid' — and the confession is a method, creating trust between writer and reader that a public article would not allow. B is writing for a newspaper, and her perspective is conveyed instead through a public, ironic tone: 'the council, in its wisdom, has provided one bench' — the mock-praise invites a whole readership to share her contempt. A conveys feeling through privacy; B through performance." Two methods that only non-fiction offers, compared.
:::

::: example Similarity within difference
"Both writers admire the mountain, yet the admiration differs in kind. A's is religious — the mountain 'stood as it had stood before there were eyes to see it', a sentence whose length and repetition give the reader the mountain's patience; his perspective is one of awe before something older than himself. B's admiration is athletic and possessive: 'I had it; the summit was mine', with the short clauses and 'mine' conveying a perspective in which the mountain is an achievement. The similarity (admiration) makes the difference sharper: A wants to be small before it, B wants to be on top of it." Finding the shared ground and then splitting it is a reliable route to "perceptive".
:::

::: q
How many marks is Paper 2 Question 4 worth and which assessment objective does it test?
---
Sixteen marks; AO3 — comparing writers' ideas and perspectives and how they are conveyed.
:::

::: q
What are the three things the question bullets?
---
Compare their perspectives on the subject; compare the methods used to convey them; support with references to both texts.
:::

::: q
What is a writer's "perspective"?
---
Their attitude to or feelings about the subject — stated precisely (nostalgic, appalled, amused, sceptical), not just positive or negative.
:::

::: q
Name two methods specific to non-fiction that are easy to compare.
---
Tone (e.g. ironic versus intimate) and form (letter, diary, article, speech) — and what each allows the writer to do.
:::

::: q
What is the difference between Question 2 and Question 4?
---
Question 2 compares what the texts say (content and inference); Question 4 compares perspectives and how the writers' methods convey them.
:::

::: q
Why does "A is angry and B is calm" need developing?
---
It is a start, but a perceptive comparison says what the difference means — e.g. where the anger is aimed, or why the calm is a strategy.
:::

::: q
What is "similarity within difference"?
---
Both writers share an attitude (e.g. admiration) but for different reasons or of a different kind — finding the shared ground makes the contrast sharper.
:::

::: q
How long should Question 4 take, and what shape?
---
Twenty minutes; an opening sentence naming both perspectives, then three or four integrated comparative paragraphs.
:::

::: watch
- **Three things every paragraph**: perspective, method, comparison — miss one and the paragraph drops a level.
- Name perspectives **precisely**, and notice **shifts** within a text.
- **Integrated** comparison: both texts in every paragraph, joined by *whereas / similarly / by contrast*.
- **Tone and form are methods** — say what the letter or the article or the irony *lets the writer do*.
- Evidence from **both** texts, short and precise.
- **Twenty minutes**; it is the biggest reading question on the paper.
:::
