## What it is

The narrative option in Paper 1 Section B gives you a short prompt — *"Write a story about a time when things went wrong"*, *"Write the opening of a story set in a deserted place"* — and the same 40 marks (24 content and organisation, 16 technical accuracy) as description. A narrative can score just as highly as a description, but it fails in predictable ways: too much plot, too many characters, too much time, and an ending written in the last ninety seconds. The fix for all of them is one word: **restraint**.

**Shape: small story, big moment.** A 45-minute narrative should cover **one event**, in **one place**, with **one or two characters**, over **a short span of time** — an hour, an afternoon, a single conversation. You are not writing a film; you are writing one scene from it. The best stories often have almost no plot at all: a girl waits for a phone call; a man decides whether to knock on a door; a boy finds something on a beach and has to decide what to do with it. The interest comes from **how it is told**, not what happens.

**A shape that works: the five-part arc in one scene.**

1. **Open in the middle** of something — a detail, an action, a line of dialogue. No "It was a normal Tuesday and my name is Sam."
2. **Establish** the situation and the want: what does this character need in the next hour?
3. **Complication** — one thing gets in the way, or one thing is revealed.
4. **Turning point** — the character does something, or understands something.
5. **End on an image**, not an explanation. No "and I learned that…". No waking up. No "then everything went black".

**Voice.** First person gives immediacy and lets thought and feeling sit inside the sentences; third person gives control and distance. Either is fine. What matters is **consistency** — pick one and stay in it — and a **voice** that sounds like someone: a narrator with attitude, humour, fear, precision. "I noticed that the room was messy" has no voice. "The room looked like it had been burgled by someone who then gave up and had a nap" has a voice.

**Show, don't tell — the mechanics.** Instead of naming an emotion, give the reader the physical evidence and the behaviour: not "she was nervous" but "she had folded the receipt into a square so small it had stopped folding." Dialogue is a form of showing: what people say, and especially what they avoid saying, tells the reader who they are. Keep dialogue **short**, **punctuated correctly** (new speaker, new line; comma or full stop inside the closing speech mark), and **doing work** — every line should reveal character or move the scene.

**Pace.** Slow down for the moment that matters — spend a paragraph on three seconds. Speed up through what does not: "The bus took forty minutes. She did not read." A story that gives equal time to everything has no pace at all.

::: terms
- **Narrative writing**: writing that tells a story.
- **Narrative perspective**: who tells the story — first person ("I") or third person ("she").
- **In medias res**: starting in the middle of the action.
- **Dialogue**: characters' speech, set out in speech marks with a new line for each speaker.
- **Climax**: the point of highest tension in a story.
- **Resolution**: how the story's problem is settled at the end.
- **Flashback**: a scene set earlier than the main action.
:::

::: method
1. **Plan for five minutes**: one character, one place, one hour, one change. Write the last image first, then work out the opening that will make it land.
2. **Open with a detail or a line**, in the middle of the situation. Ground the reader with place and person inside the first three sentences — without a paragraph of set-up.
3. Write in **scenes**, not summary. If you catch yourself writing "then… then… then", stop and dramatise one moment properly instead.
4. **Show emotion physically** and through dialogue; name a feeling at most once in the whole piece.
5. Control pace: a long, detailed paragraph for the turning point; short sentences or a one-line paragraph for the shock.
6. **End on an image** that echoes or reverses the opening. Write the ending *before* you are out of time — at 35 minutes, whatever you have, start the last paragraph.
7. **Five minutes checking**: speech punctuation, tense consistency, paragraphing for each new speaker, apostrophes.
:::

::: example Choosing a small story
Prompt: *"Write a story about a time when things went wrong."*

Overscoped: a school trip abroad, a lost passport, a missed flight, a stranger, a chase, a reunion at the airport — six locations, two days, eight characters; the ending arrives in a rush.

Right-sized: a boy is left in charge of his grandmother's shop for one hour. He sells the wrong thing to the wrong person. One place, one hour, two characters. The whole story is the hour, the decision, and what he says when she comes back. The "things going wrong" is small enough to be told well, and the story is really about the boy and his grandmother — which is where the marks are.
:::

::: example Opening in the middle
Weak: "It was a cold winter morning. My name is Ellie and I am fifteen. I live with my mum in a small flat. One day something happened that I will never forget."

Strong: "The key was still in the door when I got home, which meant one of two things, and I stood on the step deciding which one I could afford." One sentence: a place, a narrator with a mind, a problem, a tone. Three of the four weak sentences are set-up the reader does not need; the fourth promises a story instead of starting one.
:::

::: example Showing through dialogue
Telling: "Dad was angry about the car but he didn't want to show it."

Showing: "'It's fine,' Dad said. He was looking at the dent the way he looked at my reports. 'It's — no, it's fine.' He went inside and I heard him put the kettle on, then not make tea." The reader gets the anger, the restraint and the relationship from what is said, how it stalls, and one wordless action. Note the punctuation: comma inside the speech mark before "Dad said"; new paragraph for the change of action.
:::

::: example Ending on an image
Weak: "In the end I learned that honesty is always the best policy and I never made that mistake again."

Strong: "She put the coins back in the till, one at a time, without counting them. Then she turned the sign to OPEN, and the bell above the door rang for nobody." The story has already made its point; the ending trusts the reader. The bell, if it rang in the opening, now rings differently — that is a cyclical ending doing its work.
:::

::: q
What single word sums up what makes narratives fail or succeed under exam conditions?
---
Restraint — one event, one place, one or two characters, a short span of time.
:::

::: q
Why should you write the ending before you are out of time?
---
Rushed endings ("then I woke up", "everything went black") lose content marks; starting the final paragraph at about 35 minutes guarantees a controlled close.
:::

::: q
Give one example of showing an emotion instead of naming it.
---
For example: instead of "she was nervous", "she had folded the receipt into a square so small it had stopped folding."
:::

::: q
What are two rules for punctuating dialogue?
---
New speaker, new line; the comma or full stop goes inside the closing speech mark.
:::

::: q
What does "open in the middle" mean?
---
Start with a detail, action or line inside the situation rather than paragraphs of set-up about who and where you are.
:::

::: q
How do you control pace in a story?
---
Slow down with detail for the moment that matters; speed through unimportant time in a sentence; use short sentences or one-line paragraphs for shocks.
:::

::: q
What kind of ending should you avoid, and what should you aim for?
---
Avoid explained morals, dreams, or "everything went black"; aim for a final image that echoes or reverses the opening.
:::

::: q
Why is a story with almost no plot often better than one with a lot?
---
The marks are for how it is told — voice, structure, language — and a small story leaves room to do those things well.
:::

::: watch
- **One scene, one hour, two people.** Anything bigger will be rushed.
- **No set-up paragraph**; open inside the situation with a detail or a line.
- **Name an emotion at most once**; show the rest through action and dialogue.
- **Dialogue punctuation** is where accuracy marks vanish — new line per speaker, punctuation inside the speech marks.
- **Start the ending at 35 minutes**, and end on an image.
- **Consistent tense and person** throughout — check for it in the last five minutes.
:::

::: test
Write a story about a journey that does not go to plan. (24 marks for content and organisation, 16 for technical accuracy.) (40)
---
Mark against these levels. Reward a narrative with a limited cast and timescale, an engaging opening, a clear turning point, scenes rather than summary, and a controlled ending.

Content and organisation (24 marks):
- Level 4 (19–24): compelling and convincing; tone, style and register assured and matched to audience and purpose; extensive, ambitious vocabulary; varied and inventive structure; fluent, linked paragraphs.
- Level 3 (13–18): consistent and clear; tone and register matched; vocabulary chosen for effect; effective structural features; coherent paragraphs.
- Level 2 (7–12): some success; some awareness of audience and purpose; some vocabulary chosen for effect; some structural features; some paragraphs.
- Level 1 (1–6): simple awareness of the task; simple vocabulary; random or limited structure.

Technical accuracy (16 marks):
- Level 4 (13–16): sentences always correctly marked; a wide range of punctuation used accurately; a full range of sentence forms for effect; Standard English secure; spelling accurate, including ambitious words.
- Level 3 (9–12): sentences mostly secure; a range of punctuation, mostly accurate; varied sentence forms; mostly accurate spelling of complex words.
- Level 2 (5–8): sentences sometimes secure; some control of punctuation; some variety of forms; some accurate spelling of more complex words.
- Level 1 (1–4): occasional sentence control; some punctuation; simple sentence forms; accurate basic spelling.
:::
