## What it is

Paper 2 Question 3 is worth **12 marks** and asks **"How does the writer use language to…"** — to describe, to convey a feeling, to present a place or a person — within a line range of **one** source (usually the older, 19th-century one, though not always). It tests **AO2**, exactly as Paper 1 Question 2 does, but with twelve marks instead of eight, and with **non-fiction**: a letter, a diary, an article, a travel piece, a speech. The skill is the same — quote, zoom in on a word, explain its connotations and effect — but the *kind* of language you will find is different, and top answers notice that.

**What non-fiction language does.** Fiction describes to make you *see*; non-fiction usually describes to make you *think or feel something about the subject* — it has a **purpose** and an **attitude**. So the best Question 3 answers connect the language to the writer's **viewpoint**: not just "this metaphor makes the slum sound dark" but "this metaphor makes the slum sound like a pit, which fits the writer's purpose of shocking his comfortable readers into action." Features that appear constantly in non-fiction:

- **Emotive language** — words chosen to provoke pity, anger, disgust, admiration.
- **Hyperbole and understatement** — exaggeration for effect, or deliberate calm about something terrible.
- **Contrast and juxtaposition** — the rich street beside the poor one.
- **Lists and accumulation** — piling up details to overwhelm or to catalogue.
- **Direct address and rhetorical questions** — pulling the reader in (especially in speeches and articles).
- **Anecdote and specific detail** — one named child instead of "the poor".
- **Irony and sarcasm** — saying the opposite; common in 19th-century writing and easy to miss.
- **Tone** — the writer's attitude heard in the words: outraged, amused, weary, affectionate.
- **Sentence forms** — long periodic sentences building to a point; a short sentence delivering a verdict.

**Twelve marks means depth.** Level 4 (10–12) wants "perceptive and detailed" analysis of "judicious" examples with "sophisticated" terminology. In practice: **three or four** quotations, each analysed at word level, each linked to the writer's purpose or tone, and the analysis linked together into an account of *how the language works as a whole* in the passage. A twelve-mark answer is about fifteen minutes and four paragraphs.

**19th-century texts.** Vocabulary may be unfamiliar; sentences are longer; irony is common. Do not be put off. The examiner rewards what you can do with what you *do* understand. Pick the quotations you are sure of, and comment on **sentence length** where the old style gives you long, clause-heavy sentences — that is a legitimate and easy point about form.

::: terms
- **Non-fiction**: writing about real events, people and ideas — letters, articles, speeches, diaries.
- **Emotive language**: words chosen to provoke strong feelings in the reader.
- **Rhetorical question**: a question asked for effect, not needing an answer.
- **Irony**: saying the opposite of what is meant, often to mock.
- **Hyperbole**: deliberate exaggeration for effect.
- **Tone**: the writer's attitude, conveyed through language — angry, sarcastic, admiring.
- **Direct address**: speaking to the reader as "you".
:::

::: method
1. Read the question's focus (e.g. "how the writer uses language to convey his disgust at the conditions") and the line range. Underline **four** language choices that clearly serve that focus.
2. Before writing, decide the writer's **tone and purpose** in one phrase — "outraged, wants reform". Every paragraph will connect back to it.
3. Paragraph shape: **quote → technique (if any) → word-level connotation → effect on reader → link to tone/purpose**.
4. Make at least one point about **sentence form** (a long accumulating sentence; a short verdict sentence; a rhetorical question).
5. Link paragraphs: "This is intensified when…", "Having shocked the reader, the writer then…" — show the language *developing* across the passage.
6. Fifteen minutes. Four paragraphs. Stop.
:::

::: example Emotive language and purpose
Line range from a 19th-century article: *"In one room, eleven feet by nine, I found a family of seven and a lodger, the youngest child asleep in a drawer, the fire out, the window stuffed with rags."*

"The writer's language is built on **precision** rather than adjectives: the room is 'eleven feet by nine', the family is 'seven and a lodger' — the numbers do the emotional work, because the reader is made to calculate the space per person. The image of the child 'asleep in a drawer' is the most emotive detail: a drawer is for objects, so the child is quietly presented as something stored rather than cared for, without the writer ever saying 'neglect'. The list that ends the sentence — 'the fire out, the window stuffed with rags' — accumulates deprivation in short flat phrases, and the absence of any comment is itself the tone: a controlled, documentary anger designed to make comfortable readers ashamed. The language serves the writer's purpose of reform by making the facts unbearable rather than by shouting."
:::

::: example Irony in older texts
*"The proprietors, with a generosity that does them the greatest credit, allow the children to rest for a full half-hour in the middle of a fourteen-hour day."*

"The writer uses **irony**: 'generosity' and 'the greatest credit' are the words of praise, but they are attached to a 'half-hour' inside 'a fourteen-hour day', so the praise collapses under the numbers. The reader is invited to hear the mock-respectful tone and to feel contempt for the owners that the writer never states directly. The word 'allow' is the sharpest: it presents rest as a favour granted, exposing the owners' view of the children as property. Irony lets the writer attack while sounding polite — a technique that suits a readership that would resist open anger."
:::

::: example Sentence forms in non-fiction
*"I have seen the docks at dawn, the men waiting at the gates, the foreman walking the line, the chosen dozen going in and the hundred going home; and I have seen it the next day, and the next, and I am told it will be the same tomorrow."*

"The writer builds one long sentence from a list of scenes separated by commas, so the reader is made to wait through the whole line of men as the foreman does; the semicolon then pivots from one morning to 'the next, and the next', and the repetition enacts the monotony of the men's hopelessness. The sentence's length is the point: it refuses to stop, as the situation refuses to end. The final clause, 'I am told it will be the same tomorrow', is quieter and flatter — resignation in the grammar."
:::

::: example What loses marks at twelve
A candidate lists seven quotations from a travel piece with a technique name and a one-line effect each: "'endless' is hyperbole — it shows the desert is big." That is a Level 2 answer regardless of how many quotations it contains. The same candidate rewriting one point: "'endless' is a slight exaggeration — the desert does end — but the writer chooses it because her subject is not the desert's size but her own fear of it; 'endless' is what it *felt* like, and the hyperbole lets the reader inside that feeling. This is the first of several words in the passage that describe the desert through her emotions rather than its geography." Depth, plus a link forward. Three of those beat seven labels.
:::

::: q
How many marks is Paper 2 Question 3 worth and how does the task differ from Paper 1 Question 2?
---
Twelve marks; same AO2 language-analysis skill, but on a non-fiction text and needing more depth and a link to the writer's purpose or tone.
:::

::: q
Name four language features common in non-fiction.
---
Any four of: emotive language, hyperbole/understatement, contrast, lists/accumulation, direct address, rhetorical questions, anecdote/specific detail, irony, tone.
:::

::: q
What should every paragraph link back to in this question?
---
The writer's tone and purpose — what the language is making the reader feel or think about the subject, and why.
:::

::: q
What is irony, and why is it easy to miss in 19th-century texts?
---
Saying the opposite of what is meant, usually in a polite or approving tone; older writers use it heavily and the mock-praise can be mistaken for real praise.
:::

::: q
Give one easy, legitimate point to make about form in an older text.
---
Long, clause-heavy sentences that accumulate detail or delay the point — and the effect of a short sentence when one finally appears.
:::

::: q
How many quotations should a twelve-mark answer analyse, and in how long?
---
Three or four, analysed at word level; about fifteen minutes.
:::

::: q
Why is "'endless' is hyperbole — it shows the desert is big" a weak point?
---
It labels a technique with a surface effect and never explains the word's connotations or the writer's purpose.
:::

::: q
How do you show language "developing" across the passage?
---
Link paragraphs with phrases like "this is intensified when…" or "having shocked the reader, the writer then…".
:::

::: watch
- Same skill as Paper 1 Q2, **more depth**: word-level connotation on every quotation, plus a link to **purpose or tone**.
- Look for **irony** in older texts; mock-praise attached to bad numbers is the usual sign.
- One point on **sentence form**, always.
- **Three or four** quotations developed and linked; not seven labelled.
- Stay in the **line range** and on the **focus** of the question.
- Fifteen minutes; Question 4 needs the rest.
:::
