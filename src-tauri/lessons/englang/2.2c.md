## What it is

The 24 content-and-organisation marks on Paper 2 Section B are split in the mark scheme into **content** (register, vocabulary, ideas matched to purpose) and **organisation** (structure, paragraphs, cohesion — the way ideas are linked and the whole piece hangs together). Level 4 organisation wants "varied and inventive use of structural features", "fluently linked paragraphs with seamlessly integrated discourse markers", and "writing is engaging with a range of complex ideas". In plain terms: a **shape the reader can feel**, **paragraphs that lead into each other**, and an argument that **builds**. This lesson is about the skeleton of a viewpoint piece, and about protecting the 16 accuracy marks when you are writing fast.

**A structure that builds.** The default and best shape for a 45-minute argument:

1. **Hook and position** (one paragraph) — an anecdote, a striking fact, a question; end with your view.
2. **Reason 1** — the most obvious; get it out of the way well.
3. **Reason 2** — stronger, more surprising.
4. **Counter-argument and rebuttal** — the other side, fairly, then turned.
5. **Reason 3** — the strongest, often the one that answers the counter-argument's real concern.
6. **Close** — short; return to the hook; a quotable last line.

Six paragraphs, roughly 500–700 words. Paragraphs 2–5 each have the same internal shape: **topic sentence** (the point, plainly) → **development** (why it is true) → **evidence or example** (anecdote, plausible statistic, expert) → **link** (back to the position, or forward to the next point). The link sentence is what makes paragraphs "fluently linked" rather than a stack.

**Discourse markers — the invisible kind.** *Firstly, secondly, in addition, furthermore, in conclusion* are signposts a reader can see, and too many of them make the writing sound like a list. "Seamlessly integrated" means the links are built into the sentences: "That is the obvious argument. The less obvious one is about money." / "If cost were the only issue, the case would be closed. It is not." / "Which brings us to the objection everyone raises." Vary: a question that the next paragraph answers; a one-line paragraph as a pivot; a repeated phrase that returns changed.

**Structural features that count as "inventive"** (use one or two): a **one-sentence paragraph** for the verdict; **anaphora** across a paragraph; a **cyclical** return to the opening anecdote; a **flash-forward** ("Imagine this school in five years…"); a **list of three paragraphs** each starting the same way; an ending that **answers the opening question** in different words.

**Accuracy under time.** The 16 AO6 marks are lost to the same errors under pressure as in Paper 1 (see lesson 1.2c): comma splices, apostrophes, homophones, tense drift, capitals — plus, in argument writing, two extra ones: **rhetorical questions without question marks**, and **sentence fragments that were meant to be sentences** ("Which is why we need change" — fine as a deliberate fragment, once; a page of them reads as an inability to write a sentence). Ambitious vocabulary is rewarded *when spelt correctly*: if you are unsure of a word, use the one you are sure of.

::: method
1. **Plan the skeleton** in five minutes: hook; position; three reasons ordered weakest→strongest; the objection; the closing line. Write it as six short lines down the margin.
2. Write **topic sentences first** if you tend to ramble: one plain sentence per paragraph that states the point. Then expand each.
3. **Link every paragraph** to the previous one in its first sentence or the previous one's last — by contrast, by consequence, by answering a question.
4. Place **one structural device** deliberately: a one-line paragraph as the pivot to the counter-argument, or an anaphoric triple in the penultimate paragraph.
5. **Watch the clock**: paragraphs 1–3 by 20 minutes, counter-argument by 28, reason 3 by 35, close by 38. Whatever happens, **write the close** — a piece with an ending beats a longer piece without one.
6. **Proofread for seven minutes** hunting only errors: full stops, apostrophes, homophones, capitals, question marks, tense, fragments.
:::

::: example Topic sentences as a skeleton
Statement: *"Social media does more harm than good."* Position: it does more harm than good *for the way we currently use it* — the fix is use, not abolition.

Topic sentences, in order: (1) "I deleted one app for a month, and the strangest thing was how little I missed it." (2) "The first harm is the obvious one: time." (3) "The second is less visible and worse: comparison." (4) "The defence of social media is that it connects people, and it does." (5) "But the connection is not the problem, and neither is the technology; the problem is the design that keeps us scrolling past the connection." (6) "I reinstalled the app. I also turned off every notification. Both were the right decision." Each sentence is a point; the piece already has a shape before any paragraph is written.
:::

::: example Seamless links
Stacked: "Firstly, homework takes up time. Secondly, homework causes stress. Thirdly, homework is not always useful."

Linked: "Homework takes time — about ten hours a week for a Year 11, if you believe the surveys, and more if you believe Year 11. Time is the cheap objection, though; the expensive one is what the time is spent *on*. Because most homework is not practice but repetition, and repetition of something you can already do is not learning. It is filing. Which is where the stress comes from: not from the work, but from the pointlessness of it." No numbered signposts; each sentence hands to the next; a one-line pivot ("It is filing."); the third reason grows out of the second.
:::

::: example The one-line pivot and the cyclical close
Pivot into the counter-argument: after three paragraphs of reasons, a single line: "None of which is what the people who set homework will say." Then the counter-argument paragraph. The one-liner changes pace, signals a turn, and reads as control.

Cyclical close: the piece opened with "My little brother is seven and has a homework diary." It ends: "My brother's homework diary has a space for a parent's signature on every page. I would like there to be a space for a reason." The opening image returns with the argument inside it.
:::

::: example Proofreading for argument-specific errors
Draft: "Why should we accept this. Because it has always been done this way, because nobody has asked, because change is hard, none of these are reasons."

Fixes: the rhetorical question needs a question mark ("Why should we accept this?"). The second sentence is a comma splice: "Because it has always been done this way, because nobody has asked, because change is hard — none of these are reasons." The dash turns the anaphoric list into one controlled sentence. Two corrections, both in the category the examiner is specifically looking for.
:::

::: q
What is the six-paragraph shape for a viewpoint piece?
---
Hook and position; reason 1; reason 2; counter-argument and rebuttal; reason 3 (strongest); short close returning to the hook.
:::

::: q
What are the four parts of an argument paragraph?
---
Topic sentence; development; evidence or example; link back to the position or forward to the next point.
:::

::: q
What does "seamlessly integrated discourse markers" mean in practice?
---
Links built into the sentences — contrast, consequence, a question answered — rather than visible signposts like "firstly, secondly".
:::

::: q
Name three structural devices that count as inventive.
---
Any three of: one-sentence paragraph as a pivot, anaphora, cyclical return to the opening, flash-forward, an ending that answers the opening question.
:::

::: q
By what time should the close be written, and why?
---
By about 38 minutes — a piece with an ending beats a longer piece without one, and seven minutes are needed for proofreading.
:::

::: q
What two accuracy errors are specific to argument writing?
---
Rhetorical questions without question marks, and unintended sentence fragments used repeatedly.
:::

::: q
Why write topic sentences before the paragraphs?
---
They give the piece a shape and a clear point per paragraph before any expansion, preventing rambling.
:::

::: q
Should you use an ambitious word you cannot spell?
---
No — vocabulary is rewarded only when accurate; use the word you are sure of.
:::

::: watch
- **Six paragraphs, one job each**; strongest reason last, close short.
- **Links inside the sentences**; "firstly/secondly/finally" reads as a list.
- **One deliberate structural device** — a one-line pivot or an anaphoric triple — placed, not scattered.
- **Clock checkpoints**: 20 / 28 / 35 / 38 minutes. Always write the close.
- **Question marks on rhetorical questions**; fragments once for effect, not as a habit.
- **Seven minutes' proofreading** for the sixteen accuracy marks.
:::
