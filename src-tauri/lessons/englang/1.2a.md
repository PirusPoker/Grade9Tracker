## What it is

Paper 1 Section B is one piece of writing worth **40 marks** — as much as the whole reading section. You choose **one** of two tasks: usually a **description suggested by a picture** and a **narrative** from a short prompt (sometimes two descriptive tasks). The marks split into **24 for content and organisation** (AO5: ideas, tone, style, register, vocabulary, structure, paragraphing, linking) and **16 for technical accuracy** (AO6: spelling, punctuation, sentence variety, grammar, Standard English). You have about **45 minutes** including planning and checking.

**Why description is usually the better choice.** A description has no plot to go wrong. You cannot run out of story, rush the ending, or leave a character in a burning building because time was up. Description lets you control pace, structure and vocabulary — which is exactly what the 24 content marks reward — without the narrative risks. Most top-band answers are descriptive or narrative-flavoured descriptions.

**What a top-band description is.** Not a list of what is in the picture. The picture is a *prompt*: the examiner has seen it; do not report it. Instead, choose a **viewpoint** (a person, a moment, a time of day), a **mood**, and a **structure** — a way of moving through the scene that gives the piece a shape. The best pieces feel like a camera with intentions: a slow pan, a zoom in to one detail, a cut to what is beneath or behind the obvious, a return at the end to the opening image, changed.

**Structures that work:**

- **Zoom** — wide (the whole beach) → medium (one family) → close (a child's hand holding a shell) → back out, now with meaning.
- **Time shift** — the same place at dawn, midday, dusk; or now / an hour ago / now.
- **Movement through** — walking down a street, each paragraph a new stretch.
- **Contrast** — the surface / what is underneath; the crowd / one still figure.
- **Cyclical** — the opening image returns in the last paragraph, altered.

**The language the marks are for.** Precise, chosen vocabulary (not long words — *right* words); imagery that is fresh rather than borrowed ("the sea was a mirror" is dead; "the sea had the flat shine of a phone screen in the dark" is alive); **sensory detail beyond sight** — sound, smell, texture, temperature; and **varied sentences**: a long sentence that accumulates, then a short one that lands. Personification and metaphor used sparingly and with intent. Avoid the thesaurus — "the azure firmament" has failed more candidates than "the blue sky" ever will.

::: method
1. **Five minutes planning.** Decide: viewpoint, mood, structure (pick one from the list above). Write five or six paragraph headings — each one a *focus*, not a sentence.
2. **Open with a detail, not a summary.** Not "The beach was busy and hot" but one precise thing — a sound, an object, a movement — that sets the mood.
3. Each paragraph: **one focus**, three to five sentences, at least one non-visual sense, at least one image you invented rather than remembered.
4. **Vary sentence length on purpose.** After a long accumulating sentence, use a short one. One-sentence paragraphs are allowed — once or twice.
5. **Change something** in the middle: the light, the weather, a person entering, a sound stopping. Description with no change reads as a list.
6. **End by returning** to the opening image or detail, changed by what the piece has shown.
7. **Five minutes checking**: full stops, capital letters, homophones, tense consistency, apostrophes. Sixteen marks depend on it.
:::

::: example Planning from a picture
Picture: an empty railway platform at night, one bench, one light.

Plan — viewpoint: the last passenger, waiting. Mood: suspended, expectant. Structure: zoom in then out, cyclical. Paragraphs: (1) the light — its buzz, the moths, the colour it makes of everything; (2) the bench — cold through the coat, initials scratched in it; (3) the sounds of the town beyond the fence, life going on without her; (4) the change: the rails begin to sing before the train can be seen; (5) the train arrives and, in the last line, the light is still buzzing over an empty bench. Notice: nothing in this plan describes the whole picture at once; everything is a chosen focus.
:::

::: example Opening lines
Weak: "It was a dark and quiet night at the train station. There was nobody around except one woman on a bench." — reports the picture; two clichés; no mood beyond "quiet".

Strong: "The platform light had one moth left, and it was losing." — one concrete detail, an unexpected verb, a mood (worn-down, late, a little comic) established in eleven words. The reader wants the next sentence. Openings need a *detail with attitude*, not a scene summary.
:::

::: example Sensory detail beyond sight
Most candidates describe only what can be seen. A paragraph on a market that adds the other senses: "The air was thick enough to chew — frying onions, wet cardboard, a sweetness from the doughnut van that caught in the throat. Under the shouting there was a steadier noise, the shuffle of a thousand shoes on concrete, and under that, felt more than heard, a generator's drone in the soles of your feet." Smell, taste, hearing, touch — and the sentence shape itself moves downward through the layers, which is structure inside a paragraph.
:::

::: example The change and the return
A description of a garden at midday runs the risk of being static. The change: "Then the sun went behind the one cloud in the sky, and the garden lost its colour like a photograph left in a window." Everything can now be re-described in the new light, and the ending can return: "When the cloud moved on, the colours came back — but she had seen the garden grey, and it did not quite recover." A change gives the piece a middle; the return gives it an end.
:::

::: q
How many marks is Paper 1 Section B worth, and how are they split?
---
Forty marks: 24 for content and organisation (AO5), 16 for technical accuracy (AO6).
:::

::: q
Why is description often the safer choice than narrative?
---
It has no plot to go wrong — no rushed ending or unfinished story — and lets you control structure, pace and vocabulary directly.
:::

::: q
What is wrong with describing everything you can see in the picture?
---
The examiner has seen it; reporting it is a list. The picture is a prompt for a viewpoint, mood and structure.
:::

::: q
Name three structures a description can use.
---
Any three of: zoom in/out, time shift, movement through a place, contrast, cyclical return to the opening.
:::

::: q
Why should a description contain a "change"?
---
Without something changing — light, weather, a sound, a person — the piece is static and reads like a list; the change gives it a middle.
:::

::: q
Give an example of a sense other than sight that you could include.
---
Smell (frying onions), sound (a generator's drone), touch/temperature (cold through a coat), taste (sweetness catching in the throat).
:::

::: q
What is the problem with "the azure firmament"?
---
Thesaurus vocabulary — long words used for their own sake read as inaccurate and forced; precise ordinary words score higher.
:::

::: q
How much of the 45 minutes should go to planning and checking?
---
About five minutes each — checking protects the sixteen accuracy marks.
:::

::: watch
- **Plan the structure** before writing; a shapeless description caps at the middle band however good the sentences are.
- **Open with a detail**, not a summary of the scene.
- **Senses beyond sight** in most paragraphs; invented images, not borrowed ones.
- **Vary sentence length deliberately** — the long-then-short pattern is the most reliable tool you have.
- Include a **change** and an **ending that returns**.
- Leave **five minutes to check**: sixteen marks ride on full stops, apostrophes, homophones and tense.
:::
