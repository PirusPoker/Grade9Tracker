## What it is

Paper 1 Question 2 is worth **8 marks** and asks **"How does the writer use language to describe…?"** — a character, a setting, a moment — within a given line range. It tests **AO2**: explain, comment on and analyse how writers use language to achieve effects and influence readers, using relevant subject terminology. The question always lists three things you could write about:

- **words and phrases** — the writer's choices of individual words (diction), and why *that* word rather than another;
- **language features and techniques** — simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration, onomatopoeia, repetition, contrast, sensory detail, and so on;
- **sentence forms** — short sentences, long listing sentences, questions, fragments, sentences that build to a final word.

You do not need all three. The top band (7–8 marks) wants **perceptive, detailed** analysis of **well-chosen** examples with **accurate terminology**. Two or three quotations analysed properly beat six quotations each given a label.

**What "analysis" means here.** Naming a technique is not analysis. "The writer uses a simile" earns nothing on its own. Analysis is explaining **what the language makes the reader think, feel or picture, and how the specific words do it**. The unit of analysis is the **word**: pick out a single word from your quotation and say what its connotations are, what it suggests, why it is stronger than the obvious alternative. If your quotation is "the wind clawed at the windows", the analysis lives in *clawed* — an animal verb that makes the wind a predator trying to get in, and makes the house prey.

**The levels, roughly.** Level 1 (1–2): simple comment, maybe one quotation, terminology missing or wrong. Level 2 (3–4): attempts to comment on effect, some relevant quotation, some terminology. Level 3 (5–6): clear explanation of effects, relevant quotations, correct terminology. Level 4 (7–8): perceptive — sees layers, links words to a wider effect, judicious choice of detail, sophisticated terminology used precisely.

**Timing.** About ten minutes. Three developed paragraphs of analysis is plenty.

::: terms
- **Language analysis**: explaining how a writer's choice of words and phrases creates an effect on the reader.
- **Connotation**: the ideas or feelings a word suggests beyond its literal meaning.
- **Word class**: the grammatical type of a word — noun, verb, adjective, adverb.
- **Metaphor**: describing something as if it were something else.
- **Simile**: comparing one thing to another using "like" or "as".
- **Personification**: giving human qualities to something that is not human.
- **Sentence forms**: the types and lengths of sentences — simple, compound, complex, minor, fragments — chosen for effect.
- **Semantic field**: a group of words related in meaning, such as war or decay.
:::

::: method
1. Read the line range with the **focus** of the question in your head ("how the writer describes the storm"). Underline the three or four most striking language choices *about that focus*.
2. For each, write one paragraph: **quote** it (short — a phrase, not a sentence), **name** the technique if there is one, then **zoom in on a single word** and explain its connotations and effect on the reader.
3. Always answer "so what?" — what does the reader picture, feel, expect, or understand about the focus because of this choice?
4. Where you can, **link** your examples: "This builds on the earlier image of…" or "By contrast, the short sentence that follows…" — this is what makes analysis "perceptive".
5. Include at least one comment on **sentence form** if the extract offers one — a short sentence after long ones, a list, a question.
6. Use terminology **accurately** or not at all; a wrong label is worse than no label.
:::

::: example Zooming into a word
Line range: *"The corridor swallowed him. His footsteps came back to him from the far end, thin and late, as though someone smaller were following."*

**How does the writer use language to describe the corridor?**

The writer personifies the corridor as something that "swallowed" him. The verb suggests a mouth and a throat — the corridor is not a space he walks into but a creature that consumes him, so the reader immediately feels he has lost control and may not come out. The echo of his footsteps is described as "thin and late": "thin" makes the sound weak and starved, which extends the idea of the building draining him, while "late" is odd for a sound and gives the echo a will of its own. The simile "as though someone smaller were following" turns that echo into a second presence, and the word "smaller" is unsettling precisely because it is not threatening — a child-sized follower in an empty corridor is stranger than a monster would be. Together these choices make the corridor a living, watching place rather than a setting.
:::

::: example Sentence forms
Line range: *"She checked the lock. She checked the window. She checked the lock again. Outside, the long, patient, unhurried night went on doing nothing at all."*

The writer uses three short, near-identical sentences — "She checked the lock. She checked the window. She checked the lock again." — and the repetition of "checked" enacts the character's compulsion: the reader performs her looping anxiety by reading the same structure three times. The final sentence is deliberately different: long, slow, and padded with a list of three adjectives, "long, patient, unhurried", so that the night's calm is set against her panic. "Patient" is the key word — it personifies the night as something waiting for her, which means her checking is both pointless and justified at once. The sentence forms do the work: short and frantic inside, long and calm outside.
:::

::: example Choosing well rather than choosing many
A candidate lists six quotations from a description of a market, giving each a label: "'roared' is a verb", "'like a hive' is a simile", "'sweet, sour, burnt' is a list". No mark above Level 2, because nothing is *explained*.

A rewrite of just one: "The market 'roared' — a sound word normally used for animals or engines, so the crowd becomes a single huge creature rather than many people, and the narrator, arriving alone, is made small by it. This is reinforced by the simile 'like a hive': not only busy but organised in a way she cannot read, and with the faint threat of a sting." Two quotations, properly unpacked, take an answer into Level 3 or 4.
:::

::: example Linking examples to an overall effect
Line range: *"The old dog lifted its head, considered the ball, and lowered its head again. Its tail moved once, the ghost of a wag."*

Top-band answers connect their points. "The verb 'considered' gives the dog a slow, deliberate dignity, as though the ball were a proposal it politely declines; the reader smiles but also senses tiredness. That tiredness is then confirmed by the metaphor 'the ghost of a wag' — 'ghost' suggests something that was once alive and full, now faded, so the single tail-movement becomes a memory of the dog's younger self. The writer moves the reader from gentle amusement to something sadder within two sentences, and the shift is carried by those two words, 'considered' and 'ghost'."
:::

How many marks is Paper 1 Question 2 worth and which assessment objective does it test?

Eight marks; AO2 — analysing how writers use language to achieve effects.

What three areas does the question bullet-point for you?
---
Words and phrases; language features and techniques; sentence forms.

Why does "the writer uses a simile" score nothing on its own?
---
Naming a technique is not analysis; marks come from explaining the effect the language has on the reader.

::: q
What is meant by "zooming in" on a quotation?
---
Picking out a single word from the quotation and explaining its connotations and why that word creates the effect.

Is it better to analyse two quotations well or six briefly? Why?
---
Two well — the top band rewards perceptive, detailed analysis of well-chosen examples, not quantity.

---
Perceptive — seeing layers of meaning and linking choices to a wider effect.
:::

About ten minutes.

::: watch
- **Stay inside the line range** and **on the focus** of the question — language about something else earns nothing.
- **Word-level analysis**: every paragraph should name one word and explain its connotations.
- Technique labels only score when **attached to an effect**; wrong labels cost credibility.
- **Two or three quotations, developed**, beat a list. Link them to each other where you can.
- Mention **sentence form** at least once if the extract gives you something to say.
- Ten minutes; then move on — Questions 3 and 4 carry more marks.
:::

::: test
Write one sentence of word-level analysis for: 'The city gnawed at the edges of the countryside.' (2)
---
'Gnawed' makes the city an animal chewing steadily at the land (1); it suggests a slow, relentless, almost hungry destruction (1).
:::

::: test

---
It is a label, not analysis (1); marks come from explaining what the personification makes the reader feel or picture, and which word does it (1).

::: test

A short sentence after long ones creates shock or emphasis (1); a long list-like sentence builds pace or overwhelms; a question involves the reader (1).

Analyse the choice of 'skittered' in 'Leaves skittered across the empty playground.' (2)
---
A light, quick, nervous movement — like small animals (1); it makes the emptiness feel restless and slightly unsettling (1).

Words and phrases; language features and techniques; sentence forms (1); no — two or three well-analysed examples beat covering all three thinly (1).

---
Level 2 at best (1); nothing is explained — quantity of labels does not replace analysis of effect (1).

::: test
Explain how to 'link' two examples in a Question 2 answer. (2)
---
Show one building on or contrasting with another — 'this is intensified when…' (1); linking shows the language working as a whole, which is 'perceptive' (1).

::: test
Analyse the sentence form: 'He waited. And waited. Nothing.' (2)
---
Three fragments, getting shorter (1); the shrinking sentences enact time dragging and end on the emptiness he finds (1).
:::

::: test
In paragraph [1], Mira holds her bag 'against her chest like a shield'. Analyse the effect of this simile. (2)
---
A shield is used in battle, so the simile suggests she feels under attack from the weather and the dark (1); it also shows how vulnerable she is — a school bag is a poor defence, which builds sympathy (1).
:::

::: test
Read paragraph [2] of this source.

[2] The road was empty in both directions. Streetlights hummed and flickered, and in their orange glare the puddles shone like spilled oil. Somewhere across the fields a dog barked twice and then thought better of it. The wind pushed at the shelter, testing it, and the metal frame answered with a low, complaining groan.

How does the writer use language to describe the setting? You could include the writer's choice of words and phrases, language features and techniques, and sentence forms. (8)
---
Indicative content:

- the short opening sentence 'The road was empty in both directions' establishes isolation
- 'hummed and flickered' — the sound and unreliable light make the setting uneasy
- the simile 'puddles shone like spilled oil' makes the scene unnatural and slightly dangerous
- the dog 'thought better of it' — personification with dry humour; the silence afterwards feels heavier
- the wind 'testing' the shelter and the frame's 'low, complaining groan' — personification makes the setting threatening and the shelter weak
- the paragraph moves from sight to sound, building the sense of being alone in the dark

Mark with the levels:
- Level 4 (7–8): detailed, perceptive analysis of the effects of language; judicious, precise quotations; subject terms used accurately and to the point.
- Level 3 (5–6): clear explanation of the effects; relevant, accurate quotations; clear, accurate use of terms.
- Level 2 (3–4): some understanding of the effects; some appropriate quotations; some use of terms.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple comments; simple references; simple mention of terms.
:::
