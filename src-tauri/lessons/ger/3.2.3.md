## What it is

German spelling is far more regular than English: once you know how a letter or letter group sounds, you can read almost any word aloud and spell almost any word you hear. AQA lists the **sound–symbol correspondences** (SSCs) where German differs from English, and it tests them directly in two places. In the **Speaking reading-aloud task** you read a text of at least 50 words at Higher; the 5 marks for pronunciation focus on words containing these SSCs. In the **Listening dictation** (10 marks: 5 for meaning, 5 for transcription and grammatical accuracy), you write down at least 30 words at Higher, hearing each sentence three times. Two of those words are deliberately **not** on the vocabulary list, so you must spell them from their sounds alone.

The sounds are described below in English terms. The two example words for each come from the AQA specification. There is no audio in this app, so say each example out loud as you read it.

| **sp-** / **st-** at the start of a word or word part | "shp" / "sht" | sportlich, spannend, Stadt, studieren |

| hard **ch** (after a, o, u, au) | the throaty sound in Scottish "loch" | Buch, mochte, auch |

| **th** | plain "t", never English "th" | Mathematik, Thema |
| final **-b, -d, -g** | become "p", "t", "k" | halb, gab, jemand, Deutschland, Weg, mag |
| **-tion** | "tsyohn" | Situation, Kommunikation |
| **qu** | "kv" | bequem, Konsequenz |

**Long or short?** A vowel is usually **long** when it is doubled (*Meer*), followed by **h** (*Sohn*), written **ie**, or followed by a single consonant (*gut, geben*). It is usually **short** before a double consonant or **ck** (*kommen, wissen, Rock*) or before two different consonants (*kalt, Kopf*). **Stress** normally falls on the first syllable (*SPIElen, FREUNDlich*), but inseparable prefixes are unstressed (*beSUCHen, verGESSen*), -ieren verbs stress the *-ie-* (*stuDIEren*), and -tion words stress the end (*SituaTION*).

**Spelling what you hear** (the dictation):
- **Capitalise every noun**, including nominalised verbs (*das Essen*). This is part of the AO3 transcription mark.
- **ie or ei?** Say it: "ee" is *ie*, "eye" is *ei*. The second letter gives the sound.
- **ß or ss?** Use *ß* after a long vowel or a diphthong (*groß, heißen*) and *ss* after a short vowel (*essen, muss*).
- **Final "t" sound: -d or -t?** Think of a longer form: *Hund → Hunde* (d), *bunt → bunte* (t). The same goes for **-g / -k** (*Tag → Tage*) and **-b / -p** (*halb → halbe*).
- **"ich" at the end of an adjective** is usually **-ig** (*ruhig, lustig*), but **-lich** after an l (*freundlich, plötzlich*).
- **"e" sound or ä? "oy" sound as eu or äu?** Look for a related word with *a* or *au*: *Stadt → Städte*, *Haus → Häuser*, *Traum → träumen*. Otherwise use *e* and *eu*.
- **Double consonants** follow a short vowel: *Himmel, kommen, Mutter*.

At grade 9 you read aloud with the right vowel length, the hard/soft *ch* distinction and German *w, v, z, sp, st*, with no English "th" or "w". In the dictation, your words are recognisably and correctly spelt, with capitals and grammatical endings in place, including the two unfamiliar words.

::: terms
- **sound–symbol correspondence**: the link between a letter or letter group and the sound it makes, e.g. w = English v
- **long vowel**: a stretched vowel, shown by a double vowel, an h, ie, or a single following consonant: Paar, Sohn, gut
- **short vowel**: a clipped vowel, usually before a double consonant or ck: kalt, wenn, Rock
- **diphthong**: two vowel sounds gliding together: ei (eye), au (house), eu / äu (boy)
- **umlaut**: the two dots on ä, ö, ü that change the vowel sound: schon (already) vs schön (beautiful)
- **hard ch**: the throaty ch after a, o, u, au: Buch, auch, mochte
- **soft ch**: the hissed ch after e, i, umlauts, ei, eu and consonants: ich, möchte, euch
- **final devoicing**: b, d, g at the end of a word or syllable sound like p, t, k: Hund, Weg, halb
- **vocalic r**: an r after a vowel at the end of a syllable, which sounds like a faint uh: Uhr, Meer
- **ß (Eszett)**: a sharp s used after a long vowel or diphthong: groß, heißen, Straße
- **word stress**: normally on the first syllable, but not on be-/ver-/er- prefixes, -ieren or -tion: beSUchen
- **dictation**: Listening Section B: short sentences heard three times, to be written down accurately in German
- **reading aloud task**: Speaking Part 2: a text of at least 50 words (Higher) read aloud, then four questions
:::

::: method
1. **Reading aloud: scan first.** In your preparation time, underline the traps: *w, v, z, sp-, st-, ei/ie, ch, th, -ig, -tion, qu*, and every umlaut.
2. **Decide long or short** for each stressed vowel: doubled vowel, h, ie or a single consonant means long; a double consonant or ck means short.
3. **Put the stress on the first syllable** unless the word has an inseparable prefix, ends in *-ieren* or *-tion*, or is a clear loan word.
4. **Devoice the final b, d and g**, and say *-er* at the end as "uh".
5. **Dictation, first hearing**: listen for meaning only. **Second hearing** (in sections): write every word. **Third hearing**: check the endings, the capitals and the umlauts.
6. **Spell from the sound**: ie/ei by the second letter; ß after a long vowel, ss after a short one; *-d/-g/-b* found from a longer form; *ä/äu* where a related *a/au* word exists.
7. **Check the grammar** of what you wrote: verb endings (*er spielt*), article endings (*mit dem*), and capital letters on nouns.
:::

::: example Preparing a reading-aloud sentence
*Am Wochenende spiele ich mit meinem Vater Fußball im Stadion, aber leider regnet es oft.*

- *Wochenende*: **w** = "v"; the **o** is short; the **ch** is hard after o; stress on *WO-*.
- *spiele*: **sp-** = "shp"; **ie** = "ee".
- *Vater*: **v** = "f"; long **a**; unstressed **-er** = "uh": "FAH-tuh".
- *Fußball*: **ß** after a long *u* is a sharp "s".
- *Stadion*: **st-** = "sht".
- *leider*: **ei** = "eye"; final **-er** = "uh".
- *regnet*: the **g** before *n* stays hard; short **e** in the ending.

Read it slowly once in your head, then aloud in the test, keeping the stress on the first syllables.
:::

::: example Dictation: spelling an unfamiliar word
You hear a word you do not know: **"SHTEE-fel"**, meaning "boot" (*Stiefel* is not on the AQA list).

1. It starts with "sht" at the beginning of a word, so it is spelt **St-**.
2. The long "ee" is most likely spelt **ie**.
3. The "f" sound could be **f** or **v**. With no clue, choose **f**: it is the default spelling in the middle of a word.
4. "-el" at the end is spelt as it sounds: **-el**.
5. It is a noun, so it needs a capital: **Stiefel**.

Even if you had written *Stiefl*, the meaning would still come across, but the AO3 transcription mark rewards the exact spelling.
:::

::: example Dictation: using grammar to spell
You hear: **"Mein Freund hat einen Hund, der sehr ruhig ist."**

- *Freund*: "oy" with no related *au* word, so **eu**. The final "t" sound: think of *Freunde*, so **d**.
- *hat*: *er* form of *haben*, so **t**.
- *einen*: you hear "EYE-nen", and *Hund* is masculine accusative, so **einen**.
- *Hund*: *Hunde*, so **d**. It is a noun, so it takes a capital.
- *ruhig*: ends in an "ich" sound after *h*, so it is an adjective in **-ig**.
- Comma before the relative clause *der*.
:::

::: q
How do you pronounce the *w* in *Welt* and the *v* in *viel*?
---
w = English "v" ("velt"); v = English "f" ("feel").
:::

::: q
Dictation: you hear a word that sounds like "shpeel-en" and means "to play". How is it spelt?
---
spielen (sp- at the start = "shp"; long "ee" = ie).
:::

::: q
Is the *ch* hard or soft in *Buch*, *ich*, *auch* and *möchte*?
---
Buch hard, ich soft, auch hard, möchte soft (soft after e, i and umlauts; hard after a, o, u, au).
:::

::: q
Dictation: you hear "GROHSS" (big). Is it spelt *gross* or *groß*? Why?
---
groß: the vowel is long, so the sharp s after it is written ß.
:::

::: q
How are the endings of *Hund*, *Weg* and *halb* pronounced?
---
With final devoicing: "hunt", "vehk", "halp".
:::

::: q
Dictation: you hear a word that sounds like "FROYN-dlikh" (friendly). Spell it.
---
freundlich ("oy" = eu; d from Freund; "lich" after l).
:::

::: q
Where is the stress in *besuchen*, *studieren* and *Situation*?
---
beSUchen (the be- prefix is unstressed), stuDIEren (-ieren), SituaTION (-tion).
:::

::: q
Dictation: you hear a word that sounds like "TSIM-mer" meaning "room". Spell it.
---
Zimmer (z = "ts"; short i, so a double m follows; capital Z for a noun).
:::

::: watch
- **w, v and z are the classic English-speaker errors in reading aloud**: *Welt* is "velt", *viel* is "feel", *Zimmer* starts with "ts".
- **ei vs ie**: *sein* is "zine", *Sie* is "zee". Say the second letter.
- **sp- and st- at the start** are "shp" and "sht": *Sport*, *Stadt*.
- **No English th**: *Theater* and *Thema* start with a plain t.
- **Umlauts change the word**: *schon* / *schön*, *zahlen* / *zählen*, *Mutter* / *Mütter*. Missing one in the dictation is a transcription error.
- **Capitals on every noun** in the dictation, and verb and article endings that match the grammar.
- **Final -b, -d, -g** sound like p, t, k, but keep the b, d, g spelling. Check with a longer form of the word.
:::
