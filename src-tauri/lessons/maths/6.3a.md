## What it is

Probability measures how likely something is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Written as a fraction, decimal or percentage — never as a ratio.

For equally likely outcomes:
$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes that count}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$

The words: an **outcome** is one possible result (rolling a 4); an **event** is a set of outcomes (rolling an even number); the **sample space** is the list of every possible outcome. Two events are **mutually exclusive** if they cannot both happen (rolling a 2 and rolling a 5).

Three rules at this level:

- **Complement:** $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$. The probabilities of everything that can happen add to 1.
- **Addition rule** (mutually exclusive events): $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$.
- **Expected frequency:** in $n$ trials, expect about $n \times P(A)$ occurrences.

Probability can also be **estimated from data** — **relative frequency** $= \dfrac{\text{times it happened}}{\text{number of trials}}$ — and the estimate improves with more trials. That is how a biased dice or a dodgy coin is investigated.

::: method
1. **List the sample space** when there are two things happening — as a table (two dice), a grid, or a systematic list (HHT, HTH, …). Count the total and the favourable outcomes.
2. **Complement** for "at least one" and "not": $P(\text{at least one six in two rolls}) = 1 - P(\text{no sixes})$.
3. **Add** probabilities only for mutually exclusive events; if the events overlap, use a Venn diagram and count the overlap once.
4. **From a Venn diagram:** probability $=$ count in the region $\div$ total.
5. **Probabilities in a table that must add to 1:** find the missing one by subtraction; if two are equal and unknown, let each be $x$.
6. **Expected frequency:** $n \times P$; for "how many times would you expect…". Estimated probability from an experiment: $\dfrac{f}{n}$, and more trials give a better estimate.
7. **Answer as a fraction in simplest form**, or as a decimal — never "1 in 6" or "$1 : 6$".
:::

::: example Sample space for two dice
Two fair dice are rolled and the scores added. Find $P(\text{total} = 7)$ and $P(\text{total} > 9)$.

36 equally likely pairs. Total 7: $(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)$ — 6 ways: $\dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac16$.

Total $> 9$: 10 (3 ways), 11 (2), 12 (1): $\dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac16$.
:::

::: example Complement
The probability that a train is late is $0.15$. Find the probability it is not late. If I catch the train 40 times, how many times do I expect it to be late?

$1 - 0.15 = 0.85$. Expected late: $40 \times 0.15 = 6$ times.
:::

::: example A probability table with an unknown
A spinner has sections A, B, C, D with $P(A) = 0.3$, $P(B) = 0.25$, and $P(C) = P(D)$. Find $P(C)$.

$0.3 + 0.25 + 2x = 1 \Rightarrow 2x = 0.45 \Rightarrow P(C) = 0.225$.
:::

::: example Addition rule
A bag has 5 red, 3 blue and 4 green counters. One is chosen at random. Find $P(\text{red or blue})$.

Mutually exclusive: $\dfrac{5}{12} + \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{8}{12} = \dfrac23$.
:::

::: example From a Venn diagram
Of 50 students, 28 study French, 20 study Spanish, 6 study both. A student is picked at random. Find $P(\text{French only})$ and $P(\text{neither})$.

French only $28 - 6 = 22$; Spanish only $14$; neither $50 - 22 - 6 - 14 = 8$.

$P(\text{French only}) = \dfrac{22}{50} = \dfrac{11}{25}$; $P(\text{neither}) = \dfrac{8}{50} = \dfrac{4}{25}$.
:::

::: example Relative frequency
A coin is flipped 200 times and lands heads 124 times. Estimate the probability of heads, and say whether the coin seems fair.

$\dfrac{124}{200} = 0.62$. A fair coin would give about $0.5$; with 200 trials, $0.62$ is quite far off, so the coin seems biased towards heads. (More trials would make the estimate more reliable.)
:::

::: example Systematic listing
Three coins are flipped. List the outcomes and find $P(\text{exactly two heads})$.

HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT — 8 outcomes. Exactly two heads: HHT, HTH, THH: $\dfrac38$.
:::

::: q
A card is drawn from a standard pack of 52. Find $P(\text{a king})$, $P(\text{a heart})$ and $P(\text{a red card or a black king})$.
---
$\dfrac{1}{13}$; $\dfrac14$; $\dfrac{26 + 2}{52} = \dfrac{7}{13}$.
:::

::: q
$P(\text{rain tomorrow}) = 0.35$. What is the probability it does not rain?
---
$0.65$.
:::

::: q
Two dice are rolled. Find the probability that the total is 5, and that both show the same number.
---
$\dfrac{4}{36} = \dfrac19$; $\dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac16$.
:::

::: q
Two dice are rolled. Find $P(\text{at least one six})$.
---
$1 - P(\text{no six}) = 1 - \dfrac{25}{36} = \dfrac{11}{36}$.
:::

::: q
A biased spinner has $P(1) = 0.2$, $P(2) = 0.35$, $P(3) = x$, $P(4) = 2x$. Find $x$.
---
$0.55 + 3x = 1 \Rightarrow x = 0.15$.
:::

::: q
The probability a seed germinates is $0.8$. If 250 seeds are planted, how many are expected to germinate?
---
$200$.
:::

::: q
A dice is rolled 300 times; a six comes up 72 times. Estimate $P(\text{six})$ and comment.
---
$0.24$, against $0.167$ for a fair dice — likely biased towards six.
:::

::: q
In a class of 30, 18 like tea, 15 like coffee, 7 like both. Find the probability that a random student likes neither.
---
Tea only 11, both 7, coffee only 8: neither $= 30 - 26 = 4$. $P = \dfrac{4}{30} = \dfrac{2}{15}$.
:::

::: q
Two coins are flipped. Explain why $P(\text{one head and one tail})$ is $\frac12$, not $\frac13$.
---
The outcomes HH, HT, TH, TT are equally likely; HT and TH are different outcomes, so 2 of 4.
:::

::: q
A letter is chosen at random from the word PROBABILITY. Find $P(\text{vowel})$ and $P(\text{B})$.
---
11 letters; vowels O, A, I, I: $\dfrac{4}{11}$; B twice: $\dfrac{2}{11}$.
:::

::: q
Can $P(A) = 0.6$ and $P(B) = 0.7$ be mutually exclusive? Explain.
---
No — their sum would be $1.3 > 1$; mutually exclusive probabilities cannot add to more than 1.
:::

::: watch
- **Probabilities are fractions, decimals or percentages** — "1 in 6" or "$1 : 6$" scores nothing.
- All the probabilities in a sample space **add to 1**; use this for missing values and for "not".
- "At least one" → $1 -$ "none". Faster and safer than adding cases.
- Only add probabilities for **mutually exclusive** events; overlapping events need a Venn diagram.
- Two dice have **36** outcomes, not 12; list them or draw the grid.
- Expected frequency is a **number of times** ($n \times P$), not a probability; and an estimate from an experiment gets better with **more trials**.
:::

::: test
A fair six-sided die is rolled. Find the probability of a prime number. (1)
---
Primes 2, 3, 5: $\dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.
:::

::: test
Two fair coins are tossed. Find the probability of exactly one head. (2)
---
Sample space HH, HT, TH, TT (1); $\dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ (1).
:::

::: test
$P(A) = 0.35$. Find $P(\text{not } A)$. (1)
---
$0.65$.
:::

::: test
Two fair dice are rolled and the scores added. Find the probability that the total is 9. (2)
---
Ways: $(3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3)$ — four out of 36 (1); $\dfrac{4}{36} = \dfrac{1}{9}$ (1).
:::

::: test
A spinner lands on red with probability 0.3, blue with probability 0.45, or green. Find the probability of green. (1)
---
$1 - 0.3 - 0.45 = 0.25$.
:::

::: test
$A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, with $P(A) = 0.2$ and $P(B) = 0.5$. Find $P(A \text{ or } B)$. (1)
---
$0.2 + 0.5 = 0.7$.
:::

::: test
A bag holds 5 red, 3 blue and 2 green counters. One is taken at random. Find the probability that it is red or blue. (1)
---
$\dfrac{8}{10} = 0.8$.
:::

::: test
A die is thrown 200 times and lands on six 36 times. Estimate the probability of a six; comment on whether the die seems fair; and find how many sixes you would expect in 600 throws using your estimate. (3)
---
$\dfrac{36}{200} = 0.18$ (1). A fair die gives about $0.167$, so this is a little high but not far off — 200 throws is not enough to be sure (1). $0.18 \times 600 = 108$ (1).
:::
