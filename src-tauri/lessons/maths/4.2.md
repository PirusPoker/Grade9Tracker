## What it is

A **polygon** is a closed shape with straight sides: triangle (3), quadrilateral (4), pentagon (5), hexagon (6), heptagon (7), octagon (8), nonagon (9), decagon (10). It is **regular** if all sides and all angles are equal.

Two angle results for any polygon with $n$ sides:

$$\text{sum of interior angles} = (n - 2) \times 180° \qquad \text{sum of exterior angles} = 360°$$

The first comes from cutting the polygon into $n - 2$ triangles from one vertex. The second holds for every polygon — walk round the outside and you turn through one full circle. At each vertex, interior $+$ exterior $= 180°$.

For a **regular** polygon: each exterior angle $= \dfrac{360°}{n}$, each interior $= 180° - \dfrac{360°}{n}$. Going backwards, $n = \dfrac{360°}{\text{exterior angle}}$.

**Quadrilaterals** have their own catalogue: square, rectangle, parallelogram (opposite sides equal and parallel, opposite angles equal, diagonals bisect each other), rhombus (parallelogram with all sides equal, diagonals cross at right angles), kite (two pairs of adjacent equal sides, one pair of equal angles, diagonals cross at right angles), trapezium (one pair of parallel sides).

**Congruent** shapes are identical in shape and size — one can be placed exactly on the other, perhaps after turning or flipping. Two triangles are congruent if they match on SSS, SAS, ASA (or AAS), or RHS.

::: method
1. **Interior angle of a regular $n$-gon:** exterior $= 360 \div n$, then interior $= 180 - $ exterior. (Faster and safer than $(n - 2) \times 180 \div n$.)
2. **Number of sides from an angle:** get the exterior angle ($180 - $ interior if you were given the interior), then $n = 360 \div$ exterior.
3. **Missing angle in an irregular polygon:** sum $= (n - 2) \times 180$; subtract the known angles.
4. **Angle sum of a quadrilateral:** $360°$. Combine with the special properties (opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal; co-interior angles between parallel sides add to $180°$).
5. **Shapes joined together** (a square and an equilateral triangle sharing a side, regular polygons tiled round a point): write the angles at the shared vertex; they add to $360°$ round a point or $180°$ on a line.
6. **Congruence:** list the three matching pieces of information and name the condition: "$AB = DE$, $BC = EF$, angle $B = $ angle $E$, so the triangles are congruent (SAS)". The angle in SAS must be **between** the two sides.
:::

::: example Regular polygon angles
Find the interior angle of a regular decagon.

Exterior $= 360 \div 10 = 36°$. Interior $= 180 - 36 = 144°$.
:::

::: example Sides from an interior angle
A regular polygon has interior angles of $156°$. How many sides has it?

Exterior $= 24°$. $n = 360 \div 24 = 15$ sides.
:::

::: example Missing angle in an irregular polygon
A pentagon has angles $110°$, $95°$, $130°$, $85°$ and $x$. Find $x$.

Sum $= 3 \times 180 = 540$. $x = 540 - 420 = 120°$.
:::

::: example Polygons round a point
Three regular polygons meet at a point without gaps: two octagons and one other. What is the other polygon?

Octagon interior angle: $180 - 45 = 135°$. Two of them: $270°$. Remaining $90°$ — a square.
:::

::: example A parallelogram
In parallelogram $ABCD$, angle $A = 3x + 20$ and angle $B = 2x - 15$. Find the angles.

Adjacent angles are co-interior: $3x + 20 + 2x - 15 = 180 \Rightarrow 5x = 175 \Rightarrow x = 35$. $A = 125°$, $B = 55°$; $C = 125°$, $D = 55°$ (opposite angles equal).
:::

::: example Proving congruence
$ABCD$ is a rectangle. Prove that triangles $ABC$ and $CDA$ are congruent.

$AB = CD$ (opposite sides of a rectangle), $BC = DA$ (same), $AC$ is common. Three sides match: congruent by SSS. ∎
:::

::: q
Find the sum of the interior angles of a hexagon and of a 12-sided polygon.
---
$720°$; $1800°$.
:::

::: q
Find the interior and exterior angles of a regular nonagon.
---
Exterior $40°$; interior $140°$.
:::

::: q
A regular polygon has exterior angles of $20°$. How many sides does it have, and what is its interior angle?
---
$18$ sides; $160°$.
:::

::: q
A regular polygon has interior angles of $165°$. Find the number of sides.
---
Exterior $15°$; $n = 24$.
:::

::: q
A hexagon has five angles of $130°, 110°, 125°, 140°, 95°$. Find the sixth.
---
$720 - 600 = 120°$.
:::

::: q
Explain why regular pentagons cannot tile a floor on their own.
---
Interior angle $108°$; $360 \div 108$ is not a whole number (three make $324°$, leaving a gap; four overlap).
:::

::: q
A kite has angles $x$, $x$, $70°$ and $150°$ where the two $x$ angles are the equal pair. Find $x$.
---
$2x + 220 = 360 \Rightarrow x = 70°$.
:::

::: q
In a parallelogram one angle is $64°$. Write down the other three.
---
$116°, 64°, 116°$.
:::

::: q
The exterior angles of a triangle are $2x$, $3x$ and $4x$. Find the interior angles.
---
$9x = 360$, $x = 40$: exterior $80, 120, 160$; interior $100°, 60°, 20°$.
:::

::: q
A square and a regular hexagon share a side. Find the angle between their other sides at one end of the shared side (the "gap" angle).
---
$360 - 90 - 120 = 150°$.
:::

::: q
Triangles $PQR$ and $XYZ$ have $PQ = XY$, angle $P = $ angle $X$ and angle $Q = $ angle $Y$. Are they congruent? Which condition?
---
Yes — ASA (two angles and the included side).
:::

::: q
Which of these are always true for a rhombus: opposite angles equal; diagonals equal; diagonals perpendicular; all sides equal?
---
Opposite angles equal ✓; diagonals perpendicular ✓; all sides equal ✓; diagonals equal ✗ (only for a square).
:::

::: watch
- **Exterior angles always add to 360°** whatever the polygon. Use $360 \div n$ first; it is the easiest route to any regular angle.
- Interior $+$ exterior $= 180°$ at each vertex — don't subtract from 360.
- $(n - 2) \times 180$: count the sides carefully, and remember it gives the **sum**, not each angle.
- "Regular" is what lets you divide by $n$. An irregular polygon's angles are all different — only the sum is known.
- Congruence conditions: SSS, SAS, ASA, RHS. **AAA is not congruence** (that is similarity) and SSA is not valid.
- In SAS the angle must be the one **between** the two sides you quote.
:::

Name the quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal in length and bisect each other, but are not perpendicular. (1)
---
A rectangle.
:::

::: test
State the four conditions for two triangles to be congruent. (2)
---
SSS, SAS, ASA (or AAS), RHS (2; one mark for three).
:::

::: test
Triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$ have $AB = DE$, $BC = EF$ and angle $ABC$ = angle $DEF$. Are they congruent? Name the condition. Explain why $AB = DE$, $BC = EF$ and angle $BAC$ = angle $EDF$ would not be enough. (2)
---
Yes, SAS — the angle is between the two sides (1). In the second case the angle is not included, and SSA is not a congruence condition: two different triangles can have those measurements (1).
:::
