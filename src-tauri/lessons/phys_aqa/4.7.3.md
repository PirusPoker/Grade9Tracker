## What it is

**Separate science only: this whole subsection (AQA 4.7.3, Induced potential, transformers and the National Grid) is in GCSE Physics 8463 but not in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. It is also all (Higher tier) — Foundation-tier Physics students do not need it.** (Trilogy students meet the National Grid and step-up/step-down transformers only qualitatively, in 6.2.4.3.)

### The generator effect (Higher tier)

**If an electrical conductor moves relative to a magnetic field, or if there is a change in the magnetic field around a conductor, a potential difference is induced across the ends of the conductor. If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, a current is induced in the conductor. This is called the generator effect.**

Ways to induce a potential difference:
- move a wire through (across) a magnetic field, cutting the field lines;
- push a magnet into, or pull it out of, a coil of wire;
- rotate a coil in a magnetic field, or rotate a magnet inside a coil;
- change the current in a nearby coil (so its magnetic field changes) — the idea behind the transformer.

**No movement or no change means no induced p.d.** A magnet held still inside a coil induces nothing; moving a wire **parallel** to the field lines (not cutting them) induces nothing.

**Factors that affect the size of the induced p.d. / current (recall):**
- the **speed** of the movement (how quickly the field changes) — faster gives more;
- the **strength of the magnetic field** — a stronger magnet gives more;
- the **number of turns** on the coil — more turns gives more;
- (also the area of the coil).

**Factors that affect the direction of the induced p.d. / current (recall):**
- the **direction of the movement** — moving the magnet in gives one direction, moving it out gives the opposite;
- the **direction (polarity) of the magnetic field** — using the south pole instead of the north reverses it.
Reverse both and the direction is unchanged.

### The induced current opposes the change (Higher tier)

**An induced current generates a magnetic field that opposes the original change** — either the movement of the conductor or the change in the magnetic field.

**Example:** push the **north** pole of a magnet into a coil. The induced current makes the end of the coil facing the magnet a **north** pole, which **repels** the incoming magnet. Pull the magnet out, and that end becomes a **south** pole, which **attracts** the magnet back. Either way the induced field **opposes the motion**.

This is why it takes **work** (a force moved through a distance) to generate electricity: energy from the movement is transferred to electrical energy. A bicycle dynamo is harder to pedal when the lamp is switched on, and a generator is harder to turn when more current is drawn from it. If the induced current helped the motion instead, you would get energy for nothing — impossible.

**Applying the generator effect in a context.** In any question, look for: (1) a conductor and a magnetic field, (2) **relative movement or a changing field**, (3) a complete circuit if a current is needed. Then use the factors above to say what changes the size or the direction.

### Alternators and dynamos (Higher tier)

**The generator effect is used in an alternator to generate a.c. and in a dynamo to generate d.c.**

**Alternator (a.c. generator):**
1. A coil is rotated in a magnetic field (or a magnet rotates inside a coil).
2. As the coil turns, each side moves up through the field and then down through it, so the induced p.d. **changes direction every half turn**.
3. The coil's ends are connected to **slip rings**, which turn with the coil. **Brushes** press on the slip rings, so each end of the coil is **always connected to the same** external wire.
4. So the output is an **alternating** p.d.: it rises, falls, reverses and repeats — a smooth wave shape crossing zero.

**Dynamo (d.c. generator):**
1. Same rotating coil in a magnetic field.
2. The ends are connected to a **split-ring commutator** instead of slip rings.
3. Every half turn, just as the induced p.d. in the coil reverses, the commutator **swaps the connections** to the external circuit.
4. So the output **never reverses**: it is **direct** p.d. — a series of humps, all on the same side of zero, varying in size but always the same direction.

### Graphs of p.d. against time (Higher tier)

| Situation | Graph |
|---|---|
| Alternator | a smooth wave above and below zero (a.c.) |
| Dynamo | the same humps, but the negative halves are flipped to positive (d.c., varying) |
| Coil rotated **faster** | **higher peaks** (bigger p.d., because the field changes faster) **and** more cycles per second (higher frequency, shorter time period) |
| Stronger magnet or more turns | higher peaks, same frequency |

The induced p.d. is **largest** when the sides of the coil are cutting the field lines at right angles (coil's plane **parallel** to the field), and **zero** at the instant the sides move parallel to the field lines (coil's plane at **right angles** to the field).

**Mini-example.** An alternator's coil turns 50 times per second. One full turn gives one cycle, so the frequency is 50 Hz and the period is $1 \div 50 = 0.020$ s (20 ms). If the speed doubles, there are 100 cycles per second (period 10 ms) and the peak p.d. roughly doubles.

### Microphones (Higher tier)

**Microphones use the generator effect to convert the pressure variations in sound waves into variations in current in electrical circuits.**

**How a moving-coil microphone works:**
1. Sound waves (pressure variations) hit a thin, flexible **diaphragm**, making it **vibrate**.
2. A **coil** attached to the diaphragm moves back and forth in the field of a **permanent magnet**.
3. The coil is moving relative to a magnetic field, so a **p.d. is induced** across it (generator effect).
4. As the coil moves in and out, the induced p.d. **changes direction** and size, matching the pattern of the sound wave.
5. This gives a **varying (alternating) current** in the circuit with the same frequency as the sound. Louder sound → bigger vibrations → bigger induced p.d.

A microphone is a loudspeaker working in reverse: the loudspeaker uses the **motor effect** (current → movement), the microphone the **generator effect** (movement → current).

### Transformers (Higher tier)

**A basic transformer consists of a primary coil and a secondary coil wound on an iron core. Iron is used because it is easily magnetised.** (You do not need to know about laminations or eddy currents.)

**How a transformer works:**
1. An **alternating current** in the **primary coil** produces a **changing magnetic field** in the iron core.
2. The iron core carries this changing field through the **secondary coil**.
3. The changing magnetic field through the secondary coil **induces an alternating p.d.** across it (generator effect). If the secondary is part of a complete circuit, an alternating current flows.

A transformer **only works with a.c.** A steady d.c. gives a constant magnetic field, which induces nothing in the secondary (except a brief pulse at the instant of switching on or off).

**The ratio of the p.d.s depends on the ratio of the turns.** Each turn of the secondary has the same p.d. induced in it, so **more turns on the secondary means a larger total induced p.d.**

**Equation (given on the Physics equation sheet — Higher tier, you must be able to apply it):**

$$\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{n_p}{n_s}$$

p.d. across primary ÷ p.d. across secondary = turns on primary ÷ turns on secondary, with $V$ in volts and $n$ the number of turns (no unit).

- **Step-up transformer**: $V_s > V_p$ (more turns on the secondary).
- **Step-down transformer**: $V_s < V_p$ (fewer turns on the secondary).

**If a transformer is 100% efficient, the electrical power output equals the electrical power input.**

**Equation (given on the Physics equation sheet — Higher tier, you must be able to apply it):**

$$V_s \times I_s = V_p \times I_p$$

where $V_s I_s$ is the power output (secondary coil) and $V_p I_p$ the power input (primary coil), in watts (W). So when a transformer **steps up the p.d., it steps down the current** by the same factor, and vice versa.

**Mini-examples (Higher tier).**
1. A transformer has 2000 turns on the primary and 100 on the secondary. Input 230 V. $V_s = V_p \times n_s \div n_p = 230 \times 100 \div 2000 = 11.5$ V (a step-down transformer).
2. A power-station transformer steps 25 000 V up to 400 000 V. The primary has 1000 turns. $n_s = n_p \times V_s \div V_p = 1000 \times 400\,000 \div 25\,000 = 16\,000$ turns.
3. A phone charger's transformer gives 5.0 V and 2.0 A to the phone from the 230 V mains. Output power $= 5.0 \times 2.0 = 10$ W, so (if 100% efficient) $I_p = 10 \div 230 = 0.043$ A — the **current drawn from the supply**.

### Transformers and the National Grid (Higher tier)

The National Grid uses **step-up transformers** at power stations to increase the p.d. to as much as 400 000 V for transmission, and **step-down transformers** near homes to reduce it to 230 V for safety.

**Why high p.d. transmission is more efficient:**
- For a given power, $P = VI$, so **increasing the p.d. decreases the current** by the same factor ($V_s I_s = V_p I_p$).
- The power wasted heating the cables is $P = I^2R$ (recall), so a smaller current means **far less energy lost** as heat — reducing the current by a factor of 16 reduces the heating by $16^2 = 256$.
- Smaller currents also allow **thinner, cheaper cables**.

**Mini-example.** 1.0 MW is sent through cables of total resistance 5.0 Ω.
- At 25 000 V: $I = P \div V = 1\,000\,000 \div 25\,000 = 40$ A; power lost $= I^2 R = 40^2 \times 5.0 = 8000$ W.
- At 400 000 V: $I = 1\,000\,000 \div 400\,000 = 2.5$ A; power lost $= 2.5^2 \times 5.0 = 31.25$ W, about 31 W.
- Stepping up the p.d. by 16 times cuts the current by 16 times and the heating loss by 256 times.

::: terms
- **Generator effect**: the induction of a potential difference across a conductor that moves relative to a magnetic field, or that sits in a changing field.
- **Induced current**: a current produced by the generator effect when the conductor is part of a complete circuit.
- **Alternator**: a generator that uses slip rings and brushes to produce an alternating current.
- **Dynamo**: a generator that uses a split-ring commutator to produce a direct current.
- **Slip rings**: rings fixed to a rotating coil that keep each end of the coil connected to the same external wire.
- **Transformer**: a device of two coils on an iron core that changes the size of an alternating potential difference.
- **Step-up transformer**: a transformer with more turns on the secondary coil, so the secondary p.d. is larger than the primary p.d.
- **Step-down transformer**: a transformer with fewer turns on the secondary coil, so the secondary p.d. is smaller than the primary p.d.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Recall / state** the factors: size — speed of movement, strength of field, number of turns; direction — direction of movement, polarity of field.
- **Explain** an induced current in a new context: identify the conductor, the magnetic field and the **relative movement or change**; mention a complete circuit for a current.
- **Explain** the opposing effect: the induced current makes a magnetic field that **opposes** the change (repels an approaching magnet, attracts a leaving one), so work must be done.
- **Explain** alternator vs dynamo: slip rings keep the same connection → a.c.; split-ring commutator swaps connections every half turn → d.c.
- **Draw / interpret** p.d.–time graphs: count cycles to find the period and frequency; faster rotation means taller **and** closer-together peaks.
- **Explain** a transformer: a.c. in primary → changing field in iron core → changing field through secondary → induced alternating p.d.

**Calculations** (both equations are on the **equation sheet**, Higher tier):
1. Turns: $V_p \div V_s = n_p \div n_s$. Rearrange, for example $V_s = V_p \times n_s \div n_p$.
2. Power: $V_s I_s = V_p I_p$, assuming 100% efficiency. To find the current drawn from the supply: $I_p = V_s I_s \div V_p$.
3. National Grid: $P = VI$ (recall) gives the current; $P = I^2 R$ (recall) gives the heating loss.
4. Check sense: a step-up transformer must give $V_s > V_p$ and $I_s < I_p$.

**Extended answers (6 marks)**: on the National Grid, level 3 links step-up transformer → higher p.d. → lower current for the same power → less heating ($I^2R$) → more efficient, then step-down for safety. On generators, level 3 explains how the induced p.d. arises and how the connections (slip rings or commutator) shape the output.
:::

A magnet is pushed into a coil connected to a sensitive ammeter. Give two ways to increase the reading and one way to reverse it. (3)

Increase: push the magnet in faster / use a stronger magnet / use a coil with more turns (any two, 1 each). Reverse: pull the magnet out instead, or use the other pole of the magnet (1).

A transformer steps 230 V down to 6.0 V. The primary coil has 920 turns. Calculate the number of turns on the secondary coil. (3)

$n_s = n_p \times V_s \div V_p$ (1) $= 920 \times 6.0 \div 230$ (1) $= 24$ turns (1).

::: example Current drawn from the supply
A 12 V, 36 W lamp is run from a 230 V supply through a 100% efficient transformer. Calculate the current in the primary coil. (3)

Power output $= 36$ W, so power input $= 36$ W (1). $I_p = P \div V_p = 36 \div 230$ (1) $= 0.157$ A, about 0.16 A (1).

::: example Alternator graph
The graph of an alternator's output shows 4 complete cycles in 0.080 s. (a) Calculate the frequency. (b) Describe how the graph changes if the coil turns at half the speed. (4)

(a) Period $= 0.080 \div 4 = 0.020$ s (1); frequency $= 1 \div 0.020 = 50$ Hz (1). (b) The peaks are lower (about half the height) (1) and further apart — half as many cycles in the same time (25 Hz) (1).

A transformer has 500 turns on its primary and 2500 turns on its secondary. The primary p.d. is 40 V. Calculate the secondary p.d. and state the type of transformer. (3)

Explain why a transformer does not work with a steady direct current. (2)
---

A transformer steps 11 000 V down to 230 V. The current in the secondary is 50 A. Assuming 100% efficiency, calculate the current in the primary. (3)
---
$I_p = V_s I_s \div V_p$ (1) $= 230 \times 50 \div 11\,000$ (1) $= 1.05$ A, about 1.0 A (1).

::: watch
- **"Any magnet near a coil induces a current"**: there must be **relative movement** or a **changing** field — a stationary magnet induces nothing.
- **Mixing up motor and generator effects**: motor effect = current + field → **force/movement**; generator effect = movement/changing field → **induced p.d.**
- **Slip rings vs commutator**: **slip rings → a.c.** (alternator); **split-ring commutator → d.c.** (dynamo).
- **Transformers with d.c.**: they need **alternating** current to make a **changing** field.
- **Upside-down ratio**: in $V_p \div V_s = n_p \div n_s$, keep primary with primary on top; check a step-up answer gives $V_s > V_p$.
- **National Grid "to make the current faster"**: higher p.d. means a **lower current**, so **less heating** ($I^2R$) in the cables — that is the reason.
:::

::: test
A cyclist notices that pedalling becomes harder when the bicycle's dynamo lamp is switched on. Explain why. (3)
---
With the lamp on there is a complete circuit, so a current is induced in the dynamo's coil (1). The induced current produces a magnetic field that opposes the movement causing it (1), so the cyclist must do more work to keep the dynamo turning — that energy is transferred to the lamp (1).

::: test
Sketch or describe the p.d.–time graph from a dynamo and explain why it differs from an alternator's. (3)
---
A series of humps all on one side of the zero line (varying but never reversing) (1). In the coil the p.d. reverses every half turn (1); the split-ring commutator swaps the connections to the external circuit every half turn, so the output keeps the same direction (1).
:::

::: test
A laptop charger contains a transformer with a primary of 230 V and 1150 turns. The output is 20 V at 3.25 A. (a) Calculate the number of turns on the secondary. (b) Calculate the current drawn from the mains, assuming 100% efficiency. (5)
---
(a) $n_s = n_p \times V_s \div V_p = 1150 \times 20 \div 230$ (1) $= 100$ turns (1). (b) Power output $= 20 \times 3.25 = 65$ W (1). $I_p = 65 \div 230$ (1) $= 0.28$ A (1).

::: test
Electricity is transmitted with a power of 50 MW. The cables have a total resistance of 8.0 Ω. (a) Calculate the current if it is transmitted at 400 kV. (b) Calculate the power wasted heating the cables. (c) Calculate the power wasted if, instead, it were transmitted at 25 kV, and comment. (6)
---
(a) $I = P \div V = 50\,000\,000 \div 400\,000$ (1) $= 125$ A (1). (b) $P = I^2R = 125^2 \times 8.0$ (1) $= 125\,000$ W (0.125 MW) (1). (c) $I = 50\,000\,000 \div 25\,000 = 2000$ A; $P = 2000^2 \times 8.0 = 32\,000\,000$ W (32 MW) (1). Transmitting at 25 kV would waste 64% of the power — 256 times more than at 400 kV — so high p.d. transmission is far more efficient (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a transformer changes the size of an alternating potential difference, and why the p.d. ratio depends on the turns ratio. (4)
---
An alternating current in the primary coil produces a changing magnetic field (1) in the iron core, which passes through the secondary coil (1). The changing field through the secondary induces an alternating p.d. across it (1). Each turn of the secondary has the same p.d. induced, so the more turns on the secondary compared with the primary, the larger the secondary p.d. (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how transformers in the National Grid make the transmission of electricity efficient and safe. Use the transformer equations in your answer. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a full, logical explanation using the equations, linking p.d., current, heating loss and efficiency, plus the role of step-down transformers.
Level 2 (3–4): explains that high p.d. reduces current and energy loss, with some use of the equations or of step-down transformers.
Level 1 (1–2): isolated statements, e.g. "transformers change voltage" or "high voltage is efficient".
Indicative content:
- Step-up transformers at power stations increase the p.d. (to about 400 kV) — more turns on the secondary, $V_p \div V_s = n_p \div n_s$.
- For a 100% efficient transformer $V_s I_s = V_p I_p$, so increasing the p.d. decreases the current by the same factor.
- Power is transmitted at the same rate ($P = VI$) but with a much smaller current.
- Heating in the cables depends on $I^2R$, so a smaller current gives much less energy wasted (reducing the current 10 times cuts the loss 100 times).
- So less energy is wasted and transmission is more efficient; thinner cables can be used.
- Step-down transformers near homes reduce the p.d. to 230 V (fewer turns on secondary), increasing the current, because very high p.d. is dangerous for consumers.
- Transformers need a.c., which is why the grid uses alternating current.
:::
