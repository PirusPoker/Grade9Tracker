## What it is

AQA 4.7.2 covers the magnetic effect of a current (electromagnetism), the motor effect and Fleming's left-hand rule, electric motors and loudspeakers. **Trilogy 8464 students need electromagnetism (all tiers), and Fleming's left-hand rule, $F = BIl$ and electric motors (Higher tier) — their 6.7.2. Interpreting diagrams of electromagnetic devices, and loudspeakers, are Separate science only.**

### The magnetic effect of a current

**When a current flows through a conducting wire, a magnetic field is produced around the wire.**

**The strength of the field depends on:**
- the **current** through the wire — a bigger current gives a stronger field;
- the **distance from the wire** — the field is weaker further away.

**Field pattern around a straight wire.** The field lines are **concentric circles** centred on the wire, in a plane at right angles to it. Draw the circles **further apart** as you go outwards (weaker field).

**Direction — the right-hand grip rule.** Grip the wire with your **right hand**, **thumb pointing in the direction of the (conventional) current**; your **fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field**. Reverse the current and the field reverses.

On a diagram, a current going **into** the page is drawn as ⊗ (a cross — the tail of an arrow) and **out of** the page as ⊙ (a dot — the point of an arrow). For current into the page, the field circles go **clockwise**; for current out of the page, **anticlockwise**.

**How to demonstrate the magnetic effect of a current:**
1. Pass a vertical wire through a hole in a horizontal piece of card. Connect it to a low-voltage power supply (with a resistor or switch so it is only on briefly — the wire gets hot).
2. Place several **plotting compasses** on the card around the wire. With no current, they all point north (Earth's field).
3. Switch on: the compasses turn to point in **circles around the wire**. Reverse the current and they all reverse.
4. Alternatively, sprinkle **iron filings** on the card and tap it — they form concentric circles (shape only).

### Solenoids and electromagnets

**Shaping a wire into a coil called a solenoid increases the strength of the magnetic field** created by a current through it.

**Why a solenoid increases the magnetic effect (explain this):** each turn of the coil produces its own magnetic field. Inside the solenoid, the fields from all the turns point the **same way** and **add together**, so the field inside is much stronger than around a single straight wire.

- **The magnetic field inside a solenoid is strong and uniform** (parallel, evenly spaced field lines).
- **The field around a solenoid has a similar shape to that of a bar magnet**: one end acts as a north pole and the other as a south pole.
- **Adding an iron core increases the strength** of the field (the iron becomes an induced magnet whose field adds to the solenoid's).
- **An electromagnet is a solenoid with an iron core.** It can be switched on and off, and its strength can be changed by changing the current.

**Making an electromagnet stronger:** increase the current; increase the number of turns (coils closer together per unit length); add an iron core.

**Finding the poles of a solenoid:** use the right-hand grip rule with your **fingers curled in the direction of the current** around the coil — your **thumb points to the north pole**. Or look at an end: if the current flows **anticlockwise** as you look at it, that end is **N**; **clockwise** is **S**.

### Separate science only: electromagnetic devices

**Separate science only:** you should be able to interpret a diagram of an electromagnetic device and explain how it works. The key idea is always the same: **a current switches on an electromagnet, which attracts an iron part, which moves and does a job**; when the current stops, the electromagnet loses its magnetism and a spring returns the part.

| Device | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Relay** | a small current in one circuit energises an electromagnet, which attracts an iron armature; this pivots and closes (or opens) contacts in a second circuit carrying a large current — e.g. a car's starter motor is switched on safely by a small current from the ignition switch |
| **Electric bell** | current magnetises the electromagnet, which attracts the iron armature so the hammer strikes the gong; the movement breaks the contact, the electromagnet switches off, the spring pulls the armature back, contact is remade — and it repeats rapidly |
| **Circuit breaker** | if the current becomes too large, the electromagnet becomes strong enough to attract an iron catch, which releases a spring-loaded switch and breaks the circuit; it can be reset |
| **Electromagnetic lock** | current in the electromagnet pulls an iron bolt back to unlock the door; when the current stops, a spring pushes the bolt back |
| **Scrapyard crane** | a large electromagnet picks up iron and steel; switching off the current drops the load |

The core must be **iron** (a soft magnetic material) because it magnetises strongly and **loses its magnetism quickly** when the current is switched off. Steel would stay magnetised.

### The motor effect (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** **When a conductor carrying a current is placed in a magnetic field, the magnet producing the field and the conductor exert a force on each other. This is called the motor effect.** The force comes from the interaction between the field of the magnet and the field produced by the current.

**Fleming's left-hand rule (Higher tier)** shows the relative directions of the force, the current and the field. Hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of your **left hand** all at right angles:
- **F**irst finger → **F**ield (from N to S)
- se**C**ond finger → **C**urrent (conventional current, + to −)
- thu**M**b → **M**otion (the direction of the force)

Reverse **either** the current **or** the field and the force reverses. Reverse **both** and the force is in the original direction.

**Factors that affect the size of the force (recall):**
- the **magnetic flux density** (strength of the magnetic field);
- the **current** in the conductor;
- the **length of conductor** in the field.

The force is greatest when the conductor is **at right angles** to the field, and **zero** when the conductor is **parallel** to the field.

**Equation (given on the Physics equation sheet — Higher tier, you must be able to apply it):**

force = magnetic flux density × current × length

$$F = BIl$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| force | $F$ | newton, N |
| magnetic flux density | $B$ | tesla, T |
| current | $I$ | ampere, A |
| length of conductor in the field | $l$ | metre, m |

This applies to a conductor **at right angles** to the field. Watch the prefixes: 1 mT = 0.001 T; 1 cm = 0.01 m.

**Mini-examples (Higher tier).**
1. A wire 4.0 cm long carries 3.0 A at right angles to a field of 0.25 T. $F = BIl = 0.25 \times 3.0 \times 0.040 = 0.030$ N.
2. A force of 0.12 N acts on a 5.0 cm length of wire in a 0.40 T field. $I = F \div (Bl) = 0.12 \div (0.40 \times 0.050) = 6.0$ A.
3. A wire 8.0 cm long in a 50 mT field carries 2.5 A. $B = 0.050$ T, $l = 0.080$ m: $F = 0.050 \times 2.5 \times 0.080 = 0.010$ N.

### Electric motors (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** **A coil of wire carrying a current in a magnetic field tends to rotate. This is the basis of an electric motor.**

**How the force causes rotation (explain this):**
1. A rectangular coil sits between the poles of a magnet. Current flows **in one direction** along one side of the coil and **in the opposite direction** along the other side.
2. Both sides are at right angles to the field, so each experiences a force (motor effect). Because the currents are opposite, by Fleming's left-hand rule the **forces are in opposite directions** — one side is pushed **up**, the other **down**.
3. These two forces produce a **turning effect (moment)** about the axle, so the coil **rotates**.
4. A **split-ring commutator** connects the coil to the supply through **brushes**. Every **half turn** it **reverses the direction of the current** in the coil. This keeps the force on each side of the coil acting in the right direction, so the coil **keeps turning the same way** instead of rocking back and forth.

**To make the motor turn faster:** increase the current, use a stronger magnet, or use more turns on the coil. **To reverse the direction of rotation:** reverse the current or reverse the magnetic field.

### Separate science only: loudspeakers (Higher tier)

**Separate science only (Higher tier):** **loudspeakers and headphones use the motor effect to convert variations in current in electrical circuits to the pressure variations in sound waves.**

**How a moving-coil loudspeaker works:**
1. A **coil of wire** is attached to a paper or plastic **cone** and sits in the field of a **permanent magnet**.
2. An **alternating current** (the electrical signal from the amplifier) flows through the coil.
3. The coil is a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field, so it experiences a **force** (motor effect).
4. Because the current keeps **changing direction**, the force keeps changing direction, so the coil and cone **move in and out** — they **vibrate**.
5. The vibrating cone pushes and pulls on the air, creating **pressure variations** (compressions and rarefactions) — a **sound wave**.
6. The sound has the **same frequency** as the alternating current. A larger current gives a larger force, larger vibrations and a **louder** sound.

Headphones work the same way with a much smaller coil, magnet and diaphragm.

::: terms
- **Electromagnetism**: the production of a magnetic field around a conductor carrying an electric current.
- **Solenoid**: a long coil of wire whose magnetic field, when current flows, is strong and uniform inside and shaped like a bar magnet's outside.
- **Electromagnet**: a solenoid with an iron core, giving a strong magnetic field that can be switched on and off.
- **Motor effect**: the force between a current-carrying conductor and the magnet producing the field it sits in.
- **Fleming's left-hand rule**: a rule using the left hand to give the direction of the force when the field and current directions are known.
- **Magnetic flux density**: a measure of the strength of a magnetic field, measured in tesla.
- **Split-ring commutator**: a rotating contact in a d.c. motor that reverses the current in the coil every half turn so it keeps rotating one way.
- **Loudspeaker**: a device that uses the motor effect to turn a varying current into vibrations of a cone that produce sound waves.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** how to show the magnetic effect of a current: compasses (or iron filings) on a card around a vertical wire; switch on; compasses line up in circles; reverse current, compasses reverse.
- **Draw** fields: concentric circles for a straight wire (spacing increasing outwards, arrows from the right-hand grip rule); bar-magnet shape for a solenoid with parallel lines inside.
- **Explain** why a solenoid is stronger: fields from each turn **add together** inside the coil.
- **Explain** a device (Separate science only): follow the chain — current → electromagnet magnetised → attracts iron armature → part moves → effect; current off → loses magnetism → spring returns it.
- **Explain** a motor (Higher tier): opposite currents in the two sides → opposite forces → moment → rotation; commutator reverses current each half turn.
- **Use Fleming's left-hand rule** (Higher tier): state which finger is which, and give the force direction in the words of the question (up, down, into the page, left).

**Calculations** (Higher tier): $F = BIl$ is on the **equation sheet**. Convert cm to m and mT to T before substituting; rearrange as $I = F \div (Bl)$, $B = F \div (Il)$, $l = F \div (BI)$. Give the unit (N, T, A or m). If the wire is **parallel** to the field, the force is zero.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**: motors and loudspeakers are popular 6-markers. Level 3 explains each step with the physics (motor effect, direction reversal, moment or vibration, commutator or alternating current). Just naming parts without explaining how they cause movement stays at level 1–2.
:::

::: example Field around a wire
A vertical wire carries a current upwards through a horizontal card. Describe the magnetic field pattern on the card and how it changes if the current is increased. (3)

Concentric circles centred on the wire (1); anticlockwise when seen from above (right-hand grip rule, thumb up) (1). A bigger current makes the field stronger at every distance (1).
:::

::: example Using F = BIl (Higher tier)
A 6.0 cm length of wire is at right angles to a magnetic field of flux density 0.15 T. It carries a current of 4.0 A. Calculate the force on it. (3)

$l = 0.060$ m (1). $F = BIl = 0.15 \times 4.0 \times 0.060$ (1) $= 0.036$ N (1).
:::

::: example Fleming's left-hand rule (Higher tier)
A horizontal wire runs from left to right with current flowing to the right. The magnetic field points into the page. State the direction of the force on the wire. (1)

Upwards (towards the top of the page). (First finger into page, second finger to the right, thumb points up.)
:::

::: example Relay (Separate science only)
A car's starter motor needs a current of 150 A. Explain how a relay allows the driver to switch it on using a small current. (4)

The ignition switch lets a small current flow through the relay's coil (1). The coil (with an iron core) becomes an electromagnet (1) and attracts an iron armature, which moves and closes the contacts in the starter motor circuit (1). So the large current flows in a separate circuit, and the driver's switch only carries a small current, which is safer and needs thinner wires (1).
:::

Increase the current (1); use a stronger magnet / greater magnetic flux density (1); increase the number of turns on the coil (1).

::: watch
- **Right hand vs left hand**: the **right**-hand grip rule gives the field around a wire; Fleming's **left**-hand rule gives the force in the motor effect.
- **Mixing up the fingers**: **F**irst = **F**ield, se**C**ond = **C**urrent, thu**M**b = **M**otion.
- **Forgetting unit conversions** in $F = BIl$: length in **metres**, flux density in **tesla** (50 mT = 0.050 T).
- **Motor explanations with no forces**: say the two sides carry current in **opposite directions**, so the forces are **opposite**, giving a **turning effect**.
- **"The commutator makes it spin"**: the motor effect makes it spin; the commutator **reverses the current every half turn** so it keeps spinning the same way.
- **Steel cores in electromagnets**: the core is **iron** because it loses its magnetism quickly when the current stops.
:::

(Higher tier) Explain how a simple d.c. electric motor works. (6)
---

Level 2 (3–4): explains the forces on the coil causing rotation, but with little or confused detail on the commutator.

Indicative content:
- A coil of wire carrying a current is placed between the poles of a magnet (in a magnetic field).
- Each side of the coil is a current-carrying conductor at right angles to the field, so a force acts on it (motor effect).
- Current flows in opposite directions in the two sides, so (Fleming's left-hand rule) one side is pushed up and the other down.
- These forces produce a moment/turning effect about the axle, so the coil rotates.
- A split-ring commutator, connected through brushes, reverses the current in the coil every half turn.
- This keeps the forces acting so the coil continues to rotate in the same direction.
- Larger current, stronger magnet or more turns make it rotate faster; reversing the current or field reverses the rotation.
