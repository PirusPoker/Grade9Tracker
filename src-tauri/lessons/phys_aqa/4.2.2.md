## What it is

There are two ways of joining components: **in series** (one after another in a single loop) and **in parallel** (on separate branches, each connected across the same two points). Many real circuits contain both series and parallel parts. This topic (AQA 4.2.2) is about the rules for current, potential difference and resistance in each, and how series circuits are used for measuring and testing. Everything here is also in **Combined Science: Trilogy**.

### The rules

| | **Series** | **Parallel** |
|---|---|---|
| Layout | one loop; one path for the charge | two or more branches; several paths |
| Current | **the same through each component** | **total current = sum of the branch currents** |
| Potential difference | **the supply pd is shared** between the components | **the pd across each branch is the same** (equal to the supply pd for a simple parallel circuit) |
| Resistance | **total = sum of the resistances** | **total is less than the smallest individual resistor** |
| If one component breaks | the whole circuit stops | the other branches keep working |

For two components in series:

$$R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2$$

(resistance in ohms, Ω). You need to **know and use** this. You are **not** required to calculate the total resistance of two resistors in parallel — but you must know it is **less than the smallest one**, and you can still work out currents in each branch using $V = IR$.

Also useful: **cells in series** (all facing the same way) give a total pd equal to the sum of their pds — two 1.5 V cells give 3.0 V.

### Why series adds resistance and parallel reduces it

You need to explain this **qualitatively** (in words):

- **Series.** The charge has to pass through **every** resistor in turn, so there is more opposition to its flow. The supply pd is shared, so each resistor has less pd across it and the current is smaller — the total resistance has **increased**.
- **Parallel.** Adding another branch gives the charge **another path** to flow through. Each branch has the full supply pd across it, so each branch draws its own current, and the **total current increases** for the same supply pd. More current for the same pd means **less total resistance** ($R = V \div I$).

A good analogy: series is like making one road longer (more traffic hold-ups); parallel is like opening an extra lane (more cars get through).

### Calculating in series circuits

**Method:** add the resistances → find the current from the supply pd → use $V = IR$ for each component. Check the pds add up to the supply pd.

**Mini-example.** A 6.0 V battery is connected to a 4.0 Ω and an 8.0 Ω resistor in series.
- $R_{\text{total}} = 4.0 + 8.0 = 12$ Ω.
- $I = V \div R = 6.0 \div 12 = 0.50$ A — the same through both resistors.
- $V_1 = 0.50 \times 4.0 = 2.0$ V; $V_2 = 0.50 \times 8.0 = 4.0$ V. Check: $2.0 + 4.0 = 6.0$ V, the supply pd.

**The bigger resistor gets the bigger share of the pd** — the pd divides in the same ratio as the resistances (4 : 8 gives 2 V : 4 V).

**Equivalent resistance.** Several resistors in series can be replaced by one resistor equal to their total — the **equivalent resistance**. Three resistors of 10 Ω, 8.0 Ω and an unknown $R_3$ carry 0.25 A from a 9.0 V supply: $R_{\text{total}} = 9.0 \div 0.25 = 36$ Ω, so $R_3 = 36 - 10 - 8.0 = 18$ Ω.

### Calculating in parallel circuits

Each branch has the supply pd across it, so treat each branch separately with $V = IR$, then add the currents.

**Mini-example.** A 4.0 Ω and a 6.0 Ω resistor are connected in parallel to a 12 V supply.
- Current through 6.0 Ω: $12 \div 6.0 = 2.0$ A. Current through 4.0 Ω: $12 \div 4.0 = 3.0$ A.
- Total current from the supply $= 2.0 + 3.0 = 5.0$ A.
- Total resistance $= V \div I = 12 \div 5.0 = 2.4$ Ω — **less than 4.0 Ω**, the smallest resistor, as the rule says.

**Mixed circuits.** For a lamp in series with two parallel branches, the current into the junction equals the total current out of it, and the pds around any complete loop add up to the supply pd.

### Constructing and checking circuits

You should be able to build series and parallel circuits from a circuit diagram and check them:
- Build the main loop first, then add parallel branches and the voltmeter last.
- Check every connection follows the diagram; ammeters go **in series** (in the branch whose current you want), voltmeters **in parallel** across the component.
- Test by measuring: in series, ammeters anywhere read the same; in parallel, the branch currents should add to the supply current; voltmeters across parallel branches should read the same.

This links to **Required practical 3** (factors affecting resistance), part (b): using two identical resistors, measure V and I to find the resistance of one resistor, two in series (double) and two in parallel (half).

### Series circuits for measurement and testing

Series circuits are used in measuring and testing because the **same current flows through everything** and the **pd is shared in proportion to resistance**.

- **Measuring current.** An ammeter is placed **in series** so the current being measured flows through it. It has a very low resistance so it hardly changes the current.
- **Limiting current.** A fixed resistor in series with an LED or diode limits the current so it is not damaged. Example: an LED needs 2.0 V at 0.020 A from a 6.0 V supply. The resistor must take $6.0 - 2.0 = 4.0$ V, so $R = 4.0 \div 0.020 = 200$ Ω.
- **Controlling current.** A variable resistor in series changes the current (e.g. a dimmer, or to take a range of readings in a required practical).
- **Sensor circuits.** A thermistor or LDR in series with a fixed resistor: as the sensor's resistance changes, its share of the pd changes, and that pd can trigger a switch, an alarm or a meter.

**Mini-example (sensor).** A 12 V supply is connected to a 2.0 kΩ fixed resistor in series with a thermistor. At 20 °C the thermistor is 4.0 kΩ: total 6.0 kΩ, $I = 12 \div 6000 = 0.0020$ A, pd across the fixed resistor $= 0.0020 \times 2000 = 4.0$ V. At 60 °C the thermistor is 1.0 kΩ: total 3.0 kΩ, $I = 0.0040$ A, pd across the fixed resistor $= 8.0$ V. So a voltmeter across the fixed resistor reads higher as the temperature rises — a simple electronic thermometer or the input to a thermostat.

- **Testing components.** A cell, an ammeter and the component in series (a continuity tester) show whether a fuse, a wire or a lamp filament is broken: no current means a break in the circuit.

::: terms
- **Series circuit**: components joined one after another in a single loop, so there is only one path for the charge.
- **Parallel circuit**: components on separate branches, each connected across the same two points.
- **Total resistance**: the single resistance that has the same effect as all the resistors in a circuit together.
- **Equivalent resistance**: one resistor that could replace a combination of resistors without changing the current.
- **Branch**: one of the separate paths in a parallel circuit that current can flow along.
- **Junction**: a point where a circuit splits or joins; the current in equals the total current out.
- **Sensor circuit**: a thermistor or LDR in series with a fixed resistor, so the pd shared changes with temperature or light.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe the difference** between series and parallel: cover layout, current, pd and resistance, using "whereas" — "in series the current is the same through each component, whereas in parallel the total current is the sum of the branch currents".
- **Explain** why total resistance changes: series → charge must pass through every resistor; parallel → extra path, more total current for the same pd, so less resistance.
- **Suggest** why houses are wired in parallel: each appliance gets the full mains pd and can be switched on and off separately; one failing does not stop the others.

**Series calculations — step by step**
1. $R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$
2. $I = V_{\text{supply}} \div R_{\text{total}}$ — the same everywhere in the loop.
3. pd across each component $= I \times R$ for that component.
4. Check: the pds add up to the supply pd.
5. Unknown resistor: find $R_{\text{total}} = V \div I$, then subtract the known resistances.

**Parallel calculations**
1. Each branch has the full supply pd (for resistors connected straight across the supply).
2. Branch current $= V \div R$ for that branch.
3. Supply current $=$ sum of branch currents.
4. If asked for total resistance, use $R = V_{\text{supply}} \div I_{\text{total}}$ — you never need $1/R$ formulas at GCSE AQA.

**Unit traps**: kΩ → Ω (× 1000), mA → A (÷ 1000).

**Required practical and 6-mark answers**
For "describe how you would show…" questions (Required practical 3, part b), Level 3 (5–6) answers give the circuit (meters correctly placed), the measurements for one resistor, two in series and two in parallel, how resistance is calculated ($R = V \div I$), the expected results (series doubles, parallel halves) and an explanation linked to paths for the charge. Level 2 (3–4): a workable method or the results with partial explanation. Level 1 (1–2): isolated statements.
:::

::: example Series calculation
A 9.0 V battery is connected in series with a 20 Ω and a 25 Ω resistor. Calculate (a) the total resistance, (b) the current, (c) the pd across the 25 Ω resistor. (4)

(a) $R = 20 + 25 = 45$ Ω (1). (b) $I = 9.0 \div 45$ (1) $= 0.20$ A (1). (c) $V = 0.20 \times 25 = 5.0$ V (1).
:::

::: example Parallel branches
A 6.0 V supply is connected across two parallel branches. Branch 1 is a 6.0 Ω resistor. The supply current is 1.5 A. Calculate the resistance in branch 2. (3)

Current in branch 1 $= 6.0 \div 6.0 = 1.0$ A (1). Current in branch 2 $= 1.5 - 1.0 = 0.50$ A (1). $R_2 = 6.0 \div 0.50 = 12$ Ω (1).
:::

::: example Choosing a series resistor
A lamp works correctly with a pd of 6.0 V and a current of 0.30 A. It is to be used with a 9.0 V battery. Calculate the resistance of the resistor that must be connected in series with it. (3)

The resistor must take $9.0 - 6.0 = 3.0$ V (1). Same current in series: 0.30 A (1). $R = 3.0 \div 0.30 = 10$ Ω (1).
:::

::: example Explaining resistance in parallel
Explain why adding a second resistor in parallel with the first decreases the total resistance of a circuit. (3)

The second resistor provides an extra path for the charge (1). It has the same pd across it as the first, so it draws its own current and the total current from the supply increases (1). Since the supply pd is unchanged but the current is larger, the total resistance ($V \div I$) is smaller (1).
:::

::: example A sensor circuit
An LDR is connected in series with a 10 kΩ fixed resistor and a 6.0 V supply. In daylight the LDR's resistance is 2.0 kΩ. Calculate the pd across the LDR, and state what happens to it in the dark. (4)

Total $= 12$ kΩ $= 12\,000$ Ω; $I = 6.0 \div 12\,000 = 0.00050$ A (1). pd across LDR $= 0.00050 \times 2000 = 1.0$ V (1). In the dark the LDR's resistance increases (1), so it takes a bigger share of the 6.0 V and the pd across it rises (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Swapping the rules**: series → same **current**, pd shared. Parallel → same **pd**, current shared. Learn them as a pair.
- **Adding resistances in parallel**: $R_1 + R_2$ is only for series. In parallel the total is **less than the smallest** resistor.
- **Using the wrong pd**: in a series circuit, use the pd across **that** component (or the total resistance with the supply pd) — not the supply pd with one resistor.
- **Not checking**: the pds in a series loop must add up to the supply pd; branch currents must add up to the supply current.
- **Vague explanations**: for parallel, say "**extra path**, so the **total current increases** for the same pd" — not just "the current splits".
:::

::: test
An LED needs a pd of 2.0 V and a current of 20 mA. It is connected to a 5.0 V supply with a resistor in series. Calculate the resistance needed, and explain why the resistor is needed. (4)
---
pd across resistor $= 5.0 - 2.0 = 3.0$ V (1). $I = 0.020$ A (1). $R = 3.0 \div 0.020 = 150$ Ω (1). Without it the current would be too large and would damage the LED (1).

::: test
A 12 V supply is connected to a lamp in series with two resistors in parallel. The current from the supply is 2.0 A and the pd across the lamp is 4.0 V. The current through resistor X is 0.80 A. Calculate the current through resistor Y and the resistance of Y. (4)
---
pd across the parallel section $= 12 - 4.0 = 8.0$ V (1). Current through Y $= 2.0 - 0.80 = 1.2$ A (1). $R_Y = 8.0 \div 1.2$ (1) $= 6.7$ Ω (1).
:::

::: test
A temperature sensor uses a 9.0 V supply, a 3.0 kΩ fixed resistor and a thermistor in series. A voltmeter across the fixed resistor reads 6.0 V. (a) Calculate the resistance of the thermistor. (b) Explain what happens to the voltmeter reading as the temperature rises. (5)
---
(a) $I = 6.0 \div 3000 = 0.0020$ A (1). pd across thermistor $= 9.0 - 6.0 = 3.0$ V (1). $R = 3.0 \div 0.0020 = 1500$ Ω (1.5 kΩ) (1).
(b) The thermistor's resistance decreases, so the total resistance falls and the current increases; the fixed resistor takes a larger share of the pd, so the voltmeter reading increases (1).
:::

::: test
A student has two identical resistors, a power supply, an ammeter, a voltmeter and leads. Describe how she could show that connecting resistors in series increases the total resistance and connecting them in parallel decreases it. Explain the results she should get. (6)
---
Indicative content: connect one resistor to the supply with the ammeter in series and the voltmeter in parallel across the resistor; record V and I; calculate $R = V \div I$; repeat with the two resistors in series, voltmeter across both; repeat with the two in parallel, voltmeter across the parallel pair; keep the supply pd the same; switch off between readings so resistors do not heat up; repeat readings and take means. Expected: two in series give about twice the resistance of one; two in parallel give about half the resistance of one, less than either resistor. Explanation: in series the charge passes through both resistors, so there is more opposition; in parallel there are two paths, so the total current is larger for the same pd, giving a smaller total resistance.

Level 2 (3–4): a method for at least two arrangements, or results with partial explanation.
Level 1 (1–2): some correct statements about series or parallel resistance.
