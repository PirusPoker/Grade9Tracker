## What it is

When you heat something, where does the energy go? This topic answers that using **internal energy**: the energy stored by the particles of a substance. Heating either **raises the temperature** (calculated with **specific heat capacity**) or **changes the state** (calculated with **specific latent heat**), and you must be able to tell which is happening — especially on heating and cooling graphs. All of it is in **both** Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464, at Foundation and Higher.

### Internal energy

Energy is stored inside a system by the particles (atoms and molecules) that make it up. This is called **internal energy**.

> **Internal energy** is the **total kinetic energy and potential energy of all the particles** (atoms and molecules) that make up a system.

- The **kinetic energy** comes from the particles' **motion** — vibrating in a solid, moving around in a liquid or gas. The faster they move, the higher the **temperature**.
- The **potential energy** comes from the **forces between particles** and their positions. Pulling particles further apart against the attractive forces between them (as in melting or boiling) increases their potential energy.

**Heating** changes the energy stored within the system by **increasing the energy of the particles**. This **either**:

1. **raises the temperature** of the system — the particles' kinetic energy increases; **or**
2. **produces a change of state** — the particles' potential energy increases as they move apart and the forces between them are overcome, while the temperature stays the same.

Both increase the internal energy. Cooling does the reverse: the internal energy decreases.

### Temperature changes and specific heat capacity

If the temperature of a system increases, the size of the increase depends on:

- the **mass** of the substance heated (more mass, smaller rise for the same energy);
- the **type of material** — its **specific heat capacity**;
- the **energy input** to the system.

$$\Delta E = m \, c \, \Delta\theta$$

change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change

- change in thermal energy, $\Delta E$, in **joules, J**
- mass, $m$, in **kilograms, kg**
- specific heat capacity, $c$, in **joules per kilogram per degree Celsius, J/kg °C**
- temperature change, $\Delta\theta$, in **degrees Celsius, °C**

This equation is **given on the Physics equation sheet** — you need to select it and apply it, not recall it. (It also appears in the Energy topic, where **Required practical 1** — Trilogy **Required practical 14** — measures the specific heat capacity of a material using an electric heater and a joulemeter.)

> The **specific heat capacity** of a substance is the amount of energy required to raise the temperature of **one kilogram** of the substance by **one degree Celsius**.

Water has an unusually high specific heat capacity, 4200 J/kg °C: it takes 4200 J to warm 1 kg of water by 1 °C. Aluminium is about 900 J/kg °C, copper about 390 J/kg °C, ice about 2100 J/kg °C.

*Mini-example.* Energy to heat a 2.0 kg aluminium block from 20 °C to 45 °C: $\Delta\theta = 25$ °C, $\Delta E = 2.0 \times 900 \times 25 = 45\,000$ J.

*Mini-example (rearranging).* 12 600 J heats 0.30 kg of water. $\Delta\theta = \frac{\Delta E}{mc} = \frac{12\,600}{0.30 \times 4200} = 10$ °C.

### Changes of state and specific latent heat

The energy needed for a substance to change state is called **latent heat** ("latent" means hidden — it does not show up on a thermometer). When a change of state occurs, the energy supplied **changes the internal energy but not the temperature**: it increases the potential energy of the particles, not their kinetic energy.

> The **specific latent heat** of a substance is the amount of energy required to change the state of **one kilogram** of the substance **with no change in temperature**.

$$E = m \, L$$

energy for a change of state = mass × specific latent heat

- energy, $E$, in **joules, J**
- mass, $m$, in **kilograms, kg**
- specific latent heat, $L$, in **joules per kilogram, J/kg**

This equation is also **given on the Physics equation sheet**. There are two specific latent heats for each substance:

- **specific latent heat of fusion** — change of state from **solid to liquid** (melting; the same energy is released on freezing). For water, $L_f$ = 334 000 J/kg.
- **specific latent heat of vaporisation** — change of state from **liquid to vapour** (boiling; the same energy is released on condensing). For water, $L_v$ = 2 260 000 J/kg.

Vaporisation needs far more energy than fusion, because the particles must be separated completely, overcoming almost all the forces between them, whereas melting only loosens them from their fixed positions.

*Mini-example.* Energy to melt 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C: $E = 0.50 \times 334\,000 = 167\,000$ J.

*Mini-example.* Energy to boil away 0.20 kg of water at 100 °C: $E = 0.20 \times 2\,260\,000 = 452\,000$ J.

### Specific heat capacity versus specific latent heat

| | Specific heat capacity, $c$ | Specific latent heat, $L$ |
|---|---|---|
| What the energy does | raises the temperature | changes the state |
| Temperature during heating | rises | stays constant |
| Particles' energy that increases | kinetic energy | potential energy |
| Definition | energy to raise 1 kg by 1 °C | energy to change the state of 1 kg with no temperature change |
| Unit | J/kg °C | J/kg |
| Equation (sheet) | $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ | $E = mL$ |

How to read it:

- **Sloping sections**: one state is warming up. Energy increases the particles' **kinetic energy**; use $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$. A **steeper** slope means a smaller specific heat capacity (for the same mass and power) — ice ($c$ = 2100) warms about twice as fast as liquid water ($c$ = 4200).
- **Flat sections**: a change of state at the **melting point** or **boiling point**. Energy is still being supplied, and the internal energy is still rising, but it goes into the particles' **potential energy** (separating them), so the temperature does not change. Use $E = mL$.
- The **boiling plateau is longer** than the melting plateau because the specific latent heat of vaporisation is much larger than that of fusion.
- With a heater of power $P$, the energy supplied during any section is $E = Pt$, so the **length of a plateau** gives the latent heat: $L = \frac{Pt}{m}$.

A **cooling graph** is the mirror image: the temperature falls, then stays constant while the substance **condenses** or **freezes** (energy is released to the surroundings as particles move closer together and the forces pull them into place), then falls again.

*Mini-example (from a graph).* A 200 W heater warms 0.10 kg of a solid. The flat section at the melting point lasts 150 s. $E = Pt = 200 \times 150 = 30\,000$ J; $L_f = \frac{E}{m} = \frac{30\,000}{0.10} = 300\,000$ J/kg.

### Measuring the specific latent heat of fusion of water (AT 5)

The specification asks you to perform an experiment to measure the latent heat of fusion of water. It is not one of the numbered required practicals, but it can be examined.

1. Set up **two funnels** of crushed ice, each over a beaker. Put an **immersion heater** in one (the test); put an identical heater, **switched off**, in the other (the **control**).
2. Wait until water drips steadily from both, then empty the beakers and start a timer as you switch on the test heater.
3. Measure the energy supplied with a **joulemeter**, or calculate it from $E = Pt$ (or $E = VIt$ with an ammeter, voltmeter and stopwatch).
4. After a set time (say 5 minutes), switch off and measure the mass of meltwater in **each** beaker on a balance.
5. The control shows how much ice melted from the warmth of the room. Mass melted by the heater = test mass − control mass.
6. $L_f = \frac{E}{m}$.

*Typical result.* A 50 W heater runs for 300 s: $E = 15\,000$ J. Test beaker: 0.060 kg; control: 0.015 kg; so $m = 0.045$ kg. $L_f = \frac{15\,000}{0.045} = 3.3 \times 10^5$ J/kg — very close to 334 000 J/kg.

Sources of error: some of the heater's energy heats the funnel and surroundings, and some meltwater stays clinging to the ice; crush the ice finely so it surrounds the heater, and make sure the ice is at 0 °C (melting) before starting. Risks: hot heater element — do not touch it, and keep electrical connections away from the water.

::: terms
- **Internal energy**: the total kinetic energy and potential energy of all the particles (atoms and molecules) that make up a system.
- **Specific heat capacity**: the energy required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one degree Celsius, in J/kg °C.
- **Latent heat**: the energy needed for a substance to change state, which changes the internal energy but not the temperature.
- **Specific latent heat**: the energy required to change the state of one kilogram of a substance with no change in temperature, in J/kg.
- **Specific latent heat of fusion**: the specific latent heat for the change of state from solid to liquid.
- **Specific latent heat of vaporisation**: the specific latent heat for the change of state from liquid to vapour.
- **Heating curve**: a graph of temperature against time for a substance heated steadily, with flat sections where it changes state.
- **Control (in the ice experiment)**: an identical funnel of ice with the heater off, showing how much ice melts from the surroundings alone.
:::

::: method
**Which equation? Decide first.**
- Is the **temperature changing**? → $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$.
- Is the **state changing** (at the melting or boiling point)? → $E = mL$.
- Both happen (for example, ice at −10 °C to water at 20 °C)? → split into **stages**, calculate each, then **add**.
- Energy supplied by a heater? → $E = Pt$ (recall) links the heater to the stages. Both $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ and $E = mL$ are on the equation sheet; $E = Pt$ is not.

**Calculation layout**
1. Write the equation. 2. Convert: g to kg (÷ 1000), kJ to J (× 1000), minutes to seconds. 3. $\Delta\theta$ is final − initial (a temperature **change**). 4. Substitute, rearrange, answer with unit and sensible significant figures (match the data, usually 2 or 3).

**Explanations — the particle chain**
- Temperature rising: energy supplied → particles' **kinetic energy** increases → particles move/vibrate faster → temperature rises → internal energy increases.
- Change of state: energy supplied → overcomes forces / separates particles → **potential energy** increases → kinetic energy unchanged → **temperature constant** → internal energy still increases.
Avoid "the heat is used up" or "the energy disappears" — say where it goes.

**Graph questions**
Identify each section (state, or change of state); read temperatures from the flat sections (melting / boiling point); use the plateau time with $E = Pt$ to find $L$; compare slopes to compare specific heat capacities.

**Practical questions (latent heat of fusion)**
Name the measurements (energy from a joulemeter or $Pt$; mass of meltwater from a balance), explain the **control funnel** (subtract ice melted by the room), give error sources with direction (energy lost to surroundings → too little ice melted for the energy recorded → $L$ too high), and improvements.

**6-mark answers** are marked by levels: Level 3 links particle ideas to the graph or experiment in a logical sequence with correct terms (kinetic vs potential energy, internal energy, latent heat); Level 2 has correct ideas with gaps in the links; Level 1 has isolated facts.
:::

::: example Heating water
Calculate the energy needed to heat 750 g of water from 18 °C to 100 °C. Specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J/kg °C. (3)

$m = 0.750$ kg, $\Delta\theta = 82$ °C (1). $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta = 0.750 \times 4200 \times 82$ (1) $= 258\,300$ J ≈ 258 000 J (2.6 × 10⁵ J) (1).
:::

::: example Melting ice
Calculate the energy needed to melt 250 g of ice at 0 °C. Specific latent heat of fusion of water = 334 000 J/kg. (2)

$E = mL = 0.250 \times 334\,000$ (1) $= 83\,500$ J (1).
:::

::: example Three stages (grade 9)
0.50 kg of ice at −10 °C is heated until it is water at 20 °C. $c_{ice}$ = 2100 J/kg °C, $c_{water}$ = 4200 J/kg °C, $L_f$ = 334 000 J/kg. Calculate the total energy, and the time taken with a 500 W heater if no energy is wasted. (5)

Warm ice: $0.50 \times 2100 \times 10 = 10\,500$ J (1). Melt: $0.50 \times 334\,000 = 167\,000$ J (1). Warm water: $0.50 \times 4200 \times 20 = 42\,000$ J (1). Total $= 219\,500$ J (1). $t = \frac{E}{P} = \frac{219\,500}{500} = 439$ s, about 440 s (1).
:::

::: example Latent heat from a graph
A 0.20 kg sample of wax is heated by a 40 W heater. The temperature stays at 52 °C from 300 s to 1050 s. Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion of the wax and explain why the temperature stays constant. (4)

Time $= 1050 - 300 = 750$ s; $E = Pt = 40 \times 750 = 30\,000$ J (1). $L = \frac{E}{m} = \frac{30\,000}{0.20} = 150\,000$ J/kg (1). The wax is melting (1): the energy supplied increases the potential energy of the particles as they break free of their fixed positions, not their kinetic energy, so the temperature does not rise (1).
:::

::: example Internal energy during boiling
Water is boiling in a pan at 100 °C. The hob is still supplying energy. Explain what happens to the internal energy of the water and why its temperature does not rise. (3)

The internal energy increases, because energy is still being transferred to the water (1). The energy increases the potential energy of the particles as they separate and escape as steam (overcoming the forces between them) (1). The kinetic energy of the particles does not increase, so the temperature stays at 100 °C (1).
:::

A 2.0 kg block absorbs 36 000 J and its temperature rises by 20 °C. Calculate its specific heat capacity. (2)
---

::: q
On a heating graph for a pure substance, why is the section at the boiling point longer than the section at the melting point? (2)
---
The specific latent heat of vaporisation is much larger than that of fusion (1), so (at the same heating rate) more energy and therefore more time is needed to boil the substance than to melt it (1).

::: q
In the experiment to find the latent heat of fusion of ice, why is a second funnel of ice with the heater switched off used? (2)
---
It measures the mass of ice melted by energy from the surroundings (1), which is subtracted from the test mass so only the ice melted by the heater is used in the calculation (1).
:::

::: q
A liquid is cooling. Its temperature stays constant for several minutes even though it is still losing energy to the surroundings. Explain why. (2)
---
It is freezing (changing to a solid) (1); as the particles form bonds and settle into fixed positions their potential energy decreases, releasing energy that balances the energy lost, so the temperature stays constant (1).
:::

::: watch
- On a **flat section** the temperature is constant but the **internal energy is still changing**. "No energy is being transferred" or "the internal energy stays the same" is wrong.
- Use $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ only when the **temperature changes**, and $E = mL$ only for a **change of state** — multi-stage problems need both, added together.
- Mass in **kg**: 250 g is 0.250 kg. $\Delta\theta$ is the **change** in temperature, not the final temperature.
- Specific heat capacity unit is **J/kg °C**; specific latent heat is **J/kg**. Mixing these up loses the unit mark.
- Explain constant temperature with **potential energy of particles / separating particles**, not "the heat is used up".
:::
