## What it is

The **particle model** says all matter is made of tiny particles (atoms or molecules) that are always moving. It explains the three **states of matter**, why they have such different **densities**, and what happens when a substance **changes state**. This topic includes **Required practical 5: determining density** (numbered **Required practical 17** in Combined Science: Trilogy). Everything here is in **both** Physics 8463 and Trilogy 8464, at Foundation and Higher.

### The three states of matter

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | regular pattern (lattice) | random, irregular | random |
| Spacing | very close together, touching | close together, touching | far apart (about 10 times further apart than in a liquid) |
| Movement | vibrate about fixed positions | move around, sliding past each other | move quickly and randomly in all directions |
| Forces between particles | strong | weaker than in a solid | almost none |
| Properties | fixed shape and volume; cannot be compressed | fixed volume; flows and takes the shape of its container; almost incompressible | no fixed shape or volume; fills its container; easily compressed |

### Density

**Density** is the mass per unit volume of a material — how tightly the mass is packed.

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V} \qquad \text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}$$

- density, $\rho$ (the Greek letter rho), in **kilograms per metre cubed, kg/m³**
- mass, $m$, in **kilograms, kg**
- volume, $V$, in **metres cubed, m³**

This equation is **not on the equation sheet — you must recall it** and be able to apply it, including to changes of state where mass is conserved. In practicals you will often work in grams and cm³, giving density in **g/cm³**. To convert: **1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³**, because 1 g = 0.001 kg and 1 cm³ = 0.000 001 m³ ($1 \times 10^{-6}$ m³). Water has a density of 1.0 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.

| Material | Density (kg/m³) |
|---|---|
| air (room temperature) | about 1.2 |
| steam (100 °C) | about 0.6 |
| ice | 920 |
| water | 1000 |
| aluminium | 2700 |
| iron | 7900 |
| gold | 19 300 |

*Mini-example.* A 0.60 m³ block of concrete has a mass of 1440 kg. $\rho = \frac{1440}{0.60} = 2400$ kg/m³.

*Mini-example (rearranging).* What is the mass of air in a room 5.0 m × 4.0 m × 2.5 m? $V = 50$ m³, so $m = \rho V = 1.2 \times 50 = 60$ kg.

### Explaining density with the particle model

- **Solids and liquids** have **high** densities, and usually similar ones, because in both the particles are **packed closely together**, so there is a lot of mass in each cubic metre.
- A solid is usually slightly denser than the same substance as a liquid, because the particles in the regular arrangement are packed a little more tightly.
- **Gases** have **very low** densities because the particles are **far apart**: the same mass is spread through a far larger volume. When 1 kg of water boils, the steam takes up roughly 1700 times the volume of the liquid.
- **Water is an exception**: ice (920 kg/m³) is less dense than liquid water (1000 kg/m³) because the water molecules in ice lock into an open arrangement with gaps. That is why ice floats.

### Required practical 5 (Trilogy: Required practical 17): determining density

**Aim:** use appropriate apparatus to make and record the measurements needed to find the densities of regular and irregular solid objects and of liquids.

**A. Regularly shaped solid (for example a metal cuboid or cylinder)**
1. Measure the **mass** on a top-pan balance (check it reads zero first).
2. Measure the **dimensions**: a **ruler** (resolution 1 mm) for large lengths, **Vernier callipers** (0.1 mm) for a few centimetres, a **micrometer** (0.01 mm) for small thicknesses or wire diameters. Measure each dimension in several places and take a mean.
3. Calculate the **volume** from the dimensions: cuboid $V = l \times w \times h$; cylinder $V = \pi r^2 h$.
4. Calculate $\rho = m / V$.

**B. Irregularly shaped solid (for example a stone or a key) — displacement**
1. Measure the mass on the balance.
2. Fill a **displacement (eureka) can** with water until it overflows from the spout; wait until it stops dripping.
3. Place an empty **measuring cylinder** under the spout. Lower the object gently into the can on a thread.
4. The volume of water collected equals the **volume of the object**. (Alternative for small objects: part-fill a measuring cylinder, read the volume, add the object, read again; the volume of the object is the difference.)
5. Calculate $\rho = m / V$.

**C. A liquid**
1. Put an empty measuring cylinder on the balance and press **zero (tare)**.
2. Pour in a known volume of liquid, for example 50 cm³, reading the **bottom of the meniscus at eye level**.
3. Record the mass of the liquid and calculate $\rho = m / V$. Add more liquid (100 cm³, 150 cm³ …) and repeat; a graph of mass against volume is a straight line through the origin whose **gradient is the density**.

**Variables and errors**
- This practical is about **measurement**, not testing a relationship: the measured quantities are mass, dimensions and volume; control the temperature (density changes slightly with temperature) and use the same balance throughout.
- **Random errors**: reading the meniscus at the wrong angle (parallax); inconsistent drips from the spout. Reduce them by reading at eye level, repeating and calculating a mean.
- **Systematic errors**: a balance that does not read zero (zero error); water drops left in the spout or clinging to the cylinder (volume too small → density too high); **air bubbles** stuck to the object (volume too large → density too low); a porous object soaking up water.
- **Resolution**: a measuring cylinder with 1 cm³ divisions is fine for a stone but too coarse for a ring — the percentage uncertainty is large when the volume is small. Use the most precise instrument that fits the size of the object.

**Risks**: spilt water is a slip hazard — mop up immediately; heavy metal blocks can injure feet or break glassware — lower them gently on a thread rather than dropping them; handle glass measuring cylinders carefully.

**Typical results**: aluminium about 2.7 g/cm³, steel about 7.8–8.0 g/cm³, brass about 8.5 g/cm³, water 1.0 g/cm³, cooking oil about 0.92 g/cm³.

### Changes of state

| From → to | Name |
|---|---|
| solid → liquid | melting |
| liquid → solid | freezing |
| liquid → gas | boiling (throughout the liquid, at the boiling point) or evaporating (from the surface, at any temperature) |
| gas → liquid | condensing |
| solid → gas directly | sublimating (for example solid carbon dioxide, "dry ice") |

When a substance changes state, **mass is conserved**: the number of particles stays the same — they are only rearranged, moving further apart or closer together — so the total mass does not change. The **volume** usually does change, so the **density** changes.

Changes of state are **physical changes**. They differ from **chemical changes** because the material **recovers its original properties if the change is reversed**: freeze water and melt it again and you have the same water. In a chemical change (such as burning paper) new substances are formed and reversing the conditions does not bring back the original material.

*Mini-example (mass conserved).* 0.90 kg of water (density 1000 kg/m³) freezes to ice (density 920 kg/m³). The mass stays 0.90 kg. $V_{water} = \frac{0.90}{1000} = 9.0 \times 10^{-4}$ m³; $V_{ice} = \frac{0.90}{920} = 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$ m³. The volume increases by about 9%, which is why freezing water can burst pipes.

::: terms
- **Particle model**: the model in which all matter is made of tiny moving particles, used to explain states of matter and density.
- **Density**: the mass per unit volume of a material, found from mass divided by volume, in kg/m³.
- **Displacement can**: a can with a spout (eureka can) used to collect the water pushed out by an object, giving the object's volume.
- **Meniscus**: the curved surface of a liquid in a measuring cylinder; read the bottom of it at eye level.
- **Vernier callipers**: an instrument for measuring lengths of a few centimetres to a resolution of about 0.1 mm.
- **Micrometer**: an instrument for measuring small lengths, such as a wire diameter, to a resolution of about 0.01 mm.
- **Sublimation**: a change of state directly from solid to gas without becoming a liquid.
- **Physical change**: a change, such as a change of state, in which the material recovers its original properties when the change is reversed.
- **Conservation of mass**: the total mass stays the same during a change of state because the number of particles does not change.
:::

::: method
**Density calculations**
1. Write $\rho = m / V$ (recall it — it is not on the sheet).
2. Check the units match: either kg with m³ (answer in kg/m³) or g with cm³ (answer in g/cm³). Convert at the end if the question asks for kg/m³: multiply g/cm³ by 1000.
3. If the volume must be calculated first, do it as a separate line (cuboid $l \times w \times h$; cylinder $\pi r^2 h$ — use the radius, not the diameter).
4. Give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data, with the unit.
For a change of state, the **mass stays the same**: find the mass from the first state ($m = \rho V$), then the new volume from $V = m / \rho$.

**Particle-model explanations**
For any "explain the difference in density" question, compare the **spacing** of the particles: close together → more mass in each m³ → high density; far apart → low density. Then say the **mass of each particle is the same**, so only the spacing changes. Three-part descriptions of a state need arrangement, spacing and motion.

**Required practical questions**
- **Describe a method**: name the apparatus (balance, ruler/Vernier/micrometer, eureka can, measuring cylinder), the measurements, and the equation. Numbered steps help.
- **Choose an instrument**: match resolution to size — micrometer for a wire, Vernier for a coin, ruler for a book.
- **Evaluate / improve**: identify a specific error and its direction (bubbles → volume too big → density too low), then give a fix (shake off bubbles; wait for the spout to stop dripping; read the meniscus at eye level; repeat and average; use a cylinder with a finer scale).
- **Risks**: spills, dropping heavy masses, broken glass — with a control measure each.

**6-mark method answers** are marked by levels: Level 3 needs a complete, logically ordered method that would allow someone else to get a valid result (measurements, apparatus, how volume is found, the calculation, a way to improve accuracy); Level 2 has the main steps with some gaps; Level 1 has disconnected steps.
:::

::: example Regular block
A metal cuboid measures 5.0 cm × 2.0 cm × 1.0 cm and has a mass of 27 g. Calculate its density in kg/m³. (3)

$V = 5.0 \times 2.0 \times 1.0 = 10$ cm³ (1). $\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{27}{10} = 2.7$ g/cm³ (1) $= 2700$ kg/m³ (1). (In SI from the start: $\frac{0.027}{1.0 \times 10^{-5}} = 2700$ kg/m³.)
:::

::: example Irregular stone by displacement
A stone has a mass of 0.135 kg. When it is lowered into a full eureka can, 50 cm³ of water is collected. Calculate the density of the stone. (3)

$V = 50$ cm³ $= 50 \times 10^{-6} = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}$ m³ (1). $\rho = \frac{0.135}{5.0 \times 10^{-5}}$ (1) $= 2700$ kg/m³ (1).
:::

::: example Cylinder (grade 8)
A steel rod is 5.00 cm long and its diameter, measured with Vernier callipers, is 2.00 cm. Its mass is 123 g. Calculate its density in g/cm³ to 3 significant figures. (4)

$r = 1.00$ cm (1). $V = \pi r^2 h = \pi \times 1.00^2 \times 5.00 = 15.7$ cm³ (1). $\rho = \frac{123}{15.7}$ (1) $= 7.83$ g/cm³ (1).
:::

::: example Explaining density with particles
Explain why the density of steam is much lower than the density of liquid water. (3)

In both states the particles (water molecules) are the same and have the same mass (1). In liquid water the particles are close together, but in steam they are far apart (1). So a given mass of steam occupies a much larger volume, and density = mass ÷ volume is much smaller (1).
:::

::: example Improving the displacement method
A student finds the density of a small brass key by displacement, using a 100 cm³ measuring cylinder with 2 cm³ divisions. The key displaces about 3 cm³. Evaluate the method and suggest two improvements. (4)

The displaced volume is very small compared with the 2 cm³ resolution, so the percentage uncertainty in the volume (and therefore the density) is large (1). Air bubbles clinging to the key would make the volume too large and the density too small (1). Improvement: use a narrower measuring cylinder with finer divisions, e.g. a 10 cm³ cylinder with 0.2 cm³ divisions (1). Improvement: measure several identical keys together to increase the volume displaced, or repeat and average; shake off bubbles (1).
:::

::: watch
- $\rho = m / V$ is **not** on the equation sheet. Writing it upside down ($V/m$) loses every mark in the question.
- Watch the units: g with cm³ gives g/cm³; kg with m³ gives kg/m³. **1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³**, and 1 cm³ = $1 \times 10^{-6}$ m³, not $10^{-3}$.
- Use the **radius** in $\pi r^2 h$ — halve the measured diameter first.
- Explain density with **spacing of particles**, not "gas particles are lighter" — the particles are identical in every state.
- In practical evaluations give the **direction** of the error: bubbles make the volume too large and the density too small.

A sealed plastic bottle contains 0.45 kg of water (density 1000 kg/m³). It is frozen. The density of ice is 920 kg/m³. Calculate the increase in volume and explain why the mass does not change. (4)
---
$V_{water} = \frac{0.45}{1000} = 4.5 \times 10^{-4}$ m³ (1). $V_{ice} = \frac{0.45}{920} = 4.89 \times 10^{-4}$ m³ (1). Increase $= 3.9 \times 10^{-5}$ m³ (39 cm³) (1). Mass is conserved because the number of particles is unchanged; they are only rearranged (1).

::: test
A student measures the mass of a liquid for several volumes using a measuring cylinder on a balance that was zeroed with the empty cylinder on it. Results: 20 cm³, 16 g; 40 cm³, 32 g; 60 cm³, 48 g; 80 cm³, 64 g. Determine the density of the liquid in kg/m³, and explain why plotting a graph is better than using a single reading. (4)
---
Gradient of mass against volume $= \frac{64 - 16}{80 - 20} = \frac{48}{60} = 0.80$ g/cm³ (1), so density $= 800$ kg/m³ (1). A graph uses all the readings, so random errors in individual readings are averaged out (1), and a line not passing through the origin would reveal a systematic error such as a zero error (1).
:::

::: test
A stone has a mass of 64.8 g. A student puts it in a measuring cylinder containing 40.0 cm³ of water and the level rises to 64.0 cm³. Calculate the density of the stone in kg/m³ and suggest one reason why the value may be lower than the true density. (4)
---
$V = 64.0 - 40.0 = 24.0$ cm³ (1). $\rho = \frac{64.8}{24.0} = 2.70$ g/cm³ (1) $= 2700$ kg/m³ (1). Air bubbles clinging to the stone made the measured volume too large (or reading the meniscus from above), so the density is too low (1).
