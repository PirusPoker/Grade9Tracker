## What it is

This topic (AQA 4.2.1) is the core of electricity: circuit symbols, current as a flow of charge, the link between potential difference, current and resistance, and how real components behave. It contains **two required practicals** (3 and 4 — numbered 15 and 16 in Trilogy). Everything here is also in **Combined Science: Trilogy**.

### Circuit symbols

You must be able to **draw and interpret** circuit diagrams using standard symbols. Draw wires as straight lines with a ruler, with no gaps.

| Component | How the symbol looks | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Switch (open / closed) | a break in the line with a hinged line lifted / lying flat | breaks or completes the circuit |
| Cell | a long thin line and a short thick line (long = positive) | source of potential difference |
| Battery | two or more cells joined together | bigger potential difference |
| Resistor | a small rectangle | fixed resistance; limits current |
| Variable resistor | a rectangle with a diagonal arrow through it | resistance can be changed to vary the current |
| Lamp | a circle with a cross inside | gives out light |
| Fuse | a rectangle with a line running through it lengthways | melts and breaks the circuit if the current is too large |
| Diode | a triangle pointing at a bar | lets current flow in one direction only (the way the triangle points) |
| LED | a diode symbol in a circle with two arrows pointing out | diode that emits light |
| Thermistor | a resistor rectangle with a "hockey-stick" line through it | resistance falls as temperature rises |
| LDR | a resistor rectangle in a circle with two arrows pointing in | resistance falls as light intensity rises |
| Ammeter | a circle with A | measures current; connected **in series** |
| Voltmeter | a circle with V | measures potential difference; connected **in parallel** |

### Charge and current

For charge to flow around a closed circuit, the circuit must include a **source of potential difference** (a cell, battery or power supply) — and there must be no gaps.

**Electric current is a flow of electrical charge.** The size of the current is the **rate of flow of charge**. (In metal wires the charge is carried by electrons.)

| Equation in words | Symbols | Units | Recall or sheet? |
|---|---|---|---|
| charge flow = current × time | $Q = I t$ | C (coulombs), A (amperes), s | **Recall** |
| potential difference = current × resistance | $V = I R$ | V (volts), A, Ω (ohms) | **Recall** |

**The current is the same at every point in a single closed loop** — charge is not used up as it goes round. Two ammeters anywhere in a single loop read the same.

**Mini-example.** A current of 0.40 A flows for 2.0 minutes: $Q = It = 0.40 \times 120 = 48$ C. Convert minutes to seconds and milliamps to amps (25 mA = 0.025 A) first.

### Current, resistance and potential difference

The current through a component depends on **both** the potential difference across it **and** its resistance. **For a given potential difference, the greater the resistance, the smaller the current.** Questions use the term **potential difference** (pd); you get credit for "voltage" too.

$V = IR$, rearranged: $I = \dfrac{V}{R}$ and $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$.

**Mini-example.** A 12 V supply across a 48 Ω resistor: $I = 12 \div 48 = 0.25$ A. Doubling the resistance to 96 Ω halves the current to 0.125 A.

### Required practical 3: factors affecting resistance

(Required practical 15 in Trilogy.) Investigate **(a) the length of a wire at constant temperature** and **(b) combinations of resistors in series and parallel**.

**(a) Length of wire — method**
1. Tape a length of thin constantan (or nichrome) wire along a metre ruler.
2. Connect a cell/power pack, an ammeter in series, and a crocodile clip at the 0 cm end. Attach a second crocodile clip at a measured length, e.g. 10 cm. Connect a voltmeter across the wire between the clips.
3. Close the switch, read the current and pd, then **open the switch** straight away.
4. Repeat for lengths 20, 30 … 100 cm. Calculate $R = V \div I$ for each length. Repeat the set and calculate means.
5. Plot resistance (y) against length (x).

**Variables.** Independent: length of wire. Dependent: resistance (calculated from V and I). Control: material and **thickness (diameter)** of the wire, **temperature** of the wire, the supply pd.

**Typical results.** A straight line — **resistance is directly proportional to length** (doubling the length doubles the resistance), because electrons collide with more ions in a longer wire. If the line does not pass through the origin, there is a **systematic error**, often because the crocodile clips are not exactly at 0 cm or there is contact resistance; the gradient is still valid.

**Keeping temperature constant.** A current heats the wire and **raises its resistance**. Use a low pd, and switch off between readings, so the temperature stays constant.

**Risks.** The wire can get hot enough to burn — keep the current low and do not touch the wire while current is flowing.

**(b) Resistors in series and parallel — method.** Using two identical resistors, measure V and I and calculate R for: one resistor; two in series; two in parallel. **Results:** two in series give double the resistance of one; two in parallel give half the resistance of one — less than either resistor alone. (See the series and parallel lesson for why.)

### Resistors: ohmic and non-ohmic components

For some resistors **R stays constant** as the current changes; for others **R changes** as the current changes.

**Ohmic conductor** (e.g. a fixed resistor or a wire **at constant temperature**): the current is **directly proportional** to the potential difference, so the resistance is constant. Its I–V graph is a straight line through the origin.

**Non-ohmic components** — their resistance is **not constant**; it changes with the current through them:

- **Filament lamp.** As the current increases the filament gets hotter. **The resistance of a filament lamp increases as the temperature of the filament increases** (the metal ions vibrate more, so electrons collide with them more often). I–V graph: an S-shaped curve that gets **flatter** at higher pd.
- **Diode.** **Current flows in one direction only.** In the reverse direction the diode has a **very high resistance**, so almost no current flows. In the forward direction, current starts to flow above about 0.6 V and then rises steeply.
- **Thermistor.** **Resistance decreases as temperature increases.** Application: **thermostats** and temperature sensors — e.g. in a fire alarm or heating control, a rise in temperature lowers the thermistor's resistance, increasing the current, which switches a circuit on or off.
- **LDR (light-dependent resistor).** **Resistance decreases as light intensity increases.** Application: **switching lights on when it gets dark** — e.g. street lights; in the dark the LDR's resistance is high, which a control circuit uses to turn the lamp on.

You can investigate a thermistor by putting it in a beaker of water at different temperatures (measured with a thermometer) and finding R from V and I (or with an ohmmeter); and an LDR by moving a lamp to different distances (or using a light meter).

### Required practical 4: I–V characteristics

(Required practical 16 in Trilogy.) Investigate the I–V characteristics of a **resistor at constant temperature**, a **filament lamp** and a **diode**.

**Method**
1. Set up the circuit above with the component in place: a power supply, an ammeter in series, a voltmeter in parallel across the component, and a variable resistor (or use a variable power supply) to change the pd.
2. For the diode, include a **protective resistor** in series so the current cannot get large enough to damage the diode.
3. Adjust the variable resistor in steps; record the current and pd each time (about 10 readings, e.g. 0 to 6 V).
4. **Reverse the connections to the supply** (or the component) and repeat to get negative values of V and I.
5. Plot **current (y-axis) against potential difference (x-axis)** through all four quadrants.

**Typical results**

| Component | I–V graph | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Resistor (constant temperature) | straight line through the origin, same in both directions | I ∝ V; resistance constant (ohmic) |
| Filament lamp | S-shaped curve through the origin, symmetrical; gradient decreases as pd increases | resistance increases as the filament heats up |
| Diode | zero current for all negative pd; tiny current until about +0.6 V, then current rises steeply | current flows one way only; very high resistance in reverse |

**Reading resistance from an I–V graph.** Read V and I at the point and use $R = V \div I$. For the lamp: if $I = 0.15$ A at 2.0 V, $R = 13$ Ω; if $I = 0.30$ A at 6.0 V, $R = 20$ Ω — the resistance has increased. A graph is **linear** if it is a straight line (constant R) and **non-linear** if it curves (R changes).

**Errors.** Switch off between readings so the resistor does not heat up; avoid zero errors on meters; take repeats to spot anomalies. **Risks:** the lamp gets hot; keep currents within the components' ratings.

::: terms
- **Electric current**: a flow of electrical charge; its size is the rate of flow of charge, in amperes.
- **Charge**: the property carried by electrons that flows in a circuit, measured in coulombs.
- **Potential difference**: what drives charge round a circuit, measured in volts across a component with a voltmeter.
- **Resistance**: opposition to current; the greater it is, the smaller the current for a given potential difference.
- **Ohmic conductor**: a conductor in which current is directly proportional to potential difference at constant temperature.
- **Diode**: a component that lets current flow in one direction only, with very high resistance in reverse.
- **Thermistor**: a resistor whose resistance decreases as its temperature increases.
- **Light-dependent resistor**: a resistor whose resistance decreases as the light intensity on it increases.
- **I–V characteristic**: a graph of current against potential difference showing how a component behaves.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** an I–V graph: say what happens to the current as the pd increases, and whether the line is straight (linear) or curved — "for the lamp the current increases, but by less and less, so the graph curves and flattens".
- **Explain** a graph: link the shape to resistance and its cause — "the gradient decreases because the resistance increases, because the filament gets hotter and the ions vibrate more".
- **Draw a circuit**: ruled straight lines, correct symbols, no gaps, ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel across the component only.
- **Suggest** a use: thermistor → thermostat or temperature sensor; LDR → light that turns on in the dark.

**Calculations**
1. Write the equation ($Q = It$ or $V = IR$; both must be recalled).
2. Convert units: mA → A (÷ 1000), kΩ → Ω (× 1000), minutes → s (× 60).
3. Rearrange, substitute, and give the answer with units (C, A, V or Ω).
4. Two-step problems are common: use $Q = It$ to find $I$, then $V = IR$.
5. For resistance from a curve, use $R = V \div I$ at the point — read both values from the graph.

**Required practical questions (RP3 and RP4)**
- Variables: independent (length / pd), dependent (resistance / current), control (temperature, wire thickness and material).
- Why switch off between readings: stops the wire/resistor heating up, which would increase its resistance.
- Why the RP3 line misses the origin: systematic error (clip not at 0 cm, contact resistance) — the gradient is still correct.
- Why the diode needs a protective resistor: to limit the current so the diode is not damaged.
- How to get negative values: reverse the supply connections.

**6-mark answers (levels of response)**
Level 3 (5–6): a complete method with a correct circuit (named meters in the right places), how the pd is varied, readings taken, how the data are processed (graph, $R = V \div I$), plus a control or safety point. Level 2 (3–4): workable but with gaps (e.g. no mention of varying the pd or of the graph). Level 1 (1–2): a few relevant points or apparatus only.
:::

::: example Charge and current
A current of 250 mA flows through a lamp for 4.0 minutes. Calculate the charge that flows. (3)

$I = 250 \div 1000 = 0.25$ A and $t = 4.0 \times 60 = 240$ s (1). $Q = It = 0.25 \times 240$ (1) $= 60$ C (1).
:::

::: example Two-step problem
A charge of 360 C flows through a 20 Ω resistor in 1.5 minutes. Calculate the potential difference across the resistor. (4)

$t = 90$ s (1). $I = Q \div t = 360 \div 90 = 4.0$ A (1). $V = IR = 4.0 \times 20$ (1) $= 80$ V (1).
:::

::: example Resistance from a lamp's I–V graph
A filament lamp's I–V graph passes through (2.0 V, 0.15 A) and (6.0 V, 0.30 A). Calculate the resistance at each point and explain the change. (4)

At 2.0 V: $R = 2.0 \div 0.15 = 13$ Ω (1). At 6.0 V: $R = 6.0 \div 0.30 = 20$ Ω (1). The resistance increases because the larger current heats the filament (1); hotter metal ions vibrate more, so electrons collide with them more often (1).
:::

::: example Required practical 3 analysis
A student's results for resistance against length of wire are: 20 cm, 1.1 Ω; 40 cm, 2.1 Ω; 60 cm, 3.1 Ω; 80 cm, 4.1 Ω; 100 cm, 5.1 Ω. (a) Describe the relationship. (b) The line of best fit crosses the resistance axis at 0.1 Ω. Explain this. (c) Calculate the resistance per metre. (4)

(a) A straight line: resistance increases linearly with length — every extra 20 cm adds 1.0 Ω (1). (b) A systematic error, e.g. the crocodile clip was not exactly at 0 cm, or contact resistance at the clips (1). (c) Gradient $= (5.1 - 1.1) \div (1.00 - 0.20)$ (1) $= 5.0$ Ω/m (1).
:::

::: example Thermistor application
Explain how a thermistor can be used to switch on a fan in a greenhouse when it gets too hot. (3)

As the temperature rises, the resistance of the thermistor decreases (1). So the current in the thermistor's circuit increases (for the same pd) (1). When the current is large enough it operates a switch (e.g. a relay) that turns the fan on (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Meters in the wrong place**: ammeter in **series**, voltmeter in **parallel** across the component only — not across the whole circuit.
- **Unit conversions**: mA to A (÷ 1000) and minutes to seconds before using $Q = It$ or $V = IR$.
- **Resistance from a curve**: use $R = V \div I$ at the point, not the gradient of the curve.
- **Lamp explanation**: "the resistance increases" is only half the answer — say **because the filament gets hotter**.
- **Thermistor and LDR directions**: resistance goes **down** as temperature or light goes **up**. Students often reverse one of them.
- **"Current is used up"**: it is not — current is the same all the way round a single loop.
:::

::: test
In Required practical 3 a student finds that a wire has a resistance of 5.0 Ω per metre. (a) Predict the resistance of 1.5 m of the same wire. (b) Calculate the current when a 3.0 V pd is applied across this 1.5 m length. (c) Explain why a longer wire has a greater resistance. (5)
