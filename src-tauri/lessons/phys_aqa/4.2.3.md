## What it is

This topic is about the electricity that comes out of the wall socket: what kind of supply it is, how the three wires in a mains cable are coloured and what each one does, and why the live wire can kill you even when the appliance is switched off. It is in **both** Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464, at Foundation and Higher, and there is no Higher-tier-only content in it.

### Direct and alternating potential difference

A **direct potential difference (dc)** always pushes charge the **same way** round a circuit. Its value stays constant (or nearly so). Cells, batteries and solar cells give a dc supply, so the current flows in **one direction only**.

An **alternating potential difference (ac)** keeps **reversing direction**. The end that was positive becomes negative, then positive again, many times a second, so the current it drives flows backwards and forwards.

| | Direct (dc) | Alternating (ac) |
|---|---|---|
| Direction of current | one direction only | reverses direction repeatedly |
| Value of the pd | constant | continually changing, positive then negative |
| Typical source | cell, battery, solar cell | mains supply, generator (alternator) |
| Trace on an oscilloscope | a horizontal straight line above (or below) zero | a wave above and below zero |

**Mains electricity is an ac supply.** In the United Kingdom the domestic supply has a **frequency of 50 Hz** and a potential difference of **about 230 V**. Both of these numbers must be learned — they are not given in the exam.

**Frequency** is the number of complete cycles every second. 50 Hz means the pd goes through 50 complete cycles (forwards, back, forwards again) in one second, so the current changes direction 100 times a second. The time for one cycle (the period) is $T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{50} = 0.02$ s. (The period equation belongs to the waves topic, where you must recall it; it is useful for reading oscilloscope traces here.)

On an oscilloscope screen, a dc supply is a flat line at a fixed height; the height above the zero line is the pd. An ac supply is a wave: the **peak** is the highest point, and the horizontal distance for one full wave is the **period**. The "230 V" quoted for the mains is an effective value — the peaks of the mains wave actually reach about 325 V — but AQA only asks you to know "about 230 V".

### Mains electricity: three-core cable

Most electrical appliances are connected to the mains using **three-core cable**: three copper wires, each covered in coloured plastic **insulation**, all inside an outer plastic sheath. The colours are standard so that anyone can identify each wire.

| Wire | Colour of insulation | Potential (relative to earth) | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Live** | **brown** | alternates; about **230 V** | carries the alternating pd from the supply |
| **Neutral** | **blue** | at, or close to, **0 V** | completes the circuit |
| **Earth** | **green and yellow stripes** | **0 V** | safety wire to stop the appliance becoming live; it only carries a current if there is a fault |

Key facts to say in answers:

- The pd between the **live wire and earth** is about **230 V**.
- The **neutral** wire is at, or close to, earth potential (0 V). Current flows in along the live wire, through the appliance and back along the neutral wire.
- The **earth** wire is at 0 V and **normally carries no current**. It is connected to the metal case of an appliance. If a fault makes the live wire touch the metal case, a large current flows through the low-resistance earth wire to the ground instead of through a person who touches the case. That large current also melts the fuse or trips the circuit breaker in the live wire, cutting off the supply. (Fuses and circuit breakers are useful context; the AQA statement you must know is that the earth wire is a safety wire that stops the appliance becoming live.)
- Appliances with a plastic case (double insulated) often use **two-core cable** with no earth wire, because the case cannot become live.

### Why the live wire is dangerous even when a switch is open

Your body is roughly at earth potential, **0 V**. The live wire is at about **230 V**. If you touch the live wire, there is a **potential difference of about 230 V across your body**, so a current flows **through you to earth**. A current of only a few tens of milliamps through the heart can be fatal.

An open switch only stops current flowing **round the circuit**. It does **not** change the potential of the live wire on the supply side of the switch: that part is still at 230 V. So:

- a live wire may be dangerous **even when a switch in the mains circuit is open** — for example, a lamp holder or a damaged cable can still be at 230 V when the light switch is off, which is why you turn off the mains at the consumer unit (fuse box) before changing a fitting;
- if a switch were wrongly placed in the **neutral** wire, opening it would stop the appliance working but leave the whole appliance **still connected to 230 V** — this is why switches and fuses always go in the **live** wire.

### The danger of connecting the live wire to earth

Any connection between the live wire and earth gives a path with a pd of 230 V across it. If the path has a **low resistance**, a **very large current** flows:

- through a **person**, it causes an **electric shock**, which can stop the heart or cause serious burns;
- through a wire or tool (for example a nail driven through a cable, a damaged flex, or water getting into a socket), the large current causes **rapid heating**, which can melt insulation and start a **fire**; this is a short circuit to earth.

That is exactly why a fault in the earth wire system is dangerous, and why the earth wire is designed as a deliberate, very low-resistance path that makes the fuse or circuit breaker act at once.

### Mini-examples

- *A battery-powered torch and a table lamp are both switched on. Which uses dc?* The torch: a battery gives a direct pd, so the current flows one way only.
- *An oscilloscope shows a wave with one complete cycle every 0.02 s. What is the frequency?* $f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{0.02} = 50$ Hz — the UK mains frequency.
- *Why is the earth wire's insulation green and yellow striped rather than one colour?* The stripes make it easy to identify and hard to confuse with the live (brown) or neutral (blue) wire — colour coding is all about easy identification.

::: terms
- **Direct potential difference (dc)**: a pd that always acts in the same direction, so the current flows one way only, as from a cell or battery.
- **Alternating potential difference (ac)**: a pd that repeatedly reverses direction, so the current it drives flows backwards and forwards.
- **Mains electricity**: the UK domestic ac supply, with a frequency of 50 Hz and a potential difference of about 230 V.
- **Frequency**: the number of complete cycles of an alternating supply each second, measured in hertz (Hz).
- **Three-core cable**: a cable containing live, neutral and earth wires, each in colour-coded insulation inside an outer sheath.
- **Live wire**: the brown wire that carries the alternating pd from the supply, at about 230 V relative to earth.
- **Neutral wire**: the blue wire that completes the circuit; it is at, or close to, earth potential (0 V).
- **Earth wire**: the green and yellow striped safety wire at 0 V that stops an appliance becoming live and only carries a current if there is a fault.
:::

::: method
**Command words in this topic**
- **State / give**: one fact, no explanation — "brown", "50 Hz", "about 230 V".
- **Describe**: say what happens — e.g. describe the trace of an ac supply on an oscilloscope (a wave that goes above and below the zero line, repeating regularly).
- **Explain**: give the physics reason using **because / so**. Every safety explanation in this topic follows one chain: *the live wire is at 230 V → you are at 0 V → there is a large pd across you → a current flows through you to earth → electric shock*.
- **Compare**: make a point about **both** things in each sentence — "dc flows in one direction only, **whereas** ac repeatedly reverses direction".

**Answering a dc/ac question (2–3 marks)**
1. Name the direction behaviour of each (one way vs reverses).
2. Name what happens to the size of the pd (constant vs continually changing).
3. If asked about the mains, add **50 Hz** and **about 230 V**.

**4-mark and 6-mark safety answers (levels of response)**
Examiners mark these by level, not by counting words. To reach the top level you need a **logical chain** with physics linking each step. Indicative content they look for:
- live wire at about 230 V; earth and the person at 0 V;
- touching live creates a pd across the body, so current flows through the body to earth;
- an open switch stops current in the circuit but the live wire (before the switch) is still at 230 V;
- the earth wire connects the metal case to earth, so a fault current flows through the earth wire, not through a person;
- a large current in the live wire melts the fuse / trips the circuit breaker, disconnecting the live wire;
- a low-resistance connection between live and earth allows a very large current, causing heating and a fire risk.
Link each point to the next ("…so…", "…which means…"). A list of disconnected facts usually stays in the middle level.

**Calculations**
There is little maths here, but you may read an oscilloscope trace: count squares × the scale to find the peak pd or the time for one cycle, then use $f = 1/T$. Show the equation, the substitution, and give the answer with its unit (Hz or V).

**Precise vocabulary**
Use "potential difference" (or "pd"), not "power" or "electricity", when you mean volts. Say "current flows through the person to earth", not "electricity goes into you".
:::

::: example Comparing dc and ac
Explain the difference between a direct potential difference and an alternating potential difference. (2)

A direct pd always acts in the same direction, so the current it produces flows in one direction only (1). An alternating pd repeatedly reverses its direction, so the current it produces keeps changing direction, flowing backwards and forwards (1).
:::

::: example Switched off but still dangerous
A student turns off the wall switch before replacing a broken light fitting. Explain why this is still dangerous. (3)

The switch only breaks the circuit, so it stops the current flowing round the circuit (1). The live wire on the supply side of the switch, and possibly the fitting itself, is still at about 230 V (1). If the student touches it, there is a pd of about 230 V between the wire and the student (who is at 0 V), so a current would flow through the student to earth, giving an electric shock (1). The mains should be switched off at the consumer unit first.
:::

::: example Reading an oscilloscope trace
An oscilloscope is set so that each horizontal square is 5 ms. One complete wave of an ac supply covers 4 squares. Calculate the frequency and state whether this could be the UK mains. (3)

Period $T = 4 \times 5 = 20$ ms $= 0.020$ s (1). $f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{0.020} = 50$ Hz (1). Yes — the UK mains frequency is 50 Hz (1).
:::

::: example Fault in a metal kettle
A loose live wire inside a metal kettle touches the metal case. Explain how the earth wire protects the user. (4)

The earth wire connects the metal case to earth at 0 V (1). When the live wire touches the case, a large current flows from the live wire through the case and the low-resistance earth wire to earth (1), rather than through the user, so the case does not stay at a dangerous pd (1). The large current also melts the fuse (or trips the circuit breaker) in the live wire, which disconnects the kettle from the supply (1).
:::

Explain why touching the live wire can cause an electric shock. (2)

::: q
Explain why switches are placed in the live wire rather than the neutral wire. (2)
---
With the switch in the live wire, opening it disconnects the appliance from the 230 V supply (1). In the neutral wire, the appliance would stop working but would still be at 230 V, so touching it could cause a shock (1).

::: q
A nail is hammered into a wall and goes through a mains cable, touching both the live wire and the earth wire. Explain the danger. (2)
---
A low-resistance connection between live and earth lets a very large current flow (1), which heats the wires rapidly and could cause a fire, and anyone touching the nail could get a shock (1).
:::

::: q
How many times per second does the current from the UK mains change direction? (1)
---
100 times (two reversals in each of the 50 cycles per second).

::: watch
- Mains is **ac at 50 Hz and about 230 V**. Writing 50 V, 230 Hz or "dc" throws away easy marks.
- **Brown = live, blue = neutral, green and yellow = earth.** The earth wire is **0 V and normally carries no current** — saying it "carries current away all the time" is wrong.
- "The switch is off so there is no danger" is the trap: an open switch stops current in the circuit but the **live wire is still at 230 V**.
- Explain shocks with **potential difference across the body → current through the body to earth**, not "the electricity goes into you".
- In a compare question about dc and ac, say something about **both** in each point.
:::

Explain why a person who touches the live wire of a mains circuit can receive a shock even when the switch in that circuit is open. (3)
---

::: test
A builder accidentally makes a direct, low-resistance connection between the live wire and earth. Explain two dangers of this. (4)
---
A pd of about 230 V acts across a very low resistance, so a very large current flows (1). The large current heats the wires strongly (1), which could melt insulation and start a fire (1). If the connection is through a person or a tool they are holding, current flows through the body to earth, giving a possibly fatal shock (1).
:::

::: test
Some appliances have a metal case and three-core cable. Explain in detail how the earth wire and the fuse together make such an appliance safe if a fault makes the live wire touch the metal case. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6 marks): a detailed, coherent explanation that links the fault, the low-resistance earth path, the large current and the fuse disconnecting the live wire, with the reason the user is protected.
Level 2 (3–4 marks): a clear explanation of the role of the earth wire or of the fuse, with some linking, but the full sequence is incomplete.
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple relevant statements, such as "the earth wire is a safety wire" or "the fuse melts", with little or no linking.
0 marks: no relevant content.
Indicative content:
- the live wire is at about 230 V; the earth wire is at 0 V and joined to the metal case;
- normally no current flows in the earth wire;
- when the live wire touches the case, the case would become live (230 V) without an earth;
- with the earth wire, a large current flows from live through the case and the low-resistance earth wire to earth;
- current takes the low-resistance earth path rather than through a person touching the case;
- the large current melts the fuse (or trips a circuit breaker) in the live wire;
- this disconnects the live wire, so the appliance is no longer connected to 230 V;
- a person touching the case is therefore not exposed to a large pd and does not get a shock;
- the fuse also stops wires overheating, reducing the fire risk.
:::

::: test
A hairdryer with a plastic case is connected using two-core cable (live and neutral only). Suggest why an earth wire is not needed, and explain why the live wire inside it is still a hazard if water gets into the hairdryer. (3)
---
The plastic case is an insulator, so it cannot become live even if a wire touches it, so no earth is needed (1). Water (especially impure water) conducts, so it could make a path from the 230 V live wire to the user (1); a pd across the user's body would make a current flow through them to earth, giving a shock (1).
:::
