## What it is

A gas pushes on the walls of its container because its molecules keep hitting them. This topic uses that one idea to explain why gas pressure rises when a gas gets **hotter**, why it rises when a gas is **squashed into a smaller volume**, and why **pumping** a gas warms it up. Part 4.3.3.1 (particle motion in gases) is in **both** Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (where it is 6.3.3.1). Parts 4.3.3.2 and 4.3.3.3 are **physics only**, and 4.3.3.3 is also **Higher tier only** — they are flagged below. There is no required practical in this subsection.

### Particle motion in gases

The particle model of a gas:

- The molecules are **far apart** compared with their size, and there are almost no forces between them except when they collide.
- The molecules are in **constant random motion**: they move in straight lines at many different speeds, in all directions, until they hit another molecule or a wall.
- The **temperature** of the gas is related to the **average kinetic energy** of its molecules. Higher temperature = faster molecules on average = greater average kinetic energy.

"Random" matters: at any moment some molecules are fast and some slow, so we talk about the **average** kinetic energy, not the energy of one molecule.

### Where gas pressure comes from

When a molecule hits a wall of the container it bounces off. Its direction (and so its velocity) changes, so the wall must exert a force on the molecule — and, by Newton's third law, **the molecule exerts an equal and opposite force on the wall**.

Billions of molecules hit every square centimetre of wall every second. The total force they exert on each unit of area is the **gas pressure**.

> **Gas pressure** is caused by gas molecules colliding with the walls of the container. The pressure depends on **how often** the molecules hit the walls and **how hard** (with how much force) each collision is.

Because the molecules move randomly in all directions, the gas pushes equally on every wall, and the overall (net) force on any surface acts **at right angles (perpendicular) to the surface**.

### Temperature and pressure at constant volume

Think of a sealed, rigid container (fixed volume) — for example a gas cylinder or a sealed can. If the gas is heated:

1. the molecules gain kinetic energy, so their **average speed increases**;
2. they hit the walls **more often** (more collisions per second), because they cross the container faster;
3. each collision is **harder** — it exerts a **greater force** on the wall, because the molecules are moving faster;
4. so the total force on each unit area of wall increases: **the pressure increases**.

Cooling the gas does the reverse: slower molecules, fewer and gentler collisions, lower pressure.

| Change at constant volume | Molecules' average kinetic energy | Collisions with walls | Pressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas heated | increases (faster) | more frequent and harder | increases |
| Gas cooled | decreases (slower) | less frequent and gentler | decreases |

This is why aerosol cans warn "do not expose to temperatures above 50 °C" (the pressure could burst the can), and why car tyre pressures rise after a long drive. AQA only asks you to explain this relationship **qualitatively** — no calculation linking pressure and temperature is required.

### Separate science only: Pressure in gases and volume

A gas can be **compressed** (squashed into a smaller volume) or allowed to **expand** by changing the pressure on it — for example by pushing or pulling the plunger of a sealed syringe.

**Explaining it with particles.** Suppose a fixed mass of gas is held at **constant temperature** and its volume is **increased**:

1. the temperature is constant, so the molecules' **average kinetic energy and average speed do not change** — each collision is just as hard as before;
2. the same number of molecules is spread through a **larger volume**, so they travel further between collisions with the walls, and there is a larger wall area;
3. so there are **fewer collisions per second on each unit area of wall**;
4. the total force per unit area is smaller: **the pressure decreases**.

Decreasing the volume does the opposite: more frequent collisions with each unit area of wall, so the pressure increases.

**The equation.** For a **fixed mass of gas held at a constant temperature**:

$$pV = \text{constant}$$

pressure × volume = constant

- pressure, $p$, in **pascals, Pa**
- volume, $V$, in **metres cubed, m³**

This equation is **given on the Physics equation sheet** — you must be able to select it and apply it, not recall it. Because $pV$ stays the same, comparing a "before" state (1) and an "after" state (2):

$$p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2$$

Pressure and volume are **inversely proportional**: halve the volume and the pressure doubles; treble the volume and the pressure falls to one third. A graph of $p$ against $V$ is a curve; a graph of $p$ against $\frac{1}{V}$ is a **straight line through the origin**.

As long as you use the **same unit on both sides**, the before-and-after form also works with kPa, cm³ or litres, because the units cancel. If a question asks for the answer in Pa and m³, convert: 1 kPa = 1000 Pa; 1 cm³ = $1 \times 10^{-6}$ m³.

*Mini-example.* A sealed syringe holds 60 cm³ of air at 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in slowly (so the temperature stays constant) until the volume is 20 cm³. $p_2 = \frac{p_1 V_1}{V_2} = \frac{100 \times 60}{20} = 300$ kPa. The volume is one third, so the pressure is three times as big.

*Mini-example (finding volume).* A weather balloon holds 4.0 m³ of helium at $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa. Higher up, the pressure is $2.5 \times 10^4$ Pa. Assuming constant temperature, $V_2 = \frac{p_1 V_1}{p_2} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^5 \times 4.0}{2.5 \times 10^4} = 16$ m³.

*Why "slowly"?* If a gas is compressed quickly, it warms up (see below), so the temperature is not constant and $pV = \text{constant}$ does not apply exactly. Slow compression gives the gas time to lose that energy to the surroundings.

### Separate science only: Increasing the pressure of a gas (Higher tier)

**Work** is the transfer of energy by a force (work done = force × distance moved along the line of action of the force, $W = Fs$ — this one you must recall from 4.5.2).

When you **compress** a gas, you push a piston (or plunger) inwards with a force through a distance, so you **do work on the gas**. Energy is transferred to the gas:

- doing work on a gas **increases its internal energy**;
- this can cause an **increase in the temperature** of the gas.

**The bicycle pump.** Push the handle in quickly a few times and the barrel near the outlet becomes warm. The explanation:

1. you apply a force to the piston and it moves through a distance, so **work is done on the air** inside the pump;
2. molecules that hit the **inward-moving piston** bounce off it **faster** than they arrived (just as a tennis ball speeds up off a moving racket);
3. so the molecules' **kinetic energy increases** — the **internal energy** of the air increases;
4. the average kinetic energy of the molecules has increased, so the **temperature of the air rises** (and the warm air heats the barrel).

The reverse also happens: when a gas **expands** and pushes a piston (or the atmosphere) outwards, the gas does work, its internal energy falls and it **cools**. That is why the nozzle of an aerosol can feels cold after a long spray.

Do not confuse this with heating: here the temperature rises **without any heating** — the energy is transferred **mechanically, by a force doing work**.

::: terms
- **Random motion**: movement of gas molecules in straight lines, at many different speeds and in all directions, changing only when they collide.
- **Temperature (of a gas)**: a quantity related to the average kinetic energy of the gas molecules; hotter gas has faster molecules on average.
- **Gas pressure**: the force per unit area on a container wall caused by gas molecules colliding with it.
- **Constant volume**: the gas is in a sealed, rigid container, so the space it fills cannot change.
- **Fixed mass of gas**: a sealed sample in which no gas enters or leaves, so the number of molecules stays the same.
- **Compression**: squashing a gas into a smaller volume, which raises its pressure if the temperature is constant.
- **Work done**: the energy transferred when a force moves an object through a distance; doing work on a gas raises its internal energy.
- **Internal energy**: the total kinetic and potential energy of all the particles that make up a system.
:::

::: method
**Particle explanations — the chain examiners look for**
Every pressure explanation needs three links: what happens to the **molecules' speed / kinetic energy** → what happens to the **collisions with the walls** (how often, and how hard) → what happens to the **force per unit area**, so the **pressure**. Missing the middle link is the commonest way to drop a mark.
- **Temperature up, volume fixed**: faster molecules → **more frequent and harder** collisions → greater force on the walls → pressure up.
- **Volume up, temperature fixed** (physics only): same speed (so collisions are **not** harder) → **less frequent** collisions with each unit area of wall → pressure down.
- **Compressing quickly** (physics only, Higher tier): work done on the gas → internal energy up → molecules faster → temperature up.

**Command words**
- **Describe**: say what happens (the pressure increases). **Explain**: say *why*, using molecules, collisions and force.
- **Suggest**: apply the model to an unfamiliar situation (a tyre, a balloon, a diver's air bubble).
- **Calculate**: use $pV = \text{constant}$ — physics only.

**Calculations ($pV = \text{constant}$, on the equation sheet)**
1. Check the conditions: **fixed mass** and **constant temperature**. If the question says this (or "slowly"), the equation applies.
2. Write $p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2$.
3. Keep units consistent on both sides (both kPa, or both Pa; both cm³, or both m³). Convert only if the answer must be in a particular unit.
4. Rearrange, substitute, give the unit and a sensible number of significant figures (match the data, usually 2 or 3).
5. **Sense check**: smaller volume → bigger pressure, and vice versa. If your answer goes the wrong way you have divided the wrong way round.

**Extended responses (4 and 6 marks)** are marked by levels. Top level answers give a logical sequence — molecules → collisions → force → pressure — using correct words (kinetic energy, average, collisions per second, force per unit area). Use the word "molecules" or "particles", never "atoms getting bigger" or "the gas expanding" as a reason for pressure.

**Data and graph questions**: $p$ against $V$ is a curve (inverse proportion); check it by multiplying $p \times V$ for several points — the products should be equal. $p$ against $\frac{1}{V}$ is a straight line through the origin.
:::

::: example Heated gas cylinder
A sealed metal gas cylinder is left in hot sunshine. Explain, in terms of molecules, why the pressure of the gas increases. (4)

The temperature of the gas increases, so the molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster on average (1). They collide with the walls of the cylinder more often / more frequently (1). Each collision exerts a greater force on the wall (1). So the total force per unit area — the pressure — increases (the volume is fixed) (1).
:::

::: example Syringe calculation (physics only)
A sealed syringe contains 45 cm³ of air at a pressure of $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa. The plunger is pushed in slowly until the pressure is $1.5 \times 10^5$ Pa. The temperature does not change. Calculate the new volume. (3)

$p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2$ (1). $V_2 = \frac{p_1 V_1}{p_2} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^5 \times 45}{1.5 \times 10^5}$ (1) $= 30$ cm³ (1). Check: pressure up, volume down — sensible.
:::

::: example Units and standard form (physics only)
A diver breathes out a 6.0 cm³ bubble of air at a depth where the pressure is 300 kPa. Calculate the volume of the bubble just below the surface, where the pressure is 100 kPa. Give the answer in m³. Assume the temperature is constant. (3)

$V_2 = \frac{p_1 V_1}{p_2} = \frac{300 \times 6.0}{100}$ (1) $= 18$ cm³ (1). $18 \text{ cm}^3 = 18 \times 10^{-6}$ m³ $= 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ m³ (1).
:::

::: example Bicycle pump (physics only, Higher tier)
When a bicycle tyre is pumped up quickly, the pump becomes warm. Explain why. (4)

A force is applied to the piston and it moves through a distance, so work is done on the air in the pump (1). This transfers energy to the air, increasing its internal energy (1). Molecules hitting the moving piston rebound faster, so their average kinetic energy increases (1). The temperature of the air rises, and the warm air heats the pump (1).
:::

::: example Checking a data table (physics only)
A student compresses a gas slowly and records: $V$ = 40 cm³, $p$ = 100 kPa; $V$ = 25 cm³, $p$ = 160 kPa; $V$ = 20 cm³, $p$ = 200 kPa. Show that the data support $pV = \text{constant}$. (2)

$40 \times 100 = 4000$; $25 \times 160 = 4000$; $20 \times 200 = 4000$ (1). The product $pV$ is the same (4000 kPa cm³) for every reading, so $pV$ is constant (1).
:::

::: watch
- Pressure explanations need **both** "more often" **and** "harder / more force" for a temperature rise — but **only "more often" (less often)** for a volume change at constant temperature, because the molecules' speed has not changed. Saying "harder collisions" when only the volume changed is wrong.
- Say **"collisions with the walls"**, not "collisions with each other" — molecule–molecule collisions do not cause pressure on the container.
- Molecules do **not** get bigger, expand or "get excited" when heated. They move faster.
- $pV = \text{constant}$ only applies to a **fixed mass at constant temperature**. If the gas is compressed quickly it warms up, so the equation does not hold exactly.
- After a $p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2$ calculation, **sense check**: smaller volume must mean bigger pressure.
- Compressing a gas raises its temperature by **doing work** on it — not by "friction" or "heating".
:::

::: test
(Physics only) A weather balloon is released. As it rises, the atmospheric pressure around it falls and the balloon gets bigger. Explain why, assuming the temperature of the gas stays constant. (3)
---
The pressure outside the balloon decreases, so the gas inside can push the balloon wall outwards / expand (1). As the volume increases, the molecules (moving at the same average speed) hit each unit area of the balloon wall less often (1), so the pressure inside falls until it matches the pressure outside; $pV$ stays constant, so lower pressure means larger volume (1).

::: test
(Physics only) Air in a cylinder is trapped by a piston of area 0.0030 m². Initially the volume is 600 cm³ and the pressure is $2.0 \times 10^5$ Pa. ---

::: test
(Physics only, Higher tier) A sealed syringe of air is compressed in two ways: (A) very slowly, and (B) very quickly to the same final volume. Explain why the final pressure is higher immediately after B than after A. (3)
---
In B, work is done on the gas quickly so its internal energy and temperature rise, as there is no time for energy to be transferred to the surroundings (1). In A, the gas stays at room temperature as energy has time to escape (1). At the same volume, the hotter gas in B has faster molecules, which hit the walls more often and harder, so its pressure is higher (1).
