## What it is

**Separate science only: this whole subsection (AQA 4.5.5, Pressure and pressure differences in fluids) is in GCSE Physics 8463 but not in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464.** Trilogy students can skip it. Within it, the pressure due to a column of liquid, pressure differences with depth, upthrust and floating are **(Higher tier)**.

A **fluid** is a substance that can flow: either a **liquid** or a **gas**. The particles of a fluid move about randomly and collide with any surface they touch. These collisions create **pressure**.

**The pressure in a fluid causes a force normal (at right angles) to any surface.** This is true for every surface in every direction — the walls, floor and lid of a container, and all sides of an object placed in the fluid. Water spurts out of holes in a bottle at right angles to the side; a balloon is pushed outwards evenly in every direction.

### Pressure, force and area

**Equation (recall — you must learn it):**

pressure = force normal to a surface ÷ area of that surface

$$p = \frac{F}{A}$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| pressure | $p$ | pascal, Pa (1 Pa = 1 N/m²) |
| force normal to the surface | $F$ | newton, N |
| area | $A$ | square metre, m² |

- 1 kPa = 1000 Pa. Atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 100 000 Pa (100 kPa).
- **Rearranging:** $F = pA$ and $A = F \div p$.
- **Area conversions:** 1 m² = 10 000 cm², so divide cm² by 10 000 to get m² (50 cm² = 0.0050 m²).

**Mini-examples.**
1. Air at 100 kPa pushes on a window of area 1.5 m²: $F = pA = 100\,000 \times 1.5 = 150\,000$ N. The window does not break because the air inside pushes back with an equal force.
2. A gas in a syringe exerts a force of 30 N on a piston of area 2.0 cm² $= 0.00020$ m²: $p = 30 \div 0.00020 = 150\,000$ Pa (150 kPa).
3. A hydraulic jack: a 50 N force on a small piston of area 0.0010 m² gives a pressure of 50 000 Pa. The liquid passes this pressure to a large piston of area 0.050 m², which exerts $F = 50\,000 \times 0.050 = 2500$ N — a force multiplier.

### Pressure in a liquid (Higher tier)

The pressure due to a **column of liquid** can be calculated using:

**Equation (given on the Physics equation sheet — Higher tier, you must be able to apply it):**

pressure = height of the column × density of the liquid × gravitational field strength

$$p = h \rho g$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| pressure | $p$ | pascal, Pa |
| height (depth) of the column of liquid above the point | $h$ | metre, m |
| density of the liquid | $\rho$ | kilogram per metre cubed, kg/m³ |
| gravitational field strength | $g$ | newton per kilogram, N/kg (value given) |

This gives the pressure **due to the liquid only**. The total pressure at a depth also includes the atmospheric pressure acting on the surface.

**Why pressure increases with depth and density (explain this):**
- **Depth**: at a deeper point there is a **taller column of liquid above it**, so a **greater weight** of liquid is pressing down on each square metre. Pressure = force ÷ area, so the pressure is greater.
- **Density**: a **denser** liquid has **more mass (more particles)** in each cubic metre, so the column above the point has a **greater weight** for the same height — more force on the same area — so a greater pressure.

Where does $p = h\rho g$ come from? A column of liquid of height $h$ above area $A$ has volume $hA$, mass $\rho h A$ and weight $\rho h A g$. Pressure = weight ÷ area $= \rho h g$.

**Mini-example.** Pressure due to sea water ($\rho$ = 1030 kg/m³) on a diver at 10 m depth, $g$ = 9.8 N/kg: $p = 10 \times 1030 \times 9.8 = 100\,940$ Pa, about 100 kPa — so diving 10 m roughly doubles the total pressure on you (water plus about 100 kPa of atmosphere).

### Pressure differences at different depths (Higher tier)

The **difference** in pressure between two depths depends only on the **difference in depth**:

$$\Delta p = \Delta h \times \rho \times g$$

**Mini-example.** In fresh water ($\rho$ = 1000 kg/m³, $g$ = 9.8 N/kg), the pressure difference between 2.0 m and 5.0 m deep is $3.0 \times 1000 \times 9.8 = 29\,400$ Pa (29 kPa).

Uses: dam walls are built **thicker at the bottom** because the water pressure is greatest there; water towers are built high so that the pressure at taps is large; submarines need thick hulls to withstand the pressure at great depth.

### Upthrust (Higher tier)

A partially (or totally) submerged object experiences a **greater pressure on its bottom surface than on its top surface**, because the bottom is deeper. Greater pressure on the same area means a **greater upward force on the bottom** than the downward force on the top. This creates a **resultant force upwards**, called the **upthrust**.

**Mini-example.** A cube of side 0.10 m (face area 0.010 m²) is held under water ($\rho$ = 1000 kg/m³, $g$ = 9.8 N/kg) with its top face 0.20 m deep and its bottom face 0.30 m deep.
- Pressure on top $= 0.20 \times 1000 \times 9.8 = 1960$ Pa → downward force $= 1960 \times 0.010 = 19.6$ N.
- Pressure on bottom $= 0.30 \times 1000 \times 9.8 = 2940$ Pa → upward force $= 2940 \times 0.010 = 29.4$ N.
- Upthrust $= 29.4 - 19.6 = 9.8$ N upwards. (This equals the weight of the 0.0010 m³ of water the cube pushes aside — 1.0 kg of water weighs 9.8 N.)

The forces on the sides cancel, because the pressure at each height is the same on opposite sides.

### Floating and sinking (Higher tier)

An object in a fluid has two vertical forces on it: its **weight** (down) and the **upthrust** (up).
- If the upthrust can become **equal to the weight**, the object **floats**: it settles at the depth where the forces balance (resultant force zero).
- If the weight is **greater** than the maximum upthrust (when totally submerged), the object **sinks**.

**Factors that influence floating and sinking:**

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Density of the object compared with the fluid** | an object **less dense** than the fluid floats; one **more dense** sinks |
| **Density of the fluid** | a denser fluid gives a larger upthrust for the same depth submerged — ships float higher in sea water than in fresh water |
| **Volume of fluid displaced / shape** | the more of the object below the surface, the bigger the difference in pressure between bottom and top, so the bigger the upthrust. A steel ship floats because its hollow shape displaces a large volume of water; the same steel as a solid block sinks |
| **Weight of the object** | loading a boat increases its weight, so it sinks lower until the upthrust rises to match; overload it and it sinks |

A floating object sinks deeper until the upthrust equals its weight. A submarine sinks or rises by letting water into (or pumping it out of) ballast tanks, changing its weight while its volume stays the same.

### Atmospheric pressure

**A simple model of the atmosphere:**
- The atmosphere is a **thin layer** of air around the Earth (thin compared with the size of the Earth — most of it is within about 10–15 km of the surface, while the Earth's radius is about 6400 km).
- The atmosphere gets **less dense with increasing altitude**: there are fewer air molecules in each cubic metre higher up.
- **Air molecules colliding with a surface create atmospheric pressure.** Each collision exerts a small force; the total force on each square metre is the pressure (about 100 kPa at sea level).

**Why atmospheric pressure decreases with height (explain this):**
1. The number of air molecules — and so the **weight of air** — **above** a surface decreases as the height of the surface above ground level increases.
2. So at greater height there is always **less air above** the surface than at a lower height.
3. Also, the air is less dense, so there are **fewer molecules colliding** with each square metre each second.
4. So atmospheric pressure **decreases** with an increase in height.

Consequences: aircraft cabins are pressurised; climbers on high mountains find it harder to breathe; a sealed crisp packet taken up a mountain puffs up (the pressure inside is greater than the lower pressure outside); your ears "pop" in a lift or plane as the pressure changes. Pressure decreases with height in the air just as it increases with depth in a liquid, but the air's density changes with height, so $p = h\rho g$ cannot simply be used for the whole atmosphere.

**Pressure differences produce forces.** If a can is emptied of air (e.g. by boiling a little water in it and sealing it as it cools), the air pressure outside is much greater than inside and the resulting net force crushes the can. A suction cup sticks because the pressure outside is greater than the reduced pressure inside.

::: terms
- **Fluid**: a substance that can flow, which means either a liquid or a gas.
- **Pressure**: the force acting normal to a surface per unit area of that surface, measured in pascals.
- **Pascal**: the unit of pressure, equal to one newton per square metre.
- **Normal force**: a force acting at right angles to a surface.
- **Density**: the mass per unit volume of a substance, in kilograms per metre cubed.
- **Upthrust**: the resultant upward force on a submerged object caused by greater pressure on its bottom than its top.
- **Atmospheric pressure**: the pressure created by air molecules colliding with a surface, about 100 kPa at sea level.
- **Altitude**: the height above sea level or ground level.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Explain** why pressure increases with depth: deeper → taller column of liquid above → greater weight on each square metre → greater pressure. For density: denser → more mass in the same column → greater weight → greater pressure. Always link **weight of liquid above** to **force per unit area**.
- **Explain** upthrust: the bottom is deeper than the top → greater pressure on the bottom → greater upward force than downward force → resultant force upwards.
- **Describe** factors for floating: compare weight and upthrust; compare densities; mention volume displaced/shape.
- **Explain** why atmospheric pressure falls with height: less air (fewer molecules, less weight of air) above; less dense air → fewer collisions per second on each square metre.

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit)
1. $p = F \div A$ — **recall**. $p = h\rho g$ — **on the equation sheet** (Higher tier).
2. Convert areas to **m²** (cm² ÷ 10 000; mm² ÷ 1 000 000) and kPa to Pa (× 1000).
3. In $p = h\rho g$, $h$ is the **depth below the surface** (the height of liquid above the point), in metres.
4. Pressure differences: use the **difference in depth**.
5. Forces from pressure: $F = pA$. For upthrust from pressures: (pressure on bottom − pressure on top) × area.
6. Give the unit, Pa (or kPa), and sensible significant figures.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)** on submarines, divers, dams or floating ships: Level 3 links each fact to the next — depth → pressure → force on an area → consequence (thick hull/thick dam base, upthrust vs weight → floats or sinks). Level 2 gives correct ideas with weaker links; Level 1 gives isolated facts.
:::

::: example Pressure under a box
A crate of weight 1200 N rests on the floor on a base of 0.80 m by 0.50 m. Calculate the pressure it exerts on the floor. (3)

$A = 0.80 \times 0.50 = 0.40$ m² (1). $p = F \div A = 1200 \div 0.40$ (1) $= 3000$ Pa (1).
:::

::: example Force from a gas
The gas in a cylinder has a pressure of 250 kPa. The cylinder's end has an area of 40 cm². Calculate the force of the gas on the end. (3)

$p = 250\,000$ Pa, $A = 40 \div 10\,000 = 0.0040$ m² (1). $F = pA = 250\,000 \times 0.0040$ (1) $= 1000$ N (1).
:::

::: example Pressure at depth (Higher tier)
Calculate the pressure due to the water at the bottom of a swimming pool 2.5 m deep. Density of water = 1000 kg/m³, $g$ = 9.8 N/kg. (2)

$p = h\rho g = 2.5 \times 1000 \times 9.8$ (1) $= 24\,500$ Pa (24.5 kPa) (1).
:::

::: example Pressure difference (Higher tier)
A submarine moves from 50 m to 120 m below the surface of the sea (density 1030 kg/m³, $g$ = 9.8 N/kg). Calculate the increase in pressure on its hull. (3)

$\Delta h = 120 - 50 = 70$ m (1). $\Delta p = 70 \times 1030 \times 9.8$ (1) $= 706\,580$ Pa, about 710 kPa (1).
:::

::: example Explaining atmospheric pressure
Explain why the atmospheric pressure at the top of a mountain is lower than at sea level. (3)

Atmospheric pressure is caused by air molecules colliding with a surface (1). At the top of the mountain there is less air above, so the weight of air above each square metre is smaller (1). The air is also less dense, so there are fewer collisions with each square metre each second, and the pressure is lower (1).
:::

::: watch
- **cm² treated as m²**: 1 m² = 10 000 cm², not 100 cm². Convert before using $p = F \div A$.
- **Wrong $h$**: in $p = h\rho g$, $h$ is the depth **below the surface**, not the height above the bottom.
- **Pressure only acts downwards**: fluid pressure acts in **all directions**, at right angles to every surface.
- **Upthrust explained as "water pushes up"**: say the **pressure is greater on the bottom** because it is **deeper**, giving a resultant upward force.
- **"Heavy things sink"**: compare **density** (object vs fluid), or **weight vs upthrust** — a huge steel ship floats.
- **Atmosphere explanations without the cause**: link pressure to **molecules colliding** and to the **weight of air above** decreasing with height.
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A diver descends from 5.0 m to 25 m below the surface of the sea. Density of sea water = 1030 kg/m³, $g$ = 9.8 N/kg. (a) Calculate the increase in pressure. (b) The diver's mask has a front window of area 0.012 m². Calculate the increase in force on the window. (5)

::: test
(Higher tier) A rectangular block has a top and bottom face of area 0.050 m². It floats in water (density 1000 kg/m³, $g$ = 9.8 N/kg) with its top face above the surface and its bottom face 0.12 m below the surface. (a) Calculate the pressure due to the water on the bottom face. (b) Calculate the upthrust. (c) State the weight of the block and explain your answer. (5)
---
(a) $p = 0.12 \times 1000 \times 9.8$ (1) $= 1176$ Pa (1). (b) No water presses down on the top face, so upthrust $= 1176 \times 0.050$ (1) $= 58.8$ N, about 59 N (1). (c) 58.8 N — it is floating at rest, so the upthrust balances the weight and the resultant force is zero (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) A steel ball sinks in water but a steel ship of much greater mass floats. Explain why, and describe the factors that affect whether an object floats or sinks. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a detailed, linked explanation using pressure differences/upthrust and weight, with density and displaced volume used to explain both objects, and several factors described.
Level 2 (3–4): explains upthrust vs weight or density for at least one object, with some factors.
Level 1 (1–2): isolated points, e.g. "the ship is hollow" or "steel is denser than water".
Indicative content:
- A submerged object has greater pressure on its bottom (deeper) than its top, giving an upward resultant force — upthrust.
- An object floats if the upthrust can equal its weight; it sinks if its weight is greater than the maximum upthrust.
- The deeper the bottom of the object and the larger its area, the bigger the pressure difference force — the more water it displaces, the bigger the upthrust.
- The solid ball is denser than water; even fully submerged its small volume gives an upthrust less than its weight, so it sinks.
- The ship is hollow (contains air), so its average density is less than water; its large hull sits deep enough to displace a large volume and the upthrust equals its weight.
- Factors: density of the object vs the fluid; density of the fluid (denser fluid → more upthrust, floats higher); shape/volume displaced; the weight/load carried.
- Loading a ship makes it sit lower until the upthrust again equals the weight; overloading makes it sink.
:::
