## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.5.6.2, **Forces, accelerations and Newton's Laws of motion**, plus terminal velocity from 4.5.6.1.5. Newton's three laws, $F = ma$ and Required practical 7 are in both GCSE Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 6.5.4.2, where the practical is **Required practical 19**). Inertia and inertial mass are **(Higher tier)**. Drawing and interpreting velocity–time graphs for terminal velocity is **separate science only**.

### Resultant force — a quick recap

The **resultant force** is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting on an object together. Add forces in the same direction; subtract forces in opposite directions.

**Mini-example.** A car has a driving force of 2000 N forwards and air resistance plus friction of 800 N backwards. Resultant force = 2000 − 800 = 1200 N forwards.

### Newton's First Law

**If the resultant force acting on an object is zero:**
- **a stationary object remains stationary;**
- **a moving object continues to move at the same speed and in the same direction** — that is, at the **same velocity**.

So:
- when a vehicle travels at a **steady speed** in a straight line, the **resistive forces balance the driving force**;
- the velocity (speed **and/or direction**) of an object will **only change if a resultant force acts** on it.

| Situation | Forces | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| parked car | weight balanced by the ground's upward force | stays at rest |
| car at a steady 30 m/s on a straight road | driving force = air resistance + friction | constant velocity |
| driver presses the accelerator | driving force > resistive forces | speeds up |
| driver brakes | resistive forces (including braking force) > driving force | slows down |
| car turns a corner at constant speed | sideways friction from the road is unbalanced | direction changes, so velocity changes |

**Inertia (Higher tier).** The tendency of objects to **continue in their state of rest or of uniform motion** is called **inertia**. It is why passengers lurch forwards when a bus brakes suddenly — their bodies keep moving forwards until a force (the seat belt) acts on them.

### Newton's Second Law

**The acceleration of an object is proportional to the resultant force acting on the object, and inversely proportional to the mass of the object.**

Using the proportionality symbol ∝: $a \propto F$ (for constant mass) and $a \propto \dfrac{1}{m}$ (for constant force).

**Equation (recall — you must learn it):**

resultant force = mass × acceleration

$$F = ma$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| resultant force | $F$ | newton, N |
| mass | $m$ | kilogram, kg |
| acceleration | $a$ | metre per second squared, m/s² |

The $F$ is always the **resultant** force — work it out first if more than one force acts.

**Mini-example.** The car above has mass 800 kg and resultant force 1200 N. $a = F \div m = 1200 \div 800 = 1.5$ m/s².

**Mini-example.** Doubling the resultant force on a trolley doubles its acceleration. Doubling the mass with the same force halves the acceleration.

**Inertial mass (Higher tier).**
- **Inertial mass** is a measure of **how difficult it is to change the velocity** of an object.
- It is defined as the **ratio of force over acceleration**: $m = \dfrac{F}{a}$.

A loaded lorry has a much larger inertial mass than a car, so the same braking force gives it a much smaller deceleration.

**Mini-example (Higher tier).** A resultant force of 6.0 N gives an object an acceleration of 1.5 m/s². Inertial mass = 6.0 ÷ 1.5 = 4.0 kg.

**Estimating forces in road transport (MS 1d).** Use typical values and the symbol ≈ for approximate answers.
- A family car (≈ 1000 kg) pulling away at ≈ 3 m/s² needs a resultant force $F ≈ 1000 \times 3 = 3000$ N.
- A car (≈ 1500 kg) at ≈ 30 m/s stops in an emergency in ≈ 4 s: $a ≈ 30 \div 4 ≈ 7.5$ m/s², so $F ≈ 1500 \times 7.5 ≈ 11\,000$ N.
- A loaded lorry (≈ 40 000 kg) braking at ≈ 2 m/s² needs $F ≈ 80\,000$ N.

### Newton's Third Law

**Whenever two objects interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal and opposite.**

The two forces in a Newton's Third Law pair:
- are the **same size**;
- act in **opposite directions**;
- are the **same type** of force (both gravitational, both contact, etc.);
- act on **different objects** — so they never cancel each other out.

**Equilibrium examples.**
- **A book resting on a table.** The book pushes down on the table and the table pushes up on the book with an equal force (a third-law pair of contact forces). The Earth pulls the book down (its weight) and the book pulls the Earth up with an equal gravitational force (another third-law pair). The book is in equilibrium because the **two forces on the book** — its weight and the table's upward push — are balanced. Those two are **not** a third-law pair: they act on the same object and are different types.
- **Someone leaning on a wall.** The person pushes the wall; the wall pushes back on the person with an equal and opposite force.
- **A swimmer** pushes water backwards; the water pushes the swimmer forwards.
- **Walking**: your foot pushes backwards on the ground; the ground pushes forwards on you.

### Terminal velocity

An object falling through a **fluid** (a gas or liquid) **first accelerates** because of the force of gravity. As its speed increases, the **drag** (air resistance) on it increases. **Eventually the resultant force is zero** and the object moves at a constant speed — its **terminal velocity**.

Near the Earth, an object falling freely with no air resistance accelerates at about 9.8 m/s².

### Separate science only: velocity–time graphs for terminal velocity

Physics 8463 students must draw and interpret velocity–time graphs for falling objects and explain the changing motion in terms of the forces. (Trilogy students need only the paragraph above.)

**A skydiver jumps from a plane, then opens a parachute.**

| Stage | Forces | Motion (shape of v–t graph) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Just after jumping | only weight acts (air resistance zero at zero speed) | acceleration 9.8 m/s² — steepest gradient |
| 2. Falling faster | air resistance increases with speed; resultant force (weight − air resistance) decreases | still speeding up, but acceleration decreases — curve gets less steep |
| 3. First terminal velocity | air resistance = weight; resultant force zero | constant velocity (≈ 55 m/s) — horizontal line |
| 4. Parachute opens | much larger surface area, so air resistance suddenly much bigger than weight; resultant force upwards | decelerates — line drops steeply, then less steeply as air resistance falls with speed |
| 5. Second terminal velocity | air resistance = weight again, at a much lower speed | constant velocity (≈ 5 m/s) — horizontal line |
| 6. Landing | ground exerts a large upward force | rapid deceleration to zero |

The terminal velocity is **lower** for an object with a **larger surface area** (more drag at a given speed) or a **smaller weight**.

**Mini-example.** A skydiver of mass 80 kg (weight 784 N) has 500 N of air resistance at one moment. Resultant force = 784 − 500 = 284 N downwards. $a = 284 \div 80 ≈ 3.6$ m/s². At terminal velocity the air resistance is 784 N and $a = 0$.

**Investigation: the effect of air resistance on a falling object (e.g. a model parachute).** Make parachutes from the same material with different canopy areas, attach the same load, and drop each from the same measured height (e.g. 2.0 m). Time the fall with a stopwatch or film it against a metre rule. A larger canopy gives a longer fall time and lower terminal velocity. Repeat each drop and take a mean; drop from a greater height so that most of the fall is at terminal velocity.

### Required practical 7 (Trilogy: Required practical 19): force, mass and acceleration

**Aim:** investigate the effect of varying the force on the acceleration of an object of constant mass, and the effect of varying the mass of an object on the acceleration produced by a constant force.

**Apparatus:** trolley on a flat bench or runway, string over a pulley clamped to the end of the bench, a mass hanger with slotted masses (provides the accelerating force, $W = mg$), light gates connected to a data logger (or a ticker timer, or a stopwatch with a measured distance), card mounted on the trolley, metre rule, balance.

**Method — varying the force (constant mass):**
1. Set up the trolley so the string runs parallel to the bench over the pulley to the hanger.
2. Release the trolley from rest. Light gates measure the speed at two points (card length ÷ time to pass through each gate) and the time between them; acceleration = change in speed ÷ time. (Alternatively, one light gate a measured distance $s$ from the start: $a = v^2 \div 2s$.)
3. Move one slotted mass at a time **from the trolley to the hanger**, so the **total mass being accelerated stays constant** while the force increases. Repeat each run three times and take the mean acceleration.

**Method — varying the mass (constant force):** keep the same mass on the hanger and add masses to the trolley; measure the acceleration each time.

| Variable | Force investigation | Mass investigation |
|---|---|---|
| independent | accelerating force (weight on hanger) | mass of trolley (+ hanger) |
| dependent | acceleration | acceleration |
| control | total mass of system, same runway and surface, same release point | accelerating force, same runway and surface |

**Risks:** falling masses — put a box with padding under the hanger and keep feet clear; the trolley shooting off the end of the bench — clamp a buffer or block at the end.

**Improving accuracy:** light gates remove reaction-time errors; tilt the runway very slightly so the trolley just keeps moving at constant speed when pushed (a friction-compensated slope); repeat and average.

**Typical results.**
- Acceleration against force is a **straight line through the origin**: $a \propto F$. (If friction is not compensated, the line has a small positive intercept on the force axis.)
- Acceleration decreases as mass increases; acceleration against $\dfrac{1}{m}$ is a straight line through the origin: $a \propto \dfrac{1}{m}$.

**Sample data (total mass 1.0 kg):** forces 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80 N gave mean accelerations 0.19, 0.39, 0.58, 0.79 m/s² — each slightly under $F \div m$ because of friction.

::: terms
- **Resultant force**: the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting on an object together.
- **Newton's First Law**: with zero resultant force, an object stays at rest or keeps moving at the same velocity.
- **Newton's Second Law**: acceleration is proportional to resultant force and inversely proportional to mass, so F = ma.
- **Newton's Third Law**: when two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other.
- **Inertia**: the tendency of an object to stay at rest or keep moving with uniform motion (Higher tier).
- **Inertial mass**: a measure of how difficult it is to change an object's velocity; force divided by acceleration (Higher tier).
- **Terminal velocity**: the constant velocity reached by a falling object when drag equals its weight, so the resultant force is zero.
- **Drag**: a resistive force on an object moving through a fluid, such as air resistance, that increases with speed.
- **Equilibrium**: the state of an object when the forces on it are balanced, so the resultant force is zero.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State** a law: learn the exact wording — First Law must say "resultant force is zero"; Second Law must say "proportional to the resultant force" and "inversely proportional to the mass".
- **Explain** motion using forces: name the forces, compare their sizes, give the resultant, then the effect (speeds up, slows down, constant velocity, changes direction).
- **Describe** a velocity–time graph for a falling object (separate science only): describe each section and link it to the forces.
- **Estimate**: pick sensible values (car ≈ 1000–1500 kg; car acceleration ≈ 3 m/s²) and give an answer with ≈ to 1 or 2 significant figures.

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit)
1. Find the **resultant** force first: forces in the same direction add, in opposite directions subtract.
2. $F = ma$ is **recall**; rearrange to $a = F \div m$ or $m = F \div a$ (inertial mass, Higher tier).
3. Masses in **kg** (500 g = 0.50 kg). Weight of a hanging mass: $W = mg$ with $g$ = 9.8 N/kg.
4. Multi-step: find $a$ from a velocity change or graph ($a = \Delta v / t$), then $F = ma$ — or the other way round.

**Third Law questions**
Give the pair as "A pushes/pulls on B; B pushes/pulls on A with an equal and opposite force". Check the two forces are the same type and act on **different** objects. Balanced forces on **one** object are not a third-law pair.

**Required practical 7 (Trilogy 19)**
- Variables: force investigation — independent: force; dependent: acceleration; control: **total mass** (move masses from trolley to hanger). Mass investigation — independent: mass; control: force.
- Measuring $a$: two light gates (speeds and the time between) or one gate and a measured distance.
- Errors and improvements: friction (compensate with a slight slope), reaction time (use light gates), too few readings (take at least five values and repeat three times).
- Conclusions: straight line through the origin for $a$ against $F$ means $a \propto F$.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**
Terminal velocity: Level 3 runs through every stage in order, with forces and the effect on acceleration linked by "so" or "therefore". Practical plans: Level 3 gives apparatus, an ordered method with how $a$ is measured, what is kept constant, repeats and the graph used to analyse results.
:::

::: example Resultant force and acceleration
A cyclist and bicycle have a total mass of 75 kg. The forward force from the pedals is 180 N and the total resistive force is 30 N. Calculate the acceleration. (3)

Resultant force $= 180 - 30 = 150$ N (1). $a = F \div m = 150 \div 75$ (1) $= 2.0$ m/s² (1).
:::

::: example Force needed to decelerate a car
A car of mass 1200 kg slows from 20 m/s to rest in 4.0 s. Calculate the braking force. (4)

$a = (0 - 20) \div 4.0$ (1) $= -5.0$ m/s², a deceleration of 5.0 m/s² (1). $F = ma = 1200 \times 5.0$ (1) $= 6000$ N (1).
:::

::: example Inertial mass (Higher tier)
A force of 15 N gives trolley A an acceleration of 3.0 m/s². The same force gives trolley B an acceleration of 1.2 m/s². Calculate each inertial mass and state which is harder to accelerate. (3)

A: $m = F \div a = 15 \div 3.0 = 5.0$ kg (1). B: $m = 15 \div 1.2 = 12.5$ kg ≈ 13 kg (1). B has the larger inertial mass, so its velocity is harder to change (1).
:::

::: example Newton's Third Law pairs
A book rests on a table. A student says "the weight of the book and the upward force from the table are a Newton's Third Law pair". Explain why the student is wrong. (3)

Both forces act on the same object (the book), but a third-law pair acts on two different objects (1). They are different types of force — one is gravitational, the other a contact force (1). The third-law partner of the book's weight is the book's gravitational pull on the Earth (1).
:::

::: example Skydiver forces (separate science only)
A skydiver of mass 70 kg falls at terminal velocity. Take $g$ = 9.8 N/kg. (a) State the size of the air resistance. (b) After the parachute opens, the air resistance is 1500 N at that instant. Calculate the deceleration. (4)

(a) Weight $= 70 \times 9.8 = 686$ N (1), so air resistance = 686 N ≈ 690 N (1).
(b) Resultant $= 1500 - 686 = 814$ N upwards (1). $a = 814 \div 70 ≈ 12$ m/s² (deceleration) (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"Resultant force" missing**: First Law answers must say the **resultant** force is zero; "no forces act" is wrong — forces act but they balance.
- **Using one force in F = ma**: always subtract the opposing forces to get the resultant first.
- **Third-law pairs**: weight and the normal contact force on the same object are **not** a pair. A pair acts on two different objects and is the same type of force.
- **Constant velocity is not zero force**: a car at a steady 30 m/s still has a driving force; it is balanced by resistive forces.
- **Required practical**: forgetting to keep the **total** mass constant when varying the force, and forgetting that the hanging mass is part of the mass being accelerated.
- **Terminal velocity (separate science only)**: saying the parachute makes the skydiver "go up". The resultant force is upwards, so she **decelerates** while still falling.
- **Drag and speed**: say that air resistance **increases with speed** — that is the link that explains every stage of a falling object's motion.
:::
