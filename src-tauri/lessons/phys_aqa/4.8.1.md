## What it is

Within it, explaining how orbital velocity and radius are linked is **(Higher tier)**.

### Our solar system

**Within our solar system there is one star, the Sun, plus the eight planets and the dwarf planets that orbit around the Sun. Natural satellites — the moons that orbit planets — are also part of the solar system.**

- **The eight planets**, in order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars (small, rocky), Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune (large, mostly gas or ice). Memory aid: **M**y **V**ery **E**asy **M**ethod **J**ust **S**peeds **U**p **N**aming.
- **Dwarf planets** such as Pluto and Ceres also orbit the Sun but are too small to have cleared other objects from their orbits.
- **Moons** are natural satellites: the Earth has one Moon; Jupiter and Saturn have dozens.
- The solar system also contains asteroids and comets.

**Our solar system is a small part of the Milky Way galaxy**, which contains hundreds of billions of stars. The Milky Way is one of billions of galaxies in the universe.

Scale, smallest to largest: moon → planet → star (Sun) → solar system → galaxy (Milky Way) → universe.

### How the Sun formed

**The Sun was formed from a cloud of dust and gas (a nebula) pulled together by gravitational attraction.**

**At the start of a star's life cycle (explain this):**
1. Gravity pulls the dust and gas of the nebula together.
2. As the cloud collapses, it becomes **denser** and the particles move faster, so the **temperature rises**. This hot, dense ball is a **protostar**.
3. When the core becomes **hot and dense enough** (millions of degrees), **hydrogen nuclei fuse** to form helium nuclei. **Nuclear fusion** releases huge amounts of energy, and the star starts to shine.

**Stable stars — an equilibrium (explain this):**
- **Gravity** pulls the star's material **inwards** (gravitational collapse).
- Energy released by **fusion** heats the core, producing an **outward** pressure (from hot gas and radiation) that tends to make the star **expand**.
- When these two are **balanced**, the star is in **equilibrium** and stays a stable size for billions of years. This is the **main sequence** stage. The Sun is about halfway through its main sequence life (about 4.6 billion years old).

### The life cycle of a star

**A star goes through a life cycle, and the life cycle is determined by the size (mass) of the star.**

**A star about the size of the Sun:**

nebula → protostar → **main sequence star** → **red giant** → **white dwarf** → black dwarf

1. **Main sequence**: hydrogen fuses into helium in the core; forces balanced.
2. **Red giant**: when the hydrogen in the core runs out, the balance is lost. The core contracts and heats; fusion continues in a shell around it, and later helium fuses into heavier elements such as carbon and oxygen. The outer layers **expand and cool**, glowing red.
3. **White dwarf**: the outer layers drift away (as a planetary nebula), leaving a small, very hot, very dense core. No fusion takes place; it shines only because it is hot.
4. **Black dwarf**: the white dwarf cools until it no longer emits significant light (none yet exist — the universe is not old enough).

**A star much more massive than the Sun:**

nebula → protostar → **main sequence star** → **red supergiant** → **supernova** → **neutron star** or **black hole**

1. **Main sequence**: shorter than the Sun's, because a massive star uses its hydrogen much faster.
2. **Red supergiant**: it expands much more; fusion makes heavier and heavier elements in its core, up to **iron**.
3. **Supernova**: when fusion can go no further, the core collapses and the star **explodes** violently, briefly outshining a whole galaxy.
4. What is left depends on the mass: a **neutron star** (extremely dense core made of neutrons) or, for the most massive stars, a **black hole** (so dense that its gravity stops even light escaping).

| Stage | Sun-sized star | Massive star |
|---|---|---|
| Start | nebula → protostar | nebula → protostar |
| Stable stage | main sequence (about 10 billion years) | main sequence (much shorter) |
| After the hydrogen runs out | red giant | red supergiant |
| End | white dwarf → black dwarf | supernova → neutron star or black hole |

### How elements are formed

**Fusion processes in stars produce all of the naturally occurring elements.** (Hydrogen and most helium were made in the early universe; stars fuse them into heavier ones.)

**Explaining how fusion forms new elements:**
- In main sequence stars, **hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium**.
- In red giants and supergiants, **helium fuses into carbon and oxygen**, then heavier elements; the most massive stars fuse elements **up to iron**.
- Fusing nuclei to make elements beyond iron does not release energy, so **elements heavier than iron** (such as gold, lead and uranium) **are produced in a supernova**, where there is enormous energy available.
- **The explosion of a massive star (supernova) distributes the elements throughout the universe.** The dust and gas then form new nebulae, new stars and planets — the Earth and our bodies contain elements made in earlier stars.

### Orbits: planets, moons and artificial satellites

**Gravity provides the force that allows planets and satellites (both natural and artificial) to maintain their circular orbits.** Gravity acts towards the centre of the orbit (towards the Sun or the planet). Without it, the object would carry on in a straight line.

| | Planets | Moons (natural satellites) | Artificial satellites |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orbits | the Sun | a planet | a planet (usually the Earth) or the Moon |
| Made by | natural formation from the nebula | natural | humans (launched by rocket) |
| Force keeping it in orbit | gravity of the Sun | gravity of the planet | gravity of the planet |
| Shape of orbit | nearly circular (ellipses) | nearly circular | chosen for the purpose (circular or elliptical) |
| Examples | Earth, Mars, Jupiter | the Moon, Titan | the ISS, GPS, weather and communications satellites |

**Similarities:** all are kept in orbit by **gravity**; all move in (approximately) **circular** orbits; the closer they orbit, the faster they must move. **Distinctions:** what they orbit (the Sun vs a planet), natural vs artificial, and their size.

Artificial satellite orbits: **geostationary** satellites orbit above the equator once every 24 hours, so they stay above the same point on Earth (communications, TV). **Polar (low) orbit** satellites orbit pole to pole, quite close to Earth, in about 90 minutes, and scan the whole surface as the Earth turns beneath them (weather, mapping, spying).

### Circular orbits: changing velocity, constant speed (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** For an object in a circular orbit:
- Its **speed** (how fast it moves) is **constant**.
- Its **direction** of motion is **constantly changing** — always along a tangent to the circle.
- **Velocity** is speed in a given direction (a vector), so its **velocity is constantly changing**.
- A changing velocity is an **acceleration**, which needs a **resultant force**: this is **gravity**, acting **towards the centre** of the orbit.
- The force is always at **right angles** to the direction of motion, so it changes the direction but does **not** change the speed.

### Speed and radius of stable orbits (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** **For a stable orbit, the radius must change if the speed changes.**
- The **closer** an object orbits, the **stronger** the gravitational force on it, so it must move **faster** to stay in a stable orbit (otherwise gravity would pull it inwards).
- So **smaller orbits need higher speeds**, and larger orbits need lower speeds. Mercury orbits the Sun much faster than Neptune; low-orbit satellites move faster than geostationary ones.
- If an orbiting object's speed changes, it can no longer stay at the same radius: if it **slows down**, gravity pulls it into a **smaller** orbit (or it spirals in); if it **speeds up**, it moves out to a **larger** orbit (or escapes).

**Mini-example (using distance = speed × time, $s = vt$ — recall).** The ISS orbits at a radius of about 6780 km and takes about 92 minutes per orbit.
- Distance in one orbit $= 2\pi r = 2 \times \pi \times 6.78 \times 10^6 = 4.26 \times 10^7$ m.
- Time $= 92 \times 60 = 5520$ s.
- Speed $= s \div t = 4.26 \times 10^7 \div 5520 \approx 7700$ m/s (7.7 km/s).
A geostationary satellite (radius about 42 200 km, period 24 h) moves at only about 3.1 km/s — a larger orbit, a lower speed.

::: terms
- **Solar system**: the Sun together with the eight planets, dwarf planets, moons and other objects that orbit it.
- **Galaxy**: a huge group of billions of stars held together by gravity; our solar system is part of the Milky Way.
- **Nebula**: a cloud of dust and gas in space from which stars form.
- **Protostar**: a hot, dense ball of gas formed as gravity pulls a nebula together, before fusion starts.
- **Main sequence star**: the long, stable stage of a star in which hydrogen fuses to helium and fusion pressure balances gravity.
- **Supernova**: the explosion of a massive star, which makes elements heavier than iron and scatters them through space.
- **Natural satellite**: a natural object, such as a moon, that orbits a planet.
- **Artificial satellite**: a human-made object launched into orbit around the Earth or another body.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** a life cycle: give the stages **in order**, with arrows, for the correct mass of star. Sun-sized: nebula, protostar, main sequence, red giant, white dwarf (black dwarf). Massive: nebula, protostar, main sequence, red supergiant, supernova, neutron star or black hole.
- **Explain** how a star forms: gravity pulls dust and gas together → temperature rises → fusion of hydrogen starts.
- **Explain** why a main sequence star is stable: inward gravity **balanced** by outward pressure from fusion energy.
- **Explain** how elements form: hydrogen → helium in main sequence; heavier elements up to iron in larger stars; heavier than iron only in a supernova, which spreads them out.
- **Compare** planets, moons and artificial satellites: what they orbit, natural or not, all kept in orbit by gravity.
- **Explain** (Higher tier) circular orbits: speed constant but direction changes → velocity changes → acceleration → resultant force (gravity) towards the centre.
- **Explain** (Higher tier) orbit radius and speed: closer orbits → stronger gravity → must move faster; change speed → radius must change.

**Calculations**: rare, but you might use $s = vt$ with the distance round an orbit ($2\pi r$). Convert km to m and hours or days to seconds. Give units and sensible significant figures.

**Extended answers (6 marks)**: the life cycle of a massive star and the formation of elements are classic 6-markers. A level 3 answer has the **stages in the right order** and **explains** each change (balance lost, fusion of heavier elements, supernova making and spreading elements). A list of stages with no explanation is level 1–2.
:::

::: example The forces in a stable star
Explain why a main sequence star remains a stable size for billions of years. (3)

Gravity pulls the star's material inwards (1). Energy from fusion in the core creates an outward pressure (1). These forces are balanced, so the star is in equilibrium and does not collapse or expand (1).
:::

::: example Life cycle of the Sun
Describe what will happen to the Sun after its main sequence stage. (3)

When the hydrogen in its core runs out, it will expand to become a red giant (1). Its outer layers will then drift away, leaving a hot, dense core — a white dwarf (1). The white dwarf will cool down over a very long time to become a black dwarf (1).
:::

::: example Elements heavier than iron
Explain where the gold in a gold ring was made. (3)

Gold is heavier than iron (1). Elements heavier than iron are only made in a supernova — the explosion of a massive star (1). The explosion spread the gold through space, where it became part of the nebula that formed the solar system and the Earth (1).
:::

::: example Orbital speed (Higher tier)
Explain how the Moon can travel at a constant speed but still be accelerating. (3)

The Moon's direction of motion is constantly changing as it moves in a circle (1). Velocity includes direction, so its velocity is changing — it is accelerating (1). The acceleration is caused by the Earth's gravitational force, which acts towards the centre of the orbit, at right angles to the motion, so it changes direction but not speed (1).
:::

::: example Calculating orbital speed
The Moon orbits the Earth at a radius of $3.84 \times 10^8$ m and takes 27.3 days. Calculate its orbital speed. (4)

Distance $= 2\pi r = 2 \times \pi \times 3.84 \times 10^8 = 2.41 \times 10^9$ m (1). Time $= 27.3 \times 24 \times 3600 = 2.36 \times 10^6$ s (1). $v = s \div t = 2.41 \times 10^9 \div 2.36 \times 10^6$ (1) $\approx 1020$ m/s (1.0 km/s) (1).
:::

---
Closer to the Earth the gravitational force is stronger (1), so the satellite must move faster to stay in a stable orbit rather than being pulled inwards (1).

::: q
Explain how the elements in the Earth came to be there, even though they were not all made in the Sun. (3)
---
Elements were made by fusion in earlier stars; those heavier than iron were made in supernovae (1). Supernova explosions spread these elements through space (1). They became part of the nebula that collapsed to form the Sun and the planets, including the Earth (1).

::: watch
- **Life-cycle stages in the wrong order or for the wrong mass**: a Sun-sized star becomes a **red giant** then a **white dwarf**; only **massive** stars go **supernova**.
- **"The Sun will explode"**: it is not massive enough — it will end as a white dwarf.
- **Equilibrium without forces**: name **both**: gravity **inwards**, pressure from fusion energy **outwards**, **balanced**.
- **Heavy elements**: elements **heavier than iron** form only in a **supernova**; up to iron forms by fusion inside stars.
- **"Constant speed means no acceleration"** (Higher tier): the **direction** changes, so **velocity** changes — that is an acceleration, caused by **gravity towards the centre**.
- **Moons orbit the Sun**: moons orbit **planets**; planets orbit the Sun.
:::

::: test
The Earth orbits the Sun at a radius of $1.5 \times 10^{11}$ m once every 365 days. Calculate the Earth's orbital speed. (4)
---
Distance $= 2\pi r = 2 \times \pi \times 1.5 \times 10^{11} = 9.42 \times 10^{11}$ m (1). Time $= 365 \times 24 \times 3600 = 3.15 \times 10^7$ s (1). $v = s \div t = 9.42 \times 10^{11} \div 3.15 \times 10^7$ (1) $\approx 30\,000$ m/s ($3.0 \times 10^4$ m/s, 30 km/s) (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A satellite in a stable circular orbit has a constant speed. (a) Explain why its velocity is not constant. (b) The satellite's rocket fires briefly and its speed decreases. Explain why it cannot stay in its original orbit. (4)
---
(a) Its direction of motion is always changing (1); velocity is a vector that includes direction, so the velocity changes (1). (b) At the lower speed, the gravitational force at that radius is more than is needed to keep it moving in that circle (1), so it is pulled into a smaller orbit — a stable orbit at a lower speed needs a different radius (1).

Indicative content:

- The protostar becomes hotter and denser until hydrogen fuses into helium — the main sequence star.
- On the main sequence, outward pressure from fusion energy balances inward gravity — equilibrium.
- When hydrogen runs out, the star swells into a red supergiant.
- In the supergiant, fusion produces heavier elements (carbon, oxygen … up to iron).
- When fusion can go no further, the core collapses and the star explodes as a supernova.
- Elements heavier than iron are formed in the supernova.
- The supernova spreads all these elements throughout the universe, where they can form new stars and planets.
- The remaining core forms a neutron star or, for the most massive stars, a black hole.
