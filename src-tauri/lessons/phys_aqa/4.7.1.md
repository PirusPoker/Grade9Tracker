## What it is

AQA 4.7.1 is about permanent and induced magnets, magnetic forces and magnetic fields. **This whole subsection is in both GCSE Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 6.7.1), at both tiers** — everyone needs all of it. There are no equations in this subsection, but you must be able to describe, draw and explain.

### Poles of a magnet

**The poles of a magnet are the places where the magnetic forces are strongest.** A bar magnet has a **north (north-seeking) pole** at one end and a **south (south-seeking) pole** at the other. If a bar magnet is hung from a thread, its north-seeking pole turns to point roughly towards the Earth's geographic North.

When two magnets are brought close together they **exert a force on each other**:
- **Like poles repel** (north–north, south–south).
- **Unlike poles attract** (north–south).

Attraction and repulsion between two magnetic poles are examples of a **non-contact force**: the magnets do not need to touch. Other non-contact forces are gravity and the electrostatic force.

The forces act on **both** magnets equally and in opposite directions (Newton's third law), and they get **weaker as the magnets move further apart**.

### Permanent and induced magnets

| | Permanent magnet | Induced magnet |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | produces **its own magnetic field** all the time | a material that **becomes a magnet when it is placed in a magnetic field** |
| Force on another magnet | can **attract or repel** | **always attracts** — induced magnetism always causes a force of attraction |
| When the field is removed | stays magnetic | **loses most or all of its magnetism quickly** |
| Typical material | magnetised steel, alloys such as alnico, neodymium magnets | soft iron, nickel, cobalt (or unmagnetised steel) placed near a magnet |

**Why induced magnetism always attracts.** When a north pole is brought near an iron nail, the end of the nail nearest the magnet becomes a **south** pole (and the far end a north pole). Unlike poles are now closest, so the nail is **attracted**. Bring a south pole instead and the near end becomes a north pole — still attraction. This is why a magnet picks up paper clips with either end.

**Testing whether something is a permanent magnet.** Attraction does not prove an object is a magnet (a magnet attracts plain iron too). **Only repulsion proves it is a permanent magnet**, because only two magnets can repel.

### Magnetic fields

**The region around a magnet where a force acts on another magnet or on a magnetic material is called the magnetic field.**

**Magnetic materials** are **iron, steel, cobalt and nickel**. Most metals (copper, aluminium, brass, gold) are **not** magnetic, and neither are non-metals such as wood and plastic.

**The force between a magnet and a magnetic material is always one of attraction.**

Key facts about a magnetic field:
- **Its strength depends on the distance from the magnet**: it is weaker further away.
- **It is strongest at the poles** of the magnet.
- **Direction**: the direction of the field at any point is the direction of the force that would act on **another north pole** placed at that point.
- So **field lines point from the north (seeking) pole to the south (seeking) pole** of a magnet (outside the magnet).

### Drawing the field of a bar magnet

When you draw a magnetic field pattern:
1. Draw lines that **leave the north pole and enter the south pole**, curving around the magnet.
2. Put **arrows** on every line, pointing **from N to S**.
3. Draw the lines **closest together at the poles** (strongest field) and **further apart away from the magnet** (weaker field).
4. **Lines never cross** and never touch each other.
5. The pattern is symmetrical; it is three-dimensional in reality.

Between two magnets:
- **Unlike poles facing (N facing S)**: lines go straight across from N to S between the poles; the field in the gap is strong and nearly **uniform** (parallel, evenly spaced lines).
- **Like poles facing (N facing N)**: lines from both poles push away from each other and curve outwards; there is a **neutral point** midway between them where the fields cancel and there is no resultant field.

### Plotting a field with a compass

**A magnetic compass contains a small bar magnet** (the needle), free to turn. Its north pole points in the **direction of the magnetic field** where it is placed.

**Method to plot the magnetic field pattern of a magnet:**
1. Place the bar magnet in the middle of a sheet of paper and draw round it. Label N and S.
2. Put a small **plotting compass** near one end of the north pole.
3. Mark a dot on the paper at the point the compass **needle's north end** points to.
4. Move the compass so that the **tail** of the needle is over the dot you just made, and mark a new dot where the north end now points.
5. Repeat until you reach the south pole (or the edge of the paper).
6. Join the dots with a smooth curve and add an arrow showing the direction (N to S).
7. Start again from a different point near the north pole to draw further field lines.

**Alternative (shape only):** put a sheet of paper over the magnet and sprinkle **iron filings** on it, then tap gently. The filings line up along the field lines, showing the shape — but **not the direction**. A compass is needed for direction.

**Improvements and errors:** keep other magnets and steel objects (including the steel in benches) away; use a small compass so the dots are close together and the line is smooth; make sure the magnet does not move.

### The Earth's magnetic field

**The Earth has a magnetic field.** A compass needle (when no other magnet is nearby) **points in the direction of the Earth's magnetic field** — roughly towards geographic North.

**Evidence that the core of the Earth must be magnetic:**
- A freely suspended compass always settles pointing **north–south**, wherever it is on Earth, even far from any other magnet.
- So there must be a magnetic field across the whole Earth, which has a shape **like that of a giant bar magnet** inside the Earth.
- The field must come from **inside** the Earth — from its **core** (iron and nickel). (The core is in fact too hot to be a permanent magnet; currents in the molten outer core produce the field, but you only need to know the evidence that the core is magnetic.)

**A puzzle to explain:** since the compass's north pole is attracted towards the geographic North, and unlike poles attract, the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole is actually a magnetic **south** pole. The Earth's field lines therefore run from the south geographic region to the north geographic region.

::: terms
- **Magnetic pole**: a place on a magnet where the magnetic forces are strongest, either north-seeking or south-seeking.
- **Non-contact force**: a force that acts between objects that are not touching, such as magnetic attraction or repulsion.
- **Permanent magnet**: a magnet that produces its own magnetic field all the time.
- **Induced magnet**: a material that becomes a magnet only while it is in a magnetic field and loses its magnetism quickly when removed.
- **Magnetic material**: a material attracted by a magnet: iron, steel, cobalt or nickel.
- **Magnetic field**: the region around a magnet where a force acts on another magnet or on a magnetic material.
- **Field line**: a line showing the direction of the force on a north pole, drawn from north to south with an arrow.
- **Plotting compass**: a small compass whose needle points along the magnetic field, used to trace field lines.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** the difference between permanent and induced magnets: give **both** sides of the comparison — own field vs field only when in another field; attract or repel vs always attract; stays magnetic vs loses magnetism quickly.
- **Describe** how to plot a field: a numbered method with the key idea of moving the compass so its tail sits on the last dot, joining dots and adding arrows.
- **Draw** a field pattern: lines from N to S, arrows on every line, lines closest at the poles, lines never crossing.
- **Explain** why a compass points north / why the Earth's core must be magnetic: compass is a small magnet → aligns with the field → it does so everywhere on Earth → so the Earth has a field like a bar magnet's, coming from the core.
- **Suggest** how to test whether a bar is a magnet: only **repulsion** proves it.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**: for a method question, a level 3 answer is a sequence someone else could follow to get the result, including how to record and how to show direction. For a comparison, every point needs its matching opposite.

**Practical questions**: identify sources of error (nearby steel, other magnets, the Earth's field affecting weak parts of the pattern), and explain why a compass gives direction but iron filings do not.
:::

::: example Like and unlike poles
Two bar magnets are placed end to end with their north poles facing. State and explain what happens. (2)

They push apart / repel (1) because like poles repel each other (1).
:::

::: example Induced magnetism
Explain why a steel paper clip is attracted to either pole of a bar magnet. (3)

The paper clip becomes an induced magnet when it is in the magnet's field (1). The end nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole to the magnet's pole (1). Unlike poles attract, so it is attracted whichever pole is used (1).
:::

::: example Drawing a field
Describe the features a student should include when drawing the magnetic field around a bar magnet. (4)

Lines leaving the north pole and entering the south pole (1). Arrows on the lines pointing from north to south (1). Lines closest together at the poles, where the field is strongest (1). Lines spreading out further from the magnet and never crossing (1).
:::

::: example Testing for a magnet
A student has two identical-looking steel bars. One is a magnet. Describe how she can find out which one, using only the two bars. (3)

Repulsion cannot happen here, because only one bar is a magnet, so use where the field is strongest. Hold bar A flat and touch the end of bar B against the middle of bar A, then swap them (1). The field of a magnet is strongest at its poles and very weak at its middle (1). If the end of B strongly attracts the middle of A, then B is the magnet; if there is little or no attraction, A is the magnet (because the plain bar's end is touching the magnet's weak middle) (1).
:::

::: q
State what happens when two south poles are brought close together. (1)
---
They repel (push each other apart).
:::

::: q
Name the four magnetic materials in the AQA specification. (2)
---
Iron, steel, cobalt, nickel (2 for all four; 1 for two or three).
:::

::: q
Where is the magnetic field of a bar magnet strongest? (1)
---
At the poles (the ends of the magnet).
:::

::: q
Define the direction of a magnetic field at a point. (1)
---
The direction of the force that would act on a north pole placed at that point.
:::

::: q
Give two differences between a permanent magnet and an induced magnet. (2)
---
A permanent magnet produces its own field; an induced magnet is only magnetic while in another magnetic field (1). A permanent magnet can attract or repel; an induced magnet always attracts / an induced magnet loses its magnetism quickly when removed from the field (1).
:::

::: q
A student holds a steel rod near a bar magnet and it is attracted. Explain why this does not prove the rod is a permanent magnet. (2)
---
A magnet attracts any magnetic material, including unmagnetised steel, by inducing magnetism in it (1). Only repulsion would prove the rod is a permanent magnet (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why iron filings show the shape of a magnetic field but not its direction. (2)
---
Each filing becomes an induced magnet and lines up along the field lines, showing the shape (1), but the filings have no marked north end, so they do not show which way the field points — a compass is needed (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the magnetic field between a north pole and a south pole placed close together, facing each other. (2)
---
Field lines go straight across from the north pole to the south pole (1); they are parallel and evenly spaced, so the field is strong and uniform (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Field arrows the wrong way**: field lines go **from north to south** outside the magnet.
- **"All metals are magnetic"**: only **iron, steel, cobalt and nickel** — copper, aluminium and brass are not.
- **"A magnet attracts it, so it is a magnet"**: attraction can be induced magnetism; **only repulsion** proves a permanent magnet.
- **Field drawings without arrows, or with crossing lines**: every line needs an arrow; lines never cross; draw them closest at the poles.
- **Compass method without the key step**: say "move the compass so its **tail** is on the last dot" and "join the dots".
- **Earth's core evidence**: link the compass **always** pointing north–south to a field like a bar magnet's coming from **inside** the Earth.
:::

::: test
Which pair will attract each other? A: N and N; B: S and S; C: N and S; D: a copper rod and a magnet. (1)
---
C — unlike poles attract.
:::

::: test
Give one example of a non-contact force other than magnetism. (1)
---
Gravitational force (weight) or electrostatic force.
:::

::: test
A soft iron nail is held near a bar magnet and then taken away. Describe what happens to the magnetism of the nail. (2)
---
The nail becomes an induced magnet while in the magnet's field (1). When removed, it loses most or all of its magnetism quickly (1).
:::

::: test
Draw (or describe in words) the magnetic field pattern around a single bar magnet, showing how its strength and direction vary. (3)
---
Closed curved lines from the north pole round to the south pole (1); arrows from N to S (1); lines closest together at the poles and spreading out further away, showing the field is weaker with distance (1).
:::

::: test
Two bar magnets are placed with north poles facing, a short distance apart. (a) Describe the field pattern between them. (b) A small compass is placed exactly halfway between them. Suggest what happens to the compass and why. (4)
---
(a) Lines from each north pole curve away from each other, outwards (1); there is a neutral point midway with no resultant field (1). (b) The two fields cancel there, so there is no resultant force from the magnets on the needle (1); it points in the direction of the Earth's field (north) instead (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the behaviour of a compass is evidence that the Earth's core is magnetic. (3)
---
A compass needle is a small magnet that lines up with a magnetic field (1). It points north–south everywhere on Earth, even with no magnet nearby (1), so the Earth must have a magnetic field like that of a bar magnet, coming from inside it — its core (1).
:::

::: test
A compass needle's north pole points towards the Earth's geographic North Pole. Explain what type of magnetic pole must be near the geographic North Pole. (2)
---
A magnetic south pole (1), because the compass's north pole is attracted to it and only unlike poles attract (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how to use a plotting compass to plot the magnetic field pattern of a bar magnet. Include how to improve the accuracy of the pattern. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a complete, logical method that would produce an accurate field pattern with direction, plus at least one valid improvement.
Level 2 (3–4): a method with the main steps but some gaps (e.g. no arrows or no repeat from different start points).
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements, e.g. "move a compass around the magnet".
Indicative content:
- Place the magnet on paper, draw round it and label N and S.
- Place the compass near the north pole.
- Mark a dot where the north end of the needle points.
- Move the compass so the tail of the needle is over the dot; mark the next dot.
- Repeat until reaching the south pole.

- Improvements: use a small compass; mark dots close together; keep other magnets and steel objects away; keep the magnet still (tape it down).
:::
