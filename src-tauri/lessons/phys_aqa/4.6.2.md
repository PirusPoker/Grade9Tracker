## What it is

AQA 4.6.2 covers electromagnetic (EM) waves: the spectrum, how different substances absorb, transmit, refract and reflect them, how they are produced, their dangers and their uses. **Trilogy 8464 students need 4.6.2.1 to 4.6.2.4 (their 6.6.2); the sections on lenses and visible light are Separate science only.** Required practical 10 (Trilogy: Required practical 21) is in this topic.

### The electromagnetic spectrum

**Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves that transfer energy from the source of the waves to an absorber.** They do not need a medium: they travel through a vacuum (space). They form a **continuous spectrum**, and **all EM waves travel at the same velocity through a vacuum or air**: $3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s (you will be given this value).

The groups, from **long wavelength (low frequency)** to **short wavelength (high frequency)**:

| Group | Approximate wavelength | Example of energy transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Radio waves | about 1 m to over 10 km | a transmitter to a radio aerial |
| Microwaves | about 1 mm to 1 m | a microwave oven to water in food |
| Infrared (IR) | about 700 nm to 1 mm | a grill or the Sun to your skin |
| Visible light (red to violet) | about 700 nm (red) to 400 nm (violet) | a lamp to the retina of your eye |
| Ultraviolet (UV) | about 10 nm to 400 nm | the Sun to skin cells (tanning, sunburn) |
| X-rays | about 0.01 nm to 10 nm | an X-ray tube to a photographic detector |
| Gamma rays | less than about 0.01 nm | a radioactive nucleus to a tumour |

Memory aid, long to short: **R**ich **M**en **I**n **V**egas **U**se **X**-ray **G**lasses. Frequency increases along this order, and so does the energy the wave can transfer and how dangerous it is.

**Our eyes only detect visible light**, so we detect only a very narrow range of the EM spectrum.

**Wave speed equation (recall — you must learn it, from 4.6.1):**

wave speed = frequency × wavelength

$$v = f\lambda$$

with $v$ in m/s, $f$ in hertz (Hz) and $\lambda$ in metres (m). For EM waves in a vacuum or air, $v = 3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s.

**Mini-examples.**
1. A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz $= 1.0 \times 10^8$ Hz. $\lambda = v \div f = 3.0 \times 10^8 \div 1.0 \times 10^8 = 3.0$ m.
2. A microwave has wavelength 12 cm $= 0.12$ m. $f = v \div \lambda = 3.0 \times 10^8 \div 0.12 = 2.5 \times 10^9$ Hz (2.5 GHz).

### Absorption, transmission, refraction and reflection (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** Different substances may **absorb, transmit, refract or reflect** EM waves in ways that **vary with wavelength**. For example:
- Glass transmits visible light but absorbs much UV and long-wavelength IR.
- The body's soft tissue transmits most X-rays, but bone absorbs them.
- The atmosphere transmits visible light, most radio waves and microwaves, but the ozone layer absorbs much UV.
- Water molecules strongly absorb certain microwaves and IR.

**(Higher tier)** Some effects, such as **refraction**, happen because **waves travel at different velocities in different substances**. Light slows down when it passes from air into glass or water.

### Refraction ray diagrams

When a wave crosses a boundary between two media **at an angle**, it changes direction — **refraction**. To draw a ray diagram:
1. Draw the **normal**: a dashed line at 90° to the boundary where the ray meets it.
2. Measure the **angle of incidence** $i$ and **angle of refraction** $r$ **from the normal**, not from the surface.
3. Going into a medium where the wave is **slower** (air → glass), the ray bends **towards the normal** ($r < i$).
4. Going into a medium where it is **faster** (glass → air), the ray bends **away from the normal** ($r > i$).
5. A ray travelling **along the normal** (at 90° to the boundary) passes straight through without changing direction, although it still changes speed.

For a rectangular glass block, the ray bends towards the normal on entering and away from the normal on leaving, so it leaves **parallel to the incident ray** but shifted sideways.

### Wave front diagrams (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** A **wave front** is a line joining points on the same crest of a wave; wave fronts are drawn at right angles to the direction of travel, and the gap between them is one wavelength.

Explaining refraction at an air–glass boundary with wave fronts:
1. A wave front reaches the boundary at an angle, so **one end of the wave front enters the glass first**.
2. That end **slows down** while the rest of the wave front, still in air, keeps moving at the higher speed.
3. So the part in air travels further in the same time, and **the wave front swings round (changes direction)** — towards the normal.
4. In the glass the wave fronts are **closer together**: the **wavelength decreases**. The **frequency does not change** (it is set by the source), so from $v = f\lambda$ a lower speed means a shorter wavelength.

If the wave front meets the boundary **parallel to it** (the wave travels along the normal), the whole wave front slows at the same moment, so there is no change of direction — only the wavelength gets shorter.

### Required practical 10 (Trilogy: Required practical 21): infrared and surfaces

**Required practical activity 10: investigate how the amount of infrared radiation absorbed or radiated by a surface depends on the nature of that surface.**

**Method (emission, using a Leslie cube):**
1. A Leslie cube is a hollow metal cube whose four vertical faces have different surfaces: **matt black, shiny black, matt white and shiny silver**.
2. Fill it with hot water from a kettle and place it on a heatproof mat.
3. Point an **infrared detector** (or a thermometer with a blackened bulb) at one face from a fixed distance, e.g. 10 cm, and record the reading.
4. Repeat for each face, keeping the distance the same, working quickly so that the water temperature hardly changes.

**Alternative (absorption):** two metal plates, one matt black and one shiny silver, stand at **equal distances** either side of a radiant heater. A ball bearing is stuck to the back of each with wax. Time how long each takes to fall.

**Alternative (cooling):** identical flasks or boiling tubes painted different colours are filled with the same volume of hot water at the same starting temperature; record the temperature drop over 10 minutes.

| Variable | In this practical |
|---|---|
| Independent | type of surface (colour and shininess) |
| Dependent | IR detector reading / temperature change / time for the ball bearing to fall |
| Control | temperature of the water, distance from the detector or heater, area of each surface, starting temperature, volume of water, time |

**Typical results:** **matt black** surfaces are the **best emitters and best absorbers** of infrared; **shiny silver (or white)** surfaces are the **worst emitters and absorbers** (they are good reflectors). With the Leslie cube, matt black gives the highest reading and shiny silver the lowest; with the plates, the wax on the matt black plate melts first.

**Risks:** hot water and the hot cube can scald or burn — use a heatproof mat, do not touch the faces, and pour carefully. The radiant heater is very hot — keep a safe distance.

**Improvements:** use an IR detector rather than a thermometer (higher resolution, quicker response); repeat readings and calculate a mean; keep the cube's water temperature constant by measuring it each time.

### Producing and absorbing radio waves (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** Radio waves can be **produced by oscillations in electrical circuits**. An alternating current in a transmitting aerial makes electrons oscillate, and this produces radio waves with the **same frequency** as the current.

**(Higher tier)** When radio waves are **absorbed** by a receiving aerial they may **create an alternating current with the same frequency as the radio wave**. So radio waves can themselves induce oscillations in an electrical circuit — this is how a radio or TV receiver picks up a signal.

### Where EM waves come from

**Changes in atoms and the nuclei of atoms** can result in EM waves being **generated or absorbed** over a wide frequency range. When electrons in atoms move between energy levels, atoms emit or absorb light, UV or X-rays. **Gamma rays originate from changes in the nucleus of an atom** (after radioactive decay, the nucleus loses energy).

### Hazards and radiation dose

**Ultraviolet waves, X-rays and gamma rays can have hazardous effects on human body tissue.** The effect depends on the **type of radiation** and the **size of the dose**.

- **UV** can cause **skin to age prematurely** and **increases the risk of skin cancer**. It can also damage the eyes.
- **X-rays and gamma rays** are **ionising radiation** that can cause **mutation of genes** and **cancer**.

**Radiation dose**, measured in **sieverts (Sv)**, is a measure of the **risk of harm** resulting from exposure of the body to radiation. 1000 millisieverts (mSv) = 1 Sv. (You will not be asked to recall the unit, but you must be able to use it.)

**Mini-example (drawing conclusions from data).** A table gives: average UK background dose 2.7 mSv per year; chest X-ray 0.02 mSv; CT scan of the chest 6.6 mSv. A chest X-ray is about $2.7 \div 0.02 = 135$ times smaller than a year's background dose, so its extra risk is very small. A CT scan is $6.6 \div 0.02 = 330$ times the dose of a chest X-ray, so CT scans should be used only when the benefit (diagnosing a serious condition) outweighs the extra risk. In sieverts, 6.6 mSv = 0.0066 Sv.

### Uses of EM waves

| EM wave | Use | Why it is suitable (Higher tier) |
|---|---|---|
| Radio waves | television and radio broadcasting | long wavelengths travel long distances, are not absorbed much by the atmosphere and can diffract around hills and buildings; they can be produced and detected with electrical circuits in aerials |
| Microwaves | satellite communications | they pass through the atmosphere (and the ionosphere) to and from satellites in space without being absorbed |
| Microwaves | cooking food | they are absorbed by water molecules in food, increasing their energy; they penetrate a few cm into the food |
| Infrared | electrical heaters, cooking food (grills, toasters) | IR is absorbed by surfaces and increases their thermal energy store |
| Infrared | infrared cameras | all objects emit IR, and hotter objects emit more, so a camera can show temperature differences, even in the dark |
| Visible light | fibre optic communications | glass fibres transmit visible light with little absorption; light reflects along inside the fibre, carrying data quickly |
| Ultraviolet | energy efficient (fluorescent) lamps | UV produced in the tube is absorbed by the coating, which then emits visible light — less energy is wasted heating the surroundings |
| Ultraviolet | sun tanning | UV is absorbed by the skin and causes it to produce more of the dark pigment melanin |
| X-rays | medical imaging | X-rays pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by dense bone, so bones show up as a shadow on the detector |
| Gamma rays | medical imaging (tracers) and treatments | gamma passes out of the body to be detected outside; high-energy gamma beams can kill cancer cells. Both X-rays and gamma rays are also used to treat cancers |

### Separate science only: lenses

**Separate science only:** a **lens** forms an image by **refracting** light.

- A **convex (converging) lens** is thicker in the middle. Rays parallel to the principal axis are brought to a focus at the **principal focus**, F. The distance from the centre of the lens to F is the **focal length**. A more strongly curved (fatter) lens has a shorter focal length.
- A **concave (diverging) lens** is thinner in the middle. Rays parallel to the axis spread out as if they had come from a principal focus on the **same side as the object**.
- In ray diagrams a convex lens is drawn as a vertical line with **arrowheads pointing outwards** at both ends; a concave lens as a vertical line with **arrowheads pointing inwards**.

**Ray diagram rules.** Draw the object as an upright arrow standing on the principal axis. From its top, draw:
1. A ray **parallel to the axis**: through a convex lens it refracts **through F** on the far side; through a concave lens it diverges as if it came **from F on the object side** (draw a dashed construction line back to F).
2. A ray through the **centre of the lens**: it passes straight through, undeviated.
3. Where the rays meet is the top of the image. If real rays meet, the image is **real** (can be shown on a screen). If the rays only appear to come from a point (dashed lines traced back), the image is **virtual** (cannot be shown on a screen).

**Convex lens images** (F = focal length from the lens; 2F = twice the focal length):

| Object position | Image | Example |
|---|---|---|
| beyond 2F | real, inverted, diminished, between F and 2F on the other side | camera, eye |
| at 2F | real, inverted, same size, at 2F | — |
| between F and 2F | real, inverted, magnified, beyond 2F | projector |
| closer than F | **virtual**, upright, magnified, on the same side as the object | magnifying glass |

**A convex lens can produce a real or a virtual image. A concave lens always produces a virtual image** — upright, diminished and on the same side as the object (used to correct short sight).

**Magnification equation (given on the Physics equation sheet — you must be able to apply it):**

magnification = image height ÷ object height

$$\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image height}}{\text{object height}}$$

**Magnification is a ratio, so it has no units.** Measure both heights in the same unit (both mm or both cm).

**Mini-example.** A magnifying glass makes a 4.0 mm letter appear 1.0 cm tall. Convert: 1.0 cm = 10 mm. Magnification $= 10 \div 4.0 = 2.5$.

You may investigate the magnification produced by a range of convex lenses: place an object (e.g. an illuminated cross) and a screen either side of the lens, move the screen until the image is sharp, then measure image and object heights with a ruler.

### Separate science only: visible light and colour

**Separate science only:**
- Each colour in the visible spectrum has its **own narrow band of wavelength and frequency**: red has the longest wavelength (lowest frequency), violet the shortest (highest frequency).
- **Specular reflection**: reflection from a **smooth** surface in a **single direction** (a mirror gives a clear image).
- **Diffuse reflection**: reflection from a **rough** surface causes **scattering** in many directions (paper gives no image).
- **Colour filters** work by **absorbing certain wavelengths (colours) and transmitting other wavelengths**. A red filter transmits red light and absorbs the other colours.
- **Opaque objects**: their colour is decided by which wavelengths are **more strongly reflected**; wavelengths not reflected are **absorbed**. A red apple reflects red light and absorbs the rest. If all wavelengths are reflected equally the object looks **white**; if all are absorbed it looks **black**.
- Objects that transmit light are **transparent** (light passes straight through, you can see clearly through them) or **translucent** (light passes through but is scattered, so you cannot see clearly).

**Combining ideas.** A red shirt seen through a green filter looks **black**: the shirt reflects only red light, and the green filter absorbs red, so no light reaches your eye. A white shirt seen through a green filter looks **green**: it reflects all colours, and only the green is transmitted. A blue object in red light looks black: there is no blue light for it to reflect, and it absorbs the red.

::: terms
- **Electromagnetic wave**: a transverse wave that transfers energy from a source to an absorber and can travel through a vacuum.
- **Electromagnetic spectrum**: the continuous range of EM waves from radio waves to gamma rays, grouped by wavelength and frequency.
- **Refraction**: the change in direction of a wave when it crosses a boundary at an angle, caused by a change in speed.
- **Normal**: a construction line drawn at right angles to a boundary, from which angles of incidence and refraction are measured.
- **Wave front**: a line joining points on the same crest of a wave, drawn at right angles to the direction of travel.
- **Ionising radiation**: radiation such as X-rays and gamma rays that can knock electrons off atoms and cause gene mutations and cancer.
- **Radiation dose**: a measure of the risk of harm from exposure of the body to radiation, measured in sieverts.
- **Principal focus**: the point where rays parallel to the axis meet (convex lens) or appear to come from (concave lens).
- **Focal length**: the distance from the centre of a lens to its principal focus.
- **Virtual image**: an image formed where rays only appear to come from, which cannot be projected on a screen.
- **Specular reflection**: reflection from a smooth surface in a single direction.
- **Diffuse reflection**: reflection from a rough surface that scatters light in many directions.
:::

**Command words**
- **Name / give** an EM wave or use: one word or phrase is enough, but spell it correctly ("ultraviolet", not "ultra violent").
- **Describe** a property: state what happens (e.g. "bone absorbs X-rays, soft tissue transmits them").
- **Explain** why a wave suits a use (Higher tier): link a **property** to the **job** — "microwaves pass through the atmosphere, so they can reach satellites in orbit".
- **Explain** refraction with wave fronts (Higher tier): one side of the wave front reaches the boundary first → slows down → the rest is still faster → the wave front changes direction; wavelength decreases, frequency is unchanged.
- **Evaluate / draw conclusions** from dose data: compare numbers (calculate how many times larger), weigh benefit against risk, and give a judgement.
- **Compare** convex and concave lenses: give a similarity (both refract light, both can give upright virtual images) and a difference (only convex can form a real image; convex converges, concave diverges).

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit and sensible significant figures)
1. $v = f\lambda$ — **recall**. Convert MHz to Hz (× $10^6$), GHz to Hz (× $10^9$), cm to m, nm to m (× $10^{-9}$).
2. Magnification $=$ image height ÷ object height — **on the equation sheet**. Same units top and bottom; **no unit** in the answer.
3. Dose: 1 Sv = 1000 mSv. Watch the prefix.

**Ray diagrams**: use a ruler and sharp pencil; put arrows on rays; draw the normal dashed and at 90°; for lenses, draw two rays from the top of the object and mark the image. Dashed lines for virtual rays.

**Required practical 10 questions** (Trilogy RP21): identify variables (surface = independent; IR reading = dependent; water temperature and distance = control), explain why distance must be the same (IR intensity falls with distance), suggest an improvement (IR detector instead of thermometer; repeat and average), and give a hazard (scalding from hot water).

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**: level 3 answers give a logical sequence with every point linked ("because", "so"). For uses, give the use **and** the reason for each wave. For hazards, give the type of radiation **and** the effect on tissue **and** that the effect depends on the dose.

::: example Wavelength of a radio wave
A radio station broadcasts at a frequency of 198 kHz. Radio waves travel at $3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s. Calculate the wavelength. (3)

$f = 198\,000$ Hz (1). $\lambda = v \div f = 3.0 \times 10^8 \div 198\,000$ (1) $= 1515$ m, about 1500 m (1.5 km) (1).
:::

::: example Explaining refraction with wave fronts (Higher tier)
Light travels from air into a glass block at an angle. Use ideas about wave fronts to explain why the light changes direction. (3)

One edge of each wave front reaches the glass first and slows down (1). The rest of the wave front is still in the air and moving faster, so it travels further in the same time (1). This makes the wave front change direction, bending towards the normal; the wavelength in the glass is shorter (1).
:::

::: example Magnification (Separate science only)
A convex lens produces an image 3.6 cm tall of an object 12 mm tall. Calculate the magnification. (2)

Same units: 3.6 cm = 36 mm (1). Magnification $= 36 \div 12 = 3$ (no unit) (1).
:::

::: example Colour through a filter (Separate science only)
A white football shirt with blue numbers is viewed through a blue filter. Describe and explain what is seen. (3)

The shirt reflects all colours, but the filter absorbs every colour except blue, so the shirt looks blue (1). The numbers reflect blue light, which the filter transmits, so they also look blue (1). The numbers become very hard to see because shirt and numbers look the same colour (1).
:::

::: example Choosing a wave (Higher tier)
Explain why X-rays are used to look for a broken bone. (2)

X-rays are absorbed by bone but pass through (are transmitted by) soft tissue (1), so the bone shows up as a shadow on the detector and a break can be seen as a gap (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"Gamma has the longest wavelength"**: gamma has the **shortest** wavelength and **highest** frequency; radio has the longest.
- **EM waves "need a medium"**: they do not — they travel through a vacuum at $3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s.
- **Angles from the surface**: angles of incidence and refraction are measured **from the normal**.
- **"Frequency changes in glass"**: speed and wavelength change on refraction; **frequency stays the same**.
- **Uses without reasons**: on Higher tier, say **why** — "microwaves pass through the atmosphere", "bone absorbs X-rays".
- **"UV causes radiation sickness"**: UV damages **skin** (ageing, skin cancer) and eyes; X-rays and gamma are the **ionising** ones that mutate genes.
- **Units on magnification**: it is a ratio — no unit, and both heights must be in the same unit.
:::
