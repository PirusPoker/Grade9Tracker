## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection (4.2.5 Static electricity) is in AQA GCSE Physics 8463 only. Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 students do not need it. It is examined at Foundation and Higher, with no Higher-tier-only content and no equations.

Static electricity is charge that **builds up and stays put** on an object, usually an insulator, instead of flowing round a circuit. It explains why a balloon rubbed on your jumper sticks to the wall, why your hair stands on end when you pull off a wool hat, and why you sometimes get a small shock from a car door or a metal door handle.

### How objects become charged: electron transfer

All materials are made of atoms, which contain positive protons in the nucleus and negative **electrons** around it. Normally an object has equal numbers of each, so it is **uncharged** (neutral).

When certain **insulating materials** are **rubbed** against each other they become electrically charged:

1. Friction rubs **negatively charged electrons** off one material and on to the other.
2. The material that **gains electrons** becomes **negatively charged** (it now has more electrons than protons).
3. The material that **loses electrons** is left with an **equal positive charge** (it has fewer electrons than protons).

Only **electrons** move. The positive charges (protons) are fixed in the nuclei and never move during charging — so a positive charge always means "electrons have been lost", never "protons have been gained". This single idea is worth marks in almost every static question.

| Rubbed together | Electrons move | Result |
|---|---|---|
| polythene rod and a cloth | from the cloth to the polythene | polythene **negative**, cloth equally **positive** |
| acetate (Perspex) rod and a cloth | from the acetate to the cloth | acetate **positive**, cloth equally **negative** |
| balloon and hair (or a wool jumper) | from the hair to the balloon | balloon **negative**, hair **positive** |

The charge stays where it is put because the materials are **insulators**: electrons cannot move through them. A metal rod held in your hand would not stay charged, because electrons would flow through the metal and through you to earth.

### Forces between charges

When two electrically charged objects are brought close together they exert a **force** on each other:

- two objects carrying the **same type** of charge **repel** (push apart);
- two objects carrying **different types** of charge **attract** (pull together).

These forces act **without the objects touching**: attraction and repulsion between charged objects are examples of a **non-contact force** (like gravity and magnetism). The force gets **stronger** as the objects get **closer** together.

**Evidence that charged objects exert forces when not in contact** — the classic demonstration:

1. Rub a polythene rod with a cloth and hang it horizontally in a paper stirrup on a thread so it can rotate freely.
2. Rub a second polythene rod and bring it **near** (not touching) one end of the hanging rod. The hanging rod **turns away** — like charges repel.
3. Bring a rubbed acetate rod near instead. The hanging rod **turns towards** it — unlike charges attract.

The rod moves even though nothing touches it, which shows a force acting at a distance. Other evidence: strands of hair charged by a comb **spread apart** because they all carry the same charge and repel; two balloons rubbed on the same jumper and hung side by side on threads **push apart**.

(You may also see a charged rod pick up small pieces of uncharged paper. This happens because the charged rod pushes or pulls electrons within the paper so the near side gains the opposite charge. You are not required to explain this.)

### Sparking

As more and more charge builds up on an insulated object, the **potential difference** between the object and the earth (or a nearby earthed conductor) becomes **larger and larger**. When it is large enough, **electrons jump across the gap** through the air — you see and hear a **spark**.

- Walk across a nylon carpet in rubber-soled shoes and you gain electrons by rubbing. When your hand nears a metal door handle, electrons jump between you and the earthed handle: a spark and a small shock.
- **Lightning** is a giant spark: charge separates in storm clouds until the pd between cloud and ground is enormous.
- **Refuelling** aircraft and fuel tankers: fuel rubbing through a pipe can charge the pipe or nozzle. A spark near fuel vapour could cause an explosion, so the nozzle and tank are connected to earth by a conducting **bonding/earthing wire** so charge flows away safely before it can build up.

### Electric fields

A charged object creates an **electric field** around itself. An electric field is the **region around a charged object in which another charged object experiences a force**. You cannot see a field, but you can map it with **field lines**:

- field lines show the **direction of the force on a small positive charge** placed in the field, so they point **away from positive** charges and **towards negative** charges;
- for an **isolated charged sphere**, the lines are **radial** — straight lines like spokes, meeting the surface at right angles;
- the closer together the lines, the stronger the field. Near the sphere the lines are crowded, so the **field is strongest close to the charged object**; further away they spread out, so the **field is weaker**.

To **draw** the field of an isolated charged sphere: at least six to eight straight lines, evenly spaced, starting at the surface and **perpendicular** to it, with arrows **outwards** for positive or **inwards** for negative. Lines must never cross and must not curve.

A second charged object placed in the field **experiences a force**. Because the field is stronger closer in, the **force gets stronger as the distance between the objects decreases**.

### How the field idea explains static effects

**Non-contact forces.** Each charged object sits in the electric field of the other. The field acts on the charge directly, so there is a force even though the objects do not touch. Opposite charges: the force on each is towards the other (attraction). Like charges: the force on each is away from the other (repulsion). Moving them closer puts each in a stronger part of the other's field, so the force increases.

**Sparking.** As charge builds up on an object, its electric field gets stronger, and the field is strongest close to the object. When the field near the object becomes strong enough, it pulls electrons off the molecules in the air (the air becomes **ionised**). The ionised air can conduct, so charge flows through it to earth or to a nearby conductor — a spark. The spark is most likely where the gap is smallest, because that is where the field is strongest.

### Mini-examples

- *A cloth rubs an acetate rod, and the rod becomes positive. What happened to the cloth?* It gained the electrons the rod lost, so it has an equal negative charge.
- *Two charged balloons hang 10 cm apart and push apart. One is moved to 5 cm away. What happens to the force?* It is larger, because the balloon is now in a stronger part of the other's field.
- *Why do sparks jump from a charged dome to your knuckle rather than across the room?* The field is strongest in the small gap near the dome, so the air there is ionised first.

::: terms
- **Static electricity**: electric charge that builds up and stays on an object, usually an insulator, rather than flowing.
- **Electron transfer**: the movement of negatively charged electrons from one material to another when insulators are rubbed together.
- **Negatively charged**: describes an object that has gained electrons, so it has more electrons than protons.
- **Positively charged**: describes an object that has lost electrons, so it has fewer electrons than protons.
- **Non-contact force**: a force that acts between objects that are not touching, such as the force between two charged objects.
- **Electric field**: the region around a charged object in which another charged object experiences a force.
- **Field line**: a line showing the direction of the force on a small positive charge placed in an electric field.
- **Sparking**: the sudden flow of charge through the air when the electric field near a charged object is strong enough to ionise the air.
- **Earthing**: connecting an object to the ground with a conductor so that charge flows away instead of building up.
:::

::: method
**The one rule that wins most marks:** only **electrons** move. Every charging explanation must say electrons are transferred, which material gains them (becomes negative) and which loses them (becomes positive, by an equal amount). Writing "positive charges moved" or "protons were rubbed off" scores zero for that point.

**Command words**
- **Describe** how static charge is produced: rubbing → friction → electrons transferred from one insulator to the other → gainer negative, loser equally positive.
- **Describe evidence** for forces between charges: name the set-up (charged rod hanging freely), what you do (bring another charged rod **near without touching**), what you see (it rotates away or towards) and what that shows (like repel / unlike attract; force acts without contact).
- **Explain** a static effect: use the chain *charge builds up → electric field around the object → another charge in the field feels a force*, or for sparks *charge builds up → field becomes very strong → air ionised → charge flows through air → spark*.
- **Draw** a field: radial straight lines, perpendicular to the surface, evenly spaced, arrows **out of positive** or **into negative**, lines not touching or crossing.
- **Suggest** a safety measure: earthing, using conducting materials, keeping air humid, avoiding sparks near fuel vapour.

**4- and 6-mark answers (levels of response)**
Examiners reward a logical sequence. For "explain how a person becomes charged and gets a shock", the top level links: rubbing on carpet → electrons transferred to (or from) the person → person charged, insulated by shoes so charge cannot escape → large pd / strong field between person and earthed door handle → air ionised, electrons jump → spark / shock. A list of facts without the links stays in the lower levels.

**Data and working-scientifically questions**
There are no equations in this topic, but you may be given results (for example, the angle a hanging charged ball swings through at different distances from a charged rod) and asked to describe the pattern ("as distance decreases, the force increases") or to suggest why results vary (humid air lets charge leak away; the charge on the rod is not the same each time).
:::

::: example Charging a polythene rod
A polythene rod is rubbed with a woollen cloth and becomes negatively charged. Explain how the rod becomes charged and what happens to the cloth. (3)

Friction transfers electrons from the cloth to the rod (1). The rod has gained electrons, so it has more electrons than protons and is negatively charged (1). The cloth has lost the same number of electrons, so it is left with an equal positive charge (1).
:::

::: example Demonstrating attraction and repulsion
Describe an experiment that shows charged objects exert forces on each other without touching. (4)

Rub a polythene rod with a cloth and hang it from a thread in a paper stirrup so it can turn freely (1). Rub a second polythene rod and bring it near one end of the hanging rod without touching (1). The hanging rod turns away, showing that like charges repel (1). Bringing a rubbed acetate rod (opposite charge) near makes the hanging rod turn towards it, showing unlike charges attract; the rods never touch, so the force is a non-contact force (1).
:::

::: example Drawing a field
Draw the electric field pattern around an isolated negatively charged sphere, and state where the field is strongest. (3)

Straight radial lines starting from all around the sphere, perpendicular to the surface and evenly spaced (1), with arrows pointing **towards** the sphere (1). The field is strongest close to the surface of the sphere, where the lines are closest together (1).
:::

::: example Spark at the petrol pump (grade 8)
Explain why fuel nozzles for refuelling aircraft are connected to earth by a conducting wire. (4)

Fuel flowing through the hose rubs against it, transferring electrons, so the nozzle or aircraft can become charged (1). As charge builds up, the electric field / pd between the charged object and the earth gets larger (1). If it becomes strong enough, it ionises the air and a spark jumps (1), which could ignite fuel vapour and cause an explosion; the earthing wire lets electrons flow to or from earth so charge never builds up (1).
:::

::: example Using the field idea
Two small charged spheres repel each other. Use the idea of an electric field to explain why the force between them increases when they are moved closer together. (3)

Each sphere is surrounded by an electric field, and the other sphere sits in that field, so it experiences a force without contact (1). The field of a charged sphere is strongest close to it and weakens with distance (1). Moving the spheres closer puts each in a stronger part of the other's field, so the force (here, repulsion) increases (1).
:::

They repel (push each other apart).

Why does a metal rod held in the hand not stay charged when rubbed? (2)

Metal is a conductor (1), so electrons flow through the metal and the person to earth / the charge does not stay on the rod (1).

---
The region around a charged object in which another charged object experiences a force.

In which direction do field lines point around a positively charged sphere? (1)

::: q
A child's hair stands on end after sliding down a plastic slide. Explain why. (3)
---
Rubbing on the slide transfers electrons, so the child's hair becomes charged (1). Every hair carries the same type of charge (1), so the hairs repel each other and spread out (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a spark can jump from a person to a metal door handle after they walk across a nylon carpet. (3)
---
Electrons are transferred between the shoes/body and the carpet, so the person becomes charged (1). Charge builds up and the field / pd between the person and the earthed handle becomes very large (1). When the field is strong enough it ionises the air and electrons jump across the gap as a spark (1).
:::

::: q
How does the force between two charged objects change as they move further apart? (1)
---
It decreases (gets weaker), because the field is weaker further from a charged object.
:::

::: q
Give one way of reducing the danger of sparks when a fuel tanker unloads. (1)
---
Connect the tanker to earth with a conducting cable (earthing / bonding), so charge flows away instead of building up.
:::

::: watch
- **Only electrons move.** "Protons/positive charges moved on to the rod" loses the mark every time. A positive object has **lost** electrons.
- Say the two materials end up with **equal and opposite** charges — the gainer is negative, the loser is positive.
- Field lines for an isolated sphere are **straight, radial and perpendicular** to the surface, **out of positive, into negative**. Curved lines, crossing lines or missing arrows cost marks.
- For forces, say they act **without contact** (non-contact force) and get **stronger as distance decreases**.
- Sparking needs the chain: charge builds up → strong field / large pd → air ionised → electrons jump. "The charge escapes" alone is not enough.
:::

::: test
A student hangs a small charged ball on a thread and measures how far it is pushed sideways by a charged dome placed at different distances.

Distance from dome (cm): 20, 15, 10, 5
Sideways displacement of ball (mm): 2, 4, 9, 30

Describe the pattern in the results and use the idea of an electric field to explain it. (3)
---
As the distance decreases, the displacement (force) increases, and it increases more and more rapidly at short distances (1). The ball is in the electric field of the charged dome, which exerts a force on it (1). The field is strongest close to the dome, so the closer the ball, the greater the force (1).
:::

::: test
Repeated readings in the experiment above were very different on a damp day. Suggest why. (2)
---
Damp/humid air (water on surfaces) conducts slightly (1), so charge leaks away from the ball and the dome, and their charges are smaller and keep changing (1).
:::

::: test
Explain, in terms of the electric field, why a very highly charged object can produce a spark. (3)
---
A large charge produces a very strong electric field, strongest close to the object (1). The strong field pulls electrons from air molecules, ionising the air (1). The ionised air conducts, so charge flows through it to a nearby conductor or earth as a spark (1).
:::

::: test
A person walks across a nylon carpet in rubber-soled shoes and then gets a shock when touching a metal tap. Explain in detail how the person becomes charged and why the shock happens. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6 marks): a detailed and coherent explanation covering charging by electron transfer, why the charge stays on the person, and how the field or pd leads to a spark through the air to the tap.
Level 2 (3–4 marks): a clear explanation of either the charging or the sparking, with some linking, but the full sequence is incomplete or contains an error such as "positive charges move".
Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple relevant points such as "friction causes static" or "electricity jumps to the tap", with little or no linking.
0 marks: no relevant content.
Indicative content:
- the shoes rub against the carpet (friction);
- electrons are transferred from one material to the other;
- the person gains (or loses) electrons and becomes negatively (or positively) charged; the carpet gets an equal and opposite charge;
- the rubber soles are insulators, so the charge cannot flow away to earth and it builds up;
- the charged person has an electric field around them;
- the metal tap is connected to earth (0 V), so there is a large pd between the person and the tap;
- as the hand gets close, the field in the small gap is very strong;
- the air is ionised and electrons jump across the gap — a spark;
- the brief current through the person is felt as a shock; the person is discharged.
:::
