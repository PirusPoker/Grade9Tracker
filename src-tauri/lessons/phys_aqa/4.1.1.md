## What it is

Energy is the "accounting system" of physics. You never see energy itself; you track **where it is stored** before and after something happens, and **how it got there**. This topic (AQA 4.1.1) is the foundation for the whole Physics paper: every later topic — circuits, forces, particles, waves — reuses these equations. Everything here is also in **Combined Science: Trilogy**.

### Systems and energy stores

A **system** is an object or group of objects that you choose to study. When a system changes, the way its energy is **stored** changes. AQA uses these stores:

| Store | Energy is stored because… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Kinetic | an object is moving | a rolling ball |
| Gravitational potential | an object is raised in a gravitational field | a book on a shelf |
| Elastic potential | an object is stretched, compressed or bent | a stretched spring |
| Thermal (internal) | of the motion and positions of particles; hotter means more | hot water |
| Chemical | of chemical bonds that can react | food, fuel, a battery |
| Nuclear | of the nucleus of an atom | uranium fuel |
| Magnetic / electrostatic | of magnets or charges attracting or repelling | two magnets pulled apart |

Energy moves between stores by **energy transfers**. At GCSE AQA asks you to calculate changes caused by three ways of transferring energy:

- **heating** — energy flows from hotter to colder because of a temperature difference;
- **work done by forces** — a force moves an object (work done = force × distance, met again in Forces);
- **work done when a current flows** — charge moving through a potential difference (met again in Electricity).

(Radiation such as light and sound waves also carries energy away from a system.)

### Describing energy changes in common situations

The spec names five situations you must be able to describe. Always say **which store empties and which store fills**:

| Situation | Store decreasing | Store increasing |
|---|---|---|
| Object projected upwards | kinetic store of the object | gravitational potential store of the object (some to thermal store of the air, by air resistance) |
| Moving object hits an obstacle | kinetic store of the object | thermal stores of object and obstacle; some carried away by sound; elastic store if it deforms |
| Object accelerated by a constant force | chemical store of the person/fuel, or work done by the force | kinetic store of the object (some to thermal stores through friction) |
| Vehicle slowing down | kinetic store of the vehicle | thermal store of the brakes, then of the surroundings (friction does work) |
| Water boiled in an electric kettle | work done by the current (from the mains) | thermal store of the heating element, then of the water; some to the kettle and air |

You must also be able to show the energy in a system **on a common scale** — usually a table or bar chart in joules — to show that the total stays the same while it is redistributed.

**Mini-example (common scale).** A 0.50 kg ball is dropped from 10 m ($g = 9.8$ N/kg, ignore air resistance). At the top: gravitational potential store $= 0.50 \times 9.8 \times 10 = 49$ J, kinetic $= 0$ J. Half way down: 24.5 J and 24.5 J. Just before the ground: 0 J and 49 J. The **total is 49 J at every point**.

### The equations

AQA says which equations you must **recall** (learn by heart) and which are given on the **Physics equation sheet**.

| Equation in words | Symbols | Units | Recall or sheet? |
|---|---|---|---|
| kinetic energy = 0.5 × mass × speed² | $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ | J, kg, m/s | **Recall** |
| gravitational potential energy = mass × gravitational field strength × height | $E_p = m g h$ | J, kg, N/kg, m | **Recall** |
| elastic potential energy = 0.5 × spring constant × extension² | $E_e = \tfrac{1}{2} k e^2$ | J, N/m, m | Equation sheet |
| change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change | $\Delta E = m c \Delta\theta$ | J, kg, J/kg °C, °C | Equation sheet |
| power = energy transferred ÷ time | $P = \dfrac{E}{t}$ | W, J, s | **Recall** |
| power = work done ÷ time | $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$ | W, J, s | **Recall** |

Notes:
- In any calculation the value of **g is given** (usually 9.8 N/kg; sometimes 10 N/kg).
- The elastic potential energy equation only works if the spring has **not gone past its limit of proportionality**.
- Only the **speed** is squared in $E_k$, and only the **extension** is squared in $E_e$. Doubling speed **quadruples** kinetic energy.
- Convert first: g → kg (divide by 1000), cm → m (divide by 100), kJ → J (× 1000), minutes → seconds (× 60).

### Kinetic, gravitational and elastic stores — worked mini-examples

- **Kinetic.** A 1200 kg car travels at 15 m/s. $E_k = 0.5 \times 1200 \times 15^2 = 135\,000$ J $= 135$ kJ.
- **Rearranging for speed.** $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_k}{m}}$. A 0.20 kg ball with 10 J of kinetic energy: $v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \div 0.20} = \sqrt{100} = 10$ m/s.
- **Gravitational.** A 2.5 kg bag lifted 4.0 m ($g = 9.8$ N/kg): $E_p = 2.5 \times 9.8 \times 4.0 = 98$ J.
- **Elastic.** A spring with $k = 40$ N/m is stretched 15 cm $= 0.15$ m: $E_e = 0.5 \times 40 \times 0.15^2 = 0.45$ J.
- **Linking stores (falling object).** If no energy is dissipated, gravitational potential energy lost = kinetic energy gained, so $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ and $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. Dropped from 5.0 m: $v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 5.0} = \sqrt{98} = 9.9$ m/s. Mass cancels — a heavy and a light object hit the ground at the same speed if air resistance is negligible.

### Thermal stores and specific heat capacity

The **specific heat capacity** of a substance is the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of **1 kg** of the substance by **1 °C**. Water has a very high value (about 4200 J/kg °C), so it heats up and cools down slowly; metals such as aluminium (about 900 J/kg °C) and copper (about 385 J/kg °C) warm up much faster for the same energy.

$\Delta E = m c \Delta\theta$ works for energy going **into** a system (temperature rises) or **released from** it (temperature falls).

**Mini-example.** Energy to heat 0.50 kg of water from 20 °C to 80 °C: $\Delta E = 0.50 \times 4200 \times 60 = 126\,000$ J $= 126$ kJ.

**Rearranged.** $c = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m\,\Delta\theta}$ and $\Delta\theta = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m c}$.

### Required practical 1: specific heat capacity

(Required practical 14 in Trilogy.) Aim: find the specific heat capacity of a material by linking the **decrease of one energy store (work done by an electric current)** to the **increase in the thermal store** of a block.

**Method**
1. Measure the mass of a metal block (e.g. 1.0 kg aluminium) on a balance.
2. Wrap the block in insulation (e.g. bubble wrap or cotton wool). Put an immersion heater in one hole and a thermometer in the other; a drop of water or oil in the thermometer hole improves thermal contact.
3. Connect the heater to a low-voltage supply with a **joulemeter**, or with an **ammeter** (in series) and **voltmeter** (in parallel) so energy can be found from $E = V I t$.
4. Record the starting temperature, switch on and start a stopwatch. Record the temperature and the energy transferred every minute for about 10 minutes.
5. Plot **temperature (y) against energy transferred (x)**. Draw a line of best fit through the straight part.

**Analysis.** For the straight part, gradient $= \dfrac{\Delta\theta}{E} = \dfrac{1}{mc}$, so $c = \dfrac{1}{m \times \text{gradient}}$. Or use a single pair of values: $c = \dfrac{E}{m\,\Delta\theta}$.

**Variables.** Independent: the material of the block (or energy supplied, when finding one value). Dependent: temperature rise. Control: mass of block, potential difference of supply, amount of insulation, starting temperature.

**Typical result and errors.** For aluminium the value obtained is usually **higher** than the accepted 900 J/kg °C. Some energy is lost to the surroundings and used to heat the heater and thermometer, so the temperature rise is smaller than it should be, which makes the calculated $c$ too large (a **systematic error**). The temperature also keeps rising briefly after the heater is switched off, because the heater is hotter than the block — use the highest temperature reached. Improvements: better insulation, a lid, good thermal contact, starting slightly below room temperature.

**Risks.** The heater and block get hot enough to burn — do not touch them; let them cool before packing away. Keep water away from the electrical connections.

### Power

**Power** is the **rate at which energy is transferred**, or the rate at which work is done. **1 watt = 1 joule per second.** 1 kW = 1000 W.

**Example that illustrates the definition (spec requirement).** Two electric motors each lift a 50 N weight through 2.0 m, so each does $50 \times 2.0 = 100$ J of work. Motor A takes 4.0 s: $P = 100 \div 4.0 = 25$ W. Motor B takes 10 s: $P = 100 \div 10 = 10$ W. Same energy transferred, but **motor A is more powerful because it transfers the energy faster**.

**Rearranged.** $E = P t$ and $t = E \div P$. A 2.0 kW kettle switched on for 3 minutes transfers $2000 \times 180 = 360\,000$ J.

::: terms
- **System**: an object or group of objects being studied, whose energy stores we track.
- **Energy store**: a way energy is held by a system, such as kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential or thermal.
- **Kinetic energy**: energy stored by a moving object; it depends on mass and the square of speed.
- **Gravitational potential energy**: energy stored by an object because of its height in a gravitational field.
- **Elastic potential energy**: energy stored in an object that has been stretched, squashed or bent.
- **Specific heat capacity**: the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C.
- **Power**: the rate at which energy is transferred or the rate at which work is done, measured in watts.
- **Watt**: the unit of power; one watt is an energy transfer of one joule per second.
- **Work done**: the energy transferred when a force moves an object, or when a current flows.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** an energy change: name the store that decreases, the store(s) that increase and how the energy is transferred (by heating, by a force doing work, by an electric current). "Kinetic energy turns into heat" scores less than "energy is transferred from the kinetic store of the car to the thermal store of the brakes by friction doing work".
- **Explain**: give the change and the reason ("…because air resistance does work on the ball, so some energy is dissipated to the thermal store of the air").
- **Compare** (e.g. two motors): make a comparative statement for both — "motor A transfers the same energy in less time, so it has the greater power".
- **Suggest**: apply the idea to a new situation; any sensible physics-based answer gets credit.

**Calculations (usually 2–4 marks)**
1. Write the equation in symbols or words — this alone often earns the first mark.
2. Convert units **before** substituting: g → kg, cm → m, kJ → J, minutes → s.
3. Substitute, then rearrange if needed (it is fine to rearrange after substituting).
4. Give the answer with a **unit** and sensible significant figures (match the data, usually 2 or 3).
5. In multi-step questions ("calculate the speed…"), find the energy first, then use conservation: energy lost from one store = energy gained by another (if no energy is dissipated).
6. Remember which equations are **given** ($E_e = \tfrac{1}{2} k e^2$ and $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$) and which you must **recall** ($E_k$, $E_p$, both power equations).

**Required practical 1 questions**
- Name the variables and say how the **energy** is measured (joulemeter, or $E = VIt$ from ammeter, voltmeter and stopwatch).
- Explain why the measured $c$ is **too high** (energy lost to surroundings, so temperature rise is too small) and suggest an improvement (more insulation, lid, oil in the thermometer hole).
- From a graph of temperature against energy: $c = 1 \div (m \times \text{gradient})$; use the straight-line section.

**6-mark answers (levels of response)**
Level 3 (5–6): a detailed, logically ordered method or explanation that could be followed to get valid results. Level 2 (3–4): the main steps present, some detail missing. Level 1 (1–2): a few relevant points, not linked. For a method: equipment → measurements → how the energy is found → calculation → safety and how to improve accuracy.
:::

::: example Kinetic energy with a unit conversion
A 750 g ball moves at 12 m/s. Calculate its kinetic energy. (3)

Mass $= 750 \div 1000 = 0.75$ kg (1). $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = 0.5 \times 0.75 \times 12^2$ (1) $= 54$ J (1).
:::

::: example Energy dissipated on a roller coaster
A 500 kg roller coaster car starts from rest and descends a 20 m drop. At the bottom its speed is 18 m/s. ($g = 9.8$ N/kg.) (a) Calculate the speed it would have if no energy were dissipated. (b) Calculate the energy dissipated. (5)

(a) $E_p$ lost $= mgh = 500 \times 9.8 \times 20 = 98\,000$ J (1). If all goes to the kinetic store: $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = 98\,000$, so $v = \sqrt{2 \times 98\,000 \div 500} = \sqrt{392}$ (1) $= 19.8$ m/s (1).
(b) Actual $E_k = 0.5 \times 500 \times 18^2 = 81\,000$ J (1). Dissipated $= 98\,000 - 81\,000 = 17\,000$ J (to thermal stores of the track, car and air, by friction and air resistance) (1).
:::

::: example Required practical 1 data
A student heats a 1.0 kg aluminium block with a 12 V heater drawing 4.0 A for 300 s. The temperature rises by 15.5 °C. (a) Calculate the specific heat capacity. (b) The accepted value is 900 J/kg °C. Explain the difference and suggest one improvement. (5)

(a) $E = VIt = 12 \times 4.0 \times 300 = 14\,400$ J (1). $c = \dfrac{E}{m\Delta\theta} = \dfrac{14\,400}{1.0 \times 15.5}$ (1) $= 929$ J/kg °C, about 930 J/kg °C (1).
(b) The value is too high because some energy was transferred to the surroundings (and to heating the heater/thermometer), so the temperature rise was smaller than if all the energy went into the block (1). Improvement: add more insulation around the block, or put oil in the thermometer hole for better thermal contact (1).
:::

::: example Power of a crane
A crane lifts an 800 kg load through 15 m in 40 s. ($g = 9.8$ N/kg.) Calculate the useful power output. (3)

Work done (energy into the gravitational store) $= mgh = 800 \times 9.8 \times 15 = 117\,600$ J (1). $P = W \div t = 117\,600 \div 40$ (1) $= 2940$ W, about 2.9 kW (1).
:::

::: example Elastic to kinetic: a catapult
A catapult with spring constant 500 N/m is stretched by 12 cm and fires a 20 g stone. Assuming all the elastic potential energy becomes kinetic energy, calculate the launch speed. (4)

$E_e = \tfrac{1}{2} k e^2 = 0.5 \times 500 \times 0.12^2 = 3.6$ J (1). $E_k = 3.6$ J, so $v = \sqrt{2E_k \div m} = \sqrt{2 \times 3.6 \div 0.020}$ (1) $= \sqrt{360}$ (1) $= 19$ m/s (1). (Converting 12 cm to 0.12 m and 20 g to 0.020 kg is essential.)
:::

::: watch
- **Squaring the wrong thing**: in $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ only $v$ is squared; in $E_e = \tfrac{1}{2}ke^2$ only $e$ is squared. Doing $(0.5 \times m \times v)^2$ loses the answer marks.
- **Units not converted**: grams, centimetres, kilojoules and minutes must become kg, m, J and s before substituting.
- **Learn which equations to recall**: $E_k$, $E_p$ and both power equations are **not** on the equation sheet.
- **Vague energy language**: say "transferred from the kinetic store to the thermal store", not "kinetic energy is lost" or "energy is used up". Energy is never used up.
- **Required practical 1**: the calculated specific heat capacity is too **high**, not too low, when energy is lost to the surroundings — the temperature rise is smaller than it should be.
- **Power is a rate**: a more powerful device does not transfer more energy; it transfers energy **faster**.
:::

An electric kettle heats 1.2 kg of water from 15 °C to 100 °C. (a) Calculate the energy transferred to the water (c = 4200 J/kg °C). (b) The kettle has a power of 2.4 kW. Calculate the minimum time this takes. (4)
