## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection (4.4.4) is in Physics 8463 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. It covers the two ways of releasing energy from atomic nuclei: **nuclear fission** (splitting a large nucleus — used in nuclear power stations and weapons) and **nuclear fusion** (joining small nuclei — the energy source of the Sun). You must be able to **draw and interpret diagrams** of fission and of a chain reaction. There is no required practical and nothing here is Higher tier only.

### Nuclear fission

> **Nuclear fission** is the **splitting of a large and unstable nucleus**, such as **uranium** (uranium-235) or **plutonium** (plutonium-239).

**Spontaneous fission** (a nucleus splitting on its own) is **rare**. Usually, for fission to happen, the unstable nucleus must **first absorb a neutron**. This makes it even more unstable, and it splits.

When a nucleus undergoes fission:

1. it splits into **two smaller nuclei** (the **daughter nuclei**), **roughly equal in size**;
2. it emits **two or three neutrons**;
3. it emits **gamma rays**;
4. **energy is released** by the fission reaction.

**All of the fission products have kinetic energy** — the two daughter nuclei and the neutrons fly apart at high speed. In a reactor, this kinetic energy heats the surrounding material; the thermal energy is used to boil water, make steam and turn turbines connected to generators.

A typical fission of uranium-235 can be written as a nuclear equation (you do not need to recall it, but you should be able to check that it balances):

$${}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$$

Top numbers: $1 + 235 = 236$ and $141 + 92 + 3 = 236$. Bottom numbers: $0 + 92 = 92$ and $56 + 36 + 0 = 92$. Uranium-235 does not always split into the same pair — barium and krypton is one of many possible pairs.

### Chain reactions

The **neutrons released** by one fission **may go on to be absorbed by other uranium nuclei**, causing **more fissions**, which release **more neutrons**, and so on. This is a **chain reaction**.

In the diagram, each fission releases neutrons and two of them go on to cause further fissions, so the number of fissions doubles each "generation": 1, 2, 4, 8, ... After $n$ generations there are $2^{n}$ fissions — the energy released grows extremely quickly.

**Controlled and uncontrolled chain reactions:**

- **Nuclear reactor — controlled.** The chain reaction is **controlled** so that the **energy is released at a steady rate**. **Control rods** (made of boron or cadmium) **absorb neutrons**; lowering them further into the core absorbs more neutrons and slows the reaction, raising them speeds it up. They are adjusted so that, **on average, exactly one neutron from each fission goes on to cause another fission**. A **moderator** (water or graphite) slows the neutrons down so that they are more likely to be absorbed by uranium-235 nuclei. Thick **concrete shielding** absorbs the radiation.
- **Nuclear weapon — uncontrolled.** The explosion of a nuclear weapon is caused by an **uncontrolled chain reaction**: each fission causes more than one further fission, so the number of fissions — and the energy released — increases enormously in a tiny fraction of a second.

### Nuclear fusion

> **Nuclear fusion** is the **joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus**.

In this process **some of the mass may be converted into the energy of radiation**: the mass of the heavier nucleus (plus any particles released) is slightly **less** than the total mass of the nuclei that fused, and the "missing" mass has been released as energy.

Fusion is the energy source of **stars**: in the Sun, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium. One fusion reaction studied for power stations joins two isotopes of hydrogen:

$${}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$$

Check: top $2 + 3 = 4 + 1$; bottom $1 + 1 = 2 + 0$.

Fusion only happens at **extremely high temperatures and pressures**: nuclei are positively charged and repel each other, so they must move very fast and be squeezed very close together to fuse. This is why it is very hard to achieve on Earth; experimental fusion reactors exist, but none yet produces more useful energy than it uses.

### Fission and fusion compared

| | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | a large nucleus **splits** into two smaller nuclei | two light nuclei **join** to form a heavier nucleus |
| Typical nuclei | uranium-235, plutonium-239 | isotopes of hydrogen |
| How it starts | usually the nucleus absorbs a neutron | very high temperature and pressure |
| Products | two daughter nuclei, 2 or 3 neutrons, gamma rays | a heavier nucleus (often plus a neutron) |
| Energy released | yes | yes — more per kilogram of fuel than fission |
| Where used | nuclear power stations; weapons | stars (the Sun); experimental reactors |
| Waste | radioactive daughter nuclei, some with long half-lives | very little long-lived radioactive waste |

::: terms
- **Nuclear fission**: the splitting of a large, unstable nucleus such as uranium-235 or plutonium-239 into two smaller nuclei.
- **Spontaneous fission**: fission that happens without the nucleus first absorbing a neutron; it is rare.
- **Daughter nuclei**: the two smaller nuclei, roughly equal in size, produced when a nucleus undergoes fission.
- **Chain reaction**: a process in which neutrons released by one fission cause further fissions, which release more neutrons.
- **Control rods**: rods of boron or cadmium in a reactor that absorb neutrons to control the rate of the chain reaction.
- **Moderator**: a material such as water or graphite that slows down neutrons in a reactor so they are more likely to cause fission.
- **Nuclear fusion**: the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, in which some mass is converted to energy of radiation.
:::

::: method
**Describing fission (the four products)**
A full description needs: a neutron is **absorbed** → the nucleus becomes unstable and **splits** → **two smaller nuclei** (roughly equal in size) + **2 or 3 neutrons** + **gamma rays** → **energy released** (all the products have kinetic energy). Each item is usually a mark.

**Drawing and interpreting diagrams**
- Fission diagram: incoming neutron → large nucleus → two smaller nuclei + 2 or 3 neutrons (show the arrows).
- Chain reaction diagram: show at least **one neutron from each fission** going on to hit **another** large nucleus. Label the neutrons.
- Interpreting: count the neutrons per fission and how many cause further fissions; more than one per fission means the reaction is growing (uncontrolled); exactly one means a steady, controlled rate.

**Nuclear equations for fission and fusion**: add up the top numbers and the bottom numbers on each side, remembering that "3 n" means three neutrons, so $3 \times 1 = 3$ on top. Use this to find a missing mass number, atomic number or number of neutrons.

**Command words**
- **Explain** how a reactor is controlled: control rods **absorb neutrons** → fewer neutrons cause further fissions → rate of fission and energy release falls. Do not say control rods "slow down" neutrons — that is the moderator.
- **Compare** fission and fusion: paired statements — what happens to the nuclei, which nuclei, conditions needed, products, uses.
- **Calculate** (grade 8–9): number of fissions after $n$ generations ($2^{n}$ or $3^{n}$); or energy and power using $P = \frac{E}{t}$ (recall) with the energy per fission.

**Extended responses (6 marks)** on fission, chain reactions and reactors are marked by levels: Level 3 gives a logical sequence from neutron absorption through the chain reaction to how it is controlled (or not, in a weapon), with correct terms.
:::

::: example Describing fission
Describe what happens when a nucleus of uranium-235 undergoes fission. (4)

The uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron (1). It becomes unstable and splits into two smaller nuclei, roughly equal in size (1). Two or three neutrons are released, together with gamma rays (1). Energy is released; all the fission products have kinetic energy (1).
:::

::: example Balancing a fission equation
Find the value of $x$: ${}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{144}_{55}\text{Cs} + {}^{90}_{37}\text{Rb} + x\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$. (2)

Total mass number on the left $= 1 + 235 = 236$ (1). $236 - 144 - 90 = 2$, so $x = 2$ (two neutrons) (1). Check bottom numbers: $92 = 55 + 37$.
:::

::: example Controlled and uncontrolled
Explain the difference between the chain reaction in a nuclear reactor and in a nuclear weapon. (3)

In a reactor the chain reaction is controlled: control rods absorb neutrons so that on average only one neutron from each fission causes another fission (1), so energy is released at a steady rate (1). In a weapon the chain reaction is uncontrolled: each fission causes more than one further fission, so the energy is released almost instantly in an explosion (1).
:::

::: example Chain reaction growth
In an uncontrolled chain reaction, each fission causes 3 further fissions. Starting from one fission, how many fissions occur in the 5th generation (the 1st generation being the single starting fission)? (2)

Generations: 1, 3, 9, 27, 81 (1). So 81 fissions in the 5th generation ($3^{4} = 81$) (1).
:::

::: example Fusion
Describe nuclear fusion and explain why it releases energy. (3)

Two light nuclei (such as isotopes of hydrogen) join together (1) to form a heavier nucleus (1). The mass of the products is less than the mass of the original nuclei, and this mass is converted into the energy of radiation (1).
:::

::: watch
- Fission needs a neutron to be **absorbed** — do not say the neutron "hits and splits" the nucleus without being absorbed, or that it is "fired at an atom" (it is the **nucleus**).
- Fission gives **two** smaller nuclei **and 2 or 3 neutrons and gamma rays** — missing the neutrons loses a mark, and they are the key to the chain reaction.
- **Control rods absorb neutrons**; the **moderator slows** them. Swapping these is a common error.
- Fission **splits** large nuclei; fusion **joins** light nuclei. Learn the spelling: fis**s**ion and fu**s**ion are easy to confuse in a hurry.
- In fusion, energy comes from **mass being converted** into energy of radiation — the products have **less** mass than the original nuclei.
- A chain reaction diagram must show neutrons from one fission going on to cause **another** fission.
:::

::: test
A student draws a chain reaction in which each fission releases 2 neutrons and both cause further fissions. Starting from a single fission, calculate how many fissions occur in the 10th generation, and explain why a reactor must be designed to prevent this. (3)
---
The number doubles each generation: $2^{9}$ (1) $= 512$ fissions in the 10th generation (1). The energy released would increase very rapidly and out of control, so control rods absorb neutrons until on average only one neutron per fission causes another fission (1).

::: test
Each fission of uranium-235 releases about $3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J. A reactor core has a thermal power of $3.2 \times 10^{9}$ W. Calculate the number of fissions taking place each second. (3)
---
Energy released per second $= 3.2 \times 10^{9}$ J (since $P = \frac{E}{t}$, with $t$ = 1 s) (1). Number of fissions $= \frac{3.2 \times 10^{9}}{3.2 \times 10^{-11}}$ (1) $= 1.0 \times 10^{20}$ fissions per second (1).
:::

The missing mass has been converted into energy (the energy of radiation released) (1). Fusion releases a large amount of energy per kilogram of fuel (1), and its fuel (hydrogen isotopes) is plentiful and it produces very little long-lived radioactive waste (1).
