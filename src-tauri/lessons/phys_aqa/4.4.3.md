## What it is

**Separate science only:** this whole subsection (4.4.3) is in Physics 8463 but **not** in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. It covers **background radiation** and **radiation dose**, why the hazard from a radioactive material depends on its **half-life**, and how nuclear radiation is **used in medicine** — and how to weigh the benefits against the risks. It builds on 4.4.2 (alpha, beta, gamma, half-life, contamination and irradiation). There is no required practical and nothing here is Higher tier only.

### Background radiation

> **Background radiation** is the low level of nuclear radiation that is **around us all of the time**.

It comes from:

- **natural sources** — **rocks** (especially granite) and the **radon gas** that seeps out of the ground; **cosmic rays** from space; and small amounts of radioactive isotopes in food, drink and our own bodies;
- **man-made sources** — such as the **fallout from nuclear weapons testing** and **nuclear accidents** (for example Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011). Medical X-rays and scans also add to the dose people receive.

Most of the average person's background dose in the UK comes from natural sources, with radon gas the biggest single contributor.

**The level of background radiation and radiation dose may be affected by occupation and/or location:**

| Factor | Example | Why the dose is higher |
|---|---|---|
| Location | living in areas with granite rock (e.g. Cornwall) | more radon gas seeps out of the rock into homes |
| Location | living at high altitude | less atmosphere above to absorb cosmic rays |
| Occupation | airline pilots and cabin crew | spend long hours at high altitude, exposed to more cosmic rays |
| Occupation | nuclear power workers, radiographers, miners | work near radioactive sources or in radon-rich mines |

Background radiation must be **measured and subtracted** from count rates in experiments (4.4.2).

### Radiation dose

**Radiation dose** measures the risk of harm to the body from exposure to radiation. It is measured in **sieverts (Sv)**. Doses are usually small, so **millisieverts (mSv)** are used:

$$1000 \text{ mSv} = 1 \text{ Sv}$$

You will **not** need to recall the unit of radiation dose — it will be given — but you must be able to convert and compare doses. The average UK background dose is roughly 2.7 mSv per year.

*Mini-example.* Convert 2.7 mSv to sieverts in standard form: $\frac{2.7}{1000} = 0.0027$ Sv $= 2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ Sv.

*Mini-example.* A CT scan gives a dose of about 8 mSv. How many years of average background radiation is this? $\frac{8}{2.7} \approx 3$ years.

### Different half-lives, different hazards

**Radioactive isotopes have a very wide range of half-life values** — from fractions of a second to billions of years. You must be able to use data like these in standard form:

| Isotope | Half-life | In standard form |
|---|---|---|
| polonium-214 | 0.000 16 s | $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$ s |
| technetium-99m | 6 hours | $2.2 \times 10^{4}$ s |
| iodine-131 | 8 days | $6.9 \times 10^{5}$ s |
| carbon-14 | 5700 years | $5.7 \times 10^{3}$ years |
| uranium-238 | 4 500 000 000 years | $4.5 \times 10^{9}$ years |

**Why the hazard depends on the half-life:**

- An isotope with a **short half-life** decays quickly, so for the same number of nuclei it has a **high activity**: it gives out a lot of radiation in a short time and is **very dangerous at first**. But the activity soon falls to a safe level, so the hazard **does not last long**.
- An isotope with a **long half-life** has a **lower activity** (for the same number of nuclei), but it **stays radioactive for a very long time**, so it remains a hazard for years, centuries or longer. This is why nuclear waste containing long half-life isotopes must be stored securely for thousands of years.

The overall hazard also depends on the **type of radiation** (alpha is most dangerous inside the body; beta and gamma from outside) and on whether exposure is **contamination** or **irradiation** (4.4.2).

### Uses of nuclear radiation in medicine

Nuclear radiations are used in medicine for:

1. the **exploration of internal organs** (diagnosis);
2. the **control or destruction of unwanted tissue** (treatment, e.g. of cancer).

**Exploring internal organs — radioactive tracers.** A patient swallows or is injected with a **tracer**: a substance containing a radioactive isotope (for example **technetium-99m**, or **iodine-123** for the thyroid gland). The tracer travels to the organ, and a **gamma camera** outside the body detects the radiation, building an image of where the tracer has collected and how well the organ is working. A good tracer:

- emits **gamma** rays — they penetrate the body to reach the detector and are only weakly ionising, so they do less damage to cells. Alpha would not get out of the body and would be very damaging inside it;
- has a **short half-life** (hours) — long enough to complete the scan, short enough that the activity soon falls and the patient's dose is small;
- is not toxic and decays to a product that is not harmful.

**Controlling or destroying unwanted tissue — radiotherapy.** High doses of ionising radiation kill cells, so they can destroy cancer cells:

- **External beams**: **gamma rays** (or high-energy X-rays) are aimed at a tumour from **several directions**. The beams overlap at the tumour, which receives a high dose, while healthy tissue along each path receives a much smaller dose. The patient is **irradiated** — they do not become radioactive.
- **Internal sources**: a radioactive implant (often a beta or gamma emitter) is placed in or next to the tumour, so the radiation reaches the tumour but has a short range in tissue. Iodine-131 (a beta emitter) is swallowed to treat thyroid cancer, because the thyroid absorbs iodine. Here the patient **is** contaminated for a while, and must follow precautions.

| | Diagnosis (tracer) | Treatment (radiotherapy) |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | see an organ working | kill unwanted cells |
| Radiation usually used | gamma | gamma beams; beta or gamma implants |
| Dose | as low as possible | high, aimed precisely at the tumour |
| Half-life (internal source) | short — hours | long enough to deliver the dose; implant may be removed |

**Side effects and risks.** Radiation also damages healthy cells. In radiotherapy this causes side effects (tiredness, sore skin, hair loss in the treated area). Any dose of ionising radiation slightly increases the risk of mutations in DNA and of developing cancer later.

### Evaluating perceived risks

People's **perceived risk** of radiation (how dangerous they think it is) is often higher than the **actual risk** shown by data. To evaluate a use of nuclear radiation, weigh:

- the **benefit** — diagnosing or curing a serious illness, which may be life-saving;
- the **risk** — the **probability** of harm (which increases with dose) and the **consequences** (how serious that harm would be);
- the data — compare the dose with background radiation and with other everyday doses.

*Example of using data.* A medical scan gives 2 mSv, about the same as nine months of background radiation. The extra risk of cancer from this dose is very small, while the benefit of correctly diagnosing a serious illness is large — so the scan is justified. Doctors still keep doses **as low as possible**, and do not scan without a good reason.

::: terms
- **Background radiation**: the nuclear radiation around us all of the time, from natural and man-made sources.
- **Cosmic rays**: high-energy radiation from space that contributes to background radiation, more so at high altitude.
- **Radon**: a radioactive gas that seeps out of rocks such as granite and is the largest natural source of background dose.
- **Radiation dose**: a measure of the risk of harm from exposure to radiation, measured in sieverts (Sv).
- **Sievert**: the unit of radiation dose; 1000 millisieverts equal 1 sievert.
- **Medical tracer**: a radioactive substance put into the body so a detector outside can show how an organ is working.
- **Radiotherapy**: using ionising radiation to control or destroy unwanted tissue such as cancer cells.
- **Perceived risk**: how dangerous people believe something to be, which can differ from the risk shown by data.
:::

::: method
**Command words in this topic**
- **Describe** a use: what is put where, which radiation, what detects it or what it does to the cells.
- **Explain** the choice of isotope: link each property to a reason — **type** of radiation (penetration, ionisation), **half-life** (long enough to do the job, short enough to limit the dose), and whether it is toxic.
- **Evaluate**: give advantages **and** disadvantages (benefit vs risk), use any data given, and finish with a justified **conclusion** ("the benefit outweighs the risk because..."). Without a conclusion you cannot reach the top marks.
- **Suggest** why dose differs: link occupation or location to a specific source (cosmic rays at altitude, radon from granite).

**Half-life and hazard — the two-sided answer**
Short half-life → high activity → intense hazard **but** short-lived. Long half-life → lower activity → less intense **but** lasts a long time. Examiners want both the intensity and the duration.

**Data in standard form**
Convert units before comparing: mSv → Sv divide by 1000; hours or days → seconds if the data mixes units. Compare doses as a ratio ("about 3 times the annual background dose").

**Extended responses (6 marks)** on medical uses are marked by levels. Level 3 answers describe **both** uses (exploration and treatment), choose the radiation for each with reasons, discuss half-life, and **weigh** the risks against the benefits with a conclusion. Listing facts without reasons stays in Level 1–2.
:::

::: example Background sources
Give two natural and one man-made source of background radiation. (3)

Natural: radon gas from rocks such as granite (1); cosmic rays from space (1). Man-made: fallout from nuclear weapons testing or nuclear accidents (1).
:::

::: example Dose conversion and comparison
An airline pilot receives an extra 3.5 mSv per year from cosmic rays. The average background dose is 2.7 mSv per year. Calculate the pilot's total annual dose in sieverts, and suggest why it is higher. (3)

Total $= 3.5 + 2.7 = 6.2$ mSv (1) $= 6.2 \times 10^{-3}$ Sv (1). Pilots spend long periods at high altitude, where there is less atmosphere to absorb cosmic rays (1).
:::

::: example Choosing a medical tracer
A hospital chooses technetium-99m (gamma emitter, half-life 6 hours) as a tracer to examine a patient's kidneys. Explain why it is a suitable choice. (4)

It emits gamma rays, which pass out of the body so they can be detected by a camera outside (1), and gamma is weakly ionising so it causes less damage to cells (1). A 6-hour half-life is long enough to carry out the scan (1) but short enough that the activity falls quickly, so the patient receives a low dose (1).
:::

::: example Half-life and hazard
Radon-222 has a half-life of 3.8 days and uranium-238 has a half-life of $4.5 \times 10^{9}$ years. Compare the hazards from equal numbers of nuclei of each. (3)

Radon-222 decays much faster, so its activity is much higher and it is more hazardous at first (1). But its activity soon falls — after a few weeks very little is left (1). Uranium-238 has a very low activity but remains radioactive for billions of years, so its hazard lasts far longer (1).
:::

::: example Radiotherapy beams
Explain why gamma rays used to treat a tumour are aimed from several different directions. (2)

The beams overlap at the tumour, so it receives a high (lethal) dose (1), while each path through healthy tissue gets only a small dose, reducing damage to healthy cells (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the hazards of a radioactive material depend on its half-life. (3)
---
A short half-life means a high activity, so a lot of radiation is emitted quickly — high hazard at first (1). But the activity falls quickly, so the hazard does not last long (1). A long half-life means lower activity but the material stays radioactive for a long time, so the hazard lasts much longer (e.g. nuclear waste must be stored for thousands of years) (1).

::: test
Iodine-131 (beta and gamma emitter, half-life 8 days) is swallowed to destroy cancer cells in the thyroid gland. Suggest why this isotope is suitable and why the patient is given precautions to follow at home. (4)
---
The thyroid absorbs iodine, so the isotope collects where it is needed (1). Beta particles are ionising and have a short range in tissue, so they kill the nearby cancer cells (1). The 8-day half-life is long enough to deliver a large dose but the activity falls substantially within weeks (1). The patient is contaminated (the isotope is inside them), so they emit radiation that could irradiate others and their body fluids contain radioactive material — so they must limit close contact and follow hygiene rules for a time (1).
:::

::: test
Isotope X has a half-life of $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ s and isotope Y has a half-life of $3.0 \times 10^{4}$ years. A scientist says Y is "more dangerous to store" but X is "more dangerous to stand near when freshly made". Evaluate this statement. (4)
---
X has an extremely short half-life, so for a similar number of nuclei it has a very high activity: freshly made, it gives out intense radiation (1). But almost all of it decays within a fraction of a second (after 10 half-lives, 0.02 s, about a thousandth remains), so it is no long-term storage problem (1). Y has a very long half-life, so its activity is low but it stays radioactive for hundreds of thousands of years, so it must be stored securely (1). The statement is therefore correct (the hazard also depends on the type of radiation each emits) (1).
:::

::: test
A patient with suspected heart disease is offered a scan using a gamma-emitting tracer, which gives a dose of 10 mSv. The average annual background dose is 2.7 mSv, and the patient is worried about the risk. Evaluate the use of the scan. (4)
---
The dose is about 10 ÷ 2.7 ≈ 3.7 years' worth of background radiation (1). Ionising radiation can damage DNA, so the dose slightly increases the risk of cancer in the future (1). However, the probability of harm from this dose is very small, while undiagnosed heart disease has serious consequences, and the scan lets doctors diagnose and treat it (1). Conclusion: the benefit outweighs the small risk, so the scan is justified — the patient's perceived risk is higher than the actual risk (1).
:::
