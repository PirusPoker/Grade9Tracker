## What it is

**This whole subsection (AQA 4.5.7, Momentum) is Higher tier only.** Foundation students can skip it. The definition of momentum and the idea of conservation of momentum are in both GCSE Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 6.5.5). **Calculations on collisions and explosions, and changes in momentum ($F = m\Delta v / \Delta t$) and safety features, are separate science only.**

### Momentum is a property of moving objects (Higher tier)

Every moving object has **momentum**. The faster it moves and the more mass it has, the more momentum it has.

**Equation (recall — you must learn it):**

momentum = mass × velocity

$$p = mv$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| momentum | $p$ | kilogram metre per second, kg m/s |
| mass | $m$ | kilogram, kg |
| velocity | $v$ | metre per second, m/s |

Momentum is a **vector**: it has the same direction as the velocity. Choose one direction as positive (e.g. right = +) and give velocities in the other direction a minus sign.

**Mini-example.** A 60 kg runner moves at 5.0 m/s to the right: $p = 60 \times 5.0 = 300$ kg m/s to the right. A 1200 kg car moves at 15 m/s to the left: $p = 1200 \times (-15) = -18\,000$ kg m/s, i.e. 18 000 kg m/s to the left.

**Mini-example.** A ball of mass 0.16 kg has momentum 4.8 kg m/s. Its velocity is $v = p \div m = 4.8 \div 0.16 = 30$ m/s.

### Conservation of momentum (Higher tier)

**In a closed system, the total momentum before an event is equal to the total momentum after the event.** This is called **conservation of momentum**.

A **closed system** is one where no external forces act (or the external forces balance), e.g. friction and air resistance are negligible. An **event** may be a **collision** (objects hit each other) or an **explosion** (objects push apart, e.g. a gun firing, two skaters pushing off each other).

**Describing and explaining events (both courses):**
- **Collision**: when a moving trolley hits a stationary one and they stick together, the momentum is shared. The combined mass is larger, so the joint velocity is smaller than the first trolley's velocity, but the total momentum is the same.
- **Explosion from rest**: the total momentum before is **zero**, so the total momentum after must be zero. The parts move off in **opposite directions** with **equal-sized momenta**. The lighter part moves faster. This is why a gun **recoils** when it fires a bullet, and why a skater who pushes another skater away moves backwards.
- **Rockets and jet engines**: hot gas is pushed backwards with momentum, so the rocket gains equal momentum forwards.

**Investigating collisions.** Use laboratory trolleys on a friction-compensated runway, with **light gates and data loggers** (or ticker timers) to measure the velocities before and after a collision. Velcro or a pin and cork makes the trolleys stick together. Calculate total momentum before and after; the totals should be equal within experimental error (small losses come from friction).

### Separate science only: momentum calculations (Higher tier)

Physics 8463 students must complete calculations involving an event such as the collision of two objects.

**Method:**
1. Choose a positive direction.
2. Total momentum before $= m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2$.
3. Total momentum after $= m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2$ (or $(m_1 + m_2)v$ if they stick together).
4. Set them equal and solve.

**Mini-example — trolleys stick together.** A 2.0 kg trolley at 3.0 m/s hits a stationary 1.0 kg trolley and they move off together.
- Before: $2.0 \times 3.0 + 1.0 \times 0 = 6.0$ kg m/s.
- After: $(2.0 + 1.0) \times v = 3.0v$.
- $3.0v = 6.0$, so $v = 2.0$ m/s in the original direction.

**Mini-example — recoil.** A 4.0 kg rifle fires a 0.010 kg bullet at 400 m/s.
- Before: total momentum = 0 (everything at rest).
- After: $0.010 \times 400 + 4.0 \times v = 0$, so $4.0 + 4.0v = 0$ and $v = -1.0$ m/s.
- The rifle recoils at 1.0 m/s in the **opposite** direction to the bullet.

**Mini-example — head-on collision.** A 1000 kg car moving right at 20 m/s collides with a 1500 kg van moving left at 10 m/s. They lock together.
- Before: $1000 \times 20 + 1500 \times (-10) = 20\,000 - 15\,000 = 5000$ kg m/s.
- After: $2500v = 5000$, so $v = 2.0$ m/s to the right.

### Separate science only: changes in momentum (Higher tier)

When a force acts on an object that is moving, or able to move, a **change in momentum** occurs.

The equations $F = ma$ and $a = \dfrac{v - u}{t}$ combine to give:

$$F = \frac{m\,\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$

where $m\Delta v$ is the **change in momentum**. So **force equals the rate of change of momentum**.

**This equation is given on the Physics equation sheet — you must be able to apply it.**

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| force | $F$ | newton, N |
| change in momentum | $m\Delta v$ | kg m/s |
| time taken for the change | $\Delta t$ | second, s |

**Mini-example.** A footballer kicks a 0.45 kg ball from rest to 30 m/s. Contact lasts 0.015 s. Change in momentum $= 0.45 \times 30 = 13.5$ kg m/s. $F = 13.5 \div 0.015 = 900$ N.

### Separate science only: safety features (Higher tier)

In a collision a person's momentum must fall to zero. The **change in momentum is fixed** (by their mass and speed), but the **time** over which it happens can be changed. Since $F = \dfrac{m\Delta v}{\Delta t}$:

**Increasing the time taken for the change in momentum reduces the rate of change of momentum, so it reduces the force on the person, which reduces injury.**

| Safety feature | How it increases the time of the momentum change |
|---|---|
| **air bag** | inflates and then deflates gradually as the person moves into it, so the head and chest slow down over a longer time |
| **seat belt** | stretches slightly, so the wearer slows down over a longer time (and stops them hitting the dashboard or windscreen) |
| **gymnasium crash mat** | squashes when landed on, so the gymnast stops over a longer time |
| **cycle helmet** | its crushable foam liner squashes on impact, increasing the time for the head to stop |
| **cushioned playground surface** (rubber, bark chippings) | deforms when a child lands, increasing the time to stop |
| (also) **crumple zones** in cars | the front of the car crumples, so the car stops over a longer time |

**Mini-example.** A 70 kg passenger moving at 15 m/s is stopped by a dashboard in 0.020 s, or by an air bag in 0.15 s.
- Change in momentum $= 70 \times 15 = 1050$ kg m/s in both cases.
- Dashboard: $F = 1050 \div 0.020 = 52\,500$ N. Air bag: $F = 1050 \div 0.15 = 7000$ N — 7.5 times smaller.

**Linking the equations.** Force, mass, velocity and acceleration are all connected: $F = ma$, $a = \Delta v / t$, $p = mv$ and $F = m\Delta v / \Delta t$. A longer stopping time means a smaller deceleration ($a = \Delta v / t$), so a smaller force ($F = ma$) — the same conclusion reached using momentum. The same ideas explain why a cricketer moves the hands back when catching a ball.

::: terms
- **Momentum**: the product of an object's mass and velocity, measured in kg m/s; a vector quantity.
- **Conservation of momentum**: in a closed system the total momentum before an event equals the total momentum after it.
- **Closed system**: a system of objects on which no external resultant force acts, so its total momentum stays constant.
- **Collision**: an event in which moving objects hit each other and exchange momentum.
- **Explosion**: an event in which objects at rest push apart, gaining equal and opposite momenta that total zero.
- **Recoil**: the backward movement of a gun or person as an object is pushed forward, required by conservation of momentum.
- **Rate of change of momentum**: the change in momentum divided by the time taken; it equals the resultant force.
- **Crumple zone**: a part of a car designed to deform in a crash, increasing the time for the momentum change.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe / explain** an event using momentum (both courses): state the total momentum before; say momentum is conserved in a closed system; say what this means for the motion after (e.g. "total momentum is zero before, so the gun and bullet have equal and opposite momentum after; the gun has a larger mass, so a smaller velocity").
- **Explain** a safety feature (separate science only): use the chain **longer time → smaller rate of change of momentum → smaller force → less injury**. The change in momentum itself is the same — never say the feature reduces the momentum change.
- **Calculate** (separate science only): collisions, explosions and forces from momentum changes.

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit)
1. $p = mv$ is **recall**. Unit: **kg m/s**. Convert grams to kg.
2. Momentum is a **vector**: choose a positive direction and give opposite velocities a minus sign. A negative answer means "in the opposite direction" — say so.
3. Conservation: write "total momentum before = total momentum after", calculate each side, then solve. If objects stick together, add their masses.
4. $F = \dfrac{m\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ is on the **equation sheet**. $\Delta v$ is the change in velocity; if the object bounces back, the change is larger (e.g. from +5 to −3 m/s is a change of 8 m/s).
5. Show every step: most momentum questions give method marks for the "before" and "after" totals even if the final answer is wrong.

**Practical questions (collisions)**
Measure velocities before and after with light gates (card length ÷ time) or ticker timers. Compensate for friction by tilting the runway slightly; repeat and average. Results usually show slightly less momentum after because of friction — a closed system is an ideal.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**
Level 3 answers link every step with "so" — e.g. for air bags: the passenger's momentum must change to zero; the air bag increases the time taken; so the rate of change of momentum is smaller; so the force is smaller ($F = m\Delta v / \Delta t$); so injuries are less severe. Level 2 answers miss a link; Level 1 answers just say the feature is "soft" or "cushions".
:::

::: example Calculating momentum
A tennis ball of mass 58 g is served at 50 m/s. Calculate its momentum. (3)

$m = 0.058$ kg (1). $p = mv = 0.058 \times 50$ (1) $= 2.9$ kg m/s (1).
:::

::: example Explaining recoil
A skater of mass 60 kg and a skater of mass 40 kg stand still on ice and push each other apart. Explain, using momentum, why they move off in opposite directions and why the 40 kg skater moves faster. (4)

Total momentum before is zero because both are at rest (1). Momentum is conserved, so total momentum after is zero (1). So they must have equal momenta in opposite directions (1). Since $p = mv$, the skater with the smaller mass has the larger velocity (1).
:::

::: example Trolleys sticking together (separate science only)
A 0.50 kg trolley moving at 0.60 m/s collides with a stationary 0.25 kg trolley. They stick together. Calculate their velocity after the collision. (3)

Momentum before $= 0.50 \times 0.60 = 0.30$ kg m/s (1). After: $(0.50 + 0.25)v = 0.75v$ (1). $v = 0.30 \div 0.75 = 0.40$ m/s in the original direction (1).
:::

::: example One object continues moving (separate science only)
A 3.0 kg trolley moving at 4.0 m/s hits a stationary 2.0 kg trolley. Afterwards the 3.0 kg trolley moves at 1.0 m/s in the same direction. Calculate the velocity of the 2.0 kg trolley. (3)

Before $= 3.0 \times 4.0 = 12$ kg m/s (1). After $= 3.0 \times 1.0 + 2.0v = 3.0 + 2.0v$ (1). $2.0v = 9.0$, $v = 4.5$ m/s in the same direction (1).
:::

::: example Crash mat (separate science only)
A gymnast of mass 50 kg lands at 6.0 m/s. On a hard floor she stops in 0.050 s; on a crash mat she stops in 0.50 s. Calculate the force in each case and explain why the mat reduces injury. (5)

Change in momentum $= 50 \times 6.0 = 300$ kg m/s (1). Floor: $F = 300 \div 0.050 = 6000$ N (1). Mat: $F = 300 \div 0.50 = 600$ N (1). The mat increases the time taken for her momentum to change (1), so the rate of change of momentum, and hence the force on her, is ten times smaller (1).
:::

Calculate the momentum of a 1500 kg car travelling at 20 m/s. (2)
---

:::

::: q
State the principle of conservation of momentum. (2)
---
In a closed system (1), the total momentum before an event equals the total momentum after the event (1).

::: q
A ball of mass 0.25 kg has a momentum of 5.0 kg m/s. Calculate its velocity. (2)
---
$v = p \div m = 5.0 \div 0.25$ (1) $= 20$ m/s (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a cannon moves backwards when it fires a cannonball. (3)
---
Before firing the total momentum is zero (1). Momentum is conserved, so after firing the total is still zero (1). The cannonball has momentum forwards, so the cannon must have equal momentum backwards (1).
:::

::: q
(Separate science only) A 3.0 kg gun fires a 0.020 kg bullet at 300 m/s. Calculate the recoil velocity of the gun. (3)
---
Momentum of bullet $= 0.020 \times 300 = 6.0$ kg m/s (1). Gun: $3.0 \times v = -6.0$ (1). $v = -2.0$ m/s, i.e. 2.0 m/s in the opposite direction to the bullet (1).
:::

::: q
(Separate science only) A 1000 kg car travelling at 20 m/s hits a wall and stops in 0.10 s. Calculate the average force on the car. (2)
---
$F = m\Delta v \div \Delta t = 1000 \times 20 \div 0.10$ (1) $= 200\,000$ N (1).
:::

::: q
(Separate science only) Explain how a seat belt reduces injury in a crash. (3)
---
The seat belt stretches, increasing the time taken for the passenger to stop (1). This reduces the rate of change of momentum (1), so the force on the passenger is smaller (1).
:::

::: q
(Separate science only) A 0.20 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s and bounces straight back at 8.0 m/s. The contact time is 0.050 s. Calculate the average force on the ball. (3)
---
Change in velocity $= 10 - (-8.0) = 18$ m/s (1). Change in momentum $= 0.20 \times 18 = 3.6$ kg m/s (1). $F = 3.6 \div 0.050 = 72$ N (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Momentum is a vector**: in head-on collisions and recoil, one velocity must be negative. Adding the momenta without signs is the most common lost mark.
- **"Momentum is lost" in a collision**: total momentum is conserved in a closed system. (Kinetic energy is often not conserved — do not mix the two.)
- **Forgetting the combined mass**: when objects stick together, use $(m_1 + m_2)v$ after the collision.
- **Safety features**: they do **not** reduce the change in momentum. They **increase the time**, so they reduce the **rate of change** of momentum and so the **force**. All three links are needed.
- **Units**: momentum is **kg m/s**, not N or kg/m/s. Masses in grams must be converted to kg.
- **Bounces**: the change in velocity when an object rebounds is the sum of the two speeds, not the difference.
:::

A boy steps forwards off a small stationary boat onto a jetty. Use the idea of momentum to explain why the boat moves away from the jetty. (3)
---
The total momentum of boy and boat is zero before he steps (1). Momentum is conserved, so the total is still zero afterwards (1). The boy gains momentum towards the jetty, so the boat gains equal momentum in the opposite direction, away from the jetty (1).

::: test
(Separate science only) Trolley A (0.30 kg) moves to the right at 2.0 m/s. Trolley B (0.20 kg) moves to the left at 1.5 m/s. They collide head-on and stick together. Calculate their velocity after the collision, including its direction. (4)
---
Taking right as positive: momentum before $= 0.30 \times 2.0 + 0.20 \times (-1.5)$ (1) $= 0.60 - 0.30 = 0.30$ kg m/s (1). After: $0.50v = 0.30$ (1). $v = 0.60$ m/s to the right (1).

::: test
(Separate science only) (a) Show how the equations $F = ma$ and $a = \dfrac{v - u}{t}$ combine to give $F = \dfrac{m\Delta v}{\Delta t}$. (b) A golf club hits a 0.046 kg ball from rest to 70 m/s. The contact time is 0.000 50 s. Calculate the average force on the ball. (5)
---
(a) Substitute $a$ into $F = ma$: $F = m(v - u) \div t$ (1), and $m(v - u) = m\Delta v$ is the change in momentum, so $F = m\Delta v \div \Delta t$ (1).
(b) Change in momentum $= 0.046 \times 70 = 3.22$ kg m/s (1). $F = 3.22 \div 0.000\,50$ (1) $= 6440$ N ≈ 6400 N (1).
:::

::: test
(Separate science only) An astronaut of mass 60 kg is floating at rest outside a space station. She throws a 2.0 kg tool away from the station at 6.0 m/s. (a) Calculate her velocity after the throw. (b) She is 10 m from the station. Calculate how long she takes to drift back to it. (4)
---
(a) Total momentum before = 0; tool's momentum $= 2.0 \times 6.0 = 12$ kg m/s (1). Astronaut: $60v = -12$, so $v = -0.20$ m/s, i.e. 0.20 m/s towards the station (1).
(b) $t = s \div v = 10 \div 0.20$ (1) $= 50$ s (1).
:::

::: test
(Separate science only) Modern cars have seat belts, air bags and crumple zones. Explain how these features reduce injuries to people in a collision. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a clear explanation using rate of change of momentum (or deceleration) to link increased time to reduced force, applied to at least two of the features.
Level 2 (3–4): describes how at least two features work with a partial explanation, e.g. "increases the time so less force", without the momentum link.
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements, e.g. "the air bag is soft" or "the seat belt holds you in".
Indicative content:
- in a crash the car and passengers lose their momentum (change in momentum is fixed by mass and speed)
- force = rate of change of momentum, $F = m\Delta v / \Delta t$
- increasing the time for the change reduces the force
- crumple zones deform, so the car takes longer to stop
- seat belts stretch slightly, so the passenger takes longer to stop; they also stop the person hitting the windscreen
- air bags inflate and squash as the person moves into them, increasing the stopping time for the head and chest
- smaller force (smaller deceleration) on the body means less serious injury
- also spreads the force over a larger area (air bag, belt) — reduces pressure on the body
:::
