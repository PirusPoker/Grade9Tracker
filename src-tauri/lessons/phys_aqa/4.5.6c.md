## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.5.6.3, **Forces and braking**. It is in both GCSE Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 6.5.4.3). Estimating emergency stopping distances across a vehicle's range of speeds, and reading speed–stopping distance graphs for different vehicles, are **separate science only**. Estimating braking forces is **(Higher tier)**.

### Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance

The **stopping distance** of a vehicle is the sum of:
- the **thinking distance** — how far the vehicle travels during the driver's **reaction time** (between seeing the hazard and pressing the brake);
- the **braking distance** — how far the vehicle travels **under the braking force** once the brakes are applied.

$$\text{stopping distance} = \text{thinking distance} + \text{braking distance}$$

**For a given braking force, the greater the speed of the vehicle, the greater the stopping distance.**

**Thinking distance** = speed × reaction time (from $s = vt$). The car is still moving at its original speed while the driver reacts, so thinking distance is **directly proportional to speed**: double the speed, double the thinking distance.

**Braking distance** depends on the **square of the speed** when the braking force is the same: double the speed and the braking distance is about **four times** as large. This is because the brakes must remove the car's kinetic energy ($E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$), and work done = braking force × distance. You can also see it from $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$: with $v = 0$, braking distance $s = u^2 \div 2a$.

**Mini-example.** A car travels at 20 m/s. The driver's reaction time is 0.70 s and the car decelerates at 7.0 m/s².
- Thinking distance $= 20 \times 0.70 = 14$ m.
- Braking distance $= 20^2 \div (2 \times 7.0) = 400 \div 14 = 28.6$ m.
- Stopping distance $= 14 + 28.6 ≈ 43$ m.

### Typical stopping distances (UK Highway Code values)

| Speed | Speed in m/s | Thinking distance | Braking distance | Stopping distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 mph | 8.9 | 6 m | 6 m | 12 m |
| 30 mph | 13.4 | 9 m | 14 m | 23 m |
| 40 mph | 17.9 | 12 m | 24 m | 36 m |
| 50 mph | 22.4 | 15 m | 38 m | 53 m |
| 60 mph | 26.8 | 18 m | 55 m | 73 m |
| 70 mph | 31.3 | 21 m | 75 m | 96 m |

Thinking distance rises in equal steps (proportional to speed); braking distance rises faster and faster (proportional to speed²). At 70 mph the stopping distance is about **24 car lengths**.

### Separate science only: estimating stopping distances and reading graphs

Physics 8463 students must:
- **estimate how the distance for a vehicle to make an emergency stop varies over a range of speeds typical for that vehicle** — e.g. a car in town (≈ 13 m/s) needs ≈ 23 m; on a motorway (≈ 31 m/s) ≈ 96 m — about four times as far for a bit more than twice the speed;
- **interpret graphs relating speed to stopping distance for a range of vehicles**. On a stopping distance–speed graph the line is a **curve getting steeper** (because braking distance ∝ speed²). Heavier vehicles (lorries, coaches) have larger stopping distances at the same speed because they have more kinetic energy to remove with brakes of limited force; a lorry's curve lies above a car's. A thinking distance–speed graph is a **straight line through the origin**, and its gradient is the reaction time.

### Reaction time

**Reaction times vary from person to person. Typical values range from 0.2 s to 0.9 s.**

A driver's reaction time is increased by **tiredness, drugs and alcohol**. **Distractions** (using a mobile phone, talking to passengers, loud music, eating) can also affect a driver's ability to react. A longer reaction time gives a longer **thinking** distance — it does not change the braking distance.

**Measuring reaction time — the ruler-drop test.**
1. One person holds a metre rule vertically, with the zero mark level with the open thumb and finger of the person being tested.
2. Without warning, the ruler is dropped; the person catches it as quickly as possible.
3. Read the distance the ruler fell. The longer the distance, the longer the reaction time.
4. Convert distance to time using a conversion table (from free fall at 9.8 m/s²), or calculate it: $v^2 = 2as$ gives the ruler's speed, then $t = v \div a$.
5. Repeat several times (e.g. 5) with the same hand and take the mean; ignore anomalies.

| Distance fallen | Reaction time |
|---|---|
| 5 cm | 0.10 s |
| 10 cm | 0.14 s |
| 15 cm | 0.17 s |
| 20 cm | 0.20 s |
| 25 cm | 0.23 s |
| 30 cm | 0.25 s |

**Mini-example.** The ruler falls 20 cm. $v^2 = 2 \times 9.8 \times 0.20 = 3.92$, $v = 1.98$ m/s; $t = 1.98 \div 9.8 = 0.20$ s.

**Other methods:** a computer test where you click as soon as the screen changes colour (the computer times it automatically); a light that comes on with a button to press. Typical results for an alert student are about **0.2–0.3 s**. A reaction in a real car is longer (≈ 0.7 s) because the driver must notice the hazard, decide and move a foot to the pedal.

**Evaluating the measurements.** Results vary between attempts (random error), so repeat and take a mean. Make it a fair test: same dropper, same hand, same starting finger gap, no warning, no countdown. Anticipating the drop makes results unrealistically short. The ruler test is cheap and quick but less precise than a computer timer.

**Investigation: the effect of distractions on reaction time.** Measure a person's reaction time with the ruler-drop test, then repeat while they are distracted (e.g. answering mental maths questions or having a conversation). Independent variable: distraction or not; dependent variable: reaction time; control variables: same person, same hand, same dropper. Typically the mean distance, and so the reaction time, increases with the distraction.

**Evaluating factors from data.** If data show a driver's reaction time rising from 0.6 s to 1.2 s after drinking alcohol, the thinking distance at a given speed doubles. At 25 m/s, thinking distance rises from 15 m to 30 m.

### Factors affecting braking distance

The braking distance can be increased by:
- **adverse road and weather conditions** — **wet or icy** roads (and leaves, oil, loose gravel) reduce the **friction (grip) between the tyres and the road**, so the maximum braking force before skidding is smaller;
- **poor condition of the vehicle** — limited to **brakes or tyres**: worn brakes give a smaller braking force; worn tyres (too little tread) or tyres at the wrong pressure give less grip, especially on wet roads;
- a **greater speed** (and a heavier load, which increases the kinetic energy to be removed).

**Implications for safety:** leave a larger gap to the vehicle in front in wet or icy conditions and at higher speeds; keep brakes and tyres maintained; obey speed limits, especially near schools.

### Energy and forces in braking

When a force is applied to the brakes, **work done by the friction force between the brakes and the wheel reduces the kinetic energy** of the vehicle, and **the temperature of the brakes increases** (energy is transferred to the thermal store of the brakes and surroundings).

$$\text{work done by brakes} = \text{braking force} \times \text{braking distance} = \text{kinetic energy lost}$$

- The **greater the speed**, the **greater the braking force needed** to stop the vehicle in a certain distance.
- The **greater the braking force**, the **greater the deceleration**.
- **Large decelerations** can make the **brakes overheat** and/or make the driver **lose control** (the wheels lock and the car skids). They also mean large forces on the people inside ($F = ma$), causing injuries such as whiplash.

**Estimating braking forces (Higher tier).** A family car (≈ 1200 kg) travelling at ≈ 13 m/s (30 mph) stops in a braking distance of ≈ 14 m.
- Kinetic energy $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 13^2 ≈ 101\,000$ J.
- Braking force $≈ 101\,000 \div 14 ≈ 7200$ N, or about 7000 N.
- Check with $F = ma$: $a = 13^2 \div (2 \times 14) ≈ 6.0$ m/s², $F ≈ 1200 \times 6.0 ≈ 7200$ N.

::: terms
- **Stopping distance**: the total distance a vehicle travels to stop, equal to thinking distance plus braking distance.
- **Thinking distance**: the distance travelled during the driver's reaction time, before the brakes are applied.
- **Braking distance**: the distance travelled while the braking force acts, from pressing the brake until the vehicle stops.
- **Reaction time**: the time between a driver seeing a hazard and responding, typically 0.2 s to 0.9 s.
- **Braking force**: the friction force from the brakes that does work to reduce the vehicle's kinetic energy.
- **Deceleration**: the rate at which a vehicle slows down; larger braking forces give larger decelerations.
- **Adverse conditions**: wet or icy roads that reduce the grip between tyres and road and increase braking distance.
- **Ruler-drop test**: a simple method of measuring reaction time from the distance a dropped ruler falls before it is caught.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Explain** how a factor affects stopping distance: say **which part** it changes (thinking or braking) and **why** — e.g. "alcohol increases reaction time, so the car travels further before braking, so thinking distance increases".
- **Describe** a method (reaction time): equipment, what is measured, repeats and mean, how distance converts to time, control variables.
- **Evaluate** data on reaction times: look at the spread, repeats, anomalies, sample size and whether the test is realistic for driving.
- **Estimate**: use typical values (car ≈ 1000–1500 kg, town speed ≈ 13 m/s, motorway ≈ 31 m/s, reaction time ≈ 0.7 s) and give an approximate answer with ≈.

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit)
1. Thinking distance $= v \times$ reaction time ($s = vt$, recall).
2. Braking distance from $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$ (equation sheet) with $v = 0$: $s = u^2 \div 2a$.
3. Stopping distance = thinking + braking — remember to add them.
4. Braking force (Higher tier): either $F = ma$ after finding $a$, or kinetic energy lost $= F \times d$, so $F = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \div d$.
5. Proportionality shortcuts: double speed → double thinking distance, four times braking distance (same braking force).

**Graph questions (separate science only for vehicle comparisons)**
Thinking distance–speed is a straight line through the origin (gradient = reaction time); braking and stopping distance–speed are upward curves. Read values off carefully and compare vehicles at the **same speed**.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**
Level 3 answers split the factors into those affecting thinking distance (driver: tiredness, drugs, alcohol, distractions, speed) and braking distance (road and weather: wet, icy; vehicle: brakes, tyres; speed; mass) and explain **each** link. Level 2 lists several factors with some explanation; Level 1 lists one or two factors.
:::

::: example Calculating stopping distance
A driver with a reaction time of 0.60 s is travelling at 15 m/s. The braking distance is 16 m. Calculate the stopping distance. (3)

Thinking distance $= 15 \times 0.60$ (1) $= 9.0$ m (1). Stopping distance $= 9.0 + 16 = 25$ m (1).
:::

::: example Braking distance from a deceleration
A car travelling at 24 m/s brakes with a constant deceleration of 8.0 m/s². Calculate the braking distance. (3)

$0^2 - 24^2 = 2 \times (-8.0) \times s$ (1). $s = 576 \div 16$ (1) $= 36$ m (1).
:::

::: example Effect of doubling speed
At 15 m/s a car's thinking distance is 10 m and braking distance is 14 m. Estimate the stopping distance at 30 m/s, assuming the same reaction time and braking force. (3)

Thinking distance doubles to 20 m (1). Braking distance is proportional to speed², so it is $2^2 = 4$ times larger: 56 m (1). Stopping distance ≈ 20 + 56 = 76 m (1).
:::

::: example Ruler-drop reaction time
In a ruler-drop test the ruler falls 0.15 m before it is caught. Take $g$ = 9.8 m/s². Calculate the reaction time. (3)

$v^2 = 2 \times 9.8 \times 0.15 = 2.94$, so $v = 1.71$ m/s (1). $t = v \div a = 1.71 \div 9.8$ (1) $= 0.17$ s (1).
:::

::: example Estimating a braking force (Higher tier)
A car of mass 1500 kg travelling at 25 m/s stops in a braking distance of 40 m. Calculate the average braking force. (3)

Kinetic energy $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1500 \times 25^2 = 468\,750$ J (1). Work done $= F \times d$, so $F = 468\,750 \div 40$ (1) $≈ 12\,000$ N (11 700 N) (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Mixing up the two distances**: alcohol, tiredness, drugs and distractions affect **thinking** distance (reaction time); wet or icy roads and worn brakes or tyres affect **braking** distance. Speed affects **both**.
- **"Reaction time affects braking distance"**: it does not — once the brakes are on, the driver's reactions no longer matter.
- **Forgetting to add**: stopping distance is thinking distance **plus** braking distance.
- **Vague answers**: "the road is slippery" needs the physics — less **friction** between tyres and road, so a smaller braking force.
- **Braking distance is not proportional to speed**: doubling speed gives four times the braking distance.
- **Vehicle condition**: AQA limits this to **brakes and tyres** — answers about lights or mirrors get no credit for braking distance.
- **Energy in braking**: say **work done by friction** reduces kinetic energy and the brakes **heat up**.
:::
