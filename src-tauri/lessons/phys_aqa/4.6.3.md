## What it is

**Separate science only: this whole subsection (AQA 4.6.3, Black body radiation) is in GCSE Physics 8463 but not in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464.** Trilogy students can skip it. Within it, the balance between absorbing and emitting radiation, and the temperature of the Earth, are **(Higher tier)**.

### Every object emits and absorbs infrared

**All bodies (objects), no matter what temperature, emit and absorb infrared radiation.** An ice cube, your hand, a cup of tea and the Sun are all emitting infrared all the time. Even something at −50 °C emits some.

**The hotter the body, the more infrared radiation it radiates in a given time** — that is, the greater the **power** it emits. A mug of tea at 80 °C emits IR faster than the same mug at 30 °C; this is why a thermal (IR) camera shows hotter objects as brighter.

Very hot objects emit visible light as well as IR: an electric grill element glows dull red, a filament lamp glows yellow-white, and the Sun looks white.

### Perfect black bodies

**A perfect black body is an object that absorbs all of the radiation incident on it.** It **does not reflect or transmit any radiation**.

**A good absorber is also a good emitter**, so a perfect black body would be **the best possible emitter** at any given temperature.

No real object is a perfect black body, but a **matt black** surface is close (it absorbs almost all incident light and IR) and a small hole into a hollow box is closer still (radiation entering bounces around inside and is absorbed). This links to Required practical 10 (Trilogy RP21) in 4.6.2: **matt black surfaces are the best absorbers and best emitters; shiny silver surfaces are the worst** (they reflect most radiation).

| Surface | Absorbing | Emitting | Reflecting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect black body | all incident radiation | best possible emitter | none |
| Matt black | very good | very good | very little |
| Shiny silver / white | poor | poor | very good |

### Intensity and wavelength depend on temperature

Students must be able to explain:
- that **all bodies emit radiation**, and
- that **the intensity and the wavelength distribution of any emission depend on the temperature of the body**.

An object does not emit just one wavelength: it emits a **spread (distribution) of wavelengths**, with most of its energy around a **peak wavelength**. On a graph of **intensity** (y-axis) against **wavelength** (x-axis), each temperature gives a hump-shaped curve.

**As the temperature increases:**
1. The **intensity increases at every wavelength** — the whole curve is higher, so far more energy is emitted each second.
2. The **peak moves to shorter wavelengths** (higher frequencies).

| Object | Approximate temperature | Where the peak is | What we see |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earth's surface, people | 15–37 °C (about 290–310 K) | far infrared (about 10 µm) | nothing glows; only IR cameras detect it |
| Grill element | about 700 °C | infrared, with a little red light | dull red glow |
| Filament lamp | about 2500 °C | infrared, with much visible light | yellow-white light (most energy still wasted as IR) |
| The Sun's surface | about 5500 °C | visible light (near 500 nm) | white/yellow light |
| Very hot stars | above 10 000 °C | ultraviolet / blue end | blue-white stars |

This explains why **cooler stars look red** and **hotter stars look blue-white**, and why a piece of metal heated in a flame goes from no glow, to dull red, to orange, to yellow-white as it gets hotter.

### Balancing absorption and emission (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** Every object is **absorbing** radiation from its surroundings and **emitting** radiation at the same time.

- **A body at constant temperature is absorbing radiation at the same rate as it is emitting radiation.**
- **The temperature of a body increases when it absorbs radiation faster than it emits radiation.**
- The temperature **decreases** when it emits radiation faster than it absorbs it.

Because the rate of emission increases with temperature, an object that is heating up emits more and more until **emission catches up with absorption** — then its temperature levels off at a new, higher steady value.

**Everyday examples (Higher tier):**
- **A cup of hot tea** emits IR faster than it absorbs IR from the cooler room, so it cools. As it cools, it emits less. When it reaches room temperature, it absorbs and emits at the same rate, so its temperature stays constant.
- **A car parked in the sun** absorbs radiation faster than it emits, so it warms up. As its temperature rises its emission rises, until the two rates balance and the temperature stays steady (very hot!). A white or silver car reflects more and absorbs less, so it settles at a lower temperature than a matt black car.
- **A can of cold drink taken out of the fridge** absorbs IR from the room faster than it emits, so it warms until it reaches room temperature.

**Mini-example (linking to specific heat capacity).** A 0.50 kg aluminium block (specific heat capacity 900 J/kg °C) in sunlight absorbs radiation at 60 W and emits at 45 W. Assume these rates stay constant for 2.0 minutes.
- Net rate of energy gain $= 60 - 45 = 15$ W.
- Energy gained in 120 s: $E = Pt = 15 \times 120 = 1800$ J.
- Temperature rise: $\Delta\theta = E \div (mc) = 1800 \div (0.50 \times 900) = 4.0$ °C.

(The equation $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ is on the Physics equation sheet; $E = Pt$ must be recalled.)

### The temperature of the Earth (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** The temperature of the Earth depends on many factors, including:
- the **rate of absorption** of radiation (from the Sun),
- the **rate of emission** of radiation (into space),
- the **reflection of radiation into space** (by clouds, ice, snow and light-coloured surfaces).

**How radiation affects the temperature of the Earth's surface and atmosphere:**
1. The Sun is very hot, so it emits mostly **short-wavelength** radiation (visible light, some UV and short-wavelength IR).
2. Some is **reflected back into space** by clouds, ice and the atmosphere — this is not absorbed and does not warm the Earth.
3. The rest passes through the atmosphere and is **absorbed by the Earth's surface**, which warms up.
4. The Earth's surface is much cooler than the Sun, so it emits **longer-wavelength infrared** radiation.
5. **Greenhouse gases** in the atmosphere (carbon dioxide, water vapour, methane) **absorb some of this long-wavelength IR** and **re-emit it in all directions**, including back towards the surface, warming the lower atmosphere and the surface.
6. The rest of the IR escapes into space.
7. The Earth's average temperature stays **roughly constant** when the rate at which it **absorbs** radiation equals the rate at which it **emits** radiation into space.

**What changes the balance:**
- **More greenhouse gases** → less IR escapes to space → the Earth absorbs faster than it emits → the temperature rises until emission increases enough to restore the balance at a **higher temperature** (global warming).
- **More cloud cover** → more sunlight reflected by day (cooling), but also more IR trapped at night (warming) — so cloudy nights are warmer than clear ones.
- **Melting ice** → less sunlight reflected and more absorbed by dark sea and land → further warming.

**Reading diagrams.** Exam diagrams of the Earth usually show thick arrows of incoming short-wavelength radiation, some arrows reflected by clouds, a wavy arrow of IR leaving the surface, and some of that IR turned back by the atmosphere. Use the **arrow sizes or the given values** to decide whether the Earth is absorbing more than it emits (warming), less (cooling) or the same (steady temperature).

::: terms
- **Infrared radiation**: electromagnetic radiation emitted and absorbed by all objects, with wavelengths longer than visible light.
- **Perfect black body**: an object that absorbs all the radiation incident on it, reflecting and transmitting none.
- **Emitter**: an object or surface that gives out radiation; a perfect black body is the best possible emitter.
- **Absorber**: an object or surface that takes in incident radiation; good absorbers are also good emitters.
- **Intensity**: the power of radiation per unit area, shown on the vertical axis of an emission curve.
- **Wavelength distribution**: the spread of wavelengths emitted by a body, with a peak that shifts to shorter wavelength as temperature rises.
- **Radiation balance**: the state where a body absorbs radiation at the same rate as it emits it, so its temperature stays constant.
- **Greenhouse gas**: a gas such as carbon dioxide, methane or water vapour that absorbs long-wavelength infrared emitted by the Earth.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State / give**: short facts — "all objects emit IR", "a perfect black body absorbs all incident radiation".
- **Describe** an emission graph: say what happens to the **height of the curve** (intensity) and the **position of the peak** (wavelength) as temperature changes.
- **Explain** (Higher tier) a temperature change using the **balance**: compare the **rate of absorption** with the **rate of emission**. "Absorbs faster than it emits → temperature rises". Always say **rate**, and finish with what happens when the rates become equal.
- **Explain** the Earth's temperature (Higher tier): short-wavelength in → some reflected → rest absorbed → surface emits long-wavelength IR → greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some → steady when absorption rate = emission rate.
- **Suggest**: apply the same idea to a new situation (a black car, a solar panel, a cooling drink, a planet with no atmosphere).

**Calculations** — rarely on their own, but you may combine the balance with energy equations:
1. Net power = rate of absorption − rate of emission (in W).
2. $E = Pt$ (recall) gives the net energy gained in time $t$ (seconds).
3. $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ (equation sheet) gives the temperature change.
Give units at every step.

**Extended answers (6 marks)** on the Earth's temperature: level 3 needs a **complete sequence** — incoming radiation, reflection, absorption, emission of IR, role of the atmosphere, and the balance idea linked to whether temperature rises, falls or stays constant. Isolated facts ("CO₂ causes global warming") stay at level 1.
:::

::: example What is a perfect black body?
Give two properties of a perfect black body. (2)

It absorbs all the radiation incident on it (it reflects and transmits none) (1). It is the best possible emitter of radiation (1).
:::

::: example Describing emission curves
The graph shows the intensity of radiation emitted at different wavelengths by the same object at 3000 K and at 4000 K. Describe how the curves differ. (2)

The 4000 K curve is higher at every wavelength — the object emits a greater intensity (1). The peak of the 4000 K curve is at a shorter wavelength (1).
:::

::: example Cooling tea (Higher tier)
A cup of tea at 85 °C is left in a room at 20 °C. Explain, in terms of radiation, why its temperature falls and then stays constant. (3)

At first the tea emits radiation faster than it absorbs radiation from the cooler room, so its temperature falls (1). As it cools, its rate of emission decreases (1). At 20 °C it absorbs and emits radiation at the same rate, so its temperature stays constant (1).
:::

::: example Net energy gain (Higher tier)
A 2.0 kg stone (specific heat capacity 800 J/kg °C) absorbs radiation at 250 W and emits radiation at 170 W. Calculate the temperature rise in 5.0 minutes, assuming the rates stay constant. (4)

Net power $= 250 - 170 = 80$ W (1). $E = Pt = 80 \times 300 = 24\,000$ J (1). $\Delta\theta = E \div (mc) = 24\,000 \div (2.0 \times 800)$ (1) $= 15$ °C (1).
:::

(Higher tier) State the condition for an object to stay at a constant temperature. (1)

::: q
(Higher tier) A black car and a white car are parked side by side in strong sunshine. Explain why the black car reaches a higher steady temperature. (3)
---
The black car absorbs radiation at a greater rate (the white car reflects more) (1). It keeps warming until its rate of emission rises to equal its rate of absorption (1). Since it absorbs at a higher rate, it needs a higher temperature before emission balances absorption (1).

(Higher tier) Give three factors that affect the temperature of the Earth. (3)

Rate of absorption of radiation (1); rate of emission of radiation (1); reflection of radiation into space (by clouds/ice) (1). (Accept amount of greenhouse gases as affecting the rate of emission to space.)

::: watch
- **"Only hot objects emit IR"**: **all** objects emit IR, whatever their temperature; hotter ones emit more per second.
- **"Black bodies emit no radiation"**: a perfect black body **absorbs** all radiation and is the **best emitter**.
- **Peak shift the wrong way**: hotter means peak at **shorter** wavelength, not longer.
- **Missing "rate"**: say "absorbs radiation **at the same rate as** it emits it", not "absorbs the same as it emits".
- **Greenhouse explanations that say gases "trap heat" or "reflect radiation"**: greenhouse gases **absorb** long-wavelength IR and **re-emit** it in all directions, some back to Earth.
- **Constant temperature means no radiation**: at a steady temperature it is still emitting and absorbing — just at equal rates.
:::

::: test
A metal bar is heated in a flame. At first it does not glow, then it glows red, then orange, then yellow-white. Explain these observations. (3)
---
At all temperatures it emits radiation, mostly infrared, which cannot be seen (1). As the temperature rises, the intensity increases and the peak wavelength decreases (1), so more visible light is emitted — first long-wavelength red, then shorter wavelengths, until all colours together look yellow-white (1).

::: test
A student wants to compare which of two surfaces is the better emitter of radiation. Suggest which surface, matt black or shiny silver, will emit more, and describe how she could compare them fairly. (3)
---
Matt black (1). Fill two identical containers painted matt black and shiny silver with the same volume of water at the same starting temperature (1); measure the temperature drop over the same time / IR detector at the same distance — the faster-cooling one is the better emitter (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A solar panel absorbs radiation from the Sun at 900 W and emits radiation at 600 W. The panel has a mass of 12 kg and specific heat capacity 500 J/kg °C. (a) Calculate the temperature rise in 10 minutes, assuming the rates are constant. (b) Explain why, in reality, the temperature rise will be smaller and the panel will reach a steady temperature. (6)
---
(a) Net power $= 900 - 600 = 300$ W (1). $E = Pt = 300 \times 600 = 180\,000$ J (1). $\Delta\theta = 180\,000 \div (12 \times 500)$ (1) $= 30$ °C (1). (b) As the panel gets hotter, its rate of emission increases (1). When the rate of emission equals the rate of absorption, the temperature stops rising (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A planet with no atmosphere and a planet with a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere are the same distance from their star. Suggest which has the higher surface temperature and explain why. (3)
---
The planet with the CO₂ atmosphere (1). Its surface emits long-wavelength infrared, which the CO₂ absorbs and re-emits in all directions, some back to the surface (1), so less radiation escapes to space; the planet must be at a higher temperature before emission to space balances absorption (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Measurements show the Earth is currently absorbing slightly more radiation than it emits. Predict what will happen to the Earth's temperature and explain how a new steady temperature could be reached. (2)
---
The temperature will rise (1); as it rises the Earth emits radiation at a greater rate until emission equals absorption again, giving a new, higher steady temperature (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Explain how the temperature of the Earth's surface and atmosphere is determined by the radiation it absorbs, reflects and emits. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a detailed, logical account from incoming radiation to the balance, including the role of the atmosphere and how changes alter the temperature.
Level 2 (3–4): describes absorption and emission with some links, perhaps missing reflection or the balance idea.
Level 1 (1–2): isolated points, e.g. "the Sun heats the Earth" or "CO₂ traps heat".
Indicative content:
- The Sun emits mostly short-wavelength radiation (visible, UV, short IR).
- Some is reflected into space by clouds, ice, snow and the atmosphere.
- The rest is absorbed by the surface, which warms.
- The cooler surface emits longer-wavelength infrared.
- Greenhouse gases (CO₂, water vapour, methane) absorb some of this IR and re-emit it in all directions, some back to the surface, warming the surface and lower atmosphere.
- The rest of the IR escapes to space.
- The temperature is constant when the rate of absorption equals the rate of emission to space.
- If absorption exceeds emission (e.g. more greenhouse gases, less ice reflecting) the temperature rises until a new balance at a higher temperature.
- More reflection (more cloud or ice) means less absorbed, which tends to lower the temperature.
:::
