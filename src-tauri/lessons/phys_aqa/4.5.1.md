## What it is

A **force** is a push or a pull that acts on an object because of an **interaction with another object**. This topic (AQA 4.5.1, Forces and their interactions) covers scalars and vectors, contact and non-contact forces, weight and gravity, and how to combine several forces into one **resultant force**. Everything here is also in **Combined Science: Trilogy** (6.5.1); the parts marked **(Higher tier)** are HT only in both courses.

### Scalar and vector quantities

| | **Scalar** | **Vector** |
|---|---|---|
| Has | magnitude (size) only | magnitude **and** an associated direction |
| Examples | distance, speed, mass, energy, time, temperature | displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight, momentum |

A vector can be drawn as an **arrow**: the **length** of the arrow represents the **magnitude** and the **direction** of the arrow shows the direction. So a 10 N force to the left drawn at a scale of 1 cm : 2 N is an arrow 5 cm long pointing left.

Pairs worth learning together: **distance** (scalar) and **displacement** (vector); **speed** (scalar) and **velocity** (vector); **mass** (scalar, kg) and **weight** (vector, a force in N).

### Contact and non-contact forces

Every force between objects is one of two kinds:

| **Contact forces** — objects physically touching | **Non-contact forces** — objects physically separated |
|---|---|
| friction | gravitational force (weight) |
| air resistance (drag in a fluid) | electrostatic force (between charges) |
| tension (in a stretched rope, string or cable) | magnetic force (between magnets or a magnet and iron) |
| normal contact force (a surface pushing back at right angles) | |

**Force is a vector quantity** — it always has a direction.

**Interaction pairs.** Forces always come in pairs because a force is an interaction between **two** objects. If object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts a force on A that is **equal in size and opposite in direction**, and of the **same type**. Examples:
- A book on a table pushes down on the table; the table pushes up on the book (normal contact force pair).
- The Earth pulls a falling apple down; the apple pulls the Earth up with an equal force (gravitational pair). The Earth barely moves because its mass is enormous.
- Two north poles held close repel: each magnet is pushed away from the other.

Draw each force as an arrow **on the object it acts on**. The two forces of an interaction pair act on **different** objects, so they never cancel each other out.

### Gravity and weight

**Weight** is the force acting on an object due to gravity. Close to the Earth the force is due to the **gravitational field** around the Earth. The weight of an object depends on the **gravitational field strength** where it is: on the Moon, $g$ is about 1.6 N/kg, so you weigh about one-sixth of your Earth weight, but your **mass** (amount of matter) is unchanged.

**Equation (recall — you must learn it):**

weight = mass × gravitational field strength

$$W = mg$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| weight | $W$ | newton, N |
| mass | $m$ | kilogram, kg |
| gravitational field strength | $g$ | newton per kilogram, N/kg |

In any calculation the value of $g$ will be given (on Earth AQA uses 9.8 N/kg).

**Mini-example.** A 65 kg student on Earth ($g$ = 9.8 N/kg): $W = 65 \times 9.8 = 637$ N, about 640 N. On the Moon ($g$ = 1.6 N/kg): $W = 65 \times 1.6 = 104$ N, about 100 N. Mass is still 65 kg.

**Rearranging.** $m = W \div g$. A parcel weighing 49 N on Earth has a mass of $49 \div 9.8 = 5.0$ kg.

- **Centre of mass**: the weight of an object can be treated as acting at a **single point**, its centre of mass. For a uniform, symmetrical object it is at the centre.
- **Proportionality**: weight and mass are **directly proportional**, $W \propto m$ (the symbol $\propto$ means "is directly proportional to"). Double the mass, double the weight; the constant of proportionality is $g$. A graph of $W$ against $m$ is a straight line through the origin with gradient $g$.
- **Measuring weight**: use a **calibrated spring-balance** (a **newtonmeter**). The spring stretches in proportion to the force, and the scale reads newtons.

### Resultant forces

A number of forces acting on an object can be replaced by a single force that has the **same effect as all the original forces acting together**. This single force is the **resultant force**.

**Forces in a straight line** (all students): choose one direction as positive, then add forces in that direction and subtract forces in the opposite direction.
- 800 N forward thrust and 300 N drag on a car: resultant $= 800 - 300 = 500$ N **forwards**.
- Two people pushing a box the same way with 120 N and 90 N against 50 N friction: resultant $= 120 + 90 - 50 = 160$ N in the direction of the pushes.
- If the forces cancel, the resultant is **zero** and the forces are **balanced**: the object stays at rest or keeps moving at a steady speed in a straight line.

Always give a resultant with its **direction** — it is a vector.

### Free body diagrams (Higher tier)

A **free body diagram** shows **one isolated object** (often as a box or dot) with an arrow for **every force acting on it**, each labelled, drawn from the object, and with lengths roughly in proportion to the sizes. Forces the object exerts on other things are **not** drawn.

| Situation | Forces on the object | Resultant |
|---|---|---|
| Book resting on a table | weight down, normal contact force up, equal | zero — balanced |
| Skydiver just after jumping | large weight down, small air resistance up | downwards, so speeds up |
| Skydiver at terminal velocity | weight down = air resistance up | zero — steady speed |
| Car accelerating | driving force forward > friction and drag backward; weight down = normal force up | forwards |
| Lamp hanging from a cable | weight down, tension up, equal | zero |

Use free body diagrams to describe **qualitatively** how several forces give a resultant, including the **balanced** case when the resultant is zero.

### Resolving forces (Higher tier)

A single force can be **resolved** into **two components acting at right angles** to each other. The two components together have **exactly the same effect** as the single force. Usually you resolve into horizontal and vertical components.

**Scale-drawing method:**
1. Choose a scale (e.g. 1 cm = 10 N) and draw the force as an arrow of the correct length at the correct angle.
2. Draw a horizontal line from the tail and a vertical line through the head to make a right-angled triangle.
3. Measure each side and convert back using the scale.

**Mini-example.** A rope pulls a sledge with 50 N at 30° above the horizontal. At 1 cm = 10 N the arrow is 5.0 cm long. The horizontal side measures about 4.3 cm (43 N, which pulls the sledge along) and the vertical side 2.5 cm (25 N, which partly lifts it).

### Vector diagrams: resultants and equilibrium (Higher tier)

To find the resultant of **two forces at an angle**, draw them **tip-to-tail** to scale (or as two sides of a parallelogram). The resultant is the arrow from the **tail of the first** to the **tip of the last**. Measure its length (magnitude) and its angle with a protractor (direction). AQA requires **scale drawings only**; for forces at right angles, Pythagoras is a useful check.

**Mini-example.** 3.0 N east and 4.0 N north. At 1 cm = 1 N, draw 3.0 cm east then 4.0 cm north from its tip. The line from start to finish measures 5.0 cm, so the resultant is **5.0 N** at about **53° north of east** (a bearing of about 037°). Check: $\sqrt{3.0^2 + 4.0^2} = 5.0$.

**Equilibrium.** If the forces on an object are balanced, their arrows drawn tip-to-tail form a **closed shape** (a triangle for three forces): the last arrow ends where the first began, so the resultant is zero. You can use this to find an unknown force — for example, the tension in each half of a washing line holding up a wet towel, or the force needed to hold a sign hanging from two cables.

::: terms
- **Scalar quantity**: a quantity that has magnitude (size) only, such as speed, mass or energy.
- **Vector quantity**: a quantity that has magnitude and an associated direction, such as force or velocity.
- **Contact force**: a force between objects that are physically touching, such as friction or tension.
- **Non-contact force**: a force between objects that are physically separated, such as gravitational, electrostatic or magnetic force.
- **Weight**: the force acting on an object due to gravity, measured in newtons.
- **Gravitational field strength**: the force of gravity on each kilogram of mass at a point, in N/kg.
- **Centre of mass**: the single point at which the weight of an object can be considered to act.
- **Resultant force**: the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting on an object together.
- **Free body diagram**: a diagram of one isolated object showing every force acting on it as a labelled arrow.
- **Resolving a force**: splitting one force into two components at right angles that together have the same effect.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State / give**: a short fact — "force is a vector quantity".
- **Describe** an interaction pair: name both objects, say each exerts a force on the other, that the forces are equal in size, opposite in direction and of the same type.
- **Explain** the difference between mass and weight: mass is the amount of matter (kg, scalar, the same everywhere); weight is a force (N, vector) that depends on the gravitational field strength.
- **Compare** scalars and vectors: say "whereas" — "a scalar has magnitude only, whereas a vector also has a direction".

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit)
1. Write $W = mg$ (recall it — it is not on the equation sheet).
2. Convert mass to **kg** (g ÷ 1000) before substituting.
3. Give the unit (N for weight) and a sensible number of significant figures, matching the data (usually 2 or 3).
4. For resultants in a line: pick a positive direction, add and subtract, then **state the direction** of the answer.

**Scale drawings (Higher tier)**
- State your scale. Use a sharp pencil, ruler and protractor; make the diagram as big as the space allows to reduce error.
- Resultant: tip-to-tail, measure from start to finish, give **magnitude and direction** (an angle from a stated reference line).
- Equilibrium: arrows form a closed triangle; the unknown side gives the missing force.
- Answers within a tolerance (often ± 0.2 cm on the drawing) get the marks — so draw accurately.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**
For questions about the forces on a moving or stationary object (e.g. a skydiver, a car, a hanging sign), a Level 3 answer names **every** force, says whether it is contact or non-contact, compares their sizes, states the **resultant** and links it to what happens (balanced → steady speed or at rest; unbalanced → speeds up, slows down or changes direction). Lower levels list forces without comparing them or without linking to the motion.
:::

::: example Weight on another planet
An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg. On Mars the gravitational field strength is 3.7 N/kg. Calculate her weight on Mars and state her mass there. (3)

$W = mg$ (1) $= 80 \times 3.7 = 296$ N, about 300 N (1). Her mass is still 80 kg — mass does not depend on gravity (1).
:::

::: example Resultant in a straight line
A boat's engine gives a forward force of 2400 N. Water resistance is 1500 N and the wind pushes backwards with 400 N. Calculate the resultant force and describe the boat's motion. (3)

Resultant $= 2400 - 1500 - 400$ (1) $= 500$ N forwards (1). The forces are unbalanced, so the boat speeds up (accelerates) forwards (1).
:::

::: example Free body diagram (Higher tier)
A lamp of weight 12 N hangs at rest from a ceiling cable. Describe the free body diagram for the lamp and explain why it stays still. (3)

One arrow downwards from the lamp labelled weight, 12 N (1); one arrow upwards of equal length labelled tension in the cable, 12 N (1). The forces are equal and opposite, so the resultant force is zero and the lamp stays at rest (1).
:::

::: example Resultant by scale drawing (Higher tier)
Two tugs pull a ship. One pulls with 6.0 kN due north, the other with 8.0 kN due east. Using a scale drawing, find the resultant force on the ship. (4)

Scale e.g. 1 cm = 1 kN (1). Draw 6.0 cm north, then 8.0 cm east from its tip (1). The resultant measures 10 cm, so 10 kN (1), at about 53° east of north (a bearing of about 053°) (1). Check: $\sqrt{6.0^2 + 8.0^2} = 10$.
:::

::: example Resolving a force (Higher tier)
A child pulls a toy cart with a force of 20 N along a handle at 40° above the horizontal. Use a scale drawing to find the horizontal component of the force and explain what it does. (3)

Draw a 10 cm arrow at 40° (scale 1 cm = 2 N) and complete the right-angled triangle (1). The horizontal side measures about 7.7 cm, so about 15 N (1). This is the part of the force that pulls the cart along the ground; the vertical component (about 13 N) partly lifts it (1).
:::

::: q
Which of these is a vector quantity: mass, speed, displacement or energy? (1)
---
Displacement.
:::

::: q
Name two contact forces and two non-contact forces. (2)
---
Contact (any two): friction, air resistance, tension, normal contact force (1). Non-contact (any two): gravitational force, electrostatic force, magnetic force (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how a vector quantity can be represented on a diagram. (2)
---
By an arrow (1); its length shows the magnitude and the way it points shows the direction (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the weight of a 450 g bag of sugar on Earth. Gravitational field strength = 9.8 N/kg. (2)
---
$m = 0.450$ kg (1). $W = 0.450 \times 9.8 = 4.4$ N (1).
:::

::: q
A crate weighs 1960 N on Earth ($g$ = 9.8 N/kg). Calculate its mass. (2)
---
$m = W \div g = 1960 \div 9.8$ (1) $= 200$ kg (1).
:::

::: q
A rocket at lift-off has a thrust of 32 000 N upwards and a weight of 24 000 N. Calculate the resultant force. (2)
---
$32\,000 - 24\,000 = 8000$ N (1) upwards (1).
:::

::: q
A person stands on the floor. Describe the interaction pair that involves the force of the person on the floor. (2)
---
The person pushes down on the floor; the floor pushes up on the person (1) with a force equal in size and opposite in direction, both normal contact forces (1).
:::

::: q
State what is meant by the centre of mass of an object. (1)
---
The single point at which the weight of the object can be considered to act.
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) A force of 100 N acts at 60° above the horizontal. Explain what is meant by resolving this force, and state the approximate size of its horizontal component from a scale drawing. (3)
---
Replacing it with two forces at right angles (horizontal and vertical) (1) that together have the same effect as the single force (1). Horizontal component about 50 N (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Mass and weight muddled**: mass is in **kg** and does not change; weight is a **force in N** and depends on $g$. "I weigh 60 kg" is wrong in physics.
- **Grams not converted**: $W = mg$ needs mass in **kg** — 450 g is 0.450 kg.
- **No direction on a resultant**: "500 N" loses a mark; write "500 N forwards" or "upwards".
- **Interaction pair on one object**: the two forces of a pair act on **different** objects, so they never cancel. Weight and the normal force on a book are **not** an interaction pair.
- **Scale drawings without a scale**: always state the scale and give the angle for the direction (Higher tier).
- **Calling weight a contact force**: gravity acts at a distance — it is **non-contact**.
:::

---
$m = 1620 \div 1.6 = 1012.5$ kg (1). $W = 1012.5 \times 9.8$ (1) $= 9922.5$ N, about 9900 N (1).

::: test
A cyclist pedals with a driving force of 180 N. Air resistance is 110 N and friction is 70 N. (a) Calculate the resultant force. (b) Describe the motion of the cyclist. (3)
---
(a) $180 - 110 - 70 = 0$ N (1). (b) The forces are balanced (1), so the cyclist moves at a constant speed in a straight line (1).

::: test
(Higher tier) A sign of weight 40 N hangs from a hook in the middle of a horizontal wire, so that each half of the wire makes an angle of 30° below the horizontal. Using a vector diagram, explain how you would find the tension in each half of the wire, and give its value. (4)
---
The sign is in equilibrium, so the three forces drawn tip-to-tail make a closed triangle (1). Draw the weight 40 N vertically down to scale (e.g. 1 cm = 5 N, 8.0 cm) (1); from each end draw lines at 30° to the horizontal along the two wire directions until they meet (1). Each tension side measures about 8.0 cm, so each tension is about 40 N (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A skydiver of weight 750 N jumps from a plane. Using free body diagrams, describe the forces acting on her and how the resultant force changes from the moment she jumps until she falls at a steady speed. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a clear, logically ordered description covering the forces, how they change and the resultant at each stage, linked to motion.
Level 2 (3–4): forces identified and some changes described, with resultant partly linked to motion.
Level 1 (1–2): isolated statements, e.g. names weight and air resistance.
Indicative content:
- Two forces on her: weight 750 N downwards (non-contact) and air resistance upwards (contact).
- Free body diagram: one dot with a down arrow (weight) and an up arrow (air resistance).
- At the moment she jumps, air resistance is zero/very small, so the resultant force is about 750 N downwards and she accelerates.
- As her speed increases, air resistance increases (the up arrow grows), weight stays the same.
- So the resultant force (weight minus air resistance) decreases and her acceleration decreases.
- Eventually air resistance = 750 N, the arrows are equal, the resultant force is zero (balanced).
- She then falls at a constant (terminal) velocity.
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Two forces act on a point: 5.0 N due east and 12 N due north. Using a scale drawing, determine the magnitude and direction of the resultant force. Explain why the resultant is less than 17 N. (4)
---
Tip-to-tail scale drawing, e.g. 1 cm = 1 N (1). Resultant measured 13 cm, so 13 N (1). Direction about 67° north of east (or bearing about 023°) (1). The forces are not in the same direction, so they do not simply add; only forces acting along the same line add arithmetically (1).
:::
