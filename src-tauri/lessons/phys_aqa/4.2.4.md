## What it is

Every electrical appliance exists to transfer energy: to the thermal store of water in a kettle, to the kinetic store of a motor in a drill, to light from a lamp. This topic gives you the equations that measure how **fast** a device transfers energy (its **power**) and **how much** energy it transfers, and then explains how the **National Grid** gets energy from power stations to homes with as little waste as possible. The whole topic is in **both** Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464. One equation in the National Grid section is **Higher tier only**.

### The equations — which to recall and which are given

| Equation in words | Symbols | On the equation sheet? |
|---|---|---|
| power = potential difference × current | $P = VI$ | **No — recall it** |
| power = current² × resistance | $P = I^2 R$ | **No — recall it** |
| energy transferred = power × time | $E = Pt$ | **No — recall it** |
| energy transferred = charge flow × potential difference | $E = QV$ | **No — recall it** |
| (Higher tier) pd across primary × current in primary = pd across secondary × current in secondary | $V_p I_p = V_s I_s$ | Yes — given |

Units: power $P$ in **watts, W**; potential difference $V$ in **volts, V**; current $I$ in **amperes, A**; resistance $R$ in **ohms, Ω**; energy $E$ in **joules, J**; time $t$ in **seconds, s**; charge $Q$ in **coulombs, C**. One watt is one joule per second. You will also use, from earlier topics, $Q = It$ (charge = current × time) and $V = IR$.

### Power in a circuit

**Power** is the rate at which energy is transferred: the energy transferred per second. The power of any circuit device depends on two things:

- the **potential difference** across it — the energy transferred by each coulomb of charge passing through it (1 V = 1 J/C);
- the **current** through it — the number of coulombs passing each second (1 A = 1 C/s).

Multiply them: (joules per coulomb) × (coulombs per second) = joules per second = watts. That is why $P = VI$.

Substituting $V = IR$ into $P = VI$ gives $P = I \times IR = I^2 R$. Use $P = I^2 R$ when you know the current and the resistance — it is the equation for the **heating effect** of a current in a resistor, and the key to the National Grid. Notice that power depends on current **squared**: double the current through the same resistor and the power goes up **four** times.

*Mini-example.* A kettle on the 230 V mains takes a current of 12 A. $P = VI = 230 \times 12 = 2760$ W $= 2.76$ kW.

*Mini-example.* A current of 4.0 A flows through a 15 Ω heating element. $P = I^2 R = 4.0^2 \times 15 = 16 \times 15 = 240$ W.

### Energy transferred: E = Pt and E = QV

**Work is done when charge flows in a circuit.** The supply pushes charge through the components, and the energy transferred by this electrical work ends up in other stores.

The amount of energy an appliance transfers depends on **how long it is switched on** and its **power**: $E = Pt$. Time **must be in seconds** — convert minutes and hours first.

*Mini-example.* A 2.0 kW heater runs for 15 minutes. $t = 15 \times 60 = 900$ s, $P = 2000$ W, so $E = 2000 \times 900 = 1\,800\,000$ J $= 1.8$ MJ.

The energy can also be found from the charge that flows and the pd it moves through: $E = QV$. This is really the definition of potential difference — the pd is the energy transferred per unit charge.

*Mini-example.* A current of 0.50 A flows through a 12 V lamp for 2.0 minutes. Charge: $Q = It = 0.50 \times 120 = 60$ C. Energy: $E = QV = 60 \times 12 = 720$ J. (Check with power: $P = VI = 6.0$ W, and $E = Pt = 6.0 \times 120 = 720$ J.)

### How power relates to pd, current and energy over time

You must be able to explain that the power of a device is related to:

- **the pd across it and the current through it** — a bigger pd means each coulomb transfers more energy; a bigger current means more coulombs per second; either way more joules per second ($P = VI$);
- **the energy transferred in a given time** — power is the energy transferred per second ($P = E/t$), so a device with twice the power transfers twice the energy in the same time.

### Energy transfers in everyday appliances

Everyday electrical appliances are designed to bring about energy transfers. Energy is transferred **electrically** (by the work done as charge flows) from the **ac mains** or from the **chemical store of a battery** to:

| Appliance | Supply | Useful energy transfer | Wasted |
|---|---|---|---|
| kettle, toaster, electric heater | mains | to the **thermal store** of the water / bread / room (heating device) | thermal store of the surroundings (kettle body, air) |
| hairdryer | mains | **kinetic store** of the fan motor and **thermal store** of the air | sound, thermal store of the casing |
| washing machine / food mixer | mains | **kinetic store** of the motor and drum / blades | sound, heating of the motor |
| battery-powered toy car or drill | battery (chemical store) | **kinetic store** of the electric motor and moving parts | heating of wires and motor, sound |
| torch | battery (chemical store) | energy carried away by light | heating of the bulb |

### Power ratings and changes in stored energy

An appliance's **power rating** (for example "3 kW" on a kettle) tells you how much energy it transfers **each second**. A higher power rating means a bigger change in stored energy in the same time:

- a 3.0 kW kettle transfers 3000 J every second to the thermal store of the water, so it boils the same water in about one-third of the time a 1.0 kW travel kettle would take (ignoring losses);
- a 9.0 kW electric shower heats water quickly enough to warm a continuous flow; a 1.5 kW hairdryer would never manage that;
- a 10 W LED lamp left on for 10 hours transfers $E = 10 \times 36\,000 = 360\,000$ J, the same energy that a 2.0 kW heater transfers in only 180 s.

So a high-power appliance used briefly can transfer less energy than a low-power one left on for a long time — the energy depends on **both** $P$ and $t$. (Electricity bills use the kilowatt-hour, 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ; it is not required by the AQA specification but you may meet it.)

### The National Grid

The **National Grid** is a system of **cables and transformers** linking **power stations to consumers** (homes, offices, factories) across Great Britain. Electrical power is transferred from power stations to consumers using the National Grid.

1. Power stations generate electricity at a pd of roughly 25 000 V.
2. **Step-up transformers** increase the potential difference from the power station to the **transmission cables** — typically to 275 000 V or 400 000 V.
3. Near towns, **step-down transformers** decrease the pd, in stages, to a much lower value — about **230 V** for domestic use, which is far safer in the home.

**Why this is an efficient way to transfer energy.** For a given power, $P = VI$, so raising the pd **lowers the current** in the cables by the same factor. The power wasted heating the cables is $P = I^2 R$, so it depends on the **current squared**. Cutting the current to a small fraction cuts the heating loss to a much, much smaller fraction. Less energy is wasted to the thermal store of the cables and surroundings, so a greater proportion of the energy generated reaches consumers. Low current also means thinner, cheaper cables and pylons can be used.

*Worked comparison.* A power station sends 1.0 MW through cables with total resistance 5.0 Ω.

| Transmission pd | Current $I = P/V$ | Power lost $I^2 R$ | Percentage lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 000 V | 100 A | $100^2 \times 5.0 = 50\,000$ W | 5% |
| 400 000 V | 2.5 A | $2.5^2 \times 5.0 = 31.25$ W | about 0.003% |

Increasing the pd 40 times cuts the current 40 times and the heating loss $40^2 = 1600$ times.

### Transformer power equation (Higher tier)

If a transformer is **100% efficient**, the electrical power output equals the electrical power input:

$$V_p \times I_p = V_s \times I_s$$

potential difference across primary coil × current in primary coil = potential difference across secondary coil × current in secondary coil. This equation is **given on the equation sheet**; you must select it and use it. Combined Science students meet it here; Physics students meet it again in the Transformers topic (magnetism), where the construction of a transformer and the turns-ratio equation are taught. For this topic you do not need the detailed structure of a transformer.

*Mini-example (Higher tier).* A step-up transformer at a power station has $V_p = 25\,000$ V and $I_p = 400$ A. The output pd is 400 000 V. $I_s = \frac{V_p I_p}{V_s} = \frac{25\,000 \times 400}{400\,000} = 25$ A. The pd went up 16 times, so the current came down 16 times.

::: terms
- **Power**: the rate at which energy is transferred, or the energy transferred per second, measured in watts (W).
- **Watt**: the unit of power; one watt is a transfer of one joule of energy per second.
- **Power rating**: the power an appliance transfers in normal use, which sets how much energy it transfers each second.
- **Electrical work**: the work done when charge flows through a circuit, transferring energy from the supply to other stores.
- **Charge flow**: the quantity of charge passing a point, measured in coulombs (C); it equals current × time.
- **National Grid**: the nationwide system of cables and transformers linking power stations to consumers.
- **Step-up transformer**: a transformer that increases the potential difference, used between power stations and the transmission cables.
- **Step-down transformer**: a transformer that decreases the potential difference, used to bring the grid down to about 230 V for homes.
:::

::: method
**Calculations — the four-line method**
1. Write the equation in symbols (for example $P = I^2 R$).
2. Convert units first: kW to W (× 1000), minutes to seconds (× 60), hours to seconds (× 3600), mA to A (÷ 1000), kV to V (× 1000).
3. Substitute, then rearrange if needed (rearranging before or after substituting is fine; for $P = I^2R$ to find $I$, divide by $R$ then square-root).
4. Give the answer with the **unit**, and round to the same number of significant figures as the data (usually 2 or 3).

You must **recall** $P = VI$, $P = I^2R$, $E = Pt$ and $E = QV$. Only $V_pI_p = V_sI_s$ (Higher tier) is on the sheet. Multi-step questions usually chain two equations: find $Q$ from $Q = It$ then $E = QV$; or find $I$ from $P = VI$ then the loss from $P = I^2R$.

**Command words**
- **Describe** an energy transfer: name the starting store or supply, say "transferred electrically / by electrical work", name the store it ends up in, and mention any wasted energy (usually heating the surroundings).
- **Explain** how power is related to something: give the physical meaning (pd = energy per coulomb, current = coulombs per second, power = energy per second) and link them with "so".
- **Compare** two appliances: use the numbers — "the 3 kW kettle transfers three times as much energy per second as the 1 kW kettle".

**The National Grid 4- or 6-mark explanation — the chain examiners want**
step-up transformer increases the pd → for the same power ($P = VI$) the current is smaller → heating of the cables depends on $I^2R$ → so much less energy is wasted heating the cables and surroundings → so a higher proportion of the energy reaches consumers (efficient) → step-down transformers reduce the pd to about 230 V, which is safer for use in homes. Every arrow is a "because/so" link. Saying only "high voltage reduces energy loss" with no mention of current earns very little.

**Levels of response**
For a 6-mark question the top level needs a full, logically ordered chain using correct terms; the middle level has some correct links but gaps (for example, no reason why lower current reduces loss); the bottom level has isolated facts.
:::

::: example Using P = VI
A toaster takes a current of 4.5 A from the 230 V mains. Calculate its power. (2)

$P = VI = 230 \times 4.5$ (1) $= 1035$ W, about **1000 W** or 1.0 kW (1).
:::

::: example Rearranging P = I² R (grade 7)
A heater has a resistance of 46 Ω and a power of 1150 W. Calculate the current in the heater and the potential difference across it. (4)

$P = I^2 R$, so $I^2 = \frac{P}{R} = \frac{1150}{46} = 25$ (1). $I = \sqrt{25} = 5.0$ A (1). $V = IR = 5.0 \times 46$ (1) $= 230$ V (1). (Check: $P = VI = 230 \times 5.0 = 1150$ W.)
:::

::: example Energy, charge and time
A phone charger supplies a current of 2.0 A at 5.0 V for 1.5 hours. Calculate the charge that flows and the energy transferred. (4)

$t = 1.5 \times 3600 = 5400$ s (1). $Q = It = 2.0 \times 5400 = 10\,800$ C (1). $E = QV = 10\,800 \times 5.0$ (1) $= 54\,000$ J (1).
:::

::: example Boiling water (synoptic, grade 8)
A 2.4 kW kettle heats 1.5 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/kg °C. Assuming no energy is wasted, calculate the time taken. (4)

$\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta = 1.5 \times 4200 \times 80 = 504\,000$ J (1)(1). $t = \frac{E}{P} = \frac{504\,000}{2400}$ (1) $= 210$ s (1). In reality it takes longer because some energy heats the kettle and the air.
:::

::: example Why the National Grid uses high pd
Explain why electricity is transmitted at a very high potential difference in the National Grid. (4)

Step-up transformers increase the pd for transmission (1). For the same power transmitted, a higher pd means a lower current in the cables, since $P = VI$ (1). The power wasted heating the cables is $I^2 R$, so a lower current means much less energy is lost to the surroundings (1). A greater proportion of the energy reaches consumers, so the system is efficient (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Convert first**: kW to W and minutes or hours to seconds before using $E = Pt$. "2 kW × 15 min = 30 J" is the classic disaster.
- In $P = I^2R$ the current is **squared**. Forgetting to square, or square-rooting at the wrong step when finding $I$, loses the method mark.
- The National Grid explanation needs **current**: high pd → low current → less $I^2R$ heating in the cables. "High voltage means less energy lost" alone gets one mark at best.
- **Step-up** transformers are at the power station end; **step-down** transformers are at the consumer end. Swapping them loses the mark.
- $P = VI$, $P = I^2R$, $E = Pt$ and $E = QV$ are **not** on the equation sheet — learn them.
:::
