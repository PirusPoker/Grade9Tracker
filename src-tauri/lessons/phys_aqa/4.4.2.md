## What it is

Some atoms have nuclei that are **unstable**. Sooner or later they give out radiation and change into something more stable. This topic covers the **types of nuclear radiation** and their properties, **nuclear equations**, **half-life** and the **random** nature of decay, and the difference between **contamination** and **irradiation**. All of it is in **both** Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (where it is 6.4.2). The ratio calculation after several half-lives is **Higher tier only** and is flagged below. There is no required practical in this subsection.

### Radioactive decay

Some atomic nuclei are **unstable**. The nucleus gives out radiation as it changes to become **more stable**. This is a **random** process called **radioactive decay**: you cannot predict **which** nucleus will decay next or **when** a particular nucleus will decay, and nothing you do to it (heating, pressure, chemical reactions) changes that.

- **Activity** is the **rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays**. It is measured in **becquerels (Bq)**: 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.
- **Count rate** is the **number of decays recorded each second by a detector**, such as a **Geiger–Müller (GM) tube** connected to a counter. The count rate is lower than the activity, because the detector only catches the radiation that travels towards it.

A detector always picks up some **background radiation** too. In experiments, measure the background count rate with no source present and **subtract** it: corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate.

### The four types of nuclear radiation

| Radiation | What it is | Charge | Penetration — stopped by | Range in air | Ionising power |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **alpha particle**, $\alpha$ | **2 protons + 2 neutrons** — the same as a **helium nucleus** | +2 | a sheet of **paper** (or the outer layer of skin) | a few **centimetres** (about 5 cm) | **strongly** ionising |
| **beta particle**, $\beta$ | a **high-speed electron ejected from the nucleus** as a **neutron turns into a proton** | −1 | a few **millimetres of aluminium** | about a **metre** (tens of centimetres up to about a metre) | moderately ionising |
| **gamma ray**, $\gamma$ | **electromagnetic radiation** from the nucleus | 0 | never completely stopped; greatly reduced by **thick lead** or **several metres of concrete** | very long — **kilometres** | **weakly** ionising |
| **neutron**, n | a neutron emitted from the nucleus | 0 | — | — | — |

AQA only requires you to know the **penetration through materials**, **range in air** and **ionising power** of alpha, beta and gamma. **Ionising** means knocking electrons off atoms, turning them into ions. The pattern is a trade-off: the **more strongly ionising** a radiation is, the more quickly it gives up its energy, so the **less penetrating** it is and the **shorter its range**.

### Choosing the best source for a job

You must be able to apply these properties to uses and **evaluate the best source** for a given situation.

| Use | Best radiation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Smoke alarm | alpha | strongly ionises the air between two plates to make a small current; smoke absorbs the alphas, the current falls, the alarm sounds; short range, so it cannot escape the alarm |
| Controlling the thickness of paper or thin metal foil | beta | partly absorbed by paper, so the count through it changes noticeably with thickness; alpha would be completely stopped, gamma would pass straight through whatever the thickness |
| Detecting leaks in underground pipes (tracer) | gamma | penetrates soil to reach a detector at the surface |
| Sterilising medical equipment or food | gamma | penetrates the packaging and kills microorganisms |

In "evaluate" questions consider **penetration** (can it get out / through?), **ionisation** (how much damage, how well it is detected?) and, where relevant, **half-life** (long enough to do the job, short enough not to stay dangerous).

### Nuclear equations

Nuclear equations show radioactive decay. Each nucleus or particle has a **mass number** (top) and **atomic number / charge** (bottom). An **alpha particle** is written ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ and a **beta particle** is written ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$.

**Alpha decay** — the nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons, so the **mass number falls by 4** and the **atomic number falls by 2**. Both the mass and the charge of the nucleus decrease:

$${}^{219}_{86}\text{Rn} \rightarrow {}^{215}_{84}\text{Po} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

**Beta decay** — a neutron turns into a proton and an electron is ejected, so the **mass number stays the same** and the **atomic number goes up by 1**. The mass of the nucleus does not change, but its charge increases:

$${}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$$

You do not need to recall these two examples, but you must be able to write balanced equations for single alpha and beta decays. **Balancing rule: the top numbers must add up to the same total on both sides, and so must the bottom numbers.** Check the alpha example: top $219 = 215 + 4$; bottom $86 = 84 + 2$. Beta example: top $14 = 14 + 0$; bottom $6 = 7 + (-1)$.

**Gamma emission** does **not** change the mass or the charge of the nucleus — gamma rays have no mass and no charge; the nucleus just loses energy. **Neutron emission** (${}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$) lowers the mass number by 1 but leaves the atomic number the same, making a different isotope of the same element.

You are only asked to balance mass and atomic numbers; identifying the new (daughter) element from a periodic table is not required — its symbol will be given.

### Half-life and the random nature of decay

Radioactive decay is **random**, but a sample contains so many nuclei that the **pattern** for the whole sample is very predictable. In any fixed time interval, the same **fraction** of the remaining nuclei decays. So the number of unstable nuclei (and the activity) keeps halving in equal time intervals.

> The **half-life** of a radioactive isotope is the **time it takes for the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve**, **or** the **time it takes for the count rate (or activity) from a sample containing the isotope to fall to half its initial level**.

The half-life is the same whatever the starting amount, and it is different for different isotopes.

**Finding the half-life from a graph:** read the starting activity, halve it, draw across to the curve and down to the time axis. For accuracy, repeat from a later point (e.g. from A/2 to A/4) and average.

**Finding the half-life from data:** count how many halvings get from the starting value to the final one, then divide the time taken by that number.

*Mini-example.* A source's activity falls from 640 Bq to 80 Bq in 36 hours. 640 → 320 → 160 → 80 is **3 half-lives**, so the half-life $= \frac{36}{3} = 12$ hours.

### Net decline after a number of half-lives (Higher tier)

After $n$ half-lives, the fraction of the original activity (or nuclei) remaining is $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$:

| Half-lives | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fraction remaining | $\frac{1}{2}$ | $\frac{1}{4}$ | $\frac{1}{8}$ | $\frac{1}{16}$ | $\frac{1}{32}$ |
| Ratio initial : final | 2 : 1 | 4 : 1 | 8 : 1 | 16 : 1 | 32 : 1 |

The **net decline** is the fraction that has gone: after 3 half-lives, $1 - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8}$ of the original activity has been lost.

*Mini-example (Higher tier).* An isotope has a half-life of 5 days. After 20 days, $n = \frac{20}{5} = 4$, so the activity is $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{4} = \frac{1}{16}$ of its initial value — a ratio of final : initial of 1 : 16.

### Radioactive contamination and irradiation

> **Radioactive contamination** is the **unwanted presence of materials containing radioactive atoms on (or in) other materials**.

The hazard from contamination is due to the **decay of the contaminating atoms**, and the **type of radiation emitted affects the level of hazard**. Contamination is hard to remove and keeps on irradiating you until the atoms have decayed or been removed.

> **Irradiation** is the process of **exposing an object to nuclear radiation**. The irradiated object **does not become radioactive**.

**Comparing the hazards:**

| | Contamination | Irradiation |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | radioactive atoms get onto or into the object / person | radiation from a source outside reaches the object |
| Does it continue after you move away? | **yes** — you carry the source with you | **no** — it stops when the source is removed or shielded |
| Most dangerous radiation **outside** the body | — | gamma and beta (penetrate to organs); alpha is stopped by skin |
| Most dangerous radiation **inside** the body (swallowed / breathed in) | **alpha** — strongly ionising, all its energy is absorbed by nearby cells | — |

**Precautions** against the hazards of the source used for irradiation (and against contamination): keep the source in a **lead-lined box**; handle it with **long tongs** at **arm's length**, never with hands; **minimise the time** of exposure; **shield** with lead, concrete or water; wear protective clothing and gloves (stops contamination); workers wear **dosimeter badges** to monitor their exposure.

**Sharing results.** It is important for the findings of studies into the effects of radiation on humans to be **published and shared with other scientists**, so the findings can be checked by **peer review**. Early researchers suffered from their exposure to radiation before its effects were understood; rules for radiological protection were introduced in the 1930s and have been improved as more evidence was shared.

::: terms
- **Radioactive decay**: the random process in which an unstable nucleus gives out radiation as it changes to become more stable.
- **Activity**: the rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays, measured in becquerels (Bq).
- **Count rate**: the number of decays recorded each second by a detector such as a Geiger-Muller tube.
- **Alpha particle**: two protons and two neutrons emitted from a nucleus; the same as a helium nucleus.
- **Beta particle**: a high-speed electron ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton.
- **Gamma ray**: electromagnetic radiation emitted from an unstable nucleus, with no mass and no charge.
- **Half-life**: the time for the number of unstable nuclei in a sample, or its count rate or activity, to halve.
- **Radioactive contamination**: the unwanted presence of materials containing radioactive atoms on or in other materials.
- **Irradiation**: exposing an object to nuclear radiation; the irradiated object does not become radioactive.
:::

::: method
**Properties questions**
Use the three properties AQA asks for — **penetration**, **range in air**, **ionising power** — and always compare (e.g. "alpha is the most ionising but the least penetrating"). When asked to **identify** a radiation from absorption data: stopped by paper → alpha; passes paper but stopped by a few mm of aluminium → beta; passes aluminium, reduced by lead → gamma.

**Evaluate / choose a source**
Give the radiation, then justify with penetration (can it reach the detector or get through the material?), and for a source used near or inside people, ionisation and half-life. Explain why the **other** radiations are unsuitable — that is usually where the extra marks are.

**Nuclear equations**
1. Write the parent nucleus, an arrow, the daughter nucleus and the particle (${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ for alpha, ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ for beta).
2. Top numbers balance; bottom numbers balance. For beta, remember the electron's bottom number is −1, so the daughter's atomic number goes **up** by one.
3. Check by adding both sides.

**Half-life calculations**
- **From a graph**: halve the starting value, read across and down; check from a second starting point.
- **From numbers**: count the halvings, then half-life = total time ÷ number of half-lives. Show the halving chain (800 → 400 → 200).
- **(Higher tier) Net decline as a ratio**: number of half-lives $n$ = time ÷ half-life; fraction remaining $= \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$; ratio initial : final $= 2^{n} : 1$. Read the question: "fraction remaining" and "fraction decayed" are different.
- Subtract **background** count rate before finding a half-life from count-rate data.

**Explain half-life and randomness**: each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in a given time, but you cannot predict which one or when; with very large numbers, a constant fraction decays per unit time, so the number remaining halves in equal times.

**Contamination vs irradiation (4–6 marks)**: compare in pairs — where the source is, whether exposure stops when you move away, which radiation is most dangerous outside vs inside the body, and the precautions for each. State clearly that an irradiated object does **not** become radioactive.
:::

::: example Alpha decay equation
Polonium-210 (${}^{210}_{84}\text{Po}$) decays by alpha emission to lead (Pb). Write the balanced nuclear equation. (3)

${}^{210}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow {}^{206}_{82}\text{Pb} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. Mark scheme: alpha particle correct (1); lead mass number 206 (1); atomic number 82 (1). Check: $210 = 206 + 4$; $84 = 82 + 2$.
:::

::: example Beta decay equation
Strontium-90 (${}^{90}_{38}\text{Sr}$) decays by beta emission to yttrium (Y). Write the balanced nuclear equation and state the change in the nucleus. (3)

${}^{90}_{38}\text{Sr} \rightarrow {}^{90}_{39}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ (2: one for the beta particle, one for Y numbers). A neutron in the nucleus has turned into a proton, so the mass number is unchanged and the atomic number increases by 1 (1).
:::

::: example Half-life from data
The activity of a sample of iodine-131 falls from 2400 Bq to 300 Bq in 24 days. Calculate the half-life. (2)

2400 → 1200 → 600 → 300 is 3 half-lives (1). Half-life $= \frac{24}{3} = 8$ days (1).
:::

::: example Net decline (Higher tier)
Technetium-99m has a half-life of 6 hours. A patient is given a dose with an activity of 400 MBq. Calculate the activity after 24 hours, and express the decline as a ratio. (3)

$n = \frac{24}{6} = 4$ half-lives (1). Activity $= 400 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{4} = \frac{400}{16} = 25$ MBq (1). Ratio of final to initial activity $= 1 : 16$ (1).
:::

::: example Choosing a source
A factory controls the thickness of aluminium foil by passing it between a radioactive source and a detector. Explain which type of source should be used. (3)

Beta (1). Alpha would be completely absorbed by the foil, so the count would be zero whatever the thickness (1). Gamma would pass through almost unaffected, so the count would hardly change with thickness; beta is partly absorbed, so the count rate changes noticeably when the thickness changes (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Irradiated objects do not become radioactive.** Writing that irradiated food "becomes radioactive" is a guaranteed lost mark.
- Beta particles come **from the nucleus** (a neutron turns into a proton) — not from the electron shells.
- In a beta equation the atomic number goes **up** by 1 and the mass number **stays the same**; in alpha the mass number falls by **4** and the atomic number by **2**.
- Half-life is the time for the number of nuclei (or activity) to **halve** — not "half the time for it all to decay". A sample never completely "runs out" after two half-lives: after 2 half-lives a **quarter** remains.
- (Higher tier) Fraction **remaining** after 3 half-lives is $\frac{1}{8}$; fraction **decayed** is $\frac{7}{8}$. Read which one is asked.
- Alpha is the **most** dangerous **inside** the body but the **least** dangerous outside — say which case you mean.
:::

::: test
A student measured the count rate from a source every 10 minutes. After subtracting background, the readings were 160, 113, 80, 57, 40 counts per second. Determine the half-life. (2)
---
160 → 80 takes 20 minutes (1); 80 → 40 also takes 20 minutes, so the half-life is 20 minutes (1).

::: test
Explain how the random nature of radioactive decay still gives a predictable half-life for a sample. (3)
---
Each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in a given time, but which one decays and when cannot be predicted (1). A sample contains a very large number of nuclei (1), so the same fraction / proportion decays in each equal time interval, and the number remaining halves in a constant time — the half-life (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A sample of cobalt-60 has a half-life of 5.3 years. Calculate the fraction of the original activity remaining after 26.5 years, and the net decline as a fraction. (3)
---
$n = \frac{26.5}{5.3} = 5$ half-lives (1). Fraction remaining $= \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{5} = \frac{1}{32}$ (1). Net decline $= 1 - \frac{1}{32} = \frac{31}{32}$ (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) The activity of a radioactive source falls from 96 kBq to 3 kBq in 40 days. Calculate the half-life. (3)
---
Ratio $96 : 3 = 32 : 1$ (1). $32 = 2^{5}$, so 5 half-lives have passed (1). Half-life $= \frac{40}{5} = 8$ days (1).
:::

::: test
A smoke alarm uses americium-241, an alpha emitter with a half-life of 432 years. Evaluate the choice of this source. (4)
---
Alpha is strongly ionising, so it ionises the air between the plates well, giving a measurable current that smoke can reduce (1). Alpha has a range of only a few centimetres in air and is stopped by the plastic case, so the radiation does not escape to harm people (1). The long half-life means the activity hardly falls over the life of the alarm, so the source does not need replacing (1). A beta or gamma source would be less ionising and more penetrating, so it would work less well and could escape the alarm — americium-241 is a good choice (1).
:::
