## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.6.1, **Waves in air, fluids and solids**. Transverse and longitudinal waves, wave properties, $v = f\lambda$ and Required practical 8 are in both GCSE Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 6.6.1, where the practical is **Required practical 20**). **Reflection of waves, Required practical 9, sound and hearing, and waves for detection and exploration are separate science only**; sound and hearing and detection and exploration are also **(Higher tier)**.

Waves **transfer energy** (and can carry information) from one place to another **without transferring matter**.

### Transverse and longitudinal waves

| | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| Direction of oscillations | **perpendicular** (at 90°) to the direction of energy transfer | **parallel** to the direction of energy transfer |
| Features | peaks (crests) and troughs | **compressions** (particles close together) and **rarefactions** (particles spread out) |
| Examples | ripples on a water surface, waves on a string, all electromagnetic waves, S-waves | sound waves in air, P-waves, a pushed-and-pulled slinky |

**Evidence that it is the wave, not the medium, that travels.**
- **Ripples**: a cork or small piece of paper floating in a ripple tank or pond **bobs up and down** as ripples pass, but does **not** move along with them. The water oscillates about a fixed position; the energy moves across the surface.
- **Sound**: air is not blown from a loudspeaker to your ear — there is no wind. A candle flame in front of a loudspeaker **flickers backwards and forwards** but is not pushed away. The air particles vibrate about fixed positions while the sound energy travels.
- A ribbon tied to a slinky moves back and forth (longitudinal) or up and down (transverse) but stays at the same point along the slinky.

### Properties of waves

| Property | Definition | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| **amplitude** | the maximum displacement of a point on a wave away from its undisturbed (rest) position | m |
| **wavelength**, $\lambda$ | the distance from a point on one wave to the equivalent point on the adjacent wave (e.g. crest to crest, or compression to compression) | m |
| **frequency**, $f$ | the number of waves passing a point each second | hertz, Hz |
| **period**, $T$ | the time for one complete wave to pass a point | s |
| **wave speed**, $v$ | the speed at which the energy is transferred (or the wave moves) through the medium | m/s |

**On a diagram:** amplitude is measured from the **middle (rest) line to a crest** — not crest to trough. Wavelength is one **complete cycle**, e.g. crest to next crest. On a displacement–**time** graph, the same spacing gives the **period**, not the wavelength.

**Period equation (given on the Physics equation sheet — you must be able to apply it):**

period = 1 ÷ frequency

$$T = \frac{1}{f}$$

period, $T$, in seconds, s; frequency, $f$, in hertz, Hz.

**Mini-example.** Mains-frequency vibrations of 50 Hz have a period $T = 1 \div 50 = 0.020$ s.

**The wave equation (recall — you must learn it). All waves obey it:**

wave speed = frequency × wavelength

$$v = f\lambda$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| wave speed | $v$ | metre per second, m/s |
| frequency | $f$ | hertz, Hz |
| wavelength | $\lambda$ | metre, m |

**Mini-example.** A sound wave of frequency 256 Hz travels at 330 m/s in air. $\lambda = v \div f = 330 \div 256 = 1.29$ m ≈ 1.3 m.

**Mini-example.** Water waves of wavelength 0.030 m have a frequency of 12 Hz. $v = 12 \times 0.030 = 0.36$ m/s.

Watch the prefixes: 1 kHz = 1000 Hz; 1 MHz = 1 000 000 Hz; 1 cm = 0.01 m.

### Measuring the speed of sound in air

**Method 1 — distance and time.** Two people stand a measured large distance apart (e.g. 200 m, measured with a trundle wheel). One bangs two blocks of wood or a clapperboard together; the other starts a stopwatch on **seeing** the bang and stops it on **hearing** it (light arrives almost instantly). Speed = distance ÷ time. Repeat several times and take a mean; use a large distance so reaction times are a small fraction of the time.

**Mini-example.** Distance 200 m, mean time 0.60 s. $v = 200 \div 0.60 = 333$ m/s ≈ 330 m/s.

**Method 2 — echoes.** Stand a measured distance (e.g. 50 m) from a large wall. Clap and time the echo — the sound travels **there and back**, so distance travelled = 2 × 50 m. Clapping in rhythm with the echoes and timing 20 claps reduces timing error.

**Method 3 — two microphones and an oscilloscope (or data logger).** Place two microphones a measured distance apart in line with a sound source; the time difference between the signals arriving is measured electronically. Much more precise, because there is no human reaction time.

### Measuring the speed of ripples on a water surface

Use a **ripple tank**: a shallow tray of water with a vibrating bar (dipper) driven by a motor, and a lamp above that projects the waves onto paper or a screen below.
- **Frequency**: count the number of waves passing a point in 10 s and divide by 10 (or read it from the signal generator, or use a stroboscope).
- **Wavelength**: place a ruler on the screen and measure the length of **10 waves**, then divide by 10. (Measuring several waves reduces the uncertainty.) A photo with a ruler in shot makes this easier.
- **Speed**: $v = f\lambda$. Alternatively, time how long one wave takes to travel a measured distance across the tank.

### Required practical 8 (Trilogy: Required practical 20): waves in a ripple tank and in a solid

**Aim:** make observations to identify the suitability of apparatus to measure the frequency, wavelength and speed of waves in a ripple tank and waves in a solid, and take appropriate measurements.

**Part 1 — ripple tank.** Set up as above with about 5 mm depth of water and the motor at a low frequency so that the waves are easy to see. Measure frequency and wavelength (over 10 waves) and calculate $v = f\lambda$. Suitability: a ruler on the screen is suitable for waves of a few cm; a stroboscope "freezes" the pattern so the waves can be measured; a camera photo with a scale is more accurate than measuring moving shadows.

**Part 2 — waves in a solid (a stretched string).** Attach a string or elastic cord to a **vibration generator** connected to a **signal generator**; run it over a pulley to a hanging mass (to keep it taut). Adjust the frequency until a clear **stationary wave** pattern appears (loops that do not move along). Measure the length of as many complete loops as possible with a metre rule — each loop is **half a wavelength**. Read the frequency from the signal generator. Calculate $v = f\lambda$. The speed should be the same at different frequencies for the same string and tension.

| Variable | Ripple tank | String |
|---|---|---|
| independent | frequency of the dipper | frequency of the signal generator |
| dependent | wavelength | wavelength |
| control | depth of water | string type, length, tension (hanging mass) |

**Risks:** water near electrical equipment — keep the power supply away from the tank and mop up spills; stroboscope may trigger photosensitive epilepsy — check before use; the hanging mass could fall — keep feet clear and use a box underneath; a stretched elastic cord could snap — wear eye protection.

**Typical results:** increasing the frequency **decreases** the wavelength, while the speed stays about the same (it depends on the medium — water depth, or string tension and type). Shallower water gives slower ripples.

### Separate science only: sound waves changing medium

When sound passes from one medium into another, its **frequency stays the same** (it is set by the source), but its **speed changes**, so its **wavelength changes** in proportion ($\lambda = v \div f$).

| Medium | Typical speed of sound |
|---|---|
| air | 330 m/s |
| water | 1500 m/s |
| steel | about 5000–6000 m/s |

**Mini-example.** A 500 Hz sound passes from air into water. In air: $\lambda = 330 \div 500 = 0.66$ m. In water: $\lambda = 1500 \div 500 = 3.0$ m. Speed and wavelength increase by the same factor; frequency is unchanged.

### Separate science only: reflection of waves

At the **boundary between two different materials**, waves can be:
- **reflected** — bounce back;
- **absorbed** — energy transferred to the material (often heating it);
- **transmitted** — pass through, usually **refracting** (changing direction) if they meet the boundary at an angle.

What happens depends on the **wavelength** and the **materials**. Examples: a mirror reflects most light; black fabric absorbs most light; glass transmits most light; soft furnishings and curtains absorb sound, while hard bare walls reflect it (echoes).

**Ray diagrams for reflection.** Draw the **normal** (a dashed line at 90° to the surface where the ray hits). Measure all angles **from the normal**.

**Law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection.**

- **Specular reflection** — from a **smooth** surface (mirror, still water): parallel rays stay parallel, so a clear image forms.
- **Diffuse reflection** — from a **rough** surface (paper, wall): each ray still obeys the law, but the normals point in different directions, so the rays scatter and no image forms.

### Separate science only: Required practical 9 — reflection and refraction of light

**Aim:** investigate the reflection of light by different types of surface and the refraction of light by different substances.

**Method:**
1. In a darkened room, use a **ray box** with a single slit to produce a narrow ray of light.
2. **Reflection**: place a plane mirror (then other surfaces, e.g. white card, foil, black paper) on a line on paper. Draw a normal. Shine the ray at the surface at angles of incidence of, say, 20°, 30°, 40°… Mark the incident and reflected rays with crosses, join them with a ruler, and measure the angles with a **protractor**. Note how clear (bright, sharp) the reflected ray is for each surface.
3. **Refraction**: place a rectangular block (glass, Perspex, or a clear box of water) on the paper and draw round it. Shine a ray into one face at an angle. Mark the ray entering and leaving, remove the block, join the points, and draw the normal where the ray enters. Measure the angle of incidence and the angle of refraction. Repeat for blocks of different substances, keeping the angle of incidence the same.

**Variables:** independent — surface type / substance (or angle of incidence); dependent — angle of reflection / refraction; control — same ray box and width of ray, same angle of incidence when comparing materials.

**Risks:** ray boxes get hot — do not touch the lamp housing and let it cool; the room is dim, so keep bags clear to avoid tripping; handle glass blocks carefully.

**Improving accuracy:** use a narrow ray; mark the ray at points far apart; use a sharp pencil; repeat and average.

**Typical results:**
- Angle of incidence = angle of reflection for every surface. Smooth, shiny surfaces (mirror, foil) give a bright, clear reflected ray; matt or dark surfaces give a dim or diffuse reflection.
- Light **bends towards the normal** when it enters glass, Perspex or water from air (it slows down), and **away from the normal** when it leaves. A ray along the normal passes straight through. Glass bends light more than water. (Why refraction happens is explained in 4.6.2.)

### Separate science only: sound waves and hearing (Higher tier)

Sound waves can travel **through solids**, causing **vibrations** in the solid. Processes that convert between sound waves and vibrations in solids include:
- **the ear**: sound waves make the **ear drum** vibrate; the vibrations pass through the small bones (ossicles) to the cochlea, which produces nerve signals — the sensation of sound;
- **a microphone**: sound waves make a diaphragm vibrate, producing an electrical signal;
- **a loudspeaker**: a vibrating cone produces sound waves in the air;
- a window or table that vibrates when a loud sound (e.g. a lorry) passes.

**Why only a limited frequency range works.** The conversion of sound waves to vibrations of solids only works over a **limited frequency range**, because the solid parts (ear drum, bones) have **mass and stiffness**: they cannot vibrate quickly enough to follow very high frequencies, and they respond only weakly to very low frequencies. This **restricts the limits of human hearing**.

**The range of normal human hearing is 20 Hz to 20 kHz.** The upper limit falls with age, and loud noise can damage the ear and reduce the range.

### Separate science only: waves for detection and exploration (Higher tier)

Different types of wave have different **velocities**, and are **absorbed** and **reflected** differently by solids and liquids. These differences let us **detect and explore structures hidden from direct observation**.

**Ultrasound** is sound with a frequency **above 20 kHz** — higher than the upper limit of human hearing. Ultrasound waves are **partially reflected** when they meet a **boundary between two different media**. The **time** for the reflections to reach a detector is used to work out **how far away the boundary is**:

$$\text{distance to boundary} = \frac{\text{speed} \times \text{time for echo}}{2}$$

(÷ 2 because the pulse travels there and back.)
- **Medical imaging** — pre-natal scans of a fetus, scans of organs; ultrasound is non-ionising, so safe.
- **Industrial imaging** — detecting cracks or flaws inside metal castings, pipes and welds: an extra echo arrives early from the crack.

**Mini-example.** An ultrasound pulse travels through soft tissue at 1540 m/s; the echo from an organ returns after 0.13 ms. Distance $= 1540 \times 0.000\,13 \div 2 = 0.10$ m = 10 cm.

**Echo sounding** uses high-frequency sound waves (sonar) to **detect objects in deep water** (shoals of fish, submarines, wrecks) and **measure the depth of water**.

**Mini-example.** A ship's pulse returns from the sea bed after 0.80 s. Speed of sound in sea water = 1500 m/s. Depth $= 1500 \times 0.80 \div 2 = 600$ m.

**Seismic waves** are produced by **earthquakes** and detected by seismometers around the world.

| | P-waves | S-waves |
|---|---|---|
| type | **longitudinal** | **transverse** |
| travel through | solids **and liquids** (at different speeds) | solids only — **cannot travel through a liquid** |
| speed | faster (arrive first) | slower |

**Evidence for the Earth's core.** S-waves are not detected on the opposite side of the Earth from an earthquake (an **S-wave shadow zone**), so part of the core — the **outer core** — must be **liquid**. P-waves **refract** (change speed and direction) at the boundaries between layers, leaving a **P-wave shadow zone**; the size of the shadow zones lets scientists work out the **size of the core**. P-waves speeding up again in the centre suggest a solid inner core.

The study of seismic waves provided **new evidence** that led to discoveries about parts of the Earth that are **not directly observable** — nobody can drill to the core, so the model of the Earth's structure is built from wave data.

::: terms
- **Transverse wave**: a wave whose oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer, such as ripples on water.
- **Longitudinal wave**: a wave whose oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer, such as sound in air.
- **Compression**: a region of a longitudinal wave where the particles are closer together than normal.
- **Rarefaction**: a region of a longitudinal wave where the particles are further apart than normal.
- **Amplitude**: the maximum displacement of a point on a wave away from its undisturbed position.
- **Wavelength**: the distance from a point on one wave to the equivalent point on the adjacent wave.
- **Frequency**: the number of waves passing a point each second, measured in hertz.
- **Period**: the time taken for one complete wave to pass a point; equal to one divided by frequency.
- **Wave speed**: the speed at which energy is transferred, or the wave moves, through a medium.
- **Normal**: a line drawn at right angles to a surface where a ray meets it; angles are measured from it.
- **Ultrasound**: sound waves with a frequency above 20 kHz, the upper limit of human hearing.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe the difference** between transverse and longitudinal waves: compare the **direction of oscillation** with the **direction of energy transfer** for both, and give an example of each.
- **Describe evidence** that the wave, not the medium, travels: name the observation (cork bobbing, candle flame flickering) and say what it shows.
- **Describe a method** (speed of sound, ripples, RP8/RP9): apparatus, what you measure and how, how many waves/repeats, the equation, and one way to improve accuracy.
- **Explain** (separate science only, Higher tier): link the wave property to the use — e.g. S-waves cannot travel through liquids → shadow zone → outer core is liquid.

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit)
1. $v = f\lambda$ is **recall**; $T = 1/f$ is on the **equation sheet**.
2. Convert prefixes first: kHz × 1000, MHz × 1 000 000, cm ÷ 100, ms ÷ 1000.
3. Wavelength from a measurement of several waves: total length ÷ number of waves. On a string, each loop is **half** a wavelength.
4. Echo questions: the wave travels **there and back**, so halve the total distance.
5. Sound changing medium (separate science only): frequency stays the same; $\lambda$ changes in proportion to $v$.

**Diagram questions**
Amplitude: rest line to crest. Wavelength: one full cycle. On reflection diagrams, draw the normal, use a ruler and arrows, and make angle of incidence = angle of reflection.

**Required practicals 8 (Trilogy 20) and 9 (separate science only)**
- RP8: suitability — measure across many waves, use a stroboscope or photo to freeze the pattern, read frequency from the signal generator.
- RP9: angles measured from the normal with a protractor; narrow ray; darkened room; risks — hot ray box.
- Errors: random errors in measuring moving waves (reduced by repeats and measuring many wavelengths); parallax when reading a ruler.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**
Methods: Level 3 is a complete, ordered method that would work, with measurements, the calculation and improvements. Seismic or ultrasound explanations: Level 3 links each property (speed, reflection, absorption, ability to pass through liquids) to the conclusion drawn.

::: example Using the wave equation
A guitar string produces a note of frequency 440 Hz. The sound travels at 330 m/s. Calculate (a) the wavelength and (b) the period of the sound wave. (4)

(a) $\lambda = v \div f = 330 \div 440$ (1) $= 0.75$ m (1).
(b) $T = 1 \div f = 1 \div 440$ (1) $= 0.0023$ s (2.3 ms) (1).
:::

::: example Ripple tank measurements
In a ripple tank, 8 waves span 24 cm on the screen. The student counts 30 waves passing a point in 10 s. Calculate the speed of the ripples. (4)

$\lambda = 24 \div 8 = 3.0$ cm $= 0.030$ m (1). $f = 30 \div 10 = 3.0$ Hz (1). $v = f\lambda = 3.0 \times 0.030$ (1) $= 0.090$ m/s (1).
:::

::: example Speed of sound from an echo
A student stands 85 m from a large wall and claps. The echo is heard 0.50 s later. Calculate the speed of sound. (3)

Distance travelled $= 2 \times 85 = 170$ m (1). $v = 170 \div 0.50$ (1) $= 340$ m/s (1).
:::

::: example Sound entering water (separate science only)
A 1000 Hz sound passes from air (330 m/s) into water (1500 m/s). Describe and calculate the changes to its frequency and wavelength. (3)

The frequency stays at 1000 Hz (1). Wavelength in air $= 330 \div 1000 = 0.33$ m (1); in water $= 1500 \div 1000 = 1.5$ m — the wavelength increases because the speed increases (1).
:::

::: example Seismic evidence (separate science only, Higher tier)
Explain how S-waves provide evidence that the Earth's outer core is liquid. (3)

S-waves are transverse (1) and cannot travel through liquids (1). They are not detected in a large region on the far side of the Earth from an earthquake (the S-wave shadow zone), so they must be stopped by a liquid layer — the outer core (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Amplitude from crest to trough**: amplitude is from the rest position to a crest — half the crest-to-trough height.
- **Wavelength vs period**: on a displacement–distance graph the repeat length is the wavelength; on a displacement–time graph it is the period.
- **Unit prefixes**: kHz, MHz, cm and ms must be converted before using $v = f\lambda$ or $T = 1/f$.
- **Vague wave descriptions**: "transverse waves go up and down" is not enough — compare the oscillations with the **direction of energy transfer**.
- **Echo distances**: forgetting to halve — the pulse goes there and back.
- **Changing medium (separate science only)**: the **frequency does not change**; speed and wavelength do.
- **Angles (separate science only)**: angles of incidence, reflection and refraction are measured from the **normal**, not from the surface.
- **Seismic waves (separate science only, Higher tier)**: S-waves are transverse and **cannot pass through liquids**; P-waves are longitudinal and pass through both.
