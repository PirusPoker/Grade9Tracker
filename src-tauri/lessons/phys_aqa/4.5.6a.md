## What it is

This lesson covers AQA 4.5.6.1, **Describing motion along a line**. All of it is in both GCSE Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (Trilogy 6.5.4.1). Statements marked **(Higher tier)** are HT only. Terminal velocity and the forces behind changes in motion are in the next lesson (4.5.6b).

### Scalars and vectors: distance and displacement

- **Distance** is how far an object moves. It has size (magnitude) only, so it is a **scalar** quantity.
- **Displacement** is the distance moved **in a straight line from the start point to the finish point**, together with the **direction** of that line. It is a **vector** quantity.

You must be able to give a displacement as **both a magnitude and a direction**, e.g. "5.0 km on a bearing of 037°" or "12 m to the left".

**Mini-example.** A runner completes one lap of a 400 m track. Distance = 400 m. Displacement = 0 m, because she finishes where she started.

**Mini-example.** A walker goes 3.0 km east, then 4.0 km north. Distance = 7.0 km. Displacement: by Pythagoras $\sqrt{3.0^2 + 4.0^2} = 5.0$ km; the angle north of east is $\tan^{-1}(4.0 \div 3.0) = 53°$, which is a bearing of 037°. So the displacement is 5.0 km on a bearing of 037° (scale drawing gives the same answer).

| Scalar (magnitude only) | Vector (magnitude and direction) |
|---|---|
| distance | displacement |
| speed | velocity |
| mass, time, energy | acceleration, force, momentum |

### Speed

**Speed** is how fast something moves. It does not involve direction, so speed is a **scalar**.

The speed of a moving object is rarely constant. When people walk, run or travel in a car their speed keeps changing. How fast a person can walk, run or cycle depends on **age, terrain, fitness and the distance travelled**. The speed of the wind and the speed of sound also vary.

**Typical speeds you must recall:**

| Moving thing | Typical speed |
|---|---|
| walking | 1.5 m/s |
| running | 3 m/s |
| cycling | 6 m/s |
| car in town (30 mph) | about 13 m/s |
| car on a motorway (70 mph) | about 31 m/s |
| high-speed train | about 50–80 m/s |
| passenger aircraft (cruising) | about 250 m/s |
| sound in air | 330 m/s |
| wind | varies, about 5–20 m/s on a breezy day |

**Equation (recall — you must learn it):**

distance travelled = speed × time

$$s = vt$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| distance | $s$ | metre, m |
| speed | $v$ | metre per second, m/s |
| time | $t$ | second, s |

This equation is for **constant speed**. For **non-uniform motion** you calculate the **average speed**:

$$\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance travelled}}{\text{total time taken}}$$

**Mini-example.** A cyclist rides 3.0 km in 10 minutes, then 2.0 km in 5 minutes. Total distance = 5000 m; total time = 15 × 60 = 900 s. Average speed = 5000 ÷ 900 = 5.6 m/s. (Not the mean of the two speeds — always use totals.)

**Unit conversions (MS 1, 3c).** 1 km = 1000 m; 1 hour = 3600 s. To turn km/h into m/s, divide by 3.6: 72 km/h = 72 000 m ÷ 3600 s = 20 m/s.

**Measuring speed.** Measure a distance with a tape measure or trundle wheel and a time with a stopwatch or **light gates** and a data logger, then use speed = distance ÷ time. Light gates remove human reaction time from the timing. With a stopwatch, use a **long distance** (long time), so the reaction-time error is a small percentage of the reading, and repeat to find a mean.

### Velocity

The **velocity** of an object is its **speed in a given direction**. Velocity is a **vector**. Two cars at 20 m/s going in opposite directions have the same speed but different velocities (+20 m/s and −20 m/s).

**Circular motion (Higher tier).** An object moving in a circle at **constant speed** has a **changing velocity**, because its **direction** is changing all the time. A changing velocity means it is **accelerating**, so there must be a resultant force on it (directed towards the centre of the circle). Examples: the Moon orbiting the Earth, a satellite in orbit, a car driving round a roundabout at a steady speed, a hammer thrower's hammer, clothes in a spin dryer.

### Distance–time graphs

If an object moves along a straight line, its motion can be shown on a **distance–time graph**.

- **Gradient = speed.** Speed = change in distance ÷ change in time.
- **Horizontal line**: stationary (gradient zero).
- **Straight sloping line**: constant speed. Steeper line = faster.
- **Curve getting steeper**: speeding up (accelerating). **Curve getting less steep**: slowing down.

**Mini-example.** A line goes from (0 s, 0 m) to (20 s, 60 m). Speed = 60 ÷ 20 = 3.0 m/s.

**Speed at an instant on a curve (Higher tier).** If the object is accelerating, find its speed at a particular time by **drawing a tangent** to the curve at that time and **measuring the gradient of the tangent**. Make the tangent's triangle as large as possible to reduce error.

**Mini-example (Higher tier).** A tangent drawn at $t$ = 4.0 s passes through (2.0 s, 0 m) and (6.0 s, 24 m). Speed at 4.0 s = (24 − 0) ÷ (6.0 − 2.0) = 6.0 m/s.

You must be able to **draw** distance–time graphs from measurements (time on the x-axis, sensible scales using more than half the grid, plotted points, a line or smooth curve of best fit) and translate between the graph and numbers.

### Acceleration

**Equation (recall — you must learn it):**

acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken

$$a = \frac{\Delta v}{t}$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| acceleration | $a$ | metre per second squared, m/s² |
| change in velocity | $\Delta v$ | metre per second, m/s |
| time | $t$ | second, s |

$\Delta v$ = final velocity − initial velocity. An object that **slows down** is **decelerating** — its acceleration is negative.

**Mini-example.** A car goes from 0 to 24 m/s in 8.0 s: $a = 24 \div 8.0 = 3.0$ m/s². A cyclist slows from 8.0 m/s to 2.0 m/s in 3.0 s: $a = (2.0 - 8.0) \div 3.0 = -2.0$ m/s², a deceleration of 2.0 m/s².

**Estimating everyday accelerations (MS 1d).** Use the symbol ≈ for an approximate value.

| Situation | Rough acceleration |
|---|---|
| bus pulling away | ≈ 1 m/s² |
| family car pulling away (0–27 m/s in about 10 s) | ≈ 3 m/s² |
| sprinter leaving the blocks | ≈ 5 m/s² |
| object falling freely near the Earth | 9.8 m/s² |
| car in an emergency stop on a dry road | ≈ 7 m/s² deceleration |

**Free fall.** Near the Earth's surface any object falling freely under gravity (no air resistance) has an acceleration of about **9.8 m/s²**. An object falling through a fluid (air, water) first accelerates because of gravity; eventually the resultant force becomes zero and it moves at its **terminal velocity** (see 4.5.6b).

### Velocity–time graphs

- **Gradient = acceleration.** Steeper = greater acceleration.
- **Horizontal line**: constant velocity (zero acceleration).
- **Negative gradient**: deceleration.
- **Line on the time axis**: stationary.
- **Curve**: changing acceleration; gradient of a tangent gives the acceleration at that instant.

**Area under the graph = distance travelled (or displacement) (Higher tier).** Split the area into rectangles and triangles. Where the line is curved, **count squares** (Higher tier): work out the distance one square represents, count whole squares and those more than half covered, and multiply.

**Mini-example.** A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 12 m/s in 4.0 s, travels at 12 m/s for 6.0 s, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 3.0 s.

| Stage | Acceleration (gradient) | Distance (area) (Higher tier) |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 s | 12 ÷ 4.0 = 3.0 m/s² | ½ × 4.0 × 12 = 24 m |
| 4–10 s | 0 | 12 × 6.0 = 72 m |
| 10–13 s | −12 ÷ 3.0 = −4.0 m/s² | ½ × 3.0 × 12 = 18 m |

Total distance = 24 + 72 + 18 = 114 m.

**Counting squares (Higher tier).** If each square is 2 m/s tall and 5 s wide, one square = 2 × 5 = 10 m. An area of about 37 squares means about 370 m.

You must be able to **draw** velocity–time graphs from measurements and interpret lines and slopes to find accelerations.

### The uniform-acceleration equation

**Equation (given on the Physics equation sheet — you must be able to apply it):**

final velocity² − initial velocity² = 2 × acceleration × distance

$$v^2 - u^2 = 2as$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| final velocity | $v$ | m/s |
| initial velocity | $u$ | m/s |
| acceleration | $a$ | m/s² |
| distance | $s$ | m |

It only applies to **uniform (constant) acceleration**. It is useful when **no time** is given.

**Mini-example.** A stone is dropped from rest and falls 20 m. Ignoring air resistance, $v^2 = 0^2 + 2 \times 9.8 \times 20 = 392$, so $v = \sqrt{392} = 19.8$ m/s ≈ 20 m/s.

**Mini-example.** A car at 30 m/s brakes with a deceleration of 6.0 m/s². Stopping distance under braking: $0^2 - 30^2 = 2 \times (-6.0) \times s$, so $s = 900 \div 12 = 75$ m.

| Equation | Recall or equation sheet? |

::: terms
- **Distance**: how far an object moves, with no direction; a scalar quantity measured in metres.
- **Displacement**: the straight-line distance from start point to finish point together with its direction; a vector.
- **Speed**: the distance travelled per unit time, with no direction; a scalar measured in m/s.
- **Velocity**: the speed of an object in a given direction; a vector quantity.
- **Average speed**: total distance travelled divided by total time taken, used for non-uniform motion.
- **Acceleration**: the rate of change of velocity, change in velocity divided by time, in m/s².
- **Deceleration**: slowing down; an acceleration in the opposite direction to the motion.
- **Gradient**: the steepness of a graph line, change in y divided by change in x.
- **Tangent**: a straight line touching a curve at one point, whose gradient gives the rate at that instant.
- **Free fall**: falling under gravity alone, with an acceleration of about 9.8 m/s² near the Earth.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State / give**: a short fact, e.g. "velocity is a vector".
- **Describe** a graph: say what the object is doing in each section (stationary, constant speed, speeding up, slowing down) and quote values with units from the axes.
- **Explain** (e.g. circular motion, Higher tier): link each step — direction changes → velocity changes → the object accelerates → a resultant force must act.
- **Determine / calculate from a graph**: show the triangle or areas you used on the graph, then the arithmetic.
- **Estimate**: use sensible typical values (walking 1.5 m/s, car about 13 m/s in town) and give a rounded answer with ≈.

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit and sensible significant figures)
1. Write the equation in symbols first: $s = vt$, $a = \Delta v / t$ (both **recall**), or $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$ (on the **equation sheet**).
2. Convert units before substituting: km → m (× 1000), minutes → s (× 60), hours → s (× 3600), km/h → m/s (÷ 3.6).
3. Change in velocity is **final − initial**; a negative answer means deceleration.
4. For $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$, square the speeds before subtracting and remember to **square-root** at the end when finding $v$ or $u$.
5. Give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data (usually 2 or 3).

**Graph questions**
- Distance–time: gradient = speed. Velocity–time: gradient = acceleration, area = distance (Higher tier).
- Use a **large** triangle for gradients. On a curve, draw a tangent (Higher tier).
- Areas: split into triangles (½ × base × height) and rectangles; for curves count squares and multiply by the value of one square.
- Read the axes carefully: do not confuse a distance–time graph with a velocity–time graph — a horizontal line means "stopped" on one and "constant velocity" on the other.

**Required-practical-style questions (measuring speed)**
Independent variable (e.g. the activity or slope), dependent variable (speed), control variables (same measured distance, same surface). Use a long distance and a mean of repeats to reduce the effect of reaction time; light gates give more accurate timings than a hand-held stopwatch.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**
Describe the motion section by section **with numbers**, or give an ordered method with apparatus, measurements, the calculation and how to improve accuracy. Level 3 answers are complete and logically linked; Level 2 answers miss a stage or the processing; Level 1 answers are a few disconnected statements.
:::

::: example Average speed with unit conversion
A train travels 54 km in 20 minutes. Calculate its average speed in m/s. (3)

Distance = 54 000 m and time = 20 × 60 = 1200 s (1). $v = s \div t = 54\,000 \div 1200$ (1) $= 45$ m/s (1).
:::

::: example Acceleration and deceleration
A sprinter accelerates from rest to 9.0 m/s in 1.8 s. Later a car slows from 25 m/s to 10 m/s in 5.0 s. Calculate each acceleration. (4)

Sprinter: $a = (9.0 - 0) \div 1.8$ (1) $= 5.0$ m/s² (1).
Car: $a = (10 - 25) \div 5.0$ (1) $= -3.0$ m/s², i.e. a deceleration of 3.0 m/s² (1).
:::

::: example Using a velocity–time graph (Higher tier)
A cyclist starts from rest and accelerates uniformly to 8.0 m/s in 10 s, then rides at 8.0 m/s for a further 20 s. Calculate (a) the acceleration and (b) the total distance travelled. (4)

(a) Gradient $= 8.0 \div 10 = 0.80$ m/s² (1)(1).
(b) Area of triangle $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 8.0 = 40$ m; area of rectangle $= 20 \times 8.0 = 160$ m (1). Total = 200 m (1).
:::

::: example Using v² − u² = 2as
A ball is thrown vertically upwards at 14 m/s. Ignoring air resistance, its deceleration is 9.8 m/s². Calculate the maximum height it reaches. (3)

At the top $v = 0$. $0^2 - 14^2 = 2 \times (-9.8) \times s$ (1). $s = 196 \div 19.6$ (1) $= 10$ m (1).
:::

::: example Tangent on a distance–time graph (Higher tier)
A distance–time graph for an accelerating car is a curve. A tangent drawn at $t$ = 5.0 s passes through (1.0 s, 0 m) and (9.0 s, 120 m). Determine the car's speed at 5.0 s. (2)

Gradient of tangent $= (120 - 0) \div (9.0 - 1.0)$ (1) $= 15$ m/s (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between distance and displacement. (2)
---
Distance is how far an object moves and has no direction — it is a scalar (1). Displacement is the straight-line distance from start to finish **in a given direction** — it is a vector (1).
:::

::: q
State a typical walking speed and use it to estimate how long it takes to walk 900 m. (3)
---
Walking ≈ 1.5 m/s (1). $t = s \div v = 900 \div 1.5$ (1) $= 600$ s, i.e. about 10 minutes (1).
:::

::: q
Convert 90 km/h into m/s. (2)
---
90 km/h = 90 000 m ÷ 3600 s (1) = 25 m/s (1).
:::

::: q
A car accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 4.0 s. Calculate its acceleration. (2)
---
$a = \Delta v \div t = 20 \div 4.0$ (1) $= 5.0$ m/s² (1).
:::

::: q
A distance–time graph is a straight line from (0 s, 0 m) to (40 s, 200 m), then a horizontal line until 60 s. Describe the motion and calculate the speed in the first section. (3)
---
For 0–40 s the object moves at a constant speed (1) of $200 \div 40 = 5.0$ m/s (1). From 40 s to 60 s it is stationary (1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) Explain why a car driving round a roundabout at a steady 10 m/s is accelerating. (2)
---
Its direction is continually changing, so its velocity is changing (velocity is a vector) (1). Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, so it is accelerating (even though its speed is constant) (1).
:::

::: q
(Higher tier) A car decelerates uniformly from 20 m/s to rest in 5.0 s. Use the area under its velocity–time graph to find the distance it travels while braking. (2)
---
Area of triangle $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 5.0 \times 20$ (1) $= 50$ m (1).
:::

::: q
A car travelling at 5.0 m/s accelerates uniformly at 2.0 m/s² over a distance of 50 m. Calculate its final speed. (3)
---
$v^2 = u^2 + 2as = 5.0^2 + 2 \times 2.0 \times 50$ (1) $= 25 + 200 = 225$ (1). $v = \sqrt{225} = 15$ m/s (1).
:::

::: q
State what the gradient of (a) a distance–time graph and (b) a velocity–time graph represents. (2)
---
(a) Speed (1). (b) Acceleration (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Distance–time vs velocity–time**: a horizontal line means "stopped" on a distance–time graph but "constant velocity" on a velocity–time graph. Read the y-axis label first.
- **Average speed**: total distance ÷ total time — never the mean of two speeds.
- **Units not converted**: km, minutes and hours must become m and s before using $s = vt$ or $a = \Delta v / t$.
- **Change in velocity**: it is final − initial. Using just the final speed gives the wrong acceleration whenever the object did not start from rest.
- **Forgetting the square root**: $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$ gives $v^2$; you must square-root to get $v$.
- **Scalar or vector**: "speed is a vector" loses the mark. Speed and distance are scalars; velocity and displacement are vectors.
- **Circular motion (Higher tier)**: saying "the speed changes" is wrong — the speed is constant; the direction, and so the velocity, changes.
- **Area triangles**: forgetting the ½ in ½ × base × height.
:::

::: test
An athlete runs one 400 m lap of a track in 50 s and finishes where she started. (a) Calculate her average speed. (b) State her displacement at the finish and explain your answer. (4)
---
(a) $v = 400 \div 50$ (1) $= 8.0$ m/s (1). (b) Zero (1), because displacement is the straight-line distance from start to finish and she ends at the start point (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A car accelerates uniformly from 12 m/s to 30 m/s in 6.0 s. (a) Calculate its acceleration. (b) Use the area under the velocity–time graph to calculate the distance travelled in this time. (4)
---
(a) $a = (30 - 12) \div 6.0$ (1) $= 3.0$ m/s² (1).
(b) Area = rectangle + triangle $= 12 \times 6.0 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 6.0 \times 18$ (1) $= 72 + 54 = 126$ m, about 130 m (1). (Or trapezium: $\tfrac{1}{2}(12 + 30) \times 6.0 = 126$ m.)
:::

::: test
An aircraft must reach 70 m/s to take off. It accelerates uniformly from rest at 2.5 m/s². Calculate the minimum length of runway needed. Use the equation $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$. (3)
---
$70^2 - 0^2 = 2 \times 2.5 \times s$ (1). $s = 4900 \div 5.0$ (1) $= 980$ m (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A satellite moves in a circular orbit at a constant speed. Explain why the satellite is accelerating and what this tells you about the force on it. (3)
---
The direction of motion is always changing (1), so its velocity changes because velocity is a vector (1). A changing velocity is an acceleration, so there must be a resultant force acting on it (towards the centre of the orbit — gravity) (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) A velocity–time graph for a toy car over 20 s is a curve. Each grid square is 0.5 m/s tall and 2 s wide. The area under the curve covers about 42 squares. Estimate (a) the distance travelled and (b) the average speed. (3)
---
(a) One square $= 0.5 \times 2 = 1$ m (1), so distance ≈ 42 m (1). (b) Average speed $= 42 \div 20 ≈ 2.1$ m/s (1).
:::

::: test
A stone is dropped from rest from a bridge and hits the water 2.0 s later. Air resistance can be ignored; the acceleration due to gravity is 9.8 m/s². (a) Calculate the speed of the stone as it hits the water. (b) Calculate the height of the bridge above the water. (4)
---
(a) $\Delta v = a \times t = 9.8 \times 2.0$ (1) $= 19.6$ m/s ≈ 20 m/s (1).
(b) $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$: $19.6^2 - 0 = 2 \times 9.8 \times s$ (1), $s = 384.16 \div 19.6 = 19.6$ m ≈ 20 m (1).
:::

::: test
Describe a method that a class could use to measure and compare typical walking, running and cycling speeds, and explain how they could make their results as accurate as possible. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a clear, logically ordered method with measurements of distance and time, the calculation of speed, and several valid ways to improve accuracy, explained.
Level 2 (3–4): a workable method with the calculation, but limited detail on accuracy or some steps missing.
Level 1 (1–2): simple relevant statements, e.g. "time people running and work out the speed".
Indicative content:
- measure out a straight course, e.g. 50 m or 100 m, with a tape measure or trundle wheel
- one person walks / runs / cycles the course while another times them with a stopwatch (start when they cross the start line, stop at the finish)
- speed = distance ÷ time
- repeat for each activity and for several students, and calculate a mean
- use a long course so the timer's reaction time is a small fraction of the total time
- start the person moving before the start line so they are at full speed (measures steady speed, not acceleration)
- use the same timer each time, or light gates / video with a timer to remove reaction time
- same flat surface and conditions for all trials (control variables)
- compare results with typical values: walking ≈ 1.5 m/s, running ≈ 3 m/s, cycling ≈ 6 m/s
