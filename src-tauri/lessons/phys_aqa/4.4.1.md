## What it is

Everything is made of atoms — but what is inside an atom, and how did scientists find out? This topic covers the **structure of the atom**, **atomic and mass numbers**, **isotopes and ions**, and the **history of the atomic model**, from tiny solid spheres to the nuclear model. All of it is in **both** Physics 8463 and Combined Science: Trilogy 8464 (where it is 6.4.1), and 4.4.1.3 is **common content with chemistry**, so you may meet it on a chemistry paper too. There is no required practical and nothing here is Higher tier only.

### The structure of an atom

Atoms are **very small**: the radius of an atom is about $1 \times 10^{-10}$ m (0.1 nanometres).

The basic structure:

- a **nucleus** at the centre, which is **positively charged** and made of **protons** and **neutrons**;
- **electrons**, which are **negatively charged**, arranged around the nucleus.

The nucleus is **tiny** compared with the atom: its radius is **less than 1/10 000** of the radius of the atom (around $1 \times 10^{-14}$ m or smaller). Yet **almost all the mass** of the atom is concentrated in the nucleus, because protons and neutrons are far heavier than electrons. Most of an atom is empty space.

| Particle | Where it is | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| neutron | nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| electron | energy levels around the nucleus | −1 | very small (about 1/2000) |

*Mini-example (standard form).* An atom has radius $1 \times 10^{-10}$ m. The nucleus radius is at most $\frac{1 \times 10^{-10}}{10\,000} = \frac{1 \times 10^{-10}}{1 \times 10^{4}} = 1 \times 10^{-14}$ m. If a nucleus were the size of a 1 cm marble, the atom would be at least 10 000 cm = 100 m across — about the size of a football stadium.

### Electrons and energy levels

Electrons are arranged at **different distances from the nucleus**, called **energy levels** (or shells). Electrons further from the nucleus are in **higher** energy levels.

The electron arrangement can change:

- if an atom **absorbs electromagnetic radiation**, an electron can gain energy and **move further from the nucleus** — to a **higher energy level**;
- if an electron **moves closer to the nucleus** — to a **lower energy level** — the atom **emits (gives out) electromagnetic radiation**.

This is why heated gases give out light of particular colours: electrons drop back down to lower energy levels and emit the energy as electromagnetic radiation.

### Atomic number, mass number and the nuclear symbol

In an atom, **the number of electrons equals the number of protons** in the nucleus. The positive and negative charges cancel, so **atoms have no overall electrical charge**.

- **Atomic number** (proton number), $Z$: the number of **protons** in the nucleus. **All atoms of a particular element have the same number of protons** — the atomic number identifies the element.
- **Mass number**, $A$: the **total number of protons and neutrons** in the nucleus.
- Number of **neutrons** = mass number − atomic number.

Atoms are written like this, with the mass number at the top and the atomic number at the bottom:

$${}^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$$

Sodium-23: 11 protons, $23 - 11 = 12$ neutrons, and (as a neutral atom) 11 electrons.

### Isotopes

> **Isotopes** are atoms of the **same element** (same number of protons) with **different numbers of neutrons**.

Isotopes have the **same atomic number** but a **different mass number**. They have the same number of electrons, so they behave the same way chemically, but they have different masses — and some isotopes have unstable nuclei, which makes them radioactive (4.4.2).

| Isotope | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| carbon-12 | ${}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ | 6 | 6 | 6 |
| carbon-13 | ${}^{13}_{6}\text{C}$ | 6 | 7 | 6 |
| carbon-14 | ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$ | 6 | 8 | 6 |

### Ions

Atoms turn into **positive ions** if they **lose one or more outer electrons**. The nucleus is unchanged (same protons and neutrons), but there are now more protons than electrons, so there is an overall positive charge.

*Mini-example.* A sodium atom loses its one outer electron. ${}^{23}_{11}\text{Na}^{+}$ has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and **10** electrons; its charge is $+11 - 10 = +1$. Its mass is almost unchanged, because an electron has so little mass.

Comparing the conventional representations: a different **bottom number** means a different element; a different **top number** with the same bottom number means a different isotope (different mass); a **charge sign** (such as $^{+}$ or $^{2+}$) means an ion with electrons missing.

### The development of the model of the atom

**New experimental evidence may lead to a scientific model being changed or replaced.** The atomic model is the classic example.

| Stage | Model | What led to it |
|---|---|---|
| Early 1800s (Dalton) | atoms are **tiny spheres that cannot be divided** | chemical evidence; nothing smaller was known |
| 1897 (J J Thomson) | **plum pudding model**: a ball of **positive charge** with **negative electrons embedded** in it | **discovery of the electron** — atoms must contain smaller, negative particles |
| 1909–1911 (Geiger, Marsden and Rutherford) | **nuclear model**: mass concentrated in a tiny, positively charged **nucleus** at the centre, with electrons around it | **alpha particle scattering experiment** |
| 1913 (Niels Bohr) | electrons **orbit the nucleus at specific distances** (energy levels) | Bohr's theoretical calculations **agreed with experimental observations** |
| Around 1920 | positive charge of the nucleus is made of a whole number of smaller identical particles: **protons** | later experiments |
| 1932 (James Chadwick) | nucleus also contains **neutrons** | Chadwick's experimental work — about **20 years** after the nucleus became an accepted idea |

You do not need details of the experiments supporting Bohr's model or of Chadwick's experiment.

### The alpha particle scattering experiment

Alpha particles (positively charged, fairly massive) were fired at a **very thin sheet of gold foil** in a vacuum, and a detector was moved around the foil to see where they went.

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| **Most** alpha particles went **straight through** | most of the atom is **empty space** |
| **Some** were **deflected** (changed direction) | the centre of the atom is **charged** — positive, since it repels the positive alpha particles |
| **A very few** bounced **back** (deflected by more than 90°) | the **mass and positive charge are concentrated** in a **tiny** region at the centre — the nucleus |

**Why this replaced the plum pudding model.** In the plum pudding model, the positive charge and mass are **spread out** through the whole atom, so nothing is concentrated enough to deflect a fast alpha particle much — all of them should pass through with, at most, tiny deflections. Large deflections and rebounds could not be explained, so the model had to change.

| | Plum pudding model | Nuclear model |
|---|---|---|
| Positive charge | spread throughout the atom | concentrated in a tiny nucleus at the centre |
| Mass | spread throughout the atom | concentrated in the nucleus |
| Electrons | embedded in the positive "pudding" | around the nucleus (in energy levels, after Bohr) |
| Empty space | none — the atom is a solid ball | the atom is mostly empty space |

::: terms
- **Nucleus**: the tiny, positively charged centre of an atom, made of protons and neutrons, containing almost all its mass.
- **Atomic number**: the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom; it is the same for every atom of an element.
- **Mass number**: the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
- **Isotopes**: atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
- **Positive ion**: an atom that has lost one or more outer electrons, so it has an overall positive charge.
- **Energy level**: a fixed distance from the nucleus at which electrons are found; higher levels are further away.
- **Plum pudding model**: the model of the atom as a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
- **Nuclear model**: the model in which the mass and positive charge are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus with electrons around it.
- **Alpha particle scattering experiment**: firing alpha particles at thin gold foil, whose results showed the atom has a small, massive, charged nucleus.
:::

::: method
**Counting particles**
From ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$: protons $= Z$; neutrons $= A - Z$; electrons $= Z$ in a neutral atom. For an ion, subtract one electron per positive charge. Always show the subtraction for neutrons.

**Command words**
- **Describe** the plum pudding or nuclear model: state where the positive charge, the mass and the electrons are.
- **Explain** why the model changed: name the evidence (**alpha particle scattering**), state the **observation**, then link it to the **conclusion**. "Most went through → mostly empty space" earns a mark; "most went through" alone does not.
- **Compare** (two models, or two isotopes): make paired points using "whereas" — e.g. "in the plum pudding model the positive charge is spread out, whereas in the nuclear model it is concentrated in the nucleus".
- **Suggest**: apply ideas to unfamiliar data, for example why a few alpha particles bounce back from a heavier metal foil.

**Standard form (MS 1b)**
Recognise sizes such as $1 \times 10^{-10}$ m. Dividing by 10 000 is dividing by $10^{4}$: subtract 4 from the power. Compare sizes as a ratio (atom radius ÷ nucleus radius).

**Extended responses (4–6 marks)** on the history of the atom are marked by levels. Top-level answers give a **sequence**: the model → the new evidence → why the old model could not explain it → the new model. Use the scientists' names only if you are sure; the marks are for the **evidence and reasoning**. Remember the general idea: **new experimental evidence can lead to a model being changed or replaced** — examiners reward stating it.

**Energy level questions**: absorb EM radiation → electron moves **up / further out**; electron moves **down / closer** → EM radiation **emitted**. Do not say the electron "leaves" the atom unless the question is about ionisation.
:::

::: example Counting particles
State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in an atom of ${}^{40}_{19}\text{K}$ (potassium-40). (3)

Protons = 19 (1). Neutrons $= 40 - 19 = 21$ (1). Electrons = 19, because the atom is neutral (1).
:::

::: example Isotopes compared
${}^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$ and ${}^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$ are isotopes of chlorine. Compare the structure of their atoms. (3)

Both have 17 protons (1) and 17 electrons (1). Chlorine-35 has 18 neutrons, whereas chlorine-37 has 20 neutrons, so chlorine-37 has the larger mass number / mass (1).
:::

::: example Ions
A magnesium atom, ${}^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}$, loses two outer electrons. Give the number of each particle in the ion and its overall charge. (3)

Protons 12, neutrons 12 (1). Electrons $12 - 2 = 10$ (1). Overall charge $+2$ (a $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ion) (1).
:::

::: example Scattering evidence
Describe the results of the alpha particle scattering experiment and explain what each told scientists about the atom. (4)

Most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil, showing the atom is mostly empty space (1). Some were deflected, showing the centre of the atom is (positively) charged (1). A very few bounced back (1), showing that the mass and charge are concentrated in a very small nucleus (1).
:::

::: example Standard form
The radius of a gold atom is $1.4 \times 10^{-10}$ m and the radius of its nucleus is $7.0 \times 10^{-15}$ m. How many times larger is the radius of the atom? (2)

$\frac{1.4 \times 10^{-10}}{7.0 \times 10^{-15}}$ (1) $= 2.0 \times 10^{4}$, i.e. 20 000 times (1).
:::

What is the approximate radius of an atom? (1)
---
$1 \times 10^{-10}$ m.

::: q
Name the particles in the nucleus and give the charge of each. (2)
---
Protons, positive (+1) (1); neutrons, no charge / neutral (1).
:::

::: q
Why do atoms have no overall charge? (1)
---
The number of electrons (negative) equals the number of protons (positive), so the charges cancel.
:::

::: q
Define mass number. (1)
---
The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
:::

::: q
How many neutrons are in ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$? (1)
---
$238 - 92 = 146$.
:::

::: q
An atom absorbs electromagnetic radiation. What can happen to one of its electrons? (2)
---
It moves to a higher energy level (1), further from the nucleus (1).
:::

::: q
What discovery led to the plum pudding model, and what did that model suggest? (2)
---
The discovery of the electron (1). The atom is a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it (1).
:::

::: q
What did James Chadwick's work provide evidence for, and roughly how long after the nucleus was accepted? (2)
---
The existence of neutrons in the nucleus (1), about 20 years later (1).
:::

::: q
How did Niels Bohr adapt the nuclear model, and why was his idea accepted? (2)
---
He suggested electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances / energy levels (1); his theoretical calculations agreed with experimental observations (1).
:::

::: q
Two atoms have the same mass number but different atomic numbers. Are they isotopes? Explain. (2)
---
No (1): isotopes must have the same atomic number (same element, same number of protons); these have different numbers of protons, so they are different elements (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Isotopes**: same number of **protons**, different number of **neutrons**. Saying "different number of protons" or "different number of electrons" loses the mark.
- Neutrons $= A - Z$. Do not add, and do not use the mass number as the neutron count.
- Alpha scattering: give the **observation and the conclusion** each time. "Most went straight through" needs "so the atom is mostly empty space".
- The nucleus is **positive** and contains **most of the mass** — say both when describing the nuclear model.
- Positive ions form by **losing** electrons, not gaining protons. The nucleus does not change.
- Absorbing radiation moves electrons **further out**; emitting radiation happens when they move **closer in** — students often swap these.
:::

::: test
A calcium atom, ${}^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$, forms a $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ion. Explain how the ion forms and how its structure differs from the atom. (3)
---
The atom loses two outer electrons (1). The ion has 20 protons and 20 neutrons, as in the atom (nucleus unchanged) (1), but only 18 electrons, giving an overall charge of +2 (1).

::: test
The radius of a nucleus is about $1 \times 10^{-14}$ m. An atom's radius is about $1 \times 10^{-10}$ m. Calculate how many times wider the atom is than the nucleus, and explain what this suggests about the structure of the atom. (3)
---
$\frac{1 \times 10^{-10}}{1 \times 10^{-14}}$ (1) $= 1 \times 10^{4}$ = 10 000 times (1). Since the nucleus holds almost all the mass but is tiny, most of the atom is empty space (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how electrons in an atom can cause the atom to give out electromagnetic radiation. (2)
---
An electron moves from a higher energy level to a lower energy level / closer to the nucleus (1); the energy it loses is emitted as electromagnetic radiation (1).
:::

::: test
Scientists predicted that, if the plum pudding model were correct, all alpha particles fired at thin gold foil would pass through with only very small deflections. Explain why they predicted this. (2)
---
In the plum pudding model, the positive charge and mass are spread out evenly through the atom (1), so there is no concentrated region of charge or mass strong enough to repel an alpha particle by a large angle or turn it back (1).
:::

::: test
The experiment was repeated with foils of the same thickness made from different metals. More alpha particles were deflected through large angles by foils of metals with higher atomic numbers. Suggest why. (2)
---
Higher atomic number means more protons, so a larger positive charge on the nucleus (1); the alpha particles (positive) are repelled more strongly, so more are deflected through large angles (1).
:::
