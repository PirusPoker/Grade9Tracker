## What it is

**Separate science only: this whole subsection (AQA 4.5.4, Moments, levers and gears) is in GCSE Physics 8463 but not in Combined Science: Trilogy 8464.** Trilogy students can skip it.

A force, or a system of forces, can make an object **rotate** (turn) about a pivot. Examples:
- pushing a door open — it turns about its hinges;
- using a spanner to undo a nut;
- children on a seesaw;
- a wheelbarrow tipping about its wheel;
- pedalling a bicycle — your foot turns the crank about its axle;
- a crane's load trying to tip it over.

### The moment of a force

The turning effect of a force is called the **moment** of the force.

**Equation (recall — you must learn it):**

moment of a force = force × distance

$$M = Fd$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| moment of a force | $M$ | newton-metre, N m |
| force | $F$ | newton, N |
| distance | $d$ | metre, m |

$d$ is the **perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force**. If the force does not act at right angles to the lever, you must use the perpendicular (shortest) distance between the pivot and the line along which the force acts — not the distance along the lever.

**Mini-example.** A mechanic pushes at right angles on the end of a 0.30 m spanner with a force of 50 N. $M = 50 \times 0.30 = 15$ N m.

**Bigger turning effect** comes from a **bigger force** or a **bigger perpendicular distance**. That is why:
- door handles are placed far from the hinges;
- a longer spanner makes a stiff nut easier to undo (the same moment needs less force);
- pushing near the hinge of a door barely moves it.

Moments are measured in N m, the same unit as work, but a moment is **not** energy — it is never converted into joules.

### Balanced objects: the principle of moments

**If an object is balanced, the total clockwise moment about a pivot equals the total anticlockwise moment about that pivot.**

$$\text{total clockwise moment} = \text{total anticlockwise moment}$$

(The object is then in equilibrium: it does not start to turn.)

**Mini-example — a seesaw.** Ali (weight 400 N) sits 1.5 m from the pivot on the left. Where must Beth (600 N) sit on the right to balance?
- Anticlockwise moment $= 400 \times 1.5 = 600$ N m.
- Clockwise moment $= 600 \times d$.
- Balanced: $600 \times d = 600$, so $d = 1.0$ m from the pivot.

The heavier person sits **closer** to the pivot.

**Mini-example — several forces.** A uniform metre rule is balanced at its centre (50 cm mark). A 2.0 N weight hangs at the 10 cm mark and a 3.0 N weight at the 80 cm mark. Is it balanced? If not, what weight at the 90 cm mark balances it?
- Anticlockwise (left of pivot): $2.0 \times 0.40 = 0.80$ N m.
- Clockwise (right): $3.0 \times 0.30 = 0.90$ N m. Not balanced — it turns clockwise.
- Instead, find $W$ at the 90 cm mark in place of the 3.0 N weight: $W \times 0.40 = 0.80$, so $W = 2.0$ N.

The rule's own weight acts at its centre of mass; when the pivot is at the centre of mass, the rule's weight has **no moment** about the pivot (distance zero).

### Levers

A **lever** is a rigid bar that turns about a pivot. A simple lever **transmits the rotational effect** of a force: you apply an **effort** at one point and it exerts a force on a **load** at another point.

Because the moments about the pivot are equal when it balances:

$$\text{effort} \times \text{effort's distance} = \text{load} \times \text{load's distance}$$

If the effort is **further from the pivot** than the load, a **small effort produces a large force on the load** — the lever is a **force multiplier**. The trade-off: the effort must move through a larger distance than the load.

**Mini-example — a crowbar.** A worker pushes with 200 N at 1.2 m from the pivot. The load (a nail or a heavy stone) is 0.10 m from the pivot on the other side.
- Moment of the effort $= 200 \times 1.2 = 240$ N m.
- Force on the load $= 240 \div 0.10 = 2400$ N — twelve times the effort.

| Lever | Pivot | Effort | Load | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crowbar | edge it rests on | hand at the long end | object being lifted | long effort arm, short load arm |
| Wheelbarrow | the wheel | hands on handles | contents in the middle | load nearer the pivot than the effort |
| Scissors / pliers | the rivet | hands on handles | object being cut near the rivet | cut near the pivot for a bigger cutting force |
| Spanner | the nut | hand at the end | the nut turning | long handle increases the moment |

### Gears

A **gear** is a wheel with **teeth** around its edge. Gears that mesh together **transmit the rotational effect** of a force from one axle to another. The teeth push on each other with **equal and opposite forces**, so the force at the teeth is the same for both gears, but the **moments differ** because the gears have different radii ($M = Fd$).

- Two meshing gears turn in **opposite directions**.
- **Small gear driving a large gear** (low gear on a bike when climbing, a car in first gear, an electric screwdriver): the large gear turns **more slowly** but with a **larger moment** — a force multiplier for turning. Moment is multiplied by the ratio of the radii (or the ratio of the numbers of teeth).
- **Large gear driving a small gear** (high gear on a bike for speed, a whisk): the small gear turns **faster** but with a **smaller moment**.

**Mini-example.** A driving gear with 10 teeth (radius 2.0 cm) meshes with a driven gear of 30 teeth (radius 6.0 cm). The driving gear's axle applies a moment of 0.40 N m.
- Force at the teeth: $F = M \div d = 0.40 \div 0.020 = 20$ N.
- Moment on the driven gear: $M = 20 \times 0.060 = 1.2$ N m — **three times** larger.
- The driven gear turns **once** for every **three** turns of the driver, so it turns three times more slowly, in the opposite direction.

**How to explain transmission** (levers and gears): the effort creates a moment about the pivot or axle; the lever or the meshing teeth pass the force on; because the output acts at a **different distance** from the pivot or axle, the output force or moment is **larger or smaller** than the input — larger when the output distance is smaller (lever) or the output gear is bigger (gears).

::: terms
- **Moment**: the turning effect of a force, equal to force times perpendicular distance from the pivot.
- **Pivot**: the fixed point about which an object turns, also called the fulcrum.
- **Perpendicular distance**: the shortest distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force, at right angles to it.
- **Principle of moments**: for a balanced object, total clockwise moment equals total anticlockwise moment about a pivot.
- **Lever**: a rigid bar that turns about a pivot and transmits the rotational effect of a force.
- **Force multiplier**: a device such as a lever or gear system that gives a larger output force than the input force.
- **Gear**: a toothed wheel that meshes with another to transmit the rotational effect of a force between axles.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** an example of rotation: name the force, the pivot and the direction of turning (clockwise / anticlockwise).
- **Explain** why a longer spanner or handle helps: moment = force × distance; a larger distance gives the same moment with a smaller force.
- **Explain** how a lever or gear transmits the rotational effect (see the model answer pattern in the lesson): input moment → force transmitted (through the bar or the teeth) → output at a different distance → larger/smaller output force or moment.

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit)
1. $M = Fd$ — **recall** (not on the equation sheet).
2. Convert cm to **m**: 30 cm = 0.30 m. The unit of moment is **N m**.
3. Use the **perpendicular** distance from the **pivot**, not from the end of the object.
4. **Balanced problems**: write "clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment", put **every** force's moment on the correct side, then solve for the unknown force or distance.
5. Mass given? Convert to weight with $W = mg$ first.
6. Gears: force at the teeth is the same for both gears; $M = F \times$ radius for each. Turning speed changes in the opposite ratio.

**Extended answers (4–6 marks)**
For "explain how a gear system on a bike helps a cyclist climb a hill": Level 3 links a small front gear driving a large rear gear → same force at the teeth but larger radius → larger moment on the rear wheel → larger driving force from the same pedal force, at the cost of the wheel turning more slowly (more pedal turns per wheel turn). Level 2 has the idea of a larger moment with partial explanation; Level 1 states that gears make it easier.
:::

::: example Calculating a moment
A force of 25 N is applied at right angles to the handle of a door, 80 cm from the hinge. Calculate the moment. (3)

$d = 0.80$ m (1). $M = Fd = 25 \times 0.80$ (1) $= 20$ N m (1).
:::

::: example Balancing a seesaw
A child of weight 300 N sits 2.0 m to the left of a seesaw pivot. Calculate the distance from the pivot at which an adult of weight 750 N must sit to balance it. (3)

Anticlockwise moment $= 300 \times 2.0 = 600$ N m (1). Balanced, so $750 \times d = 600$ (1). $d = 0.80$ m to the right of the pivot (1).
:::

::: example Finding a force with a lever
A gardener uses a 1.5 m long wheelbarrow. The wheel is the pivot. The load of soil, weight 900 N, acts 0.50 m from the wheel. She lifts at the end of the handles, 1.5 m from the wheel. Calculate the upward force she needs. (3)

Clockwise moment of load $= 900 \times 0.50 = 450$ N m (1). Her anticlockwise moment $F \times 1.5 = 450$ (1). $F = 300$ N (1).
:::

::: example Gears
A gear with 12 teeth drives a gear with 36 teeth. Explain how the moment and turning speed of the larger gear compare with those of the smaller gear. (4)

The teeth exert equal forces on each other (1). The larger gear has three times the radius (36 ÷ 12 = 3), so the moment ($M = Fd$) on it is three times larger (1). It turns three times more slowly — once for every three turns of the small gear (1) — and in the opposite direction (1).
:::

::: example Several forces on a beam
A uniform plank is pivoted at its centre. A 50 N weight hangs 0.60 m to the left of the pivot and a 20 N weight hangs 0.40 m to the left. Calculate the single force needed 0.80 m to the right of the pivot to balance it. (3)

Total anticlockwise moment $= 50 \times 0.60 + 20 \times 0.40 = 30 + 8.0 = 38$ N m (1). Clockwise $= F \times 0.80 = 38$ (1). $F = 47.5$ N, about 48 N (1).
:::

::: q
A 2.0 N weight hangs 30 cm from the pivot of a balanced rule. Calculate the distance from the pivot, on the other side, at which a 5.0 N weight must hang. (3)
---
Moment $= 2.0 \times 0.30 = 0.60$ N m (1). $5.0 \times d = 0.60$ (1). $d = 0.12$ m = 12 cm (1).

::: q
Explain why a crowbar lets a person move a heavy rock with a small force. (3)
---
The effort is applied far from the pivot and the rock is close to it (1). Moments balance, so effort × large distance = load force × small distance (1); the force on the rock is therefore much larger than the effort — the crowbar is a force multiplier (1).
:::

::: q
A small gear drives a larger gear. State two ways in which the motion of the larger gear differs from the small gear. (2)
---
Any two: it turns in the opposite direction (1); it turns more slowly (1); it has a larger moment.

::: watch
- **Distance from the wrong point**: $d$ is measured from the **pivot**, perpendicular to the force — not from the end of the beam or between the two forces.
- **cm not converted**: give moments in **N m**, so distances in metres (or say N cm consistently — but N m is expected).
- **Missing a force**: in balance problems list **every** force on each side, including the beam's weight if the pivot is not at its centre of mass.
- **Mass instead of weight**: kg must be turned into N with $W = mg$.
- **Gears "multiply energy"**: they don't. A larger moment comes with slower turning — energy is conserved.
- **Moments in joules**: N m for a moment is never converted to J.
:::

::: test
A child of mass 25 kg sits 1.6 m from the pivot of a seesaw. $g$ = 9.8 N/kg. Calculate the weight of the child on the other side, 1.0 m from the pivot, that would balance the seesaw. (3)
---
Weight of first child $= 25 \times 9.8 = 245$ N (1). Moment $= 245 \times 1.6 = 392$ N m (1). $W = 392 \div 1.0 = 392$ N, about 390 N (1).

::: test
A uniform beam is pivoted at its centre. On the left: 60 N at 0.50 m. On the right: 40 N at 0.30 m and an unknown force $F$ at 0.60 m. The beam is balanced. Calculate $F$. (3)
---
Anticlockwise $= 60 \times 0.50 = 30$ N m (1). Clockwise $= 40 \times 0.30 + 0.60F = 12 + 0.60F$ (1). $0.60F = 18$, $F = 30$ N (1).
:::

::: test
A mechanic cannot undo a nut using a 25 cm spanner, even with her maximum force of 300 N. The nut needs a moment of 90 N m to undo. (a) Show that she cannot undo it. (b) Calculate the minimum length of spanner she needs. (4)
---
(a) Maximum moment $= 300 \times 0.25 = 75$ N m (1), which is less than 90 N m (1). (b) $d = 90 \div 300$ (1) $= 0.30$ m = 30 cm (1).

::: test
A driving gear of radius 3.0 cm meshes with a driven gear of radius 9.0 cm. The motor applies a moment of 0.60 N m to the driving gear. (a) Calculate the force between the teeth. (b) Calculate the moment on the driven gear. (c) The driving gear turns 90 times per minute. State how many times per minute the driven gear turns. (5)
---
(a) $F = M \div d = 0.60 \div 0.030$ (1) $= 20$ N (1). (b) Same force at the teeth: $M = 20 \times 0.090$ (1) $= 1.8$ N m (1). (c) 30 times per minute (one-third as fast) (1).
:::

::: test
A tower crane has a horizontal jib pivoted on the tower. The load hangs on one side of the tower; a large concrete counterweight sits on the short arm on the other side. Explain, using the idea of moments, why the counterweight is needed and how the crane can safely lift loads at different distances from the tower. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a clear explanation using moments about the tower, linking the size and distance of the load to the anticlockwise/clockwise moments, the risk of toppling and how balance is kept.
Level 2 (3–4): uses the idea of moments with some correct links, but incomplete.
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements, e.g. "the counterweight stops it falling over".
Indicative content:
- The load's weight produces a moment about the tower (pivot): moment = weight × perpendicular distance.
- Without a counterweight this moment is unbalanced, so the crane would rotate/topple towards the load.
- The counterweight produces a moment in the opposite direction about the tower.
- For safety, clockwise moment ≈ anticlockwise moment (principle of moments), so the resultant moment is (near) zero.
- A heavier load or a load further out gives a larger moment.
- So a heavy load must be lifted closer to the tower, or the counterweight moved further out / made heavier.
- The counterweight is on a short arm, so it must be very heavy to give a big enough moment.
- Cranes have a maximum load for each distance — the load × distance must not exceed the moment the counterweight can balance.
:::
