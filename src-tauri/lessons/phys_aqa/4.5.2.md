## What it is

When a force causes an object to move through a distance, **work is done** on the object. In other words, a force does work on an object when the force causes a **displacement** of the object. Doing work is a way of **transferring energy**. This topic (AQA 4.5.2, Work done and energy transfer) is also in **Combined Science: Trilogy** (6.5.2), with no Higher-tier-only content.

### The equation

**Equation (recall — you must learn it):**

work done = force × distance moved along the line of action of the force

$$W = Fs$$

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| work done | $W$ | joule, J |
| force | $F$ | newton, N |
| distance moved along the line of action of the force | $s$ | metre, m |

**"Along the line of action of the force"** matters. The distance must be measured in the **same direction as the force**:
- Lifting a box straight up: the force is upwards, so use the **vertical height** it rises.
- Pushing a trolley along a level floor: use the distance moved along the floor.
- Carrying a bag horizontally at a steady height: your upward force holding it does **no work** on the bag, because the bag does not move in the direction of that force.
- Pushing hard against a wall that does not move: **no work** is done on the wall (zero displacement), even though you get tired.

**Rearranging:** $F = W \div s$ and $s = W \div F$.

### Joules and newton-metres

**One joule of work is done when a force of one newton causes a displacement of one metre.**

$$1\ \text{joule} = 1\ \text{newton-metre} \quad (1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N m})$$

So you can convert directly: 350 N m of work = 350 J; 2.4 kJ = 2400 J = 2400 N m. Watch the prefixes: 1 kJ = 1000 J, 1 MJ = 1 000 000 J, 1 km = 1000 m, 1 cm = 0.01 m.

(The **moment** of a force is also measured in N m — see the moments topic — but a moment is a turning effect, not an amount of energy. Only work and energy are converted into joules.)

### Mini-examples

1. A removal worker pushes a piano 4.0 m across a floor with a horizontal force of 250 N. $W = 250 \times 4.0 = 1000$ J (1.0 kJ).
2. A crane lifts a 600 kg steel beam 15 m vertically at a steady speed. The lifting force equals the weight: $W = mg = 600 \times 9.8 = 5880$ N. Work done $= 5880 \times 15 = 88\,200$ J, about 88 kJ.
3. A winch does 3600 J of work dragging a boat 12 m up a slipway. Force $= 3600 \div 12 = 300$ N.
4. A force of 40 N does 6.0 J of work. Distance $= 6.0 \div 40 = 0.15$ m (15 cm).

### Work done is energy transferred

The work done by a force **equals the energy transferred**. Describing the energy transfer means saying **which store** the energy leaves and **which store** it goes to:

| Situation | Work done by | Energy transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Lifting a box onto a shelf | your muscles | chemical store of your body → **gravitational potential energy** store of the box |
| Kicking a ball | your foot | chemical store → **kinetic** store of the ball |
| A car engine accelerating the car | the driving force | chemical store of the fuel → **kinetic** store of the car (plus thermal stores) |
| Brakes slowing a car | friction between pads and discs | **kinetic** store of the car → **thermal** store of the brakes and surroundings |
| Stretching a spring | your hand | chemical store → **elastic potential energy** store of the spring |

Mini-check: lifting the 600 kg beam above transferred 88 kJ to its gravitational potential energy store. Using $E_p = mgh$ from the energy topic: $600 \times 9.8 \times 15 = 88\,200$ J — the same answer, because the work done lifting it **is** the energy transferred to the GPE store.

### Work done against friction

**Work done against the frictional forces acting on an object causes a rise in the temperature of the object.**

When surfaces rub, friction acts against the motion, so work is done against it. The energy transferred ends up in the **thermal energy stores** of the object and its surroundings, so they warm up.

- Rubbing your hands together warms them.
- A car's brake discs get very hot when stopping from high speed (the kinetic energy becomes thermal energy).
- A spacecraft re-entering the atmosphere is heated strongly by work done against air resistance — it needs a heat shield.
- A drill bit and the wood get hot during drilling.
- A sledge sliding to rest on a level surface: **all** of its kinetic energy becomes thermal energy through work done against friction.

Mini-example. A 1200 kg car brakes from 20 m/s to rest. Its kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 20^2 = 240\,000$ J. All of this is transferred by work done against friction in the brakes. If the braking force is 6000 N, the braking distance is $s = W \div F = 240\,000 \div 6000 = 40$ m.

Friction is often **wasteful**: energy dissipated as heat is not useful, which lowers efficiency. Lubricating moving parts (with oil) reduces friction, so less work is done against it and less energy is wasted. Friction is sometimes **useful** — brakes and grip all rely on it.

### Links

- Power: $P = W \div t$ (work done ÷ time) — the rate of doing work, from the energy topic.
- Stopping distances: the braking force does work equal to the car's kinetic energy, so a greater speed needs a much greater braking distance.
- Springs: the work done stretching a spring is stored as elastic potential energy (next topic).

::: terms
- **Work done**: the energy transferred when a force causes an object to move through a distance.
- **Joule**: the work done when a force of one newton causes a displacement of one metre.
- **Newton-metre**: a unit equal to one joule when used for work done or energy transferred.
- **Line of action**: the direction along which a force acts; work uses distance moved along it.
- **Displacement**: the distance moved in a stated direction, a vector quantity.
- **Friction**: a contact force that opposes motion between surfaces in contact.
- **Energy dissipation**: energy spreading out into thermal stores of the surroundings, where it is less useful.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Define / state** what work done is: "energy transferred when a force moves an object through a distance".
- **Describe** an energy transfer: name the store it comes **from**, how it is transferred (work done by a force) and the store it goes **to**. "The kinetic energy store of the car decreases; work is done by friction in the brakes; the thermal energy store of the brakes increases."
- **Explain** why something gets hot: work is done **against friction**, which transfers energy to the **thermal store**, so the temperature rises.
- **Suggest** how to reduce wasted energy: lubrication, streamlining — and say it reduces friction, so less work is done against it.

**Calculations** (equation → substitute → answer with unit)
1. Write $W = Fs$ (recall it).
2. Convert: km → m (× 1000), cm → m (÷ 100), kN → N (× 1000).
3. Use the distance **in the direction of the force** — the vertical height for lifting.
4. If you are given a mass, not a force, for a lift at steady speed: find the weight with $W = mg$ first.
5. Give the unit: J (or N m — they are the same). Rearranging: $F = W \div s$, $s = W \div F$.

**Linked energy questions**
- "Work done = energy transferred": you may need $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ or $E_p = mgh$ with $W = Fs$ in a two-step problem (e.g. braking distance from kinetic energy and braking force).
- Questions with power: $P = W \div t$.

**4–6 mark answers** on, for example, a car braking or a meteor burning up: identify the force (friction / air resistance), say work is done against it, name the store transfers (kinetic → thermal), state the temperature rise and link to any consequence (brake fade, need for heat shields, wasted energy and lubrication).
:::

::: example Pushing a car
A driver pushes a broken-down car 25 m along a level road with a steady force of 420 N. Calculate the work done. (2)

$W = Fs = 420 \times 25$ (1) $= 10\,500$ J (10.5 kJ) (1).
:::

::: example Lifting with a given mass
A student lifts a 12 kg box from the floor onto a shelf 1.5 m high at a steady speed. $g$ = 9.8 N/kg. Calculate the work done and state the energy store that increases. (4)

Force = weight $= 12 \times 9.8 = 117.6$ N (1). $W = 117.6 \times 1.5$ (1) $= 176.4$ J, about 180 J (1). The gravitational potential energy store of the box increases (1).
:::

::: example Finding a distance
A tractor does 2.7 MJ of work pulling a plough with a constant force of 9.0 kN. Calculate how far the plough moves. (3)

$W = 2\,700\,000$ J, $F = 9000$ N (1). $s = W \div F = 2\,700\,000 \div 9000$ (1) $= 300$ m (1).
:::

::: example Braking and temperature
A cyclist brakes hard and stops. Explain why the brake blocks become hot. (3)

Friction acts between the brake blocks and the wheel rim (1). Work is done against friction, transferring energy from the kinetic energy store of the bike and cyclist (1) to the thermal energy store of the brake blocks and rim, so their temperature rises (1).
:::

::: example Converting units
A force does 450 N m of work moving a crate. (a) State the work done in joules. (b) The crate moved 180 cm. Calculate the force. (3)

(a) 450 J, since 1 N m = 1 J (1). (b) $s = 1.80$ m (1); $F = 450 \div 1.80 = 250$ N (1).
:::

::: q
State the equation linking work done, force and distance. (1)
---
Work done = force × distance (moved along the line of action of the force), $W = Fs$.
:::

::: q
Calculate the work done when a force of 60 N moves a sledge 8.0 m. (2)
---
$W = 60 \times 8.0$ (1) $= 480$ J (1).
:::

::: q
State how many joules are equal to 75 N m. (1)
---
75 J, because 1 N m = 1 J.
:::

::: q
A weightlifter holds a bar steady above his head for 3 seconds. Explain why he does no work on the bar during this time. (2)
---
The bar does not move (1), so there is no displacement in the direction of the force and $W = F \times 0 = 0$ (1).
:::

::: q
A lift motor does 54 kJ of work raising a lift 18 m. Calculate the force exerted by the motor. (3)
---
$W = 54\,000$ J (1). $F = 54\,000 \div 18$ (1) $= 3000$ N (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the energy transfer when a person pushes a shopping trolley from rest so that it speeds up. (2)
---
Energy from the chemical store of the person (1) is transferred by work done by the pushing force to the kinetic energy store of the trolley (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a spacecraft's heat shield gets very hot during re-entry into the atmosphere. (3)
---
Air resistance / friction with the air acts on the spacecraft (1). Work is done against this force (1), transferring energy from the kinetic store to the thermal store of the shield, so its temperature rises (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why oil is put on the moving parts of a machine. (2)
---
To reduce friction (1), so less work is done against friction and less energy is wasted heating the parts / the machine is more efficient (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Wrong distance**: for lifting, use the **vertical height**, not the length of a ramp or the horizontal distance walked.
- **Mass used as force**: if given kg, find the weight first with $W = mg$.
- **Unit slips**: kJ, MJ, km and cm must be converted before or after — and the answer is in **J** (= N m).
- **Two W's**: $W$ for weight and $W$ for work done look the same — write the word equation to stay clear.
- **"Friction produces heat"**: say **work is done against friction**, transferring energy to **thermal stores**, so the **temperature rises**.
- **No stores named**: "energy is transferred" alone scores nothing — name where it goes from and to.
:::

::: test
A climber of mass 70 kg climbs a cliff 32 m high. $g$ = 9.8 N/kg. Calculate the minimum work she must do against gravity. (3)
---
Weight $= 70 \times 9.8 = 686$ N (1). $W = 686 \times 32$ (1) $= 21\,952$ J, about 22 kJ (1).
:::

::: test
A box is dragged 6.0 m across a rough floor at a steady speed. The work done is 900 J. (a) Calculate the friction force on the box. (b) Describe what happens to the energy transferred. (4)
---
(a) Steady speed, so pulling force = friction (1); $F = 900 \div 6.0 = 150$ N (1). (b) Work is done against friction (1), so the energy goes to the thermal energy stores of the box and the floor, which get warmer / it is dissipated to the surroundings (1).
:::

::: test
A 900 kg car travelling at 15 m/s brakes to a stop. The average braking force is 4500 N. (a) Calculate the kinetic energy of the car. Use $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. (b) Calculate the braking distance. (5)
---
(a) $E_k = 0.5 \times 900 \times 15^2$ (1) $= 101\,250$ J (1). (b) Work done by brakes = kinetic energy lost (1); $s = W \div F = 101\,250 \div 4500$ (1) $= 22.5$ m, about 23 m (1).
:::

::: test
The car in the previous question brakes from 30 m/s instead, with the same braking force. Show that the braking distance is four times as great, and explain the effect on the temperature of the brakes. (4)
---
Kinetic energy $= 0.5 \times 900 \times 30^2 = 405\,000$ J (1), which is 4 times 101 250 J because $E_k \propto v^2$ (1). Same force, so $s = 405\,000 \div 4500 = 90$ m, four times 22.5 m (1). Four times as much work is done against friction, so four times as much energy is transferred to the thermal store of the brakes, giving a much larger temperature rise (1).
:::

::: test
A meteor enters the Earth's atmosphere at very high speed and glows brightly. Explain, in terms of work done and energy transfers, why this happens and why most small meteors never reach the ground. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a full explanation linking the force, work done, store transfers and the temperature rise to the glowing and burning up.
Level 2 (3–4): identifies air resistance/friction and the energy transfer to thermal stores, with partial links.
Level 1 (1–2): isolated relevant points, e.g. "friction makes it hot".
Indicative content:
- The meteor collides with air particles / there is air resistance (friction with the air).
- This force acts against its motion, so work is done against it.
- Work done = force × distance; large force (high speed) over a long path, so a lot of work.
- Energy is transferred from the meteor's kinetic energy store.
- To thermal energy stores of the meteor and the surrounding air.
- The temperature of the meteor rises enormously, so it glows (and heats the air).
- The surface melts and vaporises, so a small meteor's material is used up before reaching the ground.
- The meteor also slows down because it loses kinetic energy.
:::
