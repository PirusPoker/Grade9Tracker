## What it is

This topic (AQA 4.1.3) is about where our energy comes from, what we use it for, and the price the environment pays. It is mostly **describe, compare and evaluate** — very few equations — but questions often give you **data in tables, bar charts or pie charts** to interpret, so percentage and fraction skills matter. Everything here is also in **Combined Science: Trilogy**. You do **not** need to describe how each resource is used to generate electricity (no power-station diagrams).

### The main energy resources

| Resource | Renewable? | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Coal, oil, natural gas (**fossil fuels**) | No | remains of ancient plants and animals, burned to release energy from their chemical store |
| Nuclear fuel (uranium, plutonium) | No | energy released from the nuclear store of the fuel by nuclear fission |
| Bio-fuel | Yes | fuel made from plants or animal waste (e.g. bioethanol, biodiesel, wood, biogas) |
| Wind | Yes | moving air turns turbines |
| Hydro-electricity | Yes | water stored behind a dam flows downhill through turbines |
| Geothermal | Yes | energy from hot rocks underground (heated by radioactive decay inside the Earth) |
| Tides | Yes | the rise and fall of the sea, caused mainly by the Moon |
| The Sun (solar) | Yes | solar cells generate electricity; solar panels heat water |
| Water waves | Yes | up-and-down motion of waves at the coast |

A **renewable energy resource** is one that is being (or can be) **replenished as it is used**. A **non-renewable** resource will run out, because it is used far faster than it can be replaced — fossil fuels took millions of years to form, and there is a finite amount of uranium.

Note: "renewable" does **not** mean "no environmental impact" — dams flood valleys and wind turbines take up land. And "non-renewable" is about running out, not about pollution.

### The three uses: transport, electricity generation and heating

| Use | Resources mainly used |
|---|---|
| **Transport** | oil (petrol, diesel, jet fuel); bio-fuels blended into petrol and diesel; electricity for electric cars and trains (which may itself come from any resource) |
| **Electricity generation** | gas, nuclear, wind, solar, bio-fuel (e.g. wood pellets), hydro, coal (now almost gone in the UK), small amounts of tidal and wave |
| **Heating** | natural gas (most UK homes), oil, electricity, wood/bio-fuel, solar water heating, geothermal (and heat pumps) |

Fossil fuels dominate transport and heating because they store a lot of energy in a small mass, are easy to transport and can be used on demand. Electric vehicles and heat pumps move these uses on to electricity, which can then come from low-carbon sources.

### Reliability

A **reliable** resource can supply energy whenever it is needed, in the amounts needed.

| More reliable | Why | Less reliable | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fossil fuels | can be burned on demand; power stations can be turned up or down | Wind | no wind → no electricity; too much wind → turbines shut down |
| Nuclear | runs constantly (but slow to start up and shut down) | Solar | none at night; less on cloudy days and in winter |
| Bio-fuel | can be stored and burned on demand | Waves | depend on the weather |
| Hydro-electric | water stored behind the dam can be released within seconds (needs rainfall over the year) | — | — |
| Geothermal | constant supply | — | — |
| Tides | highly **predictable** — tides happen twice a day — but output varies through the day and with the Moon | — | — |

Because demand changes through the day, a country needs some resources that can be switched on quickly (gas, hydro, pumped storage, batteries) to back up less reliable ones.

### Environmental impact

| Resource | Main environmental issues |
|---|---|
| Coal, oil, gas | release **carbon dioxide** (greenhouse gas → global warming / climate change); coal and oil also release **sulfur dioxide** (acid rain) and particulates (air pollution, breathing problems); oil spills; spoil heaps; habitat damage from mining and drilling |
| Nuclear | no carbon dioxide when generating, but produces **radioactive waste** that stays dangerous for thousands of years and must be stored safely; risk of a major accident; high decommissioning costs |
| Bio-fuel | described as **carbon neutral** because plants take in the carbon dioxide as they grow that is released when burned; but land used for fuel crops can't grow food, and forests may be cleared (habitat loss) |
| Wind | visual and noise pollution; land use; some risk to birds; no pollution when running |
| Hydro-electric | flooding of valleys destroys habitats and may displace people; rotting vegetation releases methane |
| Tidal | barrages change estuary habitats (wading birds, fish) and can block shipping |
| Solar | large areas of land for solar farms; mining materials for cells; no pollution when running |
| Geothermal | only possible in a few places (volcanic areas); can release some gases |
| Waves | may affect marine habitats; visual impact on coastlines |

### Patterns and trends in energy use

You must be able to **explain** patterns and trends, often from a graph. Key ideas:

- **World energy use has risen** over the last century because the population has grown and more countries have industrialised; more people own cars, appliances and heating.
- **Fossil fuels still supply most of the world's energy**, especially for transport and heating.
- **In the UK, use of coal for electricity has fallen sharply** since about 2012, and the last coal-fired power station closed in 2024. Reasons: coal produces the most carbon dioxide per unit of energy; government targets to cut emissions; carbon taxes; cheaper gas and renewables.
- **Renewables (especially wind) have grown rapidly** and now generate roughly half of the UK's electricity. Reasons: falling costs, government subsidies, climate change targets, energy security (less reliance on imported fuel).
- **Natural gas** is still widely used for heating and as a flexible back-up for electricity, because it can be switched on quickly.
- **Daily and seasonal patterns**: electricity demand peaks in the early evening and is higher in winter (lighting and heating).

### Science identifies the problems — but cannot always solve them

Science can **identify** environmental issues (e.g. measuring rising carbon dioxide levels and linking them to global warming), but it does not always have the **power to deal with them**, because decisions also depend on:

- **Political** considerations — governments need international agreement; policies change with elections.
- **Social** considerations — people may object to wind farms near their homes, or to higher energy bills.
- **Ethical** considerations — is it fair to leave radioactive waste for future generations, or to grow fuel crops instead of food?
- **Economic** considerations — new power stations are expensive; renewable projects have high building costs; jobs in mining areas may be lost.

### Working with data

Questions often give a bar chart, pie chart or table and ask you to calculate.

**Mini-example.** In one year a country generated its electricity as follows: gas 35%, wind 25%, nuclear 15%, bio-fuel 10%, coal 5%, solar 5%, hydro 2%, other 3%.
- Percentage from renewables $= 25 + 10 + 5 + 2 = 42\%$.
- Fraction from fossil fuels $= 35 + 5 = 40\%$, or $\tfrac{2}{5}$.
- If total generation was 300 TWh, wind generated $0.25 \times 300 = 75$ TWh.
- On a pie chart, wind's sector angle $= 0.25 \times 360° = 90°$.

**Percentage change** $= \dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100$. If solar output rose from 4 TWh to 14 TWh, the increase is $\dfrac{14 - 4}{4} \times 100 = 250\%$.

::: terms
- **Energy resource**: a source of energy that can be used, such as a fuel, the wind or the Sun.
- **Renewable resource**: an energy resource that is being, or can be, replenished as it is used.
- **Non-renewable resource**: an energy resource that will run out because it is used faster than it is replaced.
- **Fossil fuels**: coal, oil and natural gas, formed over millions of years from the remains of living things.
- **Bio-fuel**: a fuel made from plants or animal waste, such as bioethanol, biodiesel or wood.
- **Reliable resource**: one that can supply energy whenever it is needed, in the amounts needed.
- **Carbon neutral**: releasing no more carbon dioxide overall than was absorbed, as claimed for bio-fuels.
- **Radioactive waste**: used nuclear fuel and materials that stay dangerous for a very long time and must be stored safely.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe**: state the resource and its features — "wind is renewable; turbines produce no carbon dioxide when running but produce no electricity when there is no wind".
- **Compare** two resources: give **both** sides for each point, using comparatives — "gas is more reliable than wind because it can be burned on demand, but gas releases carbon dioxide while wind does not". One-sided lists score poorly.
- **Explain** a trend: say what the trend is (with data from the graph), then **why** — "coal use fell from about 40% to under 2% because coal releases the most carbon dioxide and the government set targets to reduce emissions".
- **Evaluate** a proposal (e.g. building a tidal barrage or a nuclear station): advantages, disadvantages, then a **justified conclusion** — "overall… because…". Without a conclusion you cannot reach the top level.
- **Suggest** why a country uses a certain resource: link to geography (hills and rain for hydro, volcanic regions for geothermal, coasts for tides/wind) or economics.

**Data questions**
1. Read values carefully from the axis (check the units — TWh, %, millions of tonnes).
2. For percentages: part ÷ total × 100. For percentage change: (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
3. Quote figures from the data in "describe the trend" answers — "rose from 10% in 2010 to 45% in 2024".

**6-mark answers (levels of response)**
Level 3 (5–6): a balanced comparison or evaluation covering several factors (reliability, environmental impact, cost, renewability, location), with points clearly linked and a reasoned conclusion. Level 2 (3–4): several relevant points, some comparison, conclusion weak or missing. Level 1 (1–2): isolated facts about one resource. Useful factors to cover: renewable or not, reliability, carbon dioxide and other pollution, waste, land use and habitat, set-up and running costs, where it can be built.
:::

::: example Renewable or not
Classify coal, wind, nuclear fuel, bio-fuel and the tides as renewable or non-renewable, and explain what "renewable" means. (3)

Renewable: wind, bio-fuel, tides; non-renewable: coal, nuclear fuel (1 for all five correct, 1 for four). Renewable means the resource is being (or can be) replenished as it is used (1).
:::

::: example Comparing gas and wind
Compare natural gas and wind as resources for generating electricity. (4)

Gas is non-renewable but wind is renewable (1). Gas is reliable because it can be burned on demand, while wind does not generate when the wind is not blowing (1). Burning gas releases carbon dioxide, contributing to global warming, but wind turbines release none when running (1). Wind farms need large areas of land and cause visual/noise pollution; a gas power station takes up less space (1).
:::

::: example Explaining a trend
A graph shows the percentage of UK electricity generated from coal falling from about 40% in 2012 to under 2% in 2020, while wind rose from about 5% to about 25%. Explain these trends. (4)

Coal fell sharply (1) because burning coal releases the most carbon dioxide (and sulfur dioxide), and the government introduced emissions targets and carbon taxes, making coal expensive (1). Wind rose (1) because it is renewable and produces no carbon dioxide when running, and turbines have become cheaper / were subsidised (1).
:::

::: example Data calculation
In one year a country generated 250 TWh of electricity. Nuclear power generated 40 TWh and renewables generated 105 TWh. (a) Calculate the percentage from nuclear. (b) Calculate the percentage from renewables. (c) The next year renewables generated 126 TWh. Calculate the percentage increase. (4)

(a) $40 \div 250 \times 100 = 16\%$ (1). (b) $105 \div 250 \times 100 = 42\%$ (1). (c) $(126 - 105) \div 105 \times 100$ (1) $= 20\%$ (1).
:::

::: example Why science cannot always fix the problem
Scientists have shown that burning fossil fuels increases carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. Suggest why many countries still burn large amounts of fossil fuel. (3)

Any three: fossil fuels are reliable and can meet sudden demand (1); building renewable or nuclear capacity is expensive (economic) (1); people may oppose wind farms or higher bills (social) (1); governments need international agreement and may prioritise jobs or cheap energy (political) (1); existing cars, boilers and power stations are designed for fossil fuels (1).
:::

Name three fossil fuels. (1)
---
Coal, oil and natural gas.
:::

::: q
What is meant by a renewable energy resource? (1)
---
One that is being (or can be) replenished as it is used.
:::

::: q
Name two energy resources used for transport. (2)
---
Any two: oil (petrol/diesel) (1); bio-fuels such as bioethanol or biodiesel (1); electricity (for electric cars/trains).
:::

::: q
Explain why solar power is less reliable than nuclear power. (2)
---
Solar cells produce no electricity at night and less on cloudy days (1), whereas a nuclear power station can produce electricity continuously, day and night (1).
:::

::: q
Give one environmental problem caused by burning coal, and its effect. (2)
---
Releases carbon dioxide (1), a greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming / climate change. Or: releases sulfur dioxide (1), causing acid rain (1).
:::

::: q
Why are bio-fuels described as carbon neutral? (2)
---
The plants absorb carbon dioxide from the air by photosynthesis as they grow (1), which balances the carbon dioxide released when the fuel is burned (1).
:::

::: q
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of nuclear power. (2)
---
Advantage: no carbon dioxide released when generating / reliable / large output from a small mass of fuel (1). Disadvantage: radioactive waste that must be stored for thousands of years / risk of accidents / expensive to decommission (1).
:::

::: q
Tidal power is renewable and predictable. Explain why it still supplies only a tiny fraction of UK electricity. (2)
---
Few suitable estuaries / barrages are very expensive to build (1), and they damage estuary habitats for birds and fish and can block shipping (1).
:::

::: q
A pie chart shows gas as a sector of 126°. What percentage of electricity came from gas? (2)
---
$126 \div 360 \times 100$ (1) $= 35\%$ (1).
:::

::: q
Give one political and one ethical reason why it is difficult to deal with climate change, even though scientists have identified its cause. (2)
---
Political: countries must agree internationally / governments may not want unpopular policies before elections (1). Ethical: e.g. is it fair for richer countries that polluted most to restrict poorer countries' development, or to leave nuclear waste to future generations (1).
:::

::: watch
- **One-sided comparisons**: "wind is renewable" is not a comparison. Say what is true of **both** resources: "wind is renewable but gas is not".
- **Renewable ≠ clean, non-renewable ≠ polluting**: nuclear is non-renewable but emits no carbon dioxide; hydro is renewable but floods habitats.
- **Reliability vs predictability**: tides are predictable but output still varies through the day; wind is neither.
- **Trends without data or reasons**: quote numbers from the graph and give a reason for each change.
- **Evaluate needs a conclusion**: finish with "overall… because…". Not required: explaining how power stations work.
:::

::: test
Hydro-electric power stations can start generating within seconds. Explain why this makes them useful in the National Grid. (2)
---
Demand for electricity changes quickly, e.g. at peak times (1); hydro-electric stations can release stored water on demand to meet sudden increases / back up unreliable resources such as wind (1).

Describe two environmental effects of building a hydro-electric dam. (2)
---
Flooding a valley destroys habitats / farmland (1); rotting plants under the water release methane, a greenhouse gas / people may have to be moved (1).
:::

::: test
In 2015 a country generated 20 TWh from solar cells. In 2025 it generated 48 TWh. Calculate the percentage increase. (2)
---
$(48 - 20) \div 20 \times 100$ (1) $= 140\%$ (1).
:::

::: test
A graph shows that world energy use has increased every decade since 1950 and that fossil fuels still supply most of it. Explain these patterns. (4)
---
World population has increased (1); more countries have industrialised and people use more cars, appliances and heating (1). Fossil fuels still dominate because they are reliable, can be used on demand and are easy to transport and store (1); much existing infrastructure (vehicles, boilers, power stations) is designed for them and renewables are not yet able to replace them fully (1).
:::

::: test
A wind turbine generates about 6.0 GWh of electricity in a year. A country uses 300 TWh of electricity a year. (a) Calculate how many such turbines would be needed to supply the yearly total. (b) Explain why wind turbines alone could still not supply the country's electricity. (4)
---
(a) $300 \text{ TWh} = 300\,000 \text{ GWh}$ (1); $300\,000 \div 6.0 = 50\,000$ turbines (1).
(b) Wind is unreliable — on calm days (or very stormy days) the turbines produce little or nothing (1), so the supply would not match demand at every moment; other reliable sources or energy storage are needed as back-up (1).
:::

::: test
Scientists know that burning fossil fuels causes climate change, yet the problem has not been solved. Explain why science alone cannot deal with this issue. (3)
---
Science can identify the issue and suggest solutions but decisions are made by people/governments (1). Any two from: economic — renewable/nuclear power is expensive to build and jobs in fossil-fuel industries would be lost (1); political — needs international agreement and votes (1); social — people oppose wind farms nearby or higher bills (1); ethical — fairness between rich and poor countries or future generations (1).
:::

::: test
A coastal town currently gets its electricity from an old coal-fired power station, which is about to close. The council must choose between a new gas-fired power station, an offshore wind farm and a tidal barrage across the nearby estuary. Evaluate these options. (6)
---
Indicative content: gas — non-renewable; reliable, can be switched on quickly to meet demand; releases carbon dioxide (less than coal), contributing to global warming; relatively cheap and quick to build; fuel costs and supply depend on imports. Wind — renewable; no carbon dioxide when running; unreliable — no output in calm weather, needs back-up; offshore is windier and avoids visual impact on land, but expensive to build and maintain. Tidal barrage — renewable; no carbon dioxide when running; predictable (tides twice a day) but output varies; very high building cost; destroys estuary habitats (wading birds, fish) and may block shipping; long lifetime. Conclusion: e.g. wind with gas as back-up balances low emissions with reliability, or a tidal barrage gives predictable low-carbon supply if the habitat damage is acceptable — any justified conclusion.
Level 3 (5–6): advantages and disadvantages of all three options, linked to reliability, environmental impact and cost, with a justified conclusion.
Level 2 (3–4): advantages and disadvantages of at least two options, some linking; conclusion missing or not justified.
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements about one or more options.
:::
