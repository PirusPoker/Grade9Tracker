## What it is

This topic (AQA 4.1.2) is about the most important law in physics — **energy is conserved** — and about the catch: every real change **dissipates** some energy into forms we cannot use. It covers energy transfers in a system, reducing unwanted transfers, thermal conductivity, and **efficiency**. Everything is in **Combined Science: Trilogy** too, except **Required practical 2**, which is separate science only.

### Conservation of energy

**Energy can be transferred usefully, stored or dissipated, but it cannot be created or destroyed.**

A **closed system** is one where no energy enters or leaves. Inside a closed system, energy moves between stores, but there is **no net change to the total energy**.

Examples you can use:
- **A pendulum swinging in a sealed box**: energy moves back and forth between the gravitational potential store and the kinetic store of the bob. Friction and air resistance gradually transfer energy to the thermal stores of the bob, the air and the pivot — but the box, air and pendulum together still contain the same total energy.
- **A hot drink in a perfectly insulated flask, with the drink and flask as the system**: energy moves from the thermal store of the drink to the thermal store of the flask, but the total stays the same.
- **A battery-powered torch, with the torch and its surroundings as the system**: the chemical store of the battery decreases by exactly as much as the thermal stores of the surroundings increase (light is absorbed and ends up as thermal energy too).

### Dissipation — "wasted" energy

In **all** system changes some energy is **dissipated**: it spreads out into the surroundings and is stored in **less useful ways**, usually the thermal store of the surroundings. This energy is often called **wasted** energy. It still exists — it has just spread out so thinly (a tiny temperature rise in a large amount of air) that it cannot be used to do anything useful.

| Device or change | Useful transfer | Main dissipation |
|---|---|---|
| Electric motor | to the kinetic store of the load | thermal store of the motor (resistance of coils, friction), sound |
| Filament lamp | light | thermal store of the surroundings (most of the input) |
| Car engine | to the kinetic store of the car | thermal store of exhaust gases, engine and air; sound |
| Bicycle freewheeling | stays in kinetic store | friction in bearings, air resistance → thermal store of the air |

### Reducing unwanted energy transfers

Two classic ways, which you must be able to explain:

- **Lubrication** (oil or grease between moving parts) reduces **friction**, so less work is done against friction and less energy is transferred to thermal stores.
- **Thermal insulation** reduces the rate of energy transfer **by heating** from a hot object to cooler surroundings. Examples: loft insulation, cavity wall insulation, double glazing, carpets, a lid on a pan, a vacuum flask.

Other examples: streamlining a car reduces air resistance; using thicker cables reduces resistance heating in wires.

### Thermal conductivity

**The higher the thermal conductivity of a material, the higher the rate of energy transfer by conduction across it.** Metals (e.g. copper) have high thermal conductivity; air, foam, wool and fibreglass have low thermal conductivity. (You do **not** need to know the definition of thermal conductivity.)

**Rate of cooling of a building.** A building loses energy more slowly if its walls are:
- **thicker** — energy has further to travel by conduction, so the rate of transfer is lower;
- made of material with **lower thermal conductivity** — e.g. brick with a foam-filled cavity rather than a single layer of concrete.

So a thick wall made of a low-conductivity material gives the lowest rate of cooling. Many insulators (foam, fibreglass, wool) work because they **trap air**, which has a very low thermal conductivity.

**Investigating thermal conductivity with rods.** Rods of different metals (same length and diameter) have drawing pins stuck along them with petroleum jelly or wax. One end of each rod is heated equally; the pins fall off sooner and further along on the rod with the higher thermal conductivity (copper before aluminium before iron). Control the rod dimensions and the heating.

### Required practical 2 (Separate science only)

**Separate science only: Required practical 2 — investigate the effectiveness of different materials as thermal insulators, and the factors (such as thickness) that may affect the thermal insulation properties of a material.** Trilogy students do not do this practical, though the ideas above about insulation and thermal conductivity are on their spec.

**Method (comparing materials)**
1. Place a small beaker inside a larger beaker. Pack the gap with the insulating material being tested (e.g. bubble wrap, cotton wool, newspaper, polystyrene beads).
2. Pour a measured volume (e.g. 80 cm³) of hot water from a kettle into the small beaker; put on a cardboard lid with a hole for the thermometer.
3. Record the starting temperature, start a stopwatch, and record the temperature every 3 minutes for 20 minutes.
4. Repeat with each material, and with no insulation as a control.

**Method (effect of thickness)** Wrap the same beaker in 1, 2, 3, 4… layers of the same material (e.g. newspaper) and measure the temperature fall over the same time each time.

**Variables.** Independent: type of material (or number of layers / thickness). Dependent: temperature fall over a fixed time (or rate of cooling). Control: volume and starting temperature of water, size of beaker, the lid, the time, the room temperature, the mass/thickness of material when comparing materials.

**Typical results.** Plot **cooling curves** (temperature against time) — they are steepest at the start, when the water is hottest. The best insulator gives the **smallest temperature drop** (the shallowest curve). More layers → smaller temperature drop, but each extra layer helps less than the one before. Materials that trap a lot of air usually insulate best.

**Errors and improvements.** Make sure each test starts at the same temperature (start the clock when the water reaches, say, 80 °C as it cools) so results are comparable. Read the thermometer at eye level; use a temperature probe and data logger for more precise readings. Stir gently before each reading.

**Risks.** Hot water can scald: do not overfill the beaker, stand up when pouring, and mop spills immediately.

### Efficiency

**Efficiency** tells you what fraction of the input energy is transferred usefully. Both equations must be **recalled**:

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy transfer}}{\text{total input energy transfer}}$$

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful power output}}{\text{total power input}}$$

- Efficiency has **no unit**. It can be given as a **decimal** (0.75) or a **percentage** (75%) — multiply the decimal by 100. AQA questions may ask for either.
- It can never be greater than 1 (100%), because that would mean energy was created. In practice it is always **less than 1** because some energy is always dissipated.
- Rearranged: useful output = efficiency × total input; total input = useful output ÷ efficiency. **Use the decimal form** when you rearrange.
- Wasted energy = total input − useful output.

**Mini-examples**
- A motor takes in 500 J and transfers 400 J to the kinetic store of the load: efficiency $= 400 \div 500 = 0.80$ = 80%.
- An LED lamp is 40% efficient and emits 6.0 W of light: total power input $= 6.0 \div 0.40 = 15$ W.
- A 2.0 kW kettle runs for 120 s, so it takes in $2000 \times 120 = 240\,000$ J. It heats 0.80 kg of water by 65 °C: useful energy $= 0.80 \times 4200 \times 65 = 218\,400$ J. Efficiency $= 218\,400 \div 240\,000 = 0.91$ = 91%.

**Sankey diagrams** are a useful way to show this: the width of each arrow is proportional to the energy (or power); the input arrow splits into a useful arrow and wasted arrows, and the widths must add up to the input.

### Increasing efficiency (Higher tier)

**(Higher tier)** You should be able to describe ways to increase the efficiency of an intended energy transfer. The idea is always the same: **reduce the wasted (dissipated) energy so that more of the input goes to the useful store.**

| Way | How it raises efficiency |
|---|---|
| Lubricate moving parts | less friction, so less energy transferred to thermal stores |
| Streamline vehicles | less air resistance, so less work done against drag |
| Thermally insulate (e.g. a kettle, an oven, a hot-water tank) | lower rate of energy transfer by heating to the surroundings |
| Use lower-resistance (thicker, copper) wires | less heating in the wires when a current flows |
| Replace filament lamps with LEDs | far less energy transferred to the thermal store of the surroundings for the same light |
| Tighten loose parts / balance wheels | less vibration and sound |

::: terms
- **Conservation of energy**: energy can be transferred usefully, stored or dissipated, but cannot be created or destroyed.
- **Closed system**: a system that no energy enters or leaves, so its total energy does not change.
- **Dissipated energy**: energy that spreads out to the surroundings and is stored in less useful ways, often called wasted.
- **Lubrication**: putting oil or grease between moving surfaces to reduce friction and so reduce unwanted heating.
- **Thermal insulation**: a material or layer that reduces the rate of energy transfer by heating.
- **Thermal conductivity**: a property of a material; the higher it is, the higher the rate of energy transfer by conduction.
- **Efficiency**: useful output energy transfer divided by total input energy transfer, as a decimal or percentage.
- **Cooling curve**: a graph of temperature against time for an object cooling, steepest when the object is hottest.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **Describe** (energy transfers): say where the energy goes, naming stores — "from the kinetic store of the wheel to the thermal store of the brake pads and surroundings".
- **Explain** (reducing energy transfer): link the action → the physics → the result. "Loft insulation has a low thermal conductivity / traps air (1), so the rate of energy transfer by conduction through the ceiling is reduced (1), so the house cools more slowly (1)."
- **Compare** (materials or walls): use comparatives for both — "thicker and lower thermal conductivity, so a lower rate of cooling".
- **Evaluate** (e.g. which insulation to buy): give points for and against, using the data, and finish with a justified conclusion.

**Efficiency calculations**
1. Decide what is **useful** for that device (heat is useful from a heater, wasted from a lamp).
2. Write the equation: efficiency = useful ÷ total. Check both values are in the same unit (both J, or both W).
3. Substitute, calculate, and give the answer as a decimal **or** percentage — whichever the question asks.
4. If the answer is above 1 (100%), you have divided the wrong way round.
5. When efficiency is given as a percentage and you need to rearrange, change it to a decimal first (35% → 0.35).

**Required practical 2 questions (separate science)**
- Identify independent, dependent and control variables; explain why the starting temperature and volume of water must be the same.
- Interpret cooling curves: shallower curve = better insulator; compare temperature drops over the same time.
- Suggest improvements: lid, same start temperature, data logger, repeats to check repeatability.

**6-mark answers**
For "explain how to reduce energy losses from a house" or "evaluate insulation" answers, AQA's levels reward **linked** reasoning: method → which transfer it reduces → why (thermal conductivity, trapped air, thickness). Level 3 (5–6) needs several methods each clearly linked to the physics; Level 2 (3–4) has methods with some explanation; Level 1 (1–2) just lists methods.
:::

::: example Conservation in a closed system
A pendulum swings inside a sealed glass box. After an hour it has stopped. Explain what has happened to its energy and whether the total energy in the box has changed. (3)

As it swings, energy moves between the gravitational potential and kinetic stores of the bob (1). Air resistance and friction at the pivot do work, dissipating energy to the thermal stores of the air, pivot and bob (1). The box is a closed system, so the total energy is unchanged — it is just stored in a less useful way (1).
:::

::: example Efficiency of a motor from powers
An electric motor has an input power of 200 W. It lifts a 40 N load through 3.0 m in 1.5 s. Calculate its efficiency as a percentage. (4)

Useful work $= 40 \times 3.0 = 120$ J (1). Useful power $= 120 \div 1.5 = 80$ W (1). Efficiency $= 80 \div 200$ (1) $= 0.40 = 40\%$ (1).
:::

::: example Using efficiency to find the input
A power station is 35% efficient and supplies 420 MJ of electrical energy each second. Calculate the energy it takes in from its fuel each second, and the energy wasted. (3)

Total input $= \text{useful} \div \text{efficiency} = 420 \div 0.35$ (1) $= 1200$ MJ (1). Wasted $= 1200 - 420 = 780$ MJ, mostly to the thermal store of the surroundings (1).
:::

::: example Comparing walls
Wall A is 10 cm of brick. Wall B is two 10 cm brick layers with a 5 cm gap filled with foam. Explain which wall gives the lower rate of cooling of the house. (3)

Wall B (1). It is thicker, so energy has further to be conducted (1), and the foam has a very low thermal conductivity (it traps air), so the rate of energy transfer by conduction through wall B is lower (1).
:::

::: example Increasing efficiency (Higher tier)
Describe and explain two ways a car manufacturer could increase the efficiency of a car. (4)

Lubricate the engine's moving parts (1) so there is less friction and less energy is dissipated to thermal stores (1). Make the body more streamlined (1) so there is less air resistance and less work is done against it, leaving more energy in the useful kinetic store (1).
:::

::: q
State the principle of conservation of energy. (2)
---
Energy can be transferred usefully, stored or dissipated (1), but cannot be created or destroyed (1).

::: q
A lamp transfers 60 J of electrical energy, of which 3.0 J is emitted as light. Calculate its efficiency as a percentage. (2)
---
$3.0 \div 60 = 0.05$ (1) $= 5\%$ (1).

::: q
A generator is 90% efficient and produces 45 kW of electrical power. Calculate its input power. (2)
---
$45 \div 0.90$ (1) $= 50$ kW (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why no real machine can be 100% efficient. (2)
---
In every energy transfer some energy is dissipated (1), e.g. to the thermal store of the surroundings by friction or electrical resistance, so the useful output is always less than the input (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how oiling a bicycle chain reduces unwanted energy transfers. (2)
---
The oil reduces friction between the moving parts (1), so less work is done against friction and less energy is dissipated to thermal stores (1).
:::

::: q
A copper rod and a wooden rod of the same size are held in a flame. Which one transfers energy by conduction faster, and why? (2)
---
Copper (1), because it has a much higher thermal conductivity (1).
:::

::: q
A heater takes in 2000 J and transfers 1900 J to the room by heating and 100 J as sound. Calculate the efficiency. (2)
---
Heating the room is the useful output (1); $1900 \div 2000 = 0.95$ (95%) (1).
:::

::: q
A student calculates the efficiency of a motor as 1.3. Explain why this must be wrong. (1)
---
It would mean more energy out than in, which breaks conservation of energy; efficiency can never be more than 1 (100%).
:::

::: q
Separate science only: in Required practical 2, why is a lid placed on the beaker of hot water? (1)
---
To reduce energy transfer from the water by evaporation and convection out of the top, so the result depends on the insulating material being tested.
:::

::: q
How does the thickness of a house's walls affect its rate of cooling? (2)
---
Thicker walls give a lower rate of cooling (1) because energy has to be conducted through more material (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Efficiency the wrong way up**: it is useful ÷ total. An answer above 1 (or 100%) is impossible — check before moving on.
- **Percentage vs decimal**: to rearrange, convert 35% to 0.35. Dividing by 35 gives a nonsense answer.
- **"Energy is lost"**: say energy is **dissipated** to the thermal store of the surroundings — it is not destroyed.
- **Insulation explanations**: say what it reduces (the **rate** of energy transfer by conduction/convection) and why (low thermal conductivity, trapped air). "It keeps the heat in" alone scores nothing.
- **Higher tier "increase efficiency"**: link each method to the wasted energy it reduces; "make it better" is not a method.
:::

::: test
A device has an efficiency of 0.25. Which statement is correct? A: it wastes 25% of its input. B: 25% of the input energy is transferred usefully. C: it transfers 25 J usefully. D: it destroys 75% of the energy. (1)
---
B — a quarter of the input energy goes to the useful store; the rest is dissipated, not destroyed.

A television takes in 150 J each second. It transfers 45 J as light, 30 J as sound and the rest to the thermal store of the surroundings. Calculate its efficiency. (2)

Energy that is dissipated is often described as "wasted". Explain what this means. (2)

The energy spreads out into the surroundings (usually their thermal store) (1) and is stored in a less useful way, so it cannot be used to do anything useful (1).

An electric motor is 60% efficient and has a useful output power of 300 W. Calculate its input power and the power wasted. (3)
---
Input $= 300 \div 0.60$ (1) $= 500$ W (1). Wasted $= 500 - 300 = 200$ W (1).

::: test
A 2.2 kW kettle is switched on for 150 s. It heats 1.0 kg of water from 20 °C to 95 °C. Specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J/kg °C. Calculate the efficiency of the kettle. (4)
---
Input $= Pt = 2200 \times 150 = 330\,000$ J (1). Useful $= mc\Delta\theta = 1.0 \times 4200 \times 75 = 315\,000$ J (1). Efficiency $= 315\,000 \div 330\,000$ (1) $= 0.95$ (95%) (1).
:::

::: test
Separate science only. In Required practical 2 a student tests three insulators. Each beaker starts with 100 cm³ of water at 80 °C; the temperature after 15 minutes is: no insulation 55 °C, newspaper 64 °C, bubble wrap 69 °C, cotton wool 67 °C. (a) Which material is the best insulator? Use the data. (b) Why must the volume and starting temperature be the same each time? (c) Suggest one improvement. (4)
---
(a) Bubble wrap (1): smallest temperature drop, 11 °C compared with 13 °C for cotton wool and 16 °C for newspaper (1).
(b) They are control variables — the rate of cooling depends on them, so only the insulating material should change for a fair test (1).
(c) Any one: use a lid; use the same thickness/mass of each material; use a temperature probe and data logger; repeat and calculate a mean (1).
:::

::: test
(Higher tier) Describe and explain two ways the efficiency of an electric kettle could be increased. (4)
---
Insulate the walls of the kettle / use a double-walled or plastic body (1) so less energy is transferred by heating to the surroundings and more stays in the thermal store of the water (1). Fit a tight lid / switch off automatically at boiling (1) so less energy is lost by evaporation and steam, and none is transferred once the water has boiled (1).
:::

::: test
Electricity reaches a home through a power station (40% efficient), the National Grid (90% efficient) and an LED lamp (50% efficient). Calculate the overall efficiency, and the power that must be taken in from fuel at the power station for the lamp to emit 9.0 W of light. (4)
---
Overall efficiency $= 0.40 \times 0.90 \times 0.50$ (1) $= 0.18$ (18%) (1). Input $= 9.0 \div 0.18$ (1) $= 50$ W (1).
:::

::: test
A family wants to reduce the rate at which their house cools in winter. Explain how the thickness and thermal conductivity of the walls, and other methods, affect the rate of energy transfer from the house. (6)
---
Indicative content: energy is transferred from the warm house to the colder surroundings by heating; the rate depends on the temperature difference; thicker walls reduce the rate of conduction because energy must pass through more material; walls of lower thermal conductivity reduce the rate of conduction; cavity wall insulation (foam) fills the gap with a low-conductivity material that traps air and stops convection currents in the cavity; loft insulation (fibreglass) traps air, which has very low thermal conductivity, reducing conduction through the ceiling; double glazing — a gap of air or gas between panes reduces conduction; draught excluders reduce convection; carpets/curtains also trap air; lower rate of cooling means less fuel needed to keep the house warm.
Level 3 (5–6): explains the effect of both thickness and thermal conductivity on the rate of cooling, and links at least two further methods to the transfer they reduce.
Level 2 (3–4): describes the effect of thickness or conductivity and gives some methods with partial explanation.
Level 1 (1–2): lists methods of insulation with little or no explanation.
