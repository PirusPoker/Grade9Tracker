## What it is

Energy flows *through* an ecosystem and is lost, but **elements cycle** — carbon and nitrogen are used again and again. The two cycles are examined as sequences of named processes.

**The carbon cycle** — carbon moves between the air (as CO₂), living things (as organic compounds) and the ground:

1. **Photosynthesis** removes CO₂ from the air: plants build it into glucose and then into their carbohydrates, fats and proteins.
2. **Feeding** passes carbon compounds from plants to animals along food chains.
3. **Respiration** by plants, animals and decomposers returns CO₂ to the air.
4. **Death and waste** pass carbon compounds to **decomposers** (bacteria and fungi), which respire and release CO₂ as they break the material down.
5. Where decomposition is prevented (no oxygen, swamps, sea beds) dead material forms **fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas) over millions of years; **combustion** of fossil fuels and wood releases CO₂ back to the air — the source of the recent rise in atmospheric CO₂.

**The nitrogen cycle** — nitrogen is needed for **amino acids, proteins and DNA**. Air is 78% nitrogen gas (N₂), but plants **cannot use nitrogen gas** — it is too unreactive. Plants take up **nitrates** from the soil; animals get nitrogen by eating plants. Four groups of **bacteria** keep nitrogen moving — learn them by the job they do:

| Bacteria | Job | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** | convert **nitrogen gas** in the air into **nitrates** (or ammonium compounds) the plant can use | free in the soil, and in **root nodules** of legumes (peas, beans, clover) — a mutualistic partnership |
| **Decomposers** (bacteria and fungi) | break down proteins and urea in **dead organisms and waste** into **ammonia / ammonium compounds** | soil |
| **Nitrifying bacteria** | convert **ammonium compounds → nitrites → nitrates** (nitrification) — need oxygen | aerated soil |
| **Denitrifying bacteria** | convert **nitrates back into nitrogen gas**, returning it to the air (denitrification) — wasteful for farmers | waterlogged, oxygen-poor soil |

Also: **lightning** fixes small amounts of nitrogen into oxides that dissolve in rain; the **Haber process** makes ammonia for fertilisers industrially.

The sequence: N₂ in air → (fixation) → nitrates in soil → **absorbed by roots by active transport** → plant proteins → eaten → animal proteins → death, faeces, urea → (decomposers) → ammonium → (nitrifying bacteria) → nitrites → nitrates → back to plants; or (denitrifying bacteria) → N₂ in air.

**Why the cycles matter**: they return the elements locked in dead material to a form producers can use, so ecosystems do not run out of carbon and nitrogen. **Decomposers** are essential to both — without them, nutrients would stay locked in dead bodies. Decomposition is fastest when it is **warm, moist and oxygen-rich** (the decomposers respire).

::: method
1. **Carbon cycle: four processes** — photosynthesis (in), respiration (out), decomposition (out, via decomposer respiration), combustion (out). Say which direction each moves carbon.
2. **Nitrogen cycle: four bacteria by job** — fixing (N₂ → nitrates), decomposers (protein → ammonium), nitrifying (ammonium → nitrites → nitrates), denitrifying (nitrates → N₂). Learn the substrate and product of each.
3. **Plants use nitrates, not nitrogen gas**, absorbed by active transport, to make amino acids and proteins.
4. **Decomposers in both cycles**: they release CO₂ by respiring and ammonium from proteins.
5. **Farming links**: waterlogged soil → denitrification → less nitrate; legumes → root nodules → more nitrate; fertilisers replace nitrate removed in crops.
:::

::: example Carbon cycle
Describe how carbon in the carbon dioxide of the air becomes part of an animal and is eventually returned to the air. (4)

A plant takes in CO₂ and uses it in photosynthesis to make glucose, which becomes part of its carbohydrates, proteins and fats (1). The animal eats the plant, digests it and builds the carbon into its own molecules (1). The animal respires, releasing some carbon as CO₂ back into the air (1). When it dies, decomposers break down its body and respire, releasing the remaining carbon as CO₂ (1).
:::

::: example Nitrogen fixation
Explain why plants cannot use the nitrogen in the air, and describe how it becomes available to them. (3)

Nitrogen gas is very unreactive and plants have no way of taking it up or combining it (1). Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the soil and in root nodules of legumes convert nitrogen gas into ammonium compounds and nitrates (1); the plant absorbs the nitrates by active transport through its root hairs and uses them to make amino acids and proteins (1).
:::

::: example Nitrogen from waste
Describe how the nitrogen in the protein of a dead animal becomes available to a plant. (3)

Decomposers break down the proteins into ammonium compounds (1). Nitrifying bacteria convert the ammonium compounds into nitrites and then into nitrates (1). The plant absorbs the nitrates from the soil through its roots (1).
:::

::: example Farming
Explain why a farmer might drain waterlogged fields and grow clover between crops. (3)

Waterlogged soil lacks oxygen, so denitrifying bacteria thrive and convert nitrates into nitrogen gas, which is lost to the air; draining keeps the soil aerated so nitrifying bacteria produce nitrates instead (2). Clover is a legume with nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules, which add nitrates to the soil when the plants are ploughed in (1).
:::

::: q
Name the process that removes carbon dioxide from the air, and two processes that return it. (3)
---
Photosynthesis removes it; respiration and combustion return it (also decomposition).
:::

::: q
What role do decomposers play in the carbon cycle? (2)
---
They break down dead organisms and waste (1) and respire, releasing the carbon as carbon dioxide (1).
:::

::: q
How do fossil fuels form, and how do they release carbon? (2)
---
Dead organisms buried where decomposition cannot occur are compressed over millions of years (1); burning them (combustion) releases CO₂ (1).
:::

::: q
Why do organisms need nitrogen? (1)
---
To make amino acids and proteins (and DNA).
:::

::: q
Name the bacteria that convert nitrates into nitrogen gas, and state where they are found. (2)
---
Denitrifying bacteria (1); in waterlogged, oxygen-poor soil (1).
:::

::: q
What do nitrifying bacteria do? (2)
---
Convert ammonium compounds into nitrites (1) and nitrites into nitrates (1).
:::

::: q
Where are nitrogen-fixing bacteria found? (2)
---
Free-living in the soil (1) and in root nodules of leguminous plants such as peas, beans and clover (1).
:::

::: q
In what form do plants absorb nitrogen, and by what process? (2)
---
As nitrate ions (1) by active transport in root hair cells (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why decomposition is slower in cold, dry conditions. (2)
---
Decomposers are living organisms whose enzymes work slowly in the cold (1) and which need water; so their respiration and growth slow, and dead matter breaks down slowly (1).
:::

::: watch
- Nitrogen cycle: **four named groups of bacteria, each with substrate → product.** "Bacteria in the soil" is not enough.
- Plants absorb **nitrates**, not nitrogen gas, by **active transport**.
- Decomposers release **CO₂ by respiring** (carbon cycle) and **ammonium from protein** (nitrogen cycle).
- Photosynthesis is the **only** process that removes CO₂ in the cycle; respiration, decomposition and combustion add it.
- Denitrifying bacteria **waste** nitrogen (nitrates → N₂) in **waterlogged** soil.
:::
