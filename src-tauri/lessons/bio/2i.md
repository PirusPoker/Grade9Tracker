## What it is

Plants have two transport tissues, found together in **vascular bundles** (the veins of a leaf, the ring of bundles in a stem, the central core of a root):

| | **Xylem** | **Phloem** |
|---|---|---|
| Carries | **water and mineral ions** | **sucrose and amino acids** (dissolved food) |
| Direction | **upwards only** — roots → stem → leaves | **both directions** — from leaves (where sugar is made) to wherever it is needed or stored (growing tips, roots, fruits, seeds) — this is **translocation** |
| Structure | dead, hollow tubes with no end walls; walls strengthened with **lignin** | living cells with perforated end walls (sieve plates) and companion cells |
| Also | supports the plant | — |

**Water uptake by root hair cells**: root hairs are long extensions of epidermal cells that reach between soil particles. They give the root a very **large surface area**. Water enters by **osmosis** — the soil water is more dilute than the cell contents, so water moves through the partially permeable membrane into the cell — and passes from cell to cell across the root into the xylem. **Mineral ions** are usually at a lower concentration in the soil than in the cell, so they are taken up by **active transport**, using energy from respiration (root hair cells have many mitochondria).

**Transpiration** is the **evaporation of water from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces of the leaf, followed by the diffusion of water vapour out through the stomata**. It pulls a continuous column of water up the xylem from the roots — the **transpiration stream** — which supplies water for photosynthesis, carries mineral ions, keeps cells turgid (support) and cools the leaf.

**Factors affecting the rate of transpiration:**

- **Light intensity** — brighter light → stomata open wider (for photosynthesis) → faster.
- **Temperature** — warmer → water evaporates faster and vapour diffuses faster → faster.
- **Wind (air movement)** — moving air removes humid air from around the stomata → steeper concentration gradient → faster.
- **Humidity** — more water vapour in the air → smaller gradient → slower.
- (Water supply — a short-of-water plant closes its stomata → slower.)

**Required practical — the potometer**: a leafy shoot is cut under water (to avoid air bubbles), fitted into a water-filled capillary tube with an air bubble at the end; as the shoot takes up water the bubble moves. Measure the distance the bubble moves in a set time → rate of water uptake (≈ transpiration rate). Change one factor at a time — a fan for wind, a lamp for light, a plastic bag for humidity — keeping the others constant. A tap and reservoir reset the bubble. Sources of error: air leaks; not all water taken up is transpired (some is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells).

Alternative: weigh a potted plant (pot sealed in a bag so only the leaves lose water) over several days in different conditions — mass lost = water transpired.

::: terms
- **Transport system**: a system that carries substances around a multicellular organism, because diffusion alone is too slow over long distances.
- **Phloem**: vessels that transport sucrose and amino acids from the leaves to the rest of the plant (translocation).
- **Xylem**: vessels that transport water and mineral ions from the roots to the rest of the plant.
- **Translocation**: the movement of sucrose and amino acids through the phloem.
- **Root hair cell**: a cell with a long extension that gives a large surface area for absorbing water by osmosis and minerals by active transport.
- **Transpiration**: the evaporation of water from the surface of the leaves, and its loss through the stomata by diffusion.
- **Transpiration stream**: the flow of water from the roots, up the xylem and out of the leaves.
- **Potometer**: apparatus that measures the rate of water uptake by a shoot, as an estimate of transpiration.
:::

::: method
1. **Xylem: water and ions, up. Phloem: sugars, both ways.** Write the substance *and* the direction every time.
2. **Root hair uptake**: two mechanisms — osmosis for water (say the gradient and the membrane), active transport for ions (say the gradient direction and the energy).
3. **Transpiration definition**: evaporation from cell surfaces → diffusion of vapour through stomata. Two steps.
4. **Factors**: name the factor → its effect on evaporation or the concentration gradient or stomata → the change in rate.
5. **Potometer**: what is measured (distance the bubble moves per minute), what is changed, what is controlled, and that it measures uptake not strictly transpiration.
6. **Wilting**: water loss exceeds uptake → cells lose turgor → plant droops; stomata close to reduce further loss.
:::

::: example Define
Define transpiration. (2)

The evaporation of water from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces of the leaf (1), followed by the diffusion of water vapour out through the stomata (1).
:::

::: example Root hairs
Explain how root hair cells are adapted for absorbing water and mineral ions. (4)

The long, thin extension gives a large surface area in contact with soil water, so more water and ions can be absorbed (1). Water enters by osmosis, because the soil water is more dilute than the cell cytoplasm, moving through the partially permeable membrane (1). Mineral ions are usually more concentrated inside the cell than in the soil, so they are absorbed by active transport against the gradient (1); the cells contain many mitochondria to supply the energy for this (1).
:::

::: example Factors
Explain why transpiration is faster on a warm, windy day than on a cool, still one. (4)

Higher temperature gives water molecules more energy, so water evaporates from the cell surfaces faster and vapour diffuses faster (2). Wind blows away the humid air near the stomata, replacing it with drier air (1), which steepens the concentration gradient between the leaf's air spaces and the outside, so vapour diffuses out faster (1).
:::

::: example Potometer
Describe how a potometer could be used to compare the rate of water uptake in still and moving air. (4)

Cut a leafy shoot under water and fit it into the water-filled potometer without air bubbles, introducing a single bubble into the capillary tube (1). Measure the distance the bubble moves in a fixed time, e.g. 5 minutes, in still air, resetting the bubble with the reservoir tap (1). Repeat with a fan blowing over the leaves, keeping temperature, light and humidity the same (1). Calculate the rate as distance per minute for each condition; the faster bubble movement in moving air shows faster uptake, and hence transpiration (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why large organisms need transport
Explain why a single-celled Amoeba does not need a transport system but a human does. (3)

Amoeba has a large surface area to volume ratio, and substances only need to diffuse a very short distance (1), so diffusion across its membrane supplies all it needs. A human has a small surface area to volume ratio and many cells are far from the surface (1), so diffusion would be far too slow; a circulatory system is needed to carry substances quickly to every cell (1).
:::

::: example Compare — xylem and phloem
Compare the functions of xylem and phloem. (3)

Xylem carries water and mineral ions (1) from the roots up to the leaves, in one direction only (1).

Phloem carries sucrose and amino acids made in the leaves to the rest of the plant, both up and down — translocation (1).
:::

::: q
What does xylem transport, and in which direction? (2)
---
Water and mineral ions (1), upwards from the roots to the leaves (1).
:::

::: q
What does phloem transport, and where from and to? (2)
---
Sucrose and amino acids (1), from the leaves to all parts of the plant — growing regions and storage organs — in both directions (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why xylem cells are dead and hollow. (2)
---
Hollow tubes with no end walls or contents let water flow freely (1); lignified walls make them strong and waterproof, supporting the plant (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how water moves from the soil into a root hair cell. (2)
---
By osmosis (1): the soil water is more dilute than the cell contents, so water moves through the partially permeable membrane into the cell (1).
:::

::: q
Give two functions of the transpiration stream. (2)
---
Supplies water for photosynthesis and keeps cells turgid; transports mineral ions from the roots (or cools the leaf).
:::

::: q
Explain the effect of high humidity on the rate of transpiration. (2)
---
Humid air already contains much water vapour, so the concentration gradient between the leaf and the air is small (1); vapour diffuses out more slowly, so transpiration slows (1).
:::

::: q
Why must the shoot be cut under water when setting up a potometer? (1)
---
To prevent air entering the xylem and blocking the water column.
:::

::: q
A bubble moved 24 mm in 6 minutes. Calculate the rate of water uptake. (1)
---
$24 \div 6 = 4$ mm per minute.
:::

::: q
Explain why a plant wilts on a hot day. (2)
---
Transpiration is faster than water uptake, so cells lose water (1) and become flaccid, no longer supporting the leaves and stem (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why measuring bubble movement in a potometer only approximates the transpiration rate. (1)
---
Some of the water taken up is used in photosynthesis or stored in cells rather than transpired.
:::

::: watch
- **Xylem up only; phloem both ways.** Reversing them undoes the whole answer.
- Root hairs: **osmosis** for water, **active transport** for ions — two separate mechanisms with their reasons.
- Transpiration = **evaporation** then **diffusion through stomata** — both halves.
- Factors work through **evaporation rate, the concentration gradient or stomatal opening** — say which.
- The potometer measures **water uptake**, not exactly transpiration; and change **one factor at a time**.
:::

::: test
Describe the pathway of water from the soil to the atmosphere through a plant. (4)
---
Absorbed by root hair cells by osmosis (1); moves across the root cortex into the xylem (1); pulled up the xylem in the stem to the leaves (1); evaporates from mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata — transpiration (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how transpiration pulls water up the xylem. (2)
---
Evaporation from the leaves reduces the water pressure at the top of the xylem (1); water molecules cohere, so a continuous column is drawn upward (1).
:::

::: test
State the effect on transpiration rate of an increase in (a) temperature, (b) wind speed, (c) light intensity, and explain one of them. (4)
---
All three increase the rate (2; one mark for two). Explanation e.g. wind removes humid air from around the stomata, steepening the diffusion gradient; or light opens the stomata; or heat speeds evaporation (2).
:::

::: test
A potometer bubble moves 30 mm in 5 minutes in a tube of cross-sectional area 2 mm². Calculate the rate of water uptake in mm³ per minute. (2)
---
Volume = 30 × 2 = 60 mm³ (1); rate = 12 mm³/min (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a potometer measures water uptake rather than transpiration directly. (1)
---
Some water taken up is used for photosynthesis or to keep cells turgid, so uptake is slightly more than the water lost.
:::

::: test
Describe the structure of phloem and state what it transports. (2)
---
Living sieve tubes with perforated end walls, supported by companion cells (1); transports sucrose and amino acids from leaves to wherever they are needed — translocation (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why translocation, unlike water transport, can occur in both directions. (1)
---
Sugars are moved from sources (leaves, or stores) to sinks (growing tips, roots, fruits), which can be above or below the source.
:::

Transpiration exceeds water uptake (1); cells lose water and become flaccid, so the leaves and stem droop (1).

::: test
A potometer bubble moves 45 mm in 3 minutes. The capillary tube has a cross-sectional area of 0.8 mm². Calculate the rate of water uptake in mm³ per minute. (3)
---
Volume = 45 × 0.8 = 36 mm³ (1); rate = 36 ÷ 3 (1) = 12 mm³ per minute (1).

::: test
Explain how humidity, temperature and light intensity affect the rate of transpiration. (6)
---
Any six: high humidity reduces the concentration gradient of water vapour between the leaf and the air, so transpiration slows; low humidity increases it; higher temperature gives water molecules more kinetic energy, so evaporation and diffusion are faster; warmer air also holds more water vapour; higher light intensity causes more stomata to open, so more water vapour can escape; in darkness stomata close and transpiration is very slow.
Marking: 1 mark for each correct point, up to 6; for full marks the answer must be in a logical order.
:::
