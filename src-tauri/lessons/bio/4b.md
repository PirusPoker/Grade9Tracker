## What it is

Energy enters almost every ecosystem as **sunlight**, captured by **producers** through photosynthesis, and passes along **food chains** as organisms eat each other.

**Trophic levels** — the feeding positions in a chain:

1. **Producers** — green plants and algae; make their own food by photosynthesis.
2. **Primary consumers** — herbivores that eat producers.
3. **Secondary consumers** — carnivores that eat primary consumers.
4. **Tertiary consumers** — carnivores that eat secondary consumers; the last is the **top carnivore**.

**Decomposers** (bacteria and fungi) feed on dead organisms and waste at every level, returning nutrients to the soil.

A **food chain** shows one line of feeding: grass → rabbit → fox. The arrows point in the direction **energy flows** — from the eaten to the eater. A **food web** links many chains, because most animals eat, and are eaten by, several species.

**Pyramids** — bars drawn to scale, producers at the bottom:

- **Pyramid of numbers** — the number of organisms at each level. Usually pyramid-shaped, but not always: one oak tree feeds thousands of caterpillars (a narrow base), and many fleas live on one fox (a wide top).
- **Pyramid of biomass** — the total dry mass of organisms at each level. Almost always a true pyramid, because each level can only support a smaller mass above it.
- **Pyramid of energy** — the energy transferred to each level in a given time (kJ m⁻² year⁻¹). Always a true pyramid.

**Why only about 10% of the energy passes to the next level** — the rest is "lost" from the food chain at each stage:

- **Not all of the organism is eaten** — roots, bones, fur, shells are left.
- **Not all that is eaten is digested and absorbed** — some passes out as **faeces** (egestion).
- Much of the absorbed energy is **used in respiration** to power **movement**, and is released as **heat** (especially in mammals and birds, which keep warm).
- Some is lost in **excretion** (urea in urine).
- Only the energy stored in **new growth** (biomass) is available to the next consumer.

$$\text{efficiency of transfer} = \frac{\text{energy in the higher level}}{\text{energy in the lower level}} \times 100$$

Because energy is lost at every step, food chains are usually **short** (four or five levels at most) and the number and mass of organisms **fall** at each level — which is why farming plants or herbivores gives more food per hectare than farming carnivores.

**Food webs and change**: removing or adding one organism ripples through the web. If the rabbits in grass → rabbit → fox are killed by disease, grass increases (less grazing), foxes decline or switch to other prey (mice), so mice decline too. Reason step by step: fewer of X → more of what X ate → less for what ate X → knock-on to their other prey or predators.

::: method
1. **Trophic level names in order**, producer first; arrows in a chain point **from food to feeder** (energy flow).
2. **Pyramids**: state what is measured; explain unusual shapes of pyramids of numbers (large producer; parasites); biomass and energy are always true pyramids.
3. **Energy loss**: name the routes — **not eaten, faeces, respiration/movement/heat, excretion** — rather than "energy is lost". Use the 10% figure.
4. **Efficiency calculation**: higher ÷ lower × 100, with the units and the answer as a percentage.
5. **Food web changes**: trace the effect one link at a time and consider alternative food sources.
:::

::: example Levels
For the food chain oak leaves → caterpillars → blue tits → sparrowhawks, name the trophic level of each organism and sketch the likely shape of the pyramid of numbers. (3)

Oak — producer; caterpillar — primary consumer; blue tit — secondary consumer; sparrowhawk — tertiary consumer (top carnivore) (2). The pyramid of numbers has a very narrow base (one oak tree), a wide caterpillar bar, then narrower bars for blue tits and sparrowhawks (1).
:::

::: example Energy loss
Explain why only about 10% of the energy in the grass eaten by a cow is transferred to a person who eats beef. (4)

Not all of the cow is eaten — bones, hide and organs are discarded (1). Some of the grass the cow ate was not digested and left the cow as faeces (1). Much of the energy the cow absorbed was used in respiration to move and to keep warm, and was lost as heat (1). Some was lost in urine; only the energy in the cow's new muscle growth remains for the person (1).
:::

::: example Calculate
Producers in a field contain 40,000 kJ/m²/year; the rabbits that eat them contain 3,200 kJ/m²/year. Calculate the efficiency of energy transfer. (2)

$\dfrac{3{,}200}{40{,}000} \times 100 = 8\%$ (2).
:::

::: example Food web
In a pond, algae are eaten by water fleas and tadpoles; water fleas are eaten by small fish; small fish and tadpoles are eaten by herons. Predict the effect on the pond of a disease that kills most of the small fish. (3)

Water fleas increase, because fewer fish eat them (1); algae may decrease as more water fleas graze them, which could also reduce food for tadpoles (1); herons lose one food source and may eat more tadpoles, so tadpole numbers fall, or the herons move elsewhere (1).
:::

::: q
What do the arrows in a food chain represent? (1)
---
The direction of energy flow — from the organism eaten to the organism that eats it.
:::

::: q
Define "producer" and "primary consumer". (2)
---
A producer makes its own food by photosynthesis (1); a primary consumer is a herbivore that eats producers (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a pyramid of biomass is nearly always pyramid-shaped even when the pyramid of numbers is not. (2)
---
Biomass measures total mass, not number of individuals, so one large tree has a large biomass (1); each level can only support a smaller mass above it because energy is lost between levels (1).
:::

::: q
Give three ways energy is lost between trophic levels. (3)
---
As heat from respiration; in faeces (undigested food); in urine (excretion) — or parts of the organism not eaten.
:::

::: q
Explain why food chains rarely have more than five links. (2)
---
About 90% of the energy is lost at each transfer (1), so after four or five levels too little energy remains to support another population (1).
:::

::: q
A trophic level contains 500 kJ. The next level contains 45 kJ. Calculate the efficiency of transfer. (1)
---
$45 \div 500 \times 100 = 9\%$.
:::

::: q
Explain why it is more energy-efficient for humans to eat crops than meat. (2)
---
Eating crops takes energy from the producer level directly (1); feeding crops to animals first loses about 90% of the energy before the meat is eaten (1).
:::

::: q
What is the role of decomposers in a food web? (2)
---
They break down dead organisms and waste from every level (1), releasing nutrients back into the soil for producers to reuse (1).
:::

::: q
In the chain grass → grasshopper → frog → snake, what would happen to the frogs and grass if the snakes were removed? (2)
---
Frogs would increase, with no predator (1); grasshoppers would then decrease, so grass would increase (1).
:::

::: watch
- Arrows show **energy flow** — from eaten to eater. Drawing them backwards is a common slip.
- Name the **routes** of energy loss: not eaten, faeces, respiration → movement and heat, excretion.
- Pyramids of numbers can be **odd shapes**; biomass and energy are **true pyramids**. Explain why with examples.
- Efficiency = **higher ÷ lower × 100** — check the numbers are for adjacent levels.
- Food web questions: reason **one link at a time**, and mention alternative prey.
:::
