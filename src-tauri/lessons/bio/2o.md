## What it is

**Hormones** are **chemical messengers** produced by **endocrine glands**, released directly into the **blood**, and carried to **target organs** whose cells have receptors for them. They coordinate slower, longer-lasting processes than nerves.

| Hormone | Made in | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Adrenaline** | **adrenal glands** (above the kidneys) | prepares the body for action ("fight or flight"): increases **heart rate** and **breathing rate**, raises **blood glucose** (glycogen → glucose), widens pupils, diverts blood to muscles |
| **Insulin** | **pancreas** | **lowers blood glucose**: makes liver and muscle cells take up glucose and convert it to **glycogen** |
| **Glucagon** | **pancreas** | **raises blood glucose**: makes the liver convert **glycogen back to glucose** |
| **Testosterone** | **testes** | male secondary sexual characteristics; sperm production |
| **Progesterone** | **ovaries** | maintains the **lining of the uterus** during the menstrual cycle and pregnancy |
| **Oestrogen** | **ovaries** | female secondary sexual characteristics; rebuilds the uterus lining after menstruation |
| **ADH** | **pituitary gland** | increases water reabsorption in the kidney collecting ducts (see 2l) |
| **FSH** | **pituitary** | stimulates an egg to mature in the ovary (and oestrogen production) |
| **LH** | **pituitary** | triggers **ovulation** — release of the egg |

**Nervous vs hormonal communication:**

| | Nervous system | Endocrine (hormonal) system |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | electrical impulse along neurones (chemical only at synapses) | chemical — hormone in the blood |
| Speed | **very fast** (milliseconds) | **slower** (seconds to hours) |
| Duration | **short-lived** | **longer-lasting** |
| Target | one precise area — the effector the neurone connects to | widespread — every cell with receptors for the hormone |
| Examples | reflexes, muscle movement | growth, blood glucose, reproduction, fight or flight |

**Adrenaline** — released in fear, stress or excitement: heart rate and stroke volume rise, so more oxygen and glucose reach the muscles; breathing rate rises; glycogen in the liver is converted to glucose, raising blood glucose for respiration; blood is diverted from the gut to muscles; pupils dilate. All of this prepares the body to run or fight.

**Control of blood glucose** — a negative feedback loop centred on the pancreas:

- **After a meal, blood glucose rises** → the pancreas detects this and releases **insulin** → insulin causes liver and muscle cells to **take up glucose** from the blood and store it as **glycogen** → blood glucose falls back to normal.
- **Between meals or during exercise, blood glucose falls** → the pancreas releases **glucagon** → the liver breaks **glycogen down into glucose** and releases it into the blood → blood glucose rises back to normal.

**Diabetes** (type 1) — the pancreas makes little or no insulin, so blood glucose rises dangerously after meals (glucose appears in the urine). Treated by **injecting insulin**, controlling carbohydrate intake and exercise. (Type 2 — cells stop responding to insulin; linked to obesity; managed by diet and exercise.)

::: terms
- **Hormone**: a chemical messenger secreted by an endocrine gland into the blood, which carries it to target organs.
- **Endocrine gland**: a gland that secretes hormones directly into the blood.
- **Target organ**: an organ that responds to a particular hormone.
- **Adrenaline**: made by the adrenal glands; increases heart rate, breathing rate and blood glucose for "fight or flight".
- **Insulin**: made by the pancreas; lowers blood glucose by making the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen.
- **Glucagon**: made by the pancreas; raises blood glucose by making the liver convert glycogen to glucose.
- **Testosterone**: made by the testes; controls male secondary sexual characteristics.
- **Oestrogen**: made by the ovaries; controls female secondary sexual characteristics and repairs the uterus lining.
- **Progesterone**: made by the ovaries; maintains the uterus lining.
- **ADH**: made by the pituitary gland; increases water reabsorption in the kidneys.
- **FSH**: made by the pituitary gland; causes an egg to mature in the ovary and stimulates oestrogen production.
- **LH**: made by the pituitary gland; triggers ovulation.
:::

::: method
1. **Define a hormone with three ideas**: chemical messenger, made by a gland, carried in the blood to a target organ.
2. **Hormone → gland → effect**: learn the table as triples; the exam asks for any of the three.
3. **Nerves vs hormones comparison**: speed, duration, signal type, target — at least three differences, each as "nerves X, hormones Y".
4. **Adrenaline**: name three effects and say how each prepares the body for action.
5. **Blood glucose**: two loops — high → insulin → glucose to glycogen; low → glucagon → glycogen to glucose. State the gland (pancreas) and the storage organ (liver) and that it is negative feedback.
6. **Glycogen ≠ glucagon** — the store vs the hormone. Spell them carefully.
:::

::: example Define
What is a hormone? (3)

A chemical messenger (1) produced by an endocrine gland and released into the blood (1), which carries it to a target organ where it causes a response (1).
:::

::: example Compare
Give three differences between nervous and hormonal coordination. (3)

Nervous responses are very fast, hormonal responses slower (1). Nervous effects are short-lived; hormonal effects last longer (1). Nerves carry electrical impulses to one precise effector; hormones are chemicals carried in the blood to any cells with the right receptors (1).
:::

::: example Adrenaline
Explain how adrenaline prepares the body for "fight or flight". (3)

It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen reaches the muscles (1). It causes the liver to convert glycogen to glucose, raising blood glucose for respiration in the muscles (1). It diverts blood from the gut to the muscles and dilates the pupils, improving readiness to run or fight (1).
:::

::: example Blood glucose
Explain how blood glucose is returned to normal after a meal rich in carbohydrate. (4)

Digestion of the carbohydrate raises the blood glucose concentration (1). The pancreas detects the rise and secretes insulin into the blood (1). Insulin causes the liver and muscle cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen for storage (1), so blood glucose falls back to normal — an example of negative feedback (1).
:::

::: example State — hormones and their sources
Copy and complete: name the gland that produces each hormone and state one effect — insulin, testosterone, FSH, ADH. (4)

Insulin — pancreas; lowers blood glucose by causing the liver to store glucose as glycogen (1).

Testosterone — testes; causes male secondary sexual characteristics such as a deeper voice and facial hair (1).

FSH — pituitary gland; causes an egg to mature in the ovary (1).

ADH — pituitary gland; makes the kidney collecting ducts reabsorb more water (1).
:::

::: q
Name the gland that produces adrenaline and the gland that produces insulin. (2)
---
Adrenal glands (1); pancreas (1).
:::

::: q
State the effect of glucagon. (2)
---
It causes the liver to convert stored glycogen into glucose (1), raising the blood glucose concentration (1).
:::

::: q
Where is progesterone produced and what does it do? (2)
---
The ovaries (1); it maintains the lining of the uterus (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why hormonal responses last longer than nervous responses. (1)
---
Hormones remain in the blood and keep acting until they are broken down, whereas an impulse is over in milliseconds.
:::

::: q
Explain why a person with type 1 diabetes has glucose in their urine after a meal. (3)
---
Their pancreas produces little or no insulin (1), so glucose is not taken up and stored as glycogen and blood glucose rises very high (1); the kidney cannot reabsorb all the glucose filtered, so some is lost in urine (1).
:::

::: q
How is type 1 diabetes treated? (2)
---
Injections of insulin (1), combined with controlling carbohydrate intake and exercise (1).
:::

::: q
Name two hormones released by the pituitary gland. (2)
---
ADH; FSH (or LH).
:::

::: q
Explain why blood glucose control is an example of negative feedback. (2)
---
A rise in glucose triggers insulin, which lowers it; a fall triggers glucagon, which raises it (1) — each response reverses the change, keeping the level near normal (1).
:::

::: q
Why does adrenaline increase blood glucose? (1)
---
So the muscles have more glucose for respiration, releasing the energy needed for rapid action.
:::

::: watch
- **Nerves: fast, short, electrical, precise. Hormones: slower, longer, chemical in blood, widespread.** Asked nearly every year.
- **Glucagon** is the hormone; **glycogen** is the store. One letter apart, very different marks.
- Insulin **lowers** glucose (glucose → glycogen); glucagon **raises** it (glycogen → glucose). Both from the **pancreas**; storage in the **liver**.
- A hormone definition needs **chemical, gland, blood, target organ**.
- Adrenaline: **adrenal glands**, and give effects with their purpose.
:::

---
FSH from the pituitary causes an egg to mature and the ovary to make oestrogen (1); oestrogen repairs the uterus lining and triggers LH release (1); LH causes ovulation (1); progesterone from the empty follicle maintains the lining; if no fertilisation, its level falls and menstruation occurs (1).
