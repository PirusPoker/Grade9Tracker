## What it is

**Sexual reproduction** involves the **fusion of two gametes** (sex cells) — **fertilisation** — producing a zygote that is **genetically different** from both parents. It needs two parents (usually) and produces **variation**. **Asexual reproduction** involves **one parent** and **no gametes**; the offspring are produced by mitosis and are **genetically identical** to the parent — **clones**. Examples: runners in strawberries, tubers in potatoes, bulbs, binary fission in bacteria, budding in yeast.

| | Sexual | Asexual |
|---|---|---|
| Parents | two | one |
| Gametes / fertilisation | yes | no |
| Offspring | genetically varied | identical clones |
| Advantage | variation → some survive if conditions change; disease resistance spreads | fast, no mate needed, all offspring suited to a stable environment |
| Disadvantage | slower, needs a mate | no variation → a disease or change can kill all |

**Flower structure**: **sepals** protect the bud; **petals**; the male part is the **stamen** — **anther** (produces **pollen**, the male gametes) on a **filament**; the female part is the **carpel** — **stigma** (receives pollen), **style**, **ovary** containing **ovules** (each with a female gamete, the egg cell). **Nectaries** produce nectar.

**Pollination** is the **transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma**. Self-pollination — same plant; cross-pollination — a different plant of the same species (more variation).

| | Insect-pollinated | Wind-pollinated |
|---|---|---|
| Petals | **large, brightly coloured, scented** — attract insects | small, dull or absent |
| Nectar | present — rewards insects | none |
| Anthers | inside the flower, where insects brush them | **hang outside** the flower, loosely attached, so wind shakes pollen off |
| Pollen | **sticky or spiky**, in moderate amounts — clings to insects | **small, smooth, light**, in **huge amounts** — carried by wind |
| Stigma | inside, **sticky** | **feathery**, hanging outside — large surface area to catch pollen |
| Examples | rose, bee-pollinated flowers | grasses, many trees |

**Fertilisation**: a pollen grain lands on a compatible stigma and **germinates**, growing a **pollen tube** down through the style to the ovary; the tube grows towards an ovule (attracted by chemicals) and enters it through a small hole (the micropyle); the **male nucleus** travels down the tube and **fuses with the female nucleus** (egg) in the ovule — **fertilisation**. Pollination and fertilisation are different events: pollination moves pollen; fertilisation is the fusion of nuclei.

**After fertilisation**: the fertilised ovule develops into a **seed** (the zygote → embryo plant with a food store, inside a tough seed coat); the ovary develops into the **fruit**, which helps seed dispersal (by wind, animals, water or explosion).

**Germination** — the seed absorbs water, the embryo uses the food store (starch broken down to glucose by enzymes) for respiration and growth until leaves can photosynthesise. Three conditions are needed: **water** (to activate enzymes and swell the seed), **oxygen** (for aerobic respiration to release energy) and a **suitable temperature** (for the enzymes). Light is *not* needed by most seeds.

**Required practical**: set up four tubes of cress or pea seeds on cotton wool — (1) water, oxygen, warm; (2) no water (dry); (3) no oxygen (seeds under boiled, oil-covered water); (4) water and oxygen but cold (fridge). Only tube 1 germinates, showing each factor is needed. Each tube changes one condition only; the others are controlled.

::: terms
- **Sexual reproduction**: reproduction involving the fusion of male and female gametes, producing genetically different offspring.
- **Asexual reproduction**: reproduction from one parent without gametes, producing genetically identical offspring (clones).
- **Gamete**: a sex cell — sperm, egg, pollen nucleus or ovule — with half the normal number of chromosomes.
- **Fertilisation**: the fusion of a male and a female gamete nucleus to form a zygote.
- **Zygote**: the fertilised egg cell, which divides to form an embryo.
- **Pollination**: the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.
- **Insect-pollinated flower**: a flower with large, brightly coloured petals, scent and nectar, and sticky pollen, adapted to attract insects.
- **Wind-pollinated flower**: a flower with small, dull petals, anthers and stigmas hanging outside the flower, and large amounts of light, smooth pollen.
- **Pollen tube**: the tube that grows from a pollen grain down through the style to the ovule, carrying the male nucleus.
- **Germination**: the start of growth of a seed, which needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature.
- **Runner**: a side shoot that grows along the ground and forms new plants — natural asexual reproduction, e.g. strawberry plants.
:::

::: method
1. **Sexual vs asexual**: parents, gametes, variation — and the advantage/disadvantage of variation for survival.
2. **Flower parts → functions**, male and female. Anther makes pollen; ovary holds ovules.
3. **Insect vs wind**: for each feature give the *reason* — feathery stigma → large surface area to catch pollen from the air.
4. **Pollination then fertilisation**, as two separate definitions; then the pollen tube sequence: germinates on stigma → grows down style → enters ovule → male nucleus fuses with egg nucleus.
5. **Germination**: three conditions with the reason for each (water — enzymes; oxygen — respiration; warmth — enzyme activity); the control experiment with one factor changed per tube.
:::

::: example Compare
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of asexual reproduction to a plant. (2)

Advantage: it is quick and needs only one parent, so a plant can rapidly colonise an area with offspring suited to the same conditions (1). Disadvantage: the offspring are genetically identical, so if the environment changes or a disease strikes, none may be resistant and all could die (1).
:::

::: example Wind pollination
Explain three ways a grass flower is adapted for wind pollination. (3)

Anthers hang outside the flower on long filaments, so the wind shakes pollen off easily (1). Pollen is produced in huge quantities and is small, smooth and light, so it is carried far by the wind and some reaches a stigma (1). The stigmas are feathery and hang outside the flower, giving a large surface area to catch pollen from the air (1).
:::

::: example Fertilisation
Describe what happens after a pollen grain lands on a stigma. (4)

The pollen grain germinates and grows a pollen tube down through the style towards the ovary (1). The tube grows into an ovule through the micropyle (1). The male nucleus from the pollen grain travels down the tube (1) and fuses with the female nucleus in the ovule — fertilisation — forming a zygote that develops into the seed's embryo (1).
:::

::: example Germination practical
Describe an experiment to show that seeds need oxygen to germinate. (3)

Place seeds on damp cotton wool in two tubes at room temperature; in one, cover the seeds with boiled and cooled water and a layer of oil to keep out oxygen (1). Keep water and temperature the same in both, so oxygen is the only difference (1). The seeds with air germinate while those without oxygen do not, showing oxygen is needed — for aerobic respiration to release energy for growth (1).
:::

::: example Compare — insect and wind pollination
Describe two ways an insect-pollinated flower differs from a wind-pollinated flower, and explain each. (4)

Insect-pollinated flowers have large, brightly coloured, scented petals and nectar (1), to attract insects, which carry pollen from flower to flower (1).

Insect-pollinated flowers produce smaller amounts of sticky or spiky pollen that clings to insects (1), while wind-pollinated flowers make huge amounts of light, smooth pollen, because most blows away and is wasted (1).
:::

::: example Explain — food reserves in a germinating seed
Explain how a germinating seed obtains energy before its leaves can photosynthesise. (3)

The seed contains a store of starch, protein and fat in its cotyledons (1). When it absorbs water, enzymes such as amylase break the starch down into glucose (1), which the seedling respires to release energy for growth until its first leaves open and begin photosynthesising (1).
:::

Define pollination. (1)
---
The transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.

::: q
Name the male and female parts of a flower and state where the gametes are made. (2)
---
Stamen (anther and filament) — pollen made in the anther (1); carpel (stigma, style, ovary) — egg cells in the ovules in the ovary (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why insect-pollinated flowers have bright petals and nectar. (2)
---
Bright colours and scent attract insects (1); nectar rewards them so they visit repeatedly, carrying pollen between flowers (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between pollination and fertilisation. (2)
---
Pollination is the transfer of pollen to a stigma (1); fertilisation is the fusion of the male nucleus with the female nucleus in the ovule (1).
:::

::: q
What does the ovule become after fertilisation, and what does the ovary become? (2)
---
The ovule becomes the seed (1); the ovary becomes the fruit (1).
:::

::: q
State the three conditions needed for germination and the reason for each. (3)
---
Water — activates enzymes and swells the seed; oxygen — aerobic respiration releases energy; warmth — enzymes work at a suitable temperature.
:::

::: q
Why does a germinating seed lose mass at first? (2)
---
It respires its stored food (starch → glucose) to release energy for growth (1) before it has leaves to photosynthesise (1).
:::

::: q
Give two examples of asexual reproduction in plants. (2)
---
Runners (strawberry); tubers (potato) — or bulbs, cuttings.
:::

::: q
Why does cross-pollination produce more variation than self-pollination? (1)
---
The gametes come from two different plants with different alleles, so offspring inherit new combinations.
:::

::: watch
- **Pollination ≠ fertilisation.** Pollination moves pollen; fertilisation is the fusion of nuclei inside the ovule.
- Wind flowers: **feathery stigma, dangling anthers, light abundant pollen** — with reasons about the wind.
- Asexual → **clones, no variation**; sexual → **variation** — and say why variation matters.
- Germination needs **water, oxygen, warmth** — not light. Explain each with enzymes or respiration.
- Seed from the **ovule**; fruit from the **ovary**.
:::
