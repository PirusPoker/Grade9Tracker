## What it is

Crop yield is limited by whatever limits **photosynthesis** and growth — light, carbon dioxide, temperature, water and mineral ions — and by the organisms that eat or compete with the crop. Farmers raise yield by controlling these factors.

**Glasshouses and polythene tunnels** enclose the crop so the grower can control its environment:

- **Temperature** — glass traps heat (the greenhouse effect); heaters can be added in winter. Warmer → enzymes of photosynthesis and growth work faster, up to the optimum; crops can be grown out of season and in cold climates.
- **Light** — artificial lighting extends the day and lets growth continue in winter; more light → more photosynthesis. Glass lets light in.
- **Carbon dioxide** — can be raised by burning fuel (paraffin heaters give both heat and CO₂) or by piping in CO₂; normal air (0.04%) is often the **limiting factor**, so raising it to about 0.1% increases the rate of photosynthesis.
- **Water and minerals** — irrigation and fertiliser can be delivered precisely (hydroponics grows plants in mineral solutions with no soil).
- **Pests and disease** are kept out, and weeds controlled; wind and frost damage avoided.
- The costs: heating, lighting and CO₂ are expensive, so glasshouses are used for high-value crops (tomatoes, cucumbers, flowers).

**Light, CO₂ and temperature** each act as limiting factors (2d): raising one increases yield only until another limits. A polythene tunnel raises temperature cheaply but not CO₂ or light, so its gain is smaller than a heated, lit glasshouse.

**Fertilisers** supply the **mineral ions** crops remove from the soil each harvest:

- **Nitrates (N)** — to make **amino acids and proteins** → more growth, larger leaves, more photosynthesis.
- **Phosphates (P)** — for DNA, cell membranes and root growth; energy transfer (ATP).
- **Potassium (K)** — for enzyme action, flowering and fruit.
- **Magnesium** — for chlorophyll.

NPK fertilisers replace what the crop took out, so the next crop is not limited by mineral shortage. **Inorganic (artificial) fertilisers** are concentrated, fast-acting and their content is known, but they are expensive, wash out easily (leaching → eutrophication, 4d) and do nothing for soil structure. **Organic fertilisers** (manure, compost) release nutrients slowly, improve soil structure and water retention and are cheap, but are bulky, smelly, of variable content and may carry pathogens.

**Pest control** — pests (insects, fungi, weeds, rodents) reduce yield by eating the crop, spreading disease or competing for light and minerals.

| | **Pesticides** (chemical: insecticides, fungicides, herbicides) | **Biological control** (introducing a natural predator or parasite of the pest) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | fast-acting, kills most pests quickly | slow to establish; never eliminates the pest, only reduces it |
| Cost | must be bought and reapplied repeatedly | one introduction may be self-sustaining; cheaper long-term |
| Specificity | often kill useful insects too (bees, pest predators) | usually specific to the pest |
| Resistance | pests evolve **resistance** (natural selection), so stronger doses or new chemicals are needed | no resistance develops |
| Environment | persist and build up along food chains (**bioaccumulation** — top predators poisoned); leach into water; residues on food | no chemicals; but the control organism may itself become a pest, eat other species or upset the food web |
| Suits | large open fields, urgent outbreaks | glasshouses (contained), long-term management |

Examples of biological control: ladybirds eat aphids; the parasitic wasp *Encarsia* lays eggs in whitefly in glasshouses; the cactus moth controlled prickly pear in Australia.

**For and against controlling pests**: control raises yield and food supply and lowers food prices; but pesticides harm non-target species and human health, encourage resistance and cost money; biological control is slower and can go wrong. **Integrated pest management** combines both with resistant crop varieties and crop rotation.

::: method
1. **Every yield answer goes through photosynthesis**: name the factor → its effect on the rate of photosynthesis (or on enzymes/growth) → more glucose → more growth → higher yield. An answer without limiting factors is missing the biology.
2. **Glasshouses**: give the factor controlled *and* how — heaters, lamps, CO₂ from burners — and the cost side.
3. **Fertilisers**: mineral → what the plant makes from it → why yield rises. Nitrate → proteins is the main one.
4. **Pesticides vs biological control**: speed, specificity, resistance, persistence/bioaccumulation, cost — compare in pairs and end with a judgement for the situation (glasshouse vs field).
5. **Resistance** is natural selection (3f): explain it as variation and selection, not as pests "getting used to" the chemical.
:::

::: example Glasshouse
Explain how a commercial grower uses a glasshouse to increase the yield of tomatoes. (4)

The glass traps heat and heaters keep the temperature near the optimum for the enzymes of photosynthesis, so the rate of photosynthesis and growth is higher all year (1). Artificial lights extend the hours of light, increasing photosynthesis in winter and letting crops be grown out of season (1). Burning paraffin heaters or piping in CO₂ raises the carbon dioxide concentration, removing it as a limiting factor so photosynthesis is faster (1). The enclosed space keeps out pests and allows precise watering and feeding, so fewer plants are lost and more glucose goes into fruit (1).
:::

::: example Fertiliser
Explain how adding a nitrate fertiliser increases the yield of a wheat crop. (3)

Nitrate ions are absorbed by the roots and used to make amino acids and hence proteins (1). More protein means more growth — larger leaves and more chlorophyll and enzymes (1), so the crop photosynthesises more and produces more grain (1).
:::

::: example Compare
Compare the use of a chemical insecticide with biological control for aphids on a lettuce crop. (4)

The insecticide kills the aphids quickly and almost completely, whereas ladybirds introduced as biological control take time to build up and only reduce the aphid numbers (1). The insecticide may also kill useful insects such as bees and the aphids' natural predators, while ladybirds are specific to the pest (1). Aphids can evolve resistance to the insecticide, so it must be reapplied and changed, whereas resistance to a predator does not develop (1). Insecticide residues can remain on the lettuce and build up in food chains; biological control leaves no chemicals but the ladybirds may move away or affect other species (1).
:::

::: example Discuss
Discuss whether farmers should use pesticides. (4)

For: pesticides raise yields by preventing loss to insects, fungi and weeds, keeping food plentiful and cheap; they act quickly in an outbreak and are easy to apply over large fields (2). Against: they kill non-target species including pollinators, persist in the environment and bioaccumulate in top predators, can leave residues harmful to people, and pests evolve resistance so the chemicals stop working (1). Judgement: pesticides are useful where losses would otherwise be severe, but should be used sparingly alongside biological control and resistant crop varieties to limit the harm and delay resistance (1).
:::

::: q
State three environmental factors a grower can control in a glasshouse. (3)
---
Temperature; light intensity; carbon dioxide concentration (or water, minerals, pests).
:::

::: q
Explain why burning paraffin heaters in a glasshouse increases yield in two ways. (2)
---
They raise the temperature, speeding up the enzyme-controlled reactions of photosynthesis (1), and release carbon dioxide, which is often the limiting factor (1).
:::

::: q
Why is a polythene tunnel cheaper than a glasshouse but less effective? (2)
---
Polythene is cheap and easy to erect and still traps heat (1), but it usually has no heating, lighting or CO₂ supply, so fewer limiting factors are overcome (1).
:::

::: q
Name the three main minerals in NPK fertiliser and state what nitrogen is used for. (2)
---
Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium (1); nitrogen (as nitrate) is used to make amino acids and proteins (1).
:::

::: q
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of organic fertiliser compared with inorganic fertiliser. (2)
---
Advantage: improves soil structure and releases nutrients slowly, so less leaching (1). Disadvantage: bulky, variable nutrient content and slower acting (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the use of a pesticide can lead to a resistant pest population. (3)
---
A few pests carry a mutation making them resistant (1); the pesticide kills the rest but the resistant ones survive and reproduce (1); the resistance allele becomes common, so the pesticide stops working (1).
:::

::: q
Define biological control and give an example. (2)
---
Using a natural predator or parasite to reduce a pest population (1), e.g. ladybirds to eat aphids (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why biological control is more suitable in a glasshouse than in an open field. (2)
---
In a glasshouse the control organism cannot escape, so it stays where the pests are (1); in a field it may disperse, and conditions are harder to control (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by bioaccumulation and why it harms top predators. (2)
---
Persistent pesticides are not broken down and build up in organisms along a food chain (1); top predators eat many contaminated prey, so the concentration in them reaches toxic levels (1).
:::

::: watch
- Every yield answer must reach **photosynthesis and limiting factors** — factor → rate → glucose → growth → yield.
- Glasshouse: **how** each factor is controlled (heater, lamp, CO₂ burner), plus the **cost**.
- Nitrate → **proteins**; magnesium → **chlorophyll**.
- Pesticide vs biological control: **speed, specificity, resistance, persistence, cost** — and a judgement for the setting.
- Resistance = **natural selection**; pests do not "become immune" or "get used to it".
:::
