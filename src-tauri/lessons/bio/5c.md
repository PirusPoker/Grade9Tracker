## What it is

**Selective breeding** (artificial selection) is the process by which **humans choose the organisms with the most desirable characteristics and breed them together**, repeating this **over many generations** so that the characteristic becomes more pronounced and common in the population. Humans have done it for thousands of years — every crop plant and farm animal is the product of it. No genes are transferred from other species; the breeder simply decides who breeds.

**The process:**

1. Decide on the desired characteristic (higher yield, disease resistance, docile temperament).
2. From the existing population, **select the individuals that show it most strongly** (the cows giving the most milk; the wheat plants with the largest grains).
3. **Breed them together** (cross-pollinate the plants; mate the animals).
4. From the **offspring, select again** those that show the characteristic best, and breed them.
5. **Repeat over many generations**; each generation the average shifts towards the desired characteristic, because the alleles for it become more frequent while others are lost.

**Examples in plants**: wheat and rice with higher yield, shorter stems (less wind damage) and disease resistance; cabbage, broccoli, kale and cauliflower all bred from one wild mustard; sweeter, seedless fruit; crops that ripen at the same time for machine harvesting; drought-tolerant varieties.

**Examples in animals**: dairy cattle producing more milk; beef cattle with more muscle; sheep with more wool; chickens laying more eggs or growing faster; dogs of every breed from wolves; racehorses for speed; pigs with leaner meat.

**Advantages**: higher yields and better quality food; disease-resistant crops need fewer pesticides; animals suited to their environment or to farming methods; no complex technology needed.

**Drawbacks — mainly reduced variation:**

- **Reduced genetic variation (gene pool)** — breeding only from a few chosen individuals loses many alleles. If a new disease, pest or change in climate appears, there may be **no individuals with resistance**, and the whole population is at risk (the Irish potato famine, banana varieties).
- **Inbreeding** — repeatedly breeding closely related individuals brings together harmful recessive alleles, causing genetic defects (hip problems in some dog breeds, breathing difficulties in flat-faced breeds).
- **Health and welfare** — animals bred for one trait may suffer: cows with huge udders, chickens that grow too fast for their legs.
- **Loss of other useful traits** — selecting for yield alone may lose flavour, hardiness or resistance.
- **Slow** — many generations are needed, and it can only work with characteristics already present in the species.

**Selective breeding vs genetic modification**: selective breeding uses only the alleles already in the species and needs many generations; GM (5d) transfers a specific gene, possibly from another species, in one step.

::: terms
- **Selective breeding (artificial selection)**: humans choosing parents with desired characteristics to breed, over many generations.
- **Desired characteristic**: a feature humans want in a crop or animal, such as high yield or disease resistance.
- **Inbreeding**: breeding closely related individuals, which reduces genetic variation.
- **Genetic variation**: differences in alleles within a population, which selective breeding reduces.
:::

::: method
1. **Definition**: humans choose + desirable characteristics + breed together + many generations. All four ideas.
2. **Process as numbered steps** applied to the organism in the question: "select the cows giving most milk → breed them with high-milk bulls → select the best daughters → repeat".
3. **Always say "over many generations"** and that the alleles for the trait become more common.
4. **Drawbacks**: reduced variation is the main one — explain *why* it matters (disease, environmental change); then inbreeding and welfare.
5. **Do not confuse with natural selection** (the environment selects) or genetic modification (genes transferred). Selective breeding: humans select, no gene transfer.
:::

::: example Process
Describe how a farmer could use selective breeding to produce cows that give more milk. (4)

Choose the cows in the herd that produce the most milk and a bull whose mother and daughters were high milk producers (1). Breed them together (1). From the calves, select the females that grow up to give the most milk and breed them with high-yielding bulls again (1). Repeat this over many generations, so the alleles for high milk yield become more common and the average yield of the herd rises (1).
:::

::: example Plants
Explain how selective breeding has been used to produce wheat with short stems, and why this is useful. (3)

Plants with the shortest stems in each crop were chosen and cross-pollinated with each other (1); the shortest of their offspring were selected and bred, and this was repeated over many generations until all plants were short-stemmed (1). Short stems are less likely to be flattened by wind and rain, and the plant puts more energy into grain rather than stem, so the yield is higher (1).
:::

::: example Drawback
Explain why selective breeding can make a crop vulnerable to a new disease. (3)

Breeding only from a few chosen plants reduces genetic variation in the crop — many alleles are lost (1). If a new disease appears, there may be no plants carrying an allele for resistance (1), so the disease can spread through the whole genetically similar crop, destroying it (1).
:::

::: example Compare
Give two differences between selective breeding and natural selection. (2)

In selective breeding humans choose which individuals breed; in natural selection the environment determines which survive to breed (1). Selective breeding aims at characteristics useful to humans, which may not help the organism survive in the wild; natural selection favours characteristics that aid survival and reproduction (1).
:::

::: q
Define selective breeding. (2)
---
Humans choosing organisms with desirable characteristics and breeding them together (1), repeated over many generations to produce offspring with those characteristics (1).
:::

::: q
Give two characteristics that farmers might select for in sheep. (2)
---
Thick wool; fast growth or more meat (or resistance to disease, hardiness).
:::

::: q
Why must selective breeding be repeated over many generations? (2)
---
Each generation only shifts the characteristic a little, because offspring inherit a mixture of alleles (1); repeated selection makes the desired alleles more and more common until the trait is fixed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by inbreeding and why it is a problem. (2)
---
Breeding closely related individuals together (1); it increases the chance that harmful recessive alleles are inherited from both parents, causing genetic defects (1).
:::

::: q
Give one welfare problem caused by selective breeding in animals. (1)
---
Chickens bred to grow very fast may have legs that cannot support their weight (or dogs with breathing difficulties, cows with oversized udders).
:::

::: q
Give one advantage of selective breeding for disease resistance in crops. (2)
---
Resistant crops lose less yield to disease (1) and need fewer pesticides, saving money and reducing environmental harm (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why selective breeding cannot introduce a characteristic that the species has never had. (1)
---
It only selects among alleles already present in the population; it does not create or import new genes.
:::

::: q
How does selective breeding differ from genetic modification? (2)
---
Selective breeding uses only the species' own alleles and takes many generations (1); genetic modification transfers a specific gene, possibly from another species, in a single step (1).
:::

::: watch
- Definition needs **humans choose, desirable characteristics, breed together, many generations.**
- Describe the process as **steps applied to the organism in the question**, ending with "repeat over many generations".
- The main drawback is **reduced variation** — explain the disease/environment risk; add **inbreeding** and **welfare**.
- **No genes are transferred** — selective breeding is not GM.
- Humans select, not the environment — do not drift into natural-selection language.
:::

::: test
Describe how a farmer could use selective breeding to produce cows with a high milk yield. (3)
---
Choose the cows with the highest milk yield and breed them with bulls whose mothers had high yields (1); select the best offspring and breed from them (1); repeat over many generations (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how selective breeding could produce wheat that is both disease-resistant and high-yielding. (2)
---
Cross a disease-resistant variety with a high-yield variety (1); select offspring showing both characteristics and breed from them for several generations (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why selective breeding reduces the gene pool and why this is a risk. (2)
---
Only individuals with the chosen alleles are bred from, so other alleles are lost (1); the population becomes less able to adapt to a new disease or change in conditions (1).
:::

::: test
Give two examples of characteristics selected in dogs and one problem this has caused. (2)
---
Size, coat, temperament, speed (any two) (1); breathing problems in flat-faced breeds, hip problems, inherited diseases from inbreeding (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why selective breeding takes many generations. (2)
---
The desired characteristic is often controlled by many genes and the offspring vary (1); only some offspring show the improvement, so selection must be repeated each generation (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between natural selection and selective breeding. (2)
---
In natural selection the environment determines which individuals survive and breed (1); in selective breeding humans choose which individuals breed (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how selective breeding of crop plants has contributed to feeding the world's population. (1)
---
Varieties with higher yields, resistance to disease or drought, or shorter growing times produce more food per hectare.
:::

::: test
Suggest why a breeder might occasionally introduce an unrelated animal into a selectively bred population. (1)
---
To bring in new alleles and reduce the harmful effects of inbreeding.
:::
