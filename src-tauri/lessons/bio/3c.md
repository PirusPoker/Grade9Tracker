## What it is

The **nucleus** of a cell contains **chromosomes**, which are long molecules of **DNA**. A **gene** is a **section of DNA that codes for one protein** (by specifying the sequence of amino acids). The **genome** is the **entire DNA of an organism** — all its genes and the DNA between them. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs) and about 20,000 genes.

**The structure of DNA**: a **double helix** — two strands twisted around each other. Each strand is a chain of **nucleotides**; each nucleotide has a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate and one of four **bases**: **A** (adenine), **T** (thymine), **C** (cytosine), **G** (guanine). The two strands are held together by weak bonds between pairs of bases, and the pairing is **complementary**: **A always pairs with T, C always pairs with G**. So if one strand reads ATGC, the other reads TACG. The **order of bases** along a gene is the code for the order of amino acids in a protein: **three bases (a triplet, or codon) code for one amino acid**.

**RNA** is a related molecule used to make proteins:

| | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Strands | **two** (double helix) | **one** |
| Sugar | deoxyribose | ribose |
| Bases | A, T, C, G | A, **U (uracil)**, C, G — U replaces T |
| Where | nucleus | made in the nucleus, moves to the cytoplasm |
| Job | stores the genetic code | **mRNA** carries a copy of a gene to the ribosome |

**Protein synthesis** — two stages in two places:

1. **Transcription** — in the **nucleus**. The DNA of the gene unzips and one strand is used as a template; complementary RNA nucleotides line up (A opposite T, U opposite A, G opposite C) and join to form a strand of **messenger RNA (mRNA)** — a copy of the gene's base sequence. The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a pore.
2. **Translation** — at a **ribosome** in the cytoplasm. The ribosome reads the mRNA **three bases (a codon) at a time**. For each codon, a **transfer RNA (tRNA)** molecule carrying the matching **amino acid** brings it to the ribosome (its anticodon pairs with the codon). The amino acids are joined in order to form a **polypeptide** chain, which folds into the protein. The **sequence of bases in the gene therefore determines the sequence of amino acids**, which determines the protein's shape and function (an enzyme's active site, for instance).

Not every gene is switched on in every cell — a muscle cell and a nerve cell contain the same DNA but express different genes, which is how cells differentiate.

::: terms
- **Genome**: the entire DNA of an organism.
- **Gene**: a section of a DNA molecule that codes for a specific protein.
- **Chromosome**: a long, coiled molecule of DNA carrying many genes, found in the nucleus.
- **DNA**: a double helix of two strands held together by complementary base pairs — A with T, C with G.
- **Base**: one of the four chemicals — adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), guanine (G) — whose order carries the genetic code.
- **Complementary base pairing**: A always pairs with T, and C with G.
- **RNA**: a single-stranded nucleic acid that contains uracil (U) instead of thymine.
- **mRNA**: a copy of a gene that carries the code from the nucleus to a ribosome.
- **Transcription**: making an mRNA copy of a gene in the nucleus.
- **Translation**: using the mRNA code at a ribosome to join amino acids in the right order to make a protein.
- **Codon (triplet)**: a sequence of three bases that codes for one amino acid.
- **tRNA**: RNA that brings a specific amino acid to the ribosome, matching its anticodon to the codon on the mRNA.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions**: gene = section of DNA coding for a protein; genome = all the DNA of an organism; chromosome = long DNA molecule carrying many genes.
2. **DNA structure**: double helix, two strands, nucleotides (sugar, phosphate, base), four bases, complementary pairing A–T, C–G. Practise writing the complementary strand.
3. **DNA vs RNA**: three differences — strands, sugar, U for T.
4. **Protein synthesis as two named stages, two named places**: transcription in the nucleus makes mRNA; translation at the ribosome joins amino acids. Include codon → amino acid and tRNA.
5. **Code logic**: base sequence → amino acid sequence → protein shape → function. Use it to explain why a base change (mutation, 3f) can matter.
:::

::: example Complementary strand
One strand of a DNA molecule has the base sequence ATTGCA. Write the sequence of the complementary strand and of the mRNA transcribed from the original strand. (2)

DNA complementary strand: TAACGT (1). mRNA (U replaces T): UAACGU (1).
:::

::: example Define
Define "gene" and "genome". (2)

A gene is a section of DNA that codes for one protein by specifying its sequence of amino acids (1). The genome is the entire DNA of an organism — all of its genes (1).
:::

::: example Transcription
Describe what happens during transcription. (3)

In the nucleus, the DNA of the gene unwinds and the strands separate (1). Free RNA nucleotides pair with the bases of one strand by complementary base pairing — A with U, C with G (1) — and join to form a single strand of messenger RNA, a copy of the gene, which leaves the nucleus (1).
:::

::: example Translation
Describe how a protein is made from mRNA. (4)

The mRNA attaches to a ribosome in the cytoplasm (1). The ribosome reads the mRNA three bases at a time; each codon codes for one amino acid (1). Transfer RNA molecules, each carrying a specific amino acid, pair their anticodons with the codons, bringing the amino acids to the ribosome in order (1). The amino acids are joined into a polypeptide chain, which folds into the protein (1).
:::

::: example Compare — DNA and RNA
Give three differences between DNA and RNA. (3)

DNA is double-stranded; RNA is single-stranded (1).

DNA contains the base thymine; RNA contains uracil instead (1).

DNA stays in the nucleus; mRNA carries the code out to the ribosomes (1).
:::

Where does transcription occur, and where does translation occur? (2)
---
Transcription in the nucleus (1); translation at ribosomes in the cytoplasm (1).

::: q
What is the role of mRNA? (1)
---
To carry a copy of the gene's base sequence from the nucleus to the ribosome.
:::

::: q
What is the role of tRNA? (1)
---
To bring the correct amino acid to the ribosome, matching its anticodon to the mRNA codon.
:::

::: q
Explain how the sequence of bases in a gene determines the function of a protein. (3)
---
The base sequence, read in triplets, determines the order of amino acids in the polypeptide (1); the amino acid sequence determines how the chain folds into a specific 3-D shape (1); the shape determines its function, e.g. the active site of an enzyme (1).
:::

::: q
A gene has 300 bases. How many amino acids will the protein contain? (1)
---
$300 \div 3 = 100$ amino acids.
:::

::: q
Explain why a nerve cell and a muscle cell look different although they contain the same DNA. (2)
---
Different genes are switched on (expressed) in each cell (1), so they make different proteins and develop different structures (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Two stages, two places**: transcription in the **nucleus**, translation at the **ribosome**. Say where as well as what.
- Base pairing: **A–T, C–G** in DNA; in RNA **U** replaces T (so A–U).
- A **codon is three bases** for one amino acid — use this in calculations.
- Gene = **section of DNA coding for a protein**; genome = **all** the DNA.
- Chain of logic: **bases → amino acids → shape → function** — the basis of mutation questions.
:::
