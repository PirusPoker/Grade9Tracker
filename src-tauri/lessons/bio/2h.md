## What it is

Humans need a specialised gas exchange system because we are large, active and multicellular — diffusion through the skin could never supply enough oxygen.

**Structure of the thorax** (chest): the **ribs** and **intercostal muscles** between them form the wall; the **diaphragm** — a sheet of muscle — forms the floor. Air enters through the nose and mouth into the **trachea** (windpipe, held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage), which divides into two **bronchi** (one per lung), branching into smaller **bronchioles**, ending in millions of tiny air sacs — **alveoli** — surrounded by capillaries. The lungs sit inside the **pleural membranes**, which are lubricated so the lungs slide as they expand.

**Ventilation (breathing)** — moving air in and out. Air moves because of **pressure changes**; muscles do not "pull air in":

| | Inhalation (breathing in) | Exhalation (breathing out) |
|---|---|---|
| External intercostal muscles | **contract** → ribs move **up and out** | **relax** → ribs move down and in |
| Diaphragm | **contracts** → **flattens** (moves down) | **relaxes** → domes upwards |
| Volume of thorax | **increases** | **decreases** |
| Pressure in thorax | **falls** below atmospheric pressure | **rises** above atmospheric pressure |
| Air | flows **in** down the pressure gradient | flows **out** |

Forced exhalation (exercise) also uses the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.

**Gas exchange in the alveoli**: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveolus into the blood in the capillary; carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveolus. **How alveoli are adapted**:

- **Millions of alveoli** → an enormous **surface area** (about 70 m²).
- Alveolar wall and capillary wall are each **one cell thick** → **short diffusion distance**.
- **Rich blood supply** — a dense capillary network; blood arriving is low in O₂ and high in CO₂, and is constantly carried away → **steep concentration gradients** are maintained.
- **Ventilation** keeps refreshing the air → also maintains the gradients.
- **Moist lining** — gases dissolve before diffusing.

**Smoking and its consequences:**

- **Tar** — a carcinogen: causes **lung cancer** (and cancers of the mouth and throat); it also **paralyses the cilia** that sweep mucus out of the airways, so mucus and dirt build up, causing coughing and infection (**bronchitis**).
- **Emphysema** — smoke damages the alveolar walls, which break down into larger sacs: **less surface area**, so less gas exchange; sufferers are breathless.
- **Carbon monoxide** — binds **irreversibly to haemoglobin** in place of oxygen, reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity; the heart works harder; in pregnancy the fetus receives less oxygen, leading to low birth weight.
- **Nicotine** — addictive; raises heart rate and blood pressure and narrows blood vessels, increasing the risk of **coronary heart disease** and stroke.

**Required practical** — the effect of exercise on breathing rate: count breaths per minute at rest, then after exercise, and at intervals during recovery. Breathing rate and depth rise to supply more oxygen and remove CO₂ faster, and to repay any oxygen debt.

::: terms
- **Thorax**: the chest cavity, containing the lungs and heart, protected by the ribs.
- **Trachea**: the windpipe, held open by rings of cartilage.
- **Bronchi**: the two tubes that branch from the trachea into each lung.
- **Bronchioles**: the small tubes that branch from the bronchi and end in alveoli.
- **Alveoli**: tiny air sacs where gas exchange takes place.
- **Intercostal muscles**: muscles between the ribs that move the ribcage during breathing.
- **Diaphragm**: the sheet of muscle under the lungs that contracts and flattens when breathing in.
- **Ventilation**: moving air into and out of the lungs.
- **Pleural membranes**: the membranes surrounding the lungs, with fluid between them to reduce friction.
- **Emphysema**: a disease in which the alveolar walls break down, reducing the surface area for gas exchange.
- **Bronchitis**: inflammation of the bronchi, with excess mucus and coughing, often caused by smoking.
:::

::: method
1. **Ventilation answers must follow the chain**: muscle action → change in thorax volume → change in pressure → air moves down the pressure gradient. Missing the pressure step loses the mark.
2. **Alveoli adaptations**: surface area, thin walls, blood supply, ventilation — each with the reason it speeds diffusion.
3. **Gas exchange vs ventilation vs respiration**: three different things — diffusion at the alveoli; moving air; releasing energy in cells.
4. **Smoking**: name the chemical, then the effect, then the disease — tar → cilia paralysed → bronchitis; tar → cancer; damage → emphysema; CO → haemoglobin; nicotine → heart.
5. **Exercise**: breathing rate rises to supply oxygen for faster respiration and remove CO₂; heart rate rises for the same reason.
:::

::: example Ventilation
Explain how air is drawn into the lungs when a person breathes in. (4)

The external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs up and out (1), and the diaphragm contracts and flattens (1). The volume of the thorax increases (1), so the pressure inside falls below atmospheric pressure and air flows into the lungs down the pressure gradient (1).
:::

::: example Alveoli
Explain how the alveoli are adapted for efficient gas exchange. (4)

There are millions of alveoli, giving a very large surface area for diffusion (1). Their walls and the capillary walls are one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is short (1). They are surrounded by a dense network of capillaries; blood constantly removes oxygen and brings carbon dioxide, maintaining steep concentration gradients (1). Ventilation refreshes the air, keeping oxygen high and CO₂ low in the alveolus, and the moist lining lets gases dissolve (1).
:::

::: example Smoking
Explain how smoking can cause bronchitis and emphysema. (4)

Tar in smoke paralyses the cilia lining the airways (1), so mucus containing dust and bacteria is not swept away but builds up, causing coughing and infection — bronchitis (1). Smoke also damages the walls of the alveoli, which break down into fewer, larger sacs (1); the surface area for gas exchange falls, so less oxygen is absorbed and the person becomes breathless — emphysema (1).
:::

::: example Carbon monoxide
Explain why smoking during pregnancy can lead to a baby with low birth weight. (3)

Carbon monoxide in the smoke binds irreversibly to haemoglobin in the mother's red blood cells (1), so her blood carries less oxygen (1). Less oxygen crosses the placenta to the fetus, so its cells respire less and it grows more slowly (1).
:::

::: example Describe — the path of air
Describe the path taken by a molecule of oxygen from the nose to the blood. (4)

The air passes through the nasal cavity and down the trachea (1), which divides into two bronchi, one to each lung (1).

It then passes along the bronchioles into an alveolus (1).

Oxygen diffuses across the alveolus wall and the capillary wall into the blood, where it combines with haemoglobin in a red blood cell (1).
:::

::: example Practical — carbon dioxide in exhaled air
Describe an experiment to show that exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide than inhaled air. (3)

Set up two tubes of limewater connected so that breathing in draws air through one tube and breathing out bubbles air through the other (1). Breathe in and out through the mouthpiece several times (1). The limewater that exhaled air passes through turns milky much sooner, showing exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide (1).
:::

::: q
Name the structures air passes through from the trachea to the alveoli. (2)
---
Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli.
:::

::: q
Why does the trachea contain rings of cartilage? (1)
---
To hold it open so it does not collapse when pressure falls during inhalation.
:::

::: q
Describe what happens to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles when a person breathes out. (2)
---
The diaphragm relaxes and domes upwards (1); the external intercostal muscles relax so the ribs move down and in, reducing thorax volume and raising pressure (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between ventilation and gas exchange. (2)
---
Ventilation is moving air into and out of the lungs by changing the pressure in the thorax (1); gas exchange is the diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide between the alveoli and the blood (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the blood supply to the alveoli helps gas exchange. (2)
---
Blood arriving is low in oxygen and high in CO₂, and blood is constantly carried away (1), so steep concentration gradients are maintained for fast diffusion (1).
:::

::: q
Name the substance in cigarette smoke that causes cancer. (1)
---
Tar (a carcinogen).
:::

::: q
Explain the effect of nicotine on the circulatory system. (2)
---
It raises heart rate and blood pressure and narrows blood vessels (1), increasing the risk of coronary heart disease and stroke; it is also addictive (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why breathing rate increases during exercise. (3)
---
Muscles respire faster, needing more oxygen and producing more carbon dioxide (1); faster, deeper breathing supplies oxygen more quickly (1) and removes the extra CO₂ to prevent it lowering blood pH (1).
:::

::: q
What is the function of the pleural membranes? (1)
---
They surround the lungs and are lubricated so the lungs can expand and slide smoothly against the chest wall.
