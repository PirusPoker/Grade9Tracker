## What it is

Organisms detect changes (**stimuli**) and respond to them. In humans the **nervous system** coordinates fast responses: the **central nervous system (CNS)** — the **brain and spinal cord** — and the nerves that connect it to every part of the body.

**Receptors** detect stimuli (light — retina; sound — ear; touch, pressure, temperature — skin; chemicals — tongue, nose). **Effectors** carry out the response — **muscles** (contract) and **glands** (secrete). Information travels as **electrical impulses** along **neurones** (nerve cells):

- **Sensory neurone** — carries impulses from a receptor to the CNS.
- **Relay neurone** — in the CNS; connects sensory to motor neurones.
- **Motor neurone** — carries impulses from the CNS to an effector.

Neurones have a long **axon** insulated by a fatty **myelin sheath** (speeds the impulse), and **dendrites** to connect with other cells.

The general pathway: **stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → CNS → motor neurone → effector → response**. For a conscious action the brain processes the information and decides.

**The reflex arc** — a **reflex** is a fast, automatic, protective response that does not involve conscious thought (pulling a hand from a hot object, blinking, the knee jerk, pupil constriction). The impulse takes the shortest route through the **spinal cord** (or brain stem), bypassing the conscious brain:

**Synapses**: neurones do not touch — there is a tiny gap, the **synapse**. When an impulse arrives at the end of a neurone, it triggers the release of a chemical **neurotransmitter**, which **diffuses across the gap** and binds to receptors on the next neurone, starting a new impulse there. Synapses make sure impulses travel in one direction only and slow transmission slightly; drugs and toxins often act on them.

**The eye** — the receptor organ for light:

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| **Cornea** | transparent front; refracts (bends) light — does most of the focusing |
| **Iris** | coloured ring of muscle; controls the size of the **pupil** and so how much light enters |
| **Pupil** | the hole light passes through |
| **Lens** | changes shape to fine-focus light onto the retina |
| **Ciliary muscles** and **suspensory ligaments** | change the shape of the lens |
| **Retina** | layer of light-sensitive receptor cells (**rods** for dim light, **cones** for colour); light triggers impulses |
| **Fovea** | part of the retina with the most cones — sharpest vision |
| **Optic nerve** | carries impulses from the retina to the brain |
| **Blind spot** | where the optic nerve leaves — no receptors |
| **Sclera** | tough white outer coat, protects |
| **Choroid** | dark layer, absorbs stray light |

**The pupil reflex**: in bright light, **circular** iris muscles contract and radial muscles relax → pupil **constricts** → less light enters, protecting the retina. In dim light, **radial** muscles contract → pupil **dilates** → more light enters.

**Accommodation** — focusing on near and distant objects by changing the lens shape:

- **Near object**: **ciliary muscles contract** → **suspensory ligaments slacken** → the lens, no longer pulled, becomes **fatter (more rounded)** → refracts light more strongly.
- **Distant object**: **ciliary muscles relax** → **suspensory ligaments are pulled tight** → the lens is pulled **thinner (flatter)** → refracts light less.

::: terms
- **Central nervous system (CNS)**: the brain and spinal cord.
- **Neurone**: a nerve cell that carries electrical impulses.
- **Sensory neurone**: carries impulses from a receptor to the CNS.
- **Relay neurone**: carries impulses within the CNS, from the sensory to the motor neurone.
- **Motor neurone**: carries impulses from the CNS to an effector.
- **Synapse**: the gap between two neurones, crossed by a neurotransmitter chemical.
- **Neurotransmitter**: a chemical released at a synapse that diffuses across the gap to start an impulse in the next neurone.
- **Reflex action**: a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus that does not involve the conscious brain.
- **Reflex arc**: the pathway of a reflex: receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector.
- **Cornea**: the transparent front of the eye that refracts light.
- **Iris**: the coloured ring of muscle that controls the size of the pupil and so the amount of light entering the eye.
- **Lens**: refracts light to focus it on the retina.
- **Retina**: the layer of light-sensitive cells (rods and cones) at the back of the eye.
- **Ciliary muscles**: muscles that change the shape of the lens to focus.
- **Accommodation**: changing the shape of the lens to focus on near or distant objects.
:::

::: method
1. **Pathway in order**, with the three neurones named and the CNS in the middle. Receptors *detect*; effectors *respond*; neurones *carry impulses*.
2. **Reflex arc**: say it is fast and automatic and does not involve conscious thought, then trace the arc; say why speed matters (protection from damage).
3. **Synapse**: impulse arrives → neurotransmitter released → diffuses across the gap → binds to receptors → new impulse. Chemical across the gap, electrical along the neurone.
4. **Eye**: for any part, give its function in one phrase; for the pupil reflex and accommodation, give the muscle action and the result.
5. **Accommodation logic**: muscle contracts → ligaments slack → lens fat (near). Muscle relaxes → ligaments tight → lens thin (far). Work through the ligaments, not straight from muscle to lens.
:::

::: example Reflex arc
Describe the pathway of a reflex arc when a person touches a hot object. (4)

Temperature receptors in the skin detect the stimulus and generate an impulse in a sensory neurone (1), which carries it to the spinal cord (1). At a synapse the impulse passes to a relay neurone and then to a motor neurone (1), which carries it to the effector — the arm muscle — which contracts to pull the hand away (1).
:::

::: example Why reflexes matter
Explain why reflex actions are fast and why this is important. (2)

The impulse passes through the spinal cord by the shortest route, without being processed by the conscious brain (1); the speed limits damage — the hand is removed before it is badly burned (1).
:::

::: example Synapse
Explain how an impulse crosses a synapse. (3)

When the impulse reaches the end of the first neurone, it causes a neurotransmitter to be released into the gap (1). The neurotransmitter diffuses across the synapse (1) and binds to receptor molecules on the next neurone, which triggers a new impulse in it (1).
:::

::: example Accommodation
Explain how the eye focuses on a near object. (3)

The ciliary muscles contract (1), which slackens the suspensory ligaments (1); the lens is no longer pulled thin, so it becomes fatter and refracts light more strongly, focusing it on the retina (1).
:::

::: example Explain — the pupil in bright light
Explain how the eye responds when a person walks from a dark room into bright sunlight. (3)

The retina detects the bright light, and a reflex is triggered (1). The circular muscles of the iris contract and the radial muscles relax (1), so the pupil gets smaller, letting less light into the eye and protecting the retina from damage (1).
:::

Describe what happens to the pupil in bright light and why. (2)

::: q
What is the function of the retina? (1)
---
It contains light-sensitive cells (rods and cones) that convert light into nerve impulses.
:::

::: q
Explain how the eye focuses on a distant object. (3)
---
Ciliary muscles relax (1), the suspensory ligaments are pulled tight (1) and stretch the lens thin, so it refracts light less (1).
:::

::: q
Why do we not see with the part of the retina where the optic nerve leaves? (1)
---
There are no light receptors there — the blind spot.
:::

::: q
Why is the synapse said to control the direction of impulses? (1)
---
Neurotransmitter is released only from one side and receptors are only on the other, so impulses cross one way.
:::
