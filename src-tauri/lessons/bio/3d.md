## What it is

Inheritance is worked out with a small set of exact terms:

- **Gene** — a section of DNA coding for a characteristic (protein).
- **Allele** — one version of a gene. Each body cell has two alleles of every gene, one from each parent, because chromosomes come in pairs.
- **Dominant** allele — always expressed in the phenotype if present; shown with a **capital letter** (B).
- **Recessive** allele — expressed only if **both** alleles are recessive; shown with a **lower-case letter** (b).
- **Homozygous** — two identical alleles (BB or bb). **Heterozygous** — two different alleles (Bb).
- **Genotype** — the alleles an organism has (Bb). **Phenotype** — the observable characteristic that results (brown eyes).
- **Codominance** — both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote (neither is dominant); written with superscripts or two capitals: red ($C^RC^R$) × white ($C^WC^W$) → pink ($C^RC^W$). Human blood groups: $I^A$ and $I^B$ are codominant (AB), both dominant to $I^O$.

**Monohybrid crosses** — one gene, two alleles. Method: state the parents' genotypes → gametes (each gamete carries one allele) → **Punnett square** → offspring genotypes → phenotypes → ratio or probability.

**Cross 1: two heterozygotes, Bb × Bb** (brown eyes B dominant to blue b):

| | **B** | **b** |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | BB (brown) | Bb (brown) |
| **b** | Bb (brown) | bb (blue) |

Genotypes 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb; phenotypes **3 brown : 1 blue**; probability of blue $= \frac14 = 25\%$.

**Cross 2: heterozygote × homozygous recessive, Bb × bb** (a test cross):

| | **B** | **b** |
|---|---|---|
| **b** | Bb (brown) | bb (blue) |
| **b** | Bb (brown) | bb (blue) |

Phenotypes **1 brown : 1 blue** — 50% each.

**Cross 3: BB × bb** → all Bb, all brown (100%). This is how a hidden recessive allele appears in a family: two brown-eyed Bb parents can have a blue-eyed child.

Ratios are **probabilities**, not guarantees: with four children, a 3:1 ratio is expected on average, but every child has an independent $\frac14$ chance. Large numbers of offspring (peas, fruit flies) come close to the ratio; small families often do not.

**Sex determination**: the 23rd pair of chromosomes. Females are **XX**, males are **XY**. All eggs carry an X; half the sperm carry X and half Y, so the sperm decides the sex:

| | **X** (egg) | **X** (egg) |
|---|---|---|
| **X** (sperm) | XX (female) | XX (female) |
| **Y** (sperm) | XY (male) | XY (male) |

Ratio **1 female : 1 male** — 50% each pregnancy, regardless of previous children.

**Pedigree (family tree) diagrams**: squares are males, circles females; shaded shapes show the characteristic; a horizontal line joins parents, a vertical line drops to their children. To interpret: if two **unaffected** parents have an **affected** child, the characteristic is **recessive** and both parents are heterozygous carriers. If it is dominant, every affected person has at least one affected parent. Work out genotypes by starting from anyone showing the recessive phenotype (must be homozygous recessive) and working outwards — each of their parents must carry at least one recessive allele.

::: method
1. **Always show the working**: parents' phenotypes and genotypes, gametes, a labelled Punnett square, offspring genotypes, phenotypes, then the ratio or probability. A bare "3:1" scores almost nothing.
2. **Choose letters that are clearly different** in upper and lower case (B/b, T/t — avoid C/c or S/s).
3. **State ratios as phenotypes** unless asked for genotypes; give probability as a fraction, percentage or "1 in 4".
4. **Codominance**: use symbols with superscripts; the heterozygote has a *third* phenotype; ratios become 1:2:1.
5. **Pedigrees**: find an affected individual with unaffected parents → recessive → parents are carriers. Then assign genotypes step by step; write "B?" where an allele cannot be determined.
6. **Sex**: draw the XX × XY square; 50:50; the sperm determines sex.
:::

::: example Heterozygous cross
In pea plants, tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t). Two heterozygous tall plants are crossed. Use a genetic diagram to find the expected ratio of tall to dwarf offspring. (4)

Parents Tt × Tt (1); gametes T or t from each (1).

| | **T** | **t** |
|---|---|---|
| **T** | TT | Tt |
| **t** | Tt | tt |

Offspring TT, Tt, Tt (tall), tt (dwarf) (1) — ratio 3 tall : 1 dwarf (1).
:::

::: example Test cross
A tall pea plant of unknown genotype is crossed with a dwarf plant. Half the offspring are dwarf. What was the genotype of the tall parent? Explain. (3)

Tt (1). A dwarf parent is tt and gives only t gametes (1); for any offspring to be dwarf (tt) the tall parent must have contributed a t allele, so it must be heterozygous — and a 1:1 ratio is exactly what Tt × tt predicts (1).
:::

::: example Codominance
In snapdragons the alleles for red ($C^R$) and white ($C^W$) flowers are codominant. Two pink plants are crossed. Give the expected phenotype ratio. (3)

Parents $C^RC^W$ × $C^RC^W$; gametes $C^R$ or $C^W$ (1).

| | **$C^R$** | **$C^W$** |
|---|---|---|
| **$C^R$** | $C^RC^R$ red | $C^RC^W$ pink |
| **$C^W$** | $C^RC^W$ pink | $C^WC^W$ white |

Ratio 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white (2).
:::

::: example Pedigree
In a family tree, two unaffected parents have a child with cystic fibrosis. State whether the allele for cystic fibrosis is dominant or recessive, and give the genotypes of the parents and the affected child. Use F/f. (3)

Recessive — the child shows the condition although neither parent does (1). Both parents must be heterozygous carriers, Ff (1); the affected child is ff (1).
:::

::: q
Define allele, genotype and phenotype. (3)
---
Allele — one version of a gene; genotype — the alleles an organism has; phenotype — the observable characteristic produced.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between homozygous and heterozygous. (2)
---
Homozygous — both alleles of the gene are the same, e.g. BB or bb (1); heterozygous — the two alleles are different, e.g. Bb (1).
:::

::: q
A heterozygous brown-eyed man (Bb) and a blue-eyed woman (bb) have a child. What is the probability the child has blue eyes? Show your working. (3)
---
Gametes: man B or b; woman b only. Offspring Bb, Bb, bb, bb (2). Probability of blue $= \frac{2}{4} = 50\%$ (1).
:::

::: q
Two parents are both Bb. They already have three brown-eyed children. What is the chance the fourth child has blue eyes? (1)
---
Still $\frac14$ (25%) — each child is an independent event.
:::

::: q
Explain why a recessive characteristic can appear in a child of two parents who do not show it. (2)
---
Both parents are heterozygous, carrying one recessive allele hidden by the dominant one (1); the child inherits the recessive allele from each parent and is homozygous recessive (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the sex of a baby is determined. (2)
---
All eggs carry an X chromosome; half of sperm carry X and half Y (1); an X sperm gives XX (female), a Y sperm gives XY (male), so the sperm determines the sex, with a 50% chance of each (1).
:::

::: q
Blood group alleles $I^A$ and $I^B$ are codominant, and both are dominant to $I^O$. Give the possible blood groups of children of an $I^AI^O$ mother and an $I^BI^O$ father. (2)
---
$I^AI^B$ (AB), $I^AI^O$ (A), $I^BI^O$ (B), $I^OI^O$ (O) — all four groups, each with probability $\frac14$.
:::

::: q
In a pedigree, every affected person has at least one affected parent, and affected parents sometimes have unaffected children. Is the allele dominant or recessive? Explain. (2)
---
Dominant (1): an affected person needs only one copy, so must have inherited it from an affected parent; heterozygous affected parents can pass on the normal allele, giving unaffected children (1).
:::

::: q
Why do observed ratios in small families often differ from the predicted 3:1? (1)
---
Fertilisation is random, so with few offspring chance causes the numbers to vary from the expected probability.
:::

::: watch
- **Show the whole diagram**: genotypes, gametes, Punnett square, offspring, ratio. The ratio alone is nearly worthless.
- Ratios are **probabilities** for each offspring, not a guarantee across a family.
- **Capital = dominant, lower-case = recessive**; pick letters that look different.
- Codominance gives **three phenotypes** and a **1:2:1** ratio; do not call it "incomplete dominance".
- Pedigrees: unaffected parents + affected child = **recessive**, parents are **carriers**.
:::

::: test
In pea plants, tall (T) is dominant to short (t). A heterozygous tall plant is crossed with a short plant. Draw a Punnett square and give the expected ratio of phenotypes. (3)
---
Parents Tt × tt; gametes T, t and t, t (1); offspring Tt, Tt, tt, tt (1); 1 tall : 1 short (1).
:::

::: test
Two heterozygous tall plants are crossed. Give the genotype ratio and the phenotype ratio of the offspring. (2)
---
1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (1); 3 tall : 1 short (1).
:::

::: test
Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele. Two healthy parents have a child with cystic fibrosis. Give the parents' genotypes and the probability that their next child is affected. (3)
---
Both parents heterozygous, Ff (1); Punnett square gives FF, Ff, Ff, ff (1); probability 1 in 4 = 25% (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a woman with blood group AB and a man with blood group O cannot have a child with blood group O. (2)
---
The woman must pass on either $I^A$ or $I^B$ (1); every child receives one of these dominant alleles and so is group A or B (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between a dominant and a recessive allele. (2)
---
A dominant allele shows its effect even when only one copy is present (1); a recessive allele only shows its effect when two copies are present (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the probability of a couple having a boy is 50% each time, whatever they have had before. (2)
---
Half the father's sperm carry X and half carry Y, and which fertilises the egg is random (1); each fertilisation is an independent event, unaffected by earlier children (1).
:::

::: test
What does it mean for a pedigree diagram to show that a condition 'skips a generation', and what does this suggest about the allele? (2)
---
Affected individuals have unaffected parents (1); the condition is recessive — the parents are carriers (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a test cross (crossing with a homozygous recessive) shows whether a dominant-phenotype organism is homozygous or heterozygous. (2)
---
If it is homozygous dominant all offspring show the dominant phenotype (1); if heterozygous, about half the offspring show the recessive phenotype (1).
:::
