## What it is

Ecology studies organisms in their environment. The vocabulary must be exact:

- **Population** — all the organisms of **one species** living in a particular area at the same time (the rabbits in a wood).
- **Community** — all the **populations of different species** living together in an area (the rabbits, grass, foxes, oak trees of the wood).
- **Habitat** — the **place** where an organism lives (a pond, the bark of a tree).
- **Ecosystem** — a **community together with the non-living parts** of its environment, interacting as a system (the wood, including its soil, water and climate).
- **Biodiversity** — the **variety of different species** (and the genetic variety within them) in an area. High biodiversity makes ecosystems more stable.

**Factors affecting population size:**

**Abiotic** (non-living) factors: **temperature**, **light intensity**, **water/moisture**, **soil pH and mineral content**, **oxygen and carbon dioxide levels**, wind, salinity. Each species tolerates a range; outside it the population falls (few plants grow in deep shade; fish die in water with little oxygen).

**Biotic** (living) factors: **food availability**, **predation**, **competition** (between species and within a species for food, water, light, space, mates), **disease and parasites**, the arrival of a new species. Predator and prey numbers rise and fall in linked cycles: more prey → more predators → fewer prey → fewer predators.

**Investigating populations** — required practical:

- **Quadrats** — square frames (often 0.25 m² or 1 m²) placed **randomly** in the area (use random coordinates, not chosen spots, to avoid bias); count the organisms of the species in each; repeat many times (at least 10) and calculate the **mean per quadrat**; then **scale up**: population estimate $= \text{mean number per quadrat} \times \dfrac{\text{total area}}{\text{area of one quadrat}}$. Used for plants and slow-moving animals.
- **Transects** — a line (tape) across an area where conditions change (from shade into sunlight, up a shore); quadrats are placed at regular intervals along it to see **how the distribution changes** with an abiotic factor, which is measured at each point.
- Also: pitfall traps, sweep nets, pooters for small animals; measuring abiotic factors with a light meter, thermometer, pH probe.

Sources of error: too few quadrats; non-random placement; counting partially covered organisms inconsistently; the area not being uniform.

::: method
1. **Definitions are exact**: population = one species; community = all species; ecosystem = community + physical environment. A "community" answer that mentions only one species loses the mark.
2. **Abiotic vs biotic**: abiotic = physical/non-living; biotic = caused by other organisms. Give a factor *and* how it changes the population.
3. **Predator–prey**: describe the cycle with the lag — prey rise first, predators follow.
4. **Quadrat calculations**: mean per quadrat → multiply by (total area ÷ quadrat area). Show the scaling. Give reasons for random placement and repeats.
5. **Transect vs quadrat**: random quadrats estimate a population; a transect shows a change in distribution along a gradient.
:::

::: example Define
Define "population" and "community". (2)

A population is all the organisms of one species living in a particular area (1). A community is all the populations of different species living and interacting in that area (1).
:::

::: example Factors
Describe one abiotic and one biotic factor that could reduce the population of frogs in a pond. (2)

Abiotic: a fall in the oxygen concentration of the water, e.g. from pollution, so tadpoles cannot respire enough and die (1). Biotic: an increase in the population of herons that eat frogs, or a disease spreading through the frogs (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — quadrats
A student placed ten 0.25 m² quadrats at random in a field of area 2,000 m² and counted daisies: 3, 5, 0, 4, 6, 2, 5, 3, 4, 8. Estimate the number of daisies in the field. (3)

Total counted $= 40$; mean per quadrat $= 40 \div 10 = 4$ (1). Number of quadrats in the field $= 2{,}000 \div 0.25 = 8{,}000$ (1). Estimate $= 4 \times 8{,}000 = 32{,}000$ daisies (1).
:::

::: example Method
Describe how you would investigate whether the distribution of a plant species changes from a shaded area into an open field. (4)

Lay a tape measure (transect) from the shaded area into the open field (1). Place a quadrat at regular intervals, e.g. every 2 m, along the tape and count or estimate the percentage cover of the species in each (1). At each quadrat measure the light intensity with a light meter (1). Plot the abundance of the species against distance and light intensity to see whether the distribution is linked to light (1).
:::

::: q
Define "ecosystem". (2)
---
A community of organisms (1) together with the non-living (abiotic) parts of their environment, interacting as a system (1).
:::

::: q
What is meant by biodiversity, and why is high biodiversity valuable? (2)
---
The variety of different species in an area (1); ecosystems with many species are more stable, since the loss of one species is less likely to collapse the food web (1).
:::

::: q
Give three abiotic factors that affect where plants grow. (3)
---
Light intensity; temperature; water availability (or soil pH, mineral content).
:::

::: q
Explain how competition can limit a population. (2)
---
Individuals compete for limited resources such as food, water or space (1); those that lose get less and die or fail to reproduce, so the population cannot grow beyond what the resources support (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how the numbers of a predator and its prey change over time. (2)
---
When prey are plentiful the predator population rises; more predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall (1); with less food, predator numbers fall, letting the prey recover, and the cycle repeats with the predator lagging behind (1).
:::

::: q
Why must quadrats be placed randomly? (1)
---
To avoid bias — choosing spots (e.g. where plants are obvious) would give an unrepresentative sample.
:::

::: q
Why is it important to use many quadrats rather than one? (1)
---
Many quadrats give a mean that is more representative of the whole area and reduces the effect of chance.
:::

::: q
Five 1 m² quadrats contain 12, 8, 10, 15 and 5 snails. Estimate the number of snails in a 300 m² garden. (2)
---
Mean $= 50 \div 5 = 10$ per m² (1); $10 \times 300 = 3{,}000$ snails (1).
:::

::: q
When would a transect be used rather than random quadrats? (1)
---
When investigating how the distribution of organisms changes across an area along an environmental gradient, such as from a pond edge outwards.
:::

::: watch
- **Population = one species; community = all species; ecosystem includes the non-living environment.** Word-perfect.
- Abiotic = **non-living** physical factors; biotic = **other organisms**. Say how the factor changes numbers.
- Quadrats: **random**, **many**, then **mean × (total area ÷ quadrat area)** — show the scaling.
- Transect = along a **line** to show a **change in distribution** with a measured abiotic factor.
- Predator–prey cycles have a **lag** — predators follow prey.
:::

::: test
Define population, community and habitat. (3)
---
Population: all the organisms of one species in an area (1); community: all the populations of different species in an area (1); habitat: the place where an organism lives (1).
:::

::: test
A student uses 10 quadrats of 0.25 m² on a field of 2000 m² and finds a total of 40 daisy plants. Estimate the number of daisies in the field. (3)
---
Area sampled = 2.5 m² (1); density = 40 ÷ 2.5 = 16 per m² (1); 16 × 2000 = 32 000 daisies (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how you would use a belt transect to investigate the change in plant species from a woodland edge into a field. (3)
---
Lay a tape from the woodland edge across the field (1); place a quadrat at regular intervals along it and record the species and their numbers or percentage cover in each (1); also record abiotic factors such as light intensity at each point to relate to the changes (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how light intensity and soil pH can affect the distribution of plants. (2)
---
Light: shade-tolerant plants grow under trees while others need open ground for photosynthesis (1); pH: some species only grow in acidic or alkaline soils because it affects mineral availability and enzymes (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why the population of a predator usually rises and falls after the population of its prey. (2)
---
More prey means more food, so more predators survive and breed — with a delay (1); more predators then reduce the prey, so predators later starve and fall, letting the prey recover (1).
:::

::: test
Give two reasons why a population of rabbits in a field might stop increasing. (2)
---
Any two of: food becomes limiting; predators increase; disease spreads; space or shelter runs out (2).
:::

::: test
Explain why random sampling gives a fairer estimate than choosing where to put quadrats. (1)
---
It avoids bias — the sampler cannot consciously or unconsciously pick areas with more or fewer organisms.
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between biotic and abiotic factors, with two examples of each. (2)
---
Biotic: living factors, e.g. predators, competition, disease (1); abiotic: non-living, e.g. temperature, light, water, pH (1).
:::
