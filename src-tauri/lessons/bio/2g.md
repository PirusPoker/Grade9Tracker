## What it is

Plants exchange two gases with the air: **carbon dioxide** and **oxygen**. Both **photosynthesis** (uses CO₂, releases O₂) and **respiration** (uses O₂, releases CO₂) go on in a leaf — respiration all the time, photosynthesis only in light. The gas the leaf *appears* to take in or give out is the **net** result of the two.

- **Bright light (day)**: photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so the leaf takes in CO₂ and gives out O₂ overall. The CO₂ from respiration is used up inside the leaf before it escapes.
- **Darkness (night)**: no photosynthesis; respiration continues, so the leaf takes in O₂ and gives out CO₂.
- **Dim light** (dawn, dusk): at one light intensity the rate of photosynthesis exactly equals the rate of respiration — the **compensation point** — and there is no net gas exchange.

The common error is to say plants only respire at night. They respire **all the time**; at night it is simply the only process running.

**How gases move**: by **diffusion** through the **stomata** — pores mostly in the lower epidermis, each surrounded by two **guard cells**. When guard cells take in water they become turgid and curve apart, opening the stoma; when they lose water they go flaccid and the stoma closes. Stomata usually open in the light (to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis) and close at night and in drought (to reduce water loss).

**How the leaf is adapted for gas exchange:**

- **Stomata** — many pores for gases to enter and leave; guard cells control them.
- **Air spaces in the spongy mesophyll** — gases diffuse freely to reach every cell; the spaces give the cells a large surface area in contact with air.
- **Thin leaf** — short diffusion distance from stoma to any cell.
- **Large surface area** of the flat leaf — many stomata and much exposure to air.
- **Moist cell surfaces** — gases dissolve before diffusing into cells.

**Required practical — the effect of light on net gas exchange**: use **hydrogencarbonate indicator**, which changes colour with the CO₂ concentration of the air around it: **red** at normal atmospheric CO₂, **yellow** when CO₂ rises, **purple** when CO₂ falls. Set up sealed boiling tubes each containing indicator and a leaf: one in **bright light**, one in **darkness** (foil-covered), one in **dim light**, and a control with no leaf. Results: bright light → **purple** (net CO₂ uptake — photosynthesis exceeds respiration); dark → **yellow** (only respiration, CO₂ released); dim light → stays **red** (compensation point, no net change); control → red (shows the changes are due to the leaf). Keep temperature and tube size the same.

::: terms
- **Gas exchange**: the movement of oxygen and carbon dioxide between an organism and its surroundings by diffusion.
- **Stomata**: pores in the leaf epidermis through which gases diffuse in and out.
- **Guard cells**: pairs of cells around each stoma that open it when turgid and close it when flaccid.
- **Net gas exchange**: the overall movement of gases, which depends on the balance between photosynthesis and respiration.
- **Compensation point**: the light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration, so there is no net gas exchange.
- **Hydrogencarbonate indicator**: an indicator that is red at normal carbon dioxide levels, yellow when carbon dioxide rises, and purple when it falls.
:::

::: method
1. **Always say both processes happen in light** and that the observed exchange is the **net** result. Then give the direction of net movement for light, dark and dim.
2. **Compensation point**: define it (photosynthesis rate = respiration rate) and say the indicator stays red.
3. **Stomata mechanism**: guard cells take in water → turgid → curve apart → stoma opens; reverse to close.
4. **Adaptations**: feature + how it helps diffusion (surface area, distance, gradient).
5. **Indicator practical**: know the three colours and what each means about CO₂, and include the no-leaf control.
:::

::: example Explain — net exchange
Explain why a leaf in bright light gives out oxygen but the same leaf in darkness gives out carbon dioxide. (4)

In bright light the leaf photosynthesises faster than it respires (1); photosynthesis produces more oxygen than respiration uses and uses more carbon dioxide than respiration makes, so the net exchange is oxygen out, CO₂ in (1). In darkness there is no photosynthesis (1), but respiration continues, so the leaf takes in oxygen and releases carbon dioxide (1).
:::

::: example Practical
A student placed leaves in tubes of hydrogencarbonate indicator in bright light, dim light and darkness. Predict the colour in each and explain the dim-light result. (4)

Bright light: purple, because net photosynthesis removes CO₂ from the air in the tube (1). Darkness: yellow, because respiration alone releases CO₂ (1). Dim light: red — unchanged (1), because the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration, the compensation point, so there is no net change in CO₂ (1).
:::

::: example Adaptations
Explain two ways the leaf is adapted for gas exchange. (2)

Air spaces in the spongy mesophyll allow gases to diffuse quickly to every cell and give a large surface area for exchange (1). Stomata provide pores through which carbon dioxide and oxygen can diffuse between the air and the leaf's air spaces (1).
:::

::: example Stomata
Describe how guard cells open a stoma, and suggest why stomata close at night. (3)

Guard cells absorb water by osmosis, become turgid and curve outwards, opening the pore between them (1). At night no CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis (1), so closing the stomata reduces water loss by transpiration without any cost (1).
:::

::: q
Name the two gases exchanged by a leaf and the process that moves them. (2)
---
Carbon dioxide and oxygen (1); diffusion (1).
:::

::: q
Do plants respire during the day? Explain. (2)
---
Yes — respiration goes on in all cells all the time (1); during the day it is masked because photosynthesis is faster, so the net exchange is CO₂ in and O₂ out (1).
:::

::: q
Define the compensation point. (1)
---
The light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration, so there is no net gas exchange.
:::

::: q
What colour is hydrogencarbonate indicator in air with a high CO₂ concentration, and with a low concentration? (2)
---
Yellow (high CO₂) (1); purple (low CO₂) (1).
:::

::: q
Why is a tube with indicator but no leaf included in the light experiment? (1)
---
As a control, to show that any colour change is caused by the leaf and not by light or the apparatus.
:::

::: q
Where are most stomata found, and why? (2)
---
On the lower epidermis (1), where they are shaded and cooler, reducing water loss (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a leaf's thinness helps gas exchange. (1)
---
It gives a short diffusion distance between the stomata and every cell.
:::

::: q
A plant in a sealed jar in bright light survives for weeks. Explain how it obtains oxygen and carbon dioxide. (2)
---
Its photosynthesis produces oxygen, which it uses in respiration (1); its respiration produces carbon dioxide, which it uses in photosynthesis (1).
:::

::: watch
- Plants **respire all the time** — day and night. "Only respire at night" is the error the examiners are waiting for.
- Gas exchange observed is the **net** of photosynthesis and respiration — use the word "net".
- Indicator colours: **red normal, yellow more CO₂, purple less CO₂** — and the **control** tube.
- **Compensation point**: rates equal, no net exchange, indicator stays red.
- Stomata open because guard cells become **turgid** — say the mechanism.
:::

::: test
Describe an experiment using hydrogencarbonate indicator to show the effect of light on gas exchange in leaves. (4)
---
Place a leaf in each of three tubes containing the indicator; cover one tube with foil, one with gauze (dim light) and leave one in bright light; include a tube with no leaf as a control (1); leave for an hour (1); the dark tube turns yellow — respiration only, carbon dioxide released (1); the bright tube turns purple — photosynthesis exceeds respiration, carbon dioxide removed; the dim tube may stay red — the two balance (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what is happening at the compensation point. (2)
---
The rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration (1); no net uptake or release of carbon dioxide or oxygen (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how gases move between the air and the cells inside a leaf. (3)
---
Gases enter and leave through the stomata (1); they diffuse through the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll (1); and dissolve into the film of moisture on the cell surfaces to enter cells (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the stomata open and close. (2)
---
Guard cells take in water and become turgid, and their uneven walls bend them apart to open the pore (1); when they lose water they become flaccid and the pore closes (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why stomata usually close at night. (2)
---
No photosynthesis occurs, so carbon dioxide is not needed (1); closing reduces water loss (1).
:::

::: test
Give two structural features of a leaf that help gas exchange. (2)
---
Thin, giving short diffusion distances (1); air spaces between spongy mesophyll cells and many stomata (1).
:::

::: test
At dawn, a leaf's net carbon dioxide exchange is zero. Explain what this means for the leaf and what happens as the light gets brighter. (2)
---
It is at the compensation point — photosynthesis and respiration balanced (1); as light increases photosynthesis exceeds respiration and the leaf takes in carbon dioxide overall (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why hydrogencarbonate indicator, not limewater, is used in leaf gas-exchange experiments. (1)
---
It shows small changes in carbon dioxide both up and down (purple or yellow from red), whereas limewater only detects an increase.
:::
