## What it is

Living things are built from three main types of large molecule, all based on carbon:

| Molecule | Elements | Building blocks (monomers) | Examples / role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | C, H, O | **simple sugars** (glucose) — starch and glycogen are long chains of glucose | energy source (glucose), energy store (starch in plants, glycogen in animals), structure (cellulose) |
| **Proteins** | C, H, O, **N** (sometimes S) | **amino acids** — chains folded into a specific 3-D shape | enzymes, antibodies, haemoglobin, muscle, growth and repair |
| **Lipids** (fats and oils) | C, H, O | **fatty acids and glycerol** (three fatty acids joined to one glycerol) | energy store, insulation, cell membranes |

**Food tests** — you must know the reagent, the method and the colour change:

| Test for | Reagent | Method | Positive result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glucose** (reducing sugars) | **Benedict's solution** | add to sample, **heat in a water bath** at about 80 °C | blue → green → yellow → **brick-red** (the further the change, the more sugar) |
| **Starch** | **iodine solution** | add a few drops to the sample | orange-brown → **blue-black** |
| **Protein** | **Biuret solution** (copper sulfate + sodium hydroxide) | add to sample, shake | blue → **purple / lilac** |
| **Fat (lipid)** | **ethanol** | shake sample with ethanol, then pour into **water** | clear → **cloudy white emulsion** |

**Enzymes** are **biological catalysts** — proteins that speed up chemical reactions in living organisms without being used up. Each enzyme has an **active site** with a specific shape that fits only one **substrate** (the **lock-and-key** idea): the substrate binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex; the reaction happens; the products leave; the enzyme is unchanged and can be used again. Because the shape is specific, each enzyme catalyses one reaction (amylase breaks down starch, not protein).

**Temperature**: as temperature rises, molecules move faster, so substrate and active site collide more often → rate rises, up to an **optimum** (about 37 °C for human enzymes). Above the optimum the extra energy breaks the bonds holding the protein in shape — the **active site changes shape** so the substrate no longer fits: the enzyme is **denatured** and the rate falls quickly to zero. Denaturing is permanent. Enzymes are not "killed" — they were never alive.

**pH**: each enzyme has an optimum pH (pepsin in the stomach about pH 2; most others about pH 7). Away from the optimum the charges on the protein change, the active site's shape distorts and the rate falls; extremes of pH denature the enzyme.

**Required practical — the effect of temperature (or pH) on amylase**: put amylase and starch solutions in separate tubes in a water bath at the test temperature; mix; every 30 s take a drop and add it to iodine on a spotting tile; record the time until the iodine stays orange (all starch digested). Rate $= 1 \div$ time. Repeat at several temperatures (or with buffers at different pH). Control: same volumes and concentrations of enzyme and starch; same starting temperature. Sources of error: judging the end point by eye; drops taken only every 30 s.

::: terms
- **Carbohydrates**: molecules made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen — sugars, starch, glycogen, cellulose.
- **Proteins**: molecules made of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, built from amino acids.
- **Lipids**: fats and oils, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, built from fatty acids and glycerol.
- **Starch and glycogen**: large carbohydrates made from many glucose units — starch in plants, glycogen in animals.
- **Enzyme**: a protein that acts as a biological catalyst, speeding up reactions without being used up.
- **Catalyst**: a substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being changed itself.
- **Active site**: the part of an enzyme where the substrate binds.
- **Substrate**: the molecule an enzyme acts on.
- **Specificity**: each enzyme only acts on one substrate, because only that substrate fits its active site.
- **Optimum temperature / pH**: the temperature or pH at which an enzyme works fastest.
- **Denatured**: when the shape of an enzyme's active site changes permanently, so the substrate no longer fits and the enzyme stops working.
- **Benedict's test**: detects glucose (reducing sugars) — blue to brick-red when heated.
- **Iodine test**: detects starch — orange-brown to blue-black.
- **Biuret test**: detects protein — blue to purple.
- **Ethanol (emulsion) test**: detects fat — a cloudy white emulsion forms.
:::

::: method
1. **Molecules**: element list, then monomer. Proteins are the odd one out — they contain **nitrogen**.
2. **Food tests**: reagent → method (heat for Benedict's; ethanol then water for fat) → colour *from* and *to*. All three parts.
3. **Enzyme explanations always use the vocabulary**: active site, substrate, specific shape, complex, denatured. "Explain why rate falls above 40 °C" → bonds break → active site changes shape → substrate no longer fits → denatured.
4. **Graphs**: describe the two phases (rise, then fall) and give the optimum; explain each phase with collisions and denaturing.
5. **Practicals**: independent variable, dependent variable (time for starch to disappear → rate = 1/time), controlled variables, how the end point is judged, and one improvement.
:::

::: example Food test
Describe how you would test a food sample for protein and for fat, and state the positive results. (4)

Protein: add Biuret solution to the sample and shake (1); a purple or lilac colour shows protein is present (1). Fat: shake the sample with ethanol, then pour the mixture into water (1); a cloudy white emulsion shows fat is present (1).
:::

::: example Explain — temperature
Explain why the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction falls when the temperature rises above the optimum. (3)

The high temperature breaks the bonds holding the enzyme's protein in shape (1), so the active site changes shape (1) and the substrate no longer fits — the enzyme is denatured and cannot catalyse the reaction (1).
:::

::: example Explain — specificity
Explain why amylase breaks down starch but not protein. (3)

Amylase's active site has a specific shape (1) that is complementary to the starch molecule, so starch can bind and form an enzyme–substrate complex (1); protein molecules have a different shape and cannot fit the active site, so no reaction occurs (1).
:::

::: example Practical
A student investigated the effect of temperature on amylase. Describe the method and state one variable that must be controlled. (4)

Place amylase and starch solutions in separate tubes in a water bath at the test temperature for a few minutes, then mix them and start a timer (1). Every 30 seconds transfer a drop to iodine on a spotting tile; when the iodine stays orange-brown, starch has all been digested — record the time (1). Repeat at other temperatures and calculate rate as 1 ÷ time (1). Control the volume and concentration of both solutions (or the pH) so temperature is the only variable changed (1).
:::

::: example State — building blocks
State the small molecules that make up (a) starch, (b) proteins and (c) lipids. (3)

(a) Starch is made from many glucose molecules joined together (1).

(b) Proteins are made from amino acids (1).

(c) Lipids are made from fatty acids and glycerol (1).
:::

::: example Explain — pH and enzymes
Explain why pepsin works well in the stomach but stops working in the small intestine. (3)

Pepsin's optimum pH is about 2, matching the acid conditions of the stomach (1). In the small intestine the pH is alkaline (1), which changes the shape of pepsin's active site, so the protein substrate no longer fits and the enzyme is denatured (1).
:::

---

::: q

---

::: watch
- **Denatured, never "killed".** And say *what* denatures: the **active site changes shape** so the substrate no longer fits.
- Proteins contain **nitrogen**; carbohydrates and lipids do not.
- Benedict's needs **heat**; the fat test needs **ethanol then water**. Colour changes must be from → to.
- Rate rises because of **more frequent collisions**, not because "the enzyme works harder".
- Rate = **1 ÷ time**; a shorter time means a faster rate.
:::

::: test
Describe an investigation into the effect of pH on the activity of amylase. (6)
---
Any six: mix amylase with starch solution at a range of pH values using buffer solutions (e.g. pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8); keep the temperature constant in a water bath; keep volumes and concentrations of starch and amylase the same; every 30 seconds take a drop of the mixture and add to iodine on a spotting tile; record the time taken until the iodine no longer turns blue-black; calculate rate = 1 ÷ time; repeat and calculate a mean; the pH with the fastest rate is the optimum.
