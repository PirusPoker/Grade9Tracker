## What it is

Microorganisms — yeast, bacteria and fungi — are used to make food and drink, and, in industrial fermenters, medicines and enzymes.

**Yeast** (a single-celled fungus) respires **anaerobically** when oxygen is short: glucose → **ethanol + carbon dioxide** (+ energy) — **fermentation**.

- **Bread**: yeast mixed into dough respires the sugars in the flour; the **carbon dioxide** forms bubbles that make the dough **rise**; baking kills the yeast and evaporates the ethanol. Warmth speeds it up; salt and too much sugar slow it.
- **Beer and wine**: yeast ferments the sugar in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) in the absence of air; the **ethanol** is the alcohol; the CO₂ gives fizz or escapes. Fermentation stops when the alcohol concentration kills the yeast (about 15%) or the sugar runs out.

**Required practical — anaerobic respiration by yeast**: a yeast and glucose (or sugar) solution in a boiling tube, covered with a layer of oil to keep out oxygen, connected by a delivery tube to a tube of limewater (turns milky) or to a gas syringe or an inverted measuring cylinder over water. Count bubbles or measure the volume of CO₂ per minute at different temperatures in water baths: the rate rises to an optimum (about 35–40 °C) then falls as the yeast's enzymes denature. Control: same volume and concentration of yeast and sugar, same time; a control tube with boiled (dead) yeast produces no gas.

**Yoghurt**: milk is **pasteurised** (heated to kill other bacteria), cooled to about 40–45 °C and inoculated with the bacterium **Lactobacillus (bulgaricus)**. The bacteria respire anaerobically, converting the milk sugar **lactose into lactic acid**; the acid **lowers the pH**, which **denatures and coagulates the milk proteins**, thickening the milk into yoghurt and giving the sharp taste; the low pH also stops harmful bacteria growing, preserving it. Everything is kept sterile so only Lactobacillus grows.

**The industrial fermenter** — a large stainless-steel vessel in which microorganisms are grown to produce a product (penicillin from the fungus *Penicillium*, insulin from GM bacteria, enzymes for washing powder, mycoprotein, yoghurt in bulk). The exam wants the **reason** for each feature:

| Condition / feature | How it is controlled | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Aseptic (sterile) conditions** | vessel and nutrients steam-sterilised; air filtered | other microbes would compete for nutrients, contaminate the product or produce toxins |
| **Nutrients** | a nutrient medium (sugar, nitrogen source, minerals) added, topped up | for respiration and growth of the microorganism |
| **Temperature** | probe; **water-cooling jacket** (respiration releases heat) or heater | keep at the optimum for the microbe's enzymes — too hot denatures them, too cold slows growth |
| **pH** | probe; acid or alkali added automatically | keep at the optimum for the enzymes; products (e.g. lactic acid) change the pH |
| **Oxygen** | **sterile air** bubbled in from the bottom | for aerobic respiration (for aerobic microbes like *Penicillium*); excluded for anaerobic fermentation |
| **Agitation** | motor-driven **stirrer / paddles** | keeps microbes and nutrients evenly mixed, distributes oxygen and heat, stops settling |
| **Monitoring** | probes for temperature, pH, oxygen linked to control systems | conditions kept constant for maximum yield |

**Fish farming** — fish (salmon, trout, tilapia) grown in cages, tanks or ponds. To maximise growth and survival the farmer controls:

- **Water quality** — oxygen levels (aeration; flowing water), removal of **waste** (uneaten food, faeces, ammonia) by filtration or water flow, correct temperature and pH.
- **Feeding** — high-protein food in the **right quality and frequency**; small, frequent feeds reduce waste and ensure all fish feed.
- **Predation** — nets and cages exclude predators (**interspecific predation** — other species eating the fish); fish are **separated by age/size** to stop larger fish eating smaller ones (**intraspecific predation**); low stocking density reduces competition and aggression.
- **Disease** — fish kept close together spread parasites and disease quickly; controlled by low density, monitoring, removing sick fish, antibiotics and vaccines.
- **Selective breeding** — fish with fast growth and disease resistance are bred (5c).

Drawbacks: waste and uneaten food pollute the water outside cages (eutrophication); escaped farmed fish breed with wild ones; antibiotics and pesticides enter the sea; farmed carnivorous fish are fed on wild-caught fish.

1. **Bread vs alcohol**: same reaction, different product wanted — CO₂ for bread, ethanol for drinks. Say why baking has no alcohol.

::: example Bread and beer
Explain why yeast is used in both bread-making and brewing, but the products are different. (3)

In both, yeast respires anaerobically, converting sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide (1). In bread the carbon dioxide is the useful product — its bubbles make the dough rise — and the ethanol evaporates during baking (1). In brewing the ethanol is the useful product, so fermentation is carried out for longer in sealed vessels without air, and the CO₂ is allowed to escape or kept for fizz (1).
:::

::: example Yoghurt
Describe how yoghurt is made from milk. (4)

The milk is pasteurised by heating to kill any bacteria present, then cooled to about 40 °C (1). A culture of Lactobacillus bacteria is added (1). The bacteria ferment the lactose in the milk into lactic acid (1); the acid lowers the pH, which coagulates the milk proteins so the milk thickens into yoghurt, and the low pH prevents the growth of harmful microbes (1).
:::

::: example Fermenter
Explain why the temperature and pH inside an industrial fermenter are monitored and controlled. (4)

The microorganisms respire, releasing heat; without a cooling water jacket the temperature would rise above the optimum and denature the microbes' enzymes, so growth and product formation would stop (2). The microbes' waste products change the pH away from the optimum for their enzymes; probes detect this and acid or alkali is added automatically to keep the pH constant, so the rate of production stays at its maximum (2).
:::

::: example Fish farming
Explain how a fish farmer controls predation and disease to increase the yield of salmon. (4)

Cages or nets keep out predators such as seals and birds — interspecific predation (1). Fish are sorted by size and kept in separate cages so large fish do not eat smaller ones — intraspecific predation (1). Stocking density is kept moderate and the water kept clean and well oxygenated, so fish are less stressed and disease spreads more slowly (1). Fish are monitored, diseased fish removed, and vaccines or treatments used against parasites such as sea lice (1).
:::

::: q
Write the word equation for the reaction carried out by yeast in bread-making. (1)
---
Glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (+ energy).
:::

::: q
Why is a layer of oil placed on the yeast suspension in a fermentation experiment? (1)
---
To exclude oxygen so the yeast respires anaerobically.
:::

::: q
In the yeast experiment, why does the rate of CO₂ production fall at 60 °C? (2)
---
The yeast's enzymes are denatured at high temperature (1), so respiration stops and no more CO₂ is produced (1).
:::

::: q
Why is milk pasteurised before making yoghurt? (1)
---
To kill other bacteria that would compete with Lactobacillus or spoil the yoghurt.
:::

::: q
What causes yoghurt to be thick and sharp-tasting? (2)
---
Lactic acid produced by Lactobacillus (1) lowers the pH, coagulating the milk proteins and giving the sour taste (1).
:::

::: q
Why are aseptic conditions needed in a fermenter? (2)
---
To prevent other microorganisms entering (1), which would compete for nutrients, reduce the yield and could contaminate the product with toxins (1).
:::

::: q
Give two functions of the stirrer in a fermenter. (2)
---
Keeps the microorganisms and nutrients evenly mixed (1); distributes oxygen and heat evenly through the vessel (1).
:::

::: q
Why is sterile air bubbled through a fermenter producing penicillin? (2)
---
Penicillium respires aerobically, so it needs oxygen to grow and make penicillin (1); the air is filtered so it does not introduce contaminating microbes (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why removing waste from a fish tank increases the yield of fish. (2)
---
Faeces and uneaten food are decomposed by bacteria that use up oxygen and release toxic ammonia (1); removing waste keeps oxygen high and the water clean, so fish grow well and stay healthy (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between intraspecific and interspecific predation in fish farming. (2)
---
Intraspecific — larger fish of the same species eating smaller ones, prevented by sorting by size (1); interspecific — other species such as birds or seals eating the fish, prevented by nets (1).
:::

::: test

Intraspecific: keep numbers per cage low enough and feed regularly so fish do not compete for food and space (1); interspecific: keep one species per cage and remove other species (1).

::: test

Selective breeding produces faster-growing, disease-resistant fish (1); separating by size stops large fish eating or out-competing small ones (1).
