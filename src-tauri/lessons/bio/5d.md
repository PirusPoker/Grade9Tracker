## What it is

**Genetic modification (genetic engineering)** is changing an organism's DNA by **inserting a gene from another organism** so that it makes a new protein. An organism carrying a gene from a different species is **transgenic**. Unlike selective breeding, the change happens in one step and can cross species barriers.

**The tools:**

- **Restriction enzymes** — cut DNA at specific base sequences. Cutting leaves short single-stranded overhangs called **sticky ends**. The **same restriction enzyme** used on the gene and on the vector produces **complementary sticky ends** that pair up by base pairing.
- **DNA ligase** — joins (ligates) the sugar-phosphate backbones of DNA fragments whose sticky ends have paired, sealing the gene into the vector to make **recombinant DNA**.
- **Vectors** — carriers that take the gene into the host cell: **plasmids** (small circular DNA molecules in bacteria, which take up plasmids readily and copy them when they divide) and **viruses** (which naturally inject their DNA into cells — used to carry genes into plant and animal cells).

**Producing human insulin with GM bacteria** — the standard example:

1. The **gene for human insulin** is **cut** from a human chromosome using a **restriction enzyme**, leaving sticky ends.
2. A **plasmid** is removed from a bacterium and **cut open with the same restriction enzyme**, giving complementary sticky ends.
3. The insulin gene and the plasmid are mixed; the sticky ends **pair by complementary base pairing**, and **DNA ligase** joins them — a **recombinant plasmid**.
4. The recombinant plasmid is inserted into a **bacterium** (*E. coli*), which now carries the human gene.
5. The GM bacteria are grown in an **industrial fermenter** (5b) under controlled conditions; they **divide rapidly**, copying the plasmid, and **express** the gene, making **human insulin**.
6. The insulin is **extracted and purified** for use by people with type 1 diabetes.

Advantages over the old method (extracting insulin from pig and cow pancreases): it is **identical to human insulin** so it causes no allergic reactions, it is made in **large quantities cheaply and reliably**, and it avoids animal products (acceptable to vegetarians and some religious groups).

**Genetically modified plants** — genes inserted (often using a bacterium, *Agrobacterium*, as the vector, or by firing DNA-coated particles into cells) to give:

- **Pest resistance** — a gene from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) makes the plant produce an insecticidal protein, so fewer pesticides are sprayed (Bt cotton, Bt maize).
- **Herbicide resistance** — the crop survives a weedkiller sprayed on the field, so weeds are killed and yield rises.
- **Improved nutrition** — **Golden Rice** carries genes for beta-carotene (vitamin A) to prevent blindness in poor countries.
- Also: drought and salt tolerance, longer shelf life, disease resistance, higher yield.

**Evaluating GM**: *benefits* — higher yields and less crop loss, fewer pesticides, food with added nutrients, crops for harsh environments, medicines such as insulin. *Concerns* — GM genes may **spread to wild plants** by cross-pollination (herbicide-resistant "superweeds"); effects on **insects and biodiversity** (pollinators, food webs); possible unknown **effects on human health** (allergies) — not demonstrated so far; **seed companies** control the technology, making poor farmers dependent; ethical objections to "playing with nature"; loss of variety if a few GM crops dominate.

::: terms
- **Genetic modification (genetic engineering)**: changing the genome of an organism by inserting a gene from another organism.
- **Restriction enzyme**: an enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific base sequence, leaving sticky ends.
- **DNA ligase**: an enzyme that joins pieces of DNA together.
- **Vector**: something used to carry a gene into another cell — a plasmid or a virus.
- **Plasmid**: a small ring of DNA in bacteria, used as a vector.
- **Recombinant DNA**: DNA formed by combining DNA from two different organisms.
- **Transgenic organism**: an organism that contains a gene from a different species.
:::

::: method
1. **Insulin sequence in six steps** with the enzymes named at the right points: restriction enzyme (cut gene *and* plasmid — *same* enzyme, sticky ends), ligase (join), plasmid (vector), bacterium, fermenter, extraction.
2. **The sticky-end detail is where the marks are**: same enzyme → complementary sticky ends → base pairing → ligase seals.
3. **Vectors**: plasmid for bacteria, virus for plant and animal cells — and *why* (they naturally enter cells / are copied).
4. **Transgenic** = contains a gene from another species; use the word.
5. **Evaluate GM crops** with two benefits, two concerns and a judgement; keep concerns specific (gene spread, biodiversity, dependence) rather than vague.
:::

::: example Enzymes
Explain the roles of restriction enzymes and DNA ligase in genetic modification. (4)

A restriction enzyme cuts DNA at a specific base sequence (1), leaving single-stranded sticky ends; the same enzyme is used to cut the gene and to open the plasmid, so their sticky ends are complementary and pair by base pairing (2). DNA ligase then joins the backbones of the gene and the plasmid together, forming recombinant DNA (1).
:::

::: example Insulin
Describe how genetically modified bacteria are used to produce human insulin. (5)

The human insulin gene is cut from human DNA with a restriction enzyme, and a bacterial plasmid is cut open with the same enzyme so both have matching sticky ends (1). The gene is inserted into the plasmid and joined with DNA ligase, making a recombinant plasmid (1). The plasmid is taken up by bacteria (1). The bacteria are grown in a fermenter with controlled temperature, pH, oxygen and nutrients; as they divide they copy the plasmid and express the gene, secreting human insulin (1). The insulin is separated from the culture and purified (1).
:::

::: example Vectors
Explain why plasmids are used as vectors in genetic modification of bacteria. (2)

Plasmids are small circular DNA molecules that bacteria readily take up and copy when they divide (1), so a gene inserted into a plasmid is carried into the bacterium and passed to all its descendants (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate
Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of growing crops genetically modified to be resistant to insect pests. (4)

Advantages: fewer insecticides need to be sprayed, saving money and reducing harm to non-target insects and to the environment; less of the crop is lost, so yields rise and food is cheaper (2). Disadvantages: the resistance gene could spread to wild relatives by cross-pollination; insects that eat the crop, including harmless ones, may be affected and pests may evolve resistance to the toxin; farmers must buy seed from the company each year, and some consumers distrust GM food (2).
:::

What are sticky ends and why are they important? (2)

Short single-stranded lengths of DNA left when a restriction enzyme cuts (1); complementary sticky ends on the gene and the plasmid pair up, allowing the gene to be joined in (1).

---

Give two advantages of insulin made by GM bacteria over insulin extracted from animals. (2)
---
Identical to human insulin, so no allergic reaction (1); produced cheaply in large amounts (1).

::: q
Why are GM bacteria grown in a fermenter? (2)
---
Conditions — temperature, pH, oxygen, nutrients — are kept at the optimum (1) so the bacteria multiply rapidly and produce large quantities of the product (1).

::: q
Describe one way genetic modification has improved a crop plant. (2)
---
Golden Rice contains genes for making beta-carotene (1), which the body converts to vitamin A, reducing blindness where rice is the staple food (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one environmental concern about herbicide-resistant GM crops. (2)
---
The resistance gene could pass to wild relatives by cross-pollination (1), producing weeds the herbicide cannot kill (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how genetic modification differs from selective breeding. (2)
---
GM inserts a specific gene, possibly from another species, directly into the DNA in one step (1); selective breeding only chooses among alleles already in the species over many generations (1).
