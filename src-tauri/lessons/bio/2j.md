## What it is

**Blood** is a tissue: cells suspended in a liquid.

| Component | What it is | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasma** | pale yellow liquid, mostly water | transports dissolved substances: **carbon dioxide** (as hydrogencarbonate), **digested food** (glucose, amino acids), **urea** to the kidneys, **hormones**, and **heat** around the body; carries the cells |
| **Red blood cells** | biconcave discs with **no nucleus**, packed with **haemoglobin** | transport **oxygen**: haemoglobin binds oxygen in the lungs to form **oxyhaemoglobin**, which releases oxygen in the tissues |
| **White blood cells** | larger, with a nucleus; two main types | defend against pathogens: **phagocytes** engulf and digest them; **lymphocytes** make **antibodies** |
| **Platelets** | fragments of cells, no nucleus | cause **blood clotting** at wounds |

**Red blood cells are adapted for oxygen transport**: (1) they contain **haemoglobin**, which binds oxygen reversibly; (2) they have **no nucleus**, leaving more room for haemoglobin; (3) the **biconcave** shape gives a **large surface area to volume ratio** for fast diffusion of oxygen in and out; (4) they are **small and flexible** so they squeeze through capillaries close to every cell.

**Clotting**: when a vessel is damaged, platelets are triggered to release chemicals that convert the soluble plasma protein **fibrinogen** into insoluble **fibrin**; fibrin forms a mesh of fibres across the wound that traps red blood cells, forming a clot. The clot stops blood loss and, as it dries into a scab, stops pathogens entering.

**The immune response** — when pathogens get in:

- **Phagocytes** move to the site, surround (engulf) the pathogen and digest it with enzymes — **phagocytosis**. Non-specific: any pathogen.
- **Lymphocytes** recognise the **antigens** — molecules on the surface of the pathogen that are foreign to the body. Each lymphocyte makes one type of **antibody**, a protein that is **specific** to one antigen (complementary shape). Antibodies bind to the antigens and destroy the pathogen: clumping the pathogens together for phagocytes, marking them for destruction, or neutralising their toxins. The lymphocytes divide rapidly, and some remain as **memory cells** that recognise the same antigen years later and respond quickly and strongly — this is **immunity**.

**Vaccination** produces immunity without the disease: a **vaccine** contains a **dead or weakened (inactive) form of the pathogen**, or its antigens. Injected, the antigens stimulate lymphocytes to produce **antibodies** and, crucially, **memory cells**. If the real pathogen later infects, the memory cells respond immediately, producing antibodies faster and in larger amounts than in a first infection, so the pathogen is destroyed before symptoms develop — the person is **immune**. Mass vaccination protects even the unvaccinated because the pathogen cannot spread (herd immunity).

Note the difference: **antigens** are on the pathogen; **antibodies** are made by lymphocytes; **antibiotics** are drugs that kill bacteria (not viruses) and have nothing to do with the immune response.

::: method
1. **Components → functions**: four rows, each with one clear job. Plasma "transports dissolved substances" — then list them.
2. **Red cell adaptations**: haemoglobin, no nucleus, biconcave (surface area : volume), small/flexible — link each to oxygen transport.
3. **Immune answers use the vocabulary**: pathogen, antigen, lymphocyte, antibody (specific), phagocyte (engulf), memory cell. "White blood cells eat germs" is worth little.
4. **Vaccination as a sequence**: inactive pathogen → antigens → lymphocytes make antibodies → memory cells → on reinfection, faster and larger antibody response → no symptoms.
5. **Clotting**: platelets → fibrinogen → fibrin → mesh traps cells → clot; then what the clot achieves.
:::

::: example Components
State the function of plasma and give three substances it transports. (2)

Plasma transports dissolved substances around the body (1): carbon dioxide, urea, glucose, hormones (any three) (1).
:::

::: example Red cells
Explain three ways red blood cells are adapted to carry oxygen. (3)

They contain haemoglobin, which binds oxygen in the lungs to form oxyhaemoglobin and releases it in respiring tissues (1). They have no nucleus, leaving more space for haemoglobin (1). The biconcave shape gives a large surface area to volume ratio, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly (1).
:::

::: example Immune response
Describe how lymphocytes help to destroy a pathogen that has entered the body. (4)

Lymphocytes recognise the antigens on the surface of the pathogen (1). They produce antibodies — proteins whose shape is complementary to that particular antigen, so they are specific to it (1). The antibodies bind to the antigens, clumping the pathogens together and marking them so phagocytes engulf and digest them, or neutralising their toxins (1). Some lymphocytes remain as memory cells, giving a faster response if the same pathogen returns (1).
:::

::: example Vaccination
Explain how vaccination makes a person immune to a disease. (4)

The vaccine contains a dead or weakened form of the pathogen, carrying its antigens but unable to cause the disease (1). The antigens stimulate lymphocytes to produce antibodies specific to them (1) and to form memory cells that stay in the blood (1). If the live pathogen later infects, the memory cells recognise it at once and produce antibodies rapidly and in large amounts, destroying it before symptoms appear (1).
:::

::: q
Name the four components of blood. (2)
---
Plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets.
:::

::: q
Describe the role of platelets. (2)
---
At a wound they trigger the conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin (1), which forms a mesh that traps cells to make a clot, stopping blood loss and the entry of pathogens (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between an antigen and an antibody. (2)
---
An antigen is a molecule on the surface of a pathogen that the body recognises as foreign (1); an antibody is a protein made by lymphocytes that binds specifically to that antigen (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how a phagocyte destroys a bacterium. (2)
---
It surrounds and engulfs the bacterium (1), then digests it with enzymes (1).
:::

::: q
Why are antibodies described as specific? (1)
---
Each antibody has a shape complementary to one antigen only, so it binds to that pathogen and no other.
:::

::: q
Explain why a person who has had measles does not usually catch it again. (2)
---
Memory cells produced during the first infection remain in the blood (1); on reinfection they produce antibodies so quickly that the virus is destroyed before symptoms develop (1).
:::

::: q
Why does a vaccine not cause the disease it protects against? (1)
---
It contains a dead or weakened pathogen (or only its antigens) that cannot reproduce or cause harm.
:::

::: q
Explain why vaccinating most of a population protects those who cannot be vaccinated. (2)
---
Vaccinated people do not catch or pass on the pathogen (1), so it cannot spread through the population to reach the unvaccinated (herd immunity) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why haemoglobin is useful for oxygen transport. (2)
---
It binds oxygen where the concentration is high (lungs) to form oxyhaemoglobin (1) and releases it where concentration is low (respiring tissues) (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Antigen** (on the pathogen) vs **antibody** (made by lymphocytes) vs **antibiotic** (a drug). Three different words.
- Antibodies are **specific** — say complementary shape to one antigen.
- **Memory cells** are the key to immunity and vaccination — the answer is incomplete without them.
- Red cells: **no nucleus** → more haemoglobin; **biconcave** → surface area to volume ratio.
- Vaccine = **dead or weakened** pathogen; the response on reinfection is **faster and larger**.
:::
