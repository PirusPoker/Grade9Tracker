## What it is

**Variation** is the differences between individuals of the same species. It has two causes:

- **Genetic (inherited) variation** — differences in alleles, from meiosis, the random fusion of gametes, and mutation. Examples: blood group, eye colour, ability to roll the tongue.
- **Environmental variation** — differences caused by surroundings and lifestyle: scars, language, a plant's size depending on light and nutrients.
- Most characteristics are affected by **both** — height and weight depend on genes and diet; skin colour on genes and sunlight.

**Continuous variation** (height, mass) shows a range of values with no distinct groups, usually from many genes plus environment; **discontinuous variation** (blood group, sex) has distinct categories, usually from one or a few genes.

**Mutation** is a **rare, random change in the DNA** — in the sequence of bases of a gene, or in the number or structure of chromosomes. Mutations happen spontaneously when DNA is copied, but their **frequency is increased** by **ionising radiation** (X-rays, gamma rays, ultraviolet) and **chemical mutagens** (tar in tobacco smoke, some dyes and pesticides). Most mutations have no effect or are harmful; occasionally one gives an advantage — and mutation is the ultimate source of all new alleles.

**How a change in DNA can change a protein**: the base sequence of a gene is read three bases at a time, each triplet coding for an amino acid (3c). If a base is changed, added or deleted, a different amino acid may be placed in the chain, or the reading frame may shift so many amino acids change; the polypeptide **folds differently**, so its **shape changes** — an enzyme may lose its active site and stop working, or a structural protein may be weaker. Sometimes the new triplet codes for the same amino acid, so nothing changes; sometimes the changed protein works better.

**Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection** — evolution is the gradual change in a species over many generations. The logic, in order:

1. Individuals in a population show **variation** because of differences in their genes (from mutation and sexual reproduction).
2. Organisms produce **more offspring than can survive**, so there is **competition** for food, mates, space — and predation and disease.
3. Individuals whose characteristics are **better adapted** to the environment are **more likely to survive and reproduce** — "survival of the fittest".
4. They **pass on the alleles** for those characteristics to their offspring.
5. Over many generations the **advantageous alleles become more common** in the population; the species changes — it **evolves**. If two populations become isolated and change differently, they may become **new species** that can no longer interbreed.

The order matters: **variation exists first, then selection acts on it**. Organisms do not change *in order to* survive; the environment selects those that already happen to be suited.

**Antibiotic resistance in bacteria** — natural selection observed in years, not millennia:

1. In a population of bacteria, **random mutation** gives a few individuals an allele that makes them **resistant** to an antibiotic (variation).
2. The antibiotic is used; it **kills the non-resistant** bacteria (selection pressure).
3. The **resistant bacteria survive and reproduce** rapidly by binary fission, passing the resistance allele to all their offspring.
4. The population becomes **mostly resistant**; the antibiotic no longer works. MRSA is an example.

The resistance arose by chance *before* the antibiotic was used — the antibiotic did not cause the mutation, it selected for it. Overuse of antibiotics (prescribing them for viral infections, not finishing courses, use in farming) increases the selection pressure; the answers are to use antibiotics only when needed, complete the course, and develop new ones.

::: method
1. **Genetic vs environmental**: give a clear example of each and one that involves both; identify continuous or discontinuous when asked.
2. **Mutation**: define (rare, random change in DNA), name what raises the rate (ionising radiation, chemical mutagens), and say most are harmful or neutral.
3. **DNA change → protein change** in the chain: base sequence → amino acid sequence → shape → function. Give the enzyme active site as the example.
4. **Natural selection in five numbered steps**, with the vocabulary: variation, competition, better adapted, survive and reproduce, pass on alleles, more common over generations. Never say organisms "adapt so that" or "develop resistance because they need to".
5. **Antibiotic resistance** is the same five steps with bacteria — start with the *mutation that already exists*.
:::

::: example Variation
Explain why identical twins raised in different homes may differ in body mass, although their eye colour is the same. (2)

Eye colour is controlled only by genes, and identical twins have identical genes, so it is the same (1). Body mass depends on genes *and* the environment — diet and exercise — which differ between homes, so the twins can differ (1).
:::

::: example Mutation and protein
Explain how a mutation in a gene could stop an enzyme working. (4)

A mutation changes the sequence of bases in the gene (1). Because each triplet of bases codes for one amino acid, a different amino acid may be inserted into the polypeptide, or the sequence after the change may be altered (1). The polypeptide then folds into a different shape (1), so the enzyme's active site is no longer complementary to its substrate and the enzyme cannot catalyse the reaction (1).
:::

::: example Natural selection
A species of moth exists in pale and dark forms. After tree trunks in an area became blackened by soot, the dark form became much more common. Explain this using natural selection. (5)

The population already showed variation: some moths carried the allele for the dark form through mutation (1). On sooty trunks, pale moths were easily seen and eaten by birds, while dark moths were camouflaged (1). Dark moths were therefore more likely to survive and reproduce (1), passing the allele for dark colour to their offspring (1). Over generations the dark allele became more common in the population, so most moths were dark (1).
:::

::: example Antibiotic resistance
Explain how a population of bacteria can become resistant to an antibiotic. (4)

A random mutation gives a few bacteria an allele that makes them resistant (1). When the antibiotic is used, the non-resistant bacteria are killed but the resistant ones survive (1). The survivors reproduce rapidly, passing on the resistance allele (1), so after several generations most of the population is resistant and the antibiotic is ineffective (1).
:::

::: q
Give two causes of genetic variation in a species. (2)
---
Mutation; meiosis and the random fusion of gametes in sexual reproduction.
:::

::: q
Give one example of a characteristic caused only by the environment. (1)
---
A scar (or accent/language, a plant grown in shade being smaller).
:::

::: q
Define mutation and name two factors that increase the rate of mutation. (3)
---
A rare, random change in the DNA (1); ionising radiation such as X-rays or UV (1); chemical mutagens such as tar in tobacco smoke (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a mutation in a gene may have no effect on the organism. (2)
---
The changed triplet may code for the same amino acid (1), or the changed amino acid may not alter the protein's shape or function (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why mutations are important for evolution. (1)
---
They are the source of new alleles, which provide the variation natural selection acts on.
:::

::: q
Why is it wrong to say that bacteria "develop resistance because they need to"? (2)
---
The resistance mutation arises by chance before the antibiotic is used (1); the antibiotic only selects the resistant bacteria that already exist — it does not cause them to change (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest two ways to slow the development of antibiotic resistance. (2)
---
Only prescribe antibiotics when needed (not for viruses); patients should complete the full course (or reduce use in farming).
:::

::: q
Explain how a new species can arise. (3)
---
Two populations of a species become isolated, e.g. by a river or mountain (1); natural selection acts differently in the two environments, so different alleles accumulate (1); eventually they are so different they can no longer interbreed — two species (1).
:::

::: q
Is human height continuous or discontinuous variation? Explain. (2)
---
Continuous (1) — it shows a range of values with no distinct groups, controlled by many genes and the environment (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Variation first, then selection.** Organisms never adapt "in order to" survive — the environment selects those already suited.
- Natural selection answers need all five steps: **variation → competition → best adapted survive and reproduce → alleles passed on → more common over generations.**
- Antibiotic resistance: the mutation is **random and pre-existing**; the antibiotic **selects**, it does not cause.
- Mutation = **rare, random change in DNA**; rate raised by **radiation and chemicals**.
- DNA change → **amino acid sequence → shape → function**: give the whole chain.
:::

::: test
Explain how natural selection can lead to a population of insects becoming resistant to an insecticide. (4)
---
There is variation in the population; by chance a mutation gives some insects resistance (1); when the insecticide is used, non-resistant insects die and resistant ones survive (1); the survivors reproduce and pass on the resistance allele (1); over generations the proportion of resistant insects increases (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between continuous and discontinuous variation, with an example of each. (2)
---
Continuous: a range of values with no distinct groups, e.g. height or mass (1); discontinuous: distinct categories, e.g. blood group (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why identical twins raised apart may differ in body mass. (2)
---
They have identical genes (1), so any difference must be due to environment — diet and exercise (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how Darwin's theory explains the evolution of a new species. (4)
---
Populations show variation caused by mutation (1); individuals with characteristics suited to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce (1); they pass on the favourable alleles (1); if two populations are isolated and selected differently for long enough, they become so different they can no longer interbreed — a new species (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why most mutations have no effect on an organism. (1)
---
They occur in non-coding DNA, or change a codon to one that codes for the same amino acid, or are in a gene not switched on.
:::

::: test
Explain why an increase in the rate of mutation is harmful to an individual but can be useful to a species. (2)
---
Mutations in an individual are usually harmful or neutral — they can cause cancer (1); in a species, occasional useful mutations provide the variation on which natural selection acts (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a characteristic acquired during an organism's life, such as a scar, is not passed on. (1)
---
It does not change the DNA in the gametes, so it cannot be inherited.
:::

::: test
Explain why hospitals restrict the use of antibiotics to slow the spread of resistant bacteria. (2)
---
Every use of an antibiotic selects for resistant bacteria by killing susceptible ones (1); fewer, better-targeted uses give resistant strains less advantage and time to spread (1).
:::
