## What it is

**Food provenance** means where food comes from and how it was produced. Every ingredient starts in one of three ways:
- **Grown**: fruits, vegetables and cereals (wheat, barley, oats, rice, maize), raised as crops in fields, orchards, polytunnels or glasshouses.
- **Reared**: meat (beef from cattle, lamb from sheep, pork from pigs) and poultry (chicken, turkey, duck), and the milk and eggs they produce.
- **Caught**: fish and shellfish taken from the sea, rivers and lakes. **Farmed** fish (such as salmon, trout and mussels) are reared in pens or on ropes instead of being caught wild.

How food is produced affects its price, quality, animal welfare and impact on the environment. That is why the exam asks you to **compare** farming systems and weigh the **advantages and disadvantages**.

### Conventional and organic farming

| | **Conventional farming** | **Organic farming** |
|---|---|---|
| Fertilisers | Synthetic (manufactured) fertilisers boost yields | No synthetic fertilisers; manure, compost and crop rotation with nitrogen-fixing plants such as clover build soil fertility |
| Pests and weeds | Synthetic pesticides and herbicides are used as needed | Only a small number of mainly naturally derived pesticides are permitted; weeds controlled by rotation and machinery; natural predators encouraged |
| Animals | Welfare meets legal minimums; routine medicines may be used | Animals must have outdoor access and more space; antibiotics used to treat illness, not routinely; organic feed |
| Other rules | GM crops permitted where the law allows | No GM ingredients; artificial additives in processed organic food are very restricted |
| Yields and price | High yields, lower prices | Lower yields, more labour, higher prices |

Organic food must be **certified** by an approved body (such as the **Soil Association**) before it can be labelled organic.

- **Advantages of organic:** fewer synthetic chemicals and residues; better for soil structure, wildlife and biodiversity; higher animal welfare; seen by many as tastier and more natural.
- **Disadvantages of organic:** higher cost; lower yields mean more land is needed to grow the same amount; produce may look less uniform and have a shorter shelf life; no strong evidence that it is more nutritious.

### Intensive and free-range production

**Intensive farming** aims to produce as much food as possible from the land, animals and labour available, at the lowest cost. It uses large numbers of animals kept indoors, controlled feeding and lighting, mechanisation, and high inputs of fertiliser and pesticides for crops.
- Advantages: cheap food; high output from little land; consistent supply all year; animals protected from weather and predators; easier disease control and monitoring.
- Disadvantages: animals have little space and cannot behave naturally, which causes stress and injury; the risk of disease spreading in crowded sheds, and heavy use of medicines; pollution from concentrated waste; reliance on imported feed such as soya; a loss of wildlife habitat.

**Free-range production** gives animals **continuous daytime access to outdoor space**, where they can roam, forage and behave naturally, with shelter indoors.
- Advantages: better animal welfare; many consumers believe the meat and eggs taste better; appeals to ethical shoppers and can command a higher price.
- Disadvantages: higher cost to produce and to buy; needs more land; animals are exposed to predators (foxes), bad weather and diseases carried by wild birds (flocks may have to be housed during bird flu outbreaks); lower output.

**Eggs** show the systems clearly, because each egg is stamped with a code: **0** organic, **1** free range, **2** barn (loose inside a shed), **3** cage (enriched colony cages, which replaced the old battery cages in the UK). The code is followed by the country and the farm ID.

### Assurance schemes and labels

| Label | What it tells the shopper |
|---|---|
| **Red Tractor** | British food produced to set standards for food safety, hygiene, animal welfare and the environment, traceable back to the farm |
| **Soil Association organic** | Certified organic production |
| **RSPCA Assured** | Higher animal-welfare standards checked on farm |
| **MSC** (blue fish label) | Wild fish from a certified sustainable fishery |
| **ASC** | Responsibly farmed fish and seafood |
| **Free range** | Animals had outdoor access |

### Sustainable fishing

**Sustainable fishing** means catching fish at a rate that leaves enough adults in the sea to breed and replace them, while limiting harm to other wildlife and the ocean environment, so that fish stocks last for future generations. **Overfishing** (taking fish faster than they can reproduce) has caused stocks of some species to collapse.

Methods:
- **Quotas** limit the total weight of each species that can be caught.
- **Minimum landing sizes** and **larger net mesh sizes** let young fish escape and grow to breeding age.
- **Closed seasons and protected areas** leave breeding grounds undisturbed.
- **Selective methods**, such as pole-and-line or hand-line fishing, reduce **bycatch** (the unwanted fish, dolphins, turtles and seabirds caught by accident).
- **Choosing less popular species** (hake, pollock, coley, mackerel) eases pressure on cod and tuna.
- **Buying MSC-labelled fish.**

**Fish farming (aquaculture)** relieves pressure on wild stocks and gives a year-round supply. But crowded pens can spread disease and parasites (such as sea lice) to wild fish, waste and chemicals pollute surrounding water, and farmed fish may be fed on fishmeal made from wild-caught fish.

### Local, seasonal and GM foods

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Locally produced** | Fewer food miles; fresher, as it is picked closer to ripeness; supports local farmers and jobs; clear provenance; less packaging | Often costs more; smaller range; limited by season; small producers may not supply enough; local produce grown in heated glasshouses may have a larger carbon footprint than imported food |
| **Seasonal** | Cheaper when plentiful; best flavour and freshness; more nutrients; fewer food miles if grown in the UK; variety through the year | Limited choice at certain times (little UK fruit in winter and early spring); some favourite foods unavailable or imported out of season |
| **Genetically modified (GM)** | Crops made resistant to pests, disease, drought or herbicides give higher, more reliable yields; less pesticide may be needed; nutrients can be added (Golden Rice with beta-carotene); longer shelf life; could help feed a growing population | Long-term health effects questioned by some consumers; modified genes may cross into wild plants; loss of biodiversity; large companies control patented seed and farmers may have to buy new seed each year; ethical objections; must be labelled, and many UK shoppers avoid it |

**Genetic modification** means altering an organism's DNA, often by inserting a gene from another species, to give it a desired trait. Few GM foods are sold directly in UK shops, but GM soya and maize are widely used in animal feed around the world. Gene editing (called "precision breeding" in England) is now treated separately from GM in law; this is context only.

::: terms
- **Provenance**: where a food comes from and how it was grown, reared or caught.
- **Conventional farming**: farming that uses synthetic fertilisers, pesticides and modern methods to achieve high yields at low cost.
- **Organic farming**: certified farming that uses no synthetic fertilisers, very few pesticides, no GM and high animal-welfare standards.
- **Intensive farming**: producing the maximum output from land, animals and labour at low cost, often keeping animals indoors in large numbers.
- **Free range**: a system in which animals have continuous daytime access to outdoor space.
- **Sustainable fishing**: catching fish at a rate that allows stocks to replace themselves and limits harm to the ocean environment.
- **Overfishing**: catching fish faster than the population can reproduce, which causes stocks to fall.
- **Bycatch**: unwanted sea creatures caught by accident while fishing for another species.
- **Quota**: a legal limit on the amount of a fish species that can be caught in a set period.
- **Genetically modified (GM) food**: food from a plant or animal whose DNA has been altered in a laboratory to give it a desired trait.
:::

::: method
1. **Define the system** in a sentence (what makes it organic, intensive, free range or sustainable).
2. **Give advantages and disadvantages from different angles**: cost to the consumer, animal welfare, environment, yield, quality and taste, the farmer.
3. **Link each point to its effect**: "hens can roam outside, so they can behave naturally, which reduces stress".
4. **Keep both sides.** One-sided answers are capped in evaluate questions.
5. **Conclude with a judgement tied to the person**: a family on a tight budget and an ethically minded shopper may reasonably choose differently.
:::

::: example Give — organic farming
Give two advantages and two disadvantages of organic farming. (4)

Advantages: fewer synthetic pesticide residues on the food (1); better for wildlife and soil because no synthetic fertilisers or routine pesticides are used (1). Disadvantages: the food costs more because yields are lower and more labour is needed (1); more land is needed to produce the same amount of food (1).
:::

::: example Explain — sustainable fishing
Explain what is meant by sustainable fishing. (2)

Catching fish in a way that leaves enough fish in the sea to breed and replace those caught (1), so stocks are kept for the future and harm to other sea life and the environment is kept low, e.g. through quotas and larger mesh sizes (1).
:::

::: example Explain — two methods
Explain two ways fishing can be made more sustainable. (4)

Using nets with larger mesh sizes (1) lets young, small fish escape, so they can grow and breed before being caught (1). Setting quotas (1) limits the total amount of each species that can be landed, so stocks are not taken faster than they can reproduce (1).
:::

::: example Explain — intensive rearing
Explain two disadvantages of intensive rearing of chickens for meat. (4)

The birds are kept in large numbers in crowded sheds (1), so they cannot behave naturally and may suffer stress, leg problems and injury (1). Disease can spread quickly through a crowded flock (1), so antibiotics or other medicines may be needed, and one outbreak can affect thousands of birds (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — eggs
- Analyse the differences between intensive (cage) and free-range egg production.
- Evaluate which type of egg a consumer should buy. (8)

In cage production, hens live indoors in enriched colony cages with nesting and perching areas, under controlled light and temperature, with automated feeding and egg collection. This gives high output at low cost with little land, clean eggs and protection from predators and wild birds. But space per hen is limited and hens cannot roam, dust-bathe or forage freely. Free-range hens have continuous daytime access to an outdoor range with shelter indoors, so they can behave naturally, which improves welfare. But production costs more, needs far more land, exposes hens to foxes, weather and disease from wild birds, and output can fall, so free-range eggs are sold at a higher price. The code on the shell, 1 for free range and 3 for cage, lets the consumer tell them apart.

The choice depends on the consumer's priorities. For someone who values animal welfare and can afford it, free-range (or organic, code 0) eggs are the better choice, and many think they taste better. Nutritionally, both provide similar protein, vitamins and minerals. For a family on a low income, cage eggs are a cheap source of high biological value protein, and eggs from any system are a nutritious, affordable food. On balance, free-range eggs are the better buy where the budget allows, because the welfare benefits are significant and the extra cost per egg is small; but cheaper eggs are still better than giving up eggs.

(Level 4, 7–8: clear analysis of both systems; balanced evaluation linked to consumer priorities; judgement.)
:::

A supermarket wants to expand its range of fish and seafood.
- Analyse the issues linked to where and how fish are caught and farmed.
- Evaluate how the supermarket could make sure its fish range is sustainable. (8)
---
Levels of response:
- **7–8:** a detailed analysis of wild-catch and farming issues, using accurate terms (overfishing, bycatch, quotas, aquaculture); a balanced evaluation of practical actions, with a supported judgement.
- **5–6:** a clear analysis of several issues; a reasonable evaluation.
- **3–4:** some relevant points with limited explanation; a brief or one-sided evaluation.
- **1–2:** simple points.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Overfishing has reduced stocks of popular species such as cod and some tuna.
- Bycatch from trawling harms dolphins, turtles, seabirds and juvenile fish; trawling can damage the seabed.
- Quotas, mesh sizes, closed seasons and protected areas support recovery.
- Fish farming gives a steady supply but brings disease, sea lice, pollution and wild-fish feed.
- Transport and freezing affect the carbon footprint; imported prawns may carry welfare and environmental concerns.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Sell MSC-certified wild fish and ASC-certified farmed fish, which is trusted and independently checked, but certified fish may cost more.
- Promote less popular species (hake, pollock, mackerel, coley), which eases pressure on cod but needs marketing because customers are reluctant.
- Choose pole-and-line tuna.
- Source from well-managed fisheries, with labels giving provenance.
- Avoid species listed as at risk.
- Limits: customers want cheap, familiar fish; certified supply may be limited.
- Judgement: certification plus promoting alternative species is the most effective and credible approach.
