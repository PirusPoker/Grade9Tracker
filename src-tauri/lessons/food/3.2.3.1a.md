## What it is

**Healthy eating guidelines** are government advice on what and how much to eat to get enough of every nutrient, stay a healthy weight and lower the risk of diet-related disease. In the UK the main visual guide is the **Eatwell Guide**, a plate-shaped diagram showing the proportions of each food group to eat **over a day or a week**, not necessarily at every meal. It applies to people from about **two years old**; babies and toddlers under two have different needs.

**The five food groups of the Eatwell Guide**

| Group | Rough share | Main nutrients | Advice |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fruit and vegetables** | just over a third | vitamin C, beta-carotene, folate, potassium, fibre | at least **5 portions a day** of a variety; fresh, frozen, tinned and dried all count |
| **Potatoes, bread, rice, pasta and other starchy carbohydrates** | just over a third | starch for energy, fibre, B vitamins, calcium and iron in fortified flour | base meals on these; choose **wholegrain or higher-fibre** versions; leave skins on potatoes |
| **Beans, pulses, fish, eggs, meat and other proteins** | about an eighth | protein, iron, zinc, B vitamins, omega-3 in oily fish | eat more beans and pulses; **two portions of fish a week, one of them oily**; eat less red and processed meat |
| **Dairy and alternatives** | a small section | calcium, protein, vitamins A, B2, B12 | choose lower-fat and lower-sugar options; plant alternatives should be unsweetened and calcium-fortified |
| **Oils and spreads** | a tiny section | unsaturated fat, vitamin E | choose **unsaturated** oils and spreads and use them in **small amounts** |

Outside the plate sit **foods high in fat, salt or sugar** (chocolate, cakes, biscuits, crisps, sugary drinks, pastries): eat these **less often and in small amounts**. The guide also shows **6–8 drinks a day**, preferably water, lower-fat milk and sugar-free drinks, and the reference intakes on food labels.

**The current guidelines in words**

1. Base meals on **higher-fibre starchy foods**.
2. Eat **at least 5 portions of fruit and vegetables** a day.
3. Eat more **fish**, including a portion of **oily fish** each week.
4. Cut down on **saturated fat** and **free sugars**.
5. Eat **less salt**: no more than **6 g a day** for adults.
6. **Get active** and be a healthy weight.
7. **Don't get thirsty**: 6–8 drinks a day.
8. **Don't skip breakfast.**

**What is a portion?**

| Food | One portion |
|---|---|
| Fruit or vegetables | **80 g**, e.g. an apple, a banana, two plums, three heaped tablespoons of peas |
| Dried fruit | 30 g (best with a meal, as it is sticky and sugary) |
| Fruit juice or smoothie | **150 ml**, and counts **once a day** only |
| Beans and pulses | three heaped tablespoons, counts **once a day** only, however much is eaten |
| Fish | about 140 g cooked |

Potatoes do not count towards the 5 a day because they are a starchy food.

**Controlling portion size.** Many foods are now served in larger portions than people need. Ways to judge a portion without scales:
- **Hand-size guides**: for example, a fist-sized portion of rice or pasta, a palm-sized piece of meat or fish, a thumb-sized piece of cheese. Hands grow with the body, so the guide roughly fits children and adults.
- **Packet portions**: the nutrition label states the portion the figures are based on.
- **Smaller plates and bowls**, measuring spoons for oil, and serving food onto plates rather than leaving dishes on the table.
- **Adjust for the person**: portions depend on life stage and activity (3.2.3.2). A teenager training daily needs more than an office worker.

**Costing when meal planning.** Planning meals for the week keeps them balanced and within budget.
1. **Cost of the amount used** = pack price ÷ pack size × amount used.
2. **Total cost** = add up every ingredient.
3. **Cost per portion** = total ÷ number of portions.

Ways to lower cost and stay healthy: plan a weekly menu and shopping list; buy **seasonal** fruit and vegetables; use **frozen or tinned** vegetables and fish; use **own-brand** products; **replace some meat with pulses**; buy in bulk if it will be used; cook in batches and freeze portions; use leftovers. This links to 3.5.1.1.

**Maintaining a healthy body weight throughout life**

Body weight stays steady when **energy in (food and drink) equals energy out (BMR and activity)**: this is **energy balance**.
- **Positive energy balance**: energy in is greater than energy out; the extra is stored as fat and weight increases. Necessary in growth and pregnancy, harmful when it leads to overweight.
- **Negative energy balance**: energy out is greater; stored fat is used and weight falls.

For adults, **BMI (body mass index)** = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². A BMI of **18.5 to 24.9** is in the healthy range, **25 to 29.9** is overweight and **30 or more** is obese. It is a rough guide: a very muscular athlete may have a high BMI while being healthy. Children's weight is judged against growth charts for their age and sex, not adult BMI bands.

Across life: children need enough energy to grow but should build habits of active play and fewer sugary snacks; teenagers need more energy during the growth spurt; adults should match intake to activity, as many jobs are sedentary; older adults need **less energy** because BMR and activity fall, but the **same or more of many nutrients**, so they need **nutrient-dense** food. At every stage, staying active, following the Eatwell proportions, controlling portions and limiting foods high in fat and sugar keep weight steady.

::: terms
- **Eatwell Guide**: the UK's healthy eating model, showing the proportions of five food groups to eat over a day or week.
- **5 a day**: the advice to eat at least five 80 g portions of a variety of fruit and vegetables every day.
- **Portion size**: the amount of a food served to one person, which should suit their age and activity.
- **Starchy carbohydrates**: foods such as bread, rice, pasta and potatoes, which should be the base of meals, ideally wholegrain.
- **Oily fish**: fish such as salmon, mackerel and sardines, rich in omega-3 fatty acids and vitamin D.
- **Foods high in fat, salt or sugar**: foods outside the Eatwell plate, such as cakes, crisps and sugary drinks, to eat less often.
- **Energy balance**: the state in which energy taken in from food equals the energy the body uses.
- **Body mass index (BMI)**: weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared, a rough guide to whether an adult's weight is healthy.
- **Cost per portion**: the total cost of a recipe's ingredients divided by the number of portions it makes.
- **Nutrient-dense food**: food that supplies a lot of nutrients for its energy, such as vegetables, pulses and fish.
:::

::: method
1. **Know the five Eatwell groups and their rough shares:** fruit and veg just over a third, starchy foods just over a third, protein foods about an eighth, dairy smaller, oils tiny.
2. **Quote the specific guideline:** at least 5 a day, 80 g portions, two fish a week with one oily, 6 g salt, 6–8 drinks.
3. **For "change the meal" questions, name the exact swap** and the guideline it meets: "add a side salad, so the meal contributes to the 5 a day".
4. **For costings, set out every step:** amount used from the pack price, the total, then divide by portions; round money to the nearest penny.
5. **For weight questions, use energy balance:** energy in versus energy out, and what happens to the extra.
6. **For menus and diaries, work through them day by day,** naming foods that meet or miss each guideline, then evaluate realistic changes for that family's time and budget, and make a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain — 5 a day
Explain the term "5 a day". (2)

It is the advice to eat at least five portions of a variety of fruit and vegetables every day (1), where a portion is about 80 g, such as an apple or three heaped tablespoons of vegetables, with juice and pulses each counting only once (1).
:::

::: example Suggest — meeting the Eatwell Guide
A lunch is a white bread sandwich with ham and butter, a packet of crisps and a can of cola. Suggest four changes to make it closer to the Eatwell Guide. (4)

Use wholemeal bread instead of white, to base the meal on higher-fibre starchy food (1). Swap the ham for lean chicken, egg or tuna and add salad leaves and tomato to the filling, so there is less processed meat and more vegetables (1). Replace the crisps with a piece of fruit or carrot sticks, which counts towards the 5 a day and cuts fat and salt (1). Drink water or semi-skimmed milk instead of cola, to avoid free sugars (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — cost per portion
Turkey meatballs with spaghetti serves 4. Calculate the cost of one portion.

| Ingredient | Amount used | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Turkey mince | 500 g | £3.50 for 500 g |
| Spaghetti | 300 g | £0.80 for 500 g |
| Chopped tomatoes | 2 cans | £0.45 a can |
| Onion | 1 | £0.12 each |
| Breadcrumbs | 50 g | £1.00 for 250 g |
| Egg | 1 | £1.50 for 6 |

(3)

Spaghetti £0.80 ÷ 500 × 300 = £0.48; breadcrumbs £1.00 ÷ 250 × 50 = £0.20; egg £1.50 ÷ 6 = £0.25; tomatoes 2 × £0.45 = £0.90 (1). Total £3.50 + £0.48 + £0.90 + £0.12 + £0.20 + £0.25 = £5.45 (1). Per portion £5.45 ÷ 4 = £1.36 (to the nearest penny) (1).
:::

::: example Explain — energy balance
Explain how an adult can maintain a healthy body weight. (4)

Weight stays the same when the energy taken in from food equals the energy used (energy balance) (1). If more energy is eaten than used, the extra is stored as fat and weight rises (1), so portions should suit their activity level, and foods high in fat and sugar should be limited (1). Being active, such as walking or cycling most days, raises energy output and helps keep the balance (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — BMI
An adult weighs 81 kg and is 1.80 m tall. Calculate their BMI and state what it shows. (3)

1.80 × 1.80 = 3.24 (1). 81 ÷ 3.24 = 25.0 (1). This is just into the overweight range (25–29.9), so a small reduction in energy intake or more activity would be advised (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why an older adult needs less energy than a younger adult but still needs nutrient-dense food. (3)
---
BMR falls with age as muscle is lost, and many older people are less active (1), so they need less energy to avoid weight gain (1); but their need for nutrients such as calcium, vitamin D, iron and fibre stays the same or rises, so each portion must supply more nutrients for its energy (1).
:::

- In costings, show **each step** and carry the pennies correctly. The method marks count even if the answer slips.

:::
