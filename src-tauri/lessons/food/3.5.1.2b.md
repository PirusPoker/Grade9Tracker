## What it is

This topic covers two very different reasons for food choice: **beliefs** about how food should be produced, and **medical conditions** that make some foods harmful. In the exam you explain the reasons behind a choice and give the counter-arguments, and you adapt a recipe so a person with an allergy or intolerance can eat it safely.

### Ethical and moral choices

| Belief | What people choose | Why | The other side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Animal welfare** | Free-range or organic meat and eggs; RSPCA Assured products; vegetarian or vegan diets | Animals should have space, natural behaviour and a humane death; intensive systems can cause stress and injury | Higher-welfare food costs more; not everyone can afford it; labels can be confusing |
| **Fairtrade** | Products with the Fairtrade Mark, such as bananas, coffee, tea, cocoa and sugar | Farmers and workers in developing countries receive a guaranteed minimum price, plus a **Fairtrade Premium** to spend on community projects such as schools, clean water and equipment; safer working conditions and no child labour | Usually costs the shopper more; only a small share of farmers are certified; the products still travel long distances |
| **Local produce** | Farm shops, farmers' markets, veg boxes, British-labelled food | Supports local farmers and jobs; fewer food miles; fresher food; knowing where food comes from | Limited range and season; may cost more; local is not always lower-carbon (heated greenhouses) |
| **Organic** | Organic fruit, vegetables, dairy and meat | Grown with very limited, mainly natural pesticides and no synthetic fertilisers; higher animal welfare; seen as better for wildlife and soil | Costs more; lower yields need more land; no strong evidence it is more nutritious |
| **Genetically modified (GM) foods** | Some people avoid GM food; others accept it | Worries about long-term safety, cross-pollination with wild plants, and large companies controlling seed | GM crops can resist pests or drought, raise yields and add nutrients; they are tested before approval |

People also become **vegetarian or vegan** for ethical reasons (animal welfare, the environmental impact of livestock) as well as for religion, health or taste.

### Allergy or intolerance?

| | **Food allergy** | **Food intolerance** |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | The **immune system** wrongly treats a food protein as harmful and releases chemicals such as histamine | The **digestive system** cannot deal with a food properly; the immune system is not involved in the reaction |
| Speed | Usually quick, within minutes | Usually slower, often hours |
| Amount | A **trace** can trigger it | Symptoms usually depend on how much is eaten |
| Symptoms | Itchy mouth, rash or hives, swelling of the lips, face and throat, wheezing, vomiting; in severe cases **anaphylaxis** | Bloating, wind, stomach pain, diarrhoea |
| Danger | Anaphylaxis can close the airway and drop blood pressure, and can kill. It is treated with an **adrenaline auto-injector** and an emergency call | Uncomfortable, not usually life-threatening |

**Gluten.** **Coeliac disease** is an autoimmune condition: gluten (a protein in wheat, barley and rye) damages the lining of the small intestine, so nutrients are poorly absorbed. This causes tiredness, anaemia, weight loss and diarrhoea. The spec groups it with intolerances. The only treatment is a strict **gluten-free diet** for life. Oats are often contaminated with wheat, so only oats labelled gluten-free are safe. Look for the **crossed grain symbol** and "gluten-free", which means very little gluten is present.

**Lactose.** **Lactose intolerance** is caused by a lack of the enzyme **lactase**, so the milk sugar lactose is not broken down, and bacteria in the gut ferment it, causing bloating and diarrhoea. It is different from a **milk allergy**, which is a reaction to milk *protein*. Swap in lactose-free milk (lactase already added) or calcium-fortified plant milks (soya, oat); hard cheeses contain little lactose, so many people tolerate small amounts.

### The six allergies in the spec

| Allergen | Where it hides | Swaps |
|---|---|---|
| **Nuts** (peanuts and tree nuts such as almonds, walnuts, cashews, hazelnuts) | Pesto, satay, some curries, marzipan, praline, cereal bars, nut oils, bakery items made on shared lines | Seeds such as sunflower or pumpkin (if safe), toasted oats for crunch, nut-free pesto made with basil and sunflower seeds |
| **Egg** | Cakes, mayonnaise, fresh pasta, meringue, glazes, quiche, some breadcrumb coatings | Commercial egg replacer, flax or chia "egg", aquafaba (chickpea water) for meringue, extra liquid and raising agent in cakes; milk or oil to glaze |
| **Milk** | Butter, cheese, cream, many sauces, chocolate, some breads | Plant milks and dairy-free spread; dairy-free cheese; oil |
| **Wheat** | Bread, pasta, pastry, flour-thickened sauces, couscous, soy sauce, batter | Rice, potato, corn, buckwheat, gluten-free flour blends; cornflour to thicken; tamari |
| **Fish** | Worcestershire sauce, Thai fish sauce, some pizza toppings (anchovies), Caesar dressing | Soy or mushroom-based sauces for savoury flavour |
| **Shellfish** (crustaceans such as prawns and crab; molluscs such as mussels) | Paella, seafood sticks, some stocks and Asian pastes, oyster sauce | Chicken or vegetables; shellfish-free stocks |

**Label law (context).** UK law says **14 allergens** must be emphasised (for example in bold) in the ingredients list of pre-packed food: celery, cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, lupin, milk, molluscs, mustard, peanuts, sesame, soya, sulphur dioxide and sulphites, and tree nuts. Since 2021, **Natasha's Law** has required food **pre-packed for direct sale** (such as sandwiches made and wrapped on the premises) to carry a full ingredients list with allergens emphasised. Restaurants and cafés must be able to tell customers which of the 14 are in each dish. **"May contain"** warnings are voluntary and show a risk of cross-contact in the factory; someone with a severe allergy should avoid those products. Learn the spec's six in detail; the 14 are context.

**Preventing cross-contact in the kitchen.** Allergens are not killed by cooking. Clean surfaces and equipment before preparing the allergen-free dish, make it **first**, use separate utensils, boards and oil, store it covered and labelled, and never just "pick off" nuts or prawns from a dish.

::: terms
- **Food allergy**: a reaction of the immune system to a food protein, which can be triggered by a trace amount and can be life-threatening.
- **Food intolerance**: a digestive reaction to a food that does not involve the immune system and is usually related to the amount eaten.
- **Anaphylaxis**: a severe, rapid allergic reaction that can cause swelling of the throat, breathing difficulty and collapse, and needs adrenaline straight away.
- **Adrenaline auto-injector**: a pen-like device that injects adrenaline into the thigh to treat anaphylaxis.
- **Coeliac disease**: an autoimmune condition in which gluten damages the lining of the small intestine, treated by a lifelong gluten-free diet.
- **Lactose intolerance**: an inability to digest lactose, the sugar in milk, because the body makes too little of the enzyme lactase.
- **Cross-contact**: the transfer of an allergen from one food or surface to another, for example through shared utensils.
- **Fairtrade**: a certification scheme that guarantees farmers in developing countries a minimum price and a premium for community projects.
- **Animal welfare**: the physical and mental well-being of farm animals, including space, natural behaviour and humane slaughter.
- **Organic**: food produced with very limited use of synthetic chemicals, no GM ingredients and high animal-welfare standards.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the condition precisely**: allergy (immune), intolerance (digestive), or coeliac disease (autoimmune).
2. **Find every source** of the problem food in the recipe, including hidden ones (soy sauce contains wheat; pesto contains nuts; fish sauce).
3. **Give a named swap and a reason** for each, and say how the swap affects taste, texture or nutrition.
4. **Add the safety step** for allergies: clean equipment, cook it first, separate utensils, label and check ingredient labels.
5. **For ethical choices**, give the reason for the choice and, if asked to evaluate, the counter-argument (cost, access, evidence).
6. **For extended questions**, analyse each person's need, then evaluate the menu and the kitchen routine, and reach a judgement on how safe it is.
:::

::: example Explain — allergy vs intolerance
Explain the difference between a food allergy and a food intolerance. (3)

A food allergy is a reaction by the immune system to a protein in a food (1), which can happen within minutes, can be caused by a tiny amount and can lead to life-threatening anaphylaxis (1). A food intolerance is a reaction of the digestive system, such as being unable to digest lactose; it does not involve the immune system, usually comes on more slowly and causes symptoms like bloating and diarrhoea that are unpleasant but not usually dangerous (1).
:::

::: example Explain — adapting for a nut allergy
A student is making a chicken satay stir fry with peanut sauce, finished with chopped cashews and cooked in groundnut oil. Explain how to adapt the recipe and preparation for a guest with a nut allergy. (4)

Replace the peanut sauce with a nut-free sauce, such as one made with sunflower seed butter, coconut milk, soy sauce and lime, so there is still a creamy, savoury flavour (1). Leave out the cashews and add toasted sesame seeds or crispy shallots for crunch, if the guest is not allergic to them (1). Groundnut oil is peanut oil, so use vegetable or rapeseed oil instead (1). Clean the wok and utensils thoroughly, cook the dish first and check all labels for "may contain nuts" warnings to prevent cross-contact (1).
:::

::: example Give — Fairtrade
Give two ways that buying Fairtrade bananas helps the farmers who grow them. (2)

Farmers receive at least the Fairtrade minimum price, which protects them when world prices fall (1). They also receive a Fairtrade Premium to invest in their community, for example in schools, clean water or better equipment (1).
:::

::: example Explain — adapting for coeliac disease
Explain how to adapt a chicken and leek pie with shortcrust pastry and a flour-thickened sauce for someone with coeliac disease. (4)

Wheat flour contains gluten, which damages the gut lining of someone with coeliac disease (1). Make the pastry with a gluten-free flour blend, adding a little xanthan gum or egg to help bind it, because without gluten the pastry is crumbly and hard to roll (1). Thicken the sauce with cornflour instead of wheat flour, and check that the stock cube is gluten-free (1). Use clean equipment and a separate rolling pin and board, as crumbs of ordinary flour would contaminate it (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — organic food
- Analyse the reasons why some consumers choose organic food.
- Evaluate whether organic food is worth its higher price. (8)

Consumers may choose organic food because it is grown with very few pesticides, so they believe residues are lower; because no synthetic fertilisers are used and crop rotation is used instead, which they see as better for soil and wildlife; because organic animals must have outdoor access and more space, so welfare is higher; and because GM ingredients are not allowed. Some also think organic food tastes better.

On the other hand, organic food usually costs noticeably more, because yields are lower and production is more labour-intensive, so it may not be affordable for low-income families. Scientific reviews have not found strong evidence that it is more nutritious, and conventional produce must already meet legal limits for pesticide residues. Lower yields mean more land is needed for the same amount of food, which is a problem for food security. Organic is worth the price for people who value animal welfare and wildlife and can afford it; for a family on a budget, eating more fruit and vegetables of any kind matters more for health than whether they are organic.

(Level 4, 7–8: several developed reasons, a balanced evaluation and a supported judgement.)
:::

::: q
Explain why an adrenaline auto-injector may be needed by someone with a severe egg allergy. (2)
---
Eating even a small amount of egg can trigger anaphylaxis, where the throat swells and blood pressure falls (1); adrenaline quickly reverses these effects, opening the airway, while emergency help is called (1).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why someone might choose to buy free-range eggs. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): better animal welfare, as hens can go outside and behave naturally; belief that the eggs taste better; ethical objection to caged systems; willingness to support farms with higher standards.
:::

::: q
Explain why oats may not be safe for someone with coeliac disease unless they are labelled gluten-free. (2)
---
Oats are often grown, harvested or milled alongside wheat, barley or rye (1), so they can be contaminated with gluten, which would damage the person's intestine (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how to adapt a Victoria sponge for someone with a milk allergy. (4)
---
Up to 4 from: replace butter with a dairy-free spread or vegetable oil (1), which still creams with sugar and traps air, or keeps the cake moist (1); fill with jam and a dairy-free buttercream made with dairy-free spread rather than butter or cream (1); check the labels of other ingredients such as chocolate or ready-made icing for milk (1); use clean bowls and utensils to avoid cross-contact (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of buying GM foods. (4)
---
Advantage: GM crops can be made resistant to pests or drought (1), so yields rise and food can be cheaper or more available (1). Or nutrients can be added, such as beta-carotene in Golden Rice (1), to reduce deficiency (1). Disadvantage: some people worry about unknown long-term effects on health (1), so they avoid it and prefer natural foods (1). Or GM genes may spread to wild plants (1), reducing biodiversity (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Allergy = immune system; intolerance = digestive system.** Mixing them up loses the mark every time.
- **Name the swap.** "Take out the nuts" is weak; "replace the almonds with toasted pumpkin seeds for crunch" scores.
- **Hidden sources** matter: groundnut oil is peanut, soy sauce contains wheat, pesto contains nuts, Worcestershire sauce contains fish.
- **Cooking does not destroy allergens**, and picking the allergen off is never safe.
- **Lactose intolerance is not a milk allergy.** Lactose-free milk still contains milk protein.
- For ethical choices, **evaluate**: cost, availability and evidence are the usual counter-arguments.
:::

::: test
Jack is organising a birthday party for 12 teenagers. Among the guests, one has a peanut allergy, one has coeliac disease, and one is lactose intolerant.
- Analyse the needs of these three guests.
- Evaluate how Jack could plan the menu and its preparation to keep all the guests safe. (12)
---
Levels of response:
- **10–12:** a detailed, accurate analysis of each condition, clearly distinguishing allergy from intolerance and coeliac disease; a well-developed evaluation of both menu and preparation, weighing practical difficulties, with a supported judgement.
- **7–9:** a clear analysis of all three needs with some detail; a sound evaluation of menu and preparation.
- **4–6:** some accurate points about the conditions; the evaluation is limited, mainly a list of foods to avoid.
- **1–3:** simple points; possible confusion between allergy and intolerance.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Peanut allergy: immune reaction; traces can trigger anaphylaxis; the guest may carry an adrenaline auto-injector. Hidden sources: satay, some sauces, groundnut oil, chocolate, cereal bars, "may contain" items.
- Coeliac disease: gluten in wheat, barley and rye damages the small intestine even in small amounts; not an emergency like anaphylaxis, but harmful. Hidden sources: soy sauce, stock cubes, breadcrumbs, beer-battered foods, cross-contamination from crumbs.
- Lactose intolerance: lactase deficiency causes bloating and diarrhoea; the amount eaten matters; hard cheese may be tolerated.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Menu: choose some dishes safe for everyone, e.g. baked potatoes, rice dishes, grilled chicken, salads, fruit, homemade salsa and corn tortilla chips; gluten-free pizza bases with dairy-free or lactose-free cheese.
- Cake: a gluten-free, dairy-free, nut-free cake is possible but texture may be drier; a fruit-based dessert or a separate labelled cake may be better.
- Preparation: clean all surfaces first; cook allergen-free food first; separate utensils, boards and toaster; fresh oil; cover and label dishes.
- Buying: read every label, including "may contain nuts".
- Serving: labelled buffet, separate serving spoons; the peanut-allergic guest's auto-injector on hand and an adult aware of what to do.
- Communication: ask the guests or their parents in advance about severity and safe brands.
- Limits: a buffet is hard to control, since teenagers may bring snacks or swap utensils; the peanut allergy is the most serious risk, so the safest option is a completely peanut-free party.
- Judgement: making most dishes suitable for everyone, removing peanuts entirely, and strict preparation routines keep all guests safe and included; labelling alone is not enough for a buffet.
:::
