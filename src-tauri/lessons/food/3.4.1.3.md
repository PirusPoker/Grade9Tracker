## What it is

Not all microorganisms are the enemy. Bread, yoghurt and cheese — some of the oldest foods in the world — only exist because **yeasts, bacteria and moulds** have been put to work under **controlled conditions**. The spec names three uses:
- **yeasts** to raise bread;
- **bacteria** in yoghurt and cheese production;
- **moulds** in blue cheese.

### Three kinds of microorganism in food

| Kind | What they do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Useful** | Deliberately added to make or change a food | Yeast in bread, bacteria in yoghurt, mould in blue cheese |
| **Spoilage** | Make food unpleasant — slime, mould, sour or alcoholic smells | Mould on a loaf, yeast fizzing in juice |
| **Pathogenic** | Cause illness, often with no visible sign | Salmonella, listeria, E. coli |

Production works by giving the **useful** microorganism exactly the conditions it needs — **warmth, moisture, food, time**, and the right **pH** — while controlling the others through **heat treatment**, **hygiene** and **acid**. Many of these processes are types of **fermentation**: microorganisms break down sugars into other substances (carbon dioxide, alcohol, lactic acid) that change the food.

### Yeast in bread making

Yeast is a single-celled fungus. In a warm, moist dough it **ferments** sugars — some added, most produced from the flour's starch by natural enzymes — to make **carbon dioxide** and **alcohol**.

**Stages and what each one does:**
1. **Mix** strong flour, yeast, salt and lukewarm water (plus oil or sugar for some doughs). The warm liquid activates the yeast; water lets gluten form.
2. **Knead** for about 10 minutes, by hand or with a dough hook or bread machine. This develops a stretchy **gluten network** that will trap gas.
3. **First prove**: leave the covered dough somewhere warm until about doubled in size. The yeast produces CO₂, which is trapped by the gluten, so the dough rises. Flavour develops too.
4. **Knock back**: punch or knead briefly to remove large gas pockets and even out the texture.
5. **Shape** the dough for its final use.
6. **Second prove** (short for flat breads and pizza).
7. **Bake** in a hot oven: the gas expands (**oven spring**), the yeast is **killed** at about 60 °C, alcohol evaporates, gluten **coagulates** and starch **gelatinises** to set the crumb, and the crust browns by **dextrinisation**.

**Shaping and finishing for different breads:**
- **Pizza**: dough rolled or stretched thin into a round, topped, and baked very hot so the base crisps quickly.
- **Calzone**: a pizza dough round, filled on one half, folded over into a half-moon and the edge **crimped** to seal so the filling does not leak; brushed with egg or oil and slashed to let steam out.
- **Flat breads**: rolled thin and cooked quickly in a very hot oven, pan or griddle. In **pitta**, steam puffs the dough into a pocket; **naan** is often shaped into a teardrop and cooked at very high heat.

### Bacteria in yoghurt

1. **Milk** is **heat-treated** to kill unwanted bacteria (and often homogenised, sometimes with added milk powder for a thicker yoghurt).
2. It is **cooled** to a warm temperature — roughly **37–44 °C** — which suits the chosen bacteria.
3. A **starter culture** of harmless bacteria, typically *Lactobacillus bulgaricus* and *Streptococcus thermophilus*, is added.
4. The milk is kept warm (**incubated**) for several hours. The bacteria **ferment lactose** (milk sugar) into **lactic acid**.
5. The acid **lowers the pH**, which makes the milk protein (**casein**) **coagulate**, so the milk **thickens** and sets; the acid also gives the **sharp, tangy flavour**.
6. The yoghurt is **chilled** to slow the bacteria and stop the fermentation, then fruit, sweeteners or flavourings may be added.

The acidity also helps **preserve** yoghurt: most pathogenic and spoilage bacteria cannot grow at such a low pH. "**Live**" or "**bio**" yoghurts still contain living bacteria.

### Bacteria and enzymes in cheese

1. **Milk** is usually **pasteurised** to kill harmful bacteria, then cooled.
2. A **starter culture** of lactic acid bacteria is added. They turn lactose into **lactic acid**, souring the milk and lowering its pH.
3. **Rennet** — an **enzyme** (traditionally from calves' stomachs; vegetarian rennet is made by microorganisms) — is added. It makes the casein **coagulate** into a soft solid called **curd**, separating it from the liquid **whey**.
4. The curd is **cut** into small pieces, so more whey drains out. For hard cheeses it is often **heated and stirred** to release more.
5. The whey is **drained** off.
6. The curd is **milled** (broken up) and **salted**. Salt adds flavour, draws out more moisture and helps preserve the cheese.
7. The curd is put into **moulds** and **pressed**. More pressing gives a harder, drier cheese.
8. The cheese is **ripened** (matured) in controlled temperature and humidity for weeks, months or years. **Bacteria and enzymes** break down the protein and fat, developing flavour and texture. Longer ripening gives a stronger flavour.

### Moulds in blue cheese

Blue cheeses such as Stilton, Roquefort and Gorgonzola are made with a **harmless mould**, usually a type of *Penicillium*.
- **Mould spores** are added to the milk or the curd.
- The cheese is made and shaped as normal, but not pressed hard, so it has an open texture.
- During ripening, the cheese is **pierced with stainless-steel needles**. The holes let **air (oxygen)** into the cheese, because the mould needs oxygen to grow.
- The mould grows along the channels, forming the **blue-green veins**. Its enzymes break down fat and protein, producing the **strong, tangy flavour** and a **soft, creamy** texture.

Soft white-rinded cheeses such as Brie and Camembert use a different mould that grows on the **surface**, forming the white rind.

### Why the controls matter

- **Heat-treating the milk** first removes pathogens and unwanted bacteria, so only the chosen culture grows.
- **Temperature** is controlled at every stage: warm to let the culture work, chilled to stop it.
- **Hygiene**: sterilised equipment and clean handling stop spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms getting in.
- **Soft and blue cheeses**, especially if made from **unpasteurised** milk, can carry **listeria**, so they are among the foods pregnant women are advised to avoid (unless cooked until steaming hot).

### Benefits to the consumer

- **Variety** of flavours and textures from simple ingredients (hundreds of cheeses, many breads).
- **Longer shelf life**: cheese and yoghurt keep much longer than fresh milk, because acid, salt and lower moisture slow spoilage.
- **Nutrition**: cheese and yoghurt are good sources of **protein** and **calcium**; bread supplies starch, fibre (wholemeal) and B vitamins.
- **Digestibility**: much of the lactose has been fermented, so some people with lactose intolerance tolerate yoghurt and hard cheese better than milk.
- **Drawbacks**: many cheeses are high in **saturated fat** and **salt**; some flavoured yoghurts are high in **free sugars**.

Other foods made with microorganisms include soy sauce, vinegar, salami, sauerkraut, kimchi and **mycoprotein** (Quorn), which is grown from a fungus.

::: terms
- **Fermentation**: the breakdown of sugars by microorganisms to produce substances such as carbon dioxide, alcohol or lactic acid.
- **Starter culture**: a measured amount of chosen harmless bacteria added to milk to begin making yoghurt or cheese.
- **Lactic acid**: the acid produced when bacteria ferment lactose, which sours, thickens and preserves yoghurt and cheese.
- **Lactose**: the natural sugar found in milk, fermented by bacteria in yoghurt and cheese making.
- **Casein**: the main protein in milk, which coagulates when acid or rennet is added.
- **Rennet**: an enzyme added in cheese making that makes milk protein coagulate into curds.
- **Curds**: the solid lumps of coagulated milk protein and fat that are made into cheese.
- **Whey**: the watery liquid that separates from the curds in cheese making.
- **Ripening**: storing cheese in controlled conditions so bacteria, moulds and enzymes develop its flavour and texture.
- **Incubation**: keeping a mixture warm for a set time so the added microorganisms can grow and work.
- **Proving**: leaving yeast dough in a warm place so carbon dioxide from fermentation makes it rise.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the microorganism** used (yeast, bacteria, mould) and, where the question asks, the specific type or the enzyme (rennet).
2. **Give the conditions** it is provided with: temperature, moisture, food, time.
3. **Explain what it produces**: carbon dioxide (bread), lactic acid (yoghurt, cheese), enzymes that break down fat and protein (ripening, blue veins).
4. **Link that to the change in the food**: rise, thickening, sharp flavour, curds, blue veins.
5. **For stage questions**, give each stage **and its purpose**. "Pierced — to let oxygen in so the mould can grow" earns more than "pierced".
6. **For extended questions**, analyse the process for each named food, then evaluate the benefits (and drawbacks) to the consumer.
:::

::: watch
- **Name the right microorganism**: yeast for bread, **bacteria** for yoghurt and cheese, **mould** for blue cheese. Rennet is an **enzyme**, not a microorganism.
- For yoghurt, the chain is **lactose → lactic acid → lower pH → casein coagulates → thick**. Missing the acid step is the usual gap.
- Blue cheese is **pierced to let oxygen in** for the mould — say why.
- Every stage answer needs its **purpose**, not just the action.
- Don't call useful microorganisms "bad bacteria"; distinguish **useful, spoilage and pathogenic**.
- In bread, the yeast is **killed** in the oven — it doesn't keep working.
