## What it is

Almost everything in a kitchen is about getting **heat** into food in a controlled way. This topic has two halves: **why** we cook food at all, and **how** heat actually travels from the hob, oven or grill into the food. The exam asks both, often inside a recipe context: "Explain how heat is transferred when…", "Give three reasons why this food is cooked".

### Why food is cooked

| Reason | What cooking does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **To make food safe to eat** | Heat kills most **pathogenic** (harmful) bacteria and destroys some natural toxins | Chicken cooked to 75 °C in the centre; dried red kidney beans must be boiled hard because raw they contain a toxin |
| **To develop flavour** | Browning reactions, caramelised sugars, melted fat and released aromas create new flavours | Roasted onions taste sweet; toasted spices smell stronger; seared meat tastes "meaty" |
| **To improve texture** | Heat softens tough fibres and starch, and sets proteins so mixtures hold their shape | Carrots soften; stewing steak becomes tender; a cake batter sets into a sponge |
| **To improve shelf life** | Heat kills microorganisms and **denatures enzymes** that cause spoilage | Jam, stewed fruit and cooked rice keep longer than the raw food (if cooled and stored correctly) |
| **To give variety in the diet** | One ingredient can be served many ways, so meals stay interesting | Potatoes boiled, mashed, roasted, baked, chipped or made into rösti |

Other reasons you can use as supporting points: cooked starch is **easier to digest**, cooking makes food **more appetising** (smell, colour), and hot food is warming and comforting.

### How cooking changes the sensory qualities of food

Preparation (cutting, marinating, mashing) and cooking together change how food looks, smells, tastes and feels. **Palatability** means how pleasant food is to eat overall.

| Quality | Typical changes when food is prepared and cooked |
|---|---|
| **Appearance** | Food may shrink (meat loses water and fat), swell (rice, pasta absorb water), rise (cakes, bread) or set (eggs) |
| **Colour** | Browning from dry heat (toast, roast potatoes, seared meat); green vegetables brighten when briefly cooked but turn dull olive-green if overcooked; red meat turns brown as its protein changes |
| **Flavour** | New savoury and sweet flavours from browning; sugars caramelise; overcooking gives bitter burnt flavours; seasoning, herbs and marinades add flavour |
| **Texture** | Vegetables soften; meat becomes tender (slow moist heat) or tough and dry (overcooked); crisp crusts form on baked and fried foods |
| **Smell** | Heat releases volatile aroma compounds, which is why baking bread or frying onions smells so strong |

### Heat transfer: conduction, convection and radiation

Heat always moves from something hotter to something cooler. It reaches food in three ways.

**Conduction** — heat passing **through a solid**, or between two solids that are **touching**. The particles in the hot part vibrate faster and pass the energy on to the particles next to them.
- The hob heats the **base of the pan**, the pan heats the **food touching it**, and heat then conducts slowly **through the food itself** to the centre.
- **Metals are good conductors**: copper and aluminium are excellent, which is why good stainless-steel pans often have a copper or aluminium base. Cast iron heats slowly but holds heat well, so it suits searing.
- **Poor conductors (insulators)** such as wood, silicone and plastic are used for handles and spoons so they stay cool to touch.
- Examples: frying an egg, searing a steak in a pan, dry-frying seeds, heat passing from the outside of a joint of meat to its centre.

**Convection** — heat moving **through a liquid or a gas** (air). The liquid or air near the heat source warms, **expands**, becomes **less dense** and **rises**; cooler, denser liquid or air sinks to take its place and is heated in turn. This circulation is a **convection current**.
- In a pan of water: heated water at the base rises, cooler water sinks, and the whole pan heats up. This is how food is cooked by **boiling, simmering, poaching** and **deep frying** (hot oil is a liquid too).
- In a conventional oven: hot air rises, so the **top of the oven is hotter** than the bottom. Food on the top shelf browns faster.
- In a **fan oven**: a fan forces the hot air round the oven, so heat is **more even** on every shelf and the oven heats up quicker. Recipes usually suggest a lower temperature for fan ovens.

**Radiation** — heat travelling as **infrared rays** in straight lines through air, with **no contact** and no need for a liquid. The rays heat the **surface** they hit; heat then conducts inwards.
- Examples: grilling, toasting, barbecuing, a salamander grill browning a gratin, the glowing element in a toaster.
- Only the side **facing** the heat source cooks, so grilled food must be **turned**.
- Radiant heat is intense at the surface, so it is ideal for browning, **charring** (peppers, courgettes), **toasting** (nuts, seeds, bread) and **grilling** thin foods such as halloumi, fish fillets and kebabs. Thick foods burn outside before the centre is cooked.

**Microwaves** work differently. Microwave energy penetrates a few centimetres into the food and makes **water molecules vibrate**, which produces heat inside the food; the heat then **conducts** to the rest. AQA sometimes groups microwaving under radiation, which is acceptable. Because heating is uneven, food can have **cold spots**, so it must be stirred or turned and left to **stand** so the heat evens out. Food does not brown in a standard microwave, because the surface never gets hot and dry enough.

### Most cooking uses more than one method

| Dish or method | How heat is transferred |
|---|---|
| **Boiling pasta** | Conduction through the pan base to the water; convection currents circulate the boiling water; conduction from the surface of the pasta to its centre |
| **Baking a loaf** | Convection of hot oven air; radiation from the hot oven walls; conduction through the tin and from the crust into the crumb |
| **Grilling a burger** | Radiation from the grill to the surface; conduction from the surface to the centre |
| **Stir frying** | Mainly conduction from the hot wok and thin layer of oil; the food is kept moving so every piece touches the hot metal |
| **Steaming vegetables** | Convection of steam from the boiling water; the steam condenses on the food and gives up its heat |
| **A cheese sauce topped and grilled** | Conduction and convection cook the sauce in the pan; radiation from the grill browns the cheese topping |

### Sauce making and the need for agitation

When a starch-thickened sauce is heated, heat **conducts** through the pan base into the liquid at the bottom, and **convection** currents move heated liquid round the pan. But starch granules are **heavier than the liquid** and sink. If the sauce is not stirred, they settle on the hottest part — the pan base — where they swell and stick together into **lumps**, then **scorch**. Stirring (**agitation**) keeps the granules **suspended** so they swell evenly, spreads the heat so there are no hot spots, and gives a smooth, glossy sauce. Use a wooden spoon or a balloon whisk, reaching into the corners of the pan, and keep the heat moderate. (The chemistry of the thickening is gelatinisation — see 3.3.2.2.)

::: terms
- **Palatability**: how pleasant and appetising a food is to eat overall, judged by its look, smell, taste and texture.
- **Pathogenic bacteria**: bacteria that cause illness; thorough cooking kills most of them.
- **Conduction**: transfer of heat through a solid, or between solids in contact, as vibrating particles pass energy to their neighbours.
- **Convection**: transfer of heat through a liquid or gas, as heated fluid expands, rises and is replaced by cooler, denser fluid.
- **Convection current**: the circular flow of rising hot liquid or air and sinking cooler liquid or air that spreads heat.
- **Radiation**: transfer of heat as infrared rays that travel in straight lines and heat the surface they strike, with no contact needed.
- **Conductor**: a material, usually a metal such as copper or aluminium, that lets heat pass through it quickly.
- **Insulator**: a poor conductor, such as wood or plastic, used for handles and spoons so they stay cool.
- **Agitation**: stirring or whisking a mixture as it heats so heat spreads evenly and starch or solids do not settle and burn.
- **Microwave cooking**: heating in which microwave energy makes water molecules in food vibrate, producing heat that then conducts inwards.
- **Fan oven**: an oven in which a fan circulates hot air so the temperature is even throughout and on every shelf.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the dish and find every heat source.** Hob, oven, grill or microwave? Many dishes use two (a sauce made on the hob, then grilled).
2. **Name the method of heat transfer for each stage**, and say where the heat goes: "conduction from the hob through the pan base into the sauce".
3. **Explain the mechanism in one linked sentence**: particles vibrating (conduction), hot fluid rising and cool fluid sinking (convection), infrared rays hitting the surface (radiation).
4. **Link it to the result in the food**: even cooking, browning, scorching, lumps, a raw centre.
5. **For "reasons for cooking" questions**, give a different reason each time (safety, flavour, texture, shelf life, variety) and tie each to the named food.
6. **For 8-mark analyse/evaluate questions**, analyse each stage of the dish, then judge whether the method chosen gives the best result and what could go wrong (uneven heating, burnt outside and raw centre).
:::

::: example Explain — boiling rice
Explain how heat is transferred to rice when it is boiled in a pan. (3)

Heat from the hob passes by conduction through the metal base of the pan into the water (1). The heated water at the bottom rises and cooler water sinks, setting up convection currents that carry heat to all the rice grains (1). Heat then passes by conduction from the surface of each grain to its centre, so the starch inside softens and swells (1).
:::

::: example Give — reasons for cooking
Give three reasons why chicken thighs are cooked before being eaten. (3)

To kill harmful bacteria such as salmonella and campylobacter, making the chicken safe to eat (1). To develop flavour, as the skin browns and the fat renders (1). To improve the texture, as the connective tissue softens and the meat becomes tender enough to chew (1).
:::

::: example Explain — grilling
Explain why kebabs of halloumi and peppers need to be turned during grilling. (3)

The grill cooks by radiation: infrared rays travel in straight lines from the element (1) and only heat the surface facing the grill (1). Turning the kebabs lets every side receive radiant heat, so they brown and char evenly and are cooked all the way round instead of burnt on top and pale underneath (1).
:::

::: example Explain — agitation in a sauce
A student made a parsley sauce by the all-in-one method but did not stir it. The sauce was lumpy and had burnt on the base of the pan. Explain why. (4)

Heat passed by conduction from the hob through the pan base, so the liquid at the bottom was the hottest (1). The starch granules are heavier than the liquid and sank to the base rather than staying suspended (1). There they swelled and stuck together into lumps instead of thickening the sauce evenly (1). With no agitation to move them away from the hot base, they overheated and scorched (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — a baked dish
A café bakes a tray of vegetable lasagne in a conventional (non-fan) oven.
- Analyse how heat is transferred to the lasagne.
- Evaluate whether a fan oven would give a better result. (8)

Analysis: the oven element heats the air, which rises and circulates as convection currents; the top of the oven is hottest. The oven walls radiate heat onto the dish. Heat conducts through the metal or ceramic dish into the base layers, then slowly through the pasta, sauce and vegetables to the centre, which is why a deep lasagne takes a long time to cook through. The cheese on top browns because it receives the most direct heat. Evaluation: a fan oven circulates the air, so the temperature is even and the lasagne cooks evenly whatever the shelf, which matters when a café bakes several trays at once. It heats faster and can run at a lower temperature, saving energy. However, the moving air can dry out and over-brown the top before the centre is hot, so the café might cover the dish with foil for most of the time. Overall a fan oven is better for batch cooking, as long as the centre is checked with a probe to reach 75 °C. (Level 4: 7–8 marks — detailed analysis of all three methods, with a balanced, justified judgement.)
:::

Explain why many saucepans have wooden or plastic handles. (2)
---
Wood and plastic are poor conductors/insulators (1), so heat does not pass along the handle and the cook can hold it without being burnt (1).

::: q
Explain how heat is transferred through the air in a conventional oven. (3)
---
The element heats the air near it (1); hot air expands, becomes less dense and rises while cooler air sinks (1); this convection current circulates heat, so the top of the oven is the hottest (1).

::: q
Give two ways in which cooking changes the texture of vegetables. (2)
---
Any two: they soften as cell walls/fibres break down; starchy vegetables become fluffy (e.g. potato); roasting gives a crisp outside; overcooking makes them mushy/soggy.

::: q
Explain why food heated in a microwave oven should be stirred and left to stand. (3)
---
Microwaves heat unevenly, leaving cold spots (1); cold spots may not reach a temperature high enough to kill bacteria (1); stirring and standing lets heat conduct through the food so it is evenly hot (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a thick pork chop can burn on the outside but be undercooked in the centre when grilled at a very high heat. (3)
---
Radiant heat from the grill heats only the surface intensely (1); heat reaches the centre slowly by conduction through the meat (1); the surface overcooks/burns before enough heat has conducted to the centre, which may be unsafe (1). (Credit: reduce the heat or move further from the grill, or finish in the oven.)
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why onions are cooked before being added to a bolognese sauce. (4)
---
To develop flavour (1) — heat caramelises their natural sugars, making them sweeter and less harsh (1). To improve texture (1) — the onion softens so it is not crunchy or raw in the finished sauce (1).

::: watch
- Name **more than one** method when a dish uses more than one. Boiling is conduction **and** convection; grilling is radiation **then** conduction inwards.
- Don't just name the method — say **where the heat goes** and **how** (vibrating particles, rising hot liquid, infrared rays).
- Convection needs a **liquid or gas**. Heat passing through a solid pan is always conduction.
- "To make it taste nice" is too vague. Name the change: browning, caramelised sugars, released aroma.
- In sauce questions, the reason for stirring is that **starch sinks** onto the hot base. Say so.
- Radiation only heats the **surface facing** the heat: that is why food is turned under a grill.
:::
