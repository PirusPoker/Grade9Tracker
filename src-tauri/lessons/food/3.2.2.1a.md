## What it is

**Vitamins** are **micronutrients**: needed in tiny amounts (milligrams, mg, or micrograms, µg), yet essential for health. They give no energy but control body processes. They are grouped by what they dissolve in.

**Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K)** dissolve in fat. That has three consequences:
1. They are found mainly in **fatty foods**, and are absorbed with fat, so a very low-fat diet can lead to a shortage.
2. The body can **store** them, mostly in the **liver** and fat tissue, so a daily intake is not essential if the stores are full.
3. Because they are stored, they can **build up to harmful levels** if too much is taken, usually from supplements or from eating liver very often.

They are not lost into cooking water, and they are fairly stable to normal cooking heat, unlike vitamin C.

**Vitamin A**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Forms** | **retinol**, the ready-made form, from animal foods; **beta-carotene**, an orange pigment in plants that the body converts to vitamin A |
| **Functions** | makes a pigment in the eye needed to see in **dim light**; keeps **skin** and the moist linings of the throat, lungs and gut healthy; supports the **immune system**; beta-carotene is an **antioxidant** |
| **Sources** | retinol: **liver**, oily fish, eggs, whole milk, cheese, butter; margarine and spreads are fortified with it. Beta-carotene: **carrots, sweet potatoes, butternut squash, apricots, mangoes**, red peppers and dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach and kale |
| **Deficiency** | **night blindness** (cannot see in dim light), which in severe, long-term deficiency can progress to permanent blindness, a serious problem in some low-income countries; dry skin; more infections |
| **Excess** | stored in the liver and can become toxic, damaging the liver; long-term high intakes may weaken bones; in **pregnancy** too much retinol can harm the unborn baby, so **pregnant women are advised to avoid liver, liver pâté and vitamin A supplements** |
| **Reference value** | UK adult reference nutrient intake about **700 µg a day for men and 600 µg for women**; most people get this from a varied diet |

**Vitamin D**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | helps the body **absorb calcium** (and phosphorus) from food, so it is needed for strong **bones and teeth**; also supports the muscles and immune system |
| **Sources** | the main source is **sunlight on the skin**: the skin makes vitamin D in summer sunshine. Food sources are few: **oily fish** (salmon, mackerel, sardines, trout), **egg yolk**, red meat and liver, and **fortified foods** such as fat spreads and some breakfast cereals and plant milks |
| **Deficiency** | calcium is not absorbed properly. In children the bones stay soft and bend under the body's weight: **rickets** (bowed legs). In adults the bones soften and ache: **osteomalacia**. Over years, low vitamin D and calcium add to the risk of **osteoporosis** |
| **Excess** | from supplements only (the sun cannot cause it): too much calcium is absorbed, which can weaken bones and damage the kidneys and heart |
| **Reference value** | **10 µg a day** for everyone over one year old. UK advice is that everyone should consider a **10 µg daily supplement in autumn and winter**, when sunlight is too weak for the skin to make enough |

**Who is at risk of low vitamin D:** people who are rarely outdoors (housebound older adults, care-home residents); people who cover most of their skin when outside; people with darker skin, who make vitamin D more slowly in weak UK sunlight; babies and young children; pregnant and breastfeeding women. Older people's skin also makes vitamin D less efficiently. These groups are advised to take a supplement all year.

**Vitamin E**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | an **antioxidant** that protects **cell membranes** from damage by free radicals; supports healthy skin and eyes and the immune system |
| **Sources** | **vegetable oils** (sunflower, rapeseed, olive), **nuts and seeds**, wheatgerm and wholegrain cereals, avocado |
| **Deficiency** | rare; can cause nerve and muscle damage and weaker immunity |
| **Excess** | unlikely from food; very high supplement doses are not advised |
| **Reference value** | no fixed UK reference nutrient intake; a "safe intake" is set, and a varied diet provides enough |

**Vitamin K**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | needed to make the proteins that help **blood to clot**, so wounds stop bleeding; also has a role in bone health |
| **Sources** | **green leafy vegetables** (spinach, kale, broccoli, cabbage), vegetable oils, cereals; some is **made by bacteria in the large intestine** |
| **Deficiency** | rare in adults; blood takes longer to clot, so bleeding and bruising. Newborn babies have little vitamin K, so in the UK they are usually offered a vitamin K dose at birth |
| **Excess** | rare from food; people on some blood-thinning medicines are told to keep their vitamin K intake steady |
| **Reference value** | no fixed UK reference nutrient intake; a "safe intake" is based on body weight |

**Antioxidants (links to 3.2.2.1b).** Vitamins **A (as beta-carotene), C and E** act as antioxidants. They neutralise **free radicals**, reactive molecules that damage cells, which may help protect against heart disease and some cancers. Brightly coloured fruit and vegetables, nuts, seeds and vegetable oils are the best sources.

**In the kitchen: filleting and slicing fish (S2).** Oily fish is one of the few foods rich in vitamin D, and it also gives vitamin A and omega-3 fats. To fillet a round fish such as a mackerel, use a sharp, flexible filleting knife on a **blue board** (raw fish). Cut behind the head down to the backbone, then run the knife along the bones towards the tail, keeping the blade flat against them, so the fillet comes away cleanly with little flesh wasted. Check for pin bones with fingertips and remove them with tweezers. Slice fillets into even portions so they cook evenly. Grilling, baking or steaming the fish keeps the fat-soluble vitamins in, because they are not lost into water.

**Fat-soluble vitamins and low-fat diets.** A diet very low in fat, or a condition that stops fat being absorbed, can reduce the absorption of A, D, E and K. That is one reason spreads and low-fat spreads are fortified with vitamins A and D, and why a salad of carrots or spinach is best eaten with a little oil-based dressing.

::: terms
- **Micronutrient**: a nutrient needed in very small amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms, such as vitamins and minerals.
- **Fat-soluble vitamin**: a vitamin that dissolves in fat and can be stored in the body; vitamins A, D, E and K.
- **Retinol**: the ready-made form of vitamin A found in animal foods such as liver, eggs and oily fish.
- **Beta-carotene**: an orange plant pigment that the body turns into vitamin A; also an antioxidant.
- **Night blindness**: difficulty seeing in dim light, caused by a lack of vitamin A.
- **Rickets**: a childhood condition in which bones stay soft and bend, caused by a lack of vitamin D and calcium.
- **Osteomalacia**: softening of the bones in adults, causing bone pain, caused by a lack of vitamin D.
- **Antioxidant**: a substance that protects body cells from damage by free radicals, such as vitamins A, C and E.
- **Fortified food**: a food with extra nutrients added by the manufacturer, such as vitamin D in spreads and cereals.
- **Toxicity**: harm caused when too much of a nutrient builds up in the body, a risk with stored vitamins A and D.
:::

::: method
1. **Match vitamin to function carefully:** A = vision in dim light and skin; D = calcium absorption for bones; E = antioxidant protecting cells; K = blood clotting.
2. **Always link vitamin D to calcium.** "Vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium, which is needed for strong bones" is a two-mark chain.
3. **For sources, give a specific food,** and say whether it is an animal (retinol) or plant (beta-carotene) source of vitamin A when asked.
4. **For excess, explain storage:** fat-soluble vitamins are stored in the liver, so they can build up to toxic levels.
5. **For at-risk groups, give the group and the reason:** housebound older people → little sunlight on skin → little vitamin D made.
6. **In extended answers, analyse causes (sunlight, diet, age, lifestyle) then evaluate advice** with named foods, the supplement advice and a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain — vitamin D and bones
Explain why vitamin D is important for bone health. (3)

Vitamin D is needed for the body to absorb calcium from food in the intestine (1). Calcium is used to build and maintain strong, hard bones and teeth (1). Without enough vitamin D, even a calcium-rich diet cannot keep bones strong, so they soften, leading to rickets in children or osteomalacia in adults (1).
:::

::: example Give — sources of vitamin A
Give two plant sources and one animal source of vitamin A. (3)

Plant sources (as beta-carotene): carrots (1); sweet potato, mango, spinach or red peppers (1). Animal source (as retinol): liver, eggs, oily fish, cheese or whole milk (1).
:::

::: example Explain — pregnancy and liver
Explain why pregnant women are advised not to eat liver or liver pâté. (2)

Liver is very high in retinol, the animal form of vitamin A (1). Vitamin A is fat-soluble and stored in the body, so large amounts can build up and may harm the development of the unborn baby (1).
:::

::: example Explain — fat-soluble vitamins in cooking
Explain why steaming or boiling spinach causes little loss of vitamin K, but a larger loss of vitamin C. (2)

Vitamin K is fat-soluble, so it does not dissolve into the cooking water and is fairly stable to heat (1). Vitamin C is water-soluble and heat-sensitive, so it leaches into the water and is partly destroyed (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — who lacks vitamin D
Explain why a teenager who spends most of their free time indoors gaming may be short of vitamin D, and suggest how to improve their intake. (4)

The main source of vitamin D is sunlight on the skin, and spending most of the time indoors means little is made (1). Few foods contain much vitamin D, so diet alone rarely makes up the gap (1). They could spend time outdoors in summer daylight, such as walking to school or playing sport (1), eat oily fish such as sardines or salmon, eggs and fortified cereals, and take a 10 µg supplement in autumn and winter (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why too much vitamin A can be harmful, but too much vitamin C is less likely to be. (3)
---

::: q
Explain why fat spreads in the UK are fortified with vitamins A and D. (2)
---
Many people, especially those who get little sun, do not get enough vitamin D from food, and fortification of a widely eaten food increases intake (1); spreads replace butter, which naturally contains vitamins A and D, so fortification keeps the vitamin content similar and helps prevent rickets and night blindness (1).

Describe how to fillet a mackerel safely and with little waste. (4)
---
Use a sharp, flexible filleting knife on a blue (raw fish) chopping board, with a damp cloth underneath so it does not slip (1); cut behind the head down to the backbone (1); keep the blade flat against the bones and cut towards the tail, so the fillet comes away with little flesh left on the bones (1); feel for pin bones and remove them with tweezers, then wash the board and knife in hot soapy water (1).

::: watch
- **Vitamin D helps absorb calcium.** It does not make bones on its own; miss the calcium link and you miss the mark.
- **Retinol (animal) and beta-carotene (plant)** are both vitamin A. Carrots don't contain retinol.
- Fat-soluble vitamins are **stored**, so **excess is a real risk**, especially A in pregnancy. Water-soluble vitamins are mostly excreted.
- **Sunlight** is the main source of vitamin D. Diet-only answers are incomplete.
- Don't confuse **rickets** (children, vitamin D) with **scurvy** (vitamin C) or **anaemia** (iron).
:::

Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis of several reasons, each linked to how vitamin D is obtained and to bone health (calcium absorption, osteomalacia, osteoporosis, falls and fractures); the evaluation weighs specific dietary and lifestyle advice, including the practicality for older people, and reaches a justified judgement.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Older skin makes vitamin D less efficiently from sunlight.
- Many older people are housebound, in care homes or less mobile, so they spend little time outdoors; they may also cover up more.
- From about October to early March, UK sunlight is too weak for the skin to make enough vitamin D.
- Appetite falls with age, so they may eat less oily fish, eggs and fortified foods; some may live alone and cook less.
- Consequences: less calcium absorbed, osteomalacia (bone pain, muscle weakness), faster bone loss and osteoporosis, so a higher risk of falls and fractures such as a broken hip.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- A 10 µg daily vitamin D supplement all year for those rarely outdoors is the most reliable measure, as it does not depend on appetite or weather; cheap and easy, but they must remember to take it.
- Oily fish such as sardines on toast or tinned salmon twice a week, eggs, fortified cereals and spreads: useful, but it is hard to meet needs from food alone.
- Sit or walk outside in daylight in summer with arms and face uncovered for short periods; limited by mobility, weather and care-home routines.
- Combine with calcium-rich foods (milk, cheese, yoghurt, fortified plant milks) and gentle weight-bearing exercise to protect bones.
- Judgement: diet and sunlight help but are unlikely to be enough on their own for many older adults, so a supplement combined with calcium-rich food and gentle activity is the most effective advice.
