## What it is

**Carbohydrates** are macronutrients made by plants from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide. Their main job in the diet is to supply **energy**: 4 kcal per gram. They are grouped by the size of their molecules.

| Group | What it is | Examples and sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Monosaccharides** (single sugars) | one sugar unit; absorbed straight into the blood | **glucose** (fruit, honey, sports drinks), **fructose** (fruit, honey), **galactose** (formed when lactose in milk is digested) |
| **Disaccharides** (double sugars) | two sugar units joined; digested into monosaccharides | **sucrose** = glucose + fructose (table sugar, from sugar cane and beet); **lactose** = glucose + galactose (milk); **maltose** = glucose + glucose (malted cereals, formed when starch is digested) |
| **Polysaccharides** (complex carbohydrates) | many sugar units in long chains | **starch** (bread, pasta, rice, potatoes, cereals, pulses); **dietary fibre** (wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetable skins, pulses, nuts); also pectin and glycogen |

**Functions**

- **Starch and sugars** are the body's **main source of energy** for movement, keeping warm, growth and the brain, which runs mainly on glucose.
- Carbohydrate **spares protein**: if there is enough carbohydrate, protein can be used for growth and repair instead of energy.
- Unused energy is stored briefly as glycogen in the liver and muscles, and any beyond that is converted to **body fat**.
- **Dietary fibre** is not digested and gives no usable energy. It adds bulk and holds water, so waste moves easily through the bowel; it makes meals **filling (satiety)**, which helps weight control; and **soluble fibre** (in oats, barley, beans and lentils) helps **lower blood cholesterol**.

**Starch versus sugars: the glycaemic effect (in simple terms).** Sugars are absorbed quickly and cause a fast rise in blood glucose, followed by a dip that can leave a person hungry and tired. Starches, especially wholegrain ones eaten with their fibre, are digested more slowly, so energy is released gradually and the person feels fuller for longer. This is why starchy wholegrain foods are the basis of a healthy diet.

**Free sugars and naturally occurring sugars**

- **Free sugars** are sugars **added** to foods and drinks by the manufacturer, the cook or the consumer, **plus** sugars naturally present in **honey, syrups, unsweetened fruit juices, smoothies and fruit purées**. Juicing or blending breaks the plant cells, so the sugar is released.
- Sugars **naturally inside whole fruit and vegetables**, and **lactose in milk**, are **not** counted as free sugars. They come packaged with fibre, water, vitamins or calcium and are less harmful to teeth.

**Deficiency and excess**

| | Effects |
|---|---|
| **Too little carbohydrate** | tiredness and lack of energy; weight loss; protein is used for energy instead of growth and repair; the body breaks down fat and muscle |
| **Too much carbohydrate overall** | positive energy balance, weight gain and obesity |
| **Too many free sugars** | **tooth decay (dental caries)**: bacteria in plaque feed on sugar and make acid that dissolves enamel; excess energy and weight gain; raised risk of **type 2 diabetes** linked to obesity; "empty calories" that crowd out nutrient-rich food |
| **Too little fibre** | **constipation**; straining can lead to piles and diverticular disease; a higher risk of bowel cancer; hunger soon after meals; higher cholesterol |
| **Very high fibre** (rare) | bloating and wind; can reduce the absorption of some minerals such as iron and calcium; too filling for young children to meet their energy needs |

**Dietary reference values**

- Carbohydrate should give about **50% of food energy**. Of this, about 45% should come from starches, lactose in milk and sugars in whole fruit, and **no more than 5% from free sugars**.
- For adults, 5% of energy is roughly **30 g of free sugars a day**, about seven sugar cubes. Children's limits are lower.
- **Fibre: 30 g a day for adults**, with lower targets for children (for example 25 g at 11–16). Most people in the UK eat well under their target.

**Ways to increase fibre**

| Change | Fibre gained from |
|---|---|
| Wholemeal or half-and-half flour in bread, pastry, pizza bases and crumbles | bran of the whole grain |
| Brown rice, wholewheat pasta, wholegrain cereals, porridge oats | whole grains |
| Leave skins on potatoes, apples and pears | cell walls in the skin |
| Add pulses (lentils, beans, chickpeas) to soups, casseroles and mince dishes | pulses are rich in fibre |
| Add oats, seeds or nuts to crumble toppings, flapjacks and muffins | oats (soluble fibre), seeds and nuts |
| Add extra vegetables and salad; fruit as dessert | fruit and vegetables |

**In the kitchen**

- **Starch to set a mixture (S12).** When a starch mixture such as a cornflour-thickened custard, a blancmange or a lemon meringue pie filling is heated, the starch gelatinises; as it cools it **sets** into a gel that can be cut or turned out. (The science is in 3.3.2.2.)
- **High-fibre bread rolls (S10).** Wholemeal flour contains bran, which cuts through the gluten strands and absorbs more water, so wholemeal dough needs a little more liquid, can take longer to **prove** (rise in a warm place until doubled) and gives a denser roll. Many recipes use half wholemeal and half strong white flour to keep a lighter texture while still raising the fibre.

::: terms
- **Monosaccharide**: a single sugar unit, such as glucose, fructose or galactose.
- **Disaccharide**: a sugar made of two units joined together, such as sucrose, lactose or maltose.
- **Polysaccharide**: a complex carbohydrate made of many sugar units, such as starch and dietary fibre.
- **Starch**: a polysaccharide that plants use to store energy, found in cereals, potatoes, rice and pasta.
- **Dietary fibre**: the plant material that is not digested, which adds bulk and keeps the bowel healthy.
- **Free sugars**: sugars added to food and drink plus those in honey, syrups, fruit juices and smoothies.
- **Naturally occurring sugars**: sugars held within whole fruit, vegetables and milk, which are not counted as free sugars.
- **Satiety**: the feeling of fullness after eating, increased by fibre and starchy foods.
- **Dental caries**: tooth decay caused when plaque bacteria turn sugar into acid that dissolves enamel.
- **Glycaemic effect**: how quickly a food raises blood glucose; sugars raise it quickly, wholegrain starches slowly.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify precisely:** mono (glucose, fructose, galactose), di (sucrose, lactose, maltose), poly (starch, fibre).
2. **For functions, keep starch and fibre apart.** Starch and sugar give energy; fibre gives no energy but keeps the bowel healthy and makes you feel full.
3. **For free sugars, say what counts and what doesn't:** added sugar, honey, syrups and juices count; whole fruit and milk sugar don't. Give the 5% limit.
4. **For effects, write a chain:** too little fibre → hard, small stools → constipation → risk of bowel disease.
5. **For "increase fibre" questions, name the swap in the given dish** and the source of fibre it adds.
6. **In extended answers, go through the day meal by meal,** naming the foods, then evaluate specific changes against the 30 g fibre and 5% free-sugar guidelines.
:::

::: example Explain — free sugars
Explain the difference between free sugars and naturally occurring sugars. (2)

Free sugars are sugars added to food or drink, plus the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice, where they have been released from the plant cells (1). Naturally occurring sugars are held inside the cells of whole fruit and vegetables, or are lactose in milk, and are not counted as free sugars (1).
:::

::: example Explain — fibre
Explain why dietary fibre is important in the diet. (3)

Fibre adds bulk and absorbs water, so faeces are soft and pass easily, preventing constipation (1). It keeps the bowel healthy, reducing the risk of diverticular disease and bowel cancer (1). It makes meals more filling, so people are less likely to overeat, and soluble fibre from oats and pulses helps lower blood cholesterol (1).
:::

::: example Suggest — adding fibre
Suggest ways to increase the fibre in a tuna pasta bake and a fruit crumble. (4)

In the pasta bake: use wholewheat pasta instead of white pasta, as it contains the bran of the grain (1); add vegetables such as broccoli, peas and peppers, or a tin of beans, which add fibre from plant cell walls and pulses (1). In the crumble: replace half the white flour in the topping with wholemeal flour and add rolled oats, which contain soluble fibre (1); leave the skins on the apples or pears and add dried fruit or berries (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — free sugars in a drink
A 330 ml can of cola contains 35 g of sugar. An adult's maximum is 30 g of free sugars a day. Calculate the can's sugar as a percentage of the daily maximum. (2)

35 ÷ 30 × 100 (1) = 117% (to the nearest whole number), so one can is more than the whole day's limit (1).
:::

::: example Explain — wholemeal rolls
Explain why bread rolls made with wholemeal flour are often smaller and denser than rolls made with strong white flour. (3)

Wholemeal flour contains the bran of the wheat grain (1); the sharp bran particles cut the gluten strands and interfere with the gluten network (1), so less carbon dioxide from the yeast is trapped during proving and the rolls rise less, giving a denser texture (1).
:::

---

Explain how free sugars cause tooth decay. (3)
---
Bacteria in plaque on the teeth feed on the sugar (1); they produce acid (1); the acid dissolves (demineralises) the tooth enamel, forming cavities (1).

---

::: q
Explain how a cornflour mixture sets a custard-style filling for a tart. (2)
---
When heated with milk, the starch granules absorb liquid, swell and burst, thickening the mixture (gelatinisation) (1); as it cools, the starch forms a gel that holds its shape, so the filling sets (1).
:::

::: q

::: watch
- **Fibre gives no energy.** "Slow-release energy" is starch, not fibre.
- **Free sugars include fruit juice, smoothies and honey.** Whole fruit and milk sugar are not free sugars.
- **Lactose** is the milk sugar; **sucrose** is table sugar. Keep them straight in MCQs.
- Tooth decay needs the **bacteria → acid → enamel** chain.
- In "increase fibre" answers, change **named ingredients** in the given dish, not "eat more fibre".
:::

::: test
A 13-year-old's usual day is shown below.
- **Breakfast:** a bowl of frosted cereal with milk; a glass of orange juice.
- **Break:** a chocolate muffin.
- **Lunch:** white bread ham sandwich; a packet of crisps; a flavoured yoghurt; a can of lemonade.
- **After school:** a bag of sweets.
- **Evening meal:** chicken nuggets, white rice and sweetcorn; ice cream.

- Analyse the carbohydrate content of this day's meals.
- Evaluate how the teenager's diet could be changed to meet the guidelines for free sugars and fibre.

(8)
---
Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis that identifies named sources of free sugars, starch and fibre, linked to guidelines (5% free sugars, fibre targets) and to health effects; the evaluation gives specific, realistic swaps and weighs how acceptable they would be to a teenager, reaching a judgement.
- **Level 3 (5–6):** clear analysis of several foods with some reference to guidelines, and sound changes with reasons.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** some foods identified, limited explanation; changes are general.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** a list of sugary foods with little reasoning.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Free sugars: frosted cereal coating, orange juice, muffin, flavoured yoghurt, lemonade, sweets, ice cream. The total is likely several times the limit for this age, raising the risk of tooth decay, weight gain and later type 2 diabetes.
- Starch mostly refined: white bread, white rice, frosted cereal, nugget coating; little slow-release energy.
- Fibre is very low: no wholegrains, no pulses, few fruit and vegetables (only sweetcorn); risk of constipation and poor satiety, making snacking more likely.
- Milk and yoghurt provide lactose (not free sugar) and calcium, which is useful.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Breakfast: porridge or wholegrain cereal with milk and fruit; whole orange instead of juice (keeps fibre, removes free sugar).
- Break: fruit, plain popcorn or a wholemeal toasted muffin instead of a chocolate muffin.
- Lunch: wholemeal bread; add salad; plain yoghurt with fruit; water or milk instead of lemonade.
- Evening: brown rice, homemade oven-baked chicken strips in wholemeal crumbs, extra vegetables; fruit-based dessert.
- Limit sweets to an occasional treat, eaten at mealtimes to protect teeth.
- Judgement: swapping drinks gives the biggest cut in free sugars for the least effort; fibre rises most by switching to wholegrain versions of foods already eaten, which a teenager is more likely to accept than a total change of diet.
:::
