## What it is

**Food security** exists when **all people, at all times**, can obtain **enough** food that is **safe**, **nutritious** and **affordable**, and that meets their dietary needs and preferences so they can live an active, healthy life. Each part of the definition matters, and each can earn credit:

| Part | What it means | Example of failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Availability** | Enough food is produced or imported | Harvest failure after drought |
| **Access** | People can afford and reach it | Low income, high prices, no shop nearby |
| **Utilisation** (safe and nutritious) | Food is safe, varied and nutritious, and people can prepare it | Contaminated water; a diet of cheap, energy-dense food lacking vitamins and minerals |
| **Stability** | Supply is reliable over time, not just today | Price spikes, conflict or floods cutting supply |

**Food insecurity** is the opposite: not being sure of having enough to eat, skipping meals, or eating less nutritious food because of cost. In the UK, it shows up as **food poverty**: households cutting back on food to pay bills, and a large growth in **food banks**, which hand out emergency parcels to people referred by agencies such as doctors or advice services.

### The challenge

The world's population is about **8 billion** and is projected to rise towards **10 billion by 2050**. The challenge is to provide all those people with a **sustainable, secure** supply of **safe, nutritious, affordable, high-quality** food, without destroying the land, water, climate and wildlife that future food production depends on.

### The threats

- **Climate change and global warming.** **Global warming** is the rise in the Earth's average temperature caused by greenhouse gases. **Climate change** is the wider, long-term shift in weather patterns that results. For food:
  - hotter summers and heatwaves reduce the yields of crops such as wheat and maize;
  - growing seasons and the areas where crops grow shift;
  - pests and diseases spread to new regions;
  - warming seas move fish stocks;
  - rising sea levels flood coastal farmland with salt water.
- **Drought**: a long period of low rainfall. Crops fail, animals die or are sold off, soil dries and erodes, and prices rise. It hits hardest where farming depends on rain rather than irrigation.
- **Flooding**: crops are destroyed, soil is washed away, stored food is spoiled, roads are cut so food cannot be distributed, and water supplies can be contaminated, causing illness.
- **Insufficient land for growing food**: land is lost to towns and roads, to soil erosion and **desertification**, and to crops for biofuels. A large share of crops is fed to livestock rather than eaten by people, and clearing forests for more farmland harms the climate further.
- **Sustainability of food sources**: overfishing, soil exhaustion from intensive farming, over-use of water for irrigation and loss of pollinating insects all threaten future supplies.
- **Availability of food**: war and conflict, trade restrictions, poor roads and storage, and **post-harvest losses** (food that spoils before reaching market, common in low-income countries without refrigeration). The war in Ukraine from 2022 disrupted exports of wheat and sunflower oil and pushed up prices far beyond the region.
- **Food waste**: a large share of the food produced is lost or wasted between farm and fork, while millions go hungry (see 3.6.1.2).

### Local and global markets and communities

Food is traded globally, so a problem in one place affects prices everywhere. A drought in a major wheat-exporting country raises bread prices in countries that import wheat, and the poorest households, who spend a large share of their income on food, are hit hardest.
- **Global effects:** price spikes; countries banning exports to protect their own supply; poorer countries unable to compete to buy food; farmers growing **cash crops** (coffee, cocoa, flowers) for export rather than food for local people.
- **Local effects:** small farmers losing their income when world prices fall; UK households facing higher shopping bills; **food deserts** (areas without affordable fresh food); dependence on food banks; community responses such as community fridges, allotments, lunch clubs and "pay as you feel" cafés.

### The solutions and their limits

| Solution | How it helps | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce food waste** | More food is available without extra land; saves money | Needs change at every stage, from farm to home |
| **Eat more plant protein**, less meat | Plants feed people directly; beef needs far more land, water and feed per gram of protein | Cultural habits; people enjoy meat; some nutrients (B12, iron) need planning |
| **GM and improved crops** | Drought, flood and pest-resistant varieties give more reliable yields; added nutrients (Golden Rice with beta-carotene) | Consumer distrust; seed costs and control by large companies; environmental concerns |
| **Fairtrade** | A fair minimum price and a premium give farmers a stable income to invest in land, schools and water; stronger farming communities | Covers a small share of farmers; costs shoppers more; mostly cash crops, not staple foods |
| **Sustainable farming and fishing** | Protects soil, water and fish stocks so they keep producing | Lower yields or higher costs in the short term |
| **Better water use** (drip irrigation, rainwater storage) | Crops survive dry periods | Investment and infrastructure needed |
| **Better storage and transport** in developing countries | Cuts post-harvest losses | Cost; needs power and roads |
| **Local growing**: allotments, community gardens, urban and vertical farms | Fresh, cheap food; less reliance on imports; skills and community | Small scale; vertical farms use lots of energy |
| **New proteins**: insects, lab-grown (cultivated) meat, mycoprotein | High protein from little land, water and feed | Acceptability, cost, regulation (context only) |
| **Education and support** | Cooking and budgeting skills help families eat well on low incomes; food banks give emergency help | Food banks treat the symptom, not the cause (low income) |

No single solution is enough. Food security needs producing more **sustainably**, wasting less, sharing it more **fairly**, and protecting supply against shocks.

::: terms
- **Food security**: when all people at all times have access to enough safe, nutritious, affordable food to lead an active and healthy life.
- **Food insecurity**: being without reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food.
- **Food poverty**: being unable to afford or access a healthy diet, often because of low income.
- **Food bank**: a charity that gives emergency food parcels to people in crisis.
- **Global warming**: the rise in the Earth's average temperature caused by greenhouse gases trapping heat.
- **Climate change**: long-term changes in temperature and weather patterns, including more extreme heat, drought and flooding.
- **Drought**: a long period of unusually low rainfall that causes water shortages and crop failure.
- **Desertification**: the process by which fertile land becomes desert, through drought, over-grazing or poor farming.
- **Post-harvest loss**: food that is lost between harvest and sale, for example through poor storage or transport.
- **Cash crop**: a crop grown to sell for money, often for export, rather than to feed the farmer's family.
:::

::: method
1. **Define food security in full**: enough, safe, nutritious, affordable, for all, at all times.
2. **Name the threat and explain the chain**: cause, then the effect on crops, animals or supply, then the effect on price and access.
3. **Distinguish local from global** effects when a question asks about markets and communities.
4. **For solutions**, explain how each helps *and* its limits.
5. **In the 12-mark question**, cover several threats, weigh several solutions, and conclude which combination works best and why.
:::

::: example Explain — food security
Explain the term food security. (2)

Food security is when all people, at all times, can get enough food (1) that is safe, nutritious and affordable to meet their needs for an active, healthy life (1).
:::

::: example Explain — climate change
Explain how climate change affects the supply of food. (4)

Higher temperatures and heatwaves stress crops such as wheat and maize, reducing yields (1). More frequent droughts mean crops fail and livestock die, especially where farming relies on rain (1). Heavier rainfall and flooding destroy crops, wash away soil and cut transport routes, so food can't reach markets (1). Pests and diseases spread into new areas as it gets warmer, damaging harvests, so less food is available and prices rise (1).
:::

::: example Give — Fairtrade
Give two ways Fairtrade supports farming communities. (2)

It guarantees farmers a minimum price, so they have a stable income even when world prices fall (1). It pays a Fairtrade Premium that communities spend on projects such as schools, clean water or better farming equipment (1).
:::

::: example Explain — land
Explain why insufficient land is a threat to food security. (4)

As the population grows, towns, roads and housing spread onto farmland (1). Soil erosion, over-grazing and desertification make some land unable to grow crops (1). Land is also used for biofuels and to grow animal feed rather than food for people (1). Clearing forests to create more farmland releases carbon and speeds up climate change, which then reduces yields further, so simply farming more land is not a sustainable answer (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — the UK
- Analyse why some families in the UK experience food insecurity.
- Evaluate the role of food banks in solving the problem. (8)

UK food insecurity is mainly about **access**, not availability: shops are full, but some households cannot afford a healthy diet. Low wages, insecure work, benefit delays and rising costs of energy and rent squeeze the food budget, which is often the only flexible spending. When food prices rise, the poorest are hit hardest because food is a larger share of their income. Families may live in food deserts without a car, relying on small, more expensive shops. They may lack cooking equipment, skills or the money to run an oven, so they depend on cheaper, energy-dense processed food, which is filling but low in fruit, vegetables and fibre. That is food insecurity in the "nutritious" sense even when there is enough to eat.

Food banks give essential emergency help: they stop families going hungry in a crisis and often signpost debt, benefits and housing advice. But they rely on donations and volunteers, so parcels are mostly long-life foods with limited fresh produce, and they are meant for short-term crises, not ongoing need. Many people feel embarrassed to use them. Food banks treat the symptoms, not the cause, which is low income. Longer-term solutions include fair wages and benefits, cooking skills, community kitchens and affordable fresh food locally. Food banks are vital as a safety net but cannot achieve food security on their own.

(Level 4, 7–8: several causes analysed through the dimensions of food security; balanced evaluation and judgement.)
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between global warming and climate change. (2)
---
Global warming is the rise in the Earth's average temperature caused by greenhouse gases (1); climate change is the wider long-term change in weather patterns that results, including more droughts, floods and extreme weather (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how flooding can reduce food security. (3)
---
Floods destroy crops and drown livestock (1); soil is washed away and stored food is spoiled (1); roads are cut, so food can't be delivered, and contaminated water spreads disease, so prices rise and people cannot access safe food (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why eating less meat could help global food security. (3)
---
Animals eat crops that could feed people directly (1); producing meat, especially beef, needs much more land and water per gram of protein than plant foods (1); eating more pulses and grains frees land and resources to feed more people and lowers greenhouse gas emissions (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by food poverty in the UK. (2)
---
When a household cannot afford or access enough healthy food (1), for example cutting back on meals, buying cheaper, less nutritious food or using food banks because of low income (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a drought in a major wheat-exporting country could affect families in the UK. (3)
---
Less wheat is harvested, so less is available on the world market (1); world wheat prices rise (1); UK prices of bread, pasta and flour-based foods go up, so families on low incomes struggle to afford them (1).
:::

::: watch
- The definition of food security has **several parts**: enough, safe, nutritious, affordable, for all, at all times. "Having enough food" alone is one mark at most.
- **Global warming is not the same as climate change.** One causes the other.
- Explain the **chain** for each threat: drought, then crop failure, then less supply, then higher prices, then lower access.
- Solutions need **limits**. Fairtrade, GM and food banks all have them.
- UK food insecurity is mostly about **access and affordability**, not a lack of food in the shops.

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of GM crops for food security. (4)
---
Advantage: crops can be modified to resist drought, pests or disease (1), so yields are higher and more reliable in difficult climates (1); or crops such as Golden Rice have added nutrients (1) to reduce deficiency diseases (1). Disadvantage: seeds are often patented and costly (1), so poor farmers may not afford them or become dependent on large companies (1); or there are concerns about biodiversity and cross-pollination (1) that could harm the environment (1).
