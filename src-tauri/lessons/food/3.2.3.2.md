## What it is

Everything the body does uses **energy**, and all of it comes from food and drink. Energy is measured in **kilocalories (kcal)** or **kilojoules (kJ)**. **1 kcal ≈ 4.2 kJ**, so to change kcal into kJ multiply by 4.2. Food labels give both.

**Energy from nutrients**

| Nutrient | Energy per gram |
|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | **4 kcal** (about 17 kJ) |
| Protein | **4 kcal** (about 17 kJ) |
| Fat | **9 kcal** (about 37 kJ) |
| Alcohol (context only) | 7 kcal |

Vitamins, minerals, water and fibre provide no usable energy. Fat gives more than twice the energy of the same weight of carbohydrate or protein, which is why fatty foods are so **energy dense**.

**Basal metabolic rate (BMR)**

**BMR** is the energy the body needs to stay alive when **completely at rest**: to keep the heart beating, breathe, keep warm, keep the brain and organs working and replace cells. It accounts for most of the energy a typical, not very active person uses in a day.

Factors that affect BMR:

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Age** | BMR is highest per kg in babies and children (growth) and **falls with age**, as muscle is lost |
| **Gender** | **males usually have a higher BMR** than females of the same size, because they tend to have more muscle and less body fat |
| **Body size** | larger, taller and heavier bodies need more energy to maintain |
| **Muscle mass (body composition)** | muscle uses more energy at rest than fat, so fit, muscular people have a higher BMR |
| **Growth** | children and teenagers need extra energy to build new tissue |
| **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** | energy is needed to grow the baby and make milk |
| **Physical activity level (PAL)** | regular exercise builds muscle, which raises BMR |
| Illness, fever and hormones | fever and an overactive thyroid raise BMR; an underactive thyroid lowers it |

**Physical activity level (PAL)**

**PAL** describes how active a person is across the day. It is used as a **multiplier** of BMR: the higher the PAL, the more energy is used on top of BMR.
- Mostly sitting: a desk job, little exercise: PAL about 1.4.
- Moderately active: on their feet much of the day, or regular exercise: PAL about 1.6–1.7.
- Very active: a heavy manual job or hard daily training: PAL about 1.9 or more.

**Estimated daily energy requirement ≈ BMR × PAL.** For example, a person with a BMR of 1,500 kcal and a PAL of 1.5 needs about 1,500 × 1.5 = **2,250 kcal a day**.

UK guidance on food labels uses **2,000 kcal** as the reference intake for an adult; average needs are about **2,000 kcal for women** and **2,500 kcal for men**, but individuals differ a lot.

**Energy balance**

| State | What is happening | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Energy balance** | energy in = energy out | weight stays the same |
| **Positive energy balance** | energy in > energy out | extra energy stored as **body fat**; weight rises; needed for growth and pregnancy, but otherwise leads to overweight and obesity |
| **Negative energy balance** | energy in < energy out | body fat, and eventually muscle, is broken down for energy; weight falls; useful for weight loss, harmful if prolonged or in children |

Energy balance matters for health: obesity raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, CHD, high blood pressure and some cancers (3.2.3.4); too little energy leads to tiredness, poor growth and, in extreme cases, muscle wasting.

**Where the energy should come from**

| Source | Recommended share of food energy |
|---|---|
| Protein | about **15%** |
| Fat | **35% or less** (saturated fat no more than 11%) |
| Carbohydrate | about **50%**: of which about **45%** from **starches, lactose in milk and sugars in whole fruit** and **no more than 5% from free sugars** |

**Energy calculations: the method**
1. **Energy from each nutrient** = grams × energy per gram (4, 4 or 9).
2. **Total energy** = add them together.
3. **Percentage from a nutrient** = energy from that nutrient ÷ total energy × 100.
4. **Grams allowed from a percentage**: energy allowed = total energy × percentage ÷ 100; grams = energy ÷ kcal per gram.

*Example.* A pasta dish contains 20 g protein, 15 g fat and 45 g carbohydrate.
- Protein 20 × 4 = 80 kcal; fat 15 × 9 = 135 kcal; carbohydrate 45 × 4 = 180 kcal.
- Total = 80 + 135 + 180 = **395 kcal** (395 × 4.2 = about 1,659 kJ).
- Fat = 135 ÷ 395 × 100 = **34.2%**, just within the 35% guideline.

*Grams from a percentage.* For someone needing 2,400 kcal, free sugars should be no more than 5%: 2,400 × 5 ÷ 100 = 120 kcal, and 120 ÷ 4 = **30 g** of free sugars a day.

**Portion sizes by life stage and PAL (S1)**

Portions should match the person's needs. A 16-year-old footballer training daily needs larger portions of starchy foods and protein, plus nutritious snacks, than a sedentary office worker. A young child needs small portions, and an older adult with a small appetite needs smaller but nutrient-dense portions. When cooking, portion by weighing (for example 75 g of dried pasta for an adult, less for a child), using a measured scoop or ladle, dividing a dish evenly with a knife or portion guide, and serving extra vegetables rather than extra energy-dense food.

::: terms
- **Kilocalorie (kcal)**: a unit used to measure the energy in food and the energy the body uses.
- **Kilojoule (kJ)**: the metric unit of energy; 1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ.
- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)**: the energy the body needs to keep alive at complete rest, for breathing, heartbeat, warmth and organ function.
- **Physical activity level (PAL)**: a measure of how active a person is, used as a multiplier of BMR to estimate energy needs.
- **Energy requirement**: the total energy a person needs in a day, roughly BMR multiplied by PAL.
- **Energy balance**: the state in which energy taken in equals energy used, so body weight stays the same.
- **Positive energy balance**: taking in more energy than is used, so the extra is stored as fat and weight rises.
- **Negative energy balance**: taking in less energy than is used, so body stores are broken down and weight falls.
- **Energy dense**: providing a lot of energy for a small weight of food, like fatty and sugary foods.
:::

::: method
1. **Define BMR and PAL with their key words:** BMR = energy at complete rest to stay alive; PAL = how active, multiplies BMR.
2. **For BMR factors, give the factor and its direction:** "BMR falls with age because muscle mass decreases".
3. **For energy balance, compare in and out and give the consequence** for weight and health.
4. **For calculations, show every step:** grams × 4 or 9, the total, then ÷ total × 100 for a percentage. Label units and round sensibly.
5. **Compare the result with the guideline:** protein 15%, fat 35% or less, carbohydrate 50%, free sugars 5% or less.
6. **For comparing two people, go factor by factor** (age, gender, size, muscle, activity, growth) and then say how their diets and portions should differ.
:::

::: example Define — BMR and PAL
Explain what is meant by basal metabolic rate (BMR) and physical activity level (PAL). (4)

BMR is the amount of energy the body needs to stay alive when it is completely at rest (1), used for vital processes such as breathing, the heartbeat, keeping warm and organ function (1). PAL is a measure of how physically active a person is during the day (1); it is multiplied by BMR to estimate their total daily energy requirement, so a more active person has a higher PAL and needs more energy (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — energy and fat
A portion of cheese and bean nachos contains 18 g protein, 28 g fat and 52 g carbohydrate. Calculate the total energy in kcal and the percentage of energy from fat. (4)

Protein 18 × 4 = 72 kcal; carbohydrate 52 × 4 = 208 kcal (1). Fat 28 × 9 = 252 kcal (1). Total = 72 + 252 + 208 = 532 kcal (1). Fat = 252 ÷ 532 × 100 = 47.4%, well above the 35% guideline (1).
:::

::: example Explain — athlete versus office worker
Explain why a professional cyclist needs more energy than an office worker of the same age and size. (3)

The cyclist has a much higher physical activity level, using a lot of energy in hours of training each day (1). Regular training builds muscle, which uses more energy at rest than fat, so the cyclist's BMR is also likely to be higher (1). Total energy need is roughly BMR × PAL, so both a higher BMR and a higher PAL give a much higher requirement; the office worker sits most of the day and has a low PAL (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — BMR × PAL
Priya has a BMR of 1,350 kcal a day. She walks her dog and does a dance class most days, giving a PAL of 1.6. Calculate her estimated daily energy requirement, and convert it to kJ using 1 kcal = 4.2 kJ. (3)

1,350 × 1.6 (1) = 2,160 kcal a day (1). 2,160 × 4.2 = 9,072 kJ (1).
:::

::: example Explain — energy balance
Explain what will happen to a person who regularly eats 300 kcal a day more than they use. (3)

They are in positive energy balance, taking in more energy than they use (1). The body stores the extra energy as fat (1), so over weeks and months they will gradually gain weight, increasing the risk of obesity and related conditions such as type 2 diabetes and CHD (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Show the working.** Grams × kcal per gram, then the total, then the percentage. A bare answer usually gets only the final mark.
- **Fat is 9, protein and carbohydrate are 4.** Using 4 for fat is the commonest slip.
- For a percentage, divide by the **total energy**, not the total grams.
- BMR is energy **at rest**. Don't define it as "the energy you burn exercising".
- For BMR factors, give the **direction**: higher in males, lower with age, higher with more muscle.
:::

::: test
Ben is 23, male, 1.88 m tall and works as a scaffolder; he plays five-a-side football twice a week. His grandmother, Joan, is 74, 1.58 m tall, retired and mostly sits at home, walking only to the local shop.
- Analyse why the energy needs of Ben and Joan are different.
- Evaluate how their diets should differ to meet their energy needs.

(8)
---
Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis of several factors (age, gender, body size, muscle mass, PAL) with clear links to BMR and total energy need, possibly with a BMR × PAL explanation; the evaluation makes specific, justified diet recommendations for each person, including nutrient needs as well as energy, and reaches a judgement.
- **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear analysis of several factors, and sound dietary differences with some justification.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** some factors identified with limited explanation; dietary advice general.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** simple statements, such as "Ben is more active".
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Age: Ben is a young adult with a higher BMR; Joan's BMR has fallen with age as muscle is lost.
- Gender and body composition: Ben is male with more muscle and less fat, giving a higher BMR.
- Size: Ben is much taller and probably heavier, so needs more energy to maintain his body.
- PAL: Ben's scaffolding is heavy manual work plus football, a very high PAL; Joan is sedentary with a low PAL.
- So Ben's requirement (BMR × PAL) is far higher, perhaps more than twice Joan's; if Joan ate as much as Ben she would be in positive energy balance and gain weight.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Ben: large portions of starchy wholegrain foods (porridge, wholemeal bread, pasta, rice, potatoes) for sustained energy; HBV protein for muscle repair (lean meat, fish, eggs, beans); plenty of fluids because of sweating; nutritious snacks such as bananas, sandwiches and yoghurt rather than energy drinks and crisps.
- Joan: smaller portions of nutrient-dense foods so she gets vitamins and minerals without excess energy; calcium and vitamin D for bone health (plus a supplement); fibre and fluids to prevent constipation; protein to limit muscle loss; limit energy-dense snacks such as biscuits and cakes.
- Both should follow the Eatwell proportions and the energy split (protein 15%, fat 35% or less, free sugars 5% or less); only the amounts differ.
- Joan could raise her PAL with gentle activity such as walking or chair exercises, which helps maintain muscle and BMR.
- Judgement: the key difference is quantity of energy, driven by activity and age, not the type of healthy diet; Ben needs more food overall, while Joan needs less energy but the same or higher nutrient density.
:::
