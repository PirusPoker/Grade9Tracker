## What it is

Slice an apple and within minutes the cut surface turns brown. Chop a cabbage and leave it for an hour and it has lost much of its vitamin C. Both are the result of cutting open plant cells and exposing what is inside to **oxygen**. This topic covers **enzymic browning** and **oxidation**, and — the part that earns the marks — how to **prevent** them when preparing and cooking fruit and vegetables.

### Enzymes in fruit and vegetables

An **enzyme** is a **biological catalyst**: a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. Fruit and vegetable cells contain many enzymes. While the cells are whole, the enzymes are kept apart from the substances they act on. Once cells are **damaged** — by cutting, peeling, grating, bruising or biting — the contents mix and the reactions start.

Because enzymes are **proteins**, they are sensitive to **temperature** and **pH**. Each works best at an **optimum** temperature and pH; heat **denatures** them, cold **slows** them, and a pH far from the optimum (for example, acid) **slows or stops** them.

### Enzymic browning

**Enzymic browning** is the browning of the cut or damaged surface of some fruits and vegetables.
1. Cutting damages the cells and releases the enzyme **polyphenol oxidase** (often shortened to PPO).
2. The enzyme meets **phenolic compounds** from the cell and **oxygen** from the air.
3. It speeds up a reaction between them that produces **brown pigments** (called melanins).

Foods that brown this way include **apples, pears, bananas, avocados, potatoes, mushrooms, aubergines** and **peaches**. Citrus fruits hardly brown, because they are acidic and rich in vitamin C.

Browning is **not harmful**, but it makes food look old and unappetising, and flavour and texture can deteriorate. It is also one of the signs of **spoilage** by enzymes (see 3.4.1.1 and 3.4.1.2). Not all enzymic browning is unwanted: it gives the colour of **tea**, **cocoa**, **sultanas** and dried **dates**.

### Preventing enzymic browning

Each method works by removing one thing the reaction needs: an **active enzyme**, **oxygen**, or **warmth**.

| Method | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Acid** — lemon, lime or orange juice, citric acid | **Lowers the pH** away from the enzyme's optimum, so it slows or stops working. The vitamin C in citrus juice also reacts with oxygen first | Brushing lemon juice on sliced apple for a tart; lime juice in guacamole |
| **Blanching / heat** | Heat **denatures** the enzyme (a protein), so it can no longer work | Blanching vegetables before freezing; cooking apples into a purée |
| **Chilling** | Low temperature **slows** the enzyme (it does not stop it) | Keeping prepared fruit in the fridge until serving |
| **Excluding air** | Stops **oxygen** reaching the cut surface | Covering with cling film pressed onto the surface; keeping peeled potatoes in cold water; fruit in sugar syrup or juice; vacuum packing |
| **Timing and technique** | Less damage and less time exposed | Preparing just before serving; using a **sharp** stainless-steel knife that cuts cleanly instead of crushing cells |
| **In industry** | **Sulphites** (sulphur dioxide) inhibit the enzyme; modified-atmosphere packs reduce oxygen | Dried apricots stay orange; bagged salads |

Some methods have **trade-offs**. Leaving peeled potatoes in water stops browning but lets vitamin C and B vitamins **leach** into the water. Lemon juice changes the flavour. Blanching softens texture, so it does not suit a fresh fruit salad.

### Oxidation and vitamin loss

**Oxidation** is a chemical reaction with **oxygen**. In fruit and vegetables, the most important example is the loss of **vitamin C** (ascorbic acid), which is easily oxidised.
- When vegetables are **cut, grated or shredded**, the damaged cells release enzymes that speed up the oxidation of vitamin C, and the cut surfaces are exposed to air.
- The **smaller** the pieces, the **greater the surface area** and the greater the loss.
- Vitamin C is also destroyed by **heat**, **light**, **alkali** (such as bicarbonate of soda added to green vegetables) and **keeping food hot**, and it **dissolves** into cooking water.

To **conserve** water-soluble vitamins when preparing and cooking vegetables:
- buy fresh, store in a cool, dark place (or the fridge) and use quickly;
- **prepare just before cooking** — don't cut and leave;
- cut into **larger pieces**, use a **sharp** knife, and avoid peeling where the skin can be eaten (many nutrients lie just under the skin);
- don't **soak** cut vegetables in water;
- cook in the **minimum water**, add to **already boiling** water, use a **lid**, cook for the **shortest time** — or **steam, microwave or stir fry**;
- **serve immediately**; don't keep hot;
- use the cooking water for gravy, sauces or soup.

Oxidation also affects **fats**: exposed to air, the fats in oils, nuts and crisps slowly oxidise and turn **rancid**, giving an unpleasant smell and taste. Airtight storage, cool dark places and antioxidants slow this.

### Practical skills this topic uses

Preparing fruit and vegetables — peel, slice, dice, **segment** oranges, **scoop** melon, **shred** cabbage, **juice** citrus, **mash** avocado — all damage cells, so each needs a plan to control browning and vitamin loss. The standard response: work quickly with a sharp knife, treat cut surfaces with acid where flavour allows, cover and chill, and prepare as close to serving as possible.

::: terms
- **Enzyme**: a biological catalyst, usually a protein, that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up.
- **Enzymic browning**: the browning of cut or damaged fruit and vegetables when enzymes released from the cells react with oxygen.
- **Polyphenol oxidase**: the enzyme released from damaged plant cells that causes enzymic browning.
- **Oxidation**: a chemical reaction in which a substance combines with oxygen, such as vitamin C being destroyed at cut surfaces.
- **Optimum**: the temperature or pH at which an enzyme works fastest.
- **pH**: a scale of acidity; lowering the pH with acid such as lemon juice slows the browning enzyme.
- **Blanching**: briefly heating food in boiling water, then cooling it quickly, to denature enzymes.
- **Denature**: to change the structure of a protein, such as an enzyme, so that it can no longer work.
- **Ascorbic acid**: the chemical name for vitamin C, which is easily oxidised and also slows browning.
- **Surface area**: the amount of cut surface exposed to air; smaller pieces have more and lose more vitamin C.
- **Rancidity**: the unpleasant smell and taste that develops when fats and oils oxidise.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the process**: browning of cut fruit/vegetables = enzymic browning; loss of vitamin C at cut surfaces = oxidation.
2. **Explain the cause as a chain**: cells damaged → enzyme released → reacts with oxygen/phenolic compounds → brown pigments (or vitamin C destroyed).
3. **Name the control and say what it removes**: acid lowers pH; heat denatures the enzyme; cold slows it; covering keeps out oxygen.
4. **Keep the two acid ideas and the air idea separate.** Lemon juice works mainly by lowering the pH; cling film works by excluding oxygen.
5. **Apply it to the named food** and note any trade-off (flavour change, leaching, soft texture).
6. **For 8-mark questions**, analyse every cause of quality loss in the scenario, then evaluate each method for that food and time frame.
:::

::: example Explain — a cut apple
Explain why the cut surface of an apple turns brown. (3)

Cutting the apple damages its cells, which releases the enzyme polyphenol oxidase (1). The enzyme reacts with phenolic compounds in the apple and oxygen from the air (1). This forms brown pigments on the cut surface — enzymic browning (1).
:::

::: example Explain — lemon juice
Explain how lemon juice prevents avocado from browning in guacamole. (2)

Lemon juice is acidic, so it lowers the pH of the avocado (1). The browning enzyme works poorly at low pH, so the reaction slows and the avocado stays green for longer (1).
:::

::: example Give — ways to prevent browning
Give three ways to prevent sliced pears from browning in a fruit tart. (3)

Brush the slices with lemon juice (1). Glaze the arranged pears with a thin layer of apricot jam to keep out the air (1). Slice them just before assembling the tart and keep it chilled until serving (1).
:::

::: example Explain — vitamin C loss
Explain two ways to reduce the loss of vitamin C when preparing a cabbage for a stir fry. (4)

Shred the cabbage just before cooking rather than hours ahead (1), so the cut surfaces are exposed to oxygen for less time and less vitamin C is oxidised (1). Cut it with a sharp knife into strips rather than grating it very finely (1), so fewer cells are damaged and the surface area exposed to air is smaller (1).
:::

Explain why finely grated carrot loses more vitamin C than carrot cut into large chunks. (2)

---
Oranges are acidic (low pH), which slows the browning enzyme (1), and they contain plenty of vitamin C, which reacts with oxygen and protects the fruit (1).

::: watch
- The browning needs **oxygen** and an **enzyme** — name both, and say the cells were **damaged** first.
- Lemon juice works because its **acid lowers the pH** and slows the enzyme. Don't say it "blocks the air".
- Chilling **slows** the enzyme; it does **not** kill or stop it. Heat **denatures** it.
- Enzymic browning is **not** dangerous — it's a **quality** problem. Don't call it food poisoning.
- Oxidation of vitamin C increases with **surface area** and **time**: small pieces cut early lose most.
- Remember trade-offs: water stops browning but causes **leaching**.
:::
