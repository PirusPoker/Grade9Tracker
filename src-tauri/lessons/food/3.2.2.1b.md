## What it is

**Water-soluble vitamins** dissolve in water. That shapes everything about them:
- The body **cannot store much** of them (B12 is the exception, held in the liver), so they are needed **regularly**, ideally every day.
- **Excess is usually removed in urine**, so harm from too much is rare and mostly linked to big supplement doses.
- They are **easily lost in food preparation and cooking**: they dissolve into cooking water, and several (especially vitamin C, thiamin and folate) are damaged by heat, air and alkali.

The spec names the **B group** (B1, B2, B3, folic acid, B12) and **vitamin C**.

**The B group**

Most B vitamins help **release energy from carbohydrate, fat and protein**, and keep the **nervous system** healthy. They are found together in many foods: wholegrain cereals, meat, milk, eggs and fortified breakfast cereals.

| Vitamin | Functions | Main sources | Deficiency | Reference value (UK, adults) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **B1 thiamin** | releases energy from carbohydrate; healthy nerves | wholegrain cereals, **fortified white flour** and bread, pork, peas, beans, nuts, yeast extract | **beri-beri**: muscle weakness, nerve damage, heart problems; tiredness | linked to energy intake, so higher for active people |
| **B2 riboflavin** | releases energy from food; healthy skin, eyes and nervous system | **milk** and dairy, eggs, liver, fortified cereals, yeast extract | sore, cracked skin at the corners of the mouth; sore tongue; dry skin | about 1.1–1.3 mg a day; destroyed by sunlight, so milk is kept out of the light |
| **B3 niacin** | releases energy from food; healthy skin and nerves | meat, poultry, fish, **fortified white flour**, peanuts, cereals | **pellagra**: rough, dark skin in sunlight, diarrhoea, and confusion or memory loss | linked to energy intake |
| **Folic acid (folate)** | forms healthy **red blood cells**; in pregnancy, helps the baby's **spinal cord and brain develop** | green leafy vegetables, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, peas, chickpeas, oranges, **fortified breakfast cereals** | a type of anaemia (large, immature red cells), tiredness; in pregnancy, a higher risk of **neural tube defects** such as spina bifida | about 200 µg a day; women who could become pregnant are advised a **400 µg supplement** before conception and for the first 12 weeks |
| **B12 (cobalamin)** | forms red blood cells; keeps **nerves** healthy; releases energy from food | **only animal foods naturally**: meat, fish, eggs, milk, cheese; fortified yeast extract, cereals and plant milks | a type of anaemia, tiredness, and **nerve damage** such as tingling or numbness | about 1.5 µg a day; **vegans** must use fortified foods or a supplement |

**Excess B vitamins** from food is not harmful because extra is excreted. Very high supplement doses of some B vitamins can cause side effects, so the advice is not to take more than needed.

**Vitamin C (ascorbic acid)**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | makes **collagen**, the protein that binds cells together in skin, gums, blood vessels and connective tissue; helps **wounds heal**; helps the body **absorb non-haem iron** from plant foods; an **antioxidant**; supports the immune system |
| **Sources** | **citrus fruit** (oranges, lemons), kiwi fruit, **blackcurrants**, strawberries, **peppers**, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, tomatoes, **potatoes** (a big source in the UK because so many are eaten) |
| **Deficiency** | tiredness; bleeding, swollen **gums**; slow wound healing; easy bruising; more infections; in severe cases **scurvy**. Low vitamin C can also reduce iron absorption, adding to anaemia risk |
| **Excess** | mostly excreted; very large supplement doses can cause stomach pain and diarrhoea |
| **Reference value** | about **40 mg a day** for adults |

**Antioxidants: vitamins A, C and E**

During normal body processes, and from pollution and smoking, reactive molecules called **free radicals** form. They damage cell membranes and DNA, which may contribute to heart disease, some cancers and ageing. **Antioxidants** neutralise free radicals before they cause harm. Vitamin C works in the watery parts of cells, vitamin E in the fatty cell membranes, and beta-carotene (vitamin A) in plant foods. The best way to get them is a varied diet rich in fruit, vegetables, nuts and seeds. A **salad** with peppers, tomatoes, spinach, carrot, nuts or seeds and an oil dressing provides all three (S2/S3).

**How preparation and cooking destroy water-soluble vitamins**

| Cause | What happens | Worst affected |
|---|---|---|
| **Leaching** | the vitamins dissolve out of the cut food into the soaking or cooking water, which is then poured away | vitamin C, B vitamins |
| **Heat** | high temperatures and long cooking break the vitamins down | vitamin C, thiamin, folate |
| **Oxidation** | when cut or bruised surfaces are exposed to air, oxygen destroys vitamin C; enzymes released by cutting speed this up | vitamin C |
| **Alkali** | adding bicarbonate of soda to keep green vegetables bright destroys the vitamins | vitamin C, thiamin |
| **Keeping food hot** | holding cooked vegetables hot for a long time continues the loss | vitamin C, B vitamins |
| **Storage and light** | old, wilted produce has already lost vitamin C; riboflavin is destroyed by light | vitamin C, riboflavin |

**How to conserve them: each method has a reason**

- **Buy fresh and use quickly**, and store produce in the fridge or a cool, dark place, *because* losses continue during storage.
- **Prepare just before cooking**, and don't leave cut vegetables soaking in water, *because* cut surfaces are exposed to air and water.
- **Cut into larger pieces**, or leave whole or unpeeled, *because* a smaller surface area is exposed; much of the vitamin C lies just under the skin.
- **Use a sharp knife** to limit bruising and cell damage, *because* damaged cells release the enzymes that speed oxidation.
- **Steam or microwave**, or use the **smallest amount of water**, *because* less water means less leaching.
- **Add vegetables to water that is already boiling** and cook for the **shortest time**, *because* heat damage builds up with time, and boiling water contains less oxygen.
- **Put a lid on the pan**, *so* it cooks faster with less water.
- **Use the cooking water** for gravy, sauce or soup, *so* the dissolved vitamins are eaten.
- **Serve immediately** rather than keeping hot, and don't add bicarbonate of soda.
- **Eat some fruit and vegetables raw** where suitable, such as salads and fruit.

**Knife skills (S2) and water-based methods (S6).** Cutting carrots or courgettes into **batons** (thick sticks) or **julienne** (thin matchsticks) gives even-sized pieces, so they cook evenly and in the same time and look professional; but very small pieces have a larger surface area, so cook them briefly, by **steaming** or quick **stir frying** rather than long **boiling**. **Simmering** (just below boiling, gentle bubbles) and **poaching** (food barely covered in liquid kept below simmering) are gentler, but both still leach vitamins into the liquid, so serve the liquid as a sauce where possible.

::: terms
- **Water-soluble vitamin**: a vitamin that dissolves in water, is not stored in large amounts and is easily lost in cooking; the B group and vitamin C.
- **Thiamin (B1)**: a B vitamin that releases energy from carbohydrate; lack of it causes beri-beri.
- **Riboflavin (B2)**: a B vitamin that releases energy from food and is found in milk; destroyed by light.
- **Niacin (B3)**: a B vitamin that releases energy from food; lack of it causes pellagra.
- **Folic acid**: a B vitamin that forms red blood cells and helps prevent neural tube defects in unborn babies.
- **Vitamin B12**: a B vitamin, found naturally only in animal foods, needed for red blood cells and healthy nerves.
- **Collagen**: a protein made with the help of vitamin C that binds cells together in skin, gums and blood vessels.
- **Scurvy**: a disease caused by severe lack of vitamin C, with bleeding gums and poor wound healing.
- **Leaching**: the loss of water-soluble vitamins into soaking or cooking water.
- **Free radicals**: reactive molecules that damage body cells, which antioxidant vitamins A, C and E neutralise.
:::

::: method
1. **Match the B vitamin to its deficiency:** B1 beri-beri, B3 pellagra, folic acid neural tube defects and anaemia, B12 anaemia and nerve damage, C scurvy and bleeding gums.
2. **Link to the group at risk:** B12 → vegans (animal foods only); folic acid → women planning pregnancy; vitamin C → people eating little fruit and vegetables.
3. **For vitamin loss questions, name the cause:** leaching, heat, oxidation, alkali or keeping hot.
4. **For conservation questions, use the 2 + 2 pattern:** one mark for the method, one for the reason it saves the vitamin.
5. **For antioxidants, say what they protect against:** they neutralise free radicals that damage body cells.
6. **In extended answers, trace the food from delivery to plate,** naming every point where vitamins are lost, then evaluate realistic changes for that setting.
:::

::: example Explain — reducing vitamin C loss
Explain two ways to reduce the loss of vitamin C when preparing and cooking cabbage. (4)

Shred the cabbage just before cooking rather than leaving it cut in water (1), so less vitamin C is lost by oxidation in air and by leaching into the soaking water (1). Steam it or cook it in a small amount of already boiling water for a short time with a lid on (1), because less water and less time in the heat mean less vitamin C dissolves out or is destroyed by heat (1).
:::

::: example Explain — antioxidants
Explain the term antioxidant. (2)

An antioxidant is a substance, such as vitamins A, C and E, that protects body cells (1) by neutralising free radicals, which would otherwise damage cell membranes and may raise the risk of heart disease and some cancers (1).
:::

::: example Explain — vitamin C and iron
Explain why drinking a small glass of orange juice with a bowl of fortified cereal can help a teenager's iron intake. (2)

The iron in fortified cereal is non-haem iron, which is poorly absorbed on its own (1); vitamin C in the orange juice changes it into a form the body can absorb more easily, so more iron is taken up (1).
:::

::: example Explain — folic acid
Explain why women who are planning a pregnancy are advised to take folic acid. (3)

Folic acid is needed for the healthy development of the baby's brain and spinal cord (neural tube) in the first weeks of pregnancy (1). A lack raises the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida (1). The neural tube forms very early, often before a woman knows she is pregnant, so a 400 µg supplement is advised before conception and until the twelfth week (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — two cooking methods
Compare boiling and steaming broccoli for their effect on vitamin C. (4)

When broccoli is boiled, it is surrounded by water, so vitamin C leaches out of the cut stems into the water, which is usually poured away (1); boiling for a long time also destroys more vitamin C with heat (1). Steaming keeps the broccoli above the water, so very little vitamin C dissolves out (1), although it is still heated; steaming is therefore the better method, especially if the broccoli is cooked briefly and served at once (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why adding bicarbonate of soda to the water when boiling green beans is not recommended. (2)
---
Bicarbonate of soda makes the water alkaline (1), and alkali destroys vitamin C and thiamin, so the beans lose nutrients even though they stay bright green (1).

::: q
Explain why cutting carrots into julienne strips before boiling them loses more vitamin C than boiling them in large chunks. (2)
---
Julienne strips have a much larger total surface area exposed (1), so more vitamin C leaches into the water and is oxidised by air (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a salad of spinach, red pepper, carrot and sunflower seeds is a good source of antioxidants. (3)
---
Red pepper provides vitamin C (1); carrot and spinach provide beta-carotene (vitamin A) (1); sunflower seeds, and an oil dressing, provide vitamin E (1). Together these antioxidants protect body cells from free radical damage.

::: test
A hospital uses a cook-chill system. Vegetables are delivered twice a week and stored in a warm storeroom. They are peeled and chopped the day before, left in water overnight, boiled in large batches, chilled, then reheated and kept hot on the ward trolleys for up to an hour before patients are served.
- Analyse how these methods reduce the vitamin content of the vegetables.
- Evaluate changes that would help conserve the vitamins.

(8)
---
Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis of each stage, linking it to a named cause of loss (storage, oxidation, leaching, heat, reheating, hot holding) and to named vitamins; the evaluation proposes specific changes, weighs them against the practical needs of a large hospital kitchen, and reaches a supported judgement.
- **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear analysis of several stages with correct causes, and sound changes with some evaluation.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** some losses identified, limited explanation; changes general.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** simple points such as "cooking kills vitamins".
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Warm storage for several days: vegetables wilt and lose vitamin C while stored.
- Peeling removes the vitamin-rich layer under the skin; chopping the day before exposes cut surfaces to air (oxidation).
- Soaking overnight: vitamin C and B vitamins leach into the water.
- Large-batch boiling: lots of water and long heating mean more leaching and heat damage to vitamin C, thiamin and folate.
- Chilling then reheating adds a second heating, and hot holding for an hour continues the loss.
- Patients who are ill need vitamin C for wound healing and immunity, so the losses matter.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Store deliveries in a cool, dark store or chiller; order more often in smaller amounts.
- Prepare close to cooking; scrub rather than peel where possible; do not soak in water.
- Steam in batches (combination steamers) rather than boiling, and cook for the minimum time.
- Batch cook close to service instead of cook-chill for vegetables, or chill rapidly and reheat once only.
- Cut hot holding time; serve promptly; use cooking liquid in soups and sauces.
- Add raw fruit or salad to menus to supply vitamin C that has not been heated.
- Judgement: cook-chill is chosen for safety and efficiency, so it cannot all be changed, but cutting the soaking, steaming instead of boiling and shortening hot holding would save the most vitamins at little extra cost; food safety rules (reheat to 75 °C, hold above 63 °C) must still be followed.
