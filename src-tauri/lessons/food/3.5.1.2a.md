## What it is

For many people, food is bound up with **faith and culture**: what may be eaten, how an animal is killed, when to fast and what to cook for a festival. The spec names seven religions. For each you need the **practice** and the **reason** behind it, and the ability to **adapt a recipe** so a guest can eat it.

**Word your answers carefully.** Believers differ in how strictly they follow the rules, so write "many Hindus are vegetarian" or "most Muslims avoid…", not "all". Precise wording earns marks that sweeping claims lose.

| Religion | Main food practices | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **Buddhism** | Many Buddhists are vegetarian, and some are vegan. Many avoid alcohol. Some monks and nuns eat only before midday and accept whatever food is offered. | The first precept is to avoid harming living beings; alcohol clouds the mind and mindfulness. |
| **Christianity** | Few fixed rules. Some give up a food for **Lent**; pancakes on Shrove Tuesday use up rich foods before it. Some eat fish rather than meat on **Good Friday** (some Catholics on every Friday). Festival foods: Christmas turkey and cake, Easter eggs and hot cross buns, harvest festival. Some groups have stricter rules, e.g. many Seventh-day Adventists avoid pork and many are vegetarian. | Lent remembers Jesus' 40 days in the wilderness; not eating meat on Friday marks the day of the crucifixion; festivals celebrate key events. |
| **Hinduism** | Beef is avoided because the **cow is sacred**. Many Hindus are vegetarian (often lacto-vegetarian, eating dairy). Some avoid eggs, onions, garlic or alcohol. Fasting on certain days or festivals is common. Festival sweets at Diwali. | *Ahimsa*, non-violence to living things; the cow as a giver of life (milk); fasting as spiritual discipline. |
| **Islam** | Food must be **halal** (permitted). **Haram** (forbidden) foods include pork and pork products, alcohol, blood, and meat not slaughtered in the halal way. During **Ramadan**, adults fast from dawn to sunset; the fast is often broken with dates and water (*iftar*). **Eid al-Fitr** ends Ramadan with a feast. | Rules come from the Qur'an. Halal slaughter is carried out by a Muslim, with a prayer, and drains the blood. Fasting builds self-discipline and empathy with the poor. |
| **Judaism** | Food must be **kosher** (fit). Allowed: animals that chew the cud and have split hooves (so beef and lamb, not pork); fish with fins and scales (so no shellfish). Meat is slaughtered by *shechita* and salted to remove blood. **Meat and dairy are kept apart**: not eaten together, with separate utensils. No cooking on the **Sabbath** (Shabbat), so dishes are cooked beforehand and kept warm. At **Passover**, no leavened bread; *matzah* (unleavened bread) is eaten. Yom Kippur is a day of fasting. | Rules from the Torah; the Passover rules recall the Israelites leaving Egypt in haste, with no time for bread to rise. |
| **Rastafarianism** | Many follow an **Ital** diet: natural, unprocessed, often vegetarian or vegan food. Many avoid pork, shellfish, added salt, alcohol and chemical additives; some eat small fish. | Food should increase *livity* (life energy) and be pure and close to nature; the body is a temple. |
| **Sikhism** | Many Sikhs are vegetarian. Those who eat meat avoid **ritually slaughtered** meat, so they do not eat halal or kosher meat. Many avoid alcohol and tobacco. The **langar** (free community kitchen at the gurdwara) serves vegetarian food to everyone, of any faith. | Equality: everyone sits and eats together at langar, and vegetarian food means nobody is excluded; the rule on meat rejects ritual killing. |

### Adapting a recipe for a religious group

The method: identify the forbidden ingredient, name a suitable replacement, and give the reason. Check **hidden** sources too:
- **Gelatine** comes from pork or beef bones and skin, so it is found in jelly, some sweets, marshmallows, some yoghurts and cheesecakes. Use agar (from seaweed) or a halal/kosher-certified gelatine.
- **Animal rennet** in some cheeses is from calves' stomachs. Use cheese made with vegetarian rennet.
- **Lard** in pastry or refried beans. Use vegetable fat or butter (but butter is dairy, which matters for a kosher meat meal).
- **Alcohol** in sauces, desserts, some vanilla extract and flavourings. Use stock, fruit juice or alcohol-free flavourings.
- **Stock cubes** and **Worcestershire sauce** may contain beef, chicken or anchovy.
- **Pork** in sausages, bacon, pepperoni, chorizo and some pâtés. Use turkey, chicken, beef (not for Hindus) or plant-based versions.

For **kosher** meals, a creamy chicken pasta cannot simply swap the chicken: the problem is the combination of meat and cream. Either make it meat-free (vegetable or fish with cream) or keep the chicken and use a dairy-free sauce made with olive oil and stock. Fish is **parev** (neutral), so it can be eaten with dairy.

For **halal** and **kosher** meat, buy from a certified butcher or look for a certification mark; an ordinary supermarket chicken is not halal just because it is chicken.

### Culture beyond religion

Food choice is also shaped by **culture**: the traditions, habits and values shared by a community, family or region.
- **Family traditions**: Sunday roast, a grandparent's recipe, what is cooked for birthdays.
- **Regional and national diets**: Cornish pasties, Scottish porridge and haggis; rice-based meals in many South Asian and East Asian homes; Caribbean rice and peas.
- **Celebrations**: Chinese New Year dumplings, Diwali sweets (*mithai*), Christmas pudding.
- **Eating patterns**: shared dishes in the middle of the table, the timing of the main meal, eating with hands or chopsticks.
- **Migration and globalisation** mix cultures, so British diets now include curries, pizza, stir fries and more (see 3.5.2).

::: terms
- **Halal**: food that is permitted under Islamic law, including meat from animals slaughtered in the prescribed way.
- **Haram**: food that is forbidden to Muslims, such as pork, alcohol and blood.
- **Kosher**: food that meets Jewish dietary laws on which animals may be eaten, how they are slaughtered and how meals are combined.
- **Parev**: a Jewish term for neutral foods, such as fish, eggs, fruit and vegetables, that are neither meat nor dairy and can be eaten with either.
- **Ital**: the Rastafarian way of eating natural, pure, unprocessed food, usually vegetarian or vegan and often without added salt.
- **Ramadan**: the Islamic month in which adult Muslims fast from dawn until sunset.
- **Lent**: the forty days before Easter when some Christians give up a food or luxury.
- **Langar**: the free communal kitchen in a Sikh gurdwara, which serves vegetarian food to anyone who comes.
- **Ahimsa**: the principle of non-violence towards living things in Hinduism, Buddhism and other Indian traditions.
- **Ritual slaughter**: killing an animal according to religious rules, such as halal or shechita, which many Sikhs will not eat.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the religion and the rule that matters for the dish** (pork, beef, alcohol, meat with dairy, ritual slaughter, vegetarianism).
2. **Check every ingredient, including hidden ones**: gelatine, lard, rennet, stock, alcohol in sauces or flavourings.
3. **For each problem give a swap and a reason**: "Replace the pork sausages with halal chicken sausages, because Muslims do not eat pork."
4. **Keep the dish's quality**: say how flavour, texture or protein is kept.
5. **Use careful wording**: "many", "most", "some", "usually".
6. **For menus or extended questions**, look for a solution that suits several groups at once (vegetarian food often works for Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists and Rastafarians), then evaluate the limits: vegan guests, certified meat, separate preparation, fasting times.
:::

::: example Explain — adapting for a Muslim guest
A student is making toad in the hole with pork sausages and serving it with gravy made from a red-wine gravy mix. Explain how the dish could be adapted for a Muslim guest. (4)

Pork is haram for Muslims (1), so replace the pork sausages with halal-certified beef or chicken sausages, or vegetarian sausages (1). Alcohol is also forbidden (1), so replace the red-wine gravy with a gravy made from halal stock or vegetable stock and onions, checking the stock cube for non-halal meat (1).
:::

::: example Describe — Jewish food laws
Describe three rules that an observant Jewish family follows when preparing food. (3)

They eat only kosher animals, for example beef and lamb but not pork, and only fish with fins and scales, so no prawns or other shellfish (1). Meat must come from animals slaughtered by shechita and salted to remove the blood (1). Meat and dairy foods are not eaten in the same meal and are prepared with separate utensils (1).
:::

::: example Explain — Sikh langar
Explain why the food served in a Sikh langar is vegetarian. (2)

The langar feeds anyone who comes, whatever their faith, as a sign of equality (1); vegetarian food can be eaten by nearly everyone, including Hindus and people who avoid ritually slaughtered meat, so no one is excluded (1).
:::

::: example Explain — Hindu dietary choice
Explain why many Hindus avoid eating beef and many are vegetarian. (3)

The cow is regarded as sacred, a symbol of life because it gives milk (1), so beef is avoided even by Hindus who eat other meat (1). Many follow the principle of ahimsa, non-violence to living things, so they choose a vegetarian diet, often including milk products such as paneer and ghee (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — multi-faith event
A community centre is holding a lunch for 60 people, including Muslim, Hindu, Jewish, Sikh and Christian guests. The planned menu is: chicken tikka skewers; beef lasagne; cheese and onion quiche; green salad; trifle made with sherry and jelly.
- Analyse how religious beliefs affect the planning of the menu.
- Evaluate the proposed menu. (8)

Muslims need halal meat and no alcohol; Hindus avoid beef and many are vegetarian; observant Jews need kosher meat and do not mix meat and dairy; many Sikhs are vegetarian and will not eat halal or kosher meat; Christians have few rules. So meat is the main problem: no single meat suits every group, because halal chicken is unacceptable to many Sikhs and not kosher.

The beef lasagne excludes Hindus, and for Jewish guests it combines meat and cheese, so it should be replaced with a vegetable or lentil lasagne. The chicken tikka suits Muslims only if halal, and excludes Sikhs who avoid ritually slaughtered meat and many vegetarians, so a paneer or chickpea tikka is a better option. The quiche is vegetarian, but the cheese must be made with vegetarian rennet, and some Hindus avoid eggs. The trifle contains sherry, which is haram, and jelly made with gelatine, which may be pork; agar jelly and fruit juice solve both. The salad suits everyone. A mainly vegetarian menu with clear labels and separate serving utensils is the most inclusive choice; the organisers could offer certified kosher meals for strictly observant guests, and they should check whether the event falls during Ramadan, when Muslim guests would not eat until sunset.

(Level 4, 7–8: several faiths analysed accurately; each dish evaluated, with improvements and a judgement.)

::: watch
- **Give the reason as well as the rule.** "Hindus don't eat beef" is one mark; "because the cow is sacred" completes it.
- **Don't generalise.** Not every Hindu is vegetarian; many Buddhists are, but not all. Use "many", "some", "most".
- **Kosher is about combinations as well as ingredients.** A chicken and cream dish is not kosher even with kosher chicken.
- **Sikhs avoid halal and kosher meat**, which catches people out when a menu uses halal meat "to suit everyone".
- **Look for hidden ingredients**: gelatine, lard, rennet, alcohol in sauces, meat stock.
- When adapting, name a **specific replacement**, not just "take out the pork".
:::
