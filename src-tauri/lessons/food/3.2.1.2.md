## What it is

**Fats** (solid at room temperature) and **oils** (liquid at room temperature) are macronutrients called **lipids**. Each fat molecule is mostly made of **fatty acids** joined to glycerol. The kind of fatty acid decides whether a fat is saturated or unsaturated, how it behaves in cooking and how it affects health.

**Functions of fat**

| Function | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Concentrated energy** | 9 kcal per gram, more than twice protein or carbohydrate |
| **Energy store** | extra energy is stored as body fat (adipose tissue) |
| **Insulation** | a layer under the skin reduces heat loss and keeps the body warm |
| **Protects vital organs** | fat around the kidneys and heart cushions them from damage |
| **Carries fat-soluble vitamins** | vitamins A, D, E and K are found in, and absorbed with, fat |
| **Essential fatty acids** | supplies fatty acids the body cannot make, such as omega-3 and omega-6, needed for cell membranes, the brain and the eyes |
| **In food** | flavour, texture, a feeling of fullness (satiety), shortening and aeration in baking (3.3.2.3) |

**Saturated and unsaturated fats**

| | Saturated | Monounsaturated | Polyunsaturated |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | every carbon in the fatty-acid chain holds as many hydrogen atoms as it can: no double bonds | one double bond in the chain | two or more double bonds |
| **At room temperature** | usually solid | liquid, may cloud in the fridge | liquid |
| **Main sources** | mostly animal: butter, lard, ghee, cream, cheese, fatty meat, meat products (sausages, pies, salami), and in pastry, cakes and biscuits made with them; two plant exceptions are **coconut oil** and **palm oil** | olive oil, rapeseed oil, avocados, most nuts such as almonds and hazelnuts | sunflower oil, corn oil, soya oil, seeds, walnuts, **oily fish** (salmon, mackerel, sardines) for omega-3 |
| **Health effect** | raises **LDL cholesterol** in the blood | can help lower LDL cholesterol when used instead of saturated fat | can help lower LDL cholesterol; omega-3 supports heart health |

**Visible and invisible fats.** *Visible* fats can be seen and measured: butter on toast, oil in a pan, the fat on a chop. *Invisible* (hidden) fats are inside foods: cheese, pastry, crisps, chocolate, cakes, biscuits, sausages, egg yolk, nuts and many ready meals. Invisible fat makes up much of what people eat, so reading labels matters.

**Cholesterol.** Cholesterol is a fatty substance the body makes in the liver; it is carried in the blood by two types of lipoprotein.
- **LDL** ("bad") carries cholesterol to the cells. Too much of it can be laid down in the walls of the arteries as fatty deposits.
- **HDL** ("good") carries extra cholesterol back to the liver to be removed.

A diet high in saturated fat raises LDL. The fatty deposits narrow the coronary arteries, so less blood and oxygen reach the heart muscle. This is **coronary heart disease (CHD)**; a blood clot at a narrowed point can cause a heart attack. Replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat helps keep LDL lower.

**Trans fats** (context only) form when oils are partly hardened by hydrogenation, and small amounts occur naturally in some dairy and meat fats. They raise LDL and lower HDL. Most UK manufacturers have removed artificial trans fats from their products.

**Deficiency and excess**

- **Deficiency** is uncommon but causes: low energy and weight loss; feeling cold because of little insulation; a lack of vitamins A, D, E and K, with their deficiency effects; a lack of essential fatty acids, affecting the skin and growth; organs less protected.
- **Excess** causes: a **positive energy balance**, because fat is energy dense, leading to weight gain and **obesity**; raised LDL cholesterol from saturated fat, so a higher risk of **CHD** and stroke; obesity in turn raises the risk of **type 2 diabetes** and high blood pressure.

**Dietary reference values**

- Total fat should provide **no more than 35%** of daily food energy.
- **Saturated fat no more than 11%** of food energy.
- On labels, the adult **reference intake** is **70 g of fat** and **20 g of saturates** a day, based on 2,000 kcal.
- Two portions of fish a week, one of them oily, is advised for omega-3.

**Reducing fat in recipes and cooking methods**

| Change | Why it reduces fat |
|---|---|
| **Grill instead of fry** | fat drips away through the grill rack and no extra fat is added |
| **Bake instead of roast** | baking on a rack or tray without a pool of added fat |
| **Dry fry mince** and pour off the fat | fat melts out of the meat and is removed before the sauce is added |
| **Steam, poach or microwave** | no fat is needed |
| Measure oil with a spoon or use a spray | less oil than pouring freely |
| Choose lean mince (5% fat), remove skin from chicken, trim visible fat | removes the fattiest parts |
| Swap butter for an unsaturated spread; cream for low-fat yoghurt or crème fraîche | lower saturated fat |
| Use a strong cheese such as mature Cheddar and less of it | the same flavour from a smaller amount |
| Replace some meat with pulses or vegetables | fewer total fat grams, more fibre |
| A filo or single-crust top instead of a double shortcrust case | much less pastry fat |

**Pastry making (S10, S5).** Shortcrust is made by rubbing fat into flour in the proportion of about half fat to flour, by hand with cool fingertips or quickly in a food processor using the pulse button so the fat stays cold. It is then rolled, shaped (lining a flan tin, a pie lid, crimped edges) and finished with an egg or milk glaze. Pastry is a major source of saturated fat, so a lower-fat pie might use a single top crust, or filo brushed lightly with oil. How fat creates the short texture is covered in 3.3.2.3.

::: terms
- **Saturated fat**: fat whose fatty acids have no double bonds; usually solid at room temperature and mainly from animal foods.
- **Unsaturated fat**: fat whose fatty acids have one or more double bonds; usually liquid at room temperature and mainly from plants and oily fish.
- **Monounsaturated fat**: unsaturated fat with one double bond in its fatty acids, found in olive oil, rapeseed oil and avocados.
- **Polyunsaturated fat**: unsaturated fat with two or more double bonds, found in sunflower oil, seeds, walnuts and oily fish.
- **Essential fatty acids**: fatty acids the body cannot make, such as omega-3 and omega-6, so they must come from food.
- **Cholesterol**: a fatty substance made by the liver and carried in the blood by LDL and HDL lipoproteins.
- **LDL cholesterol**: the form that carries cholesterol to cells and can build up as fatty deposits in artery walls.
- **HDL cholesterol**: the form that carries excess cholesterol back to the liver for removal.
- **Invisible fat**: fat hidden inside foods such as pastry, cheese, biscuits and processed meat, which cannot be seen or easily measured.
- **Coronary heart disease (CHD)**: narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty deposits, reducing the blood supply to the heart muscle.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the fat type every time.** "Saturated fat from butter", not "fat" or "unhealthy fat".
2. **For health effects, write the chain:** saturated fat → raised LDL cholesterol → fatty deposits narrow the arteries → CHD or heart attack.
3. **For functions, give the function and what it does:** "insulation, reducing heat loss from the body".
4. **For "reduce fat" questions, pair every change with its reason:** the swap or method, then why it lowers fat or saturated fat.
5. **Use the reference values when judging a diet:** fat no more than 35% of energy, saturates no more than 11%, 70 g and 20 g on labels.
6. **In extended answers, pick out named foods from the context,** say which fat each supplies, then weigh realistic swaps and reach a judgement.
:::

::: example Give — functions
Give two functions of fat in the body. (2)

Fat provides a concentrated source of energy (1). It supplies the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K (1). (Also creditworthy: insulation; protection of vital organs; essential fatty acids.)
:::

::: example Explain — saturated fat and CHD
Explain why eating too much saturated fat increases the risk of coronary heart disease. (4)

A diet high in saturated fat raises the level of LDL cholesterol in the blood (1). LDL can be deposited as fatty material in the walls of the coronary arteries (1), which narrows them and reduces the flow of blood and oxygen to the heart muscle (1). If a clot blocks a narrowed artery, part of the heart is starved of oxygen, causing a heart attack (1).
:::

::: example Suggest — reducing fat in a recipe
A sausage and bean casserole uses pork sausages fried in 3 tablespoons of oil, a tin of beans in sauce, onions and a topping of grated cheese. Suggest four ways to reduce the fat, with a reason for each. (4)

Grill or bake the sausages on a rack instead of frying them, so their fat drips away and no oil is added (1). Choose reduced-fat or chicken sausages, which contain less saturated fat than standard pork sausages (1). Soften the onions in a teaspoon of oil or a spray rather than 3 tablespoons, cutting the added fat sharply (1). Use a smaller amount of a strong mature cheese, or swap it for a breadcrumb and herb topping, so less saturated fat is added but there is still flavour and crunch (1).
:::

::: example Compare — butter and olive oil
Compare butter and olive oil for their effect on health. (4)

Butter is high in saturated fat, which raises LDL cholesterol (1), increasing the risk of fatty deposits in the arteries and CHD (1). Olive oil is mostly monounsaturated fat (1), which can help lower LDL cholesterol when it replaces saturated fat, so it is the better choice for heart health, although both give 9 kcal per gram, so both should be used in small amounts (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — a pastry swap
A bakery wants to lower the fat in its steak pies, which have a shortcrust base and lid. Evaluate replacing the pastry with a single puff-pastry lid, or with a mashed potato topping. (4)

A single lid removes the whole pastry base, so total fat and saturated fat fall a lot, and the pie still looks like a pie (1); but puff pastry is itself very high in fat, so the saving is smaller than it seems (1). A mashed potato topping contains far less fat if made with semi-skimmed milk and a little unsaturated spread, and adds starch and some vitamin C (1); but customers may not see it as a "pie", so the potato version is the healthier change, while the single lid is the safer option for sales (1).
:::

::: test
A student is making a tomato and onion tart using a shortcrust pastry case. Explain how the student should use a food processor to make the pastry, and why. (4)
---
Put the flour and cold fat, cut into cubes, into the bowl (1); use the pulse setting in short bursts until the mixture looks like fine breadcrumbs (1), so the fat is not overworked and stays cold, and does not melt into the flour (1); add cold water a little at a time and pulse just until it clumps, then rest it in the fridge, which keeps the pastry short and stops it shrinking (1).
