## What it is

What we eat over years affects our health: a varied, balanced diet supports growth, energy and resistance to illness, while too much of some nutrients, or too little of others, raises the risk of long-term **diet-related diseases**. Most of them also have non-dietary causes (genes, age, smoking, activity), so an exam answer should give a **chain**: **the nutrient or food → what it does in the body → the disease**, and then the advice that breaks the chain.

**Obesity**

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | an excess of body fat that harms health; in adults, a **BMI of 30 or more** (25–29.9 is overweight) |
| **Dietary causes** | a long-term **positive energy balance**: eating more energy than is used. Drivers are **energy-dense foods** high in fat and sugar (takeaways, fried food, pastries, biscuits, chocolate), **sugary drinks**, **large portions**, frequent snacking, eating out and low intake of filling, high-fibre foods |
| **Other causes** | **low physical activity** (screen time, car travel, desk jobs); genes and family habits; poor sleep; some medicines and medical conditions; cheap, heavily marketed energy-dense food |
| **Effects on the body** | raises the risk of **type 2 diabetes**, **CHD**, **high blood pressure** and stroke, some cancers; strain on joints (back, knees, hips); breathing problems and poor sleep; low self-esteem and bullying |
| **Prevention** | match energy intake to activity; smaller portions; base meals on wholegrain starchy foods and vegetables; cut free sugars and saturated fat; water instead of sugary drinks; **be active** (for adults, about 150 minutes a week of moderate activity is advised; for children and teenagers, an hour a day on average) |

**Cardiovascular health: coronary heart disease (CHD)**

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | fatty deposits build up in the walls of the **coronary arteries**, which supply the heart muscle, narrowing them; less blood and oxygen reach the heart, causing chest pain (angina); a clot at a narrowed point causes a **heart attack** |
| **Dietary causes** | **high saturated fat intake** raises **LDL cholesterol**, which forms the deposits; **high salt** raises blood pressure, damaging artery walls; **obesity**; low fibre and low fruit and vegetables; too much alcohol |
| **Other causes** | smoking, inactivity, diabetes, family history, age, being male, stress |
| **Prevention** | swap saturated fat for **unsaturated** oils and spreads; **grill, bake or steam rather than fry**; lean meat, skin off chicken, lower-fat dairy; **oily fish** for omega-3; more soluble fibre (oats, beans, lentils); 5 a day; less salt; healthy weight; exercise; no smoking |

**Cardiovascular health: high blood pressure (hypertension)**

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | blood is pushed against the artery walls at a higher pressure than normal; it usually has **no symptoms**, so many people do not know they have it |
| **Dietary causes** | **too much salt (sodium)**, which makes the body hold on to water and increases blood volume; **obesity**; too much alcohol; low fruit and vegetables |
| **Other causes** | age, family history, inactivity, smoking, stress; it is more common in some ethnic groups |
| **Effects** | strains the heart and damages arteries, raising the risk of **stroke**, **heart attack**, heart failure and **kidney disease** |
| **Prevention** | **no more than 6 g of salt** a day; cut processed and salty foods; flavour with herbs and spices; plenty of fruit and vegetables; healthy weight; activity; limit alcohol |

**Bone health: rickets and osteoporosis**

| | Rickets (children; osteomalacia in adults) | Osteoporosis |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | bones are soft and weak because they are not mineralised properly; they bend under the body's weight (**bowed legs**), and there may be bone pain and poor growth | bones lose density and become **porous and fragile**; they fracture easily, often the **hip, wrist and spine** (a stooped back) |
| **Dietary causes** | lack of **vitamin D**, so calcium is not absorbed; lack of **calcium** | low **calcium** and **vitamin D** over many years, especially while building bone; a **low peak bone mass** by about age 30; very low body weight or restrictive dieting |
| **Other causes** | little **sunlight** on the skin (indoor lifestyles, covered skin, darker skin in UK sunlight) | **age**; women after the **menopause** (bone loss speeds up); **lack of weight-bearing exercise**; smoking; heavy drinking; family history |
| **Prevention** | vitamin D from sunlight, oily fish, eggs and fortified foods; a **10 µg supplement** for young children and in winter; calcium from milk, cheese, yoghurt, fortified alternatives | build **peak bone mass** in childhood and teens with calcium, vitamin D and weight-bearing exercise (running, dancing, team sports); keep it up in adulthood to slow loss |

**Dental health**

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | **dental caries** (tooth decay): holes in the teeth; also gum disease and **erosion** of enamel by acids |
| **Dietary causes** | **free sugars**: bacteria in **plaque** feed on sugar and produce **acid**, which dissolves the enamel. The **frequency** matters most: sipping sugary drinks or snacking on sweets through the day keeps the mouth acidic for longer. Sticky foods (toffee, dried fruit) cling to teeth. Acidic drinks (fizzy drinks, fruit juice) erode enamel directly |
| **Other causes** | poor brushing; not using fluoride toothpaste; not visiting the dentist |
| **Prevention** | limit free sugars and keep sugary foods to **mealtimes**; water or milk between meals; **fluoride** toothpaste twice a day; **calcium, phosphorus and vitamin D** for strong teeth; crunchy fruit and vegetables and cheese as snacks; sugar-free gum after meals |

**Iron-deficiency anaemia**

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | too little iron to make enough **haemoglobin**, so red blood cells carry less oxygen |
| **Dietary causes** | not enough iron-rich food; relying on non-haem iron without vitamin C; drinking tea with meals (tannins); poorly planned vegetarian or vegan diets |
| **Other causes** | blood loss (heavy periods, injury); fast growth in teenagers; pregnancy |
| **Effects** | tiredness, lack of energy, **pale skin**, breathlessness, poor concentration, brittle nails |
| **Prevention** | red meat, pulses, dark green vegetables, fortified cereals; **vitamin C** at the same meal; avoid tea with meals |

**Type 2 diabetes**

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | the body does not make enough **insulin**, or its cells stop responding to it (**insulin resistance**), so **blood glucose stays too high**. (Type 1 diabetes is different: an autoimmune condition, usually starting in childhood, not caused by diet) |
| **Dietary causes** | **overweight and obesity**, especially fat around the waist, is the biggest risk factor; diets high in free sugars, sugary drinks and refined carbohydrates, and low in fibre, add to the risk mainly through weight gain |
| **Other causes** | inactivity, age, family history; higher risk in people of South Asian, African-Caribbean and Black African origin |
| **Effects** | thirst, passing a lot of urine, tiredness, blurred vision, slow wound healing; long term, damage to the **eyes, kidneys, nerves and blood vessels**, a higher risk of CHD and stroke, and foot problems that can lead to amputation |
| **Prevention and control** | healthy weight; **regular meals** based on wholegrain, high-fibre starchy foods that release glucose slowly; fruit and vegetables; limit free sugars and sugary drinks; less saturated fat; regular activity |

**Adjusting cooking to reduce risk (S1).** Choosing the method is part of preventing disease: **grill meat rather than fry it**, so fat drips away and none is added, lowering saturated fat, a risk factor for CHD. Similarly, bake or steam rather than deep fry, dry fry mince and drain it, and taste before adding salt.

::: terms
- **Diet-related disease**: an illness whose risk is increased by long-term eating habits, such as obesity, CHD or type 2 diabetes.
- **Obesity**: an excess of body fat that harms health, shown in adults by a BMI of 30 or more.
- **Coronary heart disease (CHD)**: narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty deposits, reducing blood flow to the heart muscle.
- **High blood pressure**: blood pushing against artery walls at a higher pressure than normal, straining the heart and raising stroke risk.
- **Rickets**: soft, bent bones in children caused by a lack of vitamin D or calcium.
- **Osteoporosis**: loss of bone density that makes bones fragile and easily broken, mainly in later life.
- **Dental caries**: tooth decay caused by acid from plaque bacteria feeding on sugars.
- **Plaque**: a sticky layer of bacteria on the teeth, which produces acid from sugar.
- **Type 2 diabetes**: a condition in which the body cannot control blood glucose because of too little insulin or insulin resistance.
- **Insulin resistance**: when the body's cells stop responding properly to insulin, so blood glucose stays high.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the disease precisely** and what happens in the body: narrowed arteries, soft bones, too much glucose in the blood.
2. **Write the chain:** nutrient or food → effect in the body → disease. "Saturated fat → raised LDL cholesterol → fatty deposits narrow the coronary arteries → CHD."
3. **Separate dietary and non-dietary causes** when asked, and give both for full marks where the question allows.
4. **Give advice as a specific action with its reason:** "grill rather than fry, so less saturated fat is eaten".
5. **Link to the person in the question:** age, gender, activity, life stage and the foods they actually eat.
6. **For 12-mark questions, analyse the causes in depth, then evaluate strategies** (in the home, at school, by government or industry), weighing how effective and realistic each one is, and finish with a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain — dietary causes of CHD
Explain two dietary causes of coronary heart disease. (4)

A diet high in saturated fat, from foods such as butter, fatty meat and pastry, raises the level of LDL cholesterol in the blood (1), which is deposited in the coronary artery walls, narrowing them so less blood and oxygen reach the heart (1). A diet high in salt raises blood pressure (1), which damages and strains the artery walls and the heart, making blockages and heart attacks more likely (1).
:::

::: example Give — reducing the risk of type 2 diabetes
Give four pieces of dietary advice to reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes. (4)

Keep to a healthy weight by matching energy intake to activity, as obesity is the main risk factor (1). Swap sugary drinks for water or sugar-free drinks to cut free sugars (1). Base meals on wholegrain, high-fibre starchy foods such as brown rice and wholemeal bread, which release glucose slowly (1). Eat regular meals with plenty of fruit and vegetables, and fewer energy-dense snacks high in fat and sugar (1).
:::

::: example Explain — tooth decay
Explain why sipping a sugary drink throughout the morning is worse for teeth than drinking it with lunch. (3)

Plaque bacteria turn sugar into acid, which dissolves tooth enamel (1). Each time sugar is taken in, the mouth stays acidic for a period afterwards (1), so sipping all morning keeps the teeth under acid attack for hours without a chance to recover, whereas drinking it once with a meal limits the attack to one short period (1).
:::

::: example Explain — osteoporosis risk in women
Explain why women are at a higher risk of osteoporosis than men. (3)

Women usually reach a lower peak bone mass than men, as they have smaller, lighter bones (1). After the menopause, hormone changes cause bone to be lost more quickly (1). Women also tend to live longer, so bone loss has longer to progress, and any low calcium or vitamin D intake when younger adds to the risk (1).
:::

::: example Explain — grilling instead of frying
Explain why grilling sausages rather than frying them is advised for someone at risk of CHD. (2)

When grilled, the fat melts and drips away through the rack, and no extra fat is added (1), so less saturated fat is eaten, which helps keep LDL cholesterol lower and reduces the risk of fatty deposits in the coronary arteries (1).
:::

::: q
Give two effects of obesity on health. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): type 2 diabetes; CHD; high blood pressure; stroke; some cancers; joint pain or damage; breathing or sleep problems; low self-esteem.
:::

::: q
Name the disease in which bones become soft and bend in children, and give its dietary cause. (2)
---
Rickets (1); a lack of vitamin D (or calcium) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why high blood pressure is sometimes called a "silent" condition. (2)
---
It usually causes no symptoms, so many people do not know they have it (1); it can damage the heart, arteries and kidneys for years before a stroke or heart attack occurs, so regular checks are important (1).
:::

::: q
Give three ways to prevent dental caries. (3)
---
Any three (1 each): limit free sugars; keep sugary foods and drinks to mealtimes; drink water or milk between meals; brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste; visit the dentist regularly; eat foods rich in calcium, phosphorus and vitamin D; chew sugar-free gum after meals.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. (2)
---
Type 1 is an autoimmune condition in which the body cannot make insulin, usually starting in childhood and not caused by diet (1); type 2 is when the body makes too little insulin or the cells stop responding to it, and it is strongly linked to obesity and lifestyle (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how weight-bearing exercise and diet in the teenage years can reduce the risk of osteoporosis later. (4)
---
Calcium is laid down in bone to build density (1), and vitamin D is needed to absorb it (1); weight-bearing exercise such as running or dancing stimulates bone to become denser (1); together they build a higher peak bone mass by about 30, so bones stay strong for longer as bone is lost in later life (1).
:::

::: watch
- Diet-related disease answers need a **chain**: nutrient → body change → disease. A list of "bad foods" doesn't reach the higher levels.
- **Salt → blood pressure; saturated fat → LDL cholesterol → CHD.** Don't mix them up.
- **Rickets is children; osteoporosis is older adults.** Both involve calcium and vitamin D, but osteoporosis is about **peak bone mass** and bone loss.
- Tooth decay: give **frequency** of sugar, not just amount.
- Type 2 diabetes is driven mainly by **obesity**. "Eating sugar gives you diabetes" is too simple.
:::

::: test
The number of teenagers in the UK who are overweight or obese has become a public health concern.
- Analyse the dietary causes of obesity in teenagers.
- Evaluate strategies that could help reduce obesity in teenagers.

(12)
---
Levels of response (12):
- **Level 4 (10–12):** a detailed, balanced analysis of several dietary causes, explained through energy balance and linked to teenagers' lifestyles, with non-dietary factors acknowledged; a thorough evaluation of strategies at different levels (individual, family, school, industry, government), weighing effectiveness and practicality, and a clear, justified judgement. Specialist terms such as energy density, positive energy balance, free sugars and PAL used accurately.
- **Level 3 (7–9):** a good analysis of several causes with links to energy balance, and a sound evaluation of several strategies; some imbalance.
- **Level 2 (4–6):** some causes identified with limited explanation; strategies listed rather than evaluated.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** simple points, such as "they eat too much junk food".
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Obesity results from a long-term positive energy balance: more energy eaten than used, stored as fat.
- Energy-dense foods high in fat and sugar are cheap, easy and widely available: fast food, takeaways, crisps, chocolate, pastries.
- Sugary drinks, energy drinks and large coffees add energy without filling you up, and are a major source of free sugars for many teenagers.
- Large portion sizes, meal deals and "upsizing"; frequent snacking; skipping breakfast then overeating later.
- Eating on the go and less home cooking; low intake of fruit, vegetables and wholegrains, so less satiety.
- Marketing and social media promote high-fat, high-sugar products; peer influence; eating while gaming or watching screens.
- Non-dietary factors combine with diet: low PAL from screen time and travel by car; poor sleep; family habits; genes.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Education in school (food lessons, cooking skills): builds long-term knowledge, but knowing does not always change behaviour.
- School food standards, water fountains, no sugary drinks, healthy tuck shops: effective during school hours, but teenagers can buy food outside school.
- Families cooking from scratch, eating together, smaller plates and portions, keeping fruit available: effective but depends on time, money and skills.
- Swapping sugary drinks for water: a simple change with a big effect on energy and teeth.
- Government measures, such as the soft drinks levy and restrictions on promoting and advertising foods high in fat, salt and sugar: can reformulate products and reduce exposure across the whole population, but some argue they limit choice, and effects take time.
- Industry reformulation and clear traffic-light labelling: helps informed choice, but only if people read labels.
- Increasing activity through sport, active travel and clubs: raises energy output and improves health, but must be enjoyable to last.
- Judgement: no single strategy works alone; the most effective approach combines whole-population measures that change the food environment (drinks, marketing, school food) with family and individual changes, especially cutting sugary drinks and increasing activity, because obesity has many causes.
:::
