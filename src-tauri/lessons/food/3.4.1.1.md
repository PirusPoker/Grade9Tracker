## What it is

Food goes off because living things and chemical reactions change it. The living things are **microorganisms** — bacteria, yeasts and moulds. The chemical reactions are driven by **enzymes**. Both need certain conditions, so every method of keeping food safe and fresh works by taking one of those conditions away. This topic is the science behind all of food safety.

### Three kinds of microorganism

| Type | What it is | How it multiplies | Role in food |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | Tiny single-celled organisms, visible only under a microscope | **Binary fission**: one cell splits into two | **Pathogenic** bacteria cause food poisoning; **spoilage** bacteria make food slimy, sour or smelly; **useful** bacteria make yoghurt and cheese |
| **Yeasts** | Single-celled **fungi** | **Budding**: a small cell grows off the parent and breaks away | Spoil sugary, moist foods such as fruit and juice (fizzing, alcohol smell); used to raise bread |
| **Moulds** | Multicellular **fungi** that grow as fine threads (**hyphae**) and produce **spores** | Spores carried in the air land on food and grow | Visible fuzzy growth on bread, cheese and fruit; some produce harmful toxins; some are used in blue cheese |

Microorganisms are everywhere — in soil, water, air, on animals, on people and on food. Most are harmless, some are useful, and some cause illness.

### What microorganisms need to grow

**Temperature**
- Most bacteria that cause food poisoning grow fastest at about **37 °C** (body temperature).
- The **danger zone** is **5–63 °C**: bacteria multiply rapidly between these temperatures.
- Below **5 °C** (the fridge) growth is **slowed**. At **−18 °C** (the freezer) bacteria are **dormant** — they stop growing but are **not killed**, and become active again when the food thaws.
- Above **63 °C** bacteria stop multiplying and start to die; cooking to **75 °C** at the core kills most of them.

**Moisture** — microorganisms need water to take in nutrients and grow. Dry foods (flour, pasta, dried fruit) keep for a long time; once water is added, they can support growth. Water can also be made **unavailable** by adding lots of sugar or salt, which draw water out of microbial cells.

**Food** — they need nutrients. Moist foods high in **protein** (meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy) are especially good for bacteria; sugary foods suit yeasts and moulds.

**Time** — in ideal conditions, bacteria can divide by binary fission roughly **every 20 minutes**. One bacterium can become 2, 4, 8, 16… so numbers rise extremely quickly. After 3 hours (9 doublings) one cell could become 512; after 7 hours (21 doublings), over **two million**. This is why food must not be left out.

**Other factors**
- **pH**: most bacteria prefer **neutral** conditions (around pH 7). **Acid** slows or stops their growth, which is why vinegar preserves pickles. Yeasts and moulds tolerate more acid, so they can spoil fruit.
- **Oxygen**: many microorganisms need oxygen; others grow without it. Removing air (vacuum packing) slows some but not all.

**Spores and toxins** — some bacteria form **spores**, tough resting forms that can **survive cooking** and then grow if the food is cooled slowly or kept warm. Some bacteria produce **toxins** (poisons) in food; certain toxins are **not destroyed** by reheating. This is why cooked food must be cooled quickly and not kept warm.

### High-risk foods

**High-risk foods** are **ready-to-eat**, **moist** foods, usually **high in protein**, that easily support the growth of pathogenic bacteria and **will not be cooked or heated again** before they are eaten. Because nothing will kill bacteria that get into them, they need the greatest care.

Examples: **cooked meat and poultry** (sliced ham, cooked chicken), **cooked meat products** (pâté, gravy, stock), **dairy foods** (milk, cream, soft cheese), **egg products** (mayonnaise, mousse, custard), **shellfish and seafood** (prawns, crab), and **cooked rice**.

Raw chicken is **not** a high-risk food by this definition, because it will be cooked — but it is a major **source** of bacteria that can contaminate high-risk foods.

### Enzymes

**Enzymes** are **biological catalysts**, usually made of **protein**, which speed up chemical reactions. In food they continue working after harvest or slaughter and cause:
- **ripening**, then over-ripening (bananas turning yellow then brown and soft);
- **enzymic browning** of cut fruit and vegetables;
- **softening** and loss of texture;
- **off flavours** and loss of colour and vitamins.

Enzymes also work best at a particular temperature and pH:
- **heat** (above about 60–70 °C) **denatures** them, stopping them permanently;
- **cold** **slows** them, but they carry on slowly in the fridge and very slowly even in the freezer;
- **acid** slows or stops them.

**Controlling enzymes**:
- **Blanching** vegetables before freezing: dipping them in boiling water for a short time denatures the enzymes, then cooling them rapidly in iced water stops the cooking. Without blanching, frozen vegetables slowly lose colour, flavour, texture and vitamins in the freezer.
- **Acids** such as lemon juice prevent enzymic browning (see 3.3.2.4).

### How preservation methods work

Every method removes or changes at least one growth condition.

| Method | Condition controlled | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chilling** (0 to below 5 °C) | Temperature | Slows the growth of bacteria and enzyme action | Milk, cooked meats, yoghurt |
| **Freezing** (−18 °C) | Temperature and water | Microbes are dormant; water turns to ice so it is unavailable | Peas, meat, bread |
| **Heating** (cooking, pasteurising, canning) | Temperature | Kills microorganisms and denatures enzymes; canning also seals out new microbes | Pasteurised milk, canned soup |
| **Drying** | Moisture | Removes water so microbes cannot grow | Dried pasta, fruit, milk powder |
| **Sugar** (high concentration) | Water availability | Draws water out of microbial cells | Jam, candied peel |
| **Salt** | Water availability | Draws water out of cells | Bacon, salted fish |
| **Acid / vinegar** | pH | Low pH stops most bacteria growing | Pickled onions, chutney |
| **Vacuum packing / modified atmosphere** | Oxygen | Removes or replaces air to slow aerobic microbes and oxidation | Bagged salad, cured meats |

Preservation also changes **quality**: freezing can soften texture as ice crystals burst cells; heat changes flavour and destroys some vitamin C; drying changes texture; sugar, salt and vinegar change flavour and add sugar or salt to the diet.

### Useful microorganisms and the same conditions

The same four conditions are **used** deliberately in bread making: yeast is given **warmth**, **moisture**, **food** (sugar) and **time** to prove, and it is then killed by oven heat (see 3.3.2.5 and 3.4.1.3).

::: terms
- **Microorganism**: a living thing too small to see without a microscope, such as a bacterium, yeast or mould.
- **Pathogenic bacteria**: bacteria that cause illness such as food poisoning.
- **Binary fission**: the way bacteria multiply, one cell dividing into two, as often as every twenty minutes in ideal conditions.
- **Danger zone**: the temperature range of 5 °C to 63 °C in which bacteria multiply most rapidly.
- **Dormant**: alive but inactive; bacteria in a freezer at −18 °C are dormant, not dead.
- **High-risk food**: a ready-to-eat, moist food, usually high in protein, that supports bacterial growth and will not be cooked again.
- **Spore**: a tough resting form of some bacteria and moulds that can survive heat and grow later.
- **Toxin**: a poison produced by some bacteria or moulds in food, sometimes not destroyed by heating.
- **Enzyme**: a biological catalyst, usually a protein, that speeds up reactions such as ripening and browning.
- **Blanching**: briefly boiling vegetables and cooling them quickly to denature enzymes before freezing.
- **Water availability**: how much water in a food microorganisms can use; drying, sugar and salt reduce it.
- **pH**: a measure of acidity; low pH (acid) slows or stops the growth of most bacteria.
:::

::: method
1. **Recall the four growth conditions**: temperature, moisture, food, time (plus pH and oxygen for top marks).
2. **Identify which condition a method removes**: chilling/freezing/heating = temperature; drying/sugar/salt = water; vinegar = pH; vacuum = oxygen.
3. **Explain the effect on the microorganism**: slowed, dormant, killed, or water drawn out.
4. **Separate microorganisms and enzymes**: enzymes are not alive; they are denatured, not killed.
5. **Use the correct temperatures** (−18 °C, 0 to below 5 °C, 5–63 °C, 75 °C) and the high-risk definition in full.
6. **For extended questions**, analyse several named foods and methods, then evaluate how each preserves safety but changes quality (texture, flavour, nutrients).
:::

::: example Define — high-risk food
Explain what is meant by a high-risk food. Give one example. (3)

A high-risk food is ready to eat and moist, and usually high in protein (1). It easily supports the growth of pathogenic bacteria and will not be cooked again before eating, so any bacteria present are not killed (1). Example: cooked chicken, prawns, or fresh cream (1).
:::

::: example Explain — freezing
Explain how freezing preserves food. (3)

At −18 °C bacteria are dormant, so they cannot multiply (1). The water in the food turns to ice, so it is not available for microorganisms to use (1). Enzyme action is also slowed greatly, so the food keeps its quality for months — but bacteria are not killed and become active again on thawing (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — bacterial growth
A single bacterium lands on a bowl of cooked rice left at room temperature. It divides every 20 minutes. Calculate how many bacteria there could be after 2 hours. Show your working. (2)

2 hours = 120 minutes ÷ 20 = 6 divisions (1). 2⁶ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 64 bacteria (1).
:::

::: example Explain — blanching
Explain why green beans are blanched before freezing. (3)

Enzymes in the beans are not destroyed by freezing and keep working slowly in the freezer (1). Blanching in boiling water denatures the enzymes (1), so the beans keep their colour, flavour, texture and vitamins during frozen storage (1).
:::

::: example Explain — sugar as a preservative
Explain why strawberry jam does not go mouldy quickly, even though it is stored in a cupboard. (3)

Jam contains a very high concentration of sugar (1). The sugar draws water out of the cells of microorganisms and makes water unavailable to them (1). The jam is also heated to boiling point, which kills microorganisms, and sealed in a sterilised jar, so none get in until it is opened (1).
:::

- Freezing does **not kill** bacteria — they are **dormant**. Chilling **slows** them.
- Enzymes are **not alive**: they are **denatured** by heat, not "killed".
- Match the method to the **condition it removes**: sugar and salt work on **water availability**, not temperature.
- Use exact temperatures: −18 °C, 0 to below 5 °C, 5–63 °C, 75 °C.
