## What it is

Fats and oils do far more than add richness. They make pastry **short** and crumbly, let butter be **spread** and rolled, hold **air** in a creamed cake, and — with help — let oil and water mix into a smooth **sauce**. The four working properties to know are **shortening**, **plasticity**, **aeration** and **emulsification**.

**Fats** are solid at room temperature (butter, lard, block margarine); **oils** are liquid (olive, sunflower, rapeseed). Both are made of molecules of **glycerol** joined to three **fatty acids** (triglycerides). The mix of fatty acids decides how hard the fat is and at what temperature it melts.

### Shortening

**Shortening** is the ability of fat to give a **short**, crumbly, melt-in-the-mouth texture to pastry, shortbread, crumble toppings and biscuits.
1. When fat is **rubbed in** to flour, it **coats** the flour particles with a waterproof layer.
2. The fat stops water reaching the gluten-forming proteins (glutenin and gliadin), so **long gluten strands cannot develop**.
3. Only **short**, broken strands of gluten form, so the baked product breaks and crumbles easily instead of being chewy and elastic.

Practical points:
- **Keep everything cold**: cold fat, cold water, cool hands, and lift the mixture while rubbing in with the fingertips. Warm fat **melts** into the flour and oils it instead of coating it, giving greasy, hard pastry. Lifting also traps some air.
- **Add water carefully**: just enough to bind. Too much water lets gluten form, so the pastry is **tough** and **shrinks**.
- **Choose the fat**: **lard** and **white vegetable fat** are 100% fat and shorten very well; **butter** gives the best flavour and colour but is a little less short; many cooks use half butter, half lard. **Low-fat spreads** contain a lot of water, so they shorten poorly and make tough pastry.
- **Rest** the pastry in the fridge so the fat firms and the gluten relaxes, reducing shrinkage.
- A **food processor** rubs in quickly and keeps the fat cold, but over-processing makes the mixture too fine and pasty.

### Plasticity

Fats do not melt at one exact temperature. Each fat is a **mixture** of triglycerides that melt at different temperatures, so it **softens gradually over a range** of temperatures. This ability to be spread, shaped and rolled without breaking is **plasticity**.
- **Butter** from the fridge is hard and tears bread; at room temperature it spreads. It is plastic over a fairly narrow range.
- **Soft margarine** and **spreadable** butter contain more liquid (unsaturated) oils, so they spread straight from the fridge — good for sandwiches and the **all-in-one** cake method.
- **Pastry making** depends on plasticity. For **flaky, rough puff and puff pastry**, a firm but plastic fat is rolled and folded into the dough to make many thin **layers** of fat and dough. In the oven, water in the dough turns to **steam** and pushes the layers apart, while the fat melts, leaving a light, flaky, layered texture (as in palmiers and sausage rolls). If the fat is too soft it squashes into the dough and the layers are lost; if too hard it breaks through the dough.
- In shortcrust, a plastic fat can be rubbed in evenly and the dough **rolled** and **lifted** over a rolling pin to **line a flan ring** without cracking.

### Aeration

**Aeration** is the ability of fat to **trap and hold air** when it is beaten with sugar — the **creaming** method for cakes such as a Victoria sandwich, cupcakes or a lemon drizzle cake.
1. Fat and caster sugar are beaten together (by hand, with an electric whisk, or in a **food mixer** with a paddle beater).
2. The sharp edges of the sugar crystals cut into the fat, creating tiny **air bubbles**.
3. The fat, being plastic, surrounds and **holds** the bubbles, forming a stable foam. The mixture turns **pale, light and fluffy** and increases in volume.
4. Egg is beaten in, then flour is **folded** in gently to keep the air.
5. In the oven, the trapped air **expands**, and carbon dioxide from the baking powder in the flour adds to it; the egg and flour proteins **coagulate** and starch gelatinises, setting the risen structure.

**Curdling**: if the eggs are added **too quickly** or straight from the **fridge**, the creamed fat cannot hold the extra water, so the mixture **separates** into greasy lumps and loses much of its air. The cake will be heavier and denser, with a coarse texture. Prevent it by using eggs at room temperature, adding them **a little at a time** and beating well after each addition; a spoonful of the flour helps bring a curdling mixture back.

### Emulsification

Oil and water are **immiscible** — they do not mix. Shaken together, they form a **temporary emulsion** (droplets of one liquid dispersed in the other), but the droplets soon join up again and the layers **separate**, as in a simple oil and vinegar dressing left to stand.

An **emulsion** is a mixture of two immiscible liquids, one dispersed as tiny droplets in the other.
- **Oil-in-water**: oil droplets in a watery liquid — mayonnaise, hollandaise, milk, salad cream.
- **Water-in-oil**: water droplets in fat — butter, margarine.

An **emulsifier** makes the emulsion **stable** (permanent). Its molecules have two ends:
- a **hydrophilic** ("water-loving") head, which sits in the water;
- a **hydrophobic** ("water-hating", fat-loving) tail, which sits in the oil.

The emulsifier molecules gather round each oil droplet, tails in, heads out, forming a coating that **stops the droplets joining together**, so the two liquids stay mixed. **Lecithin** in **egg yolk** is the classic natural emulsifier; **mustard** also helps hold a vinaigrette together. **Stabilisers** (such as starch or gums) thicken the continuous liquid so droplets move less and separate more slowly.

**Hollandaise**: egg yolks and a reduction of lemon juice or vinegar are whisked over **gentle** heat while warm melted butter is added **slowly**; lecithin coats the butter droplets and the yolk thickens slightly as it warms.
- It **splits** (separates into greasy liquid with curds) if the butter is added too fast, or if it gets **too hot**, because the egg protein over-coagulates and can no longer hold the fat.
- **Rescue**: start again with a fresh yolk (or a spoonful of warm water) in a clean bowl and whisk the split sauce into it drop by drop.

**Other functions of fat** you can use as supporting points: adds **flavour** and **colour** (butter); gives **moistness** and keeps cakes fresher for longer; a **cooking medium** that transfers heat (frying); gives a **glossy** finish to sauces.

::: terms
- **Shortening**: the ability of fat to coat flour particles and stop long gluten strands forming, giving a short, crumbly texture.
- **Rubbing in**: lightly rubbing cold fat into flour with the fingertips so the fat coats the flour and air is incorporated.
- **Plasticity**: the ability of a fat to soften over a range of temperatures, so it can be spread, shaped and rolled.
- **Aeration**: the trapping of air bubbles in a mixture, for example when fat and sugar are creamed.
- **Creaming**: beating fat and sugar together until pale and fluffy, so the fat holds tiny air bubbles.
- **Curdling**: separation of a creamed mixture when eggs are added too quickly or too cold, so the fat cannot hold the water and loses air.
- **Immiscible**: describes two liquids, such as oil and water, that will not mix and separate on standing.
- **Emulsion**: a mixture of two immiscible liquids, one dispersed as tiny droplets in the other.
- **Emulsifier**: a substance with a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail that holds oil and water together, such as lecithin in egg yolk.
- **Hydrophilic**: water-loving; the part of an emulsifier molecule that is attracted to water.
- **Hydrophobic**: water-hating or fat-loving; the part of an emulsifier molecule that is attracted to oil.
- **Stabiliser**: a substance such as a gum or starch that thickens an emulsion so the droplets separate more slowly.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the property** the question is about: shortening, plasticity, aeration or emulsification.
2. **Explain what the fat does** at particle level: coats flour; softens over a range; holds air bubbles; emulsifier surrounds droplets.
3. **Say what that stops or allows**: stops gluten forming; allows spreading and layering; allows the mixture to rise; stops droplets joining.
4. **Link to the texture or result**: crumbly shortbread, flaky layers, light sponge, smooth sauce that does not separate.
5. **For faults**, give the cause and the science: "fat too warm, so it melted and oiled the flour instead of coating it".
6. **For 8-mark questions**, analyse each function in each product, then evaluate how swapping the fat (lower-fat spread, oil) changes the result.
:::

::: example Explain — shortening
Explain how shortening gives shortbread its crumbly texture. (4)

The butter is worked into the flour, where it coats the flour particles with a layer of fat (1). The fat is waterproof, so it stops water reaching the gluten-forming proteins, glutenin and gliadin (1). This prevents long gluten strands developing, so only short strands form (1). The baked shortbread therefore has little stretch and breaks and crumbles easily in the mouth (1).
:::

::: example Explain — aeration
Explain how the creaming method traps air in a cupcake mixture. (3)

Butter and caster sugar are beaten together (1). The sharp sugar crystals cut into the fat, making tiny air bubbles (1). The plastic fat surrounds and holds the bubbles, so the mixture becomes pale, fluffy and larger in volume, and the air expands in the oven to help the cakes rise (1).
:::

::: example Explain — emulsification
A vinaigrette is made with olive oil, cider vinegar and a teaspoon of mustard. Explain the role of the mustard. (4)

Oil and vinegar are immiscible, so without help the oil droplets join together and separate out (1). Mustard acts as an emulsifier (1). Its molecules have a hydrophilic part attracted to the vinegar and a hydrophobic part attracted to the oil, so they surround the oil droplets (1). This stops the droplets joining, so the dressing stays mixed for longer and coats the salad evenly (1).
:::

::: example Explain — plasticity
Explain why plasticity is important when making rough puff pastry. (3)

Plasticity lets the butter soften and stretch without melting, so it can be rolled out between the layers of dough (1). Repeated rolling and folding builds up many thin layers of fat and dough (1). In the oven, steam from the water in the dough pushes the layers apart while the fat melts, giving a light, flaky texture (1).
:::

::: example Faults — cake mixture
Explain why a cake mixture curdled when the eggs were added, and how the student could have prevented it. (4)

The eggs were added too quickly/all at once (1), so the creamed fat could not hold the extra water in the eggs and the mixture separated, losing air (1). The student should have used eggs at room temperature and added them a little at a time, beating well after each addition (1), and added a spoonful of flour if the mixture began to curdle, so the cake stays light and even-textured (1).
:::

::: watch
- For shortening, say **what the fat prevents**: long **gluten strands** forming. "Fat makes pastry short" just repeats the word.
- **Aeration by creaming** is fat holding air; don't confuse it with whisking eggs (a protein foam — 3.3.2.1).
- Plasticity is **softening over a range of temperatures**, not "melting".
- For emulsions, describe the emulsifier's **two ends** and that it **stops droplets joining**.
- Know which way round: mayonnaise and milk are **oil-in-water**; butter and margarine are **water-in-oil**.
- Curdling is caused by adding eggs **too fast or too cold** — give the remedy too.
:::
