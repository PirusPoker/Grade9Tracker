## What it is

**Protein** is a macronutrient: the body needs it in large amounts (grams, not milligrams). Every protein is a long chain of smaller units called **amino acids**, joined in a particular order. When you eat protein, digestion breaks the chains back into amino acids, and the body rebuilds them into the proteins it needs: muscle, skin, hair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies.

**Functions of protein**

| Function | What it means in the body |
|---|---|
| **Growth** | building new cells and tissues, so needs are highest per kg of body weight in babies, children, teenagers and pregnant women |
| **Repair** | mending tissues after injury, illness, surgery or hard training |
| **Maintenance** | replacing cells that wear out every day (skin, blood cells, the gut lining) |
| **Making body chemicals** | enzymes, some hormones and the antibodies of the immune system are all proteins |
| **Secondary source of energy** | if the diet is short of carbohydrate and fat, or if more protein is eaten than the body can use, protein is broken down for energy (4 kcal per gram) |

The last function matters in exam answers: protein is only a *secondary* energy source. Using it for energy is wasteful, because it is then not available for growth and repair.

**Essential amino acids and biological value**

About twenty amino acids are used to build body proteins. The body can make some of them itself: these are **non-essential** amino acids. Others cannot be made fast enough, or at all, so they must come from food: these are **essential amino acids**. Children need some extra amino acids from food, because they are growing quickly and cannot make enough.

**Biological value (BV)** describes how well a food's protein matches the body's needs.
- A **high biological value (HBV)** protein contains **all the essential amino acids** in the amounts the body needs. Nearly all animal proteins are HBV: meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Two plant foods are the famous exceptions: **soya beans** (and so tofu and most soya products) and **quinoa**. Mycoprotein is also counted as a high-quality protein.
- A **low biological value (LBV)** protein is **missing, or short of, one or more essential amino acids**. Most plant proteins are LBV: beans, lentils, peas, chickpeas, nuts, seeds and cereals such as wheat, rice, oats and maize.

HBV does *not* mean "a lot of protein". Lentils contain plenty of protein; it is the *range* of amino acids that makes them LBV.

**Protein complementation**

Different LBV proteins are short of different amino acids. Cereals tend to be low in one amino acid (lysine) that pulses have plenty of, while pulses are low in others that cereals supply. Eating two LBV proteins together, or over the same day, so that each supplies what the other lacks, is **protein complementation**. The combination gives the body a full set of essential amino acids, just as an HBV food would.

Classic pairings: **beans on toast** (pulse + cereal), **dhal with rice or chapati**, **hummus with pitta**, **a bean burrito in a wheat tortilla**, **peanut butter on wholemeal bread**, **lentil soup with a bread roll**. Complementation is the key idea for vegans, who eat no animal HBV protein.

**Protein alternatives**

Meat alternatives let vegetarians and vegans, and people cutting their meat intake, get protein in familiar dishes.

| Alternative | How it is made | Nutrition | Uses and working points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **TVP** (textured vegetable protein) | soya flour is processed and extruded into chunks or mince, then dried | HBV protein, low in fat, contains fibre; often fortified | bland, so it takes on the flavour of sauces; rehydrate in stock; used in bolognese, chilli and pies; cheap and keeps for months dry |
| **Soya** | soya beans, also made into soya milk, mince, burgers and yoghurt alternatives | HBV protein; low in saturated fat; some soya milks are fortified with calcium and B12 | wide range of products; soya is one of the 14 labelled allergens |
| **Tofu** | soya milk is curdled (coagulated) and the curds pressed into a block, like making cheese | HBV protein; low in saturated fat; can be a good source of calcium if a calcium salt was used to set it; some iron | firm tofu is pressed, sliced or cubed and marinated, then stir fried, grilled or baked; silken tofu for desserts and dressings; bland alone, so it needs strong flavours |
| **Mycoprotein** (sold as Quorn) | a fungus is grown by fermentation, then the mycoprotein is mixed with a binder and shaped | high-quality protein, high in fibre, low in fat and saturated fat, no cholesterol | pieces, mince and fillets; most products are bound with egg white, so they suit vegetarians but not vegans (check the label for vegan versions) |

Advantages of alternatives compared with meat: usually lower in saturated fat, contain fibre, often cheaper, lower environmental impact, suitable for vegetarians (and many for vegans). Disadvantages: some are highly processed and can be high in salt; bland on their own; soya is an allergen; some people dislike the texture.

**Deficiency and excess**

- **Deficiency** is rare in the UK but common where food is scarce. Effects: **poor or stunted growth in children**, slow healing of wounds, loss of muscle, hair and skin problems, weakened immunity so more infections, and oedema (fluid swelling). Severe deficiency in young children causes **kwashiorkor**: a swollen belly and limbs caused by fluid, with thin muscles and poor growth.
- **Excess**: protein eaten beyond needs is not stored as protein. It is broken down; the energy part is used or **stored as body fat** (so it can lead to weight gain), and the nitrogen part is removed as urea, which puts **strain on the liver and kidneys**. High-protein diets built on red and processed meat also bring saturated fat and salt.

**Dietary reference values**

- Adults need roughly **0.75 g of protein per kilogram of body weight a day**. So a 60 kg adult needs about 60 × 0.75 = **45 g** a day. UK reference figures are about 45 g a day for adult women and about 55 g for adult men.
- Protein should provide about **15% of daily energy**.
- Needs per kilogram are higher for babies, children and teenagers (growth), in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and during recovery from illness or injury.

**In the kitchen**

- **Knife skills (S2).** Portioning a chicken or trimming meat uses a sharp knife on a red board for raw meat, with the claw grip. Firm tofu or halloumi is pressed dry, then sliced into even slabs or cubes with a bridge or claw hold so the pieces cook evenly.
- **Acids and protein (S9).** A marinade containing lemon juice, vinegar or yoghurt **denatures** protein on the surface, which starts to tenderise meat and helps flavour penetrate. Acid can also set protein without heat: lime juice turns raw fish opaque in ceviche. (The science is in 3.3.2.1.)
- **Bolognese sauce (S8).** Beef mince can be replaced, fully or half-and-half, with soya mince, TVP or lentils. Half-and-half keeps a meaty texture while cutting saturated fat and cost and adding fibre.

**Modifying recipes for vegetarians and vegans**

| Group | Avoids | Replace with |
|---|---|---|
| **Lacto-ovo vegetarian** | meat, poultry, fish, and products from slaughter such as gelatine, animal rennet and meat stock | Quorn, tofu, TVP, beans, lentils, eggs, vegetarian cheese, vegetable stock, agar instead of gelatine |
| **Vegan** | all animal foods, including milk, eggs, honey and dairy fats | tofu, TVP, soya mince, pulses with a cereal (complementation), plant milks fortified with calcium and B12, vegan spread, vegan cheese, a flax or chickpea-water "egg" |

Check hidden ingredients: Worcestershire sauce often contains anchovies; Parmesan is made with animal rennet; many stock cubes contain meat or milk.

::: terms
- **Protein**: a macronutrient made of chains of amino acids, needed for growth, repair and maintenance of the body.
- **Amino acids**: the small units that join in chains to make proteins.
- **Essential amino acids**: amino acids the body cannot make, or cannot make fast enough, so they must come from food.
- **High biological value (HBV)**: a protein food that contains all the essential amino acids the body needs, such as meat, fish, eggs, milk, soya and quinoa.
- **Low biological value (LBV)**: a protein food that is missing or short of one or more essential amino acids, such as lentils, beans, nuts and cereals.
- **Protein complementation**: combining two or more LBV proteins in a meal or a day so each supplies the amino acids the other lacks.
- **Textured vegetable protein (TVP)**: a meat alternative made from processed soya flour, sold dried as chunks or mince.
- **Tofu**: a soya bean curd made by coagulating soya milk and pressing the curds into a block.
- **Mycoprotein**: a protein made by fermenting a fungus, used for meat-free pieces, mince and fillets.
- **Kwashiorkor**: a severe protein deficiency in young children, causing fluid swelling, a swollen belly and poor growth.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the command word and the tariff.** Give/State = short points; Explain = point plus the effect or reason; one mark per linked idea.
2. **For biological value, use the words "all the essential amino acids".** Name a source, and remember soya and quinoa are HBV plant foods.
3. **For complementation, give three things:** LBV proteins lack different amino acids; eaten together they supply each other's gaps; a named pairing such as dhal with rice.
4. **For alternatives, compare on the same features:** what it is made from, protein quality, fat and fibre, suitability for vegans, flavour and texture, cooking uses.
5. **For recipe modifications, name the swap and give its reason:** "replace the beef mince with soya mince, which is HBV protein and lower in saturated fat". Check every ingredient, including stock and cheese, for the group.
6. **For extended questions, analyse needs first** (growth, activity, amino acids, risk nutrients) **then evaluate the diet** with named foods and a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain — biological value
Explain the difference between high biological value and low biological value protein. Give one example of each. (4)

A high biological value protein contains all of the essential amino acids that the body cannot make for itself (1), for example eggs, fish or soya beans (1). A low biological value protein is missing one or more of the essential amino acids, or has too little of them (1), for example lentils, kidney beans or wheat (1).
:::

::: example Compare — two meat alternatives
Compare tofu and mycoprotein as alternatives to chicken in a stir fry. (4)

Both are high-quality proteins that are lower in saturated fat than chicken skin and meat, so both suit a healthier stir fry (1). Tofu is made by curdling soya milk and pressing the curds, so it is suitable for vegans, whereas most mycoprotein products are bound with egg white, so they suit vegetarians but not vegans (1). Mycoprotein pieces already have a fibrous, meat-like texture and contain fibre, while tofu is softer and must be pressed and cubed so it holds its shape when stir fried (1). Both are bland, so each needs a marinade such as soy, garlic and ginger to absorb flavour; tofu contains soya, a major allergen, which must be declared (1).
:::

::: example Explain — adapting a bolognese
Explain how a beef bolognese sauce can be adapted for a vegan. (6)

Replace the beef mince with soya mince or rehydrated TVP (1), because it is a high biological value protein and so still supplies all the essential amino acids (1). Alternatively use red lentils with the pasta (1), since the pulse and the wheat complement each other's missing amino acids (1). Check the stock and use a vegetable stock cube with no milk or meat, and leave out any Worcestershire sauce that contains anchovies (1). Serve with a vegan cheese or toasted breadcrumbs with nutritional yeast in place of Parmesan, which is made with animal rennet and milk (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — a day's meals
Leo, 16, is vegan and plays rugby. His meals are: toast with jam for breakfast; a jacket potato with baked beans for lunch; a vegetable stir fry with rice for tea. Evaluate whether his meals meet his protein needs. (6)

Leo needs plenty of protein because he is still growing and needs to repair muscle after training (1). The baked beans at lunch are LBV protein, and the potato adds little protein, so lunch does not complement well unless he adds a cereal such as wholemeal bread (1). Breakfast gives only a little LBV protein from the toast and none from the jam, so it could be improved with peanut butter or a fortified soya milk (1). The stir fry has no protein food at all, only vegetables and rice, so adding tofu, edamame beans or cashew nuts would give HBV or complementary protein (1). Overall his total protein is probably too low and relies on LBV sources that are not well combined (1), so the diet does not yet meet his needs; adding a protein food to every meal, with a pulse and a cereal together, would fix this (1).
:::

::: q
Give two functions of protein in the body. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): growth of new cells and tissues; repair of damaged tissue; maintenance of body cells; making enzymes, hormones or antibodies; a secondary source of energy.
:::

::: q
Name one plant food that is a high biological value protein. (1)
---
Soya beans (or tofu, soya milk, TVP) or quinoa (1).

::: q
Explain what is meant by protein complementation, using a named meal. (3)
---
LBV proteins each lack one or more essential amino acids (1); eating two different LBV proteins together means one supplies what the other lacks, giving all the essential amino acids (1); named meal, e.g. hummus with pitta bread, dhal with rice (1).
:::

::: q
Give two effects of a diet that contains too much protein. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): excess is used for energy or stored as fat, leading to weight gain; puts strain on the liver and kidneys, which have to remove the waste nitrogen; high-protein diets based on meat may also be high in saturated fat.
:::

::: q
Explain why a strict vegan should not choose most mycoprotein products. (2)
---
Most mycoprotein products are mixed with egg white as a binder (1); egg is an animal product, so they are suitable for vegetarians but not vegans unless labelled vegan (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two effects of protein deficiency in young children. (4)
---
Poor or stunted growth (1), because there is not enough protein to build new cells and tissue (1). Kwashiorkor / swelling of the belly and limbs (1), caused by fluid collecting in the tissues in severe deficiency (1). Or: frequent infections (1), because antibodies are proteins, so the immune system is weakened (1).
:::

::: q
A café sells a halloumi and vegetable kebab. Explain how the halloumi should be prepared so that it cooks evenly and safely on the grill. (3)
---
Pat the halloumi dry and cut it with a sharp knife on a clean board using a claw or bridge grip (1); cut it into even-sized cubes or slices so every piece cooks at the same rate and browns evenly (1); keep it chilled until cooking because it is a high-protein, high-risk dairy food, and use separate boards from raw meat (1).
:::

::: watch
- **HBV means all the essential amino acids**, not "lots of protein". Lentils are protein-rich but LBV.
- **Soya and quinoa are HBV plant proteins.** Examiners reward knowing the exception.
- **Mycoprotein usually contains egg**, so it is not vegan. Tofu and TVP are vegan.
- Complementation answers need a **named pairing**; beans on toast earns the mark, "mix proteins" does not.
- Protein is a **secondary** energy source. Don't call it the main one.
- In modification answers, **check every ingredient**: stock cubes, Parmesan, Worcestershire sauce, gelatine.
:::

Aisha is 15 and has decided to follow a vegan diet. She is a keen swimmer and trains five times a week.
- Analyse Aisha's protein needs.
- Evaluate how a vegan diet can meet these needs.

(8)
---
Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis of Aisha's needs linked to her age, growth and training, using terms such as HBV, LBV, essential amino acids and complementation accurately. The evaluation uses specific vegan foods and meals, weighs strengths and difficulties, and reaches a supported judgement.
- **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear analysis with some links to her age and sport, and a sound evaluation with named foods; the judgement may be brief.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** some relevant points about protein and vegan foods, with limited links to Aisha; the evaluation is one-sided.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** simple statements, such as a list of vegan protein foods, with no analysis.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- At 15 she is in a growth spurt, so she needs protein to build new tissue as well as for maintenance.
- Regular training causes small muscle damage, so she needs protein for repair; her requirement per kilogram is higher than a sedentary adult's.
- She needs all the essential amino acids; vegans have no animal HBV foods, so she relies on soya, quinoa and on combining LBV proteins.
- If her energy intake is too low for her training, protein will be used for energy instead of growth and repair.
- Protein-rich animal foods also supply iron, calcium and B12, which she must replace elsewhere.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Soya (tofu, soya milk, soya yoghurt alternatives, TVP) and quinoa give HBV protein, e.g. tofu stir fry with quinoa.
- Complementation works well: beans on wholemeal toast, lentil dhal with rice, hummus with pitta, peanut butter sandwiches.
- Include a protein food at every meal and in snacks after training: soya milk smoothie, nuts and seeds, roasted chickpeas.
- Drawbacks: plant proteins are bulkier and higher in fibre, so she may feel full before eating enough; some meat alternatives are processed and salty; she must plan carefully and may need fortified foods for B12 and calcium.
- Judgement: a well-planned vegan diet can meet her protein needs, but only if she eats enough energy overall and combines sources at each meal; careless planning makes deficiency more likely during growth and heavy training.
