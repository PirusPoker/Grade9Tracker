## What it is

**Water** is not usually called a nutrient, but it is essential: a person can survive weeks without food but only a few days without water. More than half of an adult's body weight is water. It is in every cell, in blood, in digestive juices and around the joints and eyes. **Hydration** means having enough water in the body for it to work properly; **dehydration** is when more water is lost than is taken in.

**Functions of water**

| Function | How water does it |
|---|---|
| **Eliminating waste** | the kidneys filter waste products (such as urea) out of the blood and remove them in **urine**; water also keeps faeces soft so, with fibre, it prevents **constipation** |
| **Cooling (controlling body temperature)** | when the body gets hot, **sweat** is released onto the skin; as it **evaporates** it takes heat away and cools the body |
| **Digestion** | **saliva** moistens food so it can be chewed and swallowed; **digestive juices** are mostly water; water helps move food through the gut and carries digested nutrients into the blood |
| **Transport** | blood plasma is mostly water and carries nutrients, oxygen and hormones to the cells |
| **Other roles** | lubricates joints and the eyes; cushions organs and the brain; part of every cell; keeps skin and the linings of the mouth and nose moist |

**How water is lost from the body**
- **Urine**: the largest loss each day.
- **Sweat** through the skin, which rises greatly in heat and exercise.
- **Breath**: water vapour is breathed out (you can see it on a cold day).
- **Faeces**, and much more during **diarrhoea**.
- **Vomiting**, and extra losses through **fever**, which raises sweating and breathing.

**How much fluid is needed**

UK guidance is about **6 to 8 glasses of fluid a day** for adults, roughly **1.5 to 2 litres**, and more in the situations below. Children need less in total but dehydrate more quickly for their size.

**What counts:** all non-alcoholic drinks count, including water, milk, tea, coffee, sugar-free squash and fruit juice. **Water and lower-fat milk** are the best choices: they hydrate without free sugars or much energy. Tea and coffee count, although caffeine can have a mild effect on some people. Sugary fizzy drinks hydrate but add free sugars that harm teeth and add energy; alcohol does not help hydration because it makes the body lose more water.

**Water in food.** Food provides some of our water too, especially **fruit and vegetables** (cucumber, lettuce, melon, tomatoes and oranges are mostly water), **soups, stews, yoghurt, milk puddings and jelly**. This matters for people who drink little, such as older adults and young children.

**Juices and smoothies.** Fruit juice and smoothies count towards fluid and one of your 5 a day, but **only 150 ml a day** counts as a portion, however much is drunk, and only once. That is because juicing or blending **releases the sugars from the fruit's cells**, making them **free sugars** that can damage teeth and add energy, and juicing also removes much of the fibre. They are best drunk **with a meal** to reduce the harm to teeth.

**Signs of dehydration**
- **Thirst** (already a sign of mild dehydration).
- **Dark yellow, strong-smelling urine**, and passing urine less often: pale straw-coloured urine is a good sign.
- **Headaches**, tiredness and **poor concentration**.
- Dizziness or light-headedness; dry mouth, lips and eyes.
- Constipation.
- In severe dehydration: confusion, a fast heartbeat, very little urine; it can be life-threatening, especially for babies and older people.

**Occasions when extra fluid is needed**

| Occasion | Why |
|---|---|
| **Exercise and sport** | more sweat is produced to cool the body |
| **Hot weather** or hot working conditions | sweating increases |
| **Illness with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea** | large amounts of water (and salts) are lost; oral rehydration drinks replace both |
| **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** | extra fluid is needed for the baby and to produce breast milk |
| **Older people** | the sense of thirst weakens and the kidneys are less able to hold on to water, so they may not realise they need a drink |
| **Babies and young children** | they lose water faster relative to their size and cannot always ask for a drink |
| **A high-fibre diet** | fibre absorbs water in the gut, so more fluid is needed to prevent constipation |
| **Hot, dry or air-conditioned places and long flights** | more water is lost from the skin and breath |

**Why older people are at particular risk**, beyond a weaker thirst: they may deliberately drink less to avoid getting up at night or because of worries about reaching the toilet in time; some medicines increase urine; memory problems mean they forget to drink; limited mobility makes getting a drink harder; and living alone means no one reminds them. Dehydration in older people can cause confusion, falls, urine infections and constipation.

**In the kitchen: soups, juices and smoothies (S3)**

Preparing fruit and vegetables for **soup** practises several skills:
- **Peel** carrots, potatoes or butternut squash with a swivel peeler, taking off a thin layer so fewer nutrients are lost.
- **Grate** carrot or courgette on a box grater, keeping fingers clear of the blades.
- **Crush** garlic with the flat of a knife or a garlic press.
- **Scissor snip** chives, parsley or spring onions straight into the pan or over the bowl as a garnish.

Soups are a good way to hydrate and to use the cooking liquid, so water-soluble vitamins that leach out are still eaten. A **smoothie** blends whole fruit (sometimes with yoghurt, milk or oats), so it keeps more fibre than a juice, but its sugars are still free sugars. Keep portions to 150 ml and serve them freshly made so less vitamin C is lost by oxidation.

::: terms
- **Hydration**: having enough water in the body for it to work properly.
- **Dehydration**: a state in which the body has lost more water than it has taken in, affecting how it works.
- **Fluid intake**: the total amount of drinks taken in a day, including water, milk, tea and juice.
- **Sweat**: water released through the skin, which cools the body as it evaporates.
- **Urine**: the liquid made by the kidneys that removes waste from the body; pale urine shows good hydration.
- **Oral rehydration drink**: a drink containing water, salts and a little sugar, used to replace fluid lost in vomiting or diarrhoea.
- **Smoothie**: a drink made by blending whole fruit or vegetables, often with milk or yoghurt; its sugars count as free sugars.
- **Thirst mechanism**: the body's signal to drink, which becomes weaker in older people.
:::

::: method
1. **Learn the three spec functions exactly:** eliminating waste (urine, preventing constipation), cooling (sweat evaporating), digestion (saliva, digestive juices, moving food).
2. **For "how is water lost", list the routes:** urine, sweat, breath, faeces, plus vomiting and diarrhoea when ill.
3. **For fluid needs, give the figure:** 6 to 8 glasses (about 1.5–2 litres) for adults, more in heat, exercise and illness.
4. **For "extra fluid" questions, give the occasion and the reason:** "exercise, because more sweat is produced to cool the body".
5. **For drink choice, go beyond quantity:** water and milk rather than sugary drinks; juice and smoothies limited to 150 ml.
6. **In extended answers on a named person, analyse why they are at risk,** then evaluate practical ways to help that suit their situation, and judge which would work best.
:::

::: example Give — functions of water
Give three functions of water in the body. (3)

It removes waste products from the body in urine (1). It cools the body when sweat evaporates from the skin (1). It is needed for digestion, for example in saliva and digestive juices (1).
:::

::: example Identify — extra fluid
Identify two occasions when a person needs extra fluid, and give a reason for each. (4)

During exercise (1), because the body sweats more to cool down, so more water is lost (1). When ill with diarrhoea or vomiting (1), because large amounts of water are lost from the body and must be replaced to avoid dehydration (1).
:::

::: example Explain — the 150 ml limit
Explain why only 150 ml of fruit juice or smoothie a day counts towards the 5 a day. (2)

Juicing or blending breaks down the fruit's cells and releases the sugars, so they become free sugars that can damage teeth and add extra energy (1); juice also has less fibre than whole fruit, so drinking more does not give more benefit, and the limit keeps free sugar intake down (1).
:::

::: example Explain — dehydration and schoolwork
Explain how being dehydrated could affect a student's performance in a morning exam. (3)

Even mild dehydration can cause headaches and tiredness (1), and it reduces concentration and alertness (1), so the student may think less clearly, make mistakes and not finish the paper; drinking water at breakfast and taking a bottle of water into the exam helps (1).
:::

::: example Explain — water and fibre
Explain why someone who increases the fibre in their diet should also drink more fluid. (2)

Fibre absorbs water in the large intestine to make faeces soft and bulky (1); without enough fluid, the faeces can become hard and dry, causing constipation rather than preventing it (1).
:::

::: test
Arthur is 84 and lives alone. He has arthritis in his hands, walks slowly with a stick and is starting to become forgetful. He drinks one cup of tea at breakfast and another in the afternoon, and says he avoids drinking in the evening so he does not have to get up at night.
- Analyse the hydration needs of Arthur and the risks he faces.
- Evaluate ways to help Arthur stay well hydrated.

(8)
---
Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis of Arthur's needs, clearly linked to his age, arthritis, mobility, memory and living alone, with the health risks of dehydration; the evaluation weighs several practical solutions against his situation and reaches a supported judgement.
- **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear analysis with several links to Arthur, and sound solutions with some evaluation.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** some relevant points about hydration and older people, not well applied; solutions general.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** simple points such as "he should drink more water".
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- He needs about 6 to 8 drinks a day; two cups of tea is far too little.
- His thirst sense is likely weaker with age and his kidneys conserve water less well, so he may not feel thirsty.
- Arthritis makes lifting a kettle or opening bottles hard; slow walking makes getting drinks and reaching the toilet difficult, which is why he avoids evening drinks.
- Forgetfulness means he may not remember to drink; living alone means no one prompts him.
- Risks: confusion (worsening his memory), dizziness and falls (dangerous with his mobility), urine infections, constipation, headaches and tiredness.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Keep a lidded, lightweight cup or a bottle with a straw and a jug of water beside his chair, so drinks are always within reach; easy and cheap.
- A drinks routine linked to daily events (with each meal, each TV programme), a chart, or an alarm or reminder from family or a carer; helps with forgetfulness but relies on support.
- Most drinks earlier in the day, with a smaller drink in the evening, to reduce night-time toilet trips while still meeting needs.
- Offer a variety: water, milk (also calcium), decaffeinated tea, sugar-free squash, soup; include water-rich foods such as fruit, jelly, yoghurt and milk puddings.
- Kitchen aids for arthritis, such as a kettle tipper or a hot water dispenser, make drinks easier to prepare.
- Judgement: no single change is enough; the most effective combination is keeping drinks within reach plus a routine or reminder, because his main barriers are access and memory rather than knowledge.
:::
