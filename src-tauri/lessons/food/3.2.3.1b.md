## What it is

Everyone needs the same nutrients, but **how much** of each changes with **life stage** (age, growth, activity) and with **dietary needs** (beliefs, medical conditions). Planning a balanced diet means following the Eatwell Guide (3.2.3.1a) and then adjusting for the person: naming the nutrients they need more or less of, **and why**.

**Life stages**

| Life stage | Key needs | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Young children (about 1–5)** | **energy-dense** food in **small, frequent meals** and snacks; **protein**; **calcium and vitamin D**; **iron**; vitamins A and C | growing fast and very active, but with small stomachs, so bulky high-fibre food can fill them before they get enough energy; bones and teeth are forming; iron stores run low after the first year |
| | **limit salt and free sugars** | kidneys cannot cope with much salt (about 2 g a day at 1–3 years); sugar causes tooth decay in new teeth |
| | whole milk until 2, then semi-skimmed if eating well; no skimmed milk before 5 | fat gives energy and vitamin A for growth |
| **Older children (5–12)** | energy and protein for growth and play; calcium, vitamin D; iron; fibre building towards adult levels | steady growth, active lives; habits formed now tend to last |
| **Teenagers (13–18)** | more **energy** and **protein** | the **adolescent growth spurt** and, often, sport |
| | **calcium and vitamin D** | building towards **peak bone mass** at about 30; about 1,000 mg calcium a day for boys and 800 mg for girls |
| | **iron** | growth increases blood volume; **girls lose iron in menstruation**; teenage girls often fall short |
| | limit free sugars, saturated fat and salt | snacking, sugary drinks and takeaways are common; risk of tooth decay and weight gain |
| **Adults** | **maintenance**: enough energy to match activity, no more | growth has stopped; many adults have sedentary jobs, so weight gain is common |
| | fibre, fruit and vegetables; limit saturated fat and salt | reduce the risk of CHD, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes and bowel disease |
| | women: iron until the menopause; folic acid if planning a pregnancy | menstruation; neural tube development |
| **Older adults (65+)** | **less energy** but the **same or more nutrients** (nutrient-dense food) | BMR falls as muscle is lost; activity falls; appetite may be small |
| | **calcium and vitamin D** | bone loss and osteoporosis, especially women after the menopause; skin makes less vitamin D and many are indoors |
| | **fibre and fluids** | the gut slows, so constipation is common; thirst weakens |
| | **vitamin B12, iron, vitamin C** | absorption of B12 falls with age; small appetites; poor dentition may limit fruit and vegetables |
| | soft, easy-to-eat, tasty food | chewing problems, reduced sense of taste and smell, cost, living alone |

**Pregnancy** (context): folic acid supplement before conception and in the first 12 weeks; vitamin D supplement; iron and calcium; avoid liver and vitamin A supplements, and foods that could carry listeria; energy needs rise only slightly, late in pregnancy.

**Dietary groups**

**Vegetarians** eat no meat, poultry or fish, or products of slaughter such as gelatine and animal rennet.

| Type | Eats | Avoids |
|---|---|---|
| **Lacto-ovo vegetarian** | dairy and eggs | meat, poultry, fish |
| **Lacto-vegetarian** | dairy | meat, poultry, fish, eggs |
| **Ovo-vegetarian** | eggs | meat, poultry, fish, dairy |
| **Pescatarian** (not strictly vegetarian) | fish, dairy and eggs | meat and poultry |
| **Vegan** | only plant foods | all animal foods, including dairy, eggs and honey |

Nutrients to plan for: **protein** (use HBV soya, quinoa and eggs, or complement LBV proteins such as beans with rice); **iron** (non-haem only, so combine pulses, dark greens and fortified cereals with vitamin C). Vegans also need to plan for: **vitamin B12** (fortified foods or a supplement, as it occurs naturally only in animal foods); **calcium** (calcium-fortified plant milks and yoghurts, tofu set with calcium, fortified bread, kale, almonds); **vitamin D**; **iodine** (usually from milk and fish); **omega-3** (walnuts, ground linseed, chia seeds, rapeseed oil).

**Coeliac disease** is a lifelong **autoimmune condition**: the immune system reacts to **gluten**, a protein in **wheat, barley and rye**, and damages the lining of the small intestine. Nutrients are then poorly absorbed, causing diarrhoea, bloating, tiredness, weight loss and anaemia, and poor growth in children. The only treatment is a strict **gluten-free diet** for life; even crumbs from a toaster or a shared board can cause a reaction.
- **Naturally gluten-free**: rice, potatoes, corn (maize, polenta, cornflour), quinoa, buckwheat, millet, gram (chickpea) flour, meat, fish, eggs, dairy, fruit, vegetables, pulses.
- **Watch for hidden gluten**: stock cubes, gravy granules, soy sauce, sausages, breadcrumbs, batter, some sauces and malt vinegar. **Oats** are often contaminated with wheat, so choose oats labelled gluten-free; most coeliacs can eat these.
- Look for the **crossed-grain symbol** or the words "gluten free", and use gluten-free flour, pasta and bread. Cook with clean, separate equipment.

**Lactose intolerance** means the body makes too little of the enzyme **lactase**, so **lactose** (milk sugar) is not digested. It is fermented by gut bacteria, causing bloating, wind, stomach pain and diarrhoea. It is not an allergy.
- Use **lactose-free milk and yoghurt**, or **plant milks fortified with calcium** (soya, oat, almond).
- Hard cheeses such as Cheddar contain very little lactose, and many people can tolerate small amounts.
- The risk is low **calcium**, so include fortified alternatives, canned fish with bones, tofu and green vegetables.

**A high-fibre diet** is advised for constipation, for some bowel conditions and to help weight control and cholesterol. Plan it with wholegrain bread, pasta and rice, porridge, pulses, fruit and vegetables with skins, nuts and seeds. **Increase fibre gradually** and **drink more fluid**, or it can cause bloating and constipation.

**Planning a balanced meal for a group: a checklist**
1. Include a food from each main Eatwell group: starchy (wholegrain), fruit and vegetables, protein, dairy or alternative.
2. Remove anything the person must avoid, and check hidden ingredients.
3. Add the nutrients the group is at risk of missing.
4. Choose cooking methods that keep fat and salt low and conserve vitamins.
5. Justify every choice: the food, the nutrient, and why that person needs it.

::: terms
- **Life stage**: a period of life with its own nutritional needs, such as childhood, adolescence, adulthood or old age.
- **Growth spurt**: the rapid increase in height and body size during adolescence, which raises needs for energy, protein, calcium and iron.
- **Energy-dense food**: food that provides a lot of energy in a small amount, useful for young children with small stomachs.
- **Lacto-ovo vegetarian**: a vegetarian who eats dairy foods and eggs but no meat, poultry or fish.
- **Pescatarian**: a person who eats fish, dairy and eggs but no meat or poultry; not strictly vegetarian.
- **Vegan**: a person who eats no food from animals, including milk, eggs and honey.
- **Coeliac disease**: an autoimmune condition in which gluten damages the lining of the small intestine.
- **Gluten**: a protein found in wheat, barley and rye, which people with coeliac disease must avoid.
- **Crossed-grain symbol**: a symbol on packaging showing that a product is gluten-free.
- **Lactose intolerance**: an inability to digest lactose because the body makes too little of the enzyme lactase.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the life stage or dietary group** and what makes it different: growth, activity, a medical condition or a belief.
2. **Name the nutrient and give the reason for that group:** "calcium, because teenagers are building peak bone mass".
3. **For vegans and vegetarians, cover the at-risk nutrients:** protein, iron, and for vegans B12, calcium, vitamin D, iodine and omega-3.
4. **For coeliac and lactose intolerance, name safe substitutes** and warn about hidden sources and cross-contamination.
5. **For "modify this recipe", change each problem ingredient** and say what the replacement supplies or avoids.
6. **For extended questions, analyse the needs first, then go through the given meals one by one,** saying what they supply and what they miss, and finish with a justified judgement.
:::

::: example Explain — teenager to older adult
Explain how nutritional needs change from being a teenager to being elderly. (6)

Teenagers need a lot of energy and protein for the growth spurt and activity (1), whereas older adults need less energy because their BMR falls with loss of muscle and they are usually less active (1). Teenagers need calcium and vitamin D to build bone towards peak bone mass (1); older adults still need them, but now to slow bone loss and reduce the risk of osteoporosis and fractures (1). Teenage girls need extra iron because of menstruation (1). Older adults need more fibre and fluid to prevent constipation, and may need B12 and vitamin D supplements because absorption and skin synthesis fall with age (1).
:::

::: example Plan — a coeliac meal
Plan a two-course evening meal for a teenager with coeliac disease and justify your choices. (6)

Main course: grilled chicken with a homemade tomato and pepper sauce, served with rice and broccoli (1). Rice is naturally gluten-free, so it replaces pasta or couscous as the starchy food (1); the sauce is made from scratch because ready-made sauces and stock cubes may contain wheat (1). Chicken gives HBV protein for growth and the vegetables add vitamin C and fibre (1). Dessert: fruit crumble with a topping of gluten-free flour and oats labelled gluten-free, with custard made with cornflour (1). Cornflour and certified oats avoid gluten; all equipment is cleaned first so crumbs from wheat products do not contaminate the meal (1).
:::

::: example Modify — a vegan moussaka
A moussaka is made with lamb mince, a white sauce of butter, flour, milk and egg yolk, and a Parmesan topping. Explain how to modify it for a vegan. (6)

Replace the lamb mince with green lentils or soya mince (1), which supply protein (HBV from soya; lentils complement the wheat in the sauce) and add fibre (1). Make the white sauce with a calcium-fortified soya or oat milk and a vegan spread, and leave out the egg yolk (1), so there is no dairy or egg while calcium is still provided (1). Replace the Parmesan, which contains milk and animal rennet, with a vegan cheese or nutritional yeast and breadcrumbs (1), so the top still browns and has a savoury flavour; use a vegetable stock cube checked for milk (1).
:::

::: example Explain — lactose intolerance
Explain how a person who is lactose intolerant can still get enough calcium. (3)

They can drink lactose-free milk, which has the lactose broken down but keeps its calcium (1). They can use plant milks and yoghurts that are fortified with calcium, such as soya or oat (1). They can also eat other calcium sources such as canned sardines with the bones, tofu, fortified bread, kale and small amounts of hard cheese, which contains very little lactose (1).
:::

::: example Explain — young children and fibre
Explain why young children should not be given a very high-fibre diet. (2)

High-fibre foods are bulky and filling, and young children have small stomachs, so they may feel full before they have eaten enough energy and nutrients for growth (1); a lot of fibre can also reduce the absorption of minerals such as iron and calcium (1).
:::

::: q
Name the protein that a person with coeliac disease must avoid, and two cereals that contain it. (3)
---
Gluten (1); any two of wheat, barley, rye (1 each).
:::

::: q
Give two nutrients teenagers need in larger amounts, with a reason for each. (4)
---
Any two, 1 for the nutrient and 1 for the reason: energy or protein (1) for the growth spurt and activity (1); calcium or vitamin D (1) to build peak bone mass (1); iron (1) for growing blood volume and, in girls, to replace iron lost in menstruation (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a lacto-ovo vegetarian and a vegan. (2)
---
A lacto-ovo vegetarian eats dairy foods and eggs, but no meat, poultry or fish (1); a vegan eats no animal products at all, including milk, eggs and honey (1).
:::

::: q
Give two gluten-free alternatives to wheat pasta. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): rice; rice noodles; potatoes; gluten-free pasta made from corn, rice or lentils; quinoa; polenta; buckwheat.
:::

::: q
Explain why older people may need a vitamin D supplement. (2)
---
Older skin makes less vitamin D from sunlight, and many older people spend little time outdoors (1); vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium, so low levels speed up bone loss and increase the risk of osteoporosis and fractures (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a person following a high-fibre diet should increase fibre gradually and drink more fluid. (2)
---
A sudden increase can cause bloating, wind and discomfort as the gut adjusts (1); fibre absorbs water, so without extra fluid faeces become hard and constipation can result (1).
:::

::: watch
- Every nutrient needs a **reason for that life stage**: "iron, because of menstruation" earns the second mark.
- **Older people need less energy but not fewer nutrients.** Say "nutrient-dense".
- **Coeliac disease is an autoimmune reaction to gluten**, not an allergy (the spec lists gluten under intolerances). Mention hidden gluten and cross-contamination.
- **Pescatarians are not vegetarians.** Vegans avoid honey too.
- For vegans, don't forget **B12**, **calcium** and **iodine** as well as protein and iron.
:::
