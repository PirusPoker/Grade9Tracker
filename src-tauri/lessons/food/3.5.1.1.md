## What it is

Nobody chooses food on nutrition alone. What ends up on the plate is the result of a set of **factors** pulling in different directions: money, time, taste, habit, health, what the shops have and what the day is for. The spec names twelve factors, and the exam expects you to take a named person, pick the factor that matters most for *them*, and explain **how** it changes what they eat.

| Factor | How it works | Example of its effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical activity level (PAL)** | The more active someone is, the more energy (and often protein and fluid) they need | A county-level swimmer training twice a day chooses larger portions of starchy carbohydrate, plus a protein-rich recovery snack; an office worker who drives to work needs smaller portions to avoid weight gain |
| **Celebration or occasion** | Special events have traditional or expected foods, often richer and more expensive | Birthday cake, a roast for Sunday lunch, turkey at Christmas, a buffet at a christening, food for Diwali or Eid |
| **Cost of food** | The price of individual items steers people towards cheaper choices or cheaper versions | Buying own-brand baked beans rather than a leading brand; using frozen vegetables instead of fresh |
| **Preferences** | Likes and dislikes of taste, texture, smell and appearance | A child who refuses anything "lumpy"; someone who dislikes spicy food |
| **Enjoyment** | Food is a pleasure and a social activity, not just fuel | Eating out with friends; choosing a favourite comfort food after a bad day |
| **Food availability** | What the local shops stock, and what can be reached | Someone in a rural village without a car relies on a small, dearer shop; online delivery widens choice |
| **Healthy eating** | Knowledge of, and interest in, the guidelines | Choosing wholemeal bread, grilled not fried, reading labels for salt; following advice after a diagnosis such as high blood pressure |
| **Income** | The total money a household has decides the food budget, not just the price of one item | A family on a low income plans meals around cheap staples (pasta, rice, potatoes, pulses) and buys reduced-price items |
| **Lifestyles** | Work patterns, family structure, living alone, hobbies | A shift worker eats at odd times; a busy single parent relies on quick meals; a student in shared housing has limited kitchen space |
| **Seasonality** | Foods are cheaper, fresher and tastier when in season in the UK | Strawberries in June, root vegetables and squash in autumn and winter; soups and stews in cold weather, salads in summer |
| **Time of day** | Different meals and foods suit different times | Cereal or toast for breakfast; a light lunch; the main meal in the evening; snacks after school |
| **Time available to prepare and cook** | How much time and energy someone has to shop, prepare and cook | A nurse coming off a 12-hour shift may choose a microwave meal; someone at home at the weekend may bake bread |

**Income and cost are different factors.** *Cost* is the price of a food. *Income* is how much money a household has in total. A student who can afford the more expensive item may still choose the cheaper one because of habit or value; a family whose income has fallen changes its whole shopping pattern. Examiners give separate credit, so do not merge them.

**Factors interact.** A factor rarely works alone. A pensioner living alone on a small income (income, lifestyle) may find cooking for one wasteful (time, cost), and may have a reduced appetite and dental problems (preference). The best answers link two factors and explain the result for that person.

### Costing recipes

The spec asks you to **cost recipes and make modifications**. Every costing question follows the same three steps:

1. **Cost of the amount used** = amount used ÷ pack size × pack price. For 150 g of flour from a 1.5 kg bag costing £0.90: 150 ÷ 1500 × 0.90 = £0.09.
2. **Total cost** = add the cost of every ingredient.
3. **Cost per portion** = total cost ÷ number of portions.

Units must match before you divide. Convert kilograms to grams (1 kg = 1000 g) and litres to millilitres (1 litre = 1000 ml). Where an item is used whole, such as "1 can" or "1 onion", its cost is the price of one. Round only at the end, to the nearest penny, and write money with the £ sign and two decimal places.

**Worked costing: a tuna pasta bake that serves 4**

| Ingredient | Used | Pack price | Cost of amount used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pasta | 300 g | £1.00 for 500 g | 300 ÷ 500 × 1.00 = £0.60 |
| Tuna | 2 cans | £1.10 a can | 2 × 1.10 = £2.20 |
| Sweetcorn | 1 can | £0.60 a can | £0.60 |
| Milk | 500 ml | £1.00 a litre | 500 ÷ 1000 × 1.00 = £0.50 |
| Cheddar | 100 g | £3.20 for 400 g | 100 ÷ 400 × 3.20 = £0.80 |
| Butter | 25 g | £2.50 for 250 g | 25 ÷ 250 × 2.50 = £0.25 |
| Plain flour | 25 g | £1.00 for 500 g | 25 ÷ 500 × 1.00 = £0.05 |
| **Total** | | | **£5.00** |
| **Per portion** | | | 5.00 ÷ 4 = **£1.25** |

**Modifying a recipe to cut cost.** The skill examiners want is to lower the price *without* losing too much nutritional value or eating quality. Useful swaps:
- **Own-brand or value ranges** instead of branded products: often much cheaper, with similar nutrition.
- **Seasonal fruit and vegetables**, which are plentiful and so cheaper, and at their best flavour.
- **Frozen or canned vegetables and fruit**: cheaper than fresh out of season, no waste, and frozen vegetables keep their vitamins well because they are frozen soon after harvest.
- **Pulses to extend meat**: replacing some mince with lentils or beans cuts cost and saturated fat and adds fibre.
- **Cheaper cuts of meat**, cooked slowly (braising, stewing), in place of prime cuts.
- **A strong-flavoured cheese**, so less is needed.
- **Bulk buying** of foods that keep (rice, pasta, oats, frozen goods): a lower price per kilogram, but only if the food is used before it spoils and there is money and space to buy in bulk.
- **Batch cooking and freezing portions**, and **using leftovers**, to avoid waste.

Replacing one can of tuna (£1.10) in the pasta bake with 200 g of frozen peas (£1.50 for 1 kg, so £0.30) brings the total to £4.20 and the portion to £1.05. It saves 20p a portion, lowers the protein slightly and adds fibre and colour. Saying what a swap does to nutrition and sensory quality is what turns a costing answer into an evaluation.

::: terms
- **Food choice**: the decisions people make about which foods to buy, cook and eat, shaped by many factors at once.
- **Physical activity level (PAL)**: a measure of how active a person is, which raises or lowers the energy they need each day.
- **Income**: the total money a person or household has, which sets the overall food budget.
- **Cost of food**: the price of individual foods, which pushes people towards cheaper items or versions.
- **Availability**: whether a food can actually be bought or reached, which depends on shops, transport, delivery and season.
- **Seasonality**: the time of year when a food is naturally harvested in the UK, when it is usually cheapest and best in flavour.
- **Lifestyle**: the pattern of someone's daily life, such as work hours, family set-up and hobbies, which affects when and how they eat.
- **Cost per portion**: the total cost of a recipe divided by the number of servings it makes.
- **Own-brand**: products sold under a supermarket's own label, usually cheaper than well-known brands.
- **Bulk buying**: buying large quantities to get a lower price per unit, worthwhile only for foods that keep.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the person.** Underline everything in the scenario that hints at a factor: job, age, activity, money, family, time, health, where they live.
2. **Name the factor exactly as the spec does** (income, lifestyle, PAL, time available…).
3. **Explain its effect on this person's choice.** Factor, then a because, then the food they would choose. "Her low income means… so she will choose…".
4. **For "explain" questions give a linked second point**: a consequence for health, cost or waste.
5. **For costings**, set out every step: amount used ÷ pack size × price, then the total, then ÷ portions. Label each figure and give £ to two decimal places.
6. **For modifications**, name the swap, give the new cost if asked, and say what it does to nutrition or quality.
7. **For extended questions**, analyse several factors that interact, then evaluate: which matters most for this person, the trade-offs, and a justified conclusion.
:::

::: example Identify and explain — three people
For each person, identify one factor that may influence their food choice and explain how. (a) Callum, 19, a trainee electrician who plays rugby three evenings a week. (b) Irene, 82, a widow living alone on her state pension. (c) Priya, 35, a hospital doctor who works long night shifts. (6)

(a) Physical activity level (1): Callum's high PAL means he needs more energy, so he will choose larger portions of starchy foods such as pasta and potatoes, and protein foods to repair muscle after matches (1).
(b) Income (1): a pension gives Irene a limited budget, so she is likely to buy cheaper staples and small packs or reduced items; cooking for one can also feel wasteful, so she may choose soups, toast or ready meals (1).
(c) Time available and lifestyle (1): after a long night shift Priya has little time or energy to cook and eats at unusual times, so she may choose quick ready meals or snacks from vending machines, unless she batch cooks and freezes meals on her days off (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — cost per portion
A fruit crumble serves 6. It uses 600 g apples (£2.25 for 1.5 kg), 150 g plain flour (£0.90 for 1.5 kg), 75 g butter (£2.00 for 250 g), 100 g sugar (£1.00 for 1 kg) and 50 g oats (£1.00 for 1 kg). Calculate the cost of one portion. Show your working. (3)

Apples 600 ÷ 1500 × 2.25 = £0.90; flour 150 ÷ 1500 × 0.90 = £0.09; butter 75 ÷ 250 × 2.00 = £0.60; sugar 100 ÷ 1000 × 1.00 = £0.10; oats 50 ÷ 1000 × 1.00 = £0.05 (1). Total £0.90 + £0.09 + £0.60 + £0.10 + £0.05 = £1.74 (1). Per portion £1.74 ÷ 6 = £0.29 (1).
:::

::: example Explain — seasonality
Explain why a family might choose to eat seasonal vegetables. (4)

When vegetables are in season in the UK they are plentiful, so they are usually cheaper (1), which helps a family on a tight budget buy more of them (1). They are harvested at their peak and travel a shorter distance, so they are fresher, with better flavour and texture (1), and they lose fewer vitamins, such as vitamin C, between harvest and eating (1).
:::

::: example Suggest — modify for cost
Suggest three ways to reduce the cost of a beef lasagne while keeping it nutritious. Give a reason for each. (6)

Replace half the minced beef with red lentils (1): lentils are much cheaper than meat, still provide protein and add fibre while lowering saturated fat (1). Use own-brand passata, pasta and cheese (1): own-brand products cost less and are nutritionally very similar (1). Add seasonal or frozen vegetables such as carrots, onions and peppers to bulk out the sauce (1): they are cheap, extend the number of portions and add vitamins and fibre (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — first term at university
Josh has just started university and lives in a shared house with a small kitchen. He has £30 a week for food.
- Analyse the factors that may influence Josh's food choices.
- Evaluate how he could eat well on his budget. (8)

Josh's **income** is small, so price will drive every purchase; with no experience of budgeting he may overspend early in the week and live on instant noodles by Friday. His **lifestyle** has changed: lectures at irregular times, late nights and socialising mean meals are skipped and takeaways are tempting, especially with delivery apps. **Time available** and a shared, cramped kitchen with limited fridge and freezer space discourage cooking from scratch. **Preferences** learnt at home and **enjoyment** of eating with housemates also shape what he buys, and a lack of cooking skills limits his options.

He could plan meals for the week and write a list, which stops impulse buys and waste. Staples such as pasta, rice, oats, potatoes, eggs, canned tomatoes, beans and lentils are cheap and filling, and give protein, fibre and energy. Buying frozen vegetables and own-brand products keeps costs low while meeting 5 a day. Batch cooking a chilli or curry and freezing portions saves time and money, and cooking with housemates shares costs and adds enjoyment. The limit is storage space and the time to learn skills; but even two batch-cooked meals a week would cost far less per portion than takeaways and would be much healthier, so planning and batch cooking are the most effective changes.

(Level 4, 7–8: several factors analysed and linked to Josh; a balanced evaluation with a reasoned judgement.)
:::

::: q
Give two factors, other than cost, that could influence a family's choice of food for a weekday evening meal. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): time available to prepare and cook; lifestyle (work and school commitments); preferences of family members; healthy eating; availability; PAL; seasonality; time of day.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between "cost of food" and "income" as factors in food choice. (2)
---
Cost is the price of individual food items, which can push people to choose cheaper versions (1); income is the total money a household has, which limits the overall food budget and so the whole pattern of shopping (1).
:::

::: q
A recipe uses 250 g of cheese from a 400 g block costing £3.60. Calculate the cost of the cheese used. (2)
---
250 ÷ 400 × 3.60 (1) = £2.25 (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a celebration can influence food choice. (2)
---
Celebrations have traditional or expected foods, such as a cake for a birthday or a roast turkey at Christmas (1), so people buy richer, more expensive or special foods they would not choose every day (1).
:::

::: q
Rice costs £1.60 for a 1 kg bag or £6.00 for a 5 kg bag. Calculate the saving per kilogram from buying the larger bag, and give one reason why bulk buying may not suit every household. (3)
---
5 kg bag: £6.00 ÷ 5 = £1.20 per kg (1); saving £1.60 − £1.20 = £0.40 per kg (1). Reason (1): a household on a low income may not have the money to pay more at once; there may be no storage space; food that is not used in time is wasted, which cancels the saving.
:::

::: q
Explain how lifestyle could influence the food choices of a single parent working full time. (4)
---
Long working hours and school runs leave little time to shop and cook (1), so they may rely on quick convenience foods or ready meals (1), which can be higher in salt, fat and cost (1); they may plan and batch cook at weekends or use online shopping to save time (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Name the factor, then explain its effect on this person.** "Cost" alone scores nothing without saying what the person would buy as a result.
- **Income is not cost**, and **lifestyle is not time available**: they are separate factors and earn separate marks.
- In costings, **convert units first** (kg to g, litres to ml) and **finish with the per-portion step**. Most lost marks come from stopping at the total.
- Write money as **£0.29**, not "0.29p" or "29p£".
- A cost-cutting swap should keep the dish **nutritious**. Say what it does to protein, fibre, fat or vitamins.
- Evaluate: bulk buying and seasonal buying save money **only if** the food is used, stored and affordable up front.
:::

::: test
A soup serves 4. It uses 1 kg carrots (£0.70 for 1 kg), 2 onions (£0.15 each), 1 litre stock (£0.80 for 1 litre of stock made from cubes), 50 ml single cream (£1.20 for 300 ml) and 10 g fresh coriander (£0.80 for 40 g). Calculate the cost of one portion. Show your working. (3)
---
Carrots £0.70; onions 2 × 0.15 = £0.30; stock £0.80; cream 50 ÷ 300 × 1.20 = £0.20; coriander 10 ÷ 40 × 0.80 = £0.20 (1). Total £2.20 (1). Per portion £2.20 ÷ 4 = £0.55 (1).

::: test
Explain how the time of day can influence food choice. (2)
---
Different meals are expected at different times, for example cereal or toast at breakfast and a larger cooked meal in the evening (1); time of day also links to routine and hunger, so people snack after school or eat lighter foods late at night (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how healthy eating knowledge might change the food choices of a man who has been told he has high cholesterol. (3)
---
He will understand that saturated fat raises blood cholesterol (1), so he may switch from fried foods, fatty meat and butter to grilled lean meat, fish and unsaturated oils (1); he may also read labels and choose foods high in soluble fibre, such as oats, or cholesterol-lowering spreads (1).
:::

::: test
Explain two ways preferences could influence the food choices of a young child. (4)
---
Two ways, each explained (2 + 2): young children often dislike bitter or strong flavours (1), so they may refuse green vegetables unless they are hidden in sauces or served with familiar foods (1); children may reject unfamiliar textures such as lumps or skins (1), so parents choose smooth or familiar foods, which can limit variety (1); bright colours and fun shapes appeal (1), so they prefer foods with attractive packaging or presentation (1).
:::

::: test
A student buys a branded pasta sauce at £1.80 a jar. The own-brand version costs £0.75. She uses one jar a week. Calculate how much she would save over a 10-week term by switching, and give one possible drawback of switching. (3)
---
Saving per jar £1.80 − £0.75 = £1.05 (1); over 10 weeks 10 × £1.05 = £10.50 (1). Drawback (1): she may prefer the taste of the branded sauce; the own-brand may be higher in salt or sugar, so she should compare labels.
:::

::: test
Explain how a person's income could affect the nutritional quality of their diet. (4)
---
A low income means a small food budget (1), so people may buy cheap, energy-dense foods high in fat and sugar that are filling for the money (1), and buy less fresh fruit, vegetables, fish and lean meat, which cost more per calorie (1); this can lead to low intakes of vitamins, minerals and fibre and a higher risk of obesity; credit that careful planning with pulses, frozen vegetables and own-brand staples can give a healthy diet cheaply (1).
:::

::: test
Suggest three ways to reduce the cost of a chicken curry for six people while keeping it nutritious. Give a reason for each. (6)
---
Three changes, each with a reason (1 + 1 each):
- Use chicken thighs instead of breast (1): cheaper and still high in protein, and they stay moist in a curry (1).
- Replace some of the chicken with chickpeas or lentils (1): cheaper protein that adds fibre and extends the number of portions (1).
- Use canned tomatoes and frozen or seasonal vegetables (1): cheaper than fresh out of season and still provide vitamins and fibre (1).
- Make the curry paste from spices rather than buying a jar (1): cheaper per portion and lower in salt (1).
- Serve with own-brand rice bought in bulk (1): lower cost per kilogram for a food that keeps (1).
:::

::: test
Fatima is a single mother of two primary-school children. She works part time and money is tight.
- Analyse the factors that influence the food Fatima buys and cooks for her family.
- Evaluate how she could provide healthy meals at low cost. (8)
---
Levels of response:
- **7–8:** a detailed analysis of several factors, clearly applied to Fatima and linked to one another; a balanced evaluation of realistic strategies, with a supported judgement.
- **5–6:** a clear analysis of several factors with some application; the evaluation is reasonable but less balanced.
- **3–4:** some relevant factors with limited explanation; the evaluation is brief or mostly a list.
- **1–2:** simple points with little application.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Income is limited, so cost dominates; own-brand, reduced items and offers are attractive.
- Lifestyle: working and caring alone leaves little time to shop and cook, so convenience foods appeal.
- Children's preferences: fussy eating leads to safe, familiar foods; food refused is wasted money.
- Healthy eating: she may want to meet the Eatwell Guide but find fresh fruit, fish and lean meat expensive.
- Availability: no car could mean using a nearby, dearer convenience store.
- Time of day: breakfast and packed lunches must be quick.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Plan a weekly menu and shop with a list, which cuts impulse buys and waste.
- Cheap nutritious staples: oats, eggs, pulses, canned fish, potatoes, pasta and rice; wholemeal versions cost little more.
- Frozen and canned vegetables and fruit are cheap and keep their nutrients.
- Batch cook on a day off and freeze portions, which saves time and money.
- Extend mince with lentils; use cheaper cuts in slow-cooked dishes.
- Involve the children in cooking to widen their preferences.
- Limits: time and energy are real constraints; bulk buying needs cash up front; convenience foods are sometimes the practical option.
- Judgement: planning plus a small number of cheap staples and batch cooking can give a healthy diet at lower cost than convenience food, but it depends on having time to cook at least a few times a week.
:::

::: test
Evaluate whether buying frozen vegetables instead of fresh is a good way for a family to save money. (4)
---
For (up to 2): frozen vegetables are often cheaper than fresh, especially out of season (1); they keep for months, so nothing is wasted, and portions can be measured out (1); they are frozen soon after harvest, so vitamin content is often as good as or better than fresh vegetables stored for days (1). Against (up to 2): some vegetables are cheaper fresh when in season (1); texture can be softer after freezing, so they suit cooked dishes more than salads (1); a freezer is needed, and space may be limited (1). Maximum 4, and full marks need a supported conclusion, e.g. "a good saving for cooked dishes, but buy fresh when in season".
:::
