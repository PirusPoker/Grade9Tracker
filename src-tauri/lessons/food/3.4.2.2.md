## What it is

This is food safety in action: the habits and checks that stop bacteria getting into food, stop them multiplying, and make sure they are killed. The spec lists **personal hygiene**, **clean work surfaces**, keeping **raw and cooked food separate** with separate utensils, **correct cooking times**, **temperature control** (including **defrosting** and **reheating**), **care with high-risk foods**, and using a **food temperature probe**. It also links to practical skills: knife grips, washing vegetables, blending, shaping wet mixtures and testing when food is ready.

The exam rewards a **rule plus a reason** every time: "tie hair back **so that** hair and the bacteria on it do not fall into the food".

### Personal hygiene

People carry bacteria on their hands, skin, hair, nose and mouth (see Staphylococcus aureus in 3.4.1.4).

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| **Wash hands** before starting, after handling raw meat, fish or eggs, after the toilet, after touching the face, hair, phone, money or bins, and between tasks | Removes bacteria picked up from raw food, the body and dirty surfaces before they reach food |
| **Tie hair back** or wear a hat or hairnet | Stops hair and the bacteria on it falling into food |
| **Wear a clean apron** | Protects food from bacteria on outdoor clothes |
| **Remove rings, watches and bracelets** | Bacteria collect under them, and jewellery can fall into food |
| **Keep nails short and clean**, with no varnish | Bacteria and dirt collect under long nails; varnish can chip into food |
| **Cover cuts** with a **blue waterproof plaster** | Cuts carry Staphylococcus aureus; blue is easy to spot if the plaster falls into food |
| **Don't cough or sneeze over food**; don't touch the nose or mouth | Droplets from the nose and mouth carry bacteria |
| **Taste with a clean spoon**, not the stirring spoon or fingers | Saliva carries bacteria back into the food |
| **Don't prepare food when ill** with vomiting or diarrhoea; wait **48 hours** after symptoms stop | Bacteria are still passed on after symptoms end |

**How to wash hands properly**: wet hands under warm running water → apply soap → rub palms, backs of hands, between the fingers, thumbs, fingertips and nails, for about **20 seconds** → rinse well → **dry thoroughly** with a clean disposable paper towel (damp hands spread bacteria more easily) → turn off the tap with the towel if possible.

### Clean work surfaces and equipment

- **Cleaning** removes visible dirt, grease and food debris, using hot soapy water.
- **Disinfecting** (sanitising) **kills bacteria**, using an antibacterial spray or very hot water, left for the time on the label.
- Surfaces that have had raw meat on them need **both**: clean first, then disinfect.
- **Clean as you go**: wipe spills straight away and keep the work area tidy.
- Use **clean cloths** and change them often, or use **disposable paper towels** for raw meat juices; dirty cloths and sponges spread bacteria around the kitchen.
- Wash equipment in **hot soapy water**, rinse, and leave to **air-dry** or dry with a clean tea towel.

### Keeping raw and cooked food separate

- Use **colour-coded chopping boards** and knives. A widely used scheme is **red** for raw meat, **blue** for raw fish, **yellow** for cooked meat, **green** for salad and fruit, **brown** for vegetables and **white** for bread and dairy.
- If only one board is available, prepare **ready-to-eat** food first and **raw meat last**, then wash the board and knife in hot soapy water.
- Use **separate utensils** — tongs for raw chicken are not used to serve cooked chicken.
- Keep raw meat **below** and **away from** ready-to-eat food, in the fridge and on the worktop.
- **Wash hands** between handling raw and ready-to-eat food.

### Knife skills and hygiene

- **Bridge hold**: make a bridge over the food with thumb and fingers on either side, and cut **under** the bridge — used to halve round foods such as onions, tomatoes and apples safely.
- **Claw grip**: tuck the fingertips under the knuckles and rest the knife blade against the knuckles as you slice or dice — the fingers stay out of the way.
- A **sharp** knife is safer than a blunt one, because it doesn't slip.
- Wash knives between tasks, and never use the same knife for raw meat and salad without washing it.

### Preparation skills with a hygiene focus

- **Washing and drying vegetables and fruit**: rinse under cold running water to remove **soil** (which can carry E. coli), grit and surface bacteria, especially if eaten raw; dry in a salad spinner or with paper towels.
- **Making a fruit coulis in a blender**: wash and dry the fruit; make sure the blender jug, **blades** and **seals** are clean, because food trapped there harbours bacteria; sieve with a clean sieve; cover and chill. A coulis is ready to eat, so nothing will kill bacteria later.
- **Shaping wet mixtures** (falafel, fishcakes, meatballs): wash hands before and after; shape on a clean surface; use disposable gloves or wet hands and change gloves between tasks; **chill** the shaped mixture so it firms up and stays below 5 °C; cook until the **core reaches 75 °C** — meatballs made with raw mince carry E. coli throughout, and fishcakes are often bound with raw egg.

### Correct cooking and temperature control

- **Cook to 75 °C** in the centre (core). Follow recipe and packet **cooking times** and **preheat** the oven.
- **Poultry, pork, burgers, sausages and minced meat** must be cooked all the way through — no pink meat, and juices run clear. Whole cuts of beef and lamb (steaks, joints) can be served pink inside if the outside is fully cooked, because bacteria are on the surface.
- **Defrosting**: thaw covered in the **fridge**, or use the microwave **defrost** setting and cook straight away. Don't defrost at room temperature, where the outside warms into the danger zone while the centre is frozen.
- **Reheating**: until **steaming hot** throughout, **75 °C** at the core; stir food heated in a microwave to remove cold spots; reheat **only once**.
- **Hot holding**: keep hot food **above 63 °C** until served.
- **Cooling**: cool cooked food quickly (within about 90 minutes) and then refrigerate.
- **Cold holding**: keep cold ready-to-eat food **below 5 °C**. Food can be kept out of the fridge for serving for a maximum of **4 hours**; after that it should be thrown away.

### Care with high-risk foods

High-risk foods (cooked meats, dairy, egg dishes, seafood, cooked rice) are eaten without further cooking, so:
- keep them **chilled** until needed and return leftovers quickly;
- handle them as little as possible — use **tongs** or clean utensils;
- keep them **separate** from raw food and **covered**;
- respect **use-by** dates.

### Using a food temperature probe

1. **Clean** the probe with an antibacterial wipe **before** use, so it doesn't carry bacteria into the food.
2. Insert the tip into the **centre** or **thickest part** of the food, not touching bone, fat or the dish, which would give a false reading.
3. Wait for the reading to **settle**.
4. Check it reads **at least 75 °C** (for cooking and reheating), or **above 63 °C** for hot holding. Check more than one piece or place in large batches.
5. **Clean** the probe again **after** use, so bacteria are not transferred from one food to the next.
6. Check its **accuracy** regularly: in iced water it should read about 0 °C, and in boiling water about 100 °C.

### Testing whether food is ready

| Test | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature probe** | Core reaches 75 °C | Chicken, meatballs, reheated curry |
| **Knife or skewer** | Comes out clean from a cake; juices run clear from chicken; a knife slides easily into vegetables | Fruit cake, roast chicken, jacket potato |
| **Finger or "poke" test** | A sponge springs back when pressed lightly | Victoria sandwich |
| **Bite** | Pasta is tender but firm (al dente); vegetables cooked to the right texture | Spaghetti, green beans |
| **Visual colour check** | Golden-brown pastry; white, not pink, chicken; opaque fish that flakes | Pasties, stir-fried chicken, baked cod |
| **Sound** | A loaf sounds hollow when tapped underneath | Bread |

::: terms
- **Personal hygiene**: keeping the body and clothes clean and following rules that stop a food handler contaminating food.
- **Cleaning**: removing visible dirt, grease and food debris, usually with hot soapy water.
- **Disinfecting**: killing bacteria on a surface or piece of equipment with a sanitiser or very hot water.
- **Cross-contamination**: the transfer of bacteria from raw food, equipment or hands to food that is ready to eat.
- **Colour-coded boards**: chopping boards of different colours kept for different food types to prevent cross-contamination.
- **Bridge hold**: a knife grip in which the hand forms an arch over the food and the knife cuts underneath it.
- **Claw grip**: a knife grip in which the fingertips are tucked under the knuckles, which guide the blade.
- **Core temperature**: the temperature at the centre of the food, which must reach 75 °C when cooking or reheating.
- **Temperature probe**: a digital thermometer pushed into the centre of food to check its core temperature.
- **Hot holding**: keeping cooked food above 63 °C until it is served.
- **Reheating**: heating cooked food again until it is steaming hot and 75 °C at the core, and doing this only once.
:::

::: method
1. **State the rule precisely**: "use a red board for raw meat", "probe the thickest part", "reheat to 75 °C once only".
2. **Give the reason for every rule**, naming the risk: which bacteria, from where, onto what.
3. **Use the correct temperatures and times**: 75 °C core, above 63 °C hot, below 5 °C cold, 4 hours out of the fridge, 48 hours after illness.
4. **Follow the order of the task** in longer answers: before cooking (hygiene, surfaces), preparation (separation, washing), cooking (time, temperature, probe), serving (hot or cold holding, handling).
5. **Link to the named food**: high-risk ingredients need extra care; raw mince and poultry need cooking through.
6. **For 12-mark questions**, analyse the risks at each stage of the named dish, then evaluate how each rule prevents food poisoning and which rules matter most.
:::

::: example Explain — hygiene rules with reasons
Explain two personal hygiene rules to follow when making sandwiches for a school trip. (4)

Wash hands thoroughly with soap and hot water before starting and after handling any raw food (1), because bacteria on hands would be transferred to the sandwich fillings, which are not cooked (1). Cover any cuts with a blue waterproof plaster (1), because cuts carry Staphylococcus aureus, and the blue colour makes a lost plaster easy to see (1).
:::

::: example Explain — cross-contamination with burgers
Explain how to prevent cross-contamination when making beefburgers served in buns with lettuce and tomato. (4)

Prepare the salad first on a green board and keep it covered in the fridge (1), so raw meat juices cannot reach food that won't be cooked (1). Shape the raw burgers on a separate red board and wash hands thoroughly afterwards, before touching the buns or salad (1). Use separate utensils to handle raw and cooked burgers, and wash the boards and knives in hot soapy water, then disinfect the work surface (1).
:::

::: example Explain — using a probe
Explain how to use a temperature probe correctly to check that meatballs are cooked. (4)

Clean the probe with an antibacterial wipe before use (1). Push the tip into the centre of the largest meatball, not touching the pan (1). Wait for the reading to settle and check that it is at least 75 °C (1). Clean the probe again afterwards so bacteria are not transferred to other food (1).
:::

::: example Explain — reheating
Explain the rules for reheating leftover chilli safely. (3)

Reheat it until it is steaming hot throughout, with a core temperature of 75 °C, to kill any bacteria (1). Stir it during reheating, especially in a microwave, so there are no cold spots (1). Reheat it only once, because each cooling and reheating gives bacteria more time in the danger zone (1).
:::

::: example Explain — cleaning vs disinfecting
Explain the difference between cleaning and disinfecting a work surface. (2)

Cleaning removes visible dirt, grease and food debris, using hot soapy water (1). Disinfecting kills the bacteria left on the surface, using an antibacterial spray or very hot water (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Rule + reason** every time. A rule on its own usually earns only one mark.
- Name the **bacterium** where you can: Staph aureus from hands and cuts; campylobacter and salmonella from raw poultry; E. coli from mince and soil.
- **Cleaning** removes dirt; **disinfecting** kills bacteria. Don't treat them as the same.
- Probe answers need **clean before and after**, **thickest part**, **75 °C**.
- Reheat to **75 °C, once**; hold hot **above 63 °C**; cold food out of the fridge for **no more than 4 hours**.
- Don't claim washing raw chicken is hygienic — it splashes bacteria.
:::

---

---
Each colour is kept for one type of food, e.g. red for raw meat and green for salad (1). This stops bacteria from raw meat being transferred to ready-to-eat food via the board (1), which would not be cooked again, so the bacteria would not be killed (1).
:::

::: test

---
Washing removes soil, which can carry bacteria such as E. coli, and dirt from the surface (1); drying stops water diluting dressings and removes moisture in which microorganisms grow (1).
