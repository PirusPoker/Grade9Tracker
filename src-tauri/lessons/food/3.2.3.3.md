## What it is

**Nutritional analysis** means working out the energy and nutrients in a recipe, meal or day's diet, and comparing the results with the guidelines for the person who will eat it. It lets a cook show, with numbers, whether a dish is suitable and whether a modification has worked.

**Sources of nutritional data**

- **Food composition tables**: printed or online lists of the average energy and nutrients in 100 g of each food (the UK's main dataset is published by the government).
- **Nutritional analysis software** (and apps): you enter each ingredient and its weight; the program adds up the energy and nutrients for the whole recipe, then gives them **per portion** and **per 100 g**, often as a chart or star profile, and compares them with **dietary reference values** or reference intakes for a chosen age and sex.
- **Food labels**: the nutrition declaration per 100 g, and often per portion, with **% reference intake (RI)**.

**Limits of the data.** The figures are **averages**: real foods vary with variety, season, breed, brand and ripeness. Software does not usually allow for **cooking losses** (vitamin C and B vitamins destroyed or leached), for **fat that drains away** or is **absorbed** in frying, or for **water lost** in baking. Results are only as accurate as the weighing, and an ingredient missing from the database may be replaced by a similar one. The numbers are a good guide, not exact.

**Reading a nutrition table**

A table usually gives values **per 100 g** (so foods can be compared fairly) and **per portion** (what one person actually eats). To convert:

**per portion = value per 100 g × portion weight (g) ÷ 100**

*Example.* A chilled lasagne has 6.8 g fat per 100 g, and a portion is 450 g. Fat per portion = 6.8 × 450 ÷ 100 = **30.6 g**.

**Comparing with reference intakes and DRVs**

**% of reference intake = amount in the portion ÷ reference intake × 100.** The adult reference intakes used on labels are:

| Energy | Fat | Saturates | Carbohydrate | Sugars (total) | Protein | Salt |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2,000 kcal | 70 g | 20 g | 260 g | 90 g | 50 g | 6 g |

For the lasagne: 30.6 ÷ 70 × 100 = **43.7%** of an adult's daily fat in one portion. For a child or older adult, compare with the **dietary reference values** for their age and sex instead; a child's needs for energy and salt are lower.

**Scaling a recipe.** To make a different number of portions, find the **scale factor** = portions wanted ÷ portions in the recipe, then multiply every ingredient by it. A recipe for 4 needs × 2.5 to serve 10 (200 g of flour becomes 500 g). Nutrients scale in the same way, but the amount **per portion stays the same** if the portion size is unchanged. Cooking times do not scale in a simple way: a bigger dish may need longer at a slightly lower temperature, and seasoning should be tasted rather than multiplied blindly.

**Energy from the analysis** can be checked with 4 kcal per g for protein and carbohydrate and 9 kcal per g for fat (3.2.3.2); the percentage of energy from fat, for example, is energy from fat ÷ total energy × 100.

**Planning and modifying recipes to meet the guidelines**

The process: **analyse** the original, **compare** it with the guidelines for the target group, **identify** what is too high or too low, **modify** named ingredients or methods, then **re-analyse** to prove the change worked, and **taste** to make sure it is still acceptable.

| Aim | Modification | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Less fat and saturated fat** | lean mince (5% fat) instead of standard; dry fry and drain | fewer grams of fat and saturates per portion |
| | remove the skin from chicken; trim fat from meat | the skin and visible fat hold much of the fat |
| | reduced-fat cheese, or a **strong mature cheese so less is needed** | the same flavour from less saturated fat |
| | low-fat natural yoghurt or crème fraîche instead of cream | lower fat and energy; adds calcium |
| | grill, bake, steam or poach instead of frying | no added fat; fat can drain away |
| **More fibre** | wholemeal flour, pasta or rice instead of white | the bran of the grain is kept |
| | add pulses, extra vegetables, oats, seeds; keep skins on | more fibre, vitamins and minerals |
| **Less salt** | low-salt stock; herbs, spices, garlic and lemon for flavour | less sodium, lower blood pressure risk |
| | less bacon, cheese or soy sauce; fresh rather than processed meat | removes hidden salt |
| **Less sugar** | reduce sugar by a quarter to a third; sweeten with fruit, vanilla or cinnamon | fewer free sugars and less energy |
| **More fruit and vegetables** | add grated carrot or courgette to sauces, a side salad, fruit as dessert | towards the 5 a day; more vitamin C and fibre |
| **More iron or calcium** | add lean red meat, pulses or dark green vegetables; use milk, yoghurt, cheese or fortified alternatives | meets the needs of teenagers and other at-risk groups |

Each change has knock-on effects: wholemeal pastry is heavier and darker; less sugar in a cake gives a paler, flatter, drier result, as sugar helps aeration, browning and moisture; less salt in bread affects the yeast and the flavour; reduced-fat cheese melts less well. A good answer says what the change **improves** and, where relevant, what it might **affect**.

::: terms
- **Nutritional analysis**: working out the energy and nutrients in a recipe, meal or diet, usually with food tables or software.
- **Food composition tables**: lists of the average energy and nutrients in 100 g of each food.
- **Nutritional analysis software**: a program that calculates the nutrients in a recipe from its ingredients and weights and compares them with guidelines.
- **Per 100 g**: nutrient values given for 100 g of food, so that different foods can be compared fairly.
- **Per portion**: nutrient values for the amount one person eats, which shows the real intake.
- **Reference intake (RI)**: a guide to the daily amount of energy and key nutrients for an average adult, used on food labels.
- **Dietary reference values (DRVs)**: estimates of the energy and nutrients needed by different groups of people in the UK, by age and sex.
- **Scale factor**: the number every ingredient is multiplied by to change the number of portions a recipe makes.
- **Modification**: a change to an ingredient, amount or method to improve a recipe's nutritional value or suit a dietary need.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the table carefully:** is it per 100 g or per portion? What are the units (g, mg, kcal)?
2. **For "identify which is higher", quote the figures** from both recipes.
3. **For calculations, write the formula, then the numbers, then the answer with units:** per portion = per 100 g × portion ÷ 100; % RI = amount ÷ RI × 100.
4. **Compare with the right guideline for the person:** adult RI, or DRVs for a child, teenager or older adult; name the nutrient that is too high or too low.
5. **For modifications, name the ingredient changed, the replacement and the nutritional effect;** add a sensory or practical effect for the higher marks.
6. **In extended questions, use the data as evidence** for strengths and weaknesses, then evaluate modifications and state whether the dish would then suit the person.
:::

::: example Identify and calculate — two recipes
The table shows the nutritional analysis of one portion of two burgers.

| Per portion | Beef burger | Bean burger |
|---|---|---|
| Energy (kcal) | 410 | 290 |
| Fat (g) | 28 | 11 |
| Saturates (g) | 12 | 2 |
| Fibre (g) | 1 | 8 |
| Salt (g) | 1.8 | 1.2 |

(a) Identify which burger is higher in fibre. (1) (b) Calculate the percentage of an adult's reference intake for saturates (20 g) in the beef burger. (2)

(a) The bean burger, with 8 g against 1 g (1). (b) 12 ÷ 20 × 100 (1) = 60% (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — per portion
A beef and vegetable casserole contains 1.1 g of salt per 100 g. One portion is 250 g. Calculate the salt in one portion and the percentage of the adult maximum of 6 g. (3)

1.1 × 250 ÷ 100 = 2.75 g of salt (1 for the method, 1 for the answer). 2.75 ÷ 6 × 100 = 45.8% of the daily maximum (1).
:::

::: example Scale — a recipe
A scone recipe makes 8 scones and uses 225 g self-raising flour, 50 g butter, 25 g sugar and 150 ml milk. Calculate the amounts needed for 20 scones. (3)

Scale factor = 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5 (1). Flour 225 × 2.5 = 562.5 g; butter 50 × 2.5 = 125 g (1). Sugar 25 × 2.5 = 62.5 g; milk 150 × 2.5 = 375 ml (1).
:::

::: example Explain — three modifications
A creamy chicken curry uses chicken thighs with the skin on, fried in 4 tablespoons of ghee, with double cream stirred in, served with white rice. Explain three modifications to make it healthier. (6)

Use skinless chicken breast instead of thighs with skin (1), as the skin holds a lot of the fat, so saturated fat per portion falls (1). Soften the onions and spices in 1 tablespoon of rapeseed oil instead of 4 tablespoons of ghee (1), cutting total fat and replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat (1). Replace the double cream with low-fat natural yoghurt stirred in off the heat, and serve with brown rice (1), which lowers fat and energy, adds calcium, and increases the fibre (1).
:::

::: example Explain — limits of software
Explain two reasons why the results from nutritional analysis software may not exactly match the cooked dish. (4)

The software uses average values for each ingredient (1), but real foods vary with brand, variety and ripeness, so the actual nutrients differ (1). The software does not usually allow for changes during cooking (1), such as vitamin C destroyed by heat or leached into water, or fat draining away during grilling (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways of finding the nutritional content of a recipe. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): nutritional analysis software or an app; food composition tables; food labels on the ingredients; online databases.
:::

::: q
Explain why nutritional values are often given per 100 g as well as per portion. (2)
---
Per 100 g allows different foods to be compared fairly, whatever their pack or portion size (1); per portion shows how much the person actually eats (1).
:::

::: q
A yoghurt contains 12.5 g sugar per 100 g. A pot is 125 g. Calculate the sugar in one pot. (2)
---
12.5 × 125 ÷ 100 (1) = 15.6 g (accept 15.625 g) (1).
:::

::: q
Give two modifications to reduce the saturated fat in a cheese sauce, with a reason for each. (4)
---
Any two, each with a reason (2 + 2): use semi-skimmed or skimmed milk (1), which has less fat than whole milk but the same calcium (1); use less of a strong mature cheese (1), so the flavour is kept with less saturated fat (1); use an unsaturated spread or oil in the roux instead of butter (1), replacing saturated with unsaturated fat (1); use an all-in-one or blended method with less fat (1), cutting total fat (1).
:::

::: q
A recipe for 6 portions uses 450 g lean mince. Calculate the amount needed for 4 portions. (2)
---
450 ÷ 6 × 4 (or × 0.667) (1) = 300 g (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one possible disadvantage of reducing the sugar in a sponge cake by half. (2)
---
Sugar helps trap air when creamed and holds moisture (1), so the cake may be smaller, denser, drier and paler, with less browning and a less sweet taste (1).
:::

::: watch
- Modifications must be **specific**: "use healthier ingredients" scores nothing; "use wholemeal flour instead of white to increase fibre" scores two.
- Check whether the data is **per 100 g or per portion** before calculating.
- For **% RI**, divide the amount by the reference intake, not the other way round.
- Scaling: multiply **every** ingredient by the same factor, and label units.
- In extended answers, **use the numbers from the table as evidence** for each point.
:::

---

::: test
Explain three limitations of using nutritional analysis software to analyse a stir fry. (3)
---
Any three (1 each): values are averages and brands or varieties differ; it does not allow for vitamin losses during cooking; it may not allow for oil left in the wok or absorbed; weighing errors make the results inaccurate; some ingredients, such as a particular sauce, may not be in the database, so a substitute is used; it assumes equal portions.
:::

::: test
The nutritional analysis below is for one portion of a creamy chicken and bacon pasta bake, planned as a school meal for 14- to 16-year-olds. Adult reference intakes are shown for comparison; a teenager's target for fibre is about 25 g a day.

| Per portion | Pasta bake | Reference intake (adult) |
|---|---|---|
| Energy (kcal) | 770 | 2,000 |
| Protein (g) | 38 | 50 |
| Fat (g) | 42 | 70 |
| Saturates (g) | 22 | 20 |
| Carbohydrate (g) | 60 | 260 |
| Fibre (g) | 3 | — |
| Salt (g) | 3.1 | 6 |
| Calcium (mg) | 350 | — |
| Iron (mg) | 2.5 | — |

- Using the nutritional analysis, analyse the strengths and weaknesses of the pasta bake for a teenager.
- Evaluate modifications that would make it more suitable.

(8)
---
Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis that uses the figures accurately as evidence, including calculations such as the percentage of energy from fat, and links them to teenagers' needs; the evaluation proposes specific modifications, explains their effect on the figures and on sensory qualities, and reaches a supported judgement.
- **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear analysis using several figures, with sound modifications and some evaluation.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** some use of the data; modifications general.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** simple statements with little use of the table.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Strengths: 38 g protein supports the growth spurt and muscle repair; 350 mg calcium helps towards peak bone mass; it gives plenty of energy for active teenagers.
- Weaknesses: saturates 22 g is 110% of an adult's daily reference intake in one portion, from cream, cheese, bacon and possibly butter, raising LDL cholesterol.
- Fat: 42 × 9 = 378 kcal, 378 ÷ 770 × 100 = 49% of energy, well above the 35% guideline.
- Salt 3.1 g is over half the 6 g daily maximum, mostly from bacon and cheese.
- Fibre 3 g is low against a target of about 25 g; white pasta and few vegetables.
- Iron 2.5 mg is modest; teenage girls need much more, because of menstruation.
- 770 kcal is a large share of a day's energy for one course, risking positive energy balance for less active pupils.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Replace cream with a white sauce made with semi-skimmed milk (keeps calcium, cuts saturated fat).
- Use less of a strong reduced-fat cheese; replace bacon with extra chicken or lean ham in smaller amounts (cuts salt and saturates).
- Wholewheat pasta and added vegetables such as broccoli, peas, peppers and spinach (fibre, vitamin C, some iron, towards 5 a day).
- Low-salt stock and herbs such as basil, oregano and garlic for flavour.
- Smaller portion served with salad and fruit to balance the meal.
- Sensory effects: less creamy and rich, and wholewheat pasta is firmer and darker; pupils may need to get used to it, so changes could be introduced gradually and taste tested.
- Judgement: the dish is a good source of protein and calcium, but in its current form is too high in saturated fat and salt and too low in fibre for regular school meals; with these modifications it would meet guidelines much better while still being popular, and the new version should be re-analysed to check.
