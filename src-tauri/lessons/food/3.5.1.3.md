## What it is

A food label tells the consumer what is in a product and how to store and use it safely; the rest of the packaging is there to sell. This topic has three parts: the information the **law requires**, the **optional** extras, and how **marketing** steers what people buy.

**Which law?** The spec was written before Brexit and refers to EU law. Today the rules are **UK law**: the old EU labelling rules were kept in UK law and are enforced in England through the Food Information Regulations. Local authority trading standards and environmental health officers enforce them, and the **Food Standards Agency (FSA)** leads on food safety and allergen labelling. In the exam, "UK law" or "the law" is the right wording.

### Mandatory information on pre-packed food

| Information | Why it is required |
|---|---|
| **Name of the food** | Says what the product really is, e.g. "smoked" or "reformed" where relevant, so the consumer is not misled |
| **List of ingredients**, in **descending order of weight** | Shows what is in it and which ingredients make up most of it |
| **Allergens emphasised** in the ingredients list (e.g. in bold) | Protects people with allergies and intolerances; there are 14 allergens that must be emphasised |
| **Quantity of certain ingredients (QUID)** as a percentage | Where an ingredient is in the name or pictured, e.g. "% chicken" in a chicken pie |
| **Net quantity** (weight or volume) | Lets consumers compare value |
| **Date mark**: **use by** or **best before** | Use by for safety on perishable foods; best before for quality |
| **Storage conditions** and conditions of use | e.g. "Keep refrigerated below 5 °C; once opened, eat within 2 days" |
| **Name and address** of the manufacturer, packer or seller | So consumers can contact the business with a complaint or query |
| **Country of origin or place of provenance** | Where leaving it out could mislead, and always for some foods, such as fresh beef, lamb, pork, poultry, fish and many fruits and vegetables |
| **Instructions for use** or cooking | Where the food would be hard to use correctly without them |
| **Nutrition declaration** | Energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt per 100 g or 100 ml |
| **Alcohol strength** | Drinks with more than 1.2% alcohol by volume |

There are also special warnings, for example "contains a source of phenylalanine" where the sweetener aspartame is used, or a warning that some artificial colours "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children".

**Non-mandatory information** is added by choice to inform or attract: **provenance** details (the farm or region, "British", "Red Tractor"), **serving suggestions** (a photo of the dish with garnish, usually marked "serving suggestion" because the extras aren't included), recipe ideas, **front-of-pack traffic lights**, health and nutrition claims, cooking tips, logos such as Fairtrade, organic, vegetarian or vegan, and recycling information.

### Interpreting nutrition labelling

- **Per 100 g** values let you compare two products fairly. **Per portion** values show what you would actually eat, but the manufacturer's "portion" may be smaller than a real one.
- **Reference intakes (RIs)** are guideline amounts for an average adult: energy 2000 kcal, fat 70 g, saturates 20 g, carbohydrate 260 g, sugars 90 g, protein 50 g, salt 6 g. The label shows the percentage of the RI in one portion.
- **Traffic-light labelling** (front of pack) colours fat, saturates, sugars and salt **green** (low), **amber** (medium) or **red** (high), with the energy value alongside. It is voluntary, but most large UK retailers use it. The exam gives you the thresholds, so the skill is applying them.
- **Nutrition and health claims** must meet legal conditions. For example, "low fat" means only a small amount of fat per 100 g, and "reduced" means a set percentage less than a standard product. Claims can distract: a "low fat" yoghurt may be high in sugar.
- **Ingredients list order** is a quick clue: if sugar is first or second, the product is sugary. Sugar can also hide under other names, such as glucose syrup, dextrose, honey, fruit juice concentrate and invert sugar.

**Calculating from a label.** Granola: sugars 24 g per 100 g; portion 45 g. Sugars in one portion = 24 ÷ 100 × 45 = 10.8 g. As a percentage of the 90 g RI: 10.8 ÷ 90 × 100 = 12%.

### How marketing influences choice

| Technique | How it works on the shopper |
|---|---|
| **Buy one get one free (BOGOF)** and multi-buys | Feels like a bargain, so people buy more than they need; extra perishable food may be wasted |
| **Special offers** and price cuts, loyalty-card prices | Create urgency and brand switching; "was £3, now £2" makes the product seem good value |
| **Meal deals** | A fixed price for a main, snack and drink encourages people to add a crisps or sugary drink they would not otherwise buy |
| **Media influences** | TV cookery shows, social media influencers, celebrity chefs and viral trends create demand for particular foods |
| **Advertising** | TV, online, billboards and sponsorship build brand loyalty; jingles, cartoon characters and celebrity endorsement target children and teenagers |
| **Point of sale (POS)** marketing | Displays at eye level, at the end of aisles, and by the checkouts prompt impulse buys; free samples and in-store smells (bakery) tempt shoppers |
| **Packaging design** | Bright colours, characters, "new" flashes and health-style imagery attract attention |

**Context: restrictions on less healthy food.** In England, larger shops are now restricted from placing foods high in fat, sugar or salt (HFSS) in prominent places such as the checkouts, aisle ends and entrances, and the government has brought in rules limiting how such foods can be advertised and promoted. The aim is to reduce impulse buying of less healthy foods, especially by children. Treat this as context: the exam tests the techniques and how they work.

::: terms
- **Mandatory information**: the details that UK law requires on the label of pre-packed food.
- **Non-mandatory information**: optional details added by the producer, such as serving suggestions, provenance or recipe ideas.
- **Provenance**: where a food comes from and how it was produced.
- **Nutrition declaration**: the legally required table of energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt.
- **Reference intake (RI)**: a guideline daily amount of energy or a nutrient for an average adult, used to show the percentage in a portion.
- **Traffic-light labelling**: front-of-pack colour coding showing whether fat, saturates, sugars and salt are low, medium or high.
- **QUID**: the percentage of a named or pictured ingredient that must be shown on the label.
- **Point of sale**: marketing at the place where products are bought, such as displays at checkouts or the end of aisles.
- **Meal deal**: a fixed price for a combination of items, such as a sandwich, snack and drink.
- **Health claim**: a statement that a food or nutrient benefits health, which is only allowed if it meets legal conditions.
:::

::: method
1. **Mandatory lists**: give distinct items (name, ingredients, allergens, quantity, date mark, storage, business name and address, origin, instructions, nutrition). "Ingredients" and "allergens" count separately.
2. **Label calculations**: per 100 g value ÷ 100 × portion size; then ÷ RI × 100 for a percentage. Show each step.
3. **Traffic lights**: compare each value with the thresholds given, one nutrient at a time, and watch "more than" versus "or less".
4. **Marketing**: name the technique, then explain **why it works** on the consumer and what they end up buying.
5. **Evaluate** labels and marketing: helpful when understood; limited by small portions, confusing claims, time pressure and price.
:::

::: example Give — mandatory information
Give four pieces of information that must appear by law on a pack of fresh chicken breasts. (4)

Any four, 1 each: the name of the food (1); a use-by date (1); storage instructions, such as keep refrigerated (1); the net weight (1). Also creditworthy: the name and address of the business; the country of origin; cooking instructions; the nutrition declaration.
:::

::: example Use a label — traffic lights
A granola contains, per 100 g: fat 16.0 g, saturates 5.5 g, sugars 24.0 g, salt 0.2 g. The thresholds per 100 g are: fat low 3.0 g or less, high more than 17.5 g; saturates low 1.5 g or less, high more than 5.0 g; sugars low 5.0 g or less, high more than 22.5 g; salt low 0.3 g or less, high more than 1.5 g. Values in between are medium. State the colour for each nutrient. (4)

Fat 16.0 g is above 3.0 and not above 17.5, so amber (1). Saturates 5.5 g is above 5.0, so red (1). Sugars 24.0 g is above 22.5, so red (1). Salt 0.2 g is 0.3 or less, so green (1).
:::

::: example Calculate — percentage of reference intake
A 45 g portion of the granola is eaten. Calculate the sugars in the portion and the percentage of the 90 g reference intake. (3)

24 ÷ 100 × 45 (1) = 10.8 g sugars (1); 10.8 ÷ 90 × 100 = 12% of the reference intake (1).
:::

::: example Explain — marketing and teenagers
Explain two ways food marketing influences teenagers. (4)

Social media influencers and online adverts show popular people eating a product (1), so teenagers want to copy them and fit in, and buy that snack or drink (1). Meal deals near schools give a main, snack and drink for a fixed price (1), which seems good value on limited money, so teenagers add crisps and sugary drinks they might not otherwise buy (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — a supermarket's tactics
- Analyse how a supermarket's marketing encourages a family to spend more than they planned.
- Evaluate whether this marketing benefits the family. (8)

The store places bread, milk and fresh produce at the back, so the family walks past many displays. Multi-buys and BOGOF deals make bigger quantities look like a bargain, so they buy more than the list. End-of-aisle displays and eye-level shelving put branded or high-margin products where they are seen first, while cheaper own-brand goods sit on low shelves. Loyalty cards give personalised offers, which pull the family back and encourage extra items. Bakery smells and free samples tempt impulse buys, and brightly packaged products with cartoon characters target children, who then pester their parents.

Some marketing does benefit the family: genuine price cuts on staples such as pasta, or multi-buys on foods that keep, such as canned tomatoes, save money, and loyalty points can be exchanged for vouchers. But BOGOF on perishable foods often leads to waste, which cancels the saving, and many promotions are on sugary snacks and drinks, which is poor for health. The family benefits only if they shop with a list, compare prices per 100 g and buy offers only on foods they would use anyway; otherwise the marketing mainly benefits the supermarket.

(Level 4, 7–8: several techniques analysed with how they work; balanced evaluation and a judgement.)
:::

::: q
State two pieces of non-mandatory information that may be shown on a food label. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): serving suggestion; recipe ideas; provenance, such as the farm or region; traffic-light front-of-pack labelling; health or nutrition claims; logos such as Fairtrade, organic or vegan; recycling information.
:::

::: q
Explain why the ingredients list is in descending order of weight. (2)
---
It shows which ingredients the product contains most of (1), so consumers can judge its quality or healthiness, e.g. sugar listed first means a high sugar content (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list. (2)
---
People with allergies can react to tiny amounts, which may cause anaphylaxis (1); emphasising allergens in bold makes them quick and easy to spot, so the person can avoid the product (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a product with a "serving suggestion" photograph might mislead a consumer. (2)
---
The photo may show extra items, such as garnishes, salad or bread, that are not included in the pack (1), so the consumer may expect more food or a better-looking product than they get (1).
:::

::: q
A ready-to-eat soup contains 0.6 g salt per 100 g. A portion is 300 g. Calculate the percentage of the 6 g adult reference intake in one portion. (3)
---
0.6 ÷ 100 × 300 = 1.8 g salt per portion (2, 1 for method and 1 for answer); 1.8 ÷ 6 × 100 = 30% (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how point-of-sale displays influence food choice. (3)
---
Displays at checkouts, aisle ends and eye level catch the shopper's attention (1); they encourage unplanned impulse buys (1), often of snacks and confectionery, especially when children are present and pester their parents (1).
:::

::: watch
- Say **"UK law"**, not EU law. The spec's wording is out of date.
- **Name items the law requires**. Serving suggestions, recipe ideas and traffic lights are **optional**.
- Don't count one thing twice: "use-by date" and "best-before date" are one point (the date mark) in a list of mandatory items.
- In label maths, **use the portion size** and show both steps (the grams, then the percentage).
- With traffic lights, read the thresholds carefully: "more than 5.0 g" means 5.0 g itself is not red.
- For marketing, **explain the effect**: what the technique makes the shopper do, and why.
:::

::: test
Explain two advantages of traffic-light labelling for shoppers. (4)
---
Two advantages, each explained (2 + 2): the colours can be read at a glance (1), so busy shoppers can judge a product quickly without studying the table (1); it makes comparisons easy (1), so a shopper can choose the product with more greens and fewer reds (1); it needs no maths or nutrition knowledge (1), so it helps people with low literacy or numeracy (1).
:::

::: test
Complete the table to explain how each marketing technique influences food choice: meal deals; buy one get one free; celebrity endorsement. (6)
---
Meal deals: a fixed price for three items seems good value (1), so shoppers add a snack and drink they had not planned, often high in sugar or fat (1).
BOGOF: two items for the price of one feels like a bargain (1), so people buy more than they need, which may lead to overeating or waste (1).
Celebrity endorsement: people trust and admire the celebrity (1), so they buy the product to copy them; it is powerful with children and teenagers (1).

::: test
Evaluate whether nutrition labelling helps older people make healthy choices. (4)
---
For (up to 2): the nutrition table and traffic lights show fat, salt and sugar, which helps people managing conditions such as high blood pressure (1); per 100 g values allow comparison (1). Against (up to 2): print is often small and hard to read for people with poor eyesight (1); portion sizes and percentages can be confusing, and habit or price may matter more (1). Full marks need a supported conclusion, e.g. front-of-pack colours help most because they are quick and easy to read.
:::

::: test
Suggest how a supermarket could use point-of-sale marketing to encourage healthier choices. (3)
---
Any three (1 each): place fruit, vegetables or water at the checkouts instead of sweets; put healthier versions at eye level; offer price promotions on fruit, vegetables and wholegrain foods rather than on snacks; give free tastings of healthy products; use shelf labels to highlight lower-salt or lower-sugar options.
:::

::: test
The Brennan family (two working parents and three children aged 6, 10 and 14) do their weekly shop in a large supermarket.
- Analyse how food labelling and marketing may influence the family's choices.
- Evaluate whether the family would be better off shopping online with a planned list. (8)
---
Levels of response:
- **7–8:** a detailed analysis of labelling and marketing, applied to the family members; a balanced evaluation of online shopping with a supported judgement.
- **5–6:** a clear analysis of several influences; a reasonable evaluation.
- **3–4:** some relevant points with limited development; the evaluation is brief.
- **1–2:** simple points.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Labelling: traffic lights and nutrition tables help the parents choose lower-sugar cereals and lower-salt foods; allergen information; "one of your 5 a day" and health claims; provenance and ethical logos; date marks guide what to buy for the week.
- Marketing: children pester for products with characters or in eye-level displays; multi-buys on snacks and fizzy drinks; point-of-sale confectionery; the teenager is influenced by social media; loyalty offers; smells and samples; time pressure makes the parents grab familiar brands.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Online: a saved list reduces impulse buying and pester power; prices per kg are easy to compare; nutrition information is on screen; saves time for working parents.
- But: online shops also show promotions and "you may also like" suggestions; delivery charges; substitutions; less control over choosing fresh produce and dates.
- In store, the family can check freshness and use reduced items, but faces more point-of-sale temptation.
- Judgement: shopping online with a planned list is likely to reduce spending and unhealthy impulse buys, as long as they ignore online promotions; in-store shopping with a list works if children are not taken along.
