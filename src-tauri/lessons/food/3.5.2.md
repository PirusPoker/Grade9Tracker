## What it is

AQA defines a **cuisine** as, in effect, the style of cooking that belongs to a particular country or region. It has grown up there over time and is recognisable by its **typical ingredients**, its **ways of preparing and cooking** food and the **equipment** used, and the way food is **presented and served**. You study food from **British tradition** plus **two other cuisines** chosen by your school. This lesson uses **Italian** and **Indian**, with notes on Chinese for comparison. If your school chose different cuisines (Mexican, Thai, Japanese, Caribbean…), learn the same five headings for them.

For each cuisine the spec wants five things:
1. **Distinctive features and characteristics**: staple foods, flavourings and signature dishes.
2. **Equipment and cooking methods**.
3. **Eating patterns**: when, how and with whom food is eaten.
4. **Presentation styles**.
5. **Traditional and modern variations** of recipes.

### British cuisine

| Heading | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Features** | Based on home-grown produce: wheat, potatoes, root vegetables, apples, beef, lamb, pork, dairy and fish from surrounding seas. Mild flavours seasoned with herbs (sage, thyme, mint, parsley), mustard, horseradish. Regional dishes: Cornish pasty, Lancashire hotpot, Yorkshire pudding, Welsh rarebit and bara brith, Scottish porridge, cullen skink and haggis, Irish stew, Ulster fry. Baking: scones, Victoria sponge, shortbread, crumbles, pies and pastries. |
| **Equipment and methods** | The oven for **roasting** meat and potatoes and **baking** pies, bread and cakes; slow **stewing and braising** of cheaper cuts in casseroles; **frying** (fish and chips, full breakfast); steaming puddings; pie dishes, roasting tins, casserole dishes, pudding basins. |
| **Eating patterns** | Three meals a day: breakfast (cereal, toast, or a cooked breakfast at weekends), a light lunch (sandwiches), and an evening main meal ("dinner" or "tea" depending on region). The **Sunday roast** as a family meal. Afternoon tea. Christmas turkey with all the trimmings. |
| **Presentation** | Plated "meat and two veg" with gravy poured over; pies served whole at the table; afternoon tea on tiered stands. |
| **Traditional and modern** | Traditional shepherd's pie with lamb mince and mashed potato → modern: lentil or Quorn mince, sweet-potato and cauliflower mash topping, extra vegetables. Fish and chips: beer batter and deep frying → oven-baked, breadcrumbed fish with wedges. Modern British "gastropub" food: local, seasonal ingredients and plated presentation. |

### Italian cuisine

| Heading | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Features** | Simple dishes built on a few high-quality ingredients. **Pasta** (dried pasta made from durum wheat semolina; fresh egg pasta), **risotto** rice (arborio), **polenta**, **bread** (focaccia, ciabatta). Olive oil, tomatoes, garlic, basil, oregano, Parmesan, mozzarella, cured meats (prosciutto, salami). Strong **regional** differences: butter, rice and polenta in the north; olive oil, tomatoes, dried pasta and seafood in the south. Dishes: lasagne, ragù alla bolognese, carbonara, pizza Margherita, minestrone, tiramisu, panna cotta. |
| **Equipment and methods** | **Pasta machine** for rolling fresh pasta, **wood-fired pizza oven**, large pan for boiling pasta, risotto pan, mezzaluna, pestle and mortar for pesto. Methods: **boiling** pasta al dente, slow **simmering** of sauces (ragù), **stirring** risotto as stock is added gradually, **baking** (lasagne, pizza), chargrilling vegetables. |
| **Eating patterns** | Traditionally a long **family meal**, with courses: antipasto (starter: cured meats, olives, bruschetta), primo (pasta, risotto or soup), secondo (meat or fish) with contorno (vegetable side), then dolce (dessert) and espresso. Lunch has traditionally been the main meal; a light breakfast. |
| **Presentation** | Rustic dishes brought to the table to share, pasta in wide bowls, a grating of Parmesan and torn basil leaves; bright colours of tomato, basil and mozzarella. |
| **Traditional and modern** | Traditional ragù simmered for hours with beef and pork → modern: quick versions with lean mince or lentils, whole-wheat pasta, hidden vegetables. Pizza: thin, wood-fired, few toppings → modern: stuffed crusts, cauliflower or gluten-free bases, fusion toppings. |

### Indian cuisine

| Heading | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Features** | Wide regional variety across a huge country. Staples: **rice** (basmati) especially in the south and east, **wheat breads** (chapati, naan, paratha) especially in the north. **Pulses** (dhal from lentils, chickpeas in chana masala) and many vegetarian dishes, since many Hindus are vegetarian. **Spices**: cumin, coriander, turmeric, cardamom, cloves, chilli, garam masala; fresh ginger, garlic and green chilli; ghee; yoghurt; paneer. Dishes: biryani, dhal, tandoori chicken, dosa (south), samosas, pakoras, raita, chutneys, kulfi. |
| **Equipment and methods** | **Tandoor** (clay oven for naan and tandoori meat), **tawa** (flat griddle for chapatis), **karahi** (deep, wok-like pan), pressure cooker for pulses, spice grinder or pestle and mortar. Methods: **tempering** (tadka, frying whole spices in hot oil or ghee to release flavour), making a **paste** of onion, garlic and ginger, **simmering** curries, **marinating** in yoghurt and spices, **deep frying** (samosas, pakoras), **steaming** (idli). |
| **Eating patterns** | Meals are often shared; several dishes served together with rice and bread. A **thali** is a platter of small bowls: dhal, vegetable dishes, raita, pickle, rice, bread and a sweet. Many people traditionally eat with the right hand, using bread to scoop. Festival foods such as sweets (*mithai*) at Diwali. |
| **Presentation** | Colourful dishes in small bowls (katoris) on a thali, garnished with fresh coriander, a swirl of yoghurt or cream; in the south, food served on a banana leaf. |
| **Traditional and modern** | Butter chicken with cream, butter and ghee → modern: yoghurt instead of cream, less oil, grilled chicken, brown rice. British adaptations: chicken tikka masala and the "balti" are widely thought to have been created in Britain for British tastes. |

**For comparison: Chinese cuisine** is built on rice (south) or wheat noodles and dumplings (north), soy sauce, ginger, garlic, spring onion, five-spice and sesame oil. It is cooked in a **wok** (stir frying, deep frying, steaming in bamboo baskets) and uses a **cleaver**. Dishes are shared in the centre of the table and eaten with chopsticks.

### Why cuisines change

- **Migration** brings cuisines to Britain, and they adapt to local ingredients and tastes.
- **Fusion food** combines cuisines, e.g. a naan pizza or a Korean-style burger.
- **Health**: recipes are updated to meet the Eatwell Guide, with less fat, salt and sugar and more fibre.
- **Convenience**: jar sauces, ready meals and meal kits replace long traditional methods.
- **New ingredients and diets**: plant-based and gluten-free versions of classic dishes.

::: terms
- **Cuisine**: a style of cooking typical of a country or region, developed over time through its own ingredients, methods, equipment and ways of serving food.
- **Staple food**: a food that forms the main part of a diet and provides most of the energy, such as rice, wheat or potatoes.
- **Al dente**: an Italian term for pasta cooked until tender but still slightly firm to the bite.
- **Tandoor**: a cylindrical clay oven heated to a very high temperature, used in Indian cooking for naan and tandoori meats.
- **Tawa**: a flat or slightly curved griddle pan used to cook chapatis and other flatbreads.
- **Thali**: an Indian meal served as a selection of small dishes arranged on a round platter.
- **Tempering (tadka)**: frying whole spices briefly in hot oil or ghee to release their flavour into a dish.
- **Wok**: a large round-bottomed pan used for stir frying, steaming and deep frying, especially in Chinese cooking.
- **Fusion food**: dishes that combine ingredients or techniques from two or more cuisines.
- **Regional dish**: a traditional dish linked to a particular area, often based on local ingredients.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the cuisine and give named specifics**: dishes, staple, flavourings, equipment, methods. Named detail earns the marks; general statements do not.
2. **Link feature to reason**: "much Indian cooking is vegetarian, based on pulses, because many Hindus avoid meat".
3. **For eating patterns**, say when, how and with whom (courses, shared dishes, the thali, the Sunday roast).
4. **For modernising a recipe**, name each change, what it replaces and why (health, time, cost, diet), and check it still looks and tastes like the dish.
5. **In extended answers**, analyse the cuisine's features, then evaluate adaptations: what is gained and what is lost in authenticity and flavour.
:::

::: example Describe — distinctive features
Describe the distinctive features of Italian cuisine. (4)

Italian cooking is based on a small number of good-quality fresh ingredients, such as olive oil, ripe tomatoes, garlic and basil (1). Staples include pasta made from durum wheat, risotto rice, polenta and breads such as focaccia (1). Cheeses such as Parmesan and mozzarella and cured meats such as prosciutto are typical (1). There are regional differences: northern Italy uses more butter, rice and polenta, while the south uses more olive oil, tomatoes, dried pasta and seafood (1).
:::

::: example Explain — equipment
Explain how two pieces of equipment are used in Indian cooking. (4)

A tawa is a flat iron griddle heated on the hob (1), used to dry-cook chapatis quickly so they puff and char lightly in spots (1). A tandoor is a clay oven that reaches very high temperatures (1): naan dough is slapped onto the hot inside wall and meat on skewers is lowered in, cooking fast with a smoky, charred flavour (1).
:::

::: example Explain — modernising a British recipe
Explain how a traditional steak and kidney pudding with suet pastry could be modernised to suit current healthy eating guidelines. (4)

Use lean braising steak and trim the fat, and replace some of the meat with mushrooms and lentils, reducing saturated fat and adding fibre (1). Replace the suet pastry with a topping of thinly rolled shortcrust made with unsaturated spread, or a sliced potato topping, reducing saturated fat (1). Add more vegetables such as carrots and onions to the filling, adding vitamins and fibre towards 5 a day (1). Use reduced-salt stock and herbs such as thyme for flavour instead of extra salt (1).
:::

::: example Describe — eating patterns
Describe the eating pattern of a traditional Italian family meal. (3)

The meal is eaten together over a long time, often at lunch (1). It is served in courses: antipasto such as cured meats and olives, then a primo of pasta or risotto, then a secondo of meat or fish with a vegetable side (1). It ends with a dessert such as panna cotta, then an espresso (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — adapting a curry
- Analyse the features of Indian cuisine.
- Evaluate how a traditional lamb korma could be adapted to meet current healthy eating guidelines. (8)

Indian cuisine is regional: wheat breads dominate the north and rice the south. It relies on layers of spice (cumin, coriander, turmeric, cardamom, garam masala), built by tempering whole spices in hot fat and cooking a paste of onion, garlic and ginger. Pulses and vegetables are central, and many dishes are vegetarian. Meals are shared, with several dishes, rice and breads, often as a thali. Rich dishes such as korma use ghee, cream and ground nuts for a mild, creamy sauce.

The korma could use lean lamb leg with the fat trimmed, or half lamb and half chickpeas, cutting saturated fat and adding fibre. Measuring a small amount of oil instead of ghee reduces saturated fat, though some richness is lost. Swapping cream for low-fat natural yoghurt keeps the creaminess and tang, but yoghurt can split if boiled, so it is stirred in off the heat. Spices give flavour without salt, so salt can be cut. Serving with brown basmati rice and a vegetable side adds fibre. Ground almonds could stay in small amounts as they add unsaturated fat, but are an allergen to label. The adapted korma would be lower in saturated fat and higher in fibre while keeping its mild, spiced character, so the adaptation is worthwhile, even if it is a little less rich than the original.

(Level 4, 7–8: accurate, detailed features; each adaptation evaluated for health and sensory effect; judgement.)
:::

::: watch
- Use **named** dishes, ingredients, equipment and methods. "Italians eat pasta" is too thin to score well.
- Keep the **five headings** in mind: features, equipment and methods, eating patterns, presentation, traditional and modern.
- A modernisation must say **what changes and why** (health, time, cost, dietary need) and should still be recognisable as the dish.
- Don't stereotype. Say "many" dishes are spicy, and note regional differences within a cuisine.
- Paraphrase the definition of cuisine, **ingredients, methods, equipment, presentation**, rather than reciting "food from a country".

::: test
A café wants to add dishes from one international cuisine to its menu.
- Analyse the features of the cuisine you have chosen.
- Evaluate how a traditional dish from it could be adapted to meet current healthy eating guidelines. (8)
---
Levels of response:
- **7–8:** a detailed and accurate analysis of the cuisine's features (ingredients, methods, equipment, eating patterns, presentation) using named examples; a thorough evaluation of adaptations weighing health against sensory and authentic qualities, with a judgement.
- **5–6:** a clear analysis with some named examples; a reasonable evaluation of adaptations.
- **3–4:** some features, mostly general; adaptations are listed with little evaluation.
- **1–2:** simple points, limited knowledge.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis, e.g. Italian):
- Few fresh ingredients: olive oil, tomatoes, garlic, basil; pasta, risotto, polenta, bread; Parmesan, mozzarella, cured meats.
- Methods: boiling pasta al dente, slow-simmered sauces, risotto stirring, wood-fired pizza.
- Regional contrasts, courses (antipasto, primo, secondo, dolce), rustic sharing presentation.

Indicative content (evaluation, e.g. carbonara):
- Traditional carbonara uses guanciale or pancetta, egg yolks and lots of cheese: high in saturated fat and salt.
- Use lean bacon or smoked chicken, or mushrooms; drain fat.
- Use whole eggs plus a little milk instead of extra yolks or cream; a smaller amount of a strong Parmesan.
- Whole-wheat pasta for fibre; add peas or spinach for vegetables.
- Black pepper and herbs for flavour, less salt.
- Trade-offs: a less rich, less authentic flavour and texture; whole-wheat pasta has a nuttier taste; customers expecting tradition may be disappointed, so it could be sold as a lighter option alongside the original.
- Judgement: several small changes keep the dish recognisable while lowering saturated fat and salt, which suits a café that wants healthier options.
