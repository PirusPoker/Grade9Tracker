## What it is

Food poisoning happens when **pathogenic bacteria** (or the toxins they make) get into food and then into us. To prevent it you need to know **where bacteria come from** (the sources of contamination), **which bacteria** cause most food poisoning, what **controls** each one, and what the **symptoms** are. Questions often give a setting — a kitchen, a takeaway, a picnic — and ask you to spot the risks and control them.

### Contamination and cross-contamination

**Contamination** is the presence of anything harmful in food — here, bacteria. **Cross-contamination** is the **transfer** of bacteria from a contaminated source to a food that was clean, usually from **raw** food to **ready-to-eat** food. It happens in three main ways:
- **food to food**: blood from raw meat dripping onto cooked ham in the fridge;
- **equipment to food**: a board or knife used for raw chicken, then for salad;
- **hands to food**: touching raw meat, a bin or a phone, then making sandwiches.

### The sources of bacterial contamination

| Source | Why it carries bacteria | Examples of control |
|---|---|---|
| **Raw meat and poultry** | Bacteria from the animals' guts get onto the meat during slaughter | Store sealed at the bottom of the fridge; separate boards; cook to 75 °C; don't wash raw chicken (splashing spreads bacteria) |
| **Eggs** | Can carry salmonella on the shell or inside | Buy eggs from vaccinated flocks (British Lion mark); store in the fridge; wash hands after handling; cook thoroughly for vulnerable people |
| **Seafood and shellfish** | Shellfish filter large amounts of sea water, collecting bacteria and viruses | Buy from reputable suppliers; keep chilled; cook thoroughly |
| **Vegetables and salad** | Soil can carry bacteria from animal manure | Wash and dry thoroughly, especially if eaten raw; store away from ready-to-eat food |
| **Work surfaces and equipment** | Bacteria survive on boards, knives, cloths, sponges, tea towels, can openers and blender blades | Clean and disinfect; colour-coded boards; wash equipment in hot soapy water between uses; replace cloths often |
| **The people cooking** | Bacteria live on skin, in the nose and mouth, in cuts and boils, and in the gut | Hand washing; covering cuts with blue waterproof plasters; hair tied back; no coughing or sneezing over food; no cooking when ill |
| **Pests** | Flies, mice, rats and cockroaches carry bacteria from waste and droppings onto food and surfaces | Cover food; fly screens; seal gaps; store food off the floor in sealed containers; pest control |
| **Waste food and rubbish** | Bacteria multiply in waste, and bins attract pests | Lidded, pedal bins; empty them often; keep bins away from food; clean bins regularly |

Food handlers who have had **diarrhoea or vomiting** should not handle food until they have been free of symptoms for **48 hours**, because they can still carry the bacteria.

### The five main food poisoning bacteria

| Bacterium | Main sources | Main controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Campylobacter** | **Raw and undercooked poultry** (the biggest source), raw meat, **unpasteurised milk**, untreated water. It is the most common cause of bacterial food poisoning in the UK, and only a few bacteria are needed to make someone ill | Cook poultry thoroughly to 75 °C, with no pink meat and clear juices; **don't wash raw chicken**; separate raw from ready-to-eat; wash hands and equipment after handling raw poultry |
| **Salmonella** | **Raw poultry**, **eggs**, raw meat, unpasteurised milk; also pets and reptiles | Cook meat and eggs thoroughly; use British Lion eggs; store raw meat at the bottom of the fridge; wash hands after touching eggs, raw meat or pets |
| **E. coli** (harmful strains such as O157) | **Raw and undercooked beef**, especially **mince and burgers**; unpasteurised milk and juice; **soil** on vegetables; animal faeces (e.g. farm visits) | Cook burgers and mince all the way through to 75 °C, with no pink; wash vegetables; separate raw and ready-to-eat food; hand washing after contact with animals |
| **Listeria** | **Soft, mould-ripened cheese** (brie, camembert), **pâté**, **cooked chilled foods** (sliced meats, smoked fish, pre-packed sandwiches, coleslaw), unpasteurised milk. It can **grow slowly at fridge temperatures** | Keep the fridge **below 5 °C**; respect **use-by** dates; reheat chilled foods until steaming hot; **vulnerable groups** (especially pregnant women) avoid the riskiest foods |
| **Staphylococcus aureus** | **People**: skin, nose, throat, hair, cuts, boils and infected wounds. It gets into foods that are **handled a lot** and not cooked afterwards — sandwiches, cooked meats, cream cakes | **Personal hygiene**; cover cuts; don't touch the face, nose or hair; use tongs and gloves; keep handled foods chilled. It makes a **toxin** that is not easily destroyed by heat, so cooking or reheating is not a reliable fix |

**Matching the control to the source is the key skill.** Staphylococcus aureus comes from **people**, so the control is **personal hygiene** — cooking afterwards will not help because its toxin survives. Listeria **grows in the fridge**, so the control is **temperature below 5 °C** plus **use-by dates**. Campylobacter, salmonella and E. coli come mainly from **raw animal foods**, so the controls are **thorough cooking** and **preventing cross-contamination**.

### Symptoms of food poisoning

The general symptoms are:
- **nausea** (feeling sick) and **vomiting**;
- **diarrhoea** (with some E. coli strains it can contain blood);
- **abdominal pain** and cramps;
- **fever** and headache;
- in severe cases **dehydration**, and with some bacteria serious complications (for example, kidney damage from E. coli O157 in young children, or miscarriage from listeria).

Symptoms can start **anywhere from an hour or two** after eating (toxin-producing bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus) to **several days** later (campylobacter), and for listeria it can take **weeks**. This is why people often blame the wrong meal.

### Vulnerable groups

Some people are more likely to become seriously ill:
- **pregnant women** (listeria can harm the unborn baby);
- **babies and young children**;
- **older people**;
- people who are **ill** or have **weakened immune systems**.

Food for these groups needs extra care: avoid high-risk foods such as pâté, soft mould-ripened cheese and undercooked eggs unless advised otherwise, and cook and reheat thoroughly.

::: terms
- **Contamination**: the presence of something harmful in food, such as pathogenic bacteria.
- **Cross-contamination**: the transfer of bacteria from one source, such as raw meat, to another food, usually one that is ready to eat.
- **Pathogenic bacteria**: bacteria that cause illness such as food poisoning.
- **Campylobacter**: food poisoning bacteria found mainly in raw poultry; the most common bacterial cause of food poisoning in the UK.
- **Salmonella**: food poisoning bacteria found in raw poultry, eggs and raw meat.
- **E. coli**: bacteria found in animal guts; harmful strains come from undercooked beef, soil on vegetables and unpasteurised milk.
- **Listeria**: food poisoning bacteria found in soft cheese, pâté and chilled ready-to-eat foods, able to grow slowly in a fridge.
- **Staphylococcus aureus**: bacteria carried on human skin, nose and cuts, which produce a heat-resistant toxin in handled food.
- **Toxin**: a poison produced by some bacteria; some toxins are not destroyed by cooking.
- **Vulnerable groups**: people at greater risk of serious food poisoning — pregnant women, babies, young children, older people and the ill.
- **Pests**: animals such as flies, mice, rats and cockroaches that carry bacteria onto food and surfaces.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the sources** in the scenario: raw foods, surfaces and equipment, people, pests, waste.
2. **Name the bacterium** most linked to each food or source (poultry → campylobacter/salmonella; mince → E. coli; soft cheese/pâté → listeria; hands/nose → Staphylococcus aureus).
3. **Explain the route**: how the bacteria get from the source to a food that will be eaten without further cooking.
4. **Give the control that matches the source**: cooking for raw foods, hygiene for people, temperature and use-by for listeria, pest and waste control.
5. **Use exact temperatures** (75 °C core, fridge below 5 °C) and the 48-hour rule for ill staff.
6. **For 12-mark questions**, work through every stage of the setting (delivery, storage, preparation, cooking, serving), name the bacterium and control at each, then evaluate which controls matter most and why.
:::

::: example Name — bacteria and sources
Name the food poisoning bacterium most closely linked to each of the following: (a) undercooked beefburgers, (b) raw chicken, (c) a food handler with an infected cut. (3)

(a) E. coli (1). (b) Campylobacter (accept salmonella) (1). (c) Staphylococcus aureus (1).
:::

::: example Give — sources of contamination
Give four sources of bacterial contamination in a home kitchen. (4)

Raw meat or poultry (1). Unwashed chopping boards and knives (1). The cook's hands (1). Flies or other pests (1).
:::

::: example Explain — controlling salmonella
Explain how to control salmonella when making a homemade egg-fried rice. (4)

Use British Lion eggs, which come from hens vaccinated against salmonella (1). Wash hands after cracking the eggs, because the shells can carry the bacteria (1). Cook the egg thoroughly until it is completely set, as heat kills salmonella (1). Serve at once or cool the rice quickly and refrigerate, so any bacteria that survive cannot multiply (1).
:::

::: example Explain — cross-contamination
Explain how cross-contamination could happen when preparing a chicken salad wrap. (3)

Raw chicken carries campylobacter and salmonella (1). If the same board and knife are used to cut the raw chicken and then the lettuce and tomatoes without washing, bacteria transfer to the salad (1). The salad is not cooked, so the bacteria are not killed and can cause food poisoning when eaten (1).
:::

::: example Explain — Staphylococcus aureus
Explain why good personal hygiene is the most important control for Staphylococcus aureus. (3)

Staphylococcus aureus lives on people — on the skin, in the nose and in cuts and boils (1). It gets into food through handling, especially foods such as sandwiches and cream cakes that are not cooked afterwards (1). It produces a toxin that is not easily destroyed by heat, so preventing it getting into the food through hand washing, covering cuts and minimal handling is the only reliable control (1).
:::

---
Any three: nausea; vomiting; diarrhoea; abdominal pain/cramps; fever; headache.

::: q
Give two groups of people who are especially vulnerable to food poisoning. (2)
---
Any two: pregnant women; babies/young children; older people; people who are ill/have weakened immune systems.
:::

::: q
Explain why raw chicken should not be washed before cooking. (2)
---
Washing splashes bacteria such as campylobacter onto the sink, surfaces and nearby foods (1); cooking thoroughly kills the bacteria anyway, so washing only spreads them (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how flies can cause food poisoning. (3)
---
Flies land on waste, rubbish and faeces, picking up bacteria (1); they then land on food and surfaces (1), transferring bacteria through their feet and saliva/vomit, contaminating food that may be eaten without further cooking (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why burgers must be cooked all the way through, but a steak can be served rare. (3)
---
Bacteria such as E. coli are on the outside surface of meat (1); searing the surface of a whole steak kills them (1), but mincing spreads surface bacteria throughout the burger, so the centre must reach 75 °C (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Match the control to the source.** Staph aureus → hygiene (its toxin survives heat). Listeria → fridge below 5 °C and use-by dates.
- Campylobacter and salmonella are both linked to **raw poultry**; E. coli to **undercooked mince** and **soil**.
- Say **how** cross-contamination happens: from what, via what, onto what — and that the food is **not cooked again**.
- Give symptoms as **general** ones; don't invent precise onset times.
- Don't say "washing chicken removes bacteria" — FSA advice is **not** to wash raw chicken.
- Name **vulnerable groups** when a question mentions pregnancy, babies or the elderly.
:::
