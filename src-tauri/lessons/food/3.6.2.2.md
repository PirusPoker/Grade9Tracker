## What it is

Food technology is used for more than making food cheaper and longer-lasting. It can also be used to **improve health**. This topic covers three examples: **fortification** (adding nutrients), **modified foods with health benefits** (such as cholesterol-lowering spreads), and the **additives** and **GM** foods that divide opinion. The exam asks you to explain how each works and to **evaluate** it: does it work, who benefits, and what are the drawbacks?

### Fortification

**Fortification** means adding vitamins or minerals to a food to improve its nutritional value. Two related terms:
- **Restoration** puts back nutrients lost during processing, e.g. iron, thiamin and niacin in white flour.
- **Enrichment** is sometimes used for adding nutrients in greater amounts than the food naturally had.

Fortification is used to prevent deficiency across a whole population, because people don't have to change what they eat.

| Fortified food | Nutrients added | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **White (and brown) flour** | **Calcium, iron, thiamin (B1) and niacin (B3)**, required by UK law | Milling removes the bran and germ, which contain much of the iron and B vitamins. Calcium is added to support bone health across the population. Wholemeal flour is exempt because it keeps its natural nutrients |
| **Breakfast cereals** | **Folic acid and iron**, often with other B vitamins and vitamin D (added voluntarily by manufacturers) | **Folic acid** helps prevent **neural tube defects** such as spina bifida in babies when eaten before and in early pregnancy. **Iron** helps prevent **iron-deficiency anaemia**, a risk for teenage girls and women |
| **Fat spreads** (margarine and low-fat spreads) | **Vitamins A and D** | Originally added so margarine matched butter's vitamin content. Vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium for bones; vitamin A supports vision, skin and immunity. It helps people who get little sun or don't eat oily fish |

Other examples include plant milks fortified with calcium, vitamin B12 and vitamin D (important for vegans), and vitamin D added to some yoghurts.

**Folic acid in flour (context).** The UK government has announced plans to add folic acid to non-wholemeal wheat flour as well, to reduce neural tube defects. Whether this is already in force depends on when you read this. It isn't in the spec, so learn the spec's list above.

**Health benefits and limits of fortification.**
- *Benefits*: reaches almost everyone through everyday foods; helps groups at risk (women planning pregnancy, teenagers, older people, vegans); cheap and effective at population level.
- *Limits*: fortified cereals are often high in **sugar or salt**, so the healthier choice isn't clear-cut; people who don't eat the food miss out; there's a small risk of excess intake if people also take supplements; people may think a fortified product is "healthy" overall; and fortification can't replace a balanced diet.

### Modified foods with health benefits: cholesterol-lowering spreads

High levels of **LDL cholesterol** in the blood are a risk factor for **coronary heart disease**. **Cholesterol-lowering spreads** (and some yoghurt drinks and milks) contain added **plant sterols or stanols**. These substances come from plants and have a similar structure to cholesterol, so in the gut they **compete with cholesterol for absorption**. Less cholesterol is absorbed into the blood, and LDL levels fall.

**How well do they work (efficacy)?**
- They need to be eaten **every day** in the recommended amount (a few grams of sterols or stanols a day, usually spread over meals). The effect wears off if people stop.
- They lower LDL cholesterol **modestly**, typically by up to around a tenth after a few weeks, and a healthy diet and exercise are still needed.
- They are intended for **people who need to lower their cholesterol**. They aren't needed by people with normal cholesterol, and the labels warn that they may not be suitable for children, or pregnant or breastfeeding women. People on cholesterol-lowering medicine should use them under medical advice.
- They cost **more** than ordinary spreads.
- They can slightly reduce the absorption of **beta-carotene**, so eating fruit and vegetables is advised.

### Additives

**Additives** are substances added to food in small amounts to do a technical job: to keep it safe for longer, improve its appearance, flavour or texture, or help processing. Approved additives are given **E numbers**, which show they have been safety-assessed and approved for use. In the UK, new approvals now go through the **Food Standards Agency**. Additives must be listed in the ingredients by their category and name or E number, e.g. "preservative: sodium nitrite" or "colour (E160a)".

| Additive type | Job | Examples | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Colourings** | Make food look attractive, replace colour lost in processing, make batches consistent | Natural: beetroot red, caramel, curcumin (turmeric), beta-carotene. Synthetic: tartrazine, sunset yellow | Improve appeal; restore colour to canned or processed foods | Can make unhealthy foods more attractive to children; some synthetic colours are linked to **hyperactivity** in some children, so six of them (the "Southampton six") must carry a warning that they may affect activity and attention in children; can hide poor-quality ingredients |
| **Emulsifiers and stabilisers** | Emulsifiers stop oil and water separating; stabilisers keep mixtures thick and stable | Lecithin (from soya or egg yolk), mono- and diglycerides; xanthan gum, guar gum, pectin, carrageenan | Smooth, consistent texture in mayonnaise, sauces, ice cream and low-fat spreads; longer shelf life; allow lower-fat products | Mean highly processed foods can be made cheaply from low-cost ingredients; some come from allergenic sources (soya, egg) |
| **Flavourings** and flavour enhancers | Add or strengthen flavour | Natural flavourings, smoke flavouring, vanillin; flavour enhancer monosodium glutamate (MSG, E621) | Replace flavour lost in processing; make lower-sugar, lower-fat or lower-salt foods tasty; cheaper than real ingredients | Can disguise poor-quality or cheap ingredients; enhancers such as MSG add sodium; some people report sensitivity |
| **Preservatives** | Slow the growth of bacteria, yeasts and moulds, or slow oxidation (antioxidants) | Sulphur dioxide and sulphites (dried fruit, wine), sodium nitrite (bacon, ham), sorbic acid, vinegar, ascorbic acid (vitamin C as an antioxidant) | Keep food safe for longer and cut food poisoning (nitrite prevents dangerous bacteria in cured meats); reduce waste; wider choice all year | **Sulphites** can trigger asthma and are one of the 14 allergens; **nitrites** in processed meats are linked to compounds that may increase cancer risk; can destroy some vitamins (sulphites destroy thiamin) |

**Overall evaluation of additives.** They let us have safe, varied, convenient food with less waste, and all approved additives are tested and limited in quantity. But they are most common in **ultra-processed** foods, which tend to be high in fat, sugar and salt. Some people react to particular additives. And many consumers prefer "clean label" food with fewer additives. The fair conclusion is that additives are mostly safe and useful, but a diet built mainly on heavily processed food is not healthy, whatever the additives.

### Genetically modified (GM) foods

**Genetic modification** changes an organism's DNA, often by inserting a gene from another species, to give a desired trait.

**Positive aspects:**
- **Pest resistance**: crops make their own insecticide protein, so less spraying.
- **Herbicide tolerance**: weeds can be sprayed without harming the crop.
- **Drought, flood or salt tolerance**: crops keep growing in harsh conditions.
- **Better nutrition**: **Golden Rice** makes beta-carotene (which the body converts to vitamin A) to tackle vitamin A deficiency and blindness.
- **Longer shelf life**, higher yields and lower prices.
- Could improve **food security** for a growing population.

**Negative aspects:**
- Concern about **long-term health effects**.
- Modified genes may **cross-pollinate** wild plants or non-GM crops.
- **Loss of biodiversity**, and weeds or pests may become resistant.
- Seed is patented, so farmers may depend on large companies and pay more.
- **Ethical** objections to "playing God".
- Many UK consumers **distrust** GM, and GM ingredients must be labelled.
- Golden Rice has taken many years to reach farmers and remains controversial.

**Context:** few GM foods are sold directly in UK shops, although GM crops are widely grown in other countries and used in animal feed. England now treats **gene-edited** ("precision-bred") crops separately from GM, as they don't contain genes from another species. This is context only.

::: terms
- **Fortification**: adding vitamins or minerals to a food to improve its nutritional value.
- **Restoration**: putting back nutrients that were lost during processing, such as iron and B vitamins in white flour.
- **Plant sterols and stanols**: plant substances added to some spreads that reduce the absorption of cholesterol in the gut.
- **LDL cholesterol**: the type of cholesterol that can build up in artery walls and increase the risk of heart disease.
- **Efficacy**: how well a product or treatment actually achieves its intended effect.
- **Additive**: a substance added to food in small amounts to preserve it or improve its colour, flavour, texture or stability.
- **E number**: a code showing that a food additive has been safety-assessed and approved for use.
- **Emulsifier**: an additive that allows oil and water to mix and stay mixed, such as lecithin.
- **Preservative**: an additive that slows the growth of microorganisms or the oxidation of food, extending its shelf life.
- **Neural tube defect**: a birth defect of the brain or spine, such as spina bifida, whose risk is reduced by folic acid.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the development and the nutrient or additive exactly** (e.g. "calcium, iron, thiamin and niacin in white flour").
2. **Explain how it works in the body or food**: the mechanism (sterols block cholesterol absorption; folic acid in early pregnancy; emulsifier holds oil and water together).
3. **Say who benefits**: the target group.
4. **Evaluate efficacy and drawbacks**: how big the effect is, cost, the need for regular intake, sugar and salt in fortified foods, allergies and sensitivities.
5. **Conclude** with a balanced judgement. Answers that list only negatives can't reach the top level.
:::

::: example Give — flour fortification
Give two nutrients that must by law be added to white flour in the UK, and explain why. (3)

Any two from calcium, iron, thiamin and niacin (1 each, maximum 2). Milling removes the bran and germ, which contain much of the iron and B vitamins, so these are restored, and calcium is added to improve bone health across the population (1).
:::

::: example Explain — cholesterol-lowering spreads
Explain how cholesterol-lowering spreads work. (3)

The spreads contain added plant sterols or stanols (1). These have a similar structure to cholesterol, so in the gut they compete with it and block some of it from being absorbed into the blood (1). This lowers the level of LDL cholesterol in the blood, reducing a risk factor for coronary heart disease, as long as the spread is eaten every day (1).
:::

::: example Explain — preservatives
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using preservatives in food. (4)

Advantage: preservatives slow the growth of bacteria, yeasts and moulds (1), so food stays safe for longer, which reduces food poisoning and food waste (1). Disadvantage: some people react to certain preservatives; for example, sulphites can trigger asthma attacks (1), so these products must be labelled and avoided by sensitive people (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — fortified cereal
Evaluate the use of fortified breakfast cereals to improve the diets of teenage girls. (4)

For: cereals are widely eaten, so added iron helps prevent iron-deficiency anaemia, which teenage girls risk through menstruation and growth (1); added folic acid builds up levels before any future pregnancy, lowering the risk of neural tube defects (1). Against: many fortified cereals are high in free sugars and salt, so they may add to tooth decay and excess energy (1). Judgement: fortified cereals help if a low-sugar, wholegrain type is chosen and eaten with milk and fruit, but they can't replace a varied diet with iron-rich foods (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — additives in a sponge cake
A supermarket sells a packaged sponge cake that contains: colour (E160a), emulsifiers (E471, soya lecithin), preservative (potassium sorbate) and flavouring.
- Analyse why the manufacturer adds these additives.
- Evaluate whether consumers should be concerned about them. (8)

The **emulsifiers** help the fat and liquid in the batter stay evenly mixed, so the cake has a fine, even crumb, a light texture and a consistent result in large-scale production; they also slow staling. The **preservative** potassium sorbate slows the growth of moulds and yeasts, which spoil moist cakes quickly, so the cake can travel to shops and stay on the shelf for longer, reducing waste. The **colour** (beta-carotene) gives a warm golden colour that customers expect, making each batch look the same, and **flavouring** such as vanilla flavour gives a strong, consistent taste more cheaply than real vanilla.

Consumers need not be very concerned about safety: all these additives are approved, with E numbers, and used within legal limits; beta-carotene is a natural pigment, and none of them is one of the "Southampton six" colours. The preservative reduces the risk of mouldy cake being eaten and cuts waste. However, soya lecithin is a problem for people with soya allergy, which must be emphasised on the label. The bigger concern is that additives make it possible to mass-produce a cake that is high in sugar and fat and keeps for weeks, so it becomes an easy, cheap everyday snack. The additives themselves are low-risk; what should concern consumers is how often they eat processed cake, not the E numbers.

(Level 4, 7–8: each additive's function analysed; balanced evaluation including allergy and health context; judgement.)
:::

::: q
Give two nutrients commonly added to breakfast cereals. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): folic acid; iron; other B vitamins such as thiamin, riboflavin, niacin or B12; vitamin D.
:::

::: q
Explain why vitamins A and D are added to fat spreads. (2)
---
To give spreads a similar vitamin content to butter, which they replace (1); vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium for strong bones, and vitamin A supports vision and immunity, so fortification helps people who get little sunlight or eat little oily fish (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by an emulsifier and give one example. (2)
---
An additive that helps oil and water mix and stay mixed without separating (1); e.g. lecithin from soya or egg yolk, or mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why some consumers avoid products containing synthetic colourings. (3)
---
Some synthetic colours have been linked to hyperactivity in some children (1), so products containing them must carry a warning that they may affect activity and attention (1); some consumers also dislike artificial ingredients or feel colourings make poor-quality or unhealthy foods look more appealing (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between restoration and fortification. (2)
---
Restoration puts back nutrients that were lost during processing, such as iron and B vitamins in white flour (1); fortification is the general term for adding vitamins or minerals to a food to improve its nutritional value, whether or not they were there originally (1).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why a person with normal cholesterol levels does not need to buy cholesterol-lowering spreads. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): they are designed for people who need to lower cholesterol, so there is little benefit; they cost more than ordinary spreads; labels advise they may not be suitable for some groups; a healthy diet low in saturated fat already keeps cholesterol in check.
:::

::: watch
- **Learn the spec's fortification list exactly**: flour gets calcium, iron, thiamin and niacin; cereals get folic acid and iron; spreads get vitamins A and D.
- Cholesterol-lowering spreads: the effect is **modest**, needs **daily** intake, and is for people with **high cholesterol**.
- Evaluations of additives and GM need **both sides and a conclusion**; lists of negatives are capped.
- Say what the additive **does in the food** (emulsifier stops separation; preservative slows microbial growth) before judging it.
- Don't state folic acid in flour as a current spec fact; it is **context**.
- E numbers mean **approved**, not "harmful chemicals".
:::

::: test
Evaluate the use of cholesterol-lowering spreads. (6)
---
Up to 6 marks: for (up to 3), against (up to 3); an answer with no judgement is capped at 5.
- For: plant sterols or stanols block some cholesterol absorption in the gut (1); regular daily use lowers LDL cholesterol modestly within a few weeks (1); this reduces a risk factor for coronary heart disease, and spreads are an easy swap to make (1).
- Against: the reduction is modest, typically up to around a tenth, and only while eaten daily in the recommended amount (1); they cost more than ordinary spreads (1); they are not intended for people with normal cholesterol, children, or pregnant or breastfeeding women, and can slightly reduce beta-carotene absorption (1).

::: test
A manufacturer makes a chilled ham and mushroom pasta bake ready meal with the following additives on the label: colour (beta-carotene), stabiliser (xanthan gum), flavour enhancer (monosodium glutamate), preservative (sodium nitrite in the ham).
- Analyse the reasons why the manufacturer adds these additives.
- Evaluate whether consumers should be concerned about eating the ready meal. (8)
---
Levels of response:
- **7–8:** a detailed analysis of the function of each additive linked to the product and to large-scale production; a balanced evaluation of safety, health and nutritional concerns with a well-supported judgement.
- **5–6:** a clear analysis of most additives; a reasonable evaluation with some balance.
- **3–4:** some correct functions with limited explanation; the evaluation is brief or one-sided.
- **1–2:** simple points; functions may be confused.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Beta-carotene gives the cheese sauce an attractive golden colour, replaces colour lost in processing and keeps every batch consistent; it is a natural colour.
- Xanthan gum thickens and stabilises the sauce so it doesn't separate or become watery during chilling and reheating; it gives a smooth, glossy texture.
- MSG boosts savoury (umami) flavour, making the sauce taste rich more cheaply and making up for flavour lost in processing.
- Sodium nitrite in the cured ham prevents the growth of dangerous bacteria, keeps a pink colour and extends shelf life.
- Overall: the additives allow mass production, transport and a longer shelf life.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- All are approved and used within legal limits.
- Beta-carotene and xanthan gum are low concern.
- MSG adds sodium, which adds to the meal's salt; some people report sensitivity.
- Nitrites in processed meat are linked to compounds that may raise cancer risk, so processed meat should be limited.
- The preservative reduces food poisoning risk and waste.
- Wider concerns: ready meals are often high in salt and saturated fat and low in vegetables and fibre; reliance on them reduces cooking skills.
- Judgement: an occasional ready meal with these additives is not a serious concern, but consumers should check the traffic lights for salt and fat, and not eat processed meat or ready meals every day.
