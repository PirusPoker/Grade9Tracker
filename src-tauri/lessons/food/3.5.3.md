## What it is

**Sensory evaluation** is judging food with the senses (sight, taste, smell, touch and hearing) in a **structured, fair** way, so the results can guide decisions. Manufacturers use it to develop products, to check that a modified recipe (less sugar, salt or fat) is still acceptable, to compare their product with a competitor's, and for quality control. You use it when you taste and season while cooking and when you evaluate your own dishes.

### How we taste: the senses working together

**Taste.** The tongue is covered in small bumps (**papillae**), many of which contain **taste buds**. Each taste bud holds **taste receptor cells**. Chemicals from food dissolve in **saliva**, reach the receptors, and a nerve message goes to the brain. There are five **basic tastes**:

| Taste | Triggered by | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Sweet** | sugars | honey, ripe fruit |
| **Salty** | sodium (salt) | crisps, bacon |
| **Sour** | acids | lemon, vinegar, yoghurt |
| **Bitter** | many plant compounds, caffeine | dark chocolate, coffee, grapefruit, kale |
| **Umami** (savoury) | glutamates | Parmesan, soy sauce, mushrooms, tomatoes, meat stock |

All five can be detected across the tongue. The old "tongue map", with separate zones for each taste, is a myth.

**Smell (the olfactory system).** Aromas are **volatile** molecules that evaporate from food. They reach the **olfactory receptors** high in the nose, both through the nostrils (sniffing) and from the back of the mouth as we chew and swallow. The brain combines smell with taste to create **flavour**; most of what we call flavour is actually smell. With a heavy cold the nasal passages are blocked, the aromas cannot reach the receptors, and food tastes **bland**. The five basic tastes still work, so we can tell sweet from salty but not strawberry from raspberry.

**Sight.** Appearance sets expectations before we taste: colour signals ripeness and freshness (bright green beans, golden-brown pastry), and a strange colour can make people reject food or even taste it differently. **Touch (mouthfeel)** covers texture in the mouth and hands: crisp, crunchy, creamy, chewy, smooth, gritty. **Hearing**: the crunch of a crisp or the sizzle of a steak signals freshness and quality.

**Why this matters in food choice.** People choose food they expect to enjoy, and the senses judge that. They reject limp salad, burnt edges or an off smell (a spoilage warning), and they are drawn to the smell of baking bread or the look of a glossy glaze.

### Sensory vocabulary

Be precise. "Nice" and "tasty" earn nothing.
- **Appearance**: golden, glossy, even, crumbly, speckled, vibrant, pale, charred.
- **Aroma**: fragrant, spicy, yeasty, buttery, smoky, pungent.
- **Taste and flavour**: sweet, salty, tangy, sharp, bitter, savoury, mild, rich, bland, zesty.
- **Texture**: crisp, crunchy, flaky, light, airy, dense, moist, dry, chewy, creamy, smooth, lumpy, gritty.

### The tests

| Type | Test | How it works | Used to find out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preference** | **Paired preference** | Tasters get two coded samples and say which they prefer | Whether people like a new version more or less than the original |
| **Preference** | **Hedonic rating** | Tasters score how much they like a sample on a scale (e.g. 1 "dislike a lot" to 5 "like a lot", or a row of faces for children) | How acceptable a product is |
| **Discrimination** | **Triangle test** | Three coded samples: two are the same, one different. Tasters must identify the odd one out | Whether people can **tell the difference**, e.g. after replacing an ingredient with a cheaper or healthier one |
| **Grading** | **Ranking** | Tasters put several samples in order for one attribute (e.g. least to most salty) or for preference | The order of intensity or liking |
| **Grading** | **Rating** | Tasters score each sample for an attribute on a scale (e.g. crunchiness 1–5) | How strong an attribute is |
| **Grading** | **Profiling** | Tasters score several attributes (e.g. sweetness, crunch, colour, aroma) and the average scores are plotted on a **star (radar) diagram** | The full sensory profile; comparing a product with a target or a competitor |

In a triangle test, a taster guessing at random would pick the right sample one time in three. So many correct answers are needed before you can say there is a real difference.

### Setting up a taste panel

1. **Choose tasters.** Use enough people to be reliable, typically ten or more. For the target market, use those who will buy it (e.g. children for a lunchbox snack); **trained** tasters are used for profiling. Check for **allergies** and dietary needs.
2. **Prepare the samples.** The **same size**, at the **same temperature**, on **identical plain plates or containers**.
3. **Code** each sample with random numbers or letters, not names or "A, B, C", so tasters are not biased.
4. **Randomise the order** in which samples are served, so the first-tasted effect is spread evenly.
5. **Control the environment.** Tasters work **individually** in quiet booths or separated spaces, so they don't influence each other. Use the same lighting; coloured (e.g. red) lighting can hide colour differences when only taste is being tested.
6. **Clear the palate** between samples with **water** or a plain cracker.
7. **Give clear instructions and a results chart** with the same scale for everyone.
8. **Hygiene**: clean spoons for each sample, and never double-dip.
9. **Collect and analyse** the results: average the scores, draw a star diagram, and act on them by modifying the recipe and retesting.

### Taste and season while cooking (S1)

Good cooks judge and adjust sensory qualities as they go:
- **Taste with a clean spoon** each time and season gradually, since salt cannot be taken out.
- **Infusions** carry flavour into a liquid by heating it with aromatics and then straining, e.g. milk infused with onion, bay and peppercorns for a béchamel, or cream with vanilla.
- **Herbs and spices** add aroma and flavour and reduce the need for salt. Add woody herbs (rosemary, thyme) early; add delicate herbs (basil, coriander) at the end. Toasting whole spices releases their aroma.
- A **paste**, such as a curry paste, pesto or garlic and ginger paste, gives concentrated flavour.
- A **jus** is the thin, natural juice from roast meat, deglazed from the tin and seasoned.
- A **reduction** boils a sauce or stock to evaporate water, which concentrates the flavour and thickens it; season *after* reducing, or the salt concentrates too.

::: terms
- **Sensory evaluation**: judging food in a structured way using sight, taste, smell, touch and hearing.
- **Taste buds**: clusters of taste receptor cells, mainly on the tongue, that detect the five basic tastes.
- **Olfactory system**: the smell receptors in the nose and the nerves that send aroma signals to the brain.
- **Umami**: the savoury basic taste caused by glutamates, found in foods such as Parmesan, mushrooms and soy sauce.
- **Palatability**: how pleasant and acceptable a food is to eat.
- **Paired preference test**: a test where tasters say which of two samples they prefer.
- **Hedonic scale**: a rating scale that measures how much a taster likes a food, sometimes using faces.
- **Triangle test**: a discrimination test in which tasters must pick the odd sample out of three, two of which are identical.
- **Profiling test**: a test in which several attributes are rated and the results plotted on a star diagram.
- **Controlled conditions**: steps that keep a taste test fair, such as coded samples, equal portions and individual booths.
:::

::: method
1. **Match the test to the aim**: a difference means triangle; liking means paired preference or hedonic; order means ranking; one attribute means rating; a full picture means profiling.
2. **Describe how the test is done**: number of samples, coding, what the taster records.
3. **For fair-test questions**, give **distinct** controls, one mark each: coding, portion size, temperature, plates, order, booths, palate cleanser, lighting, instructions.
4. **Use sensory vocabulary** in every description, never "nice" or "good".
5. **For improvements**, name the change, the sense it affects and why (herbs for aroma, toasted topping for crunch, glaze for gloss).
6. **When evaluating results**, comment on reliability: number and type of tasters, controls, and whether results were repeated.
:::

::: example Name and describe — detecting a difference
A company has replaced butter with a vegetable spread in its shortbread. Name and describe a test to find out whether consumers can tell the difference. (3)

A triangle test (1). Each taster is given three coded samples, two of one shortbread and one of the other (1), and asked to identify the odd one out; if significantly more than a third of tasters pick correctly, people can tell the difference (1).
:::

::: example Explain — taste and smell
Explain why food tastes bland when you have a heavy cold. (3)

Much of flavour comes from aroma molecules reaching the olfactory receptors in the nose (1). A cold blocks the nasal passages with mucus, so the aromas cannot reach the receptors (1). Only the five basic tastes detected by the taste buds remain, so food seems flat and flavourless (1).
:::

::: example Give — fair conditions
Give four conditions needed to carry out a fair taste test on three brands of tomato soup. (4)

Any four, 1 each: label each sample with a random three-digit code (1); serve the same amount of each at the same temperature in identical cups (1); let tasters work alone in separate booths so they are not influenced by others (1); give water to drink between samples (1). Also: randomise the order; use the same lighting; give the same chart and clear instructions.
:::

::: example Explain — improving sensory qualities
Explain how to improve the sensory qualities of a plain vegetable soup. (6)

Sweat the onions slowly until soft and golden before adding liquid (1), which caramelises some sugars for a sweeter, deeper flavour (1). Add herbs such as thyme and a bay leaf, or roasted garlic (1), to give a fragrant aroma and flavour so less salt is needed (1). Blend part of the soup and leave some chunks, for a creamy but interesting texture (1). Finish with a swirl of yoghurt, chopped parsley and toasted seeds (1), adding colour contrast, a glossy appearance and a crunchy texture.
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — reduced-sugar product
A manufacturer wants to launch a reduced-sugar version of its chocolate chip cookie.
- Analyse how the manufacturer could use sensory testing to develop the product.
- Evaluate the reliability of the results. (8)

At the start, a **triangle test** would show whether consumers can detect the difference between the original and a cookie with 30% less sugar. If they cannot, the change can be made without losing sales. If they can, **profiling** by a trained panel, scoring sweetness, chocolate flavour, crunch, spread and colour on a star diagram, shows exactly which attributes changed. Sugar also affects texture and browning, so the cookie may be paler and softer. The developers can then try fixes, such as more vanilla or cinnamon to boost perceived sweetness, or a longer bake for colour, and retest. Finally, a **hedonic** or **paired preference** test with the target consumers checks that the new cookie is liked as much as the original.

The results are reliable only if the tests are well controlled: coded, randomly ordered, equal samples at the same temperature, booths, and water between samples. A large panel of the actual target market (e.g. families and children) gives more trustworthy preference data than a few staff, who may be biased or used to the product. Profiling needs trained tasters, or the scores are inconsistent. Taste tests are also a snapshot: people may react differently at home, over several weeks, or when they see "reduced sugar" on the pack. So sensory testing gives strong guidance when the panel is large, appropriate and controlled, but it should be repeated and backed by consumer trials before launch.

(Level 4, 7–8: tests correctly matched to purposes; developed evaluation of reliability; judgement.)
:::

---
Names or brands, or even letters like A and B, can make tasters expect one sample to be better (1), so random codes stop bias and make the results fairer (1).

We see food before we taste it, so appearance creates expectations (1); colour signals freshness, ripeness or correct cooking, e.g. golden-brown pastry (1); food with a dull, burnt or unexpected colour is often rejected even if it tastes good (1).

A liquid such as a stock or sauce is boiled so that water evaporates (1), which concentrates the flavour and thickens the sauce (1).

::: q
A star profile of a new granola bar shows low scores for crunchiness and sweetness. Suggest two changes to the recipe and explain each. (4)
---
Two changes, each explained (2 + 2): toast the oats and nuts or seeds before mixing (1), to drive off moisture and give a crisper, crunchier texture and nuttier flavour (1); bake a little longer or thinner (1) so the bar dries and crisps (1); add dried fruit such as chopped dates or apricots (1) for natural sweetness without much added sugar (1); add vanilla or cinnamon (1), which increases the perception of sweetness (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Triangle = difference; paired preference or hedonic = liking.** Picking the wrong test for the aim loses the marks.
- Each **control** is a separate mark. Don't repeat "make it fair" in different words.
- **Use sensory words**: crisp, glossy, tangy, aromatic. "Nice" and "good" score nothing.
- Explain **why** a control matters (bias, order effect, palate fatigue) when asked to explain.
- Smell contributes more to flavour than the tongue does. Link the **olfactory system** to flavour, not just to "smelling food".
- On the star diagram, **higher scores sit further from the centre**.
:::

::: test
Explain how taste buds detect flavour in food. (3)
---
Food is chewed and mixed with saliva, which dissolves the flavour chemicals (1); these reach taste receptor cells in the taste buds on the tongue (1); the receptors send nerve signals to the brain, which identifies the taste as sweet, salty, sour, bitter or umami (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the sense of smell contributes to the flavour of food. (3)
---
Volatile aroma molecules are released from food (1) and reach olfactory receptors in the nose, through the nostrils and from the back of the mouth when chewing (1); the brain combines these signals with taste, so smell provides most of the detailed flavour, such as telling strawberry from raspberry (1).
:::

Give each child a small, equal, coded piece of the flapjack (1); ask them to rate how much they like it on a simple scale, e.g. a five-point scale of faces from very unhappy to very happy (1); collect the charts and work out the average score to judge how acceptable the flapjack is (1). Credit checking for allergies first.

::: test
A school canteen is testing three new pasta sauces. Give four ways to set up the taste panel so the results are reliable. (4)
---
Any four (1 each): use a good number of tasters from the target group (pupils); code each sauce with a random number; serve equal portions on the same pasta, at the same temperature, in identical dishes; randomise the order of tasting; tasters work alone without discussion; water between samples; the same scoring chart and instructions for everyone; check allergies first.
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between a rating test and a profiling test. (3)
---
In a rating test, tasters score samples on a scale for one attribute, e.g. saltiness 1–5 (1). In a profiling test, tasters score several attributes of a product, e.g. sweetness, crunch, colour and aroma (1), and the average scores are plotted on a star (radar) diagram to show the whole sensory profile (1).
:::

::: test
A supermarket is developing a reduced-salt range of ready-made soups.
- Analyse how sensory testing could be used to develop the range.
- Evaluate how reliable the results of sensory testing are likely to be. (8)
---
Levels of response:
- **7–8:** a detailed analysis of several appropriate tests, each clearly linked to a stage or purpose in developing the soups; a well-balanced evaluation of reliability that considers panels, controls and real-world limits, with a judgement.
- **5–6:** a clear analysis of appropriate tests; a reasonable evaluation of reliability.
- **3–4:** some correct tests with limited explanation; the evaluation is brief.
- **1–2:** simple points; tests may be mismatched to aims.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- A triangle test shows whether tasters can detect the lower salt; if they cannot, the change is invisible to customers.
- Rating or ranking for saltiness and flavour intensity at different salt levels finds the lowest acceptable level.
- Profiling (star diagram) by a trained panel shows effects on savouriness, aroma, thickness and aftertaste, and guides fixes such as herbs, spices, umami ingredients (tomato, mushroom, yeast extract), roasting vegetables and reductions.
- Hedonic or paired preference testing with target customers compares the new soup with the standard one before launch.
- Retesting after each modification.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Reliable if controlled: coded samples, random order, same temperature and portion, booths, water between samples, enough tasters.
- The panel must suit the target market; staff panels may be biased.
- Trained panels give consistent profiles, but untrained consumers show real preference.
- Palate fatigue and the order effect with salty samples.
- A lab setting is not like eating at home; label information ("reduced salt") may change perception, and tastes adapt over time as people get used to less salt.
- Judgement: well-controlled testing is a reliable guide for development, but results should be confirmed with larger home-use trials.
