## What it is

Carbohydrates change in three ways when food is cooked, and the exam expects you to keep them apart:
- **Gelatinisation** — starch + **liquid** + heat → thickening (sauces, custard, gravy).
- **Dextrinisation** — starch + **dry heat** → browning (toast, crusts).
- **Caramelisation** — **sugar** + heat → browning and new flavour (caramel, roasted vegetables).

### Starch in food

Starch is a **polysaccharide**: long chains of glucose, stored in plants as tiny **granules**. Flour, cornflour, arrowroot, potato, rice and pasta are all rich in starch. Starch granules **do not dissolve** in cold water: they just float and then sink.

### Gelatinisation

**Gelatinisation** is the thickening of a liquid by starch when it is heated. It needs **both liquid and heat**.
1. **Cold liquid**: the starch granules are **suspended** in the liquid — they do not dissolve, and sink if not stirred.
2. **From about 60 °C**: the granules begin to **absorb liquid** and **swell**, and the mixture starts to thicken.
3. **Nearer 100 °C** (boiling): the swollen granules are at their largest; some **burst**, releasing starch molecules into the liquid. These form a network that traps the liquid, so the sauce reaches its full thickness (**viscosity**).
4. The sauce should **boil gently for a couple of minutes**, stirring, so gelatinisation is complete and the **raw flour taste** is cooked out.
5. **On cooling**, the starch network firms up and the mixture thickens further or sets into a **gel** — this is why custard sets in a trifle, a lasagne sauce firms as it stands, and blancmange holds its shape (sometimes called **gelation**).

Stirring (**agitation**) matters throughout: heat passes by **conduction** through the pan base and by **convection** through the liquid, but starch granules sink onto the hot base, where they clump into **lumps** and **scorch** unless kept moving.

On standing, starch molecules slowly realign and squeeze out water (**retrogradation**). This is why cold sauces can go watery and bread goes stale. A **skin** forms on sauces and custard as the surface dries; covering the surface with greaseproof paper or cling film prevents it.

### The starch-to-liquid ratio controls viscosity

The more starch per volume of liquid, the thicker the result. Roughly, for 250 ml milk:

| Consistency | Fat and flour | Looks like | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pouring** | about 15 g of each | Flows freely, glossy | Parsley sauce, custard, sauces served in a jug |
| **Coating** | about 25 g of each | Coats the back of a spoon and clings to food | Cauliflower cheese, lasagne, fish pie |
| **Binding (panada)** | about 50 g of each | Very thick; leaves the sides of the pan | Holding fishcakes, croquettes or soufflé bases together |

To make a sauce **thicker**: add more starch (a paste of cornflour and cold water, or **beurre manié** — butter and flour kneaded together, whisked in), or **simmer** longer so water evaporates (reduction). To make it **thinner**: whisk in more warm liquid.

Other factors: **acid** (lemon juice, tomato, wine) weakens the starch gel, so a sauce with lots of acid needs more starch; **sugar** competes for water and makes a starch mixture set less firmly.

### Four ways to make a starch-based sauce

| Method | How it is made | Why it avoids lumps |
|---|---|---|
| **Roux** | Melt fat, stir in flour and cook for 1–2 minutes to make a paste (the roux); take off the heat, add liquid gradually, stirring; return to the heat and boil, stirring | The **fat coats and separates** each starch granule, so they swell separately |
| **All-in-one** | Cold milk, flour and fat whisked together in the pan, then heated, whisking continuously until it boils | Continuous **whisking** keeps granules apart as they swell |
| **Blended** | Cornflour or custard powder mixed to a smooth paste with a little **cold** liquid, then hot liquid stirred in and the mixture heated | The **cold liquid** separates the granules before heat is applied |
| **Infused** | Milk heated gently with onion, bay leaf and peppercorns, left to stand, then strained and used to make a roux sauce (béchamel) | Adds flavour; lump prevention as for a roux |

A **velouté** is a roux sauce made with **stock** instead of milk. **Béchamel** is a white roux sauce made with infused milk.

### Sauce faults

| Fault | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Lumpy** | Liquid added too quickly; not stirred; roux not cooked; heat too high so granules gelatinised in clumps |
| **Too thin** | Too little flour for the liquid; not brought to the boil, so not fully gelatinised; too much liquid |
| **Too thick** | Too much flour; overcooked, so too much water evaporated; left to stand and set |
| **Raw, floury taste** | Roux or sauce not cooked for long enough after boiling |
| **Burnt taste / brown flecks** | Heat too high; not stirred, so starch caught on the base |
| **Greasy** | Too much fat in the roux |
| **Skin on top** | Surface left uncovered, so it dried out as the sauce cooled |

### Dextrinisation

**Dextrinisation** is what happens when starch is heated **without liquid** (dry heat). The long starch chains break down into shorter chains called **dextrins**, which are **brown** and taste slightly sweet and toasted.
- **Toast**: the surface of the bread turns golden-brown under the grill or in the toaster.
- **Bread crust** and **baked goods**: the outside of a loaf, scone or biscuit browns in the dry oven heat, while the moist inside stays pale.
- **Roux**: cooking the flour in fat for longer gives a darker, nuttier roux.
- **Crumbed or battered coatings**, pizza and flatbread bases, croutons.

Too much dry heat carries the process on to **burning** — black, bitter and charred.

### Caramelisation

**Caramelisation** is the browning of **sugar** when heated. Sucrose melts to a clear syrup, then breaks down into new compounds that are **golden to dark brown** and have a rich, complex, slightly bitter-sweet flavour. It happens at high temperatures, above about **160 °C**, and a few degrees too far gives a **burnt, bitter** taste.
- **Caramel sauce** and spun sugar; the crisp top of a **crème brûlée**.
- **Caramelised vegetables**: onions cooked slowly in a little fat soften and turn deep brown and sweet, because their **natural sugars** caramelise; roasted carrots, parsnips and squash brown at the edges for the same reason.
- **Glazes**: honey or sugar glazes on roasted meats and vegetables.
- The golden crust of **cakes and biscuits** (alongside other browning reactions).

Caramel is dangerously hot and sticks to skin. Stirring sugar syrup before it has dissolved can make it **crystallise** (go grainy).

::: terms
- **Starch**: a polysaccharide made of long chains of glucose, stored in plants as insoluble granules.
- **Gelatinisation**: the thickening of a liquid when starch granules absorb liquid, swell and burst as they are heated.
- **Viscosity**: how thick a liquid is and how easily it flows; more starch per volume of liquid gives higher viscosity.
- **Gelation**: the setting of a gelatinised starch mixture into a firm gel as it cools, as when custard sets.
- **Roux**: a paste of equal amounts of melted fat and flour, cooked, used as the base for a sauce.
- **Blended method**: mixing starch such as cornflour to a paste with cold liquid before heating, to keep the granules separate.
- **Panada**: a very thick binding sauce made with a high proportion of starch to liquid.
- **Agitation**: stirring or whisking a sauce as it heats so the starch stays suspended and does not form lumps or burn.
- **Dextrinisation**: the browning of starch by dry heat, as long starch chains break into shorter brown dextrins.
- **Caramelisation**: the browning of sugar by heat, giving a golden-brown colour and a rich flavour.
- **Retrogradation**: starch molecules realigning as a gel stands, squeezing out water and causing staling or watery sauces.
:::

::: method
1. **Decide which process it is**: liquid present → gelatinisation; starch with dry heat → dextrinisation; sugar with heat → caramelisation.
2. **Give the conditions**: gelatinisation needs liquid and heat (from about 60 °C, complete near 100 °C); caramelisation needs high heat.
3. **Explain the stages in order**: granules suspended → absorb liquid → swell → burst → network traps liquid → thickens.
4. **Link to the result in the named dish**: glossy coating sauce, golden crust, sweet brown onions.
5. **For faults**, pair each cause with the science: "liquid added too quickly, so the granules clumped before they could swell separately".
6. **For extended questions**, follow each carbohydrate through the dish from start to finish, then evaluate the controls: ratio, heat, stirring, time.
:::

::: example Explain — gelatinisation
Explain how gelatinisation thickens a béchamel sauce. (4)

The flour contains starch granules, which are suspended in the milk when it is added to the roux (1). As the sauce is heated, from about 60 °C the granules absorb the liquid and swell (1). At around boiling point they reach full size and some burst, releasing starch molecules (1) that form a network trapping the liquid, so the sauce becomes thick (1).
:::

::: example Explain — dextrinisation
Explain why crumpets turn golden-brown when they are toasted. (2)

The dry radiant heat of the grill or toaster acts on the starch at the surface of the crumpet (1), breaking the long starch chains into shorter dextrins, which are brown — this is dextrinisation (1).
:::

::: example Explain — caramelisation
Explain why onions become brown and sweet when they are cooked slowly for a French onion soup. (3)

Onions contain natural sugars (1). Long, gentle cooking drives off the water and heats the sugars until they caramelise (1), forming brown compounds with a sweet, rich flavour, which colours and flavours the soup (1).
:::

::: example Explain — making a sauce thicker
A coating sauce for a fish pie is too thin. Explain two ways the student could make it thicker. (4)

Mix a little cornflour to a paste with cold milk and stir it into the sauce, then bring it back to the boil (1), so the extra starch gelatinises and increases the viscosity (1). Or simmer the sauce for longer while stirring (1), so water evaporates and the sauce reduces and thickens (1).
:::

::: watch
- Gelatinisation needs **liquid**. Dextrinisation is **dry heat on starch**. Caramelisation is **heat on sugar**. Never swap them.
- Toast browning is **dextrinisation**, not caramelisation — bread is mainly starch.
- Describe gelatinisation as a **sequence**: suspended → absorb → swell → burst → network → thick. Missing the swelling loses marks.
- The **ratio** of starch to liquid decides the consistency; learn pouring, coating and binding.
- Faults need a **cause and a reason**, not just "didn't stir".
- Stirring is needed because starch **sinks** onto the hot pan base.
:::
