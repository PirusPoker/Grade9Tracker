## What it is

**Food spoilage** is any change that makes food unacceptable to eat — it looks, smells, tastes or feels wrong. The spec names three causes you must recognise: **enzymic action**, **mould growth** and **yeast action**. You need to describe the **signs** of each, explain **why** they happen, and know how to prepare and store fruit and vegetables to **slow** them down.

### Spoilage is not the same as food poisoning

This distinction earns marks in almost every paper.
- **Spoiled food** usually shows **visible signs**: mould, slime, a sour or alcoholic smell, soft patches, browning. People usually throw it away because it is unpleasant.
- **Food contaminated with pathogenic bacteria** can look, smell and taste **completely normal**. Salmonella on a cooked chicken sandwich gives no warning at all.

So "it looks fine" does not mean food is safe, and some spoiled food (a brown banana) is still safe to eat. Microorganisms that spoil food are often different from those that cause illness, although some moulds produce harmful **toxins**.

### Enzymic action

Enzymes in fruit and vegetables keep working after harvest. They are **biological catalysts** made of protein.

**Ripening** is enzyme-driven:
- Starch is converted to **sugars**, so the fruit becomes sweeter.
- **Cell walls** are broken down, so the flesh **softens**.
- The **colour** changes — a banana goes from green to yellow, then develops brown **spots** and finally turns black; the flesh becomes soft and mushy.
- **Aroma** develops, then becomes over-strong.

Ripe fruit gives off a gas (**ethylene**) that speeds up ripening in fruit stored nearby, so one over-ripe fruit in a bowl hastens the others.

**Enzymic browning**: when fruit is **cut, peeled or bruised**, damaged cells release enzymes that react with oxygen and turn the flesh brown (apples, pears, bananas, avocados, potatoes — see 3.3.2.4). **Bruises** on whole fruit brown for the same reason and are a common entry point for moulds and yeasts.

Signs of enzymic spoilage: **over-soft texture**, **brown discolouration**, **very sweet or fermented smell**, **wrinkling** as water is lost.

### Mould growth

**Moulds** are fungi. Their **spores** float in the air and land on food. On a moist surface a spore grows fine threads called **hyphae**, which spread **into** the food like roots. Then the mould grows the visible **fruiting bodies**, which produce thousands more spores.

Moulds grow well on:
- **bread** and baked goods;
- **cheese** (hard and soft);
- **soft fruits** — strawberries, raspberries, peaches;
- citrus fruit, jam, yoghurt and cooked leftovers.

Moulds can grow in conditions bacteria dislike: **drier**, **more acidic** or **sugary** foods, and even slowly in the **fridge**.

**Signs of mould growth**:
- **fuzzy, furry or powdery patches**, often green, blue, white, grey or black;
- **coloured spots** that spread;
- a **musty, stale** smell;
- soft, discoloured or slimy areas underneath.

**Why mouldy soft foods should be thrown away**: what you can see is only the surface. The **hyphae spread invisibly** through soft, moist foods such as bread, soft cheese, soft fruit and jam, and some moulds produce **toxins**, which can spread through the food too. Cutting off the visible mould does not remove them. Very hard, dense foods — a block of hard cheese, a firm carrot — are harder for hyphae to penetrate, and a generous piece can be cut away, but when in doubt, **throw it out**.

### Yeast action

**Yeasts** are single-celled fungi. They grow well on foods that are **moist** and contain **sugar**, especially fruit, and they tolerate acid.
- Yeasts **ferment** the natural sugars, producing **carbon dioxide** and **alcohol**.
- Fruits commonly spoiled: **grapes, strawberries, tomatoes**, plums, and also fruit juices, honey and fruit yoghurt.

**Signs of yeast spoilage**:
- **fizzing** or **bubbles** in juice or around fruit, or a swollen carton (from carbon dioxide);
- an **alcoholic** or **winey**, sour smell and taste;
- fruit that is **soft**, **leaking juice** and sometimes **slimy**;
- a white film on the surface of liquids.

### Other kinds of spoilage (for context)

- **Bacterial spoilage**: slime on meat, a sour smell and lumps in milk, grey or green colour in meat.
- **Moisture loss**: wilted lettuce, wrinkled apples, dried-out bread.
- **Staling** of bread (starch changes — not microbial) and **freezer burn**.
- **Rancidity**: fats oxidising, giving an off taste in nuts and crisps.
- **Pests**: insects, mice and flies damage and contaminate food.

### Preparing and storing fruit and vegetables to control spoilage

**Washing and chilling**:
- **Washing** removes soil, dirt and many of the microorganisms on the surface. It is best done **just before** use: fruit washed and then stored damp, especially berries, goes mouldy faster.
- **Chilling** (0 to below 5 °C) slows enzymes, yeasts and moulds. Soft fruits, salad and most vegetables last much longer in the fridge. Bananas, potatoes, onions and tomatoes (for best flavour) are kept in a **cool, dry, dark** place instead.

**Other good practice**:
- Remove any **damaged or mouldy** item promptly so spores do not spread.
- Don't **overcrowd** fruit; keep ripening fruit such as bananas **apart** from other fruit.
- Buy **little and often**, in the quantity you will use.
- Store in **ventilated** bags or the crisper drawer to control moisture.

**Preparation skills** that help:
- **De-skinning and de-seeding tomatoes**: cut a small cross in the base, dip in **boiling** water for about 10–30 seconds until the skin starts to curl, plunge into **iced** water, then peel. Quarter the tomato and scoop out the seeds with a teaspoon. This gives a smoother sauce or salsa, removes the watery centre that would make a dish soggy, and removes the skin and seed area where yeasts and moulds first take hold once tomatoes are cut.
- **Blanching** fruit and vegetables briefly **denatures enzymes** before freezing or to stop browning.
- **Mash, shred, scoop, segment, juice**: every cut damages cells, so treat cut surfaces with **acid** (lemon juice), keep them **covered** and **chilled**, and prepare them close to serving.

::: terms
- **Food spoilage**: any change that makes food unacceptable to eat because of its appearance, smell, taste or texture.
- **Ripening**: enzyme-driven changes after harvest in which fruit becomes sweeter, softer and changes colour.
- **Enzymic action**: changes to food caused by enzymes, such as ripening, softening and browning.
- **Mould**: a fungus that grows as fine threads on food and produces visible, fuzzy, coloured patches of spores.
- **Hyphae**: the fine thread-like "roots" of a mould that spread invisibly into food.
- **Spore**: a tiny reproductive cell released by moulds, carried in the air to land on new food.
- **Yeast**: a single-celled fungus that spoils sugary fruit by fermenting it.
- **Fermentation**: the breakdown of sugars by yeast, producing carbon dioxide and alcohol.
- **Mycotoxin**: a poison made by some moulds, which can spread through food beyond the visible growth.
- **De-seeding**: removing the seeds from fruit or vegetables such as tomatoes and peppers.
- **Contamination**: the presence of harmful microorganisms or other substances in food, which may give no visible sign.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the cause**: soft, brown, over-ripe = enzymes; fuzzy coloured patches = mould; fizzing, alcohol smell = yeast.
2. **Describe the signs precisely**, using senses: what you see, smell, feel.
3. **Explain the science**: enzymes act after harvest; mould spores land and hyphae grow in; yeasts ferment sugars to CO₂ and alcohol.
4. **Link the control to the cause**: chill to slow; wash to remove; separate to stop spread; acid or blanching for enzymes.
5. **Distinguish spoilage from food poisoning** whenever the question hints at safety.
6. **For extended questions**, cover all three causes for the named foods, then evaluate each storage method — how well it works, its cost, its effect on quality, and whether it cuts waste.
:::

Describe two signs of yeast spoilage in a punnet of grapes. (2)

::: example Explain — mouldy bread
Explain why a loaf of bread with a patch of mould on one slice should be thrown away. (3)

The visible patch is only the surface growth; the mould's hyphae spread invisibly through the soft, moist bread (1). Spores will also have spread to other slices in the bag (1). Some moulds produce toxins that can make people ill, so cutting off the mould does not make the rest safe (1).
:::

::: example Explain — ripening bananas
Explain the changes that take place as a banana ripens. (3)

Enzymes in the banana keep working after it is picked (1). They convert starch into sugars, so the banana becomes sweeter, and break down the cell walls, so it softens (1). The skin changes from green to yellow, then brown spots develop and it eventually turns black as it over-ripens (1).
:::

::: example Explain — washing and chilling
Explain why strawberries should be chilled but only washed just before they are eaten. (4)

Chilling below 5 °C slows the growth of moulds and yeasts and the enzymes that soften the fruit (1), so the strawberries last longer (1). Washing adds moisture to the surface, and moisture helps mould spores grow (1), so washing just before eating removes dirt and microorganisms without making them spoil faster in storage (1).
:::

::: example Explain — de-skinning tomatoes
Explain how to de-skin and de-seed tomatoes for a fresh salsa. (4)

Cut a small cross in the base of each tomato with a sharp knife (1). Place in boiling water for a few seconds until the skin begins to peel back at the cross (1). Lift out and plunge into iced water to stop them cooking, then slip off the skins (1). Cut into quarters and scoop out the seeds with a teaspoon before dicing, so the salsa is not watery (1).
:::

::: watch
- Match the **sign to the cause**: fizzing and alcohol = **yeast**; fuzzy patches = **mould**; soft, brown, sweet = **enzymes**.
- Spoilage ≠ food poisoning. Pathogens often give **no signs**.
- For mouldy soft food, the reason to bin it is the **hyphae spreading invisibly** (and toxins), not just "it looks disgusting".
- Chilling **slows** spoilage; it doesn't stop it.
- Washing: explain **why** — removes soil and microbes — and **when** — just before use.
- Don't confuse **enzymes** (not alive) with **yeasts and moulds** (living fungi).
