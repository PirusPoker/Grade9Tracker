## What it is

A **raising agent** puts **gas** into a mixture so that it rises and becomes light and open-textured. There are only three gases involved — **air**, **steam** and **carbon dioxide** — and four ways of getting them in: **mechanical**, **steam**, **chemical** and **biological**. The exam asks you to name the raising agent in a product, explain how it works, and diagnose what went wrong when a product is flat, sunken or dense.

### The principle behind every raising agent

1. Gas is **introduced** into, or **produced** inside, the mixture.
2. In the oven, the gas **expands** as it heats, pushing the mixture up.
3. The gas is **trapped** by a stretchy framework — **gluten** in bread and flour mixtures, **egg protein** in sponges and choux.
4. Heat **sets** the framework around the gas: protein **coagulates** and starch **gelatinises**, fixing the risen shape. If the gas escapes before the structure sets, the product sinks.

### Mechanical raising agents: air

Air is added by working the mixture:

| Method | How air is added | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Whisking** | Beating eggs (and sugar) fast traps air in a protein foam | Whisked sponge, Swiss roll, savoury roulade, meringue, soufflé |
| **Beating** | Vigorous mixing with a wooden spoon or electric mixer | Batters, all-in-one cakes |
| **Creaming** | Fat and sugar beaten until pale and fluffy; the fat holds the air | Victoria sandwich, cupcakes |
| **Rubbing in** | Fat rubbed into flour with fingertips while lifting the mixture high | Scones, crumble topping, rock cakes |
| **Sieving** | Air is trapped between flour particles as they fall | Most cakes and batters |
| **Folding** | Gently cutting and turning a mixture in a figure-of-eight with a metal spoon, so air already in the mixture is **kept**, not knocked out | Adding flour to a whisked sponge |

Egg is the key ingredient in the best air-raised products. **Whisking** denatures the egg proteins so they form a film around the air bubbles, making a **gas-in-liquid foam** (see 3.3.2.1). Whisking eggs and sugar until the whisk leaves a trail on the surface (the **ribbon stage**) gives enough air to raise a sponge with no chemical raising agent at all. For a **savoury roulade**, egg whites are whisked to peaks and folded into a cheese or spinach base, baked flat, then rolled around a filling.

### Steam

Any mixture with a high water content makes **steam** when the water reaches **boiling point (100 °C)**. Steam takes up far more space than the water it came from — well over a thousand times the volume — so it pushes the mixture up and outwards very powerfully. Steam-raised products need:
- a **high proportion of liquid** (water, milk, egg);
- a **high oven temperature**, so steam forms quickly;
- a structure (egg protein, gluten, gelatinised starch) that stretches and then **sets** in the heat.

**Choux pastry**: water and butter are boiled, flour is beaten in to form a thick paste (the starch gelatinises), then eggs are beaten in. In a hot oven, the water turns to steam, which puffs the paste up into a **hollow shell**; the egg protein coagulates and sets it. The buns are pierced to let the steam out and returned to the oven to dry, or they go soggy. Opening the oven door early lets the steam condense before the shell has set, so the buns collapse.

**Yorkshire puddings and batters**: the fat in the tin must be **smoking hot** and the oven very hot, so the batter sizzles and steam forms instantly, lifting the sides of the pudding.

Steam also helps raise **flaky and puff pastry** (separating the layers) and contributes to the rise of bread and cakes.

### Chemical raising agents: carbon dioxide

**Bicarbonate of soda** (sodium bicarbonate) breaks down when heated with moisture to produce **carbon dioxide**, water and **sodium carbonate**. Sodium carbonate is alkaline and gives a **soapy, bitter taste** and a **yellow-brown colour**. So bicarbonate is used:
- with an **acid** — buttermilk, yoghurt, lemon juice, cream of tartar, treacle — which reacts with it to release the CO₂ quickly and neutralises the soapy taste (soda bread, buttermilk scones);
- or in **strongly flavoured, dark** products where the taste and colour are hidden (gingerbread, parkin, chocolate cake).

**Baking powder** is a ready-made mix of **bicarbonate of soda and an acid** (such as cream of tartar), with a starch such as cornflour to keep it dry. When liquid is added and the mixture is heated, the acid and alkali react and release **carbon dioxide**, leaving no soapy taste.

**Self-raising flour** is plain flour with baking powder already mixed evenly through it — convenient and reliable for cakes, scones and puddings.

**Getting the quantity right**: too much chemical raising agent makes the mixture rise **too fast**; the gluten or egg structure stretches beyond what it can hold, the bubbles burst, and the product **collapses** with a coarse, open texture and an unpleasant taste. Too little gives a small, **dense**, heavy product.

### Biological raising agent: yeast

**Yeast** is a single-celled **fungus**. Given the right conditions, it carries out **fermentation**: it feeds on **sugars** and produces **carbon dioxide** and **alcohol** (ethanol).

Yeast needs:
- **warmth** — it is most active in lukewarm conditions (roughly body temperature); cold slows it; hot liquid **kills** it, and it dies in the oven at about **60 °C**;
- **moisture** — water or milk to activate it;
- **food** — sugar, either added or produced from the starch in flour by enzymes;
- **time** — to produce enough gas (proving).

Things that slow yeast: **salt** (needed for flavour and gluten strength, but kept away from direct contact with yeast), **too much sugar** (draws water out of the yeast cells), and a lot of **fat** (in rich doughs such as brioche, which prove slowly).

**In bread making**, the CO₂ is trapped in the **gluten network** developed by kneading, so the dough rises as it **proves**. In the oven the gas expands (**oven spring**), the yeast is killed, the alcohol evaporates, the gluten coagulates and the starch gelatinises to set the crumb, and the crust browns by dextrinisation. Yeast also adds the characteristic **flavour** of bread.

Types: **fresh** yeast, **dried active** yeast (activated in warm liquid first) and **fast-action** (easy-blend) yeast, mixed straight into the flour.

### Common faults

| Fault | Likely causes |
|---|---|
| **Cake sunk in the middle** | Too much raising agent; oven door opened too soon; oven too cool; underbaked; too much sugar or liquid |
| **Cake dense and flat** | Too little raising agent; plain flour used by mistake; air knocked out by over-mixing when folding; mixture curdled; oven too cool |
| **Cake peaked and cracked** | Oven too hot, so the outside set before the centre had risen; tin too small |
| **Dense, heavy bread** | Yeast killed by hot liquid; not enough proving time or too cold; too much salt; plain flour (weak gluten); not kneaded enough |
| **Bread collapsed, large holes, sour smell** | **Over-proved**, so the gluten over-stretched and the gas escaped |
| **Flat Yorkshire puddings** | Fat not hot enough; oven too cool; door opened during baking; batter too thin |
| **Soapy, bitter taste, yellow colour** | Too much bicarbonate of soda, or no acid to neutralise it |

::: terms
- **Raising agent**: anything that introduces or produces gas in a mixture so that it rises and becomes light.
- **Mechanical raising**: adding air by physical action — whisking, beating, creaming, rubbing in, sieving and folding.
- **Folding**: gently combining ingredients in a figure-of-eight with a metal spoon so that trapped air is not knocked out.
- **Gas-in-liquid foam**: air bubbles held in a liquid by a protein film, as when eggs are whisked.
- **Steam**: water vapour formed when the water in a mixture reaches boiling point; it expands hugely and lifts the mixture.
- **Bicarbonate of soda**: an alkaline chemical raising agent that gives off carbon dioxide when heated with moisture, but leaves a soapy taste unless an acid is present.
- **Baking powder**: a chemical raising agent made of bicarbonate of soda, an acid such as cream of tartar and a starch filler.
- **Self-raising flour**: plain flour with baking powder evenly mixed in.
- **Yeast**: a single-celled fungus used as a biological raising agent in bread.
- **Fermentation**: the process in which yeast feeds on sugar and produces carbon dioxide and alcohol.
- **Proving**: leaving a yeast dough in a warm place so that the carbon dioxide produced makes it rise.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the raising agent(s)** in the product: air, steam, CO₂ (chemical or yeast). Most products use more than one.
2. **Explain how the gas gets in**: whisked or creamed in; produced by heat on water; produced by a chemical reaction; produced by fermentation.
3. **Explain what traps it**: gluten network or egg protein film.
4. **Explain what happens in the oven**: gas expands, product rises, protein coagulates and starch gelatinises to set it.
5. **For faults**, link cause to science: "too much baking powder → too much gas too fast → structure over-stretched → collapsed".
6. **For 8-mark questions**, compare several products, then judge why each raising agent suits that product (texture wanted, liquid content, flavour).
:::

::: example Explain — bicarbonate of soda
Explain why buttermilk is used with bicarbonate of soda in scones. (3)

Bicarbonate of soda gives off carbon dioxide when heated with moisture, but also leaves sodium carbonate, which tastes soapy and bitter (1). Buttermilk is acidic, so it reacts with the bicarbonate to release the carbon dioxide quickly (1) and neutralises the alkali, so the scones rise well without a soapy taste or yellow colour (1).
:::

::: example Explain — yeast
Explain how yeast makes pizza dough rise. (4)

Yeast is a living fungus that ferments the sugars in the dough when it is warm and moist (1). Fermentation produces carbon dioxide gas (and alcohol) (1). The gas is trapped by the gluten network formed by kneading the strong flour, so the dough rises as it proves (1). In the hot oven the gas expands, the yeast is killed and the gluten sets, fixing the airy structure of the crust (1).
:::

::: watch
- Name the **gas** as well as the agent: air, steam, or **carbon dioxide**.
- Always say what **traps** the gas (gluten, egg protein) and what **sets** the structure (coagulation, gelatinisation).
- Bicarbonate of soda without acid leaves a **soapy, bitter taste and yellow colour** — a favourite mark.
- Steam needs a **high oven temperature** and a **high liquid content**.
- Yeast is **killed** by heat (hot liquid, the oven), **slowed** by cold, salt and lots of sugar.
- Faults need **cause + science**, not just "the oven was wrong".
:::
