## What it is

Every food has an environmental cost: the land, water, fuel and fertiliser used to grow it, the energy to process, chill and transport it, the packaging, and whatever ends up as waste. This topic asks you to explain those costs and to **evaluate** ways to cut them, recognising that the "obvious" answer isn't always right.

### Carbon footprint and food miles

A **carbon footprint** is the total amount of **greenhouse gases** (carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide, usually expressed as a CO₂ equivalent) released over a food's whole life. That covers farming, processing, packaging, transport, storage, cooking and disposal. Greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere and contribute to **global warming** and **climate change**.

Where the emissions come from:
- **Farming** is often the biggest share. Cattle and sheep release **methane** as they digest grass. Fertilisers release **nitrous oxide**. Clearing forest for farmland releases stored carbon. This is why beef and lamb have much higher footprints than pulses, cereals or vegetables.
- **Processing and storage**: energy for factories, cooking, chilling and freezing.
- **Transport**: fuel for lorries, ships and planes.
- **Packaging**: making materials and disposing of them.
- **Waste**: food rotting in **landfill** without oxygen produces methane.

**Food miles** are the distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is eaten. Fewer miles usually means less fuel and fewer emissions, but food miles are **only part** of the footprint:
- **How** it travels matters more than how far. **Air-freighted** food (such as fresh berries, asparagus or green beans flown in out of season) has a far higher footprint per kilogram than food shipped by sea, even over the same distance.
- **How it was grown** can outweigh transport. Tomatoes grown in a heated UK glasshouse in winter can have a larger footprint than tomatoes grown outdoors in Spain and brought by lorry.
- The **last mile** counts: a separate car trip to buy one item adds emissions.

### Seasonal and local food

**Seasonal food** is eaten at the time of year it is naturally ready to harvest in the UK, for example asparagus in late spring, strawberries and salad crops in summer, apples, squash and blackberries in autumn, and leeks, parsnips, cabbage and Brussels sprouts in winter. Choosing it:
- avoids heated glasshouses and air freight, so it cuts the carbon footprint;
- gives fresher food with better flavour and more nutrients, as less time passes between harvest and eating;
- is usually cheaper, because supply is plentiful.

When planning recipes, **select fruit and vegetables that are in season** (S2, S3). For example, make a summer berry pudding in July, not December, or a winter root vegetable soup in January.

**Reasons for buying locally produced food:** fewer food miles; fresher produce, picked riper; supports local farmers, jobs and the local economy; knowing the provenance; often less packaging (farmers' markets, veg boxes); seasonal by nature. The limits are that it can cost more, the range is limited, and local is not always lower-carbon.

### Sustainability, fish farming and organic food

**Sustainability** means meeting today's food needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs: protecting soil, water, fish stocks, wildlife and the climate.
- **Fish farming** can take pressure off wild stocks, but it causes pollution, disease and sea lice, and the feed may use wild fish (see 3.6.1.1).
- **Organic food** avoids synthetic fertilisers and pesticides, which benefits soil and wildlife and cuts the energy used to make fertiliser. But lower yields need more land, so its overall environmental benefit is debated.

### Food waste

| Where | Causes | Ways to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| **Food production** (farms, factories) | Crops rejected for size, shape or blemishes; overproduction; bad weather or labour shortages leaving crops unharvested; trimmings and offcuts in processing; breakdowns in cold storage | Selling "wonky" produce; using trimmings in other products (e.g. soups, stock); turning by-products into animal feed; **anaerobic digestion** to make biogas and fertiliser; better forecasting |
| **Retailers** (shops, supermarkets) | Over-ordering; strict cosmetic standards; damaged packs; date codes passing; promotions creating overstock | Better stock control and forecasting; reduced-price stickers near the date; donating surplus to charities and food banks; surplus-food apps; wonky veg ranges; removing best-before dates from some fresh produce; selling loose fruit and vegetables so customers buy only what they need |
| **The home** | Buying too much; poor planning; misunderstanding date marks; poor storage; cooking too much; not using leftovers | Meal planning and shopping lists; understanding use by vs best before; correct fridge storage; freezing; portion control; using leftovers |

Wasted food wastes everything used to produce it (land, water, energy, fertiliser, transport and packaging), and when it goes to landfill it produces **methane**. Cutting waste is one of the most effective ways to shrink food's footprint. It also saves money and helps food security.

**Using leftovers safely.** Cool leftovers quickly and refrigerate them within about 90 minutes, eat them within about two days, and reheat only once, until steaming hot (75 °C in the centre). Ideas:
- Leftover roast vegetables and potatoes become bubble and squeak or a frittata.
- Stale bread becomes breadcrumbs, croutons or bread and butter pudding.
- Overripe bananas go into banana bread.
- Leftover rice (cooled quickly) makes egg-fried rice.
- Vegetable peelings and chicken bones make stock.
- Wilting vegetables go into soup.

### Packaging and the environment

Packaging **protects** food, **preserves** it, **informs** the buyer and **helps sell** it. Packaging can prevent more waste than it creates: a shrink-wrapped cucumber lasts far longer than an unwrapped one. But packaging has environmental costs:
- It uses raw materials (oil for plastic, trees for card, sand and energy for glass, ore for metals) and energy to produce.
- Most plastics are made from non-renewable oil and **don't biodegrade**. They persist for hundreds of years and break into **microplastics** that pollute oceans and harm wildlife.
- **Landfill** space is limited.
- Mixed materials (such as plastic-lined card) are hard to recycle.
- Excess packaging (multi-layers, oversized boxes) wastes resources.

**The 3 Rs, in order of preference:**
- **Reduce**: buy loose produce, choose products with less packaging, and bulk buy.
- **Reuse**: refillable containers, reusable bags and water bottles.
- **Recycle**: glass, aluminium and steel cans, card and many plastics. Follow the on-pack recycling labels. Codes 1 (PET) and 2 (HDPE) are widely recycled.

**Biodegradable** materials break down naturally through the action of microorganisms, but the time this takes isn't guaranteed. **Compostable** materials break down into non-toxic compost under composting conditions, often in an industrial facility, not a landfill or the sea. Making materials from **recycled content** saves energy and raw materials.

::: terms
- **Carbon footprint**: the total greenhouse gases released during the whole life of a food, from farm to disposal.
- **Food miles**: the distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is eaten.
- **Greenhouse gases**: gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide that trap heat in the atmosphere.
- **Seasonal food**: food eaten at the time of year it is naturally harvested in the UK.
- **Sustainability**: meeting today's needs for food without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Landfill**: burying waste in the ground, where rotting food produces methane.
- **Anaerobic digestion**: breaking down food waste without oxygen in a sealed tank to produce biogas and fertiliser.
- **Biodegradable**: able to be broken down naturally by microorganisms, though not within a set time.
- **Compostable**: able to break down into non-toxic compost under composting conditions.
- **Air freight**: transporting food by plane, which gives a much higher carbon footprint than transport by sea or road.
:::

::: method
1. **Define precisely**: carbon footprint is the *whole life*, not just transport; food miles are *distance*.
2. **Explain a chain**: action, then emissions or resources, then the environmental effect ("food in landfill rots without oxygen, releasing methane, a greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming").
3. **Place waste at the right stage**: production, retail or home, with causes and solutions for each.
4. **Evaluate**: note exceptions (air vs sea freight, heated glasshouses, packaging that prevents waste) and trade-offs (cost, convenience, food safety).
5. **Finish with a judgement** stating which change would make the biggest difference and why.
:::

::: example Explain — carbon footprint
Explain the term carbon footprint. (2)

The total amount of greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane, released over the whole life of a food product (1), from growing and processing to packaging, transporting, storing, cooking and disposing of it (1).
:::

::: example Identify — seasonal food
Identify three advantages of choosing vegetables in season in the UK. (3)

Any three, 1 each: they are cheaper because supply is plentiful (1); they are fresher, with better flavour and texture (1); they contain more vitamins, as less time passes from harvest to plate (1). Also: fewer food miles; no heated glasshouses, so a lower carbon footprint; they support British farmers.
:::

::: example Explain — air freight
Explain why fresh green beans flown in from Kenya may have a larger carbon footprint than frozen British peas. (4)

The beans are air-freighted (1), and planes burn far more fuel per kilogram of food than ships or lorries, so emissions are high (1). The peas are grown in the UK and travel a short distance by road (1); although freezing uses energy, the peas are frozen soon after harvest and none is wasted, whereas fresh beans may spoil (1).
:::

::: example Explain — retail waste
Explain two reasons why supermarkets waste food and one way each could be reduced. (6)

Supermarkets reject fruit and vegetables that are the wrong shape or size (1), so edible produce is left on the farm or thrown away (1); selling "wonky" ranges at a lower price uses this produce (1). They over-order to keep shelves full (1), so perishable food passes its date before it is sold (1); better forecasting, reduced-price stickers near the date, and donating surplus to food banks or charities cut this waste (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — packaging
- Analyse the environmental problems caused by food packaging.
- Evaluate how food manufacturers and consumers could reduce these problems. (8)

Making packaging uses raw materials and energy: plastics come from oil, a non-renewable resource, and glass and metal need high-temperature processing, so they add to the carbon footprint. Most plastics do not biodegrade, so they persist in landfill or escape into rivers and seas, where they break into microplastics and harm wildlife. Mixed-material packs, such as crisp packets and plastic-lined cartons, are hard to recycle, and over-packaging, such as fruit in plastic trays, uses more material than is needed.

Manufacturers could reduce packaging weight, use a single recyclable material, include recycled content, and label clearly how to recycle each part. Compostable materials help only if they reach composting facilities. Consumers could choose loose fruit and vegetables, take reusable bags and containers, buy refills and recycle correctly. But packaging also protects food and extends its shelf life: removing it from foods like cucumbers or meat can increase food waste, which has a larger footprint than the packaging saved. Glass is recyclable but heavy, so it raises transport emissions. The best approach is to cut unnecessary packaging and make what remains recyclable, rather than to remove packaging that prevents food being wasted.

(Level 4, 7–8: several problems analysed with causes and effects; balanced evaluation with trade-offs and judgement.)
:::

::: q
Explain why beef has a higher carbon footprint than lentils. (3)
---
Cattle release methane as they digest their food (1); large areas of land, sometimes cleared forest, are needed for grazing and growing feed (1); lentils are plants that need less land, water and energy per kilogram of protein and even fix their own nitrogen, so less fertiliser is needed (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Carbon footprint is not food miles.** The footprint covers the whole life of the food.
- **Local isn't always greener**: heated glasshouses and air freight versus sea freight are the classic evaluation points.
- Waste questions: say **where** (production, retail, home), **why**, and **what fixes it**.
- Packaging answers need **both sides**, since packaging also prevents food waste.
- Using leftovers: always include the **safety** rules (cool quickly, refrigerate, reheat once to 75 °C).
- Link environmental points to **greenhouse gases** and **global warming**, not just "it's bad for the planet".
:::
