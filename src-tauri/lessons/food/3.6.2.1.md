## What it is

Very little food reaches us exactly as it left the farm. **Processing** changes raw materials to make them safe, usable, longer-lasting and more varied. It happens in two stages:

- **Primary processing** turns a raw food material into something that can be sold or used as an ingredient, **without changing it into a different product**. It follows the **rearing, fishing, growing and harvesting** of the food and includes **cleaning**, sorting, grading, trimming, cutting, chilling, **milling** and **heat treating**. Examples: wheat → flour; raw milk → pasteurised milk; potatoes washed and sorted; animals slaughtered and jointed; fish gutted and filleted.
- **Secondary processing** turns primary-processed ingredients into a **different food product**. Examples: flour → bread, pasta, cakes; milk → cheese, yoghurt, butter; fruit → jam; meat → sausages or burgers.

A quick test: after **primary** processing, you can still recognise the original food (flour is still wheat, just ground). After **secondary** processing, it has become something new (bread).

### Primary processing 1: milling wheat into flour

A wheat grain has three parts:
- **Bran**, the outer layers: rich in **fibre**, B vitamins and iron.
- **Germ**, the embryo: B vitamins, vitamin E, some fat and protein.
- **Endosperm**, the bulk of the grain: mainly **starch**, plus the proteins that form **gluten**.

The milling stages:
1. **Cleaning**: removing stones, straw, dust and other seeds.
2. **Conditioning**: adding water so the bran toughens and separates cleanly.
3. **Breaking**: fluted rollers crack the grain open.
4. **Sieving (sifting)**: separating bran and germ from the endosperm.
5. **Reducing**: smooth rollers grind the endosperm into fine flour.

The flour is then blended.

| Flour | Extraction rate | What is removed | Nutritional effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wholemeal** | 100%: the whole grain | Nothing | Highest in fibre, B vitamins, iron and vitamin E |
| **Brown** | about 85% | Some bran | Less fibre than wholemeal |
| **White** | about 70–75% | Bran and germ | Much less fibre, B vitamins and iron, so UK white and brown flour have calcium, iron, thiamin and niacin added by law (see 3.6.2.2) |

The wheat variety matters too:
- **Strong (bread) flour** comes from hard wheat with more protein, so it forms plenty of gluten.
- **Soft (plain) flour** has less protein and suits cakes, biscuits and pastry.
- **Durum wheat** is milled into **semolina** for dried pasta.

### Primary processing 2: heat-treating milk

Raw milk can carry harmful bacteria such as *E. coli*, salmonella, campylobacter and listeria. Heat treatment kills them, making milk safe and extending its shelf life.

| Treatment | Process | Shelf life and storage | Sensory effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pasteurised** | Heated to about **72 °C for 15 seconds**, then cooled quickly | A few days in the fridge; once opened, use within a few days | Very little change: tastes like fresh milk |
| **UHT (ultra heat treated)** | Heated to about **135 °C for 1–2 seconds**, then packed in sterile cartons | Several months unopened, at room temperature; refrigerate once opened | Slightly cooked, caramelised flavour; slightly creamier colour |
| **Sterilised** | Bottled, sealed, then heated to about **110–130 °C for 10–30 minutes** | Several months unopened | Strong caramel or "cooked" flavour and a darker, creamy colour; thicker |
| **Micro-filtered** | Passed through very fine filters that remove most bacteria, then pasteurised | Longer fridge life than standard pasteurised milk | Tastes like fresh pasteurised milk, as it is not heated more |

Other treatments:
- **Homogenisation**: milk is forced under pressure through tiny holes, which breaks the fat into tiny droplets that stay spread through the milk, so a cream layer doesn't form. This is a physical process, not a heat treatment.
- **Evaporated milk**: heated to evaporate much of the water, then canned and sterilised. It is thick, creamy and pale brown, with a caramel flavour.
- **Condensed milk**: like evaporated milk, but with sugar added. The sugar preserves it and makes it very sweet and sticky.
- **Dried (powdered) milk**: most of the water is removed, usually by spray-drying. It keeps for months at room temperature without refrigeration and has a slightly cooked flavour when mixed with water.

**Why heat changes milk's flavour and colour.** At high temperatures, milk sugar (lactose) reacts with milk protein in **browning reactions**, and some sugar caramelises. The longer and hotter the treatment, the stronger the "cooked" flavour and the darker the colour. That's why sterilised and evaporated milk taste most different, and pasteurised milk least.

### How processing affects nutrients

- **Heating** destroys **heat-sensitive vitamins**, especially **vitamin C** and some **B vitamins** (thiamin, folate, B12). Losses rise with temperature and time: small in pasteurisation, greater in UHT, greatest in sterilisation and canning. Protein, calcium and fat are hardly affected.
- **Drying** (dried fruit, dried milk, dried herbs) exposes food to heat and air, so vitamin C is lost by **oxidation**. Removing water also concentrates sugars and energy per gram, as in dried fruit.
- **Milling** removes fibre, vitamins and minerals from white flour (above).
- Processing can also **add** value: fortification, removing water to preserve, or killing bacteria to make food safe.

### Secondary processing examples

- **Flour into bread.** Strong flour, yeast, water and salt are kneaded to develop **gluten**. The yeast ferments sugars to make carbon dioxide, which gluten traps as the dough **proves**. Baking sets the structure, the starch gelatinises, and the crust browns by dextrinisation.
- **Flour into pasta.** Durum semolina (for dried pasta) or strong "00" flour with egg (for fresh pasta) is made into a stiff dough, kneaded to develop gluten, rolled, shaped and cut. Dried pasta is then dried slowly to a low moisture content so it keeps for a long time.
- **Milk into cheese and yoghurt.** Bacteria ferment lactose into **lactic acid**, which lowers the pH and makes the milk proteins set. For cheese, rennet then forms curds, which are separated from the whey, pressed and ripened (see 3.4.1.3).
- **Fruit into jam.** Fruit is boiled with sugar. **Pectin** from the fruit, **acid**, and a high **sugar** concentration form a gel at the **setting point**, about **104–105 °C**. The high sugar level preserves the jam by making water unavailable to microorganisms.

### Practical skill: fresh pasta with a pasta machine (S5, S10, S6)

1. Mix strong "00" flour and beaten egg (about 100 g flour to 1 egg) into a dough, then knead until smooth and elastic. This develops gluten, so the pasta holds together.
2. Wrap and **rest** the dough for about 30 minutes, so the gluten relaxes and it rolls without springing back.
3. Flatten a piece and feed it through the machine on the **widest setting**. Fold it in three and repeat several times; this laminates and further kneads the dough.
4. Roll through progressively thinner settings, dusting with flour or semolina so it doesn't stick.
5. Cut with the machine's cutters (tagliatelle) or by hand, or fill and shape (ravioli). Dust and hang or nest the strands so they don't stick together.
6. **Boil** in a large pan of plenty of salted, rapidly boiling water. Convection currents keep the pasta moving and the starch gelatinises. Fresh pasta needs only a few minutes; dried takes longer. Stir at the start to stop it sticking, cook until **al dente**, then drain.

::: terms
- **Primary processing**: changing a raw food material after harvesting or slaughter so it can be used or sold, without turning it into a different product.
- **Secondary processing**: turning primary-processed ingredients into a different food product, such as flour into bread.
- **Milling**: grinding cereal grains between rollers and sieving them to produce flour.
- **Extraction rate**: the percentage of the whole wheat grain that ends up in the flour.
- **Bran**: the fibre-rich outer layers of a cereal grain, which are removed to make white flour.
- **Endosperm**: the starchy inner part of the grain, which is ground to make white flour.
- **Pasteurisation**: heating milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds, then cooling it quickly, to kill harmful bacteria with little change in flavour.
- **UHT**: ultra heat treatment, heating milk to about 135 °C for 1–2 seconds so it keeps unopened for months.
- **Homogenisation**: forcing milk through tiny holes under pressure to break up the fat so the cream does not separate.
- **Setting point**: the temperature, about 104–105 °C, at which jam boils to form a gel as it cools.
:::

::: method

2. **Describe the process in order**, using the correct terms (conditioning, breaking, sieving, reducing; temperatures and times for milk).
3. **Explain the effect**: on safety, shelf life, sensory qualities (flavour, colour, texture) and nutrition (which vitamins are lost and why).
4. **In comparisons**, use the same headings for both items: process, temperature and time, shelf life, taste and colour, nutrients.
5. **In evaluations**, weigh the gains (safety, convenience, keeping quality, fortification) against the losses (vitamins, fibre, flavour change).

::: example Explain — primary and secondary
Explain the difference between primary and secondary processing. Use wheat as an example. (4)

Primary processing changes the raw material after harvest so it can be used, without changing it into a different product (1): for example, wheat is cleaned and milled into flour (1). Secondary processing uses primary-processed ingredients to make a different food product (1): for example, flour is mixed with yeast, water and salt, kneaded and baked to make bread, or made into pasta (1).
:::

::: example Compare — two heat treatments
Compare sterilised milk with micro-filtered milk. (4)

Sterilised milk is sealed in bottles and heated to about 110–130 °C for 10–30 minutes (1), whereas micro-filtered milk is passed through fine filters to remove bacteria and then pasteurised, so it is heated far less (1). Sterilised milk keeps for months unopened without refrigeration, while micro-filtered milk must be kept in the fridge but lasts longer than ordinary pasteurised milk (1). The long heating gives sterilised milk a caramel flavour, a darker creamy colour and greater losses of vitamins such as B12 and C, whereas micro-filtered milk tastes like fresh milk (1).
:::

::: example Explain — pasta ingredients and processes
Explain the functions of the ingredients and processes when making fresh pasta. (4)

Strong "00" flour contains the proteins glutenin and gliadin, which form gluten when mixed with the liquid in the egg (1). Kneading and repeatedly rolling the dough through the pasta machine stretch and align the gluten strands, making the dough smooth, elastic and strong enough to roll thinly (1). Egg adds liquid, colour and richness, and its protein coagulates during cooking, helping the pasta hold its shape (1). Resting relaxes the gluten so the dough rolls out without shrinking back, and boiling gelatinises the starch to give a tender, al dente texture (1).
:::

::: example Explain — vitamin loss
Explain why dried apricots contain less vitamin C than fresh apricots. (3)

Vitamin C is water-soluble and easily destroyed by heat and by oxygen (1). During drying, the fruit is exposed to warm air for a long time (1), so much of the vitamin C is lost by heat damage and oxidation (1).
:::

::: example Analyse and evaluate — fruit into jam
- Analyse how strawberries are processed from the field into jam.
- Evaluate the effect of this processing on the sensory and nutritional qualities of the fruit. (8)

The strawberries are grown, then picked by hand when ripe. In primary processing they are chilled quickly, sorted and graded, washed and hulled. In secondary processing they are boiled with a large amount of sugar and often lemon juice. Heat softens the fruit and releases **pectin** from the cell walls; the **acid** helps the pectin chains link together; the **sugar** draws water away from the pectin so a gel network can form. The jam is boiled rapidly to the setting point, about 104–105 °C, tested (a wrinkle or flake test), then poured into sterilised jars and sealed while hot. The high sugar level means little water is available to microorganisms, so the jam keeps for months unopened.

The sensory qualities change completely: fresh strawberries are firm, juicy and sharp, while jam is soft, spreadable, glossy, very sweet and darker red, with a cooked flavour; over-boiling can make it dark, stiff and caramelised. Nutritionally, the long boiling destroys much of the vitamin C, which is heat-sensitive and also oxidised; the fruit's fibre largely remains, but it is diluted by sugar. Jam is mostly free sugars, which are linked to tooth decay and excess energy, so it no longer counts towards 5 a day. On the other hand, processing preserves a seasonal crop so it can be eaten all year, with little waste. Overall, jam is a way to preserve fruit and add flavour, not a nutritious alternative to fresh fruit, and should be eaten in small amounts; reduced-sugar jams keep less well and must be refrigerated once opened.

(Level 4, 7–8: clear primary and secondary stages with the science of setting; balanced sensory and nutritional evaluation; judgement.)
