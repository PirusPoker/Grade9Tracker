## What it is

Proteins are why an egg sets, meringue holds its peaks, bread rises into a springy loaf and a marinade softens meat. To explain any of these you need four processes: **denaturation**, **coagulation**, **gluten formation** and **foam formation**. Each works because of how a protein is built.

### What a protein molecule looks like

A protein is a long **chain of amino acids**, folded and coiled into a particular 3D shape and held in that shape by weak **bonds**. When those weak bonds break, the shape — and the way the protein behaves in food — changes.

### Denaturation

**Denaturation** is the **unfolding** of a protein: the weak bonds holding its shape break, so the coiled chain opens out. The protein's structure and properties change, and it usually cannot go back. What causes it:

| Cause | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Heat** | Energy makes the chain vibrate until the bonds break | Egg white turning from clear to white; meat changing colour as it cooks |
| **Acid** (low pH) | Acid disrupts the bonds holding the shape | A lemon or yoghurt marinade tenderising chicken; lime juice "cooking" raw fish in ceviche |
| **Mechanical action** | Whisking, beating or kneading physically stretches and unfolds the chains | Whisking egg white into a foam; kneading dough |
| **Salt** | Salt alters the bonds in the protein | Brining or curing meat and fish |

In **marinades**, the acid denatures protein at the surface of meat, fish or alternatives such as tofu and paneer. The loosened protein holds on to more moisture and is softer to eat, while oil and flavourings add moisture and taste.

### Coagulation

**Coagulation** is what happens **after** denaturation. The unfolded protein chains bump into each other and **join together**, forming a solid, set network that traps water. This is irreversible: you cannot unset a boiled egg. Heat is the main cause, but acid can also coagulate protein (milk curdling in lemon juice).

Egg proteins coagulate at different temperatures, roughly:
- egg **white** starts to set at about **60 °C**;
- egg **yolk** sets at about **65–70 °C**;
- **egg mixtures** diluted with milk and sugar (custard, quiche filling) set higher, at about **70–80 °C**, because the proteins are more spread out.

Coagulation is what **sets** a quiche or baked custard, **binds** burgers, fishcakes and falafel, **holds** breadcrumbs onto food, and firms the structure of cakes and bread.

**Over-coagulation**: if egg or meat protein is overheated, the network tightens and squeezes out the water it was holding. This is **syneresis**:
- scrambled eggs go **rubbery** and sit in a **watery** puddle;
- a baked egg custard or quiche filling **weeps** liquid and has holes;
- a stirred egg custard **curdles** into lumps;
- overcooked meat and fish **shrink**, become **tough** and dry.

The fixes are **gentle heat** and **timing**: cook scrambled eggs slowly and take them off while still soft; bake custards in a **bain-marie** (a tin of water) to keep the heat gentle; stir custard constantly and stop before it boils; take eggs off the heat just before they look done, because carry-over heat keeps cooking them.

### Gluten formation

Wheat flour contains two proteins, **glutenin** and **gliadin**. On their own they are not gluten.
1. When **water** is added, the two proteins absorb it and combine to form **gluten**.
2. **Kneading** (by hand, in a mixer with a dough hook, or in a bread machine) stretches the gluten and lines it up into a strong, springy **network**.
3. **Glutenin** gives the network **elasticity** — it springs back. **Gliadin** gives **extensibility** — it can stretch without tearing.
4. In bread, yeast produces carbon dioxide, and the stretchy gluten network **traps** the bubbles so the dough rises (proving).
5. In the oven, the gluten **coagulates** and sets, fixing the risen shape of the loaf.

**Flour choice** matters because flours differ in protein:
- **strong (bread) flour** has more protein, so it forms more gluten — ideal for bread, pizza and pasta, which need stretch and chew;
- **soft (plain) flour** has less, so it forms a weaker gluten — ideal for cakes, biscuits and shortcrust pastry, which should be tender and crumbly.

Other factors: **salt** in small amounts strengthens gluten; **fat** coats the proteins and blocks gluten formation (shortening — see 3.3.2.3); **resting** dough lets the stretched gluten **relax**, so pastry does not shrink and pasta is easier to roll. A **pasta machine** develops the gluten further each time the dough is passed through, giving smooth, silky sheets.

**Bread making in stages**: mix flour, yeast, salt, water → knead to develop gluten → prove (rise) in a warm place → knock back to even out the gas → shape → second prove → bake (gluten coagulates, crust browns).

### Foam formation

A **foam** is a **gas dispersed in a liquid** — for example, air bubbles in egg white.
1. **Whisking** (mechanical action) **denatures** the egg-white proteins: they unfold.
2. The unfolded proteins arrange themselves around the air bubbles, forming a thin **film** that holds the air in.
3. More whisking makes the bubbles smaller and more numerous, so the foam grows and stiffens (soft peaks, then stiff peaks).
4. **Heat** then **coagulates** the protein film, setting the foam permanently — as in meringue, a whisked sponge, a soufflé or a savoury roulade.

What spoils a foam:
- **Fat** — a trace of **yolk**, grease on the bowl or whisk — stops the proteins forming a film round the bubbles, so the whites will not whisk up. Use a spotlessly clean, dry bowl.
- **Over-whisking** stretches the proteins too far: the foam goes dry, grainy and lumpy, then collapses and liquid separates out.
- **Sugar** added all at once, or too early, weighs the foam down. Added **gradually** once soft peaks form, sugar dissolves into the film and **stabilises** it, giving a glossy meringue. A little **acid** (cream of tartar or lemon juice) also stabilises the foam.

### Eggs: one ingredient, many protein jobs

| Function | Protein process | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | Coagulation | Quiche, baked custard |
| Binding | Coagulation holds the ingredients together | Burgers, fishcakes, falafel |
| Coating | Coagulation sets a layer that holds crumbs on | Breaded fish, chicken goujons |
| Thickening | Coagulation traps liquid | Stirred custard, lemon curd |
| Aerating | Foam formation | Meringue, whisked sponge, roulade, mousse |
| Glazing | Coagulation and browning on the surface | Egg-washed pastry and bread |
| Emulsifying | Lecithin in the yolk (see 3.3.2.3) | Mayonnaise, hollandaise |

::: terms
- **Amino acids**: the small units that join in long chains to make protein molecules.
- **Denaturation**: the unfolding of a protein's structure when the bonds holding its shape are broken by heat, acid, salt or mechanical action.
- **Coagulation**: the setting of denatured proteins as they join together into a solid network, usually by heat; it cannot be reversed.
- **Syneresis**: liquid being squeezed out of an over-coagulated protein network, as in watery scrambled egg or a weeping custard.
- **Gluten**: a stretchy protein network formed when glutenin and gliadin in wheat flour are mixed with water and kneaded.
- **Glutenin**: the wheat protein that gives gluten its elasticity, so dough springs back.
- **Gliadin**: the wheat protein that gives gluten its extensibility, so dough stretches without tearing.
- **Strong flour**: wheat flour with a high protein content that forms plenty of gluten, used for bread and pasta.
- **Foam**: a gas dispersed in a liquid, such as air bubbles held in whisked egg white.
- **Marinade**: a mixture containing an acid, oil and flavourings that denatures surface protein to tenderise and flavour food.
- **Bain-marie**: a tin or pan of water that surrounds a dish so egg mixtures cook gently and do not over-coagulate.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the process precisely**: denaturation (unfolding), coagulation (setting), gluten formation, foam formation. Don't use "denature" when you mean "set".
2. **Say what causes it** in this food: heat, acid, whisking, kneading, water.
3. **Describe what happens to the protein**: bonds break → chain unfolds → chains join/network forms → traps air, water or gas.
4. **Link to the result in the dish**: sets the quiche, traps CO₂ so the bread rises, holds air so the meringue is light.
5. **For faults**, give the cause and the remedy as a pair: "overheated → proteins over-coagulate and squeeze out water → cook at a lower heat in a bain-marie".
6. **For extended answers**, work through several dishes, give the protein science for each, then evaluate how temperature, time and technique change the quality.
:::

::: example Explain — scrambled egg
Explain what happens to the egg protein when scrambled eggs are cooked. (3)

The heat breaks the bonds holding the egg proteins in shape, so they unfold (denature) (1). The unfolded proteins then join together and set into a solid network, which is coagulation (1). If the eggs are heated too long or too fiercely, the network tightens and squeezes out water (syneresis), so the eggs become rubbery and watery (1).
:::

::: example Explain — gluten formation
Explain how gluten is formed when making fresh pasta dough. (4)

Strong or '00' wheat flour contains the proteins glutenin and gliadin (1). When the liquid (egg) is mixed in, these proteins absorb water and combine to form gluten (1). Kneading, and then passing the dough through a pasta machine, stretches and lines up the gluten into a strong network (1). Glutenin makes it elastic and gliadin makes it stretchy, so the dough can be rolled into thin, silky sheets that hold together when boiled (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a foam
Explain how whisking egg whites produces a stable foam for meringue. (4)

Whisking is a mechanical action that denatures the egg-white proteins, unfolding them (1). The unfolded proteins form a film around the air bubbles being beaten in, trapping them (1). Sugar added gradually dissolves into the film and stabilises the foam so it becomes stiff and glossy (1). In the oven, the heat coagulates the protein, setting the foam permanently into a crisp meringue (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a marinade
Explain how a marinade of lemon juice and olive oil tenderises chicken breast. (3)

The acid in the lemon juice lowers the pH at the surface of the chicken (1). This denatures the protein, unfolding the chains so the meat is softer and holds more moisture (1). The oil coats the chicken and adds moisture so it does not dry out during cooking (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Denaturation is unfolding; coagulation is setting.** Swapping them is the most common error.
- Gluten is **formed**, not "in the flour": glutenin + gliadin + **water**, then **kneading**.
- Give the **job** of each gluten protein: glutenin = elastic, gliadin = extensible.
- For foams, say what the protein does: **forms a film around the air bubbles**. "Traps air" alone is thin.
- Faults need cause **and** science: "overheated, so the protein over-coagulated and squeezed out water".
- Don't write that heat "kills" protein. It denatures and coagulates it.
:::
