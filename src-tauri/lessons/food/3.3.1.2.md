## What it is

Choosing a cooking method is a decision with consequences: it changes the **nutritional value** of the food (vitamins kept or lost, fat added or removed) and its **palatability** (colour, flavour, texture, smell). The exam wants you to **select** a method for a food and **justify** it.

### The three groups of methods

**Water-based methods** (moist heat; the food never gets hotter than about 100 °C, so it does not brown):

| Method | What happens | Suits | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Boiling** | Food cooked in water at 100 °C, with large rolling bubbles | Pasta, rice, potatoes, eggs | No fat added; simple; kills bacteria | Water-soluble vitamins (C and B group) **leach** into the water; vegetables go soggy and dull if overcooked |
| **Simmering** | Liquid kept just below boiling, with small gentle bubbles | Soups, stews, sauces, rice | Food keeps its shape; less evaporation | Long times destroy heat-sensitive vitamins |
| **Steaming** | Food sits **above** boiling water and is cooked by the steam | Green vegetables, fish, dumplings, sponge puddings | Food is not in the water, so fewer vitamins leach out; no fat; keeps colour and shape | Slower; can taste bland; no browning or crisp texture |
| **Blanching** | Brief boiling (often 1–3 minutes), then plunging into iced water | Vegetables before freezing; tomatoes before peeling | Denatures enzymes that would spoil frozen vegetables; sets bright colour; loosens skins | Some vitamin C leaches out; not a full cooking method |
| **Poaching** | Food cooked gently in liquid **below** simmering | Eggs, fish fillets, chicken breast, pears | Delicate foods keep their shape; no fat; liquid can flavour the food | Little flavour development; no colour |
| **Braising** | Food browned first, then cooked slowly in a **small** amount of liquid in a covered dish, on the hob or in the oven | Tough cuts of meat, red cabbage, fennel | Tenderises tough cuts; nutrients stay in the liquid, which is eaten as the sauce; cheap cuts become rich and tasty | Takes hours; uses energy |

**Casseroles and tagines** are close relatives of braising: long, slow, moist cooking in a covered pot in the oven. A tagine's tall cone-shaped lid collects steam, which condenses and runs back into the food, keeping it moist.

**Dry methods** (dry heat can go well above 100 °C, so food browns and crisps):

| Method | What happens | Suits | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Baking** | Food cooked by dry heat in the oven | Cakes, bread, pastry, jacket potatoes, fish in a parcel | Little or no fat needed; even cooking; batch cooking | Uses a lot of energy for one item; can dry food out |
| **Roasting** | Food cooked in the oven with a little fat, often basted | Joints of meat, potatoes, root vegetables | Browns and crisps the outside; strong flavour | Adds fat unless food is on a rack; long time |
| **Grilling** | Food cooked by **radiant** heat under or over a grill | Sausages, thin steaks, fish, halloumi, kebabs, toast | Fat drips out of the food; quick; chars and browns | Only suits thin, tender foods; burns easily; needs turning |
| **Dry frying** | Food cooked in a hot pan with **no added fat** | Mince, bacon, spices, seeds, pancakes in a non-stick pan | Fat in the food melts out and can be drained away | Food can stick or burn; needs a non-stick pan |

**Fat-based methods** (hot fat reaches much higher temperatures than water, so food cooks fast and becomes crisp and golden):

| Method | What happens | Suits | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Shallow frying** | Food cooked in a thin layer of hot fat in a frying pan and turned once | Eggs, fishcakes, fish fillets, pancakes | Quick; crisp golden outside; tasty | Food absorbs fat, raising energy; fat spits (burns) |
| **Stir frying** | Small, evenly cut pieces tossed constantly in a very hot wok with a little oil | Strips of meat, tofu, vegetables, noodles | Very fast, so vegetables keep colour, crunch and vitamins; little oil | All preparation must be done first; pieces must be the same size |

The **microwave oven** is quick and uses very little water, so it keeps water-soluble vitamins well (good for vegetables and reheating), but it does not brown food and heats unevenly.

### Conserving (or modifying) nutritive value

- **Water-soluble vitamins (C and the B group)** are the ones most easily lost. They **dissolve** into cooking water, are **destroyed by heat**, and are lost the **longer** food is cooked or kept hot. To keep them: use as little water as possible, put vegetables into water that is already boiling, put a lid on, cook for a short time, serve at once, and use the cooking water for gravy or soup. Steaming, microwaving and stir frying are the best choices.
- **Fat**: grilling, dry frying, baking on a rack, steaming and poaching add little or no fat; grilling and dry frying also **remove** fat from the food. Frying and roasting add fat and energy.
- **Protein**: heat sets it; overcooking makes meat and fish tough and dry.
- **Starch**: cooking softens it and makes it easier to digest.

### Choosing the method for the food

The key example is **meat**. Muscle fibres are held together by **connective tissue**, made mainly of the protein **collagen**.
- **Tough cuts** (shin, brisket, stewing steak, lamb shoulder) come from hard-working muscles and have a lot of connective tissue. They need **long, slow, moist** cooking — braising, stewing, casseroling — so the collagen turns into **gelatine**, which is soft and gives a rich, glossy sauce.
- **Tender cuts** (fillet, sirloin, chicken breast) have little connective tissue. They suit **quick dry heat** — grilling, frying, roasting. Long cooking just squeezes water out of the muscle proteins and makes them tough and dry.

Similarly, delicate fish suits poaching or steaming; potatoes can be boiled for mash or roasted for a crisp outside; green vegetables suit steaming or stir frying; boiling vegetables for longer is a way to **change texture**, e.g. to make a soft purée for a young child.

### Improving the sensory qualities

- **Browning**: dry heat and fat-based methods brown the surface of meat, bread and vegetables, producing savoury, toasted flavours and an attractive colour. Sear meat before braising for this reason.
- **Glazing**: brushing with beaten egg, milk, a sugar syrup, honey or a reduced sauce before or after cooking gives a shiny, golden finish (bread rolls, pastry, honey-glazed carrots, sticky ribs).
- **Seasoning**: salt, pepper, herbs, spices, citrus and garlic added during cooking; **taste and adjust** as you go.
- **Garnishing and decorating**: fresh herbs, lemon wedges, toasted seeds, a swirl of yoghurt, piped cream or dusted icing sugar add colour, texture contrast and visual appeal.

### Marinades

A **marinade** is a flavoured liquid that raw meat, fish, tofu or vegetables are soaked in before cooking. It usually contains:
- an **acid** (lemon or lime juice, vinegar, yoghurt, wine), which **denatures** the protein on the surface — the protein chains unfold and loosen, so the food becomes more **tender**;
- **oil**, which adds **moisture** and stops the food drying out when grilled;
- **flavourings** (garlic, ginger, herbs, spices, soy sauce, honey), which soak into the surface.

Some fruits — pineapple, papaya and kiwi — contain **enzymes** that break down protein and tenderise too. Delicate fish should only be marinated for a short time, or the acid makes it mushy. For safety, marinate raw meat **covered in the fridge**, and never reuse the marinade on cooked food unless it has been boiled.

::: terms
- **Water-based methods**: cooking in or over water or stock — boiling, simmering, steaming, blanching, poaching and braising.
- **Dry methods**: cooking by dry heat without liquid — baking, roasting, grilling and dry frying.
- **Fat-based methods**: cooking in hot fat or oil, such as shallow frying and stir frying.
- **Leaching**: the loss of water-soluble vitamins and minerals out of food into the cooking water.
- **Blanching**: briefly boiling food and then cooling it rapidly in iced water, often to denature enzymes before freezing.
- **Braising**: browning food and then cooking it slowly in a small amount of liquid in a covered dish.
- **Poaching**: cooking food gently in liquid kept below simmering point, so delicate foods keep their shape.
- **Collagen**: the protein in connective tissue that holds muscle fibres together; slow moist heat converts it to gelatine.
- **Marinade**: a mixture of acid, oil and flavourings used to tenderise and flavour food before cooking.
- **Glazing**: brushing food with egg, milk, syrup or a sauce to give a shiny, golden finish.
- **Palatability**: how pleasant food is to eat overall, judged by its appearance, flavour, texture and smell.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the food and the goal** in the question: keep vitamins, cut fat, tenderise a tough cut, add colour, save time.
2. **Choose the method from the right group** (water-based, dry or fat-based) and name it precisely: "steaming", not "cooking it healthily".
3. **Justify with a linked reason**: method → what happens → result. "Steaming, because the broccoli is not in the water, so less vitamin C leaches out."
4. **Give a disadvantage or limit** where the question asks for one: no browning, extra fat, long cooking time.
5. **For sensory questions**, treat colour, flavour and texture separately and give a specific technique for each (glaze, sear, toast seeds, garnish).
6. **For 8-mark questions**, analyse the effect of several methods on both nutrients and sensory qualities, then weigh them and reach a justified choice.
:::

::: example Explain — a healthy method
Explain why steaming is a healthy way to cook green beans. (2)

The beans sit above the water rather than in it, so fewer water-soluble vitamins such as vitamin C leach out (1). No fat is added, so the beans stay low in fat and energy (1).
:::

::: example Advantages and disadvantages — grilling
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of grilling sausages. (2)

Advantage: fat melts and drips out of the sausages, so they are lower in fat than fried sausages (1). Disadvantage: radiant heat only reaches the side facing the grill, so they must be turned often or they burn outside while the centre is undercooked (1).
:::

::: example Explain — choosing a method for meat
Explain why braising is a suitable method for cooking beef brisket. (4)

Brisket is a tough cut with a lot of connective tissue (1). Braising cooks it slowly in a small amount of liquid at a gentle heat (1), which converts the collagen in the connective tissue into gelatine (1). This makes the meat tender, and the gelatine and meat juices enrich the sauce, so the flavour and nutrients are eaten rather than lost (1).
:::

::: example Explain — a marinade
Explain how a marinade of lime juice, oil and garlic improves chicken strips for fajitas. (4)

The acid in the lime juice denatures the protein on the surface of the chicken (1), unfolding the protein chains so the meat is more tender (1). The oil adds moisture, so the chicken does not dry out in the hot pan (1). The garlic and lime add flavour that soaks into the surface of the meat (1).
:::

::: example Explain how to improve sensory qualities
A student's baked salmon fillet with boiled new potatoes and peas was described as "pale and bland". Explain how the colour, flavour and texture of the meal could be improved. (6)

Colour: brush the salmon with a honey and soy glaze and finish it under the grill so the top browns and becomes glossy (1); garnish with chopped parsley and a lemon wedge for contrasting green and yellow (1). Flavour: season the salmon with black pepper, dill or a herb crust, and add a squeeze of lemon, which lifts the taste (1); toss the potatoes in a little butter, mint and sea salt (1). Texture: roast the potatoes instead of boiling so the outsides are crisp against the soft salmon (1); add toasted flaked almonds or a crunchy herb-and-breadcrumb topping to the fish for contrast (1).
:::

::: q
Name two water-based methods of cooking. (2)
---
Any two: boiling, simmering, steaming, blanching, poaching, braising.
:::

::: q
Explain why vegetables are blanched before they are frozen. (2)
---
Blanching denatures the enzymes in the vegetables (1), which would otherwise continue to act in the freezer and spoil their colour, flavour and texture (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why stir frying is a good way to keep the colour and vitamins of vegetables. (3)
---
Cooking is very fast (1), so less vitamin C is destroyed by heat (1); no water is used, so no vitamins leach out, and the short time keeps the bright colour and crunch (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a tender sirloin steak is not usually stewed. (2)
---
It has little connective tissue, so it does not need slow cooking to tenderise it (1); long cooking would squeeze out moisture and make it tough and dry, and waste an expensive cut (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways to reduce the loss of vitamin C when boiling cabbage. (2)
---
Any two: use the minimum water; add to water that is already boiling; cook for the shortest time; cover with a lid; shred just before cooking; serve immediately; use the cooking water in gravy.
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of roasting potatoes compared with boiling them. (4)
---
Advantage: roasting browns and crisps the outside (1), giving a more appealing flavour, colour and texture (1). Disadvantage: they absorb fat during roasting (1), so they are higher in fat and energy than boiled potatoes (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why poaching is suitable for cooking eggs. (2)
---
The water is kept below simmering, so the gentle heat sets the egg without breaking it up (1); no fat is added, so the egg stays lower in fat than a fried egg (1).
:::

::: watch
- Always **justify**: name the method, then what it does, then the result for the food.
- "Healthy" is not a reason. Say **which** nutrient is kept (vitamin C, B group) or **what** is reduced (fat, energy).
- Know which methods **brown** (dry and fat-based) and which **don't** (water-based, microwave).
- Tough cut → **slow moist heat** (collagen to gelatine). Tender cut → **quick dry heat**. Mixing these up is common.
- In a marinade, the **acid** denatures protein, the **oil** adds moisture, the **flavourings** add taste. Give each its job.
- In sensory questions, deal with colour, flavour and texture **separately**, with a specific technique for each.
:::

---

::: test
Explain why green vegetables turn a dull olive-green if they are boiled for too long. (2)
---
Long heating changes the green pigment (chlorophyll) (1), especially as acids released from the vegetable build up in the water, so the bright green colour turns dull and less appetising (1).

::: test
A family eats vegetables with every evening meal, but the children say the vegetables are "soggy and boring".
- Analyse how different cooking methods affect the nutritional value and sensory qualities of vegetables.
- Evaluate which methods the family should use. (8)
---
Levels: 7–8 — detailed analysis of several methods covering both nutrients and sensory qualities, with a well-justified judgement for this family; 5–6 — clear analysis of some methods with some evaluation; 3–4 — some relevant points, mainly descriptive; 1–2 — simple statements with little explanation.
Indicative content — analysis: boiling for a long time in lots of water leaches vitamin C and B vitamins and destroys them with heat; it softens cell walls so vegetables go soggy and colour dulls; steaming keeps more vitamins and colour but gives little flavour; microwaving with a little water is fast and keeps vitamins; stir frying keeps crunch, colour and vitamins and adds flavour from garlic or soy but adds some oil; roasting caramelises natural sugars, giving sweetness and crisp edges that children often prefer, but adds fat and loses some vitamin C through long heat; grilling or charring adds smoky flavour with little fat.
Evaluation: the best choice depends on the vegetable — stir fry or steam green vegetables to keep colour and crunch; roast root vegetables with a little oil for sweetness; the family should cut just before cooking, cook for a short time and serve at once; adding herbs, lemon or a sprinkle of cheese can increase appeal; a justified conclusion, e.g. a mix of stir frying and roasting keeps children eating vegetables, which matters more than a small vitamin loss.
:::
