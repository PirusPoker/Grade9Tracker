## What it is

**Minerals** are **micronutrients**: inorganic elements the body cannot make, needed in small amounts to build body structures (bones, teeth, blood) and to control processes (nerves, muscles, fluid balance, hormones). The spec names six.

**Calcium**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | builds and maintains strong **bones and teeth** (with phosphorus and vitamin D); needed for **blood clotting**; for **nerve impulses** and **muscle contraction**, including the heartbeat |
| **Sources** | **milk, cheese, yoghurt**; fortified plant milks; tofu made with a calcium setting agent; **canned fish eaten with the bones** (sardines, salmon); **white and brown bread** (calcium is added to non-wholemeal flour in the UK); green leafy vegetables such as kale; almonds, sesame seeds |
| **Deficiency** | children: poor bone growth and **rickets** (with low vitamin D). Adults: soft bones (osteomalacia) and, over years, **osteoporosis**: bones lose density, become porous and fracture easily |
| **Excess** | rare from food; high supplement doses may cause kidney stones |
| **Reference value** | about **700 mg a day for adults**; more for teenagers (about 1,000 mg for boys and 800 mg for girls aged 11–18) because bones are growing fast |

**Peak bone mass.** Bones keep getting denser until the body reaches **peak bone mass**, at around **30 years old**. After that bone is slowly lost, and faster in women after the menopause. The more bone built by 30, the longer it takes to reach a fragile level, so plenty of calcium, vitamin D and **weight-bearing exercise** in childhood and the teens protects against osteoporosis later.

**Iron**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | makes **haemoglobin** in red blood cells, which carries **oxygen** around the body; helps release energy |
| **Sources** | **haem iron** (easily absorbed), from animal foods: **red meat**, liver, dark poultry meat, oily fish such as sardines. **Non-haem iron** (less easily absorbed), from plants and fortified foods: **lentils, beans, chickpeas**, tofu, **dark green leafy vegetables** such as kale, **fortified breakfast cereals**, fortified white bread, dried apricots, nuts, seeds, egg yolk |
| **Absorption** | **vitamin C** eaten at the same meal helps the body absorb non-haem iron (e.g. orange with fortified cereal, peppers in a lentil chilli). **Tannins in tea** and coffee, and phytates in bran, reduce absorption, so avoid tea with meals |
| **Deficiency** | **iron-deficiency anaemia**: fewer or smaller red cells carry less oxygen, so **tiredness, lack of energy, pale skin, breathlessness**, poor concentration and brittle nails |
| **Excess** | from supplements: constipation and stomach pain; iron tablets can poison young children |
| **Reference value** | about **8.7 mg a day for men** and **14.8 mg for women and teenage girls**, because of **blood lost in menstruation** |

Groups at risk of low iron: **teenage girls** and women with heavy periods; toddlers moving off milk; pregnant women; vegetarians and vegans, who rely on non-haem iron and must plan to combine it with vitamin C.

**Sodium (salt)**

Salt is sodium chloride. Sodium is the part that matters for health: roughly, salt = sodium × 2.5.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | controls the **water balance** in the body and body fluids; needed for **nerve impulses** and **muscle contraction** |
| **Sources** | table salt; **hidden salt in processed foods**, which gives most of the salt people eat: bread, cheese, **bacon, ham and sausages**, crisps, stock cubes, **soy sauce**, canned soups, ready meals, takeaways, pizza |
| **Deficiency** | rare; after heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhoea: **muscle cramps**, dizziness, tiredness |
| **Excess** | **raises blood pressure**, which strains the heart and blood vessels and increases the risk of **stroke, CHD** and kidney disease |
| **Reference value** | adults **no more than 6 g of salt a day** (about a level teaspoon); children less (for example 2 g at 1–3 years, 5 g at 7–10); babies should have no added salt as their kidneys cannot cope |

**Reducing salt in cooking**
- Taste before seasoning, and add salt at the table only if needed.
- Flavour with **herbs, spices, garlic, black pepper, lemon or lime juice, chilli, ginger or vinegar** instead.
- Use low-salt stock cubes or homemade stock; cook from scratch rather than using ready-made sauces.
- Use fewer salty ingredients (bacon, cheese, soy sauce) or a stronger version so less is needed.
- Buy tinned vegetables and pulses in water, not brine; check labels (more than 1.5 g salt per 100 g is high).
- Reduce gradually, as the taste buds adapt within a few weeks.

**Fluoride**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | strengthens **tooth enamel**, making it more resistant to the acid that causes **tooth decay**; helps bones |
| **Sources** | **fluoride toothpaste**; tap water where it is naturally present or added; tea; sea fish |
| **Deficiency** | weaker enamel and more tooth decay |
| **Excess** | **dental fluorosis**: white or brown mottling of the teeth while they are forming in childhood |

**Iodine**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | makes the **thyroid hormones**, which control the **metabolic rate** (how fast the body uses energy); in pregnancy, needed for the baby's brain development |
| **Sources** | **milk and dairy foods**, **sea fish** and shellfish, eggs; seaweed (amounts vary widely) |
| **Deficiency** | **goitre** (a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck); tiredness, weight gain; in pregnancy, can harm the baby's development |
| **Excess** | can also upset thyroid function |
| **Reference value** | about **140 µg a day for adults**; people avoiding dairy and fish, such as vegans, may need a fortified milk alternative or other source |

**Phosphorus**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functions** | works with **calcium** to form strong **bones and teeth**; part of every cell; needed to **release energy** from food |
| **Sources** | found in most protein foods: **milk, cheese, meat, fish, eggs**, nuts, pulses and wholegrain cereals |
| **Deficiency** | very rare, because it is in so many foods; can cause weak bones |
| **Excess** | a very high intake, for example from many processed foods with phosphate additives, may upset the balance with calcium |

**In the kitchen**

- **Iron-rich foods (S2).** Trim and dice lean beef or lamb for a stew on a red board with the claw grip; wash and shred kale or spinach; pick over and rinse lentils. Add peppers or tomatoes (vitamin C) to lentil or bean dishes to help non-haem iron absorption.
- **A calcium-rich white sauce (S8).** A white sauce made with milk (roux or all-in-one) adds calcium to dishes such as a fish pie or cauliflower cheese. Semi-skimmed milk contains just as much calcium as whole milk, with less saturated fat. A little strong cheese stirred in adds flavour and more calcium.
- **Tasting and seasoning.** Taste a sauce or soup at the end of cooking and season with herbs and pepper first, only adding salt if needed.

::: terms
- **Mineral**: an inorganic element needed in small amounts to build body structures and control body processes.
- **Haemoglobin**: the iron-containing protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen round the body.
- **Haem iron**: iron from animal foods such as red meat, which the body absorbs easily.
- **Non-haem iron**: iron from plant foods and fortified foods, which is less easily absorbed without vitamin C.
- **Iron-deficiency anaemia**: a condition in which too little haemoglobin is made, causing tiredness, pale skin and breathlessness.
- **Peak bone mass**: the greatest density and strength bones reach, at about 30 years old.
- **Osteoporosis**: a condition in which bones lose density, become porous and fracture easily, usually in later life.
- **Hidden salt**: salt already in processed foods such as bread, bacon, cheese and ready meals, which makes up most of the salt eaten.
- **Goitre**: a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck, caused by a lack of iodine.
- **Dental fluorosis**: white or brown mottling of the teeth caused by too much fluoride while teeth are forming.
:::

::: method
1. **Keep the six minerals' jobs apart:** calcium = bones, teeth, clotting, muscles; iron = haemoglobin, oxygen; sodium = fluid balance, nerves; fluoride = enamel; iodine = thyroid hormones; phosphorus = bones and teeth with calcium, energy.
2. **Always name the helper vitamin:** vitamin D for calcium absorption; vitamin C for non-haem iron absorption.
3. **For "why does this group need…", give the group's reason:** teenage girls → menstruation and growth for iron; teenagers → peak bone mass for calcium.
4. **For salt questions, give a named swap and its effect:** "use garlic and herbs to flavour instead of salt, so less sodium is eaten and blood pressure is lower".
5. **For deficiency effects, give the symptom, not a vague "illness":** anaemia means tiredness and pale skin, not weak bones.
6. **For diaries, pick out the named foods,** say which mineral each gives too much or too little of, link to the person's condition, then evaluate specific changes.
:::

::: example Explain — iron and teenage girls
Explain why teenage girls need a good supply of iron. (3)

Iron is needed to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in red blood cells (1). Teenage girls lose iron in blood during menstruation each month (1), and they are also growing quickly with an increasing blood volume, so their needs are high and they are at risk of iron-deficiency anaemia, causing tiredness and pale skin (1).
:::

::: example Give — reducing salt
Give three ways to reduce the salt in a homemade vegetable and lentil soup. (3)

Flavour it with herbs, garlic, black pepper, cumin or lemon juice instead of salt (1). Use a low-salt stock cube or homemade vegetable stock (1). Taste at the end and only add salt if really needed, rather than adding it at the start (1).
:::

::: example Explain — vitamin C and iron
A vegetarian eats a lentil and spinach curry with a cup of tea. Explain how the meal could be changed to help her absorb more iron. (4)

The iron in lentils and spinach is non-haem iron, which is poorly absorbed (1). Adding a vitamin C source, such as tomatoes and red peppers in the curry or a glass of orange juice or fresh fruit with the meal, helps the body absorb non-haem iron (1). The tannins in tea reduce iron absorption (1), so she should drink water or juice with the meal and have tea at least an hour afterwards (1).
:::

::: example Explain — calcium and bones
Explain why a 16-year-old should eat enough calcium and do regular weight-bearing exercise. (4)

Calcium is needed to build bone density during the teenage growth spurt (1). Bones keep strengthening until peak bone mass is reached at about 30 (1), so the more calcium stored now, the stronger the bones will be later (1). Weight-bearing exercise such as running or dancing also increases bone density; together they reduce the risk of osteoporosis and fractures in later life (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Anaemia is iron, not calcium.** It causes tiredness and pale skin, not weak bones.
- Give the **absorption partner**: calcium needs **vitamin D**, non-haem iron needs **vitamin C**; tea's tannins block iron.
- Salt answers: say **blood pressure**, and give a **named herb, spice or swap**, not "use less salt".
- Teenage girls need iron because of **menstruation**; teenagers need calcium for **peak bone mass**. The reason for the group earns the second mark.
- **Most salt is hidden** in processed food: the salt shaker is only part of the story.
:::

::: test
Jo is 58 and works as a taxi driver, often on night shifts. Her doctor has told her that her blood pressure is high, and she has recently gone through the menopause. This is a typical day's food.
- **On waking (2 pm):** two cups of tea; a bacon sandwich on white bread with brown sauce.
- **Before her shift:** instant noodles with the flavour sachet; a packet of salted crisps.
- **During her shift:** a garage cheese and pickle sandwich; a large coffee; a chocolate bar.
- **After her shift (3 am):** a microwave chicken tikka masala ready meal with pilau rice; a can of lemonade.

- Analyse the suitability of Jo's diet for her needs.
- Evaluate changes Jo could make to her diet to improve her health.

(12)
---
Levels of response (12):
- **Level 4 (10–12):** a detailed, balanced analysis that refers to specific foods in the diary and links them to Jo's high blood pressure, her post-menopausal bone health and her shift pattern; the evaluation gives specific, realistic changes that suit a night-shift worker, weighs them, and reaches a clear judgement. Terms such as sodium, hidden salt, calcium, peak bone mass and osteoporosis are used accurately.
- **Level 3 (7–9):** a good analysis of several foods with links to her conditions, and relevant improvements that are mostly justified; some imbalance between analysis and evaluation.
- **Level 2 (4–6):** some analysis with limited links to Jo's needs; improvements are generic ("eat less salt", "eat healthily").
- **Level 1 (1–3):** a few basic points, often a list of unhealthy foods.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Salt is very high: bacon, brown sauce, white bread, noodle flavour sachet, salted crisps, garage sandwich with cheese and pickle, ready meal. Excess sodium raises blood pressure, increasing her risk of stroke and CHD.
- After the menopause bone is lost faster, so she needs calcium and vitamin D to slow osteoporosis; the only calcium comes from milk in drinks, cheese and fortified white bread. Night shifts and sleeping in the day mean little sunlight, so vitamin D is likely low.
- Almost no fruit or vegetables: low potassium, vitamin C and fibre; potassium helps balance the effect of sodium on blood pressure.
- Saturated fat from bacon, cheese, crisps, ready meal cream sauce and chocolate; free sugars from lemonade, brown sauce and chocolate; energy-dense food with little activity (sitting all shift) risks weight gain, which also raises blood pressure.
- Caffeine from tea and coffee; little water; the tea with her meal reduces iron absorption.
- Her irregular eating times and reliance on convenience food from garages drive the high salt intake.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Batch cook meals from scratch on days off using herbs and spices instead of salt, then chill or freeze portions to reheat on shift; this cuts hidden salt but needs time and planning.
- Swap the bacon sandwich for porridge made with milk and fruit, or eggs on wholemeal toast; adds calcium and fibre.
- Take a packed meal and snacks in a cool bag: wholemeal sandwiches with lean chicken or tuna and salad, reduced-fat cheese, yoghurt, fruit, unsalted nuts; cheaper than garage food.
- Replace noodles and crisps with a homemade vegetable soup or plain popcorn; if she keeps instant noodles, use half the flavour sachet.
- Drink water or semi-skimmed milk instead of lemonade; cut caffeine near the end of her shift.
- Calcium from milk, yoghurt, cheese (in moderation) and fortified plant milks; oily fish such as sardines; a 10 µg vitamin D supplement, as she gets little daylight.
- Choose lower-salt ready meals by reading labels (green or amber for salt) when she must buy convenience food.
- Judgement: the most effective single change is replacing processed and garage food with prepared meals, as that is where most of her salt comes from; this also improves calcium and fibre. Changes must fit her night shifts, so planning and packed food are more realistic than cooking at 3 am.
