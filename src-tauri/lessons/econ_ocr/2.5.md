## What it is

In a market economy, firms selling similar products **compete** for the same customers. This topic covers **why and how producers compete**, how **competition affects price**, the **economic impact of competition on producers and consumers** (which the spec asks you to evaluate), and the meaning of **monopoly** and **oligopoly** and how they differ from **competitive markets**.

### Why producers compete

**Competition** is rivalry between producers to win customers. OCR mark schemes want a full reason, not a single word: "profit" scores nothing, "**to make a profit**" scores. Producers compete:

- **to make a profit** (or a bigger profit), the reward for enterprise;
- **to increase sales and revenue**, winning customers from rivals;
- **to gain market share**, a larger percentage of total sales in the market;
- **to enter a market and survive**, because customers who switch to rivals may leave a firm unable to cover its costs;
- **to build a reputation and customer loyalty**, so customers come back without being persuaded each time;
- **to grow** and gain economies of scale (2.6b), lowering average costs.

### How producers compete

| Method | Example (invented firms) |
|---|---|
| **Price competition**: charging less than rivals, discounts, offers | Brightway gym cuts its monthly fee below the gym across the road |
| **Quality**: better ingredients, materials, reliability | a bakery uses organic flour and bakes fresh every morning |
| **Innovation**: new products and features | a phone maker adds a longer-lasting battery |
| **Branding and advertising**: making the product seem different | a trainer brand pays athletes to wear its shoes |
| **Customer service**: free delivery, easy returns, guarantees | an online shop offers next-day delivery and free returns |
| **Location and convenience**: being close to customers, long opening hours | a corner shop open until midnight |

Anything other than price is **non-price competition**. It is common when firms want to avoid a **price war**, in which each firm keeps cutting prices until none makes a profit.

### How competition affects price

When more firms compete in a market:

1. **Market supply rises.** New firms entering add to the quantity offered at every price, so the market supply curve shifts right.
2. **Consumers have more substitutes.** Demand for any single firm's product becomes **more price elastic**: if one firm raises its price, customers switch to a rival.
3. **Prices are pushed down towards costs.** A firm charging more than its rivals loses sales, so prices fall until firms make only enough profit to stay in the market.
4. **Firms cut costs to survive.** To keep making a profit at lower prices, firms look for cheaper suppliers, better technology and less waste.

### The economic impact of competition

| | **Benefits** | **Costs / drawbacks** |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | lower prices; more **choice**; better **quality** and service; more **innovation**; **consumer sovereignty**, as firms must provide what consumers want or lose them | heavy **advertising** may persuade people to buy what they do not need; firms cutting costs may cut corners on **quality or safety**; too much choice can be confusing; if firms fail, after-sales service and guarantees may be lost |
| **Producers** | pressure to be **efficient** and cut waste; the best firms grow and win market share; incentive to innovate | **lower prices and profits**; less money to invest; small firms may be too small to gain economies of scale; weaker firms close, causing **job losses**; heavy spending on advertising raises costs |

**Evaluation points**: how many firms there really are and how close the substitutes are; whether competition is on **price** (consumers gain directly) or mostly **advertising** (costs may rise); whether lower prices come from efficiency (good) or from cutting wages and safety (bad); the **time period** (a price war can drive out rivals and leave one dominant firm that then raises prices); and **who** the question names. In a "for consumers" question, an answer about the firm's profit scores nothing.

### Monopoly, oligopoly and competitive markets

A **monopoly** is a market with a **single seller**. A **pure monopoly** has 100% of the market, with no close substitutes. In practice, UK competition rules have traditionally treated a firm with **25% or more** of a market as having monopoly power. A monopolist is a **price maker**: it can raise its price without losing all its customers, because they have nowhere else to go. High **barriers to entry** keep rivals out, for example the huge cost of setting up, control of a key resource, patents, or legal rights.

An **oligopoly** is a market **dominated by a few large firms**, for example a market where four firms make most of the sales. Each firm is **interdependent**: it must think about how rivals will react before changing its price. If one cuts its price, the others may follow, so oligopolists often prefer **non-price competition** (branding, advertising, loyalty schemes). They may be tempted to **collude**, agreeing not to compete. A formal agreement is a **cartel**; OPEC, the group of oil-exporting countries that agrees oil output, is a well-known example between countries. Collusion between firms to fix prices is illegal in the UK.

A **competitive market** has **many small firms**, none big enough to control the price (they are **price takers**), with **low barriers to entry**, similar products and plenty of information for consumers.

| Feature | Competitive market | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Number and size of firms** | **many small firms** | **a few large firms** dominate | **one firm** dominates |
| **Barriers to entry** | low | high | very high |
| **Control over price** | none: price takers | some, but must watch rivals | a lot: price maker |
| **Type of competition** | mainly price | mainly non-price; risk of collusion | little or none |
| **Typical profits** | low, normal | can be high | can be high for a long time |
| **Choice for consumers** | wide | limited | very limited |

The 2024 examiners noted that "large firms versus smaller firms" earned only one mark; write "**a few** large firms" against "**many** small firms".

**Competition authorities.** The government can investigate firms with too much market power and **block mergers** that would reduce competition; in the UK this is the job of the **Competition and Markets Authority (CMA)**. This protects consumers from higher prices, though large firms argue that their size brings economies of scale and money for research.

::: terms
- **Competition**: rivalry between producers selling similar products to win customers and sales.
- **Market share**: the percentage of total sales in a market made by one firm.
- **Price competition**: competing by charging lower prices than rivals or offering discounts.
- **Non-price competition**: competing through quality, branding, advertising, innovation, service or location rather than price.
- **Competitive market**: a market with many small firms, low barriers to entry and no firm able to control the price.
- **Monopoly**: a market dominated by a single seller; in UK rules, often a firm with 25% or more of the market.
- **Oligopoly**: a market dominated by a few large firms that are interdependent.
- **Barriers to entry**: obstacles that make it hard for new firms to enter a market, such as high set-up costs or patents.
- **Collusion**: firms agreeing to limit competition, for example by fixing prices; a formal agreement is a cartel.
- **Price maker**: a firm with enough market power to set its own price.
- **Price taker**: a firm too small to influence the market price, so it must accept it.
- **Consumer sovereignty**: the idea that consumers' spending decides what is produced in a competitive market.
:::

::: method
1. **"State two reasons why producers compete" (2)**: give full phrases, "to make a profit", "to increase market share". One-word answers score nothing.
2. **"Explain how an oligopoly differs from a competitive market" (2)**: compare **both** sides on one feature: "an oligopoly has **a few large** firms (1), whereas a competitive market has **many small** firms (1)", or barriers to entry, or control over price.
3. **"Explain one…" (2)**: point (1) + development applied to the product or firm in the question (1).
4. **"Using a diagram, analyse how competition/entry affects price" (6)**: market supply shifts right S to S1; surplus at old price; price falls P to P1, quantity rises Q to Q1; then the effect on the group named. No evaluation.
5. **Evaluate competition for consumers or producers (6)**: two or three benefits analysed, each paired with "however, this depends on…"; stay with the group named; finish with a judgement on "the extent to which".
:::

::: example State two reasons why producers compete
Invented context: Pedalwise is a new bicycle repair shop in a town that already has two others. State two reasons why Pedalwise will compete with the existing shops. (2)

To win customers and increase its sales (1). To make a profit / to survive in the market (1).
:::

::: example How an oligopoly differs from a competitive market
Explain how an oligopoly differs from a competitive market. (2)

An oligopoly is dominated by a few large firms (1), whereas a competitive market has many small firms, none of which can control the price (1).

::: example How competition affects price
Explain why the opening of a second petrol station in a village is likely to lower the price of petrol there. (2)

Drivers now have a close substitute, so demand for each station's petrol is more price elastic (1); if one station keeps its price higher, drivers switch to the other, so both are pushed to cut prices to keep customers (1).
:::

::: example Monopoly and price
Explain one reason why a monopoly may charge higher prices than firms in a competitive market. (2)

A monopoly is the only seller, so consumers have no close substitutes to switch to (1); it can raise its price without losing many customers, as high barriers to entry stop new rivals offering a cheaper alternative (1).
:::

::: example Using a diagram, analyse entry into a market
Invented context: the number of electric-car charging companies in Bramford has risen from two to eight in three years. Using a diagram, analyse how this is likely to affect the price drivers pay to charge their cars. (6)

Diagram: axes Price and Quantity; market supply shifts right from S to S1; price falls from P to P1 and quantity rises from Q to Q1 (K1 + An1).

With eight companies instead of two, many more charging points are available at every price, so the market supply of charging shifts right from S to S1 (Ap). At the original price P there is a surplus of charging capacity: chargers stand unused (An). Drivers can now choose between many companies, so demand for each one's charging is more price elastic; a company charging more than its rivals loses customers (An). Companies therefore cut prices per kilowatt-hour or offer subscriptions to win drivers, and the price falls towards P1, as shown on the diagram (An). The lower price extends demand, so more charging takes place at Q1; companies must also cut costs, for example using cheaper electricity contracts, to stay profitable at the lower price (An).
:::

::: q

::: q
Explain what is meant by an oligopoly. (2)
---
A market dominated by a few large firms (1), which are interdependent, so each must consider rivals' reactions / often use non-price competition / high barriers to entry (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit to consumers of competition between supermarkets. (2)
---
E.g. supermarkets cut prices to win shoppers from rivals (1), so households pay less for their weekly shop and have more income left for other goods (1). Or wider choice / better quality / longer opening hours, developed.
:::

::: q
Explain one way competition may harm producers. (2)
---
E.g. firms must cut prices to keep customers (1), which lowers their profit, leaving less to invest, and weaker firms may close with job losses (1). Or higher advertising costs, developed.
:::

::: q
Invented data: in a market worth £40 million a year, Firm X has sales of £11 million. Calculate Firm X's market share and state whether, under the UK's 25% guideline, it could be treated as having monopoly power. (2)
---
11 ÷ 40 × 100 = 27.5% (1); this is above 25%, so it could be treated as having monopoly power (1).
:::

::: q
Using a diagram, analyse how the entry of new firms into the market for mobile phone cases affects the price consumers pay. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 correct diagram (S shifts right, P falls, Q rises) used in developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: low barriers to entry (cheap to make or import) let new firms join (K); market supply shifts right S → S1, P → P1 lower, Q → Q1 higher (K1 + An1); a surplus at the old price leads to price cuts (An); more substitutes make demand for each seller more price elastic, so no firm can charge much above rivals (An); consumers pay less and have more designs to choose from (An); firms cut costs to stay profitable (An).

::: watch
- **Full phrases for reasons to compete**: "to make a profit", not "profit". The 2025 mark scheme gave no marks for single words.
- **"A few large firms" versus "many small firms."** Comparing "large firms" with "smaller firms" scored only 1 mark in 2024: the key difference is the **number** of firms.
- In a "how does X differ" question, **describe both sides**.
- **Answer for the group named.** In 2025, answers about the firm when the question asked about consumers scored nothing.
- Competition affects price through **more supply and more substitutes**; show the supply shift on the diagram, not a demand shift.
- **Evaluation must pair with the analysis**: "lower prices… however, if competition is mainly through advertising, costs and prices may not fall."
- Do not confuse monopoly (one firm dominates) with oligopoly (a few firms dominate).
:::

::: test

::: test

---
Firms that charge more than rivals lose customers, so to keep prices low and still make a profit (1) they look for cheaper suppliers, cut waste or invest in more efficient machinery, lowering the cost of each unit (1).

::: test
Invented extract: Five years ago Harrowgate had one taxi firm, which charged £3.50 per mile. Ride-hailing apps now let any driver with a licensed car take bookings, and over 200 drivers operate in the town. Fares are now about £2.20 per mile.

Using a diagram, analyse how competition has affected the price of taxi journeys in Harrowgate. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — correct diagram (S shifts right, P falls, Q rises) referred to in developed chains applied to Harrowgate; L2 3–4 — correct but mostly single-step, limited use of the extract, or diagram missing; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: market supply shifts right S → S1; P → P1 lower; Q → Q1 higher (K1 + An1); competition defined.
- Application: one firm to 200+ drivers; fares from £3.50 to about £2.20 per mile; apps lowering barriers to entry.
- Analysis: the old firm was close to a monopoly and could charge a high price as passengers had no alternative; apps lowered barriers to entry, so many more drivers supply journeys at every price; at the old fare there is a surplus of drivers waiting for work, so drivers cut fares to win passengers; passengers can compare drivers on the app, so demand for each driver is highly price elastic; fares fall towards £2.20 and more journeys are taken; drivers must keep costs low to earn enough at lower fares.

Invented extract: Five airlines compete on flights between the UK and the island of Corvella. Over two years, average return fares fell from £310 to £240. Airlines now charge extra for bags and seat choice, and passengers complain that delays have increased. Two of the airlines have warned they may stop flying the route because they are making losses.

Evaluate the extent to which competition between airlines is beneficial for consumers. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement on the extent of benefit to consumers; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. Answers about the airlines' profits only = 0. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: five airlines; fares down from £310 to £240 (about 23%); extra charges; delays; two airlines making losses.
- Analysis: competition forces fares down, so travellers pay £70 less per return trip and more people can afford to visit Corvella; choice of five airlines lets passengers pick times and service; rivalry encourages better service to win loyalty.
- Evaluation: the saving may be smaller than it looks once bag and seat charges are added; cost-cutting may explain the extra delays, so quality has fallen; the benefit may not last: if two airlines leave because of losses, fewer rivals remain and fares could rise again; benefit depends on the passenger: a traveller with no luggage gains most.
