## What it is

An **exchange rate** is the **price of one currency in terms of another**: for example £1 = €1.15, or £1 = 1.25 US dollars. Because the UK trades with the world, the exchange rate affects the price of everything the UK imports and exports. This topic covers how the exchange rate is **determined by supply and demand** (with a diagram), how to **calculate currency conversions**, how to **analyse exchange rate data**, and how to **evaluate the effects of a change in the exchange rate on consumers and producers**.

### Appreciation and depreciation

- If the pound buys **more** foreign currency (£1 = €1.10 → £1 = €1.20), the pound has **appreciated**: it is **stronger**.
- If the pound buys **less** foreign currency (£1 = €1.20 → £1 = €1.10), the pound has **depreciated**: it is **weaker**.

In the UK the value of the pound is set in the **foreign exchange market** by the demand for and supply of pounds. It is a **floating** exchange rate.

### Who demands pounds and who supplies them

| Demand for £ (people **buying** pounds with foreign currency) | Supply of £ (people **selling** pounds for foreign currency) |
|---|---|
| foreign buyers of **UK exports** of goods and services, who must pay UK firms in pounds | UK buyers of **imports**, who must swap pounds for the seller's currency |
| **foreign tourists** visiting the UK | **UK tourists** going abroad |
| foreign firms **investing in the UK** (building factories, buying UK firms) | UK firms and people **investing abroad** |
| foreign savers putting money in UK banks, attracted by **higher UK interest rates** | UK savers moving money abroad for higher interest rates elsewhere |
| **speculators** who expect the pound to rise | speculators who expect the pound to fall |

### The currency diagram

The vertical axis is the **price of £ in a foreign currency** (here, US dollars) and the horizontal axis is the **quantity of £**. The demand curve slopes down and the supply curve slopes up, just like any market. The exchange rate is where they cross.

|---|---|---|

| UK consumers buy more imports / take more holidays abroad | supply of £ **right** (S → S1) | **depreciates** |
| UK residents buy fewer imports | supply of £ **left** | appreciates |
| UK inflation is higher than abroad | demand for £ left (exports less competitive) and supply of £ right (imports relatively cheaper) | depreciates |

### Currency conversion

- **Pounds → foreign currency: multiply** by the rate. At £1 = €1.15, £400 = 400 × 1.15 = **€460**.
- **Foreign currency → pounds: divide** by the rate. At £1 = 190 Japanese yen, 57,000 yen = 57,000 ÷ 190 = **£300**.

**How a change in the rate changes prices (invented figures).** A holiday villa in France costs €1,380 a week.
- At £1 = €1.20 it costs a UK family 1,380 ÷ 1.20 = **£1,150**.
- If the pound depreciates to £1 = €1.15, it costs 1,380 ÷ 1.15 = **£1,200**: £50 more, even though the price in euros has not changed.

A UK-made machine priced at £20,000 costs a US buyer 20,000 × 1.40 = **28,000 US dollars** at £1 = 1.40 US dollars, but only 20,000 × 1.25 = **25,000 US dollars** if the pound falls to 1.25 US dollars. A weaker pound makes UK exports cheaper abroad.

### Analysing exchange rate data

With a table or line chart of the pound's value:
- say whether the pound **appreciated or depreciated**, by how much and when (use the units, e.g. "from €1.18 to €1.12");
- calculate the **percentage change** if asked: change ÷ original × 100;
- give a **reason** (a change in interest rates, inflation, trade, investment or confidence) and the likely **effects**.

### Effects of a change in the exchange rate

Memory aids: **SPICED** — a **S**trong **P**ound makes **I**mports **C**heaper and **E**xports **D**earer. **WPIDEC** — a **W**eak **P**ound makes **I**mports **D**earer and **E**xports **C**heaper.

| | A stronger pound (appreciation) | A weaker pound (depreciation) |
|---|---|---|
| **UK consumers** | imports and holidays abroad are cheaper; lower inflation | imports and holidays abroad cost more; prices of imported food, fuel and clothes rise, so inflation may rise |
| **UK exporters** | goods dearer abroad, so sales and profit may fall; jobs at risk | goods cheaper abroad, so sales rise; more output and jobs |
| **Firms that import inputs** | raw materials and components cheaper, so costs fall | costs rise, which may squeeze profit or push up prices |
| **UK firms competing with imports** | face cheaper foreign rivals | rivals' goods become dearer, so domestic firms win sales |
| **Current account** | tends to worsen (exports fall, imports rise) | tends to improve |

**Evaluation points**: the size of the effect depends on
- the **price elasticity of demand** for exports and imports: if demand is price inelastic, a change in price changes sales very little;
- how much of a product's **inputs are imported**: a weaker pound helps exporters less if their components cost more too;
- **contracts and hedging**: firms often agree prices in advance, so effects come with a time lag;
- **how long** the change lasts and **how big** it is;
- **non-price factors** such as quality, brand and reliability, which matter as much as price for many buyers;
- whether the firm or consumer is mainly a **buyer** or a **seller** abroad.

::: terms
- **Exchange rate**: the price of one currency in terms of another currency.
- **Foreign exchange market**: the market where currencies are bought and sold.
- **Appreciation**: a rise in the value of a currency, so it buys more of another currency.
- **Depreciation**: a fall in the value of a currency, so it buys less of another currency.
- **Floating exchange rate**: an exchange rate set by the demand for and supply of the currency in the foreign exchange market.
- **Demand for pounds**: the quantity of pounds that people abroad want to buy, for example to pay for UK exports, visit the UK or invest in the UK.
- **Supply of pounds**: the quantity of pounds offered for sale, for example by UK residents paying for imports or travelling abroad.
- **Hot money**: savings moved between countries to earn the highest interest rate or a gain from a currency change.
- **Speculation**: buying or selling a currency in the hope of making a profit from a future change in its value.
- **Competitiveness**: how attractive a country's goods are to buyers compared with rivals, on price and quality.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain how X affects the value of the pound" (2)**: say which side changes — demand for or supply of £ — and why (1) + the direction: appreciation or depreciation (1).
2. **Conversion (2)**: pounds → foreign currency, multiply; foreign currency → pounds, divide. Show working, give the currency sign or name, and round sensibly (to the nearest penny or cent).
3. **"Draw and label" (2)**: axes "Price of £ in [currency]" and "Quantity of £", D and S, the correct shift (D → D1 or S → S1) and the new price P1.
4. **Analyse (6)**: chain the cause → demand or supply of £ shifts → the new exchange rate → effects on export and import prices → sales, jobs or inflation. If the question says "using a diagram", draw it and refer to it. No evaluation.
5. **Evaluate (6)**: use SPICED or WPIDEC for the group named, then qualify each point: PED, imported inputs, contracts and time lags, how long the change lasts, non-price factors. Judge the extent.
:::

::: example Interest rates and the pound
Explain how higher interest rates in the UK may lead to the value of the pound increasing. (2)

Higher interest rates make saving in UK banks more attractive to foreign savers, so they buy pounds to deposit in the UK (1); demand for pounds rises, so the price of the pound rises / the pound appreciates (1).
:::

::: example Pounds into euros
Invented figures: a student changes £250 into euros at £1 = €1.16. Calculate how many euros she receives. Show your working. (2)

250 × 1.16 (1) = **€290** (1).
:::

::: example Foreign price into pounds
Invented figures: a UK shop buys a guitar from the USA for 1,040 US dollars. The exchange rate is £1 = 1.25 US dollars. Calculate the cost in pounds. Show your working. (2)

1,040 ÷ 1.25 (1) = **£832** (1).
:::

::: example Draw a fall in the value of the pound
Draw and label a diagram showing a fall in the value of the pound caused by UK consumers buying more imports. (sketch it on paper, or describe the axes, curves and shift in words) (2)

Axes labelled "Price of £ in [foreign currency]" and "Quantity of £", with a downward-sloping D and upward-sloping S (1); S shifts right to S1, so the price of the pound falls from P to P1 (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a fall in tourism
Invented context: the number of foreign tourists visiting the UK falls by a quarter after a series of rail strikes and poor weather. Using a diagram, analyse how this could affect the exchange rate of the pound. (6)

Foreign tourists need pounds to spend in the UK, so they are part of the demand for pounds (K). If visits fall by a quarter, fewer tourists buy pounds, so the demand curve for pounds shifts left from D to D1 (Ap/An). On the diagram, with "Price of £ in euros" on the vertical axis and "Quantity of £" on the horizontal, the price of the pound falls from P to P1 and the quantity traded falls from Q to Q1 (K/An): the pound depreciates. As the pound depreciates, UK exports become cheaper abroad, so foreign buyers purchase more UK goods, which increases demand for pounds again and limits the fall (An). At the same time, imports become dearer for UK consumers, so they buy fewer imports and supply fewer pounds (An). The final fall in the pound therefore depends on how large the drop in tourism is compared with these other flows (Ap/An).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by an exchange rate. (2)
---
The price of one currency (1) expressed in terms of another currency, e.g. £1 = €1.15 (1).
:::

::: q
State two sources of demand for pounds. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): foreign buyers of UK exports; foreign tourists visiting the UK; foreign firms investing in the UK; foreign savers attracted by UK interest rates; speculators expecting the pound to rise. Do not credit "UK tourists going abroad" (supply).
:::

::: q
Invented figures: a hotel room in Tokyo costs 24,700 yen a night. The exchange rate is £1 = 190 yen. Calculate the cost of the room in pounds. Show your working. (2)
---
24,700 ÷ 190 (1) = **£130** (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one effect of a depreciation of the pound on UK exporters. (2)
---
UK goods become cheaper for foreign buyers in their own currency (1), so demand for UK exports rises, increasing exporters' sales, revenue and possibly profit / letting them expand output and jobs (1).
:::

::: q
Invented figures: the pound fell from €1.20 to €1.14. Calculate the percentage change in the value of the pound against the euro. Show your working. (2)
---
(1.14 − 1.20) ÷ 1.20 × 100 = −0.06 ÷ 1.20 × 100 (1) = **−5%**, a 5% depreciation (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a rise in UK inflation above that of other countries could affect the value of the pound. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: the exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of pounds (K); UK goods become dearer relative to foreign goods, so foreign buyers buy fewer UK exports and demand fewer pounds — demand shifts left (An); UK consumers switch to relatively cheaper imports, so they supply more pounds — supply shifts right (An); both shifts lower the price of the pound, so it depreciates (An); diagram with D → D1 and/or S → S1 and the lower price (K/An); a weaker pound then raises import prices, which may add further to inflation (An).
:::

- **Show a shift**, not a movement along a curve.

::: test
Invented extract: Over the last year the pound has risen by 12% against the euro. Fernley Bikes, a UK firm, makes high-quality bicycles and sells 60% of them to customers in the EU. It buys its frames and gears from suppliers in the EU. Its customers are loyal to the brand and its products win awards for quality.

Evaluate the extent to which the rise in the value of the pound will affect Fernley Bikes. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement that addresses "extent"; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: pound up 12% against the euro; 60% of sales in the EU; frames and gears imported from the EU; loyal customers; award-winning quality.

::: test
Invented extract: The pound has fallen by 15% against the US dollar and the euro. The UK imports much of its food, fuel and clothing. UK tourism firms report a rise in bookings from overseas visitors, and UK manufacturers say export orders are increasing.

Evaluate whether the fall in the value of the pound is good for UK consumers. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: 15% fall against the US dollar and euro; much food, fuel and clothing imported; more overseas tourists; rising export orders.

Judgement: e.g. the fall is bad for most consumers in the short run because the prices of essential imports rise straight away, while the benefits from more jobs arrive more slowly and are spread unevenly.
:::
