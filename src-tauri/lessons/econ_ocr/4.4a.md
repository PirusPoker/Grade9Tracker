## What it is

**Globalisation** is the process by which the world's economies become more **interconnected and interdependent**: more trade in goods and services, more movement of money (capital) and investment between countries, more movement of people, and faster spread of technology, ideas and culture. This topic covers **what globalisation is and its driving factors**, and **how development is measured**, including **GDP per capita, life expectancy, access to health care, technology and education**. The costs and benefits of globalisation are in 4.4b.

### What globalisation looks like

| Flow | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Goods and services** | a phone designed in one country, assembled in another from parts made in a dozen more; UK insurance sold worldwide |
| **Capital and investment** | a multinational building a factory abroad (foreign direct investment); savers buying shares in foreign companies |
| **People** | workers moving to other countries for jobs; international students; tourism |
| **Technology and culture** | the same apps, films, brands and fast-food chains found in most countries |

**Interdependence** means that what happens in one economy affects others: a recession in a large economy cuts demand for other countries' exports; a factory shutdown in one country can halt production lines on the other side of the world.

### Driving factors of globalisation

| Driving factor | How it has increased globalisation |
|---|---|
| **Improvements in transport** | standard **shipping containers**, larger ships and cheaper air freight have cut the cost and time of moving goods, so it is worth making things far from where they are sold |
| **Information and communications technology (ICT) and the internet** | firms can manage factories and suppliers worldwide by video call and email; consumers can buy from overseas websites; services such as software, design and customer support can be supplied from anywhere |
| **Growth of multinational companies (MNCs)** | firms operating in many countries locate production where costs are lowest and sell to markets everywhere, spreading investment, jobs and technology |
| **Trade liberalisation** | cutting tariffs and quotas, and **free trade agreements** (4.1), make trading cheaper |
| **Freer movement of capital** | removing controls on moving money between countries makes foreign investment easier |
| **Economies opening up to trade** | large countries that had restricted trade, such as China and India, opened their economies and became major producers and markets |
| **Global brands and marketing** | firms advertise to world markets, creating global demand for the same products |

A good answer explains **how** the factor makes trade or investment between countries cheaper, quicker or more profitable; naming it is not enough.

### Developed and less developed countries

A **developed country** (such as the UK) has high average incomes, a large service sector, and good health care, education and infrastructure. A **less developed country** has lower average incomes, often relies more on primary industries such as farming and mining, and has weaker access to health care, education and technology. **Development** means an improvement in people's **living standards and well-being**, not just more output.

### How development is measured

| Measure | What it shows | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **GDP per capita** = GDP ÷ population | the average income or output per person; higher means people can, on average, buy more goods and services | it is an **average**, so it hides **inequality**; it leaves out unpaid work and the informal economy; it says nothing directly about health, education or the environment; it does not allow for different price levels between countries |
| **Life expectancy** | the average number of years a person is expected to live; reflects diet, clean water, sanitation and health care | it is also affected by things unrelated to development, such as war or lifestyle |
| **Access to health care** | e.g. doctors or hospital beds per 1,000 people, vaccination rates, infant and child death rates | a doctor count does not show the quality of care, or whether poor and rural people can reach it |
| **Access to technology** | e.g. the share of people with electricity, internet access or a mobile phone | access does not show how it is used; spreads quickly, so differences may narrow |
| **Education** | e.g. the **literacy rate** (share of adults who can read and write), school enrolment rates, average years of schooling | enrolment does not show whether children attend regularly or what quality of teaching they receive |

**Why use several measures?** A country can have a high GDP per capita (for example from oil exports) but low life expectancy and literacy if the income is shared unequally or not spent on health and schools. Combining measures gives a fuller picture. The United Nations' **Human Development Index (HDI)** does this, combining income per person, life expectancy and education into one number.

**How the measures are linked.** Better education raises workers' skills and **productivity**, which raises output and GDP per capita. Higher incomes pay for better health care and schools, through taxes and household spending. Healthier workers take fewer days off and live longer working lives. So development measures tend to move together, but not always at the same pace.

**Quick calculations (invented figures).**
- GDP £36 billion, population 24 million: GDP per capita = 36,000 million ÷ 24 million = **£1,500**.
- Literacy rate rises from 60% to 72%: an increase of **12 percentage points**, which is 12 ÷ 60 × 100 = a **20%** rise.

::: terms
- **Globalisation**: the growing integration and interdependence of the world's economies through trade, investment, movement of people and the spread of technology.
- **Interdependence**: when countries rely on each other, so that events in one economy affect others.
- **Multinational company**: a firm that has its headquarters in one country and produces or operates in several others.
- **Foreign direct investment**: when a firm from one country sets up or buys a business, such as a factory, in another country.
- **Trade liberalisation**: reducing barriers to trade, such as tariffs and quotas, between countries.
- **Containerisation**: moving goods in standard-sized steel containers that transfer easily between ships, trains and lorries, cutting transport costs.
- **Development**: an improvement in people's living standards and well-being, including income, health and education.
- **GDP per capita**: a country's gross domestic product divided by its population; the average output or income per person.
- **Life expectancy**: the average number of years a person born in a country can be expected to live.
- **Literacy rate**: the percentage of adults in a population who can read and write.
- **Human Development Index**: a United Nations measure combining income per person, life expectancy and education.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain what is meant by globalisation" (2)**: the growing integration / interdependence of economies (1) + through trade, investment, movement of people or technology, with an example (1).
2. **"Explain one driving factor" (2)**: name the factor (1) + explain **how** it makes trading or investing across borders cheaper, quicker or easier (1).
3. **"Explain why [measure] could be used to measure development" (2)**: what it shows (1) + why that reflects living standards, e.g. higher income means more goods and services (1).
4. **Calculations (2)**: GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population (watch billions and millions); % change = change ÷ original × 100; distinguish percentage points from per cent. Show working and units.
5. **Analyse (6)**: chains such as more schooling → literacy and skills → productivity → output and GDP → tax revenue and health spending. Use the data. No evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: weigh what a measure shows against its limits (average, inequality, quality, informal economy); compare with other measures; judge whether it is the best on its own.
:::

::: example Analyse — more children in school
Invented context: in the country of Taruma, the share of children attending primary school rose from 55% to 85% over ten years after school fees were abolished. Analyse how this could affect Taruma's GDP. (6)

Education is a measure of development, and GDP measures the total output of an economy (K). With 85% rather than 55% of children in primary school, many more young people learn to read, write and use numbers (Ap). When they reach working age, they are more productive: they can follow instructions, use machinery and technology, and take skilled jobs, so output per worker rises (An). Higher productivity means firms produce more with the same workers, so total output and GDP rise; firms may also be attracted to invest, including multinationals looking for a literate workforce, adding to output (An). Educated workers earn more, so they spend more and pay more tax, which raises demand and lets the government fund further schooling and health care, raising GDP again (An). The full effect comes years later, when these children enter the labour force (Ap/An).
:::

Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.

::: watch
- **Explain how a driving factor works**: "cheaper transport" earns 1 mark; saying it lets firms make goods where costs are lowest and sell them worldwide earns the second.
- **GDP per capita is an average.** It hides inequality and says nothing directly about health, education or the environment.
- **Per cent versus percentage points**: a literacy rate from 50% to 60% is a 10 percentage point rise but a 20% increase.
- **Watch units in GDP per capita**: convert billions and millions before dividing (£84 billion ÷ 35 million = £2,400, not £2.4).
- **Development is broader than growth**: more output is not the same as better health or education.
- **In analyse questions on education or health, build the chain to output**: skills → productivity → output → incomes → tax and spending.
- **Name the measure precisely**: "literacy rate" or "school enrolment", not just "education".
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Country A has GDP per capita of £9,000, but life expectancy of 61 years and an adult literacy rate of 55%; most of its income comes from oil, and the richest 10% receive half of all income. Country B has GDP per capita of £6,000, life expectancy of 72 years and adult literacy of 88%.

---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: A £9,000 vs B £6,000; life expectancy 61 vs 72; literacy 55% vs 88%; oil income; richest 10% receive half of income in A.

- Evaluation: A's average is raised by oil and shared unequally, so most people may be poorer than the figure suggests; A's lower life expectancy and literacy show its income has not been turned into better health or education; B's people appear better off in health and education despite a lower average income; GDP per capita ignores the informal economy and price differences; but GDP per capita is still useful because higher income pays for future improvements.
