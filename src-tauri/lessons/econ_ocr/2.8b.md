## What it is

An **interest rate** is the price of money: what borrowers pay to use someone else's money, and what savers earn for letting it be used. When interest rates change, millions of households and firms change how much they **save**, **borrow** and **invest**. This topic covers what interest rates are, **how different interest rates affect saving, borrowing and investment** (which the spec asks you to analyse), and how to **calculate the effect on savings and borrowing of a change in the rate**. It links forward to monetary policy (3.6), where the Bank of England changes interest rates on purpose.

### What an interest rate is

- For a **borrower**, interest is the **cost of borrowing**: extra paid on top of the amount borrowed, as a percentage per year.
- For a **saver**, interest is the **reward for saving**: income earned on money deposited, as a percentage per year.

Banks pay savers a **lower** rate than they charge borrowers; the gap covers their costs and profit. Rates also differ between products: **riskier** loans (credit cards, loans to people with poor credit histories, unsecured loans) carry **higher** rates than **secured** loans such as mortgages, and savings locked away for longer usually earn more than instant-access accounts. Most rates move up and down with the **Bank Rate** set by the Bank of England.

A **fixed-rate** loan or mortgage keeps the same rate for an agreed period; a **variable-rate** one changes when interest rates in general change. This matters for how quickly a rate change affects people.

### How interest rates affect saving, borrowing and investment

| | **Interest rates rise** | **Interest rates fall** |
|---|---|---|
| **Saving** | the reward for saving rises, so saving becomes more attractive; households save more and spend less now | saving earns less, so households have less reason to save and may spend more |
| **Borrowing by consumers** | loans, credit cards and new mortgages cost more, so people borrow less and cut spending on big items such as cars, furniture and holidays | borrowing is cheaper, so people borrow and spend more |
| **Existing borrowers** | people with **variable-rate** mortgages or loans pay more each month, leaving less **disposable income** for other spending | variable-rate repayments fall, so borrowers have more income to spend |
| **Investment by firms** | borrowing to buy machinery or build premises costs more, so fewer projects are profitable and **investment falls**; the **opportunity cost** of using the firm's own savings also rises, as it could earn more by leaving them in the bank | more investment projects become worthwhile, so firms invest more |
| **Wider effect** | less spending leads to lower sales for firms, possibly fewer jobs and less pressure on prices | more spending raises firms' sales and may create jobs, but can push up prices |

**Investment and the cost of borrowing.** A firm compares the **expected return** on a project with the **interest cost**. If a new machine is expected to add 6% a year to profit, it is worth borrowing at 4% but not at 8%. So higher rates cut investment; lower rates encourage it.

**Limits (useful for evaluation):**

- **Confidence and expectations**: in a recession, firms may not invest even at very low rates because they doubt they can sell more; households worried about jobs keep saving.
- **Size of the change**: a small cut, e.g. a quarter of a percentage point, may make little difference to a firm's decision. The 2025 mark scheme credited "the cut may not be enough".
- **Fixed-rate deals**: households on fixed-rate mortgages are unaffected until their deal ends.
- **Time lags**: changes take months to affect spending and investment.
- **Banks' willingness to lend**: a lower Bank Rate helps only if banks pass it on and approve loans.
- **Savers versus borrowers**: a rise helps savers (often older households) but hurts borrowers (often younger households and firms with loans), so the overall effect depends on who holds more savings or debt.

### Calculating the effect of a change in the interest rate

At GCSE, use **simple annual interest** unless told otherwise.

**Interest for one year = amount × rate ÷ 100**

**1. Interest on savings (invented figures).** £8,000 saved at 3.5% a year: £8,000 × 3.5 ÷ 100 = **£280** a year. If the rate falls to 2.5%: £8,000 × 2.5 ÷ 100 = £200, so the saver earns **£80 less** a year.

**2. The cost of a loan at two different rates.** A firm borrows £15,000 for one year.

| Rate | Working | Interest |
|---|---|---|
| 7% | £15,000 × 7 ÷ 100 | £1,050 |
| 5.5% | £15,000 × 5.5 ÷ 100 | £825 |
| **Saving** | £1,050 − £825 | **£225** |

**3. Monthly cost.** A household has an interest-only mortgage of £180,000. At 4%, yearly interest = £7,200, so **£600 a month**. If the rate rises to 5%: yearly interest = £9,000, so **£750 a month**, a rise of **£150 a month** (£1,800 a year). The monthly figure is the yearly figure ÷ 12.

**4. Extension: interest over two years.** If interest is added to the account each year (compound interest), £5,000 at 4% becomes £5,200 after one year and £5,200 × 1.04 = **£5,408** after two. With simple interest it would be £5,400.

Always state the answer in **pounds with the £ sign**, and say whether it is a rise or a fall.

::: terms
- **Interest rate**: the cost of borrowing money and the reward for saving it, shown as a percentage per year.
- **Interest**: the money paid by a borrower or earned by a saver, on top of the amount borrowed or saved.
- **Saving**: income not spent on consumption, often held in a bank or building society account.
- **Borrowing**: taking money from a lender that must be repaid later, usually with interest.
- **Investment**: spending by firms on capital goods such as machinery and buildings, to increase future output.
- **Bank Rate**: the official interest rate set by the Bank of England, which influences the rates banks charge and pay.
- **Fixed-rate mortgage**: a mortgage whose interest rate stays the same for an agreed period, whatever happens to other rates.
- **Variable-rate mortgage**: a mortgage whose interest rate changes when interest rates in general change.
- **Disposable income**: income left after taxes, which households can spend or save.
- **Simple interest**: interest worked out only on the original amount, the same amount each year.
:::

::: method
1. **Calculations (2)**: interest = amount × rate ÷ 100. For a change in rate, work out the interest at each rate, then the difference (or use the change in the rate × amount ÷ 100). ÷ 12 for monthly. Show working; give £ and say rise or fall.
2. **"Explain how a change in interest rates affects…" (2)**: the change in cost or reward (1) + the effect on the behaviour of the group named (saving, borrowing, spending, investing) (1).
3. **"Analyse the effect of a change in interest rates on X" (6)**: build chains: rate change → cost of borrowing / reward for saving → decision of X → effect on spending, investment, sales, jobs. Use the figures given. No evaluation.
4. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse the expected effect, then qualify it: confidence, size of the change, fixed-rate deals, time lags, banks' lending, who saves and who borrows; finish with a supported judgement.
:::

::: example Calculating interest on savings
Invented figures: Maya has £4,600 in a savings account paying 3% a year. Calculate the interest she earns in one year. Show your working. (2)

£4,600 × 3 ÷ 100 (1) = **£138** (1).
:::

::: example The effect of a rate cut on borrowing costs
Invented figures: a café owner borrows £24,000 for one year. Calculate how much she saves in interest if the rate falls from 6.5% to 5%. Show your working. (2)

At 6.5%: £24,000 × 6.5 ÷ 100 = £1,560; at 5%: £24,000 × 5 ÷ 100 = £1,200 (1). Saving = £1,560 − £1,200 = **£360** (1). (Or 1.5% × £24,000 = £360.)
:::

::: example A rise in rates and a mortgage
Explain how a rise in interest rates affects a household with a variable-rate mortgage. (2)

The household's monthly mortgage repayments rise because the interest charged goes up (1), so it has less disposable income left to spend on other goods such as meals out and holidays (1).
:::

::: example Monthly payments
Invented figures: a firm has a £60,000 interest-only loan at 6% a year. Calculate its monthly interest payment. (2)

Yearly interest = £60,000 × 6 ÷ 100 = £3,600 (1); monthly = £3,600 ÷ 12 = **£300** (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a cut in interest rates for a firm
Invented context: Halden Engineering Ltd is considering borrowing £500,000 to buy robotic welding machines. Interest rates have just fallen from 6% to 4%. Analyse the effect of the reduction in interest rates on Halden Engineering. (6)

The fall in the rate cuts the yearly interest on a £500,000 loan from £30,000 to £20,000, saving Halden £10,000 a year (Ap). The cost of borrowing is now lower than the extra profit the machines are expected to bring, so the investment is more likely to be worthwhile, and Halden may go ahead (An). The machines would raise productivity, lowering the average cost of each product, so Halden can cut prices or increase its profit margin (An). If Halden already has variable-rate loans or an overdraft, its interest payments on those fall too, reducing its costs and raising profit immediately (An). Lower rates also make borrowing cheaper for Halden's customers, such as manufacturers who buy its parts, so demand for its products may rise, increasing its sales and revenue (An). However the firm could also choose to use its own savings, and the lower rate means those savings earn less in the bank, so the opportunity cost of spending them on the machines is lower (An).
:::

::: q
Explain how a rise in interest rates may affect saving. (2)
---
The reward for saving rises, so saving becomes more attractive (1), meaning households save more of their income and spend less now (1).

::: q
State two reasons why a fall in interest rates may not lead to more investment by firms. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): low business confidence / firms expect low sales; the cut is too small to make a difference; banks unwilling to lend; firms already have spare capacity; firms worried rates will rise again; time lags.
:::

::: q
Explain why a fall in interest rates may reduce the income of retired people. (2)
---
Many retired people rely on interest from their savings for income (1); when rates fall, their savings earn less interest, so their income falls and they may cut spending (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a rise in interest rates could affect the level of borrowing by consumers. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: interest is the cost of borrowing, so a rise makes loans, credit cards and new mortgages dearer (K); monthly repayments on a given loan rise, so fewer people can afford them (An); consumers postpone big purchases bought on credit, such as cars and furniture (An); fewer people take out mortgages, so demand for houses falls (An); those with variable-rate debt pay more, cutting disposable income and further spending (An); saving becomes more attractive, so some pay off debt or save instead of borrowing (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Work out the interest at each rate, then find the difference.** Give the answer in **£**, and say whether it is a rise or a fall.
- **Monthly = yearly ÷ 12.** The 2024 paper asked for a monthly payment after a rate rise.
- **Interest is a cost to borrowers and a reward to savers**: say which group you are writing about.
- **Investment** in economics means firms buying capital goods, not people buying shares.
- A cut in rates does not automatically raise spending: confidence, the size of the cut, fixed-rate deals and banks' willingness to lend all matter.

::: test
Invented extract: Interest rates in the country of Ostrava have been cut three times this year and are now at 2%. Business surveys show that many firms are worried about weak consumer spending and expect sales to fall. Banks say they have become more cautious about lending to small firms.

Evaluate whether the cut in interest rates will lead Ostravan businesses to invest more. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement on whether investment will rise; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: three cuts; rates at 2%; firms worried about weak spending; banks more cautious with small firms.
- Analysis: lower rates cut the cost of borrowing, so more investment projects earn more than their interest cost and firms invest more; firms with existing variable-rate loans pay less interest, raising profit available for investment; lower rates reduce the return on firms' own savings, lowering the opportunity cost of investing them; consumers may borrow and spend more, raising expected sales.
- Evaluation: investment depends mainly on expected sales: if firms expect sales to fall, they will not add capacity however cheap borrowing is; cautious banks mean small firms may not be able to borrow even at low rates; the effect takes time to work through; large firms with their own funds or easy access to credit are more likely to invest than small firms. Judgement: e.g. the cuts will raise investment only slightly in the short run, because weak confidence and cautious banks outweigh cheaper borrowing; investment is more likely to rise once consumer spending recovers.
:::
