## What it is

**Low unemployment** is one of the government's main economic objectives. This topic covers what **employment** and **unemployment** mean, how unemployment is measured with the **Claimant Count**, how to **calculate the unemployment rate**, how to **analyse unemployment figures**, the four **types** of unemployment (cyclical, frictional, seasonal and structural), and the **causes and consequences** of unemployment for **individuals, regions and the government**.

### Employment and unemployment

- **Employment**: people in paid work, full-time or part-time, including the self-employed.
- **Unemployment**: people of working age who are **without a job**, **available for work** and **actively looking** for work.

Not everyone without a job is unemployed. People who are not working and not looking for work are **economically inactive**: full-time students, retired people, people caring for family members full-time, and people who are long-term sick. They are not counted as unemployed.

The **labour force** (the **economically active**) is everyone who is either employed or unemployed:

**Labour force = employed + unemployed**

### Measuring unemployment: the Claimant Count

The **Claimant Count** measures unemployment as the **number of people claiming unemployment-related benefits** (in the UK, Jobseeker's Allowance and those on Universal Credit who are required to look for work). It is quick and cheap to collect, because the figures come from benefit records.

Limits of the Claimant Count:
- Some unemployed people **are not eligible** to claim (for example, because they have savings or a partner who earns) or **choose not to claim**, so it can **understate** unemployment.
- When the government **changes the benefit rules**, the count changes even if the number of people out of work does not, which makes comparisons over time harder.
- Some claimants may be on benefits while working a few hours, depending on the rules.

The UK also uses a **survey** measure (the Labour Force Survey, using the international ILO definition) which asks a sample of households whether people are looking for work. You need to know that it exists, but OCR questions use the Claimant Count.

### Calculating the unemployment rate

**Unemployment rate (%) = number unemployed ÷ labour force (economically active) × 100**

Invented figures for the town of Ashby Vale: 40,000 people are employed and 2,500 are unemployed.
- Labour force = 40,000 + 2,500 = **42,500**.
- Unemployment rate = 2,500 ÷ 42,500 × 100 = **5.9%** (to 1 decimal place).

Do not divide by the number employed or by the whole population. Read carefully: if the question gives "economically active" as one figure, divide straight by it. Give the % sign and round as the question asks.

### Analysing unemployment figures

Invented data for the country of Harrowby:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unemployment rate (%) | 4.2 | 4.0 | 6.5 | 7.8 | 7.1 |

- Years 1–2: low and slightly falling.
- Years 3–4: a sharp rise, typical of a **recession** (falling demand, firms cutting jobs).
- Year 5: starting to fall as the economy recovers, but still well above Year 1.

To **explain** a trend you must give the direction with figures **and** a reason, for example "unemployment rose from 4.0% to 7.8% because a recession cut consumer spending, so firms needed fewer workers". A change in the **rate** is not the same as a change in the **number**: the number unemployed can rise while the rate stays the same if the labour force grows.

### Types of unemployment

| Type | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cyclical** (demand-deficient) | a fall in total spending in a downturn or recession; firms sell less, so they need fewer workers | a recession cuts sales of cars and furniture, so factories lay off workers |
| **Frictional** | people moving **between jobs** and taking time to search; usually short-term | a graduate spends six weeks applying for jobs; a worker leaves one job before starting another |
| **Seasonal** | demand for some jobs varies with the **time of year** | beach-resort staff in winter; fruit pickers outside the harvest; Christmas temporary staff in January |
| **Structural** | a **long-term decline in an industry**, so workers' skills or locations no longer match the jobs available | a region's steelworks close; checkout staff replaced by self-service tills |

**Structural** unemployment is linked to **immobility of labour**:
- **Occupational immobility**: workers lack the skills to move into a different job (a factory worker cannot simply become a software engineer).
- **Geographical immobility**: workers cannot easily move to where the jobs are (house prices, family ties, cost of moving).

Structural unemployment tends to be **long-term** and concentrated in particular **regions**.

### Consequences of unemployment

| Who | Costs | Possible gains or qualifications |
|---|---|---|
| **Individuals** | loss of income and lower living standards; debt; loss of skills the longer they are out of work (harder to get a job again); stress, poor mental health, lower self-esteem; family strain | time to retrain or search for a better-matched job (frictional) |
| **Regions** | less spending in local shops, so they close and more jobs go (a **downward spiral**); young people move away; empty buildings, crime and social problems; lower local tax revenue | lower wages and available land may attract new firms |
| **The government** | more spending on benefits and less income tax, National Insurance and VAT revenue, so the **budget deficit** widens; the **opportunity cost** of benefit spending (less for schools or hospitals); lower GDP; more pressure on health services | wage pressure eases, reducing inflation risk |
| **Producers** | lower sales as consumers spend less | a larger **pool of available workers** to recruit from, and less pressure to raise wages |

**Careful**: higher unemployment means **more** workers are available to firms, not fewer.

Evaluating consequences: they depend on **how long** people are unemployed (short frictional spells cost little; long-term structural unemployment does lasting damage), **which type** it is, **how concentrated** it is in one region, the level of benefits and savings, and whether retraining is available.

::: terms
- **Employment**: people in paid work, full-time or part-time, including the self-employed.
- **Unemployment**: people of working age who are without a job, available for work and actively seeking work.
- **Economically inactive**: people of working age who are not in work and not looking for work, such as students and full-time carers.
- **Labour force**: the economically active, meaning everyone who is employed plus everyone who is unemployed.
- **Claimant Count**: a measure of unemployment based on the number of people claiming unemployment-related benefits.
- **Unemployment rate**: the number unemployed as a percentage of the labour force.
- **Cyclical unemployment**: unemployment caused by a fall in total demand during a downturn or recession.
- **Frictional unemployment**: short-term unemployment while people move between jobs.
- **Seasonal unemployment**: unemployment caused by demand for some jobs changing with the time of year.
- **Structural unemployment**: long-term unemployment caused by the decline of an industry, leaving a mismatch between workers' skills or locations and the jobs available.
- **Occupational immobility**: when workers find it hard to change jobs because they lack the skills needed.
- **Geographical immobility**: when workers find it hard to move to another area where jobs are available.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions (2)**: the key words (1) + development or an example (1). Structural must include the skills or location mismatch caused by a declining industry.
2. **Unemployment rate (2)**: unemployed ÷ labour force × 100. Work out the labour force first if needed. Show working; give % and the decimal places asked.
3. **"Explain the trend" (2)**: direction with figures (1) + a reason (1).
4. **"Explain one consequence for…" (2)**: the effect (1) + a developed result for the group named (1).
5. **Analyse (6)**: chains from unemployment to income, spending, firms, tax revenue or benefits, using the extract; no evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse the consequences for the named group, pair each with a qualification (how long, which type, which region, benefits, retraining) and finish with a judgement about the extent.
:::

::: example Unemployment rate
Invented figures: in the city of Dunmere, 180,000 people are employed and 12,000 are unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate to one decimal place. Show your working. (2)

Labour force = 180,000 + 12,000 = 192,000; 12,000 ÷ 192,000 × 100 (1) = **6.3%** (1). (6.25% rounds to 6.3%.)
:::

::: example Structural unemployment
Explain what is meant by structural unemployment. (2)

Unemployment caused by the long-term decline of an industry (1), so the workers' skills or location do not match the jobs available, e.g. former miners lacking skills for jobs in finance (1).
:::

::: example The Claimant Count
Explain why the Claimant Count may understate unemployment. (2)

It counts only people claiming unemployment-related benefits (1), but some people who are looking for work are not eligible or choose not to claim, so they are left out (1).
:::

::: example Consequence for the government
Explain one consequence of rising unemployment for the government. (2)

More people claim benefits while fewer pay income tax (1), so the budget deficit widens and the government may have to borrow more or cut spending on services such as schools (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a factory closure and the region
Invented context: Brenford Motors is closing its car plant in the town of Selby Moor, with the loss of 3,000 jobs. Most of the workers have worked there for over 15 years. Analyse the consequences of the closure for the region. (6)

The closure creates **structural** unemployment, because a whole industry has left the area (K). The 3,000 workers lose their wages, so they spend far less in Selby Moor's shops, pubs and restaurants (Ap/An). These businesses lose revenue and some close or cut staff, so unemployment spreads to other local workers in a **downward spiral** (An). Local suppliers that made parts for Brenford also lose orders and cut jobs (An). Because the workers have spent 15 years in car-making, they may lack the skills for other jobs (occupational immobility), so they stay unemployed for longer, and younger people leave the region to find work, reducing local spending further (Ap/An).
:::

::: q
State two groups of people without a job who are not counted as unemployed. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): full-time students; retired people; full-time carers / people looking after family; long-term sick or disabled people not seeking work. (They are economically inactive.)
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between frictional and structural unemployment. (2)
---
Frictional is short-term unemployment while people move between jobs (1); structural is long-term unemployment caused by the decline of an industry, leaving workers with the wrong skills or in the wrong place (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one consequence of long-term unemployment for an individual. (2)
---
E.g. their skills become out of date the longer they are out of work (1), so employers are less willing to hire them and they find it even harder to get a job / or loss of income → debt / poor mental health → lower self-esteem (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a fall in unemployment can affect the government's budget. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: the budget compares government spending with revenue (K); fewer people claim unemployment benefits, so welfare spending falls (An); more people in work pay income tax and National Insurance, so direct tax revenue rises (An); workers have more income to spend, so VAT revenue rises (An); firms sell more and make more profit, raising corporation tax revenue (An); so a deficit narrows or a surplus grows, and the government could spend more on services or cut taxes without extra borrowing (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Unemployed means looking for work.** Students, retired people and full-time carers are economically inactive, not unemployed.
- **Rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100**, not ÷ employed and not ÷ the whole population. Give the % and round as told.
- **"Explain the trend"** needs a reason. Describing the numbers alone scores 1.
- **Structural** unemployment needs both the declining industry and the skills or location mismatch. Occupational immobility is its most likely cause in an MCQ.
- **Higher unemployment means more workers available**, not fewer. Examiners saw this mistake often.
- Answer for the group named (individuals, a region or the government), and for the government write "budget deficit", not "a bigger budget".
:::

::: test
Invented extract: A new self-service technology means the banking firm Corrin Bank plans to close 120 branches across the country over two years, with the loss of 2,400 jobs. Most staff are cashiers. Meanwhile, the country has 30,000 unfilled vacancies for IT and cyber-security workers, mainly in its two largest cities.

Using the information, analyse the type of unemployment likely to result from the branch closures. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Corrin Bank; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: structural unemployment as a long-term decline in demand for certain jobs, with a skills or location mismatch; occupational and geographical immobility.
- Application: 120 branches; 2,400 cashiers; new technology; 30,000 IT vacancies in two cities.
- Analysis: technology permanently replaces cashier jobs, so demand for these skills has fallen for good, which makes this structural rather than cyclical; the cashiers lack IT and cyber-security skills, so they cannot fill the 30,000 vacancies without lengthy retraining (occupational immobility); the vacancies are in two large cities, while branches close across the country, so workers would have to move, facing high housing costs and family ties (geographical immobility); so unemployment and vacancies exist at the same time and the unemployment could be long-term; some may find other jobs quickly, which would be frictional.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The coastal region of Westholm relied on a large oil refinery that closed last year. The region's Claimant Count has risen from 3% to 9% of the labour force. Local shops report falling sales. The government has offered a two-year retraining scheme, and a firm is planning to build a wind-turbine factory in the region in three years' time.

Evaluate the consequences of the rise in unemployment for individuals in Westholm. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: refinery closed; Claimant Count up from 3% to 9%; falling shop sales; retraining scheme; wind-turbine factory in three years.
- Analysis: unemployed workers lose their wages, so living standards fall and some fall into debt; the longer they are out of work, the more their skills decline, so finding work gets harder; stress and loss of self-esteem harm health; falling shop sales mean jobs are lost in shops too, so unemployment spreads to others in the region.
- Evaluation: depends on how long people stay unemployed: retraining could let some move into new jobs, and the wind-turbine factory may need similar engineering skills, so for some it may be temporary; but three years is a long wait and older workers may struggle to retrain; benefits soften the loss of income but are much lower than refinery wages; younger people may move away, so individuals who stay face a weaker local economy. Judgement: e.g. the consequences are severe for most individuals in the short run, especially older and less-skilled workers, but less serious in the long run for those who retrain in time for the new factory.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The country of Norlund has seen unemployment rise from 4% to 7% during a recession. Benefit spending has risen by £6 billion, and income tax revenue has fallen by £4 billion. The government is considering cutting spending on road building to keep its borrowing down.

Evaluate the consequences of the rise in unemployment for the government of Norlund. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: unemployment 4% → 7%; benefit spending up £6bn; income tax down £4bn, so the budget worsens by £10bn; road-building cut considered.
- Analysis: more benefit spending and less tax revenue widen the budget deficit, so the government must borrow more, adding to debt and interest payments; opportunity cost: cutting road building reduces future capacity and jobs in construction, which may raise unemployment further; lower GDP and incomes also cut VAT revenue.
- Evaluation: the effect depends on whether unemployment is cyclical: if so, it should fall as the economy recovers and the budget will improve without cuts; cutting spending in a recession may deepen it, making the deficit worse; borrowing may be acceptable for a short time if interest costs are low; lower wage pressure may help keep inflation down. Judgement: e.g. a serious but probably temporary cost; the government should accept higher borrowing rather than cut road building, as long as the recession is short.
:::
