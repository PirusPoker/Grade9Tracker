## What it is

**Economic growth** is an increase in the output of goods and services produced in an economy over time. It is the first of the government's main economic objectives in section 3 (with low unemployment, a fair distribution of income and price stability). This topic covers what growth is, **how it is measured with GDP and GDP per capita** (including the calculations), how to **analyse GDP data**, the **determinants** of growth, and the **costs and benefits** of growth, including its effect on economic, social and environmental **sustainability**.

### Measuring growth: GDP and GDP per capita

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** is the total value of all the goods and services produced in a country in a period, usually a year (or a quarter of a year). When GDP rises, the economy has grown; when it falls, the economy has shrunk (**negative growth**). Two quarters in a row of falling GDP is usually called a **recession**.

The **rate of economic growth** is the percentage change in GDP:

**Growth rate (%) = (GDP this year − GDP last year) ÷ GDP last year × 100**

**GDP per capita** is GDP divided by the population. It shows the **average output, and so the average income, per person**, and is a better guide to living standards than total GDP, because a country with a large population can have a high GDP but low incomes per person.

**GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population**

**Worked figures (invented country, Vandria):**

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | £480 billion | £492 billion |
| Population | 12 million | 12.3 million |

- Growth rate = (492 − 480) ÷ 480 × 100 = 12 ÷ 480 × 100 = **2.5%**.
- GDP per capita in Year 1 = £480,000 million ÷ 12 million = **£40,000**; in Year 2 = £492,000 million ÷ 12.3 million = **£40,000**.
- So GDP grew by 2.5%, but the population also grew by 2.5%, so **GDP per capita did not change**: the average person is no better off.

Watch the units: billions ÷ millions needs care. Convert both to the same unit first (£480 billion = £480,000 million), or divide 480 by 12 and multiply by 1,000.

**Real versus nominal GDP.** If prices rise, GDP measured at current prices (**nominal GDP**) rises even if no more is produced. **Real GDP** is adjusted for inflation, so it measures the change in actual output. If nominal GDP rises by 5% while prices rise by 3%, real growth is roughly **2%**. Growth means a rise in **real** GDP. (Real and nominal values are covered fully in 3.4.)

### Analysing GDP data

Examiners like data where the **growth rate falls but stays positive**. If growth slows from 3% to 1%, **GDP is still rising**, just more slowly. GDP only falls when the growth rate is **negative**.

Invented data for the country of Morrow:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Growth rate (%) | 3.0 | 2.0 | 0.5 | −1.5 | 1.0 |

- Years 1–3: GDP rose every year, but at a slower rate each year.
- Year 4: GDP **fell** (negative growth), so output was lower than in Year 3.
- Year 5: GDP rose again, but from a lower level.
- The **highest GDP** could be in Year 3 or Year 5. Year 5 GDP = Year 3 × 0.985 × 1.01 ≈ 0.995 of Year 3, so **Year 3 was still higher**.

### Determinants of economic growth

Growth happens when an economy produces more, either by using more resources or by using them better. The spec names six determinants:

| Determinant | Chain to more output |
|---|---|
| **Investment** | firms buy more capital (machinery, buildings, IT), so each worker can produce more and the economy's capacity rises |
| **Changes in technology** | new methods and inventions (automation, software, better crops) raise productivity: more output from the same inputs |
| **Size of the workforce** | more workers (through migration, a growing population, more women or older people working) means more labour to produce goods and services |
| **Education and training** | more skilled workers are more productive, adapt to new technology and fill skilled vacancies |
| **Availability of natural resources** | discovering or exploiting land, minerals, energy or fertile soil allows more to be produced and exported |
| **Government policies** | low and stable taxes, spending on infrastructure, education and research, and a stable economy encourage firms to invest and expand (links to fiscal, monetary and supply side policy in 3.5–3.7) |

A useful distinction: more **spending** (consumers, firms, government, exports) uses spare capacity in the short run, while more and better **resources** raise the economy's capacity in the long run.

### Costs and benefits of growth

| | **Benefits** | **Costs** |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | higher incomes and living standards; more jobs and lower unemployment; more profit for firms; more tax revenue without raising tax rates; less spending on benefits | demand-pull **inflation** if spending grows faster than capacity; more imports; growth may be unevenly shared, widening **inequality** |
| **Social** | tax revenue can fund health care, schools and pensions; better housing and diet; longer life expectancy | stress and longer working hours; congestion in growing cities; regions left behind |
| **Environmental** | richer countries can afford cleaner technology and renewable energy | **pollution**, carbon emissions and climate change; **depletion** of non-renewable resources; loss of habitats and green space |

**Sustainability.** Growth is **sustainable** when it can continue without harming the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
- **Economically sustainable**: steady growth without high inflation or unaffordable debt.
- **Socially sustainable**: the benefits reach most people, and communities and public services are not damaged.
- **Environmentally sustainable**: resources are not used up faster than they can be replaced, and pollution is kept within limits.

For evaluation, the key questions are: **who** gains and who loses; whether the growth is in **real GDP per capita**; whether it is **short-term** or lasting; and whether the extra tax revenue is used to reduce the costs (for example, funding cleaner energy).

::: terms
- **Economic growth**: an increase in the output of goods and services in an economy over time, measured by a rise in real GDP.
- **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**: the total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a period, usually a year.
- **Rate of economic growth**: the percentage change in GDP from one period to the next.
- **GDP per capita**: GDP divided by the population, showing the average output or income per person.
- **Negative growth**: a fall in GDP, meaning the economy produced less than in the previous period.
- **Recession**: a period when GDP falls, usually two quarters in a row of negative growth.
- **Real GDP**: GDP adjusted for inflation, so that it measures changes in actual output rather than prices.
- **Nominal GDP**: GDP measured at current prices, without any adjustment for inflation.
- **Investment**: spending by firms on capital goods such as machinery and buildings, which increases future output.
- **Productivity**: output per worker (or per unit of input) in a given time.
- **Sustainable growth**: growth that can continue without harming the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
- **Resource depletion**: the using up of natural resources, especially non-renewable ones, faster than they can be replaced.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain what is meant by economic growth / GDP" (2)**: definition (1) + development: how it is measured or an example (1).
2. **Calculations (2)**: growth rate = change ÷ original × 100; GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population. Put both figures in the same units, show the working, give the unit (%, £) and the decimal places asked.
3. **Data and trends (2)**: say what happened to GDP or the growth rate, with figures (1), and give a reason (1). Remember a falling positive rate means GDP is still rising.
4. **"Explain how X leads to growth" (2)**: X (1) → more or better resources, or more spending → more output (1).
5. **Analyse (6)**: two or three developed chains, each ending in more (or less) output, jobs or incomes; use the extract's figures; no evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse a benefit and a cost for the group or area named, pair each point with a "but/depends on" (who gains, sustainability, inflation, how the tax revenue is used), and finish with a judgement about the extent.
:::

::: example Growth rate
Invented figures: the GDP of Teralia rose from £250 billion to £257.5 billion. Calculate the rate of economic growth. Show your working. (2)

(£257.5bn − £250bn) ÷ £250bn × 100 (1) = 7.5 ÷ 250 × 100 = **3%** (1).
:::

::: example GDP per capita
Invented figures: Belmarra has a GDP of £36 billion and a population of 4.5 million. Calculate GDP per capita. Show your working. (2)

£36,000 million ÷ 4.5 million (1) = **£8,000** per person (1).
:::

::: example A falling growth rate
Invented data: Ostland's growth rate was 4% in Year 1, 2.5% in Year 2 and 1% in Year 3. Explain what happened to Ostland's GDP over the three years. (2)

GDP **rose** in every year, because the growth rate stayed positive (1), but it rose more slowly each year, as the rate of increase fell from 4% to 1% (1).
:::

::: example Education and growth
Explain how an increase in spending on education can lead to a higher rate of economic growth. (2)

Better-educated workers gain more skills, so they are more productive (1), which means the economy can produce more output from its workforce, raising GDP (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — new technology
Invented context: firms in the country of Kestra are adopting robots and AI software quickly, helped by government grants for research. Analyse how changes in technology can increase Kestra's economic growth. (6)

Technology is a determinant of growth because it raises **productivity** (K). Robots and AI let each Kestran worker produce more output per hour, so total output, and so GDP, rises even with the same workforce (Ap/An). Lower production costs let firms cut prices or raise profits; higher profits can be reinvested in more capital, raising capacity further (An). Cheaper, better Kestran products become more competitive abroad, so exports rise and firms expand output to meet the extra demand (An). The government's research grants speed this up, so new products are invented, creating new industries and jobs whose output adds to GDP (Ap/An).
:::

::: q
State two determinants of economic growth other than investment. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): changes in technology; size of the workforce; education and training; availability of natural resources; government policies.
:::

::: q
Explain why GDP per capita may fall even though GDP rises. (2)
---
GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population (1); if the population grows faster than GDP, output per person falls (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one environmental cost of economic growth. (2)
---
E.g. more production and transport burn more fossil fuels (1), increasing carbon emissions and air pollution that harm health and add to climate change (1). Or resource depletion with development.
:::

::: q
Analyse how an increase in the size of the workforce can affect economic growth. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: the workforce is a factor of production, labour (K); more workers (e.g. through migration or more older people working) means firms can fill vacancies and produce more, raising GDP (An); extra workers earn incomes and spend them, raising demand for goods and services, so firms expand further (An); filled skill shortages let firms take on more orders and invest (An); more income tax revenue can be spent on infrastructure, boosting capacity (An); but if the workforce grows faster than output, GDP per capita may not rise (An).
:::

::: watch
- **A falling growth rate is not falling GDP.** If the rate is still above zero, GDP is still rising.
- **Use the same units** before dividing: £ billion ÷ million people needs converting. Give the unit (% or £) and the decimal places asked.
- **Growth rate = change ÷ the ORIGINAL figure**, not the new one.
- **Explaining a trend** needs the direction with figures **and** a reason. Describing alone scores 1.
- **Determinant answers need a chain to more output**: "education" alone is a list; "education → more skilled, productive workers → more output" is an explanation.
- In "evaluate" answers, pair each cost with the benefit it qualifies and say how far, for whom and whether it is sustainable. A summary is not a judgement.
- Do not draw AD/AS diagrams: OCR does not use them.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Following the discovery of large natural gas reserves off its coast, the government of Sarnova has signed deals with energy firms to build drilling platforms and a pipeline. The firms expect to hire 8,000 workers. Gas exports are forecast to bring in £4 billion a year.

Using the information, analyse how the gas discovery is likely to affect Sarnova's GDP. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Sarnova; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: availability of natural resources as a determinant of growth; GDP as the value of output.
- Application: gas reserves; platforms and pipeline; 8,000 workers; £4 billion of exports a year.
- Analysis: building the platforms and pipeline is investment, which adds to output and raises capacity; 8,000 new jobs create incomes that are spent in local shops and services, so other firms raise output too; gas production itself adds up to £4 billion of output a year to GDP; export earnings bring money into the economy, raising incomes further; more tax revenue lets the government spend on infrastructure, raising growth further.

::: test
Invented extract: Over the past ten years, real GDP in the country of Dorran has grown by an average of 6% a year. Average incomes have risen sharply and unemployment has fallen. However, air pollution in the capital city has doubled, two large forests have been cleared for farming and factories, and the richest 10% of households have gained most of the extra income.

Evaluate whether the benefits of economic growth in Dorran outweigh the costs. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: 6% a year; higher incomes; lower unemployment; pollution doubled; forests cleared; richest 10% gained most.
- Analysis: higher incomes raise living standards and spending; lower unemployment cuts benefit spending and raises tax revenue for public services; but pollution harms health, raising health-care costs and lowering quality of life; clearing forests depletes resources and adds to climate change.
- Evaluation: the benefits are not shared fairly if the richest 10% gain most, so for many households the gain may be small (not socially sustainable); pollution and deforestation may damage future output (not environmentally sustainable), so the growth may not last; but extra tax revenue could fund clean technology and benefits, reducing the costs; depends on time period and on whether real GDP per capita rose. Judgement: e.g. benefits outweigh costs in the short run, but not in the long run unless the government uses the extra revenue to tackle pollution and inequality.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The government of Kelvar plans to spend £12 billion over five years on high-speed rail lines and broadband for rural areas. It hopes this will raise the growth rate from 1.2% to 2%. To pay for it, the government will borrow, and it has delayed building two new hospitals.

Evaluate whether this government spending will increase economic growth in Kelvar. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: £12 billion over five years; rail and broadband; growth target from 1.2% to 2%; borrowing; hospitals delayed.
- Analysis: building work creates jobs and incomes, raising spending and output now; faster transport and broadband cut firms' costs and let rural firms reach more customers, raising productivity and capacity; better connections attract investment to rural areas.
- Evaluation: benefits take years to arrive, so the growth target may not be met soon; projects often go over budget; opportunity cost: delayed hospitals may leave workers in poor health, reducing the workforce's productivity; borrowing adds to debt interest, which may later mean higher taxes; growth depends also on business confidence and other determinants. Judgement: e.g. likely to raise long-run growth, but by less and later than hoped, and only if the projects are completed on time.
:::
