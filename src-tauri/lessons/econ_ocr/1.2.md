## What it is

This is the idea that the whole subject rests on. Resources are **scarce** but human wants are **unlimited**, so every consumer, producer and government must make **choices**. Every choice has an **opportunity cost**, and every choice has costs and benefits, including effects on **economic, social and environmental sustainability**.

### Scarce resources and unlimited wants

- **Scarce resources**: the factors of production (land, labour, capital and enterprise) are **finite**. There is only so much farmland, so many skilled workers, so many machines at any time, and their use has a cost.
- **Unlimited wants**: people always want more or better goods and services. Once basic **needs** (food, shelter, clothing) are met, new **wants** appear: a bigger home, a faster phone, more holidays, better public services.

**Scarcity** is the gap between the two: there are not enough resources to satisfy all wants. So choices must be made about how to use them.

Two points that examiners say students get wrong:

- **Scarce does not mean "running out".** A resource can be plentiful in total yet scarce in the economic sense because using it for one thing means it cannot be used for another. Sand, labour and time are all scarce even though none is about to disappear.
- **Scarcity affects everyone**: a consumer with a limited income, a firm with a limited budget and factory space, a government with limited tax revenue, a rich country as well as a poor one.

Signs that a resource is scarce include a **rising price** (people compete to buy it), **rationing** (a hosepipe ban in a dry summer shows water is scarce, as its use for gardens is restricted to keep enough for drinking and hygiene), **waiting lists** (for hospital treatment or social housing) and **shortages** of skilled workers.

### The economic problem and its key questions

The **economic problem** is how to allocate scarce resources between competing uses to satisfy as many wants as possible. It breaks down into key questions every economy has to answer:

| Question | What it asks | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **How should resources be allocated?** | which uses should get the land, labour and capital, and who decides (the market, the government or both) | should a field be used for housing, farming or a solar farm? |
| **What** should be produced? | which goods and services, and in what quantities | should the government spend more on hospitals or on roads? |
| **How** should it be produced? | which methods and which combination of factors | should a car plant use robots or workers? should electricity come from gas or wind? |
| **For whom** should it be produced? | who gets the goods and services produced | those who can afford to pay, or everyone who needs it, e.g. free healthcare and state schools? |

In a market economy, prices and consumers' spending largely answer these questions; in public services such as the NHS, the government answers them. You will see these links again in topics 2.4 and 3.8.

### Opportunity cost

**Opportunity cost** is **the next best alternative given up** when a choice is made. Three rules:

1. It is **one** alternative, the next best, not everything that could have been done.
2. It is the **alternative itself**, not the money spent. If you spend £30 on a concert ticket rather than a pair of trainers, the opportunity cost is the trainers, not the £30.
3. It applies to **time and resources**, not just money.

| Decision-maker | Choice | Opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumer** | spends birthday money on a games console | the next best item, e.g. a new bike |
| **Worker** | leaves a salaried job to set up her own business | the salary (and job security) she would have earned |
| **Student** | works a Saturday shift | the revision or leisure time given up |
| **Producer** | a farmer plants wheat on a field | the potatoes that field could have grown |
| **Government** | spends £50 million on a new prison | e.g. the new schools that money could have built |

Opportunity cost can be calculated when outputs are measured. If a field can grow **either** 200 tonnes of wheat **or** 500 tonnes of potatoes, switching a quarter of it to potatoes gains 125 tonnes of potatoes and the opportunity cost is 50 tonnes of wheat.

### Costs and benefits of economic choices, and sustainability

Every choice has **costs** and **benefits**. Some fall on the decision-maker (**private** costs and benefits); some fall on other people who were not part of the decision (**external** costs and benefits, which you meet in 3.8). Some appear now (**short run**); others build up over years (**long run**).

OCR asks you to judge choices using three lenses of **sustainability**: whether a choice can continue without harming the ability of people in the future to meet their own needs.

| Type | Asks | Improved by | Harmed by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Economic sustainability** | can incomes, jobs and output be kept up over time? | new long-lasting industries, investment, skills, stable jobs | relying on one industry that may close, using up a resource the economy depends on, unaffordable debt |
| **Social sustainability** | can communities keep healthy, fair and good-quality lives? | good schools, healthcare, affordable housing, local services, low crime | job losses breaking up communities, rising inequality, loss of local services, poor housing |
| **Environmental sustainability** | can the natural environment keep supporting life and production? | renewable energy, recycling, protecting habitats, cleaner transport | pollution, carbon emissions, overfishing, deforestation, loss of green space |

A single choice often helps one lens and harms another. A new quarry may raise **economic** sustainability (jobs, income) while harming **environmental** sustainability (dust, noise, lost countryside) and, through heavy lorries on village roads, **social** sustainability. Strong evaluation weighs these up: which effects are bigger, who bears them, and whether they last.

::: terms
- **Scarcity**: the situation where finite resources are not enough to satisfy all of people's wants.
- **Scarce resources**: factors of production that are limited in supply, so using them one way means they cannot be used another way.
- **Unlimited wants**: the desire of people for ever more and better goods and services, which can never all be met.
- **Economic problem**: how to allocate scarce resources between competing uses to satisfy as many wants as possible.
- **Allocation of resources**: the way land, labour, capital and enterprise are shared out between different uses.
- **Opportunity cost**: the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
- **Sustainability**: meeting the needs of people today without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Economic sustainability**: keeping incomes, jobs and output going over the long term.
- **Social sustainability**: keeping communities healthy, fair and able to enjoy a good quality of life.
- **Environmental sustainability**: protecting the natural environment so that it can keep supporting life and production.
:::

::: method
1. **Spot the question type.** Scarcity, a key economic question, opportunity cost or sustainability? Underline the decision-maker (consumer, firm, worker, council, government).
2. **Opportunity cost answers**: name the **single next best alternative** from the information, not the money. "The opportunity cost is the swimming pool the council could have built instead."
3. **Key questions**: decide whether the choice is about **what** to make, **how** to make it or **for whom**, and say why.
4. **Calculations**: state the method, show the working and give units (tonnes, £, %).
5. **Sustainability**: name the lens (economic, social, environmental) and explain the effect with "this means that…".
6. **6-mark evaluate (Ap1 An2 Ev3)**: use the extract's figures; analyse one benefit and one cost in chains; weigh them ("however, this depends on…", "in the long run…", "for local residents rather than the council…"); finish with a judgement that answers the exact question, e.g. "to a large extent, because…".
:::

::: example Explain what is meant by opportunity cost
Explain what is meant by opportunity cost. (2)

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative that is given up (1) when a choice is made, e.g. a student who spends the evening at a part-time job gives up the revision she would otherwise have done (1).
:::

::: example Explaining scarcity using a context
Invented context: in a hot summer the water company for Elmsford bans the use of hosepipes and sprinklers. Explain how the hosepipe ban shows that water is a scarce resource. (2)

There is not enough water available to meet every use people want, such as drinking, washing and watering gardens (1), so a choice has to be made: water is kept for essential uses and its use for gardens is restricted (1).
:::

::: example Calculating opportunity cost
Invented data: a field can produce either 120 tonnes of wheat or 300 tonnes of potatoes. The farmer switches 40% of the field from wheat to potatoes. Calculate the opportunity cost of the extra potatoes. Show your working. (2)

Wheat given up = 40% of 120 tonnes = 0.4 × 120 (1) = **48 tonnes of wheat** (1).
(Potatoes gained = 0.4 × 300 = 120 tonnes, so each tonne of potatoes costs 0.4 tonnes of wheat.)
:::

::: example Identifying the key economic question
A city council has 200 new council flats. It decides they will be let first to nurses, teachers and care workers on low pay, rather than sold at market prices. Which key economic question is the council answering? Explain your answer. (2)

"For whom should goods and services be produced?" (1) — the council is deciding who receives the flats, giving them to low-paid key workers rather than to whoever can pay the most (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — a council's choice
Invented extract: Tollerby Council has £8 million to spend. It can build a bypass round the town centre, which carries 15,000 lorries and cars a day, or turn a disused railway yard into a park with a cycle path. Evaluate whether the council should choose the bypass, including the impact on environmental sustainability. (6)

The bypass would take much of the 15,000 vehicles a day out of the centre, so journeys for local firms would be quicker and cheaper, which could attract shoppers and protect town-centre jobs, improving economic sustainability (Ap/An). Less traffic in the centre would also cut air pollution where people live and shop (An). However, the bypass itself would be built across countryside, destroying habitats, and faster roads may encourage more car journeys overall, so total emissions might not fall (Ev). The opportunity cost is the park, which would provide green space and a cycle path that cuts car use without new roads (Ev). On balance, the bypass is the better choice only if the town's traffic problem is mainly through-lorries, because then it moves pollution away from homes; if most trips are local, the park and cycle path does more for environmental sustainability at the same cost (Ev — supported judgement).
:::

::: q
State two of the key economic questions that every economy must answer. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): what should be produced; how should it be produced; for whom should it be produced; how should resources be allocated.
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by scarce resources. (2)
---
Resources / factors of production are finite / limited in supply (1), so there are not enough to satisfy all wants and choices must be made about how to use them (1). Do not credit "resources that are running out" on its own.
:::

::: q
Kofi leaves his £28,000-a-year job as a chef to open his own food van. Explain what the opportunity cost of his decision is. (2)
---
The opportunity cost is the next best alternative he gives up (1): his £28,000 salary / the security of his job as a chef (1).
:::

::: q
A car manufacturer decides to replace a production line staffed by 200 workers with robots. Which key economic question is it answering? Explain your answer. (2)
---
How should goods be produced? (1) — it is choosing a production method / combination of factors, using more capital and less labour (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: a government's budget for new projects is £60 billion. It spends £9 billion on a new high-speed rail line. Calculate the percentage of the budget spent on the rail line and state one possible opportunity cost. (3)
---
£9 billion ÷ £60 billion × 100 (1) = 15% (1). Opportunity cost: the next best project, e.g. new hospitals/schools that £9 billion could have funded (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse the possible effects on social sustainability of a large factory closing in a small town. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point.
Indicative content: workers lose jobs and income (K), so families have less to spend and may fall into poverty, harming wellbeing and health (An); local shops lose customers and may close, so the town loses services and becomes less pleasant to live in (An); young people move away to find work, so schools and clubs lose numbers and the community weakens (An); long-term unemployment can raise crime and stress, increasing pressure on public services (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Scarcity is not "running out".** It means finite resources compared with unlimited wants, so choices must be made. The 2025 examiners noted this confusion.
- **Opportunity cost is the next best alternative**, one item, and it is not the money spent. "£2 million" is not an opportunity cost; "the new library the £2 million could have built" is.
- Learn the **key questions with an example each**: what (hospitals or roads), how (robots or workers), for whom (those who pay or those in need).
- In sustainability answers, **name the type** (economic, social, environmental) and keep to the one the question asks about. An answer about pollution in a question on social sustainability will score little unless linked to health or communities.
- **Answer for the group named**: residents, the council, a firm, future generations.
- A 6-mark evaluate needs a **judgement**, not a summary: say which side wins and why, and on what it depends.
:::

::: test
Invented data: a factory can make either 2,000 chairs or 800 tables a week. It decides to switch 25% of its capacity from chairs to tables. Calculate the opportunity cost, in chairs, of the extra tables. Show your working. (2)
---
25% × 2,000 = 0.25 × 2,000 (1) = 500 chairs (1). (The extra output is 0.25 × 800 = 200 tables.) Correct answer alone = 2.
:::

::: test
Explain one reason why scarcity affects governments as well as consumers. (2)
---
A government has a limited budget, raised mainly from taxes (1), so spending more on one service, such as healthcare, means less is available for another, such as defence or education / it faces an opportunity cost (1).
:::

State two signs that a resource is scarce. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): its price rises; rationing / restrictions on its use (e.g. a hosepipe ban); waiting lists or queues; shortages / unfilled vacancies; people competing to buy it.

::: test
Invented extract: Harrowgate Primary Care Trust has a fixed budget of £12 million for the year. It must choose between opening a new walk-in health centre and extending a mental-health service with a waiting list of 1,800 people.

Using the information, analyse the costs to the local community of choosing the walk-in centre. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to the case; L2 3–4 — correct but mostly single-step points; L1 1–2 — isolated knowledge. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: scarcity; a fixed budget forces a choice; opportunity cost is the next best alternative.
- Application: £12 million fixed budget; 1,800 people waiting.
- Analysis: the opportunity cost is the extended mental-health service, so the waiting list of 1,800 stays or grows; people waiting longer may become more ill, so they may be unable to work, lowering household incomes and spending in local shops; their families may have to give up work to care for them; untreated conditions may lead to more costly emergency care later, putting further pressure on the trust's fixed budget; social sustainability of the community is reduced as wellbeing falls.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Porthcarrow is a fishing village. In five years the number of homes used as holiday lets has risen from 40 to 190, out of 520 homes. Visitors spend heavily in cafés and boat-trip firms, and house prices have doubled. The primary school's numbers have fallen from 110 to 62, and the village shop now opens only in summer.

Evaluate the extent to which the growth of holiday lets has had a negative effect on social sustainability in Porthcarrow. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation with a fully supported judgement on "the extent to which"; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: holiday lets 40 → 190 (over a third of 520 homes); house prices doubled; school roll 110 → 62 (down about 44%); shop open only in summer.
- Analysis (negative): fewer homes for local families and prices doubled, so young locals are priced out and move away; fewer children means the school may close, and services shrink, so the community weakens in winter.
- Analysis (positive): visitor spending supports cafés and boat-trip jobs, giving local people income, which may let some families stay; tourism tax revenue could fund services.
- Evaluation: the jobs are seasonal and often low paid, so they may not support year-round community life; the effect depends on whether income stays in the village or goes to owners living elsewhere; the falling school roll suggests the harm is already serious and long-lasting. Judgement: e.g. to a large extent negative, because losing the school and year-round shop removes services a community needs, which tourist spending in summer does not replace.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The government of an imaginary country, Kestria, has a choice for a large area of upland. It can allow a company to open an open-cast coal mine, creating 1,200 jobs for 25 years, or turn the area into a national park with walking trails, expected to bring in tourists and support about 300 jobs.

Evaluate whether Kestria's government should allow the coal mine, including the impact on economic, social and environmental sustainability. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — applied analysis of at least two types of sustainability and a supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some analysis, unsupported judgement; L1 1–2 — assertions only. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: 1,200 jobs for 25 years versus about 300 jobs; upland area; coal.
- Analysis (for the mine): four times as many jobs, so higher incomes and tax revenue, supporting local shops and services (economic and social sustainability) for 25 years.
- Analysis (against): burning coal raises carbon emissions; open-cast mining destroys landscape and habitats (environmental); dust and lorries harm residents' health; after 25 years the jobs end, so the area may face structural unemployment (economic sustainability falls in the long run).
- Evaluation: the opportunity cost is the national park, whose 300 jobs could last indefinitely; the comparison depends on whether the tourism forecast is reliable and whether mining skills transfer to other jobs; global moves away from coal may shorten the mine's life. Judgement: e.g. reject the mine, because its gains are temporary while the environmental damage and the job cliff-edge after 25 years are long-lasting.
:::
