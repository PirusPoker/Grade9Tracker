## What it is

The law of demand tells you that a higher price means less is bought. **Price elasticity of demand (PED)** tells you **how much** less. It decides whether a firm should raise or cut its price, how much consumers suffer when a price jumps, and how much of a supply shock shows up as a higher price. This topic covers the meaning of PED, elastic, inelastic and unitary demand, **drawing demand curves of different elasticity**, the factors that make demand elastic or inelastic, and the importance of PED for **consumers and producers**, which the spec asks you to evaluate.

### Meaning and calculation

**Price elasticity of demand** measures the **responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price**.

**PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price**

where **% change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100**.

Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, PED is **negative**. Judge it by its **size**, ignoring the minus sign:

| Value (ignoring the sign) | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| greater than 1 | **price elastic** | % change in quantity demanded is **bigger** than the % change in price |
| less than 1 | **price inelastic** | % change in quantity demanded is **smaller** than the % change in price |
| exactly 1 | **unitary** | the two % changes are equal |
| 0 | **perfectly inelastic** | quantity demanded does not change at all when price changes |
| infinity | **perfectly elastic** | any rise in price makes quantity demanded fall to zero |

**Worked example 1 (inelastic).** Invented data: a theme park raises its day ticket from £40 to £44, and daily visitors fall from 5,000 to 4,700.
- % change in price = (44 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = +10%
- % change in quantity = (4,700 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = −6%
- PED = −6 ÷ 10 = **−0.6**, so demand is price inelastic.
- Revenue (price × quantity) goes from £40 × 5,000 = £200,000 to £44 × 4,700 = £206,800: it **rises by £6,800**.

**Worked example 2 (elastic).** Invented data: a streaming service cuts its monthly price from £8 to £6, and subscribers rise from 20,000 to 27,000.
- % change in price = (6 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = −25%
- % change in quantity = (27,000 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 × 100 = +35%
- PED = 35 ÷ −25 = **−1.4**, so demand is price elastic.
- Revenue goes from £8 × 20,000 = £160,000 to £6 × 27,000 = £162,000: it **rises by £2,000**.

### What makes demand elastic or inelastic?

| Factor | More **elastic** when… | More **inelastic** when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Number and closeness of substitutes** | many close substitutes (one brand of crisps) | few or no substitutes (tap water, insulin) |
| **Necessity or luxury** | a luxury or non-essential (designer handbags, a holiday) | a necessity (basic food, energy for heating) |
| **Share of income** | takes a large share of income (a car, a sofa) | takes a tiny share of income (salt, matches) |
| **Habit and addiction** | bought occasionally, without habit | habit-forming or addictive (cigarettes, coffee for some) |
| **Brand loyalty** | little loyalty, easy to switch | strong loyalty (a favourite phone brand) |
| **Time** | in the **long run**, consumers find substitutes (switching to an electric car when petrol prices stay high) | in the **short run**, people cannot change habits or equipment quickly |
| **How widely the product is defined** | narrowly defined (one supermarket's own-brand cola) | broadly defined (all soft drinks; "petrol" rather than one petrol station's petrol) |

### PED and total revenue

**Total revenue = price × quantity sold.** PED tells a firm which way revenue moves when it changes its price:

| Demand is… | Raise the price | Cut the price |
|---|---|---|
| **Price inelastic** | revenue **rises** (quantity falls by a smaller %) | revenue **falls** |
| **Price elastic** | revenue **falls** (quantity falls by a bigger %) | revenue **rises** |
| **Unitary** | revenue unchanged | revenue unchanged |

### Why PED matters: consumers and producers

**For producers:**
- **Pricing**: a firm with inelastic demand (few substitutes, loyal customers) can raise prices and earn more revenue; a firm with elastic demand should avoid price rises and may gain revenue by cutting prices.
- **Passing on cost rises**: if energy or wage costs rise, a firm facing inelastic demand can pass most of the rise to customers; with elastic demand it must absorb it, squeezing profit.
- **Supply shocks**: when supply falls (bad harvest, a factory fire), inelastic demand means the price jumps a lot and quantity falls little.

**For consumers:**
- People who buy **necessities with inelastic demand** (energy, fuel, rent, basic food, medicines) cannot easily cut back when prices rise, so price rises hit their **real incomes** hard, especially those on low incomes, for whom these items are a large share of spending.
- Consumers with **elastic demand** are protected: if one brand gets dearer they switch, so firms must keep prices competitive.
- **Indirect taxes** (e.g. on tobacco or fuel) fall heavily on consumers when demand is inelastic, because firms can pass the tax on in higher prices.

**Evaluation points for 6-markers**: PED figures are **estimates** and may be wrong; PED **changes over time** (in the long run substitutes appear); **rivals may react** to a price change; other factors (income, tastes, advertising) may shift demand at the same time, so the predicted revenue change may not happen; a price rise that raises revenue may still damage **reputation** or loyalty.

::: terms
- **Price elasticity of demand (PED)**: the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price, calculated as % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.
- **Price elastic demand**: when the percentage change in quantity demanded is greater than the percentage change in price (PED above 1, ignoring the sign).
- **Price inelastic demand**: when the percentage change in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage change in price (PED below 1, ignoring the sign).
- **Unitary elasticity**: when the percentage change in quantity demanded equals the percentage change in price (PED of 1).
- **Perfectly inelastic demand**: quantity demanded does not change at all when price changes, shown by a vertical demand curve.
- **Perfectly elastic demand**: any rise in price causes quantity demanded to fall to zero, shown by a horizontal demand curve.
- **Total revenue**: the money a firm receives from sales, calculated as price × quantity sold.
- **Substitute**: a good that can be bought instead of another to satisfy the same want.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions**: always use "**percentage** change in quantity demanded" compared with "**percentage** change in price". "Demand does not change much" is worth 1 at most.
2. **Calculations**: % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100 for quantity and for price; PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP; state elastic or inelastic. Revenue = price × quantity before and after. Show working, give units.
3. **Interpreting a value**: −0.5 means a 10% price rise cuts quantity demanded by 5% (inelastic); −2 means a 10% rise cuts it by 20% (elastic).
4. **"Draw and label demand curves of different elasticity" (2)**: labelled axes (1); a steeper curve labelled inelastic and a shallower curve labelled elastic, crossing (1).
5. **"Using a diagram, analyse…" (6)**: draw the right shape of curve for the elasticity, show the shift and P/Q changes, and build chains about revenue, consumers or producers from the diagram.
6. **Evaluate the importance of PED (6)**: say who (consumers or producers), show how PED changes the outcome, then weigh it: time period, accuracy of estimates, rivals, other factors. End with a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain what is meant by price inelastic demand
Explain what is meant by price inelastic demand. (2)

Price inelastic demand is when the percentage change in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage change in price (1), so PED is less than 1 (ignoring the sign), e.g. a 10% rise in the price of electricity cuts the quantity demanded by only 2% (1).
:::

::: example Calculating PED
Invented data: a hairdresser raises the price of a cut from £25 to £30, and the number of cuts a week falls from 120 to 90. Calculate the PED. Show your working. (2)

% change in price = (30 − 25) ÷ 25 × 100 = +20%; % change in quantity = (90 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = −25% (1).
PED = −25 ÷ 20 = **−1.25**: price elastic (1).
:::

::: example Revenue after a price change
Using the hairdresser data above (£25 → £30; 120 → 90 cuts a week), calculate the change in weekly revenue. Show your working. (2)

Before: £25 × 120 = £3,000; after: £30 × 90 = £2,700 (1).
Revenue **falls by £300 a week** (1), as expected when price rises and demand is elastic.
:::

Invented context: before a new rival bakery opened, demand for Rook's Bakery's bread was price inelastic; now it is price elastic. Draw and label a diagram showing the original and new demand curves. (Sketch it on paper, or describe it in words.) (2)

Axes labelled Price and Quantity (1). A steep curve labelled "original, inelastic demand" and a shallower curve crossing it labelled "new, elastic demand" (1).
:::

::: example Using a diagram, analyse — supply shock with inelastic demand
Invented context: a storm closes the only bridge to the island of Skerra for three weeks, so tankers can deliver much less fuel to its petrol stations. Using a diagram, analyse how this decrease in supply affects the price of fuel on Skerra. (6)

Diagram: steep (inelastic) demand curve D; supply shifts left from S to S1; price rises sharply from P to P1 while quantity falls only a little, Q to Q1 (K1 + An1).

Fewer deliveries mean less fuel is available at every price, so supply shifts left (Ap). At the old price there is now a shortage, so petrol stations raise prices until the market clears at P1 (An). Because demand for fuel on Skerra is price inelastic (islanders need to drive to work and there are few alternatives, such as buses), the quantity demanded falls only a little as the price rises, so the price must rise a long way to ration the smaller supply (An). Drivers therefore pay much more for each litre and keep buying, which leaves them less income for other goods (An). Petrol stations' revenue may even rise despite selling less, because the percentage price rise is larger than the percentage fall in sales (An).
:::

Explain what is meant by price elastic demand. (2)
---
When the percentage change in quantity demanded is greater than the percentage change in price (1), so PED is greater than 1, e.g. a 10% price rise causes a 25% fall in quantity demanded (1).
:::

::: q
State two factors that make the demand for a product price inelastic. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): few or no close substitutes; it is a necessity; it takes a small share of income; habit/addiction; strong brand loyalty; a short time period to respond; broadly defined product.
:::

::: q
Invented data: PED for a brand of trainers is −2.5. The firm cuts its price by 8%. Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded. (2)
---
%ΔQd = PED × %ΔP = −2.5 × −8 (1) = +20% (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a firm facing price inelastic demand might raise its price. (2)
---
The percentage fall in quantity demanded will be smaller than the percentage rise in price (1), so total revenue will rise / profit may rise (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why demand for petrol may become more price elastic over time. (2)
---
In the long run consumers can find substitutes (1), such as buying electric cars or using public transport/cycling, so a rise in the price of petrol leads to a bigger fall in quantity demanded than in the short run (1).
:::

::: q
Using a diagram, analyse why a firm selling a product with price elastic demand may increase its revenue by cutting its price. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 correct shallow demand curve with P → P1 and Q → Q1 used in developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: shallow demand curve; price cut shown as a movement down D, Q rises by more than P falls (K1 + An1). Many close substitutes mean customers switch to the firm when it cuts its price (An); the % rise in quantity is greater than the % fall in price (An); revenue lost on existing customers (smaller price on each unit) is outweighed by revenue from extra units sold (An); so total revenue (P × Q, the area under the point) rises (An). Application: a named product with many substitutes.
:::

::: watch
- **Elastic and inelastic are not interchangeable.** The 2024 examiners found many students muddled them and so got consequences the wrong way round. Inelastic = quantity responds **less** than price, in **percentage** terms.
- Always say **"percentage change"** in definitions.
- PED is **negative**; judge elastic or inelastic by the **size** of the number.
- **Revenue is not profit.** A price rise can raise revenue but you need costs to say anything about profit.
- On a diagram, inelastic = **steep**, elastic = **shallow**, and label which is which.
- "Using a diagram" questions on supply shocks need a **steep demand curve** for inelastic goods; without it the large price rise cannot be shown.
- **Answer for the group named.** "The importance of PED for consumers" is about consumers paying more or switching, not the firm's revenue.
:::

- Analysis: demand is price elastic because there are seven close substitutes, so a price cut draws customers from rivals and revenue should rise (e.g. 100 meals at £12 = £1,200 becomes about 127 meals at £10 = about £1,270); more customers may also buy drinks and sides.
- Analysis (against): profit per meal falls from £6 to £4, so on these figures profit falls (100 × £6 = £600 versus about 127 × £4 = about £508) even though revenue rises; rivals may match the cut, so the extra customers never arrive.
- Evaluation: the survey PED is an estimate for the whole street, not Spice Route itself; the outcome depends on rivals' reactions and on whether the cut is temporary (promotion) or permanent. Judgement: e.g. the cut raises revenue but not profit, so Spice Route should only cut if it can win long-term regulars or if rivals are unlikely to respond.
