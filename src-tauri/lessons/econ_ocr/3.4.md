## What it is

**Price stability** is one of the government's main economic objectives. This topic covers what **price stability** and **inflation** mean, the difference between **real and nominal values**, how inflation is measured with the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, how to **calculate the effect of inflation on prices**, how to **analyse inflation figures**, and the **causes** of inflation and its **consequences for consumers, producers, savers and the government** (which the spec asks you to evaluate).

### Inflation and price stability

- **Inflation** is a **sustained rise in the general level of prices** in an economy over time. It is not one product getting dearer: it is prices **on average** rising.
- The **rate of inflation** is the **percentage change in the average price level over a year**. Inflation of 4% means that, on average, prices are 4% higher than a year ago.
- **Deflation** is a **fall** in the general price level (a negative inflation rate).
- **Disinflation** is when the inflation rate falls but stays positive: prices are still rising, just more slowly.

**Price stability** means prices rising at a **low and steady** rate, so people and firms can plan ahead. In the UK, the government sets an inflation **target of 2%** for CPI inflation, and the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee uses interest rates to try to meet it (see 3.6).

Two traps:
- Price stability is **not zero inflation**: a low, steady positive rate is the aim.
- Price stability is **not just a constant rate**: inflation stuck at a steady 15% is not price stability, because prices are rising fast.

### Real and nominal values

- A **nominal** value is measured in money terms at **current prices**, not adjusted for inflation.
- A **real** value is **adjusted for inflation**, so it shows what the money can actually **buy**.

A quick approximation: **real change ≈ nominal change − inflation rate**.

| Nominal pay rise | Inflation | Real pay | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6% | 2% | about +4% | the worker can buy more |
| 3% | 3% | about 0% | the worker can buy the same as before |
| 3% | 5% | about −2% | a **real pay cut**: the worker can buy less, even though they have more money |

So a rise in **nominal income** with a fall in **real income** means a person **can no longer buy the same quantity** of goods and services, not that they have less money. The same idea applies to **real GDP** (output adjusted for inflation; see 3.1) and real interest on savings.

### Measuring inflation: the Consumer Price Index

The **CPI** is the main measure of UK inflation, compiled by the Office for National Statistics. In outline:

1. A **survey of household spending** finds out what a typical household buys.
2. A **basket** of several hundred goods and services is chosen to represent that spending (food, clothing, fuel, rent, transport, leisure and so on). Items are updated each year as spending habits change.
3. Each item is given a **weight** according to the **share of household spending** it takes: items people spend more on (such as transport or housing costs) count more than items they spend little on.
4. **Prices** of the items are **collected every month** from many shops, websites and places around the country.
5. The weighted average price is turned into an **index number**, with a **base year = 100**.
6. The **percentage change in the index over twelve months** is the rate of inflation.

**Index numbers.** If the CPI is 100 in the base year and 106 a year later, prices have risen by 6%. Between two years that are not the base year, use the percentage change formula:

**Inflation (%) = (index this year − index last year) ÷ index last year × 100**

Invented example: CPI 125 → 130. Inflation = 5 ÷ 125 × 100 = **4%** (not 5%).

**Weights (invented).** A basket has food (weight 0.2) up 10%, housing (0.5) up 4% and transport (0.3) up 2%. Weighted inflation = 0.2 × 10 + 0.5 × 4 + 0.3 × 2 = 2 + 2 + 0.6 = **4.6%**. Food rose fastest, but housing matters most because it has the largest weight.

### Calculating the effect of inflation on prices

**New price = old price × (1 + inflation rate ÷ 100)**

- A £40 item with 5% inflation: £40 × 1.05 = **£42**.
- Over two years at 5% then 3%: £40 × 1.05 × 1.03 = **£43.26**.
- To find the inflation rate from prices: (new − old) ÷ old × 100. A £2.50 loaf rising to £2.70: 0.20 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = **8%**.

### Analysing inflation figures

Invented data for the country of Pellan:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CPI inflation (%) | 1.8 | 2.4 | 7.5 | 5.0 | 2.6 |

- Year 1: close to a 2% target (price stability).
- Year 3: a sharp rise, e.g. after an energy price shock (cost-push) or a boom in spending (demand-pull).
- Years 4–5: inflation falls, but **prices are still rising** (disinflation), just more slowly. The price level is still higher than ever.

### Causes of inflation

| | **Demand-pull** | **Cost-push** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | total spending rises faster than the economy's ability to produce, so firms raise prices | firms' costs of production rise, and they pass them on as higher prices |
| Causes | low interest rates; income tax cuts; rising confidence and borrowing; more government spending; strong export demand | rising wages not matched by productivity; higher energy, fuel and raw-material prices; a weaker pound making imported materials dearer; higher indirect taxes such as VAT |
| Typical sign | strong growth, low unemployment, shortages | rising costs while growth may be weak |

### Consequences of inflation

| Group | Costs | Possible gains or qualifications |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | if prices rise faster than incomes, **real incomes fall** and living standards drop; people on **fixed incomes** (some pensions, benefits not raised) hit hardest; uncertainty | if wages rise faster than prices, real incomes still rise; borrowers find the real value of their debts falls |
| **Producers** | rising costs of materials and wages; **uncertainty** makes planning and investment harder; **menu costs** of changing prices; if UK prices rise faster than abroad, **exports become less competitive** and imports more attractive | low, steady inflation can let firms raise prices and profits; the real value of their loans falls |
| **Savers** | if the interest rate is **below** inflation, the **real value** of savings falls (a negative real interest rate); this discourages saving | if the interest rate is above inflation, savings still grow in real terms |
| **Government** | higher spending on benefits and pensions linked to prices; higher costs for public services; pressure to raise interest rates, which may slow growth; public-sector pay demands | more tax revenue (VAT on higher prices; **fiscal drag**, where rising money incomes push people into higher tax bands if thresholds are not raised); the real value of government debt falls |

When evaluating, the key questions are: **how high** and **how stable** the rate is, whether **incomes and interest rates** keep pace with prices, and **who** holds savings or debts.

::: terms
- **Inflation**: a sustained rise in the general level of prices in an economy over time.
- **Rate of inflation**: the percentage change in the average price level over a year.
- **Price stability**: a low and steady rate of inflation, such as the UK's 2% CPI target, so people and firms can plan ahead.
- **Deflation**: a fall in the general level of prices, shown by a negative inflation rate.
- **Nominal value**: a value measured in money at current prices, not adjusted for inflation.
- **Real value**: a value adjusted for inflation, showing what the money can actually buy.
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**: a weighted index of the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by households, used to measure inflation.
- **Weight**: the importance given to an item in the CPI, based on its share of household spending.
- **Base year**: the year whose index is set at 100, against which later prices are compared.
- **Demand-pull inflation**: inflation caused by total spending rising faster than the economy can increase output.
- **Cost-push inflation**: inflation caused by rising costs of production, such as wages, energy or imported materials, being passed on as higher prices.
- **Fiscal drag**: when rising money incomes pull people into paying more tax because tax thresholds are not raised with inflation.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions (2)**: "sustained rise in the **general** price level" (1) + measured as the % change over a year / by the CPI (1). Price stability = low **and** steady.
2. **"Explain how inflation is measured using the CPI" (2)**: basket of goods and services, weighted by spending (1) + prices collected monthly and the % change in the index over a year (1).
3. **Calculations (2)**: new price = old × (1 + rate ÷ 100); inflation from index numbers = change ÷ old index × 100. Show working; give £ or %.
4. **Real versus nominal (2)**: compare the money change with inflation; say whether the person can buy more or less.
5. **Analyse (6)**: chain from a cause (energy prices, wages, low interest rates) through firms' costs or total spending to higher prices, using the extract; no evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse the effect on the group named (consumers, producers, savers or government), pair each point with a qualification (size and stability of the rate, whether incomes or interest keep up, who saves or borrows) and finish with a judgement.
:::

::: example Real and nominal pay
Invented figures: Chloe's pay rose by 2.5% while inflation was 6%. Explain what happened to her real pay. (2)

::: example Analyse — a rise in energy prices
Invented context: world gas and electricity prices have risen by 50% this year, and firms in the country of Tarsk use a lot of energy. Analyse how the rise in energy prices may cause inflation in Tarsk. (6)

A rise in energy prices raises firms' costs of production, a cause of **cost-push inflation** (K). Tarsk's manufacturers, bakeries and transport firms must pay 50% more for energy, so their costs per unit rise sharply (Ap/An). To protect profit margins they pass the costs on as higher prices for goods and delivery (An). Because energy is used by almost every firm, prices rise across the whole economy, not just for one product, so the general price level rises (An). Households also pay more for heating, so workers may demand higher wages to keep their real pay, which raises firms' costs again and causes a further round of price rises, a wage–price spiral (An).
:::

---
Demand-pull is caused by total spending rising faster than output can increase (1); cost-push is caused by rising costs of production, such as wages or energy, being passed on as higher prices (1).

::: q
Explain why weights are used in the CPI. (2)
---
Households spend more on some items than others (1), so items with a larger share of spending are given more importance, making the index reflect the effect of price changes on a typical household (1).

::: q
Explain one consequence of high inflation for savers. (2)
---
If the interest rate on savings is below inflation (1), the real value of savings falls, so savers can buy less with their money and may be discouraged from saving (1).

::: q
Analyse how low interest rates can cause inflation. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: low interest rates make borrowing cheap and saving less rewarding (K); consumers borrow more for cars, holidays and homes, so spending rises (An); firms borrow to invest in machinery and buildings, adding to spending (An); if total spending grows faster than firms can raise output, shortages appear and firms raise prices: demand-pull inflation (An); higher demand for houses raises house prices and rents (An); firms competing for workers raise wages, which raises costs and prices further (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Inflation is a rise in the GENERAL price level**, not one product getting dearer.
- **Price stability = low AND steady.** A constant 15% is not stable prices, and zero is not the target.
- **Falling inflation is not falling prices.** If the rate is still positive, prices are still rising.
- **Real versus nominal**: a nominal rise below inflation is a real cut. The person can buy less, but they do not have "less money".
- **Inflation from index numbers** is change ÷ the OLD index × 100, not just the difference (unless the old index is 100).
- **A CPI answer needs weights and the basket**, not just "they measure prices".
- Answer for the group the question names (consumers, producers, savers or government), and only say savers lose if the interest rate is below inflation.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: In the country of Galvin, unemployment has fallen to a record low and firms report shortages of workers. Interest rates have been at 1% for three years, and household borrowing has risen by 20%. The government cut income tax last year. Inflation has risen from 2% to 6%.

Using the information, analyse the causes of the rise in inflation in Galvin. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Galvin; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: demand-pull and cost-push inflation.
- Application: record low unemployment; worker shortages; interest rates at 1%; borrowing up 20%; income tax cut; inflation 2% → 6%.
- Analysis: low interest rates made borrowing cheap, so household borrowing and spending rose; the income tax cut raised disposable income, adding to spending; with unemployment at a record low, firms cannot raise output fast enough, so demand outstrips supply and prices rise (demand-pull); worker shortages force firms to offer higher wages, raising costs, which are passed on as higher prices (cost-push); higher prices lead workers to demand further pay rises.

::: test
Invented extract: In the country of Merrow, inflation has risen to 8%, while banks pay an average of 3% interest on savings accounts. Many retired people in Merrow rely on their savings for income. The central bank says it expects inflation to fall to 3% within two years.

Evaluate the consequences of high inflation for savers in Merrow. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: inflation 8%; savings interest 3%; retired people rely on savings; inflation expected to fall to 3% in two years.
- Analysis: the interest rate is 5 percentage points below inflation, so the real value of savings falls by about 5% a year; retired savers can buy fewer goods and services with their interest and capital, so living standards fall; saving becomes less attractive, so people may spend now instead, adding to inflation.
- Evaluation: the loss may be temporary if inflation falls to 3% as expected and banks raise savings rates; savers who also own houses or shares may see those assets rise in value; savers with fixed-rate accounts cannot switch; those who also borrow gain as the real value of their debts falls; retired people with no other income suffer most.
