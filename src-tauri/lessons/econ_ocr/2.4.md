## What it is

Demand (2.2a) tells you what buyers want at each price; supply (2.3a) tells you what sellers offer at each price. **Price** is where the two meet. This topic covers **price as a reflection of worth** and its role in an **efficient distribution of resources**, what **equilibrium price and quantity** mean, how the market gets there, and how to **draw and analyse** shifts in demand and supply. It is one of the most examined topics on J205/01: expect a diagram in at least one 6-mark answer.

### Price as a reflection of worth

The price a consumer is willing to pay shows how much **worth (value, or satisfaction)** they place on the good **compared with the other things** they could buy with the same money. If someone pays £12 for a cinema ticket rather than spend it on a takeaway, they value the film at least as much as the takeaway.

Prices do three jobs in a market economy:

| Job | What it means | Example (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| **Signal** | a rising price tells producers that consumers value the good more, or that it has become scarcer | the price of electric bikes rises, telling firms that buyers want more of them |
| **Incentive** | a higher price makes it more profitable to produce, so firms move resources into making it | bike makers hire more workers and switch factory space to e-bikes |
| **Rationing** | when a good is scarce, price decides who gets it: those willing **and able** to pay the most | only buyers who value an e-bike above the new price buy one |

Together these give an **efficient distribution of resources**: land, labour, capital and enterprise flow towards the goods consumers value most and away from goods they value less, with no central planner. This is the price mechanism answering the basic questions from 1.2: **what** to produce (what people will pay for), **how** to produce (the cheapest method keeps costs below price) and **for whom** (those who pay).

**A limit to remember for evaluation**: price reflects **ability to pay** as well as worth. A wealthy person may outbid someone who values a good far more but has a lower income, so the market outcome is efficient in one sense but not necessarily **fair**. Some goods (health care, education) are therefore not left entirely to price.

### Equilibrium price and quantity

**Equilibrium** is where **quantity demanded equals quantity supplied**. At the **equilibrium price** (P) the market clears: every buyer who wants the good at that price gets it, and every seller who wants to sell at that price can. The amount traded is the **equilibrium quantity** (Q). There is no pressure for the price to change.

The examiners stress that a definition is not enough; you need the **mechanism** that moves the market there:

- **Excess supply (surplus)**: if the price is **above** equilibrium, quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded. Sellers are left with unsold stock, so they **cut prices**. As price falls, quantity demanded rises (extension of demand) and quantity supplied falls (contraction of supply) until the surplus disappears at P.
- **Excess demand (shortage)**: if the price is **below** equilibrium, quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied. Buyers compete for scarce goods and sellers see queues or sell-outs, so the **price rises**. Quantity supplied extends and quantity demanded contracts until the shortage disappears at P.

### Drawing and analysing the interaction of demand and supply

Every diagram needs: axes labelled **Price** and **Quantity**; a downward-sloping **D** and an upward-sloping **S**; the original equilibrium **P** and **Q**; the new curve labelled **D1** or **S1**; and the new **P1** and **Q1**. Remember from 2.2a and 2.3a: a change in the good's **own price** never shifts its own curve; the market moves along the curves to the new equilibrium.

| Change | Example cause (invented market: fresh strawberries) | Effect on price | Effect on quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Demand rises** (D shifts right) | a heatwave; a health campaign; incomes rise | rises | rises |
| **Demand falls** (D shifts left) | a wet summer; a cheaper substitute (raspberries) | falls | falls |
| **Supply rises** (S shifts right) | a bumper harvest; new picking machines; a subsidy | falls | rises |
| **Supply falls** (S shifts left) | frost damage; higher wages for pickers; an indirect tax | rises | falls |

**Writing the chain** for an analyse answer: cause → which curve shifts and which way → shortage or surplus at the old price → price moves → quantity demanded and supplied respond along the curves → new P1 and Q1 → the effect on the group named (consumers pay more, producers' revenue changes, and so on).

**Two shifts at once.** If demand and supply both shift, one result is certain and the other depends on the **size** of each shift. For example, if demand rises and supply rises together, quantity definitely rises, but price could rise, fall or stay the same. This is a useful evaluation point: "the overall effect on price depends on whether the rise in demand is bigger than the rise in supply."

**Elasticity matters too.** The size of the price change for a given shift depends on PED and PES (2.2b, 2.3b): a rise in demand with inelastic supply mostly raises price; with elastic supply it mostly raises quantity.

### The role of markets in determining price and allocating resources

A **market** brings buyers and sellers together, and the **market forces of demand and supply** set the price without anyone deciding it. The price then **allocates resources**:

1. Consumers want more of a good, so demand rises and the price rises (signal).
2. Higher prices raise potential profit, so existing firms expand and new firms enter (incentive). Resources such as workers and capital move into this market and out of markets where demand and prices are falling.
3. The good goes to those willing and able to pay the price (rationing).

Strengths of this: it is quick, needs no expensive planning, responds to millions of separate choices, and rewards efficient firms. Weaknesses (developed in 3.8): prices may not include external costs such as pollution; some people cannot afford essentials; some goods, like street lighting, would not be provided at all; and prices can swing sharply when supply or demand is inelastic. This is why the government sometimes **sets or influences prices** (maximum prices, taxes, subsidies), and why a 6-mark evaluate may ask whether "the market or the government" should decide a price.

::: terms
- **Price**: the amount of money a buyer pays for a good, which reflects the worth they place on it compared with alternatives.
- **Price mechanism**: the way changes in price, set by demand and supply, signal, give incentives and ration goods to allocate resources.
- **Equilibrium price**: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears.
- **Equilibrium quantity**: the amount bought and sold at the equilibrium price.
- **Excess supply (surplus)**: when quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded at the current price, which pushes the price down.
- **Excess demand (shortage)**: when quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied at the current price, which pushes the price up.
- **Market forces**: the pressures of demand and supply that move price and quantity towards equilibrium.
- **Efficient distribution of resources**: resources flowing to produce the goods and services consumers value most.
- **Allocation of resources**: how scarce factors of production are shared out between different uses.
- **Rationing function**: the role of price in deciding which consumers get a scarce good, namely those willing and able to pay.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions (2)**: equilibrium = quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (1) + the market clears / no pressure for price to change (1).
2. **"Explain the role of the market" (2)**: give the mechanism, not just the definition: a surplus pushes price down / a shortage pushes it up (1) until demand equals supply at the equilibrium (1).
3. **Calculations**: read equilibrium off a schedule where Qd = Qs; total revenue = price × quantity; surplus or shortage = the gap between Qs and Qd at a given price. Give units (£, units, kg).
4. **Draw (2)**: labelled axes (1); the correct curve shifted the correct way with P, Q, P1, Q1 shown (1). Indirect taxes and cost changes shift **supply**, not demand.
5. **"Using a diagram, analyse…" (6)**: draw it, then write a chain: cause → curve shift → shortage/surplus at old price → price and quantity change → effect on the named group. Refer to your diagram ("as shown by P to P1"). No evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse both sides, then qualify each point (size of the shift, elasticity, other shifts at the same time, time period), and end with a supported judgement for the group named.
:::

::: example Explaining the role of the market
Explain the role of the market in determining the equilibrium price of a good. (2)

If the price is above equilibrium there is excess supply, so sellers cut the price; if it is below, there is excess demand, so the price is bid up (1). Price keeps changing until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, which is the equilibrium price where the market clears (1).
:::

::: example Equilibrium and total revenue from a schedule
Invented data for weekly sales of jars of honey at a market stall:

| Price per jar | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| £4 | 220 | 100 |
| £5 | 180 | 140 |
| £6 | 160 | 160 |
| £7 | 130 | 190 |

Using the data, calculate the total revenue at the equilibrium price. Show your working. (2)

Equilibrium is £6, where quantity demanded = quantity supplied = 160 jars (1). Total revenue = £6 × 160 = **£960** (1).
:::

::: example Calculating a shortage
Using the honey data above, calculate the excess demand at a price of £4 and explain what will happen to the price. (2)

Excess demand = 220 − 100 = **120 jars** (1). Buyers want more than is available, so the price will be pushed up towards £6 (1).
:::

::: example Price as a reflection of worth
Invented context: a second-hand guitar sells at an online auction for £450. Explain what this price shows about the worth of the guitar to the buyer. (2)

The buyer valued the guitar at least as highly as the other things £450 could buy (1), and was willing and able to pay more for it than any other bidder, so the price rations it to the person who values it most in money terms (1).
:::

::: example Using a diagram, analyse a fall in supply
Invented context: storms damaged olive groves across southern Europe, cutting the harvest by a third. Using a diagram, analyse the effect on the price of olive oil in UK shops. (6)

Diagram: axes Price and Quantity; S shifts left to S1; equilibrium moves from P, Q to a higher P1 and lower Q1 (K1 + An1).

The storms reduce the olives available, so producers can supply less olive oil at every price and the supply curve shifts left from S to S1 (Ap). At the original price P there is now excess demand: shops and consumers want more oil than is available (An). Buyers compete for the smaller supply and sellers find they can sell out at higher prices, so the price is pushed up (An). As the price rises, quantity demanded contracts, as some shoppers switch to sunflower or rapeseed oil, until a new equilibrium is reached at the higher price P1 and the lower quantity Q1, as shown on the diagram (An). Olive oil has few close substitutes for many cooks, so demand is fairly inelastic and the price rise is large compared with the fall in quantity, meaning UK shoppers pay noticeably more per bottle (An).
:::

Explain what is meant by equilibrium price. (2)

---

Any two (1 each), with direction: a fall in demand (e.g. lower incomes for a normal good, a fall in the price of a substitute, a change in tastes); a rise in supply (e.g. lower costs of production, better technology, a subsidy, good weather for crops).

::: watch
- **Define AND explain the mechanism.** The 2025 examiners found most candidates could define equilibrium but could not explain how the market reaches it: always mention the surplus or shortage and the price change that removes it.
- **Shift the right curve.** A cost change, an indirect tax, a subsidy or the weather shifts **supply**; income, tastes and the price of substitutes or complements shift **demand**. A change in the good's own price shifts neither.
- **Label everything**: Price and Quantity on the axes, D, S, D1 or S1, P, Q, P1, Q1. A diagram showing only a movement along a curve when the question needs a shift loses marks.
- **Use the diagram in the writing.** In a "Using a diagram" 6-marker, an unexplained diagram caps you below Level 3.
- **"Market forces of demand and supply"** means consider both sides of the market, not only one.
- **Revenue is price × quantity**, not profit, and needs £.
- Do not write evaluation in an "Analyse" answer: it scores nothing there.
:::
