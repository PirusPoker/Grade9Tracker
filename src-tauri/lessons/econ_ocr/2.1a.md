## What it is

Markets are where the economic problem is solved in practice: they bring together the people who want things and the people who make them. This topic covers what a market is, the three **sectors** of production, and the difference between **factor markets** and **product markets**, and how they depend on each other.

### What is a market?

A **market** is any arrangement that brings **buyers and sellers together** so that goods and services can be **exchanged** and a **price** is set. It does not have to be a physical place.

| Type of market | Example |
|---|---|
| Physical, local | a Saturday farmers' market; a high-street hairdresser |
| Online | a website selling second-hand books; an app booking taxis |
| National | the UK market for new houses |
| Global | the world market for coffee beans, oil or smartphones |

A clothes shop that starts selling on the internet has not left "the market": it has reached the same kind of exchange through a new channel. Whenever there are buyers, sellers and a price, there is a market.

### The three sectors of production

| Sector | What it does | Examples | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **extracts or grows natural resources** | farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying, oil and gas extraction | raw materials (mostly goods) |
| **Secondary** | **turns raw materials into finished or part-finished goods**: manufacturing and construction | car making, food processing, steel works, furniture making, house building | goods |
| **Tertiary** | **provides services** to consumers and firms | retail, banking, insurance, transport, tourism, healthcare, education, hairdressing | services |

**Goods** are tangible: you can touch and store them (a loaf, a laptop, a brick). **Services** are intangible: they are performed for you and usually used up as they are produced (a haircut, a bus journey, a bank account, a lesson). The primary and secondary sectors mainly produce goods; the tertiary sector produces services.

**The chain of production** links the sectors. A wooden table might follow this path: a forestry firm fells trees (primary) → a sawmill and furniture maker turn timber into a table (secondary) → a haulage firm delivers it and a shop sells it (tertiary). Each stage adds value, and each depends on the stages either side of it.

In the UK, as in most developed economies, the **tertiary sector** employs by far the most people and produces most of national output, while the primary sector is small. Over time, as countries develop, employment tends to move from primary to secondary and then towards tertiary industries. (Avoid quoting exact UK percentages unless the question gives them.)

### Factor markets and product markets

- **Factor markets** are where the **factors of production** are bought, sold or hired: the **labour market** (firms hire workers; households supply labour), the market for **land and property** (renting a shop, buying farmland), and markets for **capital** (machinery, equipment, and the finance used to buy them).
- **Product markets** are where **finished goods and services** are bought and sold: the market for bread, cars, haircuts, cinema tickets, holidays.

The two kinds of market are mirror images. In a factor market, **firms are the buyers** and **households are the sellers** (of their labour). In a product market, **households are the buyers** and **firms are the sellers**.

**Important:** factor markets are **not** the primary sector, and product markets are **not** the secondary and tertiary sectors. A bakery buys flour (an input) and hires bakers in factor markets; it sells bread in a product market. A mining firm hires miners in the labour market (a factor market) and sells coal in a product market. Every sector uses factor markets and sells in product markets.

### Interdependence of factor and product markets

The two markets depend on each other:

1. **Demand for factors comes from demand for products.** Firms only hire workers, rent premises and buy machines because consumers want the products those factors make. Economists call this **derived demand**. If demand for electric cars rises, car makers need more engineers, factory space and battery machinery.
2. **Spending in product markets comes from income earned in factor markets.** Households earn wages, rent, interest and profit by supplying factors, and spend that income on goods and services.
3. **Costs in factor markets affect prices in product markets.** If wages for lorry drivers rise because drivers are in short supply, delivery costs rise and the prices of many goods in shops may rise too.

So a shock to one market spreads to the other. If a disease destroys much of a country's potato crop, potato prices rise in the product market; crisp makers cut output and need fewer workers in the labour market; those workers earn less and spend less in other product markets.

::: terms
- **Market**: any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to exchange goods and services at a price.
- **Primary sector**: the part of the economy that extracts or grows natural resources, such as farming, fishing and mining.
- **Secondary sector**: the part of the economy that turns raw materials into goods, such as manufacturing and construction.
- **Tertiary sector**: the part of the economy that provides services, such as retail, banking, transport and healthcare.
- **Goods**: tangible products that can be touched and stored, such as food, clothes and cars.
- **Services**: intangible products performed for customers, such as haircuts, insurance and teaching.
- **Chain of production**: the stages a product passes through from raw material to the final consumer, across the three sectors.
- **Factor market**: a market where factors of production such as labour, land and capital are bought, sold or hired.
- **Product market**: a market where finished goods and services are bought and sold to consumers.
- **Derived demand**: demand for a factor of production that comes from the demand for the product it helps to make.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify by what the business does**, not by its size: growing or extracting = primary; making or building = secondary; providing a service = tertiary. Some firms span sectors (a farm shop), so classify the activity named.
2. **"Explain what is meant by…" (2)**: a definition and an example. An example alone scores only 1.
3. **Factor or product market?** Ask what is being bought and who is buying it. A firm hiring or buying an input = factor market; a household buying a finished good or service = product market.
4. **Interdependence answers**: write a chain that crosses between the two markets: "fall in demand for X in the product market → firm needs fewer workers → in the labour market, demand for labour falls → households earn less → spend less on other products".
5. **Explain the trend (2)**: state the direction (rising/falling, by how much) AND give a reason for it. Describing alone scores 1.
6. **6-mark analyse**: follow the shock along the chain of production and between factor and product markets, using the extract. **6-mark evaluate**: weigh up, say what it depends on, and judge.
:::

::: example Explain what is meant by the primary sector
Explain what is meant by the primary sector. (2)

The primary sector is the part of the economy that extracts or grows natural resources (1), for example fishing for cod or quarrying stone (1).
:::

::: example Why a business is in the product market
Invented context: Lena runs a mobile dog-grooming van and charges £35 per visit. Explain why Lena's business is part of the product market. (2)

Lena sells a finished service, dog grooming, directly to consumers (1). Pet owners buy the service from her at a price of £35, so she is a seller in the product market, not selling a factor of production (1).
:::

::: example Explaining a trend
Invented data: employment in manufacturing in the town of Dunmore was 8,200 in 2005, 6,100 in 2015 and 4,300 in 2025. Using the data, explain the trend in manufacturing employment. (2)

Manufacturing employment has fallen steadily, by 3,900 jobs (about 48%) over 20 years (1). This could be because factories have replaced workers with automated machinery, or because goods can be made more cheaply abroad, so fewer workers are needed in the secondary sector (1).
:::

::: example Calculating a sector's share
Invented data: in the region of Wexmoor, 9,000 people work in the primary sector, 36,000 in the secondary sector and 135,000 in the tertiary sector. Calculate the percentage of workers employed in the tertiary sector. Show your working. (2)

Total = 9,000 + 36,000 + 135,000 = 180,000 (1).
Tertiary share = 135,000 ÷ 180,000 × 100 = **75%** (1).
:::

::: example Interdependence of factor and product markets
Invented context: a new fashion makes oat milk very popular. Explain how this could affect the interdependence of the factor and product markets. (2)

Higher demand for oat milk in the product market means producers need more oats, workers and processing equipment, so their demand for these factors rises in factor markets (1). The extra workers hired earn wages in the labour market, which they then spend on goods and services in product markets (1).
:::

::: q
State two features of the tertiary sector. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): provides services rather than goods; output is intangible; includes e.g. retail, banking, transport, healthcare, education, tourism; usually the largest sector by employment in developed economies such as the UK; often relies on the primary and secondary sectors for the goods it sells.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between the production of goods and the production of services. (2)
---
Goods are tangible products that can be touched and stored, e.g. a chair (1); services are intangible, performed for the customer and used up as they are provided, e.g. a haircut (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a factor market, using an example. (2)
---
A market where factors of production (land, labour, capital) are bought, sold or hired (1), e.g. the labour market in which a hospital hires nurses / a firm renting a warehouse (1). Do not credit "the primary sector".
:::

::: q
A builder buys bricks from a brickworks and hires two labourers to build an extension for a family. Classify the brickworks and the builder by sector. (2)
---
Brickworks: secondary sector — manufacturing bricks from clay (1). Builder: secondary sector — construction (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: a country's primary sector output was £40 billion in Year 1 and £34 billion in Year 2. Calculate the percentage change. Show your working. (2)
---
(34 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 (1) = −15% / a fall of 15% (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how the closure of a large car factory could affect both factor markets and product markets in the surrounding area. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains across both markets; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point.
Indicative content: in the labour market, demand for car workers falls, so many become unemployed and wages for similar skills may fall as more people seek fewer jobs (An); demand for factory land and buildings falls, so property may lie empty (An); local suppliers of parts lose orders and cut their own workers (An). In product markets, unemployed workers have less income, so spending in local shops, pubs and car dealers falls (An); these firms lose revenue and may in turn reduce staff, showing interdependence (An). Knowledge: factor vs product markets, derived demand (K).
:::

::: watch
- **Factor market ≠ primary sector**, and **product market ≠ secondary or tertiary sector.** Every business, in any sector, hires factors and sells products.
- For "Explain what is meant by the primary sector", give a **definition and an example**. An example on its own scores 1.
- **Construction is secondary**, not tertiary. A **shop selling goods is tertiary**: it provides a retail service even though it handles goods.
- A market **need not be a physical place**: online and telephone sales are markets.
- For "Explain the trend", describe the direction **and** give a reason. The examiners regularly award only 1 for description.
- In interdependence answers, cross between the two markets: start in one, show the effect in the other.
:::

::: test
A firm builds new houses. In which sector of the economy does it operate? A — Primary; B — Public; C — Secondary; D — Tertiary. (1)
---
C — construction is part of the secondary sector.
:::

::: test
Which of the following is an example of a factor market? A — A family buying a new washing machine; B — A hospital recruiting nurses; C — A tourist booking a hotel room; D — A student buying a train ticket. (1)
---
B — the hospital is hiring labour, a factor of production. A, C and D are households buying finished goods or services in product markets.
:::

::: test
What is **not** part of the tertiary sector? A — A bank; B — A dental practice; C — A hotel; D — A sawmill. (1)
---
D — a sawmill processes timber into goods, so it is in the secondary sector.
:::

::: test
Which statement about markets is correct? A — A market must be a physical place where buyers and sellers meet; B — Factor markets are the same as the primary sector; C — Product markets include goods and services from every sector; D — Only firms in the secondary sector sell in product markets. (1)
---
C — finished goods and services from all three sectors are sold in product markets. A ignores online markets; B and D are the misconceptions examiners report.
:::

::: test
A farmer sells his free-range eggs through a website to customers across the country. What is this an example of? A — A factor market; B — A market; C — The secondary sector; D — The chain of production ending in the primary sector. (1)
---
B — buyers and sellers are brought together to exchange a good at a price, even though they never meet.
:::

::: test
Explain what is meant by the secondary sector. (2)
---
The part of the economy that turns raw materials into finished or part-finished goods / manufacturing and construction (1), e.g. a factory making biscuits or a firm building houses (1). Example alone = 1.
:::

::: test
Invented context: Sam is a self-employed driving instructor. Explain why Sam's business is part of the product market. (2)
---
Sam sells a finished service, driving lessons, to consumers (1), who pay him a price for each lesson, so he is a seller in the product market (1). Do not credit "because he is in the tertiary sector" on its own.
:::

::: test
Explain how a large rise in the wages of farm workers could affect the interdependence of factor and product markets. (2)
---
Higher wages in the labour market (a factor market) raise farms' costs of production (1), so farms may raise the prices of vegetables and other food sold in product markets / supply less to them (1). Or: farm workers earn more and spend more in product markets — developed.
:::

::: test
Invented data: the table shows the number of people employed in fishing in the coastal district of Carnby.

| Year | 2010 | 2015 | 2020 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| People employed in fishing | 2,400 | 2,050 | 1,700 | 1,320 |

Using the data, explain the trend in employment in fishing in Carnby. (2)
---
Employment in fishing has fallen in every period, by 1,080 people (45%) between 2010 and 2025 (1). Reason (1), e.g. fishing quotas/overfishing reducing fish stocks; larger, more mechanised boats needing fewer crew; falling demand or cheaper imported fish. Description only = 1.
:::

::: test
Invented data: in Year 1 a country's output was £50 billion from the primary sector, £150 billion from the secondary sector and £800 billion from the tertiary sector. Calculate the percentage of output produced by the secondary sector. Show your working. (2)
---
Total = 50 + 150 + 800 = £1,000 billion (1). 150 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 15% (1). Correct answer alone = 2.
:::

::: test
Explain one way the primary and secondary sectors depend on each other. (2)
---
The secondary sector needs raw materials from the primary sector, e.g. a sawmill needs timber from forestry (1); the primary sector depends on the secondary sector to buy its output and provide machinery such as tractors and fishing boats (1).
:::

::: test
Invented extract: On the island of Haldon, apple orchards cover a third of the farmland. A fungus has destroyed half of this year's apple crop. Haldon Cider Ltd, the island's biggest employer, makes cider from local apples and sells it to shops, pubs and visitors. Apple picking normally employs 600 seasonal workers.

Using the information, analyse the possible effects of the fungus on Haldon's primary, secondary and tertiary sectors. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains of reasoning across the sectors, applied to Haldon, may link factor and product markets; L2 3–4 — correct effects, mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — an isolated point or definition. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: primary = orchards; secondary = cider making; tertiary = shops, pubs, tourism; the chain of production.
- Application: half the crop lost; a third of farmland in orchards; 600 seasonal pickers; Haldon Cider is the biggest employer.
- Analysis: primary — orchards' output and revenue fall, so fewer of the 600 pickers are hired, cutting incomes in the labour market. Secondary — Haldon Cider has fewer apples, so must cut production or import apples at higher cost, raising its costs and perhaps cider prices; it may cut shifts for its workers. Tertiary — shops and pubs have less local cider to sell or must charge more; workers with lower incomes spend less in local shops; visitors may be less attracted if the cider trail closes. Interdependence: a shock in one sector runs along the chain of production.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Over 15 years the town of Fellbridge has lost three large factories making washing machines, glass and tyres. Manufacturing jobs fell from 9,500 to 3,000. Over the same period, a new distribution centre, a call centre and a retail park have opened, creating 5,800 jobs, many of them part-time and lower paid.

Evaluate the impact on Fellbridge of the decline in its secondary sector. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and a well-supported judgement weighing both sides; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: manufacturing jobs down 6,500 (9,500 → 3,000, a fall of about 68%); 5,800 tertiary jobs created; many part-time and lower paid.
- Analysis (negative): factory workers with specialist skills may not suit call-centre or retail work, so some face long-term unemployment; lower average pay reduces spending in local product markets, so shops and services suffer; fewer high-paid jobs may lead young people to leave.
- Analysis (positive/offsetting): 5,800 new jobs replace most of the 6,500 lost; tertiary jobs may be more stable; less heavy industry may improve the environment and make the town more attractive to new firms.
- Evaluation: the net loss of 700 jobs is small, but the quality of jobs matters as much as the number; the effect depends on retraining and whether ex-factory workers get the new jobs; part-time work may suit some households (students, carers). Judgement: e.g. a significant negative impact on incomes, even though the number of jobs has largely recovered, because higher-paid manufacturing work has been replaced by lower-paid work.
:::
