## What it is

The **balance of payments** is a record of all the money flowing into and out of a country from its transactions with the rest of the world. For GCSE you need the **current account**: the part that records **trade in goods and services** and **income flows**. This topic covers what the current account contains, what a **balanced current account, a surplus and a deficit** mean, how to **calculate** them, how to **analyse data on exports and imports**, and how to **evaluate the causes** of deficits and surpluses and **their importance to the UK economy**.

### The four parts of the current account

| Part | Money flows IN (credit) | Money flows OUT (debit) |
|---|---|---|
| **Trade in goods** (sometimes called visible trade) | exports of goods, e.g. UK-made cars or medicines sold abroad | imports of goods, e.g. clothes, fuel or electronics bought from abroad |
| **Trade in services** (invisible trade) | exports of services, e.g. foreign firms buying UK insurance or banking, overseas students at UK universities, foreign tourists spending in the UK | imports of services, e.g. UK tourists spending abroad, UK firms paying foreign shipping or software companies |
| **Primary income** | wages, profits, interest and dividends earned abroad by UK residents and firms | wages, profits, interest and dividends paid to foreign owners of assets in the UK |
| **Secondary income** (transfers) | transfers received, e.g. money sent home by UK citizens working abroad | transfers paid, e.g. foreign aid, money sent abroad by migrants (remittances), payments to international organisations |

The **balance of trade in goods** = exports of goods − imports of goods. The **balance of trade in services** = exports of services − imports of services. The **current account balance** = the balance of goods + the balance of services + net primary income + net secondary income.

A tourist is an easy one to get wrong: a **German tourist spending in London** is a UK **export** of services (money comes into the UK). A **UK family on holiday in Spain** is a UK **import** of services (money leaves the UK).

### Balanced, surplus and deficit

| Situation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Balanced current account** | money flowing in = money flowing out; the balance is zero |
| **Current account surplus** | money flowing in > money flowing out; the balance is **positive** |
| **Current account deficit** | money flowing out > money flowing in; the balance is **negative** |

The current account is **not** the government's budget. A budget deficit is when **government spending** exceeds **tax revenue** (3.5a). A current account deficit is about the **whole country's** trade and income with the rest of the world. Raising taxes "to pay off" a current account deficit is a common wrong answer.

### Calculating a deficit or surplus

**Worked example (invented figures, £ billion) for the country of Lantra:**

| Item | Inflows | Outflows | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade in goods | 300 | 360 | 300 − 360 = **−60** |
| Trade in services | 180 | 120 | 180 − 120 = **+60** |
| Primary income | 45 | 40 | **+5** |
| Secondary income | 10 | 25 | **−15** |
| **Current account** | | | −60 + 60 + 5 − 15 = **−£10 billion** (a deficit) |

Keep the **sign** and the **units** ("−£10 billion" or "a deficit of £10 billion"). A deficit can be described as a share of GDP: if Lantra's GDP is £400 billion, the deficit is 10 ÷ 400 × 100 = **2.5% of GDP**. Expressing it this way shows whether the deficit is large compared with the size of the economy.

### Analysing data on exports and imports

When a question gives a table or line chart of exports and imports:
- **Describe the balance**, not just the values: is the deficit **widening** (getting bigger) or **narrowing**? Has a surplus turned into a deficit? Saying "trade is increasing" misses the point.
- **Compare trade in goods with trade in services**: in the UK's case there is usually a **deficit on trade in goods** and a **surplus on trade in services**, because the UK imports many manufactured goods but sells financial, business, education and other services worldwide.
- **Quote figures with units** and say when the change happened.
- **Explain** the change with a cause (see below).

The UK has had a **current account deficit in most years for several decades**: its services surplus has usually not been big enough to cover its deficit on goods.

### Causes of surpluses and deficits

| Cause | How it leads to a deficit (the reverse gives a surplus) |
|---|---|
| **Low competitiveness** | if a country's firms have higher costs or lower **productivity** than rivals, its goods are dearer, so exports fall and imports rise |
| **A strong exchange rate** | if the currency rises, exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home (4.3) |
| **Higher inflation than other countries** | domestic prices rise faster than foreign ones, so exports become less competitive |
| **High consumer incomes and spending** | in a boom people buy more of everything, including imports, so imports rise faster than exports |
| **Structure of the economy** | a country that has lost much of its manufacturing (deindustrialisation) imports more goods |
| **Quality and non-price factors** | poor design, reliability or after-sales service reduces demand for exports |
| **Natural resources** | a country that must import its oil or food has big import bills; one rich in oil may run a surplus |

### Why the current account matters to the UK

| A deficit can be a problem because… | …but it may not be serious because |
|---|---|
| it must be **financed**: the UK pays for the extra imports by borrowing from abroad or selling UK assets (firms, property, shares) to foreigners | foreign investment into the UK can bring jobs, technology and new firms |
| it may signal that UK firms are **uncompetitive** | it may reflect strong spending by consumers who are confident about their jobs, and firms importing machinery to invest |
| money spent on imports is not spent on UK-made goods, so **demand, output and jobs** in the UK can be lower | a small deficit as a share of GDP is easy to finance |
| a large, persistent deficit may lead to a **fall in the exchange rate** | a lower pound makes exports cheaper, which can help to correct it |

A **surplus** is not automatically good either: it can mean consumers are spending little, or that other countries running deficits may introduce trade barriers. What matters most is **how big** the imbalance is compared with GDP, **how long** it lasts, and **why** it is happening.

::: terms
- **Balance of payments**: a record of all financial transactions between a country and the rest of the world over a period of time.
- **Current account**: the part of the balance of payments that records trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income.
- **Trade in goods**: exports and imports of physical products, such as cars, food and fuel.
- **Trade in services**: exports and imports of services, such as banking, insurance, education and tourism.
- **Primary income**: wages, profits, interest and dividends earned from abroad or paid to foreigners.
- **Secondary income**: transfers between countries where nothing is bought, such as foreign aid and money migrants send home.
- **Balanced current account**: when money flowing into the current account equals money flowing out, so the balance is zero.
- **Current account surplus**: when money flowing into the current account is greater than money flowing out.
- **Current account deficit**: when money flowing out of the current account is greater than money flowing in.
- **Competitiveness**: the ability of a country's firms to sell their goods and services against foreign rivals, on price and quality.
- **Deindustrialisation**: the long-term decline of manufacturing as a share of an economy's output and jobs.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain what is meant by a current account deficit" (2)**: outflows are greater than inflows on the current account (1) + what is included, e.g. imports of goods and services exceed exports, with an example (1).
2. **Calculations (2)**: balance = inflows − outflows for each part; current account = goods + services + primary + secondary. Show working, keep the minus sign and write £ million or £ billion.
3. **"Explain the trend" (2)**: say whether the deficit or surplus widened or narrowed with figures (1) + give a reason (1).
4. **Analyse (6)**: chains from a cause (exchange rate, inflation, incomes, productivity) → prices or demand for exports and imports → the balance. Use the figures given. No evaluation.
5. **Evaluate (6)**: weigh why it matters (financing, jobs, competitiveness) against why it may not (size relative to GDP, cause, investment inflows). Judge how serious it is, and keep it separate from the government's budget.
:::

::: example Current account deficit
Explain what is meant by a current account deficit. (2)

When the money flowing out of a country on its current account is greater than the money flowing in (1), for example because the value of imports of goods and services is greater than the value of exports (1).
:::

::: example Classifying a transaction
Explain how a Japanese student paying fees to a UK university affects the UK's current account. (2)

It is an export of a service by the UK, because money from abroad is paid to a UK provider (1), so it adds to the UK's inflows on trade in services, improving the current account balance (1).
:::

::: example Calculating the current account
Invented figures (£ billion) for the country of Marlow: exports of goods 220, imports of goods 250, exports of services 95, imports of services 70, net primary income −8, net secondary income −4. Calculate Marlow's current account balance. Show your working. (2)

(220 − 250) + (95 − 70) − 8 − 4 = −30 + 25 − 8 − 4 (1) = **−£17 billion**, a deficit of £17 billion (1).
:::

::: example Explaining a trend
Invented data: the country of Tamsin's current account went from a deficit of £12 billion in Year 1 to a deficit of £31 billion in Year 3, while its consumers' incomes rose quickly. Using the data, explain the trend in Tamsin's current account. (2)

The deficit widened by £19 billion, from £12bn to £31bn (1), because rising incomes meant consumers bought more goods and services, including more imports, so imports grew faster than exports (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — rising inflation
Invented context: inflation in the country of Ardent has been 7% a year for three years, while in its main trading partners it has been 2%. Analyse how this could affect Ardent's current account. (6)

The current account records exports and imports of goods and services (K). With prices rising by 7% a year against 2% abroad, Ardent's goods become steadily dearer compared with foreign goods (Ap). Foreign buyers switch to cheaper suppliers in other countries, so demand for Ardent's exports falls and export revenue falls (An). At the same time, imported goods become relatively cheaper than domestic goods for Ardent's consumers, so they buy more imports (An). Falling export revenue and rising import spending mean outflows grow faster than inflows, so the current account moves towards a deficit, or an existing deficit widens (An). Over three years the gap builds up, so the effect becomes larger each year; firms that lose export sales may also cut output and jobs (Ap/An).
:::

::: q
State two items that are recorded in the current account of the balance of payments. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): exports of goods; imports of goods; exports of services; imports of services; primary income (wages, profits, interest, dividends); secondary income / transfers (aid, remittances). Do not credit "government spending" or "taxes".
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between trade in goods and trade in services. (2)
---
Trade in goods is the export and import of physical products, such as cars (1), whereas trade in services is the export and import of non-physical products, such as banking, insurance or tourism (1).
:::

::: q
Invented figures: a country's exports of goods and services were £540 million and its imports were £585 million. Its net primary and secondary income were zero. Calculate its current account balance. Show your working. (2)
---
£540m − £585m (1) = **−£45 million**, a deficit of £45 million (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a current account deficit might be a problem for the UK. (2)
---
The extra spending on imports must be financed by borrowing from abroad or selling UK assets to foreigners (1), so the UK builds up debts or loses ownership of firms and future profit flows abroad / it may mean UK firms are uncompetitive, so jobs are lost to imports (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a rise in UK consumers' incomes could affect the UK's current account. (2)
---
With higher incomes consumers spend more, including on imported goods and holidays abroad (1), so imports rise and the current account deficit widens / surplus narrows (1).
:::

::: q
Evaluate whether a current account deficit is always bad for a country. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 good application, analysis and well-supported judgement; L2 3–4 judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content: a deficit must be financed by borrowing or selling assets, building up debt and sending future profits abroad; it may signal uncompetitive firms, so jobs are lost to imports. But it may reflect strong consumer confidence and firms importing machinery to invest, which raises future output; a small deficit relative to GDP is easily financed; foreign investment that finances it may bring jobs and technology. Judgement: not always bad — it depends on its size relative to GDP, how long it lasts and its cause.
:::

::: watch
- **The current account is not the government's budget.** Do not suggest raising taxes or cutting public spending "to pay off" a current account deficit.
- **Describe the balance, not the values**: "the deficit widened from £8bn to £20bn", not "trade went up".
- **Tourism trips people up**: foreign tourists spending in the UK are UK **exports**; UK tourists abroad are UK **imports**.
- **Keep the minus sign and units**: −£17 billion, not 17.
- **"Explain the trend" needs a reason** as well as a description.
- **Evaluate the cause, not just the size**: a deficit from strong investment is less worrying than one from falling competitiveness.
- **A surplus is not always good**, and a deficit is not always bad. Judge using size relative to GDP, length of time and cause.
:::

::: test
Explain how a fall in the productivity of a country's manufacturing firms could affect its current account. (2)
---
Lower productivity raises the cost of producing each unit, so firms must charge higher prices and their goods become less competitive (1), so exports fall and imports rise, widening a current account deficit (1).

::: test
Invented extract: Over the past five years, the country of Kestrel's current account deficit has grown from 1% to 4% of GDP. Kestrel's currency has risen in value by 20% over the period, many of its factories have closed and moved production abroad, and consumer spending has grown strongly because of low unemployment.

Using the information, analyse how changes in Kestrel's economy have led to an increasing current account deficit. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Kestrel; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: current account deficit = outflows exceed inflows; exports and imports of goods and services.
- Application: deficit 1% → 4% of GDP; currency up 20%; factories closed and moved abroad; strong consumer spending; low unemployment.
- Analysis: a 20% rise in the currency makes Kestrel's exports dearer for foreign buyers, so export sales fall, and makes imports cheaper at home, so imports rise; factories closing means Kestrel produces fewer goods to export and must import goods it used to make itself (deindustrialisation); low unemployment and rising incomes mean consumers buy more of everything, including imports and holidays abroad; with falling exports and rising imports, outflows grow faster than inflows, so the deficit widens as a share of GDP.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The country of Rennet has had a current account deficit of about 5% of GDP for ten years. The deficit has been financed mainly by foreign firms buying Rennet's companies and building new factories there. Rennet's exports of services are growing, but its manufacturing firms have lower productivity than their rivals abroad.

Evaluate the importance of the current account deficit to Rennet's economy. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: about 5% of GDP; for ten years; financed by foreign firms buying companies and building factories; services exports growing; low manufacturing productivity.
- Analysis: a persistent deficit means spending on imports is not spent on Rennet's own output, so demand and jobs are lower; low productivity shows manufacturers are uncompetitive, which may cause structural unemployment; selling companies to foreigners means future profits flow abroad as primary income, worsening the current account later; a large deficit could eventually cause the currency to fall.
- Evaluation: foreign firms building new factories create jobs and bring technology, which may raise productivity and future exports; growing services exports may narrow the deficit over time; but 5% of GDP for ten years is large and persistent, not a short-term effect of a boom; whether it matters depends on whether foreign investors keep financing it. Judgement: e.g. the deficit is important because it is large, long-lasting and caused partly by low competitiveness, but it is less serious than it looks while foreign investment in new factories is financing it and adding to Rennet's productive capacity.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The country of Dorran has a current account surplus of 6% of GDP. It exports large amounts of oil, whose world price has been high. Dorran's households save a high share of their incomes and spend little on imports. Some of Dorran's trading partners are complaining about its surplus.

Evaluate the causes of Dorran's current account surplus and whether it is likely to last. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: surplus 6% of GDP; oil exports; high world oil price; high household saving; little spending on imports; trading partners complaining.
- Analysis: a high oil price raises export revenue without any rise in the quantity sold, so inflows rise; high saving means low consumer spending, so imports are low; together inflows exceed outflows, producing a surplus.
- Evaluation: the oil price is the biggest cause but the least reliable — if it falls, export revenue falls sharply and the surplus could shrink quickly; dependence on one export is risky; high saving is a longer-lasting habit, so imports may stay low; partners' complaints could lead to trade barriers, reducing Dorran's exports; the surplus could also push up Dorran's currency, making other exports dearer. Judgement: e.g. the surplus is mainly caused by the high oil price, so it is unlikely to last at 6% of GDP if the price falls, although high saving will keep imports low and a smaller surplus may continue.
:::
