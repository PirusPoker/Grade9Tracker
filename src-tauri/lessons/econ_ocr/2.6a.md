## What it is

**Producers** turn the factors of production (land, labour, capital and enterprise) into goods and services. This topic covers **who the producers are** (individuals, firms and the government), the difference between **production** and **productivity**, how to **calculate labour productivity**, what raises it, and the **importance of production and productivity for the economy**, which the spec asks you to evaluate. Costs, revenue and economies of scale follow in 2.6b.

### The role of producers

| Producer | Who they are | Why they produce | Examples (invented where named) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Individuals** | **sole traders** and the **self-employed**, working for themselves | to earn an income and profit; independence | a self-employed plumber; a freelance web designer; a market-stall holder |
| **Firms** | private-sector businesses, from small partnerships to large companies | mainly **to make a profit** for their owners; also growth and survival. **Social enterprises** trade to meet a social or environmental aim | a supermarket chain; Lindale Engineering Ltd; a community café that trains young people |
| **The government** | the **public sector**: central and local government and public bodies | to provide goods and services that the market would under-provide or that everyone should have whatever their income, not mainly for profit | the NHS (health care), state schools, roads, the armed forces, street lighting |

Producers **hire factors** in factor markets and **sell output** in product markets (2.1a), paying wages, rent and interest, and earning revenue. The government produces services that are often **free at the point of use**, paid for by taxation (3.5a).

### Production and productivity

These two words are confused every year, and the 2024 examiners flagged it.

- **Production** is the **total output** of goods and services in a period, e.g. a factory makes 50,000 bottles a week.
- **Productivity** is **output per unit of input** in a period. The most common measure is **labour productivity**: **output per worker** (or per worker-hour).

**Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers** (or ÷ number of hours worked)

**Worked example (invented data).** Fenwick Furniture's weekly output of chairs:

| | Workers | Chairs made per week | Labour productivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 8 | 240 | 240 ÷ 8 = **30 chairs per worker** |
| After hiring | 10 | 280 | 280 ÷ 10 = **28 chairs per worker** |

**Production rose** (240 to 280 chairs), but **productivity fell** (30 to 28 per worker). New workers may need training or there may not be enough machines for them all. More workers can raise production while lowering productivity, so always check which one the question asks about.

**Percentage change in productivity** = (new − old) ÷ old × 100 = (28 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = **−6.7%** (to 1 d.p.).

### What raises productivity?

| Factor | How it raises output per worker |
|---|---|
| **Education and training** | skilled workers work faster, make fewer mistakes and can use advanced equipment |
| **Capital investment and technology** | better machines, robots and software let each worker produce more |
| **Specialisation and division of labour** | workers who repeat one task become quicker at it (2.1b) |
| **Motivation and management** | good pay, conditions and organisation encourage effort and cut wasted time |
| **Infrastructure** | good roads, broadband and reliable power mean less time lost in delays |
| **Working practices** | better layout of a workplace, less waste, flexible shifts |

### Why production and productivity matter for the economy

**Higher production** means more goods and services available, so living standards can rise. Firms need more workers, so **employment and incomes** rise, and the government collects more **tax revenue**. A rise in the country's total production is **economic growth** (3.1).

**Higher productivity** brings:

- **Lower costs per unit**: if each worker makes more, the wage cost of each item falls.
- **Lower prices or higher profits**: firms can cut prices (good for consumers) or keep the extra as profit to reinvest.
- **Higher wages**: workers who produce more are worth more to employers, so wages can rise without raising costs per unit.
- **International competitiveness**: UK goods become cheaper relative to foreign goods, helping exports (4.1).
- **Economic growth** without needing more workers, and more tax revenue for public services.

**Limits and drawbacks (for evaluation):**

- **Job losses**: if machines replace workers, productivity rises but some people lose their jobs, at least until they retrain (structural unemployment, 3.2).
- **Quality**: pushing workers for speed may lead to more mistakes, stress or safety risks.
- **Cost of raising it**: training and new technology are expensive and take time to pay back.
- **It depends on demand**: producing more per worker only helps if the extra output can be sold; otherwise firms simply need fewer workers.
- **Who gains**: the benefit may go to owners as profit rather than to workers as higher wages.
- **Sustainability**: more production can mean more use of resources and more pollution.
- **Measuring productivity is hard** in services: how do you measure the output of a teacher or a nurse?

::: terms
- **Producer**: an individual, firm or government body that uses factors of production to make goods or provide services.
- **Sole trader**: an individual who owns and runs a business alone, keeping all the profit.
- **Public sector**: the part of the economy owned and run by the government, such as state schools and the NHS.
- **Private sector**: the part of the economy owned by individuals and firms, usually aiming to make a profit.
- **Production**: the total output of goods and services made in a period of time.
- **Productivity**: the output per unit of input, such as per worker or per hour, in a period of time.
- **Labour productivity**: output per worker or per worker-hour, calculated as total output divided by the number of workers or hours.
- **Capital investment**: spending on machinery, equipment, buildings and technology used to produce goods.
- **Unit cost**: the cost of producing one unit of output, which falls when productivity rises and wages stay the same.
- **Competitiveness**: the ability of a firm or country to sell its goods at prices or quality that beat rivals.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the word carefully**: production = total output; productivity = output per worker or per hour.
2. **Calculations (2)**: productivity = output ÷ workers (or hours); % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Show working; give units ("chairs per worker", "%").
3. **"Explain one way the government acts as a producer" (2)**: name a service (1) + why the government provides it, e.g. free at the point of use, funded by taxes, would be under-provided by the market (1).
4. **"Explain one way to raise productivity" (2)**: method (1) + how it raises output per worker, applied (1).
5. **Analyse (6)**: chain: cause (training, machinery) → more output per worker → lower cost per unit → lower prices / higher profit / higher wages → wider effect. No evaluation.
6. **Evaluate the importance of productivity (6)**: benefits, each paired with a "however… depends on" (demand, job losses, cost, who gains, quality), then a judgement for the group named.
:::

::: example Production versus productivity
Explain the difference between production and productivity. (2)

Production is the total output made in a period, e.g. 5,000 shirts a week (1). Productivity is output per unit of input, such as per worker, e.g. 50 shirts per worker per week (1).
:::

::: example Calculating labour productivity
Invented data: a call centre with 40 staff handles 9,600 calls a week. After a new computer system is installed, 36 staff handle 9,720 calls a week. Calculate the change in labour productivity. Show your working. (2)

Before: 9,600 ÷ 40 = 240 calls per worker; after: 9,720 ÷ 36 = 270 calls per worker (1). Productivity rises by **30 calls per worker per week** (a 12.5% rise) (1).
:::

::: example The government as a producer
Explain one way the government acts as a producer. (2)

The government provides education through state schools (1), which are free at the point of use and paid for from taxes, so every child can be educated whatever their family's income (1).
:::

::: example Raising productivity
Invented context: Orrin Printworks wants to raise the productivity of its workers. Explain one way it could do this. (2)

It could train its staff to use its digital presses (1), so each worker sets up jobs faster and makes fewer errors, raising the number of print jobs completed per worker each day (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — productivity and costs
Invented context: Kestrel Packaging buys automated folding machines. Each worker can now fold and pack 900 boxes an hour instead of 600. Analyse how this could affect Kestrel's costs and prices. (6)

Productivity rises from 600 to 900 boxes per worker-hour, a 50% increase (Ap). If a worker is paid the same hourly wage, that wage is now spread over 900 boxes instead of 600, so the labour cost of each box falls by one third (An). Lower unit costs mean Kestrel can cut the price it charges customers and still make the same profit on each box, making it more competitive than rival packaging firms (An). Lower prices are likely to win orders, so Kestrel's sales and revenue rise (An). Alternatively, it could keep its price and enjoy a larger profit per box, which could pay back the cost of the machines and fund further investment (An). The machines themselves are a cost, but once paid for, every extra box produced lowers their cost per box too (An).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by productivity. (2)
---
Output per unit of input (1), e.g. output per worker per hour / week; not total output (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: a farm employs 6 workers and harvests 4,800 kg of potatoes a day. Calculate labour productivity. (2)
---
4,800 ÷ 6 (1) = 800 kg per worker per day (1). Units needed for the second mark.
:::

::: q
State two ways a firm could increase its labour productivity. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): train workers; invest in new machinery/technology; specialisation/division of labour; better management or motivation, e.g. bonuses; improve working conditions or layout.
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why the government produces some goods and services itself. (2)
---
E.g. the private sector may not provide enough of them, because people would not pay enough (1), so the government provides them free at the point of use, funded by taxes, e.g. health care or roads (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: labour productivity at a clothing factory rises from 40 to 46 garments per worker per day. Calculate the percentage increase. (2)
---
(46 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 (1) = 15% (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how better training of workers can benefit an economy. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation.
Indicative content: training raises skills, so output per worker rises (K); lower cost per unit lets firms cut prices, so more is bought at home and abroad (An); exports become more competitive, raising demand for UK goods (An); higher productivity supports higher wages, raising incomes and spending (An); firms expand, creating jobs and raising production, i.e. economic growth (An); higher incomes and profits raise tax revenue for public services (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Production is not productivity.** Productivity is per worker (or per input); production is the total. The 2024 examiners saw many answers using them as if they were the same.
- **More workers can mean lower productivity**: production may rise while output per worker falls.
- **Always give units**: "800 kg per worker per day", not "800".
- For "the role of producers", remember all **three**: individuals, firms and the **government**.
- In "evaluate the importance" answers, weigh benefits against **job losses, costs, quality and demand**, and say who gains.
- Higher productivity lowers **cost per unit**, not necessarily total cost.
:::

::: test
Explain why higher productivity can allow firms to pay higher wages. (2)
---
Each worker produces more output, so earns more revenue for the firm (1); the firm can raise wages while the labour cost per unit stays the same or falls, so profits are not reduced (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one way investment in new technology could raise productivity. (2)
---
E.g. faster machines or robots let each worker produce more units per hour (1), so output per worker rises, e.g. a packing line can fill more boxes with the same staff (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one disadvantage for workers of a rise in productivity. (2)
---
If machines let fewer workers produce the same output (1), some workers may lose their jobs / be made redundant unless demand rises, and may need retraining (1). Or more pressure and stress, developed.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Quarry Lane Distribution runs a warehouse employing 120 pickers, who each fill about 80 orders a day. The firm plans to spend £2 million on a robotic system that will let each picker fill about 200 orders a day. It expects to need fewer pickers but more technicians.

Analyse how the robotic system could affect Quarry Lane's costs. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Quarry Lane; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: productivity = output per worker; unit cost; capital investment.
- Application: 80 to 200 orders per picker per day (a 150% rise); £2 million; fewer pickers, more technicians.
- Analysis: each picker fills 2.5 times as many orders, so the wage cost per order falls sharply; the firm can fill the same orders with fewer pickers, cutting its total wage bill; the £2 million is a large fixed cost at first, raising total cost in the short run, but spread over many more orders the cost per order falls; technicians may be paid more, offsetting some savings; faster picking means fewer delays and errors, cutting the cost of returns; lower unit costs let Quarry Lane cut prices or raise profit.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Over the last decade, output per hour worked in the country of Varnia has grown very slowly, and Varnian wages have hardly risen. The government plans to spend more on apprenticeships and on improving broadband and rail links, and to give tax breaks to firms that invest in new machinery.

Evaluate the importance of productivity for Varnia's economy. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement on the importance of productivity for the economy; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: slow growth in output per hour; flat wages; apprenticeships, broadband, rail, tax breaks.
- Analysis: higher productivity lowers unit costs, so firms can raise wages without raising prices, ending the wage stagnation; cheaper goods make Varnian exports more competitive; higher output is economic growth, raising incomes and tax revenue for public services; investment in skills and infrastructure raises output per hour across many firms.
- Evaluation: productivity matters only if the extra output can be sold: without demand, firms will cut jobs instead; gains may go to profits rather than wages unless workers have bargaining power; apprenticeships and rail take years to pay off, so the effect is long term; automation may raise productivity but cause structural unemployment in some regions; other things such as low unemployment and stable prices also matter. Judgement: e.g. very important, as it is the main route to higher wages and living standards in the long run, but the plans will take years and the gains must be shared with workers to raise living standards widely.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The town of Carrow relies on a large shipyard, which employs about a third of the town's workers. Orders for new ships have fallen and the yard's production has dropped by 40% in two years. Many local shops and cafés say their sales have fallen.

Evaluate the importance of production at the shipyard for the town of Carrow. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation with a fully supported judgement about the town; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: a third of workers at the yard; production down 40%; local shops' sales falling.
- Analysis: falling production means the yard needs fewer workers, so jobs and incomes fall; workers spend less in local shops and cafés, so their sales fall too and they may cut staff (a knock-on effect); lower incomes mean less tax revenue and more spending on benefits; the yard buys from local suppliers, who also lose orders.
- Evaluation: the importance depends on whether workers can find other jobs: shipbuilding skills may not transfer easily, so unemployment could last; other employers might move in if the government offers support; if the fall in orders is temporary, production may recover; a third of workers is a large share, so the town is very dependent on one producer, which is a risk in itself. Judgement: e.g. very important, because so many jobs and so much local spending depend on it, unless new employers can be attracted to replace the lost production.
:::
