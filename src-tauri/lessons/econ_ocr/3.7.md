## What it is

**Supply side policies** are measures that aim to increase the **productive capacity** of the economy: the maximum amount of goods and services it can produce. They work by improving the **quantity and quality of the factors of production** (land, labour, capital and enterprise) and by making markets work more efficiently. This topic covers what supply side policy is, **how it can be used to achieve economic objectives**, and the **costs, including opportunity cost, and benefits** of supply side policies for the economy (which the spec asks you to evaluate).

### Demand side versus supply side

Fiscal policy (3.5b) and monetary policy (3.6) mainly change **total spending** in the economy: the demand side. If spending rises but the economy cannot produce more, the result is inflation rather than growth. Supply side policies instead raise the economy's **ability to produce**, so output can grow **without** pushing up prices.

A simple way to think about it: demand side policies decide how much of the economy's capacity is **used**; supply side policies **increase that capacity**.

### Examples of supply side policies

| Policy | How it raises productive capacity |
|---|---|
| **Education and training** (schools, colleges, apprenticeships, retraining schemes) | workers gain skills, so they are more **productive** and can move into growing industries (less **occupational immobility**) |
| **Infrastructure** (roads, railways, ports, energy networks, broadband) | cuts firms' transport and communication costs, opens up new markets and areas for business, raises productivity |
| **Lower income tax** | a bigger reward for working gives an **incentive** to work longer hours, take a job or seek promotion, increasing the supply of labour |
| **Lower corporation tax and investment allowances** | firms keep more profit and are encouraged to **invest** in capital; may attract foreign firms |
| **Support for research and innovation** (grants, tax relief for research) | new technology and products raise productivity and competitiveness |
| **Reducing regulation ("red tape")** | lower costs of complying with rules, making it cheaper and quicker to start and run a business |
| **Encouraging competition and privatisation** | more competition pushes firms to cut costs, improve quality and innovate (links to 2.5) |
| **Welfare reform** | changes to benefits that make work pay more than not working, encouraging people into jobs |
| **Help for geographical mobility and childcare** | help with moving or with childcare costs lets more people take available jobs |

Some are **government spending** (training, infrastructure, research) and some are **market-based** (tax cuts, deregulation, competition).

### How supply side policies achieve economic objectives

| Objective | How supply side policy helps |
|---|---|
| **Economic growth** | more and better resources raise capacity, so the economy can produce more in the long run (3.1) |
| **Low unemployment** | training and mobility help reduce **structural** unemployment by matching workers' skills and locations to vacancies (3.2) |
| **Price stability** | higher productivity cuts firms' costs per unit, and more capacity lets output rise without demand-pull inflation (3.4) |
| **The balance of payments** | lower costs and better products make the country's exports more **competitive** (4.2) |

### Costs and benefits

| Benefits | Costs, including opportunity cost |
|---|---|
| **long-term growth** without inflation | **time lags**: training a worker or building a railway can take years before output rises |
| tackles the **cause** of structural unemployment, not just its symptoms | **financial cost** to the government: spending on training or infrastructure, or lost revenue from tax cuts, may widen the **budget deficit** |
| makes firms more **competitive** at home and abroad | **opportunity cost**: the money could have been spent on health care, benefits or other services, or used to cut other taxes |
| can raise **living standards** and wages through higher productivity | **uncertain results**: trained workers may still not find jobs; tax cuts may be saved rather than used to work or invest |
| more tax revenue in future from higher output can repay the cost | **tax cuts** for higher earners and firms may widen **inequality** |
| | **deregulation** may weaken protection for workers, consumers or the environment |
| | infrastructure projects can go **over budget**, damage the environment and disrupt local communities |

**Evaluation questions to ask**: How long before the benefits arrive? Who gains and who pays? What else could the money have done? Does the policy fix the real problem (for example, a skills shortage), or is the problem a lack of demand, in which case supply side policy will not help much in the short run?

**Quick calculations (invented figures).**
- A retraining scheme costs £45 million and trains 15,000 workers: cost per worker = £45m ÷ 15,000 = **£3,000**.
- If training raises output per worker from 40 to 46 units a day, productivity rises by 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = **15%**.

::: terms
- **Supply side policy**: a measure that aims to increase the productive capacity of the economy by improving the quantity or quality of the factors of production or the way markets work.
- **Productive capacity**: the maximum output of goods and services an economy can produce with its resources.
- **Productivity**: output per worker, or per unit of input, in a given period of time.
- **Infrastructure**: the basic physical systems an economy needs, such as transport, energy and communication networks.
- **Apprenticeship**: a job with training that combines paid work with study, giving workers new skills.
- **Deregulation**: removing or simplifying rules and regulations on firms to reduce their costs.
- **Privatisation**: transferring the ownership of a business or industry from the government to the private sector.
- **Incentive**: something that encourages people or firms to act in a particular way, such as a lower tax rate encouraging work.
- **Occupational mobility**: how easily workers can move between different jobs; training increases it.
- **Competitiveness**: the ability of a country's firms to sell goods and services at home and abroad against rivals, on price and quality.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain what is meant by supply side policy" (2)**: measures to increase the economy's productive capacity (1) + by improving the factors of production / how markets work, with an example (1).
2. **"Explain how [policy] can…" (2)**: what the policy changes (skills, costs, incentives) (1) → the effect on output, jobs, prices or competitiveness (1).
3. **Calculations (2)**: cost per worker = total cost ÷ number; % change in productivity = change ÷ original × 100. Show working; give £ or %.
4. **Analyse (6)**: chains from the policy → better or more factors of production → higher productivity or capacity → the objective (growth, jobs, inflation, exports); use the extract; no evaluation.
5. **Evaluate (6)**: weigh the long-run benefits against the costs: time lags, financial cost and **opportunity cost** (name the alternative), uncertainty, inequality or environmental harm. Reach a judgement about the extent and the time period.
:::

::: example Supply side policy
Explain what is meant by supply side policy. (2)

A policy to increase the productive capacity of the economy (1) by improving the quantity or quality of the factors of production, e.g. spending on training to make workers more skilled (1).
:::

::: example Training and structural unemployment
Explain how government spending on training can reduce structural unemployment. (2)

Training gives unemployed workers from declining industries the skills needed in growing industries (1), so they can fill vacancies, reducing the mismatch of skills that causes structural unemployment (1).
:::

::: example Why it is supply side
Invented context: the government of Corlan is spending £8 billion to expand its main seaport. Explain why this is a supply side policy. (2)

A larger port lets firms import materials and export goods faster and more cheaply (1), raising firms' productivity and the economy's capacity to produce and trade (1). (Not because it creates profit for construction firms.)
:::

::: example Cost per trainee
Invented figures: an apprenticeship scheme costs the government £72 million and supports 9,000 apprentices. Calculate the cost per apprentice. Show your working. (2)

£72,000,000 ÷ 9,000 (1) = **£8,000** per apprentice (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — spending on education and health
Invented context: the government of Ellis plans to spend an extra £5 billion a year on schools, colleges and health care. Analyse how this spending could increase the economy's output. (6)

This is a supply side policy, because it improves the quality of the labour force (K). More spending on schools and colleges means young people leave with better skills and qualifications, so they are more productive at work and can fill skilled vacancies (An). Higher productivity means firms produce more output with the same number of workers, lowering costs per unit and making Ellis's goods more competitive, so sales and output rise further (An). Better health care cuts sickness absence and lets people with long-term illnesses return to work, so the size of the workforce grows, raising capacity (An). With £5 billion a year sustained, a more skilled and healthier workforce may attract foreign firms to invest, adding to output and jobs (Ap/An).
:::

::: q
Explain how a cut in corporation tax can increase the productive capacity of an economy. (2)
---
Firms keep more of their profit after tax, so they have more to invest in capital such as machinery (1), increasing the economy's ability to produce goods and services / attracting foreign firms to set up (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one opportunity cost of a government spending £10 billion on a new motorway. (2)
---
The £10 billion could have been spent on something else, e.g. hospitals or schools (1), so health care or education may suffer / or taxes could have been cut or debt repaid instead (1).
:::

::: q
Invented figures: training raises the output of a factory worker from 50 to 58 units a day. Calculate the percentage increase in productivity. (2)
---
8 ÷ 50 × 100 (1) = **16%** (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why supply side policies may not reduce unemployment quickly. (2)
---
Many take a long time to work: training courses and infrastructure projects take years (1), so in the short run workers are still unemployed / if unemployment is caused by a lack of demand, more capacity will not create jobs (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how supply side policies can help to keep inflation low. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: supply side policies raise productive capacity (K); training and new technology raise productivity, so firms' costs per unit fall and they need not raise prices, reducing cost-push pressure (An); better infrastructure cuts transport costs, lowering costs further (An); more capacity means output can rise when demand rises, so shortages and demand-pull inflation are less likely (An); more competition through deregulation forces firms to keep prices down (An); a larger, more skilled labour supply eases wage pressure (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Supply side policy raises the economy's ability to produce.** It is not about boosting spending (that is fiscal or monetary policy).
- **Explain WHY it is supply side**: higher productivity or capacity, not profits for builders or more imports.
- **Link training to structural unemployment** (skills mismatch), not to cyclical unemployment.
- **Opportunity cost must name the alternative**: "the money could have funded hospitals", not just "it costs a lot".
- **Time lags matter**: benefits often take years, so a judgement should say short run versus long run.
- Tax-cut policies can widen inequality, and deregulation can harm workers or the environment. These are good evaluation points if linked to the policy in the question.
:::

Invented extract: The government of Haldor has cut corporation tax from 25% to 20% and introduced a tax relief that lets firms deduct the full cost of new machinery from their taxable profit. Haldor's manufacturing firms have used old equipment for many years, and productivity is lower than in neighbouring countries.

Using the information, analyse how these policies could affect Haldor's economy. (6)
---

Indicative content:
- Knowledge: supply side policy; corporation tax as a tax on profit; investment.
- Application: 25% → 20%; full relief for new machinery; old equipment; low productivity compared with neighbours.
- Analysis: lower corporation tax leaves firms with more retained profit to invest; the tax relief lowers the effective cost of machinery, so replacing old equipment becomes worthwhile; new machinery raises productivity, cutting costs per unit; lower costs make Haldor's goods more competitive at home and abroad, so exports and output rise; higher capacity allows growth without inflation; lower tax may attract foreign firms to set up in Haldor, creating jobs.

::: test
Invented extract: The government of Brancaster plans to spend £3 billion over five years on high-quality apprenticeships in engineering and digital skills. Brancaster has 40,000 unfilled engineering and IT vacancies and 250,000 unemployed people, many of them former factory workers. To fund the scheme, the government will cut spending on road repairs.

Evaluate the costs and benefits of the apprenticeship scheme for Brancaster's economy. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: £3bn over five years; engineering and digital apprenticeships; 40,000 vacancies; 250,000 unemployed former factory workers; road repairs cut.
- Analysis: apprentices gain the skills firms need, so vacancies are filled, output rises and structural unemployment falls; more skilled workers raise productivity and competitiveness; employed workers pay tax and stop claiming benefits, improving the budget; opportunity cost: worse roads raise firms' transport costs and may reduce productivity.
- Evaluation: only 40,000 vacancies against 250,000 unemployed, so most unemployed people will not be helped directly; benefits take years, as apprenticeships last several years; older former factory workers may be less willing or able to retrain; the road cuts may undo some of the gain; but tackling the skills gap addresses a cause of structural unemployment, so benefits could last. Judgement: e.g. the benefits outweigh the costs in the long run, because filling skilled vacancies raises output permanently, but in the short run the effect on unemployment will be small and the road cuts are a real cost.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The government of Mosser wants to raise its growth rate. It is choosing between cutting the top rate of income tax from 50% to 40%, costing £6 billion a year, or spending the same £6 billion on improving railways and broadband. Inequality in Mosser is already high.

Evaluate which of the two supply side policies would be better for Mosser's economy. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: top rate 50% → 40%; £6bn a year either way; railways and broadband; high inequality.
- Analysis: a lower top rate gives high earners and entrepreneurs an incentive to work, invest and start firms, and may attract skilled workers from abroad; infrastructure cuts firms' costs, raises productivity across the economy and creates construction jobs now.
- Evaluation: high earners may simply keep the extra money or save it, so the effect on work and enterprise is uncertain; the tax cut widens already high inequality; infrastructure benefits a wider range of firms and regions but takes years and can go over budget; both have the same £6bn opportunity cost. Judgement: e.g. infrastructure is better, because its effect on productivity is more certain and spread more widely, and it does not worsen inequality, though its benefits come more slowly.
:::
