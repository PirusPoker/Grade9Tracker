## What it is

When the price of a good rises, producers want to supply more, but **how much more, and how quickly**, varies hugely. A toy factory with idle machines can double output in a week; an orchard needs years to grow new trees. **Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures this. This topic covers the meaning and calculation of PES, elastic and inelastic supply, **drawing supply curves of different elasticity**, the factors that affect PES, and the importance of PES for **consumers and producers**, which the spec asks you to evaluate.

### Meaning and calculation

**Price elasticity of supply** measures the **responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price**.

**PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price**

Price and quantity supplied move in the **same** direction, so PES is **positive**.

| Value | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| greater than 1 | **price elastic supply** | the % change in quantity supplied is **bigger** than the % change in price |
| less than 1 | **price inelastic supply** | the % change in quantity supplied is **smaller** than the % change in price |
| 0 | **perfectly inelastic** | quantity supplied cannot change at all, whatever the price |
| infinity | **perfectly elastic** | producers will supply any quantity at one price, but none below it |

**Worked example.** Invented data: the price of oak garden benches rises from £150 to £180, and a joinery workshop increases weekly supply from 40 to 46 benches.
- % change in price = (180 − 150) ÷ 150 × 100 = +20%
- % change in quantity supplied = (46 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = +15%
- PES = 15 ÷ 20 = **0.75**, so supply is **price inelastic**: the workshop cannot raise output as fast as the price rose (perhaps because skilled joiners and seasoned oak are hard to find quickly).

A definition must use **percentages**. "Supply is not very responsive" earns at most 1 mark; "the percentage change in quantity supplied is smaller than the percentage change in price" earns 2.

### Drawing supply curves of different elasticity

Over the same price range, a **steep** supply curve is **inelastic** (quantity barely changes) and a **shallow** curve is **elastic** (quantity changes a lot). A **vertical** curve is perfectly inelastic, for example the number of seats in a stadium for one match. A **horizontal** curve is perfectly elastic.

### What affects PES?

| Factor | Supply is more **elastic** when… | Supply is more **inelastic** when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | there is a **long** period to respond: firms can build factories, train workers, plant crops | the period is **short**: in the very short run output is fixed (the fish already landed today); crops need a growing season; new factories take years |
| **Spare capacity** | firms have idle machines, empty floor space, workers who could do overtime | firms are already working at full capacity |
| **Stocks and storage** | the product can be stored, so stocks can be released quickly when the price rises (tinned food, clothing) | the product is perishable or cannot be stored (fresh flowers, a hotel room for tonight) |
| **Availability and mobility of factors** | extra workers, materials and land are easy to find and move | skilled labour, raw materials or land are scarce (housing in a city with little building land) |
| **Ease of switching production** | firms can switch machines from another product quickly (a bakery switching from rolls to pizza bases) | production is specialised and hard to change |
| **Production time** | the good can be made quickly | production takes a long time (aircraft, wine that must age, new homes) |

**Time is usually the most important factor.** Almost all supply is more elastic in the long run than in the short run. This is why, after a sudden jump in demand, prices often rise steeply at first and then ease back as producers expand.

The same rise in demand (D to D1) causes a **large price rise and a small rise in quantity** when supply is inelastic (left), but a **small price rise and a large rise in quantity** when supply is elastic (right).

| | **Inelastic supply** | **Elastic supply** |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** when demand rises | prices jump; many go without or face shortages and waiting lists; less affordable (e.g. city housing, festival tickets, seasonal fruit) | prices rise only a little; firms quickly make more, so consumers get the extra goods |
| **Producers** when demand rises | earn more per unit but cannot sell many more units; existing producers may make large profits for a while; lost sales to anyone who cannot be served | can capture the extra sales and grow; revenue rises mainly through volume; competitors can also expand, so prices stay keen |
| When demand **falls** | prices fall sharply because output cannot be cut quickly (farmers with a harvest already grown) | firms cut output quickly, so prices stay fairly stable |

**Evaluation points for 6-markers**: the **time period** (short-run inelastic supply often becomes elastic in the long run); **how big and how lasting** the change in demand is; **PED** too (inelastic supply matters most when demand is also inelastic); whether firms can **build capacity, hold stocks or import**; **competition** (new firms entering raise market supply even if one firm cannot expand).

::: terms
- **Price elasticity of supply (PES)**: the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price, calculated as % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price.
- **Price elastic supply**: when the percentage change in quantity supplied is greater than the percentage change in price (PES above 1).
- **Price inelastic supply**: when the percentage change in quantity supplied is smaller than the percentage change in price (PES below 1).
- **Perfectly inelastic supply**: quantity supplied is fixed whatever the price, shown by a vertical supply curve.
- **Perfectly elastic supply**: producers will supply any amount at one price, shown by a horizontal supply curve.
- **Spare capacity**: unused resources such as idle machines or space, which let a firm raise output quickly.
- **Stocks**: goods already produced and stored, which can be released for sale when the price rises.
- **Short run**: the period in which at least one factor of production, such as factory size, cannot be changed.
- **Long run**: the period in which all factors of production can be changed, so supply can respond fully.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions**: always compare the **percentage change in quantity supplied** with the **percentage change in price**. Do not write about demand.
2. **Calculations**: % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100 for quantity supplied and for price; PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP; say elastic or inelastic. Show working.
3. **"Draw and label supply curves of different elasticity" (2)**: labelled axes (1); steep curve labelled inelastic and shallow curve labelled elastic, both upward sloping (1).
4. **Explain a factor (2)**: name it (1) + explain how it changes how quickly firms can raise output when price rises, applied to the product (1).
5. **"Using a diagram, analyse…" (6)**: draw the supply curve with the right steepness, shift demand, show P → P1 and Q → Q1, and analyse what this means for consumers or producers. No evaluation.
6. **Evaluate the importance of PES (6)**: for the group named, analyse the effect of the PES, then weigh it with time, size of the demand change, PED, competition and capacity, ending with a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain what is meant by price inelastic supply
Invented context: Larkhill Orchards grows cherries. Explain what is meant by "Larkhill's supply is price inelastic". (2)

The percentage change in the quantity of cherries Larkhill supplies is smaller than the percentage change in price (1), so if the price of cherries rises by 20%, Larkhill can increase supply by much less than 20%, because new cherry trees take years to bear fruit (1).
:::

::: example Calculating PES
Invented data: when the price of bags of compost rises from £5.00 to £5.50, a garden-supply firm increases weekly supply from 2,000 to 2,500 bags. Calculate the PES. Show your working. (2)

% change in price = (5.50 − 5.00) ÷ 5.00 × 100 = +10%; % change in quantity supplied = (2,500 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = +25% (1).
PES = 25 ÷ 10 = **2.5**: price elastic supply (1).
:::

::: example Explaining a factor affecting PES
Explain how spare capacity affects the price elasticity of supply. (2)

If a firm has spare capacity, such as idle machines or space for an extra shift (1), it can raise output quickly when the price rises, so the percentage change in quantity supplied can be larger than the change in price and supply is more elastic (1).
:::

::: example Draw and label supply curves
Invented context: in the short run Hollin Brewery cannot increase output, but in the long run it can build a second brewhouse. Draw and label a diagram showing its short-run and long-run supply curves. (Sketch it on paper, or describe it in words.) (2)

Axes labelled Price and Quantity (1). A steep (or vertical) curve labelled "short-run supply: inelastic" and a shallower upward-sloping curve labelled "long-run supply: elastic" (1).
:::

::: example Using a diagram, analyse — inelastic supply
Invented context: a popular band announces a single reunion concert at the 20,000-seat Harbour Arena. Using a diagram, analyse why the price of tickets on resale websites rises sharply. (6)

Diagram: a vertical (perfectly inelastic) supply curve at 20,000 tickets; demand shifts right from D to D1; price rises from P to P1 with no change in quantity (K1 + An1).

The arena has a fixed number of seats for one night, so whatever the price, no more than 20,000 tickets can be supplied: supply is perfectly inelastic (Ap). The reunion creates a huge rise in demand from fans, shifting demand far to the right (An). Because quantity cannot rise to meet the extra demand, the whole adjustment falls on price: at the original price there is a large shortage, so tickets are resold at much higher prices until only fans willing and able to pay P1 remain (An). This means fans on lower incomes are priced out, while those who bought at face value and resell can make large gains (An). The promoter could only have increased supply by adding more concerts, which is the long-run way to make supply more elastic (An).
:::

::: q
State two factors that make supply more price elastic. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): a longer time period; spare capacity; stocks that can be stored and released; factors of production easily available/mobile; easy to switch production from other goods; short production time.
:::

::: q
Explain why the supply of fresh flowers is usually price inelastic in the short run. (2)
---
Flowers are perishable and take time to grow, so stocks cannot be held (1), meaning growers cannot quickly raise the quantity supplied when the price rises, so the % change in quantity supplied is smaller than the % change in price (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: PES for a brand of sports socks is 1.5. The price rises by 6%. Calculate the percentage change in quantity supplied. (2)
---
%ΔQs = PES × %ΔP = 1.5 × 6 (1) = +9% (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between price elastic supply and price inelastic supply. (2)
---
Elastic: the percentage change in quantity supplied is greater than the percentage change in price (PES > 1) (1). Inelastic: the percentage change in quantity supplied is smaller than the percentage change in price (PES < 1) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the supply of new houses in a city is often price inelastic. (2)
---
Building land in the city is scarce and new homes take a long time to plan and build (1), so when house prices rise, the number of homes supplied increases by a much smaller percentage (1).
:::

::: q
Using a diagram, analyse how a manufacturer with spare capacity benefits from a rise in demand for its product. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 correct diagram (shallow S, D shifts right, small P rise, large Q rise) used in developed chains; L2 3–4 single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: spare capacity makes supply elastic (K); diagram shows D → D1 with a shallow S, so Q rises a lot and P a little (K1 + An1); the firm can quickly run idle machines or extra shifts, so it captures the extra sales rather than turning customers away (An); revenue rises mainly through higher volume (An); average costs may fall as fixed costs are spread over more units, raising profit (An); keeping prices stable helps it keep customers who might otherwise switch to rivals (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Inelastic supply means the percentage change in quantity supplied is SMALLER than the percentage change in price.** The 2025 examiners gave only 1 mark to "less responsive" without percentages, and none to answers about demand.
- **Do not mix PES with PED.** PES is about producers and quantity supplied; it is positive.
- **Time matters most.** Nearly half the 2025 candidates missed the multiple-choice question on why time is important for supply: different goods take very different times to increase output.
- On diagrams, **steep = inelastic, shallow = elastic**, and label which curve is which.
- When analysing a demand rise with inelastic supply, show that the **price** does most of the adjusting, and say what that means for the group named.
- In "evaluate the importance for consumers" answers, write about consumers (prices, shortages, choice), not the firm's revenue.
:::
