## What it is

Globalisation (4.4a) creates winners and losers. This topic asks you to **evaluate the costs and benefits of globalisation for producers, workers and consumers** in **developed countries** (such as the UK) and in **less developed countries**, including the impact on **economic, social and environmental sustainability**. Exam questions always name a group and a type of country, so answer for exactly that group.

### Developed countries (e.g. the UK)

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Producers** | much bigger markets to sell to, so more sales and **economies of scale**; cheaper raw materials and components from around the world; can move production (**offshoring**) to lower-cost countries; can attract foreign investment and partners | more **competition** from low-cost foreign firms, so some lose sales or close; supply chains spread across the world can be disrupted by events far away |
| **Workers** | new jobs in exporting industries and in services such as finance, design and education that sell worldwide; jobs created when foreign multinationals invest in the UK; higher wages for skilled workers in growing industries | jobs lost when factories close or move abroad, often concentrated in particular towns, causing **structural unemployment**; competition from lower-wage countries holds down pay for **low-skilled** workers; less job security |
| **Consumers** | cheaper goods, because they are made where costs are lowest; far more **choice**; access to products from all over the world | **dominance of global brands**, with fewer local and independent businesses and less local variety; job insecurity for consumers who are also workers; dependence on imports of essentials |

### Less developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Producers** | access to **world markets** for their goods; investment, technology and management skills from multinationals; chances to **diversify** away from relying on a few primary products (crops, minerals); cheaper imported machinery | local firms may be **crowded out** by large global firms; dependence on multinationals that can move elsewhere; prices of primary exports can be unstable |
| **Workers** | **new jobs**, often paying more than the local alternatives such as subsistence farming; training and new skills; a move from low-productivity farming to factory and service work | poor **working conditions**, long hours and low pay by world standards; risk of **exploitation**, unsafe factories and child labour; little bargaining power because employers can move to another country |
| **Consumers** | more goods available, including imported medicines, phones and machinery; rising incomes let people buy more | some prices may rise as demand grows; traditional products and local culture may be replaced by global brands; gains are often unequal between cities and rural areas |

### Multinationals in less developed countries

A **multinational company (MNC)** building a factory in a less developed country can raise **GDP** through investment and jobs, pay taxes that fund schools and clinics, and bring technology that local firms learn from. But **profits are often sent back** to the MNC's home country, the jobs may be low-skilled, the MNC may push for low taxes or weak regulations, and it may leave if costs rise. Whether a country gains depends on how much of the value stays in the country, the conditions of work and the government's ability to regulate and tax.

### Sustainability

Globalisation must be judged against all three kinds of sustainability:

| Type | Can improve because… | Can worsen because… |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic sustainability** (lasting growth and incomes) | trade and investment raise growth and incomes; diversification reduces dependence on one product | dependence on MNCs or a few exports makes growth fragile; jobs lost in developed countries' declining industries |
| **Social sustainability** (fairness, communities, well-being) | higher incomes can pay for health care and education; poverty falls | **inequality** may widen within countries; poor working conditions and exploitation; towns hit by factory closures decline; local cultures weakened |
| **Environmental sustainability** (protecting resources for the future) | new, cleaner technology spreads; richer countries can afford to protect the environment | **pollution** from factories with weak regulation; **deforestation** for farmland and mining; transport **emissions** from shipping and flying goods long distances; over-use of natural resources |

### Moral and ethical issues

Firms and consumers in developed countries buying from abroad face **ethical questions**: are the goods made in safe conditions, by adults, for a fair wage? Are forests or water supplies being destroyed? Responses include **Fairtrade**-type schemes, firms checking their suppliers, and consumers choosing ethically produced goods, but these can raise prices.

### Evaluating globalisation

Useful "it depends" points:
- **which group** — consumers often gain while some workers lose;
- **skills** — skilled workers in developed countries usually gain; low-skilled workers in declining industries may lose;
- **regulation** — strong labour and environmental laws reduce the costs;
- **time** — structural unemployment can be long-lasting, but retraining helps;
- **how gains are shared** — higher GDP may not reach the poorest people;
- **alternatives** — a factory job may be poorly paid by UK standards but better than the local alternative.

**Quick calculation (invented figures).** A multinational's factory in a less developed country pays £5.60 a day; local farm work pays £3.50 a day. The factory pays (5.60 − 3.50) ÷ 3.50 × 100 = **60% more**.

::: terms
- **Developed country**: a country with high average incomes, a large service sector and good health care, education and infrastructure.
- **Less developed country**: a country with lower average incomes and weaker access to health care, education and technology, often relying on primary industries.
- **Multinational company**: a firm with its headquarters in one country that produces or operates in several countries.
- **Offshoring**: moving part of a firm's production or services to another country, usually to cut costs.
- **Structural unemployment**: unemployment caused by the long-term decline of an industry, so workers' skills no longer match the jobs available.
- **Exploitation**: treating workers unfairly, for example with very low pay, unsafe conditions or excessive hours.
- **Diversification**: spreading an economy's output across more industries so it relies less on a few products.
- **Economic sustainability**: the ability of an economy to keep growing and supporting incomes over the long term.
- **Social sustainability**: meeting people's needs fairly, supporting communities, well-being and reasonable equality now and in the future.
- **Environmental sustainability**: using resources and the environment in a way that does not damage them for future generations.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the group and the country type** in the question (UK consumers? workers in a less developed country?) and stay with them.
2. **"State two benefits/costs" (2)**: two short, correct points for that group, each a phrase rather than one word.
3. **"Explain one cost/benefit" (2)**: the point (1) + why it happens or what follows for that group (1).
4. **Calculations (2)**: % difference in wages, % change in jobs or investment = change ÷ original × 100. Show working and units.
5. **Analyse (6)**: chains such as MNC investment → jobs and incomes → spending and tax → growth; use the extract. No evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: pair each benefit with a qualification of the same point (jobs, but conditions; cheaper goods, but jobs lost), consider sustainability, and reach a judgement on the extent for the group named.
:::

::: example Cost for UK workers
Explain one cost of globalisation for UK workers. (2)

Firms may move factories to countries with lower wages (1), so UK workers in those industries lose their jobs and may become structurally unemployed if their skills do not suit the jobs available (1).
:::

::: example Benefits for producers in a less developed country
State two benefits of globalisation for producers in a less developed country. (2)

Access to larger world markets for their goods (1); investment, technology or new skills from multinational companies (1).
:::

::: example Cost for UK consumers
Explain one cost of globalisation for consumers in developed countries. (2)

Large global brands come to dominate high streets and markets (1), so smaller local firms close and consumers have less local variety and distinctive products to choose from (1).
:::

::: example Wage comparison
Invented figures: a multinational clothing factory in the country of Pellana pays workers £6.30 a day, while work on local farms pays £4.50 a day. Calculate the percentage by which factory pay exceeds farm pay. Show your working. (2)

(6.30 − 4.50) ÷ 4.50 × 100 = 1.80 ÷ 4.50 × 100 (1) = **40%** (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — multinational investment
Invented context: a multinational electronics firm is building a £400 million factory in the less developed country of Ombara, employing 6,000 people. Most of Ombara's workers currently earn a living from farming. Analyse how this investment could affect Ombara's economic growth. (6)

The factory is foreign direct investment by a multinational company (K). Building a £400 million factory adds directly to investment spending in Ombara, raising output while it is built (Ap/An). Once open, 6,000 people move from farming into factory work, where each worker produces more value per day, so productivity and total output rise (Ap/An). The workers earn higher incomes and spend them in local shops and services, creating more jobs and further output in Ombara (An). The firm and its workers pay taxes, which the government can spend on roads, schools and health care, raising the economy's productive capacity (An). Local suppliers may win contracts and learn new technology and methods from the firm, spreading higher productivity to Ombara's own businesses and raising growth further (An).
:::

::: q
State two benefits of globalisation for consumers in the UK. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): lower prices for goods made where costs are lowest; more choice of products; products from all over the world, such as out-of-season food; better quality from competition.
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit of globalisation for workers in a less developed country. (2)
---
Multinationals create new factory and service jobs (1), which often pay more than local alternatives such as subsistence farming, raising workers' incomes and living standards / workers gain training and skills (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one cost of globalisation for workers in a less developed country. (2)
---
Workers may face poor conditions such as long hours, low pay and unsafe factories (1), because they have little bargaining power — if they demand more, the firm can move production to another low-cost country (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way that globalisation can reduce environmental sustainability. (2)
---
Goods are transported long distances by ship and air (1), increasing carbon emissions and pollution / factories in countries with weak regulations may pollute rivers and air, damaging resources for future generations (1).
:::

::: q
Invented figures: the number of manufacturing jobs in the UK town of Aldwick fell from 12,500 to 9,500 after two firms moved production abroad. Calculate the percentage fall in manufacturing jobs. Show your working. (2)
---
(12,500 − 9,500) ÷ 12,500 × 100 = 3,000 ÷ 12,500 × 100 (1) = **24%** fall (1).
:::

::: q
Evaluate whether globalisation benefits producers in developed countries. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 good application, analysis and well-supported judgement; L2 3–4 judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content: producers gain bigger markets, so sales and economies of scale rise; cheaper inputs and offshoring cut costs and raise profit. But they face more competition from low-cost rivals, so less efficient firms lose sales or close; global supply chains can be disrupted. It depends on the firm: those with strong brands, high quality or exportable services gain most; low-cost manufacturers competing on price may lose. Judgement: e.g. most producers benefit overall, but the gains go mainly to large firms able to export and offshore, while smaller manufacturers competing on price lose out.
:::

::: watch
- **Answer for the group and country named.** A cost for UK consumers (e.g. dominance of global brands) is not the same as a cost for UK workers (job losses).
- **"State two" needs two different points**, each more than a single word: "cheaper goods" and "more choice", not "cheap" and "cheaper".
- **Use the right type of unemployment**: factory closures caused by global competition create **structural** unemployment.
- **Sustainability has three parts**: economic, social and environmental. Say which you mean.
- **Pair your evaluation**: "factory jobs pay more than farm work, but conditions may be unsafe", not a list of benefits then a separate list of costs.
- **Judge the extent**: "for most workers", "in the short run", "only if regulation is strong". Do not just summarise.
:::

::: test
What is the most likely cost of globalisation for consumers in developed countries? A — Higher prices for imported goods; B — Less choice of imported products; C — The dominance of global brands, with less local variety; D — Higher tariffs on goods from abroad. (1)
---
C — global brands can crowd out local and independent businesses; A, B and D are not typical results of globalisation.
:::

(1)

A — the others are not benefits for workers in a less developed country.

B — deforestation damages resources for future generations.

::: test
Invented extract: Over 15 years, the less developed country of Moriva has attracted many multinational companies making clothing and car parts. The share of Moriva's workers in factory jobs has risen from 10% to 35%. Average incomes have doubled. However, two rivers near the main industrial zone are now heavily polluted.

Using the information, analyse how globalisation has affected Moriva's economy. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Moriva; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: globalisation; multinational companies; foreign direct investment; sustainability.
- Application: 15 years; clothing and car parts; factory share of workers 10% to 35%; average incomes doubled; two polluted rivers.
- Analysis: multinational investment created factory jobs, so workers moved from lower-productivity work, raising output and GDP; doubled incomes mean more spending, creating jobs in local shops and services; more tax revenue lets the government spend on infrastructure, health and education; the economy has diversified into manufacturing, reducing dependence on primary products; pollution of rivers damages water supplies, fishing and farming, raising health costs and reducing environmental sustainability, which could harm future output.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The developed country of Arnley has lost 200,000 manufacturing jobs over ten years as firms moved production to lower-cost countries. Over the same period, 260,000 jobs were created in Arnley's software, design and financial services industries, most requiring degree-level skills. Prices of clothes and electronics in Arnley have fallen by about a fifth.

Evaluate the benefits of globalisation for workers in Arnley. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: 200,000 manufacturing jobs lost; 260,000 service jobs created; degree-level skills needed; clothes and electronics prices down about a fifth.
- Analysis: more jobs were created than lost, so overall employment rose by 60,000; service jobs are often better paid, raising incomes; cheaper clothes and electronics raise all workers' real incomes as consumers; but manufacturing workers who lost jobs face structural unemployment.
- Evaluation: the new jobs need degree-level skills, so many former factory workers cannot fill them without retraining, meaning the gains go to different people from those who lost out; job losses may be concentrated in particular towns, which decline, harming social sustainability; cheaper goods are a small benefit if a worker has lost their job; retraining and time could help some workers move. Judgement: e.g. globalisation has benefited Arnley's workers as a whole, but not those in manufacturing, for whom the costs are large and long-lasting unless the government funds retraining.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Multinational firms have built mines and plantations in the less developed country of Tavira. Exports have tripled and GDP has grown by 6% a year. Large areas of rainforest have been cleared, and some mines have been fined for releasing chemicals into rivers. Tavira's government has used some of the tax revenue to bring electricity to rural villages.

Evaluate the impact of globalisation on sustainability in Tavira. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation of the three kinds of sustainability, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: mines and plantations; exports tripled; GDP growth 6% a year; rainforest cleared; chemical releases and fines; electricity for rural villages.
- Analysis: economic sustainability improves as exports and GDP grow, raising incomes and tax revenue; social sustainability improves as tax revenue brings electricity to villages, helping education and small businesses; environmental sustainability falls as rainforest is cleared and rivers polluted, damaging resources for future generations.
- Evaluation: growth based on mining and plantations depends on natural resources that may run out or be destroyed, so economic gains may not last — economic and environmental sustainability are linked; fines show some regulation exists but may be too small to change behaviour; the benefits of electricity reach rural people, but profits may flow abroad to the multinationals; pollution of rivers harms the health and farming of local people, reducing social sustainability too. Judgement: e.g. globalisation has improved Tavira's economic and some social sustainability in the short run, but because the growth relies on destroying the rainforest and polluting rivers, it is not sustainable in the long run unless regulation is strengthened.
:::
