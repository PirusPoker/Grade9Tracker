## What it is

**Monetary policy** is the use of **interest rates** (and, occasionally, the supply of money) to influence spending in the economy and achieve economic objectives, above all **price stability**. This topic covers what monetary policy is and **how it can be used to achieve economic objectives**; how it can affect **growth, employment and price stability** (which the spec asks you to analyse); and its effects on **consumer spending, borrowing, saving and investment** (which you must be able to evaluate). It builds directly on 2.8b (how interest rates affect saving, borrowing and investment) and 3.4 (inflation).

### Who runs monetary policy, and why

In the UK, monetary policy is run by the **Bank of England**, the central bank, not by the government. Its **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** meets regularly and votes on **Bank Rate**, the official interest rate that the Bank pays on money that commercial banks hold with it. Changes in Bank Rate pass through to the interest rates that banks and building societies charge on loans and mortgages and pay on savings.

- The **main aim** is **price stability**: the government sets an inflation target of **2%** for CPI inflation, and the MPC sets Bank Rate to try to meet it.
- Subject to that, monetary policy also supports the government's other objectives of **growth** and **employment**.
- In very weak economies, the Bank has also used **quantitative easing** (creating new money to buy bonds, pushing longer-term interest rates down). You only need to know it exists.

**Monetary versus fiscal policy**

| | **Monetary policy** | **Fiscal policy** (3.5b) |
|---|---|---|
| Tool | interest rates (Bank Rate), money supply | government spending and taxation |
| Who decides | the Bank of England's MPC (independent) | the government (the Chancellor's Budget) |
| Main target | inflation (2% CPI) | growth, jobs, distribution, the budget |

### How a change in interest rates affects the economy

**A cut in Bank Rate (loosening monetary policy)**
- **Borrowing** becomes cheaper → households take out more loans and mortgages, and existing variable-rate borrowers pay less each month → more **disposable income** → more **consumer spending**.
- **Saving** earns less → less reason to save → more spending now.
- **Investment**: borrowing to buy machinery and buildings costs less, so more projects are worthwhile → firms **invest more**.
- Firms sell more → they raise **output** (**growth**) and **hire more workers** (**employment**).
- Risk: if spending rises faster than the economy's capacity, prices rise → **demand-pull inflation**.

**A rise in Bank Rate (tightening monetary policy)**
- Borrowing is dearer and saving more rewarding → consumers borrow and spend less, and variable-rate mortgage payments rise.
- Firms invest less.
- Total spending grows more slowly → firms have less scope to raise prices → **inflation falls** towards the target.
- Cost: slower **growth** and possibly **higher unemployment** as firms sell less.

**The exchange-rate route (links to 4.3).** Higher UK interest rates make it more attractive for foreign savers to hold money in pounds, so demand for the pound rises and the **pound strengthens**. Imports become cheaper (helping to reduce inflation), but UK exports become dearer abroad, which hurts exporters. A cut tends to weaken the pound, with the opposite effects.

**Always finish the chain** with the effect on the objective in the question (growth, employment or price stability). "Interest rates fall so people borrow more" is only the start.

### Calculations you may meet (invented figures)

- **Mortgage payments.** An interest-only mortgage of £200,000 at 4.5% costs £9,000 a year (£750 a month). If Bank Rate rises and the mortgage rate goes to 5.25%, it costs £10,500 a year (£875 a month): **£125 a month more**.
- **Savings.** £12,000 saved at 3% earns £360 a year; after a cut to 2.25% it earns £270, **£90 less**.
- **Real interest rate** (approximately) = interest rate − inflation. Savings at 4% with inflation at 6% lose about **2%** of their value in real terms each year.

### Evaluating the effects of monetary policy

How much a change in interest rates affects spending, borrowing, saving and investment **depends on**:

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Size of the change** | a 0.25 percentage-point change may make little difference to decisions |
| **Confidence and expectations** | in a recession, worried households save and firms will not invest, however cheap borrowing is; in a boom, people may keep borrowing despite higher rates |
| **Fixed-rate mortgages and loans** | many borrowers on fixed deals are unaffected until their deal ends |
| **Time lags** | changes can take a year or more to have their full effect on spending and inflation |
| **Banks' behaviour** | banks may not pass the full change on, or may refuse to lend whatever the rate |
| **Levels of debt and saving** | households with large debts are hit hard by a rise; savers (often older people) gain |
| **The cause of inflation** | higher rates work well against demand-pull inflation, but against **cost-push** inflation (e.g. world energy prices) they mostly reduce growth and jobs instead |

**Winners and losers.** A rise helps **savers** and people living on interest, and hurts **borrowers**, people with variable-rate mortgages, firms with loans and exporters (if the pound rises). A cut does the reverse.

::: terms
- **Monetary policy**: the use of interest rates, and sometimes the money supply, to influence spending and achieve economic objectives, mainly price stability.
- **Bank Rate**: the official interest rate set by the Bank of England, which influences the rates that banks charge borrowers and pay savers.
- **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**: the committee of the Bank of England that decides Bank Rate.
- **Central bank**: the bank that manages a country's currency and monetary policy, such as the Bank of England.
- **Inflation target**: the rate of inflation the central bank aims for, 2% CPI inflation in the UK.
- **Loosening monetary policy**: cutting interest rates to encourage borrowing and spending.
- **Tightening monetary policy**: raising interest rates to discourage borrowing and spending and reduce inflation.
- **Quantitative easing**: when a central bank creates new money to buy financial assets such as government bonds, lowering longer-term interest rates.
- **Real interest rate**: the interest rate minus the rate of inflation, showing the true return on savings.
- **Time lag**: the delay between a change in interest rates and its full effect on spending and inflation.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain what is meant by monetary policy" (2)**: the use of interest rates (1), set by the central bank, to influence spending and meet objectives such as the 2% inflation target (1).
2. **"State two objectives…" (2)**: price stability / low inflation; economic growth; low unemployment; (also a stable exchange rate). Give the ones the question has not already named.
3. **"Explain how…" (2)**: interest change → cost of borrowing or reward for saving (1) → effect on spending, investment and the objective (1).
4. **Calculations (2)**: interest = amount × rate ÷ 100; monthly = yearly ÷ 12; find the interest at each rate and the difference. Show working; give £.
5. **Analyse (6)**: two or three chains (consumers, firms, the exchange rate), each ending in growth, jobs or inflation; use the extract; no evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse the expected effect, then qualify it with confidence, size of change, fixed-rate deals, time lags, debt levels, the cause of inflation and who wins or loses; reach a judgement about the extent.
:::

Explain what is meant by monetary policy. (2)

::: example Inflation above target
Explain how the Bank of England is likely to respond if inflation rises well above its target. (2)

:::

::: example Analyse — a cut in interest rates and growth
Invented context: growth in the country of Davara has slowed to 0.3%. Its central bank has cut its interest rate from 4.5% to 3%. Analyse how the cut in interest rates can affect economic growth in Davara. (6)

Monetary policy uses interest rates to influence spending (K). The cut of 1.5 percentage points makes loans and mortgages cheaper, so Davaran households borrow more for cars, furniture and homes, and those on variable-rate mortgages have more disposable income to spend (Ap/An). Saving earns less, so households save less and spend more now (An). Firms find it cheaper to borrow for new machinery, so more investment projects become profitable and investment rises, increasing both demand and future capacity (An). Higher spending raises firms' sales, so they increase output and hire workers, who spend their wages, raising growth above 0.3% (Ap/An). A lower rate may also weaken Davara's currency, making exports cheaper abroad, so export sales and output rise (An).
:::

::: q
Explain one difference between monetary policy and fiscal policy. (2)
---
Monetary policy uses interest rates set by the central bank (1), whereas fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation decided by the government (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a rise in interest rates can reduce inflation. (2)
---
Borrowing becomes dearer and saving more attractive, so consumers and firms spend less (1); with total spending growing more slowly, firms cannot raise prices as easily, so inflation falls (1).
:::

::: q
Invented figures: a saver has £20,000 in an account. Bank Rate rises and the account's rate goes from 2.5% to 4%. Calculate the change in yearly interest. Show your working. (2)
---
2.5%: £500; 4%: £800 (1); an increase of **£300** a year (1). Or 1.5% × £20,000 = £300.
:::

::: q
State two reasons why a cut in interest rates may not increase consumer spending. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): low consumer confidence / fear of job losses; many borrowers on fixed-rate deals; the cut is too small; banks do not pass it on or refuse to lend; households already have high debts; time lags; savers lose interest income and cut spending.
:::

::: q
Explain how a rise in interest rates could affect UK exporters. (2)
---
Higher interest rates attract savings into pounds, so the pound rises in value (1), making UK exports dearer in foreign currency, so exporters' sales and revenue fall (1). Or: higher borrowing costs for exporters' investment.
:::

::: q
Analyse how a rise in interest rates can affect employment. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: Bank Rate is the tool of monetary policy (K); higher rates make loans and mortgages dearer, so consumers cut spending on big items, and firms' sales fall (An); variable-rate borrowers' repayments rise, cutting disposable income and spending further (An); firms invest less because borrowing costs more, so capital-goods and construction firms lose orders (An); with lower sales, firms need fewer workers, so they stop hiring or make redundancies: cyclical unemployment rises (An); a stronger pound makes exports dearer, cutting jobs in exporting firms (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Monetary = interest rates (Bank of England); fiscal = tax and spending (government).** A tax cut is never monetary policy.
- **The main aim is price stability** (the 2% CPI target). Growth and jobs come second.
- **Finish every chain** with the objective: growth, employment or inflation. The 2024 examiners penalised chains that stopped at "people borrow more".
- Say **who** is affected: a rise helps savers and hurts borrowers.
- Higher rates **reduce** inflation by slowing spending. They do not cut firms' costs directly, so they work less well against cost-push inflation.
- Evaluate with the specific reasons: confidence, fixed-rate deals, time lags, size of the change, banks and debt levels. "It depends" without a reason earns nothing.
:::

Using the information, analyse how the cut in interest rates can affect employment in Varnia. (6)

::: test
Invented extract: The central bank of Tamsk has raised its interest rate from 2% to 4.5% to bring down inflation of 7%. Most of the inflation has been caused by a sharp rise in world energy and food prices. About 40% of Tamsk's households have fixed-rate mortgages, and household debt is high.

Evaluate the effect of the rise in interest rates on consumer spending in Tamsk. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: rate rise 2% → 4.5%; inflation 7%; caused by world energy and food prices; 40% fixed-rate; high household debt.
- Analysis: borrowing becomes dearer, so households borrow less for cars and holidays; variable-rate mortgage payments rise, cutting disposable income; saving becomes more attractive, so households spend less now; with high debts, many households must cut spending sharply to meet repayments.
- Evaluation: 40% on fixed rates are unaffected until their deals end, so the effect is delayed and smaller in the short run; high debt means those on variable rates cut spending a lot; savers gain more interest and may spend more; the inflation is cost-push, so lower spending will not bring energy and food prices down much, and households already squeezed by 7% inflation may cut spending further anyway. Judgement: e.g. consumer spending will fall significantly, mainly among indebted variable-rate households, but the effect on inflation may be limited because its cause is world prices.
:::
