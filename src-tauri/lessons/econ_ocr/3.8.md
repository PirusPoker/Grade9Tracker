## What it is

Markets do not always produce the best outcome for society. One important reason is **externalities**: effects of producing or consuming a good that fall on **third parties**, people outside the transaction, and are not reflected in the price. This topic covers what **positive and negative externalities** are, the **government policies** used to correct them (**taxation and subsidies, state provision, legislation and regulation, and information provision**), and how to **evaluate** the use and impact of these policies and their **costs, including opportunity cost, and benefits**.

### Externalities

When a buyer and seller agree a price, they think about their **own** costs and benefits. Other people may also be affected:

- A **negative externality** is a **cost** imposed on third parties who are not involved in the transaction and are not compensated. Examples: **air pollution** from factories and traffic; **noise** from an airport or a nightclub; **congestion** caused by car journeys; **CO₂ emissions** contributing to climate change; **passive smoking**; the **health-care costs** of illness linked to alcohol or tobacco paid by taxpayers; **litter** from fast-food packaging.
- A **positive externality** is a **benefit** enjoyed by third parties who did not pay for it. Examples: **vaccination** (it protects people around you by slowing the spread of disease); **education and training** (a more skilled workforce benefits employers and society, and educated people pay more tax); **public parks** and well-kept gardens; **beekeeping** (bees pollinate neighbours' crops); **restoring old buildings**; **public transport** (fewer cars means less congestion and pollution for everyone).

**Why this is a limitation of markets:**
- Goods with **negative** externalities are **over-produced and over-consumed**, because the price is too **low**: it does not include the cost to third parties.
- Goods with **positive** externalities are **under-produced and under-consumed**, because people consider only their own benefit, not the benefit to others.

So the market allocates resources in a way that does not give the best outcome for society, and there is a case for **government intervention**.

### Government policies to correct externalities

**1. Taxation (for negative externalities).** An **indirect tax** on the harmful good raises producers' costs, so the **supply curve shifts left**: the **price rises** and the **quantity falls**. The tax makes buyers and sellers pay something closer to the full cost to society, and raises **revenue** that can fund clean-up or health care. Examples: taxes on fuel, tobacco, alcohol, landfill, plastic packaging.

**2. Subsidies (for positive externalities).** A **subsidy** is a payment to producers that lowers their costs, so the **supply curve shifts right**: the **price falls** and the **quantity rises**. Examples: subsidies for public transport, home insulation, electric vehicles, apprenticeships.

Left: an indirect tax shifts supply left (S → S1), raising price (P → P1) and cutting quantity (Q → Q1). Right: a subsidy shifts supply right, lowering price and raising quantity. **Shift supply, never demand, and never use AD/AS diagrams for this.**

**Price elasticity matters (2.2b).** If demand is **price inelastic** (cigarettes, fuel), a tax causes only a **small fall in quantity**: it raises a lot of revenue but does little to cut the externality. If demand is **price elastic**, quantity falls more.

**3. State provision.** The government provides the good or service itself, often **free at the point of use**, paid for by taxes: vaccinations, state schools, public parks, street lighting. This raises consumption of goods with positive externalities to a level the market would not reach.

**4. Legislation and regulation.** Laws and rules that **ban** or **limit** harmful activities or set **minimum standards**, backed by **fines** or other penalties: smoking bans in public places, age limits on buying alcohol and tobacco, emission limits on factories and new cars, clean-air zones, compulsory school attendance, building standards for insulation.

**5. Information provision.** Giving people information so they make better choices: health warnings on cigarette packets, calorie and nutrition labels, energy-efficiency ratings, campaigns on the benefits of vaccination or the dangers of drink-driving.

### Evaluating the policies

| Policy | Benefits | Costs, limits and opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Tax** | uses the price system; raises revenue to fund health care or clean-up; polluters pay | hard to set the right rate; little effect if demand is **inelastic**; **regressive** (hits poorer households harder); may encourage smuggling or a **black market**; firms may lose competitiveness against rivals abroad |
| **Subsidy** | lowers the price and raises use of beneficial goods; encourages new green technology | costs taxpayers money, an **opportunity cost** (that money could fund schools or hospitals); firms may keep it as profit instead of cutting prices; hard to remove once given |
| **State provision** | everyone has access regardless of income; consumption reaches the desired level | very costly; needs tax revenue (opportunity cost); may be less efficient without a profit motive; long waiting lists |
| **Legislation and regulation** | clear and direct; can ban the most harmful activities completely | needs **enforcement** (inspectors, police) which costs money; fines may be too low to deter; can raise firms' costs; illegal markets may appear |
| **Information** | cheap; respects freedom of choice; tackles lack of knowledge | slow to work; people may ignore warnings, especially for addictive goods; hard to measure success |

Good evaluation weighs **how effective** a policy is (does it actually change behaviour?) against its **cost** and **opportunity cost**, and often concludes that a **combination** of policies works best (for example, tobacco tax plus age limits plus health warnings).

**Sustainability links.** Correcting negative externalities such as pollution supports **environmental sustainability**; subsidising education and health supports **social sustainability**; but taxes and regulations can cost jobs or raise prices, affecting **economic sustainability**.

::: terms
- **Externality**: a cost or benefit to a third party, not involved in the transaction, that is not reflected in the price.
- **Third party**: a person or group affected by a transaction who is neither the buyer nor the seller.
- **Negative externality**: a cost imposed on third parties by the production or consumption of a good, such as pollution or noise.
- **Positive externality**: a benefit enjoyed by third parties from the production or consumption of a good, such as protection from vaccination.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending on a good, collected by the seller, which raises costs and shifts supply to the left.
- **Subsidy**: a payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs and shifts supply to the right.
- **State provision**: when the government supplies a good or service itself, often free at the point of use and funded by taxes.
- **Legislation and regulation**: laws and rules that ban, limit or set standards for an activity, backed by penalties.
- **Information provision**: giving people information, such as health warnings or labels, so they make better choices.
- **Opportunity cost**: the next best alternative given up, such as other uses of the money spent on a subsidy.
- **Black market**: illegal buying and selling of goods, often to avoid a tax, a ban or a regulation.
- **Regressive tax**: a tax that takes a higher percentage of a low income than of a high income.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain what is meant by a negative/positive externality" (2)**: a cost/benefit to a third party not involved in the transaction (1) + an example or "not reflected in the price" (1).
2. **"State two…" (2)**: two distinct goods or policies, e.g. two goods with positive externalities (vaccinations, education).
3. **"Explain why governments subsidise / tax X" (2)**: how the policy changes costs and the price (1) + the effect on quantity and the externality (1).
4. **Diagrams (2)**: axes Price and Quantity; D and S; tax shifts S left (P up, Q down), subsidy shifts S right (P down, Q up). In the app, sketch it on paper or describe it in words.
5. **"Using a diagram, analyse…" (6)**: correct diagram (K + An), then chains from the policy to price, quantity and the externality, using the extract's figures; include elasticity if relevant; no evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse how the policy reduces the externality, then qualify it (elasticity, enforcement, regressive effect, black markets, cost and opportunity cost, firms keeping subsidies), compare with an alternative policy, and judge.
7. **Calculations (2)**: tax revenue = tax per unit × quantity after tax; subsidy cost = subsidy per unit × quantity after subsidy. Show working; give £.
:::

::: example Negative externality
Explain what is meant by a negative externality. (2)

A cost imposed on a third party who is not involved in the transaction and is not compensated (1), e.g. people living near a factory suffering from its air pollution (1).
:::

::: example Subsidy for vaccinations
Explain why governments often give subsidies for vaccinations. (2)

Vaccinations have positive externalities, as they protect other people from catching the disease, so without help too few people would be vaccinated (1); a subsidy lowers the cost and price, so more people are vaccinated and the benefit to society rises (1).
:::

::: example Tax revenue
Invented figures: a tax of £3 per item is placed on single-use vapes. Sales fall from 8 million to 6.5 million a year. Calculate the tax revenue. Show your working. (2)

Revenue = £3 × 6.5 million (the quantity after the tax) (1) = **£19.5 million** (1).
:::

::: example State two positive externalities
State two goods or services that create positive externalities. (2)

Vaccinations (1); education (1). (Also training, public parks, public transport, beekeeping.)
:::

::: example Analyse — a tax on short-haul flights
Invented context: the government of Ardenne plans an extra £30 tax on every short-haul flight ticket to reduce carbon emissions. Many trips have a fast train alternative. Using a diagram, analyse how the tax might change the market for short-haul flights. (6)

Diagram: Price on the vertical axis and Quantity of flights on the horizontal; D and S crossing at P and Q; the tax shifts supply left to S1, giving a higher price P1 and a lower quantity Q1 (K, An). Flights cause a **negative externality**, because CO₂ emissions harm third parties through climate change (K). The £30 tax raises airlines' costs per ticket, so they supply fewer flights at each price and pass much of the tax on as higher fares (Ap/An). Because many trips have a fast train alternative, demand is fairly price elastic, so passengers switch to trains and the number of flights falls significantly (Ap/An). Fewer flights mean lower emissions, so the external cost falls, and the tax raises revenue on the flights still taken, which could fund rail improvements (An).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a positive externality. (2)
---
A benefit enjoyed by a third party who is not involved in the transaction and does not pay for it (1), e.g. neighbours benefiting from a beekeeper's bees pollinating their crops (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why goods with negative externalities tend to be over-consumed. (2)
---
The price paid only covers the private cost to buyers and sellers, not the cost to third parties (1), so the price is too low and more is bought than is best for society (1).
:::

::: q
Invented figures: the government pays a subsidy of £400 for each heat pump installed. After the subsidy, 35,000 heat pumps are installed a year. Calculate the yearly cost of the subsidy. (2)
---
£400 × 35,000 (1) = **£14 million** (1).
:::

::: q
State two forms of legislation or regulation used to reduce smoking. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): a ban on smoking in enclosed public places; a minimum age for buying tobacco; a ban on advertising; plain packaging rules; limits on where tobacco can be displayed. Do not credit a tax (that is taxation).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of using information provision to reduce a negative externality. (2)
---
E.g. people may ignore warnings, especially for addictive goods (1), so consumption and the external cost fall only slowly or not at all / it takes a long time to change habits (1).
:::

::: q
Using a diagram, analyse how a subsidy for bus travel could affect the market for bus journeys. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains with a correct diagram; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks. Diagram (K1 + An1) needed for L3.
Indicative content: diagram with Price and Quantity axes, D and S, supply shifted right to S1, lower price P1, higher quantity Q1; a subsidy lowers bus companies' costs (K); they can run more services and charge lower fares (An); lower fares make buses more attractive than driving, so more journeys are taken (An); fewer cars mean less congestion and pollution for third parties, a positive externality (An); more passengers can make extra routes viable, raising quantity further (An).
:::

::: watch
- **An externality affects a THIRD PARTY**, not the buyer or seller. "The smoker damages their own health" is not an externality; passive smoking is.
- **A tax shifts SUPPLY left; a subsidy shifts SUPPLY right.** Do not shift demand, and never use an AD/AS diagram (the 2025 examiners marked these wrong).
- **Inelastic demand blunts a tax**: quantity falls only a little, though revenue is high.
- **"Explain why subsidies are given"** needs how the subsidy works (lower costs and price → more consumed), not just "to help".
- **Opportunity cost** must name what else the money could fund.
- In evaluation, compare with another policy and judge which works best, or argue for a combination. A list of disadvantages is not evaluation.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The government of Carrow has placed a tax of 20p per litre on petrol and diesel, raising the average pump price from £1.40 to £1.55. Most drivers in Carrow's countryside have no public transport alternative. Car journeys cause congestion and air pollution in Carrow's towns.

Using a diagram, analyse how the tax might change the market for petrol and diesel in Carrow. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — correct diagram and developed chains applied to Carrow; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application or no diagram; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks. Diagram (K1 + An1) needed for L3.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: diagram with Price and Quantity axes, D and S, supply shifted left to S1, higher price P1, lower quantity Q1; negative externality.
- Application: 20p tax; price £1.40 → £1.55, so 15p of the 20p is passed on to drivers and 5p is absorbed by sellers; no public transport in the countryside; congestion and pollution.
- Analysis: the tax raises sellers' costs, so supply shifts left and the price rises; drivers buy less fuel, so journeys, congestion and pollution fall; but because rural drivers have no alternative, demand is price inelastic, so quantity falls only a little; the tax still raises substantial revenue because sales stay high, which could fund public transport; sellers' profit margins fall as they absorb 5p a litre.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The country of Elmbury has low vaccination rates against a common childhood illness, which spreads easily in schools. The government is deciding whether to offer free vaccinations through local clinics, costing £60 million a year, or to run an information campaign about the benefits of vaccination, costing £5 million.

Evaluate whether free vaccinations would be better than an information campaign for increasing vaccination rates in Elmbury. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: low vaccination rates; illness spreads in schools; free vaccinations £60m a year; information campaign £5m.
- Analysis: vaccinations have positive externalities, protecting other children; free provision removes the price barrier, so low-income families vaccinate more, raising rates directly; fewer cases mean fewer school absences and lower health-care costs; an information campaign corrects a lack of knowledge, so parents who did not realise the benefits choose to vaccinate.
- Evaluation: if the main reason for low rates is cost, free provision works better; if it is lack of knowledge or fear of side effects, free vaccinations alone will not help and information is needed; the campaign costs £55m less, a large opportunity cost saving (that money could fund other health care); information is slow and some may ignore it; a combination may be most effective. Judgement: e.g. free vaccinations are likely to raise rates more quickly and reliably, justifying the higher cost because the illness spreads easily, but they work best alongside a smaller information campaign.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Factories along the river Wend in the country of Hassel release waste chemicals that kill fish and harm farmers and towns downstream. The government is choosing between a tax on each tonne of waste released and a law setting a strict limit on waste, with large fines for firms that break it.

Evaluate whether regulation would be better than a tax for reducing pollution in the river Wend. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: chemical waste; fish killed; farmers and towns downstream harmed (third parties); tax per tonne versus a strict limit with large fines.
- Analysis: a strict limit directly caps the amount of waste, so pollution falls by a known amount, protecting third parties; large fines give firms a strong reason to comply; a tax per tonne raises the cost of polluting, so firms cut waste where it is cheap to do so, and it raises revenue that could clean the river or compensate farmers.
- Evaluation: regulation only works if it is enforced: inspecting many factories is costly, and if firms are rarely caught they may keep polluting; with a tax, firms that can afford it may simply pay and keep polluting, so the outcome is less certain; if the chemicals are very harmful, a guaranteed limit matters more than revenue; higher costs from either policy may lead firms to cut jobs or raise prices. Judgement: e.g. regulation is better here, because the harm is serious and a firm limit guarantees a cut, provided the government spends enough on inspection; a tax could be added to fund the clean-up.
:::
