## What it is

Almost every transaction in the economy uses **money**, and much of that money passes through the **financial sector**: the banks, building societies, insurance companies and other institutions that look after savings, lend, make payments and manage risk. This topic covers money's role as a **medium of exchange**, the **role of the financial sector and its main institutions**, and the **importance of the financial sector for consumers, producers and the government**, which the spec asks you to evaluate. Interest rates follow in 2.8b.

### Money as a medium of exchange

A **medium of exchange** is anything that is **generally accepted in payment for goods and services**. Without money, people would have to **barter**: swap goods directly. Barter needs a **double coincidence of wants**: a baker who wants shoes must find a shoemaker who wants bread, at the same time, and agree how many loaves a pair of shoes is worth.

Money solves this. The baker sells bread to anyone for money, then uses the money to buy shoes from anyone. Because money is accepted by everyone:

- **trade is quicker and cheaper**: no time wasted searching for a matching swap;
- **specialisation becomes possible** (2.1b): people can concentrate on one job and buy everything else with their wages;
- **prices can be compared** easily, because everything is valued in the same unit.

Today most money is not notes and coins but **bank deposits**, moved by debit cards, contactless payments, phone apps and bank transfers. (Money also acts as a **store of value**, a **unit of account** and a means of **deferred payment**, but the spec names only the medium-of-exchange role.)

The financial sector's main jobs for the economy:

1. **Channelling savings into borrowing and investment**: savers deposit money they do not need now; banks lend it to households and firms who want to spend or invest now. Savers earn interest; borrowers pay a higher rate; the difference covers the bank's costs and profit.
2. **Making payments**: current accounts, debit and credit cards, transfers and direct debits let people and firms pay each other safely.
3. **Keeping money safe**: deposits are safer than cash at home.
4. **Managing risk**: insurance lets people and firms protect themselves against large, unexpected losses.
5. **Advice and other services**: financial advice, foreign currency for travel and trade.

### The main financial institutions

| Institution | What it is | Main services |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial banks** | private companies (owned by shareholders) that take deposits and lend, aiming to make a profit | current accounts and savings accounts; **loans**, **overdrafts** and **mortgages**; debit and credit cards; payments and transfers; foreign exchange; business advice |
| **Building societies** | **mutual** organisations owned by their **members** (savers and borrowers), not shareholders; profits are returned to members through better rates | mainly **savings accounts** and **mortgages** for home buyers; many now offer current accounts too |
| **Insurance companies** | firms that **pool risk**: many customers pay a regular **premium**, and the company pays out to the few who suffer a loss | insurance for cars, homes, contents, travel, life and health; for firms: buildings, stock, vehicles, **liability** (if a customer is injured) |
| **The central bank** (Bank of England, more in 3.6) | the government's bank and the banks' bank, not a high-street bank | issues banknotes; sets the official interest rate (**Bank Rate**); helps keep the financial system stable; can lend to banks in difficulty |

**How insurance works (invented figures).** 10,000 shop owners each pay a premium of £400 a year, so the insurer collects £4 million. In a typical year perhaps 50 shops suffer a fire or flood costing £60,000 each: £3 million in claims. Each shop owner swaps a small, certain cost (£400) for protection against a large, uncertain one (£60,000). Without insurance, many firms would not dare take on risks such as buying expensive equipment or opening a new shop.

**Banks lend; they do not give grants or subsidies.** In 2025 some candidates wrote that banks give businesses subsidies: subsidies come from the government, and bank loans must be repaid with interest.

### Why the financial sector matters

| Group | Importance | Limits / risks (for evaluation) |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | a safe place to save and earn interest; borrowing lets people **spread the cost** of big purchases (cars, university, home improvements); **mortgages** make home ownership possible; easy payments; insurance protects against disaster | debt can become unaffordable if incomes fall or rates rise; high charges on overdrafts and credit cards; mis-selling of unsuitable products; people on low incomes may be refused credit |
| **Producers** | **start-up and growth finance** (loans); **overdrafts** to cover short-term **cash-flow** gaps; payment services so customers can pay by card; insurance so firms can take risks; foreign exchange for importers and exporters | loans must be repaid with interest, even if sales fall; banks may refuse small, new or risky firms; overdrafts can be withdrawn; insurance premiums are a cost |
| **Government** | the government **borrows** by selling bonds to financial institutions, funding spending above tax revenue; the sector creates jobs and pays large amounts of **tax**; it earns money from overseas customers; it lets monetary policy (3.6) work, since changes in Bank Rate pass through banks to borrowers and savers | if banks lend recklessly and collapse, the whole economy suffers: in the 2008 financial crisis the UK government had to rescue several large banks at great cost to taxpayers; an economy too dependent on finance is exposed when it struggles |

**Evaluation points**: it depends on the **type** of consumer or firm (a start-up needs loans far more than an established, profitable firm with retained profit); on **interest rates** (borrowing is useful only if affordable); on **regulation** (well-regulated banks are safer); and on **alternatives** (savings, family, crowdfunding, government grants).

::: terms
- **Money**: anything generally accepted as payment for goods and services or in settling debts.
- **Medium of exchange**: the role of money as something generally accepted in payment, which avoids the need for barter.
- **Barter**: swapping goods or services directly for other goods or services without using money.
- **Double coincidence of wants**: the need, under barter, for each trader to want exactly what the other has to offer.
- **Financial sector**: the institutions, such as banks, building societies and insurance companies, that provide services to do with money.
- **Commercial bank**: a profit-making company that accepts deposits, makes loans and provides payment services.
- **Building society**: a mutual financial institution owned by its members that mainly provides savings accounts and mortgages.
- **Insurance company**: a firm that collects premiums from many customers and pays compensation to those who suffer a covered loss.
- **Premium**: the regular payment made to an insurance company in return for cover.
- **Mortgage**: a long-term loan used to buy property, secured on that property.
- **Overdraft**: an arrangement that lets an account holder spend more than is in their current account, up to a limit.
- **Central bank**: the bank of the government and of other banks, which issues notes and sets the official interest rate.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain the role of money as a medium of exchange" (2)**: generally accepted in payment (1) + so it avoids barter / the double coincidence of wants, making trade and specialisation easier (1).
2. **"Explain the role of [an institution]" (2)**: what it does (1) + how this helps the customer named, applied (1). Banks lend and hold deposits; building societies are mutuals offering savings and mortgages; insurers pool risk.
3. **"Explain one other way banks can help…" (2)**: read the question: if it says "other than lending", use payment services, advice, foreign exchange or savings accounts (an overdraft is still borrowing).
4. **Analyse (6)**: chain from the service → what it lets the customer do → the effect on spending, investment, growth or risk. No evaluation.
5. **Evaluate the importance for consumers / producers / government (6)**: two developed benefits, each paired with a limit (cost of interest, debt, refusal, alternatives, risk of collapse); a supported judgement for the group named.
:::

::: example Money as a medium of exchange
Explain the role of money as a medium of exchange. (2)

Money is generally accepted in exchange for goods and services (1), so people do not need to barter and find someone who wants exactly what they have to offer; e.g. a teacher can be paid in money and spend it on food, rather than swapping lessons for groceries (1).
:::

::: example The role of an insurance company
Invented context: Hawley Bikes hires out 200 bicycles to tourists. Explain the role of an insurance company for Hawley Bikes. (2)

Hawley pays a regular premium to the insurer, which agrees to pay out if bikes are stolen or damaged or a customer is injured (1). This turns a large, uncertain loss into a small, known cost, so one bad event will not force Hawley to close (1).
:::

::: example The role of a building society
Explain how a building society differs from a commercial bank. (2)

A building society is a mutual, owned by its members (its savers and borrowers), whereas a bank is owned by shareholders (1); so the society does not pay dividends to shareholders and can return profits to members through better savings or mortgage rates (1).
:::

::: example Other ways banks help a business grow
Invented context: Ferris Tiles is a small firm that wants to start exporting. Explain one way, other than lending, that a bank can help Ferris Tiles to grow. (2)

The bank can provide foreign-exchange services (1), converting the euros or dollars paid by overseas customers into pounds, so Ferris can sell abroad and win new customers (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a building society and first-time buyers
Invented context: Thornbury Building Society has launched a mortgage for first-time buyers that needs only a 5% deposit. Analyse how this could help young people in Thornbury buy a home. (6)

A mortgage is a long-term loan secured on a house, and a building society is a mutual that specialises in savings and mortgages (K). Most young buyers cannot pay the full price of a home in cash, so the mortgage lets them spread the cost over many years in monthly repayments (Ap/An). A 5% deposit means a buyer of a £200,000 home needs to save £10,000 rather than, say, £20,000 at 10%, so they can buy years earlier (An). Instead of paying rent to a landlord, their monthly payments gradually buy them a share of their own home, so they build up wealth (An). The building society also offers savings accounts, which help young people save for the deposit in the first place, earning interest while they do so (An). Because the society is owned by its members rather than shareholders, it may offer slightly lower mortgage rates, making monthly repayments more affordable (An).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a double coincidence of wants. (2)
---
Under barter, each person must want what the other has to offer (1), at the same time and in suitable quantities, e.g. a farmer with eggs must find a tailor who wants eggs (1).
:::

::: q
State two services provided by commercial banks. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): current accounts; savings accounts; loans; overdrafts; mortgages; debit/credit cards; payment services/transfers; foreign exchange; financial advice. Do not credit "subsidies" or "grants".
:::

::: q
Explain the role of an insurance company. (2)
---
It collects premiums from many customers and pays out to those who suffer a covered loss (1), so it pools risk, letting households and firms protect themselves against large, unexpected costs (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way the financial sector helps the government. (2)
---
E.g. the government borrows by selling bonds to banks and other financial institutions (1), so it can spend more than its tax revenue, e.g. on roads or hospitals (1). Or tax revenue / jobs from the sector, developed.
:::

::: q
Invented figures: 5,000 drivers each pay an annual premium of £600. Claims for the year total £2.4 million. Calculate how much more the insurer collected in premiums than it paid out in claims. (2)
---
5,000 × £600 = £3,000,000 (1); £3,000,000 − £2,400,000 = £600,000 (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how an overdraft can help a small business. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: an overdraft lets the firm spend more than is in its account, up to a limit (K); money often comes in after costs are paid, e.g. customers pay 30 days after delivery (An); the overdraft covers wages and suppliers during the gap, avoiding missed payments (An); suppliers keep supplying, so production continues (An); the firm can take on a large order it could not otherwise fund, raising sales (An); interest is charged only on what is used, so it can be cheaper than a fixed loan for short gaps (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Medium of exchange** means **generally accepted in payment**: link it to avoiding barter and the double coincidence of wants.
- **Banks lend; the government gives subsidies.** The 2025 examiners saw candidates say banks give grants or subsidies, which scores nothing.
- **Read "other than lending"**: if the question excludes lending, do not use loans, overdrafts or mortgages (all forms of borrowing); use payment services, advice, foreign exchange or savings accounts.
- **Building societies are mutuals** owned by members; banks are owned by shareholders.
- **Insurance pools risk** with premiums; it does not lend.
- The central bank (Bank of England) is not a high-street bank: it does not offer accounts to the public.
- In evaluation, answer for the group named: consumers, producers or government.
:::

Invented extract: Glenvale Community Savings is a building society with 40,000 members in a rural area. It has opened a branch in the market town of Cresby, where the last bank branch closed a year ago. It offers savings accounts and mortgages for first-time buyers and local businesses.

Analyse how the new branch could benefit households in Cresby. (6)
---

Indicative content:
- Knowledge: building society as a mutual; savings and mortgages.
- Application: last bank branch closed; rural area; first-time-buyer mortgages; 40,000 members.
- Analysis: households can deposit savings safely and earn interest locally, rather than travelling to another town; savings accounts help young people build up a deposit for a home; first-time-buyer mortgages let them spread the cost of a home over many years; as a mutual, profits go to members in better rates; face-to-face advice helps older residents who do not bank online; local businesses can borrow, creating jobs and incomes for households.

::: test
Invented extract: Small firms in the country of Dornia often struggle to borrow. A survey found that one in four applications for a business loan was refused, and firms that were approved paid higher interest rates than larger firms. Meanwhile, Dornian banks earned record profits and employ over a million people.

Evaluate the importance of the financial sector for small businesses in Dornia. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement about small businesses; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: one in four loan applications refused; higher rates for small firms; record bank profits; a million jobs.
- Analysis: loans let small firms start up and buy equipment, raising output and growth; overdrafts cover cash-flow gaps so firms can pay wages before customers pay; payment services and insurance let firms trade and take risks.
- Evaluation: the sector is important but not always helpful: one in four refused means many firms cannot get finance, limiting growth; higher interest rates raise costs and cut profit; firms may use other sources: retained profit, savings, family, crowdfunding or government schemes, so the sector's importance depends on the firm; record bank profits suggest banks could lend more, so the problem may be risk-aversion. Judgement: e.g. very important, as most small firms rely on banks for overdrafts, payments and insurance even if not for loans, but its usefulness for growth is limited by refusals and high rates.
:::
