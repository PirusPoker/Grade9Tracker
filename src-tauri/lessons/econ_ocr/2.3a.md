## What it is

Supply is the producers' side of the market. This topic mirrors demand (2.2a): what supply means, how to **draw individual and market supply curves from data**, the difference between a **movement along** the supply curve and a **shift** of it, and how to **analyse the causes and consequences** of these changes for consumers and producers. OCR has set "Using a diagram, analyse…" questions on supply in recent papers, so the diagrams matter.

### What supply means

**Supply** is the quantity of a good or service that producers are **willing and able** to sell at each price over a given period of time.

- **Willing**: the price must make it worth their while.
- **Able**: they must have the factors of production, materials and capacity to produce it.
- **At each price, over a period**: a whole schedule, per day, week or year.

**Individual supply** is the supply of one producer. **Market supply** is the total supply of all producers in the market: at each price, **add up the quantities** every producer is willing to sell.

### Why the supply curve slopes upwards

As the price rises, producers are willing to supply more because:

- a higher price makes each unit **more profitable**, giving an **incentive** to produce and sell more;
- a higher price lets firms **cover the extra costs** of producing more (overtime, extra materials, using older or less efficient machines);
- **new firms** are attracted into the market, and existing firms switch resources from less profitable products.

So the supply curve slopes **upwards from left to right**, with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.

### Drawing supply curves from data

Invented data: daily supply of punnets of strawberries from the three farms in the Ellerby growers' group.

### Movements along versus shifts

| | **Movement along** the supply curve | **Shift** of the supply curve |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | a change in the **good's own price** (usually because demand has changed) | a change in **any other factor**, such as costs |
| Names | **extension** of supply (price rises, quantity supplied rises); **contraction** (price falls, quantity supplied falls) | **increase** in supply (shift right); **decrease** in supply (shift left) |
| Diagram | from one point to another on the **same** curve | a **new** curve: S → S1 |

**Left:** demand rises (D → D1). The price is bid up from P to P1, and producers respond by supplying more, moving **up the existing supply curve** from Q to Q1. The supply curve has not moved: this is an **extension of supply**. A question asking about a "movement along the supply curve" needs this: something on the **demand** side changes the price.

**Right:** costs rise, so producers supply less at every price and the curve shifts **left** (S → S1). The price rises from P to P1 and the quantity traded falls from Q to Q1.

### What shifts supply

| Factor | Supply **increases** (shifts right) when… | Supply **decreases** (shifts left) when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Costs of production** (wages, raw materials, energy, rent) | costs fall | costs rise |
| **Technology and productivity** | better machines or methods let firms produce more at the same cost | machinery breaks down or becomes outdated |
| **Indirect taxes** (e.g. a tax per unit) | a tax is cut or removed | a tax is introduced or raised, adding to firms' costs |
| **Subsidies** (government payments to producers) | a subsidy is introduced, lowering costs | a subsidy is removed |
| **Number of firms** | new firms enter the market | firms leave or close |
| **Weather, disease and natural events** (especially in farming) | good growing conditions | drought, floods, frost, crop disease, a fire at a factory |
| **Prices of other goods the firm could make** | other products become less profitable, so firms switch into this one | another product becomes more profitable, so firms switch resources to it |
| **Expectations** | producers expect conditions to stay favourable | producers expect higher prices later and hold stock back |

### Consequences for consumers and producers

| Change | Consequences for **consumers** | Consequences for **producers** |
|---|---|---|
| **Decrease in supply** (S shifts left) | pay a **higher price** and buy less; some priced out; less choice; possible shortages at first | sell fewer units at a higher price; **revenue may rise or fall**, depending on PED (2.2b): with inelastic demand revenue can even rise; firms whose costs rose may see profits squeezed |
| **Increase in supply** (S shifts right) | pay a **lower price** and buy more | sell more at a lower price; revenue depends on PED; lower costs may raise profit per unit |
| **Extension of supply** (demand rises, price rises) | pay more; some priced out | sell more at a higher price, so revenue rises; may hire workers and expand capacity |

The 2025 examiners pointed out that a fall in output does **not** necessarily mean a fall in revenue: if demand is price inelastic, the higher price can more than make up for the lower quantity.

::: terms
- **Supply**: the quantity of a good or service producers are willing and able to sell at each price over a given period.
- **Individual supply**: the quantity one producer is willing and able to sell at each price.
- **Market supply**: the sum of all producers' supply of a product at each price.
- **Supply curve**: a line showing the quantity supplied at each price, usually sloping up from left to right.
- **Extension of supply**: a rise in quantity supplied caused only by a rise in the good's own price.
- **Contraction of supply**: a fall in quantity supplied caused only by a fall in the good's own price.
- **Increase in supply**: a shift of the supply curve to the right, so more is supplied at every price.
- **Decrease in supply**: a shift of the supply curve to the left, so less is supplied at every price.
- **Costs of production**: what firms pay for the factors and materials used to make their output, such as wages and energy.
- **Subsidy**: a payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs and increases supply.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending, such as a tax per unit sold, which raises firms' costs and decreases supply.
:::

::: method
1. **Is it the good's own price?** Yes → movement along (extension or contraction), usually triggered by a change in demand. No → shift (increase right, decrease left).
2. **Name the cause and why it changes how much firms are willing and able to sell at every price**: "energy prices rise, so costs per unit rise, so firms supply less at each price".
3. **Plotting from data**: label axes with units; even scales from 0; plot every point from the data and join them; label S. No line of best fit, no invented points.
4. **"Explain what is meant by a decrease in supply" (2)**: a shift of the supply curve to the left (1) + less is supplied at every price / caused by a non-price factor such as higher costs (1).
5. **"Using a diagram, analyse…" (6)**: draw D and S, the correct shift (supply for costs and taxes; demand if the question is about a movement along S), mark P → P1 and Q → Q1, and refer to the diagram in applied chains about consumers and producers. No evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse the effect, then weigh it with PED, the time period, how large and lasting the shock is, and alternatives available. Finish with a judgement for the group named.
:::

::: example Explain what is meant by supply
Explain what is meant by supply. (2)

Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell (1) at each price over a period of time, e.g. the number of loaves a bakery will bake each day at £1.50 each (1).
:::

::: example Explain what is meant by a decrease in supply
Explain what is meant by a decrease in supply. (2)

A decrease in supply is when the supply curve shifts to the left (1), so producers supply less at every price, caused by a non-price factor such as a rise in the cost of raw materials (1).
:::

::: example Market supply calculation
Invented data: at £3 a punnet, Farm A supplies 35 punnets a day, Farm B 20 and Farm C 10. At £4, they supply 45, 25 and 15. Calculate the percentage increase in market supply when the price rises from £3 to £4. Show your working. (2)

Market supply at £3 = 35 + 20 + 10 = 65; at £4 = 45 + 25 + 15 = 85 (1).
% change = (85 − 65) ÷ 65 × 100 = **30.8%** (1). (An extension of supply.)
:::

::: example Explain one reason for an increase in supply
Explain one reason why the supply of solar panels might increase. (2)

New technology could make solar panels cheaper to manufacture (1), so producers can make more panels at the same cost and are willing to supply more at every price, shifting the supply curve to the right (1).
:::

::: example Using a diagram, analyse — movement along supply
Invented context: a new national cycling campaign leads to many more people wanting their bikes serviced. Spokewell Repairs, one of many bike-repair shops in the town of Hadley, finds customers queuing. Using a diagram, analyse the possible consequences of a movement along the supply curve for bike repairs in Hadley. (6)

Diagram: D and S crossing at P and Q; demand shifts right to D1; price rises to P1 and quantity supplied extends along S from Q to Q1 (K1 + An1).

The campaign raises demand for repairs at every price, so the demand curve shifts right (Ap). At the old price there is excess demand (queues at Spokewell), so repair shops raise their prices to P1 (An). The higher price makes each repair more profitable, so shops are willing to do more repairs, for example opening longer or hiring another mechanic, so quantity supplied extends along S from Q to Q1 (An). This means shops' revenue rises, as they sell more repairs at a higher price, so they may expand and create jobs in Hadley (An). For consumers, more bikes are repaired but each repair costs more, so some cyclists on low incomes may delay repairs or learn to do them themselves (An).
:::

::: q
State two factors that could cause the supply of milk to decrease. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): rise in costs (animal feed, energy, wages); disease among cattle; drought reducing grass; farmers leaving the industry; removal of a subsidy; a new tax; farmers switching to more profitable products. Do not credit "a fall in the price of milk" (a movement).
:::

::: q
Explain why the supply curve slopes upwards from left to right. (2)
---
A higher price makes producing the good more profitable (1), so firms have an incentive to supply more / can cover the higher costs of extra output / new firms enter (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between an extension of supply and an increase in supply. (2)
---
An extension is a movement up the existing supply curve caused by a rise in the good's own price (1); an increase is a shift of the whole supply curve to the right caused by a non-price factor such as lower costs or new technology (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: four firms each supply 250 phone cases a week at £8. Two new firms enter, each supplying 150 cases a week at £8. Calculate the percentage increase in market supply at £8. Show your working. (2)
---
Before = 4 × 250 = 1,000; after = 1,000 + 2 × 150 = 1,300 (1). (1,300 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 30% (1).
:::

::: q
Draw and label a diagram showing the effect on the supply of wooden furniture of a rise in the price of timber. (Sketch it on paper, or describe it in words.) (2)
---
Labelled axes (Price, Quantity) and an upward-sloping supply curve S (1); new curve S1 to the left of S, labelled (1). A shift of demand scores 0.
:::

::: q
Using a diagram, analyse the consequences for producers of a government subsidy paid to farmers who grow oats. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 correct diagram (S shifts right, P falls, Q rises) used in developed chains; L2 3–4 single-step or no diagram; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: the subsidy lowers costs, so supply increases, S → S1 (K1 + An1); farmers can grow more oats at the same cost, so more is supplied at every price (An); the market price falls from P to P1 and the quantity sold rises (An); each farmer receives the market price plus the subsidy, so income per unit may still rise (An); more farmers may switch land to oats, while other crops lose land (An); oat-milk and cereal producers, who buy oats, face lower input costs and may expand (An).
:::

::: watch
- **A cost rise or an indirect tax shifts supply, not demand.** The examiners report this error every year.
- A **movement along the supply curve** is caused by a change in the good's own price, which usually comes from a **demand** shift. Draw the demand shift and show the extension or contraction along S.
- When plotting, **plot the points from the data and join them**. A line of best fit or made-up points lose marks.
- **Lower output does not mean lower revenue.** If demand is price inelastic, a decrease in supply can raise revenue.
- Use the right words: **extension/contraction** for movements; **increase/decrease** for shifts. Say the direction of every shift.
- In "Using a diagram" answers, refer to the diagram (P to P1, Q to Q1) in your writing. An unused diagram cannot reach Level 3.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Rising energy prices have pushed up the cost of running glasshouses. Many tomato growers in the country of Brennland have left some glasshouses empty this winter, and supermarkets report fewer Brennland tomatoes on their shelves.

Using a diagram, analyse the consequences for consumers and producers of the decrease in supply of tomatoes in Brennland. (6)
---

- Knowledge: decrease in supply = shift left; diagram S → S1, P → P1, Q → Q1 (K1 + An1).
