## What it is

Governments spend huge sums every year and raise most of the money through taxes. This topic covers the **purposes of government spending**, the **sources of government revenue**, including **direct** and **indirect taxes**, and what is meant by a **balanced budget**, a **budget surplus** and a **budget deficit**, with the calculations you need. It is the foundation for fiscal policy (3.5b), where the government changes spending and taxes on purpose to achieve its objectives.

### Purposes of government spending

| Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Provide public services and goods the market would under-provide** | the National Health Service, state schools, defence, police, fire services, street lighting, flood defences |
| **Welfare and social security** | state pensions, Universal Credit, disability and child benefits: protecting people on low incomes and reducing poverty |
| **Reduce inequality** | benefits and free services paid for by taxes make the distribution of income fairer (3.3, 3.5b) |
| **Support growth and employment** | infrastructure such as roads, railways and broadband; education and training; support for research |
| **Correct market failure** | subsidies for goods with positive externalities, such as vaccinations or public transport (3.8) |
| **Pay interest on government debt** | the cost of past borrowing |

Spending can be split into **current spending** (day-to-day costs such as nurses' and teachers' pay, medicines and benefits) and **capital spending** (long-lasting assets such as new hospitals, schools and roads).

Note what is **not** a purpose of government spending: it is not to "keep the current account of the balance of payments balanced" (that is trade, 4.2) or to make a profit.

### Sources of government revenue

Most revenue comes from **taxes**. Taxes are split into two kinds:

| | **Direct taxes** | **Indirect taxes** |
|---|---|---|
| Taxed on | **income and wealth** | **spending** on goods and services |
| Paid | directly to the government by the person or firm that earns the income or owns the wealth | to the seller as part of the price; the **seller** passes the tax to the government |
| Examples | **income tax** (on earnings); **National Insurance contributions** (on earnings); **corporation tax** (on company profits); **capital gains tax** (on profits from selling assets such as shares); **inheritance tax** (on large estates when someone dies); council tax (on property) | **VAT** (value added tax, a percentage of the price of most goods and services); **excise duties** on fuel, alcohol and tobacco (a fixed amount per litre or packet); other specific taxes on particular products |
| Who pays more | usually depends on income or wealth, so it can take a higher share from the rich | depends on what people buy, so everyone pays the same tax on the same item |

Other sources of revenue: **borrowing** (selling government bonds), **charges and fees** (prescriptions, licences, tolls), and profits from state-owned businesses or sales of assets.

**The tax on a product (invented figures).**
- **Ad valorem tax (a percentage, like VAT).** If the price before VAT is £80 and VAT is charged at 20%, VAT = £80 × 20 ÷ 100 = £16, so the consumer pays **£96**. To find the price before VAT from a VAT-inclusive price at 20%, divide by 1.2: £96 ÷ 1.2 = £80.
- **Specific tax (a fixed amount per unit, like fuel duty).** A tax of 50p per litre on 12 billion litres raises 0.50 × 12 billion = **£6 billion**.

### The government budget

The **budget** compares the government's **revenue** with its **spending** over a year.

**Budget balance = government revenue − government spending**

| Position | Meaning | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Balanced budget** | revenue = spending | no new borrowing needed |
| **Budget surplus** | revenue > spending | the extra can repay debt or be saved for later |
| **Budget deficit** | spending > revenue | the gap must be **borrowed**, adding to the **national debt** |

Invented example: the government of Lindor spends £420 billion and receives £385 billion in revenue. Budget balance = 385 − 420 = **−£35 billion**, a **deficit of £35 billion**. If spending is unchanged and revenue rises to £430 billion, there is a **surplus of £10 billion**.

**Why it matters.** Each year's deficit is added to the **national debt** (the total owed from past borrowing). The government must pay **interest** on that debt, and debt interest has an **opportunity cost**: money spent on interest cannot be spent on schools or hospitals, or used to cut taxes. Large debts can worry lenders, who may then demand higher interest rates.

But a deficit is not always bad:
- In a **recession**, tax revenue falls (less income and spending) and benefit spending rises, so a deficit appears automatically. Borrowing then supports spending and jobs.
- Borrowing to pay for **capital spending** (roads, broadband, schools) can raise future growth and tax revenue.
- A deficit is easier to afford when **interest rates are low** and the economy is **growing**.

**Do not confuse** the government's budget deficit with a **current account deficit** (when a country's imports are worth more than its exports, 4.2). Always say which deficit you mean, and use "deficit", "surplus" or "balanced", never "a bigger budget".

::: terms
- **Government spending**: money spent by the government on public services, welfare benefits, infrastructure and debt interest.
- **Government revenue**: the money the government receives, mainly from taxes, plus charges, fees and asset sales.
- **Direct tax**: a tax on income or wealth, paid directly to the government by the person or firm that earns or owns it, such as income tax.
- **Indirect tax**: a tax on spending on goods and services, collected by the seller and passed to the government, such as VAT.
- **VAT (value added tax)**: an indirect tax charged as a percentage of the price of most goods and services.
- **Excise duty**: an indirect tax charged as a fixed amount per unit on specific products such as fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
- **Corporation tax**: a direct tax on the profits of companies.
- **Balanced budget**: when government revenue is equal to government spending over a year.
- **Budget surplus**: when government revenue is greater than government spending over a year.
- **Budget deficit**: when government spending is greater than government revenue over a year, so the government must borrow.
- **National debt**: the total amount owed by the government as a result of past borrowing.
- **Debt interest**: the interest the government pays on the national debt, which has an opportunity cost.
:::

::: method
1. **"State two…" (2)**: two clear answers, e.g. two direct taxes ("corporation tax", "inheritance tax"), not the example already given in the question.
2. **Definitions (2)**: the comparison of revenue and spending (1) + what follows (borrowing, debt, repayment) or an example (1).
3. **Calculations (2)**: budget balance = revenue − spending (say deficit or surplus); VAT = price × rate ÷ 100; specific-tax revenue = tax per unit × quantity. Show working; give £ and the unit (million or billion).
4. **"Explain why/how…" (2)**: a point (1) + its consequence for the budget or the economy (1).
5. **Analyse (6)**: chains from a change (unemployment, growth, a tax rise) to spending and revenue, ending with the effect on the deficit or surplus; use the extract's figures; no evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: weigh the benefits of the spending or borrowing against its costs (debt interest, opportunity cost, future taxes), qualify by the state of the economy and what the money is spent on, and reach a judgement.
:::

::: example Direct taxes
Other than income tax, state two direct taxes. (2)

Corporation tax (1); inheritance tax (1). (Also National Insurance or capital gains tax.)
:::

::: example Budget deficit
Explain what is meant by a budget deficit. (2)

When government spending is greater than government revenue over a year (1), so the government must borrow to cover the gap, adding to the national debt (1).
:::

::: example Budget calculation
Invented figures: the government of Sorvia receives £96 billion in tax and other revenue and spends £104 billion. Calculate its budget balance and state whether it is a deficit or a surplus. (2)

£96bn − £104bn = −£8bn (1), a budget **deficit of £8 billion** (1).
:::

::: example VAT
Invented figures: a games console costs £250 before VAT. Calculate its price to the consumer if VAT is charged at 20%. Show your working. (2)

VAT = £250 × 20 ÷ 100 = £50 (1); price = £250 + £50 = **£300** (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a fall in unemployment and the budget
Invented context: in the country of Aldmoor, unemployment has fallen from 1.2 million to 0.8 million, and the government had a budget deficit of £15 billion last year. Analyse how the fall in unemployment is likely to affect Aldmoor's budget. (6)

The budget balance is revenue minus spending (K). With 400,000 fewer people unemployed, fewer claim unemployment benefits, so welfare spending falls (Ap/An). The 400,000 extra workers earn wages and pay income tax and National Insurance, so direct tax revenue rises (Ap/An). They also have more to spend, so more VAT is collected on their purchases, and firms selling more make more profit and pay more corporation tax (An). With spending falling and revenue rising, the £15 billion deficit narrows, so the government needs to borrow less and adds less to the national debt, lowering future debt interest (Ap/An).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a direct tax and an indirect tax. (2)
---
A direct tax is on income or wealth and is paid directly to the government by the person or firm, e.g. income tax (1); an indirect tax is on spending, collected by the seller as part of the price, e.g. VAT (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a budget deficit may lead to higher government spending on debt interest. (2)
---
A deficit must be financed by borrowing, which adds to the national debt (1); the government must pay interest on the larger debt each year, so debt-interest spending rises (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a recession is likely to affect the government's budget. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: the budget compares revenue and spending (K); in a recession firms cut output and jobs, so unemployment rises and more people claim benefits, raising spending (An); fewer people in work pay less income tax and National Insurance (An); households spend less, so VAT and excise revenue fall (An); firms make lower profits and pay less corporation tax (An); so a surplus shrinks or a deficit widens, increasing borrowing and the national debt (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Direct = income and wealth; indirect = spending.** VAT and excise duties are indirect; income tax, National Insurance and corporation tax are direct.
- **Budget balance = revenue − spending.** State "deficit" or "surplus" and the unit (£ million or £ billion). A minus sign alone does not say which.
- **Never write "a bigger budget"**. Examiners want deficit, surplus or balanced.
- **Do not confuse the budget deficit with the current account deficit.** One is the government's revenue and spending, the other is exports and imports.
- "State two direct taxes **other than income tax**" means income tax scores nothing.
- A deficit means borrowing, which adds to the national debt and debt interest. That is its opportunity cost.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The government of Brennish plans to raise the main rate of income tax by 2 percentage points. It expects this to raise £9 billion a year. At present its budget deficit is £20 billion. Unemployment is low and the economy is growing at 2.5% a year.

Using the information, analyse how the rise in income tax could affect Brennish's budget. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Brennish; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: income tax as a direct tax; budget deficit as spending greater than revenue.
- Application: +2 percentage points; £9bn expected; £20bn deficit; low unemployment; 2.5% growth.
- Analysis: higher income tax takes more from each worker's pay, so direct tax revenue rises, by up to £9bn, cutting the deficit towards £11bn; less borrowing means a slower rise in the national debt and lower debt interest in future; but lower disposable income means households spend less, so VAT revenue falls, offsetting part of the gain; lower spending may slow growth, reducing firms' profits and corporation tax revenue; with low unemployment and growth, most workers keep their jobs, so the revenue gain is likely to be large.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The government of Tarrow has a budget deficit of £30 billion, and its national debt is growing. Interest rates are low. Ministers want to borrow a further £10 billion to build a new rail network, which they say will raise growth in poorer regions. Opposition politicians say the debt is already too high and that taxes will have to rise in future.

Evaluate whether the government of Tarrow should borrow to pay for the new rail network. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: £30bn deficit; growing debt; low interest rates; £10bn borrowing; rail network for poorer regions; worry about future taxes.
- Analysis: building the network creates jobs and incomes, raising spending and tax revenue; better transport cuts firms' costs and attracts investment to poorer regions, raising future growth and tax revenue, which could help repay the borrowing; but extra borrowing raises the deficit to £40bn and adds to the debt, so debt interest rises; opportunity cost: interest payments cannot be spent on schools or health, and taxes may have to rise.
- Evaluation: low interest rates make borrowing cheaper now, but rates could rise later; the case is stronger because it is capital spending that raises future output, not day-to-day spending; depends on whether the project is delivered on time and on budget, and whether firms really move to the regions; if the debt is already very high, lenders may demand higher interest. Judgement: e.g. the government should borrow, because low interest rates and the long-term growth benefits outweigh the cost, as long as the project is well managed and the debt is reduced once the economy grows.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The country of Kelmar has had a budget surplus of £6 billion for two years. Some of its hospitals have long waiting lists, and many schools need repairs. The economy is growing slowly at 0.8% a year, and unemployment has started to rise.

Evaluate whether the government of Kelmar should use its budget surplus to increase government spending. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: £6bn surplus for two years; hospital waiting lists; schools needing repairs; slow growth of 0.8%; rising unemployment.
- Analysis: more spending on hospitals and schools improves health and education, raising the productivity of the workforce; spending creates jobs for builders, nurses and teachers, so incomes and consumer spending rise, helping to reduce rising unemployment; more output and jobs raise tax revenue.
- Evaluation: alternatively the surplus could repay national debt, cutting future debt interest, or fund tax cuts; using it all would remove the surplus, so if a recession comes there is less room before borrowing is needed; spending on schools and hospitals takes time to raise productivity; with growth slow and unemployment rising, spending now is more useful than saving the surplus. Judgement: e.g. the government should spend most of the surplus on hospitals and schools, as slow growth and rising unemployment make extra spending valuable now, while keeping some to repay debt.
:::
