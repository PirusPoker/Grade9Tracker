## What it is

Almost nobody in a modern economy makes everything they use. Instead, people, firms, regions and whole countries **specialise** in what they do best and **exchange** their output for everything else. This topic covers what specialisation and the division of labour are, why specialisation depends on exchange and money, and the **costs and benefits** of specialising for **producers, workers, regions and countries**. The spec says "evaluate", so expect 6-mark questions that weigh benefits against costs for a named group.

### Specialisation and the division of labour

**Specialisation** is concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services, or on a narrow set of tasks, rather than doing everything. It happens at four levels:

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Individual / worker** | a dentist, an electrician, a payroll clerk, a goalkeeper |
| **Firm** | a company that makes only bicycle brakes; a bakery that sells only sourdough |
| **Region** | an area known for one industry, such as a cluster of software firms around a university town, or a coastal area built around fishing and fish processing |
| **Country** | a country that concentrates on exporting coffee, tourism, financial services or cars |

The **division of labour** is a form of specialisation inside a firm: the production process is **split into separate tasks**, and each worker does one task. In a sandwich factory one worker slices bread, another adds fillings, another packs and labels. Each worker becomes quicker at their task, does not waste time switching tools, and can be trained more quickly.

A simple invented example shows the effect on **output** and **productivity** (output per worker):

| Workshop with 5 workers | Daily output of chairs | Output per worker |
|---|---|---|
| Each worker makes whole chairs | 10 | 2 |
| Division of labour (cutting, joining, sanding, finishing, packing) | 18 | 3.6 |

Output rises by 8 chairs, an increase of 8 ÷ 10 × 100 = **80%**, with the same workers and tools.

### Why specialisation needs exchange

A specialist produces **far more of one thing than they need** and none of the many other things they want. The electrician cannot eat cables; the coffee-growing country cannot run on coffee. So specialisation only works if people can **exchange** their surplus for other goods and services.

- **Barter** (swapping goods directly) is clumsy: you must find someone who has what you want **and** wants what you have (a "double coincidence of wants"), and it is hard to agree how many loaves equal one haircut.
- **Money** solves this. It is a **medium of exchange**: the electrician sells her services for money, then spends the money on anything she likes. Money also measures value (prices) and lets people save (more on this in 2.8a).

So specialisation, exchange and money go together: the more an economy specialises, the more it relies on markets and money, and on **trade** between regions and countries.

### Costs and benefits of specialisation

Answer for the **group the question names**. A cost "for a region" is not a worker getting bored.

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Producers (firms)** | higher output and **productivity**; lower **average (unit) cost**, so lower prices or higher profit; workers are easier to train for one task; can use specialist machinery; reputation for expertise and quality | **dependence**: if one stage stops (illness, a broken machine), the whole line stops; bored workers may be absent more or make mistakes, lowering quality; inflexible if demand changes |
| **Workers** | become highly **skilled** and fast at their task; skills can bring higher pay; quicker to train; may enjoy being an expert | **monotony** and boredom; **deskilling**: narrow skills may not transfer to another job; higher risk of **redundancy** if their task is automated or demand for the product falls |
| **Regions** | build a pool of skilled workers; supporting firms (suppliers, training colleges, transport links) cluster nearby, cutting costs for all firms there; a reputation that attracts investment and jobs | **over-dependence** on one industry; if it declines, many people lose jobs at once (**structural unemployment**), local shops lose trade, and young people move away |
| **Countries** | produce what they are most efficient at, so total world output rises; earn export revenue to buy imports they cannot make cheaply; economies of scale; consumers get a wider range of goods through trade | dependence on **imports** for essentials (food, energy); vulnerability to **falls in world prices** or demand for their main export; supply shocks (war, bad weather) can cut off imports; loss of some industries and jobs |

**Evaluation** comes from the conditions that decide whether the benefits outweigh the costs:

- **Size of the market**: specialisation only pays if the firm can sell all the extra output.
- **How diverse the economy is**: a region with several industries can absorb a decline in one; a one-industry town cannot.
- **Stability of demand and world prices** for the product.
- **Time period**: productivity gains come quickly; structural unemployment can last decades.
- **Job rotation, training and retraining** can reduce boredom and help workers move if their task disappears.

::: terms
- **Specialisation**: concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services, or on a narrow set of tasks.
- **Division of labour**: splitting the production process into separate tasks, with each worker carrying out one task.
- **Exchange**: trading goods and services with others, usually using money, so specialists can obtain what they do not make.
- **Barter**: the direct swapping of one good or service for another without using money.
- **Double coincidence of wants**: the need, in barter, to find someone who has what you want and wants what you have.
- **Medium of exchange**: something, usually money, that is widely accepted in payment for goods and services.
- **Productivity**: output per worker (or per unit of another input) in a given time period.
- **Regional specialisation**: when a particular area concentrates on one industry or a small number of industries.
- **Structural unemployment**: long-term unemployment caused by the decline of an industry, leaving workers with the wrong skills.
- **Deskilling**: the loss of a range of skills when a worker repeats one narrow task for a long time.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the group** (producer, worker, region, country) and keep every point about that group.
2. **"Explain what is meant by division of labour" (2)**: splitting production into separate tasks (1) + each worker does one task / an example (1).
3. **"Explain one cost/benefit" (2)**: name it (1) + develop it with "this means that…" applied to the context (1). A list scores 1.
4. **Calculations**: percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; productivity = output ÷ number of workers. Show working and units.
5. **6-mark analyse (K1 Ap2 An3)**: two or three chains, for example: division of labour → workers repeat one task → faster → more output per worker → lower unit cost. No evaluation.
6. **6-mark evaluate (Ap1 An2 Ev3)**: analyse the benefit, then qualify it with a cost or a condition that pairs with it, and end with a judgement on the group named. Use the extract's numbers.
:::

::: example Explain what is meant by division of labour
Explain what is meant by division of labour. (2)

Division of labour means splitting the production of a good into separate tasks (1), with each worker specialising in one task, for example on a bicycle production line one worker fits wheels while another fits brakes (1).
:::

::: example Explaining one cost for a region
Explain one cost of specialisation for a region. (2)

The region becomes dependent on one industry (1), so if demand for that industry's product falls, many firms close or cut jobs at the same time, causing high structural unemployment and lower spending in local shops (1).
:::

::: example Calculating the change in productivity
Invented data: a kitchen-unit workshop with 8 workers made 32 units a week. After introducing division of labour, the same 8 workers make 44 units a week. Calculate the percentage increase in output per worker. Show your working. (2)

Output per worker before = 32 ÷ 8 = 4 units; after = 44 ÷ 8 = 5.5 units (1).
Increase = (5.5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = **37.5%** (1).
(The same answer comes from total output: 12 ÷ 32 × 100 = 37.5%, as the workforce is unchanged.)
:::

::: example Why specialisation needs exchange
Explain why specialisation leads to exchange. (2)

A specialist produces much more of one good or service than they need and none of the other things they want (1), so they must trade their surplus, usually for money, to obtain everything else, e.g. a baker sells bread and uses the money to buy clothes and fuel (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — specialisation for a business
Invented extract: Corlett Pies makes 2,000 hand-made pies a day. Each of its 25 bakers makes a whole pie from start to finish. The manager wants to introduce division of labour, with separate teams for pastry, filling, baking and packing, and expects output to rise to 3,000 pies a day. Several bakers enjoy making a whole pie and fear they will be bored.

Evaluate the benefits of increased specialisation for Corlett Pies. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)

Model answer: Division of labour should raise output from 2,000 to 3,000 pies a day, a 50% rise, with the same 25 bakers (Ap). Each team repeats one task, so becomes faster and wastes no time switching tasks, which means output per baker rises and the cost of each pie falls, so Corlett could cut prices to win supermarket orders or keep the same price and earn more profit (An). However, this benefit depends on Corlett being able to sell an extra 1,000 pies a day; if demand does not rise, it will just hold unsold stock (Ev). The bakers' fears also matter: bored workers may be absent more or take less care, so quality, which is the selling point of a "hand-made" pie, could fall, and absence on one team would halt the whole line (An/Ev). Overall the benefits are large if the extra pies can be sold, but Corlett should rotate staff between teams to keep quality and morale up, otherwise the lower cost per pie could be offset by lost reputation (Ev — supported judgement).
:::

::: q
State two benefits of specialisation for workers. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): become more skilled at the task; work faster / higher productivity; may earn higher pay as an expert; quicker to train; job satisfaction from expertise.
:::

::: q
Explain one cost of the division of labour for a firm. (2)
---
Workers repeating one task may become bored (1), so absence rises or mistakes increase, lowering quality / output (1). Or: dependence: if one stage stops, the whole production line stops (1 + 1 developed).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of barter compared with using money. (2)
---
Barter needs a double coincidence of wants (1): a person must find someone who both has what they want and wants what they have, which takes time and may be impossible (1). Or: hard to agree relative values / goods cannot be divided easily — developed.
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit to a country of specialising in producing a small range of goods. (2)
---
It can produce goods it is most efficient at, in large quantities, at low cost (1), and export them to earn revenue that pays for imports it cannot produce cheaply, giving consumers more choice / raising incomes (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: before division of labour, 6 workers each assembled 5 lamps a day. After it, the team assembles 42 lamps a day. Calculate the percentage increase in daily output. Show your working. (2)
---
Before = 6 × 5 = 30 lamps; increase = 42 − 30 = 12 (1); 12 ÷ 30 × 100 = 40% (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how the division of labour could increase output for a firm making mobile phone cases. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed applied chains; L2 3–4 single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: splitting production into moulding, printing designs and packing (K); each worker repeats one task, so they become faster and more accurate, raising output per worker (An); no time is lost moving between tasks or changing tools, so more cases are made each hour (An); simpler tasks mean new workers are trained quickly, so the firm can expand output when demand rises (An); specialist machines can be used for each stage, raising output further and cutting the cost per case (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Answer for the group named.** Region → dependence on one industry and structural unemployment; country → reliance on imports and world prices; worker → boredom, deskilling, redundancy; firm → dependence on each stage, quality, inflexibility.
- **Division of labour** must mention splitting production into **separate tasks**. "Workers doing different jobs" is too vague for 2 marks.
- **Specialisation needs exchange**: say why. Specialists produce a surplus of one thing and must trade for the rest, and money makes that easier than barter.
- In a 6-mark evaluate, **pair** each benefit with a cost or a condition. A list of advantages followed by an unrelated list of disadvantages is not evaluation.
- Increased output only helps a firm if it can **sell** it. This is a strong evaluative point.
- Productivity is **output per worker**, not total output.
:::

::: test
Invented data: a bike-repair workshop employs 4 mechanics, each repairing 6 bikes a day. After splitting the work into diagnosis, parts fitting and testing, the 4 mechanics repair 30 bikes a day in total. Calculate the percentage increase in output per mechanic. Show your working. (2)
---
Before: 6 bikes per mechanic; after: 30 ÷ 4 = 7.5 bikes per mechanic (1). (7.5 − 6) ÷ 6 × 100 = 25% (1). Correct answer alone = 2.

::: test
Explain how money makes specialisation easier. (2)
---
Money is a medium of exchange accepted by everyone (1), so a specialist can sell their output for money and use it to buy whatever they need, without having to find someone who wants exactly what they produce (no double coincidence of wants) (1).
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Torrin Glass employs 40 workers. Each worker currently makes whole glass vases, cutting, shaping, polishing and packing them. The firm plans to reorganise so that each worker does one stage only, and to buy a specialist polishing machine for the polishing team.

Using the information, analyse how this specialisation could increase Torrin Glass's output. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Torrin Glass; L2 3–4 — correct but mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated knowledge. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: division of labour; productivity as output per worker.
- Application: 40 workers; stages of cutting, shaping, polishing, packing; specialist polishing machine.
- Analysis: each worker repeats one stage, so they become faster and more skilled, raising the number of vases each worker produces; no time is wasted moving between stages or changing tools, so the 40 workers make more vases a day; the polishing machine works faster than hand polishing, so that stage no longer slows the line; new workers can be trained quickly for one stage, so output can be expanded; higher output per worker lowers the cost of each vase.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Pell & Sons is a small engineering firm with 30 staff who each make complete garden gates to order. It plans to specialise: one team will cut steel, one weld, one paint and one fit hinges and locks. It expects output to rise from 60 to 90 gates a week. Its main customer, a chain of DIY stores, has offered to buy up to 80 gates a week.

Evaluate the benefits of increased specialisation for Pell & Sons. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application and analysis with a fully supported judgement weighing benefits against costs or conditions; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: 30 staff; 60 → 90 gates a week (a 50% rise); DIY chain will buy up to 80.
- Analysis (benefits): each team repeats one task, so becomes faster, raising output per worker from 2 to 3 gates a week; cost per gate falls, so profit per gate rises or prices can be cut to win more orders; it can meet the DIY chain's order of 80, raising revenue.
- Analysis (costs/limits): workers who made whole gates may find one task boring, lowering morale and quality; if the welding team is short of staff, the whole line stops.
- Evaluation: the chain only buys up to 80, so 10 extra gates a week need other buyers or the firm holds unsold stock; but even selling 80 instead of 60 is a 33% rise in sales; the benefit depends on keeping quality while relying heavily on one customer. Judgement: e.g. strongly beneficial, as long as the firm finds buyers for the last 10 gates and rotates staff to protect quality.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The imaginary country of Lunora earns 70% of its export revenue from cocoa beans. Its warm climate and fertile soil make cocoa cheap to grow. Over the last five years the world price of cocoa has risen and fallen sharply, and Lunora imports most of its machinery, fuel and medicines.

Evaluate whether Lunora should continue to specialise in cocoa. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, analysis of benefits and costs for the country, and a supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: 70% of export revenue from cocoa; climate and soil make it cheap to grow; volatile world prices; imports machinery, fuel and medicines.
- Analysis (for): Lunora can produce cocoa at low cost, so it earns export revenue to pay for imports it cannot make cheaply; specialisation supports jobs and farming skills in cocoa areas.
- Analysis (against): heavy dependence means a fall in the world price cuts export revenue sharply, so Lunora may be unable to afford essential imports such as medicines; disease or bad weather could destroy the crop; workers' skills are narrow.
- Evaluation: continue to grow cocoa where it is efficient, but the risk depends on how volatile prices are and whether Lunora has savings to smooth bad years; diversifying (e.g. processing cocoa into chocolate, or developing tourism) could keep the benefits while reducing risk. Judgement: e.g. specialise, but not to the extent of 70% of exports, because a single price fall threatens essentials.
:::
