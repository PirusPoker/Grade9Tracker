## What it is

**Fiscal policy** is the use of **government spending and taxation** to influence the economy and achieve the government's economic objectives. This topic covers what fiscal policy is and **how it can be used to achieve economic objectives**; how to **calculate and analyse how taxes and government spending affect markets and the overall economy**; the **costs, including opportunity cost, and benefits** of fiscal policy; and the **economic consequences of measures to redistribute income and wealth, including progressive taxes**. It builds on 3.5a (spending, taxes and the budget).

### Fiscal policy and the economic objectives

The government's main objectives are **economic growth**, **low unemployment**, **price stability** and a **fair distribution of income** (plus aims such as protecting the environment). Fiscal policy can push the economy towards them:

| | **Expansionary fiscal policy** | **Contractionary fiscal policy** |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | **more** government spending and/or **lower** taxes | **less** government spending and/or **higher** taxes |
| Effect on the budget | deficit widens (or surplus shrinks) | deficit narrows (or surplus grows) |
| Used when | growth is weak and unemployment high (e.g. a recession) | spending is growing too fast and inflation is above target |
| Aim | raise total spending → more output and jobs | slow spending → reduce inflationary pressure |

Fiscal policy is set by the **government** (in the UK, the Chancellor of the Exchequer announces tax and spending plans in the Budget). It is different from **monetary policy**, which uses interest rates and is set by the Bank of England (3.6).

### How taxes and spending affect the overall economy

**A cut in income tax (direct tax)**
income tax falls → workers keep more of their pay, so **disposable income** rises → **consumer spending** rises → firms sell more and **increase output** → they **hire more workers** → incomes rise again → **growth** and **lower unemployment**. Risk: if spending rises faster than output, **demand-pull inflation**. A lower tax rate may also give an **incentive to work** more hours.

**A cut in corporation tax**
firms keep more of their **profit** → more retained profit to **invest** in capital → more capacity and productivity → growth; may attract foreign firms to set up.

**More government spending (e.g. on roads or hospitals)**
spending creates **jobs** in construction and services → workers spend their wages → other firms' sales rise → more jobs (a knock-on effect) → growth and lower unemployment. Spending on infrastructure, education and health can also raise **productivity** in the long run.

**Higher taxes or spending cuts** work the other way: less spending, slower growth, possibly higher unemployment, but less inflationary pressure and a smaller deficit.

### How taxes and spending affect markets

- How much quantity falls depends on **price elasticity of demand** (2.2b): if demand is **price inelastic** (fuel, tobacco), quantity falls only a little, but the tax **raises a lot of revenue**.
- A **subsidy** does the opposite: costs fall, supply shifts **right**, price falls and quantity rises (useful for public transport or renewable energy).
- **Government spending on a service** raises demand for the goods and labour it uses: a hospital-building programme raises demand for builders, steel and nurses.

**Calculations (invented figures).**
- Tax revenue from a specific tax = tax per unit × quantity sold **after** the tax. A £2 tax per unit with sales falling from 5 million to 4 million units raises £2 × 4 million = **£8 million**.
- Effect on price: if a £1.50 tax raises the market price from £6.00 to £7.00, consumers pay **£1.00** of the tax and producers absorb **£0.50**.

### Costs and benefits of fiscal policy

| Benefits | Costs, including opportunity cost |
|---|---|
| can **target** particular groups, regions or industries (e.g. tax relief for small firms, spending in poorer regions) | **opportunity cost**: money spent on one thing cannot be spent on another, or used to cut taxes or repay debt |
| spending on infrastructure, health and education raises **long-run capacity** | expansionary policy widens the **deficit**, adding to the **national debt** and **debt interest** |
| can boost spending quickly in a recession, protecting jobs | **time lags**: projects take years to plan and build |
| can reduce **inequality** (benefits, progressive taxes) | tax cuts may be **saved** or spent on **imports**, so the boost is smaller |
| tax changes can discourage harmful goods and encourage good ones | risk of **demand-pull inflation** if the economy is near full capacity |
| | higher taxes may reduce **incentives** to work, invest and take risks |

### Redistribution of income and wealth

**Redistribution** means taking income or wealth from richer households and transferring it, directly or through services, to poorer ones, to make the distribution **fairer** (3.3).

**Types of tax by how they affect different incomes:**
- **Progressive tax**: takes a **higher percentage** of income as income rises. Not just "the rich pay more pounds"; the **proportion** must rise.
- **Proportional tax**: takes the **same percentage** of income at every level.
- **Regressive tax**: takes a **higher percentage of a low income** than of a high income. VAT is regressive in effect, because poorer households spend a larger share of their income.

**Progressive income tax (invented system):** 0% on the first £10,000; 20% on income from £10,001 to £40,000; 40% on income above £40,000.

| Income | Tax | % of income |
|---|---|---|
| £20,000 | 20% × £10,000 = £2,000 | **10%** |
| £60,000 | 20% × £30,000 + 40% × £20,000 = £6,000 + £8,000 = £14,000 | **23.3%** |

**VAT as a regressive tax (invented):** Household A earns £15,000 and spends £10,000 on goods with 20% VAT: VAT = £2,000 = **13.3%** of income. Household B earns £90,000 and spends £45,000 on such goods: VAT = £9,000 = **10%** of income. B pays more pounds but a smaller percentage.

**Other redistribution measures**: means-tested **benefits** and tax credits; free **public services** (health care, education) used by everyone; **wealth taxes** such as inheritance tax and capital gains tax; a **national minimum wage** (set by law, not a tax, but it lifts low pay).

Taxes alone do not redistribute: the **revenue must be spent** on benefits or services that help lower-income households.

| Consequences of redistribution: benefits | Consequences: costs |
|---|---|
| less **relative poverty** and inequality | high tax rates may reduce **incentives** to work extra hours, gain promotion or start firms |
| poorer households spend most of any extra income, so **consumer spending** rises | **tax avoidance** and evasion; rich people and firms may move abroad (**brain drain**) |
| better access to education and health, so more **equality of opportunity** and higher productivity | generous benefits may reduce the incentive to find work (a **benefit trap**) |
| fewer social problems, lower health and crime costs | administration costs; the opportunity cost of the revenue |

::: terms
- **Fiscal policy**: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy and achieve economic objectives.
- **Expansionary fiscal policy**: increasing government spending or cutting taxes to raise total spending in the economy.
- **Contractionary fiscal policy**: cutting government spending or raising taxes to reduce total spending in the economy.
- **Disposable income**: income left after direct taxes have been deducted and benefits added, which households can spend or save.
- **Subsidy**: a payment by the government to producers that lowers their costs, so supply increases and the price falls.
- **Opportunity cost**: the next best alternative given up when a choice is made, such as the other uses of government money.
- **Redistribution**: transferring income or wealth from richer to poorer households through taxes, benefits and public services.
- **Progressive tax**: a tax that takes a higher percentage of income as income rises.
- **Proportional tax**: a tax that takes the same percentage of income at every income level.
- **Regressive tax**: a tax that takes a higher percentage of a low income than of a high income.
- **Tax avoidance**: legally arranging affairs to pay less tax.
- **Time lag**: the delay between a policy being decided and its full effect on the economy.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain what is meant by fiscal policy" (2)**: government spending and taxation (1) used to influence the economy / achieve objectives such as growth (1).
2. **Progressive tax (2)**: a higher **percentage** of income as income rises (1) + how the revenue redistributes (spent on benefits or services for lower earners) or a numerical example (1).
3. **Calculations (2)**: tax = rate × the right slice of income; % of income = tax ÷ income × 100; revenue = tax per unit × quantity after the tax. Show working; give £ or %.
4. **Market effects**: an indirect tax shifts **supply** left (higher price, lower quantity); a subsidy shifts supply right. Describe axes, curves and the shift if asked to draw.
5. **Analyse (6)**: chain from the tax or spending change → disposable income or costs → spending, output and jobs → the objective (growth, unemployment, inflation); use the extract; no evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: weigh benefits against costs including opportunity cost (deficit, debt interest, incentives, time lags, saving or imports), say for whom and how far, and reach a judgement.
:::

::: example Fiscal policy
Explain what is meant by fiscal policy. (2)

The use of government spending and taxation (1) to influence the economy and achieve objectives such as growth, low unemployment or low inflation (1).
:::

::: example Progressive tax
Explain how a progressive income tax system redistributes income. (2)

Higher earners pay a higher percentage of their income in tax than lower earners (1), and the revenue can be spent on benefits and public services used by lower-income households, narrowing the gap (1).
:::

::: example Tax as a percentage of income
Invented figures: in the country of Ravel, income tax is 0% on the first £12,000 and 25% on all income above £12,000. Calculate the percentage of income paid in tax by someone earning £32,000. Show your working. (2)

Tax = 25% × (£32,000 − £12,000) = 25% × £20,000 = £5,000 (1); £5,000 ÷ £32,000 × 100 = **15.6%** (1).
:::

::: example Indirect tax and a market
Explain how an indirect tax on petrol is likely to affect the petrol market. (2)

The tax raises suppliers' costs, so the supply curve shifts left (1), causing the price of petrol to rise and the quantity bought and sold to fall, though only slightly because demand is price inelastic (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — a cut in income tax
Invented context: the government of Ostrel has cut the basic rate of income tax by 2 percentage points, giving 20 million workers an average of £400 a year more. Analyse how this cut in direct tax could affect Ostrel's economy. (6)

Income tax is a direct tax, and cutting it raises **disposable income** (K). Workers keep an extra £400 each, so about £8 billion more is available to spend (Ap). Households spend more in shops, restaurants and on holidays, so firms' sales and revenue rise (An). To meet the extra demand firms increase output and hire more workers, reducing unemployment, and the new workers' incomes are spent too, giving a further boost to growth (An). The lower tax rate may also encourage some people to work more hours or take a job, increasing the supply of labour (An). However, if firms cannot raise output fast enough, the extra spending pushes up prices, causing demand-pull inflation (An).
:::

::: q
State two economic objectives that fiscal policy can be used to achieve. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): economic growth; low unemployment; price stability / low inflation; fair distribution of income; environmental protection.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a progressive and a regressive tax. (2)
---
A progressive tax takes a higher percentage of income as income rises (1); a regressive tax takes a higher percentage of a low income than of a high income, e.g. VAT in effect (1). Do not credit "the rich pay more" without percentages.
:::

::: q
Invented figures: a tax of £0.40 per unit is placed on a product. After the tax, 15 million units are sold. Calculate the tax revenue. (2)
---
£0.40 × 15 million (1) = **£6 million** (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one opportunity cost of an increase in government spending on defence. (2)
---
The money could have been spent on something else, e.g. the NHS or schools (1), so hospital waiting lists or school standards may suffer / or taxes cannot be cut / debt cannot be repaid (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a cut in income tax may not increase consumer spending by much. (2)
---
Households may save the extra disposable income, or use it to repay debts, especially if they are worried about the future (1) / or spend it on imports, so less of the extra spending reaches domestic firms and output rises little (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how an increase in government spending on building new hospitals could affect unemployment. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: government spending is part of fiscal policy (K); building contracts create jobs for construction workers, reducing unemployment directly (An); suppliers of steel, concrete and equipment get more orders and hire more staff (An); the new workers spend their wages in local shops and services, so other firms hire too (An); once open, the hospitals employ doctors, nurses and support staff (An); better health care reduces sickness, so more people are able to work, though they may enter the labour force as job-seekers (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Fiscal = government spending and taxation; monetary = interest rates.** Do not mix them up.
- **Progressive means a higher PERCENTAGE**, not just more pounds. The 2024 examiners saw many "the rich pay more" answers.
- **Redistribution needs the spending side**: say how the revenue reaches lower-income households.
- **An indirect tax shifts SUPPLY left.** Do not shift demand and do not draw AD/AS.
- **Finish the chain**: a tax cut → disposable income → spending → output → jobs or growth. Stopping at "people have more money" is single-step.
- In evaluation, name the **opportunity cost** specifically (what else the money could fund) and qualify with time lags, saving, imports, incentives or the deficit.
:::

Using the information, analyse how the spending programme could affect Pelland's economy. (6)

- Application: £20bn over four years; railways, roads and broadband; 0.5% growth; 7% unemployment; spare capacity in construction.
- Analysis: construction firms use their spare workers and machinery, so output rises without much pressure on prices; firms hire some of the unemployed, cutting the 7% rate; workers spend their wages, raising sales and jobs elsewhere; better transport and broadband cut firms' costs and raise productivity, increasing long-run growth; more jobs and output raise tax revenue, although the budget deficit widens at first.

::: test
Invented extract: The government of Fenmark plans to raise the top rate of income tax, paid only on income above £150,000, from 45% to 55%. It will spend the extra revenue on free school meals and childcare for low-income families. Business groups warn that some highly paid workers and entrepreneurs may move abroad.

Evaluate whether raising income tax on the highest earners will benefit lower-paid households in Fenmark. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: top rate 45% → 55%; income above £150,000; revenue spent on free school meals and childcare; warning about people moving abroad.
- Analysis: a more progressive tax takes a higher percentage from the richest; the revenue funds school meals and childcare, raising the real living standards of low-income families; cheaper childcare lets parents work more hours, raising their earnings; better-fed children may learn more, improving future opportunities and productivity.
- Evaluation: the benefit depends on how much revenue is actually raised: high earners may avoid tax, work less or move abroad, so revenue may be lower than expected; entrepreneurs leaving could mean fewer jobs for lower-paid workers; but only a small number pay the top rate, so the effect on jobs may be small; the gains are larger if the spending is well targeted. Judgement: e.g. lower-paid households are likely to benefit overall, because the spending is targeted at them, but by less than planned if high earners avoid the tax or leave.

Its budget deficit is already £40 billion. Households are worried about losing their jobs, and many imported goods are sold in Sallow's shops.

Evaluate whether cutting VAT would help Sallow's economy recover from the recession. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
