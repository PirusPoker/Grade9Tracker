## What it is

Demand is one half of every market. This topic covers what demand means, how to **draw a demand curve from data** for one consumer and for the whole market, the difference between a **movement along** the curve and a **shift** of it, and how to **analyse the causes and consequences** of these changes for consumers and producers. OCR often asks you to do this "using a diagram", so practise the drawings until they are automatic.

### What demand means

**Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are **willing and able** to buy at each price over a given period of time.

- **Willing and able**: wanting a holiday is not demand unless you can afford it.
- **At each price**: demand is a whole schedule of quantities, one for every possible price.
- **Over a period**: 40 coffees means nothing until you say per day, per week or per month.

**Individual demand** is the demand of one consumer. **Market demand** is the total demand of all consumers in the market: at each price, **add up the quantities** every consumer would buy.

### Drawing demand curves from data

Invented data: monthly demand for bubble tea at the Kiko Tea café from its three regular customers.

| Price per cup (£) | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer A | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Consumer B | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| Consumer C | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 |
| **Market demand (A + B + C)** | **3** | **6** | **10** | **14** |

To draw a demand curve: put **Price (£)** on the vertical axis and **Quantity** (with its time period) on the horizontal axis; choose even scales starting at 0; **plot each price–quantity pair** as a point; **join the points**; label the curve **D**. Do not draw a "line of best fit" through them, and do not invent extra points.

The market curve lies further right than any individual curve, because at each price it adds every consumer's quantity together.

**Why the demand curve slopes downwards:** as the price falls, the good becomes **more affordable** (consumers' incomes stretch further) and **better value compared with substitutes**, so existing buyers buy more and new buyers enter the market. As the price rises, the opposite happens.

### Movements along versus shifts

| | **Movement along** the demand curve | **Shift** of the demand curve |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | a change in the **good's own price** only | a change in **any other factor** |
| Names | **extension** (price falls, quantity demanded rises); **contraction** (price rises, quantity demanded falls) | **increase** in demand (shift right); **decrease** in demand (shift left) |
| Diagram | from one point to another on the **same** curve | a **new** curve: D → D1 |

### What shifts demand

| Factor | Demand **increases** (shifts right) when… |
|---|---|
| **Income** | consumers' incomes rise (for most goods) |
| **Price of a substitute** | a rival product becomes **dearer**, e.g. train fares rise → demand for coach travel rises |
| **Price of a complement** | a good used with it becomes **cheaper**, e.g. cheaper printers → more demand for ink |
| **Tastes and fashion** | the product becomes more popular, e.g. a social-media trend |
| **Advertising and promotion** | a successful campaign persuades more people to buy at every price |
| **Population** | more people, or a change in age structure that suits the product (more retired people → more demand for mobility scooters) |
| **Seasons and weather** | the time of year suits the product (barbecue food in a heatwave) |
| **Interest rates and credit** | borrowing becomes cheaper, raising demand for goods often bought on credit (cars, furniture, houses) |
| **Expectations** | consumers expect prices to rise or their incomes to grow |

The opposite change shifts demand **left** (a decrease).

An **increase in demand** (D → D1) creates a shortage at the old price P, so the price is bid up to P1 and the quantity traded rises from Q to Q1 (an extension of supply).

| Change | Consequences for **consumers** | Consequences for **producers** |
|---|---|---|
| **Increase in demand** | pay a higher price; some on low incomes priced out; possible queues or waiting lists at first | sell more at a higher price, so **revenue** and probably **profit** rise; may hire workers, invest and expand; new firms attracted |
| **Decrease in demand** | pay a lower price; more affordable | sell less at a lower price; revenue and profit fall; may cut jobs, hold unsold stock, or close |
| **Movement along** (price rises, contraction) | buy less; switch to substitutes | sell fewer units at a higher price; revenue may rise or fall (depends on price elasticity, 2.2b) |

Write these as chains: "Demand for X increases **so** at the old price there is a shortage, **therefore** the price rises from P to P1, **which means** consumers pay more and some on lower incomes stop buying, **as a result**…".

::: terms
- **Demand**: the quantity of a good or service consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a given period.
- **Individual demand**: the quantity one consumer is willing and able to buy at each price.
- **Market demand**: the sum of all consumers' demand for a product at each price.
- **Demand curve**: a line showing the quantity demanded at each price, usually sloping down from left to right.
- **Extension of demand**: a rise in quantity demanded caused only by a fall in the good's own price.
- **Contraction of demand**: a fall in quantity demanded caused only by a rise in the good's own price.
- **Increase in demand**: a shift of the demand curve to the right, so more is demanded at every price.
- **Decrease in demand**: a shift of the demand curve to the left, so less is demanded at every price.
- **Substitute**: a good that can be used in place of another, such as coach travel instead of train travel.
- **Complement**: a good used together with another, such as a games console and its games.
:::

::: method
1. **Ask "is it the good's own price?"** Yes → movement along (extension or contraction). No → shift (increase right, decrease left).
2. **Name the cause and the direction**: "train fares rise, so coach travel becomes relatively cheaper, so demand for coach travel increases: the curve shifts right".
3. **Plotting from data**: label both axes with units, even scales from 0, plot each point accurately, join them, label D. Market demand = add quantities at each price.
4. **"Draw and label a diagram showing…" (2)**: labelled axes (Price, Quantity) and correct original curve (1); correct new curve D1 in the right direction, labelled (1).
5. **"Using a diagram, analyse…" (6)**: draw D, S, the shift, P→P1 and Q→Q1, then refer to the diagram in at least two applied chains of reasoning (consumers and/or producers, as the question asks). Do not evaluate.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: analyse the effect, then weigh it with conditions (how big the shift is, how long it lasts, what rivals do, what else is changing) and judge.
:::

::: example Explain what is meant by demand
Explain what is meant by demand. (2)

Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy (1) at each price over a given period of time, e.g. the number of cinema tickets bought per week at £8 (1).
:::

::: example Market demand from individual data
Invented data: at £3 a cup, Consumer A buys 3 cups of bubble tea a month, Consumer B buys 2 and Consumer C buys 5. At £2, they buy 5, 3 and 6. Calculate the percentage change in market demand when the price falls from £3 to £2. Show your working. (2)

Market demand at £3 = 3 + 2 + 5 = 10; at £2 = 5 + 3 + 6 = 14 (1).
% change = (14 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = **+40%** (1). This is an extension of demand: a movement down the curve.
:::

::: example Draw and label a decrease in demand
Invented context: a news report links a brand of energy drink to health problems. Draw and label a diagram showing the effect on demand for the drink. (Sketch it on paper, or describe the axes, curves and shift in words.) (2)

Axes labelled Price and Quantity, with a downward-sloping demand curve labelled D (1). A new demand curve D1 drawn to the **left** of D, showing that less is demanded at every price (1). (A movement along D scores 0 for the second mark.)
:::

::: example Explain one reason for a shift to the left
Explain one reason why the demand curve for new cars might shift to the left. (2)

Interest rates could rise, making car loans more expensive (1), so fewer households are willing and able to buy a new car at every price, and demand decreases (1).
:::

::: example Using a diagram, analyse — consequences for consumers
Invented context: a famous singer is pictured wearing Tarn Outdoor's waterproof jackets, and demand for them rises sharply. Using a diagram, analyse the consequences for consumers of the increase in demand for Tarn Outdoor's jackets. (6)

Diagram: Price and Quantity axes; S and D crossing at P and Q; D shifts right to D1; new equilibrium at a higher price P1 and higher quantity Q1 (K1 + An1).

The celebrity photo changes tastes, so at every price more consumers want a Tarn jacket and demand shifts from D to D1 (Ap). At the old price P there is now a shortage, so jackets sell out and Tarn and its retailers can raise the price to P1 (An). This means consumers who buy a jacket pay more than before, leaving them less income to spend on other goods (An). Some consumers on lower incomes who were willing to pay P but not P1 are priced out of the market and must buy a cheaper substitute brand (An). In the short run, before Tarn can make more jackets, consumers may also face waiting lists or empty shelves (Ap/An). Overall, more jackets are bought (Q to Q1), so fans of the singer get the jacket, but at a higher price.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between an extension of demand and an increase in demand. (2)
---
An extension is a movement down the existing demand curve caused by a fall in the good's own price (1); an increase is a shift of the whole curve to the right caused by a non-price factor such as higher incomes (1).
:::

::: q
State two factors, other than price, that could increase the demand for holidays abroad. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): rise in incomes; fall in the price of a complement (flights, hotels); rise in the price of a substitute (holidays at home); advertising; change in tastes/fashion; good exchange rate making them cheaper (credit as relevant); easier credit. Do not credit "a fall in the price of holidays abroad".
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why the demand curve slopes downwards from left to right. (2)
---
As price falls, the good becomes more affordable for consumers (1), so they are able to buy more of it with the same income / it becomes better value than substitutes, so people switch to it (1).
:::

::: q
Invented data: market demand for a gym's day passes is 400 a week at £10 and 520 a week at £8. Calculate the change in total spending by consumers on day passes. Show your working. (2)
---
At £10: 400 × £10 = £4,000; at £8: 520 × £8 = £4,160 (1). Change = +£160 a week (1).
:::

::: q
Draw and label a diagram to show the effect on the demand for umbrellas of a forecast of a very wet summer. (Sketch it on paper, or describe it in words.) (2)
---
Labelled axes (Price, Quantity) and original downward-sloping D (1); new curve D1 to the right of D, labelled (1).
:::

::: q
Using a diagram, analyse the consequences for producers of a decrease in demand for petrol cars. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains with a correct, referenced diagram; L2 3–4 mostly single-step or diagram missing; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: diagram with D shifting left to D1, price falling P → P1 and quantity Q → Q1 (K1 + An1). Producers sell fewer cars at a lower price, so revenue falls (An); with lower revenue, profits fall and some may make losses (An); they may cut production, close lines and make workers redundant (An); unsold cars build up as stock, adding storage costs (An); firms may switch resources to making electric cars where demand is rising (An). Application: e.g. the shift caused by rising fuel prices or tastes moving to electric cars.
:::

::: watch
- **Only the good's own price causes a movement along the curve.** Everything else shifts it, and you must say **which way**.
- Use the right words: **extension/contraction** for movements; **increase/decrease** for shifts. "Demand rose" when the price fell is wrong: quantity demanded rose.
- **Substitutes and complements work in opposite directions**: a dearer substitute raises demand; a dearer complement lowers it.
- When plotting from data, **plot every point and join them**. No line of best fit, no invented points. Label axes with units.
- "Using a diagram" questions need the diagram **and** reference to it (P to P1, Q to Q1). Without a correct diagram you cannot reach Level 3.
- When a question asks about **consumers**, analysing producers' profits earns little, and the reverse also applies.
- Examiners report students drawing only a movement when a shift was needed. Read whether a non-price factor has changed.
:::

::: test

::: test
Invented extract: Many towns have seen a big rise in the number of households owning dogs. Pawsome Ltd makes premium dog food sold through pet shops. Its sales rose by 30% last year, even though it did not change its prices.

Using a diagram, analyse the consequences for consumers of the increase in demand for premium dog food. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — correct labelled diagram (D shifts right, P and Q rise) referred to in developed chains applied to dog owners; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step effects, limited application, or diagram missing/incorrect; L1 1–2 — an isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: increase in demand = shift right; diagram with D → D1, P → P1, Q → Q1 (K1 + An1).
- Application: more households own dogs (a population/taste factor); sales up 30%; prices unchanged so far.
- Analysis: more dog owners means demand increases at every price; at the old price there is a shortage, so pet shops may run out and prices are likely to rise to P1; consumers will pay more for the same food, reducing the income left for other goods; some owners on lower incomes are priced out and switch to cheaper brands; in the long run, higher prices may attract new producers, increasing choice for consumers.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Bramble Bakes sells cakes through supermarkets. It plans to spend £120,000 on a TV and social-media advertising campaign. Its annual revenue is £1.5 million. Last year a rival launched a similar range at lower prices, and some customers have been cutting back on treats because their incomes have not kept up with rising prices.

Evaluate the importance to Bramble Bakes of a successful advertising campaign. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: campaign costs £120,000 (8% of £1.5 million revenue); rival with lower prices; customers' incomes squeezed.
- Analysis (important): successful advertising changes tastes and builds brand loyalty, shifting demand for Bramble's cakes to the right; it can sell more and/or charge a higher price, so revenue and profit rise; it helps win back customers from the cheaper rival.
- Analysis (limits): if real incomes are falling, consumers may cut treats whatever the advertising says; £120,000 is a large cost, so revenue must rise by enough to cover it.
- Evaluation: importance depends on how strong the rival's price advantage is and how long the effect lasts; other factors (income, prices of substitutes) may shift demand more than advertising; a campaign that does not work wastes 8% of revenue. Judgement: e.g. important to defend its market share against the rival, but income pressures limit how much it can achieve, so it may matter less than keeping prices competitive.
:::
