## What it is

Every producer has to pay for the factors it uses and hopes to sell its output for more than that. This topic covers the calculation and meaning of **total cost, average cost, total revenue, average revenue, profit and loss**, how **costs and revenues affect profit and supply**, the **importance of these for producers** (which the spec asks you to evaluate), and **economies of scale**: why average costs often fall as firms grow. Expect at least one calculation on J205/01 from this topic, and remember the **units** (£, £ million).

### Costs

- **Fixed costs** do **not** change with output in the short run: rent, insurance, loan repayments, salaries of managers. They must be paid even if the firm makes nothing.
- **Variable costs** **change directly with output**: raw materials, packaging, electricity for machines, wages of workers paid per item.
- **Total cost (TC) = fixed costs + variable costs**
- **Average cost (AC) = total cost ÷ output**: the cost of making one unit, on average. It is also called **unit cost**.

### Revenue

- **Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold**: all the money received from sales.
- **Average revenue (AR) = total revenue ÷ quantity sold**. If every unit sells at the same price, **AR = price**.

**Revenue is not profit.** The 2024 examiners found many candidates writing "revenue" when they meant profit. Revenue is money coming in; profit is what is left after costs.

### Profit and loss

- **Profit = total revenue − total cost** (when TR is greater than TC).
- A **loss** occurs when **total cost is greater than total revenue**: loss = TC − TR.
- **Profit per unit = average revenue − average cost** (= price − AC).

**Worked example (invented data).** Calder Candles has fixed costs of £2,000 a month and a variable cost of £3 per candle. It sells 1,500 candles at £5 each.

| Item | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Variable costs | £3 × 1,500 | £4,500 |
| Total cost | £2,000 + £4,500 | **£6,500** |
| Average cost | £6,500 ÷ 1,500 | **£4.33** (to the nearest penny) |
| Total revenue | £5 × 1,500 | **£7,500** |
| Average revenue | £7,500 ÷ 1,500 | **£5.00** |
| Profit | £7,500 − £6,500 | **£1,000** |

If sales fell to 800 candles: TC = £2,000 + (£3 × 800) = £4,400; TR = £5 × 800 = £4,000; so a **loss of £400**. The fixed costs still have to be paid, so fewer sales spread them over fewer candles and average cost rises to £5.50, above the £5 price.

### How costs and revenues affect profit and supply

| Change | Effect on profit | Effect on supply |
|---|---|---|
| **Costs rise** (wages, raw materials, energy, an indirect tax) | each unit is less profitable, so profit falls unless price rises | firms supply less at each price: **supply shifts left** |
| **Costs fall** (cheaper materials, better technology, a subsidy) | each unit is more profitable | firms supply more at each price: **supply shifts right** |
| **Price and revenue rise** (higher demand) | more profitable to produce | firms have an **incentive to expand supply** (a movement along S) and new firms may enter |
| **Price and revenue fall** | profit falls; losses possible | firms cut output; loss-making firms may leave the market |

### Why cost, revenue, profit and loss matter to producers

**Profit** is:
- a **reward** for the risk taken by entrepreneurs; without it, people would not start businesses;
- a **signal** showing which goods consumers want, which draws resources into those markets;
- a **source of finance**: **retained profit** pays for new machinery and growth without borrowing;
- a way to **attract investors and lenders**, who want to see a profitable business.

**Losses** are a warning: a firm making losses must **cut costs, raise prices or change its product**, or it will eventually close as cash runs out. A loss in the short run may be acceptable for a new firm building up customers, or during a temporary fall in demand.

**Evaluation points**: profit is not the only aim (survival, growth, social enterprises' social aims); one year's profit or loss may not reflect the long term; **cash** matters as well as profit; raising prices to cover costs depends on **PED**; cutting costs may hurt quality or staff morale.

### Economies of scale

**Economies of scale** are the **fall in average cost** that comes from **increasing the scale of production**, i.e. growing larger. Total cost rises as a firm grows, but the cost of each unit falls.

As output grows, average cost first falls (economies of scale), but beyond a certain size it may rise again (diseconomies of scale).

**Internal economies of scale** happen **inside the firm** as **it** grows:

| Type | How the larger firm cuts average cost |
|---|---|
| **Purchasing (bulk-buying)** | buys materials in huge quantities and negotiates discounts from suppliers |
| **Technical** | can afford large, advanced machinery and production lines that make each unit more cheaply |
| **Managerial** | can employ specialist managers (finance, marketing, HR) who work more efficiently than one manager doing everything |
| **Financial** | banks see it as less risky, so it borrows more easily and at lower interest rates |
| **Marketing** | an advertising campaign costs the same whether it sells 1 million or 10 million units, so the cost per unit falls |
| **Risk-bearing** | sells many products in many markets, so a fall in one is offset by others |

**External economies of scale** happen **outside the firm**, from the **growth of the whole industry in an area**. They benefit **all firms** in that area, and no single firm controls them:

- **better transport links**: new roads, rail or ports built to serve the industry cut every firm's delivery costs;
- **a pool of skilled labour**: workers with the right skills live nearby, so training and recruitment costs fall;
- **specialist suppliers** set up nearby, cutting transport costs and delivery times;
- **local colleges and shared research**: courses designed for the industry; ideas spread between firms.

The 2025 examiners found external economies poorly understood: the key words are **"outside the firm"**, **"growth of the industry or area"** and **"available to all firms there"**.

**Diseconomies of scale** (useful for evaluation): a very large firm can suffer **rising** average costs: poor communication through many layers of management, slow decisions, demotivated workers who feel like "a small cog", and difficulty co-ordinating many sites.

::: terms
- **Fixed costs**: costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run, such as rent or insurance.
- **Variable costs**: costs that change directly with the level of output, such as raw materials.
- **Total cost**: the sum of fixed costs and variable costs at a given level of output.
- **Average cost**: total cost divided by output, the cost of producing each unit on average.
- **Total revenue**: the money a firm receives from sales, calculated as price multiplied by quantity sold.
- **Average revenue**: total revenue divided by the quantity sold, equal to the price when every unit sells at one price.
- **Profit**: the amount by which total revenue exceeds total cost.
- **Loss**: the amount by which total cost exceeds total revenue.
- **Economies of scale**: the fall in average cost that results from increasing the scale of production.
- **Internal economies of scale**: falls in average cost that come from the growth of the firm itself, such as bulk-buying.
- **External economies of scale**: falls in average cost that come from the growth of the industry in an area and benefit all firms there.
- **Diseconomies of scale**: the rise in average cost when a firm grows too large, for example through poor communication.
:::

::: method
1. **Write the formula first** (this earns the method mark): TC = FC + VC; AC = TC ÷ output; TR = P × Q; AR = TR ÷ Q; profit = TR − TC.
2. **Show working and give units**: "£8.7 million", not "8.7". If TC is greater than TR, say "a **loss** of £…".
3. **"Explain what is meant by economies of scale" (2)**: a fall in **average** cost (1) as the **scale of production / size** increases (1).
4. **"Explain one internal / external economy of scale" (2)**: name it (1) + how it lowers average cost, applied to the firm or area (1). External = outside the firm, from the industry's growth.
5. **Analyse (6)**: chain from cost or revenue change → profit → what the firm can do (invest, cut prices, expand supply). No evaluation.
6. **Evaluate (6)**: weigh the benefit (lower AC, profit) against limits (diseconomies of scale, cost of growth, demand, PED, time), then a supported judgement for the producer named.
:::

::: example Calculating profit or loss
Invented data: in one month Ashby Cycle Repairs has fixed costs of £3,400 and variable costs of £1,850. It completes 250 repairs at £20 each. Calculate its profit or loss. Show your working. (2)

TR = £20 × 250 = £5,000; TC = £3,400 + £1,850 = £5,250 (1). TR − TC = −£250, so a **loss of £250** (1).
:::

::: example Calculating average cost
Invented data: a water-bottling plant's total costs are £1.44 million a year and it produces 1.2 million bottles. Calculate the average cost per bottle. Show your working. (2)

AC = total cost ÷ output = £1,440,000 ÷ 1,200,000 (1) = **£1.20 per bottle** (1).
:::

::: example An internal economy of scale
Invented context: Fieldfare Foods has grown from 3 to 60 supermarkets. Explain one internal economy of scale it may gain. (2)

Purchasing economies: it buys stock for 60 stores at once, so it can negotiate bulk-buying discounts from suppliers (1), lowering the cost of each item it sells and so its average cost (1).
:::

::: example An external economy of scale
Invented context: many video-game studios have set up in the city of Dunmere, and a local university now runs a games-design degree. Explain why the degree is an external economy of scale for studios in Dunmere. (2)

The degree comes from the growth of the games industry in the area, not from any one studio, and is available to all studios there (1); it creates a pool of skilled graduates nearby, so each studio spends less on recruiting and training, lowering its average cost (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — the advantages of making a profit
Invented context: Marlow Signs Ltd made a profit of £180,000 last year. Analyse the advantages for Marlow Signs of making a profit. (6)

Profit is the surplus of total revenue over total cost, here £180,000 (K). Marlow can keep part of this as retained profit to buy new cutting and printing machines without taking out a loan, so it avoids paying interest (Ap/An). The new machines raise productivity, lowering the average cost of each sign, so Marlow can cut prices and win orders from rival sign-makers (An). Profit also rewards the owners for the risk they took, encouraging them to keep investing in the business rather than closing it (An). A record of profit shows banks that Marlow can repay a loan, so if it wants to expand into a larger unit, it can borrow more easily and at a lower interest rate (An). Finally, profit provides a cushion: if demand falls next year, Marlow can cover its fixed costs for a while without making staff redundant (An).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between total revenue and profit. (2)

Invented data: a firm sells 4,000 units for total revenue of £26,000. Calculate the average revenue. (2)
---
£26,000 ÷ 4,000 (1) = £6.50 per unit (1).

::: q
State two internal economies of scale. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): purchasing/bulk-buying; technical; managerial; financial; marketing; risk-bearing.
:::

::: q
Explain one external economy of scale. (2)
---
E.g. as an industry grows in an area, specialist suppliers set up nearby (1), cutting delivery costs and times for all firms there, so their average costs fall (1). Or skilled labour pool / better transport links / local training, developed. Do not credit bulk-buying (internal).
:::

::: q
Invented data: a firm has fixed costs of £12,000 and variable costs of £4 per unit. It produces 3,000 units. Calculate the average cost. (2)
---
TC = £12,000 + (£4 × 3,000) = £24,000 (1); AC = £24,000 ÷ 3,000 = £8 per unit (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a rise in the price of raw materials could affect a firm's profit and supply. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation.
Indicative content: raw materials are a variable cost, so total and average cost rise (K); at the same price, profit per unit falls (An); some output becomes unprofitable, so the firm supplies less at every price and supply shifts left (An); if it raises its price to cover costs, demand contracts, so revenue may fall if demand is price elastic (An); lower profit leaves less retained profit for investment (An); a firm already close to breaking even may make a loss (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Revenue is not profit.** Profit = total revenue − total cost.
- **Units always**: £, £ million, "per unit". The 2025 examiners penalised answers like "8.7" when the answer was £8.7 million.
- If costs are greater than revenue, say "**a loss of** £…", not just a negative number.
- **Economies of scale lower AVERAGE cost**, not total cost; total cost rises as a firm grows.
- **External economies are outside the firm**, from the growth of the industry in an area, and are shared by all firms there. Bulk-buying is internal.
- Higher costs shift **supply** left; they do not shift demand.
- In "evaluate the importance for producers", write about the producer: investment, survival, rewards, signals, not about consumers.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Orla's Ice Cream is a small firm selling tubs to cafés. Last summer was wet: Orla sold 30,000 tubs at £2.50 each. Her fixed costs were £36,000 and variable costs were £1.40 per tub. She is considering raising her price to £2.80 next year.

Analyse why Orla's Ice Cream made a loss last year and how the price rise could affect its revenue. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains using the data accurately; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, or partial use of the data; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: TR = P × Q; TC = FC + VC; loss when TC > TR; PED.
- Application: TR = £2.50 × 30,000 = £75,000; VC = £1.40 × 30,000 = £42,000; TC = £36,000 + £42,000 = £78,000; loss = £3,000; AC = £2.60, above the £2.50 price.
- Analysis: the wet summer cut sales, so the fixed costs of £36,000 were spread over too few tubs and average cost rose above the price, causing a loss; at £2.80, if sales stay at 30,000, TR rises to £84,000 and Orla makes a profit of £6,000; but a higher price causes demand to contract: if cafés can switch to rival brands (elastic demand), sales could fall far enough to reduce revenue; e.g. sales of 26,000 at £2.80 give TR of £72,800, less than last year's £75,000; if demand is inelastic, revenue rises; the effect also depends on the weather next summer, which shifts demand.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Pennock Pottery makes hand-painted mugs in one workshop with 14 staff. It has won a contract with a national store chain and plans to open a second, much larger workshop, which would raise output from 40,000 to 150,000 mugs a year. The owner hopes this will cut the average cost of each mug.

Evaluate whether growing larger will lower Pennock Pottery's average costs. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement about Pennock's average costs; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: one workshop, 14 staff; national chain contract; output from 40,000 to 150,000 mugs; hand-painted.
- Analysis: bulk-buying clay, glazes and boxes for 150,000 mugs earns discounts (purchasing economies); a larger kiln and workshop make each mug more cheaply (technical economies); fixed costs such as design and marketing are spread over nearly four times as many mugs; a contract with a national chain makes banks more willing to lend at lower rates (financial economies).
- Evaluation: hand-painting is labour-intensive, so each extra mug still needs a painter's time, limiting technical economies; running two sites can cause co-ordination and communication problems (diseconomies of scale); the new workshop is a large cost that only lowers average cost if the 150,000 mugs are actually sold, so it depends on the contract lasting; quality may fall if new staff are less skilled, risking the contract. Judgement: e.g. likely to lower average cost to some extent through purchasing and spreading fixed costs, but less than for a mass producer because hand-painting does not scale easily; the result depends on demand from the chain holding up.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Rennick Builders made a profit of £420,000 last year. Its costs are rising because the price of timber and bricks has gone up, and it is finding it hard to recruit skilled bricklayers. House prices in its area have stayed the same.

Evaluate the importance of rising costs for Rennick Builders. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement for the producer; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: £420,000 profit; timber and bricks dearer; skilled bricklayers scarce; house prices flat.
- Analysis: higher material and labour costs raise total and average cost per house; with house prices flat, Rennick cannot pass costs on, so profit per house falls; less retained profit to buy land or invest; it may build fewer houses, so its supply falls; a shortage of bricklayers may delay projects, adding further costs.
- Evaluation: a £420,000 profit gives a cushion, so a moderate cost rise may only reduce profit rather than cause a loss; the importance depends on how large and lasting the rises are: material prices may fall back; Rennick may cut costs by bulk-buying or using new methods; if demand for houses rises later, prices could rise and restore profit; small builders with no profit cushion would be hit harder. Judgement: e.g. important, because flat house prices mean costs come straight off profit and could limit future investment, but not yet threatening its survival given last year's profit.
:::
