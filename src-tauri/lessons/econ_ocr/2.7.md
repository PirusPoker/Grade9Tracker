## What it is

Labour is a factor of production, and like any good it has a market and a price. The **labour market** is where **workers supply labour** and **employers demand it**; the **wage** is the price of labour. This topic covers the role and operation of the labour market, how **wages are determined by the demand for and supply of labour** (with a diagram), the factors behind each side, and how to explain and calculate **gross and net pay**. Net-pay calculations appear almost every year on J205/01.

### The role and operation of the labour market

- **Workers (households)** supply labour in return for wages. They decide whether to work, how many hours, and in which job, depending on pay, conditions and their skills.
- **Employers (firms and the government)** demand labour to produce goods and services. They decide how many workers to hire, with which skills, and what to pay.
- The labour market brings the two together through **recruitment** (adverts, agencies, job websites), **applications and interviews**, and **negotiation** over pay and conditions.
- **Interaction** goes beyond a single hiring. Employers train workers, offer promotion and non-wage benefits; workers may join a **trade union**, which negotiates pay and conditions for its members as a group. The government sets a **minimum wage** below which employers cannot legally pay, and sets employment law.
- The labour market **allocates labour** between jobs: higher wages attract workers towards jobs where demand is strong, just as prices allocate other resources (2.4).

### How wages are determined

The **wage rate** is set where the **demand for labour** equals the **supply of labour**: the **equilibrium wage** (W) and **equilibrium quantity of labour** (L). If the wage is above W, more people want the job than there are vacancies (a surplus of labour), so wages tend to fall; if it is below W, employers cannot fill vacancies (a shortage of labour), so they offer higher wages.

Fewer people are willing and able to do the job, so the supply of labour shifts left from S to S1. At the old wage W employers have unfilled vacancies, so they raise pay to attract workers: the wage rises to W1 and the number employed falls to L1. A **rise in demand** for labour (D shifts right) would also raise the wage, but with **more** people employed.

### What affects the demand for labour?

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Demand for the product** | labour is a **derived demand**: if people buy more of the good, firms need more workers to make it (more home deliveries mean more delivery drivers) |
| **Productivity of workers** | more productive workers bring in more revenue each, so employers want more of them and can pay more |
| **Cost of capital that could replace labour** | if machines or software become cheaper, firms may replace workers with them, so demand for labour falls |
| **The wage itself** | a higher wage causes a **movement along** the demand curve: fewer workers demanded |

### What affects the supply of labour to a job?

| Factor | Supply is **lower** (wages tend to be higher) when… |
|---|---|
| **Skills and qualifications needed** | the job needs rare skills or long qualifications (surgeons, airline pilots) |
| **Length of training** | training takes many years, so few people can join quickly |
| **Working conditions and non-wage benefits** | the job is dangerous, stressful, dirty or has unsociable hours, and lacks benefits such as a pension or flexible hours |
| **Attractiveness and status of the job** | few people find the work appealing |
| **Size of the working population** | there are few people of working age in the area; migration can raise or lower supply |
| **Wages in other jobs** | other jobs with similar skills pay more, drawing workers away |

**Why some jobs pay more than others**: high demand **and** low supply (scarce skills, long training) give high wages; low demand or a large supply of people able to do the work (little training needed) give low wages. Non-wage factors matter: some people accept lower pay for a job they enjoy or that offers security.

### Gross and net pay

- **Gross pay** is pay **before** any deductions.
- **Net pay** ("take-home pay") is pay **after** deductions.

The three deductions named in the spec:

1. **Income tax**: a **direct tax** on income, paid to the government. People earning more pay a higher percentage on the top part of their income (a progressive tax, 3.5a).
2. **National Insurance (NI) contributions**: also paid to the government, by both workers and employers; they help pay for state benefits such as the state pension.
3. **Pension contributions**: paid into a workplace pension scheme, saved for the worker's retirement (often a percentage of gross pay).

**Net pay = gross pay − income tax − National Insurance − pension contributions**

**Worked example (invented figures; rates in exam questions are always given).** Priya's gross salary is £28,800 a year. Her annual deductions are income tax £2,960, National Insurance £1,180 and a pension contribution of 5% of gross pay.

| Step | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Pension | 5% × £28,800 = 0.05 × £28,800 | £1,440 |
| Total deductions | £2,960 + £1,180 + £1,440 | £5,580 |
| Annual net pay | £28,800 − £5,580 | **£23,220** |
| Monthly net pay | £23,220 ÷ 12 | **£1,935** |
| Monthly gross pay | £28,800 ÷ 12 | £2,400 |

**Converting**: annual ÷ 12 = monthly; annual ÷ 52 = weekly; hourly wage × hours = weekly gross pay. The 2024 examiners found many students could not work out monthly net pay: subtract **all** deductions, then divide by 12 (or divide everything by 12 first: the answer is the same).

::: terms
- **Labour market**: the market in which workers supply labour and employers demand it, setting wages and employment.
- **Wage rate**: the price paid for labour, for example per hour or per year.
- **Demand for labour**: the number of workers employers are willing and able to hire at each wage rate.
- **Supply of labour**: the number of workers willing and able to work in a job at each wage rate.
- **Derived demand**: demand for labour that comes from the demand for the goods and services workers produce.
- **Equilibrium wage**: the wage at which the demand for labour equals the supply of labour.
- **Gross pay**: a worker's pay before any deductions are taken off.
- **Net pay**: a worker's take-home pay after income tax, National Insurance and pension contributions are deducted.
- **Income tax**: a direct tax paid to the government on a person's income.
- **National Insurance**: contributions paid to the government by workers and employers that help fund state benefits such as the state pension.
- **Pension contribution**: money taken from pay and saved in a pension scheme for the worker's retirement.
- **Trade union**: an organisation of workers that negotiates with employers over pay and conditions.
:::

::: method
1. **Diagrams (2)**: axes labelled **Wage rate** and **Quantity of labour** (1); the correct curve shifted the correct way with W, W1, L, L1 (1). Fewer workers able or willing = supply left; more demand for the product = demand right.
2. **Explain a factor (2)**: name it (1) + explain its effect on demand for, or supply of, labour in that job, applied (1).
3. **Net pay (2)**: list every deduction, total them, subtract from gross; convert annual to monthly (÷ 12) or weekly (÷ 52) only when asked. Show working, give £.
4. **"Using a diagram, analyse why wages for X have risen/fallen" (6)**: diagram, then a chain: cause → curve shift → shortage or surplus of workers at the old wage → wage changes → number employed changes → effect on workers or employers. No evaluation.
5. **Evaluate (6)**: for the group named (workers, employers), weigh higher pay against limits (employers replacing labour with machines, workload, firms closing, migration, training of new workers), then a supported judgement.
:::

::: example Explain one factor affecting the supply of labour
Explain one factor that affects the supply of labour to the job of a commercial diver. (2)

Commercial diving is dangerous and needs specialist training and certificates (1), so relatively few people are willing and able to do it, keeping the supply of labour low and the wage high (1).
:::

::: example Calculating monthly net pay
Invented figures: Tom earns £33,600 a year before deductions. Each year he pays £4,120 income tax, £1,640 National Insurance and £1,680 pension contributions. Calculate his monthly net pay. Show your working. (2)

Total deductions = £4,120 + £1,640 + £1,680 = £7,440; annual net pay = £33,600 − £7,440 = £26,160 (1). Monthly net pay = £26,160 ÷ 12 = **£2,180** (1).
:::

::: example Gross and net pay
Explain the difference between gross pay and net pay. (2)

Gross pay is a worker's pay before any deductions (1); net pay is take-home pay after deductions such as income tax, National Insurance and pension contributions (1).
:::

::: example Derived demand
Explain why the demand for labour is a derived demand. (2)

Firms do not want workers for their own sake but for the goods and services they produce (1), so when demand for the product rises, e.g. more people booking holidays, demand for the workers who make it, such as holiday reps, also rises (1).
:::

::: example Using a diagram, analyse a rise in wages
Invented context: online shopping has grown rapidly in Teesmouth, and warehouse firms there say they need hundreds more forklift drivers. Using a diagram, analyse why wages for forklift drivers in Teesmouth have risen. (6)

Diagram: axes Wage rate and Quantity of labour; demand for labour shifts right D to D1; wage rises W to W1 and employment rises L to L1 (K1 + An1).

Demand for forklift drivers is derived from demand for the goods they move: as online shopping grows, warehouses handle more parcels and need more drivers at every wage, so demand for labour shifts right (Ap). At the original wage W there is now a shortage of drivers: firms have more vacancies than there are qualified people wanting the work (An). To fill their vacancies, warehouses compete for the limited number of licensed drivers by offering higher hourly rates, so the wage is pushed up towards W1 (An). The higher wage attracts more people to train for a forklift licence or to move from other jobs, so the quantity of labour supplied extends to L1, as shown on the diagram (An). Because training takes only a short time, supply can respond fairly quickly, so the wage rise may be moderate rather than very large (An).
:::

Explain what is meant by net pay. (2)
---
A worker's take-home pay (1) after deductions such as income tax, National Insurance and pension contributions have been taken from gross pay (1).

State two deductions taken from gross pay. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): income tax; National Insurance contributions; pension contributions. (Student loan repayments may be credited if explained, but the spec names these three.)
:::

::: q
Invented figures: a worker earns £15.20 an hour and works 35 hours a week. Calculate the worker's weekly gross pay. (2)
---
£15.20 × 35 (1) = £532 (1).

::: q
Explain one reason why airline pilots are paid more than shop assistants. (2)
---
E.g. pilots need long, expensive training and rare skills, so the supply of labour is low (1), whereas many people can work as shop assistants with little training, so supply is high and wages lower (1). Or demand/productivity/responsibility, developed with comparison.
:::

::: q
Explain how the introduction of self-service checkouts may affect the demand for supermarket workers. (2)
---
Machines can do the work of checkout staff, replacing labour with capital (1), so supermarkets need fewer cashiers and demand for this labour falls / shifts left, putting downward pressure on wages (1).
:::

::: q
Using a diagram, analyse why wages for software engineers have risen. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 correct labour-market diagram (D right and/or S limited) used in developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: firms in every industry need software, so demand for engineers rises, D → D1 (K); diagram Wage rate / Quantity of labour, W → W1, L → L1 (K1 + An1); long training and specialist skills mean supply is slow to grow (An); at the old wage there is a shortage of engineers, so firms bid up pay (An); engineers are highly productive, so firms can afford higher wages (An); higher pay attracts more students to train, increasing supply over time (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Net pay subtracts ALL the deductions**: income tax, National Insurance and pension. Missing one loses the calculation mark.
- **Monthly = annual ÷ 12.** The 2024 examiners found many errors converting annual net pay to monthly.
- **Label the labour diagram correctly**: Wage rate (not Price) and Quantity of labour (not Quantity).
- Work out **which curve shifts**: fewer people willing or able to do the job = **supply** shifts left; more demand for the product = **demand** shifts right.
- Explain the **mechanism**: a shortage of workers at the old wage leads employers to raise pay.
- Higher unemployment means **more** workers are available, not fewer.
- Always give **£** in pay answers.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The number of people training to become plumbers in the region of Ardmore has fallen for five years, and many experienced plumbers are retiring. At the same time, a government scheme is paying households to install heat pumps, which plumbers fit. The average hourly rate charged by plumbers in Ardmore has risen by 30% in two years.

Using a diagram, analyse why wages for plumbers in Ardmore have risen. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — correct labour-market diagram (S left and/or D right, W → W1) referred to in developed chains applied to Ardmore; L2 3–4 — correct but mostly single-step, limited use of the extract, or diagram missing/mislabelled; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: Wage rate and Quantity of labour axes; S → S1 left and/or D → D1 right; W → W1 (K1 + An1); derived demand.
- Application: fewer trainees for five years; retirements; heat-pump scheme; 30% rise.
- Analysis: fewer trainees and retirements mean fewer plumbers willing and able to work at every wage, shifting supply left; the heat-pump scheme raises demand for installations, so demand for plumbers (derived demand) shifts right; both create a shortage of plumbers at the old wage; households and firms compete for scarce plumbers, bidding up hourly rates; because training takes years, supply cannot respond quickly, so the wage rise is large (30%); the higher pay may eventually attract more trainees, increasing supply in the long run.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: Lanbury's hotels and restaurants cannot fill about one in eight of their jobs. Many workers have left for warehouses and retail, where hours are more regular. Hotels have raised hourly pay by 12% this year, and the remaining staff are working longer shifts to cover vacancies.

Evaluate whether a shortage of workers will benefit the hotel and restaurant workers who remain in Lanbury. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement focused on the remaining workers; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported assertions. Answers only about employers = Level 1 max. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: one in eight jobs unfilled; workers leaving for warehouses and retail; 12% pay rise; longer shifts.
- Analysis: a shortage of labour at the old wage forces employers to raise pay, so remaining workers earn 12% more per hour; employers may also improve conditions, training or bonuses to keep staff; workers have more bargaining power and can move to the best-paying employer; longer shifts at higher pay raise weekly gross pay.
- Evaluation: longer shifts may cause tiredness and stress, so the gain in pay is partly offset by worse conditions; the real benefit depends on whether prices are rising too, as a 12% rise could be eaten up by inflation; part of the extra pay is lost in income tax and National Insurance, so net pay rises by less; if costs rise too far, some hotels may close or cut hours, threatening jobs; in the long run employers may use technology (online check-in) to need fewer staff. Judgement: e.g. remaining workers benefit to a large extent through higher pay and bargaining power, but less than the 12% suggests once longer hours and stress are considered.
:::
