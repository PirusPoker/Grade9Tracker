## What it is

A **fair distribution of income** is one of the government's economic objectives. This topic covers what the **distribution of income** means, the different **types of income**, the difference between **income and wealth**, how to **calculate** income and wealth from data, and the **causes** of differences in income and wealth and their **consequences for an economy** (which the spec asks you to evaluate).

### The distribution of income

The **distribution of income** is how a country's total income is shared out among its households or people. It is often shown by splitting households into five equal groups, **quintiles** (fifths), from the poorest 20% to the richest 20%, and showing the share of total income each group receives, in a table or pie chart.

- If income were shared **equally**, each fifth would receive 20%.
- In reality the richest fifth receives far more than 20% and the poorest fifth far less: income is **unequally distributed**.
- If the share of the richest fifth grows and the poorest shrinks, the distribution has become **more unequal**.

"Fair" is a **value judgement**: few people think every household should have the same income, but many think the gap should not be too large. A government can make the distribution **more equal** through taxes and benefits (see 3.5b).

### Types of income

| Type | Who receives it |
|---|---|
| **Wages and salaries** | employees, in return for their labour |
| **Profit** | business owners and the self-employed, the reward for enterprise |
| **Interest** | savers, on money held in banks and building societies |
| **Dividends** | shareholders, from a company's profits |
| **Rent** | owners of land or property let to others |
| **State benefits and pensions** | people receiving government payments (Universal Credit, the state pension, child benefit) and private or workplace pensions |

Most households get most of their income from wages; richer households also get a lot from interest, dividends and rent, which come from owning **wealth**.

### Income versus wealth

- **Income** is a **flow** of money received **over a period of time** (per week, month or year): wages, profit, interest, dividends, rent and benefits.
- **Wealth** is a **stock** of assets owned **at a point in time**: houses and other property, savings, shares, pension funds, land, valuable possessions.

The two are linked: **wealth generates income** (savings earn interest, shares pay dividends, property earns rent), and **income can be saved to build wealth**. Wealth is distributed **much more unequally** than income, because:
- it builds up over a lifetime and can be **inherited** across generations;
- assets such as property and shares tend to **rise in value**, so those who already own them get richer;
- people on low incomes cannot save much, so they build little wealth.

**Net wealth** (net worth) = total assets − total debts (for example, a house worth £300,000 with a £180,000 mortgage gives £120,000 of housing wealth).

### Calculating income and wealth

**1. Total income from several sources (invented).** Priya earns a salary of £31,000, receives £600 interest on savings, £1,400 of dividends and £7,200 rent from a flat. Total income = 31,000 + 600 + 1,400 + 7,200 = **£40,200** a year.

**2. Shares of income (invented country, Calderra):**

| Quintile | Share of total income |
|---|---|
| Poorest fifth | 7% |
| Second fifth | 11% |
| Middle fifth | 16% |
| Fourth fifth | 23% |
| Richest fifth | 43% |

- The **richest 40%** = fourth fifth + richest fifth = 23% + 43% = **66%**.
- The **poorest 40%** = 7% + 11% = **18%**.
- The richest fifth receives 43 ÷ 7 ≈ **6.1 times** the share of the poorest fifth.
- If total income is £800 billion, the richest fifth receives 43% × £800bn = **£344 billion**.

Read the key carefully: adding the wrong fifths is a common way to drop marks.

**3. Net wealth (invented).** A household owns a house worth £260,000, savings of £15,000 and a car worth £9,000, and owes £140,000 on its mortgage and £4,000 on a loan. Net wealth = (260,000 + 15,000 + 9,000) − (140,000 + 4,000) = 284,000 − 144,000 = **£140,000**.

### Causes of differences in income and wealth

| Cause | How it creates inequality |
|---|---|
| **Skills, qualifications and education** | workers with scarce skills (surgeons, engineers) command high wages; unskilled workers earn less (links to wage determination in 2.7) |
| **Occupation and pay differentials** | pay differs between jobs because of differences in demand and supply of labour, responsibility and risk |
| **Employment status** | the unemployed and those in part-time or insecure work earn much less |
| **Ownership of wealth** | owners of property, shares and savings receive rent, dividends and interest on top of wages |
| **Inheritance** | wealth passed down the generations gives some people assets they did not earn |
| **Age** | income tends to peak in middle age; wealth builds over a lifetime |
| **Region** | wages and house prices are higher in some areas, so property owners there grow richer |
| **Discrimination** | some groups may be paid less or denied promotion |
| **Taxes and benefits** | a less progressive tax system or lower benefits widens inequality |

### Consequences for an economy

| Possible costs of wide inequality | Possible benefits of some inequality |
|---|---|
| **relative poverty**: low-income households cannot afford what most people consider normal | higher pay **rewards skill, effort and risk-taking**, giving an **incentive** to train, work hard and start businesses |
| **inequality of opportunity**: poorer children may get less education, so talent is wasted and productivity lower | richer households **save more**, providing funds for investment |
| **health and social problems**: poor health, crime and lower life expectancy, raising government spending | entrepreneurs who build successful firms create jobs |
| **lower total spending**: poorer households spend almost all their income, richer ones save more, so heavy inequality can weaken demand | |
| **resentment and social tension**; more government spending on benefits | |

When evaluating, ask: **how large** is the gap, is it **growing**, are the rewards linked to **effort and skill** or to **inheritance**, and do poorer households still have **opportunities** to improve their position?

::: terms
- **Distribution of income**: the way a country's total income is shared out among its households or people.
- **Income**: a flow of money received over a period of time, such as wages, profit, interest, dividends, rent or benefits.
- **Wealth**: a stock of assets owned at a point in time, such as property, savings, shares and pension funds.
- **Net wealth**: the value of a person's assets minus the value of their debts.
- **Quintile**: one fifth (20%) of households when they are ranked from the poorest to the richest.
- **Dividend**: a share of a company's profit paid out to its shareholders.
- **Rent**: income received by the owner of land or property for letting others use it.
- **State benefits**: payments from the government to people on low incomes, the unemployed, the disabled, families and pensioners.
- **Inheritance**: wealth passed on to someone when the previous owner dies.
- **Relative poverty**: having an income well below the average, so that a household cannot afford the standard of living most people consider normal.
- **Pay differentials**: differences in wages between jobs or groups of workers.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain the difference between income and wealth" (2)**: income is a flow over time with an example (1); wealth is a stock of assets at a point in time with an example (1).
2. **Calculations (2)**: add the right sources or quintiles; for a share in £, multiply the % by the total; for net wealth take debts from assets. Show working; give £ or %.
3. **"Explain how the distribution has changed" (2)**: say which way the shares moved, with figures (1), and whether the distribution became more or less equal (1).
4. **Analyse (6)**: chains from a cause (house prices, skills, inheritance, unemployment) to who gains and who loses income or wealth, using the extract; no evaluation.
5. **Evaluate (6)**: weigh costs against benefits of inequality for the economy (or weigh several causes), qualify each point (size of gap, opportunity, incentives, how long) and finish with a judgement.
:::

::: example Income and wealth
Explain the difference between income and wealth. (2)

Income is a flow of money received over a period of time, such as a monthly salary (1); wealth is a stock of assets owned at a point in time, such as a house or savings (1).
:::

::: example Quintile shares
Invented data: in the country of Fenwick, the poorest fifth of households receives 6% of total income, the second fifth 10%, the middle fifth 15%, the fourth fifth 22% and the richest fifth 47%. Calculate the share of income received by the richest 40% of households. (2)

Richest 40% = fourth fifth + richest fifth (1) = 22% + 47% = **69%** (1).
:::

::: example Total income
Invented figures: Tom earns £27,500 a year from his job and receives £850 a year in interest and £2,650 in rent. Calculate his total annual income. (2)

£27,500 + £850 + £2,650 (1) = **£31,000** (1).
:::

::: example Change in distribution
Invented data: the richest fifth's share of income in a country rose from 38% to 44% over ten years, while the poorest fifth's fell from 8% to 6%. Explain how the distribution of income has changed. (2)

The richest fifth's share rose by 6 percentage points and the poorest fifth's fell by 2 (1), so the distribution of income became **more unequal** (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — rising house prices and wealth
Invented context: in the country of Brastow, house prices have risen by 40% in five years, while wages have risen by 10%. Analyse how rising house prices affect the distribution of wealth in Brastow. (6)

Wealth is a stock of assets, and a house is most households' largest asset (K). Homeowners' houses have risen in value by 40%, so their wealth has grown sharply without them doing anything (Ap/An). Non-owners, often younger and lower-paid households, see no gain; and because house prices rose four times as fast as wages, saving for a deposit becomes harder, so they are less likely to ever own property (Ap/An). Owners of several properties can charge higher rents, increasing their income, which can be saved to build more wealth, while renters pay more and save less (An). Homeowners' children may later inherit more valuable houses, passing the gap down the generations, so the distribution of wealth becomes more unequal (An).
:::

::: q
State two types of income other than wages. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): profit; interest; dividends; rent; state benefits; pensions. Single words acceptable here as they name the income type.
:::

::: q
Invented figures: a household has assets of £210,000 and debts of £95,000. Calculate its net wealth. (2)
---
£210,000 − £95,000 (1) = **£115,000** (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income. (2)
---
Wealth builds up over a lifetime and can be inherited (1), and assets such as property and shares rise in value and earn more income, so those who own them get richer while those on low incomes cannot save to build wealth (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one cause of differences in income between workers. (2)
---
E.g. workers with scarce skills or high qualifications are in high demand relative to supply (1), so employers pay them higher wages than unskilled workers, whose supply is large (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one possible benefit of some inequality of income for an economy. (2)
---
Higher pay for skilled or risky work gives an incentive (1) for people to train, work harder or start businesses, raising productivity and output (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how a rise in unemployment in a region can affect the distribution of income. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: income distribution is how income is shared among households (K); people who lose their jobs fall from wages to benefits, which are much lower, so their income drops sharply (An); they move into the poorest fifth, widening the gap with those still in work (An); less local spending means shops and other firms cut hours or wages, lowering other incomes in the region (An); workers with in-demand skills keep their jobs or move away for higher pay, so the gap within the region grows (An); so the distribution becomes more unequal, unless benefits rise to compensate (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Income is a flow, wealth is a stock.** Give an example of each, and mention time ("per year" versus "at a point in time").
- **Read the quintile key carefully.** The richest 40% is the fourth fifth plus the richest fifth.
- Shares are in **percentages**; a change in a share is in **percentage points**. A £ answer needs share × total.
- **"How has the distribution changed?"** needs "more equal" or "less equal", not only the numbers.
- Do not confuse **income** with **wealth** in causes: inheritance and house prices affect wealth first, which then creates income.
- In evaluate answers, weigh costs (poverty, lost opportunity, weaker demand) against benefits (incentives, saving) and say which matters more for the economy named.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: In the country of Ostmere, pay for highly skilled workers in finance and technology has risen by 30% over five years, while pay for care workers, cleaners and shop staff has risen by 5%. Over the same period, average prices rose by 12%.

Using the information, analyse how these changes are likely to affect the distribution of income in Ostmere. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — developed chains applied to Ostmere; L2 3–4 — mostly single-step, limited application; L1 1–2 — isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content:
- Knowledge: distribution of income; pay differentials; real and nominal pay.
- Application: +30% for finance and technology; +5% for care, cleaning and shop work; prices +12%.
- Analysis: high earners' pay rises much faster, so the gap in pay widens; prices rose 12%, so low-paid workers' real pay fell (a 5% pay rise is below 12% inflation) while high earners' real pay rose strongly (by roughly 16%), widening the gap further; high earners can save more, earning interest and dividends, adding to their income; low earners may borrow to cover living costs, paying interest; so the share of income going to the richest fifth rises and the distribution becomes more unequal; scarce skills and high demand for finance and technology workers explain the differential.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: In the country of Lavant, the richest 10% of households own 55% of all wealth, mostly property and shares. Many young adults rent and cannot afford a deposit for a home. Lavant's economy has grown steadily, and its technology firms are among the most successful in the region.

Evaluate the consequences of the unequal distribution of wealth for Lavant's economy. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: richest 10% own 55% of wealth; property and shares; young adults cannot afford deposits; steady growth; successful technology firms.
- Analysis: wealth earns rent and dividends, so the rich receive more income, widening income inequality; young adults pay high rents, leaving less to spend or save; poorer households cannot afford education or to start firms, wasting talent; the chance of large rewards may encourage entrepreneurs, helping explain the successful technology firms; wealthy households' savings fund investment.
- Evaluation: the economy is growing, so inequality has not yet stopped growth, but the costs may build up over time if young adults cannot move to where jobs are or start families; depends on whether the wealth was earned through enterprise or inherited; resentment may lead to social tension; the government could use wealth or property taxes to fund housing. Judgement: e.g. the inequality is harming young people's living standards and may limit future growth, so the costs outweigh the benefits in the long run, even if successful firms have benefited so far.
:::
