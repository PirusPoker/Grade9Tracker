## What it is

**International trade** is the buying and selling of goods and services across national borders. **Exports** are goods and services produced in one country and sold to buyers abroad, so money flows **in**. **Imports** are goods and services bought from abroad, so money flows **out**. This topic covers **why countries import and export**, the **benefits of trade for consumers and producers**, and **free trade agreements, including the European Union (EU)**.

### Why countries import and export

No country can produce everything it wants efficiently, because resources are spread unevenly around the world.

| Reason | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Different natural resources** | some countries have oil, metals, fertile land or fishing grounds; others do not | a country with no oil fields must import oil |
| **Climate** | some crops need heat, rain or long growing seasons | the UK imports bananas, coffee and cocoa because they cannot be grown here on a commercial scale |
| **Different skills and labour** | workers in different countries have different skills, and wages differ | a country with many trained engineers exports engineering services |
| **Capital and technology** | some countries have more advanced machinery, factories and research | high-tech medicines or aircraft parts are made where the know-how is |
| **Specialisation** | each country concentrates on what it does best, produces more of it, and **exchanges** the surplus (link to 2.1b) | trade lets total world output rise |
| **Goods that cannot be produced at home** | some products are simply not available domestically | rare minerals, tropical fruit |

Trade is **specialisation and exchange** on an international scale. When countries specialise, they use their resources where they are most productive, gain **economies of scale**, and then swap goods and services. Without trade, each country would have to produce everything itself, much less efficiently.

The UK exports **goods** (such as cars, machinery, medicines and drinks) and a large amount of **services** (such as financial services, insurance, education, consultancy and tourism by overseas visitors). It imports goods it cannot make cheaply, such as food out of season, fuel, clothing and electronics.

### Benefits of trade for consumers

| Benefit | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| **More choice** | consumers can buy products from all over the world, not just those made at home |
| **Lower prices** | goods come from where they are produced most cheaply, and foreign **competition** forces domestic firms to keep prices down |
| **Better quality** | competition pushes firms to improve products to keep customers |
| **Products not otherwise available** | goods that cannot be grown or made at home, such as tropical fruit, can be bought |

### Benefits of trade for producers

| Benefit | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| **A bigger market to sell to** | firms can sell to millions of extra customers abroad, so sales, revenue and profit can rise |
| **Economies of scale** | producing more for a larger market lowers **average cost** per unit |
| **A larger market for buying inputs** | firms can buy raw materials, components and machinery from the cheapest or best supplier anywhere, cutting costs |
| **Spreading risk** | if sales fall in one country, sales elsewhere can make up for it |
| **Competition spurs efficiency** | facing foreign rivals encourages firms to cut costs and innovate |

Watch the difference: **more choice and lower prices** are benefits for **consumers**; **a bigger market to sell in** and **a larger market for buying inputs** are benefits for **producers**.

Trade also has **costs**: domestic firms that cannot compete may close, causing job losses (often structural unemployment in particular regions); a country may become **dependent** on imports of essentials such as food or energy; and transporting goods long distances adds to pollution.

### Barriers to trade (protectionism)

Governments sometimes **protect** domestic industries from foreign competition:
- a **tariff** is a tax on imports, which raises their price;
- a **quota** is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported;
- **regulations and standards** (rules on safety or labelling) and paperwork at the border can make importing harder and more expensive.

Protectionism may save jobs in protected industries in the short run, but consumers pay higher prices and have less choice, firms that use imported inputs face higher costs, and other countries may **retaliate** with their own tariffs, hurting exporters.

**Tariff calculation (invented figures).** A UK shop imports a coat costing £60. A 15% tariff is charged: tariff = £60 × 15 ÷ 100 = **£9**, so the coat now costs the shop **£69** before any profit or VAT is added.

### Free trade agreements

A **free trade agreement (FTA)** is an agreement between two or more countries to **remove or reduce barriers to trade**, such as tariffs and quotas, on goods (and sometimes services) traded between them. Some agreements also make rules and standards more alike so that firms face less paperwork. Each member still sets its own tariffs on goods from countries **outside** the agreement.

An example is the agreement between the **USA, Canada and Mexico**, which allows most goods to be traded between the three countries without tariffs. The UK has trade agreements with many countries.

**Effects of joining a free trade agreement:**
- **Producers**: exporters can sell to members without tariffs, so their goods are cheaper abroad and sales rise; cheaper imported inputs cut costs; but they face **more competition** from firms in member countries, and less efficient firms may lose sales or close.
- **Consumers**: lower prices and more choice as tariff-free imports arrive.
- **Workers**: new jobs in exporting industries; job losses in industries that cannot compete (structural unemployment).
- **Government**: loses tariff revenue on imports from member countries.

### The European Union

The **European Union (EU)** is a group of European countries that goes further than a free trade agreement:
- a **single market** with **free movement of goods, services, capital and people** between members — there are no tariffs or quotas between members, and products made to EU rules can be sold anywhere in it;
- a **customs union**: all members charge the **same tariffs** on goods coming in from outside the EU (a common external tariff), and negotiate trade deals as one group;
- many (but not all) members also share a single currency, the **euro**.

The UK was a member of the EU until it **left in 2020**. The UK and the EU now trade under a **trade agreement** that allows most goods to cross without tariffs or quotas, but because the UK is outside the single market and customs union, firms face **more paperwork and border checks** than before, and the free movement of people between the UK and EU has ended. The EU remains one of the UK's most important trading partners because it is large and nearby.

**Evaluating free trade agreements**: the gains depend on how big the partner market is, how competitive domestic firms are, how many workers can move from shrinking to growing industries, and whether lower prices for consumers outweigh job losses in particular areas.

::: terms
- **International trade**: the exchange of goods and services between countries.
- **Export**: a good or service produced in one country and sold to buyers in another, bringing money into the country.
- **Import**: a good or service bought from another country, so money flows out of the country.
- **Specialisation**: when a country concentrates on producing the goods and services it is best at producing.
- **Free trade**: trade between countries without barriers such as tariffs or quotas.
- **Tariff**: a tax charged on imported goods, which raises their price.
- **Quota**: a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported in a period of time.
- **Protectionism**: government measures, such as tariffs and quotas, that restrict imports to protect domestic producers.
- **Free trade agreement**: an agreement between countries to remove or reduce barriers to trade, such as tariffs and quotas, between them.
- **Single market**: an area in which goods, services, capital and people can move freely between member countries.
- **Customs union**: a group of countries that trade freely with each other and charge the same tariffs on imports from outside the group.
- **European Union**: a group of European countries with a single market and customs union; the UK left it in 2020.
:::

::: method
1. **"Explain one benefit of trade for consumers/producers" (2)**: name the benefit for the group asked about (1) + say why or what follows, using the context (1). Do not give a producer benefit when the question says consumers.
2. **"Explain what is meant by a free trade agreement" (2)**: an agreement between countries (1) + to remove or reduce barriers such as tariffs and quotas on trade between them (1).
3. **Calculations (2)**: tariff = price × tariff rate ÷ 100; share of trade = part ÷ total × 100; % change = change ÷ original × 100. Show working and give £ or %.
4. **Analyse (6)**: chains from tariffs removed → lower prices of exports abroad / imports at home → sales, output, jobs, competition, costs. Use the figures given. No evaluation.
5. **Evaluate (6)**: weigh gains (bigger market, cheaper inputs, lower prices) against costs (more competition, job losses in some industries, loss of tariff revenue). Pair each point: "…however, this depends on…". Judge for the group named in the question.
:::

::: example Why a country imports
Explain one reason why the UK imports coffee. (2)

Coffee needs a hot climate to grow, which the UK does not have (1), so it cannot be produced in the UK on a commercial scale and must be bought from countries where it grows well (1).
:::

::: example Benefit for producers
Explain one benefit of international trade for producers. (2)

Producers can sell to a much larger market abroad (1), so they can produce on a larger scale, gaining economies of scale that lower their average cost and raise profit (1).
:::

::: example Free trade agreement
Explain what is meant by a free trade agreement. (2)

An agreement between two or more countries (1) to remove or reduce barriers to trade, such as tariffs and quotas, on goods and services traded between them (1).
:::

::: example Tariff calculation
Invented figures: a firm imports electric scooters costing £320 each. The government charges a 20% tariff. Calculate the price of each scooter including the tariff. Show your working. (2)

Tariff = £320 × 20 ÷ 100 = £64 (1); price including tariff = £320 + £64 = **£384** (1).
:::

::: example Analyse — joining a free trade agreement
Invented context: the country of Velmar, which makes furniture and machinery, has joined a free trade agreement with five neighbours. Before joining, Velmar's exports to these countries faced a 10% tariff. Analyse how joining the agreement could affect Velmar's producers. (6)

A free trade agreement removes tariffs and other barriers to trade between members (K). With the 10% tariff gone, Velmar's furniture and machinery become cheaper for buyers in the five neighbouring countries, so demand for its exports rises (Ap/An). Higher sales mean firms expand output, which lets them gain economies of scale, so average costs fall and profit rises (An). Velmar's producers can also import components and materials tariff-free from the five neighbours, cutting their costs of production further (An). However, firms in the neighbouring countries can now also sell in Velmar without tariffs, so Velmar's less efficient producers face more competition and may lose sales, which pushes them to cut costs or improve quality to survive (An).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit of international trade for consumers. (2)
---
More choice / lower prices / better quality / products not available at home (1) + development, e.g. foreign competition forces domestic firms to keep prices down, so consumers' money buys more (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a tariff. (2)
---
A tax on imported goods (1), which raises their price and so protects domestic producers from foreign competition / raises revenue for the government (1).
:::

::: q
Invented figures: in a year, the country of Orvia exported £64 billion of goods, of which £40 billion went to members of its free trade agreement. Calculate the percentage of Orvia's exports that went to members of the agreement. Show your working. (2)
---
£40bn ÷ £64bn × 100 (1) = **62.5%** (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one difference between a free trade agreement and the European Union's single market. (2)
---
A free trade agreement only removes tariffs and quotas on goods (and some services) between members (1), whereas the EU single market also allows free movement of services, capital and people and has a common external tariff on imports from outside (1).
:::

::: q
Analyse how removing tariffs on imported food could affect consumers and farmers in a country. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 developed chains; L2 3–4 mostly single-step; L1 1–2 isolated point. No evaluation marks.
Indicative content: a tariff is a tax on imports (K); removing it lowers the price of imported food, so consumers pay less and their real incomes go further (An); more imported food widens choice (An); domestic farmers face cheaper foreign competition, so they sell less or must cut their prices, reducing revenue and profit (An); less efficient farms may close, causing job losses in rural areas (An); farmers may respond by improving efficiency or specialising in high-quality produce (An).
:::

::: watch
- **Match the benefit to the group**: lower prices and more choice are for **consumers**; a bigger market to sell in and a larger market for buying inputs are for **producers**.
- **A free trade agreement removes or reduces tariffs and quotas between its members.** It is not a single currency, and it is not the same as the EU's single market.
- **Explain, don't list**: "more choice" earns 1 mark; saying why it happens or what it means for the consumer earns the second.
- **A tariff is a tax on imports**, not on exports and not a limit on quantity (that is a quota).
- **Keep EU facts general and accurate**: the UK left in 2020 and now trades with the EU under a trade agreement.
- **In evaluation, pair each benefit with a "but" about the same point** (cheaper imports help consumers, but the same competition can close domestic firms), then judge for the group named.
- **Show working and units** in tariff and percentage calculations.
:::

::: test
Invented extract: The UK government is considering a new free trade agreement with the country of Ostrava. Ostrava has a population of 120 million and fast-growing demand for financial services and education. It also has a large clothing industry where wages are much lower than in the UK. Evaluate whether the free trade agreement would benefit UK workers. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.

- Application: 120 million population; growing demand for financial services and education; low-wage clothing industry; tariffs of up to 12% removed.

::: test
Invented extract: The country of Brennick left a large trading bloc similar to the EU three years ago and now trades with it under a free trade agreement. Tariffs are still zero, but Brennick's firms report that exporting to the bloc now involves customs paperwork and border checks, adding about 5% to their costs. Brennick has since signed new trade agreements with several distant countries.

Evaluate the extent to which leaving the trading bloc is likely to affect Brennick's producers. Use the information given and your own knowledge. (6)
---
Levels: L3 5–6 — good application, reasonable analysis and good evaluation, with a fully supported judgement that addresses "extent"; L2 3–4 — some application and analysis, judgement not fully supported; L1 1–2 — unsupported statements. No knowledge marks.
Indicative content:
- Application: three years ago; free trade agreement with zero tariffs; paperwork and border checks adding about 5% to costs; new agreements with distant countries.
- Analysis: higher costs of exporting cut exporters' profit margins or force them to raise prices, so sales to the bloc fall; small firms may stop exporting because paperwork costs are hard to spread over few sales; delays at the border matter for perishable goods or parts that must arrive on time; new agreements open other markets, giving firms new customers.
- Evaluation: because tariffs are still zero, the effect is smaller than if tariffs had returned; a 5% cost rise matters more to firms with low profit margins or price elastic demand; large firms can absorb the cost more easily than small ones; new agreements with distant countries may not replace a large, nearby market because transport costs are higher; the impact may fade as firms adapt to the paperwork. Judgement: e.g. producers are moderately harmed rather than seriously harmed, mostly small exporters to the bloc, because zero tariffs limit the damage but new markets are unlikely to make up for higher costs with a large neighbour in the short run.
:::
