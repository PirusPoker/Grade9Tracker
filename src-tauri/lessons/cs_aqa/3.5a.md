## What it is

A **computer network** is **two or more computers or devices connected together** so that they can **communicate** and **share data and resources**. Your home Wi-Fi, a school's computer system and the Internet are all networks.

### Advantages and disadvantages of networks

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Share files and data**: users can work on the same documents and access them from any connected computer | **Cost**: switches, routers, cabling, servers and installation must be paid for |
| **Share hardware**: one printer or scanner can serve many users | **Expertise**: a larger network needs a network manager to set it up and maintain it |
| **Share an Internet connection** between all devices | **Security risks**: malware can **spread** from one computer to others, and attackers may gain access to data across the network |
| **Communication**: email, messaging and video calls between users | **Dependence**: if a key device such as a server or router fails, many users may be unable to work |
| **Central management**: software can be installed and updated, users managed and data **backed up** centrally | **Performance**: heavy traffic can slow the network for everyone |
| Users can **log in from any computer** and find their own files | |

### Types of network

| Type | Area | Ownership | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal Area Network (PAN)** | a few metres around **one person** | the individual | a phone connected by **Bluetooth** to wireless earbuds, a smartwatch and a car's audio system |
| **Local Area Network (LAN)** | a **relatively small geographical area**: one home, building or site | usually **owned and managed by a single person or organisation**, which owns the cables, switches and wireless access points | a school's network across its buildings; a home network |
| **Wide Area Network (WAN)** | a **wide geographical area**: across towns, countries or continents | often **collective or distributed ownership**: it uses communication links owned by many different organisations, such as telecommunications companies | a bank connecting its branches; **the Internet, the biggest WAN** |

For a PAN you only need to know about **Bluetooth**: a short-range wireless technology for connecting personal devices.

**What makes a LAN a LAN** is the **small area** and **single ownership**: not the number of devices, and not whether it is wired or wireless. A large school with 1,000 computers on one site is still a LAN.

### Wired and wireless networks

Networks can be **wired** (devices connected by cables) or **wireless** (devices connected by radio waves). Many LANs use both.

| | **Wired** | **Wireless** |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed** | usually **faster** and more consistent | usually slower; speed drops with distance and obstacles such as walls |
| **Reliability** | **reliable**; little interference | affected by **interference** from other devices and networks, and by walls |
| **Security** | data can only be intercepted with **physical access** to the cables | signals travel through the air and **can be intercepted** by anyone in range, so encryption is essential |
| **Mobility** | devices are tied to where the cables are | users can **move around** freely with laptops and phones |
| **Installation** | cables must be bought and laid, which is costly and disruptive, especially in old buildings | **easy and cheap** to add devices; no cables to run |
| **Range** | cables can be long; signals can be boosted | limited range from each access point |

**Types of cable.** Wired networks use **copper** or **fibre optic** cable.

| | **Copper cable** | **Fibre optic cable** |
|---|---|---|
| **How it works** | electrical signals along copper wires | **pulses of light** along thin strands of glass or plastic |
| **Speed (bandwidth)** | good, but lower | **very high** |
| **Distance** | signal weakens over long distances | carries data **long distances** with little loss |
| **Interference** | affected by electrical interference | **not affected** by electrical interference |
| **Cost and installation** | **cheaper**, flexible, easy to install and connect | **more expensive**; installing and joining it needs specialist skills |
| **When to use** | connecting individual computers, printers and access points within a room or building | links between buildings, a network's main backbone, long-distance and WAN links, and anywhere very high bandwidth is needed |

### Network security

**Why it is needed.** Networks carry and store valuable data: personal details, financial records, business secrets. Without security, attackers could **steal or change data**, **spread malware** to every connected computer, **disrupt** the network so nobody can work, or use it to attack others. Organisations also have a **legal duty** to protect the personal data they hold, and a breach can cost them money and **reputation**.

The spec names four methods.

| Method | What it is and how it works | Example of use |
|---|---|---|
| **Authentication** | checking that a user (or device) **is who they claim to be** before allowing access: something they **know** (a password or PIN), something they **have** (a phone receiving a code) or something they **are** (a fingerprint). Using two of these is **two-factor authentication**. | staff log in to the office network with a username and password, plus a code sent to their phone |
| **Encryption** | scrambling data with an algorithm and a **key** so that, if it is intercepted, it **cannot be understood** without the key; the intended recipient uses the key to decrypt it | a wireless network encrypts all its traffic; a website uses HTTPS (3.5b); a laptop's drive is encrypted |
| **Firewall** | a network security device (hardware or software) that **monitors incoming and outgoing network traffic** and **allows or blocks** each piece of traffic according to a set of **security rules** | blocking incoming connections from the Internet that nobody inside requested; blocking traffic to known malicious sites |
| **MAC address filtering** | every network adapter has a unique **MAC address**, its physical address, embedded in it. The router or access point keeps a list and **allows** only listed devices to connect (or **blocks** listed ones). | a small office's wireless network only accepts the company's own laptops and phones |

**Limits.** No single method is perfect: passwords can be guessed or phished; encryption does not stop data being deleted; a firewall cannot stop a user opening a malicious email attachment that has already been let through; MAC addresses can be **faked (spoofed)** by a skilled attacker, and lists take effort to maintain.

**Working together (layered security).** Each method blocks a different route of attack, so combining them gives a much greater level of security:
- the **firewall** stops unwanted traffic from the Internet reaching the network;
- **MAC address filtering** stops unknown devices joining the network at all;
- **authentication** makes sure that people using allowed devices are authorised users;
- **encryption** means that even if data is intercepted or a device is stolen, it is unreadable.

An attacker would have to beat every layer, not just one.

::: terms
- **Computer network**: two or more computers or devices connected together so that they can communicate and share data and resources.
- **PAN**: a personal area network connecting devices around one person, for example by Bluetooth.
- **LAN**: a local area network covering a relatively small area, usually owned and managed by one person or organisation.
- **WAN**: a wide area network covering a large geographical area, often under collective or distributed ownership; the Internet is the largest.
- **Bluetooth**: a short-range wireless technology used to connect personal devices in a PAN.
- **Fibre optic cable**: cable that carries data as pulses of light, giving very high bandwidth over long distances without electrical interference.
- **Authentication**: checking that a user or device is who or what it claims to be, e.g. with a password or fingerprint.
- **Encryption**: scrambling data with a key so that it cannot be understood if intercepted, unless the key is used to decrypt it.
- **Firewall**: a network security device that monitors incoming and outgoing traffic and allows or blocks it according to security rules.
- **MAC address filtering**: allowing or blocking devices from a network based on the physical MAC address of their network adapter.
:::

::: method
1. **Define a network**: two or more devices **connected** so that they can **communicate/share** resources.
2. **Network types**: for each, give the **area**, the **ownership**, and an **example**. PAN = Bluetooth.
3. **Wired vs wireless / copper vs fibre**: make **two-sided** comparisons and link them to the scenario (mobility, speed, distance, cost, interference).
4. **Security methods**: say **what it is**, **how it works**, and **an example of when it would be used**.
5. **Combining methods**: explain **which threat each one stops**, and why together they are stronger.
6. **6-mark recommendations**: three developed points applied to the organisation in the question.
7. **9-mark discuss**: cover every bullet, apply throughout, give a balanced judgement.
:::

::: example Define and type
Describe two differences between a PAN and a WAN. (2)

A PAN covers a very small area around one person, whereas a WAN covers a wide geographical area such as several countries (1).

A PAN is owned by the individual, whereas a WAN is usually under collective/distributed ownership, using links owned by many organisations (1).
:::

::: example Wired or wireless
A café wants to offer customers Internet access. Explain why it should use a wireless network rather than a wired one. (2)

Customers can connect their own phones and laptops anywhere in the café without needing a cable or socket (1); it is also cheaper and easier to install, as no cables need to be laid to every table (1).
:::

::: example Fibre or copper
A university is connecting two buildings 800 metres apart. Explain why it should use fibre optic cable for this link. (2)

Fibre carries data over long distances with little loss of signal (1) and has a very high bandwidth, so it can carry the traffic of everyone in both buildings without slowing down (1).
:::

::: example MAC address filtering
Explain how MAC address filtering helps protect a wireless network. (2)

The router keeps a list of the MAC addresses of approved devices (1); when a device tries to connect, its MAC address is checked and it is only allowed on if it is on the list, so unknown devices are blocked (1).
:::

::: example Combining methods
A dentist's surgery has a LAN storing patient records. Explain how a firewall and encryption together protect the records. (4)

The firewall monitors incoming traffic from the Internet and blocks anything that does not meet its rules (1), so attackers outside the surgery cannot easily connect to the computers holding the records (1).

Encryption scrambles the records using a key (1), so if they are intercepted on the wireless network, or a laptop is stolen, they cannot be read without the key (1).
:::

::: test
A small accountancy firm has a LAN with a wireless network and an Internet connection. Explain how authentication, encryption, a firewall and MAC address filtering could be used together to protect its clients' financial data. (6)
---
Award up to 6 for points applied to the firm, max 2 per method, and credit explanation of how they work together.
- Firewall: monitors traffic between the Internet and the LAN and blocks traffic that breaks its rules (1), so outside attackers cannot easily connect to the firm's computers (1).
- MAC address filtering: only the firm's own devices, whose MAC addresses are on the router's list, can join the wireless network (1), so a stranger in range cannot connect their laptop (1).

- Together: each method covers a weakness of the others (e.g. spoofed MAC addresses are still stopped by authentication), so an attacker would need to defeat several layers (1).

---
