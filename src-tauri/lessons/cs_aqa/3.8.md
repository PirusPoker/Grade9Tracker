## What it is

This topic is about the **effects of digital technology on society**: who gains, who loses, what the law has to deal with, and what it costs the planet. It is examined in Paper 2, almost always as a **9-mark "Discuss" question** with three bullet points, sometimes with shorter 2–4 mark explains.

The spec names the **areas** questions are taken from:

| Area | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Cyber security** | protecting systems and data from attack, and the effects when that fails |
| **Mobile technologies** | smartphones, tablets, apps, location services, mobile payments |
| **Wireless networking** | Wi-Fi, mobile data and public hotspots, connecting devices without cables |
| **Cloud storage** | keeping files and backups on a provider's servers, reached over the internet |
| **Hacking** | **unauthorised access** to a computer system |
| **Wearable technologies** | smartwatches, fitness trackers, health monitors, smart glasses |
| **Computer-based implants** | devices placed inside the body, such as pacemakers, hearing implants or chips |
| **Autonomous vehicles** | cars, lorries and drones that drive or fly themselves using sensors and software |

You need the **general principles** behind the issues, not details of particular news stories or named laws. The examiners reward clear, explained points **applied to the technology in the question**.

### The four lenses

For any technology, think through four kinds of impact. Every 9-mark answer is built from them.

**1. Ethical issues: is it right or fair?**
- **Who benefits and who is harmed?** Convenience, safety, health and new opportunities against job losses, exclusion and harm.
- **Consent**: do people know what is happening and agree to it, for example data being collected or shared?
- **Surveillance and control**: employers, companies or governments tracking what people do.
- **The digital divide**: people without devices, connection or skills (often older, poorer or rural) are left behind as services move online.
- **Wellbeing**: screen time, addiction to apps designed to hold attention, cyberbullying, pressure to be always available.
- **Responsibility**: when software makes a decision that harms someone, who is to blame?
- **Bias**: decisions made by software can be unfair if they are built on unfair data.

**2. Legal issues: what does the law have to deal with?** You don't need the names of particular laws; explain the principles:
- **Unauthorised access** to computer systems (hacking) and spreading malware are **crimes**.
- **Data protection**: organisations that hold personal data must have a lawful reason, keep it **secure and accurate**, use it only for the stated purpose, not keep it longer than needed, and let people **see** what is held about them. A data breach can lead to fines.
- **Copyright and intellectual property**: copying software, music or films without permission is illegal, and easy to do with digital technology.
- **Liability**: who is legally responsible when technology causes harm, such as a self-driving car crash or a faulty implant?
- **Jurisdiction**: data stored in the cloud may sit in **another country**, under that country's laws, and criminals may attack from abroad, so it is hard to prosecute them.
- **Laws lag behind technology**: new devices create situations the law did not foresee.

**3. Environmental issues: what does it cost the planet?**
- **Energy**: data centres, networks and the devices themselves use large amounts of electricity, and data centres need **cooling**, often using water.
- **Raw materials**: devices contain metals that must be **mined**, which damages landscapes and uses energy.
- **E-waste**: phones and gadgets are replaced often, and discarded electronics contain **toxic** substances that can pollute land and water if not recycled properly.
- **Positive effects**: video calls and online working cut **travel**; cloud storage replaces paper; smart systems can **reduce** energy use; shared data centres can be more efficient than many separate servers; autonomous vehicles might drive more efficiently.

**4. Privacy: who can see data about me?**
- Devices collect **personal data** constantly: location, contacts, health, browsing, voice.
- People often **don't read** the terms and don't know what is collected or who it is **shared with or sold to**.
- Data held by companies can be **stolen** in a breach and used for identity theft or fraud.
- Combined data can reveal a lot: where you live and work, your habits, your health.

### Privacy against security

The spec singles out one debate you must be able to argue **both ways**.

**Citizens value their privacy.** Most people do not want governments or security services reading their messages, tracking their movements or keeping records of their internet use. Their concerns:
- privacy is a basic **right**; innocent people should not be watched;
- powers can be **misused**, for example against journalists, protesters or political opponents;
- collected data can be **leaked or hacked**;
- if messaging apps have to build in a "**backdoor**" for the authorities, criminals may find and use it too, making everyone less secure.

**Governments and security services argue they need access.** They say they cannot keep citizens safe from **terrorism**, serious crime and other attacks unless they can see private data:
- criminals use **encrypted** messaging to plan attacks, and investigators need to read it;
- data from phones, networks and cloud accounts can **prevent** an attack or **solve** a crime;
- "if you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear".

**A balanced conclusion** usually says access should be **targeted** (a suspect, not everyone), **approved** by an independent body such as a court, and **overseen**, so that safety and privacy are both protected. Either side can be argued, as long as it is reasoned.

### The areas, one by one

**Cyber security**
- Benefits: protects people's money, identities and organisations' data; allows trust in online banking and shopping.
- Issues: attacks such as ransomware can shut down hospitals or businesses; organisations have an **ethical and legal duty** to protect the personal data they hold; strong security can be inconvenient; monitoring staff for security reasons raises privacy concerns; the encryption that protects users also hides criminals (the privacy against security debate).

**Mobile technologies**
- Benefits: communication anywhere, mobile banking and payments, maps, help in emergencies, education and work on the move.
- Issues: apps collect **location** and contacts, often shared with advertisers; **lost or stolen** phones expose data; addiction, distraction (for example while driving) and cyberbullying; people without smartphones are excluded as services become app-only; frequent **upgrades** create e-waste and demand for mined metals.

**Wireless networking**
- Benefits: connection without cables, public Wi-Fi for everyone, connecting many devices in a home or school.
- Issues: data sent by radio can be **intercepted** if not encrypted; open public hotspots are risky and can be **fake**; using someone's network without permission is **unauthorised access**; poor coverage in rural areas widens the digital divide.

**Cloud storage**
- Benefits: access files from any device; automatic **backup**, so data survives a lost or broken device; easy sharing and collaboration; organisations don't need their own servers.
- Issues: the user **no longer controls** where the data is or who can reach it; the provider could be **hacked**, read the data, or share it; data may be held in **another country** under its laws (jurisdiction); needs an internet connection; reliance on the provider staying in business; data centres use large amounts of **energy** and water.

**Hacking (unauthorised access)**
- Hacking without permission is **illegal**, whatever the motive.
- Harms: theft of personal and financial data, fraud, blackmail, damage to systems, loss of trust; organisations may be fined if poor security let it happen.
- Debates: **ethical hackers** are paid, with permission, to find weaknesses (penetration testing), which is legal and useful; people who hack "to expose a problem" without permission are still breaking the law.

**Wearable technologies**
- Benefits: track fitness and sleep, motivate healthy habits, warn of health problems early, help doctors monitor patients remotely, help elderly people call for help.
- Issues: highly **sensitive health and location data** is collected; users may not know it is shared, for example with insurers or advertisers, who might use it to set prices; employers could use wearables to **monitor staff**; data can be stolen; batteries and short product lives add to e-waste; constant tracking can cause anxiety.

**Computer-based implants**
- Benefits: medical implants keep people alive or restore abilities, such as pacemakers that regulate the heart, hearing implants, and devices that monitor or treat conditions; implanted chips can be used for identification or contactless access.
- Issues: implants that communicate wirelessly could in principle be **hacked**, with life-threatening results, so security is critical; who **owns** and can see the data they produce; **consent** (for example if an employer encouraged chipping); updating or removing them is difficult; **liability** if the software fails; ethical questions about **enhancing** healthy people beyond normal ability, and fairness if only the rich can afford it.

**Autonomous vehicles**
- Benefits: could reduce accidents caused by **human error** (tiredness, distraction, drink); mobility for people who cannot drive; smoother driving that saves fuel; delivery without drivers.
- Issues: **liability** when one crashes: the owner, the manufacturer or the software developer?; **ethical decisions** programmed in advance, such as how to act when a collision cannot be avoided; job losses for drivers; vehicles could be **hacked** and controlled remotely; they collect location data about passengers; sensors can fail in bad weather; laws and insurance have to be rewritten.

### Writing the 9-mark Discuss

AQA marks these in three levels:
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: a well-developed discussion of **every** bullet, logically structured, with a range of relevant points.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: explanations of some points, but at least one bullet thin or missing.
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: statements or brief descriptions, with little explanation.

What lifts an answer to Level 3:
- **Cover every bullet**, roughly equally. A brilliant answer on two of three bullets cannot reach the top.
- **Explain**, don't list: state the point, then say **why** it matters or what it leads to ("…so…", "…which means…").
- **Apply it** to the technology and people in the question. Generic points about "computers" earn little; examiners credit only points relevant to the named technology.
- **Show both sides** where the bullet allows (a benefit and a risk), and finish with a short **judgement**.
- Use the key vocabulary: personal data, consent, unauthorised access, data protection, liability, e-waste, energy, digital divide.

A strong paragraph: "Fitness trackers record users' heart rate and location around the clock. **This is personal data**, and many users accept the terms without reading them, **so** they may not know it is being shared with advertisers or insurers, **who could** use it to raise their premiums. **This means** the user loses control over very sensitive information."

::: terms
- **Ethical issue**: a question of whether something is right, fair or harmful, such as surveillance, job losses or exclusion.
- **Legal issue**: a matter the law must deal with, such as unauthorised access, data protection, copyright or liability.
- **Environmental issue**: an effect on the planet, such as energy use by data centres, mining for raw materials or e-waste.
- **Data privacy**: people's right to control what personal data is collected about them and who can see or use it.
- **Personal data**: information that identifies a living person or relates to them, such as name, location or health records.
- **Unauthorised access**: using a computer system or data without permission, which is what hacking means in this spec.
- **Data protection**: legal rules that organisations must follow when they collect, store and use personal data.
- **Liability**: legal responsibility for harm or loss, for example deciding who is to blame when a driverless car crashes.
- **E-waste**: discarded electronic devices, which contain toxic materials and valuable metals and need proper recycling.
- **Digital divide**: the gap between people who have access to digital technology, connections and skills and those who don't.
- **Surveillance**: monitoring people's communications, movements or activities, by governments, employers or companies.
- **Backdoor**: a hidden way for authorities to get past encryption or security, which criminals could also exploit.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the stem**: name the technology and the people involved (users, companies, government, employees).
2. **Take each bullet in turn**, in its own paragraph, and give it a fair share of the time.
3. For each point, use **point → explanation → consequence**, applied to the named technology.
4. Run through the **four lenses**: ethical, legal (principles, not law names), environmental, privacy. Use the ones the bullets ask for.
5. Where the bullet allows, give **both sides**: a benefit and a risk, or citizens' view and government's view.
6. End with a short **judgement** that weighs the sides.
7. For 2–4 mark explains: one point per mark pair, each **explained**, applied to the context.
:::

::: example Environmental impact
Explain two environmental impacts of the increasing use of cloud storage. (4)

Cloud data is held in large data centres that run all the time and use a lot of electricity (1), much of which may be generated from fossil fuels, adding to carbon emissions (1). Data centres must be cooled to stop servers overheating (1), which can use large amounts of water and more energy (1).

Accept a positive impact, for example: storing documents in the cloud replaces paper and filing (1), reducing the trees and transport used (1).
:::

::: example Privacy against security
Explain why governments may want access to people's encrypted messages, and why many citizens object. (4)

Governments argue that terrorists and criminals use encrypted messaging to plan attacks (1), so the security services need to read them to prevent attacks and keep citizens safe (1). Citizens value their privacy and do not want innocent people's messages read (1); if a backdoor is built in for government, criminals might find and use it, making everyone's data less secure (1).
:::

::: example Autonomous vehicles and the law
Explain one legal issue raised by autonomous vehicles. (2)

If a self-driving car causes a crash, it is unclear who is legally responsible (liable): the passenger, the manufacturer or the software company (1), because no human was driving, so existing laws and insurance rules have to be changed (1).
:::

::: example Wearable data
Describe two ways the data from a fitness tracker could be misused. (2)

It could be sold or shared with insurance companies, who might raise prices for less healthy users (1). It could be stolen in a data breach, revealing the user's location patterns, such as when they are away from home (1).
:::

::: example Ethics of hacking
A student says hacking is acceptable if the hacker only wants to show that a company's security is weak. Explain why this is still a problem. (3)

Accessing a computer system without permission is illegal, whatever the motive (1). The hacker may see or damage personal data they have no right to, breaching other people's privacy (1). The legal way is ethical hacking/penetration testing, done with the owner's permission (1).
:::

State two environmental impacts of the manufacture and disposal of mobile phones. (2)

Any two of: mining raw materials/metals damages the environment (1); manufacturing uses energy (1); e-waste/toxic materials pollute land or water if not recycled (1); short replacement cycles increase waste (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by the digital divide, with an example. (2)
---
The gap between people who have access to digital technology/the internet/skills and those who do not (1); e.g. older people or those in rural areas with poor connections who cannot use app-only services (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one risk to privacy of using public Wi-Fi in a café. (2)
---
Data sent over an open/unencrypted network could be intercepted by someone else on the network (1), revealing passwords or personal messages (1). Or: a fake hotspot set up by a criminal could capture everything the user sends (1)(1).
:::

::: q
Explain one ethical issue with a company giving its delivery drivers wearable devices that track their location and heart rate. (2)
---
It is a form of surveillance/invasion of privacy (1); drivers may feel pressured to consent to share personal health data in order to keep their job (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one benefit and one risk of computer-based medical implants. (4)
---
Benefit: an implant such as a pacemaker can keep a patient alive / restore hearing (1), improving quality of life without constant hospital visits (1). Risk: an implant that communicates wirelessly could be hacked (1), which could put the patient's life at risk / expose medical data (1).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why storing personal files in the cloud raises legal issues. (2)
---
Any two of: the data may be stored in another country, under different laws (1); the provider must follow data protection rules, and a breach may break them (1); unclear who is responsible if data is lost (1); copyright issues when files are shared (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Cover every bullet.** The level descriptors need all of them; one missing bullet usually caps the answer in Level 2.
- **Explain and apply.** "It is bad for privacy" scores little. Say **what data**, **who** might get it, and **what could happen**.
- Stay on the **named technology**. Only points about it are credited.
- Don't bluff **law names** or dates. Explain the principle: unauthorised access is illegal; personal data must be kept secure and used only with a lawful reason.
- Environmental means **the physical environment**: energy, materials, waste. Not "the online environment".
- For privacy against security, give **both** the citizens' view and the government's view.
- Finish with a **judgement**; it shows a discussion, not just a list.
:::

Discuss the:
- benefits to the town and its residents of the free Wi-Fi;
- privacy and security issues for people who use it;
- ethical and legal issues raised by collecting data about people's movements. (9)
---
Level 3 (7–9): well-developed discussion of all three bullets, applied to public Wi-Fi in a town; logically structured with a range of relevant points and a judgement.
Level 2 (4–6): explanations of some points, but one bullet thin or missing, or points not always applied.
Level 1 (1–3): statements or brief descriptions of some benefits or issues; little structure.
0: nothing worthy of credit. Only points relevant to the scenario are creditworthy.
Indicative content:
- Benefits: free access for people who cannot afford mobile data (reduces digital divide); visitors and tourists can find information, maps, shops; businesses attract customers; access to public services online; movement data helps plan transport, crowds and safety.
- Privacy and security: an open network may be unencrypted, so data could be intercepted; criminals could set up fake hotspots with similar names; users may not understand the risks; login may require email/phone details, which must be kept secure; advice to use secure websites; the council could see which sites users visit.
- Ethical and legal: tracking movements is surveillance; consent must be clear and informed, and people may not read the terms; data protection principles: lawful reason, only data needed, kept secure, deleted when no longer needed, anonymised where possible; risk of data being shared with police or companies; people may feel watched; breach would harm residents.
- Judgement: e.g. benefits are real if data is anonymised, consent is clear and the network is secured; otherwise privacy costs may outweigh them.
:::
