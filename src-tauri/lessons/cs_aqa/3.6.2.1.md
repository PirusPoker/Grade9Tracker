## What it is

**Social engineering** is the art of **manipulating people** so that they **give up confidential information** (or do something that helps an attacker, such as clicking a link or opening a door).

It targets **people rather than technology**. Firewalls, encryption and anti-malware are hard to break, but a helpful, busy or worried person can often be persuaded to hand over a password. The attacker exploits ordinary human traits:
- **trust** in someone who seems to be from IT, the bank or the boss;
- **helpfulness**, especially towards someone who sounds stressed;
- **fear and urgency** ("your account will be closed in 24 hours");
- **curiosity and greed** ("you have won a prize");
- **carelessness**, such as typing a PIN where others can see.

Because the weakness is human, the main protection is also human: **training, awareness and clear procedures**. Technology helps (spam filters, two-factor authentication), but installing antivirus does nothing to stop someone reading your PIN over your shoulder.

The spec names three forms.

### Blagging (pretexting)

**Blagging** is **inventing a scenario** (a **pretext**) and using it to engage a chosen victim, so that they are more likely to **reveal information or do something they normally would not**.

The attacker plays a role, usually by phone or in person, and often researches the target first so the story is believable.

Examples:
- a caller claims to be from the company's IT support, says there is a problem with the employee's account, and asks for their password "to fix it";
- someone phones a receptionist pretending to be a new manager who has forgotten the door code;
- a caller poses as a bank's fraud team and asks a customer to "confirm" their security details;
- a person in a delivery uniform asks to be let into a secure area.

**Protection against blagging:**
- **staff training**: legitimate IT staff and banks **never ask for your password**;
- **procedures to verify identity**: call the person back on a number from the company directory, not the one they give; check ID badges;
- a policy that certain information is **never given out by phone**;
- a culture where staff feel able to say no and report suspicious calls, even to someone claiming to be senior.

### Phishing

**Phishing** is a technique for **fraudulently obtaining private information**, usually by **email or SMS (text message)**. The message pretends to come from a trusted organisation and tries to get the victim to **click a link** to a fake site and enter details, **open an attachment** (often malware), or **reply** with information.

It is usually sent to **huge numbers of people** at once; the attacker only needs a few to fall for it. A version aimed at one specific, researched person is sometimes called spear phishing.

**Signs of a phishing message:**
- a **generic greeting** ("Dear customer") rather than your name;
- **urgency or threats** ("act now or your account will be suspended");
- a sender address or link that **does not match** the real organisation, perhaps one letter different;
- a request for **personal details, passwords or payment**;
- **spelling and grammar errors** or unusual formatting;
- an **unexpected attachment** or a prize you did not enter for.

**Protection against phishing:**
- **training** users to spot the signs, sometimes with **fake phishing tests** run by the organisation;
- **email filters** that detect and quarantine likely phishing;
- never clicking links in messages: **type the address yourself** or use the official app;
- **two-factor authentication**, so a stolen password alone is not enough;
- anti-malware software to catch harmful attachments;
- a quick way to **report** suspicious messages.

### Shouldering (shoulder surfing)

**Shouldering** is **observing a person's private information over their shoulder**: for example, watching someone type their **PIN at a cashpoint** or their **password** on a laptop on a train. It can also be done from a distance, for example with a camera.

**Protection against shouldering:**
- **shielding the keypad** with your hand when entering a PIN;
- standing close to the machine and being aware of who is nearby;
- **privacy screens** (filters that make a laptop screen hard to read from the side);
- passwords shown as dots or asterisks, not in plain text;
- **biometrics** (fingerprint or face), because there is nothing for an observer to watch;
- positioning reception desks and screens so the public cannot see them.

### Comparing the three

| | **Blagging** | **Phishing** | **Shouldering** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **How** | invented scenario, played out by phone or in person | fake email or text message | watching someone enter information |
| **Target** | usually one chosen person | usually many people at once | whoever is nearby |
| **Gets** | information or an action, e.g. a password or entry to a building | login details, card numbers, or malware installed | PINs and passwords |
| **Key protection** | verify identity; never give passwords | training to spot signs; filters; type addresses yourself | cover the keypad; privacy screens; biometrics |

### Why organisations must take it seriously

A single employee who falls for social engineering can give an attacker a **valid login**, which gets past the firewall and authentication as if the attacker were a real user. From there, the attacker may reach far more data, especially if access rights are misconfigured (3.6.2). This is why organisations train **all** staff, not just the IT department.

::: terms
- **Social engineering**: manipulating people so that they give up confidential information or perform actions that help an attacker.
- **Blagging (pretexting)**: creating and using an invented scenario to persuade a target to reveal information or do something they normally would not.
- **Pretext**: the invented story or false role an attacker uses when blagging, such as pretending to be IT support.
- **Phishing**: fraudulently obtaining private information using fake messages, often by email or SMS, that appear to come from a trusted source.
- **Spear phishing**: a phishing message researched and aimed at one particular person or organisation.
- **Shouldering (shoulder surfing)**: observing a person's private information, such as a PIN or password, over their shoulder.
- **Privacy screen**: a filter fitted to a display so that it can only be read from directly in front.
- **Two-factor authentication**: requiring two different types of proof of identity, so that a stolen password alone is not enough.
:::

::: method
1. **Define** social engineering: manipulating **people** into giving up **confidential information**.
2. **Name the form** and **describe** it precisely: blagging = invented scenario; phishing = fake email/SMS; shouldering = watching over the shoulder.
3. **Give an example** that fits the scenario in the question (an employee, a bank customer, a pupil).
4. **Protection**: match it to the form. Mostly **training and procedures**; add technology where it genuinely helps (filters, two-factor authentication, biometrics, privacy screens).
5. **Explain how** the protection works, not just its name.
6. **6- or 9-mark questions**: for each form, give how the attack works, its consequence, then the protection, all applied to the scenario.
:::

::: example Shouldering protection
Explain one way a person can protect themselves from shouldering when using a cashpoint. (2)

::: example Why training
Explain why staff training is the most important protection against social engineering. (2)

Social engineering exploits people rather than technical weaknesses, so firewalls and antivirus cannot stop someone choosing to give away information (1); training teaches staff to recognise the tricks and follow procedures, such as never sharing a password (1).

::: q
Explain why social engineering is often more successful than trying to break a system's technical security. (2)
---

Explain how two-factor authentication reduces the harm from phishing. (2)
---

::: q
A receptionist receives a call from someone claiming to be a director who needs the code to the server room. Explain what procedure the receptionist should follow. (2)
---
Do not give the code over the phone (1); verify the caller's identity by calling them back on the number in the company directory / checking with their manager or IT before doing anything (1).
:::

::: watch
- Social engineering targets **people**, not computers. "Install antivirus" does **not** protect against blagging or shouldering.
- **Blagging** needs the idea of an **invented scenario**; "lying to someone" alone is too vague.
- **Phishing** is by **message** (email or SMS). Redirecting a genuine web address to a fake site is **pharming** (3.6.2).
- **Shouldering** is physical observation; it has nothing to do with the network.
- Protections must **match the form**: a privacy screen does nothing against phishing.
- Always say **how** a protection works, e.g. "verify the caller by phoning back on the directory number".
:::
