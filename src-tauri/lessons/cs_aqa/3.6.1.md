## What it is

**Cyber security** is the set of **processes, practices and technologies** used to **protect networks, computers, programs and data** from **attack, damage or unauthorised access**.

The definition has two halves, and an exam answer needs both:
- **what is protected**: networks, computers (the hardware), programs (the software) and data;
- **what it is protected from**: deliberate **attack**, accidental or malicious **damage**, and **unauthorised access** by people who have no right to see or use the system.

It also has three kinds of tool. **Technologies** are things like firewalls, anti-malware software and biometric scanners. **Processes** are the organisation's rules and procedures, such as how often backups are taken or how a password is reset. **Practices** are what people actually do day to day, such as locking a screen when leaving a desk or checking a sender's address before clicking a link. A system can have excellent technology and still be breached because people's practices are poor.

### The main purposes of cyber security

A useful way to remember the purposes is to ask what an attacker could do, and then turn each one round.

| Purpose | What it means | What goes wrong without it |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep data confidential** | only authorised people can **see** data | personal, medical or financial data is stolen and sold, leaked or used for fraud |
| **Keep data accurate (integrity)** | data cannot be **changed or deleted** without permission | records are altered (a grade, a bank balance, a medicine dose) or wiped |
| **Keep systems available** | the network, computers and programs **work when needed** | a shop cannot take orders, a hospital cannot see patient records, staff cannot log in |
| **Prevent unauthorised access** | only the right people can **log in and use** each resource | an attacker uses the system to steal, spy, spread malware or attack others |
| **Protect hardware and software** | devices and programs are not **damaged** or taken over | malware corrupts programs, devices are made unusable, computers are hijacked |

In an exam, "describe the main purposes" is best answered with two or three of these, each **applied** to the organisation in the question.

### Why everyone needs it

**Organisations** hold large amounts of valuable data and depend on their systems to operate.
- **Financial loss**: theft of money, lost sales while systems are down, the cost of investigating and repairing a breach, and the cost of compensating customers.
- **Reputation**: customers, patients or parents lose trust and may go elsewhere.
- **Legal consequences**: organisations have a **legal duty** to keep the personal data they hold secure. A breach can lead to investigation, fines and legal action by the people affected.
- **Loss of data**: records may be destroyed or encrypted by an attacker, and may not be recoverable without good backups.
- **Disruption**: staff cannot work and services stop. In a hospital or transport system this can put **lives at risk**.

**Individuals** also have a lot to lose.
- **Identity theft**: an attacker uses your stolen details to open accounts or take out loans in your name.
- **Financial fraud**: money taken from bank accounts, or card details used for purchases.
- **Privacy**: photos, messages, location history and health data exposed.
- **Loss of personal files**: photos and documents deleted or locked by malware.
- **Accounts taken over**: email or social media used to scam friends and family.

**Wider society** depends on networked systems for power, water, banking, healthcare and communication. An attack on one large organisation can affect millions of people who have never heard of it.

### Scenarios: applying it

Exam questions nearly always name an organisation. The marks come from saying **what that organisation holds** and **what would happen to it**.

| Organisation | Valuable data and systems | Consequence of a breach |
|---|---|---|
| **School** | pupils' names, addresses, dates of birth, medical needs, safeguarding notes, grades, parents' contact details; coursework; the timetable and registers | private data about children exposed; parents lose trust; the school may be investigated for failing to protect personal data; lessons disrupted if the network is down |
| **Hospital** | patient records, test results, prescriptions; systems that book appointments and run equipment | treatment delayed or wrong if records are unavailable or altered; highly sensitive medical data leaked; lives put at risk |
| **Online shop** | customer accounts, addresses, payment details, order history; the website itself | fraud against customers; lost sales while the site is down; loss of reputation and legal action |
| **Bank** | account balances, transactions, customers' identities | money stolen; customers defrauded; huge loss of trust |
| **Individual at home** | email, photos, banking app, social media | identity theft; money lost; private photos and messages exposed |

### Thinking like an examiner

A 9-mark Discuss question on this topic usually asks **why** an organisation needs cyber security, **what** it must protect, and **how**. A top-level answer:
- names the specific data and systems the organisation holds;
- explains the specific consequences for **that** organisation and for the people it serves;
- matches each threat to a suitable protection;
- covers every bullet in the question and reaches a judgement, for example that people (not just technology) must be part of the protection.

::: terms
- **Cyber security**: the processes, practices and technologies that protect networks, computers, programs and data from attack, damage or unauthorised access.
- **Unauthorised access**: using or viewing a computer system or its data without permission from the owner.
- **Confidentiality**: making sure data can only be seen by the people who are authorised to see it.
- **Integrity**: making sure data is accurate and cannot be changed or deleted without permission.
- **Availability**: making sure systems and data can be used by authorised people whenever they are needed.
- **Data breach**: an incident in which data is accessed, stolen, changed or destroyed without authorisation.
- **Identity theft**: using someone's stolen personal details to pretend to be them, for example to open accounts.
- **Threat**: anything that could exploit a weakness to cause harm to a system or its data.
- **Vulnerability**: a weakness in a system, its software or its users that an attacker could exploit.
- **Backup**: a separate copy of data kept so that it can be restored if the original is lost or damaged.
:::

::: method
1. **Define** cyber security with **both halves**: protects networks, computers, programs and data **from** attack, damage or unauthorised access.
2. **Purposes**: keep data confidential, accurate and available, and stop unauthorised access. Say which matters most for the organisation in the question.
3. **Why needed**: name the organisation's **specific data** (patient records, pupils' details, card numbers).
4. **Consequences**: money, reputation, legal action, lost data, disruption, harm to people. Link each one to the scenario.
5. **Protection**: match each **threat** to a **measure**, and explain how the measure reduces that threat.
6. **9-mark Discuss**: cover every bullet, keep applying to the scenario, and finish with a reasoned judgement.
:::

::: example Define
Define the term cyber security. (2)

The processes, practices and technologies designed to protect networks, computers, programs and data (1) from attack, damage or unauthorised access (1).
:::

::: example Purpose for a hospital
Describe two main purposes of cyber security for a hospital. (2)

To keep patient records confidential, so that only authorised staff can see sensitive medical details (1).

To keep systems available, so that doctors can access test results and records when treating patients (1).
:::

::: example Consequences for a business
Explain two consequences for an online shop if its customer database is stolen. (4)

Customers' payment details could be used for fraud (1), so the shop may have to compensate them and could face legal action or fines for failing to protect personal data (1).

News of the breach would damage the shop's reputation (1), so customers may stop trusting it and shop elsewhere, reducing its sales (1).
:::

::: example Individuals
Explain why an individual needs to protect their own email account. (2)

Email accounts are used to reset passwords for other accounts such as banking (1), so an attacker who controls the email could take over those accounts too, or use the email to scam the person's contacts (1).
:::

::: example People and technology
Explain why cyber security involves processes and practices as well as technology. (2)

Many attacks target people, for example tricking a member of staff into giving away a password (1), so technology alone cannot stop them; staff need training and clear procedures to follow (1).
:::

::: q
State the four things that cyber security aims to protect. (2)
---
Networks, computers, programs, data. All four for 2 marks; two or three for 1 mark.
:::

::: q
State three things cyber security protects them from. (1)
---
Attack, damage and unauthorised access (1).
:::

::: q
Give two consequences for an organisation of a cyber security breach. (2)
---
Any two of: financial loss/cost of recovery; loss of reputation/trust; legal action/fines; loss of data; disruption to the business (1 each).
:::

::: q
Explain why data integrity is important in a school's record system. (2)
---
Records such as grades or medical needs must be correct (1), because if they were changed without permission staff could make wrong decisions, e.g. the wrong exam entry or failing to manage a pupil's allergy (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way that a cyber attack on a hospital could put patients at risk. (2)
---
e.g. If systems are made unavailable, staff cannot see records or test results (1), so treatment could be delayed or the wrong treatment given (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by identity theft and how a data breach can lead to it. (3)
---
Identity theft is using someone else's personal details to pretend to be them (1). A breach can expose names, addresses, dates of birth or account details (1), which a criminal can then use to open accounts, take out loans or access existing accounts in the victim's name (1).
:::

::: watch
- The definition needs **what** is protected **and** what **from**. "Keeping computers safe" scores nothing.
- Name the **specific data** the organisation in the question holds. Generic answers stay in the lower levels.
- **Legal consequences**: say organisations have a legal duty to protect personal data. Don't invent specific fine amounts or name laws you are unsure of.
- Don't only list consequences. **Explain** each one: what happens, and why it matters to that organisation.
- Cyber security is not only technology. Credit is given for **training, procedures and backups**.
- In a 9-mark question, **every bullet** must be covered, or the answer is capped.
:::

::: test
Which one of these best describes cyber security? A — making a computer run programs faster; B — protecting networks, computers, programs and data from attack, damage or unauthorised access; C — making backups of data every night; D — encrypting all files on a computer. (1)
---
B. A backup and encryption are parts of cyber security, not the whole of it.
:::

::: test
Which two of these are main purposes of cyber security? A — to stop unauthorised people accessing data; B — to make networks transmit data faster; C — to keep systems available for authorised users; D — to reduce the cost of hardware; E — to compress data before it is stored. (2)
---
A (1) and C (1).
:::

C. The others are consequences for organisations.
:::

::: test
A sixth-form student says, "Cyber security is just about buying good antivirus software." Explain why this is not correct. (3)
---
Cyber security also includes processes and practices, not just technology (1). Many attacks such as phishing or blagging trick people rather than computers, which antivirus cannot stop (1). Other measures are needed, such as staff training, strong password systems, access rights, updates and backups (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why a hospital might regard the availability of its systems as even more important than an online shop does. (2)
---
If a shop's systems are down it loses money, but if a hospital's systems are unavailable, staff may not be able to see records, results or prescriptions (1), so patients' treatment could be delayed or wrong and lives could be put at risk (1).
:::

:::

::: test
A secondary school stores all its records on a network and gives every pupil and teacher a login.
Discuss why the school needs cyber security. In your answer you should discuss:
- the data and systems that the school needs to protect;
- the consequences of a cyber security breach for the school, its pupils and their families;
- how the school could protect its systems and data. (9)
---
Level 3 (7–9): detailed discussion of all three bullets, consistently applied to the school, with accurate terminology; consequences are clearly linked to the data identified; protections are matched to threats; a reasoned conclusion.
Level 2 (4–6): relevant points on at least two bullets with some application and explanation; some links between threats and protections.
Level 1 (1–3): a few relevant but mostly generic points, little explanation, little or no application.
0: nothing worthy of credit.
Indicative content:
- Data and systems: pupils' names, addresses, dates of birth; medical needs and allergies; safeguarding records; grades, reports and coursework; parents' contact details; staff records and pay; registers and the timetable; the network and email systems that lessons depend on.
- Consequences: very sensitive data about children could be exposed or sold; families could be targeted by fraud or identity theft; safeguarding information leaking could put pupils at risk; records altered (grades, attendance); lessons and exams disrupted if the network is down; coursework lost; the school has a legal duty to protect personal data and could be investigated or face legal action; parents lose trust and the school's reputation suffers; cost of recovery.
- Protection: individual logins with strong password rules; access rights so pupils cannot see staff areas or other pupils' records; firewall and anti-malware software; automatic updates; regular backups kept separately; staff training on phishing and blagging; blocking or scanning USB media; encryption of laptops that leave the site; penetration testing to find weaknesses.
- Judgement: e.g. the most serious risk is to children's personal data, so access rights and staff training matter as much as technology.
:::
