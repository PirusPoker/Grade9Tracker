## What it is

Instead of the performer question (x.4) you can answer x.5 as a **designer**, also for **20 marks**. The question reads: "You are a designer working on one aspect of design for this extract. Describe how you would use your design skills to create effects which support the action. Explain why your ideas are appropriate for **this extract** and **your chosen design skill in the play as a whole**." You choose **one** design skill: **lighting, sound, set, costume or puppets**.

The key phrase is **effects which support the action**. A designer answer is not a still picture of what the stage looks like. It is a **sequence of design moments** that change with the action, each tied to a line or event, each with a purpose (location, time, mood, focus, character, tension, transition, symbolism).

**How it is marked.** It uses the same bands as the performer question.
- **16–20**: excellent, exact ideas, an extensive range of design skills precisely targeted at the action, and excellent knowledge of the whole play.
- **11–15**: clear ideas, a wide range, good knowledge of the play.
- **6–10**: some range and relevance, limited use of the wider play.
- **1–5**: a narrow range and little detail.

Answers that describe only the extract, or only a static look, can't reach the top.

### Choosing your skill

Pick the skill you know the **vocabulary** for and can tie to **specific moments**. Set is the most popular, followed by costume and lighting, but a well-prepared sound or lighting designer can stand out. Whatever you choose, prepare it for your whole set play *before* the exam: the locations, the key moments and how your design changes across the play. Consider the play's **style**. A naturalistic play (The Crucible, A Taste of Honey) wants believable, motivated effects. An epic or storytelling play (Noughts and Crosses, Around the World in 80 Days, The Great Wave) invites fast transformations, symbolic effects and visible theatricality.

### Toolkit by skill

| Skill | Vocabulary to use precisely |
|---|---|
| **Lighting** | lanterns (profile, Fresnel, flood, parcan, LED, moving head, follow spot); angle (front, side, back, top, uplight); colour (gel names or colour temperature: warm amber, cold steel blue); intensity (percentage); gobos (window, foliage, bars); gauze; haze; states and cues; snap, fade, cross-fade, blackout; practical lights (lamps, candles on stage) |
| **Sound** | diegetic (heard by the characters) and non-diegetic (only the audience); live and recorded; sound effects; music and underscoring; soundscape; volume and fades; speaker position and direction (surround, a single speaker upstage left); reverb, echo, distortion; microphones and amplification; silence |
| **Set** | configuration; box, composite, minimal or symbolic set; flats, rostra, stairs, levels; trucks, revolve, flying, trapdoor; cyclorama and projection; materials, texture, colour palette, scale; furniture and set dressing; entrances; scene changes and transitions |
| **Costume** | garment, cut, fit, fabric, texture, colour, condition, accessories, footwear, hair, make-up; how costume changes over time; quick changes; **design for at least two characters in the extract** |
| **Puppets** | type (rod, shadow, hand, string/marionette, body or life-size, object puppetry); materials (cane, fabric, foam, card); scale; how the puppeteers are seen or hidden; manipulation, breath and movement; what the puppet makes possible |

### What the answer needs

1. **A concept.** In one or two sentences, say what your design is doing for this play: a naturalistic period room that closes in, colour-coded dystopian control, a playful travelling-trunk world.
2. **The extract as a sequence.** Give three or four moments, each tied to an event or line: what changes, how (named equipment, colour, cue timing, fabric), and why (the effect on the audience and how it supports the action).
3. **The play as a whole.** Give two or three moments elsewhere, especially locations, turning points and the ending, and show how your design develops across the play.
4. **Practicalities.** Where it helps, show how the design works on the stage: transitions, quick changes, sightlines, where the speakers are, how a truck moves on. A small labelled sketch or ground plan can support this, but the marks come from the writing.

About **25 minutes and 550–700 words**.

::: terms
- **Design concept**: the central idea behind a design that shapes all the choices, linked to the play's meaning and style.
- **Lighting state**: a particular combination of lanterns, colours and intensities that creates a picture on stage.
- **Cue**: a planned point in the action at which a lighting or sound change happens.
- **Gobo**: a metal or glass stencil placed in a lantern to project a pattern such as window bars or leaves.
- **Diegetic sound**: sound that exists in the world of the play and can be heard by the characters.
- **Non-diegetic sound**: sound heard only by the audience, such as underscoring, used for mood or tension.
- **Underscoring**: music or sound played quietly underneath dialogue to shape the mood.
- **Composite set**: one set that shows several locations at once, allowing quick movement between them.
- **Truck**: a platform on wheels carrying set pieces, rolled on and off for fast scene changes.
- **Revolve**: a turntable section of stage floor that rotates to change scenes or show movement.
- **Rod puppet**: a puppet moved by rods attached to its limbs or head, operated from below or behind.
:::

::: method
1. **Choose your skill** before the exam (the one whose vocabulary and whole-play plan you know best), and design one skill only.
2. **State your concept** in one or two sentences linked to the play's style and themes.
3. **Map the extract into three or four design moments** tied to specific lines or events, and describe each precisely with named equipment, materials, colours and cue timings.
4. **Explain how each moment supports the action**: location, time, mood, focus, character or tension, and the effect on the audience.
5. **Move to the whole play** with two or three moments (other locations, turning points, the ending), showing how the design develops.
6. **For costume, design for at least two characters**; for set, include how transitions work; for sound and lighting, give actual cues.
:::

::: example Lighting: The Crucible, an extract from Act Two (model extracts)
You are a designer working on lighting for an extract from the opening of Act Two, in the Proctors' home. Describe how you would use your design skills to create effects which support the action. Explain why your ideas are appropriate for this extract and your chosen design skill in the play as a whole. (20)

*These paragraphs model the top band in compressed form.*

My concept is naturalistic light that seems to come only from real sources (the sun, the fire, candles), and it dims and cools act by act as Salem closes in on the Proctors (1). At the start of the extract it is early evening in spring. A profile lantern rigged high stage right, with a straw-coloured gel and a leaded-window gobo, throws a long pool of low, warm sunlight across the floor, and the hearth upstage left glows from a flickering orange LED unit hidden in the logs (1). Warm light makes the house look like a place of safety as Proctor comes in from the fields, so the coldness that follows is more painful (1).

As Elizabeth's suspicion surfaces, over two minutes a slow, almost unnoticed cross-fade takes the sunlight down to 30% and shifts the general cover towards a paler, colder lavender (1). The audience feels the temperature of the marriage drop without noticing a lighting cue (1). The fire stays constant, so the two figures at the table are side-lit orange on one side and grey on the other, their faces half in shadow, which suggests the guilt and suspicion between them (1).

When Mary Warren arrives and the talk turns to the court, the last of the sunlight snaps out as if the sun has set, leaving firelight and a single candle on the table (a practical lamp) (1). The room shrinks to a pool around the table and the shadows at the edges grow, suggesting the outside world pressing in (1). At the arrest, lanterns carried by the officials (practical, flickering) throw moving shadows across the back wall from a low side angle, creating fear and chaos in the home (1).

**The play as a whole.** Act One: cold, pale early-morning light through the narrow window of Betty's bedroom, with a white-blue side light on the bed, so the girl's stillness looks eerie (1). Act Three: hard, bright top light and a high-window gobo in the meeting-house room, flat and shadowless, like an interrogation where nothing can hide (1). Act Four: the jail almost dark, a single steel-blue shaft through a barred-window gobo, then a very slow sunrise, warm light rising on Elizabeth's face as Proctor goes to his death, returning the warmth lost in Act Two as a sign of his redemption (1).
:::

::: example Set: Blood Brothers (compressed plan)
Outline a set design for Blood Brothers that supports the action in an extract with Mickey and Edward aged seven, and across the play as a whole. (8)

Concept: a permanent composite set of a Liverpool street, with the Johnstones' terrace downstage left and the Lyonses' more spacious house suggested upstage right, so class is always visible (1). A worn brick wall and pavement give the children space to play downstage centre; the lamp post, where the Narrator watches, is always in view, so fate watches over them (1). In the extract the two boys meet in the no-man's-land between the houses, so the design places the friendship between the two classes (1). The Lyonses' furniture (a white leather sofa, a glass coffee table) is trucked on from stage right for interiors, and the change happens in view, keeping the pace of the storytelling (1). For the move to the country, the terraces are flown out and a painted cyclorama of green fields and new houses fills the back wall (1), an image of hope that later turns bleak. For the teenage and adult scenes, graffiti and boarded-up windows are added to the terrace, showing economic decline (1). The final scene uses a raised council-chamber platform upstage centre, so Mickey climbs to confront Edward (1), and the whole company is gathered on the street below as the Narrator returns us to the opening image, completing the cycle (1).
:::

::: example Costume: Romeo and Juliet (two characters)
Describe costume designs for two characters in an extract from Act One, Scene Five (the Capulet feast), and explain how they support the action. (6)

Juliet wears a floor-length gown of ivory silk with a square neckline and fitted bodice embroidered in gold thread, and long sleeves slashed to show rose-pink underneath (1). It marks her as the rich, protected daughter being shown off to suitors, and its brightness makes her the focus of the crowded dance (1). Romeo, gatecrashing in a masquerade half-mask of black leather, wears a dark wool doublet in deep midnight blue and plain black hose, with no Montague colours visible, so he blends into the shadows at the edge of the party (1). The contrast of light and dark lets the audience pick the two lovers out of the crowd, and when Romeo lifts his mask to kiss her, the gesture reveals who he is (1). The Capulets wear red and gold, which makes Tybalt, in a harder, blood-red leather jerkin with a sword at his hip, stand out as a threat when he recognises Romeo (1). Across the play, Juliet's costumes lose colour, from ivory to a plain white nightgown in Act Four, so the final image in the tomb echoes both a wedding and a death (1).
:::

::: example Puppets: Around the World in 80 Days
Describe a puppet design for the elephant in Around the World in 80 Days and explain how it supports the storytelling style. (4)

A life-size elephant head and trunk made from a lightweight cane frame covered in grey-brown hessian and patched fabric, carried on poles by two visible puppeteers, with the body suggested only by a draped canvas (1). The trunk is a flexible chain of foam segments on a rod, so it can swing, curl and snort, and the ears are hinged fabric flaps operated by strings (1). Because the puppeteers are seen, the puppet fits the storytelling style, where the audience enjoys watching the company build the journey from simple materials (1). The characters "ride" by sitting on a plank between the poles, bouncing with its rolling gait, which creates comedy and a sense of travel on a bare stage (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what "create effects which support the action" means in the designer question. (2)
---
The design should change and respond to what happens, at specific moments tied to lines and events (1), helping the audience understand location, time, mood, character or tension, rather than a static description of the look (1).
:::

::: q
Give three lighting skills, other than colour, that a designer can control. (3)
---
Any three (1 each): intensity; angle/direction; type of lantern; focus/beam size and edge; gobos; timing of cues (snap/fade/cross-fade); special effects (haze, strobe); position of rig.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between diegetic and non-diegetic sound, with an example of each. (4)
---
Diegetic: sound within the world of the play that the characters can hear (1), e.g. a doorbell or a radio playing (1). Non-diegetic: sound only the audience hears (1), e.g. tense underscoring beneath a confrontation (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a costume designer answering x.5 should design for at least two characters. (2)
---
The design must support the action of the extract, which involves more than one character, and contrasts between costumes show relationships and status (1); describing only one costume gives a narrow range of ideas (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the style of a play might affect a set design. Refer to two different styles. (4)
---
Naturalistic style (1): detailed, believable box set with real furniture and period detail, e.g. a cramped flat (1). Epic, storytelling or dystopian style (1): minimal, multi-purpose or symbolic pieces, trucks or projection for fast episodic changes, visible transitions (1).
:::

::: q
Describe a sound design for a scene set on a stormy night by the sea, giving three cues. (6)
---
Three cues, each with precise detail (1) and purpose (1): e.g. cue 1, a recorded soundscape of wind and crashing waves through surround speakers at 60%, establishing place and danger; cue 2, a sudden rise to full volume with a low rumble through a sub-bass speaker under the stage as a character goes missing, creating panic; cue 3, a snap to silence except a single faint wave from a speaker upstage, creating emptiness and loss.
:::

::: watch
- A designer answer must describe effects that support the action. A static description of what the stage looks like misses half the question.
- Writing about more than one design skill. Choose one only.
- Covering only the extract, with no ideas for the play as a whole.
- Lighting answers that only talk about colour. Also use intensity, angle, lantern type, gobos and cue timing.
- Designing only one costume when several characters are in the extract.
- Ignoring the play's style: a cluttered naturalistic set for a fast epic play, or wildly symbolic effects that fight a naturalistic one.
- Describing a sketch but not writing the design in sentences.
:::

::: test
Define "cross-fade". (1)
---
A lighting (or sound) change in which one state fades out as another fades in, so they overlap (1).

::: test
Name two types of puppet and describe how each is operated. (4)
---
Any two, 1 for the type and 1 for the operation: rod puppet, moved by rods from below or behind; shadow puppet, a flat figure held between a light and a screen; marionette/string puppet, strings from a control bar above; hand/glove puppet, the operator's hand inside; life-size/body puppet, operated by one or more visible puppeteers.

::: test
Explain two ways lighting could show a change of time in a play. (4)
---
Two ideas, each precise (1) and explained (1): e.g. colour temperature shifting from warm amber morning light to cold blue moonlight, showing day turning to night; the angle of a window gobo moving from high to low to show the sun setting; a blackout and new state for a jump in years.
:::

::: test

---
Two methods, each precise (1) and explained (1): e.g. a composite set with areas for each location lit in turn; trucks rolled on by the cast in view; a revolve turning to reveal a new room; projection on a cyclorama changing the background; multi-purpose boxes rearranged. Effect: keeps pace and flow.
:::
