## What it is

The third part of Section B (x.3) is worth **12 marks**. The question takes the same shape every year. "You are performing the role of X. Focus on the shaded part of the extract. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Y to show the audience [a named idea]." The named idea changes. It might be tension between two characters, a character's power, that they care (or do not care) about each other, surprise, fear, or the growing closeness of a relationship.

The question has three demands, and all three are marked.
1. **The performance space**: where you are on stage, how you move through it, levels, proxemics, and how you use the set and furniture.
2. **Interaction with the other actor**: eye contact, touch (or the refusal of it), how you react while they speak, and how you deliver lines *to* them. Interaction includes verbal delivery aimed at your partner, but the answer is still about space and interaction, not a vocal-skills essay.
3. **The focus**: everything must show the audience the idea the question names.

**How it is marked.** Four bands of three marks.
- **10–12**: an exact, well-developed explanation, entirely appropriate, with precise detail throughout.
- **7–9**: clear and developed, with a number of precise details.
- **4–6**: some detail, some relevance, generalised in places.
- **1–3**: vague, with little sense of the space or the other actor.

Answers that list stage positions without saying what they show, or that ignore the other actor, stay low.

### Think like a director blocking a scene

Imagine the shaded section as a short **journey** with a beginning, middle and end. The strongest answers pick **three or four key lines or moments** across the shaded section, including its first and last lines, and walk the examiner through them in order. For each moment, say:

- **where** you are and **where you move** (stage positions, in the actor's left and right: "I cross from upstage right to the chair downstage left");
- **how** you move (speed, directness, circling, backing away);
- **what happens between you and the other actor** (eye contact made, held or broken; a touch accepted or shrugged off; a listening reaction to their line);
- **what the audience learns** about the named focus.

### The space toolkit

| Tool | What it shows | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Proxemics** (the distance between characters) | closeness, threat, trust, rejection | "I close the gap to arm's length on 'Look at me'" |
| **Levels** | status and power | "she stays seated on the bed as he stands over her" |
| **Stage position** | strength or weakness in the stage picture | "downstage centre claims focus; retreating upstage corner shows defeat" |
| **Furniture and set** | barriers, refuges, status objects | "the table stays between us", "I sit in his chair" |
| **Pathways** | pursuit, avoidance, control of the room | "I circle him slowly clockwise"; "I block the door" |
| **Facing and turning** | engagement and rejection | "I turn my back on her line"; "we face each other square on" |
| **Stillness** | control or shock | "I don't move while he rages, and my stillness is the power" |

**Proxemics** is the distance itself, so describe the movement that changes it. "I step close so our faces are a hand's breadth apart" is precise. "I increase proxemics" is muddled.

### The interaction toolkit

- **Eye contact**: held, avoided, broken at a key word, a look that lingers after they turn away.
- **Touch**: a hand on the arm, grabbing a wrist, a hand reached out and withdrawn, flinching from a touch.
- **Reacting while listening**: what you do on *their* lines, such as a slow shake of the head, arms folding, turning to the window, a sharp intake of breath. This is where much of the interaction lives.
- **Delivery aimed at them**: a line spoken into their ear, thrown over the shoulder, or said to their back.
- **Objects passed between you**: a letter snatched, a cup refused, a ring handed back.

Describe only your own performance in detail. Mention what the other actor does only as far as you are reacting to it; don't direct them.

### Staying true to the play

The style and period of the play matter. In a naturalistic 1690s Puritan household, for example, a man shoving a minister would be wildly out of character, but a table used as a barrier and a refusal to meet his eye can be charged with tension. Check what your character knows and wants at this point, and don't make every conflict a shouting match. Stage directions in the shaded section are starting points: "she grabs him" earns nothing until you say how, where and with what effect.

If you want to name a configuration, say so in one clause ("on an end-on stage"). Otherwise assume the audience is in front of you.

About **15 minutes and 350–450 words** is right for 12 marks.

::: terms
- **Performance space**: the stage area and everything in it that an actor can use: positions, levels, furniture and entrances.
- **Proxemics**: the distance between performers on stage, used to show the relationship between their characters.
- **Blocking**: the planned positions and movements of actors during a scene.
- **Levels**: the different heights at which actors are placed, often used to show status or power.
- **Stage picture**: the image formed by the positions of the actors and set at a given moment, as the audience sees it.
- **Interaction**: how characters respond to each other through eye contact, touch, reaction and delivery.
- **Focus**: the point on stage where the audience's attention is drawn at a given moment.
- **Upstage / downstage**: upstage is the area furthest from the audience; downstage is nearest the audience.
- **Stage left / stage right**: the actor's left and right when facing the audience.
- **Barrier**: a piece of furniture or set placed between characters to show distance or conflict.
:::

::: method
1. **Underline the focus** of the question (e.g. "to show she does not trust him") and keep returning to it.
2. **Read the shaded section and choose three or four moments**: its opening line, one or two turning points, and its final line.
3. **Set the starting stage picture**: where each of you is, what furniture is where, and who holds the higher level.
4. **For each moment, give position, move and interaction**: where you go, how you move, eye contact, touch or reaction, all tied to a specific line.
5. **Explain what each moment shows the audience**, using the question's own wording.
6. **End with the final stage picture** of the shaded section and the effect it leaves.
:::

::: example An invented two-hander: showing power
Scene: in a cramped kitchen at night, an older brother (Dean) confronts his younger sister (Kaz), who has secretly taken money from the family savings tin to pay for a college course. The shaded section runs from Dean holding up the empty tin to Kaz's final line, "I'm not sorry."

You are performing the role of Kaz. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Dean to show the audience that Kaz refuses to be controlled by her brother. (12)

At the start of the shaded section Kaz is seated at the table downstage right, and Dean stands at the fridge upstage left holding up the empty tin (1). I don't look up when he rattles it. I carry on writing in my notebook, a deliberate refusal to give him eye contact that shows I won't be summoned (1). When he crosses to loom over me at the table, I close the notebook slowly, stand, and move round to the far side of the table, so the table is between us and I am standing at his level rather than below him (1). The audience sees her take away his height advantage, a first sign that she won't be bullied (1).

On his demand to know where the money went, I place both palms flat on the table, lean in and hold his gaze across it without blinking while I tell him (1). The steady, level look and short distance across the table show she is not ashamed (1). When he reaches across to grab my wrist, I pull my arm back sharply and step back a pace, then deliberately step forward again, closer than before (1). She shows him she can't be pushed back, and the tension rises as the audience wonders who will give way (1).

For "I'm not sorry" I walk round the table to him, taking the tin from his hand and placing it back on the shelf upstage centre with my back to him, then turn and deliver the line to his face from arm's length, quietly (1). Taking the object he is using as evidence and putting it away takes control of the scene from him (1). The final stage picture has Kaz centre, upright and still, and Dean downstage right, looking at the empty shelf (1). The shift of positions from the opening picture shows the audience that the power has moved to her, and that she refuses to be controlled (1).
:::

::: example Romeo and Juliet: Juliet and the Nurse (Act Three, Scene Five)
After her parents leave, Juliet asks the Nurse for comfort, and the Nurse advises her to marry Paris. Focus on the section from Juliet's appeal for comfort to her decision to go to Friar Laurence.

You are performing the role of Juliet. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing the Nurse to show Juliet's growing isolation. (6)

At the start I am on the floor downstage centre where my father left me, and I reach up with both hands to the Nurse, gripping her apron like a child (1). This shows Juliet still turns to the Nurse as a mother figure. As the Nurse praises Paris, my grip loosens, and I slowly let go and sit back on my heels, breaking eye contact and staring out front (1). The growing distance shows her realising she has lost her last ally (1). I rise and move away upstage to the window, giving the Nurse my back, and I speak my calm dismissal of her without turning round (1). When the Nurse leaves, I turn and watch the door she has gone through, alone in the widest space on stage (1). The empty stage around Juliet shows the audience she is now completely alone and must act for herself (1).
:::

::: example Planning: moments in the shaded section
Explain why the best answers choose three or four specific lines across the shaded section rather than describing the whole section in general terms. (3)

Specific lines let you pin each move and interaction to an exact moment, which is the precision the top band needs (1). Covering the start, middle and end shows how the relationship or focus develops through the section (1). General description ("I would stay away from him throughout") gives the examiner nothing precise to credit and loses the journey (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between proxemics and levels, giving an example of each that could show one character's power over another. (4)
---
Proxemics is the distance between characters (1), e.g. one advances until the other is backed against the wall (1). Levels are heights (1), e.g. one stands on the stairs while the other kneels (1).
:::

::: q
A student writes: "At the start I am upstage left. Then I move downstage right." Explain why this would earn few marks, and improve it. (3)
---
Positions alone show nothing: no reason, no interaction, no effect (1). Improvement: tie it to a line and the other actor, e.g. "on his accusation I cross from upstage left straight to him downstage right, stopping a hand's breadth from his face" (1), and say what it shows: "the sudden closeness shows she is no longer afraid of him" (1).
:::

::: q
Give three ways a performer can interact with another actor without speaking. (3)
---
Any three (1 each): eye contact held or broken; touch or refusing touch; reactions while listening (a nod, a flinch, turning away); passing or withholding an object; mirroring their movement; blocking their path.
:::

::: q
Scene: two old friends meet at a railway station after twenty years apart. One (Pat) is delighted; the other (Viv) is awkward, because she has come to tell Pat she can't be a bridesmaid. Shaded section: from Pat's greeting to Viv's "There's something I need to say."

You are performing the role of Viv. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Pat to show Viv's discomfort. (12)
---
Bands: 10–12 exact, developed, precise throughout, entirely appropriate; 7–9 clear and developed with a number of precise details; 4–6 some detail and relevance; 1–3 limited. Indicative: starts downstage left holding a suitcase in front of her like a shield; Pat rushes in for a hug and Viv lets the case take the first contact, then gives a stiff one-armed hug, patting Pat's back twice; steps back to widen the distance; eyes drift to the departure board as Pat talks; sits at the end of a bench leaving a gap when Pat sits; half-turns away with her body angled to the exit; on the final line faces Pat directly, putting the case down between them. Effect: awkwardness and dread are clear to the audience, and tension builds towards the news.
:::

::: q
Explain how a piece of furniture could be used as part of the interaction in a 12-mark answer. (2)
---
E.g. a table kept between two characters as a barrier to show conflict or fear (1); one character sitting in another's chair to claim status, or sweeping objects off it in anger, with the effect explained (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why you should not spend much of a 12-mark answer describing how the other actor performs. (2)
---
Marks are for your own use of space and interaction as the named character (1); describing the other actor's performance at length is not creditable, so refer to them only as you react to them (1).
:::

::: watch
- The question is about the performance space and interaction, not voice. Answers that drift into vocal skills lose focus and marks.
- Listing stage positions ("I move downstage left") with no reason, no interaction and no effect.
- Forgetting the named focus. Every moment must show what the question asks, e.g. that she doesn't care about him.
- Misusing "proxemics". It is the distance itself, so describe the move that closes or opens it.
- Describing the whole shaded section in general terms instead of three or four specific lines.
- Spending half the answer directing the other actor.
- Playing every conflict as physical aggression, which is often wrong for the character, period or style.
- Repeating a stage direction without saying how you would perform it.
:::

::: test
Give two ways of using levels to show that one character has more power than another. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): standing over a seated or kneeling character; standing on stairs, a rostrum or a raised platform; sitting in a high-backed chair while the other sits on the floor; one rising as the other sinks.
:::

::: test
Explain how breaking eye contact at a key moment could show a character is hiding something. (2)
---
Looking away (to the floor, the door) exactly on a question or accusation (1) tells the audience the character can't face the truth, suggesting guilt or a secret while the words deny it (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a performer could use the stage space to show that two characters are growing closer across a scene. Refer to three moments. (6)
---
Two marks per moment (1 for precise use of space/interaction tied to a moment, 1 for explained effect). E.g. start: at opposite sides of the stage, not facing; middle: one crosses to sit on the same bench, leaving a gap; touch offered on a line of comfort; end: side by side, shoulders touching, looking out front together. Effects: growing trust shown by the shrinking distance.
:::

::: test
Scene: in a hospital corridor, a daughter (Lena) waits for news of her mother. Her stepfather (Rob), whom she blames for her mother's illness, arrives with coffee. Shaded section: from Rob holding out a coffee to Lena's line "Just go home, Rob."

You are performing the role of Lena. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Rob to show Lena's hostility towards him. (12)
---
Bands: 10–12 an exact, well-developed explanation, entirely appropriate, precise detail throughout; 7–9 clear, developed, a number of precise details; 4–6 reasonably clear, a few details, some relevance; 1–3 limited, generalised.

Indicative: starting seated on a plastic chair downstage right, knees drawn up, facing the ward door upstage left; ignores the held-out cup, leaving his arm hanging, then gets up and moves past him to stand by the door; keeps her back to him on his lines; flinches away from his hand on her shoulder, stepping sideways out of reach; turns to face him square on for "Just go home, Rob", from across the corridor, the widest distance; final picture: Lena guarding the door, Rob alone holding two cups. Effects: hostility and blame shown through refusal of contact and control of the space; the audience senses the family breakdown.
:::

::: test
Scene: in a school office, a head teacher (Mrs Okafor) questions a pupil (Ellis), who she suspects of setting off the fire alarm. Ellis is cheeky and confident. Shaded section: from Mrs Okafor's "Sit down" to her line "I have the footage, Ellis."

You are performing the role of Mrs Okafor. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Ellis to show her authority. (12)
---
Bands as above. Indicative: behind the desk centre stage, standing while he lounges in the doorway; points to the chair without looking up from papers and waits in silence until he sits; moves round the desk to perch on its front edge, closing the distance and looking down at him (levels); holds his gaze steadily through his jokes, without smiling; walks slowly behind his chair so he has to twist round to see her; places a laptop on the desk in front of him on the final line and steps back to watch his reaction. Effects: calm control and status; the audience enjoys the shift as his confidence collapses.
:::

::: test
Scene: in a crowded pub, a young man (Sol) plucks up courage to ask a woman he has admired for months (Anya) to dance. She is amused and flattered. Shaded section: from Sol's approach to his line "So is that a yes?"

You are performing the role of Sol. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Anya to create comedy from Sol's nervousness. (12)
---
Bands as above. Indicative: hovering upstage behind a pillar, starting towards her twice and retreating (pathways); approaching in an indirect curve; stopping too close, then stepping back too far; eye contact snatched and broken; offering a hand and withdrawing it to wipe it on his trousers; mirroring her when she sips her drink; a bench or bar stool he nearly falls off; on the final line, stock still and rigid, hopeful stare. Effects: comedy from the timing and awkward proxemics; the audience roots for him.
:::

::: test
Explain why the style of the play should affect how you use the space and interact in a 12-mark answer. (3)
---
Choices must suit the play's style and period (1): e.g. in a naturalistic period drama, restrained, believable blocking such as using furniture and eye contact (1); in an epic or physical-theatre style, direct address, stylised movement or ensemble images may be appropriate; wildly out-of-style choices are not appropriate to the brief (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what an actor could do while the other character is speaking to add to the interaction. (3)
---
Precise reactions while listening (1 each for up to two, e.g. a slow shake of the head; folding arms; turning away to the window; flinching; a step closer) plus effect (1): the audience sees the character's response and the relationship even when they are silent.
:::

::: test
A stage direction in the shaded section says a character "turns away". Explain how a student could gain marks from this rather than just repeating it. (3)
---
Say how and where: e.g. a slow half-turn upstage towards the window, head lowered (1); link it to a specific line and the other actor's reaction (1); explain what it shows the audience in terms of the question's focus, e.g. shame or rejection (1).
:::

::: test
Describe the final stage picture you might create at the end of a shaded section to show that one character has defeated the other. (3)
---
Precise positions (1): e.g. winner centre stage, standing; loser downstage corner or on the floor. Interaction (1): e.g. winner not looking at them, loser's eyes fixed on the winner. Effect (1): the stage image makes the shift of power clear to the audience.
:::

::: test
Scene: in a garden shed, a grandmother (Nell) shows her teenage grandson (Jai) the old letters she has hidden from the family for fifty years. Shaded section: from Nell unlocking the tin box to Jai's question "Does Grandad know?"

You are performing the role of Nell. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Jai to show that Nell trusts Jai completely. (12)
---
Bands as above. Indicative: checks the door is closed upstage, then beckons him close; they sit side by side on upturned crates, knees almost touching (close proxemics); she places the box in his hands rather than keeping hold of it; holds his gaze and nods when he hesitates to open it; her hand on his as he unfolds the first letter; leans in to read over his shoulder; on his question she holds eye contact for a long moment before answering. Effects: intimacy and trust; a shared secret between generations; the audience is drawn into the secret with them.
:::
