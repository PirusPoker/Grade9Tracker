## What it is

*A Taste of Honey* by Shelagh Delaney (prescribed edition: **Methuen Drama**) is a **kitchen sink drama**, and AQA gives its style as **naturalism**. A teenage girl, Jo, and her careless, funny mother, Helen, share a shabby flat in Salford. Over two acts Jo falls in love, is left pregnant, makes a home with a gentle friend, Geof, and is finally left alone. The play is naturalistic in its setting and characters, but its first production added live jazz and moments when characters talked to the audience, and your answers can use both sides. Section B asks four questions on an extract (4, 8, 12 and 20 marks).

### Context

| | What it is | What it gives a theatre maker |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | **Working-class Salford in the late 1950s** (AQA's wording) | An industrial city next to Manchester: terraced streets, factories, smoke, canals, rented rooms with damp walls and shared toilets. Little money, casual work, pubs, dance halls. |
| **Social attitudes** | Britain in the late 1950s | Having a baby outside marriage brought shame; mixed-race relationships met open prejudice; **homosexuality was illegal** (partly decriminalised in England only in 1967). The play treats all three with a frankness that surprised audiences. |
| **Writing** | Written by **Shelagh Delaney**, herself from Salford, when she was about nineteen; first staged in **1958** by **Joan Littlewood's Theatre Workshop** at the Theatre Royal, Stratford East, London | Part of the late-1950s wave of **kitchen sink** plays that put working-class lives on stage. Littlewood's production was playful and theatrical: a jazz band on stage, characters entering and leaving to music, and Helen in particular speaking to the audience. |

### Structure and style

- **Two acts, each of two scenes**, with months passing between them: we see Jo at key points in about a year of her life.
- **One set**: the flat. Naturalistic detail (a bed, a sink, a gas stove, a table, damp walls).
- **Naturalistic dialogue**: quick, witty, cruel, Northern. Mother and daughter spar like a double act; the rhythm is often comic even when the content is painful.
- **Theatrical touches** from Littlewood's staging: music between scenes, dance-like entrances, songs and **direct address**. These are part of the play's performance history and are good ideas for answers.

### Plot, scene by scene

| Scene | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Act One, Scene One** | Helen and her daughter Jo arrive at a cold, run-down flat. They bicker constantly. Jo is about to leave school and loves drawing. Peter, Helen's younger, flashy boyfriend (he wears an eye patch), turns up; he mocks Jo and proposes to Helen. |
| **Act One, Scene Two** | Jo walks home with Boy, a young Black sailor, who proposes and gives her a ring; she wears it on a ribbon round her neck. Helen prepares to marry Peter and is scornful when Jo tells her. Boy's ship is due to leave; he stays with Jo over Christmas. Helen leaves to marry Peter, and Jo is left alone. |
| **Act Two, Scene One** | Months later, summer. Jo is pregnant; Boy has not come back. She works in a shoe shop by day and a bar by night. Geof, an art student who has lost his lodgings, moves in and looks after her. Helen, told by Geof, visits; Peter, drunk, turns up and drags her off. |
| **Act Two, Scene Two** | Later, near the birth. Geof has made the flat a home: he cooks, cleans and prepares for the baby. Helen, thrown out by Peter, returns and takes over, pushing Geof out; he leaves quietly. When Jo tells her the baby may be Black, Helen goes out for a drink. Jo is left alone, singing a nursery rhyme Geof taught her. |

### Characters

| Character | Performance note |
|---|---|
| **Jo** | Sharp, funny, moody, frightened of being alone; a strong Salford accent; restless physicality at first, then the slower, heavier movement of pregnancy. Her wit hides her fear. |
| **Helen** | Jo's mother: vain, self-centred, loud, funny, fond of drink and men, but not without love. A performer's energy: she plays to the audience, fixes her make-up, drapes herself on furniture. |
| **Peter** | Helen's younger, flashy, drinking boyfriend: loud suit, cigar, eye patch; charming when he wants something, vicious when drunk. |
| **Boy (Jimmie)** | A young Black sailor, gentle and playful; real tenderness with Jo, but he leaves. Warm voice, easy smile, a naval uniform. |
| **Geof** | An art student, kind, gentle, implied to be gay; he makes a home for Jo. Quiet voice, careful movement, domestic tasks (sweeping, sewing, cooking); Helen and Peter mock him. |

### Key moments and how to stage them

1. **The arrival (Act One, Scene One).** Helen dumps her bags and complains, playing to the audience; Jo prowls the room, criticising everything. The cold, bare flat: a single bulb, a stained sink, a view of chimneys. Their sharp, quick dialogue should be played like a comedy double act with fast cues.
2. **Jo and Boy.** A street corner or the doorway lit by a street lamp; the ring on its ribbon; playful physical closeness. Music underscoring (a soft saxophone line) makes the romance tender and fragile.
3. **Helen's departure.** Helen in her wedding finery, fussing at the mirror; Jo on the bed, turned away; the door slams and Jo is left alone in fading light.
4. **Geof moves in (Act Two, Scene One).** Awkward, kind; Geof tidies while Jo teases him. Their relationship is played with gentle humour and trust.
5. **Peter drunk.** Loud, lurching, a bottle in hand; he fills the small space and threatens everyone; the comedy has a nasty edge.
6. **The ending.** Helen's exit, Jo alone on the bed or at the window, singing quietly, the light narrowing on her. The final image is ambiguous: hopeful and lonely at once.

### Design

A **naturalistic box set** or an open, suggestive room: a sagging double bed, a table with an oilcloth, two hard chairs, a gas ring, a cracked sink, a bare bulb, a window onto sooty chimneys, damp stains on flowered wallpaper. In Act Two Geof's care shows in the set: flowers in a jar, clean crockery, baby clothes, a crib. Costume: Helen's cheap glamour (fake fur, bright lipstick, costume jewellery), Jo's school clothes then a shapeless smock, Peter's loud suit, Boy's naval uniform, Geof's corduroy and knitwear. Lighting can pick out times of day and seasons; music can link scenes as in Littlewood's production.

::: terms
- **Kitchen sink drama**: late-1950s British plays showing working-class domestic life realistically, often in cramped homes.
- **Naturalism**: a style that recreates real life on stage as closely as possible in acting, speech and design.
- **Theatre Workshop**: Joan Littlewood's company at the Theatre Royal, Stratford East, which first staged the play in 1958.
- **Joan Littlewood**: the director whose playful, music-filled production shaped how the play was first seen.
- **Direct address**: characters speaking to the audience, as Helen does in Littlewood's staging.
- **Box set**: a naturalistic set of a room with three walls, the fourth wall open to the audience.
- **Double act**: two performers playing off each other with quick, comic exchanges, like Helen and Jo.
- **Salford**: the industrial city next to Manchester where the play is set and where Delaney grew up.
:::

::: method
1. **Place the extract**: act and scene, who lives in the flat now, and how far Jo's pregnancy has gone.
2. **For the 4-marker, design for working-class Salford in the late 1950s**: cheap, worn, practical, with period detail.
3. **For the line**, remember the comic rhythm: Salford accent, quick cues, put-downs delivered with timing, pain under the jokes.
4. **For the shaded section**, use the small room: furniture as obstacles, tasks (cooking, dressing, packing), closeness and distance.
5. **For the 20-marker**, trace the character or design across the four scenes and the passing seasons.
6. **Use both sides of the style**: naturalism, plus Littlewood's music and direct address.
:::

::: example Costume for Helen (4 marks)
You are designing a costume for Helen on the day she leaves to marry Peter. The costume must reflect the context of *A Taste of Honey*, set in working-class Salford in the late 1950s. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

A tight-waisted, knee-length dress in cheap bright-pink rayon with a full skirt over a stiff petticoat, the fashionable late-1950s shape she can only afford in a poorer fabric (1). A short fake-fur jacket, slightly matted, and a cheap pearl necklace and clip-on earrings (1). Seamed nylon stockings, one with a ladder she hides, and white stiletto heels scuffed at the toes (1). Hair set in stiff curls, a small hat with a net veil, heavy red lipstick and powder, so she looks both glamorous and worn, fitting her vanity and her age (1).
:::

::: example Performing Jo (8 marks)
You are performing the role of Jo. When Helen says she is going to marry Peter, Jo answers with a cutting joke. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform her reply and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

I would stay lying on the bed, not looking at Helen, and deliver the joke with a flat, dry tone in a strong Salford accent (1). A pause before the punchline sharpens the timing (1), and the key word would be stressed with mock sweetness (1). Then I'd roll over to face the wall (1), pulling the blanket up so Helen can't see my face (1). The effect: the audience laughs at Jo's wit (1), but the turned back shows how hurt and frightened she is at being left (1), so the comedy hides real pain, which is the play's typical tone (1).
:::

::: example Space and interaction: Jo and Geof (12 marks, outline)
You are performing the role of Geof in Act Two when he tries to persuade Jo to prepare for the baby. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Jo to show his care for her. (12)

He comes in with a parcel and sets it on the table, then fusses around the flat, straightening the bed, clearing plates, while Jo slumps in a chair. He brings her a cup of tea and kneels to her level, holding it out until she takes it. When she snaps, he retreats to the sink but keeps talking gently. He unwraps baby clothes and holds them up; she turns away; he waits, then lays them on the bed near her. His voice stays soft; his moves are patient, never crowding her, so the audience sees a care that asks nothing back.
:::

::: example Designing the flat (20 marks, outline)
Plan a set design for Act Two, Scene Two and the play as a whole. (20)

Extract: the same flat transformed by Geof: fresh flowers in a milk bottle, a clean checked oilcloth, a crib he has made from a basket, a line of drying nappies across the window; then Helen's clutter (suitcases, a fur coat thrown over the bed) invading as she takes over. Whole play: Act One the flat bare, damp and cold, peeling wallpaper, unmade bed, a single bulb; a window with a view of chimneys and smoke; a door to a landing so entrances and exits are clear; in summer the window open. The changing state of one room shows who holds the home together.
:::

::: q
Who wrote *A Taste of Honey*, and who first directed it? (2)
---
Shelagh Delaney (1); Joan Littlewood with Theatre Workshop, 1958 (1).
:::

::: q
Why was the play seen as bold in 1958? Give two reasons. (2)
---
Any two: a single mother's pregnancy; a mixed-race relationship; a gay (implied) character when homosexuality was illegal; a frank, unflattering mother-daughter relationship; working-class women at the centre.
:::

::: q
What does Boy give Jo, and how does she wear it? (2)
---
A ring (1), on a ribbon round her neck (1).
:::

::: q
How does Geof help Jo? (2)
---
He moves in and looks after the flat and Jo (1), and prepares for the baby, making a home (1).
:::

::: q
What features of Littlewood's production went beyond naturalism? (2)
---
Live jazz music (1); direct address to the audience, especially by Helen (1).
:::

::: q
How does the play end? (2)
---
Helen goes out on learning the baby may be Black (1), leaving Jo alone, singing a nursery rhyme (1).
:::

::: watch
- It is naturalistic, but not only naturalistic: Littlewood's **music and direct address** are valid ideas.
- Design for **working-class Salford, late 1950s**: no modern items, no wealth.
- Play the **comedy**: Helen and Jo's sparring needs precise timing.
- Treat Geof, Boy and Jo's pregnancy **with the play's warmth**, not as stereotypes.
- The flat is the only set: show how it **changes** across the play.
- Always link choices to the audience's response.
:::
