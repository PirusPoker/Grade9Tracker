## What it is

In Section C, one of the three questions is almost always about **performers**. It follows a set pattern: "Describe how [one or two or more] actors used their vocal and physical skills to [a focus]. Analyse and evaluate how successful they were in [that focus] for the audience." Below it are prompts: "You could make reference to vocal skills, for example pitch, pace and tone of voice; physical skills, for example body language and facial expression; a scene or section and/or the production as a whole." It is worth **32 marks**.

### The focuses to prepare for

| Focus | What it needs from your moments |
|---|---|
| **A relationship** between two or more characters | interaction: proxemics, eye contact, touch, how they speak *to* each other, how the relationship develops |
| **Conflict** between characters | escalation: volume, pace, overlapping lines, advancing and retreating, status shifts |
| **Comedy or tension** | timing: pauses, pace, reactions, physical comedy, stillness, building to a release |
| **A character**, or how a character **changed** | contrasting moments at the start and the end, and the turning point |
| **A key moment** or climax | slow-motion detail of one scene, with its build-up and aftermath |
| **Mood or atmosphere** created by actors | ensemble work, stillness, rhythm, direct address, the whole stage picture |

Whatever the focus, **every paragraph must serve it**. A beautiful description of an actor's grief earns little AO4 credit in a question about comedy unless you connect it to the focus.

### How it is marked: two assessment objectives

- **AO3, 12 marks: knowledge and understanding.** How wide a range of skills you describe, and how precisely. Bands: 10–12 excellent range with exact detail; 7–9 good range, clear with a number of precise details; 4–6 fair range, some detail; 1–3 narrow and vague.
- **AO4, 20 marks: analysis and evaluation.** How well you explain *what* each choice communicated and *how*, and how convincingly you judge *how successful* it was. Bands: 16–20 perceptive, detailed analysis with convincing, justified judgements throughout; 11–15 clear, developed analysis and judgements; 6–10 some analysis, often slipping into description; 1–5 description or unsupported opinion.

**Twenty of the 32 marks are for analysis and evaluation.** An answer full of excellent description but with thin judgements can score 11 for AO3 and 8 for AO4, which is 19 out of 32. Balance is everything.

### The paragraph formula: describe, analyse, evaluate

Build the answer from **four or five moments**, each a paragraph with three steps.

1. **Describe** (AO3). Name the actor or character, the moment and the exact skills: "In the scene where Ed confesses, the actor playing Ed sat on the floor at arm's length from Christopher, his voice dropping to a hoarse whisper, hands open on his knees."
2. **Analyse** (AO4). Say what this communicated and how: "The low level and open hands showed a father making himself as unthreatening as possible, and the whisper suggested shame."
3. **Evaluate** (AO4). Judge how successful it was and why, and for whom: "This was highly effective, because the audience, who had just seen Ed's temper, was pulled into pitying him, and the silence in the auditorium showed how completely we were held."

Evaluation needs **reasons and evidence**, not adjectives. "It was really good" is an opinion. "It was successful because the pause let the audience realise the truth before Christopher did, so the tension was almost unbearable" is a justified judgement.

### Evaluative language

- *Degree*: highly effective, largely successful, only partly convincing, less successful.
- *Why*: because..., which meant that..., so the audience...
- *Evidence*: the audience laughed / fell silent / gasped; I felt...; the contrast with the earlier scene made...
- *Comparison*: more powerful than..., in contrast to the opening..., this built on...
- *Balance*: although..., one weaker moment was..., however... A top-band answer can criticise, as long as the criticism is reasoned.

### Structure and timing

Spend about **45 minutes**.
- **Introduction (2–3 sentences):** the production, when and how you saw it, its style, and a one-line summary of the actors' approach to the focus.
- **Four or five moment paragraphs,** chosen from across the production so you can show development. Each follows describe, analyse, evaluate.
- **A brief conclusion:** an overall judgement about how successful the performances were in the focus, with the production's aims in mind.

Write about **more than one actor** if the question says "two or more", and include the *other* actor's skills when you describe an interaction. Examiners notice when a pre-learnt answer knows only one performer.

### A worked model: The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time

The model below uses the National Theatre production (adapted by Simon Stephens, directed by Marianne Elliott, movement by Frantic Assembly's Scott Graham and Steven Hoggett). It shows the method. Casts changed across the production's many runs and tours, so if you saw it, describe *your* performance from your own notes. Otherwise, substitute your own production.

::: terms
- **Describe**: give an exact account of what was done, such as the skill, the moment and precise detail.
- **Analyse**: explain what a choice communicated to the audience and how it did so.
- **Evaluate**: judge how successful a choice was, giving reasons and evidence.
- **Vocal skills**: the performer's use of voice, including pitch, pace, pause, tone, volume, accent and emphasis.
- **Physical skills**: the performer's use of body, including posture, gait, gesture, facial expression and eye contact.
- **Proxemics**: the distance between performers, showing the relationship between their characters.
- **Ensemble**: a group of actors working together to create images, movement and atmosphere.
- **Multi-roling**: one actor playing several different characters in a production.
- **Physical theatre**: a style in which movement, lifts and the body tell the story as much as words do.
- **Justified judgement**: an evaluation supported by a reason and evidence from the performance or the audience's response.
:::

::: method
1. **Underline the focus** (relationship, conflict, comedy or tension, character, mood) and the number of actors required.
2. **Introduce the production** in two or three sentences: the title, the company, how you saw it, and its style.
3. **Choose four or five moments across the production** that best serve the focus, showing development.
4. **In each paragraph, describe** the precise vocal and physical skills of named actors or characters at that moment.
5. **Analyse** what those skills communicated about the focus and how.
6. **Evaluate** how successful they were, with a reason and evidence (the audience's response, contrast, your own reaction), and include at least one balanced or critical point.
7. **Conclude** with an overall judgement linked to the production's aims. Spend about 45 minutes.
:::

::: example Model paragraphs: a relationship (Christopher and Ed)
Describe how two or more actors used their vocal and physical skills to show a relationship between their characters. Analyse and evaluate how successful they were in showing this relationship to the audience. (32)

*Two model paragraphs from a full answer. A complete answer would add two or three more moments and a conclusion.*

In the NT production of The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time, the relationship between Christopher and his father Ed was shown through touch, or rather the careful absence of it. Early in the play, when Christopher is upset, Ed does not hug him. Instead he holds up his right hand with the fingers spread wide, and Christopher, after a long pause, presses his own fingers against his father's, palm not quite touching (AO3: precise physical detail). The actor playing Christopher kept his eyes fixed on the hands rather than his father's face, and the actor playing Ed kept very still and quiet, his voice softening, as if one sudden move might break the contact (AO3). This showed a love that has had to find its own language: Ed has learned what his son can bear, and the gesture replaces the hug he cannot give (AO4: analysis). It was highly successful because the gesture became a motif. When Ed held up his hand again later, after breaking his son's trust, the audience instantly understood how much hung on whether Christopher would answer it (AO4: evaluation using comparison).

The same relationship was tested in the scene where Ed finds that Christopher has been investigating after being told to stop. The actor playing Ed moved fast from the doorway into Christopher's space, his volume rising and his pace quickening until the words ran together, and he grabbed Christopher's arm. The actor playing Christopher collapsed to the floor, curling up with his hands over his ears, and made a high, repetitive noise (AO3). The contrast between Ed's escalating, uncontrolled anger and Christopher's retreat into himself showed the gulf between them: Ed reacts with a father's temper, and Christopher experiences it as an attack on his senses (AO4). I found this the most effective moment of the relationship, because the sudden speed and noise broke the calm of the earlier scenes, and the audience's shocked silence showed that we now feared for Christopher. My only reservation is that the moment was so fast that some of Ed's words were lost, although this could itself have been a deliberate choice to put us in Christopher's position (AO4: balanced judgement).
:::

::: example Model paragraph: ensemble and tension
Write one paragraph describing, analysing and evaluating how the ensemble created tension in a key moment. (8)

In the London sequence of Curious Incident, the ensemble surrounded Christopher on the Underground platform, moving in fast, criss-crossing lines, brushing past him and turning sharply at the last moment (1). They spoke fragments of station announcements and signs over one another at rising volume, building into a wall of noise (1). The actor playing Christopher stood rooted at the centre, rigid, shoulders up to his ears and eyes squeezed shut, rocking slightly (1). The crowd's speed and closeness showed the city as Christopher experiences it, as a threat coming from every side, and his stillness in the middle made him look tiny and exposed (1). The tension came from the gap between the ordinary situation of a busy station and his terror (1). This was very successful because the audience could not look anywhere calm: the whole stage was in motion (1), so we felt a little of his overload rather than simply watching it (1). It was more gripping than a naturalistic crowd, because the choreography was precise enough that every near-collision made the audience flinch (1).
:::

::: example Improving evaluation
A student writes: "The actor was really good in this scene and it was very emotional." Rewrite this as a justified evaluation. (3)

The actor's decision to stop mid-sentence and turn away upstage, rather than cry openly, was highly effective (1), because it let the audience fill the silence with the character's grief, so the moment felt private and true (1). The stillness in the auditorium, and the fact that I noticed people around me holding their breath, showed how completely the audience was engaged (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between analysis and evaluation in Section C. (2)
---
Analysis explains what a choice communicated and how (1); evaluation judges how successful it was, with reasons and evidence (1).
:::

::: q
Why is it risky to spend most of a Section C answer describing? (2)
---
Description earns AO3 (12 marks) (1), but 20 of the 32 marks are AO4 for analysis and evaluation, so a descriptive answer is capped well below its potential (1).
:::

::: q
Rewrite this as a precise description: "She walked in and was sad." (3)
---
Up to 3 for precise skills tied to the moment: e.g. slow, dragging gait from the door upstage left (1); shoulders rounded, head down, arms wrapped around herself (1); a quiet, flat tone and long pauses between words, not meeting anyone's eyes (1).
:::

::: q
Give three phrases that show a degree of success in an evaluation. (3)
---
Any three (1 each): highly effective; largely successful; only partly convincing; less successful; completely convincing; the most powerful moment; not as effective as.
:::

::: q
Write one paragraph describing, analysing and evaluating how an actor used vocal skills to create comedy in a production you have seen. (6)
---
Describe (2): precise vocal skills (timing, pause, pitch, accent, emphasis) tied to a line or moment. Analyse (2): how this created comedy (surprise, contrast, character). Evaluate (2): how successful, with evidence (audience laughter, build of the running joke) and a reason.
:::

::: q
Explain why you should describe both actors' skills when writing about a relationship. (2)
---
A relationship is shown through interaction, so the other actor's reactions are part of how meaning is created (1); describing only one performer suggests limited knowledge and narrows the range for AO3 (1).
:::

::: watch
- Twenty of the 32 marks are for analysis and evaluation. Retelling the plot or describing without judging keeps the answer below half marks.
- Losing the focus of the question, e.g. writing about character when the question asks about a relationship or comedy.
- Writing about only one actor when the question says "two or more", or ignoring the other actor in an interaction.
- Unjustified opinions: "it was amazing". Every judgement needs a reason and evidence.
- Vague description ("he acted angry"). Name the skills, the words and the moments.
- Choosing moments from only one scene, which shows no development across the production.
- Misusing terms (loud pitch, high tone) or confusing actors' names with characters' names. Using character names is fine.
:::

::: test
Explain why including a balanced or critical judgement can strengthen an evaluation. (2)
---
It shows independent, critical thinking, weighing success rather than just praising (1), and makes positive judgements more convincing because they are clearly reasoned (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how choosing moments from across the whole production can improve an answer about how a character changed. (3)
---
Contrasting early and late moments shows the change clearly (1); a turning point explains why it happened (1); it lets you evaluate how convincingly the actor charted the journey (1).
