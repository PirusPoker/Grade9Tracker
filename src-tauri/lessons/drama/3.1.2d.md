## What it is

The last part of Section B is a choice between x.4 (performer) and x.5 (designer), each worth **20 marks**, the biggest question in Section B. Every student must be ready to answer as a **performer**. The question has the same shape every year. "You are performing the role of X. Describe how you would use your acting skills to interpret X's character. Explain why your ideas are appropriate for **this extract** and for **the performance of your role in the play as a whole**."

That gives you four demands:
1. **Interpretation**: a clear idea of who this character is and what drives them.
2. **Acting skills**: a wide range of vocal and physical skills, described precisely at specific moments.
3. **This extract**: moments from the printed extract.
4. **The play as a whole**: moments from elsewhere in the play that show the character's journey, and how the extract fits into it.

**How it is marked.** Four bands.
- **16–20**: excellent, exact ideas; an extensive range of skills, precisely targeted; entirely appropriate for the character; excellent knowledge of the whole play.
- **11–15**: clear, secure ideas; a wide range of skills; good knowledge of the play.
- **6–10**: some range and detail; some relevance; limited use of the wider play.
- **1–5**: a narrow range, little detail, little sense of the character or the play.

An answer that never leaves the extract can't reach the top band. An answer that retells the plot, or discusses the playwright's message without describing any acting, stays low.

### Step one: an interpretation, not a label

"Mickey is poor" is a label. "Mickey is a boy whose energy and humour are slowly crushed by poverty and unemployment, so I want the audience to see the lively seven-year-old still inside the broken man at the end" is an **interpretation**. It gives every later choice a purpose. Build it from:
- **objectives and motivation**: what the character wants in the extract and across the play;
- **the journey**: how they change, and where the turning points are;
- **relationships**: whom they love, fear, resent or depend on;
- **context**: age, class, period and place, which affect accent, posture, manners and movement.

Avoid misreadings that ignore evidence in the play. For example, playing Proctor's tearing-up of his confession as weakness misses that it is a moral victory. Playing a worried parent as simply aggressive misses what drives them.

### Step two: the range of skills

| Vocal | Physical | Also counts |
|---|---|---|
| accent, pitch, pace, pause, volume, tone, emphasis, inflection, timbre, phrasing | posture, gait, gesture, facial expression, eye contact, movement, stillness, proxemics, levels | **casting and appearance** (build, age, height, facial features), **playing age**, **interaction** with other characters, **use of space**, **direct address** where the play uses it, **song or movement** where the play demands it |

"Extensive range" means you use many of these, and in different ways at different moments, not "a loud voice" five times.

### Step three: moments

Plan **three or four moments in the extract** and **two or three moments elsewhere in the play**. For each, describe the skills precisely (pinned to a word or action), then explain why they fit: what they show about the character and what the audience feels. Link the extract to the rest of the play. The extract should plant something that pays off later, or echo or contrast with an earlier moment.

There are several valid structures:
- **Extract first, then the whole play**: the most common and the safest.
- **Paired moments**: each extract moment paired with a later or earlier moment it echoes or contrasts ("her silence here returns in the final scene...").
- **Chronological journey**: this works, but it risks squeezing the extract. Give the extract at least half of the answer.

### Timing and length

About **25 minutes and 550–700 words**. Answer x.1–x.3 first. They make you read the extract closely, and your ideas for the 20-marker will be better for it.

### Interpretation starters from the set plays

| Character | An interpretation to test in your own copy |
|---|---|
| **John Proctor** (The Crucible) | a proud, practical farmer tormented by guilt over Abigail, whose journey is from self-contempt to reclaiming his name; physical strength and rough, plain speech gradually stripped away until the final choice restores his upright dignity |
| **Mickey** (Blood Brothers) | a quick, cheeky, generous boy worn down by class and circumstance; the adult actor plays seven with energy and restless physicality, never on their knees, then the energy drains through unemployment, prison and dependence on pills |
| **Juliet** (Romeo and Juliet) | a thirteen-year-old who becomes decisive and brave faster than anyone around her; from obedient daughter to a young woman who defies her father and faces the tomb alone |
| **Jo** (A Taste of Honey) | sharp, funny and defensive, using wit as armour against Helen's neglect; tenderness shows through with Boy and Geof, and fear shows as the baby approaches |
| **Phileas Fogg** (Around the World in 80 Days) | a man ruled by clocks and routine, whose precise, restrained physicality loosens as the journey and Aouda change him |
| **Rosie** (Things I Know to Be True) | the youngest, whose certainties about her family fall apart over the year; her opening monologue's hurt and hope set up a journey towards independence |

::: terms
- **Interpretation**: the performer's view of who the character is, what drives them and how they change, which shapes every acting choice.
- **Objective**: what a character wants in a scene or across the play.
- **Motivation**: the reason behind a character's actions and objectives.
- **Character journey**: how a character changes from their first appearance to their last, including turning points.
- **Playing age**: the age an actor portrays, which may differ from their real age, e.g. adults playing children.
- **Casting**: choosing an actor whose build, age, height or features suit the interpretation of a role.
- **Status**: a character's power or importance in relation to others, shown through posture, space and voice.
- **Extract**: the printed section of the set play on which the Section B questions are based.
- **Play as a whole**: moments from the rest of the set play outside the printed extract.
- **Range of skills**: the variety of vocal and physical skills an answer uses across different moments.
:::

::: method
1. **Write a one-sentence interpretation**: who the character is, what they want and how they change.
2. **Choose three or four extract moments**, each a line or action. Include one where the character's feelings change or are hidden.
3. **Choose two or three moments from the rest of the play** that show the journey: an earlier moment that contrasts, a turning point, and the final appearance.
4. **For every moment, describe vocal and physical skills precisely** (pinned to words and actions) and add casting, space or interaction where they help.
5. **Explain why each choice fits** the character and the moment, and what it makes the audience feel or understand.
6. **Link the extract to the whole play**: say how the choices you make in the extract are echoed, reversed or paid off later.
7. **Vary the skills.** Don't repeat "loud and angry". Spend about 25 minutes.
:::

::: example Juliet: extract from Act Three, Scene Five (model extracts)
You are performing the role of Juliet in the extract where her parents order her to marry Paris. Describe how you would use your acting skills to interpret Juliet's character. Explain why your ideas are appropriate for this extract and for the performance of your role in the play as a whole. (20)

*The paragraphs below model the top band. A full answer would add one more extract moment.*

My interpretation is of a thirteen-year-old who grows up faster than anyone around her realises. In this scene she learns she has no allies, and her courage becomes a quiet, fixed determination (1). I would be cast as a slight, young-looking actor, so her defiance against a physically bigger, older father is all the more striking (1).

When Lady Capulet enters, Juliet has just parted from Romeo after their wedding night, and her face is still wet. I wipe my eyes quickly with the heel of my hand and stand very straight, chin up, to hide my grief behind the cover story that she mourns Tybalt (1). My pitch is controlled and slightly too even, and I stress the double meanings, for example leaning on "Romeo" with a tiny pause before it, so the audience enjoys the dramatic irony while her mother notices nothing (1).

When she hears she must marry Paris, her voice rises sharply in pitch and volume on the refusal, and I step backwards away from my mother. This is the first time we see Juliet openly defy a parent, and the shock ripples through the room (1). When Capulet rages, I sink to my knees downstage centre and reach for his hand, which he shakes off. My voice drops to a pleading, breathless whisper, and the pace breaks up with gulps of breath (1). The low level and his rejection of her touch show the audience a child cut off by her father, building pity (1).

Left with the Nurse, I lift my face to her, open and trusting, then freeze as she advises marrying Paris. There is a long pause, my face empties, and I say my thanks with icy, over-polite calm and a small, stiff smile (1). My stillness and controlled tone show the moment Juliet stops being a child: she will act alone (1). Alone at the end, I turn slowly towards the door the Nurse used and speak my resolve to go to the Friar in a low, steady voice with my hands clenched at my sides. The audience sees her courage and fears where it will lead (1).

**The play as a whole.** In Act One, Scene Three the same actor plays her obedient and a little shy with her mother, eyes lowered and hands folded, with a soft, dutiful tone on her promise to look on Paris only as far as her mother allows (1). The contrast with the defiance in Act Three shows how far love has changed her. In the balcony scene her physicality is light and quick, leaning over the rail, laughing, and her pace is fast and excited, which shows the joy the extract now takes away from her (1). In the potion scene (Act Four, Scene Three) I use the stillness and low, controlled voice I found at the end of the extract, now breaking under fear: my pitch climbs and the pace races as she imagines waking in the tomb, before I drink in one decisive movement (1). In the tomb, her last action is quick and certain, with no hesitation, because the fixed resolve we saw born in Act Three, Scene Five is now complete (1). Across the play the audience watches a girl become the bravest person on stage, which is what makes the ending tragic (1).
:::

::: example Planning: linking the extract to the whole play
You are performing Mickey in an extract from the first act of Blood Brothers where he is seven. Give three moments from the rest of the play you could use, and explain how each links to your performance in the extract. (6)

His fourteen-year-old self, awkward and embarrassed with Linda, turning away and mumbling (1): the energy of seven is still there but self-conscious, so the same restless hands now fidget in his pockets (1). The scene where he has lost his job and is losing his grip on his friendship with Edward (1): the bouncing gait of the seven-year-old has slowed and his shoulders drop, showing what poverty has taken from him (1). The final scene with the gun (1): his voice cracks back into the high, fast pitch of the child in the extract, reminding the audience of the boy who became Edward's blood brother, which deepens the tragedy (1).
:::

::: example Proctor: interpretation in one paragraph
Write the opening paragraph (interpretation) for a 20-mark answer on John Proctor in an extract from Act Two of The Crucible. (4)

I see Proctor as a proud, physically strong farmer in his thirties who is eaten up by guilt about his affair with Abigail, so he swings between wanting to be a good husband and resenting being judged (1). I would cast an actor with a broad, solid build and weathered face, who moves heavily like a man who has worked the fields all day (1). His objective in this extract is to win back Elizabeth's warmth, and his voice is warm and a little too hearty at first, then snaps into anger when her suspicion touches his guilt (1). Across the play this guilt drives him from shame to reclaiming his good name at the end, so I want the audience to see in this domestic scene the tension between pride and conscience that the play resolves (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between labelling a character and interpreting them. Give an example. (3)
---
A label is a single trait ("Helen is selfish") (1); an interpretation explains what drives the character and how they change, giving the performance a purpose (1); e.g. "Helen is a woman terrified of growing old and alone, whose selfishness is a way of grabbing what pleasure she can" (1).
:::

::: q
Why should an answer to the 20-mark performer question include moments from outside the extract? (2)
---
The question asks for ideas appropriate for the role in the play as a whole (1); without them the answer cannot show knowledge of the character's journey, and so it cannot reach the top band (1).
:::

::: q
Give four aspects of casting or physical appearance a performer might consider when interpreting a role. (4)
---
Build (1); age / playing age (1); height (1); facial features (1). Also accept: voice quality, colouring, fitness for physical demands.
:::

::: q
Explain how an adult performer could play a seven-year-old convincingly without kneeling. (3)
---
Any three precise ideas (1 each): quick, restless energy, rarely still; a higher pitch and fast pace, words tumbling out; a bouncing or skipping gait; sitting on the floor with legs sprawled; fidgeting with a toy or a sleeve; wide-eyed facial expressions; direct, unfiltered reactions.
:::

::: q
Explain what "an extensive range of skills" means in the 20-mark question. (2)
---
Using many different vocal and physical skills (and casting, space, interaction) rather than repeating the same one or two (1), varied to suit different moments of the extract and the play (1).
:::

::: q
In about 150 words, write one paragraph describing and explaining how you would perform one moment from the extract of the set play you have studied. (6)
---
2 marks: precise vocal skill pinned to a word; 2 marks: precise physical skill at the same moment; 2 marks: explained effect and link to interpretation. Deduct if vague or if the effect does not fit the character.
:::

::: watch
- Staying inside the extract caps the answer below the top band. The question explicitly asks about the role in the play as a whole.
- Retelling the plot, or writing at length about the playwright's message, instead of describing acting.
- No clear interpretation, so the skills are random rather than purposeful.
- A narrow range of skills: "loud and angry" at every moment.
- Misreading the character at a key moment (e.g. treating a moral victory as weakness, or an anxious parent as a bully).
- A chronological journey that leaves the extract with a sentence or two. Give it at least half.
- Writing the 20-marker first. Answering x.1–x.3 first gets you into the extract.
:::

---
Moment one with precise skills showing status (1), e.g. upright posture, centre stage, slow deliberate pace, others waiting on them; moment two contrasted (1), e.g. hunched, pushed to the side, hesitant voice; explanation of what caused the change (1); effect on the audience, e.g. sympathy or satisfaction (1).

---
Extract first, then the whole play (1): secure coverage of the extract before showing the journey (1). Paired moments (1): each extract moment is linked to an echo or contrast elsewhere, which shows the arc clearly (1). Also accept chronological journey, with the risk that the extract is squeezed.

---
It gives every skill a purpose, so choices are precisely targeted at the character (1); it keeps the answer coherent across extract and whole play, showing a journey (1); it shows appropriateness, which is part of the top-band criteria, rather than a list of random skills (1).

::: test

---
