## What it is

Two pieces of terminology run through the whole paper: **stage positions** (where an actor or object is on the stage) and **staging configurations** (how the stage and the audience are arranged). Section A tests them directly with 1-mark multiple-choice questions, often from a diagram. Sections B and C use them constantly. A 12-mark answer that says "I would move downstage left, away from him" is far more precise than "I would move away".

### Stage positions

Stage directions are always given **from the actor's point of view as they face the audience**. Stage left is the actor's left, which is the audience's right. **Upstage** is the back of the stage, furthest from the audience. **Downstage** is the front, nearest the audience.

The terms come from the old **raked stage**, which sloped upwards away from the audience so that everyone could see the actors at the back. An actor walking away from the audience literally walked *up* the stage.

Here is the stage seen from the auditorium, with the audience at the bottom:

| | *Audience's left* | | *Audience's right* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Upstage** (back) | Upstage right (USR) | Upstage centre (USC) | Upstage left (USL) |
| **Middle** | Stage right (SR) | **Centre stage** (CS) | Stage left (SL) |
| **Downstage** (front) | Downstage right (DSR) | Downstage centre (DSC) | Downstage left (DSL) |
| | | **AUDIENCE** | |

The specification names **upstage left, right and centre; downstage left, right and centre; and centre stage**. Learn the grid until you can draw it in ten seconds.

**What positions communicate.** **Downstage centre** is the strongest, most exposed position, used for a soliloquy, a confession or a key reveal. **Upstage centre** is also commanding on a proscenium stage, because other actors must turn to face the character there. This is why "upstaging" means pulling focus. Positions far **upstage and to the side** feel distant or isolated. Moving **downstage** towards the audience increases intimacy or confrontation. Moving **upstage** can show retreat, or can draw the other actor into turning away from the audience.

Other useful terms:
- **Wings**: the hidden areas at the sides of a proscenium stage, used for entrances and storing scenery.
- **Apron**: the part of the stage in front of the proscenium arch (or in front of the curtain line).
- **Fly tower / flies**: the space above the stage where scenery hangs, ready to be lowered.
- **Auditorium**: where the audience sits. **Sightlines**: the lines of view from audience seats to the stage. **Masking**: blocking the audience's view, either on purpose (black drapes hide the wings) or by mistake (an actor stands in front of another).

### The six staging configurations

| Configuration | Where the audience is | Advantages | Challenges |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proscenium arch** | On one side, looking through a frame (the arch) like a picture | A clear "picture frame" and a strong fourth wall for naturalism; wings and flies hide scenery and allow big scene changes; curtains (tabs) can open and close; everyone sees the same stage picture | Distance from the audience makes intimacy harder; the back rows can feel far away; the frame separates actors from audience |
| **End on** | On one side, facing the stage, but with **no arch** | Simple and flexible; common in studios and black boxes; similar clear sightlines to proscenium; backdrops and projection work well | Less hidden space for storage and changes; the audience still sees the action from one direction only |
| **Thrust** | On **three** sides of a stage that pushes out into the audience | Intimate, with many people close to the action; a back wall remains for scenery and entrances; suits Shakespeare, which was written for a thrust-like playhouse | Actors must turn and move so no side sees only their back; tall set pieces downstage block the side seats' view; set mostly goes at the back |
| **Traverse** | On **two** sides, facing each other, with the acting area like a corridor or catwalk between | Very intimate; the audience sees the other audience reacting behind the actors; good for journeys, conflict between two ends, processions | Only low set, or set at the two open ends; actors must play to both sides; sightlines are blocked if actors stand in a line along the stage |
| **Theatre in the round** | On **all** sides (circle or square) | The most intimate; everyone is close; actors are seen from every angle, which suits intense, naturalistic, "fly on the wall" drama | No back wall, so scenery must be low, on the floor or flown; entrances come through the audience (voms or aisles); actors must keep moving to share their faces |
| **Promenade** | The audience **stands and moves**, following the action to different areas of the space | Immersive: the audience becomes part of the world; scenes can happen in several locations, including real sites | Crowd control and safety; the audience may miss things or see them from a poor angle; performers must guide the crowd; lighting and sound must follow the action |

A quick way to remember the numbers: **end on and proscenium = 1 side; traverse = 2; thrust = 3; in the round = 4; promenade = the audience moves**.

Real examples help in Section C. The Royal Exchange in Manchester is a theatre in the round. The Royal Shakespeare Theatre in Stratford and the Crucible Theatre in Sheffield have thrust stages. Most large West End theatres have proscenium arches. Immersive companies such as Punchdrunk stage promenade productions in which the audience wanders through a building.

### Implications for the use of space

The specification asks for "a general understanding of the implications" of each configuration. That means three questions:

- **Sightlines and blocking.** In the round and on a thrust, actors cannot stand still facing one direction for long. Directors use diagonals, circling moves and actors placed at opposite corners so every section sees a face. On a proscenium, actors "cheat out", turning their bodies partly towards the audience.
- **Set.** Proscenium and end on can use tall flats and backdrops. Thrust keeps tall set upstage. Traverse keeps it at the two ends. In the round needs set that is low, see-through or flown above.
- **The actor-audience relationship.** Proscenium creates distance and a sense of watching through a window. The closer, more wrap-around configurations make the audience feel involved, and even complicit. Traverse lets them see each other's reactions. Promenade puts them inside the action.

::: terms
- **Upstage**: the area of the stage furthest from the audience.
- **Downstage**: the area of the stage nearest to the audience.
- **Stage left**: the actor's left as they stand on stage facing the audience (the audience's right).
- **Proscenium arch**: a staging configuration where the audience faces the stage through a frame, like looking at a picture.
- **End on**: a configuration with the audience on one side facing the stage, without a proscenium frame.
- **Thrust stage**: a stage that projects into the auditorium, with the audience on three sides.
- **Traverse**: a configuration with the audience on two opposite sides and the acting area between them.
- **Theatre in the round**: a configuration with the audience on all sides of the acting area.
- **Promenade**: a form of staging in which the audience stands and moves to follow the action.
- **Sightlines**: the lines of view between audience members and the stage, which decide what each seat can see.
- **Apron**: the part of a proscenium stage that extends in front of the arch or curtain line.
- **Raked stage**: a stage that slopes upwards away from the audience, the origin of "upstage" and "downstage".
:::

::: method
1. **For a position question**, imagine yourself standing on the stage looking at the audience. Left and right are yours. The back is up, the front is down.
2. **For a diagram**, find where the audience is first. Everything else follows from that.
3. **For a configuration question**, count the sides the audience sits on: 1 with a frame (proscenium), 1 without (end on), 2 (traverse), 3 (thrust), 4 (round), or moving (promenade).
4. **For "which statement is correct"**, test each option against the audience layout. Curtains belong to proscenium. Facing each other is traverse. Following the action is promenade.
5. **In longer answers**, name the configuration, then say what it means for **sightlines, set and the relationship with the audience**, with one precise example of blocking.
:::

::: example Reading a diagram
A diagram shows a rectangular acting area with rows of seats along both long sides, facing each other. The short ends have no seats. What type of staging is shown?

A Thrust stage
B Traverse stage
C Theatre in the round

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)

B (1). The audience is on two opposite sides only.
:::

::: example Naming a position
On an end on stage, an actor stands at the very back of the stage, on the side that is the audience's right. What is the actor's stage position?

A Upstage left
B Upstage right
C Downstage left

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)

A (1). The back is upstage. The audience's right is the actor's left.
:::

::: example Advantages and disadvantages
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of staging a tense family argument in the round. (4)

Advantage: the audience sits close on all sides, so they feel trapped inside the argument, as if they were at the family table (1). This intimacy lets small details, such as a shaking hand or a tear, register strongly and increases the tension (1).

Disadvantage: some of the audience will always see an actor's back at the key moment (1), so the actors must keep moving, for example circling the table or placing the two opponents at opposite corners, which could make a naturalistic scene feel restless (1).
:::

::: example Using a thrust stage
A scene shows a soldier returning home to find his house empty. Explain how you would use a thrust stage to stage his entrance and first moments. (6)

I would place the doorway flat at the back wall, upstage centre, so the soldier enters framed and small with the whole empty room in front of him (1). He would pause in the doorway in silence, letting all three sides of the audience see the room before he does (1). He would then walk slowly downstage along the central line, the thrust's strongest path, checking the objects left behind (1). Reaching downstage centre, the tip of the thrust, he would be surrounded by the audience on three sides (1), so his reaction on discovering the place is empty happens almost among them, making it intimate and painful (1). I would keep furniture low, such as a stool and a trunk, so the side seats' sightlines are clear (1).
:::

::: q
In which staging configuration are the audience on three sides of the stage?

A Thrust
B Traverse
C End on

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)
---
A, thrust (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why actors' left and right are used for stage directions rather than the audience's. (2)
---
Directions are written for the actors and crew working on the stage (1), so they are given from the actor's viewpoint facing the audience, which stays consistent in every theatre (1).
:::

::: q
Give two differences between a proscenium arch stage and an end on stage. (2)
---
Proscenium has a frame (arch) around the stage opening, while end on has no frame (1); proscenium theatres usually have wings, curtains and a fly tower for scenery, while end on studios usually have less hidden space (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one challenge of using tall scenery on a traverse stage. (2)
---
Tall scenery along either side would block one audience's view entirely (1), so tall pieces can only go at the two open ends, which limits the design (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of promenade staging for the audience. (4)
---
Advantage: the audience is immersed in the world of the play, moving through the locations and feeling part of the action (2). Disadvantage: they may not always get a good view or may miss parts of the action, and standing or moving for a long time can be tiring or uncomfortable (2).
:::

::: q
Explain what "upstaging" means and why it happens. (2)
---
Upstaging is when an actor positioned upstage takes the focus of the scene (1), because the other actors must turn their backs to the audience to face them, so the upstage actor's face is the one the audience sees (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Left and right reversed.** Stage left is the actor's left facing the audience. Check every diagram by finding the audience first.
- **Thrust and traverse confused.** Thrust has three sides of audience. Traverse has two sides, facing each other.
- **Proscenium and end on confused.** Both have the audience on one side. Only the proscenium has the arch, curtains and usually wings and flies.
- **"The audience can see better".** Every configuration has advantages. Say *who* can see *what*, and why.
- **Using configurations without implications.** Naming "in the round" earns little. Say what it does to sightlines, set height and intimacy in the scene you are discussing.
:::

::: test
Explain why a set designer working in the round must think carefully about the height of the set. (2)
---
The audience is on all sides, so any tall piece will block the view of the people sitting behind it (1). Set must be low, see-through, on the floor or flown above the acting area (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage of a proscenium arch stage for a naturalistic play. (2)
---
The frame creates a clear fourth wall, so the audience feels they are watching real life through a window (1); wings and flies allow a detailed, realistic box set and scene changes to be hidden (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of a thrust stage for a production of a Shakespeare play. (4)
---
Advantage (2): the audience on three sides is close to the action, like the playhouses Shakespeare wrote for; soliloquies delivered from the tip of the thrust feel shared with the audience. Disadvantage (2): some sections will see actors' backs, so blocking must keep moving; tall scenery can only go upstage, which limits the set.
:::

::: test
Explain how an actor's stage position could show that their character is isolated from a family group. (3)
---
The family is grouped together centre stage or around a table (1); the isolated character is placed far upstage, or at a distant corner such as upstage left, away from the group (1); distance and position show emotional separation and draw the audience's attention to their exclusion, for example if they are lit separately (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the implications of traverse staging for the actors in a scene between two characters who are arguing. (4)
---
The actors must play to both sides of the audience, so they should open out rather than face one side (1); placing each character at an opposite end of the traverse uses the length of the space to show the gap between them (1); moves towards each other along the corridor build tension as they close the distance (1); each audience sees the other audience's reactions behind the actors, which heightens the sense of watching a private conflict (1).
:::

::: test
A scene shows a mother and her teenage son arguing in a cramped kitchen late at night. The argument ends with the son storming out.

Explain how you would use a thrust stage to stage this scene, making reference to stage positions. (12)
---
Levels. 10–12: an exact, well-developed explanation that uses the thrust's shape throughout, with precise stage positions, moves and effects tied to moments in the scene. 7–9: a clear explanation with a number of precise positions and moves and clear effects. 4–6: a reasonably clear explanation with some positions but general effects, or limited use of the thrust's features. 1–3: a limited answer with few positions or little awareness of the configuration.

Indicative content: the kitchen placed so its back wall and door are upstage, with a small table and two chairs just downstage of centre; the cramped feel created by keeping the acting area tight, with low furniture so the side seats see clearly; the mother starts seated at the table, centre stage, facing downstage; the son enters from upstage left; as the argument builds he circles the table, which lets every side of the audience see both faces; the mother stands and blocks his path downstage centre at the peak, bringing the confrontation close to the audience on three sides; proxemics shrink as voices rise; the son pushes past to the upstage door; he pauses in the doorway upstage centre, framed, for a last line; the mother is left alone downstage, so the audience is near her as she reacts in silence. Effects: intimacy, discomfort, the audience feeling trapped in the kitchen with them; the empty space between the downstage mother and the upstage door showing the rift.
:::
