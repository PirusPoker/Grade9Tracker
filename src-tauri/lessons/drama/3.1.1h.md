## What it is

The **set** is the physical world of the production: everything on stage that is not a person, a costume or light. **Props** are the objects the actors use. The specification asks you to know the **design fundamentals** (scale, shape, colour, texture) and the design of **props** and **sets**, including stage technology: **revolves, trucks, projection, multimedia, pyrotechnics, smoke machines and flying**.

On the paper, set design appears in three places. The 4-mark Section B question may ask you to describe a **setting** that reflects the play's context. The 20-mark question lets you answer as a **set designer**. Section C may ask how the set established time and place or created mood. Every mark depends on precise detail *with a reason*: a set that lists furniture without saying what it communicates stays in the bottom bands.

### What a set does

A set can:
- establish **location** (where), **period** (when) and **time** (season, time of day);
- show **social status** and character (a tidy parlour against a squalid room);
- create **mood and atmosphere** (oppressive, open, dreamlike);
- carry **symbolism** (a crumbling wall for a collapsing family);
- make the action **work**: entrances and exits, levels for status, space for fights or dances, fast scene changes;
- suit the **configuration** and the audience's sightlines.

### Design fundamentals

| Fundamental | What to decide | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale** | The size of the set and its parts relative to the actors | Oversized doors that dwarf the characters, making them seem powerless |
| **Shape** | Lines and forms: straight or curved, regular or broken | Harsh angles and diagonals for conflict; curves for a softer, natural world |
| **Colour** | The palette and what it suggests | A washed-out grey-brown palette for poverty; a single red object for danger |
| **Texture** | The surface quality | Rough, splintered timber; peeling wallpaper; polished marble |

Add **materials** (wood, metal, fabric, Perspex), **levels** (rostra, steps, balconies), **space** (open against cluttered) and **dressing** (the small details that make it lived in).

### Types of set

| Type | Description | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Naturalistic / box set** | A realistic room with three walls, real furniture and practical doors and windows | Naturalism; one main location |
| **Composite set** | Several locations shown on stage at once, in different areas | Plays that move between a few places, with no scene changes needed |
| **Permanent / unit set** | One structure that stays throughout and is adapted by lighting and small pieces | Epic, episodic and storytelling plays |
| **Minimalist** | Very few items; a bare space suggesting location through one or two key pieces | Fast pace, focus on the actors, imagination |
| **Abstract / symbolic** | Not realistic; shapes, colours and structures represent ideas | Expressing themes and inner states |
| **Fragmentary / realistic pieces** | A few realistic objects in an open space | A balance of detail and flexibility |

### Materials and components

- **Flats**: timber frames covered with canvas or board, painted to form walls.
- **Cyclorama (cyc)**: a large, smooth, curved or flat cloth at the back of the stage, lit to show sky or colour.
- **Backdrop / cloth**: a painted cloth hung at the back.
- **Gauze**: a fine fabric that looks solid when lit from the front and becomes see-through when what is behind it is lit (used for reveals, dreams and ghosts).
- **Drapes / tabs**: curtains, used for masking or to hide changes.
- **Rostra and treads**: platforms and steps to create levels.
- **Floor coverings**: painted floors, turf, sand, water, dance floor (lino). The floor is the most-seen surface in a raked auditorium or in the round.
- **Furnishings and dressing**: furniture, pictures, rugs and the clutter that gives a place its history.
- **Trapdoor**: an opening in the stage floor for appearances, disappearances or graves.

### Stage technology (named in the specification)

| Device | What it is | What it allows |
|---|---|---|
| **Revolve** | A turntable built into the stage floor | Fast changes between locations; continuous travel; walking against the turn; showing a house from several angles |
| **Truck** (wagon) | A platform on wheels carrying a piece of set | A whole room can roll on and off in seconds; shows several locations quickly |
| **Flying** | Raising and lowering scenery (or actors) from the fly tower above the stage on bars | Instant changes of scenery; spectacular entrances; actors seeming to fly |
| **Projection** | Images or video projected onto a screen, cyc, gauze or the set | Location, time passing, abstract imagery, captions |
| **Multimedia** | Combining live action with video, live camera feeds, screens or recorded sound and images | Showing thoughts, news footage, several perspectives; a contemporary feel |
| **Pyrotechnics** | Controlled explosions, flashes, sparks or flames, operated by a licensed specialist | Gunshots, explosions, magic, fire, with strict safety planning |
| **Smoke machine / haze / dry ice** | Machines producing smoke, fine haze, or low-lying fog | Fog, battlefields, fires, eerie moods; haze makes light beams visible; dry ice or low fog creates mist that clings to the floor |

### Professional examples (use your own words)

- In the National Theatre's production of *An Inspector Calls* directed by Stephen Daldry (designed by Ian MacNeil), the Birlings' Edwardian house sits raised on stilts above a bleak, rain-soaked street. At the play's climax the house tips and partly collapses, a symbol of the family's secure world falling apart.
- The original London production of the musical *Les Misérables* used a large revolve, so the barricade could turn and the characters could march continuously.
- *War Horse* at the National Theatre used a strip-shaped screen hanging above the stage, like a torn page from a sketchbook, with projected drawings showing places and the passing of time.

### Props

**Props** (properties) are objects used by the actors.
- **Personal props** are carried by one actor: a letter, a pocket watch, a knife, a rosary.
- **Hand or set props** stay in the space and are used: a cup, a phone, a broom.
- **Set dressing** is objects that fill the space but are not handled: pictures, ornaments, books.

A good prop tells the audience about character, period and place, and can become symbolic by being repeated (a child's toy that returns in the final scene). Props must be safe: fake weapons, breakaway glass and blunt knives. The stage manager's team keeps them on a props table.

### Planning a design: ground plan and model box

Designers show their ideas in a **ground plan** (a bird's-eye scale drawing of the stage, with the set, entrances, furniture and audience marked) and a **model box** (a scale 3D model). In the exam, a quick labelled sketch or ground plan can support your answer, but the words must still explain each choice.

::: terms
- **Scale**: the size of set elements in relation to the performers and the space.
- **Texture**: the surface quality of materials, such as rough timber, smooth metal or peeling paint.
- **Composite set**: a set showing several locations on stage at the same time.
- **Box set**: a naturalistic set of a room with three walls and the fourth open to the audience.
- **Flat**: a timber frame covered with canvas or board, painted to form part of a set.
- **Cyclorama**: a large cloth or wall at the back of the stage, lit to represent sky or an open background.
- **Revolve**: a turntable in the stage floor that rotates to change or move scenery.
- **Truck**: a wheeled platform carrying a piece of set so it can be moved on and off quickly.
- **Flying**: raising and lowering scenery or performers from the fly tower above the stage.
- **Gauze**: a fabric that appears solid when lit from the front and transparent when lit from behind.
- **Pyrotechnics**: controlled explosions, flashes or flames created by a licensed operator for effect.
- **Ground plan**: a bird's-eye scale drawing of the stage showing the positions of the set.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the brief**: location, period, context, configuration and what the question wants the set to achieve.
2. **Pick the set type** (naturalistic, composite, minimalist, symbolic) and say why it suits the play's style.
3. **Describe precisely**: materials, colour, texture, scale, shape, levels, entrances, key furniture, dressing and props.
4. **Justify each detail** with what it communicates (period, status, mood, symbolism) or what it enables (movement, change, sightlines).
5. **Show how it works in action**: scene changes (truck, revolve, flying), key moments, special effects.
6. **For the 20-mark question**, cover the extract and then the play as a whole: how the set changes or develops across the play.
:::

What is the name of a platform on wheels used to move a piece of set on and off stage?

B A truck
C A flat

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)

B (1).
:::

::: example Fundamentals in use
Explain how a set designer could use scale and colour to make a character appear powerless. (4)

Scale: build the walls and doorway far taller than life size, so the actor looks small and trapped inside them (1), suggesting forces much bigger than the character (1). Colour: a cold, monochrome palette of greys and dull steel (1), so the character, perhaps in one muted colour, looks isolated and drained of energy (1).
:::

::: example Designing a setting (4 marks)
You are designing a setting for a scene in a run-down 1950s boarding house room in a northern English city. Describe your design ideas. (4)

A small box set with walls of faded, brown-and-cream floral wallpaper, peeling at the seams and stained with damp in the upstage corner (1). A narrow iron bed with a thin grey blanket, a scuffed wooden wardrobe with a door that will not quite close, and a single hard chair (1). A cracked sink with one cold tap, a gas meter on the wall and a bare bulb on a flex hanging centre stage (1). A small sash window upstage left with grimy panes, looking out onto a painted cloth of smoky chimneys against a dull sky (1).
:::

::: example Scene change solution
A play moves quickly between a family kitchen, a school corridor and a hospital ward. Explain how a set designer could use stage technology to make the changes smooth. (4)

A composite base, a bare floor with a permanent back wall, is used for all three locations, lit differently for each (1). The kitchen sits on a truck that rolls on from stage right with its table and chairs already set, and rolls off in the blackout (1). Corridor lockers and a hospital bed are also trucked or flown in, so each location has one clear signature piece (1). This lets each change take seconds under a sound transition, keeping the play's pace and making each place instantly recognisable (1).
:::

::: example Props with meaning
Explain how a single prop could be used to show a character's journey through a play. (3)

A daughter's childhood music box is on the mantelpiece in the first scene, polished and cherished by her mother (1). In the middle of the play, during an argument, it is knocked to the floor and broken, marking the family's fracture (1). In the final scene the mother quietly mends it, so the audience reads her hope of reconciliation without it being spoken (1).
:::

::: q
Define "composite set". (1)
---
A set that shows more than one location on stage at the same time (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of using a revolve. (2)
---
It allows fast scene changes by rotating a new location into view (1), keeping the pace and avoiding long blackouts; or it can show continuous movement or a journey (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a gauze could be used to create a dramatic effect. (2)
---
Lit from the front it looks solid, like a wall or painted scene (1); when light is brought up behind it, it becomes see-through and reveals a scene, such as a memory or a ghost, appearing magically (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a personal prop and set dressing. (2)
---
A personal prop is carried and used by one actor, such as a letter or watch (1); set dressing is objects that decorate the set and are not usually handled, such as pictures or ornaments (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two safety considerations when using pyrotechnics or smoke on stage. (4)
---
Any two, 2 marks each: pyrotechnics must be operated by a trained, licensed person, with safety distances from actors and audience; fire regulations and fire-proofed materials; smoke or haze can trigger fire alarms, so detectors may need managing and the theatre must be informed; some audience members may be affected (for example asthma), so warnings are displayed; low fog can make the floor slippery or hide edges.
:::

::: q
Explain how texture could help establish the period and class of a setting. (3)
---
Texture tells the audience about materials and condition (1); rough, splintered wood and worn plaster suggest a poor, older or rural setting (1); smooth, polished wood, velvet and gilt suggest wealth and a grand period interior (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Furniture lists.** Every item needs a reason: what it tells the audience or what it lets the actors do.
- **Vague descriptions.** "A table" earns less than "a long, scrubbed oak table, scarred with knife marks".
- **Ignoring the configuration.** Tall flats in the round block the audience's view.
- **Forgetting how it works.** Say how the scene changes happen and where the entrances are.
- **Machinery for its own sake.** A revolve or pyrotechnic needs a dramatic purpose.
- **Mixing up truck and revolve**, or flats and flies, in Section A.
:::

---
4 levels, 1 mark each. Indicative content: a slightly patchy lawn of artificial turf with worn paths; the back wall of a low brick or weatherboard house with a sliding glass door; a small patio of cracked paving stones with plastic garden chairs and a metal table; a washing line with clothes pegged on it; well-tended rose bushes or flower beds along a wooden fence; a barbecue under a cover; all slightly worn but cared for, showing a working family's pride in their home.

::: test
Explain how a set designer could use levels to show the difference in status between two groups of characters. (4)
---
The powerful group is placed on a raised platform or balcony upstage, reached by steps (1), while the powerless group occupies the lower stage floor (1); the powerful characters literally look down on the others, and must descend to interact (1); the audience instantly reads the hierarchy, and any moment when a character crosses between levels becomes significant (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how a set could be designed to work in a theatre in the round. (4)
---
Keep set pieces low so no section's view is blocked (1); use the floor as the main design surface, such as a painted or textured floor covering (1); locate furniture that can be moved or used from all sides, such as a table or bed (1); tall elements can be flown in above the acting area, and entrances come through the aisles or vomitories (1).
:::

Indicative content: the configuration and set type chosen, with reasons; location, period and context shown through materials, colours, textures, furniture, dressing and props; scale and shape used for meaning (oppression, isolation, grandeur); levels and entrances placed to support key moments in the scene (a confrontation, an arrival, a hiding place); technology used purposefully (truck, revolve, flying, projection, gauze, smoke, pyrotechnics); how the set helps the actors and the audience at the scene's climax; across the play: other locations and how changes happen, how the set develops to reflect the story (becoming barer, darker, more broken), and symbolic motifs repeated throughout.

::: test
Explain how a set designer could use a truck and flying together to change quickly from an interior to an exterior scene. (4)
---
The interior (for example a kitchen with its walls and furniture) is built on a truck, which rolls off into the wings at the end of the scene (1); at the same time, an exterior element such as a street lamp, a tree or a painted backdrop is flown in from the fly tower (1); the change happens under a lighting and sound transition in a few seconds (1), so the play keeps its pace and the audience immediately understands the new location (1).
:::
