## What it is

The second part of your Section B question (x.2) is worth **8 marks**. It always runs the same way. "You are performing the role of X. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform the line below and explain the effects you want to create." One line from the extract is printed underneath.

So there are three demands:
- **vocal skills**: how you say it;
- **physical skills**: what your body and face do while you say it;
- **effects**: what you want the audience to understand or feel, and why that fits the character at this moment.

**How it is marked.** Four bands of two marks.
- **7–8**: exact, detailed description of both vocal and physical skills, with effects that are entirely right for the role and the moment, clearly explained.
- **5–6**: clear description with a number of precise details, and appropriate effects.
- **3–4**: some detail and some relevant effects, but general in places.
- **1–2**: vague ("I would say it angrily and point"), with the effects missing or wrong.

Leaving out voice or body entirely keeps you in the lower bands, however good the half you wrote.

### Precision: pin every skill to a word

The biggest difference between a 4 and an 8 is **where** each choice happens. "I would use a high pitch" is general. "My pitch rises sharply on 'never', and I cut the line off before the last word" is precise. Break the line into **two or three chunks** and give each chunk its own vocal and physical choice and its own effect. Examiners reward answers that combine voice and body in each moment, rather than a list of vocal skills followed by a separate list of physical ones.

### The vocal toolkit, used correctly

| Skill | What it means | Written precisely |
|---|---|---|
| **Pitch** | how high or low the voice is | "pitch drops to the bottom of my range on 'gone'" |
| **Pace** | speed of delivery | "the first clause rushed, as if blurted out" |
| **Pause** | a deliberate silence | "a two-second pause before 'mother', eyes down" |
| **Volume / projection** | loudness / carrying to the back of the auditorium | "barely above a whisper, though still projected" |
| **Tone** | the emotional colour of the voice | "a mocking, sing-song tone"; "a flat, exhausted tone" |
| **Emphasis / stress** | which word is weighted | "heavy stress on 'you', not 'said'" |
| **Inflection / intonation** | the rise and fall within a phrase | "a rising inflection that turns it into a plea" |
| **Accent** | regional or class sound | "a broad Scouse accent"; "clipped received pronunciation" |
| **Timbre** | the texture of the voice | "a husky, cracked timbre from crying" |
| **Phrasing / timing** | where you breathe, and when you speak | "I cut in before she finishes, overlapping her line" |

Common errors: **pitch** is high or low, never "loud"; **tone** is an emotion, never "high"; **volume** is loud or quiet.

### The physical toolkit

**Posture** (upright, slumped, rigid); **gesture** (a small, specific movement of the hands or head, such as a jabbed finger or a palm pressed flat on the table); **facial expression** (name it: a tight-lipped smile, eyebrows raised, jaw clenched); **eye contact** (holding it, breaking it, looking at the floor or out front); **movement** (stepping forward, turning away, sinking onto a chair); **proxemics** (the distance between you and the other actor); **stillness** (freezing can be louder than any gesture). **Gait** means the way you walk, so it only applies when you are moving across the stage, not when you're seated.

### Effects: the "why"

An effect is what the audience **understands or feels**. It might be sympathy for a character, laughter, discomfort, tension, or the realisation that a character is lying. It is also about what the moment reveals: a motive, a relationship, a change of status. Check your effects against the **narrative context**. Ask what has just happened, what the character knows at this point and what they want from the person they are speaking to. A choice that contradicts what the character knows at this moment (anger at something they haven't yet found out, for example) is marked down. Many characters are more complicated than their loudest line. A domineering parent may be frightened, and a wisecracking servant may be anxious to impress, so an interpretation built on one emotion often misreads the line.

### Exam technique in an open-book paper

Your play is a clean copy. Before writing, find the line in the extract and read the **eight to ten lines before it**, because they are its context. Then decide:
1. What the character wants at this moment.
2. Where the line turns: which word carries the weight, and where the tone changes.
3. What you want the audience to feel.

Don't copy the whole line into your answer. Refer to its words as you go. About 10 minutes and 200–250 words is right for 8 marks.

::: terms
- **Pitch**: how high or low the voice is; it can rise or fall within a line to show emotion.
- **Tone**: the emotional quality of the voice, such as sarcastic, tender, bitter or pleading.
- **Pace**: the speed of delivery, which can show panic, confidence, hesitation or control.
- **Pause**: a deliberate silence before, within or after a line to create tension or show thought.
- **Emphasis**: stress placed on a particular word to bring out its meaning.
- **Inflection**: the rise and fall of the voice within a word or phrase, changing its meaning.
- **Timbre**: the distinctive texture of a voice, such as husky, nasal or breathy.
- **Gesture**: a specific, meaningful movement of the hands, arms or head.
- **Posture**: the way the body is held, showing status, age, mood or confidence.
- **Gait**: the way a character walks, which only applies when they are moving.
- **Effect**: what the audience feels or understands as a result of a performance choice.
:::

::: method
1. **Find the line** and read the lines before it: what has just happened, and what does the character want?
2. **Name the effect(s) in one short opening sentence**: "I want the audience to see that her anger hides fear."
3. **Split the line into two or three chunks.** For each chunk, give one or two vocal choices pinned to exact words (pitch, pace, pause, tone, emphasis) *and* a physical choice at the same moment (gesture, expression, eye contact, movement).
4. **Explain the effect of each chunk**, linking it to the character's motive and the other people on stage.
5. **Finish with the moment after the line**: a held look, a turn away, a silence. It often creates the strongest effect.
6. **Check the vocabulary**: pitch is high or low, tone is an emotion, and gait is only for walking.
:::

::: example An invented line: a father letting his daughter go
Context: a working-class father, who has always kept his feelings to himself, has just learned his youngest daughter is moving abroad. He is trying not to show he is upset. He says: "Go on then. Don't miss your flight on my account."

You are performing the role of the father. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

I want the audience to see that his gruffness is a cover for grief (1). On "Go on then" I keep my back half-turned, busying my hands winding the garden hose, and speak at a normal, brisk pace with a flat, matter-of-fact tone, as if it is nothing (1). This makes his attempt to seem casual obvious to the audience but not to his daughter, which creates a gentle, painful irony (1). I pause after "then" and my hands stop moving on the hose (1). The stillness shows the words cost him something. On "Don't miss your flight" I turn to face her but look at her shoulder rather than her eyes, and my pitch lifts slightly and wobbles on "flight" before I steady it (1). This shows the audience he is close to tears (1). "On my account" is said quickly, with a forced, lopsided smile and a small flick of the hand towards the gate, trying to make a joke of it (1). The quick, dismissive ending lets him escape the moment, and the audience feels the love he cannot say aloud, so they sympathise with both father and daughter (1).
:::

::: example Romeo and Juliet: Capulet's fury (Act Three, Scene Five)
You are performing the role of Capulet. Juliet has just refused to marry Paris. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform the line "Hang thee, young baggage! disobedient wretch!" and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

I want to show a father whose authority has been challenged in front of his wife and the Nurse, so his fury is partly wounded pride (1). Juliet is kneeling downstage centre. On "Hang thee" I stride towards her so I tower over her, and the words explode at full volume, with hard stress on the plosive "H" of "Hang" (1). The effect is shocking: the audience has seen him as a jovial host at the feast, and the change frightens them for Juliet (1). On "young baggage" my tone turns to sneering contempt, the pitch dropping low and slow, and I flick the back of my hand towards her as if brushing away dirt (1). The insult treats his only daughter as a worthless object, which shows how quickly his love depends on obedience (1). I pause and my breathing is audible, and then "disobedient wretch!" comes faster and higher-pitched, almost cracking (1). My finger jabs down at her on each stressed syllable, "dis-O-be-di-ent" (1). The rising, uncontrolled pitch suggests he is losing control, which builds tension and makes Lady Capulet and the Nurse flinch. The audience fears violence and understands why Juliet will turn to the Friar and the potion (1).
:::

::: example Explaining effects on an invented line
Context: a teenage boy has broken his mother's favourite vase and blamed his younger brother. When she asks him directly, he says: "Why is it always me? Ask him."

Describe two physical skills you would use on this line and explain the effect of each. (4)

On "Why is it always me?" I throw both arms wide, palms up, and roll my eyes to the ceiling (1). The big, over-performed innocence tells the audience he is lying, which creates comedy, as they know what he did (1). On "Ask him" I point sharply at the younger brother without looking at him, keeping my eyes fixed on my mother's face to see if she believes me (1). The refusal to look at his brother shows his guilt, and the audience enjoys the dramatic irony (1).
:::

::: example Upgrading a weak paragraph
A student writes: "I would say the line in a loud pitch and a high tone to show she is angry, and I would walk across the stage with an angry gait." Rewrite the sentence so that it is precise and uses the terms correctly. (3)

I would raise my volume sharply on the first word and push my pitch higher as the sentence goes on, with a clipped, furious tone (1). I would stride from the door to the table in three quick, heavy steps, so that my gait shows her anger, and stop dead with both palms slammed flat on the table on the last word (1). The rising pitch and sudden stillness show her fury peaking, and the audience senses that the argument is about to break (1).
:::

::: q
Name three vocal skills and give a precise example of each, pinned to a word or moment. (3)
---
Any three (1 each, must be pinned to a word or moment): e.g. pause "a long pause before 'sorry'"; emphasis "stress on 'my' in 'my house'"; pitch "pitch drops on 'dead'"; pace "the list rushed"; tone "sarcastic tone on 'wonderful'"; volume "whispered 'please'".
:::

::: q
Explain why an answer that only describes vocal skills cannot reach the top band. (2)
---
The question asks for both vocal and physical skills (1); missing one half means the description cannot be full or precise across the performance, so the answer is capped in the lower bands (1).
:::

::: q
Context: an elderly woman, proud and lonely, is visited by her grown-up son for the first time in a year. He apologises for not calling. She says: "Oh, I've been far too busy to notice."

You are performing the role of the mother. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform the line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Bands: 7–8 exact vocal and physical detail, precise throughout, effects entirely right and explained; 5–6 clear, a number of details; 3–4 some detail and relevance; 1–2 vague; one skill set only caps at 3–4. Indicative: a bright, light, over-cheerful tone; a sing-song rise on "Oh"; a brief pause before "busy"; stress on "far too"; she keeps her back to him, straightening already-straight cushions; a tight smile; she doesn't meet his eye; her voice thins on "notice"; stillness afterwards. Effect: sarcasm and pride masking hurt and loneliness; the audience sees what she won't admit; sympathy mixed with gentle comedy.
:::

::: q
Explain what is wrong with the phrase "a quiet gait" and suggest a correction. (2)
---
Gait is the way someone walks, so "quiet" is not a gait quality; and volume belongs to voice (1). Correct with e.g. "a slow, shuffling gait" or "soft-footed, creeping steps" (1).
:::

::: q
A line in your set play comes just after the character has received bad news. Explain why reading the lines before it matters before you plan the delivery. (3)
---
The lines before give the narrative context: what the character now knows and feels (1); this decides the right emotion and effects, e.g. shock not anger (1); a delivery based on something the character does not yet know or feel would be inappropriate for the moment and would be marked down (1).
:::

::: q
Context: a young waiter in a posh restaurant, desperate to keep his job, has just spilled soup on a rich customer. He says: "Madam, I am so very terribly sorry."

Describe two vocal skills you would use on this line and explain the effect of each. (4)
---
Two skills (1 each, pinned to words) plus effect (1 each). E.g. rapid, breathless pace, gabbling the words together, showing panic and creating comedy (2); an exaggerated rise in pitch and stress on "so very terribly", over-apologising, showing his desperation and creating comedy through excess (2).
:::

::: watch
- Covering only voice or only movement caps the answer. The question asks for both vocal and physical skills.
- Writing about the line as a whole ("I'd say it sadly") instead of pinning choices to specific words.
- Misusing terms: "loud pitch", "high tone", a gait for someone sitting down.
- Describing a performance without saying what effect it creates, or choosing effects the skills wouldn't actually produce.
- Misreading the character at this moment, e.g. playing anger at something they haven't yet found out, or aggression where the play shows worry or love.
- Copying the whole line out, or signposting every sentence ("A physical skill I would use is..."). It wastes time and adds nothing.
- Repeating a stage direction ("she grabs him") without describing how you would perform it.
:::

::: test
Context: a nervous teenager is meeting her girlfriend's strict father for the first time. He has just asked what she plans to do with her life. She replies: "I'm going to be a vet. Or an astronaut. Probably a vet."

You are performing the role of the teenager. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Bands: 7–8 exact description of vocal and physical skills with precise detail throughout; effects entirely appropriate and fully explained. 5–6 clear with a number of precise details; appropriate effects. 3–4 reasonably clear, a few details; some relevant effects. 1–2 limited description, effects missing or unexplained. Only voice or only physical: max 3–4.

Indicative: bright, over-confident volume and a firm nod on "vet"; a pause, then "Or an astronaut" blurted with a rising inflection as panic takes over; eyes darting to the girlfriend; hands twisting her sleeve; "Probably a vet" quieter, faster, a weak laugh, shoulders sinking; effects: comedy from the nervous escalation; sympathy for her wish to impress; the father's silence afterwards (held pause) adds to the comedy.
:::

::: test
Context: a soldier writes home before a battle and reads his letter aloud: "Tell Mum not to worry. I'll be back before the apples are ripe."

You are performing the role of the soldier. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Bands as above (7–8/5–6/3–4/1–2; both vocal and physical needed for 5+). Indicative: gentle, warm tone with a light, deliberately cheerful lift on "not to worry"; slower pace and a catch in the voice on "Mum"; pause before "I'll be back"; seated on an upturned crate, pencil in hand, stopping writing; eyes lifting out front as if seeing the orchard; a half-smile that fades on "apples are ripe"; hand pressed briefly on the letter. Effects: dramatic irony and tension, as the audience fears he will not return; sympathy; contrast between his brave words and the fear shown in his body.
:::

::: test
Context: a businesswoman who has fired half her staff to save money is confronted by a former employee in the car park. She says: "It was nothing personal. It never is."

You are performing the role of the businesswoman. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Bands as above. Indicative: cool, even pace; low, controlled pitch; clipped received-pronunciation accent; stress on "nothing"; a pause, then "It never is" lighter, almost bored, or with a flicker of regret depending on the interpretation chosen; physically upright posture, car keys in hand, pressing the fob so the car beeps mid-line; minimal eye contact, then a direct, level gaze on the last word; stepping past without breaking stride. Effects: cold detachment that makes the audience dislike her, or a hint of guilt beneath the professionalism; the power gap between the two. Reward either coherent interpretation.
:::

::: test
Context: an old man is teaching his grandson to fish and pretends not to care that the boy has caught a bigger fish than him. He says: "Beginner's luck. Pure beginner's luck."

You are performing the role of the grandfather. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Bands as above. Indicative: dismissive, grumbling tone; lower pitch; a sniff before speaking; a pause and a sideways glance at the fish; repeated "beginner's luck" slower and with stress on "Pure", the pitch rising in mock outrage; arms folded, lips pursed, then a grudging twitch of a smile he tries to hide; ruffling the boy's hair at the end; gait: a stiff, slow shuffle along the bank if he moves. Effects: warm comedy, the audience sees his pride in the boy beneath the grumbling; the affectionate relationship between the generations.
:::

::: test
Context: a young woman has just been told by a doctor that her father is seriously ill. Her brother, who hasn't visited for months, arrives late. She says: "Oh, you came. How nice for everyone."

You are performing the role of the sister. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Bands as above. Indicative: "Oh, you came" flat, falling intonation, not turning round at first; a beat of stillness; then turning slowly to face him with a thin, tight smile; "How nice for everyone" with a sharp, sarcastic lilt, emphasis on "nice", pace slowing; arms folded across her body; staying seated or blocking his path to the bed; voice cracking slightly on "everyone" to show exhaustion. Effects: tension and bitterness at his absence; the audience understands the family conflict; sympathy for her tiredness and grief.
:::

::: test
Explain why combining vocal and physical skills in each moment can be more effective in an answer than writing about them separately. (3)
---
It shows exactly what the performer does at each point in the line (1), so the effect of the whole moment is clear, e.g. a pause *with* stillness (1); it keeps the answer concise and precise and avoids a disconnected list of skills (1).
:::

::: test
You are performing a line in which a character reveals a secret. Explain how a pause could be used before the key word, and the effect it would create. (3)
---
A precise pause (e.g. two seconds, breath held, eyes down) immediately before the key word (1); it builds tension as the audience waits and focuses on the word (1); it shows the character's reluctance or fear of the consequences (1).
:::

::: test
Explain what is meant by the "narrative context" of a line, and why the 8-mark answer should reflect it. (2)
---
What is happening at that moment in the play: what the character knows, wants and has just experienced (1); effects must suit that moment, or the interpretation will be inappropriate for the role (1).
:::
