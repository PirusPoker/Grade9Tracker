## What it is

Before you can decide how to perform or design a play, you need to be able to describe *what kind of play it is* and *how it is built*. The specification calls these the **characteristics of performance texts**: genre, structure, form, style, language, stage directions and the practical demands of the text. (Character, sub-text, mood, pace and climax are in the next lesson.)

No question on the paper asks "What is the genre of your play?". But this vocabulary feeds every Section B answer, especially the 20-mark question, which asks why your ideas suit "the play as a whole". It also feeds Section C, where you describe the style of the production you saw.

### Genre, style, form and structure: four different words

| Term | The question it answers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Genre** | What *type* of story is it, and what feeling does it aim for? | Tragedy, comedy, historical drama, musical, thriller, satire, kitchen sink drama, teen drama |
| **Style** | *How* is it performed and staged: how close to real life? | Naturalism/realism, epic, physical theatre, storytelling theatre, verse drama, ensemble, surrealism, absurdism |
| **Form** | What *shape or medium* does the text take? | A full-length play in acts, a one-act play, a musical with songs, a monologue, an adaptation of a novel, verse or prose |
| **Structure** | How is the story *ordered and divided*? | Linear or non-linear, episodic, cyclical, acts and scenes, flashbacks, a frame (the ending shown first) |

The most common mistake is mixing genre and style. **Tragedy is a genre; naturalism is a style.** A play can be a tragedy performed naturalistically, or a tragedy performed as verse drama. The two words answer different questions.

### Key styles in brief

- **Naturalism / realism**: the stage aims to look and sound like real life. It uses a detailed setting, a fourth wall, believable psychology and everyday speech. The actors do not acknowledge the audience.
- **Epic**: influenced by Bertolt Brecht. It uses many short scenes across times and places, narration or direct address, doubling, and minimal or visible staging. The aim is to make the audience think about the social and political issues as well as feel.
- **Storytelling theatre**: a small cast tells the story openly. They narrate, play many roles and create trains, ships or animals with their bodies and a few props. The audience's imagination completes the picture.
- **Physical theatre**: meaning is carried by movement, lifts and choreographed sequences as much as by words.
- **Musical theatre**: songs and music carry the emotion and the story. Characters sing what they cannot say.
- **Verse drama**: the characters speak largely in poetry (in Shakespeare, mostly blank verse) with a rhythm the actor must use.
- **Ensemble**: the company works as a group, often all on stage, playing many roles and creating the world together.

### Structure in practice

- **Linear**: events happen in chronological order (*The Crucible*'s four acts move forward through the crisis).
- **Non-linear / frame**: the play shows the end first or jumps in time. *Blood Brothers* opens by showing the outcome, then goes back to tell how it happened.
- **Episodic**: many short scenes, often in different places, as in epic and storytelling plays.
- **Cyclical**: the ending returns to the beginning.
- **Acts and scenes**: an act is a large section, often with an interval between acts. A scene is a unit in one place and time. Scene changes are a practical problem the designer must solve.

### Language

Language includes **dialogue** (characters speaking to each other), **monologue** (one character speaking at length), **soliloquy** (a character alone, speaking thoughts aloud), **direct address** (speaking to the audience), **verse and prose**, **dialect and accent** written into the text, **register** (formal or informal), **pauses and silences** marked in the script, **interruptions and overlapping lines**, and **song lyrics**. Every one of these is a clue for the performer. Short, broken lines suggest pace and tension. Long speeches suggest control or a need to persuade. A shift from verse to prose may mark a change in status or mood.

### Stage directions

Stage directions are the playwright's instructions that are not spoken. They are clues, not commands: directors often change them. Types:

| Type | What it tells you | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Place, period, time of day, furniture | Starting point for set, lighting and sound design |
| **Entrances and exits** | Who comes and goes, and sometimes from where | Blocking, use of wings, doors or aisles |
| **Action / business** | What characters physically do | Movement, props, fights, handling objects |
| **Delivery** | How a line is said (angrily, quietly, after a pause) | Vocal choices, pause, tone |
| **Character description** | Age, appearance, personality on first entrance | Casting, costume, physicality |
| **Technical** | Blackout, music, sound effects, lighting changes | Cues for the designers |

Some playwrights write very full directions. Miller interrupts Act One of *The Crucible* with long prose notes on the characters and on Salem. Others write almost none (much of Shakespeare's action must be read from the dialogue).

### The practical demands of the text

This means everything the play *requires* the production to achieve. Examples:
- **Several locations** and fast transitions between them.
- **Special effects**: a storm, a gunshot, fire, a journey.
- **Time passing**: seasons, years, a character ageing from child to adult (in *Blood Brothers*, adults play the twins at seven).
- **Doubling and multi-role** when the cast is small.
- **Quick costume changes.**
- **Songs, live music or fights** that need rehearsal and safety.
- **Crowds or ensembles** in a small space.

Spotting these demands tells you where the design and performance challenges are, and those are what the examiner wants you to solve.

::: terms
- **Genre**: the type or category of a play, such as tragedy, comedy, musical or historical drama.
- **Style**: the way a play is performed and staged, such as naturalism, epic or physical theatre.
- **Form**: the shape or medium of a text, such as a full-length play in acts, a monologue or a musical.
- **Structure**: the way a play's events are ordered and divided, such as linear, episodic or cyclical.
- **Naturalism**: a style that aims to recreate real life on stage as believably as possible, behind a fourth wall.
- **Epic theatre**: a style associated with Brecht, using narration and episodic scenes to make the audience think critically.
- **Episodic**: a structure made of many short scenes, often jumping between places and times.
- **Stage directions**: the playwright's unspoken instructions about setting, action, delivery, entrances and effects.
- **Monologue**: a long speech by one character, spoken to others on stage or to the audience.
- **Soliloquy**: a speech in which a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud.
- **Practical demands**: everything a text requires the production to achieve, such as locations, effects, changes and doubling.
- **Verse drama**: a play written largely in poetry, whose rhythm shapes how actors speak it.
:::

::: method
1. **Label the play precisely**: genre *and* style, using AQA's words for your set play.
2. **Name the structure**: linear or not, acts or episodes, and where the extract sits in it.
3. **Read the stage directions as clues**: setting (for design), action (for movement), delivery (for voice). Say which one you are using.
4. **Read the language as clues**: sentence length, pauses, register, verse or prose, dialect.
5. **List the practical demands** of the extract before you plan: locations, changes, effects, doubling.
6. **Link every point to a staging choice** and its effect. A label with no staging idea attached earns little.
:::

::: example Structure and its effect
Explain how a play that begins by showing its ending could affect the audience. (3)

The audience knows the outcome from the start, so the suspense is not about *what* happens but *how* and *why* (1). This makes them watch for the causes, such as the decisions and social pressures that lead to the ending (1). It can also add dramatic irony and a sense of fate, so happy moments feel painful because the audience knows what is coming (1).
:::

::: example Style shaping staging
Explain how the style of a naturalistic play affects how it is staged. (4)

Naturalism aims to show real life, so the set is usually a detailed, realistic room with real furniture, working doors and period props (1). The lighting imitates real sources, such as daylight through a window or a lamp, rather than coloured effects (1). The actors keep the fourth wall, never addressing the audience, and use everyday movement and speech with believable motivation (1). Scene changes are often hidden by a blackout or curtain so the illusion is not broken (1).
:::

::: example Reading stage directions
A stage direction reads: *Late evening. A bare attic room, rain on the skylight. She enters slowly, still in her wet coat, and does not switch on the light.* Explain how a director and designers could use this stage direction. (4)

The setting tells the set designer to create a sparse attic with a sloping roof and a skylight, suggesting poverty or isolation (1). The lighting designer could use cold, blue-grey light through the skylight with a rain-effect gobo, and no warm practical light, because she does not switch it on (1). The sound designer needs recorded rain on glass, looping quietly under the scene (1). The action "enters slowly... does not switch on the light" tells the actor to use a heavy, slow pace and to stand in the gloom, suggesting exhaustion or grief (1).
:::

::: example Practical demands
Identify two practical demands of a play that moves between a ship, a railway station and a palace in quick succession, and explain how a production could meet them. (4)

Demand 1: several locations must be shown quickly (1). A minimalist, permanent set with a few moveable pieces, such as trunks that become a ship's rail, a bench or a throne, lets the change happen in seconds, supported by lighting and sound (1).

Demand 2: the travel itself must be conveyed (1). The ensemble could create the rocking of a ship with their bodies while sound plays waves and gulls, or projection could show a moving landscape (1).
:::

::: q
Define "genre". (1)
---
The type or category of a play, such as tragedy, comedy or musical (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a monologue and a soliloquy. (2)
---
A monologue is a long speech by one character, which may be addressed to other characters or the audience (1); a soliloquy is spoken by a character alone on stage, revealing their private thoughts (1).
:::

::: q
Give two features of an episodic structure. (2)
---
Many short scenes (1); frequent jumps between places and/or times (1). Also accept: scenes that can stand alone; often linked by narration.
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by the "practical demands" of a text, with an example. (2)
---
The things the text requires a production to achieve on stage (1), such as quick costume changes, a storm effect, many locations or doubling of roles (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two features of epic theatre. (4)
---
Any two, 2 marks each (feature + effect/explanation): direct address or narration, which reminds the audience they are watching a play and asks them to judge; episodic scenes across many times and places; multi-role or doubling; minimal or visible staging and placards; aims to make the audience think about social or political issues rather than just feel.
:::

::: q
Explain how the language of a scene could tell an actor the scene should be played at a fast pace. (3)
---
Short lines and quick exchanges between characters (1); interruptions, unfinished sentences or overlapping lines (1); exclamations or questions fired back and forth, suggesting rising anger or panic, so cues should be picked up with no gaps (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Genre and style used as the same thing.** Genre is the type of story. Style is how it is performed.
- **Labelling without applying.** "The play is naturalistic" is only the start. Say what that means for the set, the acting or the lighting in this extract.
- **Treating stage directions as fixed.** You may adapt them, but show you have read them.
- **Forgetting the play as a whole.** Structure lets you place the extract: what happened before, what it sets up, where the climax falls.
- **Guessing plot facts.** If you are unsure of a detail in your set play, describe it more generally rather than inventing it.
:::

---

::: test
Explain how the style of a play you have studied affects how it could be staged. (4)
---
1 mark for naming the style correctly, up to 3 for its staging implications with examples. For example, naturalism: detailed realistic set, lighting from motivated sources, fourth wall, believable acting. Epic: minimal and transformable set, direct address, multi-role, visible changes. Musical theatre: space for songs and dance, amplification, lighting that shifts for numbers. Storytelling: bare stage, ensemble creates locations, props transform. Verse drama: attention to rhythm and line endings, open stage for soliloquies.
:::

::: test
Explain how the language of a character's lines can give clues to the performer about their status. (4)
---
Formal vocabulary and long, controlled sentences suggest high status and confidence (1), which an actor might deliver with steady pace and stillness (1); short, hesitant lines, apologies or questions suggest low status or fear (1), played with pauses, lower volume or avoiding eye contact (1). Also credit dialect or accent as a sign of class or region, and verse versus prose shifts.
:::

::: test
Explain how a director could use genre and structure to shape the audience's response to the opening of a play you have studied. (6)
---
Levels. 5–6: accurate genre and structure terms applied precisely to the opening, with specific staging choices and developed effects. 3–4: clear terms with some staging ideas and effects. 1–2: basic identification with little application.

Indicative content: identify the genre (for example tragedy, historical drama, musical) and what the audience should feel from the start (foreboding, curiosity, comedy); identify the structure (the ending shown first; a narrator or prologue; a linear opening establishing normal life before the crisis); staging choices that exploit these, such as a spotlit narrator downstage, a tableau of the final image, a detailed realistic room that establishes normality, or a lively ensemble number; effects: dramatic irony, tension, sympathy, a sense of fate.
:::

::: test
Explain how a director could use the stage directions of a play you have studied to stage its opening. (12)
---
Levels. 10–12: an exact, well-developed explanation, with precise reference to specific stage directions (paraphrased) and detailed, appropriate staging choices for performers and design, each with a clear effect. 7–9: a clear explanation using several stage directions with a number of precise staging ideas. 4–6: some relevant reference to stage directions but ideas are general or effects underdeveloped. 1–3: limited, vague reference with little staging detail.

Indicative content: identify the kinds of direction at the opening (setting: place, period, time of day; character descriptions; action; delivery; technical cues); turn the setting direction into set, lighting and sound choices (for example a small, low-ceilinged room lit by early morning light through a narrow window, with a candle or lamp as a motivated source, sounds of the outside world); use the character descriptions for casting, costume and physicality; use action directions for the opening image and blocking (who is on stage, where, doing what, when the first entrance happens and from where); use delivery directions for the vocal tone of the first lines; explain where the director might depart from a direction and why (configuration, concept); link to genre and style (a naturalistic box set versus a bare epic stage); effects: establishing time, place and mood, drawing the audience's focus, setting up the central conflict.
:::

::: test
Explain how the practical demands of a play you have studied could be met by a production, referring to at least two demands. (8)
---
Levels. 7–8: two or more accurate demands, each met with precise, workable staging solutions and clear effects, with a sense of the play as a whole. 5–6: two demands with clear solutions and some detail. 3–4: one or two demands with general solutions. 1–2: limited identification with little solution.

Indicative content: demands such as multiple locations, fast scene changes, special effects (storm, gunfire, fire, the sea), passing time or seasons, adults playing children, doubling, quick changes, songs, fights or crowds; solutions such as a composite or permanent set, trucks, revolve, flying, projection, lighting states for time of day, recorded or live sound effects, costume changes by accessory, choreographed and rehearsed fight sequences with safety in mind, ensemble movement; each solution tied to the effect on the audience (clarity of location, pace, tension, belief).
:::
