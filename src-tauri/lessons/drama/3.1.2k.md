## What it is

*Romeo and Juliet* by William Shakespeare (prescribed edition: **Palgrave Macmillan**) is a **tragedy** and, in AQA's terms, a **verse drama**. Two teenagers from feuding families fall in love, marry in secret and die within a few days. In Drama you study it as something to perform and design, not only to read: how an actor speaks verse, how a fight is staged, how the balcony and the tomb are built, how day and night are shown. Section B asks four questions on an extract (4, 8, 12 and 20 marks).

### Context: the setting and the playhouse

| | What it is | What it gives a theatre maker |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Verona, Italy; AQA frames it as **late 16th century Verona** | A wealthy Italian city: great houses, street brawls, a powerful Prince, rich fabrics and swords. Daughters were expected to obey fathers and marry for family advantage, often very young; the Church (Friar Laurence) had great authority. Honour and revenge drove men to violence. |
| **Writing** | Written in the mid-1590s for London's open-air playhouses | Shakespeare's audience saw it by daylight, on a bare stage, with poetry doing the work of scenery. |

**Elizabethan theatrical conventions** you should know:

- **Open-air playhouse**: a **thrust stage** with standing spectators on three sides and galleries above; plays were performed in the afternoon, so night and darkness were created by **words, torches and costume**, not lighting.
- **Minimal scenery**: a few props and furniture; location was set by the dialogue.
- **The upper level** (a gallery above the stage), often used for Juliet's window in the balcony and parting scenes.
- **Boy players**: all female roles were played by boys or young men.
- **Verse and prose**: noble and romantic characters usually speak **blank verse** (unrhymed iambic pentameter) or rhyme; servants, the Nurse and comic moments often use **prose**. The lovers' first exchange is a shared **sonnet**.
- **Direct address**: the Chorus speaks the **Prologue**, telling the audience the ending before it begins.

### Plot, act by act

| Act | What happens |
|---|---|
| **One** | The Prologue announces two "star-crossed" lovers. A street brawl between Capulet and Montague servants spreads to the heads of the houses; the Prince threatens death for any further fighting. Romeo pines for Rosaline. Capulet invites Paris to woo Juliet. Romeo and friends gatecrash the Capulet feast; Romeo and Juliet meet and kiss; Tybalt recognises Romeo and is restrained by Capulet. |
| **Two** | The balcony (orchard) scene: Juliet at her window, Romeo below; they vow to marry. Friar Laurence agrees to marry them, hoping to end the feud. Mercutio mocks Romeo; the Nurse carries messages. The secret marriage. |
| **Three** | Tybalt challenges Romeo, who refuses to fight; Mercutio fights instead and is killed under Romeo's arm, cursing both houses. Romeo kills Tybalt and is banished. The Nurse brings the news to Juliet. Romeo and Juliet spend their wedding night together and part at dawn. Capulet, furious at Juliet's refusal, insists she marry Paris. |
| **Four** | Friar Laurence gives Juliet a potion to make her seem dead. She takes it; the family finds her "dead" on her wedding morning. |
| **Five** | Friar Laurence's letter never reaches Romeo; Balthasar tells him Juliet is dead. Romeo buys poison, goes to the tomb, kills Paris, drinks the poison and dies. Juliet wakes, finds him dead and stabs herself. The Prince and the families gather; the Friar explains; Capulet and Montague make peace. |

### Characters

| Character | Performance note |
|---|---|
| **Romeo** | Impulsive and passionate; moves from self-pitying lover of Rosaline to real love, then to rage and despair. Energy and speed; verse spoken with urgency. |
| **Juliet** | Nearly fourteen, but quick-witted and brave; she grows faster than anyone. Her soliloquies (waiting for the Nurse, before the potion) need control of rhythm and building fear. |
| **The Nurse** | Earthy, comic, rambling in prose; loves Juliet but finally advises her to marry Paris. Physical comedy (out of breath, aching back). |
| **Mercutio** | Brilliant, restless, bawdy; the Queen Mab speech shows a wild imagination; his death turns the play from comedy to tragedy. |
| **Tybalt** | Proud, aggressive, a skilled swordsman; stillness and a fixed stare that suggest danger. |
| **Friar Laurence** | Wise, kind, a scientist of herbs, but his plans fail; calm pace, grounded movement. |
| **Lord Capulet** | Genial host who becomes a tyrant when defied; his explosion at Juliet in Act Three needs sudden volume and physical threat. |
| **Lady Capulet** | Formal and distant with her daughter; demands revenge for Tybalt. |
| **Benvolio, Paris, the Prince** | The peace-keeper; the correct, conventional suitor; the voice of authority. |

### Key moments and how to stage them

1. **The opening brawl (1.1).** Fight choreography: rehearsed, safe moves (no contact on blows, weapons aimed wide), escalating from insults and gestures to a full street fight; the Prince's entrance freezes everyone. Sound: clashing steel, shouting, a bell or drum for the Prince.
2. **The feast (1.5).** Music and dance fill the stage; Romeo and Juliet find each other and the world slows. A lighting change (a warm special isolating them while the dancers move in slow motion) makes the sonnet private.
3. **The balcony (2.2).** Levels matter: Juliet high, Romeo below, the distance between them a symbol of the obstacles. Night can be moonlight blue with a soft gobo of leaves; in an Elizabethan-style production, torches and the words alone.
4. **Mercutio's death (3.1).** Starts as a game (comic swordplay), turns real when Tybalt's blade goes under Romeo's arm. A sudden stop in the sound; Mercutio's jokes continue even as he dies, so the change of tone must be precise.
5. **Capulet's rage (3.5).** Capulet towers over Juliet, who kneels; he may grab her arm or throw her down. The Nurse's attempt to intervene; Lady Capulet's coldness. A sharp contrast with the tender dawn parting just before.
6. **The tomb (5.3).** A dark, cold vault: a stone bier centre, candles or torchlight, deep blue and grey light. Romeo's death and Juliet's waking, seconds apart, with a slow, silent build of dramatic irony.

### Design across the play

Designers choose between **period** (Renaissance Italy: doublets, hose, gowns with square necklines, rich velvets and brocades) and **modern-dress** or updated concepts (gangs, street clothes, knives instead of swords), but the AQA 4-marker asks for **late 16th century Verona**, so keep period answers ready. Colour can divide the houses (for example, Capulet red and gold, Montague blue and silver). A permanent set with an upper level, arches and a central space can serve streets, the feast, the balcony, the Friar's cell and the tomb. Lighting and sound can track the play's fast movement from day to night and from love to death over just a few days.

::: terms
- **Tragedy**: a play in which the main characters are brought to suffering and death, often by a mix of fate and their own choices.
- **Verse drama**: a play written largely in poetry, whose rhythm the actor must handle.
- **Blank verse**: unrhymed lines of iambic pentameter, spoken by most noble characters.
- **Iambic pentameter**: a line of ten syllables with five beats, alternating weak and strong.
- **Prose**: ordinary speech without a regular rhythm, often used by servants, the Nurse and in comic scenes.
- **Soliloquy**: a speech in which a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud.
- **Prologue**: an opening speech, here a sonnet spoken by the Chorus, that tells the audience what will happen.
- **Thrust stage**: a stage with the audience on three sides, like the Elizabethan playhouse.
- **Fight choreography**: a rehearsed sequence of safe, precise moves for staged combat.
- **Shared line**: a line of verse split between two speakers, showing quick connection or conflict.
:::

::: method
1. **Place the extract**: act and scene, day or night, who knows what (the audience knows the ending).
2. **For the 4-marker, design for late 16th century Verona**: rich or poor fabric by status, Italian Renaissance cut, and the family's colours.
3. **For the line**, show how the actor handles the verse: stress, rhythm, the line ending, a pause at a caesura, pace in a shared line.
4. **For the shaded section**, use levels (balcony, tomb), distance and touch; fights need precise, safe choreography.
5. **For the 20-marker**, cover the extract and the character's or design's arc across the few days of the play.
6. **Use conventions** (Prologue, soliloquy, day and night) to explain your choices.
:::

::: example Costume for the Nurse (4 marks)
You are designing a costume for the Nurse in Act Two when she brings news to Juliet. The costume must reflect the context of *Romeo and Juliet*, set in late 16th century Verona. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

A full-length dress of heavy, practical wool in a faded russet, gathered at the waist, with a laced bodice let out for her comfortable figure (1). A white linen apron and a partlet (a fill-in covering neck and chest) show she is a servant in a great house, not a lady (1). A starched white linen headdress over grey hair, and a wide fan she flaps because she is hot from the walk (1). Stout leather shoes with dust at the toes, and a large set of household keys and a purse at her waist, showing her trusted place in the Capulet house (1).
:::

::: example Performing Juliet on the balcony (8 marks)
You are performing the role of Juliet. At her window, not knowing Romeo is listening, Juliet asks why he must be a Montague. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform the line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

I would lean on the balustrade, chin on my hands, gazing out into the dark (1). The line would begin softly, almost a sigh, with a falling pitch (1), and stress on the name, so the audience hears that the problem is his family, not him (1). I'd hold a small pause at the caesura to let the thought land (1), then rise in pitch as the frustration grows (1). A clenched fist on the rail shows her feeling (1). The effect: the audience enjoys the dramatic irony of Romeo overhearing below (1) and sees Juliet's intelligence as well as her love (1).
:::

::: example Space and interaction in 3.1 (12 marks, outline)
You are performing the role of Mercutio from Tybalt's entrance to his fatal wound. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Tybalt to show Mercutio's contempt. (12)

He lounges on a low wall downstage left as Tybalt enters with his men; he doesn't stand. He mocks Tybalt's name with a lazy, lilting tone, flicking a hand. When Tybalt turns to Romeo, Mercutio springs up and blocks his path, circling him with his sword held loose, like a toy. The fight begins as mockery (slapping Tybalt's backside with the flat of his blade). Romeo steps between them, and Tybalt's thrust goes under his arm. Mercutio staggers, laughs, and keeps joking as he moves to the edge of the stage, so contempt turns into shock.
:::

::: example Designing the tomb (20 marks, outline)
Plan a set design for the tomb scene and the play as a whole. (20)

Extract: the Capulet vault as a low stone chamber; heavy iron gate upstage centre; a stone bier on a raised plinth with Juliet under a white shroud; Tybalt's body in a winding sheet in the shadows; stone steps down from street level; candles in sconces. Whole play: a permanent structure of weathered stone arches with an upper gallery (the balcony) and a central area; pieces like the bier and the Friar's table glide in on trucks; the feast has hanging lanterns and a long banqueting table; the same central space becomes the tomb at the end, so the love story moves from celebration to death in the same place.
:::

::: q
When was *Romeo and Juliet* written, and for what kind of theatre? (2)
---
Mid-1590s (1), for an open-air playhouse with a thrust stage, performed in daylight (1).
:::

::: q
What is iambic pentameter? (1)
---
A line of ten syllables with five stresses in a weak-strong pattern (1).
:::

::: q
Who played female roles in Shakespeare's time? (1)
---
Boys or young men (1).
:::

::: q
Why does the Prologue tell the ending? (2)
---
To create dramatic irony (1), so the audience watches how, not whether, the lovers die, feeling fate at work (1).
:::

::: q
Name two differences between how the Nurse and Juliet speak. (2)
---
The Nurse often speaks rambling prose (1); Juliet speaks verse with control and imagery (1).
:::

::: q
Why does Mercutio's death change the play? (2)
---
It turns comedy into tragedy (1) and pushes Romeo into killing Tybalt, leading to banishment and disaster (1).
:::

::: watch
- Verse is a **performance** challenge: say how the actor uses rhythm, stress and pauses.
- Design for **late 16th century Verona** unless the question says otherwise.
- Fights must be described as **safe, rehearsed** choreography with clear stage positions.
- Use **levels** in the balcony and tomb scenes.
- Juliet is young but not childish; show her growth.
- Don't retell the plot; tie every point to a staging choice and an effect.
:::

---

::: test
Explain one advantage of staging the balcony scene on a thrust stage. (3)
---
The audience is close on three sides, so the intimate verse can be spoken quietly (1); the upper level lets Juliet be high above Romeo, showing the distance between them (1); the audience can see both lovers' faces from different angles, sharing the dramatic irony (1).

::: test
You are performing the role of Lord Capulet. In Act Three, when Juliet refuses to marry Paris, Capulet threatens to throw her out. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform his threat and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Levels. 7–8: exact vocal and physical choices on specific words; effects fully right for Capulet and the moment. 5–6: clear, detailed, effects appropriate. 3–4: some detail. 1–2: vague. Voice or body only cannot go above 3–4.

Indicative content: vocal - sudden loud volume, harsh tone, heavy stress on insults, speed rising; pitch dropping to a menacing growl for the threat. ::: test
