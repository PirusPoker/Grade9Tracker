## What it is

**Context** is the world a play comes from and the world it shows. The specification asks for two separate things:
1. the **social, cultural and historical context in which the play is set**: the place, period, class, beliefs, laws and customs of the characters' world;
2. the **theatrical conventions of the period in which the play was created**: how theatre worked when the playwright wrote it, such as the kind of stage, the style of acting and what audiences expected.

These can be very different. *The Crucible* is **set** in Salem, Massachusetts, in 1692, but was **written** in 1953, when Arthur Miller saw a parallel between the witch trials and the anti-communist hearings in America at the time. *Romeo and Juliet* is set in Verona but was written in the mid-1590s for an Elizabethan open-air playhouse. The setting mostly tells the designer what the world looks like. The period of writing explains why the text is built the way it is.

### Where context is examined

- **Section B, the first question (4 marks), is compulsory for everyone.** It asks you to design a costume or a setting that reflects the context of your set play, using AQA's own description of it, for example "a Puritan community in the late 17th century". This is the only question on the paper that directly rewards context, and every mark depends on turning the context into precise design detail.
- The 8, 12 and 20-mark questions reward choices that are "appropriate" to the play. Accent, manners, movement, status and props all come from context.
- In Section C, a top answer shows how a production used or updated the play's context.

### Context has to change something on stage

History on its own earns nothing in Drama. Every contextual fact must become a choice:

| Contextual fact | Becomes... |
|---|---|
| A religious community that condemns vanity | Plain, dark, modest costume; no ornament; bare wooden interiors; restrained movement |
| Working-class poverty | Worn, patched, faded fabric; second-hand furniture; a damp, peeling wallpaper texture |
| Strict class divisions | Contrasting costume quality; posture and accent; who sits and who stands |
| A historical period | Period cut and fabric, hairstyles, props and furniture, lighting sources (candle, gas, electric) |
| A place | Accent; climate in costume (heat, rain); the sounds of the location; architectural style |
| Laws and attitudes of the time | How openly characters behave; secrecy, fear, shame shown physically |

### Theatrical conventions by period

A **theatrical convention** is an accepted way of doing things on stage that the audience understands, such as a soliloquy, a narrator or a curtain between acts. Conventions change over time:

| Period | Conventions | Plays it helps with |
|---|---|---|
| **Elizabethan (late 16th century)** | Open-air playhouses, performed in daylight; a bare platform stage with the audience on three sides, some standing in the yard; little or no scenery, so the words set the scene; rich costumes; an upper level used as a balcony; boy actors in female roles; verse; soliloquies and asides; music and fights | *Romeo and Juliet* |
| **Late 19th century: naturalism** | The box set (a realistic room with three walls); the fourth wall; detailed, believable acting; gas and then electric lighting that could darken the auditorium | Background to all realistic plays |
| **Mid-20th-century American realism** | Realistic sets and psychology, often with symbolic touches; long, descriptive stage directions | *The Crucible* (1953) |
| **1950s British "kitchen sink"** | Working-class characters in ordinary, shabby rooms; frank subject matter. Joan Littlewood's Theatre Workshop, which first staged *A Taste of Honey* in 1958, also used music and moments of direct address | *A Taste of Honey* |
| **Epic theatre (Brecht and after)** | Episodic scenes, narration, direct address, multi-role, visible stage machinery, placards and projection; the audience is asked to think critically | *Noughts and Crosses*, *The Great Wave* |
| **Musical theatre** | Songs to express feeling; underscoring; a chorus or ensemble; amplified voices; a narrator | *Blood Brothers* |
| **Contemporary theatre** | Physical theatre (Frantic Assembly co-produced the first production of *Things I Know to Be True*); storytelling with a small cast; ensemble playing; projection and multimedia | *Things I Know to Be True*, *Around the World in 80 Days*, *The Empress* |

**Why conventions matter to your answer.** If your play was written for a bare Elizabethan stage, you know its scene changes must be fast and its locations can be suggested rather than built. If it was written as a naturalistic play, the fourth wall and realistic detail are expected, and a director who breaks them is making a deliberate choice. Showing you understand *why* the text works as it does is part of "knowledge of the play as a whole".

### Two approaches: period or updated

Directors either set a play **in its period** (historically accurate design) or **update or relocate** it (for example *Romeo and Juliet* in modern dress, with the feud as a gang rivalry). For the 4-mark question you must follow AQA's stated context. In the 20-mark question and in Section C you may discuss updated concepts, as long as you explain what the change communicates.

::: terms
- **Social context**: the way society was organised in the play's world: class, family, gender roles and community.
- **Cultural context**: the beliefs, customs, religion, arts and values of the play's world.
- **Historical context**: the events, laws and period in which the play is set or was written.
- **Theatrical convention**: an accepted way of presenting something on stage that audiences understand, such as a soliloquy.
- **Period detail**: design features accurate to a particular time, such as a fabric, cut, hairstyle or piece of furniture.
- **Box set**: a realistic setting of a room with three walls, the fourth wall being open to the audience.
- **Fourth wall**: the imaginary wall between the stage and the audience in naturalistic theatre.
- **Kitchen sink drama**: a 1950s style of British drama showing working-class life in realistic domestic settings.
- **Elizabethan playhouse**: an open-air theatre of the late 16th century with a thrust platform stage and little scenery.
- **Concept**: a director's overall interpretation, which may keep a play in its period or update it.
:::

::: method
1. **Separate the two contexts**: when is the play set, and when was it written?
2. **For the 4-mark question**, copy AQA's context into your plan, then list 4–6 precise details (garment or structure, fabric or material, colour, condition, accessories or props), each justified by one contextual fact.
3. **Make every fact change something**: costume, set, props, accent, movement, manners.
4. **Name the conventions of the writing period** when explaining why the text is built as it is, such as a bare stage, soliloquy, box set or narrator.
5. **Keep to "describe" when the question says describe**: exact design detail beats a paragraph of history.
:::

::: example Setting vs writing
Explain why a play's setting and the time it was written can be different, using an example. (3)

A playwright can set a play in an earlier period to comment on their own time (1). *The Crucible* is set in Salem in 1692 but was written in 1953 (1), when Miller saw the witch trials as a parallel to the anti-communist hearings of his own time, so the design shows 1692 while the meaning speaks to the 1950s (1).
:::

::: example Costume for context (Puritan)
You are designing a costume for a young servant girl in a performance of a play set in a Puritan community in the late 17th century. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

An ankle-length dress of coarse, undyed or dull brown wool, because Puritans rejected showy colour and a servant could not afford dyed cloth (1). A plain white linen coif covering all her hair and a white collar fastened at the neck, showing the modesty expected of girls in the community (1). A long, grubby apron with a frayed hem, because she does heavy housework for her employers (1). Worn, flat leather shoes and no jewellery or ribbon, since ornament was seen as sinful vanity, with a slightly-too-large dress suggesting it was handed down (1).
:::

::: example Setting for context (Salford)
You are designing a setting for a play set in working-class Salford in the late 1950s. The scene takes place in a rented flat. Describe your design ideas. (4)

A cramped box set with peeling, faded floral wallpaper stained with damp near the ceiling, showing cheap rented housing (1). A small iron-framed bed half visible through an open door, a worn sofa with a sagging cushion, and a table with an oilcloth cover and two mismatched chairs (1). A single bare bulb hanging from the ceiling and a gas ring on a cupboard for cooking, typical of poor lodgings of the period (1). A small sash window upstage showing grey, smoky sky and a jumble of rooftops and chimneys, placing it in an industrial northern city (1).
:::

::: example Conventions of the period
Explain how the theatrical conventions of the Elizabethan period are reflected in *Romeo and Juliet*. (4)

Plays were performed in daylight on a bare stage, so the language itself tells the audience whether it is night or dawn and where the scene is (1). With no scenery to change, scenes follow each other quickly, which drives the play's fast pace (1). The upper level of the playhouse could be used for scenes where a character appears above, such as the balcony scene (1). Female roles were played by boys, and soliloquies were spoken directly to an audience standing close around a thrust stage, making private thoughts feel shared (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between the context in which a play is set and the context in which it was written. (2)
---
The setting context is the time, place and society shown in the play (1); the writing context is the time and conditions when the playwright created it, including its theatrical conventions (1).
:::

::: q
State two conventions of Elizabethan theatre. (2)
---
Any two: daylight, open-air performance; thrust platform stage with audience on three sides / standing groundlings; little scenery, words set the scene; boy actors in female roles; verse; soliloquies and asides; use of an upper level (1 each).
:::

::: q
Explain how the social class of a character could be shown through costume. (3)
---
Quality and cost of the fabric, such as silk or fine wool against coarse, cheap cotton (1); the condition, such as new and pressed against worn, patched or stained (1); accessories, such as a pocket watch, jewellery or gloves against none (1). Also credit fit (tailored against ill-fitting, handed down).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a "box set" and the period it is associated with. (2)
---
A realistic room with three walls, the missing fourth wall facing the audience (1); associated with late 19th-century naturalism (1).
:::

::: q
Describe design ideas for a costume for a wealthy gentleman in late 19th-century London. (4)
---
4 levels, 1 mark each for precision and appropriateness. Indicative content: dark wool frock coat or morning coat, tailored close to the body; silk waistcoat with a gold pocket watch on a chain; high, starched white wing collar and a silk cravat or tie; pinstriped or grey trousers; top hat; polished black leather boots; gloves and a cane; neatly trimmed moustache or side whiskers and oiled hair; all pressed and immaculate to show wealth and respectability.
:::

::: q
Explain how context could affect the way a performer moves. (3)
---
Costume of the period restricts movement, such as corsets, long skirts or stiff collars producing upright posture (1); social rules shape manners, such as bowing or curtseying, or deference to a higher-status character (1); job and class affect physicality, such as the heavy tread of a farm worker against the controlled walk of a lady (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Writing history instead of design.** The 4-mark question says "describe your design ideas". Every sentence should describe something on stage.
- **Ignoring AQA's context.** If the question says the late 17th century, a modern twist is off-brief there.
- **Vague period words.** "Old-fashioned clothes" earns almost nothing. Name the garment, fabric, colour and condition.
- **Mixing the setting and the writing period.** The Crucible's costumes come from 1692, not from 1953.
- **Inventing facts.** If you are not sure of a date or detail, describe it more generally.
:::

::: test
Explain how the context in which a play is set could influence a performer's vocal choices. (4)
---
Accent and dialect should suit the place, such as a Liverpool or Lancashire accent, or an Australian one (1); class affects register and pronunciation, for example the clipped, formal speech of an upper-class Victorian against an informal working-class voice (1); social rules shape tone and volume, for example a servant speaking quietly and politely to an employer, or a woman in a strict religious community keeping her voice low in public (1); the period and setting may also call for particular forms of address or verse speaking (1).
:::

---

Indicative content: a named scene and AQA's context for the play; set: architecture, furniture, materials, condition and props accurate to period and class; costume: garments, fabric, colour, condition, hair and make-up reflecting class, religion and period; lighting: period sources (candle, firelight, gas, fluorescent, daylight) and climate; sound: period music, location sounds; performance: accent, register, manners, posture and movement shaped by social rules; the conventions of the period of writing, if relevant (for example fast, scenery-free transitions for an Elizabethan text); explaining how each choice helps the audience understand the characters' world and the pressures on them.
