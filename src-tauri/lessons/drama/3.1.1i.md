## What it is

**Costume design** covers everything a performer wears: clothing, accessories, footwear, **hair** (and wigs), **make-up** and masks. It is the design skill that appears most often on the paper. The compulsory 4-mark Section B question very often asks you to design a costume that reflects the context of your set play. The 20-mark question lets you answer as a costume designer, and Section C may ask how costume communicated character. This lesson also covers **puppet design**, one of the design options you may choose in the 20-mark question.

The examiner's key word is **precise**. "A long brown dress" is a start; "an ankle-length dress of coarse, dark brown wool, patched at the elbows, with a frayed hem stiff with mud" is a design.

### What a costume communicates

- **Character**: personality, attitude, how they see themselves (neat, showy, careless).
- **Status and class**: wealth, job, rank, respectability.
- **Period and location**: historical accuracy, climate, culture.
- **Age and health**: fit, posture the garment creates, make-up.
- **Relationships**: matching colours for a family or group; contrast for rivals.
- **Change over time**: a costume that deteriorates, brightens or changes to show a character's journey.
- **Mood and symbolism**: a colour motif (a red scarf that reappears), mourning black, wedding white.

### The costume checklist

Use this list to make any design precise:

| Element | Questions to answer | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Garment** | What exactly is it? | A calf-length tweed skirt; a collarless shirt; a frock coat |
| **Fabric** | What is it made of? | Coarse wool, crisp cotton, silk, polyester, denim, velvet |
| **Colour** | Which shade, and why? | Faded navy, mustard yellow, blood red, dove grey |
| **Cut and shape** | How is it styled? What silhouette does it make? | High-waisted; tailored to the body; boxy; a full skirt |
| **Fit** | Tight, loose, too big, too small? | A suit jacket a size too large, suggesting it is borrowed |
| **Condition** | New, pressed, worn, stained, patched, torn? | Knees worn shiny; a coffee stain on the cuff |
| **Trimmings and accessories** | Buttons, lace, belts, jewellery, bags, hats, gloves | A cheap brooch; a gold watch chain; a headscarf |
| **Footwear** | What shoes, and in what state? | Scuffed work boots; polished brogues; stilettos with worn heels |
| **Hair** | Style, length, colour, wig? | Hair scraped into a tight bun; greased-back quiff |
| **Make-up** | What does the face need? | Dark circles and sallow skin; bright red lipstick |

The specification also asks designers to consider **period detail**, **ethnic and cultural authenticity**, **movement constraints** (can the actor run, fight, dance, kneel?), **individual performer requirements** and **theatrical devices** such as quick-change fastenings (Velcro, poppers, magnets) and costumes built to tear away.

### Hair and make-up

- **Hair and wigs** show period (a 1960s beehive, Victorian ringlets), age (greying at the temples), class and grooming (unkempt or carefully set), and ethnicity and culture.
- **Types of make-up** named in the specification: **grease-paint** (a traditional stick make-up), **liquids** (foundation), **powders** (to set make-up and reduce shine under lights), **facial hair** (false beards, moustaches and sideburns, often attached with spirit **gum**), **eyelashes**, **putty** (for building up noses or scars), **prosthetics** (moulded latex or silicone pieces for wounds, ageing or fantasy features) and **stage blood**.
- **Ageing make-up** uses highlight and shadow on the natural lines of the face (under the eyes, the nose-to-mouth line, the forehead), greyed eyebrows and hair, and paler or blotchy skin.
- **Fantasy make-up** creates non-human characters: animals, spirits, monsters.
- **Corrective or straight make-up** stops actors looking washed out under strong stage light.

### Quick changes and practicalities

Costumes must survive eight shows a week. Designers think about washing, strength at the seams, sweat, safety (no trailing hems on stairs) and **quick changes** in the wings. In storytelling and epic plays a single **accessory** (a hat, a shawl, a pair of glasses) can signal a new character, letting one actor multi-role without leaving the stage.

### Puppet design

A **puppet designer** creates a figure that an actor (a puppeteer) brings to life. The specification names these types:

| Type | How it works | Qualities |
|---|---|---|
| **Rod puppet** | Controlled by rods attached to the head, hands or body, usually from below or behind | Graceful, large movements; the puppeteer can be visible |
| **String puppet (marionette)** | Hangs from strings attached to a control bar held above | Delicate, floating, can "walk"; hard to operate |
| **Human-arm puppet** | The puppeteer's own arm and hand become part of the puppet, often a large head operated by one hand while the other hand works as the puppet's hand | Expressive, lively hands and gestures |
| **Shadow puppet** | A flat cut-out figure held between a light and a screen, so the audience sees its shadow | Dreamlike, simple, good for memories, stories and scale changes |

Also: **hand or glove puppets**, and **Bunraku-style** puppets from Japan, operated by several visible puppeteers.

A puppet designer decides the **character** (what the puppet must express), **materials** (wood, cane, foam, fabric, card, wire, leather), **scale** (life-size or giant or tiny), **shape and colour**, **costume**, **structural design** (joints, weight, balance), **functionality** (which parts move: eyes, mouth, head, tail) and how the **puppeteers manipulate** it (hidden or visible, one or several operators).

**A professional example.** In the National Theatre's *War Horse*, Handspring Puppet Company built life-size horses with open frames, largely of cane and leather, so the audience can see through them. Each horse is worked by three visible puppeteers: one at the head and two inside the body working the front and back legs. They breathe, flick ears and shift weight so convincingly that the audience forgets them. The visible puppeteers become part of the storytelling. Disney's stage *The Lion King*, directed and designed by Julie Taymor, also shows its performers openly, with animal masks and puppets worn above or around them.

::: terms
- **Costume**: everything a performer wears on stage, including clothing, accessories, hair, make-up and masks.
- **Silhouette**: the overall outline or shape a costume creates, often a strong sign of period.
- **Period detail**: features of a costume that are accurate to a particular historical time.
- **Accessories**: items added to a costume, such as hats, gloves, jewellery, bags and belts, that add character detail.
- **Quick change**: a fast costume change during a performance, often helped by hidden fastenings.
- **Prosthetics**: moulded pieces, often latex, attached to the face or body to change features or create wounds.
- **Ageing make-up**: make-up using highlight and shadow to make a performer look older.
- **Grease-paint**: a traditional oil-based stage make-up.
- **Rod puppet**: a puppet controlled by rods attached to its head, body or hands.
- **Marionette**: a puppet controlled from above by strings attached to a control bar.
- **Shadow puppet**: a flat figure held between a light source and a screen so the audience sees its shadow.
- **Puppeteer**: the performer who operates and brings a puppet to life.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the brief**: character, extract, AQA's context, and what the costume must show.
2. **Go head to toe** using the checklist: garment, fabric, colour, cut, fit, condition, accessories, footwear, hair, make-up.
3. **Justify every detail** with one reason: context (period, place, religion, class), character (personality, status, age) or the moment (what they have just been doing).
4. **For 4 marks**, aim for 4–6 precise details, each with a reason, in about 5–6 minutes. The question says describe, so describe.
5. **For the 20-mark question**, add practicalities (movement, quick changes), how costume supports action in the extract, and how it changes across the whole play.
6. **For puppets**: type, materials, scale, what moves, who operates it, and the effect on the audience.
:::

::: example Costume for context (Victorian)
You are designing a costume for a working-class maid in late 19th-century London. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

A long-sleeved, ankle-length dress in a practical dark grey or black cotton print, with a fitted bodice and a simple high collar, reflecting the plain dress required of servants (1). A starched white apron over it, with a bib pinned at the chest, slightly greyed and with a scorch mark near the hem from the kitchen range (1). A frilled white cap pinned over hair that is pulled back into a tight bun, showing her tidy, controlled appearance for her employers (1). Black lace-up boots, worn down at the heel from long days on her feet, and red, rough hands with no rings, showing the hard physical work (1).
:::

::: example Costume for a contemporary character
You are designing a costume for a teenage boy who has just started at a new, expensive private school on a scholarship. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

A school blazer in dark green wool with a gold crest, a little too big across the shoulders because it was bought second-hand to last (1). Grey trousers slightly too short, showing white socks, while the other pupils wear dark ones (1). A tie knotted tightly and neatly, showing his anxiety to fit in, and a white shirt ironed but thinning at the collar (1). Black shoes that are clean but cheap and creased, and a well-worn rucksack instead of the leather satchels of the others, which tells the audience about the class gap (1).
:::

::: example Make-up
Describe how make-up could be used to show that a character has been living rough for several weeks. (3)

A pale, slightly grey foundation with dark brown and purple shading under the eyes to show exhaustion and poor sleep (1). Brown and grey powders smudged along the jawline and hands to suggest dirt, with dry, cracked-looking lips (1). Several days' stubble, applied with crepe hair or grown, and greasy, matted hair, possibly with a small scab on one cheekbone made with putty or stage blood (1).
:::

::: example Puppet design
Describe a puppet design for a small bird that must fly around the stage and land on a character's hand. (4)

A rod puppet, about the size of a real blackbird, made of lightweight carved balsa wood for the body and layered paper feathers for the wings, so it is light enough to move quickly (1). A long, thin black rod attached under the body, operated by a puppeteer dressed in black who follows it (1). The wings are hinged with fabric and attached to a second, thin rod or strings, so the puppeteer can make them beat or glide (1). The head is on a loose joint so it can tilt and peck, bringing the bird to life, and its bright yellow beak draws the audience's eye (1).
:::

::: q
Name two types of puppet. (2)
---
Any two: rod, string (marionette), human-arm, shadow, glove or hand, Bunraku (1 each).
:::

::: q
Explain how a costume could show a character's change over the course of a play. (3)
---
The costume at the start reflects their situation, for example neat and bright (1); later versions change in condition, colour or style, becoming worn, dirty or darker, or smarter and more expensive (1); the audience can see the character's rise or decline without it being stated (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by "movement constraints" in costume design. (2)
---
Limits a costume places on how an actor can move, such as a tight skirt, corset or heavy armour (1); the designer must make sure the actor can perform the required action (running, fighting, dancing) or use the constraint deliberately to show character (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how ageing make-up could be applied. (3)
---
Shading with brown or grey in the natural lines of the face, such as under the eyes, the nose-to-mouth lines and the forehead (1); highlights on the raised areas next to the lines to make wrinkles stand out (1); greying the eyebrows and hair with powder or a wig, and a paler, blotchy skin tone (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a single accessory could help a performer to multi-role. (2)
---
A distinctive item, such as a hat, shawl or pair of glasses, can be put on or taken off in seconds (1), so the audience instantly recognises a new character without a full costume change (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage of making puppeteers visible to the audience. (2)
---
The audience can see the skill of the operation and connect to both the puppet and the puppeteer (1); the puppeteers' breathing and focus add life to the puppet, and it suits a storytelling style (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Colour only.** Name fabric, cut, fit, condition and accessories too, each tied to the character or context.
- **"Old-fashioned" or "period clothes".** Name the actual garment: frock coat, coif, pinafore, doublet, cardigan.
- **No reason.** Every detail needs a *because*: context, character or the moment.
- **Forgetting the extract.** What has the character just been doing? Mud, flour or blood might be on the costume.
- **Ignoring practicality.** Can the actor fight, climb or change quickly in it?
- **Puppets with no operation.** Say which type, who operates it and which parts move.
:::

::: test
Explain how a shadow puppet could be used to create a dramatic effect. (3)
---
A flat figure is placed between a light and a screen, so the audience sees a shadow (1); moving it towards or away from the light changes its size, so it can grow into a monster or shrink away (1); this creates a dreamlike, mysterious effect suited to memories, stories within the story or frightening moments (1).
:::

::: test
You are designing a costume for a mother in a Puritan community in the late 17th century. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)
---
4 levels, 1 mark each. Indicative content: an ankle-length dress with a fitted, high-necked bodice and long sleeves in heavy dark wool or linen (brown, dark grey, deep green or muted blue); a wide, plain white linen collar; a white coif covering her hair, as married women were expected to; a plain linen apron, well used; no lace, jewellery or ribbons, since display was thought sinful; sturdy low-heeled leather shoes; a dark woollen shawl or cloak for outdoors; the worn but clean condition shows a hard-working, respectable household.
:::

::: test
You are designing a costume for a young working-class woman in Salford in the late 1950s. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)
---
4 levels, 1 mark each. Indicative content: a knee-length cotton dress with a fitted waist and full skirt in a cheap floral print, faded from washing; a thin cardigan with darned elbows; flat, scuffed shoes or cheap court shoes; a headscarf for outdoors; hair set in a short style or tied back; little make-up, perhaps a bright lipstick for going out; a cheap raincoat for the damp northern weather; the overall look shows limited money, an attempt to follow fashion, and the industrial northern setting.
:::

::: test
You are designing a costume for an 11-year-old boy in a Liverpudlian community in the late 1960s. The actor playing him is an adult. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)
---
4 levels, 1 mark each. Indicative content: a hand-knitted, bobbled jumper a size too small and stretched at the neck, in a faded colour; short trousers (or cheap jeans) with grass stains on the knees; grey socks falling down; scuffed plimsolls or worn-down school shoes; a short, uneven home haircut; smudges of dirt on the face; clothes that are hand-me-downs, showing a large, poor family; the short trousers and oversized socks help the audience accept the adult actor as a child.
:::

::: test
Explain how costume could be used to show the difference in status between two characters in the same scene. (4)
---
Fabric quality: rich, fine fabrics such as silk or tailored wool against coarse or synthetic cloth (1); condition: pristine and pressed against worn, stained or patched (1); fit: tailored to the body against ill-fitting or handed down (1); accessories and colour: jewellery, a watch, gloves or a strong confident colour against none and muted tones (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how hair and make-up could be used to show that a character is ill. (3)
---
A pale, slightly grey or yellowish foundation, set with powder so the skin looks dull under the lights (1); shading under the eyes and in the hollows of the cheeks to make the face look drawn and thin, with dry, pale lips (1); lank, greasy or untidy hair, possibly flattened on one side as if from lying in bed (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the decisions a puppet designer must make when designing a puppet for a production. (6)
---
Levels. 5–6: a range of decisions explained precisely, with examples and links to character and performance. 3–4: several decisions with some explanation. 1–2: limited.

Indicative content: type of puppet (rod, string, human-arm, shadow, hand, Bunraku) and why; scale (life-size, giant, miniature) and its effect; materials (wood, cane, foam, fabric, leather, wire) for weight, durability and look; colour, texture and costume to show character; which parts move (head, eyes, mouth, limbs, tail) and how they are controlled; how many puppeteers and whether they are visible; safety and how it will survive repeated performances; its appeal to the audience.
:::

::: test
You are the costume designer for a production of a play you have studied. Describe how you would use your design skills to create costumes for one character, and explain why your ideas are appropriate for the play as a whole. (20)
---
Levels. 16–20: an excellent, well-developed design with an extensive range of precise costume details (garment, fabric, colour, cut, fit, condition, accessories, footwear, hair and make-up), entirely appropriate to the character, the play's context and style, clearly supporting the action of key moments; convincing account of how the costume changes or develops across the whole play. 11–15: a clear, secure design with a wide range of precise details and good links to character, context and the whole play. 6–10: a reasonable design with some detail and some links. 1–5: a limited design with few precise details or little justification.

Indicative content: a named character and interpretation; a first costume described head to toe with reasons (period, class, religion, place, personality); hair and make-up; practical needs (movement, fights, quick changes); how costume supports a key moment (for example a disguise, a wedding, an arrest, a death, a fall in status); changes across the play (deterioration, colour shifts, symbolic accessories, contrast with other characters); the overall effect on the audience's understanding of the character and the play's themes.
:::

::: test
Explain how colour in costume design could be used to connect or contrast groups of characters. (4)
---
Members of one family or faction share a colour palette, such as blues against reds (1), so the audience instantly understands the loyalties and divisions on stage (1); a character caught between the two groups could wear elements of both (1); a change of colour during the play, such as adopting the rival's colour, shows a shift in allegiance (1).
:::
