## What it is

Every Section B question opens with the same compulsory **4-mark design question**. It is the first part of your set-play question (for example 05.1 for The Crucible or 06.1 for Blood Brothers), and everyone answers it, whatever route you take later. It always has three parts:

1. **The task.** You are designing either a **costume** for a named character in the extract, or a **setting** for a performance of the extract.
2. **The context.** The setting or costume "must reflect the context" of the play, and AQA names that context in a short phrase, such as a Puritan community in the late 17th century.
3. **The command.** "Describe your design ideas."

The command word is **describe**, not explain. The marks go on what your design *looks like*, in exact detail, and on how well each detail fits the context. A long justification of a vague design scores less than a short answer full of precise, well-chosen detail. You do not need any practical design experience: the spec says so, and the question tests knowledge, not drawing.

**How it is marked.** It is a single judgement with four levels, one mark each. A 4 is a precise, detailed design that fits the brief exactly. A 3 has a number of precise details with clear links to context. A 2 has a few details and some relevance. A 1 is vague or only partly fitting ("a long old-fashioned dress"). **Precision** is what separates the levels. Precision means naming the fabric, colour, cut, condition and accessories, or for a setting the materials, furniture, scale and dressing.

### Two kinds of context

Strong answers use both kinds of context.

- **Social, cultural and historical context.** This is the world AQA names in the question: the period, the place, class, religion and fashion. It tells you *what things were made of and looked like*.
- **Narrative context.** This is what is happening in this extract: the time of day, the season, what the character has just done, where they have come from, how much money they have now, and what the stage directions say. It tells you *the condition and details* of the design. Has the character just come in from the fields? Travelled a long way? Got dressed up to impress someone? Are they heavily pregnant?

Read the stage directions at the top of the extract and any line that mentions clothing, furniture or weather. **Never contradict the text.** A heavy winter cloak in a scene the playwright sets in spring, or roses in full bloom when the stage direction says otherwise, tells the examiner you haven't read carefully.

### The checklists

**Costume.** Name the **garment and cut** (ankle-length, high-necked, fitted bodice), the **fabric** (coarse undyed wool, cheap nylon, heavy velvet), a specific **colour** (faded slate grey, not "dark"), the **fit and condition** (a hand-me-down jumper two sizes too big, darned at the elbow), **accessories** (a pocket watch on a chain, a cheap gilt brooch), **footwear** (scuffed black lace-up boots), **hair or headwear** (hair scraped under a white linen coif) and **make-up** only where it adds meaning (heavy red lipstick, grime on a child's knees).

**Setting.** Name the **type and scale** (a cramped naturalistic box set, a composite street), the **walls** (rough-sawn planks, peeling floral wallpaper), the **floor** (scrubbed boards, worn linoleum, artificial turf), the **furniture** with its material, condition and position (a scarred oak table centre stage), the **props and dressing** (a chipped enamel teapot), the **colour palette**, and the **levels and entrances** (a staircase upstage left). A setting question credits set, props and dressing, but not lighting or sound, so don't spend your five minutes on a sunset.

**Traps.** "Working class" does not mean filthy and ragged: show poverty through specific items such as a coat worn thin at the cuffs or clothes bought to grow into. Avoid anachronisms such as a dog collar on a 1690s minister. A pre-learnt history paragraph earns nothing unless it becomes a fabric, colour or object. A sketch may help, but the marks come from sentences.

### Context and design clues for the nine set plays

| Play | AQA's context | Design clues (starting points, not a script) |
|---|---|---|
| **The Crucible** | a Puritan community in the late 17th century | wool, linen and leather; sober browns, greys and dark greens; coifs, plain white collars, aprons; breeches, stockings, buckled shoes; plain timber rooms with little decoration, since show was sinful |
| **Blood Brothers** | a Liverpudlian community in the 1960s | the Johnstones: worn, hand-me-down, cheap fabrics, a pinafore, headscarf, plimsolls; the Lyonses: tailored shift dresses, pearls, twinsets, polished shoes; a terraced street against a comfortable modern house |
| **Noughts and Crosses** | the conventions of contemporary dystopian theatre | stylised rather than period; a controlled colour code separating the ruling Crosses from the noughts; uniforms, badges, security; stark, multi-purpose set pieces that transform quickly |
| **Around the World in 80 Days** | late 19th-century London/England | frock coats, waistcoats, top hats, pocket watches, cravats; a manservant's plainer livery and white gloves; the club's leather armchairs, dark panelling and newspapers; a few suggestive pieces suited to storytelling |
| **Things I Know to Be True** | a contemporary working-class Australian suburb | a well-kept back garden, lawn and rose bed, a patio table and plastic chairs; practical, casual clothes: shorts, polo shirts, flip-flops, cardigans for cooler seasons |
| **Romeo and Juliet** | late 16th-century Verona | Italian Renaissance: fitted doublets and hose, gowns with square necklines, velvet, brocade and silk for the rich; the Nurse in plainer linen; stone arches, tapestries, a curtained bed |
| **A Taste of Honey** | working-class Salford in the late 1950s | a damp, shabby rented flat: stained wallpaper, a gas ring, a single divan; Helen's cheap glamour (fur-trimmed coat, lipstick, costume jewellery) against Jo's plainer cardigans and skirts |
| **The Great Wave** | North Korea in the early 1980s (and Japan from the late 1970s) | North Korea: bare, institutional rooms, grey concrete, portraits of the leaders, plain suits and uniforms, lapel badges; Japan: a family home with sliding screens and low tables, school uniforms |
| **The Empress** | the last fourteen years (1887–1901) of Queen Victoria's rule | late-Victorian bustles, high collars and corsets; the widowed Queen in black; saris, shawls and turbans for Indian characters; palace rooms thick with ornament against docks and cheap lodgings |

::: terms
- **Design and context question**: the compulsory 4-mark Section B question asking you to design a costume or setting that reflects the play's context.
- **Social, historical and cultural context**: the period, place, class, religion and customs of the world in which the play is set.
- **Narrative context**: what is happening in the extract itself: time of day, season, what has just happened and the character's situation.
- **Precision**: exact, specific detail such as a named fabric, shade of colour, cut and condition, rather than general description.
- **Cut and fit**: the shape of a garment and how closely it follows the body, which can suggest period, status and personality.
- **Condition**: whether a costume or object is new, worn, patched, stained or pristine, showing wealth, work and circumstances.
- **Set dressing**: the small objects placed on a set, such as crockery, pictures and ornaments, that make a location believable.
- **Anachronism**: a detail that belongs to the wrong period, such as a zip on a 17th-century dress.
- **Box set**: a naturalistic set showing a room with three walls, the missing fourth wall facing the audience.
- **Composite set**: a single set that shows several locations at once, so the action can move between them without scene changes.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the brief.** Is it costume or setting? Which character, or which location? Underline AQA's context phrase.
2. **Read the extract's narrative context**: the stage directions at the top, the time of day and season, what has just happened, and any line that mentions clothing, furniture or weather.
3. **Pick five or six elements** from the checklist: for costume, garment, fabric, colour, condition, accessories, footwear and hair; for setting, structure, floor, furniture, props, palette and entrances.
4. **Write each element precisely** (named fabric, named colour, condition) and tie it to the context in a few words: "...because Puritans saw ornament as vanity."
5. **Include one detail from the narrative context** (mud from the fields, a travelling cloak, a pregnancy, a birthday table) to show you know this moment and not just the period.
6. **Stop at about 120–160 words, after about five minutes.** Write in sentences, with no introduction and no lighting or sound in a setting answer.
:::

::: example The Crucible: costume for Abigail
You are designing a costume for Abigail to wear in a performance of an extract from Act One. The costume must reflect the context of The Crucible, set in a Puritan community in the late 17th century. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

Abigail would wear an ankle-length dress of coarse woven wool in a muted dark green, with a fitted bodice laced at the front and long, close sleeves, because Puritan girls were expected to cover their bodies modestly (1). Over it goes a plain, undyed linen apron, slightly greyed from work, showing that she is a servant-girl niece in Parris's household rather than a lady (1). Her hair is pinned up under a white linen coif, but I would let one or two dark strands escape at the temple, a small sign of the rule-breaking that the dancing in the woods has exposed (1). She has a plain white falling collar tied at the throat, black wool stockings and low-heeled leather shoes with scuffed toes, and a hem darkened with mud from the forest, and she wears no jewellery or lace, which would have been condemned as vanity (1).

*Why this gets 4: every item has a fabric, colour or condition; the period is right; and the loose hair and muddy hem use the narrative context.*
:::

::: example Blood Brothers: a setting
You are designing a setting for a performance of an extract set outside the Johnstones' house when Mickey is seven. The setting must reflect the context of Blood Brothers, set in a Liverpudlian community in the 1960s. Describe your design ideas for the setting. (4)

The setting is a composite street of red-brick terraced houses built from flats painted with soot-darkened brick, with the Johnstones' front door downstage left: its dark green paint is peeling and it opens straight onto the pavement, as the older terraces did (1). The pavement is grey paving slabs with a kerb running across the front of the stage, and a low brick wall centre stage gives the children somewhere to sit and climb (1). The dressing tells the story of the area: a milk crate with empty bottles on the step, a torn poster on a gable end, a lamp post upstage right and a line of washing strung between two back yards (1). The whole palette is greys, faded reds and dirty cream, which contrasts with the bright, clean lines of the Lyonses' house, a truck upstage right with a white-painted door and a potted plant (1).
:::

::: example A Taste of Honey: costume for Helen
You are designing a costume for Helen when she arrives at the flat at the start of the play. The costume must reflect the context of A Taste of Honey, set in working-class Salford in the late 1950s. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

Helen wears a knee-length, cheap imitation-fur coat in dull caramel, bald at the cuffs and pockets, over a tight-waisted, full-skirted rayon dress in a loud cherry print, the 1950s fashion she can just about afford (1). Her accessories are costume jewellery: a big glass-bead necklace, clip-on earrings and a cracked patent handbag, glamour that is showy but cheap (1). She wears black court shoes with heels too high for carrying suitcases up to a rented flat, and her nylon stockings have a ladder she hasn't noticed (1). Her hair is set in stiff waves and she wears heavy red lipstick and powder a little too pale, a woman in her forties working hard at looking younger, which shows her vanity and contrasts with Jo's plain school-age clothes (1).
:::

::: example Upgrading a weak answer
A student writes: "Mrs Johnstone wears old, cheap clothes to show she is poor. She has messy hair." Rewrite this as a 4-mark answer. (4)

Mrs Johnstone wears a faded floral cotton housecoat in washed-out blue, belted at the waist with a cardigan over it, both darned at the elbows because nothing is thrown away in a house with so many children (1). Her tan nylon stockings are wrinkled at the ankle above worn flat slippers, since she is at home cleaning (1). Her hair is pinned under a knotted chiffon headscarf, the everyday style of 1960s Liverpool housewives (1). She wears a thin gold wedding ring she still hasn't taken off although her husband has left, and a little pink lipstick she applies before going out to work, which hints at the young woman who once loved dancing (1).
:::

::: q
You are designing a costume for the Nurse in Romeo and Juliet. The costume must reflect the context of the play, set in late 16th-century Verona. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)
---
One mark per level. Indicative: a full-length gown in hard-wearing brown or russet wool, laced bodice, sleeves pushed up for work; a linen apron and a wimple or close white cap, as a servant of middle years; a bunch of keys and a small purse at her belt, showing her trusted place in the Capulet household; practical leather shoes; less ornament than Lady Capulet but better than a kitchen maid, a slightly worn velvet trim perhaps handed down by the family.
:::

::: q
You are designing a costume for Phileas Fogg at the Reform Club. The costume must reflect the context of Around the World in 80 Days, set in late 19th-century London. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)
---
One mark per level. Indicative: a charcoal wool frock coat, knee-length and immaculately pressed; a dove-grey waistcoat with a silver pocket watch on a chain, fitting a man ruled by the clock; a stiff white wing collar and black silk cravat; pinstriped trousers; polished black boots; a black top hat and kid gloves, everything precise and without a crease, showing his exactness and wealth.
:::

::: q
You are designing a setting for an interrogation scene in Noughts and Crosses. The setting must reflect the conventions of contemporary dystopian theatre. Describe your design ideas for the setting. (4)
---
One mark per level. Indicative: a bare, raked grey floor with a single steel table and two chairs; the Cross official's chair higher and padded, the nought's a plastic stacking chair; a wall of frosted panels or a mesh screen suggesting surveillance; a CCTV camera prop angled at the prisoner; symbolic colour: the ruling group's colour in the only decorative item (a flag or insignia); pieces on castors so the room can transform quickly, suiting the episodic style.
:::

::: q
You are designing a setting for a scene in the North Korean compound in The Great Wave. The setting must reflect the context of North Korea in the early 1980s. Describe your design ideas for the setting. (4)
---
One mark per level. Indicative: a small, bare classroom-like room with grey-painted concrete walls and a single high window with bars; framed portraits of the leaders hung centrally and higher than anything else; a plain metal desk, two hard chairs, a blackboard with Japanese characters; worn institutional linoleum; a locked door upstage; nothing personal in the room, showing control and isolation.
:::

::: q
State four elements of a costume, other than colour, that a precise design answer should name. (4)
---
Any four (1 each): fabric/material; cut, shape or length; fit; condition; accessories; footwear; hair or headwear; make-up; texture; trimmings.
:::

::: q
Explain why the command word "describe" matters in the 4-mark design question. (2)
---
The marks are for the design itself, what it looks like, in precise detail (1); a long justification or history lesson attached to a vague design scores low, so most of the answer should be concrete detail, with only brief links to context (1).
:::

::: watch
- Four marks go on precision. A design that is right for the period but vague about fabric, colour and condition rarely gets beyond 2.
- Writing a pre-learnt paragraph of historical context that never becomes a design choice.
- Contradicting the extract: wrong season, wrong time of day, or ignoring a costume the stage directions describe.
- Putting lighting or sound in a *setting* answer. They are not credited.
- Relying on stereotypes (all poor characters filthy and ragged) or anachronisms (a dog collar in 1692).
- Spending ten minutes on four marks. Write about five minutes' worth and move on.
- Only drawing a labelled sketch. Sketches are allowed, but the ideas must be described in sentences.
:::
