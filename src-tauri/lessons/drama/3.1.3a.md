## What it is

**Section C** of the written paper is worth **32 marks**, 40% of the paper. You answer **one question from a choice of three** about a **live theatre production you have seen**. One question is usually about **performers** and the other two about **design** (set, costume, lighting or sound). Each asks you to **describe** how something was done, then **analyse and evaluate** how successful it was. Your answer is marked twice: **AO3** (knowledge and understanding: what you describe, and how precisely) out of 12, and **AO4** (analysis and evaluation) out of 20.

This lesson is about the preparation that makes the 32 marks possible. You can't take notes into the exam: the only book allowed is a clean copy of your set play. Everything you write in Section C comes from **memory**, so you need a bank of vivid, precise moments that you can bend to whatever the question asks.

### The rules

| Rule | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| **Not your set play** | Section B and Section C must be on different plays. If you studied Blood Brothers, you can't write about a production of Blood Brothers in Section C. |
| **Live theatre** | This can be a play, physical theatre, theatre in education or musical theatre, professional or amateur, but **not a peer performance** (not your classmates or another GCSE group). |
| **Live or digital** | You must have experienced a live performance in the same room as the performers (your school confirms this to AQA), but in the exam you may write about a **digital recording or streamed production**. |
| **Big enough to answer every question** | The production your school prepares should run at least 50 minutes (not counting intervals), include at least two actors and dialogue, and use a range of lighting, sound, set and costume. |
| **Name it** | Start your answer by stating the production's title (and company, venue or how you saw it) in one sentence. |

### Before the show: background research

The spec expects research into three areas, so that you understand what you're watching and can judge it.
- **Plot and characters.** Read a synopsis or the script so you aren't following the story on the night. You're free to watch the craft.
- **The style or genre, and its hallmarks.** Is it naturalistic, physical theatre, epic, musical, verbatim, storytelling? What features do you expect (direct address, ensemble movement, projection, multi-roling)?
- **Context of the play and of the production.** When and where the play was written and set; who made this production (company, director, designers) and when; what it might be trying to say to its audience now.

You may read **reviews** and the **programme**. They're useful for names, context and design intentions. But your descriptions and judgements in the exam must be **your own**, based on what you saw and felt.

### During and straight after: the notes grid

As soon as you can after the performance (in the interval, on the coach, that evening), fill in a grid while your memory is fresh:

| Area | What to record |
|---|---|
| **Performers** | 3–4 key actors: vocal choices (accent, pitch, pace, pause, volume, tone) and physical choices (posture, gait, gesture, facial expression, eye contact, proxemics), each tied to a specific line or moment; ensemble work |
| **Set** | configuration; a quick sketch or ground plan; materials, colours, scale, levels; how scenes changed |
| **Costume** | 2–3 characters: garments, fabric, colour, fit, condition; changes across the show |
| **Lighting** | 3–4 memorable states: colour, angle, intensity, gobos, special effects, how transitions worked |
| **Sound** | music, sound effects, underscoring, silence, where the sound came from, live or recorded |
| **Key moments** | the opening, a climax, a moment of comedy, a moment of tension, the ending |
| **Audience response** | what you and the audience did: laughed, gasped, went silent, cried |

### Turning notes into a revision bank

For revision, build **6–8 precise moments for each area** (acting, and at least two design skills). A strong moment has the **when** (which scene), the **what** (exact detail: a named colour, speed, position or word) and the **so what** (what it communicated and how well it worked). Moments that can be adapted are the most valuable. A single scene may serve a question on relationships, on tension, on lighting mood and on set. Don't pre-learn a fixed essay. Examiners notice when the same few moments are forced onto a question they don't fit.

### Interpretation and message

The spec also asks you to consider **how the play was interpreted** and **what the company might be trying to communicate**. Ask what choices this production made that another might not, and what it wanted the audience to feel or think. A clear view on this makes your evaluation stronger, because you can judge whether each choice served the production's aims.

### The worked example used in these lessons

The National Theatre's **The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time**, adapted by Simon Stephens from Mark Haddon's novel, was first staged in 2012 and directed by Marianne Elliott. The set and costume were by Bunny Christie, the lighting by Paule Constable, the video by Finn Ross, and the movement by Frantic Assembly's Scott Graham and Steven Hoggett. It toured widely and was streamed by National Theatre at Home. Christopher, a fifteen-year-old who experiences the world with great intensity and precision, sets out to discover who killed a neighbour's dog, and uncovers truths about his own family that send him alone on a journey to London.

The staging shows the world from inside Christopher's mind. The stage is a black box whose walls and floor are marked with a grid, like graph paper. Lights and projections set into the walls and floor draw his thoughts, maps, numbers and stars. Ensemble actors lift and carry him, so that he seems to walk up walls or float. His journey to London becomes a chaotic assault of light, sound and movement that makes the audience share his sensory overload. Its interpretation is non-naturalistic, physical and visual, and it invites the audience to see *with* Christopher rather than look *at* him.

**Substitute your own production.** Use this example to learn the method, then build your own bank on the show your class saw. If you saw Curious Incident yourself, rely on your own memory of that performance, not this summary.

::: terms
- **Live theatre production**: a performance experienced as an audience member, professional or amateur but not by peers, which Section C must be about.
- **Digital theatre**: a recorded or streamed production, which may be written about in Section C.
- **Background research**: finding out about a production's plot, characters, style and context before or after seeing it.
- **Style**: the overall manner of a production, such as naturalistic, physical theatre, epic or musical theatre.
- **Hallmarks**: the typical features that identify a style or a company's work.
- **Interpretation**: the particular choices a production makes about how to stage a play and what it means.
- **Production values**: the design elements of a show, such as lighting, sound, set and costume.
- **AO3**: the assessment objective for knowledge and understanding of how drama is developed and performed.
- **AO4**: the assessment objective for analysing and evaluating the work of others.
- **Ensemble**: a group of performers working together as a unit, often creating images, movement and multiple roles.
:::

::: method
1. **Before the show**: research the plot, characters, style and hallmarks, the context of the play and production, and the creative team.
2. **Straight after the show**: fill in the notes grid (performers, set, costume, lighting, sound, key moments, audience response) and sketch the set.
3. **Research after**: read the programme and reviews for names and intentions, but form your own judgements.
4. **Build a revision bank** of 6–8 precise moments per area, each with when, what (exact detail) and so what (meaning and success).
5. **Decide what the production was trying to communicate**, and how its interpretation served that.
6. **Practise adapting moments** to different question focuses (relationships, tension, comedy, mood, setting, character) under timed conditions.
:::

::: example Turning a vague note into a precise one
A student's note reads: "The lighting was really good in the train scene." Rewrite it as a precise revision-bank moment. (4)

During the journey to London, the rows of lights in the floor and walls chased across the grid in fast, streaking lines of cold white, and the general light dropped so Christopher was lit only by these moving strips (1). The lighting flickered in time with a loud, pounding rhythm in the sound design (1). It communicated the speed and noise of the train as Christopher experiences it: overwhelming, with no escape (1). It was highly successful because I felt disoriented myself and understood why he curled up, so it built empathy rather than only spectacle (1).
:::

::: example Introducing the production
Write the opening sentence or two of a Section C answer. (2)

I saw the National Theatre's production of The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time, adapted by Simon Stephens from Mark Haddon's novel and directed by Marianne Elliott, as a streamed recording (1). It is a non-naturalistic production that uses physical theatre, light and projection to show the world through the eyes of its fifteen-year-old hero, Christopher (1).
:::

::: example Research into context
Explain how background research into a production's style could help you write about it in the exam. (3)

Knowing the style (for example, that Frantic Assembly's physical theatre shaped the movement in Curious Incident) helps you name what you saw accurately, such as lifts and ensemble movement rather than "dancing" (1). It helps you analyse why choices were made: a non-naturalistic style exists to show inner experience rather than a real place (1). And it gives you a standard for evaluation: you can judge whether the production achieved what its style aimed for (1).
:::

::: example One moment, several questions
Show how one moment from a production could be used for two different Section C questions. (4)

The opening of Curious Incident, with the neighbour's dead dog on the floor of the bare black box and Christopher crouched beside it (1), could answer a **set or lighting** question: the empty, dark grid space made a stark, shocking first image, with nothing to distract from the body and the boy (1). It could also answer a **performer** question: when a policeman takes hold of him, Christopher recoils, curls up on the floor and cries out, which shows at once how unbearable touch is to him and why the adults misread him (1). The notes are the same, but the focus changes to fit the question. Record exactly what *you* saw of the moment, so you can describe it precisely either way (1).
:::

::: q
State two conditions that the production you write about in Section C must meet. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): it must not be your set play; it must be live theatre (play, physical theatre, TIE or musical), professional or amateur, not peer; it may be a digital recording or stream; the production studied should be at least 50 minutes, with at least two actors, dialogue and a range of production values.
:::

::: q
Explain why you need precise notes soon after seeing a production. (2)
---
In the exam no notes are allowed, so you write from memory (1); detail fades fast, and Section C rewards precise, specific description (colours, positions, lines), not general impressions (1).
:::

::: q
Name the three areas of background research the specification expects. (3)
---
Plot and characters (1); features or hallmarks of the style or genre (1); context of the play or production (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why reading reviews can help, and one danger of relying on them. (2)
---
They give context, names of the creative team and insight into intentions (1); but opinions in the exam must be your own, based on what you saw, and copied judgements are generic and not evidenced (1).
:::

::: q
Describe precisely one moment of set or lighting from a production you have seen. (4)
---
Up to 4: when it happened (1); precise design detail, e.g. colour, angle, materials, position (1); what it communicated (1); a judgement of how well it worked, with a reason (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by the "interpretation" of a play in a production. (2)
---
The choices a company makes about how to stage the play (style, setting, casting, design) (1), which shape what the play means and what message it communicates to its audience (1).
:::

::: watch
- Section C marks precision. Students who wrote no notes after the show end up describing it in general terms and stall in the middle bands.
- Writing about your set play in Section C. It must be a different play.
- Forgetting to name the production, or a long confusing introduction about dates and venues. One clear sentence is enough.
- Pre-learning a whole essay and forcing it onto a question with a different focus.
- Knowing only one actor or one design area. Prepare for all three possible questions.
- Watching only clips of a recorded show, so you can't place moments in the production as a whole.
- Borrowing reviewers' opinions instead of making your own judgements.
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of writing about a digital recording rather than a live performance. (2)
---
Advantage: it can be rewatched, so details can be checked and noted precisely (1). Disadvantage: the camera chooses where you look and loses the atmosphere and scale of the live room, so audience response and spatial effects may be harder to judge (1).
