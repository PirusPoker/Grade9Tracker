## What it is

Two of the three Section C questions are usually about **design**. Each names **one** design area and a focus: "Describe how [the set / costume / lighting / sound] was used to [focus]. Analyse and evaluate how successful [it] was in [focus] for the audience." Prompts follow, beginning "You could make reference to...". Like the performer question, it is worth **32 marks**: **AO3** 12 for knowledge and precise description, and **AO4** 20 for analysis and evaluation.

### The design question patterns

| Design area | Typical focuses | The prompts you are likely to see |
|---|---|---|
| **Set** | establishing where and when the play takes place; creating an appropriate setting or settings; communicating key themes | materials and effects; space, scale, levels and colour; a scene or section and/or the production as a whole |
| **Costume** | communicating two or more characters; showing period and place; communicating themes; showing change | materials, style, cut and fit; colour, texture and condition; a scene or section and/or the production as a whole |
| **Lighting** | creating mood and atmosphere; changes of time and place; focusing attention | colour, intensity and direction; special effects and transitions; a scene or section and/or the production as a whole |
| **Sound** | creating different moods; building tension; establishing place | volume and direction; types of sound, amplification and special effects; a scene or section and/or the production as a whole |

Sometimes a question offers a choice ("either the set or costume"). **Write about one design area only.** Examiners credit only one, and writing about both halves the depth of each.

### Describe like a designer (AO3)

AO3 rewards a **range of design skills**, precisely described. A costume answer that only talks about colour, or a lighting answer that only says "red for anger", is narrow. Use the vocabulary.

- **Set**: configuration, ground plan, flats, rostra, levels, stairs, trucks, revolve, flying, trapdoors, projection surfaces, cyclorama; materials (steel, timber, perspex, fabric); texture and finish; colour palette; scale (towering, cramped); furniture and dressing; how scenes changed.
- **Costume**: garment, style, silhouette, cut and fit, length, fabric and texture, colour, condition (new, worn, torn, stained), accessories, footwear, hair and make-up; changes across the show; how costumes contrasted between characters or groups.
- **Lighting**: lantern types (profile, Fresnel, LED, moving heads, follow spot); angle (front, side, back, top, uplight); colour temperature and gel colour; intensity; gobos; haze; shadows and silhouette; practical lights; snap changes, slow fades, cross-fades, blackouts; strobe; how lighting was built into the set.
- **Sound**: diegetic and non-diegetic; recorded and live; music, underscoring, soundscape, sound effects; volume and fades; direction and speaker placement (surround, from behind the audience); amplification and radio microphones; reverb, echo and distortion; silence.

### Analyse and evaluate (AO4)

Every moment follows the same formula as the performer question.
1. **Describe** the design precisely at a specific moment.
2. **Analyse** what it communicated (place, period, mood, character, theme) and **how**: why *that* colour, material, angle or sound.
3. **Evaluate** how successful it was, with a reason and evidence: the audience's response, your own reaction, a comparison with another moment, or how well it served the production's style and aims. Allow some balance where it's honest.

Design answers slip into pure description more easily than performer answers. After every description, ask yourself: *so what did the audience understand or feel, and how well did it work?*

### Talking about designers' work, not actors'

A set question is about the **set**, not how the actors moved on it. The actors can appear as part of how the design worked (for example, a truck pushed on by the ensemble), but the analysis should be of the design choices. Similarly, a sound question on a musical must describe *how the sound was designed and delivered* (orchestration, amplification, volume, direction, effects), not just say that "the song was sad".

### Structure and timing

About **45 minutes**. Write an introduction of two or three sentences (production, company, how you saw it, style, and the designer's overall approach to the focus). Then give **four or five moments**, including the opening and a climax or transformation, each with describe, analyse and evaluate. End with an overall judgement of how successful the design was in achieving the focus.

### Worked example: The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time (NT)

Designer Bunny Christie (set and costume), lighting designer Paule Constable and video designer Finn Ross created a single, largely abstract space. It is a black box whose walls and floor are marked out as a grid, like graph paper. Lights and projections are built into the surfaces, so the room can draw lines, maps, numbers, words and stars, showing the world as Christopher processes it. The ensemble helps build his journeys out of the space itself, and the sound design intensifies his experience of noise and crowds, especially on the journey to London. Costumes are everyday and contemporary, which grounds the family drama inside the abstract world. Use the models below to learn the method. If you saw a different production, build your paragraphs from your own notes. If you saw Curious Incident, describe what *you* saw.

::: terms
- **AO3 (design)**: credit for describing a range of design elements precisely, with accurate technical vocabulary.
- **AO4 (design)**: credit for analysing what design choices communicated and evaluating how successful they were.
- **Abstract set**: a non-realistic set that suggests ideas or moods rather than showing a real place in detail.
- **Multi-purpose set**: a set whose pieces or surfaces are reused to represent many locations.
- **Projection**: images, text or video cast onto the set, a screen or a gauze.
- **Colour temperature**: how warm (amber, orange) or cold (blue, white) a light appears, affecting mood.
- **Silhouette**: a figure lit from behind so that it appears as a dark shape; also a costume's overall outline.
- **Soundscape**: layered sounds combined to create the atmosphere of a place or state of mind.
- **Amplification**: making sound louder through microphones and speakers, including actors' voices.
- **Transition**: the change from one scene, location or state to another, often shaped by design.
:::

::: method
1. **Underline the design area and the focus**, and write about that one area only.
2. **Introduce the production** and the designer's overall approach in two or three sentences.
3. **Choose four or five moments** that show the design serving the focus, including the opening and a key transformation or climax.
4. **Describe each moment precisely**, using the prompt headings (e.g. materials, space, scale, levels, colour for set).
5. **Analyse** what each choice communicated and how.
6. **Evaluate** how successful it was, with evidence and reasons, and include at least one balanced judgement.
7. **Conclude** with an overall judgement tied to the focus and the production's aims. Spend about 45 minutes.
:::

::: example Model paragraphs: set design and setting
Describe how the set design was used to create an appropriate setting or settings for the audience. Analyse and evaluate how successful the set design was in creating an appropriate setting or settings for the audience. (32)

*Two model paragraphs from a full answer.*

In the National Theatre's The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time, Bunny Christie did not build a realistic Swindon street or house. She built one black box, with walls and floor marked in a fine white grid like a page of graph paper (AO3: materials, colour, space). Small objects and furniture were kept to a minimum: a few simple boxes served as chairs, a table, a train seat, and the whole stage could become the street, the school, the police station or the house in seconds (AO3). The grid told the audience that we were not looking at Swindon but at Swindon *as Christopher sees it*, a world he makes sense of through order, logic and mathematics (AO4: analysis). This was highly successful as a setting because it made the play's real location Christopher's mind. However, it did mean that in the early domestic scenes I sometimes had to rely on the actors' words to know which room we were in, which is a small cost for such a clear concept (AO4: balanced evaluation).

The set's greatest success was in the journey to London, where the settings had to multiply and speed up. Lines of light raced across the grid, projected signs, maps and arrows spread over the walls and floor, and the flat surfaces seemed to tilt into escalators and platforms as the ensemble carried Christopher through them (AO3: effects, scale, transformation). The box never changed shape, but it became an overwhelming city that filled every surface, with nowhere for the eye to rest (AO4). This created a setting that was appropriate not in realistic detail but in feeling: a crowded station is exactly this frightening to Christopher, and the audience, surrounded by moving information, felt the pressure too (AO4). I judge this the most effective design moment in the production, because it turned a single set into a journey without a single scene change, keeping the pace as relentless as Christopher's panic (AO4: justified evaluation).
:::

::: example Model paragraph: lighting and mood
Write one paragraph describing, analysing and evaluating how lighting created mood at one moment. (8)

In a quiet moment when Christopher imagines himself as an astronaut, Paule Constable's lighting took the stage almost to darkness, with pin-points of light across the walls and floor like stars (1). A soft, cool, blue-white light picked out Christopher as the ensemble lifted him, so he seemed to float above the dark floor (1). The change from the hard, bright states of the busy scenes to this dim, spacious state created a calm, dreamy mood (1) and showed the peace he finds in space, where there are no people to overwhelm him (1). The cool colour and low intensity made the stage feel vast and silent (1). It was highly effective because it was the first time the audience saw Christopher happy and free, and the gentle mood made us understand what he longs for (1). The contrast with the chaotic station lighting later made both moments stronger (1). The audience's stillness showed how completely the change of mood worked (1).
:::

::: example Improving a sound paragraph
A student writes: "The music was loud and scary in the station scene, which was effective." Rewrite this as a precise paragraph. (4)

As Christopher entered the station, a low electronic pulse began under the scene and grew steadily louder, and layers of distorted, overlapping station announcements were added until they were almost impossible to separate (1). The noise seemed to press in from every side, so the audience felt surrounded by it rather than simply hearing it from the stage (1). The rising volume and the layering created a mood of mounting panic and showed how Christopher cannot filter out sound as other people do (1). This was successful because the moment it snapped to silence as he crouched on the floor, the relief was physical for the audience, which proved how tense the build-up had made us (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why you should write about only one design area when a question offers "either set or costume". (2)
---
Only one design area is credited (1); writing about both reduces the depth, range and precision possible in either (1).
:::

::: q
Give three lighting terms, other than colour, that could appear in a precise Section C answer. (3)
---
Any three (1 each): intensity; angle/direction (side light, backlight, uplight); lantern type (profile, Fresnel, follow spot, LED); gobo; haze; cross-fade; snap; blackout; strobe; silhouette.
:::

::: q
Explain how costume could communicate the difference between two groups of characters in a production. (3)
---
A precise contrast in fabric, colour or cut between the groups (1); a precise detail such as condition or accessories (1); what the contrast communicated (status, loyalty, period, values) and how clearly (1).
:::

::: q
Rewrite as a precise description: "The set was big and dark." (3)
---
Up to 3 for precise detail: e.g. a towering back wall of rusted corrugated steel reaching into the flies (1); a black, glossy floor extending to the front row (1); a single narrow staircase upstage left the only way out (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between diegetic and non-diegetic sound and why both might be used in a production. (3)
---
Diegetic: heard by the characters, e.g. a doorbell (1). Non-diegetic: heard only by the audience, e.g. underscoring (1). Diegetic sound builds a believable world; non-diegetic sound shapes mood or shows inner experience (1).
:::

::: q
Write one paragraph describing, analysing and evaluating a costume in a production you have seen. (6)
---
Describe (2): garment, fabric, colour, cut, fit, condition or accessories. Analyse (2): what it communicated about the character, period, theme or change. Evaluate (2): how successful, with a reason and evidence.
:::

::: watch
- Answer only the design area the question names. A lighting question answered with costume detail earns almost nothing.
- Pure description with no analysis or evaluation. Twenty marks depend on "so what, and how well?"
- A narrow range: only colour, or only one costume, or only one lighting state.
- Writing about how the actors performed on the set rather than about the set design.
- Musical sound answers that say "the song was sad" without explaining how the sound was designed.
- Vague vocabulary ("nice lights", "spooky noises") instead of technical terms.
- Losing the focus (mood, setting, character, theme) named in the question.
:::

---

::: test
Explain why an answer about set design should not focus mainly on the actors' movement. (2)
---
The question assesses the designer's work (1); actors' performance skills are not creditable as set design unless linked to how the design was used or worked (1).

::: test
Explain what makes an evaluation of design convincing. (3)
---
A clear judgement of degree (1); a reason linked to what the design communicated (1); evidence such as audience response, comparison with another moment, or fit with the production's style and aims (1).
:::

::: test
Write a paragraph describing, analysing and evaluating how sound created tension at one moment of a production you have seen. (9)
---
Describe (3): type of sound, live or recorded, volume, direction or speakers, timing, silence. Analyse (3): how these built tension (anticipation, layering, sudden change) and what they communicated. Evaluate (3): a justified judgement of success with evidence, including balance where appropriate.
:::
