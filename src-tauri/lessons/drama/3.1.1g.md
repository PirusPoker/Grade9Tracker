## What it is

This lesson is about **how meaning is made through space and convention**. It covers **performance conventions** (the accepted devices of theatre, such as the aside or the freeze frame), **the use of performance space and spatial relationships on stage**, **actor and audience configuration**, and **the relationship between performers and audience**.

The 12-mark Section B question is built on it: "Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Y to show...". That question is marked on exact, developed detail. Positions, moves, distances, levels, eye contact and touch, each tied to a moment, are what earn the top band. Section C questions on relationships between characters use the same tools.

### Performance conventions

A **convention** is a device the audience accepts as part of the theatrical language, even though it is not realistic.

| Convention | What it is | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct address** | A character speaks straight to the audience | Involves the audience, breaks the fourth wall, can make them complicit |
| **Aside** | A brief remark to the audience that other characters "cannot hear" | Reveals true thoughts; dramatic irony; often comic |
| **Soliloquy** | A character alone speaks their thoughts aloud | Access to inner conflict; intimacy |
| **Monologue** | One character's long speech, to others or to the audience | Focus on one perspective; storytelling |
| **Narration** | A narrator tells or comments on the story | Guides the audience, compresses time, can distance them |
| **Chorus / choral speaking** | A group speaks or moves together | Represents a community, heightens moments, rhythm |
| **Multi-role / doubling** | One actor plays several characters | Economy; can make a point (the same actor as two authority figures) |
| **Freeze frame / tableau** | Actors hold a still picture | Highlights a key image; marks the start or end of a scene |
| **Slow motion** | Action slowed down | Emphasises a crucial or violent moment |
| **Flashback / flash-forward** | A scene out of chronological order | Shows cause or consequence |
| **Split scene / cross-cutting** | Two scenes share the stage, alternating | Contrast, irony, parallel lives |
| **Physical theatre** | Story told through movement, lifts and choreography | Expresses emotion or states beyond words |
| **Breaking the fourth wall** | Acknowledging the audience in a naturalistic frame | Surprise, involvement, reminder that it is theatre |

### Using the performance space

The space is a tool for showing relationships. The main tools:

- **Proxemics**: the distance between characters. Close means intimacy, threat or conflict at its peak. Distant means estrangement, fear or formality. The *change* in distance is what tells the story.
- **Levels**: height on stage (standing, sitting, kneeling, lying, standing on a chair, stairs or rostra). A higher level usually means higher status or power, but a reversal can be powerful too.
- **Stage positions**: downstage for exposure and intensity; upstage for distance, or for commanding focus; the centre for dominance; the edges for exclusion.
- **Blocking**: the planned moves of actors. Good blocking is motivated: each move has a reason in the character's objective.
- **Focus**: where the audience looks. Directed by position, stillness against movement, levels, the eye-lines of the other actors, and lighting.
- **Sightlines**: making sure each section of the audience can see the key moments.
- **Stage picture**: the composition of bodies in space at any moment, like a photograph. Strong stage pictures show relationships at a glance.
- **Status**: power shown spatially. Who takes the centre, who moves to whom, who blocks the exit.

### Interacting with another actor

"Interact with the actor playing..." means what you do *to* and *with* them:
- **Eye contact**: held, avoided, broken at a key word, searching their face.
- **Touch**: a hand on the arm, pulling away, a slap, an embrace refused.
- **Listening and reacting**: the flinch, the smile, the stillness while they speak.
- **Following and avoiding**: pursuing them round a table; turning your back; stepping between them and the door.
- **Objects between you**: a table as a barrier; handing over or snatching a prop.
- **Mirroring or contrast**: matching their posture (connection) or opposing it (conflict).

### Configuration and the actor-audience relationship

The staging configuration changes the audience's relationship with the actors (see also the stage configurations lesson):
- **Proscenium arch / end on**: the audience watches through a frame. This gives the strongest fourth wall and more distance, which suits naturalism and spectacle.
- **Thrust**: the audience wraps around three sides, so asides and soliloquies from the tip feel shared and intimate.
- **Traverse**: two audiences face each other. They see each other's reactions and become like witnesses or a jury.
- **In the round**: the most intimate. The audience is close on all sides and feels surrounded by and part of the event.
- **Promenade**: the audience shares the space with the actors and moves with them, which can be exciting, unsettling or immersive.

The **performer-audience relationship** can also be shaped within any configuration: by direct address, by actors entering through the auditorium, by house lights left partly up, or by actors making eye contact with individuals. Each changes how involved, comfortable or complicit the audience feels.

::: terms
- **Performance convention**: an accepted theatrical device, such as an aside or freeze frame, that the audience understands.
- **Direct address**: when a performer speaks directly to the audience.
- **Aside**: a short remark spoken to the audience that other characters on stage are assumed not to hear.
- **Proxemics**: the use of distance between characters on stage to show their relationship.
- **Levels**: the different heights at which performers are placed, often used to show status.
- **Blocking**: the planned positions and movements of performers during a scene.
- **Focus**: the point on stage the audience's attention is drawn to at a given moment.
- **Stage picture**: the overall arrangement of performers and set seen by the audience at a moment.
- **Multi-role**: one performer playing more than one character in a production.
- **Split scene**: two scenes staged at the same time in different areas, often alternating.
- **Fourth wall**: the imaginary barrier between actors and audience in naturalistic performance.
- **Tableau**: a frozen stage picture, also called a freeze frame.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the effect the question wants** (tension, power, affection, distrust, comedy) and keep returning to it.
2. **Set the start**: where each actor is (stage positions), standing or seated, and the distance between them.
3. **Plan 3–5 moves**, each tied to a line or moment (paraphrase it): who moves, where to, how fast.
4. **Add interaction** at each move: eye contact, touch, reaction while listening, use of furniture or props.
5. **Use levels and status**: who is higher, and when does that change?
6. **State the effect** after every move, and end on the final stage picture.
:::

::: example Proxemics to show a change
Explain how proxemics could show two friends falling out during a scene. (4)

At the start they sit close together on a bench centre stage, shoulders almost touching, showing their closeness (1). When one reveals the betrayal, the other stands and takes two steps away, so a gap opens between them (1). As the argument grows, the hurt friend moves further, to downstage left, while the other follows but stops halfway, unsure whether to approach (1). The scene ends with them at opposite sides of the stage, the empty space between them showing the audience that the friendship has broken (1).
:::

::: example A 12-mark space and interaction answer
A scene shows a landlord arriving to evict an elderly tenant who cannot pay her rent. She pleads with him; he refuses. At the end she offers him her late husband's watch as payment, and he takes it.

You are performing the role of the landlord. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing the tenant to show his power over her. (12)

I would enter from upstage centre without knocking and stop just inside the doorway, standing tall while she is seated in a low armchair downstage right. The difference in levels and my possession of the doorway show that I control the space and the exit from the start.

As she begins to plead, I would walk slowly around the room, running a finger along the mantelpiece and picking up her ornaments to inspect them, never looking at her. My movement through her home shows that I treat it as already mine, and ignoring her eye contact shows that her words mean nothing to me.

When she stands and reaches out to touch my sleeve, I would step back sharply so her hand meets nothing, and look down at the place where she touched me. This rejection of contact is cruel, and the audience feels her humiliation.

At the refusal I would move downstage centre and stand directly over her as she sinks back into the chair, so I am at full height with her below me. I would speak my refusal looking down into her face, holding eye contact for the first time, which is more threatening than shouting.

When she offers the watch, I would not move towards her. I would hold out my open palm and wait, making her rise and cross the space to me. She has to come to me, which shows the full imbalance of power. I would pocket it without looking at it and walk out upstage, leaving her alone downstage in the chair, a small figure in a large space.

(Marks: precise positions, levels, proxemics, touch and eye contact at identified moments, all tied to the effect of power, would reach the 10–12 band.)
:::

::: example Configuration and the audience
Explain how staging a courtroom scene in traverse could affect the audience's relationship with the performers. (3)

The two halves of the audience face each other across the acting area, like the two sides of a courtroom (1). They see each other's reactions behind the actors, which makes them feel like a public gallery or jury judging the case (1). The closeness to the witness and the accused increases the tension and makes the audience feel responsible for the verdict (1).
:::

::: q
Define "proxemics". (1)
---
The use of space and distance between characters on stage to show their relationship (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a soliloquy and direct address. (2)
---
A soliloquy is a character alone on stage speaking their thoughts, as if thinking aloud (1); direct address is deliberately speaking to the audience, acknowledging them, and can happen with other characters present (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how levels could show a change in status between two characters. (3)
---
At first the powerful character stands while the other sits or kneels, showing dominance (1); at the turning point the lower character stands up or climbs to a higher level, such as a step or table (1); the reversal shows the audience that the power has shifted (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one effect of using multi-role in a production. (2)
---
It allows a small cast to play many characters, suiting storytelling or epic styles (1); it can also make a point, for example the same actor playing two figures who both oppress the hero (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a split scene could be used to create dramatic effect. (3)
---
Two scenes happen on stage at once in separate areas (1), with focus shifting between them through lighting or freezing one group (1); the contrast or parallel creates irony or tension, for example one family celebrating while another mourns (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how an actor who is not speaking can show their character's feelings towards the speaker. (3)
---
Reactions such as a flinch, smile or tightening of the jaw (1); eye contact held or avoided (1); moving closer or away, or turning the back (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Vague space.** "I would move away from him" earns little. Say where from, where to (stage positions) and on which line.
- **Forgetting the other actor.** The 12-mark question asks you to interact. Eye contact, touch and reactions are needed, not just your own movement.
- **Moves with no motive.** Every move needs a reason in the character's feeling or objective, and an effect.
- **Losing the named effect.** If the question asks about distrust, every move should show distrust.
- **Mixing up aside and soliloquy.** An aside is brief with others present. A soliloquy is spoken alone.
:::

::: test
Explain how staging a play in the round could change the relationship between performers and audience compared with a proscenium arch. (4)
---
In the round the audience surrounds the actors and is very close (1), making the experience intimate and immersive, as if they are inside the event (1); in a proscenium the frame and distance separate the audience, who watch as if through a window (1), which suits naturalism or spectacle but makes it harder to involve them directly (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how two performers could use the space to show that their characters are in love but hiding it from others at a party. (6)
---
Levels. 5–6: precise positions, moves and interaction at identified moments, clearly showing both the love and the concealment, with effects explained. 3–4: clear ideas with some precise detail. 1–2: general ideas.

Indicative content: starting on opposite sides of the space, apart, each within a different group; stolen glances across the room, held a beat too long, then quickly broken; gradually edging closer through the crowd while pretending to talk to others; brief, hidden touch as they pass (fingers brushing when handing a glass); stepping apart immediately when someone approaches; a moment alone downstage in a pool of light while the party freezes; effect: the audience shares the secret and feels the tension of possible discovery.
:::

::: test
Explain how direct address could be used to create a relationship with the audience in a play you have studied or a scene you have seen. (4)
---
1 mark per developed point, up to 4. For example: the character steps downstage out of the scene and speaks to the audience (often lit separately), making them confidants; this creates sympathy or complicity because they know the character's secret; it can create comedy through shared jokes or knowing looks; it can distance the audience (epic), prompting them to judge rather than just feel; eye contact with individual audience members increases the effect.
:::

::: test
A scene shows two sisters meeting for the first time in ten years at their father's funeral. The older sister blames the younger for leaving. By the end of the scene they have begun to forgive each other.

You are performing the role of the younger sister. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing the older sister to show the change in their relationship. (12)
---
Levels. 10–12: an exact, well-developed explanation, entirely appropriate to the scene, with precise positions, moves, proxemics, levels and interaction at identified moments, and clearly explained effects showing the change. 7–9: a clear, developed explanation with a number of precise details and a clear sense of the change. 4–6: a reasonably clear explanation with some detail, partly developed. 1–3: limited, general ideas with little detail or little interaction.

Indicative content: starting positions with maximum distance (the younger hesitating at the upstage entrance, the older downstage by the coffin or a chair, back turned); the younger's first move stops halfway, showing uncertainty; the older turns and holds her ground; avoiding eye contact during the accusations; the younger standing still and taking the blame, head lowered, or stepping back when the older approaches angrily; a turning point on a shared memory of their father, marked by the first sustained eye contact and a pause; gradual closing of the distance, possibly both sitting on the same bench (level shared); a tentative touch, first rejected, later accepted; the final stage picture of them side by side, perhaps holding hands, facing out; effects: the audience feels the hurt and anger, then the relief and warmth of reconciliation.
:::

::: test
A scene shows a manager telling an employee that she has been dismissed. The employee refuses to accept it and becomes increasingly angry, until the manager calls security.

You are performing the role of the employee. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing the manager to create tension. (12)
---
Levels. 10–12: an exact, developed explanation with precise positions, moves, levels and interaction at identified moments, entirely focused on building tension, with effects fully explained. 7–9: a clear explanation with a number of precise details and a sense of build. 4–6: some relevant detail, partly developed. 1–3: limited, general ideas.

Indicative content: she begins seated in front of the manager's desk (lower level, desk as a barrier) while the manager stands or sits behind it; her stillness and fixed eye contact as she hears the news; standing up slowly on her first refusal, matching the manager's level; leaning on the desk with both hands, invading the manager's space; the manager stepping back and the employee following round the side of the desk, removing the barrier; proxemics shrinking as her volume rises; the manager reaching for the phone, the employee putting her hand over it (touch through a prop); a sudden stop and silence when security is called; she backs away towards the door slowly, never breaking eye contact; effects: rising tension, audience uncertainty about violence, sympathy mixed with fear.
:::
