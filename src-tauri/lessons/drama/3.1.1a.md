## What it is

A professional production is made by a team, and the specification names **twelve theatre makers** you must know: what each one does **day to day**, and what each one is **accountable for** (their part in making the whole production succeed). Section A of the paper, "Theatre roles and terminology", usually gives at least one multiple-choice question on roles, worth 1 mark each. The questions are short, but the wrong answers are chosen to trap students who mix up neighbouring jobs, so learn the boundaries exactly.

It also matters beyond Section A. In Section B you write as a performer or a designer, and in Section C you evaluate the work of performers and designers you have seen. Knowing who decided what (the director chose the concept, the lighting designer chose the colour, the technician ran the cue) makes your analysis precise.

### The twelve roles

| Role | Day-to-day activities | Accountable for | When they mostly work |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Playwright** | Writes and redrafts the script; researches; may attend early rehearsals and rewrite scenes for a new play | The text: story, characters, dialogue and stage directions | Before rehearsals (and during them for a new play) |
| **Performer** | Learns lines and moves; warms up voice and body; rehearses; takes notes from the director; performs every show | Creating the character and performing it consistently, safely and to the standard rehearsed, every night | Rehearsal and every performance |
| **Understudy** | Learns one or more roles (often while playing a smaller part); attends understudy rehearsals; watches the lead | Being ready to go on at short notice so the show is never cancelled | Rehearsal and on stand-by during the run |
| **Lighting designer** | Reads the script; attends design meetings and rehearsals; draws the lighting plan (rig plan) and cue list; supervises the focus; plots each lighting state | The lighting design: visibility, mood, time, place and focus, and every cue in the show | Pre-production to the technical and dress rehearsals |
| **Sound designer** | Sources, records and edits music and effects; plans speaker positions and microphones; writes the sound cue list | The sound design: music, effects, amplification and the balance heard by the audience | Pre-production to the technical and dress rehearsals |
| **Set designer** | Researches; sketches; builds a scale model box and ground plans; briefs the workshop; chooses furniture and dressing | The look and practical working of the set: location, period, levels, entrances, scene changes | Pre-production; checks the build; technical rehearsal |
| **Costume designer** | Researches period and character; draws costume designs; chooses fabric; runs fittings; plans hair, wigs and make-up | Every costume, including how it fits, moves and changes, and what it says about character and period | Pre-production, fittings during rehearsal, dress rehearsal |
| **Puppet designer** | Designs and often builds puppets; tests materials, scale and mechanisms; trains or advises puppeteers | Puppets that work mechanically, are safe to operate and carry character for the audience | Pre-production and rehearsal |
| **Technician** | Rigs and focuses lanterns; sets up speakers and microphones; builds or moves scenery; **operates** the lighting and sound desks or flies during the show; maintains equipment | Carrying out the designs accurately and running the technical side safely and consistently in every performance | Fit-up, technical rehearsal and every performance |
| **Director** | Develops the concept (the overall interpretation); casts with the team; leads rehearsals; blocks scenes; coaches the actors; brings the design team together | The artistic vision of the production: that every element serves one coherent interpretation | From the first idea to press night |
| **Stage manager** | Runs rehearsal schedules; keeps the prompt book; organises props; leads the backstage team; runs the show once it opens | The smooth, safe running of rehearsals and performances, including **health and safety on stage** and backstage | Rehearsal and every performance |
| **Theatre manager** | Manages the building and front-of-house staff; box office; bar; ushers; licences; budgets | The audience and the building: **front-of-house** safety, the audience's experience and the business side | All year, not tied to one show |

### Roles people confuse (Section A traps)

- **Designer vs technician.** The designer *decides* (which lanterns, which gel, which cue). The technician *builds, rigs and operates* it. "Who operates the lighting board during a production?" The technician, never the lighting designer.
- **Director vs stage manager.** The director owns the interpretation and leads rehearsals. Once the show opens, the director usually moves on. From then on the **stage manager** is responsible for keeping every performance as the director set it.
- **Stage manager vs theatre manager.** The stage manager looks after the **stage and backstage**: the actors, the crew, props, the set, safety on stage. The theatre manager looks after the **building and the audience**: front of house, the box office and the auditorium's safety.
- **Deputy stage manager (DSM).** This is part of the stage management team. The DSM sits "on the book" with the prompt copy, noting every move in rehearsal and **calling the cues** (telling the technicians "Lighting cue 14... go") during performances. If a question asks who calls the cues, the answer is stage management.
- **Understudy.** An understudy learns specific parts. A *swing* (not on the specification) covers chorus or ensemble tracks. You need only the understudy.

### The production timeline

1. **Script**: the playwright writes, or a producer or company chooses an existing play.
2. **Concept and design meetings**: the director and designers agree an interpretation. The set designer presents the model box, and the costume designer presents drawings.
3. **Rehearsals**: the director blocks and shapes scenes, and the actors learn lines. The stage manager runs the rehearsal room and the prompt book, while the designers watch runs.
4. **Fit-up and technical rehearsal**: technicians install the set and rig. The designers plot cues. The "tech" goes cue to cue, and the stage manager calls it.
5. **Dress rehearsal**: a full run in costume, with all cues, as if an audience were there.
6. **Previews and press night**: the first audiences, then the night the critics review.
7. **The run**: the stage manager maintains the show, the understudies stand by, the technicians operate it and the theatre manager runs front of house.

### Accountability: why it matters to the whole

Each role has one part of the production it must answer for. If a cue is late, the question is whether the DSM called it late or the technician missed it. If a costume rips mid-scene, the wardrobe team repairs it, but the costume designer chose a fabric that could not take the movement. If a fire exit is blocked in the foyer, the theatre manager is accountable. This is what "contribution to the whole production being a success" means: every role protects one part of the audience's experience.

::: terms
- **Playwright**: the writer of the script, responsible for the story, characters, dialogue and stage directions.
- **Understudy**: a performer who learns a role so they can replace the usual actor at short notice.
- **Director**: the person who creates the production's overall interpretation and leads the actors and designers towards it.
- **Stage manager**: the person responsible for running rehearsals and performances smoothly and safely, including health and safety on stage.
- **Deputy stage manager**: the member of stage management who keeps the prompt book and calls the cues during performances.
- **Technician**: the person who rigs, maintains and operates the lighting, sound or stage equipment.
- **Theatre manager**: the person who runs the building, front-of-house staff, box office and audience safety.
- **Front of house**: the public areas of a theatre (foyer, box office, auditorium) and the staff who look after the audience.
- **Prompt book**: the stage manager's master copy of the script, recording blocking, cues and notes.
- **Technical rehearsal**: the rehearsal where lighting, sound, set and scene changes are added and practised cue by cue.
- **Concept**: the director's central idea for how the play will be interpreted and presented.
- **Model box**: a scale model of the set used by the designer to show the director and the team how the stage will look.
:::

::: method
1. **Read every option before choosing.** Section A options are close neighbours (designer, technician, stage manager).
2. **Ask: decide, operate, or manage?** Designers decide, technicians operate, stage managers run the show, theatre managers run the building.
3. **Ask: stage or audience?** Anything on or behind the stage belongs to stage management. Anything in the foyer or auditorium belongs to the theatre manager.
4. **Ask: before or after opening?** The director and designers shape the show before it opens. The stage manager, technicians, performers and understudies keep it running after.
5. **Write only the letter.** You do not need a sentence, which saves time for Sections B and C.
6. **For longer role questions**, give one day-to-day task, then say what the role is accountable for and how that helps the whole production.
:::

::: example Designer or technician?
Who has responsibility for operating the sound desk during a performance?

A The sound designer
B The technician
C The director

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)

B (1). The sound designer created the cues and levels, but a technician operates them in every performance.
:::

::: example Stage or front of house?
Who has responsibility for the safety of the audience in the foyer and auditorium?

A The theatre manager
B The stage manager
C The lighting designer

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)

A (1). The stage manager's safety duty is on stage and backstage. The audience and the building are the theatre manager's.
:::

::: example Explaining a role
Explain the role of a stage manager during the run of a production. (4)

The stage manager is in charge of each performance once the show has opened, because the director has usually left after press night (1). Their team checks that props are set and the set is safe before the show, and that the actors are in place for their entrances (1). The deputy stage manager follows the prompt book and calls each lighting and sound cue so that the technicians carry out the show exactly as rehearsed (1). They are accountable for health and safety on stage and for keeping the show consistent, sending reports and dealing with problems such as an actor's absence by arranging for the understudy (1).
:::

::: example Two roles working together
Explain how a set designer and a technician contribute differently to a scene change. (4)

The set designer decides how the change will work, for example planning a truck carrying the kitchen that rolls on from stage left while the street drops away (1). They make sure it fits the concept and can be done quickly, drawing it on the ground plan and model box (1). The technician (stage crew) physically moves the truck, or flies the piece in, in every performance, at the moment the stage manager calls the cue (1). So the designer is accountable for the idea working, and the technician for it happening safely and on time every night (1).
:::

Who calls the lighting and sound cues during a performance?

A The lighting designer
B The deputy stage manager
C The theatre manager

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)
---
B, the deputy stage manager (part of the stage management team) (1).

::: q
State two day-to-day activities of a costume designer. (2)
---
Any two, 1 mark each: researching the period and character; drawing costume designs; choosing and sourcing fabrics; running fittings with actors; planning hair, wigs and make-up; attending the dress rehearsal to adjust costumes.
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between the role of a lighting designer and a lighting technician. (2)
---
The lighting designer decides the design: which lanterns, colours, angles and cues are used and what they should achieve (1). The technician rigs and focuses the lanterns and operates the lighting desk during performances (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a professional production has understudies. (2)
---
If a performer is ill, injured or absent, the understudy can take over at short notice (1), so the performance does not have to be cancelled and the audience still sees the show (1).
:::

::: q
State two things a playwright is responsible for. (2)
---
Any two: the plot or story; the characters; the dialogue; the stage directions; the structure (acts and scenes); rewrites of a new play during rehearsal (1 each).
:::

::: q
Explain what a theatre manager is accountable for. (3)
---
The running of the theatre building (1); the audience's safety and experience front of house, including ushers and evacuation (1); the business side, such as the box office, licences and budget (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways a puppet designer contributes to a production. (4)
---
Designs puppets whose look (materials, scale, colour) communicates character to the audience (1), for example (1); makes sure the puppet works mechanically and can be manipulated safely and expressively by the puppeteers (1), for example by testing weight, joints and controls or advising in rehearsals (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Designer for operator.** Designers design; technicians operate. Section A regularly uses the designer as the tempting wrong answer.
- **Director for stage manager.** After opening night it is the stage manager, not the director, who runs each performance and calls the cues through the DSM.
- **Stage manager for theatre manager.** On stage and backstage safety belongs to the stage manager. Front-of-house safety (foyer, auditorium, audience) belongs to the theatre manager.
- **Writing sentences in Section A.** A letter is all you need. Save the time.
- **Vague "explain" answers.** "The director is in charge" earns nothing. Name what they are in charge of (the interpretation, blocking, unifying the design) and a specific task.
:::

Explain how a costume designer's work contributes to the success of a production. (4)
---
Costume tells the audience about each character's status, personality, age and period before they speak (1); the designer's choices of fabric, colour and cut support the director's concept, for example (1); costumes must allow the actor to move, fight or dance safely and change quickly between scenes (1); designs are checked in fittings and the dress rehearsal so they work under the lighting and look unified with the set (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the role of the technician in the technical rehearsal. (4)
---
Rigs and focuses the lanterns, sets up speakers and microphones or builds and positions scenery before the rehearsal (1); works through the show cue by cue, operating the lighting or sound desk or moving scenery when cued (1); records levels and timings the designers set, so they can be repeated (1); fixes problems and checks equipment is safe, so each later performance is consistent (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how the director and the stage manager work together from the first rehearsal to the run of a production. (8)
---
Levels. 7–8: a detailed, accurate explanation of both roles across the whole process, with precise examples of how their work connects and why it matters to the production's success. 5–6: a clear explanation of both roles with some examples and some sense of the process over time. 3–4: some accurate points about both roles, but general or covering only part of the process. 1–2: limited, possibly confused, points (for example mixing up the stage manager and theatre manager).

Indicative content: the director sets the concept and leads rehearsals, blocking scenes and coaching actors; the stage manager organises the schedule, calls actors, prepares rehearsal props and marks out the set on the floor from the designer's ground plan; the DSM records every move and change in the prompt book, which becomes the record of the director's decisions; the stage manager passes rehearsal notes between the director and the designers so that, for example, a new prop is sourced; in the technical rehearsal the director watches and makes decisions while the stage manager runs the rehearsal and the DSM calls cues; after press night the director leaves and the stage manager is responsible for keeping each performance faithful to the director's intentions, giving notes or rehearsing understudies; the stage manager is also accountable for safety on stage throughout. Together they turn the vision into a show that can be repeated accurately and safely every night.
:::
