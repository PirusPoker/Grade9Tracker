## What it is

*The Great Wave* by Francis Turnly (prescribed edition: **Methuen Drama**) is a **political thriller** in an **epic** style. A Japanese teenager, Hanako, vanishes from a beach one stormy night. Her family is told she drowned; in fact she has been taken to North Korea. The play cuts between her life there and her family's long search in Japan across more than twenty years. It was first staged at the **National Theatre** (the Dorfman) in **2018**, directed by Indhu Rubasingham, with extensive **video projection**. Section B asks four questions on an extract (4, 8, 12 and 20 marks).

This lesson sticks to what is certain about the play's story. When you want more detail (the order of later scenes, minor characters, exact years), use your own copy, and in the exam keep any plot reference you are unsure of general.

### Context: the real history

| | What happened | What it gives a theatre maker |
|---|---|---|
| **The abductions** | In the late 1970s and early 1980s North Korean agents secretly kidnapped Japanese citizens, many from coastal towns, and took them to North Korea. Some were used to teach Japanese language and customs to North Korean spies. | Two worlds: a free, busy, ordinary Japan and a closed, controlled North Korean state. The play is about **people stolen by politics**. |
| **The families' fight** | For many years families were not believed; the disappearances were treated as accidents or runaways. Families campaigned for decades. | Grief that cannot end; frustration with officials; the passing of time as a weapon. |
| **The admission** | In **2002** North Korea's leader admitted to Japan's prime minister that agents had abducted Japanese citizens. A few abductees returned home; the fate of others remained disputed. | A political thriller's ending: truth arrives, but on political terms, and not for everyone. |
| **Spies and a bombing** | North Korean agents trained to pass as Japanese carried out attacks; in 1987 an agent bombed a South Korean airliner. | The play's North Korean trainee, Jung Sun, is shaped by this history. |

AQA frames the context of the North Korean scenes as **North Korea in the early 1980s**. The Japanese scenes begin in a coastal town in the late 1970s. Designs must make clear **which country and which decade** each scene is in.

### Style: political thriller, epic

- **Many short scenes** in two countries and many places (a family home, a beach, a police station, a compound, a classroom, offices), jumping across years.
- **Epic** staging: fast transitions, projection, a flexible space, and the audience asked to think about politics and power, not only feel.
- **Thriller**: suspense, secrets, surveillance, reveals; the audience often knows more than the Japanese family does (**dramatic irony**).
- **Contrast** is the design principle: warm, cluttered, colourful Japan against bare, grey, regimented North Korea.

### Plot outline

| Part | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Japan, the storm** | Teenage sisters Hanako and Reiko live with their mother Etsuko in a small coastal town. Hanako is bold and outspoken; Reiko is quieter. One stormy night Hanako goes out near the sea and disappears. The authorities decide she drowned. |
| **North Korea** | The audience learns early that Hanako was abducted. In North Korea she is held, pressured and re-educated, and made to teach a young North Korean woman, **Jung Sun**, to speak and behave like a Japanese woman so that Jung Sun can pass as Japanese abroad. Their relationship moves from hostility to something closer to trust. |
| **Japan, the search** | Etsuko and Reiko refuse to accept that Hanako is dead. With Tetsuo, a local boy close to Hanako, they pursue the truth for years against official indifference and political caution. |
| **Decades later** | The play follows both worlds for more than twenty years. Jung Sun goes on to carry out a bombing of an aircraft and is later imprisoned. The Japanese family's campaign runs into the era when North Korea's abductions are finally admitted, and the question of what truth can give back. |

### Characters

| Character | Performance note |
|---|---|
| **Hanako** | Spirited, sharp-tongued, proud of who she is. In North Korea: fear, anger and resistance, then survival; physically she becomes more guarded and controlled over the years, but her spirit shows in flashes. |
| **Reiko** | Hanako's sister: quieter, the one left behind after the night of the storm; grows into a determined adult who will not let the story go. Play the passing years through posture, voice and energy. |
| **Etsuko** | Their mother: dignified, practical, stubborn in hope. Grief held in check; small, precise gestures; a voice that stays calm while the pain shows in the eyes. |
| **Tetsuo** | A boy from the town close to Hanako, deeply affected by her disappearance; grows into an adult who helps the family's search. |
| **Jung Sun** | A North Korean trainee agent: disciplined, controlled, fiercely loyal to her country and angry at Japan's history of harm to Korea. Stillness, upright posture, formal speech; her gradual softening towards Hanako is subtle. |
| **Officials and others** | North Korean minders and officers; Japanese police and politicians: each a clear, economical sketch, often by doubling. |

### Key moments and how to stage them

1. **The storm.** Sound of wind, rain and pounding waves from surround speakers; flickering blue-grey light; projected water on a gauze. Hanako's figure caught in a flash of lightning at the shoreline, then gone. The audience must be unsure what happened.
2. **The family without Hanako.** The same family room as the opening, now with an empty chair and a photograph. Etsuko sets out a bowl for her daughter; stillness and silence carry the grief.
3. **Hanako's first scenes in North Korea.** A bare room: one chair, one desk, harsh overhead light; a door with no handle on the inside. Hanako backed into a corner, then defiant; an official circling her.
4. **The lessons with Jung Sun.** A classroom: two chairs, a desk, piles of books and papers. Power shifts constantly: Hanako's knowledge is her only power, Jung Sun's loyalty is hers. Moments of humour (food, Japanese habits) bring them closer.
5. **Years passing.** Projection of dates, news footage-style images and changing costume show the decades; Reiko and Etsuko age visibly.
6. **The political turn.** Official settings (desks, flags, microphones) contrast with the intimate family scenes; the family watching news on a television, reacting in silence.

### Design across the play

A **flexible, permanent space** (for example a bare platform with a revolve or sliding screens) lets scenes change in seconds. **Projection** does much of the work: the sea, the storm, dates, places, images of the regime. Lighting separates the countries (warm, varied colours and daylight in Japan; cold, flat white or fluorescent green in North Korea). Costume must show period and place: late-1970s Japanese school uniforms and casual wear; in North Korea, drab, utilitarian **Mao-style** tunic suits, plain trousers and canvas shoes in navy, olive or grey, and later decades' clothes for the ageing family. Sound: waves as a recurring **motif**, loudspeaker announcements, marching music, Japanese radio and pop.

::: terms
- **Political thriller**: a suspenseful drama about power, secrets and politics, often based on real events.
- **Epic**: a style with many short scenes, wide scope in time and place, and staging that makes the audience think about society.
- **Abduction**: taking a person away by force or deception; the real abductions of Japanese citizens inspired the play.
- **Re-education**: forcing a person to adopt a state's ideas and way of life, as Hanako is pressured to do.
- **Dramatic irony**: when the audience knows something characters do not, such as Hanako being alive.
- **Projection**: images or video thrown onto a surface to create place, time or atmosphere.
- **Revolve**: a turning section of the stage floor used to change scenes quickly.
- **Mao suit**: a plain, buttoned tunic and trousers, worn widely in communist states to suggest equality and uniformity.
- **Motif**: a repeated image or sound, such as the waves, that links scenes and ideas.
:::

::: method
1. **Place the extract**: which country, which decade, and what the Japanese family does or does not know yet.
2. **For the 4-marker, design for the context given**, usually North Korea in the early 1980s: drab, uniform, controlled, with the condition of a prisoner or trainee where right.
3. **For the line**, show power and control: who is watched, who can speak freely, what is hidden under polite or formal speech.
4. **For the shaded section**, use the space to show power shifting: desk and chairs, standing and sitting, the door, surveillance.
5. **For the 20-marker**, trace the character or design through the years and between the two countries.
6. **Keep plot references general** where you are unsure; precise staging detail earns the marks, not plot trivia.
:::

::: example Setting for the classroom (4 marks)
You are designing a setting for a scene in which Hanako teaches Jung Sun. The setting must reflect the context of *The Great Wave*, set in North Korea in the early 1980s. Describe your design ideas for the setting. (4)

A bare, square room marked out on a grey concrete-textured floor, with walls suggested by two pale green, scuffed flats (1). A heavy, scratched wooden desk centre and two hard, upright wooden chairs, deliberately uncomfortable; a single metal lamp (1). Piles of worn Japanese textbooks, magazines and papers on the desk and floor, showing how hard they are working (1). High on the upstage wall, framed portraits of the leaders hang above a small barred window, lit so they seem to watch; a door with no handle on the inside tells us this is a prison, however it looks (1).
:::

::: example Performing Etsuko (8 marks)
You are performing the role of Etsuko. When an official tells her to accept that Hanako drowned, Etsuko politely refuses. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform her refusal and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

I would bow slightly first, perfectly polite, hands folded in front (1). Voice quiet and very even, no raised volume (1), with a pause before the key word of refusal (1) and gentle stress on it, so the steel shows through the courtesy (1). I'd hold the official's gaze without blinking (1) and then sit back down, spine straight, showing I will not leave (1). The effect: the audience sees Etsuko's quiet determination and dignity (1), and the tension between Japanese politeness and a mother's refusal to give up (1).
:::

::: example Space and interaction in the lessons (12 marks, outline)
You are performing the role of Jung Sun in an early lesson with Hanako. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Hanako to show her discipline and suspicion. (12)

Jung Sun sits bolt upright at the desk, notebook square, pencil level, while Hanako paces. She follows Hanako with her eyes but does not turn her body. When Hanako knocks books to the floor, Jung Sun waits, then picks them up one by one and stacks them precisely, never hurrying. She repeats phrases in a flat, careful voice. When Hanako comes close to mock her, she holds eye contact without leaning back. Once, when Hanako mentions Japan's past, her jaw tightens and she stands. The contrast between Jung Sun's stillness and Hanako's movement shows discipline, and her watchfulness shows suspicion.
:::

::: example Projection across the play (20 marks, outline)
Plan a projection design for the storm and the play as a whole. (20)

Extract: a front gauze with churning black and silver sea projected across it, the waves growing in scale and speed; lightning flashes built into the video; Hanako seen through the gauze, then the image cuts and she is gone. Whole play: the sea returns as a calm, glittering image in memories; in North Korea, projection becomes flat and official (propaganda slogans, stark maps); dates and place names announce each jump in time; television news images in the later Japanese scenes. Projection moves the play quickly between two countries and decades, supporting the epic style and the thriller's tension.
:::

::: watch
- Always show **which country and which decade** each scene is in.
- Don't invent plot details; the marks are for **staging**, so keep uncertain facts general.
- North Korea should feel **controlled and watched**, not simply dark or evil.
- Jung Sun is **loyal and disciplined**, not a cartoon villain; show her beliefs.
- Use the **epic** tools: projection, fast change, a flexible space.
- Use the **sea** as a motif in sound, light or projection.
:::
