## What it is

*Around the World in 80 Days* is Laura Eason's stage adaptation of Jules Verne's novel (prescribed edition: **Nick Hern Modern Plays**). AQA labels it a **comedy** in the style of **storytelling theatre**. A small company plays dozens of characters, and trains, ships and an elephant are made from actors, a few props, light and sound. Section B asks four questions on an extract (4, 8, 12 and 20 marks). The key to this play is the style: suggest, don't build; transform, don't replace.

### Context

| | When and where | What it gives a theatre maker |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | **1872**, beginning and ending in London, travelling east around the globe | The height of the **British Empire**: British officials, trade and troops across India and the East. A new age of speed: the Suez Canal, new railways across India and the USA, and steamships make a round-the-world trip in 80 days just possible. The gentleman's club, strict manners, punctuality and the stiff upper lip. |
| **The novel** | Written by the French author Jules Verne, published 1872-73 | An adventure-comedy about a very English gentleman, seen with a Frenchman's amused eye. It celebrates technology and travel; modern productions also question its view of the Empire and of other cultures. |
| **The adaptation** | Laura Eason's play, first staged in the USA in 2008 | Built for a small ensemble with doubling, direct narration to the audience, and inventive physical staging. |

AQA's 4-mark question frames the context as **late 19th century London** or **England**. For Fogg and the Reform Club that means Victorian gentlemen's dress and interiors; for the journey, remember that the other countries are seen through a Victorian traveller's eyes, and design them respectfully rather than as cartoons.

### Style: storytelling theatre

- **Narration**: characters step out of the action to tell the audience what happens next, often in the third person.
- **Multi-role**: most actors play several parts, changed with a hat, a shawl, an accent or a walk.
- **Physical theatre**: actors create trains (rocking in rhythm, a whistle), a ship's deck (swaying together) or an elephant (bodies and a few props).
- **Suggested locations**: a trunk becomes a train seat, a table, a ship's rail; a map or clock tracks progress.
- **Comic timing**: running gags, Fogg's deadpan calm against Passepartout's panic, fast pace and sudden reversals.

### Plot outline

| Stage of the journey | What happens |
|---|---|
| **London** | Phileas Fogg, a wealthy, precise, private gentleman who lives by the clock, hires a new French servant, Passepartout, who hopes for a quiet life. At the Reform Club Fogg bets his fellow members a fortune (**£20,000** in Verne's novel) that he can go round the world in 80 days, and leaves that evening. A bank robbery has just taken place, and Fogg matches the description. |
| **Suez and India** | Detective Fix of Scotland Yard decides Fogg is the robber and follows, waiting for a warrant. In India Passepartout gets into trouble at a temple; the railway turns out to be unfinished, so Fogg buys an elephant. They rescue a young widow, Aouda, who is about to be sacrificed on her husband's funeral pyre. |
| **Hong Kong and Japan** | Fix tries to recruit Passepartout; when he refuses, Fix gets him drugged so he misses the boat and cannot warn Fogg. Separated, Passepartout ends up in Japan working with a troupe of acrobats until Fogg finds him. |
| **America** | Across the USA by train: a brawl, an attack on the train, Passepartout captured and rescued by Fogg, and a sledge with a sail across the snow. |
| **The Atlantic and home** | Fogg takes command of a ship and burns its wooden fittings as fuel. In Liverpool Fix arrests him; the real robber is caught, but Fogg reaches London apparently too late and ruined. Aouda proposes to him. Passepartout, sent to arrange the wedding, discovers that by travelling east they have gained a day. Fogg reaches the club just in time, wins, and marries Aouda. |

### Characters

| Character | Performance note |
|---|---|
| **Phileas Fogg** | Upright, economical, exact: few gestures, even pace, clipped RP, checking his watch. The comedy is his calm in chaos. His journey is from clockwork to feeling; his rare impulsive moments (with Aouda) must land because they are rare. |
| **Passepartout** | Energetic, emotional, physically inventive (Verne makes him a former acrobat); a French accent; big reactions. The audience's friend: he often shares his panic with them. |
| **Detective Fix** | Dogged, sly, sure he is right; a busy, darting physicality, hiding behind newspapers; comic frustration at each missed warrant. Arguably he becomes almost likeable. |
| **Aouda** | Educated, dignified, warm; grateful but independent; she challenges Fogg's coolness and in the end proposes to him. |
| **Ensemble roles** | Club members, consuls, officials, sailors, a guide, acrobats, train passengers: each needs a clear, quick signature (hat, prop, posture, voice). |

### Key moments and how to stage them

1. **The wager at the Reform Club.** Leather armchairs (two chairs and a small table), newspapers, a card game; a huge clock projected or hung upstage and the sound of its ticking. Fogg's stillness against the members' noisy disbelief.
2. **Leaving London.** A fast montage: Passepartout packing a carpet bag, a whistle, the ensemble rocking in rhythm as a train; the first appearance of a map with a red line drawn by light.
3. **The elephant.** Physical theatre: two or three actors form the body under a grey fabric drape, one arm as the trunk; Fogg and Passepartout ride high on a trunk carried by the ensemble. Comedy from the swaying ride and Fogg's composure.
4. **Rescuing Aouda.** Firelight from orange and red floor lanterns and flickering fabric flames; drumming; a sudden reversal as Passepartout rises in disguise. Treat the moment seriously: Aouda is in real danger.
5. **The ship through the storm.** The whole company sways; a rope rail; a blue and green shifting wash, wind and creaking timbers; Fogg's order to burn the ship's wood shown by actors ripping planks from a set piece.
6. **The missing day.** Passepartout's frantic run through the audience; a projected clock counting down; Fogg entering the club at the last second as the ensemble freezes; laughter and release.

### Design across the play

A **permanent set** that can be anything: travelling trunks, a few chairs, ropes, a raised platform, and a map or clock face that shows progress. Lighting and sound do most of the travelling (colour and gobos for each place; train, ship and crowd sounds). Costume: a base of Victorian dress with quick-change accessories for multi-role. Fogg's costume barely changes (he is unflappable), while Passepartout's gets more battered with each adventure.

::: terms
- **Storytelling theatre**: a style in which performers tell the story to the audience as well as act it, using narration and imagination.
- **Multi-role**: an actor playing several characters, changing with a costume item, voice or physicality.
- **Narration**: telling the audience what is happening, often stepping out of the scene to do so.
- **Physical theatre**: using performers' bodies to create objects, places or effects, such as a train or an elephant.
- **Reform Club**: the London gentlemen's club where Fogg makes his bet.
- **Wager**: a bet; Fogg's wager starts the story.
- **British Empire**: the territories ruled by Britain, including India, at their height in the Victorian period.
- **Suez Canal**: the canal linking the Mediterranean and Red Sea, opened in 1869, which shortened the route to India.
- **Running gag**: a joke repeated through a play, such as Fix just missing his warrant.
:::

::: method
1. **Place the extract** on the journey: where they are, how many days remain, who is on stage.
2. **For the 4-marker, design for late 19th century London or England**, and keep it practical for a storytelling style (easy to move, adapt or change fast).
3. **For the line**, use contrast: Fogg's control, Passepartout's energy, Fix's scheming, Aouda's dignity; name the comic timing where there is comedy.
4. **For the shaded section**, use the style: narration to the audience, levels made from trunks, quick changes of focus.
5. **For the 20-marker**, show how your ideas travel across the journey: one idea transformed many times.
6. **Say how the effect helps the storytelling** (location, tension, comedy, time pressure).
:::

::: example Costume for Passepartout (4 marks)
You are designing a costume for Passepartout at the start of the play. The costume must reflect the context of *Around the World in 80 Days*, set in late 19th century London. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

A servant's short black wool tailcoat, slightly too small across his broad acrobat's shoulders, showing he has just arrived in a new job (1). A striped cotton waistcoat in muted red and cream, a white shirt with a detachable wing collar and a narrow black tie (1). Well-polished but cheap black ankle boots, flexible enough for the physical comedy later (1). A bowler hat he takes off and turns in his hands, and a neat moustache; the costume's sleeves are rigged with poppers so layers can be added or stripped for later scenes without leaving the stage (1).
:::

::: example Performing Fogg (8 marks)
You are performing the role of Fogg. At the Reform Club, when the members say the journey is impossible, Fogg calmly offers his bet. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

I would stay seated, back straight and not touching the chair (1), laying down my playing cards precisely before I speak (1). Voice in a level, clipped RP, not raised at all, with no emphasis until the sum, which I would say slightly slower (1). A tiny pause before the figure lets the others react (1). I would glance at my pocket watch as I finish (1). The effect is comic: the calmer Fogg is, the louder the members' shock (1). It establishes him as a man of precision who treats a wild adventure like arithmetic (1), and it sets up the running motif of time (1).
:::

::: example Space and interaction on the train (12 marks, outline)
You are performing the role of Passepartout on the train across India, when he realises the line ends in the jungle. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Fogg to show his panic. (12)

Two trunks side by side downstage as the carriage; Passepartout jiggling with the ensemble's rocking, peering out of an imaginary window. As the train stops, he lurches forward on the "brake" sound and ends sprawled at Fogg's feet. He darts between the trunks and the edge of the stage, pointing off into the jungle, then runs to the audience and addresses them in panic. Fogg stays seated and calm, and Passepartout's increasing energy bounces off his stillness; when Fogg finally rises and walks off to "buy an elephant", Passepartout is left frozen, then scrambles after him.
:::

::: example Lighting across the journey (20 marks, outline)
Plan lighting for the rescue of Aouda and across the play. (20)

Extract: dusk, a deep blue cyclorama with a thin orange horizon; red and amber floor-level lanterns, with a flicker unit, behind fabric flames for the pyre; a tight white special on Aouda; a sudden snap to a brighter state as Passepartout rises in disguise, releasing the tension into comedy. Whole play: a warm, brown-gold wash for the Reform Club with a practical lamp; a cool, smoky state for London; hard white midday sun with a palm-leaf gobo for India; rain effect and flickering greys for the Atlantic storm; a moving map lit from behind charting the route. The design changes place quickly, supports each mood and keeps the pace of the storytelling.
:::

::: watch
- **Do not build every location naturalistically.** The style is storytelling: a few objects transformed.
- Multi-role needs **precise, quick signatures**; say what changes and how.
- Fogg's calm is funny only if it is **exact**; describe the stillness precisely.
- Design other cultures **respectfully**, not as stereotypes.
- The Victorian context applies to London and to the British characters everywhere.
- Keep **time pressure** alive (clocks, maps, countdowns) in design and performance.
:::
