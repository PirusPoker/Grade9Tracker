## What it is

*Blood Brothers* by Willy Russell (prescribed edition: **Methuen Modern Classics**) is a **musical**, and AQA labels its performance style **musical theatre**. Twins are separated at birth: one is raised poor by his mother, one rich by the woman who takes him. The Narrator tells us in the first minute that both will die, so the play is a tragedy of class and fate played with songs, comedy and adults playing children. Section B asks four questions on an extract (4, 8, 12 and 20 marks), and your answers must fit this style as well as the moment.

### Context

| | When and where | What it gives a theatre maker |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Liverpool, from the late 1950s/1960s to the early 1980s, over roughly twenty years | Crowded terraced streets and **slum clearance**: families moved out of the inner city to new towns (the Johnstones are rehoused out in the countryside). Mass unemployment when factories close. Class decides school, work, housing and how the police treat you. Superstition (new shoes on the table, the "witch"). |
| **Writing** | A school play in **1981**; the full musical in **1983** | Recession and unemployment in early-1980s Britain, hitting Liverpool hard. Russell asks whether nature or **nurture** (upbringing) shapes a life. |

AQA's 4-mark question frames the context as **a Liverpudlian community in the 1960s** (sometimes "the late 1960s"). Costume and settings for the childhood scenes should show that decade; later scenes move into the 1970s and early 1980s.

**Style.** Musical theatre with a strong **Brechtian** streak: the Narrator comments on the action and the ending is shown first, so we watch how, not what. Songs are character moments (Mrs Johnstone's hopes, the boys' envy of each other). Adults play the children, which is funny at first and becomes painful as the boys grow into men.

### Plot

| Section | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Act One, opening** | The Narrator shows the ending: two brothers dead and their mother's grief. We go back: Mrs Johnstone, deserted by her husband, with many children and debts, cleans for the wealthy, childless Mrs Lyons. Expecting twins she cannot afford, she agrees to give one to Mrs Lyons and swears on the Bible. |
| **The separation** | Mrs Lyons, jealous, sacks Mrs Johnstone and frightens her with a superstition: if twins separated at birth ever learn the truth, they both die. |
| **Aged seven** | Mickey (poor) and Edward (rich) meet, find they share a birthday and become **blood brothers**, cutting their hands. Edward is wide-eyed at Mickey's swearing and freedom; Mickey envies Edward's sweets and manners. With Linda, Mickey and Edward throw stones at windows and are caught by a policeman, who threatens the Johnstones but chats politely to Mr Lyons. The Lyonses move to the country; later the Johnstones are rehoused nearby ("a bright new day"). |
| **Act Two, aged fourteen** | Both boys are suspended from school; they meet again without knowing each other's families. Edward still wears the locket Mrs Johnstone gave him as a child, with her photograph inside. Mrs Lyons, unhinged, attacks Mrs Johnstone with a knife. |
| **Young men** | Edward goes to university. Mickey works in a factory, marries a pregnant Linda, and is laid off. Out of work, he joins his brother Sammy in a robbery in which a man is shot; Mickey is jailed and comes out dependent on antidepressants. |
| **The end** | Linda turns to Edward, now a councillor, for a house and a job for Mickey, and they begin an affair. Mrs Lyons shows Mickey. He takes a gun to the town hall; Mrs Johnstone tells the twins the truth; Mickey shoots Edward and is shot by the police. The Narrator asks whether class, or superstition, is to blame. |

### Characters

| Character | Performance note |
|---|---|
| **Mrs Johnstone** | Warm, funny, worn down but never broken; a strong Scouse accent; she dances and sings about Marilyn Monroe even as life gets harder. Her physicality ages over twenty years (slower, heavier), but her energy returns whenever her children are near. |
| **Mrs Lyons** | Neat, brittle, controlling; Received Pronunciation; tight shoulders and small, precise gestures. Her fear of losing Edward turns to paranoia: hands shaking, sudden stillness, the knife. |
| **Mickey** | At seven: restless, bouncy, fidgeting, voice cracking with excitement. At fourteen: awkward, hunched, blushing around Linda. As a man: slumped, slow, flat voice under medication, then explosive at the end. |
| **Edward** | Polite, open, upright posture, clear RP; delighted by Mickey's world. Grows into easy confidence; his privilege is unconscious rather than cruel. |
| **Linda** | Loyal and brave; she defends Mickey as a child; torn in the adult scenes between love for Mickey and need. |
| **Sammy** | The older brother: swaggering, a bully, drawn to crime. |
| **The Narrator** | Always present, often on the edge of the action, sometimes playing small roles (the milkman, a teacher); a cold, steady voice that warns of the price to be paid. |

### Key moments and how to stage them

1. **The pact (Act One, Mrs Lyons's kitchen).** Mrs Lyons holds the Bible between them at arm's length; Mrs Johnstone hesitates, her hand hovering, before placing it on the book. A lighting snap to a tight cold special and a low drone as she swears; the Narrator watches from upstage.
2. **The boys meet at seven.** Adults playing children: fast, jerky energy, sitting with knees splayed, wiping noses on sleeves; Edward in shorts and a blazer, sitting neatly. The comedy comes from contrast. The cut of the hands is played small and sacred.
3. **The move to the country.** The song lifts the lighting to bright, warm yellow; the terraced backdrop flies out to reveal green fields. A hopeful moment the audience knows cannot last.
4. **Aged fourteen.** Puberty comedy: Mickey hiding behind a hand, voice cracking; physical awkwardness with Linda.
5. **Mickey's decline.** After prison, he sits slumped on a bench under a single cold top light; Linda hides his pills; the Narrator's song hangs over them.
6. **The final scene.** The town hall: Edward on a platform, Mickey below with the gun shaking; Mrs Johnstone runs in from the auditorium side; after the truth, the shots in two sharp sound cues, then blackout and the opening image of the play returns.

### Design across the play

A **composite set** usually works best: the Johnstones' terraced street on one side (soot-stained brick, washing, a front door) and the Lyonses' house on the other (clean lines, a good sofa, a bookcase), with the street centre stage as the shared ground. Twenty years pass quickly, so changes happen with **costume, lighting and small props** rather than big scene shifts. Lighting marks the Narrator's warnings (cold specials), memories and songs. Costume shows class and age: Mickey's hand-me-downs versus Edward's school uniform; 1960s children's clothes, 1970s flares, early-1980s work clothes and suits.

::: terms
- **Narrator**: a character who tells the story, comments on the action and warns of fate; always present in *Blood Brothers*.
- **Musical theatre**: a style in which songs and music carry the story and reveal characters' feelings.
- **Brechtian**: using devices that stop the audience losing themselves in the story, such as showing the ending first and narration.
- **Nature versus nurture**: the debate over whether birth or upbringing shapes a person; the twins test it.
- **Slum clearance**: the post-war demolition of crowded inner-city housing and rehousing of families in new towns.
- **Superstition**: a belief in luck or fate without evidence, such as new shoes on the table bringing bad luck.
- **Composite set**: a set showing several locations at once, such as both families' homes on one stage.
- **Received Pronunciation**: the accent traditionally linked with the educated middle and upper classes in England.
- **Motif**: a repeated image, sound or phrase, such as the Narrator's warnings or the shoes on the table.
:::

::: method
1. **Place the extract**: which act, the twins' age, and what the audience already knows (the ending).
2. **Design for Liverpool in the 1960s** for the 4-marker, and show class clearly (Johnstone or Lyons).
3. **For the line**, say how an adult actor plays the character's age and class through voice (accent, pitch, pace) and body (posture, energy, gesture).
4. **For the shaded section**, block it with the class divide in mind: who owns the space, who stands, who sits, who crosses.
5. **For the 20-marker**, cover the extract and at least two other ages or moments, showing the change (Mickey's decline, Mrs Lyons's paranoia, Mrs Johnstone's hope and grief).
6. **Remember the musical style**: songs, the Narrator, fast transitions and comedy can support every answer.
:::

::: example Costume for Mickey aged seven (4 marks)
You are designing a costume for Mickey to wear when he first meets Edward. The costume must reflect the context of *Blood Brothers*, set in a Liverpudlian community in the 1960s. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

Grey flannel shorts that are too big, held up with an old snake-clasp elastic belt, because they are a hand-me-down from Sammy (1). A darned, bobbled hand-knitted jumper in faded maroon with holes at the elbows, over a greying vest; the cuffs are shiny where he wipes his nose (1). Scuffed black plimsolls with no laces and wrinkled grey socks around the ankles, showing a family who cannot afford new shoes (1). Scabbed knees painted on with make-up and hair roughly cut at home with a pudding-bowl fringe (1).
:::

::: example Performing Mrs Lyons (8 marks)
You are performing the role of Mrs Lyons. In Act One, after she has frightened Mrs Johnstone with the superstition about twins, she tells her to leave. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

I would speak in clipped RP, each word crisp and final (1), dropping the pitch to a quiet, controlled register so the threat is cold rather than loud (1). A pause before the command, holding the audience in suspense (1). Physically I would place myself between Mrs Johnstone and the baby, one hand resting on the cot, claiming him (1). My eyes would stay fixed on Mrs Johnstone while my body stays perfectly still (1). This creates tension, because her calm shows she has planned this (1), and shows her status as the employer who holds all the power (1). The audience sees the start of the fear that will destroy her, so the stillness should crack slightly at the end with a tremor in the hand (1).
:::

::: example Space and interaction at fourteen (12 marks, outline)
You are performing the role of Mickey in Act Two, when he and Edward meet again aged fourteen. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Edward to show Mickey's awkwardness. (12)

Start far apart: Mickey downstage right on a low wall, hunched, hood of his anorak up; Edward enters upstage left. Mickey watches him sidelong, then looks away when Edward catches his eye. When Edward sits beside him, Mickey shuffles along the wall to keep a gap. His hands keep going into and out of pockets. His voice cracks on a key word, and he freezes in embarrassment. As they recognise each other the distance closes: Mickey stands, circles, then offers the old pact gesture. Every move is tied to the awkwardness of adolescence and of meeting a rich boy again.
:::

::: example Sound design for the ending (20 marks, outline)
Plan sound for the final scene and the play as a whole. (20)

Extract: an echoing crowd murmur at the town hall; the Narrator's final song underscored by a low, repeated bass note like a heartbeat, which quickens as Mickey raises the gun; two gunshots as loud, dry cracks from speakers at the back of the auditorium so the audience flinches; then silence before the final chorus. Whole play: the heartbeat motif first heard under the Narrator's warnings in Act One; children's street sounds (skipping rhymes, bicycles) for the seven-year-olds; birdsong and open air for the country move; factory noise that cuts out when Mickey is laid off. A top answer ties each cue to the action.
:::

::: q
When was *Blood Brothers* first written and in what two forms? (2)
---
1981 as a school play (1) and 1983 as a full musical (1).
:::

::: q
What does Mrs Johnstone swear on, and why? (2)
---
The Bible (1), to seal her promise to give one twin to Mrs Lyons (1).
:::

::: q
What superstition does Mrs Lyons invent? (1)
---
That twins separated at birth will both die if they learn the truth (1).
:::

::: q
Why does the play begin with the ending? (2)
---
It creates tension about how, not whether, the twins will die (1) and makes the audience look for the causes, such as class and superstition (1).
:::

::: q
Name two ways Mickey changes after prison. (2)
---
He depends on antidepressants (1); he is withdrawn and flat, pushing Linda away (1).
:::

::: q
Give one reason why adults playing children matters in performance. (2)
---
It is comic and makes the audience fond of the boys (1); it lets one actor show the full journey, so their deaths hit harder (1).
:::

::: watch
- Mrs Johnstone is poor, not pitiful: play her **humour and energy** as well as her struggle.
- Show the **class divide** in every design answer.
- The boys are seven, fourteen and then adults: an answer that ignores **how an adult plays a child** loses precision.
- Use the **Narrator** and the songs; this is a musical, not a naturalistic drama.
- Do not design modern clothes for the 1960s scenes (no hoodies or trainers with logos).
- Say what the audience feels, not only what the actor does.
:::
