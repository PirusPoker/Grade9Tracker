## What it is

This is the performer's toolkit: **vocal** and **physical** skills, and how they are used to **interpret a character**. It is the most heavily used knowledge on the paper. The 8-mark line question asks you to describe your vocal and physical skills on one line and explain the effects. The 20-mark performer question asks you to interpret a role across the extract and the play. In Section C, a performer question asks how actors used their skills and how successfully. All students must be ready to answer as a performer.

The specification lists the skills. Use its words exactly, because examiners look for accurate terminology.

### Vocal skills

| Skill | What it is | Example on a line | Possible effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accent** | Pronunciation tied to a region, country or class | A broad Lancashire accent with flat vowels | Places the character socially and geographically |
| **Volume** | How loud or quiet | Dropping to a near-whisper on the last three words | Intimacy, secrecy or menace |
| **Pitch** | How high or low the voice is | A sudden rise in pitch on "you" | Panic, disbelief, youth |
| **Timing** | When a line or word comes, including pauses | A two-second pause before answering | Hesitation, sub-text, comic effect |
| **Pace** | Speed of delivery | Rattling through a list without breathing | Nerves, excitement, urgency |
| **Intonation** | The rise and fall of the voice across a phrase | A rising, questioning inflection on a statement | Uncertainty, sarcasm |
| **Phrasing** | How a line is broken into units by breath and pauses | Breaking a sentence into single words: "Get. Out. Now." | Controlled fury, emphasis |
| **Emotional range** | The variety of feeling the voice can show | Moving from laughter to a crack in the voice within one speech | Shows a character's complexity or breaking point |
| **Delivery of lines** | The overall manner in which lines are spoken | Deadpan, sarcastic, pleading, clipped | Tone of the whole moment |

Also useful (from the performer guidance in the specification): **diction** (clarity of consonants), **inflection** (changes of pitch on words), **projection** (reaching the back of the auditorium without shouting), **pause**, **emphasis or stress** on key words, **tone** (the emotion in the voice), **tempo** and **choral speaking**.

### Physical skills

| Skill | What it is | Example | Possible effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Build** | Body size and shape (casting, padding, how you hold your body) | Broad shoulders pushed forward | Physical threat, strength |
| **Age** | How age shows in the body | Slow, careful steps, a hand on the lower back | Frailty, experience |
| **Height** | Real height, or height used through posture and levels | Drawing up to full height over a seated character | Dominance |
| **Facial features** | Natural features, and what make-up or casting emphasises | A naturally open face cast as an innocent | Instant audience reading of character |
| **Movement** | How the character moves through space | Quick, darting movement between exits | Anxiety, restlessness |
| **Posture** | How the body is held | Hunched with shoulders rounded | Defeat, low status, shame |
| **Gesture** | Movement of hands, arms or head that communicates | A dismissive flick of the hand | Contempt, impatience |
| **Facial expression** | Emotion shown in the face | A tight-lipped smile, jaw clenched | Suppressed anger |

Also useful: **gait** (the way of walking), **stance**, **eye contact** (held, avoided, darting), **stillness** (freezing to draw focus), **body language**, **spatial awareness**, **proxemics** (distance between characters) and **interaction**.

### Precision: the difference between bands

The examiner wants a description precise enough for another actor to copy it. Compare:

- *Weak:* "I would say it angrily and point at her."
- *Strong:* "I would deliver 'You knew' at low volume through clenched teeth, stressing 'knew' and dropping the pitch on it, then pause for two seconds before raising my right arm and pointing directly at her face, my eyes fixed on hers. The quiet delivery makes the accusation more menacing than a shout, and the audience realises he has known about the betrayal all along."

Precision means: **which word**, **which direction** (pitch up or down, volume up or down), **how long** (a beat, two seconds), **which part of the body**, **where** on stage, and **the effect** on the audience.

### Interpreting a character

**Interpretation** means the choices you make about who the character is and how to show it. The same line can be played many ways. Start with:
1. **Given circumstances**: age, class, job, period, where they are and what just happened.
2. **What they want** (objective) and **what they feel** in this moment, including sub-text.
3. **Status** in this moment: rising or falling?
4. **Their journey** across the play: this moment should fit the whole arc.

**Casting** matters too. The specification names build, age, height and facial features because the performer's own body is part of the interpretation. An actor may emphasise or change these through posture, costume, padding and make-up. For example, adults playing children in *Blood Brothers* change their energy, posture and gait rather than their height.

### Linking skills to effects

Effects are what the audience **understands** (the character is lying, the character is in love) or **feels** (sympathy, fear, laughter, tension). Every skill you name needs an effect, and the effect must suit the character and the moment. A grade 9 answer also varies the skills. The voice might *start* one way and *change* on a particular word, because characters react as the line goes on.

::: terms
- **Pitch**: how high or low the voice is.
- **Pace**: the speed at which lines are spoken.
- **Intonation**: the rise and fall of the voice across a phrase, which conveys meaning or feeling.
- **Phrasing**: the way a line is broken into sections by breath and pauses.
- **Emotional range**: the variety of emotion a performer can express through the voice.
- **Projection**: making the voice carry clearly to the whole audience without straining.
- **Diction**: clear and precise pronunciation of words.
- **Posture**: the way a performer holds their body, which can reveal status, age or mood.
- **Gesture**: a movement of the hands, arms or head that communicates meaning.
- **Gait**: a character's particular way of walking.
- **Facial expression**: using the face to show a character's thoughts and emotions.
- **Stillness**: deliberately not moving, to draw focus or show tension.
:::

::: method
1. **Place the line**: who says it, to whom, what has just happened, and what the character wants and feels.
2. **Choose 2–3 vocal skills** and pin each to a *word* in the line (stress, pause, pitch direction, volume change).
3. **Choose 2–3 physical skills** and pin each to a *moment* (before, on, or after a word), including where the actor is on stage.
4. **Give the effect** after each choice: what the audience understands or feels.
5. **Use the specification's terms** exactly, and never write "say it angrily" without naming the skills that create the anger.
6. **For the 8-mark question**, both vocal *and* physical are required. An answer with only one caps low.
:::

::: example Vocal skills on a line
A character discovers her younger brother has been taking money from her purse. She says: "How long has this been going on?"

Describe two vocal skills the actor could use on this line. (4)

Pace and pause: she would deliver "How long" slowly, then pause for a beat before the rest, as if she can hardly bring herself to ask (1), so the audience feels her hurt rather than simple anger (1). Pitch and volume: she would keep the volume low but let the pitch rise sharply on "going on", ending on a strained, questioning intonation (1), showing that she is struggling to stay calm and that the betrayal has shaken her (1).
:::

::: example The 8-mark line question
A father has just been told by his daughter that she is leaving home for good. He replies: "Then go. I won't stop you."

You are performing the role of the father. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

Before speaking, he stays seated at the table and continues to fold the newspaper with deliberate care, keeping his eyes down (physical, stillness and business). This shows he is refusing to let her see he is hurt.

On "Then go," I would use a flat, clipped delivery at low volume, with the pitch falling on "go" so it sounds final rather than angry. The audience hears a man trying to sound as if he doesn't care.

After "go" I would leave a pause of about two seconds. Then I would lift my head and make eye contact for the first time, and deliver "I won't stop you" more slowly, stressing "won't". My voice would crack slightly on "stop", showing the emotion he has been hiding.

On the last word I would stand, turn my back on her and walk upstage to the window, gripping the sill with both hands, my shoulders hunched and rigid.

Effect: the gap between the cold words and the cracking voice and turned back shows the sub-text, that he is heartbroken and too proud to beg. The audience feels sympathy for him and tension about whether she will really leave.

(Marks: a response like this, with precise vocal and physical skills pinned to specific words and effects entirely right for the moment, would reach the 7–8 band.)
:::

::: example Physical interpretation of age and status
Describe how an actor could use physical skills to show an elderly, powerful judge entering a courtroom. (4)

Posture: upright, chin raised and shoulders back despite his age, showing authority (1). Gait: slow, measured steps with a slight stiffness in the knees, using a cane placed with deliberate taps, so age is shown without weakness (1). Facial expression: a fixed, unsmiling stare, eyebrows slightly raised as he surveys the room (1). Gesture: a small, economical movement of one finger to indicate that everyone may sit, showing that he expects instant obedience (1).
:::

::: example Emotional range
Explain how emotional range could be shown across a single speech in which a character moves from joy to despair. (3)

The speech might begin at a quick pace, with bright, high pitch and warm tone, even laughter, as she describes her good news (1). As the memory turns painful, the pace slows and the pitch drops, with more pauses and a trembling, breathy quality (1). By the end the voice is barely audible and breaks on the final word, so the audience experiences the collapse of her hope within one speech (1).
:::

::: q
Define "intonation". (1)
---
The rise and fall of the voice across a phrase or sentence (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between pace and pause. (2)
---
Pace is the speed at which lines are delivered (1); a pause is a break or silence within or between lines (1).
:::

::: q
Describe two physical skills an actor could use to show a character is nervous. (4)
---
Any two, 2 marks each (skill 1 + precise detail/effect 1): fidgeting gesture, twisting a ring or tugging a sleeve; avoiding eye contact, eyes flicking to the door; hunched or closed posture, arms folded tight; shifting weight from foot to foot; quick, small movements.
:::

::: q
Explain how an actor could use accent to communicate character. (2)
---
Accent shows where a character comes from and often their class (1), for example a regional working-class accent contrasting with a clipped upper-class one to show the social gap between two characters (1).
:::

::: q
A character says: "I've never been so happy in my life." Describe how an actor could deliver this line sarcastically. (3)
---
A flat, monotone delivery with no rise in pitch (1); exaggerated stress on "never" or "happy", with a drawn-out vowel (1); an accompanying physical signal such as an eye-roll, a slow hand-clap or a blank expression, so the audience reads the opposite meaning (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the build or height of an actor could affect the interpretation of a character. (2)
---
A tall or broad actor can seem physically dominant or threatening, which suits a powerful character (1); casting against type (for example a small actor as a bully) could create a different interpretation, suggesting power that comes from voice or status rather than size (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"Say it angrily" is not a skill.** Name the vocal skills (volume, pitch, pace, pause) that make it sound angry.
- **Only voice, or only body.** The 8-mark question needs both.
- **No word pinned.** Say *which word* is stressed and *where* the pause falls.
- **Effect missing or generic.** "This shows the audience how he feels" earns little. Say exactly what they understand or feel.
- **Choices that don't fit the character or period.** A Puritan farmer's wife should not flounce across the stage.
- **Listing skills without developing them.** Fewer, precise, developed choices beat a long list.
:::

::: test
A young soldier is about to go into battle. He says to his friend: "Tell my mum I said goodbye."

Describe two vocal skills the actor could use on this line. (4)
---
Any two skills, 2 marks each (precise description on a specific word 1 + effect 1). For example: a pause before "goodbye" with the voice dropping in volume, showing he cannot bear to say it; a slight tremble or crack in the voice on "mum", revealing his fear and youth; a steady, controlled pace at first to appear brave, then speeding up to get the words out; a falling pitch on "goodbye" so it sounds final.
:::

::: test
Describe how an actor could use physical skills to show that a character is much older than the actor playing them. (4)
---
1 mark per precise, appropriate physical skill, up to 4. For example: a slower gait with shorter steps and slightly bent knees; a stooped posture with rounded shoulders; careful, deliberate movements when sitting or standing, using a hand on the chair for support; stiff joints in gestures; eyes narrowed or leaning in to see or hear; slower reactions.
:::

::: test
Explain how a performer's vocal and physical skills could show that a character's status changes during a scene. (6)
---
Levels. 5–6: precise vocal and physical choices before and after a clearly identified turning point, with effects explained. 3–4: clear choices with some detail. 1–2: general or one-sided.

Indicative content: at the start, low status shown by quiet volume, hesitant pace, rising uncertain intonation, hunched posture, avoiding eye contact, staying near the edge of the space; at the turning point (a revelation or victory) a pause and a change in stance; after it, a lower, steady pitch, slower and more controlled pace, upright posture, direct eye contact, moving centre stage or towards the other character, stillness; effect on the audience: they see the power shift.
:::

::: test
A daughter has been caught lying to her mother about where she spent the night. The mother says: "Don't you dare walk away from me."

You are performing the role of the mother. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Levels. 7–8: exact description of both vocal and physical skills with precise detail on specific words and moments; effects fully explained and entirely right for the character and situation. 5–6: clear description of both with a number of precise details; effects appropriate and clear. 3–4: reasonably clear description with a few details, or detail on only one of vocal/physical; effects partly explained. 1–2: limited, general description (for example "shout it"), effects unexplained. Only vocal or only physical: maximum 3–4.

Indicative content: vocal - a sharp intake of breath, then "Don't" delivered with a sudden rise in volume to stop her daughter, then the rest at a lower, controlled, clipped pace; stress on "dare"; pitch falling on "me" to make it a command; a local accent consistent with the character. Physical - she moves quickly downstage to cut off the exit, blocking the doorway; a pointing gesture or a hand held up; direct, unbroken eye contact; upright, rigid posture; after the line, stillness, arms folded. Effects: the audience sees her anger, fear for her daughter and her authority; the tension of whether the daughter will obey; possibly sympathy for both.
:::

::: test
Describe how you would use your acting skills to interpret a character of your choice from a play you have studied, in one key scene and in the play as a whole. (20)
---
Levels. 16–20: an excellent, well-developed interpretation with an extensive range of vocal and physical skills precisely described at specific moments; ideas entirely appropriate to the scene and linked convincingly to the character's journey across the whole play. 11–15: a clear interpretation with a wide range of skills, a number of precise details and good links to the play as a whole. 6–10: a reasonable interpretation with a fair range of skills, some detail, and some reference to the wider play. 1–5: a limited interpretation with a narrow range of skills, little detail and little sense of the whole play. A retelling of the plot with few skills stays in 1–5.

Indicative content: a one-sentence interpretation of the character (age, status, key traits, what they want); in the key scene, three or four moments, each with precise vocal skills (accent, pitch, pace, pause, volume, intonation, emotional range on specific words) and physical skills (posture, gait, gesture, facial expression, eye contact, proxemics, use of the space) and the effect on the audience; how these choices fit the context (period manners, class, accent); two or three moments elsewhere showing how the character changes (for example confidence at the start turning to desperation, or a shift in status), and how the voice and body change with them; the payoff at the climax or ending.
:::

::: test
Explain why precise detail matters when describing performance skills in the written exam. (3)
---
The examiner cannot see the performance, so the answer must describe it exactly enough to picture (1); naming the skill, the word or moment and the direction of change (for example pitch rising on a specific word) shows understanding of how skills create meaning (1); precise description with a clear effect is what separates higher bands from general answers such as "say it angrily" (1).
:::
