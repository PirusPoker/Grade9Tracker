## What it is

This lesson covers the parts of a text that live *underneath* the words and *across* a scene: **character**, **sub-text**, **motivation and interaction**, **mood and atmosphere**, **pace and rhythm**, and **dramatic climax**. They are the ideas that turn "I would say it loudly" into an interpretation. The 8-mark question asks for "the effects you want to create". The 12-mark question asks how you would interact with another actor to show something. The 20-mark question asks you to interpret a role across the play. Section C asks how mood, tension or relationships were created. All of them are built from this vocabulary.

### Character

A **character** is the person the actor creates from the clues in the text: what they say, what they do, what others say about them, and the stage directions. For each character you study, pin down:
- **Given circumstances**: age, class, job, family, where and when they live.
- **Personality and attitudes**: proud, anxious, defiant, loyal.
- **Status**: their power in each scene, which can shift within a scene.
- **Journey**: how they change across the play, and where the turning points are.

### Sub-text

**Sub-text** is the meaning underneath the line: what the character really thinks or feels but does not say. "I'm fine" can mean *I'm furious*, *I'm heartbroken* or *leave me alone*. Audiences read sub-text from everything that is *not* the words:

| Tool | How it reveals sub-text |
|---|---|
| **Pause** | A hesitation before "Of course I trust you" tells us she doesn't |
| **Tone** | A bright tone over a sad line shows someone hiding pain |
| **Pitch and volume** | A voice that drops suddenly can reveal fear under bravado |
| **Contradicting gesture** | Saying "I'm not angry" while gripping a chair back |
| **Eye contact** | Looking away on a promise; holding the gaze too long on a threat |
| **Proxemics** | Saying "Stay" while stepping back |
| **Business with a prop** | Wiping an already clean table too hard during a polite conversation |

When you write about sub-text in the exam, always name the *real* meaning and the *skill* that shows it: "On 'I'm pleased for you' I would smile but hold it a beat too long, with a flat, falling intonation, so the audience sees her jealousy."

### Motivation and interaction

**Motivation** is *why* a character does or says something. Actors often think in terms of an **objective** (what I want in this scene: to be forgiven, to get the money, to escape) and an **obstacle** (what stops me). A clear objective makes every line active: the character is always *trying to do something* to the other person, such as persuade, wound, charm or dismiss.

**Interaction** is how characters affect each other: listening, reacting, dominating, retreating. Much of the audience's understanding comes from the actor who is *not* speaking. The listener's reactions (a flinch, a laugh, turning away) show the relationship. **Status** is the key to interaction. When one character's status rises, the other's usually falls, and the actors can show this through levels, space, eye contact and who moves towards whom.

### Mood and atmosphere

**Mood** is the emotional feeling of a scene (tense, joyful, eerie, melancholy). **Atmosphere** is the feeling in the space that the audience experiences (oppressive, chaotic, intimate). Both are created by:
- **The text**: situation, dramatic irony, language, silences.
- **Performers**: pace, volume, stillness, proximity, energy.
- **Design**: lighting colour and intensity, sound and music, set and space.

"The mood was tense" earns little. Say *which moment*, *which choice* created it, and *what the audience felt*: "the long silence after the gunshot, with the actors frozen and only a low hum under it, made the audience hold its breath".

### Pace and rhythm

**Pace** is the speed at which a scene or line is played. **Rhythm** is the pattern of that speed: the beats of fast and slow, sound and silence. Good scenes change pace:
- **Quick, clipped exchanges** and cues picked up instantly build urgency or comedy.
- **Overlapping lines** suggest chaos, argument or excitement.
- **Pauses and silences** create suspense and let a moment land.
- **Slowing down** before a revelation makes the audience lean in.
- **Comic timing** depends on rhythm: a set-up, a tiny pause, then the punchline.

### Dramatic climax

The **climax** is the point of highest tension, where the conflict comes to a head and something decisive happens. A play has a main climax (usually late), and each scene can have its own. Tension builds towards it through **rising action**: pace increases, stakes rise, the space between characters closes, lighting and sound intensify. The moments after it, the **resolution** or falling action, usually slow down. A common staging choice is a sudden stillness or silence at the peak, which is often more powerful than shouting.

::: terms
- **Character**: the person an actor creates from the clues the text gives about their circumstances, personality and actions.
- **Sub-text**: the meaning, thought or feeling underneath a line that the character does not say out loud.
- **Motivation**: the reason a character says or does something; what drives them.
- **Objective**: what a character wants to achieve in a scene or moment.
- **Status**: the level of power or importance a character has in relation to others at a given moment.
- **Interaction**: the way characters respond to and affect each other, including how they listen and react.
- **Mood**: the emotional feeling of a scene, such as tense, joyful or melancholy.
- **Atmosphere**: the feeling created in the performance space that the audience experiences.
- **Pace**: the speed at which lines or a scene are delivered.
- **Rhythm**: the pattern of changes in pace, sound and silence across a scene.
- **Dramatic climax**: the point of highest tension, where the conflict reaches its peak.
- **Dramatic irony**: when the audience knows something that a character on stage does not.
:::

::: method
1. **Find the character's objective** in the moment: what do they want from the other person?
2. **Find the sub-text**: what do they really mean under the line? Write it in a few words.
3. **Choose the skills** that let the audience see the sub-text: pause, tone, gesture, eye contact, proxemics.
4. **Map the scene's rhythm**: where it is fast, where it slows, where the silence is.
5. **Locate the climax** and plan how tension builds towards it and releases after it.
6. **State the effect** every time: what the audience understands or feels, and why.
:::

::: example Defining sub-text
What is meant by the term "sub-text"? (1)

The underlying meaning of a line: what a character really thinks or feels but does not say directly (1).
:::

::: example Showing sub-text
A character says to her sister, "No, honestly, I'm really happy for you," after learning her sister is marrying the man she loves. Describe how an actor could show the sub-text of this line. (4)

A pause after "No" as she gathers herself, showing she has been caught off guard (1). A bright, slightly too high pitch on "really happy", so the voice sounds forced (1). A smile that does not reach her eyes, and she breaks eye contact on "for you", looking at the floor (1). She could fold her arms tightly or grip her cup, a closed gesture that contradicts the words, so the audience sees her heartbreak and jealousy while her sister does not, creating dramatic irony (1).
:::

::: example Building pace to a climax
A scene shows two friends arguing about money, ending with one of them revealing he has been lying for months. Explain how the performers could use pace and rhythm to build tension to the climax. (6)

At the start the exchanges are casual, at a relaxed pace with natural pauses, so the audience does not expect a crisis (1). As the questions become pointed, the questioner picks up cues faster and the other starts to hesitate, so the rhythm becomes uneven (1). The argument then accelerates, with lines overlapping and volume rising, and the space between them closing (1). Just before the revelation the liar stops, and there is a long silence where neither moves (1). He delivers the confession slowly and quietly, which contrasts with the shouting and makes it land hard (1). A second long pause after it lets the audience absorb the shock, and the release of tension comes as the other friend turns away (1).
:::

::: example Mood from staging
Explain how a director could create an eerie atmosphere for a scene set in an empty house at night. (4)

Lighting: low-intensity, cold blue side light through a window with a gobo casting the shadows of branches, leaving most of the stage in darkness (1). Sound: near-silence broken by a creaking floorboard and a distant, irregular tapping from an offstage speaker, so the audience cannot place it (1). Performance: the actor moves slowly with frequent stops to listen, and speaks just above a whisper (1). Pace: long pauses before each move make the audience wait for something to happen, building suspense (1).
:::

::: q
Define "motivation". (1)
---
The reason a character does or says something; what drives their actions (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between mood and atmosphere. (2)
---
Mood is the emotional feeling or tone of the scene (1); atmosphere is the feeling created in the space that the audience experiences, built by performance and design (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the actor who is listening can show the relationship between two characters. (3)
---
Through reactions while the other speaks, such as a flinch, a smile or rolling the eyes (1); eye contact, either held (trust, challenge) or avoided (guilt, fear) (1); proxemics, moving closer or away, showing attraction or tension (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how status could shift during a scene, and how actors could show it. (4)
---
A character with low status gains power, for example by revealing a secret, while the other loses it (1); the rising character could stand up or move to a higher level (1), stop apologising and use a steady, lower pitch (1), while the other stops moving freely, avoids eye contact and speaks more hesitantly (1).
:::

::: q
What is meant by "dramatic climax"? (1)
---
The point of highest tension in a scene or play, where the conflict reaches its peak (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a pause could create comedy. (2)
---
A pause before a punchline or reaction builds expectation (1); the timing then makes the line or reaction funnier, for example a character's blank stare before replying (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Naming a mood without explaining it.** Say which moment, which choice, and what the audience felt.
- **Sub-text left as a label.** Always say what the hidden meaning is and exactly which skill reveals it.
- **Playing everything at one pace.** Examiners reward contrast: a build, a silence, a release.
- **Forgetting the listener.** In interaction questions, the reactions of the character not speaking often earn the marks.
- **Mixing up climax and ending.** The climax is the peak of tension, which usually comes before the end.
:::

::: test
A scene shows a father welcoming his son home from prison. The father says the right words, but he has not forgiven him. Explain how the actor playing the father could show the sub-text of the scene. (4)
---
1 mark per precise, appropriate skill with its effect, up to 4. For example: a stiff, brief handshake instead of an embrace; a pause before greeting him; a polite but flat tone and falling intonation; keeping a table between them (proxemics); looking past the son rather than at him; busying himself with a task instead of listening. Effect: the audience senses the resentment and anticipates conflict.

::: test
Explain how motivation affects the way an actor delivers a line. (4)
---
Knowing why the character speaks gives the line an intention, such as to persuade, to hurt or to hide (1); the intention decides tone, for example gentle and warm to persuade or cold and clipped to hurt (1); it shapes the physical choices, such as moving towards the other person to win them over or turning away to dismiss them (1); so the line becomes an action on the other character, and the audience understands the relationship (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how lighting and sound could change the mood of a scene at its climax. (4)

Lighting (2): for example, a snap change from a warm general wash to a tight, cold white special on the two characters, cutting everything else, which isolates them and heightens tension. Sound (2): for example, underscoring that has been building suddenly cuts to silence at the peak, or a sharp effect such as a door slam or thunder crack marks the moment, making the audience jump or hold its breath.

::: test
A scene shows a mother and her teenage son arguing in a cramped kitchen late at night. The son has been hiding the fact that he has left college. The scene ends when he admits the truth and walks out.

Explain how you would use pace, pause and interaction to build tension to the climax of the scene. (12)
---
Levels. 10–12: an exact, developed explanation, entirely appropriate to the scene, with precise choices of pace, pause and interaction at identified moments, a clear build to the climax and fully explained effects. 7–9: a clear explanation with a number of precise choices and a sense of build. 4–6: some relevant choices with limited detail or build; effects partly explained. 1–3: limited, general ideas (for example "they shout more") with little sense of the climax.

Indicative content: the opening played at a slow, tired pace, the mother washing up with her back to him, casual questions about his day; the son's sub-text of guilt shown by short answers and a pause before each; the mother notices, stops washing and turns, a silence that shifts the rhythm; her questions quicken and she picks up cues fast while his hesitations grow; proxemics close as she moves round the table towards him and he backs towards the door; overlapping lines and rising volume at the height of the argument; at the climax a sudden stop, a long silence with both frozen, then the admission delivered quietly and slowly, with eye contact finally held; a second silence as she takes it in; the release as he walks out and she is left motionless; the audience is made to wait, to anticipate the truth and to feel the shock and sadness when it comes.
:::

::: test

Indicative content: a named character and two or three moments where they mean more than they say (hiding guilt, love, fear, jealousy, contempt); for each, the real meaning and the skills that reveal it (pause, tone, intonation, eye contact, contradicting gesture, proxemics, business with a prop); how the audience is drawn in because they must read the character, may know more than other characters (dramatic irony), and anticipate what will come out later; link to the character's journey across the play.
