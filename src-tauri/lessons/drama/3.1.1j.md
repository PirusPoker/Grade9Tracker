## What it is

Lighting and sound are the two design skills the audience experiences almost without noticing, and they shape the mood of every scene. The specification asks for the design of lighting "such as **direction, colour, intensity, special effects**" and the design of sound "such as **direction, amplification, music, sound effects both live and recorded**". The designer guidance adds the equipment and effects: gels, gobos, gauzes, lighting states, fades, cross-fades, blackout, shadow, lantern types, microphones, soundscapes, reverb, echo and more.

On the paper, you can answer the 20-mark Section B question as a lighting or sound designer, and Section C regularly offers a question on how lighting or sound created mood, atmosphere, time or place. "The lighting was dim" gains little. Name the **colour, angle, intensity and cue change**, and say **what it made the audience feel**.

## Lighting

### What lighting does

- **Visibility**: the audience must see what they need to see.
- **Focus**: directs the eye to the important action.
- **Time**: time of day, season, the passing of time.
- **Place**: interior or exterior, a forest, a prison, a dream.
- **Mood and atmosphere**: warm and safe, cold and threatening, chaotic.
- **Transitions**: moves the audience between scenes and locations.
- **Symbolism**: a colour or effect that carries a meaning through the play.

### The controllable qualities

| Quality | Choices | Effect examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Direction (angle)** | Front (flat, clear); **side** (sculpts bodies, dramatic, used for dance); **back** (a halo, separates actors from the background, silhouettes); **top / down light** (isolation, oppression, a pool of light); **uplighting** from below (unnatural, sinister, footlights) | Side light for a fight; a single down light for a prisoner; uplighting for a ghost |
| **Colour** | Set with **gels** (coloured filters) or LED mixing. **Warm** (amber, straw, orange, pink); **cool** (blue, steel, lavender); **saturated** (deep red, deep blue) | Warm amber for a safe family home; cold blue for night or grief; red for danger or violence |
| **Intensity** | Brightness from full to barely visible; a dim, low state or a harsh, full one | High intensity for a bright summer's day or an interrogation; low for night or intimacy |
| **Special effects** | **Gobos** (a stencil in a lantern that projects a pattern: window frames, leaves, prison bars); **strobe** (rapid flashing); **haze** to show beams; **gauze** reveals; flicker for fire or candles; lightning; projection; **shadow** | Leaf gobo on the floor for a forest; strobe for a panic or a fight; a flicker for firelight |

### Lanterns (lighting equipment)

| Lantern | Beam | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Profile spot** | Hard-edged, focusable; can be shaped with shutters or an iris; takes a **gobo** | Specials, precise areas, patterns, a tight spotlight on one actor |
| **Fresnel** | Soft-edged beam that blends; **barn doors** shape it | General cover (a wash) blended across the acting area |
| **Flood** | Wide, even, unfocused light | Lighting a cyclorama or a large backdrop |
| **PAR can** | Intense, near-parallel beam with a slightly oval shape | Strong shafts of colour, concerts, musicals |
| **Follow spot** | A powerful profile operated by hand to follow a performer | Musicals, a star moment, a narrator |
| **Moving light** | Motorised; colour, position, gobo and focus change remotely | Big musical numbers, shifting states |
| **Birdie** | A very small lantern that can be hidden in or on the set | Uplighting, lighting from the floor edge |
| **Strobe** | Rapid, bright flashes | Chaos, fights, slow-motion effects (warning signs needed for audience safety) |

**Rigging positions**: lanterns are hung on bars over the stage and in the auditorium (front of house), on booms at the side (for side light), on the floor, or hidden in the set. Designers decide the **angle** and **focus** of each lantern.

### States, cues and changes

A **lighting state** is a combination of lanterns at set levels (for example "dawn in the kitchen"). A **cue** is the change from one state to another, operated from the **lighting desk** by a technician when called by the stage manager. Types of change:
- **Snap**: an instant change (shock, sudden revelation).
- **Fade**: a gradual change up or down, over a set number of seconds.
- **Cross-fade**: one state fades out as another fades in (smooth transitions).
- **Blackout**: all stage light out at once (end of scene, shock, or to hide a change).
- **Fade to black**: a slow fade to darkness (endings, death, sadness).

A good lighting answer describes a **sequence of cues** tied to moments in the scene, with the timing and the effect.

## Sound

### What sound does

Sound establishes **location** (seagulls and waves), **period** (a 1960s song on a radio), **time** (dawn chorus, church bells at midnight), **mood and atmosphere** (a low drone for dread), **tension and its release**, **transitions** between scenes, and it covers or accompanies action. **Silence** is also a sound design choice.

### Types of sound

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Live sound** | Created during the performance: actors, musicians, offstage effects | A real door slam; a thunder sheet shaken in the wings; live piano |
| **Recorded sound** | Pre-recorded effects and music played through the system | Rain, traffic, a baby crying, an explosion |
| **Music** | Composed or chosen music, recorded or played live | Overture, songs, transition music |
| **Underscoring** | Music played quietly *under* dialogue | Soft strings under a reconciliation, building emotion |
| **Soundscape** | Layers of sounds building a whole environment or state of mind | A city at night: distant sirens, traffic hum, a dog barking |
| **Diegetic sound** | Sound the characters can hear, from within the world of the play | A radio playing, a phone ringing |
| **Non-diegetic sound** | Sound only the audience hears | Tense music under a chase |

### The sound designer's controls

- **Direction**: where the sound seems to come from. Speakers can be placed on the set, behind the audience or in the wings, and sound can be **panned** between them. A knock from a speaker inside an offstage door is more convincing than one from the main speakers.
- **Amplification**: making voices or sounds louder through **microphones**, a **mixing desk**, amplifiers and speakers. **Radio microphones** (a headset or a small clip-on pack) are standard in musicals. **Float mics** along the front edge of the stage pick up a whole area.
- **Volume and levels**: set for each cue and balanced against the actors' voices.
- **Effects**: **reverb** (the echoey space of a church or hall), **echo** (a distinct repeat), **distortion** (a crackling radio, a disturbed mind), **pitch shifting**, **looping**, **fades** (in, out, cross-fades) and **snap cuts** to silence.
- **Equipment**: microphones, a mixing desk, amplifiers, speakers (including **foldback**, speakers that let performers hear themselves), and playback software. The sound **cue list** is operated by a technician.

### Writing a lighting or sound design

Describe the design as **a sequence of cues** through the scene: *pre-set* (as the audience enters), *opening* state or sound, *changes* at named moments, the *climax*, and the *final* cue. For each, give the equipment or quality (colour, angle, intensity; source, direction, volume), the timing (snap, a 10-second fade) and the effect on the audience.

::: terms
- **Gel**: a coloured filter placed in front of a lantern to change the colour of the light.
- **Gobo**: a metal or glass stencil placed in a profile lantern to project a pattern, such as leaves or window bars.
- **Profile spot**: a lantern with a hard-edged, focusable beam that can be shaped and can take a gobo.
- **Fresnel**: a lantern with a soft-edged beam, used to create an even wash of light.
- **Flood**: a lantern with a wide, unfocused beam, often used to light a cyclorama.
- **Lighting state**: a particular combination of lanterns and levels that creates one look on stage.
- **Cross-fade**: a gradual change in which one lighting state or sound fades out as another fades in.
- **Blackout**: all the stage lights turned off at once.
- **Underscoring**: music played quietly beneath dialogue to support the mood.
- **Soundscape**: layers of sound combined to create an environment or atmosphere.
- **Diegetic sound**: sound that exists within the world of the play and the characters can hear.
- **Amplification**: making sound louder through microphones, amplifiers and speakers.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the purpose**: what the lighting or sound must do in this moment (time, place, mood, focus, tension).
2. **Specify the qualities**: for lighting, direction, colour, intensity and effects; for sound, source, type, direction, volume and effects.
3. **Name the equipment** where it helps: profile with a gobo, Fresnel wash, radio mics, a speaker hidden on set.
4. **Write it as cues**: what changes, when (tied to a line or action), and how (snap, fade, cross-fade, cut).
5. **Give the effect** on the audience after every cue.
6. **For the whole play** (20 marks), show how the design develops, with motifs, contrasts and a build to the climax.
:::

::: example Lighting a moment
Describe how lighting could be used to show a character alone in a prison cell at night. (4)

A single profile lantern rigged high and almost directly above the actor gives a tight, hard-edged pool of down light, isolating him in the centre of darkness (1). A steel-blue gel gives a cold, clinical colour that suggests night and hopelessness (1). A second profile with a barred-window gobo projects a pattern of bars across the floor and the actor's body, as if moonlight were falling through a high window (1). The intensity stays low, so the edges of the cell disappear and the space feels endless and lonely (1).
:::

::: example Sound for tension
Describe how sound could be used to build tension as a character searches a dark house for an intruder. (4)

A low, continuous electronic drone, non-diegetic, fades in very slowly under the scene so the audience feels unease before they know why (1). Recorded creaks and a single footstep are played from a speaker hidden upstage left, so the sound seems to come from inside the house, behind the actor (1). The drone gradually rises in volume and pitch as the character nears a closed door (1). At the moment she opens it, the sound snaps to total silence, leaving the audience holding their breath before the reveal (1).
:::

::: example Cue sequence
A scene opens at dawn on a beach and ends with a storm. Explain how a lighting designer could show this change through a sequence of cues. (6)

Pre-set: a very low, deep blue wash from Fresnels, with the cyclorama dark, suggesting the last of the night (1). Cue 1: over two minutes, a slow cross-fade brings up a pale pink and then warm straw colour on the cyc from floods below it, as the sun rises (1). The actors are lit by low-angled side light in amber, as if from the rising sun, giving a peaceful, hopeful mood (1). Cue 2: as clouds gather, a slow fade reduces the warm light and brings in a cold steel-grey wash, so the audience senses a threat (1). Cue 3: at the height of the storm, quick flashes of white light from a strobe or lightning effect, synchronised with thunder, snap the stage into brightness and back (1). A moving cloud gobo across the cyc and low overall intensity make the beach feel dangerous and chaotic (1).
:::

::: q
What does a gobo do? (1)
---
It projects a pattern or image (such as leaves or window bars) from a profile lantern (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a profile spot and a Fresnel. (2)
---
A profile gives a hard-edged, focusable beam that can be shaped and take a gobo (1); a Fresnel gives a soft-edged beam that blends into a wash (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how the direction of light can change the audience's impression of a character. (3)
---
Uplighting from below makes the face look distorted and sinister (1); side light sculpts the body, creating drama and shadow (1); strong back light can make a character a silhouette, mysterious or heroic (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways sound can establish location. (4)
---
Any two, 2 marks each: recorded effects of the place, such as waves and gulls for a harbour or traffic for a city; a soundscape layering several sounds; music associated with a region or culture; acoustic effects such as reverb for a church or a large hall; direction, such as a train heard passing behind the audience.
:::

::: q
Explain why a sound designer might use radio microphones in a musical. (2)
---
To amplify the actors' voices so they can be heard clearly over the band or orchestra (1), while allowing them to move freely around the stage and dance (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the effect of a snap blackout at the end of a scene. (2)
---
The sudden darkness is abrupt and shocking (1), leaving the final image or line hanging in the audience's mind and creating suspense or impact (1).
:::

::: watch
- **"The lighting was dim / the sound was scary".** Give colour, angle, intensity, equipment and timing, then the effect.
- **Designer and operator confused.** The designer decides; the technician operates the desk.
- **Profile and Fresnel mixed up.** Gobos go in profiles; Fresnels give soft washes.
- **One static state.** Lighting and sound designs are about change. Describe cues and transitions.
- **Forgetting silence and darkness.** Cutting sound or light is often the most powerful choice.
- **Effects with no purpose.** A strobe or reverb needs a reason in the scene.
:::

::: test
Explain how a sound designer could use reverb and echo. (2)
---
Reverb makes a voice or sound seem to be in a large, resonant space, such as a church or a cave (1); echo repeats a sound distinctly, useful for a mountain, a tunnel, a memory or a haunting voice in a character's head (1).
:::

::: test

---

:::

::: test
A scene shows a family celebrating a birthday in their kitchen when a police officer arrives with bad news. Describe how sound could be used to support the change in mood. (6)
---
Levels. 5–6: precise sound choices (type, source, direction, volume, timing) at identified moments, clearly supporting the change, with effects explained. 3–4: clear choices with some detail. 1–2: general ideas.

Indicative content: diegetic upbeat pop music from a radio on the kitchen counter (from a speaker hidden in the set), laughter and clinking glasses, perhaps a live "Happy Birthday"; the doorbell as a sharp, recorded effect from a speaker offstage; a character turns the radio off, a sudden cut to silence; the silence held under the news; a low, sustained non-diegetic note or muffled distant traffic slowly faded in to underline shock; the celebration sounds never return; effect: contrast makes the news more devastating.
:::

::: test
You are a lighting designer. A scene shows a young woman waiting alone on a railway platform late at night; she is increasingly afraid that she is being followed. Describe how you would use lighting to create effects which support the action, and explain why your ideas are appropriate. (20)
---
Levels. 16–20: excellent, precise lighting ideas across a sequence of cues, with an extensive range of qualities (direction, colour, intensity, effects) and equipment, all precisely targeted at the action and mood; fully explained effects and a clear build. 11–15: clear, secure ideas with a wide range of lighting skills and a number of precise details; clear effects. 6–10: reasonable ideas with a fair range and some precise detail. 1–5: limited ideas, vague ("dark and spooky"), few terms.

Indicative content: pre-set and opening state - low-intensity cool blue-green wash suggesting night and harsh fluorescent station lighting; a hard-edged profile special from above on the bench where she waits, a pool of light that makes the surrounding darkness feel larger; a flickering practical lamp on the set or a flicker effect to unsettle; a gobo projecting the pattern of a platform canopy or railings; as fear grows - side light from one side leaves half her face in shadow; the edges of the stage slowly darken so her world shrinks; a silhouette seen through haze by back light at the far end of the platform, suggesting a follower; a snap change as a train passes, with bright white flashes of light moving across her (chase effect with moving lights) while the sound roars; at the climax, a moment of near-blackout, then relief or shock with the lights of an arriving train; equipment: profiles, Fresnels for the wash, moving lights or a chase for the train, haze; effects: isolation, vulnerability, suspense, the audience's fear for her.
:::

::: test
You are a sound designer for a play you have studied. Describe how you would use sound to create effects which support the action of one key scene, and explain why your ideas are appropriate for the play as a whole. (20)
---
Levels. 16–20: excellent, precise sound design across a sequence of cues, with an extensive range of sound skills (live and recorded effects, music, underscoring, soundscape, direction, amplification, volume, reverb, echo, fades, silence), precisely supporting the action, context and style of the scene; convincing links to the design across the whole play. 11–15: clear, secure ideas with a wide range and a number of precise details; good sense of the play as a whole. 6–10: reasonable ideas, a fair range, some detail and some reference to the rest of the play. 1–5: limited ideas with little detail and little sense of the play.

Indicative content: pre-show and opening sound establishing period and place (location soundscapes, period-appropriate music); diegetic and non-diegetic choices; speaker placement and direction for offstage events; amplification where needed (songs, large spaces); underscoring to support emotional moments; building tension with rising volume or pitch and releasing it with silence; transitions between scenes; motifs repeated across the play (a sound linked to a character, a theme or a threat) and how the design develops to the climax and ending; each cue tied to a moment in the scene and its effect on the audience.
:::
