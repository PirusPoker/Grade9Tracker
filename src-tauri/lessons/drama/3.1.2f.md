## What it is

*The Crucible* by Arthur Miller (prescribed edition: **Methuen Student Editions**) is the most widely studied set play. AQA describes it as a **historical drama** in a **naturalistic** style. In Section B you get an extract from it and answer four questions: a 4-mark design question tied to context, an 8-mark question on performing one line, a 12-mark question on space and interaction in a shaded section, and a 20-mark question as either a performer or a designer. Every one of them rewards knowing the **whole play**, not just the page in front of you.

### Context: two dates, one play

| | When and where | What it gives a theatre maker |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Salem, Massachusetts, **1692** | A **Puritan theocracy**: church and law are one, so a sin is a crime. Plain dress, hard farm labour, no theatre, no dancing, fear of the Devil and of the forest beyond the settlement. Land disputes and old grudges (the Putnams, Corey, Proctor) feed the accusations. |
| **Writing** | USA, first staged **1953** | **McCarthyism**: the anti-communist "witch hunt" in which people were questioned by the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) and pressed to name others. Miller was himself later called before HUAC and refused to name names. The play is an **allegory**: 1692 stands for 1950s America. |

AQA's 4-mark question frames the context as **a Puritan community in the late 17th century**. That is the context your costume or setting must show, so design for 1692, not 1953, unless a question says otherwise.

**Style.** Naturalistic acting (real motives, real time within each act) and naturalistic design (four real rooms). Miller's long prose notes about each character are not spoken, but they are gold for performers: they tell you age, status and history.

### Plot, act by act

| Act | Place | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **One** | A small upper bedroom in Reverend Parris's house, spring 1692 | Parris's daughter Betty will not wake after he caught the girls dancing in the woods with Tituba. The Putnams are hungry for witchcraft; Abigail threatens the other girls to keep quiet. Alone with Proctor, Abigail reveals she still wants him (she was dismissed from the Proctors' house after their affair); he refuses her. Reverend Hale arrives with heavy books; under pressure Tituba "confesses" and names others, and Abigail and Betty join in crying out names. |
| **Two** | The Proctors' farmhouse, about eight days later | A cold, careful marriage: Proctor has not been forgiven. Mary Warren, their servant, is now an official of the court and gives Elizabeth a poppet she made there. Elizabeth learns she has been named. Hale tests the Proctors on their faith (Proctor cannot recall the commandment on adultery). Cheever finds a needle in the poppet; Abigail has claimed a pain in her belly; Elizabeth is arrested. Proctor forces Mary to agree to testify. |
| **Three** | The vestry of the meeting house, used as an anteroom of the court | Giles Corey and Francis Nurse try to present evidence and are brushed aside. Mary says the girls were pretending, but cannot faint on demand. Proctor confesses his **lechery** to discredit Abigail; Elizabeth, brought in and not knowing, lies to protect his name. The girls turn on Mary with a pretended "yellow bird"; Mary breaks and accuses Proctor. Hale quits the court. |
| **Four** | A cell in Salem jail, autumn | Abigail has fled with Parris's money. Hale begs prisoners to confess and live. Danforth needs a confession to steady the town. Elizabeth, allowed to speak with Proctor, admits her own coldness; he signs a confession, then refuses to let it be posted and tears it up to keep his **name**. He goes to hang with Rebecca Nurse. |

### Characters and a performance note for each

| Character | Who they are | Performance note |
|---|---|---|
| **John Proctor** | A farmer in his thirties, strong, respected, guilty about the affair | Physical ease and power early (a man of the soil), a temper that flares, then the stillness of a man who has chosen. His guilt shows in avoided eye contact with Elizabeth. |
| **Elizabeth Proctor** | His wife, honest to a fault, reserved | Economy: small, controlled gestures, careful speech, a voice that stays level. Warmth breaks through only in Act Four. |
| **Abigail Williams** | Parris's niece, seventeen, clever, ruthless | Two faces: seductive and direct alone with Proctor; wide-eyed, trembling innocence in public. Status shifts fast. |
| **Reverend Parris** | Salem's minister, anxious about his position | Nervous energy, hands that fidget, a whining pitch when threatened; he thinks of his reputation first. |
| **Reverend Hale** | A minister and witchcraft expert from Beverly | Arrives brisk and proud of his learning; ends hollowed out, pleading. The biggest journey in the play after Proctor's. |
| **Deputy Governor Danforth** | The senior judge | Measured, courteous, immovable; stillness and control are his power. Never shouts when he can wait. |
| **Mary Warren** | The Proctors' servant, about seventeen | Timid, easily led; a brief new confidence in Act Two, then collapse under the girls' pressure in Act Three. |
| **Tituba** | Parris's slave, from Barbados | Terrified and cornered; her confession is survival, not belief. Play the fear, not a caricature. |
| **Rebecca Nurse, Giles Corey, the Putnams** | Elderly saint; cantankerous old farmer; a wealthy, grasping couple | Rebecca's calm is moral authority; Corey brings comedy then courage; the Putnams show greed behind the accusations. |

### Key moments and how to stage them

1. **Abigail and Proctor alone (Act One).** Proxemics tell the story: she closes the distance, he holds his ground then steps back; a touch that he removes. The girls' dancing earlier can be suggested by a flash of firelight across the back wall.
2. **Tituba's confession (Act One).** Build pace: the adults crowd her in a tightening circle, Hale kneels to her level, then the girls' cries overlap faster and louder until the stage is chaos. A lighting shift to hard white from above can freeze the climax.
3. **The Proctors' supper (Act Two).** Sub-text is everything: he salts the stew she cooked, praises it, she answers politely. Long table, two chairs far apart, hearth glow in amber that does not reach them.
4. **Elizabeth's arrest (Act Two).** The poppet as prop: a crude rag doll, the needle drawn out slowly in Cheever's fingers so the audience sees it. Proctor rips the warrant; chains are heard off stage before they are seen.
5. **Proctor's confession and Elizabeth's lie (Act Three).** Staging must stop Elizabeth seeing Proctor: Danforth orders them apart, so place Proctor upstage with his back turned while she stands downstage facing the judges. The audience sees both faces; she sees neither.
6. **The yellow bird (Act Three).** Mass hysteria: the girls' eyes fixed on a point high above the audience, voices echoing Mary's words back at her. Sound can add a faint, fluttering wingbeat, or the effect can come wholly from the performers.
7. **The torn confession (Act Four).** Grey dawn through a high barred window; Proctor tears the paper and the light slowly warms on him as he goes to die. A drum roll off stage, then silence as Elizabeth stays at the window.

### Design across the whole play

The four settings move from a **private bedroom** to a **home**, to a **public court**, to a **prison**: the walls close in as the town turns on itself. Many designers **drain light and colour act by act**, from spring sunlight through small windows to the cold grey of the cell. Costume: coarse wool and linen in browns, greys and muted greens, white coifs and collars, heavy leather shoes; black and good cloth for the judges and ministers to show status. Sound: church bells, wind in the forest, the silence of a court.

::: terms
- **Theocracy**: a society ruled by religious leaders, where breaking a religious rule is also breaking the law.
- **Puritans**: strict Protestants who valued plain living, hard work and obedience, and distrusted pleasure and display.
- **McCarthyism**: the 1950s American hunt for suspected communists, which ruined careers on accusation alone.
- **HUAC**: the House Un-American Activities Committee, which questioned suspects and pressed them to name others.
- **Allegory**: a story whose events stand for something else; Salem in 1692 stands for America in the 1950s.
- **Poppet**: a small rag doll; the one Mary Warren gives Elizabeth becomes the evidence that she is a witch.
- **Coif**: a close-fitting white linen cap covering a Puritan woman's hair.
- **Crying out**: publicly naming someone as a witch, which in Salem was enough to have them arrested.
- **Vestry**: a room attached to the meeting house, used in Act Three as an anteroom of the court.
:::

::: method
1. **Place the extract.** Say which act it is in and what has just happened, in one sentence.
2. **Read the context line in the question** and design for it: Puritan Salem, 1692.
3. **For the line (8 marks)**, name vocal skills on specific words (pitch, pause, stress, pace) and physical skills (posture, gesture, eye contact, proxemics), each with its effect on the audience.
4. **For the shaded section (12 marks)**, block it: start positions, three or four moves pinned to lines, levels and furniture, touch and eye contact, and how each shows the effect named in the question.
5. **For the 20-marker**, choose three or four moments in the extract, then two or three elsewhere in the play, so the examiner sees a journey. As a designer, write cues that change with the action.
6. **Use the play's own details** (the poppet, the court, the cell, Proctor's name) and never retell the plot for its own sake.
:::

::: example Costume for Abigail (4 marks)
You are designing a costume for Abigail to wear in Act One. The costume must reflect the context of *The Crucible*, set in a Puritan community in the late 17th century. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)

An ankle-length dress of coarse, undyed wool in a faded grey-brown, laced at the front with a tight bodice and long sleeves, because Puritans rejected display and a girl in a minister's household must look modest (1). A white linen coif covers her hair, but I would let one strand escape at the temple, a hint of the defiance she shows Proctor (1). A plain white collar closes at the throat; her apron is creased and a little grubby at the hem, since she has come from the woods where the girls danced (1). Heavy flat leather shoes with a wet, muddied edge; no jewellery or ribbon, as ornament would be vanity (1).
:::

::: example Performing a line in Act Four (8 marks)
You are performing the role of Proctor. Near the end of Act Four, when Danforth demands the signed confession, Proctor refuses and cries that it is his name. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)

I would begin with the paper held against my chest, both hands flat over it, shoulders rising with heavy breaths (1). The first words would come out cracked and loud, almost a howl, pitch rising sharply because this is the first time Proctor has put his whole soul into speech (1). I would pause after "name", swallow, and then repeat the idea at a lower, steadier volume, as if the certainty has arrived as he speaks (1). On the final phrase I would look straight at Danforth for the first time in the scene, holding eye contact, standing tall from a crouch (1). The effect: the audience sees his despair turn into dignity (1). Stepping downstage towards the judges reverses the status in the room; Danforth, who has dominated the play, is now the one who looks away (1). My voice breaks on the last word because he knows the choice means death, so the audience feels both triumph and grief (1). A long silence before Danforth answers lets the moment land (1).
:::

::: example Blocking Elizabeth's lie (12 marks, outline)
You are performing the role of Elizabeth in Act Three, from her entrance to her lie about Proctor. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Danforth to show the audience her fear. (12)

A top answer blocks the moment precisely. On a thrust stage Elizabeth is led in downstage centre by the marshal and left alone facing Danforth, who sits upstage on a raised dais, so the height difference crushes her. Proctor stands upstage left with his back to her, and she keeps glancing over her shoulder, trying to read him. When Danforth orders her to look only at him she snaps her eyes forward, hands knotted at her waist. As he presses her, he rises and walks a slow half-circle round her; she turns her head to follow but not her body. Her pauses grow longer; her answers get shorter and quieter. On the lie she closes her eyes, then answers, and her shoulders sag. Every choice is linked to her fear: of the court, and of betraying the husband she loves.
:::

::: example Designing lighting for Act Four (20 marks, outline)
Plan a lighting design for the start of Act Four and for the play as a whole. (20)

Extract: a single cold blue-white profile through a high barred window gobo, throwing striped shadow across the floor; everything else near darkness so the cell feels vast and cold. As Proctor and Elizabeth talk, a slow, almost invisible cross-fade adds a pale straw colour to the window light, the first sunrise. When he tears the confession, a warm special on him rises over ten seconds. Whole play: Act One warm spring daylight through a small upstage window; Act Two amber firelight that pools around the hearth but never around both Proctors at once; Act Three bright, flat, white court light with no shadows to hide in; Act Four the coldest state, so the final sunrise is the only warmth in the play. A top answer explains how each state supports the action.
:::

::: q
When and where is *The Crucible* set, and when was it written? (2)
---
Salem, Massachusetts, in 1692 (1); written and first staged in 1953, during McCarthyism (1).
:::

::: q
Why was Abigail dismissed from the Proctors' house? (1)
---
Elizabeth discovered her affair with John Proctor (1).
:::

::: q
What is the poppet, and why does it matter? (2)
---
A rag doll Mary Warren made in court and gave to Elizabeth (1); a needle is found in it after Abigail claims a needle pain, and Elizabeth is arrested (1).
:::

::: q
What does Proctor confess in Act Three, and how does Elizabeth undo it? (2)
---
His lechery with Abigail, to prove Abigail wants Elizabeth dead (1); Elizabeth, not knowing he has confessed, denies it to protect his name, so the court believes Abigail (1).
:::

::: q
Name two ways Hale changes between Act One and Act Four. (2)
---
From confident expert to a man who denounces the court (1); from hunting witches to begging the accused to lie and live (1).
:::

::: q
Why does Proctor refuse to let his confession be posted? (2)
---
It would shame his name and his sons (1) and betray the others who died refusing to lie (1).
:::

::: watch
- Design for **1692**, not 1953. Denim, zips or modern cuts lose marks.
- Name the **act** and the moment every time you move outside the extract.
- Abigail is not simply a villain on stage; show the **two faces** (private and public).
- In the Act Three lie, Elizabeth **cannot see** Proctor. Blocking that has her look at him misreads the scene.
- Say what each choice makes the **audience** feel or understand, not only what the actor does.
- Avoid black "witch" costumes and cartoon evil. The naturalistic style needs real people.
:::

::: test
Explain how Miller's own time is reflected in the play. (3)
---
Written during McCarthyism, when people were accused of communism and questioned by HUAC (1). Like the Salem court, HUAC pressed people to confess and name others (1). Miller uses 1692 as an allegory, so Proctor's refusal to name others or sign a lie echoes the stand taken by people, including Miller, in the 1950s (1).
:::

::: test
Identify the four locations of the play in order. (2)
---
Parris's upstairs bedroom; the Proctors' house; the vestry/court anteroom; a jail cell (2 for all four in order, 1 for three correct or all four out of order).
:::

::: test
You are designing a costume for Elizabeth to wear in Act Four. The costume must reflect the context of *The Crucible*, set in a Puritan community in the late 17th century. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)
---
Mark as one judgement, 1 mark per level. 4: a precise, detailed design wholly right for the brief and the moment, with fabric, colour, cut, condition and accessories all justified. 3: several precise details with clear links to context. 2: some detail and some relevance to Puritan Salem. 1: a vague design (for example "an old grey dress").

Indicative content: the same plain wool dress she wore at home, now filthy and torn at the hem after months in jail; the coif gone or greying, so her hair shows for the first time (a sign of how prison strips Puritan modesty); she is pregnant, so the bodice is let out and strained; no apron; bare or broken shoes; chains or iron wrist cuffs; make-up gives hollow cheeks and grime.
:::

::: test
You are designing a setting for Act Two. The setting must reflect the context of *The Crucible*, set in a Puritan community in the late 17th century. Describe your design ideas for the setting. (4)
---
Mark as one judgement, 1 mark per level (as above: precision and appropriateness).

Indicative content: a low-beamed farmhouse room of rough, dark timber with whitewashed walls; a wide stone hearth upstage with an iron pot; a long bare trestle table and two plain benches or chairs; a narrow window with small panes; a musket on pegs by the door; an upstage staircase to the children's room; a stoneware jug, wooden bowls and pewter; no ornament or pictures; floor of worn planks strewn with rushes.
:::

::: test
Explain one practical challenge of staging the girls' hysteria in Act Three, and how you would solve it. (3)
---
Challenge: many performers must react together without looking rehearsed or comic (1). Solution: a choral movement sequence rehearsed to a cue, for example all heads snapping up to a fixed point above the audience when Abigail looks up (1); stagger their screams so it builds rather than sounding like one voice, keeping the focus on Mary (1).
:::

::: test
You are performing the role of Abigail. In Act One, when Proctor tells her it was only an afternoon and it is over, Abigail refuses to accept it. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform her reply and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Levels. 7–8: exact vocal and physical choices pinned to words and moments, effects fully right for Abigail and the scene. 5–6: clear choices with several details, effects appropriate. 3–4: some detail, effects partly relevant. 1–2: vague ("say it sadly"). Voice only or body only cannot go above 3–4.

Indicative content: vocal - a low, intimate tone at first, then a harder, faster delivery as she insists; stress on words that recall their past; a trembling pitch that could be real hurt or a performance. Physical - she steps into his space, reaches for his arm or chest; when he moves away she follows; eye contact held; a flash of anger in a clenched jaw when he refuses. Effects: the audience sees she is dangerous and genuinely obsessed; tension because anyone could walk in; sets up her revenge on Elizabeth.
:::

::: test
You are performing the role of Mary Warren. In Act Three, when the girls turn on her and copy everything she says, she finally breaks and points at Proctor. Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform her accusation and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Levels as the 8-mark scheme above.

Indicative content: vocal - voice climbing to a shriek; words rushed and breathless, then a pause before Proctor's name; sobbing breaths. Physical - she backs away from the girls, stumbles, arm outstretched shaking towards Proctor, then runs to Abigail's side and clings to her. Effects: shows a weak girl choosing the safe side under pressure; shocks the audience with the reversal; shows the power of mass hysteria; horror because we know Proctor is innocent of witchcraft.
:::

::: test
You are performing the role of Proctor. Focus on the section of Act Two from Mary Warren's entrance to the moment Proctor threatens to whip her. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Mary Warren to show his anger. (12)
---
Levels. 10–12: a detailed, precise and practical plan of space and interaction throughout the section, every choice clearly linked to his anger. 7–9: clear ideas with good detail and mostly clear links. 4–6: some ideas with limited detail. 1–3: little detail or relevance.

Indicative content: Proctor seated at the table as she enters, rising slowly; crossing to block her path to the stairs; towering over her (she is smaller, she backs towards the hearth); reaching for the whip hanging by the door; her flinches and her new defiance when she says she is an official of the court; his hand raised, then stopped by Elizabeth; voice clipped and low, rising to a shout; the poppet held out between them as a barrier; use of the doorway and table to trap her.
:::

::: test
You are performing the role of Danforth. Focus on the part of Act Three where Mary is asked to faint and cannot. Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Mary to show his power. (12)
---
Levels as the 12-mark scheme above.

Indicative content: Danforth seated high and centre, rising only when it matters; slow circling walk around Mary; stillness while she struggles; lowered, patient voice that frightens more than a shout; a finger lifted to silence others; leaning in close to her face; turning his back on her to show she is dismissed; his calm contrasting with her panic.
:::

::: test
You are performing the role of Proctor. Describe how you would use your acting skills to interpret Proctor in Act Two, from his entrance to Mary Warren's arrival. Explain why your ideas are appropriate for this part of the play and the performance of your role in the play as a whole. (20)
---
Levels. 16–20: a coherent, detailed interpretation; a wide range of precise vocal and physical skills; clearly explained for the section and the whole play. 11–15: clear interpretation with good detail and some whole-play links. 6–10: some relevant ideas with limited precision or little whole-play reference. 1–5: basic, general ideas.

Indicative content: section - the tired farmer, rinsing his hands, tasting and salting the stew in secret (guilt and wanting to please); forced cheerfulness, bright tone; carefully planned compliments; flare of anger when the Salem subject rises; his body turns away when Elizabeth mentions Abigail. Whole play - his desire and control in Act One with Abigail; public confession in Act Three (voice breaking, falling to his knees); Act Four stillness and exhaustion; final tearing of the confession as his recovered dignity. Journey from guilt to self-forgiveness.
:::

You are a designer working on one aspect of design for Act Three. Describe how you would use your design skills to create effects which support the action. Explain why your ideas are appropriate for this act and your chosen design skill in the play as a whole. (20)
---
Levels as the 20-mark scheme above (range and precision of design ideas; how they support the action; the act and the whole play).

Indicative content (set): the vestry as a tall, bare room with high timber walls and a single door, a raised bench for the judges, a rail separating accused from court; Mary placed on a low stool; levels show power.
