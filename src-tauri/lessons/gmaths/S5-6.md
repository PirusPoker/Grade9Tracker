## What it is

This topic has two halves: using statistics to **describe a population** in words, and using **scatter graphs** to look for a relationship between two variables.

### Describing a population

Once you have data (usually from a sample), you describe the population with **an average** (what is typical) and **a measure of spread** (how much the values vary), then say what they mean in context.

Choose the statistic that fits the data:

| Situation | Best average | Best spread |
|---|---|---|
| Roughly symmetrical, no extreme values | mean | range or IQR |
| Skewed data or outliers (house prices, salaries) | median | IQR |
| Categorical data, or "most common" size | mode | — |

In a small firm, salaries (in £1000s) are 18, 19, 21, 22, 22, 24, 25 and 95. The mean is $\frac{246}{8} = 30.75$, i.e. £30 750 — higher than 7 of the 8 salaries, because the 95 drags it up. The median, £22 000, describes a typical worker better, and the IQR describes the spread of the typical salaries without the one extreme.

A good description: "The median salary is £22 000 and the IQR is small, so most workers earn a similar amount; one salary is far higher than the rest." Always link the number to its meaning.

Remember from sampling: a description built from a **sample** is an estimate of the population, and is only as good as the sample.

### Scatter graphs and bivariate data

**Bivariate data** comes in pairs: (hours of revision, test mark) for each student. Plot each pair as a point on a **scatter graph**.

**Correlation** describes the pattern:

- **Positive correlation:** as one variable increases, the other tends to increase (revision and marks).
- **Negative correlation:** as one increases, the other tends to decrease (age of a car and its value).
- **No correlation:** no linear pattern (shoe size and test mark).

Correlation can be **strong** (points close to a straight line) or **weak** (a rough trend with lots of scatter). Describe both the type and the meaning: "strong positive correlation — students who revised for longer tended to score higher".

### Correlation does not mean causation

Two variables can be correlated without one causing the other.

- Ice-cream sales and sunburn cases are positively correlated, but ice cream does not cause sunburn: both rise in **hot, sunny weather** (a hidden third variable).
- Some correlations are pure coincidence.

So write "there is a relationship" or "they are correlated", not "more ice cream causes sunburn". To show causation you need a reason or an experiment, not just a scatter graph.

### Lines of best fit

If there is correlation, draw a **line of best fit**: a single straight ruled line following the trend, with roughly equal numbers of points on each side. It does not have to pass through the origin or through any particular data point, but it should pass close to the **mean point** $(\bar x, \bar y)$ — find the mean of the $x$-values and of the $y$-values, plot that point, and draw the line through it following the trend.

| Revision (h) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mark | 28 | 35 | 36 | 45 | 49 | 52 | 60 | 63 |

Mean point $(4.5, 46)$. A line through it with gradient about 5 fits well: roughly $y = 5x + 23.5$.

**Making predictions:** read from the line. A student revising for 5.5 hours: about $5 \times 5.5 + 23.5 = 51$ marks.

**Interpreting the gradient and intercept** (grade 8–9): the gradient 5 means "each extra hour of revision is associated with about 5 more marks"; the intercept 23.5 would be the mark for zero revision — but that is outside the data (the smallest value is 1 hour), so treat it with caution.

### Interpolation and extrapolation

- **Interpolation** — predicting **inside** the range of the data (between 1 and 8 hours). Fairly reliable, provided the correlation is reasonably strong.
- **Extrapolation** — predicting **outside** the range (20 hours of revision). **Unreliable**: the pattern may not continue — the line would predict 123.5 marks for 20 hours, which is impossible on a test out of 100.

Predictions are also less reliable when the correlation is weak, and they apply only to the kind of population the data came from.

### Outliers on a scatter graph

A point far from the pattern of the others is an **outlier**. In context it might be an error (a mistyped value) or a genuine unusual case (a student who was ill on the day). Ignore an outlier when drawing the line of best fit, and be ready to suggest a reason for it.

::: terms
- **Bivariate data**: data in pairs, recording two variables for each item, such as height and mass.
- **Scatter graph**: a graph plotting each pair of bivariate data as a point to show any relationship.
- **Positive correlation**: as one variable increases the other tends to increase.
- **Negative correlation**: as one variable increases the other tends to decrease.
- **Causation**: one variable directly causing a change in the other — correlation alone does not show this.
- **Line of best fit**: a single ruled straight line following the trend of the points, passing near the mean point.
- **Interpolation**: predicting a value inside the range of the data — usually reliable if the correlation is strong.
- **Extrapolation**: predicting a value outside the range of the data — unreliable because the trend may not continue.
:::

::: method
1. **Describe correlation** with its type (positive / negative / none) **and** its meaning in context: "as temperature rises, heating cost tends to fall".
2. **Plot points** carefully using the scales — plotting marks are usually B1 each or B2 for all correct. Check awkward scales (each square may be 2 or 5 units).
3. **Line of best fit:** one straight ruled line, points balanced either side, through or near the mean point $(\bar x, \bar y)$. Ignore any outlier. Do not join the dots and do not force it through the origin.
4. **Predict:** draw a vertical (or horizontal) line from the given value to your line of best fit and across; show these lines — they are the method.
5. **Reliability:** inside the data range → interpolation, fairly reliable; outside → extrapolation, unreliable; weak correlation → unreliable.
6. **Causation questions:** say "correlation does not prove causation" **and** give a likely third factor or reason in context.
7. **Describing a population:** pick the average and spread that suit the data (median and IQR if there are outliers), quote the values and interpret them.
8. **Gradient in context** (calculator or non-calculator): "for every one more unit of $x$, $y$ changes by about [gradient] units".
:::

::: example Describing correlation and causation
A scatter graph shows the outside temperature and the daily cost of heating a house over 20 winter days. The points slope down from left to right, close to a line. (a) Describe the correlation. (b) Jo says the scatter graph proves cold weather causes higher heating costs. Comment.

(a) Strong negative correlation: on colder days the heating cost tended to be higher.

(b) The graph shows a relationship, not proof of cause. Here there is a sensible reason (people heat more when it is cold), but the scatter graph alone cannot prove it.
:::

::: example Line of best fit, prediction and reliability
The ages (years) and values (£1000s) of seven cars of the same model are: (1, 15), (2, 13), (3, 12), (4, 10), (5, 9), (6, 7), (7, 4). (a) Find the mean point. (b) Estimate the value of a 4.5-year-old car. (c) Why should the line not be used for a 12-year-old car?

(a) $\bar x = \frac{28}{7} = 4$, $\bar y = \frac{70}{7} = 10$: mean point $(4, 10)$.

(b) A line through $(4, 10)$ following the trend has gradient about $-1.7$, so at 4.5 years the value is about $10 - 0.85 \approx 9.2$, i.e. about £9000 (any sensible reading from the line).

(c) 12 years is outside the data (extrapolation); the line would give a negative value, which is impossible — the trend cannot continue.
:::

::: example An outlier
An eighth car in the same survey is 3 years old but worth only £4000. Explain how to treat it.

It is an outlier — far below the pattern (other 3-year-old cars are worth about £12 000). The car may have been damaged or have very high mileage. Ignore it when drawing the line of best fit.
:::

::: example Describing a population
A random sample of house prices in a village (£1000s) is: 210, 225, 240, 240, 255, 260, 270, 950. Which average best describes a typical house price? Describe the population.

The 950 is an extreme value that drags the mean up to £331 250, higher than 7 of the 8 prices. The median, $\frac{240 + 255}{2} = 247.5$, i.e. £247 500, is more typical.

Description: a typical house costs about £250 000; most prices are close together (from £210 000 to £270 000), with one very expensive house. This is an estimate for the whole village, based on a sample.
:::

::: example Grade 9: interpreting a line of best fit
For a group of adults, the line of best fit for height $h$ cm against arm span $a$ cm is $h = 0.9a + 17$. The arm spans in the data run from 150 cm to 195 cm. (a) Interpret the gradient. (b) Predict the height of an adult with arm span 170 cm. (c) Should the line be used for a child with arm span 90 cm?

(a) Each extra 1 cm of arm span is associated with about 0.9 cm more height.

(b) $h = 0.9 \times 170 + 17 = 153 + 17 = 170$ cm.

(c) No: 90 cm is well outside the data (extrapolation), and children are a different population from the adults the line was fitted to.
:::

::: q
Describe the correlation you would expect between the age of a child (up to 12 years old) and their height.
---
Positive correlation: older children tend to be taller.
:::

::: q
What type of correlation would you expect between hat size and hours of TV watched per week?
---
No correlation — there is no reason for the two to be related.
:::

::: q
Find the mean point of $(2, 5)$, $(4, 9)$, $(6, 10)$, $(8, 16)$.
---
$\bar x = \dfrac{20}{4} = 5$, $\bar y = \dfrac{40}{4} = 10$: $(5, 10)$.
:::

::: q
A line of best fit is $y = 3x + 12$ for data with $x$ from 2 to 10. Predict $y$ when $x = 7$, and say whether this is interpolation or extrapolation.
---
$3 \times 7 + 12 = 33$. Interpolation — 7 is inside the data range.
:::

::: q
The number of fire engines sent to a fire is positively correlated with the cost of the damage. Does sending more fire engines cause more damage?
---
No — correlation does not show causation. Bigger fires cause both more damage and more fire engines to be sent.
:::

::: q
A company's salaries include the director's pay, which is ten times anyone else's. Which average best describes a typical salary? Why?
---
The median — the director's pay is an extreme value that drags the mean up, but barely affects the median.
:::

::: q
On a scatter graph of height against mass for 15 adults, one point shows a height of 1.8 m and a mass of 18 kg. Suggest what has happened.
---
It is an outlier and almost certainly a recording error (perhaps 81 kg or 180 lb was intended); it should be checked, and ignored when drawing the line of best fit.
:::

::: q
The line of best fit for resting heart rate (beats per minute) against daily exercise (minutes) has gradient $-0.4$. Interpret this.
---
Each extra minute of daily exercise is associated with a resting heart rate about 0.4 beats per minute lower.
:::

::: q
Why should a line of best fit not be forced to go through the origin?
---
The trend need not pass through $(0, 0)$ — the line should follow the points, balanced either side, and $(0, 0)$ may be outside the data or meaningless.
:::

::: watch
- "Positive correlation" alone often gets only half the marks — add **what it means** in context ("the more hours revised, the higher the mark tended to be").
- **Correlation is not causation.** Saying "revision causes higher marks" from a scatter graph is not justified; and for a causation question, name the likely hidden factor.
- Lines of best fit: one **straight ruled** line, not dot-to-dot, not forced through the origin, and **ignore outliers**.
- Predictions outside the data are **extrapolation** — say why they are unreliable in context (impossible values, a different population, the trend may change).
- Show the reading lines on the graph when you predict; an answer with no lines and a slightly different value can lose the method mark.
- When describing a population, pick the median if there are extreme values, and quote the value with units.
:::

::: test
What type of correlation would you expect between daily temperature and the number of ice creams sold? (1)
---
Positive correlation (B1).
:::

::: test
A scatter graph shows the age of a car and the number of miles it has been driven. The points lie close to a line sloping up from left to right. Describe the relationship. (2)
---
Strong positive correlation (B1); older cars tend to have been driven more miles (B1).
:::

::: test
The data are $(10, 42)$, $(20, 38)$, $(30, 35)$, $(40, 29)$, $(50, 26)$. Work out the mean point, which a line of best fit should pass near. (2)
---
$\bar x = \dfrac{150}{5} = 30$ (B1); $\bar y = \dfrac{170}{5} = 34$ (B1). Mean point $(30, 34)$.
:::

::: test
For data with $x$ between 10 and 50, the line of best fit is $y = 46 - 0.4x$. (a) Estimate $y$ when $x = 25$. (b) Explain why the line should not be used to estimate $y$ when $x = 150$. (3)
---
(a) $46 - 0.4 \times 25$ (M1) $= 36$ (A1).

(b) 150 is well outside the data range (extrapolation); the trend may not continue — here the line would give $-14$ (B1).
:::

::: test
For children at a primary school, shoe size and reading score show strong positive correlation. A parent says buying bigger shoes will improve reading. Explain why the parent is wrong. (2)
---
Correlation does not show causation (B1). Both shoe size and reading score increase with age — older children have bigger feet and read better (B1).
:::

::: test
The times, in minutes, for eight runners to finish a race were: 12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 18, 19, 47. (a) Work out the mean and the median. (b) Which average better describes a typical time? Give a reason. (4)
---
(a) Mean $= \dfrac{156}{8} = 19.5$ minutes (M1 A1). Median $= \dfrac{15 + 16}{2} = 15.5$ minutes (B1).

(b) The median, because the 47 is an extreme value that pulls the mean up above 7 of the 8 times (B1).
:::

::: test
A line of best fit on a scatter graph passes through $(2, 60)$ and $(10, 20)$. The graph shows $x$, the number of absences, and $y$, a test mark. (a) Find the equation of the line. (b) Interpret the gradient in context. (c) Estimate the mark of a student with 6 absences. (4)
---
(a) Gradient $= \dfrac{20 - 60}{10 - 2} = -5$ (M1); $y = -5x + 70$ (A1).

(b) Each extra absence is associated with about 5 fewer marks (B1).

(c) $-5 \times 6 + 70 = 40$ (B1).
:::

::: test
For data collected over the first 8 weeks after planting, a line of best fit for the height $h$ cm of a plant against time $t$ weeks is $h = 2.5t + 4$. (a) Interpret the value 4 in context. (b) Priya uses the line to predict the height after 52 weeks. Work out her prediction and comment on its reliability. (3)
---
(a) The estimated height of the plant (4 cm) when it was planted, at $t = 0$ (B1).

(b) $2.5 \times 52 + 4 = 134$ cm (B1). Unreliable: 52 weeks is far outside the 8 weeks of data (extrapolation) and the plant's growth is likely to slow down or stop (B1).
:::

::: test
A researcher finds strong negative correlation between the number of hours per week people spend gardening and their blood pressure. (a) State what this suggests. (b) Explain why this does not prove that gardening lowers blood pressure, suggesting another factor that might explain it. (3)
---
(a) People who spend more time gardening tend to have lower blood pressure (B1).

(b) Correlation does not prove causation (B1); for example, people who garden a lot may be more active in general or less stressed, and that could lower their blood pressure (B1).
:::
