## What it is

A **conditional probability** is a probability worked out when you already know something has happened. "Given that the student chose art, what is the probability they are a girl?" The extra information **shrinks the set of outcomes** you are choosing from: you only look at the art students.

The shorthand is $P(A \mid B)$, read "the probability of $A$ **given** $B$". The exam usually writes it out in words — "given that…", "if the person chosen…", "one of the students who…" all signal a conditional probability.

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{\text{number in both } A \text{ and } B}{\text{number in } B}$$

The denominator is **the condition** — the group you already know you are in — not the whole total.

### From a two-way table

| | Art | Music | Drama | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boys | 12 | 18 | 10 | 40 |
| Girls | 20 | 14 | 26 | 60 |
| Total | 32 | 32 | 36 | 100 |

- $P(\text{girl}) = \dfrac{60}{100}$ — no condition, divide by the grand total.
- $P(\text{girl} \mid \text{art}) = \dfrac{20}{32} = \dfrac58$ — only the 32 art students count.
- $P(\text{art} \mid \text{girl}) = \dfrac{20}{60} = \dfrac13$ — only the 60 girls count.

$P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are usually **different**, because the denominators differ.

### From a Venn diagram

Use the numbers in the regions. If $n(A \cap B) = 9$, $n(A \text{ only}) = 19$ and $n(B \text{ only}) = 13$:
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{9}{9 + 13} = \frac{9}{22}, \qquad P(B \mid A) = \frac{9}{9 + 19} = \frac{9}{28}.$$
The same works with probabilities in the regions: divide the overlap by the total of the circle you are "given".

With probabilities, $P(A) = 0.5$, $P(B) = 0.3$, $P(A \cap B) = 0.1$ gives $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{0.1}{0.3} = \dfrac13$.

### From a tree diagram

The branches on the **second stage** of a tree are already conditional probabilities: on a without-replacement tree, $\frac47$ on the red–red branch is $P(\text{2nd red} \mid \text{1st red})$.

The harder questions run the tree **backwards**: you know the *result* and ask about the *first stage*. Then

$$P(\text{first was red} \mid \text{second was red}) = \frac{P(\text{red, red})}{P(\text{second red})} = \frac{\text{the path you want}}{\text{all paths that meet the condition}}.$$

Bag with 6 red and 4 blue, two taken without replacement:
$P(\text{RR}) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac59 = \frac{30}{90}$ and $P(\text{BR}) = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac69 = \frac{24}{90}$, so
$$P(\text{1st red} \mid \text{2nd red}) = \frac{30}{30 + 24} = \frac{30}{54} = \frac59.$$

### Expected frequencies — the safest method

Turn the probabilities into a **frequency tree** or two-way table for a convenient number of people (100, 1000, or a common multiple of the denominators). Then conditional probabilities are just "count in the group".

A disease affects 2% of people. A test is positive for 95% of people with the disease and for 10% of people without it. Take 1000 people:

- 20 have the disease → 19 test positive, 1 negative.
- 980 do not → 98 test positive, 882 negative.

$P(\text{has disease} \mid \text{positive}) = \dfrac{19}{19 + 98} = \dfrac{19}{117} \approx 0.162$. Most positive results are false positives — a classic grade 9 interpretation point: when a condition is rare, even a good test gives more false positives than true ones.

### Conditional probability and independence

If knowing $B$ does not change the chance of $A$, then $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ and the events are **independent**. This is the same as $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$. In the Venn example above, $P(A \mid B) = \frac13$ but $P(A) = 0.5$, so $A$ and $B$ are **not** independent: knowing $B$ happened makes $A$ less likely.

::: terms
- **Conditional probability**: the probability of an event given that another event is already known to have happened.
- **P(A | B)**: shorthand for the probability of A given B, equal to P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B).
- **Two-way table**: a table that splits a total by two categories at once, with row and column totals.
- **Expected frequency method**: turning probabilities into numbers of people out of a convenient total, so that conditional probabilities become simple counts.
- **False positive**: a positive test result for someone who does not have the condition being tested for.
- **Independent events**: events for which P(A | B) = P(A) — knowing B happened does not change the chance of A.
:::

::: method
1. **Spot the condition.** Find the words "given that", "if", "of those who…", "one of the…". The group after those words is your **denominator**.
2. **Two-way table:** complete every row and column total first. Then read: count in both $\div$ count in the given group.
3. **Venn diagram:** overlap $\div$ total of the whole circle you are given (both its regions), never the grand total.
4. **Tree diagram, backwards question** ("given the second was red, find the probability the first was red"): list every path that satisfies the condition, add them for the denominator, and put the wanted path on top.
5. **Percentages or a rare condition:** build a frequency tree for 1000 (or 100) people. It is quicker, easier to check and earns method marks for each correct frequency.
6. **Independence:** compare $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(A)$, or $P(A \cap B)$ with $P(A) \times P(B)$, and state the conclusion in words.
7. **Answer format:** fractions are fine on Paper 1 and need not be simplified unless asked; on Papers 2 and 3 give 3 s.f. if the decimal does not end. Interpret the answer in context when the question asks you to "comment".
:::

::: example Completing a two-way table
There are 90 students in Year 10; 50 are girls. 48 students have a pet, and 28 of the girls have a pet. (a) A student who has a pet is chosen at random. Find the probability that the student is a girl. (b) Find the probability that a boy chosen at random has a pet.

| | Pet | No pet | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Girls | 28 | 22 | 50 |
| Boys | 20 | 20 | 40 |
| Total | 48 | 42 | 90 |

(a) $P(\text{girl} \mid \text{pet}) = \dfrac{28}{48} = \dfrac{7}{12}$. (b) $P(\text{pet} \mid \text{boy}) = \dfrac{20}{40} = \dfrac12$.
:::

::: example From a Venn diagram
Of 60 people, 24 swim only, 18 cycle only, 6 do both and 12 do neither. (a) Given that a person cycles, find the probability that they swim. (b) Given that a person does not swim, find the probability that they cycle.

(a) Cyclists: $18 + 6 = 24$. $P(\text{swims} \mid \text{cycles}) = \dfrac{6}{24} = \dfrac14$.

(b) Non-swimmers: $18 + 12 = 30$. $P(\text{cycles} \mid \text{doesn't swim}) = \dfrac{18}{30} = \dfrac35$.
:::

::: example A tree read backwards
Priya walks to school with probability $0.4$, otherwise she cycles. If she walks, $P(\text{late}) = 0.3$; if she cycles, $P(\text{late}) = 0.1$. Given that Priya is late, find the probability that she walked.

$P(\text{walk and late}) = 0.4 \times 0.3 = 0.12$. $P(\text{cycle and late}) = 0.6 \times 0.1 = 0.06$.

$P(\text{walked} \mid \text{late}) = \dfrac{0.12}{0.12 + 0.06} = \dfrac{0.12}{0.18} = \dfrac23$.
:::

::: example Expected frequencies with percentages
Machine A makes 60% of a factory's phones and machine B makes the rest. 3% of A's phones and 5% of B's phones are faulty. A faulty phone is picked at random. Find the probability it was made by machine B.

Out of 1000 phones: A makes 600, of which $0.03 \times 600 = 18$ are faulty; B makes 400, of which $0.05 \times 400 = 20$ are faulty.

$P(\text{B} \mid \text{faulty}) = \dfrac{20}{18 + 20} = \dfrac{20}{38} = \dfrac{10}{19} \approx 0.526$.
:::

::: example Grade 9: working with P(A | B)
For two events, $P(A) = 0.6$, $P(B \mid A) = 0.5$ and $P(B) = 0.45$. (a) Find $P(A \mid B)$. (b) Find the probability that neither $A$ nor $B$ happens. (c) Are $A$ and $B$ independent?

(a) $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A) = 0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.3$. $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{0.3}{0.45} = \dfrac23$.

(b) Venn regions: $A$ only $0.3$, both $0.3$, $B$ only $0.15$. Neither $= 1 - 0.75 = 0.25$.

(c) $P(B \mid A) = 0.5 \ne P(B) = 0.45$, so they are not independent.
:::

::: q
Use the Art / Music / Drama table in the notes. A drama student is chosen at random. Find the probability that the student is a boy.
---
Drama total 36, boys 10: $\dfrac{10}{36} = \dfrac{5}{18}$.

::: q
$P(A \cap B) = 0.12$ and $P(B) = 0.4$. Find $P(A \mid B)$.
---
$\dfrac{0.12}{0.4} = 0.3$.
:::

::: q
In a Venn diagram of 50 people, 19 are in $A$ only, 9 in both, 13 in $B$ only and 9 in neither. Given that a person is not in $A$, find the probability they are in $B$.
---
Not in $A$: $13 + 9 = 22$. $P = \dfrac{13}{22}$.
:::

::: q
A bag has 6 red and 4 blue counters. Two are taken without replacement. Given that they are the same colour, find the probability that both are red.
---
$P(\text{RR}) = \dfrac{6}{10} \times \dfrac59 = \dfrac{30}{90}$, $P(\text{BB}) = \dfrac{4}{10} \times \dfrac39 = \dfrac{12}{90}$. $P = \dfrac{30}{42} = \dfrac57$.
:::

::: q
Two fair dice are rolled. Given that at least one shows a six, find the probability that both show a six.
---
At least one six: 11 outcomes; only $(6,6)$ is a double six. $P = \dfrac{1}{11}$.
:::

::: q
Two fair dice are rolled. Given that the total is 8, find the probability that one of the dice shows a 3.
---
Total 8: $(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)$. Two contain a 3: $\dfrac25$.
:::

::: q
1% of people have a condition. A test is positive for 90% of people with the condition and for 5% of people without it. A person tests positive. Find the probability they have the condition.
---
Out of 10 000: 100 have it, 90 positive; 9900 do not, 495 positive. $P = \dfrac{90}{585} = \dfrac{2}{13} \approx 0.154$.
:::

::: q
$P(A) = 0.4$, $P(B) = 0.5$ and $P(A \mid B) = 0.4$. Are $A$ and $B$ independent?
---
Yes: $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$, so knowing $B$ does not change the probability of $A$.
:::

::: q
$P(A) = 0.7$, $P(B \mid A) = 0.2$ and $P(B \mid A') = 0.6$. Find $P(B)$ and $P(A \mid B)$.
---
$P(B) = 0.7 \times 0.2 + 0.3 \times 0.6 = 0.14 + 0.18 = 0.32$. $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{0.14}{0.32} = \dfrac{7}{16} = 0.4375$.
:::

::: watch
- **Wrong denominator** is the big one. "Given that the student is a girl" means divide by the number of **girls**, not by the grand total.
- $P(A \mid B)$ is not $P(B \mid A)$: "girls who chose art" out of art students is different from out of girls. Read which group is known.
- In a Venn diagram the "given" group is a **whole circle** (both of its regions) or the whole outside — add the regions before you divide.
- Backwards tree questions: the denominator is **every path** that meets the condition, not just one branch.
- With percentages, people multiply the percentages and stop. Build a frequency tree for 1000 people and count.
- Stating "not independent" without a numerical comparison scores nothing — show $P(A \mid B) \ne P(A)$ or $P(A \cap B) \ne P(A)P(B)$.
:::

::: test
The table shows how 150 students travel to school.

| | Walk | Bus | Car | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 10 | 25 | 30 | 15 | 70 |
| Year 11 | 20 | 40 | 20 | 80 |
| Total | 45 | 70 | 35 | 150 |

A student who travels by bus is chosen at random. Find the probability that the student is in Year 11. (2)
---
Bus total 70 (M1). $P = \dfrac{40}{70} = \dfrac47$ (A1).
:::

::: test
200 people chose between film A and film B. 120 of the people were adults. 45 children chose film A. In total 110 people chose film A. A person who chose film B is picked at random. Find the probability that this person is an adult. (3)
---
Adults choosing A: $110 - 45 = 65$; adults choosing B: $120 - 65 = 55$ (M1). Total choosing B: $200 - 110 = 90$ (M1). $P = \dfrac{55}{90} = \dfrac{11}{18}$ (A1).
:::

::: test
In a group of 40 people, 22 are in set $A$, 16 are in set $B$ and 6 are in both. (a) Find $P(A \mid B)$. (b) Given that a person is not in $A$, find the probability they are in $B$. (3)
---
(a) $\dfrac{6}{16} = \dfrac38$ (B1).

(b) $B$ only $= 10$; neither $= 40 - (16 + 6 + 10) = 8$, so 18 are not in $A$ (M1). $P = \dfrac{10}{18} = \dfrac59$ (A1).
:::

::: test
$P(A) = 0.5$, $P(B) = 0.4$ and $P(A \cup B) = 0.7$. (a) Find $P(A \cap B)$. (b) Find $P(A \mid B)$. (c) Are $A$ and $B$ independent? Give a reason. (4)
---
(a) $0.5 + 0.4 - 0.7 = 0.2$ (B1).

(b) $\dfrac{0.2}{0.4}$ (M1) $= 0.5$ (A1).

(c) Yes, because $P(A \mid B) = 0.5 = P(A)$ (B1).
:::

::: test
Two fair dice are rolled. Given that at least one of the dice shows a 5, find the probability that the total is at least 10. (3)
---
Outcomes with at least one 5: 11 (M1). Of these, $(5,5), (5,6), (6,5)$ have total at least 10 (M1). $P = \dfrac{3}{11}$ (A1).
:::

::: test
A bag contains 7 red and 3 blue counters. Two counters are taken at random without replacement. Given that at least one of the counters is blue, find the probability that both are blue. (4)
---
$P(\text{BB}) = \dfrac{3}{10} \times \dfrac29 = \dfrac{6}{90}$ (M1). $P(\text{no blue}) = \dfrac{7}{10} \times \dfrac69 = \dfrac{42}{90}$, so $P(\text{at least one blue}) = \dfrac{48}{90}$ (M1).

$P = \dfrac{6}{90} \div \dfrac{48}{90}$ (M1) $= \dfrac{6}{48} = \dfrac18$ (A1).
:::

::: test
30% of the members of a club are juniors. 80% of the juniors and 45% of the seniors went to the annual dinner. A member who went to the dinner is chosen at random. Work out the probability that this member is a junior. (4)
---
Out of 1000 members: 300 juniors, 240 went (M1); 700 seniors, 315 went (M1). Total at dinner 555 (M1). $P = \dfrac{240}{555} = \dfrac{16}{37} \approx 0.432$ (A1).
:::

::: test
0.5% of a population has a condition. A screening test is positive for 98% of people with the condition and for 3% of people without it. (a) A person tests positive. Work out the probability that they have the condition. (b) Comment on your answer. (5)
---
(a) Out of 100 000: 500 have it, $0.98 \times 500 = 490$ positive (M1); 99 500 do not, $0.03 \times 99\,500 = 2985$ positive (M1). Total positive 3475 (M1). $P = \dfrac{490}{3475} \approx 0.141$ (A1).

(b) Only about 14% of people who test positive actually have the condition — most positives are false because the condition is so rare, so a positive result should be followed by a further test (B1).
:::

::: test
For events $A$ and $B$: $P(B \mid A) = 0.4$, $P(B \mid A') = 0.25$ and $P(B) = 0.31$. (a) Find $P(A)$. (b) Find $P(A \mid B)$, giving your answer to 3 significant figures. (5)
---
(a) Let $P(A) = p$: $0.4p + 0.25(1 - p) = 0.31$ (M1). $0.15p = 0.06$ (M1), $p = 0.4$ (A1).

(b) $P(A \cap B) = 0.4 \times 0.4 = 0.16$ (M1). $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{0.16}{0.31} = 0.516$ (A1).
:::
