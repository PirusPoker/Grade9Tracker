## What it is

Probability measures how likely an event is, on the **0–1 probability scale**: 0 is impossible, 1 is certain, $\frac12$ is an even chance. Write a probability as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage — never as a ratio or "1 in 4". The words that go with the scale are *impossible, unlikely, even chance, likely, certain*.

### Theoretical probability and fairness

When all outcomes are **equally likely** (a fair dice, a fair coin, a counter picked **at random** from a bag):
$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in the event}}{\text{total number of equally likely outcomes}}$$

"Fair" and "at random" are what make the outcomes equally likely. A **biased** dice or spinner has outcomes that are not equally likely, so its probabilities can only be *estimated* from an experiment.

A bag holds 3 red, 5 blue and 2 green counters. $P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{5}{10} = \dfrac12$. If the colours are given as a ratio, say red : blue $= 2 : 7$, then $P(\text{red}) = \dfrac{2}{9}$ — add the parts to get the total.

### Probabilities that add to 1

Events are **mutually exclusive** if they cannot happen at the same time (rolling a 2 and rolling a 5). A set of outcomes is **exhaustive** if it covers everything that can happen. For an exhaustive set of mutually exclusive outcomes the probabilities **sum to 1**. Two consequences:

- **Complement:** $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$. If $P(\text{late}) = 0.18$, then $P(\text{not late}) = 0.82$.
- **Missing values in a table:** if a spinner has $P(1) = 0.2$, $P(2) = 0.35$, $P(3) = x$, $P(4) = 2x$, then $0.55 + 3x = 1$, so $x = 0.15$ and $P(4) = 0.3$.

For mutually exclusive events you may **add**: $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$. Rolling "even" and rolling "more than 3" are **not** mutually exclusive (4 and 6 are in both), so you cannot just add them.

### Relative frequency — probability from an experiment

If you cannot work out a probability by symmetry, do the experiment:
$$\text{relative frequency} = \frac{\text{number of times the event happened}}{\text{number of trials}}$$

A drawing pin lands point-up 62 times in 200 throws: estimated $P(\text{point up}) = \dfrac{62}{200} = 0.31$.

Record the results in a **frequency table** (tally, then frequency), and turn each frequency into a relative frequency. The relative frequencies of all the outcomes add to 1, just like probabilities.

### Bigger samples are better

Results from a small number of trials jump about. As the number of trials increases, the relative frequency **settles down** towards the theoretical probability (if the experiment is fair and unbiased). So:

- the estimate from the **most trials** is the most reliable;
- **combine** everyone's results (add the successes, add the trials) to get the best estimate;
- a relative frequency far from the expected value *after many trials* is evidence of **bias**; after only 10 trials it proves nothing.

A fair coin flipped 10 times giving 7 heads is not surprising. Flipped 1000 times giving 700 heads is strong evidence the coin is biased.

### Expected outcomes

In $n$ future trials you **expect** the event about
$$\text{expected frequency} = n \times P(\text{event})$$
times. A spinner with $P(\text{red}) = 0.35$ spun 240 times: expect $240 \times 0.35 = 84$ reds. It is an average — you would not be surprised by 80 or 90 — and it need not be a whole number.

Expected frequency drives questions about games and fairness. If a game costs 50p a go and you win £2 with probability $\frac15$, then over 100 games the organiser takes £50 and expects to pay out $20 \times £2 = £40$: an expected profit of £10. A game is **fair** when the expected winnings equal the cost of playing.

### Frequency trees

A **frequency tree** records how a group splits in two stages. Start with the total, split it, then split each branch; the numbers at each split add up to the number they came from.

120 people took a driving test; 70 had lessons with an instructor, 50 did not. 56 of those with an instructor passed and 20 of those without passed.

- With instructor: 56 pass, 14 fail. Without: 20 pass, 30 fail.
- Total passing $= 56 + 20 = 76$, so $P(\text{a person picked at random passed}) = \dfrac{76}{120} = \dfrac{19}{30}$.

Frequency trees are the starting point for tree diagrams and conditional probability later in the course.

::: terms
- **Probability scale**: the scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) on which every probability lies.
- **Equally likely outcomes**: outcomes with the same chance of happening, as with a fair dice or a random choice.
- **Mutually exclusive events**: events that cannot happen at the same time, so P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).
- **Exhaustive set**: a set of outcomes that covers everything that can happen; mutually exclusive and exhaustive probabilities sum to 1.
- **Relative frequency**: the number of times an event happened divided by the number of trials — an estimate of probability.
- **Biased**: not fair — the outcomes are not equally likely.
- **Expected frequency**: the number of times you expect an event in n trials, found as n × P(event).
- **Frequency tree**: a diagram that splits a total into groups in stages, with the numbers at each split adding to the number above.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the setting.** "Fair", "at random" → use equally likely outcomes. "Biased" or "an experiment" → use relative frequency $= \dfrac{\text{frequency}}{\text{trials}}$.
2. **Missing probability in a table:** write the equation "all probabilities $= 1$". If two unknowns are linked ($x$ and $2x$), collect them: $0.55 + 3x = 1$. Show this equation — it earns the method mark even if the arithmetic slips.
3. **Complement:** "not $A$" is $1 - P(A)$.
4. **Expected frequency:** $n \times P$. For a relative-frequency estimate, use the estimate as $P$.
5. **"Which estimate is most reliable?"** → the one from the most trials. **"Best estimate"** → pool all the results: total successes $\div$ total trials.
6. **"Is it biased?"** → compare the relative frequency with the theoretical probability and **mention the number of trials** in your reason. Do not say "it is definitely biased".
7. **Games and profit:** work out the takings (cost $\times$ plays) and the expected payout (expected number of each prize $\times$ prize value), then subtract.
8. **Non-calculator (Paper 1):** keep probabilities as fractions or use decimals you can add by hand; $0.35 \times 240$ is $\dfrac{35 \times 240}{100}$. Give fractions in simplest form unless the question says otherwise.
:::

::: example A probability table with linked unknowns
A biased four-sided spinner is labelled A, B, C, D. $P(A) = 0.1$, $P(B) = 0.26$, and $P(D)$ is three times $P(C)$. The spinner is spun 150 times. How many times would you expect it to land on D?

$0.1 + 0.26 + x + 3x = 1 \Rightarrow 4x = 0.64 \Rightarrow x = 0.16$.

$P(D) = 3 \times 0.16 = 0.48$. Expected number of Ds $= 150 \times 0.48 = 72$.
:::

::: example Frequency tree to probability
80 students sat a mock. 35 of them revised with flashcards. 28 of the flashcard students passed. 25 of the others failed. Complete a frequency tree and find the probability that a student chosen at random passed.

Flashcards 35: pass 28, fail 7. No flashcards $80 - 35 = 45$: fail 25, pass $45 - 25 = 20$.

Total passed $= 28 + 20 = 48$: $P(\text{passed}) = \dfrac{48}{80} = \dfrac35$.
:::

::: example Relative frequency and bias
Four students each rolled the same dice and counted sixes.

| Student | Rolls | Sixes |
|---|---|---|
| Amir | 20 | 6 |
| Beth | 60 | 17 |
| Cara | 100 | 29 |
| Dan | 120 | 38 |

(a) Whose results give the most reliable estimate? (b) Give the best estimate of $P(\text{six})$. (c) Is the dice fair?

(a) Dan's — he did the most rolls.

(b) Pool all the results: $\dfrac{6 + 17 + 29 + 38}{20 + 60 + 100 + 120} = \dfrac{90}{300} = 0.3$.

(c) A fair dice gives $P(\text{six}) = \dfrac16 \approx 0.167$. After 300 rolls the estimate is $0.3$, nearly twice that, so the dice appears biased towards six.
:::

::: example Expected profit from a game
At a fair, a game costs £1 a go. The probability of winning £5 is $0.08$, the probability of winning £2 is $0.2$, otherwise you win nothing. The game is played 500 times. Work out the expected profit for the stall holder.

Takings: $500 \times £1 = £500$.

Expected £5 wins: $500 \times 0.08 = 40$, paying £200. Expected £2 wins: $500 \times 0.2 = 100$, paying £200.

Expected profit $= £500 - £400 = £100$.
:::

::: example Grade 9: an algebraic bag
A bag contains only red and blue counters. The probability of picking red is $\frac25$. Four more red counters are added and now the probability of red is $\frac12$. How many counters were in the bag at the start?

Let there be $n$ counters, so $\frac{2n}{5}$ are red.

After adding: $\dfrac{\frac{2n}{5} + 4}{n + 4} = \dfrac12 \Rightarrow \dfrac{4n}{5} + 8 = n + 4 \Rightarrow 4 = \dfrac{n}{5} \Rightarrow n = 20$.

Check: 8 red of 20; then 12 red of 24, which is $\frac12$. ✓
:::

::: q
A biased spinner has $P(A) = 0.15$, $P(B) = 0.4$, and $P(C) = P(D)$. Find $P(D)$.
---
$0.55 + 2x = 1 \Rightarrow 2x = 0.45 \Rightarrow P(D) = 0.225$.
:::

::: q
The probability that a spinner lands on red is $0.3$. It is spun 250 times. How many reds do you expect?
---
$250 \times 0.3 = 75$.
:::

::: q
A bag contains red, blue and green beads in the ratio $2 : 3 : 5$. A bead is taken at random. Find $P(\text{blue})$ and $P(\text{not green})$.
---
$2 + 3 + 5 = 10$ parts. $P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{3}{10}$; $P(\text{not green}) = \dfrac{5}{10} = \dfrac12$.
:::

::: q
80 students: 35 are boys, and 20 of the boys walk to school. 28 of the girls walk to school. Find the probability that a student chosen at random is a girl who does not walk.
---
Girls $= 45$; girls who walk $= 28$, so $17$ do not. $P = \dfrac{17}{80}$.
:::

::: q
A drawing pin is thrown 50 times and lands point-up 37 times. Estimate the probability of point-up, and the number of point-ups in 400 throws.
---
$\dfrac{37}{50} = 0.74$; $400 \times 0.74 = 296$.
:::

::: q
Ali flips a coin 20 times and gets 8 heads. Ben flips the same coin 200 times and gets 110 heads. Whose estimate of $P(\text{head})$ is more reliable, and what is the best estimate using both sets of results?
---
Ben's — more trials. Pooled: $\dfrac{8 + 110}{220} = \dfrac{118}{220} = \dfrac{59}{110} \approx 0.536$.
:::

::: q
Are these pairs of events mutually exclusive when one fair dice is rolled? (a) "even" and "a 3"; (b) "even" and "greater than 3".
---
(a) Yes — 3 is not even. (b) No — 4 and 6 are in both events.
:::

::: q
A game costs £1. $P(\text{win £5}) = 0.1$, $P(\text{win £2}) = 0.15$, otherwise nothing. Find the expected profit for the organiser over 200 games.
---
Takings £200. Payouts: $20 \times £5 + 30 \times £2 = £160$. Expected profit £40.
:::

::: q
Explain why a relative frequency from 1000 trials is a better estimate of a probability than one from 10 trials.
---
As the number of trials increases, the relative frequency settles down towards the true probability; a small number of trials can give results a long way from it by chance.
:::

::: watch
- A probability must be between 0 and 1 and written as a **fraction, decimal or percentage**. "3 out of 10" in words, "3 : 10" or "3 in 10" lose the mark.
- In a probability table, **every** probability goes into the "sum to 1" equation — including the ones you were given.
- $P(D) = 3x$: after finding $x$, **multiply by 3** before you use it. Stopping at $x$ is the commonest slip.
- "Best estimate" means **pool the results** — add successes and add trials. Averaging the separate relative frequencies is wrong when the numbers of trials differ.
- "Is it biased?" needs a comparison with the fair probability **and** a comment on the number of trials. "Yes because it's not exactly $\frac16$" scores nothing.
- Expected frequency is $n \times P$ — do not round to a whole number unless asked, and do not give a probability as the answer.
:::

The table shows probabilities for a biased spinner.

| Colour | Red | Blue | Green | Yellow |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Probability | 0.25 | 0.3 | 0.1 | |

(a) Find $P(\text{yellow})$. (b) The spinner is spun 60 times. Work out an estimate for the number of times it lands on yellow. (3)

(a) $1 - (0.25 + 0.3 + 0.1)$ (M1) $= 0.35$ (A1).

::: test
200 people were asked whether they had been abroad this year. 85 were under 30, and 51 of these had been abroad. 64 of the people aged 30 or over had not been abroad. A person is chosen at random. Find the probability that they had been abroad. (3)
---
Aged 30 or over: $200 - 85 = 115$, of whom $115 - 64 = 51$ had been abroad (M1).

Abroad: $51 + 51 = 102$ (M1). $P = \dfrac{102}{200} = \dfrac{51}{100}$ (A1).
:::

::: test
A coin is flipped 200 times and lands on heads 124 times. (a) Work out the relative frequency of heads. (b) Does the coin appear to be fair? Give a reason. (2)
---
(a) $\dfrac{124}{200} = 0.62$ (B1).

(b) Probably not fair: a fair coin gives about $0.5$, and $0.62$ after 200 flips (a large number of trials) is well above that (B1).
:::

::: test
A biased dice has $P(6) = 0.4$. The other five numbers are equally likely. (a) Find $P(3)$. (b) The dice is rolled 250 times. Work out an estimate for the number of odd scores. (4)
---
(a) $5x = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$ (M1), $x = 0.12$ (A1).

(b) $P(\text{odd}) = 3 \times 0.12 = 0.36$ (M1); $250 \times 0.36 = 90$ (A1).
:::

::: test
Sam carried out an experiment 50 times and the event happened 18 times. Jo carried out the same experiment 150 times and the event happened 60 times. (a) Whose results give the better estimate of the probability? Give a reason. (b) Work out the best estimate of the probability using all the results. (3)
---
(a) Jo's — more trials (B1).

(b) $\dfrac{18 + 60}{50 + 150}$ (M1) $= \dfrac{78}{200} = 0.39$ (A1).
:::

::: test
A game costs 50p. The probability of winning £3 is $\frac18$, otherwise the player wins nothing. (a) The game is played 400 times. Work out the expected profit for the organiser. (b) The organiser wants the game to be fair, keeping the cost at 50p and the probability at $\frac18$. What should the prize be? (4)
---
(a) Takings $400 \times 50\text{p} = £200$; expected wins $400 \times \frac18 = 50$ (M1); payout $50 \times £3 = £150$; profit £50 (A1).

(b) Fair means expected payout equals cost: $\frac18 \times \text{prize} = 50\text{p}$ (M1), prize $= £4$ (A1).
:::

::: test
A bag contains only red and blue counters in the ratio red : blue $= 3 : 5$. Six red counters are added to the bag. The probability of taking a red counter is now $\frac12$. How many counters were in the bag at the start? (4)
---
Red $3k$, blue $5k$ (M1). After adding: $3k + 6 = 5k$ because red and blue are now equal (M1). $k = 3$ (A1). Start: $8k = 24$ counters (A1).
:::

::: test
A bag contains red, blue and green counters only. $P(\text{red}) = 0.35$. There are twice as many blue counters as green counters, and there are 21 red counters. How many green counters are in the bag? (4)
---
Total $= 21 \div 0.35 = 60$ (M1). Blue + green $= 60 - 21 = 39$ (M1). $g + 2g = 39$ (M1), so $g = 13$ green (A1).
:::

::: test
A biased coin is flipped. The probability of a head is $p$. When it is flipped 500 times, the expected number of tails is 120 more than the expected number of heads. Find $p$. (3)
---
Expected heads $500p$, tails $500(1 - p)$ (M1). $500 - 500p - 500p = 120$ (M1), so $1000p = 380$ and $p = 0.38$ (A1).
:::
