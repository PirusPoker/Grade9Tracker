## What it is

A **sequence** is an ordered list of numbers called **terms**. You need to generate terms from a rule, recognise the standard types of sequence, and find a formula for the $n$th term of linear and quadratic sequences.

### Two kinds of rule

A **term-to-term rule** says how to get from one term to the next. "Start at 5, double and subtract 3" gives $5, 7, 11, 19, 35, \ldots$ — written $u_{n+1} = 2u_n - 3$, $u_1 = 5$. To reach the 50th term you must work out all 49 before it.

A **position-to-term rule** (the **$n$th term**) gives any term directly from its position $n$. For $n^2 - 3n$: $n = 1$ gives $-2$, $n = 2$ gives $-2$, $n = 3$ gives $0$, $n = 4$ gives $4$, and the 20th term is $400 - 60 = 340$.

### Special sequences to recognise

| Sequence | Terms | $n$th term |
|---|---|---|
| Square numbers | $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, \ldots$ | $n^2$ |
| Cube numbers | $1, 8, 27, 64, 125, \ldots$ | $n^3$ |
| Triangular numbers | $1, 3, 6, 10, 15, \ldots$ | $\frac12 n(n + 1)$ |
| Arithmetic (linear) | $5, 8, 11, 14, \ldots$ (add 3) | $3n + 2$ |
| Geometric | $3, 6, 12, 24, \ldots$ (multiply by 2) | $3 \times 2^{n-1}$ |
| Fibonacci | $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \ldots$ | each term is the sum of the previous two |

Triangular numbers are the dots in triangles of rows $1, 2, 3, \ldots$; their differences go up by 1 each time. Adding two consecutive triangular numbers gives a square number: $6 + 10 = 16$.

### Arithmetic progressions: the $n$th term of a linear sequence

An **arithmetic progression** has a **common difference** $d$. Its $n$th term is $dn + c$: the difference goes in front of $n$, then adjust with the **zero term** (the term before the first).

$5, 8, 11, 14, \ldots$: difference 3, so $3n$; $3n$ gives $3, 6, 9, \ldots$, which is 2 too small. $n$th term $3n + 2$.

For a decreasing sequence $20, 16, 12, 8$: difference $-4$, zero term $24$, so $24 - 4n$.

Is 100 a term of $3n + 2$? $3n + 2 = 100 \Rightarrow n = 32.67\ldots$ — not a whole number, so **no**.

### Geometric progressions

A **geometric progression** multiplies by a **common ratio** $r$ each time. Find $r$ by dividing a term by the one before. At GCSE, $r$ can be a whole number, a fraction or a surd:

- $81, 54, 36, 24, \ldots$: $r = \frac{54}{81} = \frac23$; the next term is $24 \times \frac23 = 16$.
- $\sqrt2, 2, 2\sqrt2, 4, \ldots$: $r = \frac{2}{\sqrt2} = \sqrt2$; the next term is $4\sqrt2$, and the $n$th term is $(\sqrt2)^n$.

The $n$th term of a geometric sequence with first term $a$ is $ar^{n-1}$.

### Fibonacci-type sequences

Any sequence where **each term is the sum of the two before** it. Starting $a, b$: the terms are $a,\ b,\ a + b,\ a + 2b,\ 2a + 3b,\ 3a + 5b, \ldots$ Questions give two terms and ask for the start, which means forming and solving simultaneous equations.

### Quadratic sequences

In a **quadratic sequence** the first differences change, but the **second differences** are constant. The $n$th term is $an^2 + bn + c$ where $a$ is **half the second difference**.

$4, 11, 22, 37, 56$:

- first differences $7, 11, 15, 19$; second differences $4, 4, 4$, so $a = 2$: start with $2n^2$.
- $2n^2$ gives $2, 8, 18, 32, 50$. Subtract from the sequence: $2, 3, 4, 5, 6$.
- The remainder is linear: $n + 1$.

$n$th term $2n^2 + n + 1$. Check $n = 3$: $18 + 3 + 1 = 22$ ✓.

### Other sequences

Sequences can be built from any rule — fractions like $\frac{n}{n + 1}$ ($\frac12, \frac23, \frac34, \ldots$), alternating signs, or combinations. Look at numerators and denominators separately, and compare with $n^2$, $n^3$ and $2^n$.

::: terms
- **Term-to-term rule**: a rule that gives each term from the one before, such as "add 4" or uₙ₊₁ = 2uₙ − 3.
- **nth term**: a position-to-term formula that gives any term directly from its position n.
- **Arithmetic progression**: a linear sequence with a common difference, so its nth term is dn + c.
- **Geometric progression**: a sequence that multiplies by the same common ratio r each time; nth term arⁿ⁻¹.
- **Fibonacci-type sequence**: a sequence in which each term is the sum of the two terms before it.
- **Quadratic sequence**: a sequence whose second differences are constant; its nth term is an² + bn + c, with a half the second difference.
- **Triangular numbers**: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, …, with nth term ½n(n + 1).
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the type first.** Work out first differences. Constant → linear. First differences changing but second differences constant → quadratic. A constant ratio (divide each term by the one before) → geometric. Each term the sum of the previous two → Fibonacci-type.
2. **Linear $n$th term:** difference $\times n$, then add or subtract to fix the first term. Check with the second term (M1 for $dn$, A1 for the full expression).
3. **Quadratic $n$th term:** halve the second difference to get $a$ (M1). Subtract $an^2$ from each term (M1). Find the linear $n$th term of what is left (M1). Write $an^2 + bn + c$ and check one term (A1). This is a common grade 7–8 question on the non-calculator paper.
4. **"Is $x$ a term?"** Set the $n$th term equal to $x$ and solve. A whole-number $n$ means yes; otherwise no — and you must *say* why ("$n$ is not an integer"). For quadratics, showing the value falls between two consecutive terms also works.
5. **Geometric:** find $r$ by dividing; keep surds exact ($2\sqrt3 \times \sqrt3 = 6$). Fractions stay as fractions.
6. **Fibonacci-type with unknowns:** write the terms in $a$ and $b$, form two equations from the given terms, solve simultaneously.
7. **Explain/show questions:** "Explain why 60 is not in the sequence $4n + 3$" — all terms are odd ($4n$ is even, add 3), and 60 is even. Give a reason, not just a calculation.
:::

::: example Linear nth term, and testing a value
The sequence is $7, 13, 19, 25, \ldots$ Find the $n$th term and decide whether 150 is a term.

Difference 6, so $6n$; $6n$ gives $6, 12, 18$, one less than the sequence. $n$th term $6n + 1$.

$6n + 1 = 150 \Rightarrow n = \dfrac{149}{6} = 24.83\ldots$, not a whole number, so 150 is **not** a term.
:::

::: example Quadratic nth term
Find the $n$th term of $3, 8, 15, 24, 35, \ldots$

First differences $5, 7, 9, 11$; second differences $2$, so $a = 1$.

Sequence $- n^2$: $3 - 1, 8 - 4, 15 - 9, 24 - 16, 35 - 25 = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10$, which is $2n$.

$n$th term $n^2 + 2n$.
:::

::: example Grade 8: negative second difference
Find the $n$th term of $7, 10, 11, 10, 7, \ldots$

First differences $3, 1, -1, -3$; second differences $-2$, so $a = -1$: start with $-n^2$.

Sequence $- (-n^2)$, i.e. sequence $+ n^2$: $8, 14, 20, 26, 32$ — difference 6, so $6n + 2$.

$n$th term $-n^2 + 6n + 2$. Check $n = 3$: $-9 + 18 + 2 = 11$ ✓.
:::

::: example Grade 7: Fibonacci-type with unknowns
A Fibonacci-type sequence has third term 7 and sixth term 31. Find the first two terms.

Terms: $a,\ b,\ a + b,\ a + 2b,\ 2a + 3b,\ 3a + 5b$.

$a + b = 7$ and $3a + 5b = 31$. From the first, $a = 7 - b$: $21 - 3b + 5b = 31$, $b = 5$, $a = 2$.

Check: $2, 5, 7, 12, 19, 31$ ✓.
:::

::: example Grade 8: geometric with a surd ratio
A geometric sequence of positive terms has first term 2 and third term 6. Find the second term and the seventh term.

$2r^2 = 6 \Rightarrow r^2 = 3 \Rightarrow r = \sqrt3$ (positive terms).

Second term $2\sqrt3$. Seventh term $2 \times (\sqrt3)^6 = 2 \times 27 = 54$.
:::

::: example Grade 9: is it a term of a quadratic sequence?
The $n$th term of a sequence is $2n^2 + n + 1$. Is 200 a term?

$2n^2 + n + 1 = 200 \Rightarrow 2n^2 + n - 199 = 0 \Rightarrow n = \dfrac{-1 + \sqrt{1593}}{4} = 9.73\ldots$

Not a whole number, so 200 is not a term. (Or: the 9th term is 172 and the 10th is 211, and the sequence is increasing, so 200 is skipped.)
:::

A sequence has $u_1 = 2$ and $u_{n+1} = 3u_n + 1$. Write down the first four terms.
---
$2, 7, 22, 67$.
:::

::: q
The $n$th term of a sequence is $n^2 - 3n + 5$. Find the first three terms and the 10th term.
---
$n = 1, 2, 3$: $3, 3, 5$. 10th term: $100 - 30 + 5 = 75$.
:::

::: q
Find the $n$th term of $20, 16, 12, 8, \ldots$
---
Difference $-4$, zero term $24$: $24 - 4n$.
:::

::: q
Find the $n$th term of $5, 12, 25, 44, 69, \ldots$
---
First differences $7, 13, 19, 25$; second differences 6, so $3n^2$. Sequence $- 3n^2$: $2, 0, -2, -4, -6$, which is $-2n + 4$. $n$th term $3n^2 - 2n + 4$.
:::

::: q
Find the $n$th term of $4, 11, 20, 31, 44, \ldots$
---
Second difference 2, so $n^2$. Sequence $- n^2$: $3, 7, 11, 15, 19$, i.e. $4n - 1$. $n$th term $n^2 + 4n - 1$.
:::

::: q
Find the common ratio and the next term of $64, 48, 36, 27, \ldots$ and of $\sqrt5, 5, 5\sqrt5, \ldots$
---
$r = \dfrac{48}{64} = \dfrac34$; next term $27 \times \dfrac34 = \dfrac{81}{4} = 20.25$. Second: $r = \sqrt5$; next term $5\sqrt5 \times \sqrt5 = 25$.
:::

::: q
A Fibonacci-type sequence has first term 4 and fourth term 22. Find the second and fifth terms.
---
Terms $4, b, 4 + b, 4 + 2b$. $4 + 2b = 22$, $b = 9$. Sequence $4, 9, 13, 22, 35$: second term 9, fifth term 35.
:::

::: q
Explain why 60 is not a term of the sequence with $n$th term $4n + 3$.
---
$4n$ is always even, so $4n + 3$ is always odd. 60 is even, so it cannot be a term.
:::

::: q
The $n$th triangular number is $\frac12 n(n + 1)$. Find the 20th triangular number, and show that 55 is a triangular number.
---
$\frac12 \times 20 \times 21 = 210$. $\frac12 \times 10 \times 11 = 55$, so 55 is the 10th triangular number.
:::

::: watch
- **Linear $n$th term back to front:** for $7, 13, 19$ the difference is 6, so it starts $6n$ — not $n + 6$.
- **Forgetting to halve the second difference.** Second difference 4 means $2n^2$, not $4n^2$.
- **Subtracting $an^2$ wrongly** when there are negatives: for $a = -1$ you add $n^2$ to each term. Write the subtraction row out.
- **"Is 150 a term?" with no conclusion.** Solving gives $n = 24.83$; the mark needs "not a whole number, so not a term".
- **Geometric ratios upside down:** divide a term by the term **before** it.
- **Surds in geometric sequences:** $(\sqrt3)^6 = 27$, and $\sqrt3 \times \sqrt3 = 3$; keep answers exact unless told otherwise.
- **Always check** your $n$th term with a term you did not use to find it.
:::

::: test
The $n$th triangular number is $\frac12 n(n + 1)$. Show that the sum of the 6th and 7th triangular numbers is a square number. (2)
---
$T_6 = 21$ and $T_7 = 28$ (M1); $21 + 28 = 49 = 7^2$ (A1).

::: test
The first three terms of a geometric sequence are $250, 100, 40$. Find the fifth term. (2)
---
$r = \dfrac{100}{250} = \dfrac25$ (M1). $40 \times \dfrac25 \times \dfrac25 = \dfrac{32}{5} = 6.4$ (A1).
:::

::: test
Find the $n$th term of the quadratic sequence $2, 14, 36, 68, 110, \ldots$ (3)
---
Second difference 10, so $5n^2$ (M1). Sequence $- 5n^2$: $-3, -6, -9, -12, -15$ (M1). $n$th term $5n^2 - 3n$ (A1).
:::

::: test
A Fibonacci-type sequence has second term 3 and fifth term 21. Find the first term. (3)
---
Terms $a, 3, a + 3, a + 6, 2a + 9$ (M1). $2a + 9 = 21$ (M1), $a = 6$ (A1). Check: $6, 3, 9, 12, 21$.
:::

::: test
A geometric sequence of positive terms has third term 12 and fifth term 24. Find the first term and the exact value of the second term. (4)
---
$12r^2 = 24$ (M1), $r^2 = 2$, $r = \sqrt2$ (A1). First term $\dfrac{12}{2} = 6$ (A1). Second term $6\sqrt2$ (A1).
:::

::: test
Sequence A has $n$th term $n^2 + 4n - 1$. Sequence B has $n$th term $7n + 9$. Show that there is exactly one position $n$ where the two sequences have the same term, and find that term. (4)
---
$n^2 + 4n - 1 = 7n + 9$ (M1), $n^2 - 3n - 10 = 0$ (A1), $(n - 5)(n + 2) = 0$; $n$ must be a positive integer, so only $n = 5$ (M1). The term is $7(5) + 9 = 44$ (A1).
:::

::: test
The $n$th term of a sequence is $an^2 + bn$. The 2nd term is 10 and the 4th term is 36. Find the 10th term. (4)
---
$4a + 2b = 10$ and $16a + 4b = 36$ (M1). Halve: $2a + b = 5$ and $4a + b = 9$; subtract: $2a = 4$, $a = 2$, $b = 1$ (A1 A1). 10th term $= 200 + 10 = 210$ (A1).
:::
