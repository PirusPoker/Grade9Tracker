## What it is

A **quadratic equation** can be written as $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \ne 0$. It usually has **two** solutions (roots), sometimes one repeated root, and sometimes none — matching a parabola that crosses the $x$-axis twice, touches it, or misses it. You must solve quadratics by **factorising**, by **completing the square** and by the **quadratic formula**, including ones that need **rearranging** first, and find **approximate solutions from a graph**.

### Step zero: rearrange to "= 0"

Every algebraic method needs the equation in the form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$. Expand brackets, clear fractions, and collect everything on one side (ideally with $a$ positive).

- $(x + 3)(x - 2) = 14$: $x^2 + x - 6 = 14$, so $x^2 + x - 20 = 0$.
- $x + \dfrac6x = 5$: multiply by $x$: $x^2 + 6 = 5x$, so $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$.
- $x^2 = 7x$: $x^2 - 7x = 0$. **Never divide by $x$** — you would lose the solution $x = 0$.

### Method 1: factorising

If a product is zero, one of its factors must be zero. So once you have $(x - p)(x - q) = 0$, the solutions are $x = p$ and $x = q$.

- $x^2 - 3x - 28 = 0$: two numbers with product $-28$ and sum $-3$ are $-7$ and $4$. $(x - 7)(x + 4) = 0$, so $x = 7$ or $x = -4$.
- $a \ne 1$: $3x^2 + 10x - 8 = 0$. Find two numbers with product $ac = -24$ and sum $10$: $12$ and $-2$. Split: $3x^2 + 12x - 2x - 8 = 3x(x + 4) - 2(x + 4) = (3x - 2)(x + 4)$. So $x = \dfrac23$ or $x = -4$.
- No constant term: $x^2 - 7x = 0 \Rightarrow x(x - 7) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$ or $7$.
- Difference of two squares: $9x^2 - 25 = 0 \Rightarrow (3x - 5)(3x + 5) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm\dfrac53$.

Factorising is quickest but only works when the roots are rational. If you cannot find the pair within a minute, switch method.

### Method 2: completing the square

Write the quadratic as $(x + p)^2 + q = 0$, then undo it: square root **both** signs.

$x^2 - 8x + 3 = 0$

$(x - 4)^2 - 16 + 3 = 0$

$(x - 4)^2 = 13$

$x - 4 = \pm\sqrt{13}$, so $x = 4 \pm \sqrt{13}$.

This gives **exact** answers in surd form — exactly what "give your answers in the form $p \pm \sqrt q$" wants. If $a \ne 1$, divide the whole equation by $a$ first: $2x^2 + 12x + 5 = 0$ becomes $x^2 + 6x + \dfrac52 = 0$, so $(x + 3)^2 = 9 - \dfrac52 = \dfrac{13}{2}$ and $x = -3 \pm \sqrt{\dfrac{13}{2}}$.

### Method 3: the quadratic formula

For $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$:

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$

The specifications list this as a formula you must **know** — learn it. It always works, and it is the method to use when the question asks for answers "to 2 decimal places" or "to 3 significant figures" (a clear hint that the equation does not factorise).

$3x^2 - 7x + 1 = 0$: $a = 3$, $b = -7$, $c = 1$.

$b^2 - 4ac = 49 - 12 = 37$, so $x = \dfrac{7 \pm \sqrt{37}}{6}$: $x = 2.18$ or $x = 0.153$ (3 s.f.).

The part under the square root, $b^2 - 4ac$, tells you how many solutions there are: positive → two; zero → one repeated; negative → none (you cannot square-root a negative number).

**Surd form from the formula**: $x^2 - 6x - 9 = 0$ gives $x = \dfrac{6 \pm \sqrt{72}}{2} = \dfrac{6 \pm 6\sqrt2}{2} = 3 \pm 3\sqrt2$.

### Approximate solutions from a graph

The solutions of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ are the $x$-coordinates where $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ crosses the $x$-axis. For $y = x^2 - 3x - 1$, the curve crosses at about $x = -0.3$ and $x = 3.3$ (exactly $\dfrac{3 \pm \sqrt{13}}{2}$).

To solve a **different** equation with the same curve, rearrange it so the curve's expression is on one side and read where the curve meets a line. For $x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0$: rewrite as $x^2 - 3x - 1 = 3$, draw $y = 3$, and read $x = -1$ and $x = 4$.

### Which method?

| Question says | Use |
|---|---|
| "Solve" (nice numbers, Paper 1) | factorise |
| "to 2 d.p." / "to 3 s.f." | the formula |
| "in the form $p \pm \sqrt q$" / "exact" | completing the square (or the formula, then simplify the surd) |
| "use the graph" | read the crossings |

::: terms
- **Quadratic equation**: an equation that can be written as ax² + bx + c = 0, where a is not zero.
- **Root**: a solution of the equation; on the graph of y = ax² + bx + c it is where the curve meets the x-axis.
- **Factorising**: writing the quadratic as a product of two brackets, then setting each bracket equal to zero.
- **Completing the square**: rewriting x² + bx + c as (x + b/2)² − (b/2)² + c so the equation can be solved by square-rooting.
- **Quadratic formula**: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a, which solves any quadratic equation.
- **Discriminant**: the value b² − 4ac; negative means no real solutions, zero means one repeated solution.
- **Surd form**: an exact answer left with a square root, such as 3 ± 2√5.
:::

::: method
1. **Rearrange to $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.** Expand, clear fractions by multiplying through by every denominator, and collect all terms on one side. This rearrangement usually earns the first M1 on its own.
2. **Choose the method from the wording** — factorise for plain "solve", the formula for decimal accuracy, completing the square for "the form $p \pm \sqrt q$".
3. **Factorising:** find two numbers with product $ac$ and sum $b$; set each bracket to zero. Check by expanding.
4. **Formula:** write down $a$, $b$, $c$ with their signs; substitute with brackets, e.g. $\dfrac{-(-7) \pm \sqrt{(-7)^2 - 4(3)(1)}}{2(3)}$. Writing this substitution earns M1 even if you then slip. Work out $b^2 - 4ac$ separately.
5. **Completing the square:** divide by $a$ if needed; write $(x + p)^2 = k$; square-root with $\pm$; simplify surds.
6. **Give both solutions** to the accuracy asked. On Paper 1 give exact answers (fractions or surds); on Papers 2 and 3 round only at the end.
7. **Reject** any solution that is impossible in context (a negative length or time), and say why.
8. **From a graph:** read where the curve meets the $x$-axis, or meets the line you have drawn; state answers to 1 decimal place as approximations.
:::

::: example Rearranging then factorising
Solve $(x + 3)(x - 2) = 14$.

$x^2 + x - 6 = 14$

$x^2 + x - 20 = 0$

$(x + 5)(x - 4) = 0$, so $x = -5$ or $x = 4$.

Check $x = 4$: $7 \times 2 = 14$. ✓ (Setting $x + 3 = 14$ would be wrong — a product equals 14 in infinitely many ways.)
:::

::: example Completing the square with a ≠ 1
Solve $2x^2 - 12x + 7 = 0$, giving your answers in the form $p \pm \dfrac{\sqrt q}{r}$.

Divide by 2: $x^2 - 6x + \dfrac72 = 0$.

$(x - 3)^2 - 9 + \dfrac72 = 0 \Rightarrow (x - 3)^2 = \dfrac{11}{2}$.

$x = 3 \pm \sqrt{\dfrac{11}{2}} = 3 \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{11}}{\sqrt2} = 3 \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{22}}{2}$.
:::

::: example The formula to a given accuracy
Solve $2x^2 + 3x - 7 = 0$, giving your answers to 2 decimal places.

$a = 2$, $b = 3$, $c = -7$. $b^2 - 4ac = 9 - 4(2)(-7) = 9 + 56 = 65$.

$x = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{65}}{4}$.

$x = \dfrac{-3 + 8.0622\ldots}{4} = 1.27$ or $x = \dfrac{-3 - 8.0622\ldots}{4} = -2.77$.
:::

::: example Solving with a graph
The graph of $y = x^2 - 2x - 5$ is drawn for $-3 \le x \le 5$. (a) Use it to estimate the solutions of $x^2 - 2x - 5 = 0$. (b) What line should be drawn to solve $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$?

(a) The curve crosses the $x$-axis at about $x = -1.4$ and $x = 3.4$. (Exact: $1 \pm \sqrt6 = -1.45$, $3.45$.)

(b) $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0 \iff x^2 - 2x - 5 = 3$. Draw $y = 3$; it meets the curve at $x = -2$ and $x = 4$.
:::

::: example A context problem (grade 8)
A rectangle has length $(x + 3)$ cm and width $(2x - 1)$ cm. Its area is 60 cm². Find its perimeter.

$(x + 3)(2x - 1) = 60 \Rightarrow 2x^2 + 5x - 3 = 60 \Rightarrow 2x^2 + 5x - 63 = 0$.

$ac = -126$, sum 5: $14$ and $-9$. $(2x - 9)(x + 7) = 0$, so $x = 4.5$ or $x = -7$.

$x = -7$ gives a negative width, so reject it. $x = 4.5$: length 7.5 cm, width 8 cm (area 60 ✓).

Perimeter $= 2(7.5 + 8) = 31$ cm.
:::

::: example Algebraic fractions leading to a quadratic (grade 9)
Solve $\dfrac{x}{x + 2} + \dfrac{3}{x} = 2$.

Multiply every term by $x(x + 2)$: $x^2 + 3(x + 2) = 2x(x + 2)$.

$x^2 + 3x + 6 = 2x^2 + 4x \Rightarrow 0 = x^2 + x - 6 = (x + 3)(x - 2)$.

$x = -3$ or $x = 2$. Neither makes a denominator zero, so both stand. Check $x = 2$: $\dfrac24 + \dfrac32 = 2$. ✓
:::

Solve $x^2 + 2x - 24 = 0$.
---
$(x + 6)(x - 4) = 0$: $x = -6$ or $x = 4$.
:::

Solve $2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0$.
---
$ac = 6$, sum $-7$: $-6, -1$. $(2x - 1)(x - 3) = 0$: $x = \dfrac12$ or $x = 3$.

$x(x - 7) = 0$: $x = 0$ or $7$. $\quad (3x - 5)(3x + 5) = 0$: $x = \pm\dfrac53$.

Solve $x^2 + 4x - 7 = 0$ by completing the square. Give exact answers.
---
$(x + 2)^2 - 4 - 7 = 0 \Rightarrow (x + 2)^2 = 11 \Rightarrow x = -2 \pm \sqrt{11}$.
:::

::: q
Solve $x^2 - 6x - 9 = 0$, giving your answers in the form $a \pm b\sqrt2$.
---
$(x - 3)^2 = 18 \Rightarrow x = 3 \pm \sqrt{18} = 3 \pm 3\sqrt2$.
:::

::: q
Solve $3x^2 - 7x + 1 = 0$, giving your answers to 3 significant figures.
---
$x = \dfrac{7 \pm \sqrt{49 - 12}}{6} = \dfrac{7 \pm \sqrt{37}}{6}$: $x = 2.18$ or $x = 0.153$.
:::

::: q
Solve $x + \dfrac6x = 5$.
---
$x^2 + 6 = 5x \Rightarrow x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0 \Rightarrow (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0$: $x = 2$ or $3$.
:::

::: q
The graph of $y = x^2 - 4x + 1$ is drawn. Estimate its roots, and use the line $y = 1$ to solve $x^2 - 4x = 0$.
---
Roots about $0.3$ and $3.7$ (exactly $2 \pm \sqrt3$). $x^2 - 4x + 1 = 1$ where the line meets the curve: $x = 0$ and $x = 4$.
:::

::: q
The product of two consecutive positive integers is 132. Form and solve a quadratic equation to find them.
---
$n(n + 1) = 132 \Rightarrow n^2 + n - 132 = 0 \Rightarrow (n + 12)(n - 11) = 0$. Positive, so $n = 11$: the integers are 11 and 12.
:::

::: q
Explain why $x^2 + 3x + 5 = 0$ has no solutions.
---
$b^2 - 4ac = 9 - 20 = -11 < 0$; you cannot square-root a negative, so there are no real solutions (the graph never meets the $x$-axis).
:::

::: watch
- **Not rearranging to zero.** $(x + 3)(x - 2) = 14$ does not mean $x + 3 = 14$ or $x - 2 = 14$. Only a product equal to **zero** splits into factors.
- **Dividing by $x$.** $x^2 = 7x$ has two solutions, $0$ and $7$; dividing by $x$ throws away $x = 0$.
- **Sign errors in the formula.** With $b = -7$, $-b = +7$ and $b^2 = +49$; with $c = -7$, $-4ac$ becomes $+$. Brackets in the substitution prevent this.
- **The fraction line.** The $2a$ divides the **whole** of $-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}$, not just the square root.
- **Forgetting $\pm$** when square-rooting in completing the square — that loses the second solution.
- **Early rounding.** Keep the full calculator value of the square root until the last line.
- **Keeping impossible roots** in context (negative lengths) without comment.
:::

::: test
A right-angled triangle has shorter sides $x$ cm and $(x + 7)$ cm, and hypotenuse 13 cm. (a) Show that $x^2 + 7x - 60 = 0$. (b) Find the perimeter of the triangle. (5)
---
(a) $x^2 + (x + 7)^2 = 169$ (M1). $2x^2 + 14x + 49 = 169$, so $2x^2 + 14x - 120 = 0$ and dividing by 2 gives $x^2 + 7x - 60 = 0$ (A1).
(b) $(x + 12)(x - 5) = 0$ (M1). $x = 5$; $x = -12$ is rejected as a length (A1). Sides 5, 12, 13: perimeter 30 cm (A1).
:::

::: test
Solve $\dfrac{3}{x - 1} - \dfrac{2}{x + 2} = 1$. Give your answers in exact form. (5)
---
Multiply by $(x - 1)(x + 2)$: $3(x + 2) - 2(x - 1) = (x - 1)(x + 2)$ (M1). $3x + 6 - 2x + 2 = x^2 + x - 2$ (M1). $x + 8 = x^2 + x - 2$, so $x^2 = 10$ (A1). $x = \sqrt{10}$ or $x = -\sqrt{10}$ (A1 for each); neither makes a denominator zero, so both stand.
:::
