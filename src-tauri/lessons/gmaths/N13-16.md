## What it is

This topic is about how numbers behave in the real world: measured in **units**, **rounded** to a sensible accuracy, **estimated** to check a calculator, and only ever accurate to within some **limits**. The Higher paper turns the last idea into **upper and lower bounds** questions that are worth 3–5 marks and appear on almost every series.

### Standard units and converting them

Learn the metric links — they are not given on the formulae sheet.

| Measure | Links |
|---|---|
| Length | 10 mm = 1 cm, 100 cm = 1 m, 1000 m = 1 km |
| Mass | 1000 mg = 1 g, 1000 g = 1 kg, 1000 kg = 1 tonne |
| Capacity | 1000 ml = 1 litre, 100 cl = 1 litre, 1 cm³ = 1 ml, 1000 cm³ = 1 litre |
| Time | 60 s = 1 min, 60 min = 1 h, 24 h = 1 day, 365 days = 1 year (366 in a leap year) |

**Area and volume units scale by the square and the cube.** A square 1 m by 1 m is 100 cm by 100 cm, so $1\text{ m}^2 = 100^2 = 10\,000\text{ cm}^2$, and $1\text{ m}^3 = 100^3 = 1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3$. Similarly $1\text{ cm}^2 = 100\text{ mm}^2$ and $1\text{ km}^2 = 1\,000\,000\text{ m}^2$.

**Decimal time** catches people out: $2.4$ hours is **not** 2 h 40 min. $0.4 \times 60 = 24$, so $2.4$ h $= 2$ h 24 min. Going the other way, 3 h 15 min $= 3.25$ h.

**Compound units** combine two units: speed (m/s, km/h), density (g/cm³, kg/m³), pressure (N/m²), rates (litres per minute, £ per kg). Convert each part separately:

$$20\text{ m/s} = \frac{20 \times 3600}{1000}\text{ km/h} = 72\text{ km/h}$$

(multiply by 3600 to get metres per hour, divide by 1000 to get kilometres). A density of $2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$: 1 m³ holds $1\,000\,000$ cm³, so $2.7 \times 1\,000\,000 = 2\,700\,000$ g $= 2700$ kg, giving $2700\text{ kg/m}^3$.

### Rounding

- **Decimal places (d.p.)** count digits after the decimal point: $3.14159 = 3.142$ (3 d.p.).
- **Significant figures (s.f.)** count from the first non-zero digit: $0.004\,072\,6 = 0.004\,07$ (3 s.f.); $38\,462 = 38\,500$ (3 s.f.) — the zeros hold the place value and must stay.
- Look at the next digit: 5 or more rounds up.
- An **appropriate degree of accuracy** usually means matching the data: if the measurements are to 3 s.f., give the answer to 3 s.f. Money is to the nearest penny; people and buses are whole numbers (and you may need to round **up**: 130 pupils in coaches of 50 need 3 coaches).
- In a multi-step calculation, **keep full calculator values** until the end. Rounding early can change the final digit and lose the accuracy mark.

### Estimating

To estimate, round every number to **1 significant figure**, then calculate mentally.

$$\frac{48.7 \times 0.513}{0.198} \approx \frac{50 \times 0.5}{0.2} = \frac{25}{0.2} = 125$$

(The calculator gives $126.2$, so the estimate is a good check.) Dividing by a decimal: $25 \div 0.2 = 250 \div 2 = 125$ — multiply both numbers by 10 first.

You may be asked whether an estimate is an **overestimate or underestimate**. Look at the direction of each rounding and how it feeds through: rounding a numerator up, or a denominator down, makes the estimate too big. If some numbers went up and some down, say it cannot be decided without the exact answer.

**Estimating a square root:** find the square numbers either side. $\sqrt{53}$: $49 < 53 < 64$, so $7 < \sqrt{53} < 8$, and 53 is much nearer 49, so $\sqrt{53} \approx 7.3$ (actual $7.28$).

**Checking technology:** an estimate tells you whether a calculator answer is the right size — a misplaced decimal point or a missing bracket usually gives an answer that is 10 or 100 times too big or small.

### Error intervals

A rounded number stands for a whole range of possible values. If $x = 70$ to the nearest 10, the true value could be anything from 65 up to (but not including) 75:

$$65 \le x < 75$$

The **lower bound** is 65 and the **upper bound** is 75. Rule: go **half a unit** of the rounding each way. The upper bound uses $<$, because 75 itself would round to 80 — though for calculations we still use 75 as the upper bound.

- $m = 4.3$ kg to 1 d.p.: half of 0.1 is 0.05, so $4.25 \le m < 4.35$.
- $n = 3200$ to 2 s.f.: the unit is 100, so $3150 \le n < 3250$.

**Truncation** chops digits off without rounding. If $y$ is truncated to 1 d.p. and gives $7.3$, then $y$ was $7.3\ldots$ anything:

$$7.3 \le y < 7.4$$

The interval sits entirely above the truncated value.

### Upper and lower bounds in calculations

To find the bounds of a calculated answer, choose the bound of each input that pushes the answer the way you want.

| Calculation | Upper bound | Lower bound |
|---|---|---|
| $a + b$ | $a_U + b_U$ | $a_L + b_L$ |
| $a - b$ | $a_U - b_L$ | $a_L - b_U$ |
| $a \times b$ | $a_U \times b_U$ | $a_L \times b_L$ |
| $a \div b$ | $a_U \div b_L$ | $a_L \div b_U$ |

Subtraction and division are the traps: to make $a - b$ as large as possible you take away the **smallest** $b$; to make $a \div b$ as large as possible you divide by the **smallest** $b$.

**Example.** A rectangle measures 8.4 cm by 5.7 cm, each to the nearest mm. Upper bound of the area $= 8.45 \times 5.75 = 48.5875$ cm², lower bound $= 8.35 \times 5.65 = 47.1775$ cm².

### Choosing a suitable degree of accuracy

A grade 9 question asks you to give an answer "to a suitable degree of accuracy" and justify it. Work out the upper and lower bounds, then round both: the answer is the most accurate value that **both bounds agree on**.

A runner covers 100 m (nearest metre) in 12.4 s (nearest 0.1 s). Speed upper bound $= 100.5 \div 12.35 = 8.1376\ldots$ m/s; lower bound $= 99.5 \div 12.45 = 7.9919\ldots$ m/s. To 2 s.f. the bounds give 8.1 and 8.0 — they disagree. To 1 s.f. both give 8. So the speed is 8 m/s to 1 s.f., **because the upper and lower bounds both round to 8 to 1 s.f.** — that sentence is the final mark.

::: terms
- **Significant figures**: digits counted from the first non-zero digit; zeros that only hold place value still have to be written.
- **Estimate**: an approximate answer found by rounding every number to 1 significant figure and working mentally.
- **Error interval**: the range of values a rounded or truncated number could really have, written with inequalities such as 4.25 ≤ m < 4.35.
- **Truncation**: cutting a number off after a certain digit without rounding, so the true value is at or above the truncated one.
- **Upper bound**: the largest value a measurement or calculated quantity could take (for a rounded number, half a unit above it).
- **Lower bound**: the smallest value a measurement or calculated quantity could take (for a rounded number, half a unit below it).
- **Compound unit**: a unit made from two others, such as m/s, g/cm³ or £ per hour.
:::

::: method
1. **Units:** write the conversion factor before using it. For area square it, for volume cube it (1 m² = 10 000 cm², 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³). For compound units convert the top and the bottom separately. Change decimal hours to minutes by multiplying the decimal part by 60.
2. **Estimation (usually Paper 1, non-calculator):** round every number to 1 s.f. and **write the rounded calculation down** — that line earns the method mark even if your arithmetic slips. Do not round to 1 s.f. and then use a calculator. If asked "over- or underestimate?", say which way each number was rounded and what that does to the answer.
3. **Rounding:** keep full calculator values through the working, round once at the end, and state the accuracy ("= 7.86 (3 s.f.)"). On calculator papers, write down a few more digits than you need before rounding so the examiner sees your unrounded value.
4. **Error intervals:** half a unit each way. Use $\le$ on the lower bound and $<$ on the upper bound. For truncation, the interval goes from the given value up to the next step, never below.
5. **Bounds in a calculation:** list the bounds of every measurement first (examiners give B1 for these). Then pick: add → U+U; subtract → U−L for the maximum; multiply → U×U; divide → U÷L for the maximum. Write out the substitution before the answer — "$100.5 \div 12.35$" is the M1.
6. **"Suitable degree of accuracy":** find the upper and lower bounds, round both to fewer and fewer figures until they agree, give that value, and **write the reason**: "both bounds round to 8 to 1 s.f."
:::

::: example Units, including compound units
(a) Convert 3.6 m³ to cm³. (b) Convert 90 km/h to m/s. (c) Write 2.35 hours in hours and minutes.

(a) $1\text{ m}^3 = 100^3 = 1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3$, so $3.6\text{ m}^3 = 3\,600\,000\text{ cm}^3$.

(b) $90$ km/h $= 90\,000$ m per $3600$ s $= 90\,000 \div 3600 = 25$ m/s.

(c) $0.35 \times 60 = 21$, so 2 h 21 min.
:::

::: example Estimating a calculation
Estimate the value of $\dfrac{\sqrt{98.6} \times 3.08}{0.487}$.

Round to 1 s.f.: $98.6 \approx 100$, $3.08 \approx 3$, $0.487 \approx 0.5$.

$$\frac{\sqrt{100} \times 3}{0.5} = \frac{10 \times 3}{0.5} = \frac{30}{0.5} = 60$$

(The exact value is $62.8$ — the estimate confirms the size of the answer.)
:::

::: example Error intervals from rounding and truncation
(a) The length $l$ of a pencil is 14.6 cm to 1 d.p. Write down the error interval for $l$.

(b) Priya's calculator shows $x = 23.817\ldots$ She truncates it to a whole number and writes 23. Write down the error interval for $x$.

(a) Half of 0.1 is 0.05: $14.55 \le l < 14.65$.

(b) Truncation to an integer: $23 \le x < 24$.
:::

::: example Bounds with division
A metal block has mass 250 g, to the nearest 10 g, and volume 32.5 cm³, to 1 d.p. Find the upper and lower bounds of its density, and give the density to a suitable degree of accuracy.

Bounds: $245 \le m < 255$ and $32.45 \le V < 32.55$.

Upper bound of density $= \dfrac{255}{32.45} = 7.858\ldots$ g/cm³ (largest mass ÷ smallest volume).

Lower bound of density $= \dfrac{245}{32.55} = 7.526\ldots$ g/cm³.

To 2 s.f. these are 7.9 and 7.5 — they disagree. To 1 s.f. both are 8. Density $= 8$ g/cm³ to 1 s.f., because the upper and lower bounds both round to 8.
:::

::: example Bounds inside trigonometry (grade 9)
In a right-angled triangle, the side opposite angle $\theta$ is 7.3 cm and the hypotenuse is 11.8 cm, both to 1 d.p. Find the upper bound of $\theta$.

$\sin\theta = \dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}$. The angle is largest when $\sin\theta$ is largest: biggest opposite, smallest hypotenuse.

$$\sin\theta = \frac{7.35}{11.75} = 0.6255\ldots \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = \sin^{-1}(0.6255\ldots) = 38.7^\circ \text{ (1 d.p.)}$$

(The lower bound, from $7.25 \div 11.85$, is $37.7^\circ$.)
:::

::: q
Convert 2.35 km to cm, and 4500 g to kg.
---
$2.35 \times 1000 \times 100 = 235\,000$ cm. $\quad 4500 \div 1000 = 4.5$ kg.
:::

::: q
Convert 5.2 m² to cm².
---
$1\text{ m}^2 = 10\,000\text{ cm}^2$, so $5.2 \times 10\,000 = 52\,000\text{ cm}^2$.
:::

::: q
A journey takes 3.7 hours. Write this in hours and minutes, and convert 54 km/h to m/s.
---
$0.7 \times 60 = 42$: 3 h 42 min. $\quad 54\,000 \div 3600 = 15$ m/s.
:::

::: q
Round 0.006 084 9 to 3 significant figures, and 17.996 to 2 decimal places.
---
$0.006\,08$ (the first significant figure is the 6). $\quad 18.00$ — the zeros must be written to show 2 d.p.
:::

::: q
Estimate the value of $\dfrac{6.13 \times 39.8}{0.52}$.
---
$\dfrac{6 \times 40}{0.5} = \dfrac{240}{0.5} = 480$. (Exact: $469.2$.)
:::

::: q
$w = 380$ correct to 2 significant figures. $t$ is truncated to 1 d.p. to give 5.8. Write down the error intervals for $w$ and $t$.
---
Unit is 10, half is 5: $375 \le w < 385$. $\quad$ Truncation: $5.8 \le t < 5.9$.
:::

::: q
$a = 9.2$ and $b = 3.8$, both correct to 1 d.p. Find the upper bound of $a - b$ and the lower bound of $a \div b$.
---
Upper bound of $a - b = 9.25 - 3.75 = 5.5$. $\quad$ Lower bound of $a \div b = 9.15 \div 3.85 = 2.376\ldots = 2.38$ (3 s.f.).
:::

::: q
The radius of a circle is 4.7 cm to the nearest mm. Work out the area to a suitable degree of accuracy. Give a reason.
---
Upper bound $\pi \times 4.75^2 = 70.88\ldots$; lower bound $\pi \times 4.65^2 = 67.92\ldots$ To 2 s.f. these are 71 and 68 (disagree); to 1 s.f. both are 70. Area $= 70$ cm² to 1 s.f., because both bounds round to 70.
:::

::: q
The side of a square is 6.3 cm to 1 d.p. Find the upper bound of its perimeter and of its area.
---
Side $< 6.35$. Perimeter upper bound $= 4 \times 6.35 = 25.4$ cm. Area upper bound $= 6.35^2 = 40.3225$ cm².
:::

::: watch
- **Squaring the conversion:** 1 m² is 10 000 cm², not 100 cm²; 1 m³ is 1 000 000 cm³.
- **Decimal time:** 1.5 h is 1 h 30 min, but 1.25 h is 1 h 15 min, not 1 h 25 min.
- **Subtracting and dividing bounds:** the maximum of $a - b$ uses the **lower** bound of $b$; the maximum of $a \div b$ also uses the **lower** bound of $b$. Using U−U or U÷U scores nothing.
- **Truncation intervals** only go upwards from the value; people wrongly go half a unit either side.
- **Estimation working:** writing only "480" without the rounded numbers loses the method mark, and rounding to 2 s.f. instead of 1 s.f. makes the arithmetic impossible without a calculator.
- **"Suitable accuracy" with no reason** loses the last mark — you must say both bounds agree to that accuracy.
- Rounding intermediate values in a bounds question (e.g. using 7.86 instead of 7.858…) can change which way the final answer rounds.
:::

::: test
The density of a liquid is 1.2 g/cm³. Work out its density in kg/m³. (3)
---
1 m³ $= 1\,000\,000$ cm³ (M1). Mass of 1 m³ $= 1.2 \times 1\,000\,000 = 1\,200\,000$ g (M1). $= 1200$ kg, so density $= 1200$ kg/m³ (A1).
:::

::: test
Ella estimates the value of $\dfrac{29.3 \times 8.7}{2.1}$ as $\dfrac{30 \times 9}{2}$.
(a) Work out her estimate. (b) Is her estimate an overestimate or an underestimate? Give a reason. (3)
---
(a) $\dfrac{270}{2} = 135$ (B1).
(b) Overestimate (B1): both numbers on the top were rounded up and the number on the bottom was rounded down, so the fraction was made bigger (B1). (Exact value $121.4$.)
:::

::: test
A car travels 150 km, correct to the nearest 5 km. The journey takes 1.8 hours, correct to 1 d.p. Work out the lower bound for the average speed of the car. Give your answer to 1 d.p. (3)
---
Bounds $147.5$ and $1.85$ (B1). Lower bound $= \dfrac{147.5}{1.85}$ (M1) $= 79.7$ km/h (A1).
:::

::: test
$T = \dfrac{a - b}{c}$. $a = 7.6$ and $b = 2.4$, both correct to 1 d.p. $c = 5$ correct to the nearest integer. Work out the upper bound of $T$. Show your working clearly. (4)
---
Bounds: $a_U = 7.65$, $b_L = 2.35$, $c_L = 4.5$ (B1).
Largest numerator $= 7.65 - 2.35 = 5.3$ (M1). Divide by the smallest $c$: $\dfrac{5.3}{4.5}$ (M1) $= 1.18$ (3 s.f.) (A1).
:::

::: test
An object starts from rest and travels $s = 42$ m, correct to the nearest metre, in $t = 5.3$ s, correct to 1 d.p. Its acceleration is $a = \dfrac{2s}{t^2}$. Work out the value of $a$ to a suitable degree of accuracy. You must show all your working and give a reason for your final answer. (5)
---
Bounds $41.5, 42.5$ and $5.25, 5.35$ (B1).
Upper bound $= \dfrac{2 \times 42.5}{5.25^2} = 3.0839\ldots$ (M1). Lower bound $= \dfrac{2 \times 41.5}{5.35^2} = 2.8998\ldots$ (M1).
To 2 s.f. these are 3.1 and 2.9, which differ; to 1 s.f. both are 3 (M1). $a = 3$ m/s² to 1 s.f., because the upper and lower bounds both round to 3 (A1).
:::
