## What it is

Statistics is about finding out something about a **population** — the whole group you are interested in: every student in a school, every fish in a lake, every battery made in a factory. Asking or measuring every member is a **census**. Usually that is too slow, too expensive or impossible (testing every battery until it dies would leave none to sell), so you take a **sample** — a smaller group chosen from the population — and **infer** (estimate) the population's properties from it.

### Random samples

A sample is only useful if it is **representative**: it should look like the population in the ways that matter. The standard way to get close is a **random sample**, in which **every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen**. For example: number the 1200 students 1 to 1200 and use a random number generator to pick 60 numbers, ignoring repeats.

A sample is **biased** if it is not representative — some members were more likely to be chosen than others. Common causes:

- **Who you ask:** a survey about how often people exercise, done outside a gym, will over-count keen exercisers.
- **When or where:** a traffic survey on a Sunday morning says little about weekday rush hour.
- **Only volunteers:** people who choose to reply often have strong views.
- **Too small:** 10 people can give a result far from the truth just by chance.
- **Leading questions:** "Don't you agree that the park needs more bins?" pushes people towards one answer.

### Sample size

A **larger sample** gives a more reliable estimate, because chance variation evens out — the same idea as more trials giving a better relative frequency. But a large biased sample is still biased: 5000 people outside a gym are still gym-goers. Size and randomness both matter.

### Inferring from a sample

**Proportions scale up.** In a random sample of 50 students, 18 walk to school. Estimated number of the school's 1200 students who walk:
$$\frac{18}{50} \times 1200 = 432.$$

**Sample statistics estimate population statistics.** A random sample of 20 bags of flour has mean mass 502 g, so the mean mass of all bags is estimated as 502 g, and 2000 bags would have an estimated total mass of $2000 \times 502 \text{ g} = 1004$ kg.

These are **estimates**. A different sample would give a slightly different answer. The estimate is only as good as the sample: always be ready to state the assumption "the sample is representative of the population" and to name a limitation.

### Limitations of sampling

- Different samples give different results (**sampling variability**), so an estimate is never exact.
- A small sample may be unrepresentative just by chance.
- The method of choosing may introduce bias.
- People may not answer honestly, or may not reply at all.
- You can only infer about the population the sample came from: a sample of Year 11 students tells you nothing reliable about Year 7.

### Capture–recapture

To estimate the size $N$ of an animal population, catch $M$ animals, mark them and release them. Later catch a second sample of $n$ animals and count the number $m$ that are marked. Assume the **proportion marked in the second sample equals the proportion marked in the whole population**:
$$\frac{m}{n} = \frac{M}{N} \quad\Rightarrow\quad N = \frac{M \times n}{m}.$$

40 fish are tagged and released. Later 50 are caught and 8 are tagged: $N \approx \dfrac{40 \times 50}{8} = 250$ fish.

**Assumptions:** the marked animals mix fully back into the population; no animals are born, die, arrive or leave between samples; marks are not lost; marked animals are no more or less likely to be caught again; each sample is random.

### Stratified samples

When a population splits into groups of very different sizes (year groups, age bands), a **stratified sample** takes from each group **in proportion to its size**, so each group is fairly represented. Each group's share is
$$\frac{\text{group size}}{\text{population size}} \times \text{sample size}.$$
It is just a proportion calculation; round to whole numbers and check the parts still add to the sample size.

::: terms
- **Population**: the whole group that a statistical question is about.
- **Census**: collecting data from every member of the population.
- **Sample**: a smaller group chosen from the population, used to estimate the population's properties.
- **Random sample**: a sample in which every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.
- **Biased sample**: a sample that does not represent the population because some members were more likely to be chosen.
- **Representative**: having the same features, in the same proportions, as the population.
- **Capture–recapture**: estimating a population size N from M marked animals and a second sample of n containing m marked, using N = Mn ÷ m.
- **Stratified sample**: a sample taking from each group in proportion to the size of that group.
:::

::: method
1. **"Criticise the sample" / "give a reason the sample may be biased":** name the specific problem **in context** — who was asked, where, when, how many. "It is biased" alone scores nothing.
2. **"How could the sample be improved?"** Make it random (number the population, use random numbers), make it larger, and spread it across times, places or groups.
3. **Estimate a population total from a sample:** write the fraction of the sample, then multiply by the population size. Show the fraction — that is the method mark.
4. **Capture–recapture:** set up $\dfrac{m}{n} = \dfrac{M}{N}$ (marked in second sample over second sample, equals marked over population) and solve for the unknown. It works equally well backwards to find $n$ or $m$.
5. **State an assumption** when asked: for scaling up, "the sample is representative"; for capture–recapture, "the population did not change between the two samples" or "the marked animals mixed back into the population".
6. **Stratified sample:** group size $\div$ population $\times$ sample size for each group; round sensibly and check the total.
7. These questions are short (2–3 marks) and appear on any paper; the arithmetic is non-calculator friendly, so keep it as fractions until the end.
:::

::: example Criticising a sample
Jake wants to know how often adults in his town exercise. He asks 15 people leaving a gym at 7 am on a Monday. Give two reasons why his sample may be biased and suggest an improvement.

People leaving a gym are more likely to exercise often than adults in general; 7 am on a Monday misses people who work early or exercise at other times; and 15 is a small sample.

Improvement: choose a larger random sample of adults from the whole town (for example from the electoral register using random numbers).
:::

::: example Scaling up from a sample
A random sample of 80 households in a town found that 26 have a dog. There are 12 400 households in the town. Estimate the number with a dog, and state an assumption.

$\dfrac{26}{80} \times 12\,400 = 4030$ households.

Assumption: the sample is representative of all households in the town.
:::

::: example Capture–recapture and its assumptions
60 birds are caught, ringed and released. A week later 75 birds are caught and 12 of them are ringed. (a) Estimate the number of birds in the population. (b) Ringed birds learn to avoid the traps. Will the estimate be too high or too low?

(a) $\dfrac{12}{75} = \dfrac{60}{N} \Rightarrow N = \dfrac{60 \times 75}{12} = 375$ birds.

(b) Too few ringed birds are caught, so $m$ is too small and $N = \dfrac{Mn}{m}$ is **too high** — an overestimate.
:::

::: example A stratified sample
A school has 240 students in Year 7, 220 in Year 8, 200 in Year 9, 180 in Year 10 and 160 in Year 11. A stratified sample of 50 is taken by year group. How many from each year?

Total 1000. Year 7: $\dfrac{240}{1000} \times 50 = 12$; Year 8: $11$; Year 9: $10$; Year 10: $9$; Year 11: $8$. Check: $12 + 11 + 10 + 9 + 8 = 50$. ✓
:::

::: example Grade 8: capture–recapture backwards
30 frogs in a pond are marked and released. Later a second sample is caught, and 6 of the frogs in it are marked. The estimated population is 200. (a) How many frogs were in the second sample? (b) Give one reason why the population estimate may not be accurate.

(a) $\dfrac{6}{n} = \dfrac{30}{200} \Rightarrow n = \dfrac{6 \times 200}{30} = 40$ frogs.

(b) For example: the marked frogs may not have mixed evenly back into the pond before the second sample, or frogs may have died, been born or moved between the samples — so the proportion marked in the second sample may not equal the proportion in the whole pond.
:::

::: q
Ms Khan wants to find the mean height of Year 11 students at her school. What is the population?
---
All the Year 11 students at her school.
:::

::: q
A factory tests how long its light bulbs last. Why must it use a sample rather than a census?
---
Testing a bulb until it fails destroys it — a census would leave no bulbs to sell. (It would also take too long.)
:::

::: q
In a random sample of 40 of a school's 900 students, 14 have a part-time job. Estimate how many students in the school have a part-time job.
---
$\dfrac{14}{40} \times 900 = 315$.
:::

::: q
50 fish are tagged and released. Later 80 fish are caught and 10 of them are tagged. Estimate the number of fish in the lake.
---
$\dfrac{50 \times 80}{10} = 400$ fish.
:::

::: q
To find out how interested people in a town are in sport, a researcher interviews people outside a football stadium on match day. Explain why the sample is biased.
---
People at a football match are far more interested in sport than the town's population in general, so they are not representative.
:::

::: q
A company has 360 male and 440 female employees. A stratified sample of 40 employees by gender is taken. How many of each?
---
Men: $\dfrac{360}{800} \times 40 = 18$; women: $\dfrac{440}{800} \times 40 = 22$.
:::

::: q
Priya surveys 20 randomly chosen students and Tom surveys 200 randomly chosen students from the same school. Whose results give the more reliable estimate, and why?
---
Tom's — a larger random sample is less affected by chance variation, so it is more likely to be representative.
:::

::: q
A random sample of 25 apples from a crate has mean mass 142 g. Estimate the total mass of 3000 apples from the same orchard, in kilograms.
---
$3000 \times 142 = 426\,000$ g $= 426$ kg.
:::

::: q
Explain why even a random sample might not represent the population well.
---
By chance it could include unusually many of one kind of member, especially if the sample is small; different random samples give different results.
:::

::: watch
- "It is biased" or "it is not fair" with no reason scores nothing. Say **who** is over- or under-represented and **why**, in context.
- A bigger sample is more reliable, but **a big biased sample is still biased** — do not give "ask more people" as the only improvement if the method is the problem.
- Capture–recapture: the marked number from the **first** visit goes with the population; the marked count in the **second** sample goes with the second sample size. Mixing them up gives nonsense like $N = 16$.
- An estimate from a sample is an **estimate** — do not claim it is exact.
- In stratified samples, check that the rounded numbers still add to the sample size.
:::

::: test
Give one reason why a sample might be used instead of a census. (1)
---
It is quicker or cheaper, or testing destroys the items (B1 for any valid reason).
:::

::: test
A cycling club puts a poll on its website asking whether the town should build more cycle lanes. Give two reasons why the results may not represent the views of the town. (2)
---
Only people who visit a cycling club website will see it, so cyclists are over-represented (B1). People choose whether to reply, so those with strong views are more likely to answer; also the number replying is not controlled (B1).
:::

::: test
A village has 2150 people. In a random sample of 60 of them, 12 are left-handed. Work out an estimate for the number of left-handed people in the village. (2)
---
$\dfrac{12}{60} \times 2150$ (M1) $= 430$ (A1).
:::

::: test
45 rabbits are caught, marked and released. A week later 60 rabbits are caught and 9 are marked. (a) Work out an estimate for the number of rabbits. (b) State one assumption you have made. (3)
---
(a) $\dfrac{45 \times 60}{9}$ (M1) $= 300$ (A1).

(b) For example: the population did not change between the two visits, or the marked rabbits mixed randomly back into the population (B1).
:::

::: test
A sports club has 150 juniors, 270 adults and 180 seniors. A sample of 40 members is taken, stratified by these groups. How many adults should be in the sample? (2)
---
Total 600 (M1). $\dfrac{270}{600} \times 40 = 18$ adults (A1).
:::

::: test
Ella asked 30 randomly chosen people and found that 12 prefer brand A. Dan asked 300 randomly chosen people and found that 108 prefer brand A. (a) Work out each estimate of the proportion who prefer brand A. (b) Which estimate is more reliable? Give a reason. (3)
---
(a) Ella $\dfrac{12}{30} = 0.4$; Dan $\dfrac{108}{300} = 0.36$ (B1 B1).

(b) Dan's, because his sample is larger (B1).
:::

::: test
60 birds are ringed and released. A second sample of 40 birds is caught. Using these samples, the population is estimated to be 480. How many birds in the second sample were ringed? (3)
---
$\dfrac{m}{40} = \dfrac{60}{480}$ (M1). $m = \dfrac{60 \times 40}{480}$ (M1) $= 5$ (A1).
:::

::: test
A random sample of 30 bags of rice from a batch of 5000 has mean mass 1.02 kg. (a) Estimate the total mass of rice in the batch. (b) Give one reason why the estimate may not be accurate. (2)
---
(a) $5000 \times 1.02 = 5100$ kg (B1).

(b) A different sample of 30 would give a different mean — the sample may not be representative of all 5000 bags (B1).
:::

::: test
120 fish in a lake are tagged and released. Later, 90 fish are caught and 15 of them are tagged. (a) Estimate the number of fish in the lake. (b) It is later discovered that 20 of the tagged fish died before the second sample was taken. Explain whether your estimate in (a) is likely to be too high or too low, and work out a better estimate. (4)
---
(a) $\dfrac{120 \times 90}{15}$ (M1) $= 720$ (A1).

(b) Only 100 tagged fish were alive, so the calculation used too many tagged fish and the estimate is too high (B1). Better estimate: $\dfrac{100 \times 90}{15} = 600$ fish (B1).
:::
