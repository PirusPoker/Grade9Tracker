## What it is

**Simultaneous equations** are two equations that must be true *at the same time*. The solution is the pair of values $(x, y)$ that fits both. On a graph, it is the point (or points) where the two graphs cross. At GCSE Higher you must solve two kinds algebraically — **linear/linear** and **linear/quadratic** — and find approximate solutions from a graph.

### Linear/linear: elimination

Make the coefficients of one letter the same size, then add or subtract to get rid of it.

$$3x + 2y = 16 \qquad 5x - 3y = 14$$

Multiply the first by 3 and the second by 2 so both have $6y$:

$$9x + 6y = 48 \qquad 10x - 6y = 28$$

The signs of $6y$ are **different**, so **add**: $19x = 76$, $x = 4$. Substitute back: $12 + 2y = 16$, so $y = 2$. Check in the other equation: $5(4) - 3(2) = 14$ ✓.

Rule of thumb: **S**ame **S**igns **S**ubtract; different signs add.

### Linear/linear: substitution

If one equation already says $y = \dots$ or $x = \dots$, substitute it into the other.

$y = 2x - 1$ and $3x + 2y = 19$: $3x + 2(2x - 1) = 19 \Rightarrow 7x - 2 = 19 \Rightarrow x = 3$, and $y = 5$.

A linear/linear pair has **one** solution (the lines cross once), unless the lines are parallel (no solution) or the same line (infinitely many).

### Linear/quadratic: always substitute

When one equation has $x^2$, $y^2$ or $xy$, elimination does not work. Rearrange the **linear** equation to make $x$ or $y$ the subject, substitute into the quadratic one, and solve the resulting quadratic (by factorising or the formula — see A18).

$y = x^2 - 3x + 1$ and $y = 2x - 3$:

$$x^2 - 3x + 1 = 2x - 3 \Rightarrow x^2 - 5x + 4 = 0 \Rightarrow (x - 1)(x - 4) = 0$$

So $x = 1$ or $x = 4$. Now find **each** $y$ from the linear equation: $x = 1, y = -1$ and $x = 4, y = 5$. The answers come in **pairs**: $(1, -1)$ and $(4, 5)$.

### Lines and circles

$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ is a circle, centre the origin, radius $r$. With $y = x + 1$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 25$:

$$x^2 + (x + 1)^2 = 25 \Rightarrow 2x^2 + 2x - 24 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2 + x - 12 = 0 \Rightarrow (x + 4)(x - 3) = 0$$

$x = 3, y = 4$ or $x = -4, y = -3$. The line cuts the circle at $(3, 4)$ and $(-4, -3)$.

Expanding $(x + 1)^2$ is where most errors happen: it is $x^2 + 2x + 1$, **not** $x^2 + 1$.

### How many solutions?

The quadratic you get after substituting tells you how the line meets the curve:

- two different roots → the line crosses the curve at **two** points;
- one repeated root (discriminant $b^2 - 4ac = 0$) → the line **touches** the curve: it is a **tangent**;
- no real roots ($b^2 - 4ac < 0$) → the line **misses** the curve.

### Approximate solutions from a graph

Draw both graphs on the same axes. The solutions are the coordinates of the **intersection points**, read off to the accuracy of the grid (usually 1 decimal place). For example, $y = x^2 - 3$ and $y = x + 1$ cross at about $(2.6, 3.6)$ and $(-1.6, -0.6)$.

You may also be given a drawn curve and asked to solve a *different* equation with it. To solve $x^2 - x - 4 = 0$ using the graph of $y = x^2 - 3$, rearrange so one side is the curve: $x^2 - 3 = x + 1$. Draw the line $y = x + 1$ and read off the $x$-values where it crosses the curve: $x \approx 2.6$ or $x \approx -1.6$.

::: terms
- **Simultaneous equations**: two equations in two unknowns that must both be true for the same values.
- **Elimination**: scaling equations so one letter has matching coefficients, then adding or subtracting to remove it.
- **Substitution**: replacing one letter with an expression from the other equation, leaving one unknown.
- **Linear/quadratic pair**: one straight-line equation and one equation containing x², y² or xy; solved by substitution.
- **Point of intersection**: where two graphs cross; its coordinates are a solution of the pair of equations.
- **Tangent**: a line that touches a curve at exactly one point; the substituted quadratic has a repeated root.
:::

::: method
1. **Label the equations** (1) and (2). Examiners follow your working more easily and method marks are easier to award.
2. **Linear/linear:** choose elimination unless one equation already reads $y = \dots$. Multiply one or both equations so a coefficient matches; write the new equations out in full (M1). Add if the signs differ, subtract if they are the same.
3. **Linear/quadratic:** rearrange the linear equation for $x$ or $y$ (pick whichever avoids fractions), substitute into the quadratic equation (M1), expand brackets carefully, and collect everything to $= 0$ (M1).
4. **Solve the quadratic.** On Paper 1 (non-calculator) it will almost always factorise. On Papers 2 and 3, "give your answers to 2 decimal places" means use the quadratic formula.
5. **Find the partner value.** Substitute each $x$ into the **linear** equation to get its $y$. Pair them up: "$x = 3, y = 4$ or $x = -4, y = -3$". Unpaired lists lose the final A1.
6. **Check** in the equation you did not use for substitution — ten seconds that catches sign errors.
7. **Context questions:** define your letters ("let $a$ be the price of an adult ticket in pounds"), form the two equations, solve, then answer the question in words with units.
8. **"Show that the line is a tangent"**: substitute, reach a quadratic, and show it has a repeated root (or $b^2 - 4ac = 0$). Then write the conclusion: "one point of contact, so the line is a tangent".
9. **Graphical:** read intersections to the nearest half small square; give both coordinates if asked for solutions of a pair of equations, but only $x$-values if asked to solve a single equation in $x$.
:::

::: example Elimination with both equations scaled
Solve $4x + 3y = 6$ and $3x + 2y = 5$.

(1) $\times 2$: $8x + 6y = 12$. (2) $\times 3$: $9x + 6y = 15$.

Same signs, so subtract: $x = 3$. Substitute into (1): $12 + 3y = 6$, $3y = -6$, $y = -2$.

Check in (2): $9 - 4 = 5$ ✓. Solution $x = 3$, $y = -2$.
:::

::: example Forming equations from a context
Three adult tickets and two child tickets cost £38.50. Two adult tickets and five child tickets cost £49.50. Find the cost of each ticket.

Let $a$ and $c$ be the prices in pounds. $3a + 2c = 38.5$ (1) and $2a + 5c = 49.5$ (2).

(1) $\times 2$: $6a + 4c = 77$. (2) $\times 3$: $6a + 15c = 148.5$. Subtract: $11c = 71.5$, $c = 6.5$.

$3a + 13 = 38.5$, so $3a = 25.5$, $a = 8.5$. An adult ticket costs £8.50 and a child ticket £6.50.
:::

::: example Line and parabola
Solve $y = x^2 - 2x - 5$ and $y = x - 1$.

$x^2 - 2x - 5 = x - 1 \Rightarrow x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow (x - 4)(x + 1) = 0$.

$x = 4$: $y = 3$. $x = -1$: $y = -2$. Solutions $(4, 3)$ and $(-1, -2)$.
:::

::: example Line and circle with an awkward rearrangement
Solve $y - 2x = 1$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 13$.

$y = 2x + 1$, so $x^2 + (2x + 1)^2 = 13 \Rightarrow x^2 + 4x^2 + 4x + 1 = 13 \Rightarrow 5x^2 + 4x - 12 = 0$.

$(5x - 6)(x + 2) = 0$, so $x = \dfrac{6}{5}$ or $x = -2$.

$x = \dfrac65$: $y = \dfrac{12}{5} + 1 = \dfrac{17}{5}$. $x = -2$: $y = -3$.

Check: $\dfrac{36}{25} + \dfrac{289}{25} = \dfrac{325}{25} = 13$ ✓.
:::

::: example Grade 9: proving a line is a tangent
Show that the line $y = 3x - 10$ is a tangent to the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 10$, and find the point of contact.

$x^2 + (3x - 10)^2 = 10 \Rightarrow x^2 + 9x^2 - 60x + 100 = 10 \Rightarrow 10x^2 - 60x + 90 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0$.

$(x - 3)^2 = 0$ has the single repeated root $x = 3$ (equivalently $b^2 - 4ac = 36 - 36 = 0$). The line meets the circle at exactly one point, so it is a tangent.

$y = 9 - 10 = -1$. Point of contact $(3, -1)$.
:::

::: example Grade 9: answers to 2 decimal places
Solve $y = 2x + 3$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 12$, giving your answers to 2 decimal places.

$x^2 + (2x + 3)^2 = 12 \Rightarrow 5x^2 + 12x + 9 = 12 \Rightarrow 5x^2 + 12x - 3 = 0$.

$$x = \frac{-12 \pm \sqrt{144 + 60}}{10} = \frac{-12 \pm \sqrt{204}}{10}$$

$x = 0.2283\ldots$ or $x = -2.6283\ldots$. Use the **unrounded** values in $y = 2x + 3$: $y = 3.4566\ldots$ or $y = -2.2566\ldots$.

$x = 0.23, y = 3.46$ or $x = -2.63, y = -2.26$.
:::

::: q
Solve $2x + y = 11$ and $x - y = 1$.
---
Add: $3x = 12$, $x = 4$. Then $4 - y = 1$, $y = 3$.
:::

::: q
Solve $5x - 2y = 23$ and $3x + 4y = 19$.
---
First $\times 2$: $10x - 4y = 46$. Add to the second: $13x = 65$, $x = 5$. $25 - 2y = 23$, $y = 1$.
:::

::: q
Four pens and three rulers cost £3.90. Two pens and five rulers cost £3.70. Find the cost of one pen and one ruler.
---
In pence: $4p + 3r = 390$ and $2p + 5r = 370$. Second $\times 2$: $4p + 10r = 740$. Subtract: $7r = 350$, $r = 50$. $4p = 240$, $p = 60$. Pen 60p, ruler 50p.
:::

::: q
Solve $y = x^2 + 2$ and $y = 3x$.
---
$x^2 - 3x + 2 = 0$, $(x - 1)(x - 2) = 0$. $x = 1, y = 3$ or $x = 2, y = 6$.
:::

::: q
Solve $y = x^2 - 4x + 3$ and $y = x - 1$.
---
$x^2 - 5x + 4 = 0$, $(x - 1)(x - 4) = 0$. $x = 1, y = 0$ or $x = 4, y = 3$.
:::

::: q
Solve $x^2 + y^2 = 10$ and $y = x + 2$.
---
$x^2 + x^2 + 4x + 4 = 10$, $2x^2 + 4x - 6 = 0$, $x^2 + 2x - 3 = 0$, $(x + 3)(x - 1) = 0$. $x = 1, y = 3$ or $x = -3, y = -1$.
:::

::: q
Solve $x + y = 7$ and $xy = 12$.
---
$y = 7 - x$, so $x(7 - x) = 12$, $x^2 - 7x + 12 = 0$, $(x - 3)(x - 4) = 0$. $x = 3, y = 4$ or $x = 4, y = 3$.
:::

::: q
The graph of $y = x^2 - 2$ is drawn. What line should you draw on it to solve $x^2 - x - 2 = 0$, and what are the solutions?
---
$x^2 - 2 = x$, so draw $y = x$. They cross where $x^2 - x - 2 = 0$, i.e. $(x - 2)(x + 1) = 0$: $x = 2$ or $x = -1$.
:::

::: q
Solve $x + y = 4$ and $x^2 + 2y^2 = 22$, giving your answers to 2 decimal places.
---
$x = 4 - y$: $16 - 8y + y^2 + 2y^2 = 22$, $3y^2 - 8y - 6 = 0$, $y = \dfrac{8 \pm \sqrt{136}}{6}$. $y = 3.28, x = 0.72$ or $y = -0.61, x = 4.61$.
:::

::: watch
- **Squaring a bracket wrongly.** $(2x + 1)^2 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1$. Writing $4x^2 + 1$ throws away every later mark except the first M1.
- **Subtracting negatives.** $(10x - 6y) - (9x - 6y)$: take away each term, $-6y - (-6y) = 0$. Write the subtraction line out rather than doing it in your head.
- **Unpaired answers.** "$x = 3, -4$ and $y = 4, -3$" is ambiguous. Write $x = 3, y = 4$ **or** $x = -4, y = -3$.
- **Finding $y$ from the quadratic equation** gives extra, wrong partners (a circle has two $y$-values for each $x$). Always use the **linear** equation.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep the full calculator value of $x$ when you work out $y$.
- **Context answers without units or words** — say "an adult ticket costs £8.50".
:::

::: test
Show that the line $y = 2x - 5$ meets the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 5$ at exactly one point, and find its coordinates. (5)
---
$x^2 + (2x - 5)^2 = 5$ (M1). $5x^2 - 20x + 20 = 0$, so $x^2 - 4x + 4 = 0$ (A1). $(x - 2)^2 = 0$: one repeated root, so only one point of intersection (M1 A1 for the conclusion). $x = 2$, $y = -1$: $(2, -1)$ (A1).
:::

::: test
The line $y = x - 1$ meets the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at the points $A$ and $B$. Find the exact length of $AB$, giving your answer in the form $a\sqrt{2}$. (6)
---
$x^2 + (x - 1)^2 = 25$ (M1), $2x^2 - 2x - 24 = 0$, $x^2 - x - 12 = 0$ (A1). $(x - 4)(x + 3) = 0$ (M1): $A(4, 3)$ and $B(-3, -4)$ (A1). $AB = \sqrt{7^2 + 7^2} = \sqrt{98}$ (M1) $= 7\sqrt{2}$ (A1).
:::
