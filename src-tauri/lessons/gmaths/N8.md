## What it is

A calculator's decimal is often only an approximation: $\frac13 = 0.333\ldots$, $\sqrt2 = 1.41421\ldots$, $\pi = 3.14159\ldots$ never end. When a question says **"exact"**, **"in surd form"**, **"in terms of $\pi$"** or **"in the form $a\sqrt b$"**, you must keep these numbers as fractions, surds and multiples of $\pi$ and calculate with them directly.

### Exact fractions

Keep fractions as fractions and never round mid-calculation: $\frac23$ of $\frac{9}{10}$ is exactly $\frac{3}{5}$, whereas $0.67 \times 0.9 = 0.603$ is wrong. Exact answers are given in their simplest form (improper fractions or mixed numbers are both fine unless the question says otherwise).

### Surds

A **surd** is a root that cannot be simplified to a whole number or fraction: $\sqrt2$, $\sqrt{15}$, $\sqrt[3]{4}$ are surds; $\sqrt{25} = 5$ and $\sqrt{\frac49} = \frac23$ are not. Surds are irrational — their decimals never end or repeat.

The two rules that do almost everything:

$$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \times \sqrt b \qquad \sqrt{\frac ab} = \frac{\sqrt a}{\sqrt b}$$

and in particular $\sqrt a \times \sqrt a = a$.

**Simplifying.** Find the largest square factor, split it off and root it:

$$\sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4 \times 3} = \sqrt4 \times \sqrt3 = 2\sqrt3 \qquad \sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt2 \qquad \sqrt{75} = 5\sqrt3$$

If you use a smaller square first, keep going: $\sqrt{72} = 2\sqrt{18} = 2 \times 3\sqrt2 = 6\sqrt2$. A surd is fully simplified when the number under the root has no square factor except 1.

**Adding and subtracting.** Only like surds combine, just like like terms in algebra: $3\sqrt5 + 4\sqrt5 = 7\sqrt5$, but $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ cannot be simplified. Simplify first to reveal like surds:

$$\sqrt{50} + \sqrt{18} = 5\sqrt2 + 3\sqrt2 = 8\sqrt2$$

**Multiplying and dividing.** Multiply the numbers outside together and the numbers inside together:

$$2\sqrt3 \times 5\sqrt6 = 10\sqrt{18} = 10 \times 3\sqrt2 = 30\sqrt2 \qquad \frac{12\sqrt{10}}{3\sqrt5} = 4\sqrt2$$

**Expanding brackets.** Treat surds like algebra, using $\sqrt a \times \sqrt a = a$:

$$(3 + \sqrt2)(5 - \sqrt2) = 15 - 3\sqrt2 + 5\sqrt2 - 2 = 13 + 2\sqrt2$$

$$(\sqrt7 - 2)^2 = 7 - 2\sqrt7 - 2\sqrt7 + 4 = 11 - 4\sqrt7$$

The **difference of two squares** removes the surd completely:

$$(a + \sqrt b)(a - \sqrt b) = a^2 - b \qquad\text{e.g.}\quad (4 + \sqrt3)(4 - \sqrt3) = 16 - 3 = 13$$

### Rationalising the denominator

"Rationalise the denominator" means rewrite the fraction so there is no surd on the bottom. Multiply top and bottom by the same thing (so the value is unchanged):

- **Single surd on the bottom:** multiply by that surd.
$$\frac{6}{\sqrt3} = \frac{6 \times \sqrt3}{\sqrt3 \times \sqrt3} = \frac{6\sqrt3}{3} = 2\sqrt3 \qquad \frac{5}{2\sqrt{10}} = \frac{5\sqrt{10}}{20} = \frac{\sqrt{10}}{4}$$

- **Bracket like $a + \sqrt b$ on the bottom:** multiply by the version with the opposite sign, $a - \sqrt b$, and use the difference of two squares.
$$\frac{4}{3 + \sqrt5} = \frac{4(3 - \sqrt5)}{(3 + \sqrt5)(3 - \sqrt5)} = \frac{4(3 - \sqrt5)}{9 - 5} = 3 - \sqrt5$$

### Multiples of pi

Answers "in terms of $\pi$" treat $\pi$ like a letter: keep it as a factor and simplify the number in front.

- A circle of radius 6 cm: area $\pi r^2 = 36\pi$ cm², circumference $2\pi r = 12\pi$ cm.
- A sector of angle $120°$ in that circle: area $\frac{120}{360} \times 36\pi = 12\pi$ cm², arc length $\frac13 \times 12\pi = 4\pi$ cm, perimeter $4\pi + 12$ cm (the radii are not multiples of $\pi$, so they do not combine).
- A sphere of radius 3: volume $\frac43\pi \times 27 = 36\pi$.
- Working backwards: if a circle's area is $49\pi$ then $r^2 = 49$ and $r = 7$ — the $\pi$ cancels.

Exact answers often combine surds and $\pi$: a cone with radius 3 and height 4 has slant height $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, and a cone with radius 2 and height 4 has slant height $\sqrt{4 + 16} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt5$, so its curved surface area is $\pi r l = \pi \times 2 \times 2\sqrt5 = 4\sqrt5\,\pi$.

::: terms
- **Surd**: a root that cannot be written as a whole number or fraction, such as √2 or √15.
- **Irrational number**: a number that cannot be written as a fraction; its decimal never ends or repeats, e.g. √3 or π.
- **Simplest surd form**: a√b where b has no square factor other than 1, e.g. √72 = 6√2.
- **Like surds**: surds with the same number under the root, e.g. 3√5 and 7√5, which can be added.
- **Rationalise the denominator**: rewrite a fraction so that no surd remains on the bottom.
- **Difference of two squares**: (a + √b)(a − √b) = a² − b, which removes the surd.
- **In terms of π**: an exact answer that keeps π as a factor, e.g. 36π cm².
:::

::: method
1. **Spot the demand.** "Exact", "in surd form", "in terms of $\pi$", "in the form $a + b\sqrt c$" all forbid rounded decimals. These questions are common on Paper 1, but a calculator answer to 3 s.f. scores nothing on any paper when an exact form is asked.
2. **Simplify each surd first:** look for the largest square factor (4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100…). Then collect like surds.
3. **Multiply surds:** outside numbers together, inside numbers together, then simplify. Expand brackets fully (four terms for two brackets) and replace $\sqrt a \times \sqrt a$ by $a$.
4. **Rationalise:** single surd on the bottom → multiply top and bottom by it; $a \pm \sqrt b$ on the bottom → multiply top and bottom by $a \mp \sqrt b$. Expand the top, simplify the bottom with $a^2 - b$, then cancel any common factor.
5. **"Show that" questions** give the answer: write every line, including the multiplying-by line and the expanded bracket. The final line must match the target exactly.
6. **$\pi$ answers:** keep $\pi$ as a letter throughout; simplify the coefficient ($\frac{120}{360} \times 36\pi = 12\pi$). Do not add a multiple of $\pi$ to a number that is not.
7. **Method marks** come from the correct multiplier when rationalising, the correct square factor when simplifying, and a correct expansion — so show them.
:::

::: example Simplify and collect
Simplify $\sqrt{48} + \sqrt{27} - \sqrt{3}$.

$\sqrt{48} = \sqrt{16 \times 3} = 4\sqrt3$; $\sqrt{27} = \sqrt{9 \times 3} = 3\sqrt3$.

$4\sqrt3 + 3\sqrt3 - \sqrt3 = 6\sqrt3$.
:::

::: example Expanding surd brackets
Expand and simplify $(2 + 3\sqrt5)(4 - \sqrt5)$, giving your answer in the form $a + b\sqrt5$.

$2 \times 4 = 8$; $2 \times (-\sqrt5) = -2\sqrt5$; $3\sqrt5 \times 4 = 12\sqrt5$; $3\sqrt5 \times (-\sqrt5) = -15$.

$8 - 15 - 2\sqrt5 + 12\sqrt5 = -7 + 10\sqrt5$.
:::

::: example Rationalising a single surd
Write $\dfrac{14}{\sqrt7} + \sqrt{28}$ in the form $a\sqrt7$.

$\dfrac{14}{\sqrt7} = \dfrac{14\sqrt7}{7} = 2\sqrt7$, and $\sqrt{28} = \sqrt{4 \times 7} = 2\sqrt7$.

Total: $4\sqrt7$, so $a = 4$.
:::

::: example An exact answer in terms of pi
A sector has radius 9 cm and angle $80°$. Find its exact perimeter.

Arc: $\dfrac{80}{360} \times 2\pi \times 9 = \dfrac29 \times 18\pi = 4\pi$ cm.

Perimeter $= 4\pi + 9 + 9 = 4\pi + 18$ cm.
:::

::: example Grade 8–9: rationalising a bracket
Show that $\dfrac{3 + \sqrt2}{3 - \sqrt2}$ can be written as $\dfrac{11 + 6\sqrt2}{7}$.

Multiply top and bottom by $3 + \sqrt2$:

Top: $(3 + \sqrt2)^2 = 9 + 3\sqrt2 + 3\sqrt2 + 2 = 11 + 6\sqrt2$.

Bottom: $(3 - \sqrt2)(3 + \sqrt2) = 9 - 2 = 7$.

So $\dfrac{3 + \sqrt2}{3 - \sqrt2} = \dfrac{11 + 6\sqrt2}{7}$ as required.
:::

::: example Grade 9: surds in geometry
A right-angled triangle has legs $(2 + \sqrt3)$ cm and $(2 - \sqrt3)$ cm. Find, in simplest form, (a) its area, (b) the length of its hypotenuse.

(a) Area $= \frac12(2 + \sqrt3)(2 - \sqrt3) = \frac12(4 - 3) = \frac12$ cm².

(b) $h^2 = (2 + \sqrt3)^2 + (2 - \sqrt3)^2 = (7 + 4\sqrt3) + (7 - 4\sqrt3) = 14$, so $h = \sqrt{14}$ cm.
:::

---

::: q
Rationalise the denominators: (a) $\dfrac{10}{\sqrt5}$, (b) $\dfrac{9}{2\sqrt3}$.
---
(a) $\dfrac{10\sqrt5}{5} = 2\sqrt5$. (b) $\dfrac{9\sqrt3}{2 \times 3} = \dfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}$.

Rationalise the denominator of $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt7 - 2}$.
---
Multiply by $\sqrt7 + 2$: $\dfrac{6(\sqrt7 + 2)}{7 - 4} = 2(\sqrt7 + 2) = 2\sqrt7 + 4$.

Work out $\frac{5}{6} \times \frac{9}{10} - \frac{1}{4}$ exactly.
---

::: q
A circle has area $81\pi$ cm². Find its exact circumference.
---
$r^2 = 81$, $r = 9$. Circumference $= 18\pi$ cm.
:::

::: q
A cylinder has radius $2\sqrt3$ cm and height 5 cm. Find its exact volume.
---
$\pi r^2 h = \pi \times 12 \times 5 = 60\pi$ cm³.
:::

::: q
Find the exact value of $x$ if $x\sqrt2 = \sqrt{50} + \sqrt8$.
---
$\sqrt{50} + \sqrt8 = 5\sqrt2 + 2\sqrt2 = 7\sqrt2$, so $x = 7$.
:::

::: watch
- **Adding unlike surds.** $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3 \ne \sqrt5$, and $\sqrt{a + b} \ne \sqrt a + \sqrt b$.
- **Not simplifying fully.** $\sqrt{72} = 2\sqrt{18}$ is not finished; it is $6\sqrt2$.
- **Squaring a surd bracket with only two terms.** $(5 - \sqrt3)^2$ has a middle term: it is $28 - 10\sqrt3$, not $25 + 3$.
- **Rationalising only the bottom.** Whatever you multiply the denominator by, multiply the numerator by the same.
- **Wrong conjugate.** For $3 - \sqrt2$ on the bottom multiply by $3 + \sqrt2$ — the same numbers, opposite sign.
- **Decimal answers when "exact" is asked.** $113.1$ cm² instead of $36\pi$ cm² scores zero for the final mark.
- **Combining $\pi$ with numbers.** $4\pi + 18$ does not simplify to $22\pi$.
:::

::: test
Show that $\dfrac{(2 + \sqrt3)^2}{2 - \sqrt3}$ can be written as $a + b\sqrt3$, where $a$ and $b$ are integers to be found. (4)
---

::: test

---
Width $= \dfrac{23}{5 + \sqrt2} = \dfrac{23(5 - \sqrt2)}{25 - 2}$ (M1) $= 5 - \sqrt2$ cm (A1). Perimeter $= 2(5 + \sqrt2) + 2(5 - \sqrt2)$ (M1) $= 20$ cm (A1).
