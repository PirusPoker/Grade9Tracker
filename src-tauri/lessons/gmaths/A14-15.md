## What it is

Graphs in the real world are rarely neat straight lines. This topic is about **plotting** curves (reciprocal, exponential and "non-standard" ones), **reading** approximate solutions from them, and interpreting two measurements you can take from any graph: the **gradient** (a rate of change) and the **area underneath** (an accumulated total). There is no calculus at GCSE: gradients of curves are **estimated** with tangents, and areas under curves are **estimated** with strips.

### Plotting and reading curves

Make a table of values, plot each point with a small cross, and join with a **smooth curve** (never ruled segments). Then use the graph:

- To solve $f(x) = k$, draw the horizontal line $y = k$ and read off the $x$ value of every crossing.
- For a **non-standard function** such as $y = x^3 - 3x + 1$: the table for $x = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2$ gives $y = -1, 3, 1, -1, 3$. The curve crosses the $x$-axis three times, so $x^3 - 3x + 1 = 0$ has solutions of about $-1.9$, $0.3$ and $1.5$ — approximate, because they are read from a drawing.
- **Reciprocal** contexts: if 60 hours of work is shared between $n$ people, each works $t = \dfrac{60}{n}$ hours. The graph falls steeply at first then levels off, never reaching zero.
- **Exponential** contexts: repeated percentage change gives $y = ab^x$, where $a$ is the starting value (the $y$-intercept) and $b$ is the multiplier. $b > 1$ is growth, $0 < b < 1$ is decay. A car bought for £16 000 that loses 15% a year is worth $V = 16000 \times 0.85^t$.

To find $a$ and $b$ from two points on an exponential graph: if $y = ab^x$ passes through $(0, 3)$ and $(2, 48)$, then $a = 3$ and $3b^2 = 48$, so $b^2 = 16$ and $b = 4$.

### Gradient = rate of change

The gradient of a graph tells you how fast the $y$-quantity changes per unit of the $x$-quantity. Its **units** are ($y$-units) per ($x$-units).

| Graph | Gradient means | Units, for example |
|---|---|---|
| distance–time | speed | m/s, km/h |
| velocity–time | acceleration | m/s² |
| value–time | rate of increase or decrease | £ per year |
| cost–number bought | cost per item | £ per item |

A **negative** gradient is a decrease: on a velocity–time graph it is a deceleration; on a distance–time graph it means coming back towards the start.

For a **straight** section: gradient $= \dfrac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}$.

For a **curve** the gradient changes from point to point, so you estimate it:

- **At a point** (instantaneous rate): draw a **tangent** — a ruled line that just touches the curve there without cutting it. Pick two points on the tangent far apart, and find its gradient.
- **Between two points** (average rate): join the two points with a **chord** and find its gradient.

Example: a ball rolls so that its distance is $d = t^2$ metres after $t$ seconds. Average speed over the first 3 seconds: chord from $(0, 0)$ to $(3, 9)$, gradient $\dfrac93 = 3$ m/s. Speed at exactly $t = 3$: the tangent at $(3, 9)$ passes through about $(2, 3)$ and $(4, 15)$, gradient $\dfrac{12}{2} = 6$ m/s.

### Area under a graph = accumulated total

The area under a graph multiplies the two quantities: its units are ($y$-units) × ($x$-units). The one you must know: **the area under a velocity–time graph is the distance travelled** (m/s × s = m).

- Straight-line sections: split the area into rectangles, triangles and trapezia. Trapezium area $= \dfrac12(a + b)h$.
- Curves: split the region into vertical strips of equal width, join the tops with straight lines to make **trapezia**, and add their areas. This is an **estimate**.

**Over or under?** If the curve bends so that it lies **above** the straight tops of the strips (a curve that is "bulging upwards", like the top of a hill), the trapezia miss a sliver and the estimate is an **underestimate**. If the curve sags **below** the tops (like a valley), the estimate is an **overestimate**. More, narrower strips give a better estimate.

### Kinematics in one sentence each

- Distance–time: horizontal line = stationary; steeper = faster; curve getting steeper = speeding up.
- Velocity–time: horizontal line = constant speed; positive gradient = accelerating; negative = decelerating; area = distance.
- Average speed = total distance ÷ total time — not the average of the speeds.

::: terms
- **Rate of change**: how fast one quantity changes compared with another, given by the gradient of the graph.
- **Tangent**: a straight line that touches a curve at one point without crossing it there; its gradient estimates the gradient of the curve at that point.
- **Chord**: a straight line joining two points on a curve; its gradient is the average rate of change between them.
- **Velocity–time graph**: a graph whose gradient is the acceleration and whose area underneath is the distance travelled.
- **Trapezium strips**: equal-width vertical strips with straight tops, used to estimate the area under a curve.
- **Exponential model**: a formula y = abˣ for repeated percentage growth (b > 1) or decay (0 < b < 1), where a is the starting value.
- **Deceleration**: a negative acceleration — the object is slowing down, shown by a downward-sloping velocity–time graph.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the axes first.** Note the quantities and units on both axes, and the scale of each small square. Convert minutes to hours (or the reverse) before you work out a speed in km/h.
2. **Decide what is being asked:** a gradient (speed, acceleration, rate, cost per item) or an area (distance travelled).
3. **Gradient of a straight section:** change in $y$ ÷ change in $x$, with a negative sign if it slopes down. Always give units.
4. **Gradient of a curve at a point:** rule a tangent at that point (mark scheme: M1 for a tangent drawn), choose two points on it far apart, and compute rise ÷ run. Your answer only needs to be within the mark scheme's range, so a careful tangent matters more than the arithmetic.
5. **Area under a velocity–time graph:** split into rectangles, triangles and trapezia for straight lines. For a curve, use the number of strips the question says, find each height from the graph, and add the trapezium areas $\dfrac12(a + b)h$.
6. **Over or underestimate?** Compare the straight tops of the strips with the curve: tops below the curve → underestimate; tops above → overestimate. Say which, and why, in a sentence.
7. **Interpret** in the context's words and units: "the car travels about 350 m in the first 40 seconds", "the value falls by about £2200 per year".
8. **Solving from a graph** gives approximate answers. On calculator papers, you may be asked to check with the formula — trial and improvement or the $y^x$ key.
:::

::: example Straight-line velocity–time graph
A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 12 m/s in 4 s, travels at 12 m/s for 10 s, then slows uniformly to rest in 6 s. Find the acceleration, the deceleration and the total distance.

Acceleration $= \dfrac{12 - 0}{4} = 3$ m/s².

Deceleration: gradient $= \dfrac{0 - 12}{6} = -2$, so deceleration 2 m/s².

Distance = area: $\dfrac12 \times 4 \times 12 + 10 \times 12 + \dfrac12 \times 6 \times 12 = 24 + 120 + 36 = 180$ m.

(Or one trapezium: $\dfrac12(10 + 20) \times 12 = 180$ m.)
:::

::: example Estimating distance under a curved graph
A cyclist's velocity is recorded:

| $t$ (s) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $v$ (m/s) | 0 | 5 | 8 | 9 | 9.5 |

The graph is a smooth curve that rises steeply, then levels off. Use four strips of equal width to estimate the distance travelled in 8 s. Is it an over- or underestimate?

Each strip has width 2:

$\dfrac12(0 + 5)(2) + \dfrac12(5 + 8)(2) + \dfrac12(8 + 9)(2) + \dfrac12(9 + 9.5)(2) = 5 + 13 + 17 + 18.5 = 53.5$ m.

The curve bulges above the straight tops of the strips, so the trapezia miss some area: **underestimate**.
:::

::: example Tangent and chord on a distance–time curve
A ball's distance is given by $d = t^2$ metres. (a) Find its average speed between $t = 1$ and $t = 4$. (b) A tangent is drawn at $t = 3$; it passes through $(2, 3)$ and $(4, 15)$. Estimate the speed at $t = 3$.

(a) Chord from $(1, 1)$ to $(4, 16)$: $\dfrac{16 - 1}{4 - 1} = \dfrac{15}{3} = 5$ m/s.

(b) Tangent gradient $= \dfrac{15 - 3}{4 - 2} = 6$ m/s. The ball is speeding up: the speed at $t = 3$ is more than the average from $0$ to $3$ ($\dfrac93 = 3$ m/s).
:::

::: example An exponential model in finance
A car is bought for £16 000 and loses 15% of its value each year, so $V = 16000 \times 0.85^t$.

(a) Find the average rate of decrease over the first 2 years. (b) Use the model to find in which year the value first falls below £8000.

(a) $V(2) = 16000 \times 0.85^2 = £11\,560$. Change $= -£4440$ over 2 years: gradient $-£2220$ per year, a fall of £2220 per year on average.

(b) $V(4) = 16000 \times 0.85^4 = £8352.10$; $V(5) = 16000 \times 0.85^5 = £7099.29$. It falls below £8000 during the 5th year. (A graph would show the curve crossing $V = 8000$ at about $t = 4.3$.)
:::

::: example Finding an exponential model from its graph (grade 9)
The number of bacteria, $N$, after $t$ hours is modelled by $N = ab^t$. The graph passes through $(0, 500)$ and $(3, 4000)$.

(a) Find $a$ and $b$. (b) After how many whole hours does the number first exceed 50 000?

(a) At $t = 0$: $N = a = 500$. At $t = 3$: $500b^3 = 4000$, so $b^3 = 8$ and $b = 2$.

(b) Need $2^t > 100$. $2^6 = 64$, $2^7 = 128$. So $N(6) = 32\,000$ and $N(7) = 64\,000$: after **7 hours**.
:::

::: q
A distance–time graph is a straight line from $(0, 0)$ to (30 minutes, 12 km). Find the speed in km/h.
---
30 minutes $= 0.5$ hours. Speed $= 12 \div 0.5 = 24$ km/h.
:::

::: q
A velocity–time graph is a straight line from $(0, 4)$ to $(6, 16)$, with $t$ in seconds and $v$ in m/s. Find the acceleration and the distance travelled.
---
Acceleration $= \dfrac{16 - 4}{6} = 2$ m/s². Distance = trapezium $= \dfrac12(4 + 16) \times 6 = 60$ m.
:::

::: q
A car slows uniformly from 20 m/s to rest in 8 s. Find the deceleration and the braking distance.
---
Gradient $= \dfrac{0 - 20}{8} = -2.5$: deceleration 2.5 m/s². Distance = triangle $= \dfrac12 \times 8 \times 20 = 80$ m.
:::

::: q
The graphs of $y = \dfrac{12}{x}$ and $y = x + 1$ are drawn on the same axes. Find the $x$-coordinates of their intersections, and check them algebraically.
---
From the graph: $x = 3$ and $x = -4$. Check: $\dfrac{12}{x} = x + 1 \Rightarrow x^2 + x - 12 = 0 \Rightarrow (x + 4)(x - 3) = 0$. ✓
:::

::: q
The graph of $y = 2^x$ is drawn for $0 \le x \le 4$. Use it to estimate the solution of $2^x = 5$.
---
Draw $y = 5$ and read across: $x \approx 2.3$. (Check: $2^{2.3} \approx 4.9$, $2^{2.4} \approx 5.3$.)
:::

::: q
A tangent to a distance–time curve at $t = 5$ s passes through $(2, 0)$ and $(8, 30)$, with distance in metres. Estimate the speed at $t = 5$ s.
---
Gradient $= \dfrac{30 - 0}{8 - 2} = 5$ m/s.
:::

::: q
The cost of cheese, in £, is plotted against its mass in kg. What does the gradient represent, and in what units?
---
The price per kilogram, in £ per kg.
:::

::: q
The graph of $y = x^3 - 3x + 1$ is drawn. Which line should you draw to solve $x^3 - 3x - 1 = 0$, and what are the approximate solutions?
---
$x^3 - 3x - 1 = 0 \iff x^3 - 3x + 1 = 2$, so draw $y = 2$. Solutions about $x = -1.5$, $-0.3$ and $1.9$.
:::

::: q
£2000 is invested so that its value after $t$ years is $V = 2000 \times 1.04^t$. Find the average rate of increase over the first 5 years.
---
$V(5) = 2000 \times 1.04^5 = £2433.31$. Increase £433.31 over 5 years: about £86.66 per year.
:::

::: watch
- **Mixing up gradient and area.** On a velocity–time graph, the gradient is the acceleration and the area is the distance. On a distance–time graph the area means nothing useful.
- **Units and time scales.** A graph in minutes gives a speed in km per minute unless you convert: 12 km in 30 minutes is 24 km/h, not 0.4.
- **Gradient from the curve, not the tangent.** Take your two points from the ruled tangent line, far apart, not from the curve itself.
- **Wrong strip widths or heights.** Use exactly the number of strips asked for, read heights at the strip edges, and remember that the first strip may start at a height of 0.
- **Over/under without a reason.** Say "the curve lies above the tops of the trapezia, so it is an underestimate" — the reason earns the mark.
- **Average speed** is total distance ÷ total time, not the mean of two speeds.
:::

::: test
A walker covers 3.6 km at a steady speed in 45 minutes. Work out the gradient of her distance–time graph in km/h. (2)
---
45 minutes $= 0.75$ hours (M1). $3.6 \div 0.75 = 4.8$ km/h (A1).
:::

::: test
A train's speed increases uniformly from 5 m/s to 17 m/s in 4 seconds. (a) Work out its acceleration. (b) Work out the distance it travels in these 4 seconds. (3)
---
(a) $\dfrac{17 - 5}{4} = 3$ m/s² (B1). (b) $\dfrac12(5 + 17) \times 4$ (M1) $= 44$ m (A1).
:::

::: test
A velocity–time graph has time in seconds and velocity in m/s. State what is represented by (a) the gradient of the graph (b) the area under the graph. Give units. (2)
---
(a) Acceleration, in m/s² (B1). (b) Distance travelled, in metres (B1).
:::

::: test
The graph of $y = 3^x$ is drawn for $0 \le x \le 3$. Use the graph to find an estimate for the solution of $3^x = 20$. (2)
---
Line $y = 20$ drawn or used (M1). $x \approx 2.7$ (accept 2.6 to 2.8) (A1).
:::

::: test
A tangent is drawn to a distance–time curve at $t = 6$ s. It passes through $(3, 10)$ and $(9, 46)$, with distance in metres. Estimate the speed of the object at $t = 6$ s. (2)
---
$\dfrac{46 - 10}{9 - 3}$ (M1) $= 6$ m/s (A1).
:::

::: test
A car is slowing down. Its velocity, $v$ m/s, at time $t$ seconds is shown.

| $t$ | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $v$ | 20 | 16 | 13 | 11 | 10 |

The graph is a smooth curve that becomes less steep. (a) Use four strips of equal width to estimate the distance travelled in the 20 seconds. (b) Is your answer an overestimate or an underestimate? Give a reason. (4)
---
(a) $\dfrac52\big[(20 + 16) + (16 + 13) + (13 + 11) + (11 + 10)\big]$ (M1 for trapezia, M1 for the sum) $= 2.5 \times 110 = 275$ m (A1).
(b) Overestimate: the curve sags below the straight tops of the trapezia, so they include extra area (B1).
:::

::: test
A house is worth £250 000. Its value $t$ years later is modelled by $V = 250000 \times 1.03^t$. Work out the average rate of increase in value over the first 4 years. (3)
---
$250000 \times 1.03^4 = £281\,377.20$ (M1). $(281377.20 - 250000) \div 4$ (M1) $= £7844.30$ per year (A1).
:::

::: test
The curve $y = ab^x$, where $a$ and $b$ are positive constants, passes through $(1, 12)$ and $(3, 108)$. Find $a$ and $b$, and write down the coordinates of the point where the curve crosses the $y$-axis. (4)
---
$ab = 12$ and $ab^3 = 108$ (M1). Dividing: $b^2 = 9$, $b = 3$ (A1). $a = 4$ (A1). $y$-intercept $(0, 4)$ (B1ft).
:::

::: test
A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly for 10 seconds to a speed of $V$ m/s. It then travels at $V$ m/s for 20 seconds. The total distance travelled is 500 m. Find $V$ and the car's acceleration. (4)
---
Area $= \dfrac12 \times 10 \times V + 20V$ (M1) $= 25V$ (A1). $25V = 500$, $V = 20$ (A1). Acceleration $= \dfrac{20}{10} = 2$ m/s² (B1ft).
:::
