## What it is

Topic 7, People and the biosphere, opens **Paper 3** (Making Geographical Decisions). Section A is short, about 10 marks, and every question uses the **resource booklet**: a biome map, climate graphs, photos or facts about the country the paper is about. The same knowledge also feeds the Section D decision (12 marks plus 4 SPaG), where you must justify an option using the booklet and your wider course.

This key idea covers:
- the **global distribution and characteristics** of the major **biomes** (tropical, temperate and boreal forests; tropical and temperate grasslands; deserts; tundra), and how they are controlled by **climate**: temperature, precipitation and sunshine hours;
- how **local factors** (altitude, rock and soil type, drainage) alter biomes locally;
- how the **biotic** (plants and animals) and **abiotic** (soil, rock, water, atmosphere) parts of a biome **interact**.

Integrated skills: **comparing climate graphs** and **using world maps** of biomes. Any accurate examples earn the marks.

### What a biome is

A **biome** is a very large ecosystem, covering a large part of a continent, with a distinctive climate, vegetation and animal life. The **biosphere** is the part of the Earth where life exists: the thin layer of land, water and air that contains all biomes.

### The major biomes

| Biome | Where (latitude and examples) | Climate | Vegetation and life |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tropical rainforest** | near the equator, roughly within 10° N and S: the Amazon basin, the Congo basin, Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia) | hot all year (about 26–28 °C), very small annual range; heavy rain all year, often over 2,000 mm | tall evergreen trees in layers, the highest biodiversity of any land biome |
| **Tropical grassland (savanna)** | between the rainforests and hot deserts, about 5–20° N and S: East Africa, central Brazil, northern Australia | hot all year; a distinct **wet season** and long **dry season** | grasses with scattered drought-resistant trees such as acacia and baobab; large herds of grazing animals |
| **Hot desert** | about 15–30° N and S, often on west sides of continents: Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, central Australia | very low rainfall (under about 250 mm); very hot days, cold nights; clear skies | sparse plants adapted to drought, such as cacti with water-storing stems; many animals active at night |
| **Temperate grassland** | interiors of continents in mid-latitudes, about 40–55°: North American prairies, Eurasian steppe, Argentinian pampas | hot summers, cold winters; low to moderate rainfall | grasses; few trees because it is too dry and fires are common; deep fertile soils, now often farmed for cereals |
| **Temperate deciduous forest** | about 40–60° N, often on western coasts and in east Asia: western Europe including the UK, eastern USA, eastern China | mild, four seasons; rain all year, moderate totals | broad-leaved trees such as oak and beech that drop their leaves in winter |
| **Boreal forest (taiga)** | about 50–70° N only: Canada, Alaska, Scandinavia, Russia | long, very cold winters; short, mild summers; low precipitation, much as snow | evergreen conifers such as spruce, pine and fir; low biodiversity |
| **Tundra** | north of the taiga, roughly beyond 65° N: northern Canada, Alaska, northern Russia, Greenland's coasts; also high mountains | very cold, with only a few months above freezing; very low precipitation; **permafrost** | no trees; mosses, lichens, grasses and dwarf shrubs; short growing season |

When describing a distribution, use **latitudes**, **continents** and **named examples**, and say whether the biome is in one or both hemispheres. The taiga and tundra, for example, are found almost only in the **Northern Hemisphere**, because there is little land at those latitudes in the south.

### How climate controls biomes

**Temperature** depends mainly on **latitude**. Near the equator the sun is high in the sky all year, so its energy is concentrated on a small area. Towards the poles the sun is lower, so the same energy is spread over a larger area and passes through more atmosphere. Temperature decides the length of the **growing season** (plants grow above about 6 °C).

**Precipitation** follows the global **pressure belts** (key idea 1.1):
- **Low pressure at the equator:** air rises, cools and condenses, giving heavy convectional rain, so rainforests.
- **High pressure at about 30° N and S:** air sinks and warms, so clouds do not form, giving **hot deserts**.
- **Low pressure at about 60°:** rain-bearing depressions, so temperate forests.
- **High pressure at the poles:** cold, sinking, dry air, so tundra and polar deserts.
- Savannas lie between, getting rain only when the zone of rising air moves over them in summer, hence their wet and dry seasons.
- **Continental interiors** are far from the sea, so they are dry, explaining temperate grasslands.

**Sunshine hours** affect how much energy is available for photosynthesis. Deserts have the most sunshine but lack water. Rainforests have high energy but frequent cloud. At high latitudes, summer days are long but winter days very short, so the growing season is brief.

The key idea: water and heat together set limits. Where both are plentiful, **productivity** and **biodiversity** are highest (rainforest). Where either is lacking (desert: water; tundra: heat), life is sparse.

### Local factors

| Factor | How it alters the biome | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Altitude** | temperature falls by about 6–7 °C for every 1,000 m of height, and precipitation and wind change, so biomes form **zones** up a mountain | on high mountains near the equator, rainforest at the base gives way to montane forest, then grassland and alpine tundra-like vegetation, then bare rock and ice |
| **Rock type** | rock weathers to form soil, and controls its minerals and how permeable it is | in the UK, chalk and limestone give thin, alkaline soils supporting grassland with many flowers; sandstone gives acidic sandy soils with heathland |
| **Soil type** | fertility, depth and acidity decide what plants grow | thin, nutrient-poor soils support only hardy plants; deep fertile soils support forest or farming |
| **Drainage** | waterlogged ground supports wetland plants instead of forest; free-draining ground can be drier than the climate suggests | marshes and peat bogs within forest biomes; ribbons of trees along rivers and around **oases** in deserts |

So climate explains the **global** pattern, while local factors explain **variations within** a biome. Exam questions often ask you to weigh these.

### Biotic and abiotic interactions

| Biotic (living) | Abiotic (non-living) |
|---|---|
| **flora** (plants), **fauna** (animals), fungi, bacteria | **soil**, **rock**, **water**, **atmosphere** (climate) |

Every biome works through links between them:
- **Climate to plants:** heat and rain decide which plants can grow and how fast.
- **Plants to soil:** leaf litter falls and is broken down by **decomposers**, returning nutrients to the soil. Roots bind the soil against erosion.
- **Soil to plants:** nutrients and water in the soil support plant growth.
- **Rock to soil:** weathering of rock releases minerals and forms the soil.
- **Plants to atmosphere:** photosynthesis takes in carbon dioxide and releases oxygen; transpiration adds water vapour, which can fall as rain.
- **Animals to plants:** animals eat plants, pollinate flowers and disperse seeds; their waste and remains add nutrients.
- **Climate to decomposition:** in hot, wet rainforests decay is fast, so nutrients cycle quickly; in the cold taiga and tundra decay is slow, so dead material builds up as thick litter or peat.

A change to one component affects the others. Clearing trees, for example, exposes soil to heavy rain, so nutrients are washed out and the soil quickly becomes infertile.

### Comparing climate graphs (sample data)

| Station | Warmest month (°C) | Coldest month (°C) | Annual precipitation (mm) | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 28 | 26 | 2,600 | rain every month |
| Q | 16 | −25 | 450 | most in summer |
| R | 34 | 15 | 60 | almost none, any month |

- **Annual temperature range** = warmest month − coldest month. P: 28 − 26 = **2 °C**. Q: 16 − (−25) = **41 °C**. R: 34 − 15 = **19 °C**.
- P is a **tropical rainforest** (hot, wet, tiny range). Q is **taiga** (huge range, cold winters, low precipitation). R is a **hot desert** (very low rainfall, hot).
- When comparing, use **comparative language** ("P is 12 °C warmer in its coldest month than R") and **quote data** for both stations.

::: terms
- **Biome**: a very large ecosystem covering a large area, with its own distinctive climate, vegetation and animals.
- **Biosphere**: the part of the Earth where life exists, including all living things and the land, water and air they live in.
- **Biotic**: the living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
- **Abiotic**: the non-living parts of an ecosystem, such as soil, rock, water and the atmosphere.
- **Flora**: the plant life of an area or biome.
- **Fauna**: the animal life of an area or biome.
- **Altitude**: height above sea level, which lowers temperature and changes the vegetation found.
- **Permafrost**: ground that stays frozen all year, found under much of the tundra.
- **Growing season**: the part of the year warm enough for plants to grow, usually when temperatures are above about 6 °C.
- **Annual temperature range**: the difference between the warmest and coldest monthly mean temperatures.
- **Decomposers**: organisms such as fungi and bacteria that break down dead material and return nutrients to the soil.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the resource first:** what does the figure show, and for which country or region?
2. **Identify or state (1–2):** take the answer from the figure, or give precise knowledge (two separate points for "state two").
3. **Describe a distribution:** latitudes, continents, named examples, hemisphere, and any anomaly.
4. **Explain (2 or 4):** link a **climate control** (temperature, rain, sunshine) or **local factor** to a **characteristic** of the biome, then develop it ("so…", "which means…"). For "Using Figure n…", quote figures from it.
5. **Climate graphs:** calculate the range and totals, then compare with data for both places.
6. **8-mark assess:** weigh climate against local factors, or one interaction against others, using the resource and your own knowledge; finish with a supported judgement.
7. **12-mark decision:** choose one option, justify it with the resource, compare it with the other two, consider people and environment, short and long term.
:::

Study a world biome map. Identify the biome most likely to be found between the taiga and the Arctic Ocean in northern Canada. A: Temperate grassland. B: Tundra. C: Tropical grassland. D: Hot desert. (1)

B (1). The tundra lies north of the taiga, where it is too cold for trees.
:::

::: example State two local factors
State two local factors that can affect the distribution of biomes. (2)

Altitude (1); drainage, or rock type, or soil type (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: climate and the rainforest
Explain two ways in which climate influences the characteristics of tropical rainforests. (4)

High temperatures and rain all year mean plants can grow continuously (1), so the forest is evergreen, very dense and has the highest biodiversity of any land biome (1). Heavy daily rain and heat speed up decomposition (1), so nutrients are recycled quickly and most are held in the vegetation rather than in the thin soil (1).
:::

::: example Calculation: annual temperature range
Sample data: a taiga station has a January mean of −22 °C and a July mean of 17 °C. Calculate the annual temperature range. (1)

17 − (−22) = 39 °C (1).

::: example 8-mark: climate against local factors
A resource booklet extract shows: a biome map of East Africa with savanna across most of the lowlands; a mountain rising to over 5,000 m with forest, then moorland, then bare rock and ice; a river crossing dry savanna lined with a band of trees; and a swamp where the ground is waterlogged. Using the resource and your own knowledge, assess the importance of climate compared with local factors in explaining the distribution of biomes. (8)

At the global scale, climate is clearly the most important factor. The savanna covering most of East Africa exists because the region lies between the equatorial low-pressure belt and the subtropical highs, so it is hot all year with a wet and a dry season. This seasonal rainfall supports grass and scattered trees but is too dry for rainforest, and the same pattern is repeated at similar latitudes in Brazil and northern Australia.

However, the resource shows that local factors cause important variations within this climate zone. Altitude has the biggest effect: on the mountain, temperature falls by about 6–7 °C per 1,000 m, so savanna gives way to forest, moorland and finally bare rock and ice near 5,000 m, even close to the equator. Drainage also matters: the river provides water all year, so a band of trees survives through the dry season, and the waterlogged swamp supports wetland plants instead of grassland.

These local effects, though, cover small areas compared with the savanna, and even they work through climate: altitude matters because it changes temperature and rainfall.

Overall, climate explains the broad distribution of biomes across continents, while local factors such as altitude and drainage explain smaller patches within them. Climate is therefore more important, but a full explanation needs both.

Temperature falls by about 6–7 °C for every 1,000 m (1), so on a high mountain, forest is replaced by grassland or tundra-like vegetation higher up, even near the equator (1).

Explain one interaction between the biotic and abiotic parts of a biome. (2)

Leaves fall and are broken down by decomposers (1), returning nutrients to the soil that plants then take up (1). Accept roots binding soil, transpiration adding moisture to the air, rock weathering into soil, with development.

::: q
Compare the climate of the tropical rainforest with the climate of the taiga. (3)
---
The rainforest is much hotter all year, whereas the taiga has very cold winters (1); the rainforest has a very small annual range, whereas the taiga's is very large, often over 35 °C (1); the rainforest has far more precipitation, often over 2,000 mm compared with a few hundred mm in the taiga (1).

::: watch
- Describing a distribution with "near the poles" or "hot places". Give **latitudes**, **continents** and **examples**.
- Putting the taiga in the Southern Hemisphere. It is found almost only in the **north**.
- Mixing up **biotic** (living) and **abiotic** (non-living).
- Explaining deserts as "hot so no rain". The cause is **sinking air in high pressure**.
- Comparing climate graphs without **data for both** places or without comparative words.
- Getting the range wrong with negatives: 16 − (−25) is **41**, not −9.
- In the 8-marker, ignoring the resource. "Using the resource" means quoting it.
