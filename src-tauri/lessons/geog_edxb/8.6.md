## What it is

This key idea says **wilderness areas** in the taiga need to be **protected from over-exploitation**. It covers:
- the **challenges** of **creating and maintaining** protected **wilderness areas**, **national parks** and **sustainable forestry** in the taiga;
- the **reasons for conflicting views** on **protecting or exploiting** the forest and its natural resources.

Any real taiga examples earn the marks. This course uses **Canada** (**Wood Buffalo National Park**, the Alberta oil sands, First Nations-led protected areas) and **Russia** (the **Virgin Komi Forests**). Questions here often give you **stakeholder quotes** in the resource booklet and ask you to explain or assess why views differ, and this topic feeds directly into the Section D decision.

### Why protect the taiga?

- **Carbon store:** taiga trees, soils, **peat** and **permafrost** hold an enormous amount of carbon. Damage releases it and speeds climate change.
- **Wildlife:** caribou, wolves, bears, lynx and billions of **migratory birds** that breed in the boreal forest each summer.
- **Fresh water:** countless lakes, rivers and wetlands.
- **People:** the homelands of indigenous peoples such as the **Cree**, **Dene** and **Métis** in Canada and the **Evenki** and **Komi** in Russia.
- **Slow recovery:** because the taiga grows so slowly (see 8.2), damage lasts for generations, so prevention is far better than repair.

### Wilderness areas and national parks

A **wilderness area** is a large, mostly undisturbed natural area with little or no permanent development. A **national park** is an area protected by law for its landscape, wildlife and heritage, usually with controls on development.

**Wood Buffalo National Park, Canada**
- **Canada's largest national park**, created in **1922** to protect the last herds of **wood bison**, spanning northern Alberta and the Northwest Territories.
- A **UNESCO World Heritage Site** since **1983**. It contains the **Peace–Athabasca Delta**, one of the world's largest inland freshwater deltas, and the only natural nesting area of the endangered **whooping crane**.
- **Challenges:**
  - **Threats from outside its boundaries:** upstream **HEP dams** on the Peace River change water flows to the delta, and **oil sands** development upstream on the Athabasca River raises concerns about pollution.
  - After a petition from local First Nations, **UNESCO** investigated and warned that the park's values were at risk; Canada responded with an action plan.
  - **Climate change** is drying parts of the delta and changing fire patterns.
  - The park's early history **excluded** indigenous people from hunting on their own lands, and rebuilding trust has taken decades.

**Virgin Komi Forests, Russia**
- A World Heritage Site in the northern Urals, one of the largest areas of untouched boreal forest in Europe.
- **Challenges:** proposals for **gold mining** and pressure for logging have threatened parts of it; enforcement in remote areas is weak, and illegal logging is a wider problem across Russia's taiga.

**Indigenous-led protection, Canada**
- Canada is creating **Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas**, where First Nations lead or share management. **Thaidene Nëné** in the Northwest Territories (2019) is co-managed with the Łutsël K'é Dene First Nation.
- **Indigenous Guardians** programmes pay community members to monitor land, wildlife and water.
- **Advantages:** combines traditional knowledge with science; local people gain jobs and a say; protection is more trusted and lasting.

### Challenges of creating and maintaining protected areas

| Challenge | Why it matters in the taiga |
|---|---|
| **Vast size and remoteness** | huge areas with few roads are very expensive to patrol, monitor and fight fires in |
| **Pressure for resources** | governments and companies are reluctant to protect land containing oil, gas, minerals or timber; park boundaries may be drawn to leave out resource-rich areas |
| **Threats cross boundaries** | acid rain, polluted rivers, upstream dams, fires, pests and climate change do not stop at a park boundary |
| **Funding** | remote parks attract few tourists, so they earn little money of their own |
| **Climate change** | warming may shift the taiga northwards, so a fixed park may not protect the right habitat in future |
| **Indigenous rights** | creating parks without consent can remove people's rights to hunt, trap and fish; protection must be negotiated |
| **Enforcement** | weak governance and corruption, especially in parts of Russia, allow illegal logging and poaching |

### Sustainable forestry

**Sustainable forestry** means harvesting timber without reducing the forest's ability to regrow, its biodiversity, or its value for the future.

**Methods:**
- **Selective logging** or small cuts instead of large clear-cuts.
- **Long rotations**, allowing for the slow regrowth of taiga trees (many decades).
- **Replanting with mixed native species**, not monocultures.
- Leaving **buffer strips** along rivers and lakes, and patches of **old-growth forest** for wildlife such as caribou.
- **Certification:** the **FSC** (Forest Stewardship Council) label shows timber and paper come from responsibly managed forest. Canada has one of the largest areas of FSC-certified forest in the world.

**Challenges:**
- Slow regrowth means sustainable yields are **small**, so profits are lower than from clear-cutting.
- Certification and monitoring **cost money**, and buyers are not always willing to pay more.
- **Illegal logging**, especially in Russia, undercuts legal producers with cheap timber.
- Pests and fires can destroy managed forest regardless of how carefully it is logged.
- Forestry towns depend on mills, so cutting harvests threatens **jobs**.

### Conflicting views: protect or exploit?

| Stakeholder | Likely view | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **National and provincial governments** | mostly favour exploitation, with some protection | taxes, export income and jobs from oil, timber and minerals; energy security; but they also have climate targets |
| **Oil, mining and forestry companies** | exploit | profit; they argue reclamation, replanting and certification make it responsible |
| **Workers in resource towns** (e.g. Fort McMurray) | exploit | well-paid jobs; whole towns depend on mines and mills |
| **First Nations** | **divided** | some oppose development that harms hunting, trapping, fishing, water and health; others run businesses and sign benefit agreements bringing jobs and income; all want the right to be consulted and to give consent |
| **Environmental groups** (e.g. WWF, Greenpeace) | protect | carbon store, caribou, migratory birds, wilderness value |
| **Scientists** | mostly protect | evidence of biodiversity loss, carbon release and slow recovery |
| **The global community and consumers** | mixed | want action on climate change, but also cheap fuel, paper and packaging |

**Reasons the views conflict:**
- **Different values:** economic (income, jobs), environmental (biodiversity, carbon) and cultural (land, identity, tradition).
- **Different timescales:** exploitation brings benefits **now**; the costs (lost carbon, slow regrowth) last for **generations**.
- **Who gains and who loses:** profits and tax often go to cities, companies and governments far away, while local and indigenous communities bear the pollution and habitat loss.
- **Scale:** the taiga's carbon matters to the **whole world**, but decisions are made by **national** and **provincial** governments under pressure to create jobs.
- **Uncertainty:** people disagree about how harmful development is and how well land can be reclaimed.
- **Rights:** disputes over who owns the land and who must give consent.

**Exam tip:** the best answers explain **why** a stakeholder holds a view, recognise that groups are **not uniform** (First Nations views vary), and weigh which reasons matter most.

::: terms
- **Wilderness area**: a large, mostly undisturbed natural area with little or no permanent human development.
- **National park**: an area protected by law to conserve its landscape, wildlife and heritage, with controls on development.
- **World Heritage Site**: a place recognised by UNESCO as having outstanding value to humanity, which countries agree to protect.
- **Sustainable forestry**: harvesting timber in ways that allow the forest to regrow and keep its biodiversity for the future.
- **Selective logging**: felling only chosen trees, leaving the rest of the forest standing.
- **FSC certification**: a label from the Forest Stewardship Council showing timber or paper comes from responsibly managed forest.
- **Stakeholder**: a person or group with an interest in a decision, such as a government, company or local community.
- **First Nations**: the indigenous peoples of Canada, other than the Inuit and Métis.
- **Co-management**: shared management of a protected area by government and indigenous or local communities.
- **Over-exploitation**: using a resource faster than it can be replaced, so its stock declines.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the place and the type of protection**: national park, wilderness area, indigenous protected area, certified forestry.
2. **Challenges**: give the reason **specific to the taiga** (vast, remote, resource-rich, slow-growing, threats crossing boundaries).
3. **Conflicting views**: name the stakeholder, give the view, then **why** (values, jobs, timescale, rights).
4. **Resource quotes**: use a quote from the figure as evidence for each viewpoint.
5. **8-mark assess**: weigh which reasons for conflict matter most, recognise disagreement within groups, and reach a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain one: why First Nations views differ
Explain one reason why First Nations do not all share the same view about exploiting the taiga. (2)

Some communities depend on hunting, trapping and fishing, and fear pollution and habitat loss (1), while others gain jobs and income through business and benefit agreements with oil or forestry companies, so they support some development (1).

::: example 8-mark assess: conflicting views
Study a resource with three quotes: an oil sands worker ("this job pays for my family's home"), a First Nations elder downstream ("our river and our hunting are being poisoned"), and a conservation group ("the boreal forest is one of the planet's greatest carbon stores"). Assess the reasons why there are conflicting views about exploiting the taiga. (8)

The quotes show that views conflict mainly because people value the taiga in different ways. For the worker, its value is economic: oil sands jobs pay well and support whole towns such as Fort McMurray. For the conservation group, its value is environmental: the forest, peat and permafrost hold huge amounts of carbon, and damage releases it.

A second reason is that costs and benefits fall on different people. Profits and taxes go to companies and governments, while downstream communities, like the elder's, bear pollution and loss of hunting. This makes the elder's view understandable even if the region as a whole gains income.

Timescale and scale also matter. Jobs are needed now, but the taiga regrows so slowly that damage lasts for generations, and its carbon matters to the whole world while decisions are made locally. Views also differ within groups: some First Nations sign benefit agreements for jobs.

Overall, the most important reason is the clash of values between short-term economic gain and long-term environmental and cultural loss, made sharper because those who gain are not those who lose.

(Level 3: all three quotes used (AO4); several reasons weighed, including divisions within groups; a supported judgement (AO3).)
:::

::: watch
- Giving a stakeholder's view without the **reason** for it.
- Treating First Nations as a single group with one view. Views **differ** between and within communities.
- Forgetting the practical problem: protected areas are **vast and remote**, so monitoring is costly.
- Ignoring threats that **cross boundaries** (upstream dams, pollution, acid rain, climate change).
- Describing sustainable forestry without its **challenges** (slow growth, cost, illegal logging, jobs).
- In the 8-marker, listing views without **weighing** which reasons for conflict matter most.
:::
