## What it is

Since 1990 India has changed from a slow-growing, inward-looking economy into one of the world's largest and fastest-growing. This key idea covers:
- the **economic trends** since 1990: GDP, GNI per capita, the changing importance of sectors, imports and exports, and the type and origin of foreign direct investment (FDI);
- the role of **globalisation**: communications and transport technology, TNCs and outsourcing;
- the role of **government policy**: tied or multilateral aid, investment in education and infrastructure, and pro-FDI policy.

Figures here are rounded on purpose; in the exam, use the data in the figure you are given.

### Economic trends since 1990

| Indicator | Trend |
|---|---|
| **GDP** | has grown many times over since 1990; India is now one of the five largest economies in the world by total GDP, with growth often among the fastest of any large economy |
| **GNI per capita** | has risen several times over, but remains far below developed countries because the wealth is shared among so many people |
| **Primary sector** (farming) | its **share of GDP** has fallen steadily to well under a fifth, but it still **employs more people than any other sector** |
| **Secondary sector** (manufacturing, construction) | a fairly steady share of GDP; car making, steel, pharmaceuticals and textiles have grown |
| **Tertiary and quaternary sectors** (services, IT, research) | now **more than half of GDP**; IT services, call centres, finance, telecoms and retail have boomed |
| **Exports** | grown hugely: IT and business services, refined petroleum, generic medicines, textiles and clothing, gems and jewellery, cars |
| **Imports** | also grown: crude oil (India imports most of its oil), gold, electronics and machinery |
| **FDI** | rose sharply after the 1990s reforms; it goes mainly into **services, computer software and hardware, telecoms, cars and manufacturing**; the largest sources include Singapore and Mauritius (often used as routes for investors because of tax agreements), the USA, Japan, the Netherlands and the UK |

The pattern matters: India skipped much of the factory-led stage that Rostow's model expects (2.2) and grew through **services**, while hundreds of millions remain in farming.

### The role of globalisation

**Communications technology**
- Fibre-optic cables, satellites and cheap internet and phone calls let services be delivered from anywhere.
- This allowed **business process outsourcing** (BPO): call centres, IT support, accounting and software for Western firms.

**Transport technology**
- Container shipping and bigger ports cut the cost of exporting goods.
- Cheaper air travel links Indian businesses to world markets and brings visitors.

**TNCs and outsourcing**
- Foreign TNCs such as Microsoft, Google, IBM and Amazon have large offices in **Bengaluru** and **Hyderabad**; car makers such as Hyundai and Suzuki build cars for India and for export.
- UK and US companies **outsource** call centres and back-office work to India, attracted by lower wages and an educated, English-speaking workforce.
- Indian TNCs have gone global: **Tata** (which owns Jaguar Land Rover and Tetley), **Infosys**, **Wipro** and Tata Consultancy Services sell IT services worldwide.

### The role of government policy

**The 1991 reforms (liberalisation)**
- In 1991 India faced a financial crisis and could barely pay for imports. The government responded by opening up the economy.
- Import tariffs were cut, the system of licences controlling business (the "licence raj") was largely scrapped, some state-owned firms were privatised, and foreign firms were allowed to invest.
- This is the turning point for most of the trends above.

**Pro-FDI policy**
- Limits on foreign ownership have been raised in many sectors, and approval made automatic in many cases.
- **Special economic zones** offer tax breaks to exporters.
- The **Make in India** campaign (from 2014) tries to attract manufacturing investment.

**Education investment**
- The **Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs)** and other universities produce large numbers of engineers and IT graduates.
- A big expansion of schooling has raised literacy, although quality varies.

**Infrastructure investment**
- The **Golden Quadrilateral** highway, new and expanded airports and ports, metro systems in cities such as Delhi and Mumbai, and expanding power and broadband networks.

**Aid**
- **Tied aid** must be spent on goods or services from the donor country; it helps, but benefits the donor's firms too.
- **Multilateral aid** comes through organisations such as the World Bank, funding infrastructure, health and education.
- India has received both, but aid is now small compared with its economy, and India gives aid to neighbouring countries itself.

### Skills: economic data and proportional flow-line maps

- **Numerical data:** calculate percentage change ((new − old) ÷ old × 100), compare growth rates, and describe trends with figures.
- **Proportional flow-line maps** show trade with arrows whose **width is proportional to value**. Describe the largest flows, the direction, and the pattern (for example, large flows to the USA, China, the UAE and the EU), and read values from the scale.

::: terms
- **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**: the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
- **GNI per capita**: the total income of a country's people and businesses, including from abroad, divided by its population.
- **Economic sector**: a group of industries: primary (raw materials), secondary (manufacturing), tertiary (services) and quaternary (research and IT).
- **Liberalisation**: opening an economy by cutting tariffs and regulations and allowing foreign investment, as India did in 1991.
- **Outsourcing**: a company paying another firm, often abroad, to carry out work such as call centres or IT.
- **Business process outsourcing (BPO)**: outsourcing back-office and customer services such as call centres and accounts.
- **Tied aid**: aid given on condition that it is spent on goods or services from the donor country.
- **Multilateral aid**: aid given through international organisations such as the World Bank.
- **Special economic zone (SEZ)**: an area with tax breaks and lighter rules to attract investment and exports.
- **Proportional flow-line map**: a map using arrows whose width is proportional to the size of a flow.
:::

::: method
1. **Trend questions:** describe the direction and size of change with figures from the resource, then explain it.
2. **Calculations:** percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; show working and units.
3. **Globalisation or policy?** Sort each factor: technology and TNCs are globalisation; reforms, SEZs, IITs, infrastructure and aid are government policy.
4. **Explain one (2 or 3 marks):** factor, how it works, then the economic result.
5. **8-mark assess:** explain both sets of factors, show how they interact (policy let globalisation in), and judge which was more important.
:::

::: example Explain one: outsourcing
Explain one way outsourcing has contributed to India's economic development. (2)

UK and US firms moved call centres and IT work to cities such as Bengaluru (1), creating millions of well-paid service jobs and increasing exports of services (1).
:::

::: example Percentage change
Sample data: a country's GDP was 300 billion US dollars in 1990 and 3,300 billion in 2023. Calculate the percentage increase. (2)

(3,300 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 (1) = 1,000% increase (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: government policy
Explain two ways government policy has helped India's economic growth. (4)

The 1991 reforms cut tariffs and allowed foreign investment (1), so TNCs set up factories and offices, bringing jobs and capital (1). Investment in the Indian Institutes of Technology (1) produced many engineers and IT graduates, attracting IT companies looking for skilled, English-speaking workers (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: policy against globalisation
Assess the importance of government policy compared with globalisation in India's economic development. (8)

Globalisation has driven much of India's growth. Advances in communications made it possible to deliver IT and call-centre services from Bengaluru and Hyderabad to customers in the UK and USA. TNCs such as Microsoft and IBM opened large offices, and Indian TNCs like Tata and Infosys expanded worldwide, so exports of services boomed.

However, government policy made this possible. Before 1991 the same technology was spreading, but India's closed economy kept TNCs out. The 1991 reforms cut tariffs, removed licences and welcomed FDI, and later SEZs and the Make in India campaign added incentives. Government-funded IITs created the skilled workforce that attracted outsourcing, and infrastructure such as the Golden Quadrilateral helped firms move goods.

Policy has limits too: infrastructure gaps and red tape still put off some investors, and growth has not reached many rural areas.

Overall, government policy was more important, because it opened the door and created the skills that allowed India to benefit from globalisation. Globalisation was the engine, but policy decided when and how India could use it.

(Level 3: both factors explained with evidence, their interaction analysed, and a supported judgement.)
:::
