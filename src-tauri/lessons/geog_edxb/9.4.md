## What it is

This key idea is part of **Topic 9, Consuming energy resources**, examined in **Paper 3 Section C** (resource-based questions with two 8-mark questions) and often feeding the **Section D decision** (12 marks plus 4 SPaG). It says that the world's **continuing reliance on fossil fuels** puts pressure on companies and governments to **exploit new areas**. It covers:
- the **economic benefits and costs** of developing new **conventional** oil and gas in **ecologically sensitive** and **isolated** areas, such as the **Arctic** and **deep oceans**;
- the **environmental costs**, especially to **water quality** and **ecosystems**, of developing **unconventional** oil and gas: **tar sands** (oil sands) and **shale gas**.

Any real examples of the right type earn the marks. This course uses Alaska (Prudhoe Bay and the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge debate), Shell's abandoned Arctic drilling off Alaska, the Deepwater Horizon spill, the **Alberta oil sands** in Canada and **fracking** for shale gas in the USA and in Lancashire, England.

### Why companies are moving into new areas

The easy, cheap oil in large onshore fields is being used up, but world demand keeps rising. So companies look further:
- **further north**, into the Arctic;
- **deeper**, into ocean water more than a kilometre deep;
- **into harder rock**, using new technology to release oil and gas that could not be extracted before.

These areas are often **ecologically sensitive** (fragile ecosystems that recover slowly from damage) and **isolated** (far from people, roads and ports), which raises both costs and risks.

### Conventional against unconventional: get the label right

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Conventional** | oil or gas that flows naturally up a well drilled into a reservoir rock | Arctic fields such as **Prudhoe Bay**, Alaska; deep-water fields in the Gulf of Mexico and off Brazil; the North Sea |
| **Unconventional** | oil or gas that will not flow on its own and needs special methods to extract | **tar sands** (bitumen mixed with sand, mined or melted with steam); **shale gas** and shale oil (released by **fracking**) |

Pearson separates the two in questions. Arctic drilling is **new but conventional**. Tar sands and shale are **unconventional**.

### New conventional sources: the Arctic and deep water

**The Arctic** is thought to hold a large share of the world's undiscovered oil and especially gas. Alaska's **North Slope**, where Prudhoe Bay was developed from the 1970s, pipes its oil south through the **Trans-Alaska Pipeline**. A long-running debate surrounds drilling in the coastal plain of the **Arctic National Wildlife Refuge**, an important calving ground for caribou. Norway and Russia also produce oil and gas from Arctic seas.

**Deep water**: platforms and drill ships now work in water several kilometres deep, in the Gulf of Mexico and off Brazil and West Africa.

**Economic benefits**

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Jobs** | construction, drilling, pipelines, supply bases and services; well paid, though often temporary or for incoming workers |
| **Tax and royalty income** | governments earn large revenues; Alaska shares some of its oil income with every resident through an annual dividend from its **Permanent Fund** |
| **Energy security** | a domestic supply reduces reliance on imports from unstable regions |
| **Export earnings** | surplus oil and gas earn foreign currency |
| **Infrastructure** | roads, airstrips and ports can help remote communities |
| **Multiplier effect** | spending by workers and companies supports local businesses |

**Economic costs**

| Cost | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Very high extraction costs** | extreme cold, sea ice, darkness in winter, storms and remoteness need specialist equipment; projects are only profitable when oil prices are high |
| **Long lead times and risk** | it can take a decade from exploration to production; Shell spent billions of US dollars exploring off Alaska and abandoned the project in 2015 after disappointing results |
| **Spill clean-up and compensation** | spills in cold or deep water are very hard to clean up; BP's costs after **Deepwater Horizon** (Gulf of Mexico, 2010) ran to tens of billions of US dollars |
| **Damage to other industries** | fishing and tourism can be harmed by spills or industrial development |
| **Boom and bust** | when prices fall, projects close and jobs disappear |
| **Stranded assets** | if climate policies cut fossil fuel use, expensive new fields may never repay their cost |

**Why the Arctic is so sensitive:** the cold slows the breakdown of oil, so a spill persists for decades; ice makes clean-up almost impossible; food chains are short, so damage to plankton, fish or seals spreads to polar bears and to indigenous communities who hunt and fish. **Permafrost** melts under heated buildings and pipelines, causing subsidence.

### Unconventional sources: tar sands

**Tar sands** (or oil sands) are deposits of sand, clay and water mixed with **bitumen**, a very thick, heavy oil. The largest are in **Alberta, Canada**, beneath the **boreal forest** (taiga) around the Athabasca River.

Bitumen is extracted in two ways:
- **Open-pit mining** for shallow deposits: the forest and peat are stripped, the sands dug out with giant shovels and trucks, then washed with hot water to separate the bitumen.
- **In-situ extraction** for deeper deposits: steam is injected underground to melt the bitumen so it can be pumped up.

**Environmental costs**

| Cost | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Water use** | large volumes of water are taken from rivers such as the Athabasca to wash the sands and make steam |
| **Water quality** | waste water is stored in vast **tailings ponds** containing toxic chemicals and heavy metals; seepage into groundwater and rivers is a concern for downstream First Nations communities |
| **Ecosystems** | boreal forest and **peatland** are cleared over large areas; habitats of caribou, migratory birds and fish are lost or broken up; birds landing on tailings ponds can die |
| **Carbon emissions** | removing the peat and forest releases stored carbon; heating water and making steam uses large amounts of energy, so tar-sands oil has a higher carbon footprint than most conventional oil |
| **Landscape** | huge open pits scar the land; companies must reclaim mined land, but restoring complex peatland takes decades and may never fully succeed |
| **Air quality** | upgraders and processing plants release sulphur dioxide and other pollutants |

**Economic benefits** (for balance in 8-mark answers): Canada's oil sands hold some of the largest reserves in the world, provide many jobs and large tax and royalty revenues, and supply energy to the USA.

### Unconventional sources: shale gas and fracking

**Shale gas** is natural gas trapped in tiny pores in **shale**, a fine-grained rock that does not let gas flow. **Hydraulic fracturing (fracking)** releases it:
1. A well is drilled down, then **horizontally** through the shale.
2. Water mixed with sand and chemicals is pumped in at very high pressure.
3. The pressure **cracks** the rock; the sand props the cracks open; gas flows back up the well.

The **USA's** shale boom made it the world's largest gas producer, cut its imports and lowered gas prices. In England, fracking at **Preston New Road** in **Lancashire** triggered small earthquakes, and the UK government halted fracking in **2019**.

**Environmental costs**

| Cost | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Groundwater contamination** | faulty well casings can let methane or fracking chemicals leak into aquifers that supply drinking water |
| **Water use** | each well uses very large volumes of water, a problem in dry areas |
| **Waste water** | the fluid that returns is salty and may contain chemicals and naturally radioactive material; spills or poor disposal pollute rivers and soils |
| **Earthquakes** | fracking and waste-water injection can trigger small earth tremors |
| **Ecosystems and landscape** | well pads, roads and pipelines fragment habitats; heavy lorry traffic causes noise and dust |
| **Methane leaks** | methane is a powerful greenhouse gas, so leaks reduce gas's climate advantage over coal |

### Weighing it up

New areas improve **energy security** and earn money, but they lock in fossil fuel use and put **fragile, slow-to-recover ecosystems** at risk. In an exam judgement, consider: who gains (governments, TNCs, workers) and who loses (local and indigenous communities, wildlife, future generations); short-term against long-term; and whether the money could instead fund alternatives.

::: terms
- **Conventional oil and gas**: oil or gas that flows naturally to the surface when a well is drilled into a reservoir rock.
- **Unconventional oil and gas**: oil or gas that needs special extraction methods, such as tar sands and shale gas.
- **Tar sands**: deposits of sand, clay and water mixed with bitumen, a very thick oil, also called oil sands.
- **Bitumen**: a heavy, sticky form of oil that will not flow at normal temperatures.
- **Shale gas**: natural gas trapped in fine-grained shale rock that cannot flow without fracturing the rock.
- **Fracking**: pumping water, sand and chemicals into shale at high pressure to crack it and release gas or oil.
- **Ecologically sensitive area**: an environment with fragile ecosystems that are easily damaged and slow to recover.
- **Tailings pond**: a large lake storing contaminated waste water and fine material left after processing oil sands.
- **Aquifer**: a layer of permeable rock that stores groundwater, often used for drinking water.
- **Permafrost**: ground that stays frozen for at least two years in a row, found in Arctic regions.
- **Royalty**: a payment made to a government by a company for each unit of a resource it extracts.
:::

::: method
1. **Label correctly.** Arctic and deep-water oil = new **conventional**. Tar sands and shale = **unconventional**.
2. **Economic questions:** give benefits (jobs, taxes, energy security, exports) and costs (high extraction cost, risk, spill clean-up, boom and bust), each developed with a consequence.
3. **Environmental questions:** the spec stresses **water quality** and **ecosystems**. Say what is contaminated or destroyed, how, and the effect on wildlife or people.
4. **Explain why an area is sensitive:** cold slows recovery, short food chains, fragile peat and permafrost, remoteness making clean-up hard.
5. **Use the resource:** quote a value, a label or a stakeholder's view from the booklet.
6. **For 8-mark evaluate:** weigh benefits against costs for different groups and timescales, then give a clear judgement with a condition ("only if…").
:::

::: example Identify: unconventional source
Which one of the following is an unconventional source of oil or gas? A: The North Sea. B: Prudhoe Bay, Alaska. C: Tar sands in Alberta. D: Deep-water fields in the Gulf of Mexico. (1)

C (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: Arctic cost
Explain one economic cost of developing oil in the Arctic. (2)

Extreme cold, sea ice and remoteness mean specialist equipment and long supply routes are needed (1), so extraction costs are very high and projects only make a profit when oil prices are high (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: fracking and water
Explain two ways fracking can affect water quality. (4)

If a well casing cracks, methane or fracking chemicals can leak into aquifers (1), contaminating groundwater used for drinking (1). The waste water that flows back up is salty and contains chemicals (1), so spills or poor disposal can pollute rivers and soils (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: share of jobs
Sample data: a region has 120,000 jobs, of which 18,000 are in the oil-sands industry. Calculate the percentage of jobs in the oil-sands industry. (2)

18,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 (1) = 15% (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: tar sands
Study a resource showing a bar chart of the oil-sands industry's contribution to jobs and government revenue in Alberta, a satellite image comparing forest cover before and after mining, and a quote from a First Nations elder downstream on the Athabasca River worried about fish and water. Evaluate whether the economic benefits of developing tar sands outweigh the environmental costs. (8)

The bar chart shows the industry provides many jobs and large government revenues, which pay for services across Alberta and improve energy security for Canada and its customers. The reserves are among the largest in the world, so these benefits could last decades.

However, the satellite image shows large areas of boreal forest and peatland replaced by pits and tailings ponds. This destroys habitats for caribou and migratory birds and releases stored carbon. The elder's quote shows concern about water quality, as tailings ponds can seep into the Athabasca, affecting fish that downstream communities rely on. Extraction also uses so much energy that tar-sands oil has a higher carbon footprint than conventional oil, adding to climate change.

The benefits go mainly to companies, workers and government, while costs fall on indigenous communities, wildlife and future generations, and peatland may never fully recover. Overall, the economic benefits do not outweigh the environmental costs in the long term, unless strict water protection, reclamation and a planned move to cleaner energy are enforced.

(Level 3: uses all three parts of the resource (AO4), weighs who gains and loses over time, and reaches a conditional, supported judgement (AO3).)
:::

::: q
State one difference between conventional and unconventional oil. (1)
---
Conventional oil flows naturally up a well, whereas unconventional oil needs special methods such as mining, steam or fracking to extract it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why the Arctic is described as ecologically sensitive. (2)
---
Low temperatures slow the breakdown of oil and the growth of plants (1), so damage from a spill or construction persists for decades and ecosystems recover very slowly (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one economic benefit of developing a new oil field in an isolated area. (2)
---
It creates jobs in construction, drilling and services (1), bringing income to the region and, through the multiplier effect, supporting local businesses (1). Accept tax revenue, energy security or exports, developed.
:::

::: q
Describe how fracking releases shale gas. (3)
---
A well is drilled down and then horizontally through the shale (1); water, sand and chemicals are pumped in at very high pressure (1); this cracks the rock, the sand holds the cracks open, and gas flows back up the well (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest one reason why oil companies are willing to develop more expensive sources such as tar sands and the Arctic. (3)
---
Global demand for oil is still rising, especially from emerging economies (1), while easy, cheap onshore fields are being used up (1); when oil prices are high these expensive sources become profitable, and they offer long-term reserves (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two environmental costs of developing tar sands. (4)
---
Two from: forest and peatland cleared (1), destroying habitats and releasing stored carbon (1); toxic tailings ponds (1) can leak into rivers and groundwater, harming fish and people downstream (1); large water withdrawals from rivers (1) reduce flows for ecosystems (1); high energy use in extraction (1) gives a larger carbon footprint than conventional oil (1).
:::

::: watch
- Calling Arctic oil "unconventional". It is new, but **conventional**.
- Giving only environmental points when the question asks about **economic** costs and benefits, or the other way round.
- Vague answers such as "fracking pollutes water". Say **how** (leaking casings, waste-water spills) and **what** is harmed (aquifers, rivers, drinking water).
- Forgetting that the spec stresses **water quality and ecosystems** for unconventional sources.
- One-sided evaluations. Include the economic benefits even when you conclude the costs are greater.
- Writing a bare dollar sign for costs. Write "US dollars".
:::
