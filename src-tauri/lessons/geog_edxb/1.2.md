## What it is

The Earth's climate was changing long before humans burned any coal. Over millions of years it has swung between cold **glacial** periods, when ice sheets spread, and warmer **interglacial** periods like today. Over hundreds of years it has wobbled too: the UK has had warm spells and a "Little Ice Age". This key idea asks two things: **what natural factors cause these changes**, and **how we know** they happened.

### Four natural causes

| Cause | How it changes the climate | Timescale | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Orbital changes (Milankovitch cycles)** | Regular changes in the Earth's orbit and tilt alter how much solar energy reaches each latitude in each season | tens of thousands to about 100,000 years | the rhythm of glacials and interglacials in the Quaternary |
| **Variations in solar output** | The Sun's energy output rises and falls slightly; more **sunspots** mean a slightly more active, brighter Sun | decades to centuries | very few sunspots during the **Maunder Minimum** (late 1600s), in the coldest part of the Little Ice Age |
| **Volcanic activity** | Large explosive eruptions put **sulphur dioxide** and ash high into the atmosphere; the tiny droplets reflect sunlight, cooling the Earth | one to a few years for one eruption; longer if eruptions cluster | Tambora (Indonesia) 1815, followed by the "year without a summer" in 1816; Pinatubo (Philippines) 1991 |
| **Asteroid collisions** | A large impact throws huge amounts of dust and debris into the atmosphere, blocking sunlight for months or years | sudden, then years | the impact about 66 million years ago linked to the extinction of the dinosaurs |

**Milankovitch cycles** have three parts, which you only need in outline:
- **Eccentricity**: the orbit changes from nearly circular to more elliptical, over about 100,000 years.
- **Axial tilt**: the angle of the Earth's axis varies slightly, over about 41,000 years. More tilt means more extreme seasons.
- **Precession**: the axis wobbles like a spinning top, changing which hemisphere is nearest the Sun in its summer.

When the cycles combine to give cool northern summers, winter snow fails to melt, ice sheets grow and reflect more sunlight, and the cooling feeds itself.

Notice the difference in scale. Orbital changes explain the **big, slow** swings between glacials and interglacials. Solar output and volcanoes explain the **smaller, shorter** wobbles such as the Little Ice Age. Asteroids are rare but catastrophic.

### Three types of evidence

| Evidence | How it works | Time span | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ice cores** | Drilled from ice sheets in Antarctica and Greenland. Each year's snow forms a layer; trapped **air bubbles** record past CO₂ levels, and the chemistry of the water (oxygen isotopes) shows past temperatures | hundreds of thousands of years (Antarctic cores reach back about 800,000 years) | very long, continuous record; measures gases directly | only from polar regions; deep layers are compressed, so dating is less precise |
| **Tree rings** (dendrochronology) | Trees add one ring a year. **Wide rings** mean good growing conditions (warm, wet years); **narrow rings** mean poor ones | thousands of years, using overlapping ancient timbers | accurate to the year; available in many places | only where trees grow; rings reflect rainfall and soil as well as temperature |
| **Historical sources** | Diaries, ship logs, harvest and wine records, paintings and written accounts of events such as **frost fairs** on the frozen River Thames | about 2,000 years in Europe | detailed and local; shows impacts on people | patchy, not measured with instruments, can be exaggerated; only where people wrote things down |

Scientists combine all three, alongside other evidence such as pollen in peat and landforms left by glaciers, to cross-check each other.

### What the evidence reconstructs

**The Quaternary** is the most recent geological period, covering roughly the last 2.6 million years. Ice-core and other evidence shows many cycles of **glacials** (ice sheets covering much of northern Europe and North America) separated by shorter **interglacials**. The last glacial ended about 11,700 years ago; at its peak, around 20,000 years ago, ice covered most of Scotland, Wales and northern England. We now live in an interglacial (the Holocene).

**The UK since Roman times** (keep these dates approximate):
- **Roman Warm Period**: mild conditions while the Romans were in Britain; vines grew in parts of England.
- **Cooler period** after the Romans left.
- **Medieval Warm Period** (roughly 950–1250): warm enough for vineyards in England; the Vikings settled southern Greenland.
- **Little Ice Age** (roughly 1300s to the mid-1800s): colder winters, failed harvests, glaciers advanced in the Alps; the Thames froze several times and frost fairs were held on the ice, the last in 1814.
- **Since about 1850**: warming, now mainly because of human activity (see 1.3).

### Skill: line graphs and bar charts of climate change

Pearson gives a line graph of temperature (often as an **anomaly**, the difference from a long-term average) or CO₂ over time, or a bar chart of eruptions or sunspots. Describe the **overall trend**, quote **values with units and dates**, note **anomalies** (sudden dips after big eruptions), and say whether two lines move together. Correlation is not proof of cause, but a close match between CO₂ and temperature in ice cores is strong evidence they are linked.

::: terms
- **Natural climate change**: changes in the Earth's climate caused by natural factors, not by human activity.
- **Milankovitch cycles**: regular changes in the Earth's orbit, tilt and wobble that alter how much solar energy reaches the Earth.
- **Sunspots**: dark patches on the Sun's surface; more sunspots mean slightly higher solar output.
- **Maunder Minimum**: a period in the late 1600s with very few sunspots, during the coldest part of the Little Ice Age.
- **Ice core**: a cylinder of ice drilled from an ice sheet whose layers and trapped air record past climate.
- **Dendrochronology**: dating and reconstructing past climate from the width of annual tree rings.
- **Quaternary**: the most recent geological period, about the last 2.6 million years, with repeated glacials and interglacials.
- **Glacial period**: a long cold period when ice sheets and glaciers expand.
- **Interglacial period**: a warmer period between glacials, such as the present day.
- **Little Ice Age**: a cool period from roughly the 1300s to the mid-1800s when Thames frost fairs were held.
:::

::: method
1. **Cause questions:** name the cause, then give its mechanism: "volcanic eruptions release sulphur dioxide, which reflects sunlight, so global temperatures fall for a year or two".
2. **Evidence questions:** name the evidence, then say **what it records and how**: "ice cores contain trapped air bubbles, which show past CO₂ levels". Naming alone is one mark.
3. **Match timescales:** orbital changes for glacials and interglacials; sunspots and volcanoes for the Little Ice Age and short dips.
4. **Graphs:** trend, values with dates and units, anomalies, then any link between two variables.
5. **8-mark evaluate:** judge each source's usefulness (time span, accuracy, coverage), compare them, and conclude which is most useful **for what purpose**, noting they work best together.
:::

::: example Explain one cause
Explain one natural cause of climate change. (2)

Large volcanic eruptions eject sulphur dioxide high into the atmosphere (1). It forms droplets that reflect incoming sunlight back into space, so global temperatures fall for a year or two, as after Tambora in 1815 (1).
:::

::: example Explain two pieces of evidence
Explain two pieces of evidence for natural climate change. (4)

Ice cores from Antarctica (1) contain air bubbles trapped as snow built up, and the gases and chemistry in each layer show past temperatures over hundreds of thousands of years (1). Historical sources (1) such as records of frost fairs on the frozen Thames show that winters in the Little Ice Age were much colder than today (1).
:::

::: example Read a graph
Sample data: a graph of global temperature anomaly shows −0.2 °C in 1812, −0.6 °C in 1816 and −0.2 °C in 1820. A major eruption occurred in 1815. Describe what the graph shows and suggest a reason. (3)

Temperatures fell by 0.4 °C between 1812 and 1816 (1), then recovered to the earlier level by 1820 (1). The dip suggests the 1815 eruption put sulphur dioxide into the atmosphere, reflecting sunlight for a short period (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: evidence
Evaluate the usefulness of different sources of evidence for reconstructing past climates. (8)

Ice cores are the most useful for long timescales. Their layers and trapped air give a continuous record of temperature and CO₂ over hundreds of thousands of years, showing the Quaternary's repeated glacials and interglacials. However, they come only from polar ice, so they tell us about global averages rather than conditions in a particular place such as the UK.

Tree rings are more precise, because each ring is one year, and they can be found in many regions. They help reconstruct events such as the Little Ice Age. But they only go back a few thousand years, and ring width depends on rainfall and soil as well as temperature, so they are harder to interpret.

Historical sources such as frost-fair accounts, harvest records and paintings show exactly how past climate affected people in places like London. They are vivid but patchy, not instrumental, and only cover the last two thousand years or so.

Overall, ice cores are most useful for the long-term pattern, while tree rings and historical sources are better for recent, local detail. No single source is enough, so their real value is in cross-checking each other.

(Level 3: each source judged with strengths and limits, compared, and a conclusion tied to purpose.)
:::

State one natural cause of climate change. (1)

Any one: orbital (Milankovitch) changes; variations in solar output / sunspots; volcanic eruptions; asteroid collisions (1).

Explain one way asteroid collisions can change the climate. (2)
---
A large impact throws huge amounts of dust and debris into the atmosphere (1), blocking sunlight so temperatures fall for months or years (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way tree rings provide evidence of past climate. (2)
---
Trees grow one ring a year (1); wide rings show warm, wet years with good growth and narrow rings show cold or dry years (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the UK's climate during the Little Ice Age. (2)
---
Colder winters than today (1), e.g. the Thames froze and frost fairs were held / harvests failed / glaciers advanced (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one limitation of historical sources as evidence of climate change. (2)
---
They are not instrumental measurements (1), so they are subjective and may exaggerate unusual events (1). Or: they are patchy (1), only covering places and times where people kept records (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways orbital changes can cause glacial periods. (4)
---
Eccentricity (1): a more elliptical orbit changes the distance from the Sun and the energy received in each season (1). Axial tilt (1): less tilt gives cooler summers, so winter snow does not melt and ice sheets grow (1). Precession (1): the wobble changes which hemisphere faces the Sun in summer, altering seasonal heating (1). Feedback (1): growing ice reflects more sunlight, increasing cooling (1).
:::

::: watch
- Naming evidence without explaining **what it shows**. "Ice cores" alone is one mark; add "trapped air shows past CO₂ and temperature".
- Saying volcanoes warm the planet. Big explosive eruptions **cool** it in the short term by reflecting sunlight.
- Mixing up timescales: Milankovitch cycles do not explain the Little Ice Age; sunspots do not explain ice ages.
- Inventing precise dates. Keep the warm periods and the Little Ice Age approximate.
- Treating correlation on a graph as proof. Say the variables are linked, then give the mechanism.
- Evaluating without a conclusion. Say which source is most useful and for what.
:::
