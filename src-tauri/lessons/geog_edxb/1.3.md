## What it is

Since about 1850 the world has warmed faster than at any time in the natural record of 1.2, and the evidence points to people. This key idea has three parts: **how human activity adds greenhouse gases**, the **evidence** that warming is happening and what it means for people, and **why projections for 2100 vary** so much.

### The natural greenhouse effect and the enhanced greenhouse effect

1. Energy from the Sun arrives mainly as **short-wave radiation**, which passes through the atmosphere and warms the ground and oceans.
2. The warmed Earth gives off **long-wave (infrared) radiation**.
3. **Greenhouse gases** (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb some of this long-wave radiation and re-emit it in all directions, including back down, keeping the lower atmosphere warm.

This is the **natural greenhouse effect**, and it is essential: without it the Earth's average temperature would be well below freezing, at around −18 °C instead of roughly 15 °C.

The **enhanced greenhouse effect** is the extra warming caused when human activities add more greenhouse gases, so more long-wave radiation is trapped. Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 parts per million (ppm) before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm today.

### Where the gases come from

| Activity | Gas | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Energy** | carbon dioxide | burning coal, oil and gas in power stations to generate electricity |
| **Industry** | carbon dioxide | burning fossil fuels in factories; cement and steel making release CO₂ directly |
| **Transport** | carbon dioxide | petrol and diesel in cars, lorries and ships; jet fuel in aircraft |
| **Farming** | methane | cattle and sheep release methane as they digest food; flooded **rice paddies** release methane as vegetation rots without oxygen |
| **Farming and land use** | carbon dioxide, nitrous oxide | **deforestation** removes trees that absorb CO₂, and burning them releases it; nitrogen fertilisers release nitrous oxide |
| **Waste** | methane | rotting rubbish in landfill sites |

Methane is present in much smaller amounts than CO₂ but traps far more heat per molecule, so cutting it has a quick effect.

### The evidence that human activity is changing the climate

| Evidence | What has happened |
|---|---|
| **Global temperature rise** | the global average is now more than 1 °C above pre-industrial levels, and the warmest years on record have nearly all been recent |
| **Warming oceans and sea level rise** | oceans have absorbed most of the extra heat; water expands as it warms (**thermal expansion**) and melting land ice adds water, so global sea level has risen by roughly 20 cm since 1900, and the rate is speeding up |
| **Declining Arctic ice** | satellites since the late 1970s show summer Arctic sea ice shrinking in area and thickness |
| **More extreme weather** | more frequent and intense heatwaves and heavy rainfall events; the UK recorded 40 °C for the first time in July 2022 |

The link to people rather than natural causes is the **timing and speed**. Natural factors such as solar output have not risen enough to explain the warming, while the rise in CO₂ matches the growth of fossil-fuel use.

### Possible consequences for people

- **Coastal flooding**: low-lying, densely populated deltas and islands such as Bangladesh and the Maldives face land loss, salt water in farmland and displacement.
- **Water and food**: changing rainfall patterns bring drought in some regions and floods in others; crop yields fall where it becomes too hot or dry.
- **Health**: more heat-related deaths; diseases such as malaria may spread into new areas.
- **Economy**: damage from storms and floods; rising insurance costs; some gains, such as longer growing seasons in cooler countries.
- **Migration and conflict**: people forced to move by drought or flooding.

Impacts fall hardest on poorer countries, which have contributed least to emissions and have the least money to adapt.

### Projections to 2100 and why they vary

Scientists use climate models run under different **emissions scenarios**. The result is a fan of lines on a graph rather than one line. Broadly:
- with rapid, deep cuts in emissions, warming could be held at around 1.5–2 °C above pre-industrial levels;
- with continued high emissions, warming could exceed 4 °C;
- sea level could rise by roughly 30 cm to around a metre by 2100, and more if ice sheets become unstable.

Reasons for the uncertainty:
1. **Human choices**: future emissions depend on population growth, economic growth, energy technology and government policy. This is the biggest source of the spread.
2. **Feedbacks**: warming can cause more warming. Melting ice exposes darker sea and land, which absorb more heat (the **ice-albedo feedback**). Thawing **permafrost** releases methane. Warmer air holds more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas.
3. **Physical processes that are hard to model**: how fast the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets will melt, how much heat and CO₂ the oceans will keep absorbing, and how clouds will change.
4. **Natural factors**: future volcanic eruptions or changes in solar output cannot be predicted.

### Skill: projection graphs

Read the value for each scenario at 2100, give the **range** (highest minus lowest), and explain why the lines spread apart over time: uncertainty grows the further ahead you look.

::: terms
- **Greenhouse gas**: a gas such as carbon dioxide or methane that absorbs long-wave radiation and keeps the lower atmosphere warm.
- **Natural greenhouse effect**: the warming of the Earth by naturally occurring greenhouse gases, which makes the planet habitable.
- **Enhanced greenhouse effect**: the extra warming caused when human activity adds more greenhouse gases to the atmosphere.
- **Global warming**: the rise in the Earth's average surface temperature since the Industrial Revolution.
- **Thermal expansion**: the increase in the volume of seawater as it warms, a major cause of sea level rise.
- **Emissions scenario**: a possible future pathway of greenhouse gas emissions used to run climate models.
- **Feedback**: a process in which warming causes changes that lead to further warming, or that reduce it.
- **Ice-albedo feedback**: melting ice exposes darker surfaces that absorb more heat, causing more melting.
- **Permafrost**: ground that stays frozen all year, which releases methane and carbon dioxide when it thaws.
:::

::: method
1. **Define carefully:** the greenhouse effect is natural; the **enhanced** greenhouse effect is the human addition.
2. **Sources:** give the activity, the gas and the process: "cattle release methane as they digest food".
3. **Evidence:** say what has been measured and how it links to warming: "sea level is rising because warmer water expands and land ice melts".
4. **Projection graphs:** read values at 2100 for the scenarios, calculate the range, and explain the spread.
5. **Uncertainty:** separate human reasons (emissions choices) from physical reasons (feedbacks, ice sheets, oceans).
6. **8-mark assess or evaluate:** weigh the reasons or evidence against each other and judge which matters most, with a clear conclusion.
:::

::: example Explain one: farming
Explain one way farming can increase greenhouse gas emissions. (2)

Flooded rice paddies (1) release methane as plant material rots under the water without oxygen (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: evidence
Explain two pieces of evidence that human activity is causing climate change. (4)

Global sea level is rising (1), because warmer oceans expand and melting land ice adds water, matching the rise in temperatures since industrialisation (1). Arctic sea ice is declining (1), with satellite images since the late 1970s showing summer ice covering a much smaller area than it did (1).
:::

::: example Projection graph
Sample data: a graph shows projected global temperature rise by 2100 of 1.4 °C under a low-emissions scenario and 4.4 °C under a high-emissions scenario. Calculate the difference and suggest one reason for it. (3)

4.4 − 1.4 = 3.0 °C (1). The scenarios assume different future emissions (1): the high scenario assumes continued fossil-fuel use, while the low scenario assumes rapid cuts, so more greenhouse gases trap more heat (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: uncertainty
Assess the reasons why projections of future global temperature vary so widely. (8)

The most important reason is that nobody knows what humans will do. Future emissions depend on population, economic growth, energy technology and policy. Models are therefore run under several scenarios, and the gap between a low-emissions and a high-emissions pathway is several degrees by 2100. This is the main reason the lines on a projection graph fan out.

Physical processes add further uncertainty. Feedbacks such as the ice-albedo effect and methane release from thawing permafrost could speed warming, but their size is hard to measure. Scientists are also unsure how quickly the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets will melt and how much heat the oceans will keep absorbing. Clouds could either trap or reflect more heat.

Natural factors, such as a large volcanic eruption, could cause temporary cooling but cannot be predicted; their effect is small over a century compared with emissions.

Overall, human choices are the biggest cause of the spread, because the same physical uncertainties apply to every scenario. However, feedbacks matter more at high emissions, because they could push warming beyond the top of the projected range.

(Level 3: several reasons explained, weighed against each other, with a supported judgement.)
:::

Future emissions are unknown (1), so the amount of warming and therefore thermal expansion could vary (1). Ocean heat uptake is uncertain (1), affecting how much the water expands (1).
:::

::: watch
- Saying the greenhouse effect is caused by humans. It is natural; the **enhanced** effect is human.
- Mixing up the ozone hole and climate change. They are different problems.
- Giving a gas without the activity and process (or the reverse). Two-mark explains need both.
- Quoting precise projections as certainties. Projections are ranges based on scenarios.
- Saying sea level rises "because ice melts" only. Thermal expansion matters too, and melting **sea** ice does not raise sea level.
- In 8-markers, listing reasons without saying which matters most.
:::
