## What it is

The **taiga** (boreal forest) is the second forest biome in **Topic 8 (Paper 3 Section B)**. This key idea says the taiga shows different characteristics from the rainforest because its climate is **more extreme and highly seasonal**. It covers:
- how **biotic** and **abiotic** parts are **interdependent** (climate, soil, water, plants, animals and humans);
- how taiga **plants** (cone-shaped, needles, simple structure) and **animals** (often **migratory**) are adapted to the climate;
- why the taiga has **lower productivity**, **less active nutrient cycling** and **much lower biodiversity** than the rainforest;
- the skill of reading **nutrient cycle (Gersmehl) diagrams** and food webs.

Any real area of taiga earns the marks. This course uses the **Canadian boreal forest** (with examples from Alaska, Scandinavia and **Siberia** in Russia). Exam questions often ask you to **compare** the taiga with the tropical rainforest, so learn the two side by side.

### Where and why: a cold, seasonal climate

The taiga forms a broad belt across the **northern hemisphere**, roughly between **50° and 70° N**: across Canada and Alaska, Scandinavia and Russia. There is almost no taiga in the southern hemisphere because there is very little land at those latitudes. It is the **largest land biome** by area, lying between the temperate forests to the south and the **tundra** to the north.

| Climate feature | What it is like | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Winters** | long (six months or more) and very cold, often below −20 °C; colder still in Siberia | high latitude: low sun angle and very short days in winter; continental interiors far from the sea's warming effect |
| **Summers** | short and cool to mild, warmest month usually 10–20 °C | long days, but the sun is still low in the sky |
| **Annual range** | very large, sometimes over 40 °C | continental location |
| **Precipitation** | low to moderate, often 300–900 mm a year, with summer rain and winter snow | cold air holds little moisture; far from oceans |
| **Growing season** | short, often only three to five months | temperatures are too low for growth for most of the year |

The key contrast for the exam: in the rainforest, **nothing limits growth**; in the taiga, **cold and a short growing season limit everything**.

### Interdependence in the taiga

- **Climate → plants:** the short growing season and frozen ground limit the number of species that can survive; a few **conifers** dominate (spruce, fir, pine and larch).
- **Plants → soil:** fallen **needles** are acidic and waxy, and decompose very slowly in the cold, forming a thick **litter** layer. Water draining through it in the summer thaw makes the soil acidic and leaches nutrients, forming a grey, infertile **podzol**.
- **Soil → plants:** acidic, nutrient-poor soil favours conifers and mosses, and few other plants, so diversity stays low.
- **Water:** in the north, **permafrost** (permanently frozen ground) stops drainage, so the summer thaw creates **bogs** and waterlogged ground. Tree cover shades the ground and helps keep the permafrost frozen.
- **Plants → animals:** seeds, buds and bark feed squirrels, hares and birds; lichens feed **caribou** (reindeer) in winter.
- **Animals → plants:** birds and squirrels spread seeds; insects pollinate summer flowers; moose browse young trees.
- **Fire:** natural lightning fires release nutrients locked in the litter and open the cones of some pines, so **fire is part of the cycle**.
- **Humans:** indigenous peoples, such as the **Cree** and **Dene** in Canada and the **Evenki** in Siberia, have traditionally hunted, trapped, fished and herded reindeer at low densities. Modern logging, mining and energy extraction break these links (see 8.4).

### Plant adaptations

| Adaptation | How it helps |
|---|---|
| **Cone shape** with flexible, downward-sloping branches | heavy **snow slides off**, so branches do not snap |
| **Needles** instead of broad leaves | small surface area and a thick **waxy coating** cut water loss when the ground is frozen and roots cannot take up water |
| **Evergreen** (keeps needles all year) | can start **photosynthesising** as soon as temperatures rise in spring, making the most of a short season; no energy spent growing new leaves each year |
| **Dark green colour** | absorbs as much of the weak sunlight as possible |
| **Shallow, spreading roots** | take up water from the thin active layer above the permafrost |
| **Thick bark** | protects against extreme cold and fire |
| **Fire-adapted cones** in some pines | sealed with resin and open after fire, so seeds land on cleared, nutrient-rich ground |
| **Simple structure** | usually one canopy layer and a sparse floor of mosses, lichens and berry shrubs, because dense shade, acidic soil and cold allow little else |

**Larch** is an exception: a **deciduous** conifer that drops its needles in winter, which suits the very coldest parts of Siberia.

### Animal adaptations

- **Migration:** **caribou** (reindeer) travel long distances between summer and winter grounds; many **birds**, such as geese and warblers, fly south for the winter when insects and open water disappear.
- **Hibernation:** **black and brown bears** build up fat in summer and sleep through the winter when food is scarce.
- **Insulation:** thick fur and fat layers (moose, wolves, lynx).
- **Camouflage:** the **snowshoe hare** turns white in winter and brown in summer.
- **Large feet:** the snowshoe hare and **lynx** have broad, furry paws that work like snowshoes.
- **Food storage:** squirrels and some birds store seeds in autumn to survive the winter.

- **Litter is the largest store.** Needles fall and pile up because cold, acidic conditions slow the decomposers; it can take many years for needles to break down.
- **Biomass is moderate**: trees grow slowly, and there are few species and little undergrowth.
- **Soil is a small store**: few nutrients are released from the litter, and summer meltwater leaches what there is.
- **All flows are small and slow**, and for much of the year they stop completely because everything is frozen.

**Productivity** (net primary productivity) is low because the growing season is short, the sun is weak, the ground is frozen for months, and nutrients are locked in the litter. Less new plant growth means less food, so fewer animals can be supported. With only a few tree species and one main canopy layer, there are fewer **niches**, so **biodiversity is much lower** and **food webs are simpler**: for example, conifer seeds → red squirrels → pine martens or lynx, and lichens → caribou → wolves.

A simple food web is **less resilient**: if one key species declines (for example, the snowshoe hare in its natural population cycle), its predators (lynx) decline too.

### Rainforest and taiga compared

| | **Tropical rainforest** | **Taiga** |
|---|---|---|
| Climate | hot, wet, constant | cold, drier, highly seasonal |
| Growing season | all year | three to five months |
| Largest nutrient store | **biomass** | **litter** |
| Speed of nutrient cycling | very fast | very slow |
| Soil | red latosol, leached | grey, acidic podzol, leached |
| Structure | four or more layers | one main canopy layer |
| Leaves | broad, evergreen, drip tips | needles, evergreen (except larch) |
| Productivity | very high | low |
| Biodiversity | very high, complex food webs | low, simple food webs |
| Animal strategy | specialise, stay all year | migrate, hibernate or insulate |

**Exam tip:** a "compare" answer needs **both** biomes in every sentence, using words such as "whereas" or "but".

::: terms
- **Taiga**: the boreal forest biome of the high northern latitudes, dominated by coniferous trees.
- **Coniferous**: describes trees that bear cones and usually have needles, such as spruce, fir and pine.
- **Evergreen**: keeping leaves or needles all year, so the plant can photosynthesise whenever conditions allow.
- **Permafrost**: ground that stays frozen for at least two years in a row, found under much of the northern taiga.
- **Podzol**: an acidic, infertile, leached soil that forms under coniferous forest.
- **Growing season**: the part of the year when temperatures are high enough for plants to grow.
- **Migration**: the seasonal movement of animals between areas, such as caribou or birds moving south in winter.
- **Hibernation**: a deep sleep through winter that lets animals such as bears survive when food is scarce.
- **Net primary productivity**: the rate at which plants produce new organic matter, the food available to the rest of the ecosystem.
- **Physiological drought**: a lack of usable water for plants because the ground is frozen, even though water is present.
:::

::: method
1. **Start from the climate**: cold, long winters, a short growing season and low precipitation. Name the feature, then its effect.
2. **Adaptations**: feature, description, then **how it helps** in this climate (snow, frozen ground, short season).
3. **Interdependence**: write chains ("needles decompose slowly in the cold, so litter builds up, so the soil is poor").
4. **Gersmehl**: in the taiga the **litter** store is largest and all flows are small. Give the reason for each.
5. **Compare**: refer to both biomes in each point and use a comparative word.
6. **Resource questions**: quote the figure (a temperature, a store size, a species count) before you explain.
:::

::: example State: the largest store
Study a Gersmehl nutrient cycle diagram for the taiga. State the largest nutrient store. (1)

The litter (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: needles
Explain one way taiga trees are adapted to the climate. (2)

Conifers have needles with a thick waxy coating and a small surface area (1), which reduces water loss in winter when the ground is frozen and roots cannot take up water (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: migration
Explain one reason why many taiga animals migrate. (2)

In winter the ground is snow-covered and insects disappear, so food becomes very scarce (1); birds such as geese fly south to warmer areas where they can feed, returning in the short summer to breed (1).
:::

::: example Compare: nutrient cycles
Compare the nutrient cycles of the tropical rainforest and the taiga. (4)

In the rainforest the largest store is the biomass, whereas in the taiga it is the litter (1). Nutrient flows are large and fast in the rainforest, but small and slow in the taiga (1). Decomposition takes weeks in the hot, wet rainforest, whereas in the cold taiga needles can take years to break down (1). In both biomes the soil store is small, because rain or meltwater leaches nutrients (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: low productivity
Study a resource with climate data for a taiga station (winter months well below −20 °C, only four months above 10 °C, 450 mm of precipitation a year) and for a rainforest station (about 27 °C all year, over 2,500 mm). Assess why the taiga is less productive than the tropical rainforest. (8)

The resource shows the taiga station has only four months above 10 °C, compared with twelve warm months at the rainforest station. This short growing season is the most important factor: trees can only photosynthesise for part of the year, so less new plant matter is produced.

Cold also slows decomposition. Acidic needles pile up as a thick litter store, so few nutrients reach the podzol soil and growth is limited even in summer. In contrast, rainforest litter breaks down in weeks, so nutrients are constantly recycled.

Water is a third factor. The taiga station receives far less precipitation than the rainforest, and for months it is locked up as snow or frozen ground, causing physiological drought. Low light matters too, because the sun is low and winter days are very short at high latitude.

Overall, temperature is the most important factor, because it controls the length of the growing season, the speed of decomposition and whether water is usable. The other factors are mostly consequences of the cold, which is why productivity falls so sharply compared with the hot, wet rainforest.

(Level 3: resource data used throughout (AO4); several factors weighed and linked, with a supported judgement (AO3).)
:::

::: watch
- Copying the rainforest pattern: in the taiga the **litter** is the largest store, not the biomass.
- Saying taiga trees "lose their leaves in winter". Most are **evergreen**; only larch is deciduous.
- Stating that the taiga gets lots of rain. Precipitation is **low to moderate**; the limit on growth is mainly **cold**.
- Comparing badly: each comparison point must mention **both** biomes.
- Describing an adaptation without explaining **how it helps** (snow load, frozen ground, short season).
- Forgetting that the taiga is only in the **northern** hemisphere.
:::
