## What it is

Topic 4 opens Paper 2 Section A with an overview of **why the UK's physical landscape varies from place to place**. This key idea covers:
- the role of **geology** and of **past tectonic and glacial processes** in creating **upland** landscapes (mostly **igneous** and **metamorphic** rocks) and **lowland** landscapes (mostly **sedimentary** rocks);
- the **characteristics and distribution** of the UK's main rock types: **chalk**, **carboniferous limestone** and **clay** (sedimentary), **granite** (igneous), and **schists** and **slates** (metamorphic).

It also carries two integrated skills: **analysing photographs** of glacial, river and coastal landscapes, and using **simple geological cross-sections** to show how geology and relief are linked. Paper 2 Question 1 is a short overview question (about 5 marks: a multiple choice, then "Explain one…" items), and the Section A 8-marker ("Analyse Figure… Assess…") can be built on a map of geology or relief. Any accurate UK example earns the marks; this lesson uses the best-known ones.

### The three rock groups

| Group | How it forms | Typical properties | UK examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Igneous** | magma or lava cools and hardens. **Intrusive** rocks (granite) cool slowly underground and have large crystals; **extrusive** rocks (basalt) cool quickly at the surface | hard, crystalline, usually **impermeable**, resistant to erosion | granite of Dartmoor, Bodmin Moor, the Cairngorms; basalt of the Giant's Causeway |
| **Sedimentary** | layers (**strata**) of sediment or the remains of sea creatures are laid down, compacted and cemented, often under the sea | layered with **bedding planes**; many contain fossils; vary from soft (clay) to fairly hard (carboniferous limestone) | chalk of the South Downs; carboniferous limestone of the Yorkshire Dales; London Clay |
| **Metamorphic** | existing rock is changed by great **heat and pressure**, usually deep in mountain belts where plates collided | very hard, often in bands or sheets, resistant to erosion | schists of the Scottish Highlands; slate of Snowdonia and the Lake District |

### The six rock types you must know

**Granite (igneous)**
- Forms when large bodies of magma cool slowly underground, then are exposed when the rocks above are eroded.
- Large interlocking crystals (quartz, feldspar, mica); very **resistant**; **impermeable**, but has **joints** (cracks formed as it cooled and as the overlying rock was removed).
- Landscape: high, rounded **moorland** with boggy, acidic soils, many small streams, and **tors** (blocks of granite left standing on hilltops where weathering along joints has removed the weaker rock around them).
- Where: **Dartmoor** and **Bodmin Moor** in south-west England, the **Cairngorms** and Arran in Scotland, the Mourne Mountains in Northern Ireland.

**Carboniferous limestone (sedimentary)**
- Formed around 300–350 million years ago from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures in warm, shallow seas.
- Grey, fairly hard, made of calcium carbonate, with **joints** and **bedding planes**. It is **permeable** because water passes along these cracks, so there is little surface drainage.
- Rainwater, a weak carbonic acid, dissolves it (**carbonation**), widening the cracks. This produces **karst** scenery: **limestone pavements** (blocks called **clints** separated by gaps called **grykes**), **swallow holes** where streams disappear underground, **caves**, steep **gorges** and cliffs.
- Where: the **Yorkshire Dales** (Malham Cove, Gordale Scar), the White Peak of the **Peak District**, and the Mendips (Cheddar Gorge).

**Chalk (sedimentary)**
- A soft, white, very pure limestone made from the remains of microscopic sea organisms, laid down roughly 70–100 million years ago.
- **Porous** (water soaks into the tiny spaces in the rock), so it has few surface streams.
- Forms gently rolling hills called **downs**, often with a steep **scarp slope** and a gentle **dip slope** (an **escarpment**), and **dry valleys**.
- At the coast it forms tall white cliffs, stacks and arches.
- Where: the **South Downs**, North Downs, Chilterns, Salisbury Plain, the Yorkshire and Lincolnshire Wolds; at the coast, Dover, the Seven Sisters and Old Harry Rocks.

**Clay (sedimentary)**
- Very fine-grained, soft and easily eroded; **impermeable** when wet, so water stays on the surface.
- Forms low, flat or gently undulating **vales** and **basins**, with many streams, heavy wet soils and slumping on slopes.
- Where: the **London Basin** (London Clay), the Hampshire Basin, the Weald clay vales between the North and South Downs. The **boulder clay (till)** of Holderness and East Anglia is a different deposit: it was dumped by ice sheets, not laid down in the sea.

**Schist (metamorphic)**
- Formed when mudstones and similar rocks were put under intense heat and pressure, so new minerals grew in **bands** (it is **foliated** and often glitters with mica).
- Very resistant; forms rugged mountains with thin, poor soils.
- Where: much of the **Scottish Highlands** (the Grampians), and parts of Anglesey.

**Slate (metamorphic)**
- Formed when shale or mudstone is squeezed under pressure, at lower temperatures than schist. It splits into thin, flat sheets (**cleavage**), which is why it was quarried for roofs.
- Hard and **impermeable**; forms steep, craggy uplands with fast-flowing streams.
- Where: **Snowdonia** (Eryri) in north Wales, where huge quarries scar the hillsides, and the northern Lake District.

### The Tees-Exe line

A line drawn from the mouth of the **River Tees** in north-east England to the mouth of the **River Exe** in Devon roughly divides the UK in two.

| | **North and west of the line (upland Britain)** | **South and east of the line (lowland Britain)** |
|---|---|---|
| Rocks | older, harder **igneous** and **metamorphic** rocks, plus older, harder sedimentary rocks such as carboniferous limestone and gritstone | younger, softer **sedimentary** rocks: chalk, clays and sands |
| Relief | high, rugged mountains and moorland; all the UK's highest peaks (Ben Nevis, Snowdon, Scafell Pike) | low hills (chalk downs) and broad clay vales and basins, mostly below a few hundred metres |
| Past processes | shaped by ancient **volcanoes** and **mountain building**, then heavily **eroded by glaciers** | mostly beyond the reach of the thickest ice; shaped by **periglacial** freezing and thawing, and in the east by **glacial deposits** |
| Climate and use | wetter and cooler; rough grazing, forestry, reservoirs, tourism | drier and warmer; arable farming, large cities |

The line is a **generalisation**: south-west England has upland granite (Dartmoor) south of it, and there are lowlands such as the Vale of York and the Central Lowlands of Scotland to its north.

### Past tectonic processes

The UK is no longer near an active plate boundary, but it has been in the past. Over hundreds of millions of years, plate movement carried the land that is now Britain northwards from south of the equator, which is why it contains rocks formed in tropical seas (limestone) and deserts (some sandstones).
- **Volcanic activity**: ancient volcanoes formed the volcanic rocks of the central Lake District and Snowdonia. Arthur's Seat in Edinburgh is the remains of an extinct volcano. The basalt columns of the **Giant's Causeway** formed from lava flows when the North Atlantic began to open.
- **Mountain building (folding)**: when plates collided, rocks were crumpled into mountain ranges and turned into metamorphic rocks; this is how the schists of the Highlands formed. Granite magma was intruded into the crust beneath these mountains, later forming Dartmoor and the Cairngorms.
- **Faulting**: huge faults such as the **Great Glen Fault** (Loch Ness lies along it) and the Highland Boundary Fault mark where blocks of crust moved. Erosion picks out the weakened rock along faults.
- **Later folding**: much more recent plate collisions far to the south gently folded the chalk and clay of southern England, forming the dome of the **Weald** and the down-fold (syncline) of the **London Basin**.

### Past glacial processes

During the last 2.5 million years or so the UK has had many **glacial periods**. At the peak of the most recent one, around 20,000 years ago, ice sheets covered most of Scotland, Wales, northern England and Northern Ireland, reaching roughly as far south as the Midlands and East Anglia. The ice melted roughly 11,000–12,000 years ago.

**Glacial erosion (uplands)**: glaciers eroded by **plucking** (ice freezes onto rock and pulls pieces away) and **abrasion** (rock fragments in the ice scrape the valley floor and sides).
- **Corries** (cirques): armchair-shaped hollows high on mountains, often holding a small lake (tarn), such as Red Tarn below Helvellyn.
- **Arêtes** and **pyramidal peaks**: knife-edge ridges and sharp peaks between corries, such as Striding Edge.
- **U-shaped valleys** (glacial troughs) with steep sides and flat floors, such as Nant Ffrancon in Snowdonia or Glen Coe; **hanging valleys** with waterfalls; **ribbon lakes** such as Windermere.

**Glacial deposition (lowlands)**: as the ice melted it dropped the material it carried.
- **Till (boulder clay)**: unsorted clay and stones spread as flat or gently rolling **till plains**, as in East Anglia and Holderness.
- **Moraines** (ridges of debris), **drumlins** (smooth egg-shaped hills) and **erratics** (boulders carried far from their source rock).

**Periglacial processes**: south of the ice, the ground was frozen (permafrost). Meltwater flowed over frozen chalk that could not absorb it, carving valleys that are now **dry valleys**.

### Skills: photographs and cross-sections

**Photograph analysis.** Describe what you can see, then interpret it. For an upland glacial photo: steep valley sides, flat floor, a lake, bare rock and scree, thin vegetation. For a chalk landscape: smooth rounded hills, no streams, short grass, arable fields. Use **foreground, middle ground and background**, and compass directions if given.

**Geological cross-sections.** A cross-section shows the rock layers beneath a line across the landscape, with the land surface drawn on top. Read it by matching each high or low point to the rock beneath it:
- **resistant rocks** (granite, chalk, limestone) usually form the **high ground** and **escarpments**;
- **weak rocks** (clay) form the **vales** and **basins**;
- where rock layers **dip**, a resistant layer forms a steep scarp on one side and a gentle dip slope on the other.

::: terms
- **Geology**: the rocks that make up an area and how they are arranged, including their type, structure and age.
- **Igneous rock**: rock formed when molten magma or lava cools and solidifies, such as granite or basalt.
- **Sedimentary rock**: rock formed from layers of sediment or the remains of organisms that are compacted and cemented, such as chalk or clay.
- **Metamorphic rock**: rock that has been changed by great heat and pressure, such as schist or slate.
- **Tees-Exe line**: an imaginary line from the mouth of the River Tees to the mouth of the River Exe dividing upland, harder-rock Britain from lowland, softer-rock Britain.
- **Permeable rock**: rock that lets water pass through it, either through pores (chalk) or along joints and bedding planes (carboniferous limestone).
- **Impermeable rock**: rock that does not let water pass through it, such as clay, granite or slate, so water runs over the surface.
- **Tor**: an exposed block of jointed granite left on a hilltop after the surrounding rock has been weathered and removed.
- **Karst**: limestone scenery shaped by solution, with limestone pavements, swallow holes, caves and gorges.
- **Plucking**: glacial erosion in which ice freezes onto rock and pulls fragments away as the glacier moves.
- **Till**: unsorted material (clay, sand and boulders) deposited directly by a glacier or ice sheet, also called boulder clay.
- **Escarpment**: a ridge of resistant rock with a steep scarp slope on one side and a gentle dip slope on the other.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the rock and its group**: granite is igneous; chalk, carboniferous limestone and clay are sedimentary; schist and slate are metamorphic. Never mix these up.
2. **Give a property**: hard or soft, permeable or impermeable, jointed or porous.
3. **Link property to landscape**: say what the property does (resists erosion, so forms high ground; lets water through, so no streams).
4. **Locate it**: use the Tees-Exe line and a named place such as Dartmoor, the Yorkshire Dales or the London Basin.
5. **Add a past process**: tectonic (volcanoes, folding, faults) or glacial (erosion in the uplands, deposition in the lowlands).
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: quote evidence from the figure (heights, rock names, places), explain it with your own knowledge, weigh geology against other factors (glaciation, climate, people), and finish with a judgement on "how important".
:::

::: example Explain two: upland and lowland
Explain two reasons why the north and west of the UK is higher than the south and east. (4)

The north and west is made of older, harder igneous and metamorphic rocks such as granite and schist (1), which resist erosion and so remain as mountains (1). It was also uplifted by ancient mountain building and volcanic activity (1), whereas the south-east is formed of younger, softer chalk and clay that were only gently folded and are easily worn down into low hills and vales (1).
:::

::: example Cross-section: reading geology and relief
Sample data: a geological cross-section runs south to north across the Weald. From south to north it shows chalk (height 210 m), clay (40 m), sandstone (180 m), clay (35 m) and chalk (230 m). Describe the relationship between rock type and relief shown. (3)

The chalk forms the highest ground at both ends of the section, at 210 m and 230 m (1). The clay forms the lowest land, the vales at 35–40 m (1). This is because chalk and sandstone are more resistant than the soft clay, so the clay has been eroded into low ground between ridges of harder rock (1).
:::

::: q
Name one area of the UK where granite forms the landscape. (1)
---
Any one: Dartmoor; Bodmin Moor; the Cairngorms; Arran; the Mourne Mountains; any other correct example (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why chalk landscapes have few surface rivers. (2)
---
Chalk is porous (1), so rainwater soaks down into the rock instead of running over the surface, leaving dry valleys (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way in which glacial erosion has shaped upland areas of the UK. (2)
---
Glaciers deepened and widened river valleys by plucking and abrasion (1), creating steep-sided, flat-floored U-shaped valleys such as Nant Ffrancon or Borrowdale (1). Or: ice carved hollows on mountainsides (1), forming corries such as Red Tarn (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two characteristics of carboniferous limestone landscapes. (4)
---
Two from: limestone pavements (1), formed as rain dissolves joints into grykes between clints (1); little surface water / swallow holes (1), because water drains through joints and bedding planes (1); caves and caverns (1), where underground streams dissolve the rock (1); steep gorges and cliffs (1), such as Malham Cove or Cheddar Gorge, as the rock is strong and well jointed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one difference between the landscapes formed on clay and on slate. (3)
---
Clay is soft and easily eroded, so it forms low, flat vales and basins such as the London Basin (1); slate is hard and resistant, so it forms steep, high, craggy uplands such as Snowdonia (1). Both are impermeable, but on clay water lies on heavy, wet soils, while on slate it runs off quickly down steep slopes in fast streams (1).
:::

::: watch
- Putting rocks in the wrong group. Granite is **igneous**; chalk, limestone and clay are **sedimentary**; schist and slate are **metamorphic**.
- Calling chalk impermeable. It is **porous**, so it is permeable. Carboniferous limestone is permeable because of its **joints and bedding planes**.
- Saying upland Britain is "all granite". It includes schist, slate, volcanic rocks and older sedimentary rocks such as carboniferous limestone.
- Treating the Tees-Exe line as exact. Say it is a **rough** divide and give an exception (Dartmoor).
- Mixing up glacial **erosion** (uplands: corries, U-shaped valleys) and glacial **deposition** (lowlands: till plains, drumlins).
- Describing a rock without saying **what it does to the landscape**. Always finish the chain: property, then landform.
- In the 8-marker, not quoting the figure. Half the marks are AO4: use its heights, rock names and places.
:::
