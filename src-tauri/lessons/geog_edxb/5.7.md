## What it is

Cities and the countryside around them depend on each other. This key idea asks you to explain:
- how the city and **accessible rural areas** are **interdependent**, through **flows of goods, services and labour**, which bring **economic, social and environmental costs and benefits for both**;
- why a **rural area** has experienced **economic and social changes** because of its links with the city: **counter-urbanisation**, **pressure on housing**, **increased leisure and recreation** and **population change**.

It is examined in **Paper 2, Section B**, including the 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question, which may carry **4 SPaG marks**. This course links **London** with **the Chilterns**, the chalk hills north-west of the city in Buckinghamshire, Hertfordshire and Oxfordshire. Any rural area linked to the city you studied earns the marks.

### The Chilterns: a rural area linked to London

- A range of **chalk hills** running south-west to north-east, about 40–60 km from central London, with steep wooded slopes (famous **beech woods**), dry valleys, rare **chalk streams** such as the Chess and the Misbourne, and chalk grassland.
- Protected as an **Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty** since **1965**; since **2023** these areas have been called **National Landscapes**. Much of it is also in London's **Metropolitan Green Belt**.
- Settlements: small market towns such as **Amersham**, **Chesham**, **Great Missenden**, **Wendover** and **Princes Risborough**, and many villages, with larger towns such as **High Wycombe** and **Aylesbury** on the edges.
- **Transport links**: the **Metropolitan line** of the London Underground runs out to Amersham and Chesham; **Chiltern Railways** runs from London Marylebone through High Wycombe, Beaconsfield and Princes Risborough; the M40 and M25 cross or skirt the area. These links make it one of London's most popular **commuter** areas.

### Interdependence: the flows

| Flow | From the Chilterns to London | From London to the Chilterns |
|---|---|---|
| **Labour** | commuters travel daily to jobs in the City, West End and Canary Wharf | some workers travel out to jobs in local schools, care homes, shops, hospitality and farms; London-based firms have offices in the area |
| **Goods** | food from farms; **water** drawn from the chalk aquifer; timber; local products such as beer and wine | consumer goods, delivered from London-area warehouses; some waste goes out from the city to landfill and processing sites in the wider region |
| **Services** | **leisure and recreation**: walking, cycling, country pubs, open space, fresh air, film locations | specialist hospitals, universities, theatres, museums, airports, finance and legal services, major shopping |
| **Money** | — | London wages spent locally by commuters; spending by day visitors and tourists; investment in second homes |

### Costs and benefits of interdependence

**For the rural area (the Chilterns)**
| | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | high incomes from London jobs are spent locally, supporting shops, pubs and services; tourism spending; rising property values for owners | **house prices** far beyond local wages; local shops lose trade to London and online; village services that rely on daytime trade struggle when commuters are away |
| **Social** | access to London's jobs, hospitals and culture; well-educated, active newcomers who join community groups | young local people priced out; **dormitory villages** that are quiet in the day; a widening gap between wealthy newcomers and long-standing residents |
| **Environmental** | wealthy residents and visitors support conservation; green belt and protected status limit building | traffic congestion and parking on narrow lanes; pressure on footpaths and woods; **water abstraction** lowers chalk streams; development pressure on the green belt |

**For the city (London)**
| | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | a large, skilled workforce without having to house it; food and water supplies | congestion on commuter railways and roads at peak times; wages spent outside London |
| **Social** | open countryside for recreation and health within an hour of the city | long commutes and stress for workers; some skilled workers and families leave the city |
| **Environmental** | countryside absorbs some pollution and supplies water; green belt limits sprawl | commuting adds traffic, carbon emissions and air pollution |

### Economic and social change in the Chilterns

**Counter-urbanisation**
- Since the mid-20th century, many people have moved **out of London** into the Chilterns' towns and villages.
- **Push factors** from the city: high housing costs per square metre, crowding, pollution, crime, and the wish for space and gardens for families.
- **Pull factors** of the Chilterns: attractive scenery, good schools, large houses, low crime, and fast rail links back to London jobs.
- **Home working**, which grew sharply during and after the Covid-19 pandemic, has made it even easier to live there and commute only a few days a week.

**Pressure on housing**
- Demand is very high, but supply is limited: protected-landscape and green-belt rules restrict new building.
- The result is some of the **highest house prices in England outside London**, many times local average earnings.
- Young people who grew up in the area often cannot afford to stay; local workers in shops, care and farming may have to commute **in** from cheaper towns.
- Some homes are bought as **second homes** or holiday lets, reducing housing for local people.
- There is constant debate over where to build new homes, for example on the edges of towns such as Aylesbury and High Wycombe.

**Increased leisure and recreation**
- Millions of visits a year come from London and nearby towns: walking the **Ridgeway** National Trail and other footpaths, cycling, mountain biking in woods such as Wendover Woods, horse riding, country pubs and tea rooms.
- **Wildlife tourism**: the **red kite**, reintroduced to the Chilterns from the late 1980s, is now a common sight.
- **Film tourism**: Chilterns villages appear in many films and TV series; for example, Turville was the setting for The Vicar of Dibley.
- **Effects**: income for pubs, cafés and farm businesses (see 5.8), but also car traffic, parking problems, footpath erosion, litter and disturbance to wildlife.

**Population change**
- The population has grown through in-migration, but it is **older** and **wealthier** than the UK average: many professionals with families and many retirees, but fewer young adults aged 18–30, who leave for university, jobs and cheaper housing.
- The area's **age structure** shows a narrow band at 20–30 and a wide top.
- Services change: village schools may lose pupils, while demand for health care and care homes grows.

**HS2: the city's influence beneath the hills**
- The **HS2** high-speed railway from London to Birmingham passes under much of the Chilterns in a **tunnel about 16 km long**, built to reduce damage to the protected landscape.
- **Concerns**: construction traffic, ventilation shafts and spoil; risks to the chalk aquifer and streams; a viaduct and cuttings where the line emerges; trains do not stop in the Chilterns, so locals gain little.
- It is an example of the city's needs (fast links between major cities) being met at a cost to the rural area.

::: terms
- **Interdependence**: when two places rely on each other through flows of people, goods, services and money.
- **Accessible rural area**: countryside close enough to a city, with good transport links, for people to commute daily.
- **Counter-urbanisation**: the movement of people out of cities into towns, villages and the countryside.
- **Commuter**: a person who travels regularly from home to a place of work in another settlement.
- **Dormitory village**: a village where many residents commute elsewhere to work, so it is quiet during the day.
- **Second home**: a property owned in addition to a main home, used for holidays or weekends.
- **Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty**: protected countryside of high landscape value in England and Wales, now called a National Landscape.
- **Green belt**: a protected zone of open land around a city where new building is strictly controlled.
- **Water abstraction**: taking water from rivers or underground aquifers for homes, farms and industry.
- **Chalk stream**: a clear, spring-fed river flowing over chalk, a rare habitat found mainly in England.
:::

::: method
1. **Name both places**: London and the Chilterns (or your own rural area). Interdependence is **two-way**.
2. **Flows**: use the spec's three: **goods**, **services**, **labour**. Say which way each flows and why.
3. **Costs and benefits**: organise by **economic**, **social**, **environmental**, and by **place** (city and rural area). A top answer covers both places.
4. **Change questions**: give the **cause** (link to London: commuting, prices, leisure) and the **effect** (on housing, population, services, environment).
5. **Use real detail**: Metropolitan line to Amersham and Chesham, Chiltern Railways, AONB or National Landscape, green belt, house prices, red kites, the Ridgeway, HS2 tunnel.
6. **8-mark + SPaG**: use figure data throughout, weigh costs against benefits for different groups and both places, finish with a supported judgement, and write accurately with specialist terms.
:::

::: example 8-mark + SPaG: change in the rural area (Level 3 extract)
Analyse Figure 12. Figure 12 shows changes in a Chilterns village between 2001 and 2021 (sample data): population up from 2,400 to 2,700; residents aged 65+ up from 18% to 29%; residents aged 20–29 down from 10% to 6%; average house price up 140%; commuters to London 35% of workers; village bus service cut from hourly to three a day; pub converted to a house. Assess the impact of links with London on the village. (8)

Figure 12 shows that links with London have changed the village's population. It grew from 2,400 to 2,700 through counter-urbanisation, as families and professionals moved out of the city for space and scenery while keeping London jobs: 35% of workers commute there. At the same time, the share aged 65 and over rose from 18% to 29%, while 20–29 year olds fell from 10% to 6%, so the village is ageing.

The biggest impact is on housing. A 140% rise in prices reflects demand from wealthy incomers, which young local people cannot match, explaining the loss of young adults. Commuters spend much of their money in London or online and drive rather than use the bus, so services such as the bus and the pub have declined, which hurts older residents without cars most.

Overall, links with London have brought wealth and population growth but have made the village less balanced and less affordable. The negative social impacts, especially for young and elderly residents, outweigh the economic benefits, which go mainly to homeowners and incomers.

::: watch
- Describing only commuters into London. Interdependence is **two-way**: food, water, leisure and some workers flow the other way.
- Covering only the rural area when the question says **"for both"**. Give costs and benefits for the city too.
- Mixing up **counter-urbanisation** (moving out of cities) with **suburbanisation** (moving to the edge of the same city).
- Writing "it's prettier" as the only reason for moving. Use push and pull factors and the role of transport links.
- Treating change as all positive. Rising prices, ageing, service decline and traffic are real costs.
- Calling the Chilterns a national park. It is an **AONB**, now called a **National Landscape**.
- Losing SPaG marks in the 8-marker: write in full sentences and use terms such as counter-urbanisation and interdependence.
