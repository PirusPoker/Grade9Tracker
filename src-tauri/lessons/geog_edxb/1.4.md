## What it is

A **tropical cyclone** is a huge, rotating storm system that forms over warm tropical oceans. The same type of storm has different names in different oceans:
- **hurricane** in the North Atlantic, Caribbean and north-east Pacific;
- **typhoon** in the north-west Pacific;
- **cyclone** in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific.

This key idea covers what tropical cyclones are like, where and when they form, how the global circulation (1.1) creates them, why some intensify and how they die away.

### Characteristics

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Pressure** | very low at the centre, often below 950 millibars in a strong storm (normal sea-level pressure is about 1,013 mb). The lower the pressure, the stronger the winds |
| **Wind** | sustained winds of at least 119 km/h (74 mph) to be classed as a hurricane; the strongest exceed 250 km/h |
| **Rotation** | air spirals inwards, **anticlockwise** in the northern hemisphere and **clockwise** in the southern, because of the Coriolis effect |
| **Size** | typically several hundred kilometres across |
| **Eye** | the calm centre, often 10–50 km wide, with sinking air, light winds and clear skies |
| **Eyewall** | a ring of towering cumulonimbus clouds around the eye, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain |
| **Rain bands** | spiral bands of cloud and heavy showers stretching out from the centre |

On a satellite image you can recognise the spiral of cloud and the small, dark, cloud-free eye.

### Distribution, source areas, seasons and tracks

**Source areas** are warm tropical oceans between about **5° and 30°** north and south of the equator:
- the tropical North Atlantic and Caribbean;
- the north-east Pacific off Mexico;
- the north-west Pacific, east of the Philippines (the most active region);
- the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea;
- the south Indian Ocean, around Madagascar and north-west Australia;
- the south-west Pacific.

They are very rare in the South Atlantic and south-east Pacific, where the water is cooler and wind shear is strong.

**Seasons.** Cyclones form in **late summer and autumn**, when sea temperatures are highest. The Atlantic season runs officially from June to November and peaks from August to October. In the southern hemisphere the season is roughly November to April.

**Tracks.** Most storms are carried **westwards** by the trade winds, then **curve polewards**, and often turn **eastwards** as they reach the westerlies in the mid-latitudes. That is why Atlantic hurricanes often hit the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico and then curve up the east coast of the USA, and why typhoons strike the Philippines, China and Japan.

**How they may change over time.** With climate change, scientists expect:
- a greater **proportion** of very intense storms (Categories 4 and 5), because warmer seas supply more energy;
- **heavier rainfall**, because warmer air holds more moisture;
- **higher storm surges**, because sea level is rising;
- storms reaching peak strength **further from the equator**, so places such as Japan or the north-east USA may face stronger storms.

The total **number** of storms may not increase, and some studies suggest it could fall. This uncertainty is useful in 8-mark answers.

### How the global circulation creates tropical cyclones

At the **ITCZ**, intense heating makes warm, moist air rise and creates low pressure, and the trade winds converge there (1.1). Where this happens over a warm ocean, at least about 5° from the equator, a cyclone can start.

**Conditions needed:**
1. **Sea surface temperature of at least about 26.5 °C**, with warm water extending tens of metres deep, to supply heat and moisture.
2. **At least about 5° from the equator**, where the **Coriolis effect** is strong enough to make the air spin. At the equator itself there is no spin, so cyclones do not form there.
3. **Low wind shear**, meaning winds at different heights blow at similar speeds and directions, so the tall storm is not torn apart.
4. **Moist, unstable air** and an existing area of low pressure, such as a cluster of thunderstorms drifting west from Africa.

**How it grows:**
1. Warm, moist air rises rapidly from the ocean surface.
2. As it rises it cools and the water vapour condenses, releasing **latent heat**.
3. The latent heat warms the surrounding air, which rises faster, lowering pressure at the surface further.
4. More warm, moist air is drawn in at the surface and spirals inwards because of the Coriolis effect.
5. At the top the air flows outwards, and some sinks in the centre, forming the eye.
6. The storm is a self-feeding heat engine as long as it stays over warm water.

### Intensification and dissipation

A storm **intensifies** when it moves over very warm, deep water, wind shear is low and it stays away from land. Some storms intensify rapidly, strengthening by several categories in a day or two, which makes forecasting and evacuation harder.

A storm **dissipates** when:
- it makes **landfall**, cutting off its supply of warm, moist air, while friction with the land slows the winds;
- it moves over **cooler water**, below about 26 °C;
- it meets strong **wind shear**, which tears the vertical structure apart;
- it moves into the mid-latitudes and merges with cooler air, becoming an ordinary depression.

Even a weakening storm can still bring torrential rain and flooding far inland.

### Skill: tracking cyclones with GIS

GIS maps show a storm's path as a line of points, often coloured by category. Describe:
- the **direction** of travel, using compass points;
- where it **strengthened and weakened**, linked to sea temperature and landfall;
- its **speed**, from the distance between points taken at fixed time intervals.

You can also use the **Saffir-Simpson scale** to classify a storm from its wind speed. It is taught in full in 1.5.

::: terms
- **Tropical cyclone**: an intense, rotating low-pressure storm that forms over warm tropical oceans.
- **Eye**: the calm, cloud-free centre of a tropical cyclone, where air sinks.
- **Eyewall**: the ring of tall cumulonimbus cloud around the eye, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
- **Source area**: an ocean region where tropical cyclones form, such as the north-west Pacific.
- **Track**: the path a tropical cyclone follows across the Earth's surface.
- **Latent heat**: heat released when water vapour condenses, which powers a tropical cyclone.
- **Wind shear**: a change in wind speed or direction with height, which can tear a storm apart.
- **Coriolis effect**: the deflection caused by the Earth's rotation that makes cyclones spin.
- **Landfall**: the moment the centre of a tropical cyclone crosses the coast onto land.
- **Dissipation**: the weakening and dying away of a tropical cyclone.
:::

::: method
1. **Characteristics:** use the spec's three headings: **pressure** (very low), **rotation** (direction by hemisphere), **structure** (eye, eyewall, rain bands).
2. **Formation:** give the conditions with numbers (26.5 °C, 5° from the equator), then the latent-heat chain in order.
3. **Distribution:** use latitudes and named oceans; link the season to sea temperature.
4. **GIS track maps:** direction, change in strength, and a reason linked to sea temperature or land.
5. **Dissipation:** always say what supply is cut off: warm, moist air.
6. **8-mark assess on climate change:** explain the likely changes (intensity, rainfall, surge, poleward shift), then weigh how certain each is and conclude.
:::

::: example Explain one: the equator
Explain one reason why tropical cyclones do not form at the equator. (2)

The Coriolis effect is too weak within about 5° of the equator (1), so the rising air cannot be made to spin into a rotating storm (1).
:::

::: example Explain one way: latent heat
Explain one way a tropical cyclone gains energy. (3)

Warm, moist air rises from the ocean surface (1). As it rises it cools and condenses, releasing latent heat (1), which warms the air so it rises faster, lowering pressure and drawing in more moist air (1).
:::

::: example GIS track
Sample data: a GIS track shows a hurricane moving west across the Atlantic at Category 1, strengthening to Category 4 over the Gulf of Mexico, then falling to a tropical storm within a day of crossing the coast of Louisiana. Describe and explain the changes in strength. (4)

The storm strengthened from Category 1 to Category 4 as it crossed the Gulf (1) because the water there is very warm and supplies more heat and moisture (1). It weakened rapidly after landfall in Louisiana (1) because it was cut off from its supply of warm, moist air and friction with the land slowed the winds (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: climate change
Assess the extent to which climate change might change the distribution and strength of tropical cyclones. (8)

Climate change is likely to affect strength more than numbers. Warmer seas provide more heat energy through evaporation and latent heat, so a larger share of storms is expected to reach Categories 4 and 5. Warmer air holds more moisture, so rainfall in each storm should be heavier, and rising sea levels will make storm surges more dangerous even if wind speeds stayed the same.

Distribution may also change. As more ocean exceeds about 26.5 °C, storms could reach peak strength further from the equator, threatening places such as Japan or the north-east USA. Seasons might lengthen as seas warm earlier in the year.

However, there is real uncertainty. Wind shear may increase in some regions, which would suppress storms, and some studies suggest the total number may stay the same or even fall. Past records before satellites are incomplete, so trends are hard to prove.

Overall, climate change is likely to make individual storms more damaging, especially through rainfall and surges, but its effect on how many form and exactly where is less certain.

(Level 3: changes in strength and distribution explained and weighed against uncertainty, with a clear judgement.)
:::

Explain two conditions needed for a tropical cyclone to form. (4)
---
Sea above about 26.5 °C (1), supplying heat and moisture to power the storm (1). At least 5° from the equator (1), so the Coriolis effect can make it spin (1). Low wind shear (1), so the tall storm is not torn apart (1). Moist, unstable air (1), allowing strong upward movement and condensation (1).

::: watch
- Saying cyclones form "at the equator". They form 5–30° from it.
- Giving conditions with no reasons: "26.5 °C" alone is 1 mark; add what the warm water does.
- Getting the rotation wrong: **anticlockwise** in the northern hemisphere, **clockwise** in the southern.
- Calling the eye the most dangerous part. The **eyewall** has the strongest winds; the eye is calm.
- Overclaiming about climate change: say "more intense" and "heavier rain" with confidence, but "more frequent" only with caution.
- Forgetting that weakening storms can still cause severe inland flooding.
:::
