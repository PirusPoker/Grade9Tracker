## What it is

There is more than one way to develop a country. Some approaches start at the top, with governments, international organisations and big companies investing in huge projects. Others start at the bottom, with communities and charities improving lives village by village. This key idea covers:
- the characteristics of **top-down** and **bottom-up** strategies;
- how **TNCs and governments** drive **globalisation**, and why some countries benefit more than others;
- the advantages and disadvantages of three approaches: **NGO-led intermediate technology**, **IGO-funded large infrastructure** and **TNC investment**.

Any real, named examples of each approach earn the marks; this lesson uses well-known ones.

### Top-down and bottom-up compared

| | **Top-down** | **Bottom-up** |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale** | large: national or regional | small: a village, community or neighbourhood |
| **Aims** | economic growth; infrastructure such as power, roads, ports; national targets | meeting basic needs of the poorest: water, food, health, education, income |
| **Funding** | governments, **IGOs** such as the World Bank, loans, TNC investment | **NGOs** and charities, donations, community savings, small loans (microfinance) |
| **Technology** | high-tech, capital-intensive, often imported with foreign experts | **intermediate (appropriate) technology**: affordable, simple, built and repaired locally |
| **Decision-making** | made by governments and experts; local people may not be consulted | local people involved in planning and running projects |
| **Speed of impact** | can transform a region, but benefits may take years to "trickle down" | quick, direct benefits for the people involved, but limited reach |

### Globalisation: the processes and the players

**Globalisation** is the growing connection of the world's economies, cultures and people through trade, investment, migration and communications. It has been sped up by **container shipping**, cheaper air travel, the **internet** and mobile phones.

**Transnational corporations (TNCs)** are companies operating in more than one country.
- They **outsource** or **offshore** work to countries with lower costs, building factories, call centres and offices there.
- They invest through **foreign direct investment (FDI)**.
- They create global **supply chains**: a phone may be designed in the USA, assembled in China or Vietnam, from parts made in several countries.

**Governments** make their countries attractive to globalisation:
- opening up to trade and FDI, lowering tariffs and joining trade agreements;
- creating **special economic zones** with tax breaks and fewer regulations;
- investing in ports, roads, power and education;
- privatising state industries.

**Why some countries have benefited more than others:**

| Benefited more | Benefited less |
|---|---|
| coastal, well connected, near big markets | landlocked or remote, high transport costs |
| educated, often English-speaking workforce (India's IT sector) | low literacy and skills |
| stable government, pro-FDI policies (China's special economic zones) | conflict, corruption, unstable policies |
| good infrastructure and reliable power | poor roads, ports and power supply |
| large domestic market attracting TNCs | small market, little to attract investment |

Many East and South Asian countries have grown rapidly through globalisation, while much of sub-Saharan Africa has gained less, often exporting raw materials rather than manufactured goods.

### Approach 1: NGO-led intermediate technology (bottom-up)

**Non-governmental organisations** such as **Practical Action** and **WaterAid** work with communities to install technology that people can afford, operate and repair themselves: hand pumps, small water tanks, fuel-efficient cooking stoves, small-scale (micro) hydro schemes in mountain villages, and solar lighting.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| cheap; uses local materials and skills, so it can be maintained without outside experts | small scale: helps one community at a time |
| targets the poorest directly | depends on donations, which may stop |
| community involvement gives local ownership, so projects last | does not transform the national economy or create many jobs |
| often environmentally sustainable | NGOs may not coordinate with each other or the government |

### Approach 2: IGO-funded large infrastructure (top-down)

**Intergovernmental organisations** such as the **World Bank**, the **International Monetary Fund** and regional development banks lend or give money for large projects: hydroelectric **dams**, power grids, highways, ports and irrigation schemes.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| large amounts of electricity and water, supplying industry and cities | very expensive; loans add to national **debt** |
| infrastructure attracts TNCs and investment | large dams can **displace** thousands of people and flood farmland and habitats |
| construction creates jobs | benefits often go to cities and industry rather than the rural poor |
| can transform a whole region | relies on foreign experts and imported technology; loans may come with conditions |

### Approach 3: investment by TNCs (top-down, private)

TNCs build factories, offices and plants, for example car factories, electronics assembly plants (Samsung's large investment in Vietnam) and call centres.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| creates jobs, often better paid than farming | **profits** are sent back to the TNC's home country |
| brings new skills and technology | wages can be low and conditions poor; long hours |
| pays taxes and may improve local infrastructure | **footloose**: may move to a cheaper country |
| a **multiplier effect** as suppliers and shops grow | may cause pollution and outcompete local firms |
| exports earn foreign currency | TNCs can pressure governments over tax and regulation |

::: terms
- **Top-down development**: large-scale projects planned and funded by governments, IGOs or TNCs, imposed from above.
- **Bottom-up development**: small-scale projects that involve local communities in meeting their own needs.
- **Intermediate technology**: affordable, simple technology that local people can build, use and repair.
- **Non-governmental organisation (NGO)**: a not-for-profit organisation independent of government, such as a charity.
- **Intergovernmental organisation (IGO)**: an organisation of member governments, such as the World Bank or IMF.
- **Transnational corporation (TNC)**: a large company that operates in more than one country.
- **Globalisation**: the increasing connection of the world's economies, cultures and people.
- **Outsourcing**: moving work to another company, often in a country with lower costs.
- **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**: investment by a company in one country into businesses or facilities in another.
- **Multiplier effect**: a chain of economic benefits as new jobs create spending and more jobs.
:::

::: method
1. **Characteristics questions:** use the spec's four headings: **scale, aims, funding, technology**.
2. **Advantage or disadvantage:** say who gains or loses and how, e.g. "a dam supplies electricity to factories, attracting TNCs, but displaces farming families".
3. **Globalisation questions:** name the player (TNC or government), what it does, and the result.
4. **Why some countries benefit more:** compare location, workforce, governance and infrastructure.
5. **8-mark evaluate:** judge each approach on who benefits, how many, how long it lasts, and its costs; compare, and reach a conclusion that may depend on context.
:::

::: example Explain one: intermediate technology
Explain one advantage of NGO-led intermediate technology projects. (2)

They use simple equipment such as hand pumps that local people can repair (1), so the project keeps working after the NGO leaves, without costly foreign experts (1).
:::

::: example Explain one way: governments and globalisation
Explain one way governments can encourage globalisation. (3)

Governments set up special economic zones (1) with lower taxes and fewer regulations (1), which attract TNCs to build factories, increasing trade and investment (1).
:::

::: example One advantage, one disadvantage: dams
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of IGO-funded large dams. (4)

Advantage: they generate large amounts of hydroelectricity (1), which supplies cities and factories and can attract foreign investment (1). Disadvantage: the reservoir floods valleys (1), displacing thousands of people from their homes and farmland, often with poor compensation (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: bottom-up against top-down
Evaluate the effectiveness of bottom-up approaches to development compared with top-down approaches. (8)

Bottom-up approaches are very effective at reaching the poorest. NGOs such as Practical Action and WaterAid install hand pumps, stoves and small hydro schemes that are cheap and repaired locally, so they keep working. Because communities are involved, the projects meet real needs and last.

However, their reach is limited. Each project helps one village, depends on donations, and does little to create jobs or raise national income. They cannot build the power stations, ports and roads a country needs to industrialise.

Top-down approaches can transform whole regions. IGO-funded dams supply electricity to cities and industry, and TNC investment creates jobs, skills and exports. Yet the benefits may not reach the rural poor; large dams displace people, loans add debt, and TNC profits often leave the country.

Overall, bottom-up approaches are more effective at improving quality of life for the poorest quickly and sustainably, while top-down approaches are more effective for economic growth. Neither is enough alone; the most successful countries combine national investment with local projects.

(Level 3: both approaches judged with examples and evidence; a balanced, substantiated conclusion.)
:::

::: q
Explain one way TNCs contribute to globalisation. (2)
---
They outsource production to lower-cost countries (1), creating global supply chains that link economies through trade (1). Or: they invest FDI abroad (1), spreading factories and offices across countries (1).

::: q
Explain one reason why some countries have benefited more from globalisation than others. (2)
---
An educated, English-speaking workforce (1) attracted IT outsourcing to India (1). Or: coastal location and ports (1) make exporting cheaper (1). Or: stable, pro-FDI governments (1) give TNCs confidence to invest (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of TNC investment for a developing country. (2)
---
Profits are sent back to the TNC's home country (1), so less money stays to benefit the host economy (1). Or: TNCs are footloose (1) and may close the factory if costs rise (1).
:::

::: q
Compare top-down and bottom-up strategies in terms of funding and technology. (4)
---
Top-down projects are funded by governments, IGOs or TNCs, whereas bottom-up projects are funded by NGOs, donations or community savings (1+1). Top-down uses high-tech, imported technology needing experts, whereas bottom-up uses intermediate technology that local people can maintain (1+1).
:::

::: q
Explain two advantages of TNC investment for an emerging country. (4)
---
Two from: jobs (1), often better paid than farming (1); skills and technology transfer (1), raising productivity (1); taxes (1) to fund services (1); multiplier effect (1) as local suppliers grow (1); exports (1) earning foreign currency (1).
:::

::: watch
- Calling intermediate technology just "cheap technology". The key is that people can **use and repair it locally**.
- Using only one characteristic when the spec gives four: scale, aims, funding, technology.
- Treating TNC investment as bottom-up. It is top-down, private investment.
- Confusing NGOs and IGOs: NGOs are charities; IGOs are groups of governments.
- One-sided evaluation: every approach has costs and benefits, and who benefits matters.
- Forgetting that globalisation benefits are uneven between countries and within them.
:::
