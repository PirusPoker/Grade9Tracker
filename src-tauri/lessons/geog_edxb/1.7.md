## What it is

To understand earthquakes and volcanoes you first need to know what the Earth is like inside. This key idea covers the **layers** of the Earth, each with its own **temperature, density, composition and physical state**, and the **heat source** that drives **convection** in the mantle, which is the foundation of plate tectonics.

### The layers

Numbers here are approximate; examiners want the pattern (hotter and denser with depth) and the state of each layer.

| Layer | Depth / thickness | Composition | State | Temperature | Density |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Continental crust** | about 30–70 km thick | granite-type rocks, rich in silica and aluminium | solid | cool at the surface, a few hundred °C at its base | lowest, about 2.7 g/cm³ |
| **Oceanic crust** | about 5–10 km thick | basalt, rich in iron and magnesium | solid | as above | about 3.0 g/cm³ |
| **Mantle** | to about 2,900 km depth | silicate rock rich in iron and magnesium | mostly solid, but hot enough to flow very slowly (like toffee) | about 1,000 °C near the top to over 3,500 °C near the core | about 3.3–5.5 g/cm³ |
| **Asthenosphere** (upper mantle) | roughly 100–250 km below the surface | as the mantle | partly molten, weak and plastic | over about 1,300 °C | as the upper mantle |
| **Outer core** | about 2,900–5,100 km | iron and nickel | **liquid** | about 4,000–5,000 °C | about 10 g/cm³ |
| **Inner core** | about 5,100–6,370 km (the centre) | iron and nickel | **solid**, because the pressure is enormous | about 5,000–6,000 °C, similar to the Sun's surface | highest, about 13 g/cm³ |

Two terms are easy to confuse:
- The **lithosphere** is the rigid outer shell: the crust plus the very top of the mantle. It is broken into **tectonic plates**.
- The **asthenosphere** is the weak, partly molten layer below it. The plates move over it.

**Continental and oceanic crust compared**

| | Continental | Oceanic |
|---|---|---|
| Rock | granite (lighter) | basalt (heavier) |
| Thickness | thick (about 30–70 km) | thin (about 5–10 km) |
| Density | less dense | more dense |
| Age | can be billions of years old | younger than about 200 million years |
| Can it sink? | no, too buoyant | yes, it is **subducted** at convergent boundaries |

This density difference matters in 1.8: when oceanic and continental plates meet, the denser oceanic plate always sinks.

### The heat source

The Earth's interior is hot for two reasons:
1. **Radioactive decay.** Unstable elements such as uranium, thorium and potassium break down, releasing heat continuously.
2. **Primordial heat.** Some heat is left over from the Earth's formation about 4.6 billion years ago.

Heat flows outwards from the core into the base of the mantle. Because the mantle is so thick and rock conducts heat poorly, it cannot lose this heat fast enough by conduction alone, so it moves the heat by **convection**.

### How convection drives the plates

1. Mantle rock near the core is heated, **expands** and becomes **less dense**.
2. The less dense rock **rises** slowly towards the surface, a few centimetres a year.
3. Beneath the lithosphere it spreads out **sideways**, dragging the plates above it.
4. As it moves away from the heat source it **cools**, becomes **denser** and **sinks** back towards the core.
5. It is reheated and the loop continues. These loops are **convection currents** (or convection cells).

Where currents rise and diverge, plates are pulled apart (divergent boundaries). Where they converge and sink, plates move together (convergent boundaries).

### Other forces: slab pull and ridge push

Scientists now think convection is not the only, or even the main, force.
- **Slab pull.** At a convergent boundary, old, cold, dense oceanic plate sinks into the mantle and **pulls the rest of the plate** behind it, like a tablecloth sliding off a table. Plates attached to sinking slabs, such as the Pacific plate, move fastest, which suggests slab pull is the strongest force.
- **Ridge push.** At mid-ocean ridges, new hot rock sits higher than the surrounding sea floor. As it cools and becomes denser, gravity makes it **slide away from the ridge**, pushing the plate.

All three work together, and all depend on heat from the interior. Convection supplies the heat and the hot, rising rock that builds ridges; slab pull and ridge push are driven by density differences created by heating and cooling.

### Skill: interpreting a cross-section of the Earth

Pearson may show a labelled or part-labelled cross-section. Be ready to:
- **label** the inner core, outer core, mantle, asthenosphere and crust;
- **read** depths and temperatures from a scale;
- **describe** how temperature and density change with depth (both increase);
- **explain** why the inner core is solid but the outer core liquid: the inner core is hotter, but the pressure is so great that the iron cannot melt.

::: terms
- **Inner core**: the solid centre of the Earth, made of iron and nickel, kept solid by enormous pressure.
- **Outer core**: the liquid layer of iron and nickel around the inner core.
- **Mantle**: the thick layer of hot silicate rock between the crust and the core, which flows very slowly.
- **Asthenosphere**: the weak, partly molten upper part of the mantle on which the tectonic plates move.
- **Lithosphere**: the rigid outer shell of the Earth, made of the crust and uppermost mantle, broken into plates.
- **Continental crust**: thick, less dense crust made of granite-type rocks that forms the continents.
- **Oceanic crust**: thin, denser crust made of basalt that forms the ocean floors.
- **Radioactive decay**: the breakdown of unstable elements such as uranium, releasing heat inside the Earth.
- **Convection current**: a loop of heated material rising, spreading, cooling and sinking in the mantle.
- **Slab pull**: the pulling of a plate by its dense oceanic edge sinking into the mantle.
- **Ridge push**: the sliding of new plate away from a mid-ocean ridge under gravity.
:::

::: method
1. **Layer questions:** use the four properties the spec names (temperature, density, composition, state) and compare with "hotter than", "denser than".
2. **Crust questions:** contrast continental and oceanic crust on rock type, thickness and density, and link density to subduction.
3. **Convection questions:** give the steps in order: heated, less dense, rises, spreads, cools, denser, sinks. Name the heat source (radioactive decay).
4. **Explain one reason for plate movement (2 marks):** name the force and then how it moves the plate.
5. **Cross-section skill:** read values with units; describe the trend with depth.
6. **8-mark assess:** explain convection, then slab pull and ridge push, weigh the evidence (fastest plates have sinking slabs) and judge which matters most.
:::

::: example Explain one: plate movement
Explain one reason for plate movement. (2)

Slab pull (1): the cold, dense edge of an oceanic plate sinks into the mantle at a convergent boundary and drags the rest of the plate behind it (1).
:::

::: example Compare crust
Compare continental and oceanic crust. (3)

Continental crust is thicker than oceanic crust, about 30–70 km against 5–10 km (1). Continental crust is made of lighter granite-type rock, whereas oceanic crust is denser basalt (1). Oceanic crust is younger and can be subducted, whereas continental crust is too buoyant to sink (1).
:::

::: example Explain one way: convection
Explain one way heat from the Earth's core causes the plates to move. (3)

Heat from radioactive decay warms the mantle rock near the core, making it less dense so it rises (1). Under the lithosphere it spreads sideways, dragging the plates above it (1), then cools, becomes denser and sinks, forming a convection current that keeps the plates moving (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: forces
Assess the importance of convection currents compared with other forces in moving tectonic plates. (8)

Convection currents are the traditional explanation. Heat from radioactive decay in the core warms the base of the mantle; the rock becomes less dense and rises, spreads beneath the lithosphere and drags the plates, then cools and sinks. This explains why plates move apart above rising currents and together where currents sink.

However, other forces may be more important. Slab pull happens where old, dense oceanic crust sinks at a convergent boundary and pulls the plate behind it. Evidence supports this: plates with long sinking edges, such as the Pacific plate, move fastest, while plates mainly made of continent move slowly. Ridge push adds a smaller force, as new crust slides off the raised mid-ocean ridges.

The forces are linked, though. Without heat from the interior there would be no hot rising rock to form ridges and no density contrast to make slabs sink, so convection underlies all of them.

Overall, slab pull is probably the strongest direct force on plates, with ridge push smaller, but convection remains essential because it supplies the heat and mantle flow that make the other forces possible.

(Level 3: all three forces explained, evidence weighed, and a judgement that recognises how they are linked.)
:::

---

::: q
Describe how temperature and density change with depth inside the Earth. (2)
---
Temperature increases with depth, to about 5,000–6,000 °C at the centre (1); density also increases, from about 2.7 g/cm³ in continental crust to about 13 g/cm³ in the inner core (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why oceanic crust can be subducted but continental crust cannot. (2)
---
Oceanic crust is made of basalt, which is denser (1), so it sinks below the lighter continental crust, which is too buoyant to sink (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two sources of heat inside the Earth. (4)
---
Radioactive decay (1): unstable elements such as uranium and thorium break down, releasing heat continuously (1). Primordial heat (1): heat left over from the formation of the Earth about 4.6 billion years ago (1).
:::

::: watch
- Calling the mantle "liquid" or "molten magma". It is mostly solid rock that flows very slowly; only the asthenosphere is partly molten.
- Saying the inner core is liquid. The **outer** core is liquid; the inner core is solid because of pressure.
- Mixing up the lithosphere (rigid plates) and the asthenosphere (weak layer beneath).
- Describing convection without the density step: rock rises because heating makes it **less dense**.
- Forgetting to name the heat source when asked about convection: **radioactive decay**.
- Treating convection as the only force. Slab pull and ridge push earn credit in 4- and 8-mark answers.
:::
