## What it is

Some rivers flood far more often than others, and the risk is rising. This key idea asks you to:
- explain why the **risk of river flooding is increasing**, through **more frequent storms** and **land-use change**, and the **threats** floods pose to **people and the environment**;
- weigh up the **costs and benefits** of **hard engineering** (**flood walls**, **embankments**, **flood barriers**) and **soft engineering** (**flood-plain retention**, **river restoration**);
- use **simple cost-benefit analysis** (integrated skill 19) and **OS maps and GIS** (integrated skill 20) to judge management options and the effects of policy decisions.

It is examined in **Paper 2, Section A**, usually in the rivers question (short 1–4 mark items, including calculations) and sometimes in the 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question at the end of the section. There are no SPaG marks in Section A. Any real UK scheme earns credit; this lesson uses the **Thames Barrier**, the **Carlisle** defences on the River Eden (from 4.7), **Pickering's "Slowing the Flow"** project in North Yorkshire and river restoration on the **River Quaggy** in south-east London.

### Why some rivers are more flood-prone

A river floods when its **discharge** is greater than the **capacity** of its channel, so water spills onto the flood plain. Some basins are naturally **flashy** (short lag time, high peak), as you saw in 4.6:
- **steep slopes** and **impermeable rock** (granite, clay, shale) send water quickly to the channel;
- **thin or saturated soils** cannot absorb more water;
- a **circular basin** with many tributaries brings water to one point at the same time;
- **high rainfall**, especially relief rainfall in the west and north of the UK.

Others are naturally slower: chalk and limestone basins in southern England let water soak in, so peaks are lower and later (although they can suffer **groundwater flooding** after very wet winters).

### Why the risk is increasing

**1. More frequent and intense storms**
- A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, so the same storm can drop more rain. UK climate projections suggest **wetter winters** and **more intense downpours**.
- Recent winters have brought repeated named storms (for example Desmond in 2015 and the storms of winter 2019–20), so rivers are hit again before they have recovered.
- **Antecedent conditions** matter: a second storm on already saturated ground produces far more runoff than the first.

**2. Land-use change**
- **Urbanisation**: impermeable roofs and roads, plus drains, speed water to rivers; new estates are often built on the flood plain because it is flat and cheap to develop.
- **Farming**: compacted soils, ploughing downslope, bare winter fields, field drains and lost hedgerows increase runoff.
- **Upland drainage and grazing**: moorland ditches and heavy grazing remove the sponge-like peat and woodland that once held water back.
- **Deforestation**: less interception and transpiration, so more water reaches the river faster.

The more people and property placed on the flood plain, the higher the **risk**, because risk combines the **chance** of a flood with what it would **damage**.

### Threats to people and the environment

| People | Environment |
|---|---|
| deaths and injuries, though rare in UK floods | river banks eroded; channels change course |
| homes and businesses damaged; families out of their homes for months | sewage and chemicals (farm slurry, fuel, oil) wash into rivers and fields |
| insurance premiums rise; some homes become hard to insure or sell | habitats damaged; fish stranded when water drops |
| power, water and phone services cut when substations flood | fertile silt deposited on flood plains (a benefit) |
| roads, railways and bridges closed or destroyed | wetlands recharged and groundwater topped up (a benefit) |
| stress, anxiety and other mental health effects | crops and farmland lost for a season |

Floods are not always harmful: natural flooding is how flood plains are built and kept fertile. The problem is that people now live and work where the river needs to spread.

### Hard engineering

**Hard engineering** uses artificial structures to control the river. It usually protects a town quickly and reliably up to its **design standard**, but is expensive, may look unattractive and can move the problem downstream.

| Method | How it works | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flood walls** | concrete or brick walls beside the channel, sometimes with gates that are closed in a flood | take up little space, so fit in towns; protect high-value property | expensive; block views and access to the river; fail catastrophically if overtopped |
| **Embankments** (levees) | raised banks of earth along the river, often set back from the channel | cheaper than walls; can be grassed over; increase channel capacity | take up land; can be breached or overtopped; water trapped behind them after a flood |
| **Flood barriers** | movable gates across a river or estuary, closed when a flood or surge is forecast | protect a huge area of high-value land; open normally, so shipping and the river flow freely | very expensive to build and maintain; need accurate forecasts; have a design life |

**The Thames Barrier, London**
- A row of large steel gates across the Thames at **Woolwich**, in east London, operational since **1982**.
- It is closed when a **tidal surge** from the North Sea coincides with high tides, or to hold back the tide when the river is carrying a lot of floodwater from upstream.
- It protects a large area of central and east London, including homes, offices, the Underground and Parliament, with property worth many billions of pounds.
- It has been closed **more than 200 times**, much more often in recent decades than in its first years.
- **Costs**: it was very expensive to build and needs constant maintenance; rising sea levels mean it will eventually need replacing or upgrading, which the long-term Thames Estuary 2100 plan is preparing for.

**Carlisle, River Eden (see 4.7)**
- After the flood of **January 2005**, new **flood walls and embankments** were built along the Eden, Caldew and Petteril.
- They protected the city in smaller floods, but in **December 2015** Storm Desmond produced a river level above their design standard, so they were **overtopped** and thousands of properties flooded again.
- Lesson: hard defences only protect up to the flood they were designed for, and they can encourage more building behind them. Since 2015 defences have been raised, and **natural flood management** in the upper basin has been trialled alongside them.

### Soft engineering

**Soft engineering** works with natural processes. It is usually cheaper and better for wildlife, but it needs land, takes time to work and may not protect a large town on its own.

**Flood-plain retention**
- Leaving the flood plain **undeveloped** and allowing it to flood: fields, parks, playing fields or **washlands** become temporary storage.
- Planning controls: the Environment Agency's **flood map for planning** shows **Flood Zone 3** (high probability) and **Flood Zone 2** (medium); councils are advised against building homes in high-risk zones.
- **Benefits**: water is stored and released slowly, lowering the peak downstream; wetland habitats; fertile silt for farming.
- **Costs**: land cannot be built on, which limits housing and income; farmland loses crops or grazing during floods; farmers may need compensation.

**River restoration**
- Returning a channelised river to a more **natural** course: putting back **meanders**, removing concrete linings, opening up **culverted** (buried) streams and reconnecting the river to its flood plain.
- **Example**: on the **River Quaggy** in Lewisham, a stretch that had been buried in a concrete culvert was brought back to the surface through **Sutcliffe Park**, which was reshaped so it can store floodwater; the park became a wetland nature area and protects homes downstream.
- **Benefits**: meanders and rough banks slow the flow and lengthen the lag time; habitats for fish, birds and insects; attractive green spaces for people.
- **Costs**: some land is lost to the wider channel and wetlands; the river may flood the restored area more often by design; it does not stop a very large flood on its own.

**Natural flood management (catchment-wide soft engineering)**
- **Pickering, North Yorkshire**: after repeated floods, the "**Slowing the Flow**" partnership worked in the upper catchment of Pickering Beck from 2009.
- It used **leaky woody dams** across small streams, **heather bales** in moorland drains, **tree planting**, and a **flood storage embankment** upstream of the town that holds water back in a big storm.
- It cost a **few million pounds**, much less than the concrete flood wall that had been considered for the town centre.
- When severe storms hit northern England in winter **2015–16**, Pickering did not flood, although the scheme's designers said it reduced, rather than removed, the risk.

### Weighing hard against soft

Hard engineering is costly but gives reliable protection up to its design standard, uses little land and suits big towns; soft engineering is usually cheaper, creates habitats and adapts better as risk rises, but needs a lot of land and may not protect a large town alone. Most modern schemes **combine** both: a few walls where space is tight, upstream storage and tree planting to lower the peak, and planning rules to keep new homes off the flood plain.

### Skill: simple cost-benefit analysis

- **Costs**: building, maintenance, land bought, compensation to farmers, loss of habitats or views.
- **Benefits**: the value of **damage avoided** (homes, businesses, roads, insurance claims), plus extra benefits such as habitats, recreation and tourism.
- **Benefit-cost ratio** = total benefits ÷ total costs. Above 1, the scheme is worth more than it costs; the higher the ratio, the better value it is.
- Remember the limits: some things cannot easily be given a price (wildlife, stress, a sense of place), and a scheme that protects a rich area may score better than one protecting more, poorer people.

### Skill: OS maps and GIS

- On a **1:25 000** or **1:50 000** OS map, look for the **blue line** of the river, **contours** showing a flat, low flood plain, **buildings** close to the channel, and symbols for embankments, weirs, sluices and reservoirs.
- **GIS** layers, such as the Environment Agency's flood maps, can be placed over the OS base to show which homes, schools and roads lie in each flood zone. This helps planners decide where to allow building and which places most need defences.

::: terms
- **Flood risk**: the chance of a flood happening combined with the damage it would cause to people and property.
- **Hard engineering**: using artificial structures such as walls, embankments and barriers to control a river.
- **Soft engineering**: managing flooding by working with natural processes, for example by storing water on flood plains.
- **Flood wall**: a concrete or brick wall beside a river channel that holds back floodwater in a town.
- **Embankment**: a raised bank of earth beside a river that increases channel capacity and keeps floodwater in.
- **Flood barrier**: movable gates across a river or estuary that are closed when a flood or tidal surge is expected.
- **Flood-plain retention**: keeping the flood plain free of development so that it can store floodwater.
- **River restoration**: returning a modified river to a more natural state, for example by restoring meanders.
- **Natural flood management**: slowing and storing water across a catchment using trees, leaky dams and wetlands.
- **Design standard**: the size of flood a defence is built to withstand, beyond which it is overtopped.
- **Benefit-cost ratio**: the value of a scheme's benefits divided by its costs; above 1 means it is worth doing.
- **Culvert**: a pipe or covered channel that carries a stream underground.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the question type**: why risk is rising, threats, hard or soft, or a calculation.
2. **Rising risk**: link each cause to the hydrograph: more intense rain or impermeable land, so more runoff, so a shorter lag time and higher peak.
3. **Name the method precisely** (flood wall, embankment, flood barrier, flood-plain retention, river restoration) and say **how it works** before judging it.
4. **Costs and benefits**: give at least one of each, and think about people, money and the environment. Name a real scheme where you can (Thames Barrier, Carlisle, Pickering, Quaggy).
5. **Calculations**: benefit-cost ratio = total benefits ÷ total costs; add costs together first; say whether it is above 1.
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: use figure data in every paragraph, compare at least two approaches, consider who benefits and the long term, and finish with a clear, supported judgement.
:::

::: example Calculate: benefit-cost ratio
Sample data: a scheme of embankments would cost £6 million to build and £2 million to maintain over 50 years. It is expected to prevent flood damage worth £28 million. Calculate the benefit-cost ratio. (2)

Total costs = 6 + 2 = £8 million (1); 28 ÷ 8 = 3.5, so benefits are 3.5 times the costs (1).

::: example Advantage and disadvantage: flood barrier
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using a flood barrier to manage flood risk. (4)

Advantage (1): a barrier such as the Thames Barrier can be closed when a surge is forecast, protecting a very large area of high-value property, while staying open the rest of the time so shipping and the river flow normally (1). Disadvantage (1): it is very expensive to build and maintain, and as sea levels rise it must be closed more often and will eventually need upgrading or replacing (1).

::: example 8-mark: choosing an approach (Level 3 extract)
Analyse Figure 8. Figure 8 is a table of three options for a market town of 3,000 people (sample data). Option A, concrete flood walls: cost £15 million; benefits £30 million; protects 400 properties to a 1-in-100-year standard. Option B, upstream storage and leaky dams: cost £4 million; benefits £14 million; reduces flood peaks by about 15%; creates 20 hectares of wetland. Option C, do nothing: no cost; about 400 properties remain at risk. Assess which approach offers the best way to manage flood risk in the town. (8)

Option A gives the most certain protection: it defends all 400 properties to a 1-in-100-year standard, and its benefits of £30 million give a benefit-cost ratio of 2. However, walls only work up to their design standard, as Carlisle showed in 2015 when defences built after 2005 were overtopped, and they do nothing for places downstream.

Option B has the best value: £14 million of benefits for £4 million gives a ratio of 3.5, and it creates 20 hectares of wetland. Like Pickering's "Slowing the Flow", it lowers the peak for the whole town and adapts better to wetter winters. Its weakness is that a 15% cut in the peak may not prevent a very large flood, so some homes would still be at risk.

Option C costs nothing now but leaves 400 properties exposed, with rising insurance costs and repeated damage, so it is not acceptable for a town. Overall, Option B is the best first choice because it gives the highest return and environmental gains, but the strongest approach would combine it with short walls at the lowest points, because storage alone cannot guarantee protection in an extreme event.

::: watch
- Writing "hard engineering is bad and soft is good". Both have **costs and benefits**; soft schemes need land and may not protect a large town on their own.
- Describing a method without saying **how it reduces flooding**: always link it to channel capacity, storage, lag time or peak discharge.
- Calling planning rules or flood-plain retention "doing nothing". They are deliberate **policies** that cost landowners and councils something.
- Forgetting to **add the costs together** (build plus maintenance) before dividing in a benefit-cost calculation.
- Saying the Thames Barrier protects against river floods only. It is mainly closed against **tidal surges** from the North Sea.
- Ignoring the figure in an "Analyse Figure…" question. Quote its costs, ratios and numbers of properties in every paragraph.
- Ending an "Assess" answer with no judgement, or a judgement with no reason. Say **which** approach and **why**, and what would make you change your mind.
:::
