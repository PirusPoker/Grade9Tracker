## What it is

Rivers flood naturally, but people change how quickly water reaches them and where it goes. This key idea covers:
- how human activities (**urbanisation**, **land-use change** and **deforestation**) change river landscapes and **alter storm hydrographs**;
- how the **interaction of physical and human processes** causes **river flooding** on **one named river**, including the **significance of its location**.

Integrated skill: **drawing a simple storm hydrograph** from rainfall and discharge data. Any named UK river flood earns the marks. This course uses the **River Eden** in Cumbria and the flooding of **Carlisle** during **Storm Desmond in December 2015**. The Paper 2 8-marker has asked whether physical processes or human activity are mainly responsible for river flooding, so learn both sides.

### How human activity changes storm hydrographs

Remember the hydrograph from 4.6: a **flashy** river has a **short lag time** and a **high peak**, so it is more likely to flood. Most human activities make rivers flashier because they **reduce infiltration and interception** and **speed up the movement of water** to the channel.

**Urbanisation**
- Concrete, tarmac and roofs are **impermeable**, so rain cannot infiltrate and becomes **surface runoff**.
- **Drains, gutters and sewers** carry water straight to the river much faster than it would flow over or through the ground.
- Rivers in towns are often **channelised** (straightened and lined with concrete), so water moves through quickly and arrives downstream at once.
- Building on the **flood plain** removes the natural space where floodwater spreads and is stored, and puts property in the way.
- Effect on the hydrograph: **shorter lag time**, **steeper rising limb**, **higher peak**. Base flow may fall, because less water reaches the groundwater.

**Deforestation**
- Trees **intercept** rain on their leaves; some evaporates and never reaches the ground.
- Trees take up water through their roots and lose it by **transpiration**.
- Leaf litter and roots keep the soil open, so water **infiltrates** well.
- When trees are removed, more rain reaches the ground more quickly and runs off; soil is eroded and washed into the river, raising the bed and reducing channel capacity.
- Effect: **shorter lag time** and **higher peak**. Planting trees (**afforestation**) has the opposite effect.

**Land-use change in farming and the uplands**
- **Ploughing** up and down slopes creates furrows that channel water downhill.
- Heavy **machinery** and **livestock** **compact** the soil, reducing infiltration. Intensive sheep grazing in the uplands keeps vegetation short and compacts soils.
- **Field drains** and moorland **drainage ditches** (grips), dug to improve grazing, send water quickly to streams.
- **Bare soil** in winter (after harvest) increases runoff and soil erosion.
- **Removing hedgerows** and wetlands removes places that slow and store water.
- Effect: **shorter lag time** and **higher peak**.

Not all human activity increases flooding: reservoirs can store floodwater, and tree planting and wetland restoration slow the flow (see 4.8).

### Skill: drawing a storm hydrograph

1. Draw **time** on the horizontal axis (hours or days).
2. Plot **rainfall** as **bars** against a left-hand axis (mm).
3. Plot **discharge** as a **line** against a right-hand axis (cumecs).
4. Label the **peak rainfall**, **peak discharge**, **rising limb**, **falling limb** and **lag time**.
5. Read values carefully: check whether the axis goes up in 1s, 2s, 5s or 10s.

### Case study: the River Eden and Storm Desmond, December 2015

**The river and its location**
- The **Eden** rises in the **Pennines** in eastern Cumbria and flows **north-west** through the **Vale of Eden** (past Appleby) to **Carlisle**, then into the **Solway Firth**.
- Many tributaries drain the steep, wet slopes of the **Pennines** and the eastern **Lake District**, including the **Eamont** (draining Ullswater), the **Caldew** and the **Petteril**.
- **Carlisle**, the largest settlement, is built where the Eden is joined by the **Caldew** and the **Petteril**: three rivers meet in the city.

**What happened**
- **Storm Desmond** brought very heavy rain to Cumbria on **5–6 December 2015**. Moist air from the Atlantic, carried by strong south-westerly winds, was forced to rise over the Cumbrian fells.
- **Honister Pass** in the Lake District recorded more than **340 mm** of rain in 24 hours, a **UK record** at the time.
- **November 2015** had already been very wet, so the ground was **saturated** before Desmond arrived.
- The Eden reached **record levels**. **Flood defences in Carlisle**, built after a major flood in **January 2005**, were **overtopped**.
- **Thousands of properties** were flooded across Cumbria, with large parts of Carlisle under water, including homes, businesses and a biscuit factory that was closed for months.
- **Power cuts** affected many homes when an electricity substation in Carlisle flooded; roads and rail were disrupted; schools closed.
- Upstream, **Appleby** flooded, and the historic **bridge at Pooley Bridge** on the Eamont was swept away.
- Many people had to leave their homes for months, and insurance claims and repair costs ran into hundreds of millions of pounds across the region.

### Interaction of physical and human processes on the Eden

**Physical processes**
- **Extreme rainfall**: relief rainfall from a very moist air stream over high ground produced record totals.
- **Antecedent conditions**: a wet November had saturated the soils, so almost all of Desmond's rain became runoff.
- **Geology and soils**: much of the upper basin is on **impermeable** rocks with thin soils and peat, so infiltration is low.
- **Steep slopes** in the fells and Pennines send water quickly into tributaries.
- **Basin shape and confluences**: many tributaries meet in the lower basin, and three rivers meet at Carlisle, so flood peaks combine there.

**Human processes**
- **Urbanisation**: Carlisle has expanded across the **flood plain**; roofs, roads and drains speed runoff into the rivers.
- **Upland land use**: centuries of **sheep grazing** have removed most of the upland woodland and compacted soils; moorland **drainage ditches** speed water off the hills.
- **Farming in the Vale of Eden**: field drainage and compacted soils increase runoff.
- **Flood defences**: walls and embankments in Carlisle protected the city in smaller floods but were designed for a smaller event, so they were overtopped. Some argue that defences encouraged building behind them, increasing what could be damaged.

**How they interact**: the physical event was extreme, and some flooding would have happened whatever people had done. But human land use **sped up runoff**, urbanisation **placed property** where water naturally spreads, and defences **failed** when their design limit was exceeded. Together, these turned a natural flood into a disaster.

### Significance of the location

- **North-west England, facing the Atlantic**: the prevailing **south-westerly** winds bring moist air, and the high **Lake District** and **Pennines** force it to rise, producing some of the highest rainfall in England.
- **Carlisle at a confluence**: three rivers meet in the city, so flood peaks from several sub-basins arrive together.
- **Low-lying flood plain near the Solway Firth**: Carlisle grew on flat land beside the river, ideal for building but naturally prone to flooding.
- **Strategic transport routes**: major north–south road and rail links run through Carlisle and Cumbria, so flooding disrupted travel well beyond the city.
- **Repeated floods**: Carlisle also flooded in 2005, and parts of Cumbria flooded in 2009, so the area has become a test case for new approaches such as natural flood management in the upper basin.

::: terms
- **Urbanisation**: the growth of towns and cities, including the spread of buildings, roads and drains over land.
- **Impermeable surface**: a surface such as concrete or tarmac that water cannot soak into.
- **Surface runoff**: water flowing over the ground surface towards a river, instead of soaking in.
- **Interception**: rain caught by vegetation before it reaches the ground, some of which evaporates.
- **Infiltration**: the movement of water from the surface into the soil.
- **Deforestation**: the removal of trees and woodland from an area.
- **Channelisation**: straightening, deepening or lining a river channel so water flows through it faster.
- **Soil compaction**: the squashing of soil by machinery or livestock, which reduces the spaces that water can soak into.
- **Confluence**: the point where two or more rivers meet.
- **Storm Desmond**: the storm that brought record rainfall and severe flooding to Cumbria, including Carlisle, in December 2015.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the activity precisely**: urbanisation, deforestation, ploughing, compaction, moorland drainage.
2. **Give the chain**: activity, then less infiltration or interception, then faster surface runoff, then shorter lag time and higher peak, then more flooding.
3. **Named river**: River Eden, Carlisle, Storm Desmond, December 2015. Use a few safe facts: record rain at Honister, saturated ground after a wet November, defences overtopped, three rivers meeting at Carlisle.
4. **Interaction**: show how a physical cause and a human cause combined, not just one after the other.
5. **Location**: explain why where the river is matters (relief rainfall, confluence, flood plain).
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: use the figure's data, weigh physical against human, and judge "largely responsible" with evidence.
:::

::: example Multiple choice: hydrograph effect
Which one of the following is most likely to shorten the lag time of a river? A: Planting trees in the drainage basin. B: Building a housing estate on the flood plain. C: Restoring a wetland. D: Allowing grass to grow on fields in winter. (1)

B: urban surfaces are impermeable and drains carry water quickly to the river (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: urbanisation
Explain one way in which urbanisation affects a storm hydrograph. (2)

Roads and roofs are impermeable, so rain cannot infiltrate and drains carry it straight to the river (1). This shortens the lag time and raises the peak discharge, so flooding is more likely (1).
:::

::: example Named river: physical causes
For a named river that has flooded, explain one physical cause of the flood. (2)

On the River Eden in December 2015, November had been very wet, so the ground was already saturated (1); the record rainfall from Storm Desmond could not infiltrate and ran straight off into the rivers, raising discharge to record levels in Carlisle (1).
:::

::: example Hydrograph from data
Sample data: rainfall (mm) in six-hour blocks after midnight: 0–6 h: 8; 6–12 h: 32; 12–18 h: 14; 18–24 h: 2. Discharge (cumecs) at 0 h: 20; 6 h: 26; 12 h: 70; 18 h: 145; 24 h: 120; 30 h: 70. Identify the peak discharge and calculate the lag time, taking peak rainfall at 9 h (the middle of the 6–12 h block). (2)

Peak discharge = 145 cumecs at 18 h (1). Lag time = 18 − 9 = 9 hours (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: land use
