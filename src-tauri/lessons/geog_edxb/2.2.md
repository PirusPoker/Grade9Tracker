## What it is

The gap between the world's richest and poorest countries is huge. Some people expect to live into their eighties with a university education, while others face hunger and an early death. This key idea asks **why** that inequality exists, **what it leads to**, and how two famous theories, **Rostow's modernisation theory** and **Frank's dependency theory**, explain how and why countries develop.

### Causes of global inequality

The spec groups the causes into four types. Most countries' positions are explained by several working together.

| Type | Cause | How it creates inequality |
|---|---|---|
| **Social** | **Education** | low literacy and skills limit the jobs people can do and the investment a country attracts |
| | **Health** | diseases such as malaria and HIV kill or weaken working-age adults and absorb spending; poor water and sanitation spread disease |
| **Historical** | **Colonialism** | European powers ruled much of Africa, Asia and the Americas, taking raw materials and building economies around exporting them; transport often ran from mines and plantations to ports, not between towns; borders drawn by colonisers grouped rival peoples together, contributing to later conflict |
| | **Neo-colonialism** | after independence, richer countries and their companies kept economic control through debt, unfair trade terms, tied aid and ownership of resources |
| **Environmental** | **Climate** | drought, floods and tropical cyclones destroy crops and infrastructure; hot, wet climates suit disease carriers such as mosquitoes |
| | **Topography** | mountainous or **landlocked** countries have high transport costs and find it harder to trade |
| **Economic and political** | **Systems of governance** | corruption, conflict and unstable government scare off investment and waste resources; stable, accountable government encourages growth |
| | **International relations** | trade blocs and tariffs can shut poorer countries out of rich markets; dependence on exporting primary products with low and unstable prices; debt repayments take money from services |

### Consequences of inequality

- **Health and life expectancy**: people in developing countries die younger and face higher infant and maternal mortality.
- **Education**: fewer children complete school, which keeps incomes low: a **cycle of poverty**.
- **Migration**: people move from poorer to richer countries for work, sending **remittances** home but sometimes causing a **brain drain** of skilled workers.
- **Debt and dependence** on aid or a few exports.
- **Conflict and instability**, which can both cause and result from inequality.
- **Inequality within countries** too: the spec's skill on income quintiles shows it.

### Rostow's modernisation theory (1960)

Walt Rostow, an American economist, argued that every country can develop by passing through **five stages**, as Britain and the USA had done.

| Stage | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1. **Traditional society** | subsistence farming, little technology, little trade |
| 2. **Preconditions for take-off** | more trade, investment in infrastructure, farming becomes commercial, often helped by outside investment or aid |
| 3. **Take-off** | rapid growth of a few manufacturing industries; rising investment; growing cities |
| 4. **Drive to maturity** | industry diversifies; technology spreads across the economy; living standards rise |
| 5. **Age of high mass consumption** | most people work in services; high incomes and consumer goods for most |

**Strengths**: simple; it fits the historical path of some countries, such as the UK, and it suggests investment and aid can help countries "take off".

**Criticisms**:
- It assumes every country follows the same straight line, from a Western model.
- It ignores **colonialism** and unfair trade, which shape where a country starts.
- It assumes growth automatically benefits everyone, ignoring inequality and the environment.
- Some countries have stalled or gone backwards because of conflict or debt.

### Frank's dependency theory (1960s)

Andre Gunder Frank argued the opposite: poor countries are poor **because of** the way they are linked to rich ones.
- The world is divided into a rich **core** (developed countries) and a poor **periphery** (developing countries).
- The periphery supplies **cheap raw materials, crops and labour**. The core processes them into valuable goods and keeps the profits.
- Wealth flows from periphery to core through trade, debt repayments and TNC profits, so the core develops by **underdeveloping** the periphery.
- This began with colonialism and continues through neo-colonialism.
- Frank's answer was for poorer countries to reduce their dependence on the core and build their own industries.

**Strengths**: explains why many former colonies still depend on primary exports, and why trade and debt can keep countries poor.

**Criticisms**:
- Countries such as South Korea, China and India have grown fast by trading **more** with the core, not less.
- It underplays internal causes such as corruption and conflict.
- Cutting links with the global economy has rarely worked.

**The classic slip:** Rostow says **any** country can develop through stages (an optimistic, internal view). Frank says the **rich core keeps the periphery poor** (a pessimistic, external view).

### Skill: income quintiles

A population is split into **five equal groups (quintiles)** by income, from the poorest 20% to the richest 20%. Comparing the share of total income each receives shows inequality.
- Calculate the **ratio** between the richest and poorest quintiles: richest share ÷ poorest share.
- Globally, the richest fifth of the world's people receive most of the world's income, while the poorest fifth receive only a tiny share.

::: terms
- **Global inequality**: the uneven distribution of wealth, health and opportunity between countries.
- **Colonialism**: the rule of one country over another, taking its land and resources, as European empires did.
- **Neo-colonialism**: continued economic control of former colonies by richer countries or companies through trade, debt and investment.
- **Landlocked**: having no coastline, which raises the cost of trade.
- **Governance**: how a country is run, including how honest, stable and effective its government is.
- **Modernisation theory**: Rostow's idea that all countries can develop by passing through five stages of economic growth.
- **Dependency theory**: Frank's idea that the rich core keeps the poor periphery underdeveloped by exploiting it.
- **Core and periphery**: the rich, powerful centre of the world economy and the poorer areas that depend on it.
- **Income quintile**: one of five equal groups that a population is divided into by income.
- **Brain drain**: the loss of skilled and educated workers who emigrate to richer countries.
:::

::: method
1. **Cause questions:** name the type (social, historical, environmental, economic or political), then the chain: cause, so effect on income or wellbeing.
2. **Theory questions:** describe the theory accurately, then apply it: which stage is a country in, or how does trade link core and periphery?
3. **Compare the theories:** internal stages against external exploitation; optimistic against pessimistic.
4. **Quintile data:** divide the richest share by the poorest share; show working.
5. **8-mark evaluate or assess:** weigh several causes or both theories, use real examples, and reach a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain one: historical cause
Explain one historical cause of global inequality. (2)

Colonialism (1): European powers built colonies' economies around exporting raw materials such as cotton and minerals, so after independence many still depended on low-value primary exports (1).
:::

::: example Describe Rostow
Describe Rostow's modernisation theory. (3)

Rostow said all countries pass through five stages of economic growth (1), from a traditional society based on subsistence farming, through preconditions and take-off when industry grows rapidly (1), to maturity and finally an age of high mass consumption based on services (1).
:::

::: example Income quintiles
Sample data: in Country Q, the richest 20% of people receive 48% of income and the poorest 20% receive 6%. Calculate the ratio of the richest to the poorest quintile's share. (2)

48 ÷ 6 (1) = 8, so the richest fifth receives 8 times the share of the poorest fifth (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: historical factors
Evaluate the view that historical factors are the main cause of global inequality. (8)

Historical factors explain a great deal. Colonial powers organised economies around exporting raw materials, built transport from mines to ports, and drew borders that later fuelled conflict. After independence, neo-colonialism through debt, tied aid and foreign ownership often kept profits flowing to richer countries, as Frank's dependency theory argues. This helps explain why many African countries still rely on primary exports.

However, other factors are also important. Environmental factors matter: landlocked, mountainous countries face high transport costs, and tropical climates bring diseases such as malaria. Governance is crucial: corruption and conflict can waste resources whatever a country's history. Social factors such as low education keep productivity low.

The experience of former colonies such as South Korea and India, which have grown rapidly, shows that history is not destiny; good governance and investment in education can overcome a colonial past.

Overall, historical factors are a major root cause, because they shaped where countries started. But they are not the main cause today, since governance, education and the environment explain why some former colonies have developed and others have not.

(Level 3: several causes weighed with examples, the view challenged with evidence, and a substantiated conclusion; accurate spelling and specialist terms would earn high SPaG.)
:::

Former colonial powers or TNCs control trade and resources (1), so profits flow out of the poorer country rather than being reinvested there (1). Or: debt repayments (1) take money away from schools and hospitals (1).

::: q
Describe Frank's dependency theory. (3)
---
The world is divided into a rich core and a poor periphery (1); the periphery supplies cheap raw materials and labour to the core (1), which makes the profits, so the periphery stays underdeveloped (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one criticism of Rostow's modernisation theory. (2)
---
It assumes all countries follow the same Western path (1), ignoring colonialism and unfair trade that hold some countries back (1). Or: it ignores inequality (1), assuming growth benefits everyone (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two consequences of global inequality. (4)
---
Two from: lower life expectancy and higher infant mortality in poorer countries (1+1); migration to richer countries, with remittances and brain drain (1+1); debt and dependence on aid (1+1); conflict and instability (1+1); cycle of poverty through low education (1+1).

::: watch

- Naming a cause without the chain. "Colonialism" alone is 1 mark; add how it still affects the economy.
- Stating "Africa is poor because it is hot". Climate matters only through mechanisms such as drought or disease.
- Getting the quintile ratio upside down: richest ÷ poorest.
- In evaluate questions, agreeing with the statement without weighing other causes.
- Careless spelling of key terms in the SPaG question: colonialism, dependency, periphery, governance.
