## What it is

Cities grow or shrink because of what happens to their economies and who moves in or out. This key idea covers:
- how **economic change** and **migration**, both **national** (within a country) and **international** (between countries), make cities **grow or decline** in **developing**, **emerging** and **developed** countries;
- why **urban economies differ** between these three types of country: **formal against informal employment**, the **relative importance of economic sectors**, and **working conditions**.

Any accurate real examples earn the marks. This lesson uses Lagos and Dhaka (developing), Shenzhen and Mumbai (emerging), and Detroit, Manchester and London (developed). Keep figures rounded.

### The link between the economy, migration and city size

The basic chain works in both directions:
- **Growth:** new jobs → migrants arrive → more spending and demand for services → more jobs → more migrants. This is a **positive multiplier** (cumulative causation).
- **Decline:** factories close → jobs lost → people leave → shops and services lose customers and close → more job losses → more people leave. This is a **negative multiplier**, or downward spiral.

### Economic sectors

| Sector | What it does | Urban examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | extracts raw materials | farming on the urban fringe, quarrying, fishing |
| **Secondary** | manufactures goods | textile mills, car factories, steelworks, food processing |
| **Tertiary** | provides services | retail, transport, health, education, tourism, finance |
| **Quaternary** | knowledge and information | research and development, IT, consultancy, media |

### Developing countries: growth driven by migration, often ahead of jobs

- Cities such as **Lagos** (Nigeria), **Kinshasa** (DR Congo) and **Dhaka** (Bangladesh) are growing very fast.
- **National migration** from rural areas is the main driver: people leave poor, insecure farming for any chance of urban work.
- **Economic change** is happening, for example the **garment industry** in Dhaka, which employs millions, mostly women. But in many African cities population is growing **faster than formal jobs** are being created. This is sometimes called urbanisation without industrialisation.
- The result: most new arrivals find work in the **informal economy**, and live in **informal settlements**.
- **International migration** is smaller but present: people move from poorer neighbouring countries, or flee conflict into cities.

### Emerging countries: growth driven by industrialisation and investment

- **Shenzhen** in China was a small fishing and market town until it became a **Special Economic Zone** in 1980. Factories built with foreign investment drew millions of migrants from rural China, and it is now one of the world's largest cities, increasingly based on technology firms.
- **Mumbai** grew as India's commercial and financial capital, attracting migrants from across India.
- **National migration** is huge: in China, hundreds of millions of rural people have moved to coastal cities since 1980.
- **Economic change** shifts the economy from primary to **secondary**, then increasingly to **tertiary and quaternary** work as cities move up the value chain.
- **International migration** grows as cities globalise: TNC managers, skilled professionals and returning emigrants arrive.

### Developed countries: decline and regrowth

**Decline through de-industrialisation**
- From the 1960s to the 1990s, manufacturing in many developed countries moved to cheaper locations overseas (**global shift**) or was automated.
- **Detroit** (USA) was the centre of the US car industry. As car firms cut jobs and moved production, Detroit lost **more than half its population** from its 1950s peak, leaving tens of thousands of empty buildings. The city declared bankruptcy in 2013.
- In the UK, cities such as **Manchester**, **Liverpool** and **Glasgow** lost large numbers of factory and dock jobs and population.
- **Suburbanisation** and **counter-urbanisation** also took people out of inner cities.

**Growth through the service and knowledge economy**
- Some cities have grown again because of **finance, technology, universities, culture and tourism**.
- **London** has grown strongly since the 1990s, driven by its role as a **world city** in finance and business services and by **international migration** from the EU and the rest of the world.
- **Manchester** has regenerated, with media, universities and services, and its centre has grown again.
- **National migration** in developed countries now often flows from declining industrial regions to prosperous cities, especially of young graduates.

### Summary: causes of growth and decline

| Type of country | Main driver of growth | Main driver of decline | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Developing | national rural-urban migration; high natural increase | rarely declining | Lagos, Dhaka |
| Emerging | industrialisation, FDI and national migration | rare; some older industrial cities stagnate | Shenzhen, Mumbai |
| Developed | services, finance and international migration | de-industrialisation and out-migration | London grows; Detroit declines |

### Why urban economies differ

**Formal against informal employment**

| | Formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | work that is officially recognised, registered and taxed | work that is **not registered, taxed or regulated** by the government |
| **Examples** | bank clerk, factory worker on a contract, teacher, nurse | street vendor, rickshaw driver, waste picker, domestic worker, small workshop |
| **Pay** | regular wage | irregular, often low |
| **Protection** | contracts, sick pay, pensions, health and safety law | none; can be dismissed at any time |
| **Importance** | most jobs in developed cities | **a majority of jobs in many developing-country cities** |

Informal work is **not the same as illegal work**. Selling vegetables on a pavement is informal because it is unregistered and untaxed, not because it is criminal.

**Relative importance of sectors**

| Type of country | Dominant urban sectors |
|---|---|
| **Developing** | informal tertiary (trading, transport) and some secondary (garments, food processing); primary work on the urban fringe |
| **Emerging** | a large **secondary** sector (manufacturing for export), with a growing tertiary and quaternary sector |
| **Developed** | **tertiary** and **quaternary** dominate; secondary is small after de-industrialisation |

This links to the **Clark-Fisher model**: as countries develop, employment shifts from primary to secondary, then to tertiary and quaternary.

**Working conditions**
- **Developing:** long hours, low pay, no contracts, unsafe workplaces. The collapse of the **Rana Plaza** garment factory building near Dhaka in **2013**, which killed more than 1,100 workers, exposed the dangers.
- **Emerging:** factory work brings regular wages, but hours can be long and dormitory living common; conditions are improving as wages rise and laws tighten.
- **Developed:** strong legal protection, minimum wages and unions, but a growing **gig economy** (delivery riders, app-based taxi drivers) brings insecurity back for some workers.

### Advantages and disadvantages of the informal economy

| For workers | For the city and government |
|---|---|
| **Advantages:** easy to enter with no qualifications; provides a survival income; flexible hours; skills can be learned; services are cheap for the poor | provides goods and services the formal economy does not; recycling reduces waste; absorbs migrants who would otherwise be unemployed |
| **Disadvantages:** low and insecure income; no sick pay, pension or protection; dangerous conditions; child labour | no tax revenue to pay for services; unsafe practices; congestion from street trading; hard to regulate |

::: terms
- **Economic change**: a shift in the jobs and industries that make up an economy, such as de-industrialisation or the growth of services.
- **National migration**: the movement of people from one place to another within the same country.
- **International migration**: the movement of people from one country to another.
- **Formal economy**: employment that is registered, taxed and protected by law, with regular wages and contracts.
- **Informal economy**: work that is not registered, taxed or regulated by the government, such as street trading.
- **De-industrialisation**: the decline of manufacturing industry in a country or region, with the loss of factory jobs.
- **Global shift**: the movement of manufacturing from developed countries to emerging and developing countries.
- **Multiplier effect**: when one economic change, such as new jobs, sets off a chain of further gains or losses.
- **Tertiary sector**: the part of the economy that provides services, such as retail, health and transport.
- **Quaternary sector**: knowledge-based industries such as research, IT and consultancy.
- **Gig economy**: work made up of short-term, task-based jobs, often arranged through apps, without fixed contracts.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the country type** in the question: developing, emerging or developed. The answer must fit it.
2. **Growth or decline:** name the cause (economic change or migration), state whether it is national or international, then explain the chain of effects with "so".
3. **Urban economies:** use the three headings from the spec: formal against informal, sectors, working conditions. Compare with "whereas".
4. **Informal economy:** define it accurately (unregistered, untaxed, unregulated) and give advantages and disadvantages for both people and the city.
5. **Use real examples:** Detroit for decline, Shenzhen for industrial growth, Dhaka for garments and working conditions, London for service-led growth.
6. **8-mark evaluate:** weigh the importance for different groups, then give a supported judgement.
:::

Explain two reasons why cities in emerging countries have grown rapidly. (4)

Compare urban economies in developing and developed countries. (4)

In developing countries most urban workers are in the informal economy (1), whereas in developed countries most have formal jobs with contracts and legal protection (1). Developing cities have more secondary work such as garment making (1), whereas developed cities are dominated by tertiary and quaternary jobs such as finance and research (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: the informal economy
Evaluate the importance of the informal economy for people living in cities in developing countries. (8)

The informal economy is vital for survival. In cities such as Lagos and Dhaka, population grows faster than formal jobs are created, so for most migrants informal work such as street trading, driving rickshaws or waste picking is the only income available. It needs no qualifications or capital, so newcomers can earn money within days.

It also matters for consumers. Informal traders sell cheap food, clothes and transport, which the urban poor can afford, and informal recyclers collect waste that the city authorities do not reach.

However, its importance has a cost. Incomes are low and irregular, and workers have no sick pay, pension or protection, so an illness can push a family into deeper poverty. Conditions can be dangerous, and children may work instead of attending school. Because the work is untaxed, governments lose revenue they could spend on water, sanitation and schools, which harms the same people.

Overall, the informal economy is extremely important because without it millions would have no income at all. But it is a survival strategy, not a route out of poverty for most people. Its importance is greatest for new migrants and the poorest, while for the city as a whole a gradual move into formal work, as in emerging cities like Shenzhen, brings bigger long-term gains.

(Level 3: importance weighed for different groups, positive and negative sides, real places, and a substantiated conclusion.)
:::

::: watch
- Calling the informal economy "illegal". It is **unregistered, untaxed and unregulated**.
- Forgetting the word **decline**: the spec asks how economic change and migration cause growth **and/or decline**.
- Writing about one type of country when the question names another.
- Mixing up national and international migration.
- Describing sectors without saying which is **relatively more important** in each type of country.
- Saying Detroit "lost all its jobs". Keep figures general: it lost more than half its population.
- In the 8-marker, giving only advantages of informal work. Weigh both sides and say for whom.
:::
