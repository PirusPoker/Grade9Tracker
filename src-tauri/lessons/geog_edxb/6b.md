## What it is

Topic 6 is examined in **Paper 2 Section C1** (18 marks), where you answer **either** the coasts **or** the rivers question. This lesson is the rivers option, **Investigating river processes and pressures**. The spec sets the focus: how and why **drainage basin and channel characteristics influence flood risk** for people and property along a UK river.

The question has two halves:
- **Your own fieldwork:** you write your river location, then answer 2- and 4-mark questions on how you collected data, how a qualitative method helped, how theory or case studies helped, why secondary data mattered, or how to improve. Some years end with an 8-marker on your own enquiry.
- **Unfamiliar fieldwork:** a figure shows another group's results, such as channel measurements or a questionnaire. You calculate, criticise and, in the 8-marker, assess their conclusion **using evidence from the figure**.

Name your river, equipment, sample size and findings in every answer. The model below uses **sample data** for an unnamed small river; swap in your own.

### What the spec requires you to have done

| Requirement | What it means on a river |
|---|---|
| **Enquiry question** | developed from your location, about channel and basin characteristics and flood risk |
| **At least one quantitative method** | measuring **changes in channel characteristics** downstream |
| **At least one qualitative method** | collecting data on **factors that might influence flood risk** |
| **Secondary sources** | a **flood risk map**, such as the Environment Agency's, plus **one other** |

### The six enquiry stages

1. **Question:** answerable through fieldwork, often split into hypotheses.
2. **Methods:** quantitative and qualitative, sampling, equipment, risk assessment.
3. **Processing and presenting:** graphs, maps, cross-sections, annotated photos, statistics.
4. **Analysing and explaining** with theory (the **Bradshaw model**, hydrographs) and case studies (for example the **River Eden and Carlisle** in Storm Desmond, 2015).
5. **Concluding** with evidence.
6. **Evaluating** accuracy, reliability, methods and conclusions.

### A model investigation (sample data)

**Enquiry question:** "How do channel characteristics change downstream, and how does this affect flood risk to people and property?"
- **Hypothesis 1:** width, depth, velocity and discharge increase downstream (the Bradshaw model).
- **Hypothesis 2:** flood risk is greatest where the channel has the **least capacity** relative to discharge and where land use is **urban**.

**Site selection.** Five sites at roughly **equal intervals** downstream (systematic), adjusted where access was unsafe or blocked by private land.

### Quantitative methods: channel characteristics

| Measurement | Equipment and how |
|---|---|
| **Width** | tape measure stretched across the water surface from bank to bank at right angles to the flow |
| **Depth** | metre rule placed vertically at **regular intervals** across the channel (for example every 20 cm or ten equal points); mean depth calculated |
| **Velocity** | a **float** (a dog biscuit or orange) timed over a measured distance, such as 10 m, three times and averaged; or a **flow meter** held at 40% of the depth |
| **Gradient** | two ranging poles a set distance apart along the bank, angle read with a **clinometer** |
| **Wetted perimeter** | a chain or tape laid along the bed from one water edge to the other |
| **Bedload** | pebbles collected at set points across the channel; b-axis with callipers; roundness on the Powers index |

**Calculations you must know:**
- **Cross-sectional area (CSA)** = width × mean depth (m²).
- **Discharge** = CSA × velocity, in **cumecs** (m³/s).
- **Hydraulic radius** = CSA ÷ wetted perimeter. A larger hydraulic radius means a more **efficient** channel with less friction.
- **Velocity** = distance ÷ mean time.

### Qualitative methods: factors influencing flood risk

| Method | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Land-use survey** within, say, 50 m of each site (woodland, pasture, arable, housing, roads) | impermeable urban surfaces and drained farmland speed up run-off |
| **Annotated field sketch or photo** | bank height, defences, evidence of past floods (debris lines, flood marks, eroded banks) |
| **Flood risk assessment score** | each site scored on factors such as bank height, building nearness and defences |
| **Interviews** with residents or business owners | experience of past floods and views on defences |

### Sampling, risk and data types

- **Systematic** sampling at equal distances downstream gives even coverage; **random** points reduce bias in site choice; **stratified** sampling makes sure each land-use type or each course of the river is represented.
- **Sample size:** depth readings at 10 points rather than 3 gives a more accurate cross-section.
- **Risk assessment:** check river levels and the weather; never enter fast or deep water (keep below knee depth); wear wellies and stay in pairs; cover cuts and wash hands because of **Weil's disease**; take care on steep, slippery banks.
- Primary against secondary, and quantitative against qualitative, as in all fieldwork.

### Secondary sources

- **Environment Agency flood risk map** (required): shows which areas are at high, medium or low risk, to compare with your fieldwork judgement.
- **River level and rainfall data** from gauging stations: show how quickly the river rises after rain.
- **OS maps:** gradient from contours, settlements, land use and the size of the drainage basin.
- **Geology maps:** permeable rock (chalk, sandstone) against impermeable rock (clay, granite).
- **Historic flood records and news reports:** how often and how badly the area has flooded.

- **Cross-sections** for each site (depth plotted downwards at points across the width).
- **Line or bar graphs** of width, depth, velocity and discharge downstream.
- **Scatter graph** of distance downstream against discharge, with a best-fit line.
- **Proportional symbols** on a map (circles sized by discharge) and **annotated photos** located on a base map.
- **Pie charts** of land use at each site.

### Worked calculation (sample)

Float times over 10 m at site 3: 25 s, 31 s, 28 s.
- Mean time = 84 ÷ 3 = 28 s.
- Velocity = 10 ÷ 28 = **0.36 m/s**.

Site 4: CSA 1.2 m², wetted perimeter 4.8 m. Hydraulic radius = 1.2 ÷ 4.8 = **0.25 m**.

Percentage change in discharge from site 1 to site 5: (1.00 − 0.04) ÷ 0.04 × 100 = **2,400% increase**.

### Analysing with theory and case studies

- **The Bradshaw model** predicts that width, depth, velocity and discharge **increase** downstream as tributaries add water, while gradient and bedload size **decrease**. The sample data fits.
- **Velocity increases** downstream despite a gentler gradient because the channel becomes deeper and smoother, with a larger **hydraulic radius** and less friction.
- **Flood risk** depends on discharge compared with **channel capacity** (bankfull CSA). A narrow channel through a town, or a bridge that restricts flow, floods first.
- **Storm hydrographs:** urban surfaces, steep slopes, saturated soil and impermeable rock give a short **lag time** and a high **peak discharge**. Woodland and permeable rock do the opposite.
- **Case study:** on the River Eden in December 2015, record rainfall on already saturated ground overtopped Carlisle's defences. Use it to explain why your high-risk site needs defences, and why defences can still fail.

### Conclusions (sample)

Width, depth, velocity and discharge all rose downstream, supporting hypothesis 1. Flood risk was judged highest at site 4, where the river passes through housing, the banks are low and the Environment Agency map shows a high-risk zone, supporting hypothesis 2. Basin factors (urban land use) and channel factors (capacity) together explain flood risk.

### Accuracy, reliability and improvements

- **Accuracy:** floats are slowed by eddies or snag on vegetation; the metre rule sinks into soft beds; reading a clinometer in wind is hard; surface velocity is faster than mean velocity.
- **Reliability:** one day of fieldwork, often in low summer flow, may not represent flood conditions; only five sites; access limited site choice.
- **Improvements:** more sites; a flow meter instead of floats; ten or more depth readings per site; repeat in winter; compare with gauging-station data.

::: terms
- **Discharge**: the volume of water passing a point in a given time, measured in cumecs (cubic metres per second).
- **Cross-sectional area**: the area of a slice across the river channel, calculated as width multiplied by mean depth.
- **Wetted perimeter**: the length of the bed and banks in contact with the water across the channel.
- **Hydraulic radius**: cross-sectional area divided by wetted perimeter; a higher value means a more efficient channel.
- **Bradshaw model**: a model showing how channel characteristics such as width, depth and velocity change downstream.
- **Channel capacity**: the amount of water a channel can hold before it overflows its banks (bankfull).
- **Flow meter**: an instrument with a propeller placed in the water to give a direct reading of velocity.
- **Flood risk map**: a map, such as the Environment Agency's, showing the likelihood of flooding in different areas.
- **Lag time**: the time between peak rainfall and peak discharge on a storm hydrograph.
- **Accuracy**: how close a measurement is to the true value.
- **Reliability**: how consistent results are, so the same method repeated gives similar data.
:::

::: example 8-mark: assess a given conclusion
Students measured velocity at four sites with a float, timing it once at each site. Sample results: site 1 0.30 m/s, site 2 0.42 m/s, site 3 0.38 m/s, site 4 0.55 m/s. They concluded that "velocity increases downstream, so flood risk increases downstream". Assess the strengths and weaknesses of this conclusion. (8)

There is some support. Velocity at site 4 (0.55 m/s) is almost double site 1 (0.30 m/s), and the overall trend is upward, which matches the Bradshaw model.

But the pattern is not consistent: velocity falls from 0.42 m/s at site 2 to 0.38 m/s at site 3, so "increases downstream" is too simple. With only four sites and a single float timing at each, one snag on vegetation or an eddy could explain the anomaly; three timings averaged would be more accurate. A float measures surface speed only.

The second half of the conclusion is weaker still. Flood risk depends on discharge compared with channel capacity, on bank height and on what is next to the river, such as housing. Velocity alone does not show flood risk; a fast, deep channel in open fields may pose less risk than a slower, shallow channel through a town. They also needed width and depth, a land-use survey and the Environment Agency flood risk map.

Overall, the first part is partly supported by the data, but the link to flood risk is not justified by velocity alone, so the conclusion is only weakly valid.

(Level 3: quotes the data, identifies the anomaly and method weaknesses, and judges both parts of the conclusion.)

- Mixing up the formulas. Discharge is **CSA × velocity**; CSA is **width × mean depth**.
- Giving discharge without units. Use **cumecs** (m³/s).
