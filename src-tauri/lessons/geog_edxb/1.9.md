## What it is

This key idea compares the **impacts** and **management** of a tectonic hazard in a **developed** country and an **emerging or developing** country. The spec lets you study earthquakes or volcanoes; this course uses earthquakes:
- **Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami, Japan, 11 March 2011** (developed);
- **Gorkha earthquake, Nepal, 25 April 2015** (developing).

Any real, named pair of the right types earns the marks. Pearson's 8-mark question asks you to **evaluate the management** in one of them, so learn both under the same headings.

### The management headings from the spec

| Heading | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Short-term relief** | shelter and supplies (tents, food, water, medical care) in the days and weeks after |
| **Long-term planning** | trained and funded emergency services, land-use planning, rebuilding |
| **Preparation** | warning and evacuation systems, drills, and **building design** |
| **Prediction** | trying to forecast when and where; earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely, but risk can be mapped |

### Case study 1: Japan, 11 March 2011 (developed)

**The hazard.** A **magnitude 9.0** earthquake with its epicentre about 70 km off the north-east coast of Honshu, at a fairly shallow depth. The Pacific plate is subducted beneath the plate carrying northern Japan at the Japan Trench (a convergent boundary). The sea floor moved several metres, generating a huge **tsunami** that reached the coast within an hour, in places tens of metres high.

**Primary impacts** (from the shaking)
- Buildings shook violently across north-east Japan and Tokyo, but most modern buildings stood because of strict building codes.
- Some roads, bridges and homes damaged; ground **liquefaction** in reclaimed land around Tokyo Bay.

**Secondary impacts**
- The **tsunami** caused most of the deaths: about **18,000 people dead or missing**. It overtopped sea walls and swept away whole coastal towns.
- The **Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant** lost power for cooling and suffered meltdowns. Tens of thousands of residents had to leave an evacuation zone, many for years.
- Power shortages, damaged factories and broken supply chains hit Japanese industry and car makers worldwide.
- It was one of the most expensive natural disasters ever recorded.

**Management**
- **Preparation.** Strict **building codes**, with deep foundations, cross-bracing and shock absorbers in tall buildings; **sea walls** along much of the coast; **tsunami evacuation** routes and signs; nationwide drills, including **Disaster Prevention Day on 1 September** each year.
- **Prediction and warning.** An **earthquake early warning system** detected the first waves and sent alerts to TV and mobile phones seconds before strong shaking arrived; high-speed trains braked automatically. A tsunami warning was issued within minutes, although the first estimate of wave height was too low.
- **Short-term relief.** Around 100,000 troops of the Self-Defence Forces were deployed for rescue and supply; evacuation centres housed hundreds of thousands; international search teams helped.
- **Long-term planning.** Temporary housing, then rebuilding; higher sea walls; moving some communities to higher ground; a review of nuclear safety; well-funded emergency services.

**Evaluation.** Preparation for the **earthquake** was highly effective: few people died from collapsing buildings in a magnitude 9.0 event. Preparation for the **tsunami** was not enough: sea walls were overtopped and some people did not evacuate because they trusted the walls or underestimated the warning. The nuclear disaster showed that planning had not imagined an event this large.

### Case study 2: Nepal, 25 April 2015 (developing)

**The hazard.** A **magnitude 7.8** earthquake with a **shallow** focus, its epicentre in Gorkha district, about 80 km north-west of the capital, **Kathmandu**. The Indian plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate here, the same movement that builds the Himalayas. A large aftershock followed in May.

**Primary impacts**
- About **9,000 people died**, and many thousands were injured.
- **Hundreds of thousands of homes** were destroyed, mainly stone, brick and mud buildings in villages.
- Historic temples and towers in the Kathmandu Valley, a UNESCO World Heritage area, collapsed; schools and hospitals damaged.

**Secondary impacts**
- **Landslides** blocked mountain roads, cutting off villages for weeks.
- An **avalanche on Mount Everest** killed climbers at base camp.
- **Tourism**, a key source of income, collapsed for the season.
- Families lived in tents and temporary shelters through the monsoon, with risks of disease.

**Management**
- **Preparation.** A building code existed but was **poorly enforced**, especially in rural areas, and many homes were self-built. There was **no earthquake early warning system**. Some schools had been strengthened and drills held, and because the quake struck on a Saturday the schools were empty.
- **Prediction.** Scientists knew a large earthquake was overdue along this section of the boundary, but could not say when.
- **Short-term relief.** Nepal's army and police led rescues; India, China, the UK and many others sent search teams, helicopters, tents, food and medical help; charities set up field hospitals. Aid was slowed by blocked roads, remote villages and congestion at Kathmandu's **single international airport**.
- **Long-term planning.** International donors pledged billions of US dollars for reconstruction; a national reconstruction authority was set up; householders were offered grants to rebuild to earthquake-safe designs. **Rebuilding was slow**, and many families were still in temporary shelters more than a year later.

**Evaluation.** Management was much less effective than in Japan: weak enforcement of building codes explains most deaths, and the country depended on foreign aid. Relief was hampered by physical geography as much as by poverty.

### Why the impacts differed

| Factor | Japan | Nepal |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard | magnitude 9.0, offshore, with a tsunami | magnitude 7.8, shallow, under land, with landslides |
| Building design | enforced codes, so few collapses from shaking | poorly enforced; many collapses |
| Warnings and drills | early warning system and regular drills | no early warning; few drills |
| Relief | mainly domestic, well-funded | heavily dependent on international aid |
| Geography | densely populated coast | remote mountain villages, steep slopes |

The lesson: development reduces deaths from **shaking**, but even the best-prepared country can be overwhelmed by an extreme **secondary hazard**.

### Skill: assessing impact from different sources

Satellite images show tsunami inundation or landslides over large areas; social media gives real-time, local reports (Facebook's Safety Check was used in Nepal); socio-economic data such as GDP per capita and HDI help explain why impacts differed.

::: terms
- **Primary impact**: an impact caused directly by the ground shaking, such as buildings collapsing.
- **Secondary impact**: an impact that follows from the primary impacts, such as a tsunami, landslide, fire or nuclear accident.
- **Short-term relief**: emergency help such as shelter, food, water and medical care in the days and weeks after a hazard.
- **Long-term planning**: actions over months and years, such as funding emergency services and rebuilding more safely.
- **Building code**: legal rules setting how buildings must be designed and built, for example to resist earthquakes.
- **Earthquake early warning system**: a network that detects the first seismic waves and sends alerts seconds before strong shaking.
- **Prediction**: forecasting when and where a hazard will occur; earthquakes cannot yet be predicted precisely.
- **Liquefaction**: when shaking makes waterlogged soil behave like a liquid, so buildings sink or tilt.
- **Disaster Prevention Day**: Japan's national day of earthquake and tsunami drills, held on 1 September.
:::

::: method
1. **Sort impacts** into primary and secondary, and into people and property.
2. **Sort management** under the spec's four headings: short-term relief, long-term planning, preparation, prediction.
3. **Explain using development:** link each difference to wealth, governance or geography with "because".
4. **Use rounded, accurate facts:** magnitude, date, deaths, a named place, a named measure.
5. **8-mark evaluate:** what worked and why, what failed and why, the role of the hazard itself, then a conclusion on overall effectiveness. Fill in the **Named country** line.
:::

:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: developed country
Evaluate the effectiveness of the management of an earthquake hazard in a named developed country (named: Japan). (8)

Japan's preparation for shaking was very effective. Strict building codes meant most modern buildings survived a magnitude 9.0 earthquake, and the early warning system gave seconds of warning, stopping high-speed trains. Regular drills, including Disaster Prevention Day each September, meant people knew what to do.

Management of the tsunami was less effective. Sea walls were overtopped, and some people trusted them too much or underestimated the first warning, so about 18,000 people died or went missing. The Fukushima nuclear accident showed that planning had not considered an event this large.

The response was strong: around 100,000 Self-Defence Force troops were deployed, evacuation centres housed huge numbers, and long-term planning has raised sea walls and moved homes to higher ground.

Overall, management was highly effective against the earthquake but only partly effective against the tsunami, which was extreme. The deaths show that even a rich, well-prepared country can be overwhelmed by a secondary hazard of this size, though without its preparation the toll would have been far higher.

(Level 3: detailed evidence, strengths and weaknesses judged, hazard magnitude considered, supported conclusion.)
:::

::: watch
- Mixing primary and secondary: a tsunami, landslide or nuclear accident is **secondary**.
- Describing management without **evaluating** it. Say how well each measure worked and why.
- Claiming Japan "coped perfectly". The tsunami overtopped sea walls, and about 18,000 died or went missing.
- Saying Nepal is on a convergent oceanic boundary. It is a **collision** of continental plates; Japan is the subduction case.
- Inventing precise costs or pledges. "Billions of US dollars" and "about 9,000 deaths" are safe.
- Forgetting the **Named country** line, or writing about a developed country when the question asks for a developing one.
:::
