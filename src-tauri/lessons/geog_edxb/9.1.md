## What it is

Topic 9, **Consuming energy resources**, is examined in **Paper 3 Section C** (26 marks of resource-based questions, including two 8-mark questions) and it usually feeds the **Section D decision** (a 12-mark select-and-justify question plus 4 SPaG marks). The whole topic asks one question: how can the world meet its growing demand for energy without serious environmental damage?

This key idea covers two things:
- how energy resources are **classified** as **non-renewable** (finite stocks of coal, oil and gas), **renewable** (flows of solar, wind and HEP) and **recyclable** (nuclear and biofuels);
- the **environmental impacts** of getting energy: mining and drilling (landscape scarring, oil spills, carbon emissions, forest removal) and the **landscape impacts of renewables** (reservoirs flooding valleys, land taken by wind turbines and solar panels).

Any real example of the right type earns the marks. This course uses open-cast coal mining, the Deepwater Horizon oil spill (Gulf of Mexico, 2010), the Alberta oil sands in Canada's boreal forest, the Three Gorges Dam in China and UK wind and solar farms.

### Three ways to classify energy

Pearson uses its own three-way classification. Learn it exactly, because a 1-mark multiple-choice question often turns on it.

| Class | Meaning | Examples | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Non-renewable** | a **finite stock** that took millions of years to form and will run out | **coal, oil, natural gas** (the fossil fuels) | once burned, it is gone; reserves fall with every year of use |
| **Renewable** | a **flow** of energy that is naturally replaced and will not run out on a human timescale | **solar, wind, HEP** (also tidal, wave, geothermal) | the flow continues whether we use it or not |
| **Recyclable** | energy that can be **reused or regrown** if it is managed | **nuclear** (spent fuel can be reprocessed and reused), **biofuels** (crops and wood regrown) | renewable only if managed; nuclear fuel (uranium) is mined, and biofuels need land |

**Stock or flow?** A **stock** is a store that can be used up, like money in a savings account. A **flow** is like a salary that keeps arriving. Fossil fuels are stocks; sunlight, wind and flowing rivers are flows. This distinction is the basis of every "why is X renewable?" answer.

**Why nuclear is "recyclable" and not renewable:** the uranium that fuels reactors is mined and is finite, but used fuel can be **reprocessed** to recover material that can be used again. **Biofuels** (ethanol from sugar cane or maize, biodiesel, wood pellets) are grown, burned and grown again, so they are recyclable as long as the land keeps producing.

### Why the classification matters

- **Energy security:** a country relying on a finite stock faces supplies running down and prices rising. Flows are home-grown and do not run out.
- **Emissions:** burning fossil fuels releases **carbon dioxide**, the main greenhouse gas driving climate change. Renewables emit almost nothing while running, although building them has a carbon cost.
- **Reliability:** fossil fuels and nuclear give steady **baseload** power whenever needed. Wind and solar are **intermittent**: no wind, no power; no sun, no power. HEP can be switched on quickly.

### Environmental impacts of mining and drilling

Getting fossil fuels out of the ground damages land, water, air and ecosystems.

| Impact | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Landscape scarring** | **open-cast** (surface) mining strips soil and rock to reach coal, leaving huge pits and spoil heaps | open-cast coal mines in the UK, Germany and Australia; mountaintop removal in the Appalachians, USA |
| **Removal of forests** | forest is cleared for mines, roads, pipelines and well pads, destroying habitat | the **Alberta oil sands** in Canada, where boreal forest and wetland are stripped before the bitumen is dug out |
| **Oil spills** | blow-outs, pipeline leaks and tanker accidents release oil that coats coasts, kills seabirds and fish and damages fishing and tourism | **Deepwater Horizon**, Gulf of Mexico, 2010: an explosion killed 11 workers and the well leaked for months; **Exxon Valdez**, Alaska, 1989; repeated pipeline spills in the Niger Delta, Nigeria |
| **Water pollution** | mines produce acid drainage and toxic waste; oil sands leave large **tailings ponds** of contaminated water | rivers downstream of mines and oil sands |
| **Carbon emissions** | burning fuels releases CO₂; extraction and processing need energy too; coal mines and gas wells leak **methane** | coal is the most carbon-intensive fuel per unit of energy |
| **Air pollution** | burning coal releases sulphur dioxide (acid rain), particulates and smog | coal-burning cities in India and China |

**Ranking the fossil fuels:** coal does the most damage per unit of energy (scarring and the highest CO₂). Oil carries the risk of spills. Gas burns more cleanly, releasing roughly half the CO₂ of coal for the same electricity, but methane leaks reduce that advantage.

### Landscape impacts of renewable energy

Renewables are cleaner to run, but they are not impact-free. The spec names three:

| Renewable | Landscape impact | Other issues |
|---|---|---|
| **HEP** (hydro-electric power) | a dam **floods a valley** behind it, drowning farmland, forest, villages and heritage sites | the **Three Gorges Dam** on the Yangtze, China, displaced more than a million people; dams trap sediment, block fish migration and rotting flooded vegetation releases methane |
| **Wind** | turbines take **land use** and are visible over long distances; access roads and cables are needed | noise, bird and bat deaths; local opposition on visual grounds; offshore wind avoids most land issues |
| **Solar** | large **solar farms** cover fields with panels, taking land from farming or habitat | panels need mining of metals and silicon; less visual impact than wind as they are low |

**The balance:** renewables mostly cause **local, visual and land-use** impacts, while fossil fuels cause local damage **and** global harm through climate change. A strong answer weighs scale (local against global), duration (a spill or a mine scar is long-lasting; a turbine can be removed) and reversibility.

### Using a resource booklet

Paper 3 questions in this section are built on a **resource booklet**, often a figure showing a country's **energy mix** (the share of each source) over time. Expect to:
- identify which source has grown or declined;
- calculate a percentage or percentage change;
- suggest why a source is small or large in that country (geology, climate, landscape);
- use the figure as evidence in an 8-mark "assess" or "evaluate".

::: terms
- **Energy resource**: any source of power that people can use, such as coal, wind or uranium.
- **Non-renewable energy**: a finite stock of energy, such as coal, oil or gas, that will eventually run out.
- **Renewable energy**: a flow of energy, such as solar, wind or HEP, that is naturally replaced and will not run out.
- **Recyclable energy**: energy that can be reused or regrown if managed, such as reprocessed nuclear fuel or biofuels.
- **Fossil fuels**: coal, oil and gas, formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient plants and animals.
- **Biofuel**: a fuel made from recently grown plant or animal material, such as ethanol from sugar cane.
- **Energy mix**: the combination of different energy sources that a country uses, usually shown as percentages.
- **Open-cast mining**: surface mining in which soil and rock are stripped away to reach a mineral near the surface.
- **Intermittent**: describes an energy supply, such as wind or solar, that is not available all the time.
- **Baseload**: the steady minimum supply of electricity needed at all times, usually met by fossil fuels or nuclear.
- **Tailings pond**: a large lake of contaminated water and waste left over from mining or oil sands processing.
- **Carbon emissions**: carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere, mainly by burning fossil fuels.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify carefully.** Non-renewable = finite **stock** (coal, oil, gas). Renewable = **flow** (solar, wind, HEP). Recyclable = **reused or regrown** (nuclear, biofuels). Nuclear is never "renewable" in a Pearson answer.
2. **Read the resource.** For an energy-mix figure, name the source and quote its value or change ("coal fell from about 40% to under 5%").
3. **Explain impacts as chains:** the activity → what it does to land, water, air or wildlife → the consequence ("open-cast mining strips vegetation, so habitats are lost and the land is scarred for decades").
4. **Separate the scales.** Fossil fuels cause local damage **and** global climate change; renewables mainly cause local landscape impacts.
5. **For 8-mark evaluate or assess:** use the resource (AO4), compare both sides (AO3), consider scale, duration and reversibility, then give a clear judgement.
6. **For the 12-mark decision:** choose one option, justify it with evidence from the booklet and your own knowledge, and explain why the other two are weaker.
:::

::: example Identify: classification
Which one of the following is classified as a recyclable energy resource? A: Coal. B: Wind. C: Biofuels. D: Natural gas. (1)

C: Biofuels (1). They are made from crops that can be regrown.
:::

::: example Explain one: oil spills
Explain one environmental impact of drilling for oil. (2)

Blow-outs or pipeline leaks can cause oil spills, as in the Deepwater Horizon disaster in the Gulf of Mexico in 2010 (1), which coat coastlines and kill seabirds, fish and marine mammals, damaging ecosystems for years (1).
:::

::: example Suggest: why solar is small in an energy mix
Study a figure showing that solar power provides less than 1% of a northern country's energy. Suggest one reason why solar power is unimportant in this country's energy mix. (2)

The country is at a high latitude, so it receives low sunshine and very short days in winter (1), which means solar panels generate little power exactly when energy demand for heating is highest (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: renewable landscape impacts
Explain two landscape impacts of renewable energy. (4)

Building a dam for HEP floods the valley behind it (1), drowning farmland, forest and settlements, as when more than a million people were moved for the Three Gorges Dam in China (1). Wind farms need large areas of upland or coast (1), and the tall turbines and access roads change the character of the landscape so they are visible for many kilometres (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: renewable against non-renewable
Study a resource showing photographs described as an open-cast coal mine, an oil-sands tailings pond and a hillside wind farm, and a bar chart showing that coal releases far more CO₂ per unit of electricity than wind or solar. Evaluate the environmental impacts of renewable compared with non-renewable energy. (8)

The bar chart shows that coal releases far more CO₂ per unit of electricity than wind or solar, so non-renewables contribute directly to global climate change, an impact that crosses borders and lasts for centuries. The photographs show the local damage too: the open-cast mine has stripped vegetation and soil, and the tailings pond stores contaminated water that can leak into rivers. Oil adds the risk of spills, as Deepwater Horizon showed in the Gulf of Mexico.

Renewables are not impact-free. The wind farm photo shows turbines dominating a hillside, and HEP dams such as the Three Gorges flood whole valleys and displace people. Solar farms take land from farming.

However, most renewable impacts are local and visual and can often be reversed: turbines can be removed and offshore sites avoid land conflicts. Fossil fuel impacts are both local and global, and climate change is the hardest to reverse. Overall, non-renewable energy has the greater environmental impact, although large HEP schemes can be an exception because their flooding is permanent.

(Level 3: uses the resource throughout (AO4), weighs local against global and short against long term, and reaches a supported judgement (AO3).)
:::

::: q
State the difference between a stock resource and a flow resource. (2)
---
A stock is a finite store that is used up as it is consumed, such as coal (1); a flow is naturally replaced and keeps coming whether used or not, such as wind (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why nuclear power is classified as recyclable rather than renewable. (2)
---
Its fuel, uranium, is mined and is finite, so it is not a natural flow (1); but used fuel can be reprocessed and some of it reused, so the resource can be recycled (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one environmental impact of open-cast coal mining. (2)
---
Soil, rock and vegetation are stripped away over large areas (1), leaving pits and spoil heaps that scar the landscape and destroy habitats for decades (1). Or: water draining through the mine becomes acidic or polluted (1), harming rivers and wildlife downstream (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest one reason why some people oppose new wind farms. (3)
---
Turbines are very tall and often placed on exposed hills or coasts (1), so they can be seen for many kilometres and change the character of the landscape (1); residents fear this will reduce house prices or tourism in scenic areas (1). Accept noise, bird deaths or access-road construction, developed.
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of HEP for the environment. (4)
---
Advantage (1): it produces electricity without burning fuel, so it emits very little CO₂ once built and helps reduce climate change (1). Disadvantage (1): the dam floods a large area behind it, drowning forest, farmland and habitats and blocking fish migration (1).
:::

::: q
Sample data: a country's energy mix is coal 35%, gas 30%, oil 15%, nuclear 8%, wind 7%, HEP 3% and biofuels 2%. Calculate the percentage of its energy that comes from non-renewable sources. (1)
---
35 + 30 + 15 = 80% (1). Nuclear is recyclable, not non-renewable, in this classification.
:::

::: watch
- Calling nuclear or biofuels **renewable**. In this spec they are **recyclable**.
- Saying renewables have **no** environmental impact. The spec names HEP flooding and land use for turbines and panels.
- Writing "it pollutes" without saying **what** is polluted (air, river, sea) and **what the effect is**.
- Mixing up **stock** and **flow**: fossil fuels are stocks, solar and wind are flows.
- Ignoring the figure in resource questions. Paper 3 marks "using evidence from Figure n" down if no value or label is quoted.
- In an evaluate answer, forgetting the **global** impact of fossil fuels (climate change), which is often the decisive point.
:::

::: test
Explain one reason why fossil fuels are classified as non-renewable. (2)
---
They formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient plants and animals (1), so once a stock is extracted and burned it cannot be replaced on a human timescale and reserves eventually run out (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one way that extracting oil can damage ecosystems. (2)
---
Spills from wells, pipelines or tankers coat coasts and water with oil (1), killing seabirds, fish and shellfish and damaging food webs for years, as after Deepwater Horizon in 2010 (1). Or: forest is cleared for well pads, roads and pipelines (1), fragmenting habitats used by migrating animals (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one reason why biofuels may not be as environmentally friendly as they appear. (2)
---
Growing biofuel crops needs large areas of land (1), so forest may be cleared or food crops replaced, releasing stored carbon and destroying habitats (1). Or: farming, fertilisers and processing use fossil-fuel energy (1), so the overall carbon saving is smaller than it seems (1).
:::

::: test
Suggest one reason why a country with high mountains and heavy rainfall might rely on HEP. (3)
---
Steep valleys give a large drop in height (head) and narrow sites for dams (1); heavy rainfall keeps rivers flowing strongly all year (1); so large amounts of cheap, reliable electricity can be generated without importing fuel (1).
:::

::: test
Study a resource described as a photograph of an oil-sands mine in Canada showing cleared forest, a large open pit and a tailings pond. Using evidence from the resource, explain two environmental impacts of extracting oil from tar sands. (4)
---
Two from: forest and wetland have been cleared (1), destroying the habitat of animals such as caribou and releasing stored carbon (1); a large open pit scars the landscape (1), and restoring it to natural forest takes decades (1); the tailings pond holds contaminated water (1), which can leak into rivers and harm fish and the people who depend on them (1); processing bitumen needs lots of energy (1), so carbon emissions are higher than for conventional oil (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of solar farms. (4)
---
Advantage (1): they generate electricity from a free, natural flow without emitting CO₂ while operating, reducing climate change (1). Disadvantage (1): they cover large areas of land that could be farmed or used as habitat, and generate nothing at night and little in winter (1).
:::

::: test
Explain two reasons why coal is often described as the most damaging fossil fuel. (4)
---
Two from: it releases the most CO₂ per unit of energy (1), so it contributes most to climate change (1); open-cast mining strips land over large areas (1), scarring the landscape and destroying habitats (1); burning it releases sulphur dioxide and particulates (1), causing acid rain and health problems such as asthma (1); mines release methane and acid drainage (1), polluting the air and nearby rivers (1).
:::
