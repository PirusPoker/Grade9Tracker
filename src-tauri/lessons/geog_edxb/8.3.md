## What it is

This key idea says tropical rainforests are threatened **directly by deforestation** and **indirectly by climate change**. It covers:
- the **causes of deforestation**: commercial **hardwood logging**, **subsistence** and **commercial agriculture**, and local demand for **fuelwood**;
- how demand for **biofuels**, **mineral resources** and **electricity (HEP)** adds to deforestation;
- why **climate change** is an **indirect threat** to rainforest health, through **ecosystem stress** and **drought**;
- the skill of using **GIS** (satellite images and online maps) to identify the **pattern of forest loss**.

Any real rainforest earns the marks. This course uses the **Amazon in Brazil**, with comparisons from **Indonesia** (palm oil) and the **Congo Basin** (fuelwood). The spec separates **direct** threats (people cutting or burning trees) from **indirect** ones (changes to the conditions the forest needs), and so do the mark schemes.

### Direct threat: deforestation

**Deforestation** is the permanent removal of forest for another land use. Every year the world loses millions of hectares of tropical forest, with Brazil, Indonesia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo among the countries losing the most.

| Cause | What happens | Example and impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial agriculture: cattle ranching** | forest is cut and burned and sown with grass for beef cattle | the **largest single cause** of Amazon deforestation; most cleared land in the Brazilian Amazon ends up as pasture; soils lose fertility within years, so ranchers clear more |
| **Commercial agriculture: soya and palm oil** | large farms grow crops for export, often as animal feed or vegetable oil | soya spread north from **Mato Grosso**, partly onto old pasture, pushing ranchers further into the forest; **palm oil** plantations replaced large areas of forest in **Indonesia and Malaysia** |
| **Subsistence agriculture** | small farmers clear plots to grow food for their families | landless migrants settle along new roads and use **slash-and-burn**; traditional **shifting cultivation** becomes unsustainable when populations grow and plots are reused before the forest recovers |
| **Commercial hardwood logging** | valuable trees such as **mahogany** and other dense tropical hardwoods are cut for furniture, flooring and export | often **selective**, but the roads and tracks built to extract them open the forest to farmers and settlers; much logging is **illegal** |
| **Fuelwood** | trees are cut for firewood or made into charcoal for cooking | the main household fuel for many families in the **Congo Basin**; demand rises near growing cities, creating rings of cleared land |

**Roads are the trigger.** Highways such as the **Trans-Amazonian Highway** and the **BR-163** opened the forest to loggers, settlers and ranchers. Most clearing happens within a few tens of kilometres of a road.

### How other demands add to deforestation

**Biofuels**
- Rising demand for "green" fuels such as **biodiesel** from palm oil and **ethanol** from sugar cane.
- In South-east Asia, palm oil plantations have directly replaced rainforest and drained carbon-rich **peat swamp forest**.
- In Brazil, sugar cane is mostly grown outside the Amazon, but it takes land that was farmed or grazed, pushing cattle and soya into forest areas (an **indirect land-use change**).

**Mineral resources**
- The **Carajás** mine in the state of **Pará** is one of the world's largest iron ore mines. Mining needs roads, a railway, power and towns, all of which clear forest and attract settlers.
- Bauxite (for aluminium) and copper are also mined in the Amazon.
- **Illegal gold mining** (garimpo) clears riverbanks, digs pits and pollutes rivers with **mercury**, poisoning fish and the people who eat them. It has caused serious harm on **Yanomami** lands, where the Brazilian government declared a health emergency in 2023.

**Electricity: hydroelectric power (HEP)**
- Brazil gets most of its electricity from HEP, and many Amazon rivers are being dammed.
- The **Belo Monte** dam on the **Xingu River** in Pará is one of the largest in the world by capacity. It flooded forest and parts of the city of Altamira, displaced thousands of people and changed river flows relied on by indigenous groups and fishers.
- Reservoirs drown forest; rotting vegetation releases **methane**, a greenhouse gas; power lines and access roads cut more forest.
- But HEP is renewable and lower-carbon than coal or oil, so governments argue that dams are needed to supply growing cities.

### Indirect threat: climate change

Climate change does not cut trees, but it changes the conditions the forest depends on, putting it under **ecosystem stress**.

- **Higher temperatures** increase evaporation, so soils and plants dry out; some species are near their heat limits.
- **Longer and more severe droughts.** The Amazon suffered major droughts in **2005** and **2010** and a severe drought in **2023**, when some rivers fell to record low levels. During droughts large trees die, and the forest takes in less carbon.
- **More fires.** Rainforest plants are **not adapted to fire**. In normal years the forest is too damp to burn, but in drought years fires lit to clear farmland escape into standing forest, killing trees and making it more likely to burn again.
- **Ecosystem stress.** Species that cannot move or adapt quickly enough decline; flowering and fruiting times change, breaking links between plants and their pollinators or seed-dispersers; interdependence weakens.
- **Feedback with deforestation.** Fewer trees mean less transpiration and less recycled rain, making droughts worse. Some scientists warn of a **tipping point**, beyond which parts of the Amazon could turn into drier, savanna-like vegetation. Studies suggest that parts of the heavily cleared south-east Amazon already release more carbon than they absorb.

**Why it counts as indirect:** the damage comes from changes in **climate** caused by **global** emissions, often far away, rather than from local people cutting trees. This makes it harder for any one country to stop.

### GIS: seeing the pattern of forest loss

A **geographical information system (GIS)** layers data, such as satellite images, roads and rivers, on a digital map.
- Brazil's space agency **INPE** monitors the Amazon by satellite, publishing **annual** clearance figures and **near real-time alerts** used by enforcement officers.
- Online tools such as **Global Forest Watch** map tree-cover loss year by year for the whole world.

**Patterns to recognise:**
- **Fishbone pattern:** a main road with side roads branching off at regular intervals, each lined with cleared plots, as in the state of **Rondônia**.
- **Arc of deforestation:** the heaviest clearing along the **southern and eastern edges** of the Amazon, where the agricultural frontier advances north from Mato Grosso and Pará.
- **Clusters** around mines, dams, towns and rivers.
- **Fire hotspots** concentrated along the frontier in the dry season.

When describing a GIS map, use **compass directions**, **named places**, **distances** and the **shape** of the pattern (linear, clustered, dispersed).

::: terms
- **Deforestation**: the permanent removal of forest, usually for farming, mining, settlement or infrastructure.
- **Commercial agriculture**: farming to sell crops or livestock for profit, often on a large scale and for export.
- **Subsistence agriculture**: farming to produce food for the farmer's own family, with little left to sell.
- **Slash-and-burn**: cutting vegetation and burning it to clear land and release nutrients into the soil for crops.
- **Hardwood**: dense, slow-growing timber from broadleaved trees, such as mahogany, valued for furniture.
- **Fuelwood**: wood collected for burning as fuel, either directly or after conversion to charcoal.
- **Biofuel**: fuel made from plant material, such as ethanol from sugar cane or biodiesel from palm oil.
- **Hydroelectric power**: electricity generated by water flowing through turbines, usually from a dammed reservoir.
- **Ecosystem stress**: pressure on an ecosystem from changing conditions, reducing its health and ability to function.
- **Tipping point**: a threshold beyond which a system changes into a different state that is hard to reverse.
- **GIS**: a geographical information system, which layers different data on a digital map to show patterns.
:::

::: method
1. **Direct or indirect?** Decide which the question asks about. Direct = logging, farming, fuelwood, plus mining, biofuels and HEP clearance. Indirect = climate change (drought, heat, fire, stress).
2. **Name the cause and the place**: "cattle ranching in the Brazilian Amazon", "palm oil in Indonesia".
3. **Develop the chain**: cause → what happens to the forest → wider effect (soil, biodiversity, people, carbon).
4. **GIS and map questions**: describe the pattern first (shape, location, direction), then explain it (roads, frontier, mines).
5. **8-mark assess**: weigh direct against indirect threats, use the resource, and decide which matters more, for which area and timescale.
:::

::: example State: a direct cause
State one direct cause of deforestation in the Amazon. (1)

Cattle ranching (1). Also accept: logging, soya farming, mining, subsistence farming, HEP reservoirs.
:::

::: example Explain one: roads
Explain one way road building contributes to deforestation. (2)

Roads such as the BR-163 give loggers, settlers and ranchers access to previously remote forest (1), so land along the road and its side branches is cleared, creating a fishbone pattern of deforestation (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: HEP
Explain one negative impact of developing HEP in tropical rainforests. (2)

Building a dam such as Belo Monte floods large areas of forest behind it (1), destroying habitats and displacing people who depend on the river and forest for food and income (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: climate change as an indirect threat
Explain two ways climate change threatens the health of tropical rainforests. (4)

Rising temperatures and longer dry seasons cause more frequent droughts (1), so large trees die and the forest takes in less carbon (1). Drier forests burn more easily (1), and because rainforest plants are not adapted to fire, escaped farm fires kill trees and make further fires more likely (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: direct and indirect threats
Study a resource showing a GIS map of the Amazon with an "arc" of forest loss along the southern and eastern edges, and a graph of rainfall showing severe droughts in recent decades. Assess the relative importance of direct and indirect threats to tropical rainforests. (8)

The GIS map shows that most forest loss is concentrated in an arc along the southern and eastern edges of the Amazon, along main roads such as the BR-163. This pattern shows direct deforestation is the main threat today: cattle ranching, the largest single cause, soya expansion, logging and mining such as Carajás clear forest permanently, removing the biomass store and causing soils to degrade quickly.

However, the rainfall graph shows severe droughts becoming more common. Climate change stresses the whole forest, not just the frontier: trees die in drought, fires escape into forest that is not adapted to burning, and links between plants and pollinators can break. Because it is driven by global emissions, no single government can stop it.

The two threats reinforce each other. Clearing reduces transpiration and recycled rainfall, making droughts worse, while drought makes fire-clearing more destructive. Some scientists warn that together they could push parts of the Amazon past a tipping point towards savanna.

Overall, direct deforestation is more important now, because it causes most forest loss and can be seen on the GIS map, but indirect threats are growing and could become more important in the long term, especially if clearing continues to weaken the forest's own rainfall.

(Level 3: both resources used (AO4); threats weighed and linked; a judgement supported by evidence and timescale (AO3).)
:::

::: watch
- Calling climate change a **direct** cause of deforestation. It is an **indirect** threat to forest **health**.
- Saying "logging" without saying **what** is logged (hardwoods) or **why** it leads to wider clearing (roads, access).
- Forgetting that biofuels, minerals and HEP are separate spec bullets: learn one developed point and one example for each.
- Describing a GIS map without **directions, place names or shape**.
- Blaming only poor farmers: commercial ranching and soya, driven by global demand, clear far more.
- Making up hectares or percentages for real places. Keep figures general unless you are certain.
:::
