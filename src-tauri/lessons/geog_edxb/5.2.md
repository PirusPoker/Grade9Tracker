## What it is

The UK's people and economy are shaped by links with the rest of the world. This key idea asks you to explain:
- why **national and international migration** over the past **50 years** has changed the UK's **population geography**: its **numbers**, **distribution** and **age structure**;
- how **UK and EU immigration policy** has added to the UK's **ethnic and cultural diversity**;
- why the **decline of the primary and secondary sectors** and the **rise of the tertiary and quaternary sectors** have changed **employment structure** in contrasting regions, urban and rural;
- why **globalisation**, **free-trade policies** (UK and EU) and **privatisation** have increased **foreign direct investment (FDI)** and the role of **TNCs** in the UK.

Integrated skills: using **census data** (skill 2) and **Eurostat** (skill 3) to investigate population change, FDI and immigration. It is examined in **Paper 2, Section B**: short data items, "Explain one…" and "Explain two…", and possibly the 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question, which may carry **4 SPaG marks**. Any accurate UK region or company earns credit; this lesson uses well-known examples.

### Migration and the UK's population geography

**Numbers.** The UK population has grown from about **56 million** in the early 1970s to about **68 million** in the early 2020s. In the 1970s and 1980s most growth was **natural increase** (more births than deaths). Since the late 1990s **net international migration** has been the larger part of growth, and migrants' children have added to births.

**Waves of international migration**
| Period | Main flows | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1950s–1970s | from Commonwealth countries: the Caribbean, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, East Africa | labour shortages after the Second World War; British citizenship links; some fleeing persecution (e.g. Asians expelled from Uganda in 1972) |
| 1970s–1990s | slower; mainly family reunion, students, refugees | tighter immigration laws limited Commonwealth labour migration |
| 2004 onwards | large numbers from new EU members in Central and Eastern Europe, especially **Poland**, Lithuania and Romania | **EU free movement**: the UK was one of few existing members to give the 2004 joiners full access to work immediately; higher wages and job vacancies |
| 2021 onwards | fewer EU arrivals; more from outside the EU (India, Nigeria, Hong Kong, Ukraine and others) | end of free movement and a **points-based system** from January 2021; student and health and care worker visas; humanitarian schemes |

**National (internal) migration**
- The **north–south drift**: from the 1970s, people moved from the old industrial North, Wales and Scotland towards London and the South East, where new service jobs were growing.
- **Counter-urbanisation**: families and retirees moving out of cities to small towns, villages and the coast.
- **Young adults** move **into** cities for university and first jobs; **families** move **out** to suburbs and commuter towns.
- **Retirement migration** to the coast and countryside, e.g. Devon, Dorset, Norfolk and North Wales.

**Effects on the population geography**
- **Distribution**: international migrants mostly settle in **cities**, above all **London**, plus Birmingham, Manchester, Leicester and Bradford. After 2004, some Eastern European migrants also went to **rural and small-town areas** for work in farming and food processing, e.g. in Lincolnshire and the Fens.
- **Age structure**: most migrants arrive as **young adults** (20–35), so they **slow the ageing** of the UK population and add to births. Cities have younger pyramids; rural and coastal areas that young people leave and retirees enter have much older ones.
- **Regional growth**: London and the South East grew fastest; some northern towns and Scottish areas lost people in the 1980s and 1990s.

### Immigration policy and diversity

- **Commonwealth links** after 1945 allowed large migration from former colonies, creating long-established Caribbean, Indian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi communities.
- **Immigration Acts** from the 1960s onwards gradually restricted entry, shifting migration to family reunion, students and skilled work.
- **EU membership** (from 1973) and **free movement** let EU citizens live and work in the UK without visas. After **2004** this added large Polish and other Central and Eastern European communities.
- After the UK left the EU, free movement ended on **31 December 2020**. The **points-based system** treats EU and non-EU applicants alike, favouring skills, salary and English language. Non-EU migration then rose sharply, making new arrivals more varied in origin.
- **Result**: the UK, especially its cities, is ethnically and culturally diverse. The census shows a growing share of people from ethnic minority groups and born overseas; London is the most diverse region, with well over a third of residents born abroad. Diversity shows in languages, faiths and places of worship, food, festivals (such as Notting Hill Carnival) and businesses.

### Skill: census data

- The **census** counts everyone in the UK every **ten years** (the latest in England and Wales was **2021**; Scotland's was in 2022).
- It records age, sex, ethnic group, country of birth, religion, language, housing, health and occupation, down to small local areas.
- Comparing census years shows **change**: e.g. the share born overseas, the rise in over-65s, or the change in jobs by sector.
- Calculations: **percentage change** = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; **percentage share** = part ÷ total × 100.

### Economic change: from making to services

The UK's **employment structure**, the share of workers in each sector, has changed dramatically:

| Sector | What it is | 1970s | Today |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | extracting raw materials: farming, fishing, mining, forestry | a few per cent, but very important in coalfields | about 1–2% |
| **Secondary** | manufacturing and construction | roughly a third of jobs | well under a fifth |
| **Tertiary** | services: retail, health, education, finance, tourism | about half | more than four in five jobs together with quaternary |
| **Quaternary** | knowledge and research: IT, biotech, R&D, consultancy | small | growing fast |

**Why primary and secondary industries declined (de-industrialisation)**
- **Global competition**: countries such as China, India and South Korea produce steel, ships and textiles with **cheaper labour**, so UK factories could not compete.
- **Mechanisation and automation**: machines replaced workers in mines, farms and factories.
- **Exhaustion and cost** of coal seams; cheaper imported coal and gas; the move away from coal for climate reasons. The last deep coal mine closed in **2015**.
- **Government policy** in the 1980s withdrew subsidies from loss-making nationalised industries and privatised others.
- **Fishing quotas** and depleted stocks reduced fishing fleets.

**Why tertiary and quaternary industries grew**
- Rising incomes increased demand for **services**: shopping, leisure, tourism, health.
- **London** became a leading **global financial centre** after financial deregulation in **1986** (the "Big Bang").
- The **ICT revolution** and a highly educated workforce led to **research, digital and creative industries**, often near universities: Cambridge, Oxford, the **M4 corridor** (Reading, Swindon, Bristol).
- **Globalisation**: TNCs set up offices and call centres in the UK.

**Contrasting regions**
- **North East England**: lost coal mining, shipbuilding and steel, causing high unemployment in the 1980s. New jobs came from **Nissan's car plant in Sunderland** (FDI), call centres, public services and universities, but many are lower paid.
- **South Wales valleys**: coal mining collapsed; jobs moved to Cardiff and the M4 corridor; some valleys still have high deprivation.
- **London and the South East**: finance, business services, media, tech and tourism; high wages but very high living costs.
- **Rural areas**: farm employment has fallen with mechanisation and larger farms; new jobs in tourism, home-based digital work, retail and services, often part-time or seasonal.

### FDI and TNCs: why the UK attracts investment

**Foreign direct investment** is when a company from one country builds or buys businesses, factories or offices in another. The UK has long been one of the largest receivers of FDI in Europe.

**1. Globalisation**
- TNCs operate worldwide and choose locations for markets, skills and access. The UK offers an English-speaking, skilled workforce, a stable legal system, a global financial centre and a convenient time zone between Asia and America.

**2. Free-trade policies (UK and EU)**
- As an EU member, the UK was inside the **single market** (from 1993), so firms building in the UK could sell to the whole EU with **no tariffs**. Japanese car makers, such as **Nissan (Sunderland)** and **Toyota (Derbyshire)**, chose the UK partly for this.
- Since leaving the EU, the UK has made its own trade deals and still promotes itself as open to investment, though some firms became more cautious about trade barriers with the EU.

**3. Privatisation**
- From the 1980s, state-owned industries were **sold to private shareholders**: telecoms, gas, electricity, water, rail, airports. Foreign firms could then **buy** them.
- Many UK utilities, energy firms, airports and football clubs are now partly or wholly **foreign-owned**. For example, India's **Tata** bought the steelmaker Corus, much of it the old British Steel (including Port Talbot), and Jaguar Land Rover, and French state-owned **EDF** runs nuclear power stations.

**Effects of FDI and TNCs**
- **Benefits**: jobs, investment in places hit by de-industrialisation, new technology and skills, exports, tax revenue, supply chains for local firms.
- **Costs**: profits flow abroad; decisions are made by overseas headquarters, so plants can close or shrink if the TNC moves production; key infrastructure is foreign-owned.
- **Regional pattern**: **London and the South East** receive the most FDI projects (offices, finance, tech), while manufacturing FDI is more spread out, with notable clusters in the Midlands and North East. Remote rural areas receive little.

### Skill: Eurostat

**Eurostat** is the statistics office of the EU. It publishes comparable data for member countries on migration, population, FDI and trade, so the UK (up to its exit) can be compared with other European countries, for example the share of residents born abroad or FDI inflows per year. Use it alongside the UK census and the Office for National Statistics.

::: terms
- **Net migration**: the number of people moving into a country minus the number moving out.
- **Internal migration**: the movement of people from one part of a country to another.
- **Points-based system**: an immigration system that grants visas according to skills, salary and other criteria, used in the UK since 2021.
- **Free movement**: the EU rule allowing citizens of member states to live and work in any other member state.
- **Ethnic diversity**: the variety of ethnic groups, cultures and backgrounds living in an area.
- **Employment structure**: the proportion of workers in the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors.
- **De-industrialisation**: the decline of manufacturing and heavy industry in a country or region.
- **Quaternary sector**: jobs in research, information and knowledge-based industries such as IT and biotechnology.
- **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**: investment by a company in businesses, factories or offices in another country.
- **Transnational corporation (TNC)**: a company that operates in more than one country.
- **Privatisation**: selling state-owned industries or services to private companies and shareholders.
- **Free trade**: trade between countries without tariffs, quotas or other barriers.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the figure**: quote values, years and regions. For census tables, name the category and the change.
2. **Migration questions**: separate **national** (internal) and **international** flows; say **who** moved, **why**, and the **effect** on numbers, distribution or age structure.
3. **Policy and diversity**: name the policy (Commonwealth links, EU free movement, 2004 enlargement, points-based system) and link it to which groups arrived.
4. **Economic change**: give the **cause** of decline (global competition, cheaper labour abroad, mechanisation, exhausted resources) and the **cause** of growth (rising incomes, finance, ICT, universities), then name a contrasting region.
5. **FDI**: use the three drivers in the spec: **globalisation**, **free trade**, **privatisation**. Give a real company where you can.
6. **Calculations**: percentage change or share; show working and units.
7. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: use figure data in every paragraph, weigh several reasons against each other, and end with a judgement on which matters most.
:::

D: research and information work is quaternary (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: percentage change from census data

::: example Explain one: de-industrialisation
Explain one reason for the decline of manufacturing in the UK. (2)

::: example Explain two: why FDI has increased
Explain two reasons why foreign direct investment in the UK has increased. (4)

Privatisation sold state industries such as energy and water to private shareholders (1), so foreign companies were able to buy them, e.g. EDF running nuclear power stations (1). Being in the EU single market meant firms in the UK could sell across Europe without tariffs (1), which attracted Japanese car makers such as Nissan to build factories in Sunderland (1).
:::

::: watch
- Mixing up **national** and **international** migration. Say which one you mean, and give a flow for each if the question says "national and international".
- Writing "factories closed" with no reason. Give the **cause**: global competition, cheaper labour abroad, mechanisation, exhausted coal seams.
- Treating the UK as one place. The question often wants **contrasting regions**: the North East or South Wales against London.
- Forgetting the three FDI drivers in the spec: **globalisation**, **free-trade policy** and **privatisation**. Each needs its own explanation.
- Out-of-date policy: free movement ended at the end of 2020; the **points-based system** started in 2021.
- In calculations, confusing **percentage change** with **percentage-point change**.
- In the 8-marker, listing reasons without weighing them. Say which reason **matters most** and why.
