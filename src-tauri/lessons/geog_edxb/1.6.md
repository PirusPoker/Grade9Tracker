## What it is

Countries cannot stop tropical cyclones, but they can **prepare** for them and **respond** well. This key idea covers four methods (**weather forecasting, satellite technology, warning and evacuation strategies, and storm-surge defences**) and asks you to **judge how effective they were** in one **developed** country and one **developing or emerging** country.

This course uses **Hurricane Katrina, USA (2005)** and **Typhoon Haiyan, Philippines (2013)**. Any real, named pair of the right types earns the marks. The Philippines counts as emerging, because it has medium human development.

### The four methods

| Method | How it works | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Weather forecasting** | national agencies (the US National Hurricane Center; PAGASA in the Philippines) use computer models to predict a storm's track and strength days ahead, with a "cone of uncertainty" | strength, especially rapid intensification, is harder to predict than track; forecasts are useless if nobody acts on them |
| **Satellite technology** | satellites watch storms form and move, measure cloud-top temperatures and wind; aircraft fly into storms to measure pressure | needs expensive technology and trained staff; poorer countries rely on shared data |
| **Warning and evacuation** | warnings by TV, radio, phone alerts, sirens and volunteers; evacuation orders, routes and shelters | people need transport, money and trust to leave; shelters must be safe and stocked; warnings must be understood |
| **Storm-surge defences** | levees, sea walls and floodgates; raised cyclone shelters; mangrove forests that absorb wave energy | expensive; can fail or be overtopped; can give a false sense of security |

### Case study 1: Hurricane Katrina, USA, August 2005 (developed)

**The storm.** It reached **Category 5** over the very warm Gulf of Mexico and made landfall in **Louisiana as Category 3** at the end of August 2005. Its surge and the failure of the flood defences devastated **New Orleans**, much of which lies below sea level between the Mississippi River and Lake Pontchartrain.

**Preparation**
- The National Hurricane Center tracked the storm by satellite and aircraft and forecast landfall near New Orleans days in advance. Forecasting was **effective**.
- A **mandatory evacuation** of New Orleans was ordered the day before landfall, and road lanes were switched so traffic could all flow out of the city. Most residents left.
- But many of the city's poorest residents, often without cars, as well as the elderly and sick, **could not or did not leave**. The **Superdome** stadium became a "refuge of last resort" with too little food, water and sanitation.
- **Storm-surge defences failed.** The levees and floodwalls were overtopped and breached, and about **80% of New Orleans flooded**, in places several metres deep. Investigations found design and construction faults.

**Response**
- The Coast Guard and National Guard rescued tens of thousands of people from rooftops and flooded homes.
- The federal emergency agency **FEMA** was widely criticised for being slow and badly coordinated; supplies took days to reach people in the city.
- More than **1,800 people died**, and Katrina was one of the costliest disasters in US history.
- Long-term: billions of US dollars were spent on a stronger system of levees, floodwalls and surge barriers around New Orleans, and on rebuilding. The city's population fell sharply and took years to partly recover.

**Effectiveness.** Forecasting, satellites and the evacuation of people with cars worked well, which saved many lives. Defences and the response for the poorest failed, so the outcome was far worse than a rich country should expect.

### Case study 2: Typhoon Haiyan (Yolanda), Philippines, November 2013 (emerging)

**The storm.** Haiyan was one of the strongest tropical cyclones ever recorded at landfall. It crossed the central Philippines, and a **storm surge of several metres** swept through the city of **Tacloban** on the island of Leyte.

**Preparation**
- **PAGASA** issued warnings days ahead and the strongest public storm signal. The government and local officials evacuated hundreds of thousands of people.
- But many people **did not understand the term "storm surge"**. Some thought it meant an ordinary high tide, and later said that calling it a tsunami-like wave would have made them leave.
- Some **evacuation centres**, such as schools and churches near the coast, were themselves flooded by the surge.
- There were few engineered surge defences, and many homes were built of light materials; mangroves had been cleared from parts of the coast.

**Response**
- More than **6,000 people died**, and millions were displaced or lost their homes.
- Debris blocked roads and the airport was badly damaged, so aid was **slow to reach** Tacloban and remote islands for several days; there was looting of food stores.
- A **large international effort** followed: UN agencies, charities and foreign governments, including the UK and USA, sent ships, aircraft, food, water, shelter and medical teams.
- Long-term: a national rebuilding plan; proposed "no-build zones" along the coast (controversial, because fishing families needed to live near the sea); mangrove replanting; warnings now explain storm surges in plain local language.

**Effectiveness.** Forecasting was accurate and evacuation saved many lives, but the warning message, shelter locations and housing quality failed many people. Recovery depended heavily on foreign aid.

### Comparing the two

| | Katrina (USA) | Haiyan (Philippines) |
|---|---|---|
| Forecasting and satellites | accurate, days ahead | accurate, days ahead |
| Warnings and evacuation | mandatory evacuation; most left, but the poorest without cars stayed | hundreds of thousands evacuated; "storm surge" misunderstood; some shelters flooded |
| Defences | levees existed but failed | few engineered defences |
| Response | domestic, but slow and criticised | slowed by blocked roads and airport; reliant on international aid |
| Deaths | more than 1,800 | more than 6,000 |

**Key judgement:** both countries forecast the storm well, so technology was not the weak point. The failures were **human**: who could evacuate, whether warnings were understood, and whether defences held. Wealth did not guarantee success in the USA, and the Philippines' greater losses reflect a stronger storm and higher vulnerability as much as poorer preparation.

::: terms
- **Preparation**: actions taken before a hazard strikes to reduce its impact, such as forecasting and evacuation.
- **Response**: actions taken during and after a hazard to save lives and help recovery.
- **Weather forecasting**: using observations and computer models to predict a storm's track and strength.
- **Evacuation**: moving people away from a dangerous area before a hazard strikes.
- **Levee**: an embankment built to stop a river, lake or the sea flooding land.
- **Storm-surge defences**: structures or natural barriers, such as sea walls, levees and mangroves, that reduce surge flooding.
- **FEMA**: the US Federal Emergency Management Agency, which coordinates the national response to disasters.
- **PAGASA**: the Philippines' national weather agency, which forecasts typhoons and issues warnings.
- **International aid**: help given by other countries and organisations in the form of money, goods, equipment or people.
:::

::: method
1. **Know both case studies under the same headings**: forecasting, warnings and evacuation, defences, immediate response, long-term response.
2. **Explain one way… (3 marks):** method, how it works, and the result for people: "warnings let people evacuate, so they are away from the coast when the surge arrives".
3. **Evaluate questions need judgements.** For every method say how well it worked and why, using evidence.
4. **Compare** using "whereas" and "both".
5. **8-mark evaluate:** strengths with evidence, weaknesses with evidence, other factors (storm strength, vulnerability), then a conclusion on overall effectiveness.
6. Fill in the **Named country** line, and keep figures rounded.
:::

::: example Explain one way: preparation
Explain one way countries can prepare for tropical cyclones. (3)

Early warning systems (1) let people know a storm is approaching days in advance (1), giving them time to move to shelters or inland before the surge arrives (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: Katrina's problems
Explain two reasons why the impacts of Hurricane Katrina were so severe in New Orleans. (4)

The levees and floodwalls failed (1), so about 80% of the city, much of it below sea level, flooded (1). Many poorer residents had no car (1), so they could not follow the evacuation order and were trapped in flooded homes or the Superdome (1).
:::

::: example Compare responses
Compare the response to a tropical cyclone in a developed country and a developing or emerging country. (4)

In both the USA (Katrina) and the Philippines (Haiyan), rescue was slowed in the first days (1). Whereas the US response was mainly domestic, through FEMA and the National Guard (1), the Philippines relied heavily on international aid from the UN and foreign governments (1). Access was a bigger problem in the Philippines, where debris blocked roads and the airport, while in New Orleans the problem was poor coordination (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: emerging country
Evaluate the effectiveness of methods used to prepare for and respond to a tropical cyclone in a named developing or emerging country. (8)

Named country: the Philippines (Typhoon Haiyan, 2013).

Forecasting was effective. PAGASA tracked Haiyan by satellite and issued its highest storm signal days ahead, and hundreds of thousands of people were evacuated, which certainly saved many lives.

However, warnings were only partly effective. Many people did not understand what a "storm surge" was, so some stayed near the coast, and some evacuation centres were themselves flooded by a surge several metres high in Tacloban. There were few engineered defences, and homes of light materials could not withstand the winds. More than 6,000 people died.

The response was slow at first because debris blocked roads and the airport was damaged, but a large international aid effort brought food, shelter and medical care within days. Longer term, plain-language surge warnings and mangrove replanting have improved preparation.

Overall, the methods were only partly effective. Technology worked well, but communication, shelter planning and housing quality failed, and the country depended on foreign aid. Haiyan's extreme strength means some losses were unavoidable, but better warnings could have saved many more lives.

(Level 3: methods judged with evidence, other factors considered, and a supported conclusion.)
:::

Advantage (1): they hold back surge water, protecting large areas of low-lying city (1). Disadvantage (1): they can fail or be overtopped, as in New Orleans, causing sudden, deep flooding / they are very expensive / they give a false sense of security (1).

::: watch
- Describing the case study without **judging** effectiveness. "Evaluate" needs "this worked because…" or "this failed because…".
- Saying the USA "coped well because it is rich". Katrina showed wealth does not guarantee good preparation.
- Mixing up the two storms' details, such as the Superdome for Haiyan or Tacloban for Katrina.
- Inventing precise figures. Use "more than 1,800", "about 80%", "more than 6,000", "millions displaced".
- Forgetting the **Named country** line in an 8-marker.
- Treating forecasting as the same thing as warnings. A good forecast is useless if the warning fails.
:::
