## What it is

The Earth is heated unevenly. Near the equator the Sun's rays strike almost vertically, so their energy is concentrated on a small area; near the poles the same energy is spread over a much larger area and passes through more atmosphere. Left alone, the tropics would get hotter and hotter and the poles colder and colder. They don't, because two systems constantly **transfer heat from the equator towards the poles**: the **global atmospheric circulation** and **ocean currents**. This key idea is the foundation for the whole of Hazardous Earth: the same circulation that builds deserts also breeds tropical cyclones (1.4).

### The three-cell model

The atmosphere in each hemisphere is organised into three giant loops of moving air, called **circulation cells**.

| Cell | Latitudes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Hadley cell** | 0° to about 30° | Intense heating at the equator makes air rise; it flows polewards high up, cools and sinks at about 30° |
| **Ferrel cell** | about 30° to 60° | Driven by the cells either side; surface air flows polewards and rises at about 60° |
| **Polar cell** | about 60° to 90° | Very cold, dense air sinks over the pole and flows back towards 60° at the surface |

Where the cells meet, air is either **rising** or **sinking**, and that sets the pressure belts.

- **Rising air = low pressure.** As air rises it expands and cools. Cooler air holds less water vapour, so the vapour **condenses** into cloud and falls as rain.
- **Sinking air = high pressure.** As air sinks it is compressed and warms. Warmer air can hold more water vapour, so clouds evaporate and skies stay **clear and dry**.

That single chain (rising, cooling, condensing, rain; or sinking, warming, no cloud, dry) earns most of the marks in this topic. "High pressure is hot" is not an explanation.

### The pressure belts and where they put deserts and rainforests

| Latitude | Air movement | Pressure | Climate result | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equator (0°) | rising | low: the **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** | heavy rain most afternoons, all year | Amazon, Congo basin, Indonesia: **tropical rainforest** |
| About 30° N and S | sinking | **subtropical high** | very little rain, clear skies, hot days | Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, Australian deserts: **hot desert** |
| About 60° N and S | rising where warm and cold air meet (the **polar front**) | low | frequent depressions bringing rain | UK and north-west Europe |
| Poles (90°) | sinking | high | very cold and dry: polar deserts | Antarctica |

So the spec's point that circulation decides the location of **arid (high pressure)** and **high rainfall (low pressure)** areas is mostly about these belts. Local factors then modify the pattern: a cold ocean current offshore makes the Atacama Desert even drier, and mountains create rain shadows.

The ITCZ is not fixed. It follows the overhead Sun north in the northern summer and south in the southern summer. Places between the rainforest and the desert therefore get a **wet season** when the ITCZ arrives and a **dry season** when the subtropical high returns. This explains the savanna's seasons and contributes to monsoon rains in South Asia.

### Surface winds

Air at the surface flows from high pressure to low pressure, but the Earth's rotation deflects it (the **Coriolis effect**): to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern.
- **Trade winds** blow from the subtropical highs towards the equator, from the north-east in the northern hemisphere and the south-east in the southern. They converge at the ITCZ.
- **Westerlies** blow from the subtropical highs towards 60°. They bring mild, moist air from the Atlantic to the UK.
- **Polar easterlies** blow out from the polar highs.

### Ocean currents

Ocean currents move about as much heat as the atmosphere does.
- **Surface currents** are driven mainly by the prevailing winds. Warm currents carry tropical water polewards; cold currents carry polar water towards the equator.
- The **Gulf Stream** carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico across the Atlantic, continuing as the **North Atlantic Drift**. It keeps UK and Norwegian winters much milder than places at the same latitude in Canada, and keeps many western ports ice-free.
- **Deep ocean (thermohaline) circulation** is driven by differences in density. In the North Atlantic, cold, salty water is dense and sinks, then flows slowly around the world's oceans before rising again elsewhere. This "global conveyor belt" links all the oceans and moves heat over hundreds of years.
- Cold currents such as the **Humboldt (Peru) Current** and the **Benguela Current** cool the air above them, so it cannot hold much moisture; that helps create coastal deserts such as the Atacama and the Namib.

### Skill: climate graphs

A **climate graph** shows mean monthly temperature as a **line** (left axis, °C) and mean monthly rainfall as **bars** (right axis, mm). Pearson asks you to:
- **identify** the wettest or warmest month, or read a value;
- **calculate** the annual temperature range (highest monthly mean minus lowest) or total annual rainfall (add the twelve bars);
- **compare** two graphs, using figures from both.

An equatorial graph has a flat temperature line around 26–28 °C (a range of only a degree or two) and tall bars every month. A hot desert graph has a bigger range, very hot summers and bars that are tiny or missing. In both, say the pattern and **quote numbers with units**.

::: terms
- **Global atmospheric circulation**: the worldwide system of winds and pressure belts that moves heat from the equator towards the poles.
- **Hadley cell**: the circulation cell between the equator and about 30° where air rises at the equator and sinks in the subtropics.
- **Ferrel cell**: the middle circulation cell between about 30° and 60°, driven by the Hadley and Polar cells on either side.
- **Polar cell**: the circulation cell from about 60° to the pole, where cold dense air sinks over the pole.
- **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)**: the belt of low pressure near the equator where the trade winds meet and air rises, giving heavy rain.
- **Subtropical high**: the belt of high pressure at about 30° north and south where air sinks, giving the world's hot deserts.
- **Trade winds**: steady surface winds blowing from the subtropical highs towards the equator.
- **Coriolis effect**: the deflection of moving air and water caused by the Earth's rotation, to the right in the northern hemisphere.
- **Ocean current**: a large-scale, continuous movement of seawater that transfers heat around the globe.
- **North Atlantic Drift**: the warm continuation of the Gulf Stream that keeps winters in the UK mild for its latitude.
:::

::: method
1. **Find the air movement first.** For any place, ask: is air rising or sinking here? Rising gives low pressure and rain; sinking gives high pressure and dry weather.
2. **Write the full chain** in explain questions: "air rises, expands and cools, so water vapour condenses, forming clouds and rain". The chain is the development mark.
3. **Use latitudes.** Put numbers in: "at about 30° N the Hadley cell's air sinks…". Specific beats vague.
4. **Climate graphs:** state the pattern, quote two or three values with units, and do any arithmetic showing working (for a range, highest minus lowest).
5. **Compare questions** need both places in every sentence: "Manaus is wetter than In Salah in every month, with…".
6. **8-mark assess:** explain the circulation (AO2), then weigh it against local factors such as ocean currents, relief and distance from the sea (AO3), and finish with a clear judgement about how far circulation explains the pattern.
:::

::: example Explain one: deserts at 30°
Explain one reason why many hot deserts are found at about 30° north and south of the equator. (2)

At about 30° the air in the Hadley cell sinks, creating high pressure (1). Sinking air warms, so it can hold its moisture and clouds do not form, meaning there is very little rainfall (1).
:::

::: example Climate graph calculation
Study the sample data for an equatorial station: highest mean monthly temperature 28 °C (October), lowest 26 °C (January). Monthly rainfall is between 150 mm and 300 mm in every month. Calculate the annual temperature range, and describe the rainfall pattern. (3)

Range = 28 − 26 = 2 °C (1). Rainfall is high in every month, at least 150 mm (1), so there is no real dry season, which fits rising air at the ITCZ all year (1).
:::

::: example Explain one way: ocean currents
Explain one way ocean currents redistribute heat energy around the Earth. (3)

Warm surface currents such as the Gulf Stream carry warm water from the tropics towards the poles (1). As the North Atlantic Drift reaches north-west Europe it releases heat to the air above (1), so the UK has milder winters than places at the same latitude such as eastern Canada (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: circulation and biomes
Assess the importance of global atmospheric circulation in explaining the location of the world's hot deserts and tropical rainforests. (8)

Atmospheric circulation is the main reason for both patterns. At the equator intense heating makes air rise at the ITCZ; it cools and condenses, giving heavy convectional rain almost every day, which is why the Amazon and Congo rainforests sit within a few degrees of the equator. The same air flows polewards in the Hadley cell and sinks at about 30°, warming as it descends, so skies are clear and rainfall is very low. The Sahara, Arabian and Australian deserts all lie close to this belt.

However, circulation does not explain everything. Cold ocean currents such as the Humboldt Current cool the air over the coast of Chile, so it holds little moisture and the Atacama is one of the driest places on Earth; that is more extreme than its latitude alone would cause. Distance from the sea makes continental interiors drier, and mountains create rain shadows. Seasonal movement of the ITCZ means places between the two belts are rainforest or savanna depending on how long the rains last.

Overall, circulation is the most important factor because it sets the global belts: almost every hot desert is near 30° and every rainforest near the equator. Local factors explain the exceptions and the extremes rather than the overall pattern.

(Level 3: accurate chain of processes for both biomes, weighed against local factors, with a supported judgement.)
:::

Describe how the ITCZ moves during the year. (2)
---

::: q
Explain two ways heat is transferred from the equator towards the poles. (4)
---
Atmospheric circulation cells (1): warm air rises at the equator and flows polewards at high level before sinking (1). Warm ocean currents (1): e.g. the Gulf Stream carries tropical water to the North Atlantic, releasing heat (1). Thermohaline circulation (1): dense, cold, salty water sinks near the poles and drives a global conveyor of water and heat (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why the UK receives frequent rainfall. (3)
---
The UK lies near 60° N, where warm westerlies meet cold polar air at the polar front (1). The warm air is forced to rise, giving low pressure and depressions (1). The prevailing westerlies also cross the Atlantic, picking up moisture that falls as rain (1).
:::

::: watch
- Writing "high pressure is hot and dry" without the mechanism. You need **sinking air warms, so no condensation**.
- Confusing the cells: Hadley 0–30°, Ferrel 30–60°, Polar 60–90°. Air **rises** at 0° and 60° and **sinks** at 30° and 90°.
- Calling the Coriolis effect a wind. It is a deflection caused by rotation.
- On climate graphs, reading the rainfall bar against the temperature axis. Check which axis belongs to which variable, and always give units.
- In compare questions, describing one graph then the other. Each sentence must refer to both.
- In 8-markers, explaining circulation well but never weighing it against ocean currents, relief or continentality, which caps the answer at Level 2.
:::

Daily range (1): the desert's clear skies let heat escape at night, so nights are cold (1).
