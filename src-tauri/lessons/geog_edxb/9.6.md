## What it is

This key idea ends **Topic 9, Consuming energy resources**, and leads straight into the most valuable question on **Paper 3**: the **Section D decision**, a **12-mark select-and-justify** question plus **4 SPaG** marks (16 of the paper's 64). Section C also asks resource-based questions on this content, including **8-mark** questions. The key idea says that **attitudes to energy and environmental issues are changing**. It covers:
- how different groups (**consumers, TNCs, governments, climate scientists and environmental groups**) hold **contrasting views about energy futures**, from **business as usual** to **sustainable**;
- how, in some **developed countries**, **rising affluence**, **environmental concern** and **education** are changing attitudes to unsustainable energy use and **reducing carbon footprints**;
- the skill of **calculating carbon and ecological footprints**.

This lesson also teaches the **Section D method**, with a full model answer.

Any real examples of the right type earn the marks. This course uses the Paris Agreement (2015), the IPCC, the UK's net zero target, oil TNCs such as Shell, BP and ExxonMobil, Ørsted in Denmark, Greenpeace and Friends of the Earth, Germany's **Energiewende**, Norway's switch to electric cars and Denmark's wind power.

### Two energy futures

| | **Business as usual** | **Sustainable energy future** |
|---|---|---|
| **Idea** | carry on as now: demand grows and is met mainly by fossil fuels | meet needs today without harming future generations |
| **Energy sources** | coal, oil and gas, with new frontiers (Arctic, tar sands, shale) | renewables, some nuclear, storage, possibly green hydrogen |
| **Demand** | keeps rising with population and wealth | reduced by efficiency and conservation |
| **Emissions** | keep rising; climate change worsens | fall towards **net zero** |
| **Short-term costs** | lower, using existing infrastructure | high investment in new technology and grids |
| **Long-term risks** | extreme weather, sea-level rise, price shocks, fuels running out | stranded fossil-fuel jobs and assets; intermittency to manage |

### Who thinks what: stakeholder views

| Group | Typical view | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumers** | want energy that is **cheap, reliable and convenient**; some will pay more for green energy, many cannot | household budgets; fuel poverty; habits such as driving; views vary with income, age and education |
| **TNCs (oil and gas)** such as ExxonMobil, Shell and BP | largely **business as usual**: keep investing in new oil and gas because demand remains; some invest in renewables, though several have scaled back earlier green targets | profits and shareholders depend on fossil fuels; they own reserves and infrastructure |
| **TNCs (renewables and technology)** such as Ørsted (Denmark), which changed from oil and gas into a leading offshore wind company | **sustainable future** as a business opportunity | profits from wind, solar, batteries and electric vehicles |
| **Governments** | must balance **energy security, cost, jobs and emissions**; most signed the **Paris Agreement** (2015) to keep warming well below 2 °C; the UK has a legal target of **net zero by 2050** | voters want low bills and jobs; exporters such as Saudi Arabia or Russia rely on oil income; emerging countries argue they have a right to develop as richer countries did |
| **Climate scientists**, e.g. through the **IPCC** | urgent cuts in emissions to reach net zero around mid-century | evidence that burning fossil fuels is warming the climate, with rising risks of extreme weather and sea-level rise |
| **Environmental groups** such as Greenpeace, Friends of the Earth and WWF | **stop new fossil fuel projects**, expand renewables fast; protect sensitive areas such as the Arctic | protect ecosystems and future generations; some use protest and direct action |

**Exam tip:** a stakeholder answer needs **who**, **what they want** and **why**. "Oil companies want more oil" is one mark; "because their profits and shareholders depend on selling it, and they already own the reserves" earns the second.

### Changing attitudes in developed countries

In some developed countries, attitudes to unsustainable energy use are shifting.

| Driver | How it changes attitudes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Rising affluence** | richer people can afford electric cars, solar panels, heat pumps and insulation, and value quality of life and the environment once basic needs are met | **Norway**: most new cars sold are now electric, helped by tax breaks |
| **Environmental concern** | visible extreme weather, media coverage and campaigns make climate change feel urgent | youth climate strikes from 2018; public support for renewables |
| **Education** | people learn about climate change at school and through labels, apps and smart meters that show their footprint | energy labels on appliances; carbon labels on some products |
| **Government policy** (often driven by these attitudes) | targets, taxes and subsidies make sustainable choices cheaper | **Germany's Energiewende** (energy transition): large growth in wind and solar, and its last nuclear reactors closed in 2023; **Denmark** gets around half its electricity from wind |

**But it is not simple.** Affluence also **raises** footprints (bigger homes, more flights, more goods). There is an **attitude-behaviour gap**: people say they care but still drive and fly. And much of a developed country's footprint is **embedded** in imported goods made elsewhere.

### Calculating carbon and ecological footprints

**Carbon footprint** = the total greenhouse gases released by a person, household, product or country, in **tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (CO₂e)** per year.

How to calculate it: multiply each activity by its **emission factor**, then add them up.

| Activity (sample data) | Amount | Emission factor | CO₂e |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electricity | 3,000 kWh | 0.2 kg per kWh | 600 kg |
| Gas heating | 12,000 kWh | 0.2 kg per kWh | 2,400 kg |
| Car | 10,000 km | 0.17 kg per km | 1,700 kg |
| Flights | 2 return flights | 500 kg each | 1,000 kg |
| **Total** | | | **5,700 kg = 5.7 tonnes** |

**Ecological footprint** = the area of productive land and sea needed to provide everything a person uses and absorb their waste, in **global hectares (gha)** per person. It is compared with **biocapacity**, the area actually available per person.
- **Number of Earths needed** = average footprint ÷ biocapacity per person.
- Sample data: if a country's average footprint is 4.8 gha per person and world biocapacity is 1.6 gha per person, then 4.8 ÷ 1.6 = **3 Earths** would be needed if everyone lived like that.
- Humanity as a whole uses well over one and a half Earths' worth of resources each year, and developed countries' footprints are several times those of the poorest countries.

### The Section D decision: how to answer the 12-mark question

**What you get:** a resource booklet about a named country or region, three **options**, and the instruction to **select one option and justify your choice**, using the booklet and knowledge from the rest of your course. The marks are split evenly:
- **AO2 (4):** understanding of geographical ideas (energy, ecosystems, development).
- **AO3 (4):** applying that understanding to judge the options: weighing, linking, concluding.
- **AO4 (4):** **using the resources**: selecting and quoting evidence from the booklet.

Levels: **1–4** (isolated, little evidence), **5–8** (some justification, other options considered unevenly), **9–12** (justified throughout, both alternatives weighed, a substantiated conclusion). **SPaG** adds up to 4 marks for accurate writing and specialist terms.

**Method**
1. **Read the booklet** with physical and human geography in mind: environment, economy, society, short and long term.
2. **Choose one option.** There is no right answer; the marks are for the justification. Choose the one you can argue best.
3. **Justify it with evidence from at least three resources** (quote figures, map features, stakeholder views) and **your own knowledge** (case studies, concepts such as energy security or carbon footprint).
4. **Weigh impacts on people and the environment, in the short and long term.**
5. **Explain why each of the other two options is weaker.** This is what lifts answers into Level 3.
6. **Acknowledge a drawback** of your choice and say **how it could be reduced**.
7. **Conclude** with a clear final judgement linked to the question's aim (e.g. "benefit both the environment and the economy").

**Timing:** in a 90-minute paper, spend about 25 minutes on Section D, including 3–4 minutes planning. Write in paragraphs with specialist terms for the SPaG marks.

::: terms
- **Business as usual**: an energy future in which current trends continue, with rising demand met mainly by fossil fuels.
- **Sustainable energy future**: meeting today's energy needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Stakeholder**: a person or group with an interest in, or affected by, a decision.
- **Carbon footprint**: the total greenhouse gases released by a person, product or activity, measured in tonnes of CO₂ equivalent.
- **Ecological footprint**: the area of productive land and sea needed to supply a person's resources and absorb their waste, in global hectares.
- **Biocapacity**: the area of productive land and sea actually available to supply resources and absorb waste.
- **Emission factor**: the amount of greenhouse gas released per unit of an activity, such as kg of CO₂ per kWh.
- **Net zero**: when the greenhouse gases a country releases are balanced by those it removes from the atmosphere.
- **Paris Agreement**: the 2015 international climate deal to keep global warming well below 2 °C and aim for 1.5 °C.
- **IPCC**: the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, the UN body that assesses climate science.
- **Energiewende**: Germany's energy transition from fossil fuels and nuclear towards renewable energy.
- **Attitude-behaviour gap**: the difference between what people say they care about and what they actually do.
:::

::: method
1. **Stakeholder questions:** name the group, give its view on energy futures, then explain **why** it holds that view (profit, cost, jobs, evidence, values).
2. **Attitude questions:** link the driver (affluence, concern, education) to a specific change in behaviour, with a named developed country.
3. **Footprint calculations:** multiply each activity by its emission factor, add, convert kg to tonnes (÷ 1,000); for Earths, divide footprint by biocapacity. Show working.
4. **8-mark assess:** use the resource (AO4), weigh several reasons or views (AO3), conclude which matters most.
5. **12-mark decision:** read the booklet; choose; justify with at least three resources and own knowledge; weigh people and environment, short and long term; reject the other two options with reasons; admit a drawback and how to reduce it; conclude.
:::

::: example Calculate: carbon footprint
Sample data: a household uses 4,000 kWh of electricity (0.2 kg CO₂e per kWh) and drives 8,000 km (0.15 kg CO₂e per km). Calculate its carbon footprint for these two activities, in tonnes. (2)

(4,000 × 0.2) + (8,000 × 0.15) = 800 + 1,200 = 2,000 kg (1) = 2.0 tonnes CO₂e (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: a TNC's view
Explain one reason why many oil TNCs favour a business-as-usual energy future. (2)

Their profits and share prices depend on selling oil and gas, and they already own reserves and infrastructure worth billions (1), so a rapid move to renewables would leave these assets stranded and cut their income (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: changing attitudes
Explain two reasons why attitudes to energy use are changing in some developed countries. (4)

Rising affluence means more people can afford electric cars, solar panels and insulation (1), as in Norway where most new cars sold are electric (1). Education and media coverage have increased awareness of climate change (1), so more people are willing to change behaviour and support renewable energy policies (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: footprints in developed countries
Study a resource showing a pie chart of an average UK person's carbon footprint (home heating, transport, flights, food and goods) and a quote from a commuter in a rural village who says there is no bus and she has to drive. Assess why it might be difficult to reduce the carbon footprint of people in developed countries. (8)

The pie chart shows transport and home heating are the largest parts of the footprint. These are hard to cut because they are built into infrastructure: many homes are heated by gas boilers that are expensive to replace, and the commuter's quote shows that in rural areas poor public transport makes car use unavoidable.

Lifestyle is also a barrier. Affluent people take more flights and buy more goods, and the chart shows food and goods are a large share. Much of this is embedded in imports made abroad, so it is outside the person's direct control. There is often an attitude-behaviour gap: people say they care about climate change but keep flying.

Cost matters too: heat pumps and electric cars have high upfront prices, which lower-income households cannot afford, while efficiency savings can be spent on extra use.

Overall, infrastructure is the biggest barrier, because even willing people cannot cut their footprint without alternatives such as buses or affordable heat pumps; lifestyle and cost add to it, which is why government investment is essential.

(Level 3: uses the chart and quote (AO4), weighs infrastructure, lifestyle and cost, and reaches a supported judgement (AO3).)
:::

::: example 12-mark decision: model answer
Study a resource booklet about Indonesia (sample data). Indonesia is an emerging country of more than 270 million people. Figure 1: electricity demand is forecast to double by 2040. Figure 2: about 60% of its electricity comes from coal, which it mines in Kalimantan and exports. Figure 3: a map showing high solar potential across the islands and large unused HEP potential on rainforest rivers in Kalimantan and Papua. Figure 4: a quote from a Dayak community leader opposing a dam that would flood their forest; a quote from a city resident complaining of power cuts and air pollution.

Option 1: Build new coal-fired power stations near the coal mines.
Option 2: Build large HEP dams on rainforest rivers in Kalimantan and Papua.
Option 3: Invest in solar farms, rooftop solar and energy efficiency.

Select the option that you think Indonesia should choose to meet its energy needs while protecting the environment. Justify your choice. (12)

I would choose Option 3. Figure 3 shows high solar potential across the islands because Indonesia lies on the Equator, where sunshine is strong all year. Solar is a renewable flow, so unlike coal it will not run out and releases almost no carbon while running, which would cut Indonesia's carbon footprint and the air pollution the city resident complains about in Figure 4. Rooftop panels use no extra land, so rainforest is not cleared, and they can be installed quickly on thousands of islands that are expensive to link to one grid.

Energy efficiency is vital because Figure 1 shows demand doubling by 2040. Efficient air-conditioning, lighting and building standards would slow that growth, so fewer new power stations are needed and the power cuts in Figure 4 would ease.

Option 1 is weaker. Coal is cheap and Indonesia has its own supply, but Figure 2 shows it already relies on coal for about 60% of its electricity, so more would lock in high emissions for decades. Coal releases more CO₂ than any other fuel, worsens city air pollution and open-cast mining scars Kalimantan's forests. It also leaves Indonesia exposed if coal is phased out by trading partners.

Option 2 is also weaker. HEP is reliable and low-carbon, but Figure 4 shows the Dayak community would lose its forest. Like the Three Gorges Dam in China, large reservoirs flood valleys, displace people and destroy rainforest biodiversity. Rotting vegetation can release methane, and the dams would be far from the cities that need power.

Option 3 has a drawback: solar is intermittent, producing nothing at night and less in cloudy wet seasons. This could be reduced with batteries, by keeping some existing gas or geothermal power as back-up, since Indonesia lies on the Ring of Fire, and by phasing out coal gradually rather than at once.

Overall, Option 3 best meets Indonesia's growing needs while protecting its rainforests and people's health, because it uses a resource Indonesia has everywhere and avoids the long-term costs of both coal and large dams.

(Level 3: evidence from all four figures (AO4); understanding of renewables, emissions, HEP impacts and energy security (AO2); both rejected options weighed, a drawback reduced and a clear conclusion (AO3). Specialist terms used throughout for SPaG.)
:::

Richer people can afford more flights, bigger homes and more consumer goods (1); each of these uses more energy, much of it from fossil fuels (1); so even if they buy some green products, their total emissions often rise, especially from aviation (1).

::: q
Explain two contrasting views about energy futures held by different stakeholders. (4)
---
Two from: oil TNCs favour business as usual (1), because their profits depend on fossil fuels and they own reserves and infrastructure (1); environmental groups such as Greenpeace want no new fossil fuel projects (1), to protect ecosystems and limit climate change (1); consumers mainly want cheap, reliable energy (1), so they may resist higher bills even if they support green energy (1); renewable companies such as Ørsted favour a sustainable future (1), because wind and solar are their business (1).
:::

::: watch
- Stakeholder answers that say **what** a group wants but not **why**.
- Footprint calculations without units, or forgetting to convert **kg to tonnes**.
- In the 12-marker, only praising your option. Level 3 needs **both** other options rejected with reasons.
- Ignoring the booklet in Section D: AO4 is a third of the marks. Quote at least three resources.
- Choosing an option and then arguing for a different one. Stick to your choice; a drawback and its fix shows balance.
- Assuming attitudes have changed everywhere. The spec says **some developed countries**, and the attitude-behaviour gap remains.
- Losing SPaG marks through rushed writing. Use paragraphs and terms such as energy security, carbon footprint, intermittent and renewable flow.
:::
