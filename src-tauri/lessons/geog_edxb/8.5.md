## What it is

This key idea says **conservation and sustainable management** of tropical rainforests are vital if their **goods and services** are not to be lost for future generations. It covers:
- the **advantages and disadvantages** of **global actions** to protect rainforest species and areas: **CITES** and **REDD**;
- why **deforestation rates** are **rising in some areas but falling in others**;
- the **challenge** of achieving **sustainable forest management**;
- why **alternative livelihoods** (**ecotourism**, **sustainable farming**) might better protect the remaining forest.

Any real examples earn the marks. This course uses **Brazil** and the **Juma Sustainable Development Reserve** in Amazonas state, with comparisons from Indonesia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Costa Rica. Questions here usually ask you to **evaluate** or give **advantages and disadvantages**, so learn both sides of every strategy.

**Why protect it?** The rainforest provides **goods** (timber, fruit, nuts, rubber, medicines) and **services** (a vast **carbon store**, rainfall recycled by **transpiration**, soil protection, habitat for a large share of the world's species), and is home to many indigenous peoples. **Sustainable** use keeps these for future generations.

### Global action 1: CITES

The **Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora** came into force in **1975**. More than 180 countries have signed it. It controls **international trade** in threatened plants and animals, alive or as products (timber, skins, pets).

| Appendix | What it means | Rainforest examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Appendix I** | species threatened with extinction: **commercial trade banned** | great apes such as orangutans; jaguars |
| **Appendix II** | not yet threatened, but trade must be **controlled with permits** so it stays sustainable | **big-leaf mahogany** (listed in the early 2000s); many parrots and orchids |
| **Appendix III** | protected in at least one country, which asks others to help control trade | varies by country |

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| almost every country is a member, so it is **global** | it controls **trade only**: it does nothing to stop clearing for cattle, soya or mining, the main causes of forest loss |
| legally binding on members, with a permit system that tracks legal trade | it depends on each country to **enforce** it; poorer countries lack customs officers and funding |
| has reduced the legal trade in rare timber and animals, and raised awareness | **illegal wildlife trafficking** continues on a large scale; penalties are often small |
| listing mahogany pushed buyers towards legal, certified timber | banning trade can raise **black-market prices**, encouraging poaching |

### Global action 2: REDD and REDD+

**REDD** stands for **Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and forest Degradation**. The UN launched its **UN-REDD Programme in 2008**. **REDD+** extends it to reward conservation, sustainable management and increasing forest carbon stocks.

**How it works:** developing countries (or projects within them) are **paid to keep forests standing**, because standing forest stores carbon. The money can come from rich-country governments, international funds, or companies buying **carbon credits** to offset their own emissions.

**Example:** Brazil's **Amazon Fund** (2008) receives donations, mainly from **Norway** and Germany, that are linked to reduced deforestation. Donations were frozen for several years when clearing rose, then resumed in 2023.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| gives standing forest an **economic value**, so it is worth more alive than cleared | hard to **measure** how much carbon has really been saved compared with what would have happened |
| money can fund local communities, schools, health care and satellite monitoring | **leakage**: protecting one area can push clearing to another |
| reduces carbon emissions and helps tackle climate change | money may not reach local people, and **corruption** can divert it |
| involves developing countries in climate action on fair terms | indigenous and local people can lose access to land they have always used |
| | companies can buy credits instead of cutting their own emissions; some credits have been shown to be **overstated** |
| | protection may not be **permanent**: a protected forest can still burn or be cleared later |

### Case study: the Juma Sustainable Development Reserve

- A reserve in **Amazonas** state, Brazil, created in **2006** in an area at risk from a planned road.
- It became one of **Brazil's first certified REDD projects**, run with a Brazilian non-profit foundation and funded partly by private sponsors.
- Through the **Bolsa Floresta** (Forest Allowance) programme, families receive a **small monthly payment** if they agree not to clear primary forest, alongside money for community associations, schools, health and sustainable businesses such as Brazil-nut collection.
- **Advantages:** local people gain income and services for protecting the forest; community monitoring deters illegal clearing.
- **Disadvantages:** payments are small; the reserve depends on continued outside funding; it protects only a small area compared with the scale of the Amazon.

### Why deforestation rates rise in some places and fall in others

**Brazil: rates have gone up and down with policy**
- Deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon **peaked in the mid-2000s**.
- It then **fell sharply** (by roughly three-quarters by the early 2010s) because of:
  - near real-time **satellite alerts** guiding enforcement officers;
  - new **protected areas** and **indigenous territories**;
  - the **Soy Moratorium** (2006), when traders agreed not to buy soya grown on newly cleared Amazon land;
  - restrictions on bank loans and blacklisting of municipalities with high clearing.
- It **rose again** in the late 2010s and early 2020s as enforcement budgets were cut, penalties were less often applied and political signals encouraged clearing, while beef and soya prices were high.
- It **fell again from 2023** after enforcement was strengthened.

**Elsewhere**
- **Falling:** Indonesia's losses fell after the mid-2010s, helped by a moratorium on new plantation and peatland licences, fire prevention and palm oil companies' "no deforestation" pledges.
- **Rising:** the **Democratic Republic of the Congo** has high and persistent losses, driven by fast population growth, poverty, small-scale farming and fuelwood demand, with weak governance and conflict making protection hard.

**General factors**

| Rates fall when... | Rates rise when... |
|---|---|
| governments enforce laws and monitor by satellite | enforcement is weak, underfunded or corrupt |
| land rights of indigenous peoples are secure | land ownership is unclear, so land-grabbing pays |
| buyers demand deforestation-free products | global prices for beef, soya, palm oil or minerals are high |
| international funding rewards protection | populations are growing fast and people are poor |
| alternative incomes are available | new roads open up remote forest |

### The challenge of sustainable forest management

**Sustainable forest management** means using the forest for timber and other products without reducing its future value or its biodiversity. It is hard because:
- **Scale and remoteness:** the Amazon is vast, so monitoring and enforcement are expensive.
- **Poverty:** people need income now, and clearing land is often the quickest way to earn it.
- **Short-term profit:** cattle, soya and minerals pay more, sooner, than sustainable use.
- **Weak governance and corruption** allow illegal logging and land-grabbing.
- **Global demand** from distant consumers drives clearing that local governments cannot control.
- **Certification** such as the **FSC** (Forest Stewardship Council) label, selective logging and replanting help, but certification costs money and covers only a small share of tropical timber.

### Alternative livelihoods

These give local people an income from the **standing forest**, so they have a reason to protect it.

**Ecotourism**
- Small-scale tourism that conserves the environment and benefits local people: forest lodges, canopy walkways, guided wildlife trips.
- **Costa Rica** protects a large share of its land in national parks and reserves, and tourism to its forests is a major source of income.
- Lodges in the Amazon, such as those near Manaus, employ local guides and cooks.
- **Advantages:** income depends on the forest being intact; jobs for local people; funds conservation; raises awareness.
- **Disadvantages:** limited jobs and visitor numbers; profits can leak to foreign tour firms; flights cause emissions; remote areas are hard to reach; income falls if tourism collapses.

**Sustainable farming**
- **Agroforestry:** growing crops such as cocoa or coffee under shade trees, keeping much of the forest structure.
- **Extractive reserves:** areas where people harvest rubber, Brazil nuts and fruit without felling trees, such as the **Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve** in Acre.
- **Better use of cleared land:** improving soils and grazing on existing pasture so that less new forest is cleared.
- **Advantages:** long-term income; soils protected; keeps people on the land without clearing.
- **Disadvantages:** lower or slower profits than cattle or soya; needs training, loans and access to markets.

**Why they may work better:** strict protection that excludes local people often fails, because people have no stake in the forest. Alternative livelihoods turn local people into the forest's **guardians**, which is cheaper and more lasting than policing. But they only work if outside pressures (roads, global demand) are also controlled.

::: terms
- **CITES**: the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, a 1975 treaty that controls trade in threatened plants and animals.
- **REDD**: Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and forest Degradation, a UN-backed scheme paying countries to keep forests standing.
- **Carbon credit**: a tradeable certificate representing carbon kept out of the atmosphere, which polluters can buy to offset emissions.
- **Leakage**: when protecting one area simply pushes deforestation to another area.
- **Sustainable forest management**: using forests to meet present needs without reducing their value and biodiversity for the future.
- **Ecotourism**: small-scale tourism that conserves the environment and benefits local communities.
- **Agroforestry**: growing crops among trees, keeping forest cover while producing food or cash crops.
- **Extractive reserve**: a protected area where local people may harvest forest products such as rubber and nuts without felling trees.
- **Alternative livelihood**: a way of earning a living that does not depend on clearing forest.
- **Soy Moratorium**: a 2006 agreement by traders not to buy soya grown on newly deforested Amazon land.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the action precisely**: CITES controls **trade** in species; REDD **pays** to keep forests standing. Neither "bans logging".
2. **Give both sides**: for every strategy learn at least two advantages and two disadvantages, each developed.
3. **Use examples**: big-leaf mahogany (CITES), the Amazon Fund and Juma (REDD), Costa Rica (ecotourism), Chico Mendes (extractive reserves).
4. **Rates questions**: explain with factors (enforcement, prices, land rights, population, roads) and a named place.
5. **8-mark evaluate**: judge how effective, for whom and at what scale; use the resource; reach a conclusion.
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: CITES and REDD
Study a resource showing a timeline of Brazilian Amazon deforestation (high in the mid-2000s, falling, rising again around 2019–2022, then falling) and a description of a REDD project paying families a small monthly allowance. Evaluate the effectiveness of global actions such as CITES and REDD in protecting tropical rainforests. (8)

CITES controls legal trade in species such as big-leaf mahogany through permits, and almost every country is a member. But it only controls trade, while most forest is cleared for cattle, soya and mining, and it relies on national enforcement while trafficking continues.

REDD tackles the main cause more directly. The resource shows families paid an allowance for not clearing primary forest, and the Amazon Fund has financed monitoring. But payments are small, savings are hard to measure, clearing can leak elsewhere, and money depends on donors, whose Amazon Fund donations were frozen when clearing rose.

The timeline shows rates rising and falling with Brazil's own policies (satellite enforcement, the Soy Moratorium) more than with global agreements. Overall, CITES is effective only for traded species, and REDD is more relevant but limited in scale and reliability; global actions work best when they support strong national enforcement and local livelihoods.

(Level 3: resource used throughout (AO4); both actions judged for scale and limits; a supported conclusion (AO3).)
