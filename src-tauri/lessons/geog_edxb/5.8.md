## What it is

Rural areas look peaceful and wealthy, but many face hidden problems, and many are finding new ways to earn a living. This key idea asks you to explain:
- the **challenges** of **availability and affordability of housing**, the **decline in primary employment**, and the **provision of health care and education**, and how they affect **quality of life (measured by the IMD)** for some rural groups, especially **elderly** and **young** people;
- how **rural diversification** (**farm shops**, **accommodation**, **leisure activities**) and **tourism projects** create new income and economic opportunities, but may have **environmental impacts**.

Integrated skill: using **1:25 000 and 1:50 000 OS maps** to identify land-use types (skill 3). It is examined in **Paper 2, Section B**: short items on described maps, photos and data, "Explain one/two…", and possibly the 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question, which may carry **4 SPaG marks**. This course continues with **the Chilterns** (see 5.7), with examples from rural England in general. Any rural area you studied earns the marks.

### Challenge 1: housing availability and affordability

- **High prices**: in accessible areas like the Chilterns, demand from commuters, retirees and second-home buyers pushes prices to many times local earnings. Rural jobs (farming, care, hospitality, retail) are often low-paid, so local people cannot compete.
- **Limited supply**: protected landscapes, green belt and planning rules restrict new building; developers prefer large, expensive houses.
- **Little social housing**: much former council housing was sold through **Right to Buy**, and few replacement homes were built in villages.
- **Second homes and holiday lets** take houses out of the year-round market, especially in scenic areas.
- **Effects**: young people leave; local workers commute in from cheaper towns; some villages become places of older, wealthier residents.
- **Responses**: **rural exception sites** (small sites where affordable homes for local people are allowed); community land trusts; affordable housing quotas in new developments.

### Challenge 2: decline in primary employment

- **Mechanisation**: tractors, combines and milking machines mean far fewer workers per farm.
- **Farm consolidation**: small farms merge into larger, more efficient units.
- **Low prices**: global competition and the buying power of supermarkets keep prices paid to farmers low, especially for milk and meat.
- **Changing subsidies**: after Brexit, the EU-era payments based on the area farmed are being phased out in England and replaced by payments for **environmental work**, which some farmers find hard to adjust to.
- **Effects**: fewer and lower-paid local jobs; young people leave for towns; farms seek **other income** (diversification).

### Challenge 3: health care and education

**Health care**
- GP surgeries, pharmacies and dentists are spread thinly; some village surgeries have merged into larger practices in towns.
- **Hospitals** may be 30–60 minutes away; ambulance response times are longer than in cities.
- Demand is rising because rural populations are older.

**Education**
- Small **village primary schools** can close or merge when pupil numbers fall, as young families cannot afford to live there.
- Pupils travel long distances to **secondary schools**, colleges and sixth forms; rural bus services are infrequent.
- Patchy **broadband** in remote areas makes homework and online learning harder.

### Effects on quality of life: elderly and young people

The **Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD)** ranks small areas in England by seven domains (see 5.4). Rural areas usually score **well** on income, crime and employment overall, but often **badly** on **barriers to housing and services**, which includes the distance to a GP, school, supermarket and post office.

**Rural deprivation is often hidden**: wealthy households and poorer households live in the same village, so the area's **average** looks comfortable even when some people struggle.

| Group | Challenges | Effect on quality of life |
|---|---|---|
| **Elderly people** | no car or unable to drive; few buses; GP and hospital far away; village shop and post office closed; old, hard-to-heat homes off the gas grid | **isolation and loneliness**; difficulty reaching health care; **fuel poverty**; dependence on family or volunteers |
| **Young people** | unaffordable housing; few well-paid jobs; long journeys to college; little to do; poor transport at night | many leave for towns and cities (**out-migration**); those who stay may face low pay or long commutes; villages lose their next generation |

### Opportunities: rural diversification

**Rural diversification** means farms and rural businesses developing **new, non-farming sources of income** alongside or instead of agriculture.

| Type | Examples | Benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Farm shops and food** | farm shops, butchers, cafés, pick-your-own, farmers' markets, ice cream, cheese | higher prices for produce sold direct; local jobs; fewer food miles | competition with village shops; more traffic |
| **Accommodation** | holiday cottages in converted barns, B&B, glamping pods, shepherd's huts, campsites | year-round income from tourists; reuses old buildings | homes lost to holiday lets; visitors' cars; light and noise |
| **Leisure activities** | farm parks, horse riding, fishing lakes, clay shooting, wedding venues, mountain bike trails | jobs; attracts visitors who spend money locally | noise, traffic, loss of farmland, disturbance to wildlife |
| **Energy and new crops** | solar farms, small wind turbines, biomass, vineyards on chalk slopes | steady income; renewable energy; new products | change to the landscape; land taken from food production |
| **Film and events** | renting farms and villages for filming; festivals | one-off income; publicity that attracts visitors | short-term crowds and traffic |

In the Chilterns, examples include farm shops and cafés near main roads, converted barns let as holiday cottages and offices, **vineyards** on the chalk slopes, wedding and event venues, and film locations in villages.

### Tourism projects

- **Walking and cycling routes**: the **Ridgeway** National Trail and long-distance cycle routes around the Chilterns bring visitors who stay overnight and eat locally.
- **Visitor attractions**: country parks, woodland activity centres, the red kite, heritage railways and museums.
- **Benefits**: income for pubs, cafés, accommodation and shops; jobs for young people; money for conservation; services such as pubs and shops stay open because visitors use them too.
- **Costs**: many jobs are **seasonal and low-paid**; visitors come mainly by **car**.

### Environmental impacts of diversification and tourism

**Negative**
- **Traffic, congestion and parking** on narrow lanes; air pollution and noise.
- **Footpath erosion** and damage to verges at popular sites.
- **Light pollution** from new buildings and events in dark countryside.
- **Loss of farmland** and wildlife habitat to car parks, solar farms and buildings; litter; disturbance to birds and livestock.
- **Visual impact** of new structures in a protected landscape.

**Positive**
- Income can pay for **conservation**: hedgerow restoration, woodland management, wildflower margins.
- Reusing **old farm buildings** instead of building new ones.
- **Renewable energy** cuts carbon emissions.
- Farm shops reduce **food miles**.

Good management (planning conditions, visitor car parks with park-and-ride or public transport links, path maintenance, keeping to existing buildings) can limit the damage.

### Skill: OS maps and rural land use

On a **1:25 000** or **1:50 000** OS map, rural land uses include:
- **woodland** (symbols for coniferous, non-coniferous and mixed trees), **orchards**, and open farmland (white areas crossed by field boundaries on 1:25 000 maps);
- **farm buildings** (small black squares, often named "Farm");
- tourism and leisure symbols: **campsites**, **caravan sites**, **picnic sites**, **viewpoints**, **parking**, **visitor centres**, **public houses (PH)**, **golf courses**;
- **National Trails** and long-distance routes, footpaths and bridleways.
Use **six-figure grid references** to locate features and the **scale** to measure distances, e.g. how far a village is from the nearest GP or school.

::: terms
- **Rural diversification**: farms and rural businesses developing new, non-farming sources of income.
- **Affordable housing**: homes for rent or sale below market prices for people on lower incomes.
- **Rural exception site**: a small site where affordable homes for local people are allowed where normal housing would not be.
- **Second home**: a property owned in addition to a main home, used for holidays or weekends.
- **Mechanisation**: the replacement of human and animal labour by machines.
- **Farm consolidation**: small farms merging into fewer, larger farms.
- **Hidden deprivation**: deprivation that is not shown by area averages because poorer people live among wealthier neighbours.
- **Rural isolation**: being cut off from services and social contact because of distance and poor transport.
- **Fuel poverty**: when a household cannot afford to heat its home adequately.
- **Food miles**: the distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is eaten.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the challenge** asked about: housing, primary employment, health care, education.
2. **Explain the cause** (prices, planning, second homes, mechanisation, low farm prices, service closures, distance).
3. **Link to quality of life** for a **named group**: elderly (isolation, health access, fuel poverty) or young (housing, jobs, college, out-migration). Mention the IMD where useful.
4. **Diversification questions**: name the activity (farm shop, holiday cottage, glamping, farm park, vineyard, solar farm), say how it earns income, then give an environmental impact.
5. **Balance**: benefits for people and costs to the environment, or the reverse.
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: use the figure throughout, weigh benefits against costs for people and the environment, and reach a supported judgement.
:::

::: example Multiple choice: diversification
Which one of the following is an example of farm diversification? A: Buying a larger tractor. B: Converting a barn into holiday cottages. C: Growing more wheat. D: Selling milk to a supermarket. (1)

B (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: elderly people
Explain one way the decline of rural services affects elderly people. (2)

When village shops, post offices and bus services close (1), older people without cars find it hard to reach food, health care and friends, leading to isolation and loneliness (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: farm income share
Sample data: a farm earns £180,000 a year, of which £72,000 now comes from its farm shop and holiday cottages. Calculate the percentage of income from diversification. (2)

72,000 ÷ 180,000 × 100 (1) = 40% (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: why primary jobs have declined
Explain two reasons for the decline in primary employment in rural areas. (4)

Mechanisation means machines now do work that once needed many labourers (1), so farms employ far fewer people (1). Small farms have merged into larger units to cut costs (1), because low prices paid by supermarkets made small farms unprofitable, reducing jobs further (1).
:::

::: example Environmental impact of tourism
Explain one negative environmental impact of rural tourism. (2)

Most visitors arrive by car (1), causing congestion, parking on verges and air pollution on narrow country lanes (1).
:::

Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of converting farm buildings into holiday accommodation. (4)
---
Advantage (1): it brings in year-round income from tourists, reuses old buildings that are no longer needed and supports local pubs and shops (1). Disadvantage (1): visitors bring more traffic and noise, and properties used as holiday lets are not available as homes for local people (1).

::: watch
- Assuming all rural areas are wealthy. Explain **hidden deprivation**: averages hide poorer residents.
- Writing about "rural people" in general when the question names **elderly** or **young** people. Tailor each point to that group.
- Describing diversification without the **environmental impact** the spec asks for.
- Treating tourism as purely positive. Jobs are often **seasonal and low-paid**, and visitors bring traffic.
- Forgetting the cause of primary decline: **mechanisation**, **consolidation**, **low prices**, **subsidy changes**.
- Misreading the IMD: rural areas often score badly on **barriers to housing and services**, even when incomes are high.
- In the 8-marker, giving benefits only for people and costs only for the environment. Weigh both for both.
:::
