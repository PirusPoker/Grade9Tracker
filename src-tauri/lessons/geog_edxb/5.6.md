## What it is

This key idea is about **improving life in the city**. You need to explain:
- how **regeneration** and **rebranding** of parts of the city have had **positive and negative impacts on people**, in terms of **population increase**, **environmental quality** and **economic opportunities**;
- **strategies to make urban living more sustainable** and improve quality of life: **recycling**, **employment**, **green spaces**, **transport**, and **affordable, energy-efficient housing**.

Integrated skills: **OS maps** (skill 3) and **crime and IMD databases** to investigate the extent of inner-city problems (skill 4). It is examined in **Paper 2, Section B**, very often in the 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question carrying **4 SPaG marks**. This course uses **London**: the **Docklands**, the **Olympic Park at Stratford** and London-wide strategies. Any major UK city you studied earns the marks.

### Regeneration and rebranding

- **Regeneration** means improving a declining area through investment: new buildings, infrastructure, jobs and services, often on **brownfield** land.
- **Rebranding** means giving an area a new **image** so people see it differently and want to live, work, visit or invest there: new names, marketing, events and flagship buildings.
- They usually go together: a regenerated area is rebranded to attract investors and residents.

### Case study: London Docklands

**Before**: the docks closed between the 1960s and 1981, leaving about 20 km² of derelict land, high unemployment and a falling population in the East End (see 5.5).

**What was done**
- The government set up the **London Docklands Development Corporation (LDDC)** in **1981**. It ran until 1998, buying land, clearing contamination and using public money to attract much larger **private investment** (a **top-down**, property-led approach).
- The Isle of Dogs became an **Enterprise Zone**, with tax breaks for firms.
- **Canary Wharf** grew on the former West India Docks into a second financial district.
- **Transport**: the **Docklands Light Railway (DLR)** from 1987, **London City Airport**, and later the **Jubilee line extension** (1999) and the **Elizabeth line**.
- Thousands of new homes, the **ExCeL** exhibition centre, shops and leisure facilities.

**Impacts on people**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | the population grew strongly as new homes were built | many new homes were expensive private flats; long-standing working-class communities felt pushed out |
| **Environmental quality** | derelict, polluted land cleaned up; waterside walks; trees and open space | some areas dominated by high-rise towers; heavy traffic; early developments lacked services such as schools and health centres |
| **Economic opportunities** | tens of thousands of new office jobs; the area rebranded as a global business district | many jobs needed skills that former dock workers lacked; some locals got only low-paid cleaning and security work; nearby estates stayed deprived |

**Judgement**: Docklands was a huge economic success for London, but the benefits for the original local community were much more limited, which is why later schemes paid more attention to local people.

### Case study: the Olympic Park, Stratford

**Before**: the **Lower Lea Valley** in Newham and neighbouring boroughs was a mix of contaminated industrial land, waste sites, railway yards and polluted rivers, in one of the most deprived parts of London.

**What was done**
- The site was cleaned up and rebuilt for the **2012 Olympic and Paralympic Games**, then turned into the **Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park**, run by a legacy corporation.
- **Westfield Stratford City**, one of Europe's largest urban shopping centres, opened next to the park in 2011.
- The athletes' village became **East Village**, thousands of homes, some of them let at affordable rents; more neighbourhoods have since been built around the park.
- Venues were reused: the main stadium became West Ham United's home; the Aquatics Centre and velodrome are open to the public.
- A cultural and education quarter, **East Bank**, adds university campuses, museum and theatre space and broadcasting studios.
- Transport: Stratford became a major hub, with high-speed trains to Kent, the DLR, Overground, Central, Jubilee and Elizabeth lines.

**Impacts on people**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | thousands of new homes and new neighbourhoods; Stratford's population has grown fast | many new homes were too expensive for existing Newham residents; some residents and businesses were moved off the site before the Games |
| **Environmental quality** | contaminated land cleaned; rivers restored; a large new park with wetlands and wildlife habitats | construction disruption for years; some feel the area's character has been lost to towers and chain stores |
| **Economic opportunities** | jobs in construction, retail (Westfield), venues, culture and education; better transport to jobs elsewhere | many jobs low-paid or went to people from outside the area; rising rents and **gentrification** pressure in surrounding streets |

**Judgement**: the Olympic legacy has transformed the image and environment of Stratford and brought investment, but critics argue that the poorest residents have gained less than incomers, because housing affordability did not keep pace with rising prices.

### Strategies for more sustainable urban living

A **sustainable city** meets people's needs today without damaging the environment or the opportunities of future generations. London's strategies:

**Recycling and waste**
- Borough collections of mixed recycling and food waste; reuse centres; campaigns to cut single-use plastic, such as free public drinking fountains.
- London recycles a smaller share of household waste than England as a whole, partly because so many people live in flats with limited storage, so improving this is a key aim.

**Employment**
- Regeneration schemes include **training and apprenticeships** for local people, so they can get the new jobs.
- The **London Living Wage**, a voluntary higher minimum wage based on London's living costs, which many employers have adopted.
- Business support in deprived boroughs, so money stays in local communities.

**Green spaces**
- London has a very large share of green and blue space for a big city: Royal Parks, commons, rivers and canals. In 2019 it was declared the world's first **National Park City**, a campaign to make it greener and healthier.
- New parks on brownfield land (Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park), green roofs, tree planting and community gardens.
- **Benefits**: cleaner air, cooler streets, flood storage, wildlife, exercise and better mental health.

**Transport**
- **Congestion Charge** (from 2003): a daily charge to drive into central London on weekdays, reducing traffic.
- **Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ)**: from 2019 in central London, extended to inner London in 2021 and to the whole of Greater London in **2023**. Drivers of older, more polluting vehicles pay a daily charge, so people switch to cleaner vehicles, public transport or walking.
- **Santander Cycles** hire bikes, cycle superhighways and segregated lanes; **low-traffic neighbourhoods** that stop through-traffic on residential streets.
- Public transport investment: the **Elizabeth line**, zero-emission buses, contactless fares.
- **Debates**: ULEZ expansion was controversial in outer boroughs, where people rely more on cars and public transport is thinner; low-traffic neighbourhoods have been criticised for moving traffic onto main roads.

**Affordable, energy-efficient housing**
- The Mayor sets targets for a share of new homes to be **affordable** (below market rents or prices).
- **BedZED** (Beddington Zero Energy Development) in Sutton, completed in the early 2000s, is a well-known eco-village: thick insulation, solar panels, rainwater harvesting, wind-driven ventilation and car-sharing.
- Retrofitting older homes with insulation and double glazing cuts energy bills and emissions, helping to reduce **fuel poverty**.
- **Limits**: London's housing remains very expensive; "affordable" homes may still be too costly for the poorest; retrofitting millions of old homes is slow.

### Skill: crime and IMD databases

- **Crime maps** (police data by street or neighbourhood) show the number and type of crimes, e.g. anti-social behaviour, burglary, vehicle crime.
- The **IMD** ranks every small area in England by deprivation (see 5.4).
- Use them together to judge the **extent** of inner-city problems, or to test whether regeneration has worked: compare an area's rank or crime rate **before and after** a scheme, and with nearby areas.
- **Care**: crime counts are higher where many people visit (shopping centres, stations), so use a **rate per 1,000 people**; IMD ranks are relative, so an area can improve but keep the same rank if others improve too.

::: terms
- **Regeneration**: improving a declining area through investment in buildings, infrastructure, jobs and services.
- **Rebranding**: giving a place a new image to attract residents, visitors and investors.
- **Brownfield site**: land that has been built on before and can be cleared and redeveloped.
- **Top-down approach**: a scheme planned and run by government or large organisations, with little input from local people.
- **Sustainable urban living**: city life that meets people's needs without harming the environment or future generations.
- **Affordable housing**: homes for rent or sale below market prices for people on lower incomes.
- **Energy-efficient housing**: homes designed or upgraded to use less energy, for example with insulation and solar panels.
- **Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ)**: an area where drivers of more polluting vehicles must pay a daily charge.
- **Fuel poverty**: when a household cannot afford to heat its home adequately.
- **London Living Wage**: a voluntary hourly wage set to reflect the higher cost of living in London.
:::

::: method
1. **Regeneration or sustainability?** Check whether the question asks about impacts of a scheme or strategies for sustainable living.
2. **Name the scheme** (Docklands and the LDDC, Canary Wharf, the Olympic Park, Westfield, East Village, ULEZ, BedZED, Santander Cycles).
3. **Impacts**: use the spec headings: **population**, **environmental quality**, **economic opportunities**. Give both positive and negative, and say **for whom**.
4. **Strategies**: say **how** each makes living more sustainable (less pollution, less waste, more jobs for locals, cheaper energy) and give one limit.
5. **Data**: quote figure values (before and after; crime rate; IMD rank) and calculate change where useful.
6. **8-mark + SPaG**: use the figure in every paragraph, weigh benefits against costs for different groups, give a judgement, and write accurately with specialist terms.
:::

::: example 8-mark + SPaG: Olympic Park (Level 3 extract)
Analyse Figure 10. Figure 10 shows changes in Stratford after the Olympic regeneration (sample data): new homes built: 10,000, of which 30% affordable; jobs on and around the park: up from 5,000 to 25,000; average house price: up 120% in ten years; new green space: 100 hectares; a resident's quote: "My rent doubled and I had to move to Essex." Assess the impacts of regeneration on people. (8)

Figure 10 shows large economic gains: jobs rose from 5,000 to 25,000, a fivefold increase, in a borough that has long had high unemployment. Westfield, the venues and East Bank provide work in retail, culture and education, and Stratford's transport hub links residents to jobs across London. The environment has also improved, with 100 hectares of new green space on what was contaminated industrial land.

However, the benefits are unequal. Only 30% of the 10,000 new homes are affordable, while house prices have more than doubled. The resident who had to move to Essex shows how rising rents displace poorer people, which is the gentrification that the regeneration has encouraged.

Overall, the regeneration has been highly successful for the area's image, environment and economy, but less successful for many original residents, who face higher housing costs. Its impact therefore depends on who you are: newcomers and investors have gained most.

::: watch
- Writing only positive impacts. Regeneration has **winners and losers**: say who benefits and who does not.
- Ignoring the spec headings. Cover **population**, **environmental quality** and **economic opportunities**.
- Describing a strategy without explaining **how** it makes living more sustainable.
- Confusing the **Congestion Charge** (traffic in central London) with the **ULEZ** (pollution from older vehicles).
- Using crime totals instead of **rates per 1,000 people** when comparing areas of different sizes.
- Getting dates wrong: the LDDC started in **1981**; the Olympics were in **2012**; the ULEZ became London-wide in **2023**.
- Losing SPaG marks in the 8-marker by writing in notes. Use full sentences and specialist terms.
:::
