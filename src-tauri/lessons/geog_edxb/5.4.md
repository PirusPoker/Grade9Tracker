## What it is

London has grown and changed because people keep moving to it, and the city is one of the most unequal places in the UK. This key idea asks you to explain:
- the **causes of national and international migration** to the city, and how migration shapes the **growth and character** of its different parts: **age structure**, **ethnicity**, **housing**, **services** and **culture**;
- the **reasons for different levels of inequality** between parts of the city in **employment and services**, **education** and **health**.

Integrated skill: using **census data** to compare areas within the inner city (skill 2). It is examined in **Paper 2, Section B**, often in the 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question with **4 SPaG marks**. This course uses London, comparing **Tower Hamlets** and **Newham** (inner east) with **Richmond upon Thames** and **Kingston upon Thames** (outer south-west). Any major UK city you studied earns the marks.

### Why people migrate to London

**International migration**
- **Jobs and wages**: London has the UK's largest job market, from finance and tech to hospitality, cleaning, construction and care. Wages are higher than in most of the world.
- **Education**: its universities attract large numbers of overseas students, some of whom stay to work.
- **Existing communities**: migrants join family and friends; established communities offer housing, jobs, places of worship and familiar food and language (**chain migration**).
- **Refuge**: people fleeing conflict or persecution have settled in London for centuries.
- **Gateway**: Heathrow and the Channel Tunnel make London the first arrival point for many migrants.

**A long history of arrivals**
- Earlier waves included French Protestant (Huguenot) refugees, Irish and Jewish migrants, many of whom settled in the **East End**.
- From 1948 Caribbean migrants arrived (the **Windrush generation**), many settling in Brixton and Notting Hill.
- From the 1960s–70s, South Asian communities grew: Indian Punjabi communities in **Southall**, Bangladeshi communities in **Tower Hamlets** around Brick Lane.
- After 2004, Polish and other Eastern European migrants settled across London; since 2021 more have come from India, Nigeria, Hong Kong and elsewhere.

**National migration**
- **In**: young adults from across the UK move to London for graduate jobs, careers and nightlife.
- **Out**: many families move out to the suburbs and commuter towns for space, gardens and cheaper housing; many people leave London when they retire.
- As a result, London's population is younger than the UK average and constantly replaced.

### How migration shapes different parts of the city

| Character | Inner east (e.g. Tower Hamlets, Newham) | Outer south-west (e.g. Richmond, Kingston) |
|---|---|---|
| **Age structure** | very young: many young adults and children; few over-65s | older: more families with teenagers and more retired people |
| **Ethnicity** | very diverse: Newham is one of the most ethnically diverse places in the UK; Tower Hamlets has a large Bangladeshi community | less diverse, with a larger White British share, though becoming more mixed |
| **Housing** | dense flats, council and housing association estates, crowded private rentals; new high-rise flats; overcrowding common | larger terraced, semi-detached and detached houses; high owner-occupation; very high prices |
| **Services** | schools where many languages are spoken; halal butchers, specialist food shops; mosques, temples and churches | high-performing schools, independent shops, many leisure services |
| **Culture** | Brick Lane's curry houses and markets; festivals such as Boishakhi Mela | riverside culture, Kew Gardens, rugby at Twickenham, traditional pubs |

Migration therefore makes some parts of London **younger, more diverse and more crowded**, while other parts become **older and wealthier** as families and older people stay or move out. Communities change over time: the East End has been home to Huguenot, Jewish and then Bangladeshi communities in turn, and is now also gentrifying (see 5.5).

### Inequality between parts of London

London has many of the richest and many of the poorest neighbourhoods in the UK, sometimes close together.

**Measuring inequality: the IMD**
The **Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD)** ranks every small area in England from most to least deprived, using seven types (domains) of deprivation: **income**, **employment**, **education and skills**, **health and disability**, **crime**, **barriers to housing and services**, and **living environment**. Many neighbourhoods in Tower Hamlets and Newham fall among the most deprived in England, while most of Richmond and Kingston are among the least deprived.

**Employment and services**
- **Pattern**: unemployment and low pay are higher in the inner east; incomes are much higher in the south-west.
- **Reasons**: many residents in deprived areas lack qualifications or fluent English, so they cannot get the skilled jobs on their doorstep. **Tower Hamlets contains Canary Wharf**, with very well-paid finance jobs, yet has very high **child poverty**; most of those jobs go to commuters. Discrimination, caring responsibilities and the high cost of childcare also keep people out of work.
- **Services**: crowded areas have pressure on GP surgeries and school places, while wealthier areas have more choice.

**Education**
- **Pattern**: adults in Richmond are much more likely to have degrees than adults in parts of Newham.
- **Reasons**: parental income and education, overcrowded homes with no quiet space to study, and newly arrived children learning English. However, London's state schools improved a great deal from the 2000s, and disadvantaged pupils in London now tend to do better than similar pupils elsewhere in England, so gaps in school results are narrower than gaps in adult qualifications.

**Health**
- **Pattern**: life expectancy in the wealthiest boroughs is several years higher than in the poorest, and people in deprived areas spend more years in poor health.
- **Reasons**: poor diet and higher rates of smoking and obesity linked to low incomes; overcrowded and damp housing; higher air pollution near main roads in the inner city; less green space; stress from low income and insecure work.

**Why the gaps persist**
- **Housing costs**: high rents push poorer families into overcrowded homes in the cheapest areas, concentrating deprivation.
- **Cycle of deprivation**: low income, poor housing, poor health and low qualifications reinforce each other across generations.
- **Wealth attracts wealth**: good schools and green space raise house prices, so only wealthier families can move into those areas.

### Skill: census data for inner-city areas

- Census tables for small areas (wards or neighbourhoods) show age, country of birth, ethnicity, household size, tenure, qualifications and health.
- Compare two areas using **percentages**, not raw numbers, because the areas have different populations.
- Useful calculations: **percentage** = part ÷ total × 100; **difference** between two percentages (in percentage points); **ratio** (e.g. how many times higher).

::: terms
- **International migration**: movement of people between countries to live, work or study.
- **Chain migration**: when migrants follow family or friends who have already settled in a place.
- **Ethnicity**: belonging to a group with a shared culture, origin, language or traditions.
- **Ethnic enclave**: an area of a city where many people from one ethnic group live, with their own shops and services.
- **Inequality**: unequal access to wealth, jobs, services, education and health between people or places.
- **Deprivation**: a lack of the things most people have, such as income, good housing, jobs and health.
- **Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD)**: an official measure ranking small areas of England by seven types of deprivation.
- **Life expectancy**: the average number of years a person can expect to live from birth.
- **Overcrowding**: when a household has fewer rooms than it needs for the number of people living there.
- **Cycle of deprivation**: the way poverty, poor housing, poor health and low skills reinforce each other over time.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the figure**: quote values for **both** areas and compare them (difference, ratio, rank).
2. **Migration causes**: separate push and pull, and national and international. Use real London examples (Windrush, Brick Lane, Southall, post-2004 EU, students).
3. **Influence on parts of the city**: use the spec headings: age structure, ethnicity, housing, services, culture. Say **where** in London.
4. **Inequality**: name **two areas** (Tower Hamlets or Newham against Richmond or Kingston) and compare with evidence; then give the **reason** for the gap.
5. **Link causes together**: low income, housing, education and health reinforce each other.
6. **8-mark + SPaG**: use figure data in every paragraph, weigh several reasons, reach a judgement, and write in full sentences with specialist terms (IMD, deprivation, life expectancy, chain migration).
:::

::: example Multiple choice: IMD
Which one of the following is one of the domains of the Index of Multiple Deprivation? A: Average temperature. B: Number of tourists. C: Barriers to housing and services. D: Number of listed buildings. (1)

C (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: migration and age structure
Explain one way migration affects the age structure of inner London. (2)

Most international migrants and young people from elsewhere in the UK arrive in their twenties for work and study (1), so inner boroughs such as Tower Hamlets have a very young population with few people over 65 (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: census percentage
Sample data: a ward in Newham has 15,600 residents, of whom 8,580 were born outside the UK. Calculate the percentage born outside the UK. (2)

8,580 ÷ 15,600 × 100 (1) = 55% (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: reasons for health inequality
Explain two reasons why health varies between different parts of London. (4)

People in deprived areas often have lower incomes (1), so they may eat less healthily and suffer more stress, raising rates of obesity and heart disease (1). Inner-city areas have more traffic on main roads and less green space (1), so air pollution is higher and there are fewer places to exercise, worsening breathing problems (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark: inequality (Level 3 extract)
Analyse Figure 7. Figure 7 compares two London boroughs (sample data). Borough X (inner east): child poverty about 45%; unemployment 7%; adults with degrees 40%; male life expectancy 77 years. Borough Y (outer south-west): child poverty about 15%; unemployment 3%; adults with degrees 60%; male life expectancy 82 years. Assess the reasons for inequality between different parts of London. (8)

Figure 7 shows wide gaps: child poverty in Borough X is about three times that of Borough Y, and men live about five years less. The most important reason is income and employment. Unemployment in X is more than double Y's, and even where people work, many jobs in cleaning, retail and care are low-paid. In Tower Hamlets this happens next to the very high salaries of Canary Wharf, because many residents lack the qualifications those jobs require.

Education is linked: only 40% of adults in X have degrees compared with 60% in Y, which limits access to skilled work. Overcrowded homes and recent arrival in the UK make study harder for some young people, although London's schools have helped many disadvantaged pupils do well, so this gap may narrow.

Health gaps follow from the others. Low incomes, damp, crowded housing and polluted main roads reduce life expectancy in X. Overall, low income is the root cause, because it drives poor housing and health and is reinforced by lower qualifications in a cycle of deprivation, while high house prices keep wealthier and poorer Londoners in separate areas.
:::

Explain one reason why some residents of Tower Hamlets cannot get jobs at nearby Canary Wharf. (2)

Many finance jobs need degrees and professional skills (1), which many local residents do not have, so the jobs go to commuters from elsewhere (1).

::: q
Explain two reasons why family households move out of inner London. (4)
---
Two from: housing in inner London is expensive and cramped (1), so families seek larger homes with gardens in the suburbs or commuter towns (1); they want schools and green space (1), which are seen as better in outer areas (1); fast commuter links (1) let them keep their London jobs while living further out (1).

::: watch
- Describing one deprived area on its own. Inequality questions need **two named areas** compared with evidence.
- Giving causes of migration with no link to London. Say **why London** (jobs, universities, communities, gateway).
- Treating migration as only negative. Migrants fill jobs, start businesses and enrich culture, as well as adding pressure on housing and services.
- Mixing up **ethnicity** and **nationality**.
- Forgetting the IMD has **seven domains**, so one area can be deprived in income but not in, say, education.
- Ignoring links between causes: income, housing, education and health reinforce each other.
- Losing SPaG marks through note-form answers in the 8-marker. Write in sentences and use specialist terms.
:::
