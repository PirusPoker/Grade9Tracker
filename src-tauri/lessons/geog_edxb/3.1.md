## What it is

The world is becoming more urban every year. This key idea covers:
- **global trends in urbanisation** since 1980, today and projected into the future, and how they differ between **world regions**;
- the **global pattern of megacities**: their size, location and growth rates;
- **urban primacy**: why, in many countries, one city has far more economic or political influence than any other.

It also carries an integrated skill: reading **line graphs** and calculating **rates of change** and **annual or decadal percentage growth**. Pearson sets these calculations almost every year in Section C of Paper 1.

Any accurate real examples earn the marks. Keep figures rounded: "about", "more than half", "roughly two-thirds".

### Urbanisation: the key definitions

- **Urbanisation** is the increase in the **proportion** of a country's population living in towns and cities. It is not just a city getting bigger: a city can grow while the country's urban share stays the same if the rural population grows just as fast.
- **Urban growth** is the increase in the **number** of people living in urban areas, or in the area a city covers.
- A **megacity** is an urban area with a population of **more than 10 million**. Always give the figure in a definition.
- A **world city** (global city) has influence far beyond its own country, such as London, New York or Tokyo. A city can be a megacity without being a world city.

### Global trends since 1980

| Period | Global picture (rounded) |
|---|---|
| **1980** | roughly two in every five people lived in urban areas |
| **about 2007** | the world became **more than half urban** for the first time in history |
| **Today** | well over half the world's people live in towns and cities |
| **2050 (projection)** | about **two-thirds** of people are expected to be urban, with almost all of the extra growth in Africa and Asia |

Two features matter for exam answers:
1. **The rate** of urbanisation is now fastest in **developing and emerging** countries, especially in **sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia**.
2. **The level** of urbanisation is already highest in **developed** countries and in **Latin America**, where it is now growing only slowly.

### How urbanisation varies between regions

| Region | Level of urbanisation | Rate of change | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **North America, Europe, Australia** | high: around three-quarters or more | slow; some cities even shrank | urbanised during the Industrial Revolution in the 19th and early 20th centuries; now counter-urbanisation and an ageing population |
| **Latin America** | high: around four-fifths | slowing | rapid rural-urban migration from the 1950s to the 1980s; few people left in the countryside to move |
| **Asia** | about half | fast | industrialisation in China, India and South-East Asia draws migrants to factory and service jobs |
| **Africa** | below half | **fastest** | very high natural increase plus rural-urban migration; growth often happens without matching industrial jobs |

**Why the rates differ: two causes to use in every answer**
- **Rural-urban migration**: people are **pushed** from the countryside (drought, small plots, few services, conflict) and **pulled** to cities (jobs, wages, schools, hospitals, the "bright lights").
- **Natural increase**: cities in developing countries have **young populations** because migrants are mainly in their twenties and thirties. Young adults have children, so births far exceed deaths.

**Projections** are uncertain. They depend on future birth rates, economic growth, conflict and climate change (which could push more farmers off the land). The direction is clear, though: more urban, and more urban growth in Africa and Asia.

### The global pattern of megacities

**Size**
- In 1950 only two or three urban areas, such as **Tokyo** and **New York**, had more than 10 million people.
- By the late 2010s there were more than 30, and the number is projected to keep rising.
- The largest, **Tokyo**, has well over 30 million people in its urban area. **Delhi**, **Shanghai**, **Dhaka**, **São Paulo**, **Mexico City**, **Cairo**, **Beijing** and **Mumbai** are all among the biggest.

**Location**
- Most megacities are now in **Asia**, especially in China and India.
- Others are in **Latin America** (São Paulo, Mexico City, Buenos Aires, Rio de Janeiro), **Africa** (Lagos, Cairo, Kinshasa) and a few developed countries (Tokyo, Osaka, New York, Los Angeles, Paris). London sits close to the threshold, depending on how its urban area is measured.
- Many are **coastal** or on **major rivers**, because ports and trade drove their early growth (Shanghai, Mumbai, Lagos, Dhaka). This also exposes them to flooding and sea-level rise.

**Growth rates**
- The **fastest-growing** megacities are in **developing and emerging countries**: Lagos, Kinshasa, Dhaka and Delhi are growing by several per cent a year.
- Megacities in **developed** countries such as Tokyo and Osaka are growing very slowly, or have started to shrink, because of low birth rates and ageing.
- So the pattern has **shifted** from the developed world (1950) to the developing and emerging world (today).

| Type of megacity | Examples | Growth |
|---|---|---|
| Slow-growing, developed | Tokyo, Osaka, New York | under 1% a year; some declining |
| Growing, emerging | Shanghai, São Paulo, Mexico City, Mumbai | moderate, often slowing |
| Rapidly growing, developing | Lagos, Kinshasa, Dhaka | fast, often several per cent a year, with much informal housing |

### Urban primacy

A **primate city** is a country's leading city that is **far larger and more influential** than the next city down, often several times larger. It dominates the country's:
- **economy**: head offices, banks, the stock exchange, the main port or airport, most foreign investment;
- **politics**: often the capital, with the government and national institutions;
- **culture and services**: the best universities, hospitals, media and sport.

Examples (described in general terms):
- **Bangkok** is many times larger than any other city in Thailand.
- **Lima** holds roughly a third of Peru's population.
- **Lagos** remains Nigeria's economic centre even though the capital moved to Abuja in 1991, which shows primacy can be **economic** without being **political**.
- **London** and **Paris** are primate cities in developed countries.

**Why primacy develops**
- **Historic advantage**: the colonial port or capital received the first roads, railways and investment.
- **Cumulative causation**: once a city has jobs and services, it attracts more migrants and businesses, which attract more again.
- **Government decisions** concentrate spending in the capital.

**Effects of primacy**
- **Positive:** economies of scale, a strong base for attracting foreign investment, a national "shop window".
- **Negative:** extreme congestion and pressure on housing in the primate city; other regions are left behind (a core-periphery pattern); the country depends on one city, so a disaster or crisis there hits the whole nation.

### Skill: rates of change and percentage growth

Pearson gives a line graph or table of population over time and asks you to calculate.

| Calculation | Formula | Worked example (sample data) |
|---|---|---|
| **Rate of change** | change ÷ number of years | a city grows from 8 million (2000) to 14 million (2020): 6 ÷ 20 = **0.3 million a year** |
| **Percentage change** | (new − old) ÷ old × 100 | (14 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = **75%** |
| **Average annual percentage growth** (simple) | percentage change ÷ number of years | 75 ÷ 20 = **3.75% a year** |
| **Decadal percentage growth** | percentage change over a 10-year period | 8 million (2000) to 11 million (2010): 3 ÷ 8 × 100 = **37.5% per decade** |

Tips:
- Always use the **old** (earlier) value as the divisor.
- Give the **unit**: million, % or "per year".
- Read graph values carefully: check the scale on the y-axis before you start.
- When describing a line graph, give the **trend**, the **rate** (steepening or levelling off) and **data with units and years**, plus any **anomaly**.

::: terms
- **Urbanisation**: an increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas.
- **Urban growth**: an increase in the number of people living in a town or city, or in the area it covers.
- **Megacity**: an urban area with a population of more than 10 million people.
- **Urban primacy**: when one city in a country is far larger and more influential than any other.
- **Primate city**: a country's leading city, much larger than the second city and dominant in its economy or politics.
- **Rural-urban migration**: the movement of people from the countryside to towns and cities.
- **Natural increase**: the birth rate minus the death rate, which adds to a population when births exceed deaths.
- **Push factor**: a reason that drives people away from the place where they live, such as drought or lack of jobs.
- **Pull factor**: a reason that attracts people to a new place, such as jobs, wages or services.
- **Rate of change**: how quickly a value changes over time, such as millions of people added per year.
- **Projection**: an estimate of a future value based on present trends and assumptions.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions:** give the precise version: "proportion", "more than 10 million", "far larger than the second city".
2. **Describing trends:** state the overall trend, then the rate, then two pieces of data with years and units, then any anomaly or contrast between regions.
3. **Calculations:** write the formula, show the working with the numbers, and give the answer with units. Use the earlier value as the divisor for percentage change.
4. **Explaining patterns:** link a cause (migration, natural increase, industrialisation) to the region or city with "because" and "so".
5. **Primacy questions:** name a primate city, say what it dominates (economy, politics, services), and explain why the dominance built up.
6. **8-mark assess:** weigh several reasons, say which matters most and why, and finish with a judgement that recognises variation between places.
:::

::: example Define: megacity
Define the term megacity. (1)

An urban area with a total population of more than 10 million people (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: percentage change
Sample data: a city's population rose from 6.0 million in 2000 to 10.5 million in 2025. Calculate the percentage change. Show your working. (2)

(10.5 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 × 100 (1) = 75% (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: urban primacy
Explain one reason why some countries have a primate city. (2)

The primate city was often the colonial port or capital, so it received the first investment in transport and industry (1). This attracted more businesses and migrants, so its lead over other cities kept growing (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: why urbanisation is fastest in Africa and Asia
Explain two reasons why the rate of urbanisation is highest in developing countries. (4)

Rural-urban migration is high (1) because people are pushed off the land by small plots, drought and lack of services, and pulled to cities by jobs and higher wages (1). Natural increase is high in cities (1) because migrants are mostly young adults, so many children are born and births greatly exceed deaths (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: fast-growing megacities
Assess the reasons why most of the world's fastest-growing megacities are in developing and emerging countries. (8)

The most important reason is rural-urban migration. In countries such as Nigeria, Bangladesh and India, a large share of people still live in rural areas where farming is poorly paid and vulnerable to drought and floods. Cities like Lagos and Dhaka offer jobs in factories, services and the informal economy, so millions move each decade. In developed countries, by contrast, there is almost nobody left in the countryside to migrate, so Tokyo and New York grow only slowly.

Natural increase is almost as important, and in some cities it now adds more people than migration. Migrants are young, so birth rates in the city are high while improving health care keeps death rates low. In Lagos this means growth continues even when migration slows. In Japan, low fertility and ageing mean natural increase is close to zero or negative.

Economic investment is a third factor. Emerging economies such as China and India have attracted foreign investment and industry into a few big cities, which creates jobs and pulls in more migrants. However, this applies less to the fastest-growing African megacities, where growth is often happening without matching industrial jobs, so investment is not a universal explanation.

Overall, migration and natural increase together explain most of the pattern, with migration usually the trigger and natural increase sustaining the growth once a young population has settled. Investment matters more in emerging Asian cities than in developing African ones, so the balance of reasons varies from city to city.

(Level 3: accurate understanding of several reasons, applied to real cities, with a supported judgement about relative importance.)
:::

---

::: q
Explain one disadvantage of urban primacy for a country. (2)
---
Investment, jobs and services are concentrated in one city (1), so other regions are left behind and inequality between the core and the periphery widens (1). Or: the primate city becomes overcrowded and congested (1) because so many migrants move there (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest one reason why many megacities are located on the coast. (3)
---
Many grew up as ports (1) because trade by sea was the main way to connect with other countries during colonial times (1); the port attracted industry and jobs, which drew migrants, so the city kept growing (1).
:::

::: watch
- Defining urbanisation as "cities getting bigger". It is the rising **proportion** of people living in urban areas.
- Defining a megacity without the **10 million** figure.
- Dividing by the new value in a percentage-change calculation. Always divide by the **earlier** value.
- Describing a graph with no data. Quote figures with **units and years**.
- Treating all developing-country cities the same. Separate **level** of urbanisation from **rate**, and name regions.
- Saying a primate city is "the capital". Lagos shows a primate city need not be the capital.
- In the 8-marker, listing reasons without judging which matters most.
:::
