## What it is

Topic 6 is examined in **Paper 2 Section C1** (18 marks). You answer **either** the coasts question **or** the rivers question, whichever matches the physical fieldwork you did. This lesson is for the coasts option, **Investigating coastal change and conflict**. Its focus is set by the spec: investigate **the impact of coastal management on coastal processes and communities**.

The question has two halves:
- **Your own fieldwork:** you write your location, then answer 2- and 4-mark questions on methods, a qualitative method, theory, secondary data or improvements, and sometimes an 8-marker on your own enquiry.
- **Unfamiliar fieldwork:** a figure shows another group's data. You calculate, criticise their method and, in the 8-marker, assess their conclusion **using evidence from the figure**.

Every answer must sound as if it came from a real beach: name the place, equipment, sample size and findings. The model investigation below uses **sample data** on an unnamed groyned beach; swap in your own site and results.

### What the spec requires you to have done

| Requirement | What it means at the coast |
|---|---|
| **Enquiry question** | developed from your own location and the task, about management, processes and people |
| **At least one quantitative method** | measuring how management has affected **beach morphology** (shape) and **sediment** characteristics |
| **At least one qualitative method** | collecting data on the **management measures** and how **successful** they are |
| **Secondary sources** | a **geology map**, such as the British Geological Survey's Geology of Britain viewer, plus **one other** source |

### The six enquiry stages

Pearson tests at least two of these each year, across both investigations.
1. **Asking the question:** an enquiry question that can actually be answered with fieldwork, often broken into hypotheses.
2. **Choosing methods:** primary methods (quantitative and qualitative), sampling, equipment and risk assessment.
3. **Processing and presenting:** graphs, maps, annotated photos and statistics.
4. **Analysing and explaining:** describing patterns and explaining them with **theory** (longshore drift, wave types) and **case studies** (for example Holderness and the Mappleton groynes).
5. **Concluding:** answering the question, using evidence.
6. **Evaluating:** the accuracy and reliability of the data, the methods and the conclusion, and what you learned.

### A model investigation (sample data)

**Enquiry question:** "How have groynes affected beach processes and the local community at a seaside town?"
- **Hypothesis 1:** the beach is higher on the **updrift** side of each groyne than on the downdrift side.
- **Hypothesis 2:** sediment becomes smaller and more rounded in the direction of **longshore drift**.
- **Hypothesis 3:** residents and visitors think the groynes are a success, but people living beyond the end of the defences do not.

**Site selection.** Five sites were spaced at **equal intervals** along about 1 km of beach (systematic sampling), with one extra site beyond the last groyne, where the beach is unprotected. A geology map showed the cliffs behind the beach are soft clay, which explains why the town is defended.

### Quantitative methods: beach morphology and sediment

| Method | Equipment and how | Why it suits the question |
|---|---|---|
| **Beach profile** | two **ranging poles** placed at each **break of slope** from the sea edge to the back of the beach; a **tape measure** for the distance between them; a **clinometer** sighted between marks at the same height on each pole to read the angle | shows how management changes the height and gradient of the beach; profiles on each side of a groyne can be compared |
| **Groyne height difference** | at each groyne, a metre rule measures the distance from the top of the groyne down to the sand on the **updrift** and **downdrift** sides; the difference is recorded | a direct measure of how much sediment the groyne traps, so it tests whether longshore drift is being interrupted |
| **Sediment size** | at each site, pebbles are picked up at points along a tape (for example every 2 m, or using a random number table); the longest or the middle (**b-axis**) length is measured with **callipers** | shows how sediment changes along the coast and whether groynes are sorting it |
| **Sediment roundness** | each pebble is compared with the **Powers roundness index**, a chart of six categories from very angular (1) to well rounded (6) | rounder pebbles have been moved further by attrition, evidence of longshore drift |

### Qualitative methods: management and its success

| Method | How | Strengths and weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **Management condition survey** | each defence (groyne, sea wall, rock armour) is scored for condition, for example 1 = badly damaged to 5 = as new | quick and comparable, but subjective |
| **Bipolar survey** | opposite statements ("defences are ugly" against "defences blend in") scored from −3 to +3 | turns opinions into numbers for a radar graph, but people score differently |
| **Questionnaire or interview** | residents, visitors and business owners asked whether the defences are effective and worth the cost | gives views of the **community**, which the spec stresses; small samples and leading questions are problems |
| **Annotated field sketch or photo** | sketch or photograph each defence and label evidence of erosion or deposition | records evidence that numbers miss, but depends on the observer |

### Sampling, risk and data types

- **Random sampling:** every point has an equal chance (random number tables). Unbiased, but may miss key places such as the end of the defences.
- **Systematic sampling:** regular intervals, such as every 200 m or every fifth passer-by. Easy and even coverage, but may miss features between intervals.
- **Stratified sampling:** in proportion to sub-groups, such as defended and undefended sections, or residents and visitors. Every group is represented.
- **Sample size:** more pebbles per site or more people interviewed makes the results more reliable, but takes longer.
- **Risk assessment:** check **tide times** and work on a falling tide; keep away from cliff bases because of rockfalls; take care on slippery rocks and groynes; check the weather and wear suitable clothing; work in groups.
- **Primary** data is collected by you; **secondary** data by someone else. **Quantitative** data is numerical; **qualitative** data is descriptive, such as opinions, sketches and photos.

### Secondary sources

- **Geology map** (required): shows whether the cliffs are soft clay or harder rock, explaining why some places erode faster and need defending.
- **Shoreline Management Plan:** the policy for that stretch (hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment or no active intervention).
- **Historic maps, old photos and satellite images:** how the coastline and beach have changed.
- **Environment Agency** flood maps, erosion records, tide tables and local news reports.

Secondary data lets you see change over **longer periods** than one fieldwork day and lets you **check** your primary results.

### Presenting the data

| Data | Best presentation |
|---|---|
| beach profiles | cross-section line graphs, updrift and downdrift drawn on the same axes |
| groyne height differences | bar chart per groyne, located on a base map |
| sediment size along the beach | bar chart or **dispersion graph** per site |
| roundness | stacked bar chart of the six Powers categories per site |
| bipolar survey | **radar graph** |
| questionnaire results | divided bar or pie chart |
| defences | **annotated photographs** located on a map |

### Statistics, worked through (sample data)

Ten pebbles at site 3, b-axis in mm: 32, 45, 28, 51, 39, 60, 35, 42, 48, 30.
- Order them: 28, 30, 32, 35, 39, 42, 45, 48, 51, 60.
- **Mean** = total ÷ number = 410 ÷ 10 = **41.0 mm**.
- **Median** = middle value = (39 + 42) ÷ 2 = **40.5 mm**.
- **Range** = 60 − 28 = **32 mm**.
- **Interquartile range:** lower quartile is the median of the lower half (28, 30, **32**, 35, 39) = 32; upper quartile is the median of the upper half (42, 45, **48**, 51, 60) = 48; IQR = 48 − 32 = **16 mm**. A small IQR means the sediment is well sorted.

Groyne height differences at six groynes (m): 0.6, 0.8, 0.5, 0.9, 0.7, 1.1. Mean = 4.6 ÷ 6 = **0.77 m**.

### Analysing with theory and case studies

- **Longshore drift** moves sediment along the beach in the direction of the prevailing wind. **Groynes** interrupt it, so sand builds up on the updrift side and the downdrift side is lower.
- **Attrition** makes pebbles smaller and rounder the further they travel, so pebbles should be smaller and rounder down-drift.
- **Terminal groyne syndrome:** at the end of a groyne field, the beach is starved of sediment, so erosion speeds up. On the **Holderness coast**, rock groynes at **Mappleton** protected the village, but cliffs to the south then eroded faster. Linking your results to this case study earns analysis marks.
- **Destructive waves** in winter storms remove beach material; **constructive waves** build it up, so the day and season of fieldwork matter.
- **Conflict:** homeowners and businesses want defences; people beyond the defences, taxpayers and conservationists may not.

### Conclusions (sample)

At the model site, the beach was higher on the updrift side at every groyne (mean difference 0.77 m), and pebbles were smaller and rounder towards the down-drift end, supporting hypotheses 1 and 2. Most residents in the town supported the groynes, but the few people interviewed beyond the defences said erosion had worsened, partly supporting hypothesis 3. Overall, management has **interrupted longshore drift**, protecting the town but **moving the problem** further along the coast.

### Accuracy, reliability and improvements

- **Accuracy** is how close a measurement is to the true value. Clinometer readings, pebble selection (choosing the nearest pebble rather than an easy one) and measuring groyne heights on a sloping beach all introduce errors.
- **Reliability** is whether the results would be the same if repeated. One day, one season and small samples make the results less reliable; a storm the week before could change the beach.
- **Improvements:** more sites and more pebbles per site; repeat the survey in winter and summer; use stratified sampling of people; take three clinometer readings and average them; compare with older beach profiles or photos from secondary sources.

::: terms
- **Beach morphology**: the shape of a beach, including its height, gradient and features such as ridges and berms.
- **Beach profile**: a cross-section of a beach from the sea edge to the back of the beach, measured at breaks of slope.
- **Ranging pole**: a striped pole held upright at each break of slope so distance and angle can be measured between two points.
- **Clinometer**: an instrument used to measure the angle of slope between two ranging poles.
- **Powers roundness index**: a six-category chart, from very angular to well rounded, used to classify how worn a pebble is.
- **Updrift**: the side of a groyne facing the oncoming longshore drift, where sediment builds up.
- **Systematic sampling**: collecting data at regular intervals, such as every 200 metres or every fifth person.
- **Stratified sampling**: sampling in proportion to sub-groups so that each group in the area is fairly represented.
- **Accuracy**: how close a measurement or result is to the true value.
- **Reliability**: how consistent results are, so that repeating the method would give similar data.
- **Qualitative data**: non-numerical data such as opinions, field sketches, photographs and descriptions.
- **Shoreline Management Plan**: a long-term plan that sets the management policy for each stretch of the coast.
:::

::: method
1. **Name your location** and keep it in every answer. Use your own data, sample size and results.
2. **Method questions (2):** say what you measured, with which **equipment**, and **how** or **why** it fitted the question. Point, then development.
3. **Qualitative or secondary (2 or 4):** name the method or source, then say what it **showed** or **added** to the enquiry, such as long-term change or people's views.
4. **Calculations:** show working, give units, and round as asked. Know mean, median, mode, range, IQR and percentage change.
5. **Improvements (4):** two separate changes, each with how it makes the data more **accurate** or **reliable**.
6. **8-mark given data:** quote figures from the figure, judge the **sample**, the **questions** and whether the data actually supports the conclusion, then make an overall judgement.
7. **8-mark own work:** split into **accuracy** and **reliability** (or strengths and weaknesses), give specific examples from your own day, and finish with how far the conclusion can be trusted.
:::

::: example Calculation: interquartile range
Sample data: the b-axis of eight pebbles (mm) was 22, 25, 27, 30, 34, 38, 41, 46. Calculate the interquartile range. Show your working. (2)

Lower quartile = median of 22, 25, 27, 30 = (25 + 27) ÷ 2 = 26; upper quartile = median of 34, 38, 41, 46 = (38 + 41) ÷ 2 = 39.5 (1). IQR = 39.5 − 26 = 13.5 mm (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark: assess a given conclusion
A group interviewed 15 people on the promenade on one weekday afternoon. 9 said the new rock armour was "a good idea", 4 were not sure and 2 disagreed. They concluded that the local community supports the coastal management. Assess the strengths and weaknesses of this conclusion. (8)

There is some support for the conclusion. Nine of fifteen people, 60%, agreed the rock armour was a good idea, and only two disagreed, so most of the people asked supported it.

However, the evidence is weak. Fifteen is a very small sample for a whole community, and all were interviewed in one place at one time. On a weekday afternoon on the promenade, many would be visitors or retired people rather than working residents, so the sample is not representative of the "local community". People living beyond the rock armour, who might suffer increased erosion, were not asked at all.

The question was also vague: "a good idea" does not say whether people think it works, whether it is worth the cost or whether it looks acceptable. Four people were unsure, suggesting the question was not well understood.

Overall, the conclusion is only partly valid. It shows that most promenade users asked were positive, but a larger, stratified sample of residents in different parts of the town, at different times, with clearer questions, would be needed before claiming the whole community supports the management.

(Level 3: uses data from the figure, judges sample size, location and question design, and reaches a balanced judgement.)

::: watch
- Writing a generic textbook method with no **location**, **equipment** or **sample size**: it stays in the lowest marks.
- Mixing up **accuracy** (close to the true value) and **reliability** (repeatable, consistent).
- Forgetting that the coasts focus is **management** and **communities**, not just landforms.
- For "qualitative" methods, describing a measurement such as pebble size. Qualitative means views, sketches, photos or condition scores.
- In the given-data 8-marker, not quoting **figures** from the figure. The question says you must use evidence from it.
- Saying "more data" as an improvement without saying **what** data and **why** it would help.
- Calculating the IQR from unordered values. Always put the data in order first.
:::
