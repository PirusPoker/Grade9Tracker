## What it is

The UK's coastline is extremely varied: chalk cliffs and stacks, sweeping sandy bays, shingle spits and crumbling clay cliffs. This key idea explains **why**: geology interacting with physical processes. It covers:
- how **geological structure** (**concordant** and **discordant** coasts, **joints** and **faults**) and **rock type** (hard and soft) influence **erosional landforms**: headlands and bays, caves, arches, stacks, cliffs and wave-cut platforms;
- how **UK climate** (seasons, storm frequency, prevailing winds), **marine processes** (destructive waves) and **sub-aerial processes** (mass movement and weathering) shape coasts of erosion and control the **rate of coastal retreat**;
- how **longshore drift** and **constructive waves** build **depositional landforms**: beaches, spits and bars.

Integrated skills: calculating **mean rates of erosion** from several years of data, using **BGS geology maps** to link the shape of the coast to its rocks, and **recognising coastal landforms on OS maps**. Paper 2 Question 2 is the coasts question, usually a multiple choice on a figure, a 3-mark "Explain how…" and a 4-mark "Explain two…". Any real UK examples earn the marks; this lesson uses the Dorset coast, Spurn Head, Chesil Beach and Slapton.

### Marine erosion processes

| Process | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Hydraulic action** | waves force water and air into cracks, joints and faults; the pressure, and its sudden release as the wave retreats, widens the cracks and breaks rock away |
| **Abrasion** (corrasion) | waves hurl sand, pebbles and boulders against the cliff, scraping and chipping it like sandpaper |
| **Attrition** | rocks carried by waves knock into each other, becoming smaller and more rounded |
| **Solution** (corrosion) | seawater chemically dissolves some rocks, especially chalk and limestone |

### Destructive and constructive waves

Waves are created by **wind blowing over the sea**. Their energy depends on **wind speed**, **how long** the wind blows and the **fetch** (the distance of open water the wind blows over).

| | **Destructive waves** | **Constructive waves** |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | high and steep, short wavelength | low and flat, long wavelength |
| Frequency | high: often more than 10 a minute | low: often fewer than 10 a minute |
| Swash and backwash | **backwash stronger than swash** | **swash stronger than backwash** |
| Effect | erode cliffs and drag beach material out to sea | carry sediment up the beach and build it up |
| When | storms, especially in winter | calm weather, especially in summer |

### Geology: structure and rock type

**Rock type.** **Hard rocks** (chalk, granite, Portland limestone) resist erosion and form **headlands** and steep **cliffs**. **Soft rocks** (clay, sands, boulder clay) erode quickly and form **bays** and lower, slumping cliffs.

**Discordant coasts**: bands of hard and soft rock run **at right angles** to the coastline. The soft rock erodes faster into **bays**; the hard rock is left sticking out as **headlands**.
- **Example: Swanage, Dorset.** From north to south: chalk forms the headland at **Handfast Point** (Old Harry Rocks); softer clays and sands form **Swanage Bay**; harder limestone forms **Durlston Head**. Studland Bay lies north of the chalk.
- Once formed, headlands receive more wave energy because waves **refract** (bend) around them, while bays become sheltered and collect beaches.

**Concordant coasts**: bands of rock run **parallel** to the coastline, so a hard outer band protects softer rock behind it.
- **Example: Lulworth Cove, Dorset.** A thin band of hard **Portland limestone** faces the sea, with softer clays and sands behind it, then chalk. Where waves broke through the limestone (helped by joints and faults), they quickly eroded the soft rock behind, scooping out a **round cove** with a narrow entrance. The chalk at the back is harder, so erosion slowed there. Nearby **Stair Hole** shows an earlier stage of the same process.

**Joints and faults** are lines of weakness. **Joints** are small cracks within a rock; **faults** are larger fractures where rocks have moved. Hydraulic action and abrasion exploit them first, so caves form where joints and faults meet the sea, and cliffs collapse along them.

### Landforms of erosion

**Cave, arch, stack, stump** (on a headland)
1. Waves attack a **joint or fault** in the headland by hydraulic action and abrasion, widening it into a crack.
2. The crack grows into a **cave**.
3. Erosion continues until the cave breaks through the headland, often where caves form back to back, creating an **arch**. **Durdle Door** in Dorset is an arch in Portland limestone.
4. Waves erode the base of the arch, and weathering attacks its roof, until the roof **collapses**, leaving an isolated pillar: a **stack**.
5. The stack is undercut and eventually collapses to a low **stump**, visible at low tide. **Old Harry Rocks** at Handfast Point are chalk stacks; a neighbouring stack, Old Harry's Wife, has collapsed to a stump.

**Cliffs and wave-cut platforms**
1. Destructive waves attack the base of the cliff between high and low tide, cutting a **wave-cut notch** by hydraulic action and abrasion.
2. The notch deepens, leaving an **overhang** of rock above.
3. The overhang becomes unsupported and **collapses**. The rubble is broken down by attrition and removed by the waves.
4. The process repeats, so the cliff **retreats** inland, leaving behind a flat, gently sloping, rocky **wave-cut platform**, exposed at low tide.
5. As the platform widens, waves break further out and lose energy before reaching the cliff, so the rate of retreat slows.

### Climate and sub-aerial processes

**UK climate**
- **Seasonality**: winter brings more frequent and more powerful storms, so destructive waves and cliff erosion are greatest in winter; summer brings calmer constructive waves that rebuild beaches.
- **Storm frequency**: a run of severe storms can cause years' worth of erosion at once. In the stormy winter of 2013–14, storms battered the south and west coasts; the sea wall carrying the railway at Dawlish in Devon collapsed.
- **Prevailing winds** from the **south-west** blow across thousands of kilometres of the Atlantic. This **long fetch** gives powerful waves on south-west-facing coasts such as Cornwall. On the east coast the fetch is shorter, but north-easterly storms across the North Sea still produce destructive waves.

**Sub-aerial processes** act on the cliff face above the reach of the waves.
- **Weathering**: freeze-thaw on exposed cliffs; **salt weathering** (salt crystals grow in cracks and break the rock); chemical weathering of chalk and limestone by rain; biological weathering by roots and burrowing animals.
- **Mass movement**: **rockfall** from hard, jointed cliffs; **slumping (rotational slip)** on soft clay cliffs after heavy rain, when saturated clay becomes heavy and slides along a curved surface; **landslides** of large blocks along a slip plane.

**The rate of coastal retreat** depends on several factors acting together:
- **rock type**: soft boulder clay at Holderness retreats far faster than hard granite in Cornwall;
- **wave energy**: fetch, exposure to prevailing winds and storms;
- **beach width**: a wide beach absorbs wave energy and protects the cliff; a narrow beach leaves it exposed;
- **sub-aerial processes**: heavy rain on clay cliffs triggers slumping, which supplies loose material for waves to remove;
- **human activity**: defences slow retreat locally but can starve beaches downdrift.

**Skill: mean rate of erosion.** Add up the yearly retreat figures and divide by the number of years. For example (sample data), cliff retreat over five years of 1.2, 2.6, 0.8, 1.9 and 3.0 m totals 9.5 m; 9.5 ÷ 5 = **1.9 m per year**. Look for the year with the biggest value and link it to storms.

### Landforms of deposition

**Longshore drift**
1. Waves approach the beach at an **angle**, in the direction of the **prevailing wind**.
2. The **swash** carries sediment up the beach at that angle.
3. The **backwash** drags it straight back down the beach, at right angles to the coast, under **gravity**.
4. Repeated many times, sediment moves **along the coast in a zigzag**.

**Beaches** form where constructive waves deposit sand and shingle, often in sheltered bays. Sand beaches are gently sloping; shingle beaches are steeper, with **berms** (ridges at the high-tide line).

**Spits**: long, narrow ridges of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end and stretching out into the sea.
1. Longshore drift carries sediment along the coast.
2. Where the coast **changes direction** (for example at a river mouth), the sediment continues straight on and is deposited in the calmer water, building out the spit.
3. A change in wind direction (a **secondary wind**) curves the end into a **hook**.
4. Behind the spit, sheltered water allows mud to settle, forming **salt marsh**.
5. **Example: Spurn Head** at the mouth of the Humber, built of sediment eroded from the Holderness cliffs to the north.

**Bars**: where a spit grows right across a bay, joining two headlands, it forms a **bar**, trapping a freshwater **lagoon** behind it. **Example: Slapton Sands**, a shingle bar in south Devon, with the freshwater lake **Slapton Ley** behind it. **Chesil Beach** in Dorset is a long shingle beach joining the Isle of Portland to the mainland, with the Fleet lagoon behind it.

### Map skills

- **BGS geology maps** show rock types in colours. Match headlands to hard rock and bays to soft rock, and check whether rock bands run across the coast (discordant) or parallel to it (concordant).
- **OS maps**: cliffs are shown by black symbols along the coast; rocks and stacks offshore as black dots or shapes; beaches in yellow (sand) or with a stippled pattern (shingle); spits as a narrow strip of beach extending into water; salt marsh with marsh symbols; bays as curved stretches between headlands.

::: terms
- **Hydraulic action**: erosion by the force of waves forcing water and air into cracks, which widens them and breaks rock away.
- **Abrasion**: erosion by sand and pebbles thrown against a cliff by waves, scraping and wearing it away.
- **Destructive wave**: a high, steep, frequent wave with a stronger backwash than swash, which erodes the coast.
- **Constructive wave**: a low, flat wave with a stronger swash than backwash, which deposits sediment and builds beaches.
- **Fetch**: the distance of open water over which the wind blows to create waves.
- **Discordant coast**: a coast where bands of hard and soft rock run at right angles to the shoreline, forming headlands and bays.
- **Concordant coast**: a coast where bands of rock run parallel to the shoreline, so a hard outer band protects softer rock behind.
- **Sub-aerial processes**: weathering and mass movement acting on the cliff face above the level of the waves.
- **Wave-cut platform**: a flat, rocky area at the base of a cliff, exposed at low tide, left as the cliff retreats.
- **Longshore drift**: the zigzag movement of sediment along a coast by swash at an angle and backwash straight down the beach.
- **Spit**: a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end and extending into the sea.
- **Bar**: a ridge of sand or shingle that stretches across a bay, joining two headlands and trapping a lagoon behind it.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the process**: hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution; or a sub-aerial process such as slumping or freeze-thaw.
2. **Link to geology**: hard or soft rock; concordant or discordant; joints and faults as weaknesses.
3. **Sequence landforms**: give every stage in order (crack, cave, arch, stack, stump; notch, overhang, collapse, retreat, platform).
4. **Bring in climate**: winter storms, prevailing south-westerly winds, long fetch, destructive waves.
5. **Use a named example** (Old Harry Rocks, Lulworth Cove, Spurn Head, Slapton) and any figure data.
6. **Calculations**: for a mean rate, total ÷ number of years, with units (m per year).
7. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: quote the figure, explain how geology sets the pattern and how processes do the work, and judge which matters more.
:::

::: q
Explain two ways in which sub-aerial processes contribute to cliff retreat. (4)
---
Two from: slumping (1): heavy rain saturates clay cliffs, which become heavy and slide down a curved slip plane (1); freeze-thaw / salt weathering (1): widens cracks so blocks fall from the cliff face (1); rockfall (1): weathered blocks drop from jointed cliffs, supplying debris that waves remove (1); chemical weathering (1): rain dissolves chalk and limestone along joints, weakening the cliff (1).

- Getting swash and backwash the wrong way round. **Constructive**: swash stronger. **Destructive**: backwash stronger.
- Saying the backwash in longshore drift moves "at an angle". It runs **straight down the beach** under gravity.
- Confusing **marine** processes (waves) with **sub-aerial** processes (weathering and mass movement on the cliff face).
- Mixing up concordant (parallel) and discordant (at right angles).
- Forgetting the units (m per year) in mean erosion rates.
- In the 8-marker, ignoring the figure. Half the marks are for using its evidence.
