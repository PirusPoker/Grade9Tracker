## What it is

The last part of the megacity case study asks how quality of life can be improved and the city made more **sustainable**. This key idea covers:
- the **advantages and disadvantages** of **city-wide government (top-down)** strategies for managing **water supply**, **waste disposal**, **transport** and **air quality**;
- the **advantages and disadvantages** of **community and NGO-led (bottom-up)** strategies for improving **housing**, **health** and **education** services.

Any real megacity in a developing or emerging country earns the marks. This course uses **Mumbai**. Exam questions almost always ask you to **evaluate** or **compare**, so learn each strategy with at least one advantage and one disadvantage.

### What makes a city sustainable?

A **sustainable city** meets the needs of people today without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs. It balances three things:
- **Social:** decent housing, health, education and safety for everyone.
- **Economic:** jobs and a strong economy that can pay for services.
- **Environmental:** clean water and air, less waste, efficient transport, protection of green space.

### Top-down against bottom-up

| | **Top-down** | **Bottom-up** |
|---|---|---|
| **Who leads** | national, state or city government, often with large private firms or international lenders | local communities, residents' groups and NGOs |
| **Scale** | city-wide, large projects | local, neighbourhood projects |
| **Cost** | very high: billions | low: often funded by residents' savings, donations and small grants |
| **Speed** | slow to plan, but transforms large areas once built | quick to start, but grows slowly |
| **Who benefits** | the whole city, though often the better-off most | the poorest communities directly |
| **Risk** | local people may be ignored or displaced | may lack money and expertise to solve city-wide problems |

### Top-down strategies in Mumbai

**Vision Mumbai: the big plan**
- In the early 2000s, a business-backed plan, **Vision Mumbai**, set out to turn Mumbai into a "world-class city", with better transport, housing, air quality and finance, partly modelled on Shanghai.
- The state government adopted many of its aims.
- Its early stages included the **demolition of tens of thousands of slum homes** in 2004–05, which caused protests and was halted after criticism. This shows the risk of top-down planning that does not involve residents.

**Water supply**
- New **dams and reservoirs** in the hills outside the city, plus pipelines, to increase supply.
- Projects to **reduce leakage and illegal connections**, and to install meters.
- Upgrades to **storm-water drains** after the severe **2005 monsoon floods**, which showed that the old drains could not cope.
- Advantages: more water for the whole city; less flooding; leakage reduction saves water cheaply.
- Disadvantages: dams flood land and displace villagers outside the city; informal settlements without legal status are often not connected; projects are slow and costly.

**Waste disposal**
- Rules requiring households to **separate wet and dry waste**.
- Plans to **close or reduce** the old dumping grounds such as Deonar, and to build **waste-to-energy** and composting plants.
- Sewage treatment plants and the clean-up of the **Mithi River**.
- Advantages: less landfill and fire risk; energy from waste; cleaner creeks and less disease.
- Disadvantages: progress has been slow; enforcement is weak; waste-to-energy plants can pollute the air if poorly run; informal waste pickers, who recycle much of the city's waste, may lose their livelihood.

**Transport**
- **Mumbai Metro**: the first line opened in **2014**, and many more lines are being built across the city.
- **Suburban railway upgrades** through the Mumbai Urban Transport Project, with longer and more frequent trains, partly funded by the World Bank.
- **Sea bridges and coastal roads**: the Bandra-Worli Sea Link (2009), a trans-harbour link to Navi Mumbai (2024) and a new coastal road.
- Advantages: faster journeys; less overcrowding on trains; metro and rail cut car use and emissions.
- Disadvantages: huge cost and years of construction disruption; road projects encourage more cars; land was reclaimed from the sea and mangroves; thousands of families living beside railway tracks had to be **resettled**, sometimes far from their jobs.

**Air quality**
- Taxis, auto-rickshaws and buses switched to **compressed natural gas (CNG)**, which burns more cleanly than diesel or petrol.
- The city bus company is adding **electric buses**.
- Controls on construction dust and industrial emissions; air-quality monitoring stations.
- Advantages: lower pollution from public transport; health benefits; monitoring raises awareness.
- Disadvantages: the number of private cars and construction sites keeps rising, so air quality remains poor at many times of year; enforcement is difficult.

**Housing: the Slum Rehabilitation Authority (SRA)**
- Set up in the 1990s. Private developers rehouse eligible slum residents **free of charge** in small flats in new blocks, and in return are allowed to build extra flats on the same land to **sell at market prices**.
- A long-planned scheme to **redevelop Dharavi** in this way has been delayed for decades and was handed to a private developer in the 2020s.
- Advantages: residents get a secure, legal home with water, a toilet and electricity at no cost; the city gains formal housing without public spending.
- Disadvantages: only residents who can prove they lived there before a **cut-off date** qualify; tower blocks have no space for the **workshops** on which families earn a living; maintenance charges can be unaffordable; developers may build poor-quality blocks; communities are broken up.

### Bottom-up strategies in Mumbai

**The SPARC alliance: housing and sanitation**
- **SPARC** (an NGO founded in 1984), **Mahila Milan** (women's savings groups) and the **National Slum Dwellers Federation** work together.
- **Savings groups:** women save small amounts each day, building funds for emergencies, loans and eventually housing.
- **Enumeration:** residents survey their own settlements, counting households and mapping homes, so they can prove eligibility and negotiate with the government.
- **Community toilet blocks:** the alliance designed, built and runs hundreds of toilet blocks in informal settlements, maintained by residents, with families paying a small monthly fee. Some include children's toilets and a caretaker's room.
- **Resettlement:** the alliance helped manage the resettlement of thousands of families who lived beside railway tracks, so that the move was organised by the families themselves.
- Advantages: involves residents in decisions; very cheap; trusted by communities; empowers women; toilets are well maintained because residents run them.
- Disadvantages: small scale compared with the size of the problem; depends on government cooperation for land; cannot fix city-wide water and sewer networks.

**Health**
- Mumbai NGOs work in informal settlements on **maternal and child health**, **nutrition**, **vaccination** and preventing violence against women, often training local women as **community health workers**.
- Mobile clinics and health camps reach people far from hospitals.
- Advantages: reaches people who cannot afford or reach hospitals; prevention is cheap; local health workers understand the community.
- Disadvantages: relies on donations, so funding is insecure; cannot replace hospitals for serious illness; coverage is patchy.

**Education**
- NGOs such as **Pratham**, which began in Mumbai's informal settlements, and the **Akanksha Foundation** run pre-schools, reading classes and after-school centres, and work with government schools to improve teaching.
- Advantages: improves literacy cheaply; flexible hours suit working children; volunteers and local teachers.
- Disadvantages: small compared with need; depends on volunteers and donors; does not fix overcrowded government schools.

### Weighing them up

- Top-down strategies can tackle problems at the **scale of the whole city** (water, transport, air), which no community group could.
- But they are expensive, slow, and often benefit **wealthier** residents and businesses more, while the poorest are **displaced**.
- Bottom-up strategies are **cheap, trusted and empowering**, and work well for **local services**, but they cannot build a metro or a reservoir.
- The most successful approaches **combine** the two: for example, the government providing land and money while the SPARC alliance organises residents for rehousing and sanitation.

::: terms
- **Sustainable city**: a city that meets the needs of people today without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Top-down strategy**: a scheme planned and run by government or large organisations, usually large-scale and city-wide.
- **Bottom-up strategy**: a scheme led by local communities or NGOs, usually small-scale and focused on local needs.
- **NGO**: a non-governmental organisation, a not-for-profit group independent of government, such as a charity.
- **Vision Mumbai**: a plan from the early 2000s to turn Mumbai into a world-class city through major investment.
- **Slum Rehabilitation Authority**: a Mumbai government body that rehouses eligible slum residents in new flats built by private developers.
- **Savings group**: a group of residents, often women, who pool small regular savings to give loans and build funds.
- **Enumeration**: a survey carried out by residents to count and map households in an informal settlement.
- **Resettlement**: moving people from their homes to a new location, often to make way for a project.
- **Compressed natural gas (CNG)**: a cleaner-burning fuel used in many of Mumbai's taxis, rickshaws and buses.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the type:** top-down (government, city-wide) or bottom-up (community or NGO, local). Name a real Mumbai scheme.
2. **Match the spec:** top-down for water, waste, transport and air quality; bottom-up for housing, health and education.
3. **Advantage and disadvantage:** develop each, and say **who** gains or loses.
4. **Sustainability:** link the scheme to social, economic or environmental sustainability.
5. **8-mark evaluate:** weigh successes against failures, compare with the other approach, consider different groups, and reach a supported judgement on how successful it was.
:::

::: example Explain one advantage and one disadvantage: the SRA

An advantage is that eligible residents receive a free, legal flat with its own water and toilet (1), giving them secure tenure and better sanitation than in an informal settlement (1). Residents helped design them and run them (1), so they are cleaned and maintained, unlike many government toilets that fell into disrepair (1). They are cheap to use, with a small monthly family fee (1), so even the poorest families can afford them, reducing open defecation and disease (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: top-down success
Evaluate the success of top-down strategies in making Mumbai more sustainable. (8)

Top-down strategies have achieved things no community group could. The Mumbai Metro and upgrades to the suburban railway carry large numbers of passengers more quickly, reducing car use and emissions. Switching taxis, rickshaws and buses to CNG has cut pollution from public transport, and new dams and drain upgrades after the 2005 floods have improved water supply and reduced flood risk. These help the environmental and economic sustainability of the whole city.

However, their success is limited. Air quality is still poor because car numbers and construction keep rising. Waste remains a major problem: the Deonar dumping ground has suffered large fires and progress on waste-to-energy has been slow. Many top-down projects are expensive and take years, and some, such as sea bridges and coastal roads, encourage more car use and damage marine habitats.

Most importantly, top-down schemes have often been socially unsustainable. Vision Mumbai's early slum demolitions left tens of thousands homeless, families beside the railways were resettled far from their jobs, and SRA flats suit some residents but exclude those who arrived after the cut-off date and destroy home-based workshops.

Overall, top-down strategies have been fairly successful in environmental and economic terms, especially for transport, but much less successful socially, because the poorest residents are often ignored or displaced. They are most successful when combined with bottom-up groups such as the SPARC alliance, which give residents a voice in how change happens.

(Level 3: several strategies evaluated across social, economic and environmental sustainability, with Mumbai detail, and a substantiated conclusion.)
:::

::: watch
- Mixing up the spec's pairings: **top-down** for water, waste, transport and air quality; **bottom-up** for housing, health and education.
- Calling the SRA bottom-up. It is a **government** body working through private developers, so it is **top-down**.
- Describing a scheme without an **advantage and a disadvantage**: every question in this key idea is about weighing.
- Forgetting **who** benefits. Strong answers say whether the wealthy, the poor, businesses or the environment gain.
- Praising schemes without mentioning **displacement** or exclusion, such as the cut-off date.
