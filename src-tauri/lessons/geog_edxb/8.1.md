## What it is

Topic 8, **Forests under threat**, is examined in **Paper 3 Section B** (12 marks of short questions on a resource booklet), and forest content can also feed the **Section D decision** (12-mark select-and-justify plus 4 SPaG). This key idea says the rainforest's **structure, functioning and adaptations** reflect the **equatorial climate**. It covers:
- how **biotic** (living) and **abiotic** (non-living) parts are **interdependent**: climate, soil, water, plants, animals and humans;
- how **plants** (stratified layers, buttress roots, drip tips) and **animals** are adapted to the climate;
- why rainforests have a **very high rate of nutrient cycling**, which supports **high biodiversity** and **complex food webs**;
- the skill of interpreting **nutrient cycle (Gersmehl) diagrams** and **food webs**.

Any real tropical rainforest earns the marks. This course uses the **Amazon** (mainly in Brazil) as its example, with references to the Congo Basin and South-east Asia.

### Where and why: the equatorial climate

Tropical rainforests lie roughly between the **Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn**, mostly within about 10° of the **Equator**: the Amazon basin, the Congo basin, and South-east Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, Papua New Guinea).

| Climate feature | What it is like | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | hot all year, around 26–28 °C | the sun is high in the sky all year, so insolation is intense |
| **Annual range** | very small, often only 2–3 °C | no real seasons near the Equator |
| **Rainfall** | high, usually over 2,000 mm a year | rising air in the low-pressure belt (the ITCZ) gives **convectional rain**, often as afternoon thunderstorms |
| **Humidity** | very high | constant evaporation and **transpiration** from dense vegetation |
| **Growing season** | all year round | warmth and water are never limiting |

The key point for the exam: **heat + water + light all year = continuous plant growth**. That single fact explains most of what follows.

### Interdependence: everything depends on everything else

**Interdependence** means the parts of the ecosystem rely on each other, so a change to one part causes changes elsewhere.

- **Climate → plants:** constant heat, rain and light give rapid growth all year and the largest **biomass** of any land biome.
- **Plants → climate:** trees release water by **transpiration**; much of the Amazon's rain is **recycled** forest moisture, so removing trees reduces rainfall.
- **Plants ↔ soil:** the canopy shields the soil from heavy rain and roots bind it. Soils are deep but **infertile** red **latosols**, because rain **leaches** nutrients away; plants survive only because litter is recycled fast.
- **Plants ↔ animals:** trees give food and habitat at every height; animals **pollinate** flowers (bats, bees, birds) and **disperse seeds** (monkeys, toucans); decomposers (fungi, termites) release nutrients.
- **Humans:** indigenous peoples such as the **Yanomami** hunt, fish and practise small-scale **shifting cultivation**, clearing small plots then moving on so the forest regrows. Large-scale clearing breaks the links: soil is exposed, nutrients wash away and rainfall can decline.

**Exam tip:** an "explain the interdependence" answer must show a **chain**: "if X changes, then Y, so Z". Two linked parts are worth more than a list.

### Structure: the stratified layers

The forest is **stratified** into layers, each with its own light, humidity and wildlife.

| Layer | Height (roughly) | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergents** | above about 40 m | the tallest trees poke through the canopy; exposed to wind and full sun; small, waxy leaves; harpy eagles nest here |
| **Canopy** | about 20–40 m | a continuous roof of crowns; catches most of the light; most animals live here; rich in fruit and flowers |
| **Understorey** | about 5–20 m | young trees and shade-tolerant species waiting for a gap; little light |
| **Shrub layer and forest floor** | 0–5 m | very dark (only a few per cent of the sunlight reaches it); thin leaf litter; fungi and decomposers |

### Plant adaptations

| Adaptation | Description | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Buttress roots** | large ridged roots spreading from the base of tall trees | support very tall trees in **shallow** soil; roots stay near the surface where the nutrients are |
| **Drip tips** | leaves with waxy surfaces and pointed tips | shed heavy rain quickly, so the leaf does not rot or grow algae and fungi |
| **Shallow roots** | roots spread widely in the top layer | absorb nutrients released from the litter before rain leaches them away |
| **Lianas** | woody climbers rooted in the ground | climb trees to reach the light of the canopy |
| **Epiphytes** | plants such as orchids and bromeliads growing on branches | reach light high up; take moisture from the humid air and nutrients from debris |
| **Large leaves low down** | understorey plants have big, dark leaves | capture as much of the dim light as possible |

### Animal adaptations

- **Spider monkeys** have long limbs and a **prehensile tail** that grips branches, so they move through the canopy without coming to the ground.
- **Sloths** move slowly to save energy on a low-energy leaf diet; **algae** growing in their fur give them camouflage.
- **Toucans** have large, light beaks to reach fruit on thin branches; they disperse seeds in their droppings.
- **Jaguars** have spotted coats for camouflage in the dappled light of the forest floor, and swim well in flooded forest.
- **Poison dart frogs** have bright warning colours advertising toxins, and breed in water trapped in bromeliads.
- Many animals are **nocturnal** to avoid heat and predators, or **specialists** feeding on one plant, which reduces competition.

- **Biomass is by far the largest store.** Most nutrients are locked up in the trees and plants themselves, not in the soil.
- **Litter is a small store** because leaves that fall are broken down within weeks by fungi, bacteria and termites in the hot, wet conditions.
- **Soil is a small store** because heavy rain **leaches** nutrients out, and because plant roots take them up almost as soon as they are released.
- **All the flows are large and fast**: fallout, decomposition and uptake run all year with no cold or dry season to stop them.

**Why this supports high biodiversity:** constant, rapid cycling plus all-year warmth and water gives the highest **net primary productivity** (new plant growth) of any land biome. More plant growth means more food, at every layer, for more animals. The layered structure creates many **niches** (roles and habitats), so many species can live side by side by specialising. Tropical rainforests cover only a small share of the land surface but are often estimated to hold around **half** of all known land species.

**The trap:** because the soil is a small store, **cleared rainforest soil is poor**. Once the trees are removed, the main store is gone and the cycle is broken. Farmland on cleared rainforest loses fertility within a few years.

### Food webs

A **food web** shows feeding links between many species. Rainforest food webs are **complex**: many producers, many consumers, and many species feeding at more than one level.

- **Producers** (trees, lianas, epiphytes) feed **primary consumers** (leafcutter ants, sloths, fruit bats, macaws, monkeys), which feed **secondary consumers** (frogs, snakes, ocelots) and **top predators** (jaguars, harpy eagles, anacondas). **Decomposers** (fungi, bacteria, termites) return nutrients to the soil.
- With many alternative food sources, losing one species rarely collapses the web, but losing a **keystone species** (such as a fig tree that fruits all year) affects many others.
- Food web **arrows** point in the direction energy flows: from the eaten to the eater.

::: terms
- **Biome**: a large-scale global ecosystem, such as tropical rainforest or taiga, defined mainly by climate and vegetation.
- **Biotic**: the living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and people.
- **Abiotic**: the non-living parts of an ecosystem, such as climate, soil, water and rock.
- **Interdependence**: the way the parts of an ecosystem rely on each other, so a change to one part affects the others.
- **Stratification**: the arrangement of the rainforest into distinct vertical layers: emergents, canopy, understorey and shrub layer.
- **Buttress roots**: large ridged roots at the base of tall trees that support them in shallow soil.
- **Drip tip**: a pointed tip on a waxy leaf that sheds heavy rain quickly to stop rot.
- **Epiphyte**: a plant that grows on another plant for support, taking moisture from the air.
- **Nutrient cycle**: the movement of nutrients between the biomass, litter and soil stores in an ecosystem.
- **Leaching**: the washing of nutrients downwards and out of the soil by water draining through it.
- **Biodiversity**: the variety of plant and animal species, and of their genes and habitats, in an area.
- **Food web**: a diagram showing the many feeding relationships between species in an ecosystem.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the resource first.** Paper 3 questions say "Study Figure n" or "Using evidence from Figure n". Quote a figure, label or value from it in your answer.
2. **Link to climate.** Almost every rainforest answer comes back to hot, wet, constant conditions: name the climate feature, then the effect.
3. **Adaptations:** name the feature, describe it, then say **how it helps survival** in this climate. "Drip tips shed water" is only half the mark; add "so the leaf does not rot".
4. **Interdependence:** write a chain of at least two links ("trees transpire, which adds moisture to the air, so rainfall is recycled").
5. **Gersmehl diagrams:** say which store is largest (biomass), which are small (litter, soil), and which flows are large, then explain each with a reason.
6. **Extended answers:** for an 8-mark "assess", weigh more than one factor, use the resource (AO4) and finish with a judgement about which matters most.
:::

::: example Identify: the largest store
Study a Gersmehl nutrient cycle diagram for a tropical rainforest. Identify the largest nutrient store. (1)

The biomass (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: drip tips
Explain one way rainforest plants are adapted to the climate. (2)

Many leaves have waxy surfaces and drip tips (1), so the very heavy daily rainfall runs off quickly and the leaf does not rot or become covered in algae (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: interdependence
Explain two ways in which the biotic and abiotic parts of the tropical rainforest are interdependent. (4)

Trees take up water from the soil and release it by transpiration (1), which adds moisture to the air and helps produce the convectional rain the forest needs (1). The dense canopy intercepts heavy rain and roots bind the soil (1), which protects the thin, infertile soil from being eroded (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: nutrient cycling and biodiversity
Study a resource showing a Gersmehl diagram (large biomass store, small litter and soil stores, thick flows) and a table of species counts per hectare for rainforest and other biomes. Assess the importance of the nutrient cycle in supporting the high biodiversity of tropical rainforests. (8)

The resource shows almost all nutrients stored in the biomass, with small litter and soil stores and large flows. Leaves decompose within weeks in the hot, wet conditions and shallow roots take nutrients back up before rain leaches them away, so plants grow continuously and there is food at every level of the forest.

However, the cycle is only fast because of the climate: constant warmth and rainfall above 2,000 mm keep decomposers and plants working all year. Structure matters too. Stratification into emergents, canopy, understorey and shrub layer creates many niches, so specialists such as canopy epiphytes or frogs breeding in bromeliads can coexist. The species table shows far more species per hectare in rainforest than other biomes, reflecting both food supply and niches.

Overall, the cycle is essential, because the infertile soil could not otherwise support such dense growth, but it is the mechanism through which the equatorial climate acts, and the layered structure multiplies its effect.

(Level 3: uses the resource throughout (AO4), weighs the cycle against climate and structure, and reaches a supported judgement (AO3).)
:::

::: q
State two features of the climate of a tropical rainforest. (2)
---
Any two: high temperatures all year, around 26–28 °C (1); small annual temperature range (1); high rainfall, often over 2,000 mm a year (1); high humidity (1); daily convectional rain or thunderstorms (1).
:::

::: q
Name the layer of the rainforest that receives the least sunlight. (1)
---
The shrub layer or forest floor (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one adaptation of a named rainforest animal. (2)
---
Spider monkeys have a prehensile tail and long limbs (1), so they can grip branches and move through the canopy without coming down to the ground, where predators are (1). Accept any valid animal with a feature and a developed survival benefit.
:::

::: q
Explain why the litter store is small in a tropical rainforest. (2)
---
The hot, wet conditions all year are ideal for decomposers such as fungi and termites (1), so fallen leaves are broken down within weeks and the nutrients pass rapidly into the soil and back to plants (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest one reason why clearing a rainforest for farming may lead to falling crop yields after a few years. (3)
---
Most of the nutrients are stored in the biomass, not the soil (1); when the trees are cleared and burned, this store is removed and the cycle is broken (1); heavy rain then leaches the remaining nutrients from the exposed soil, so it quickly becomes infertile (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why tropical rainforests have complex food webs. (4)
---
Two from: high productivity all year (1) provides abundant fruit, leaves and nectar to support many consumers (1); stratification creates many niches (1), so many specialist species can coexist at different heights (1); many species feed at more than one level (1), creating many interlinked feeding relationships (1); no cold or dry season (1), so food chains are not interrupted and many top predators can be supported (1).
:::

::: watch
- Saying rainforest soils are **fertile**. They are poor: the nutrients are in the **biomass**, not the soil.
- Describing an adaptation without saying **how it helps** in this climate.
- Listing climate, soil and plants without showing the **links** between them. Interdependence needs a chain.
- Getting Gersmehl circles wrong: in the rainforest the **biomass** circle is biggest and the litter circle is smallest.
- Reading food web arrows the wrong way: they point **from food to feeder**.
- Ignoring the figure. Paper 3 marks "using evidence from Figure n" questions down if no evidence is quoted.
:::

Daily heavy convectional rain falls on the leaves (1), and the drip tip and waxy surface shed the water quickly so the leaf does not rot or grow algae that would block light (1).

---
Animals such as toucans and monkeys eat fruit and spread the seeds in their droppings (1), so new trees grow away from the parent tree and the forest regenerates (1). Or: bats, birds and insects pollinate flowers (1), allowing plants to reproduce (1).

::: test
Explain one way that indigenous people are part of the interdependence of the rainforest. (2)
---
Groups such as the Yanomami practise small-scale shifting cultivation, clearing small plots and moving on (1), so the forest regrows and the nutrient cycle recovers, keeping the system sustainable (1). ::: test
Suggest one reason why the shrub layer of the rainforest has fewer plants than the canopy. (3)
---
The dense canopy above intercepts most of the sunlight (1), so only a small fraction reaches the forest floor (1); without enough light, few plants can photosynthesise enough to grow, so only shade-tolerant species and seedlings waiting for a light gap survive (1).
:::

::: test
Using evidence from a figure showing that over half of the Amazon's rainfall is recycled moisture from the forest, explain one way in which the forest and the climate are interdependent. (3)
---
Trees take up water from the soil and release it through transpiration (1); the figure shows much of the rain is recycled from the forest itself (1); so if trees are removed, less moisture enters the air and rainfall may fall, which would stress the remaining forest (1).
:::

::: test
Explain two ways in which the climate of the tropical rainforest leads to high biodiversity. (4)
---
Two from: constant high temperatures and rainfall (1) allow plants to grow all year, giving high productivity and plentiful food (1); no cold or dry season (1), so species do not need to migrate or hibernate and many specialists survive (1); hot wet conditions speed decomposition (1), so nutrients are recycled quickly and support dense plant growth (1); intense light and warmth produce tall layered forest (1), creating many niches for different species (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage, for the ecosystem, of the rainforest storing most of its nutrients in the biomass. (4)
---
Advantage (1): nutrients are kept within living plants where roots can quickly reuse them, supporting dense growth and high biodiversity (1). Disadvantage (1): when the trees are cleared or burned the main store is lost, and the infertile soil cannot support regrowth or farming for long (1).
:::
