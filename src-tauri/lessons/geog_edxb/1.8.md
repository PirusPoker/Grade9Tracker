## What it is

The lithosphere is broken into about a dozen large plates and many smaller ones. Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen where plates meet, at **plate boundaries**, or above **hotspots**. This key idea covers where the boundaries are, what happens at each type, and why their **volcanic and earthquake hazards differ**.

Pearson uses the terms **convergent** (plates moving together), **divergent** (plates moving apart) and **conservative** (plates sliding past). Other textbooks call these destructive, constructive and conservative; both are accepted, but lead with Pearson's words. A **collision** boundary is a type of convergent boundary where two continental plates meet.

### Skill: the world map of plate boundaries

| Feature | Where | Type |
|---|---|---|
| **Pacific Ring of Fire** | around the edge of the Pacific: Andes, Japan, Philippines, Indonesia, Alaska, US north-west | mainly convergent |
| **Mid-Atlantic Ridge** | down the middle of the Atlantic, through Iceland | divergent |
| **East African Rift** | Ethiopia to Mozambique, on land | divergent |
| **Himalayas** | Indian plate meeting Eurasian plate, including Nepal | collision (convergent) |
| **San Andreas Fault** | California, Pacific plate against North American plate | conservative |
| **Hotspots** | Hawaii (middle of the Pacific plate), Iceland, Yellowstone | not at a boundary (except Iceland) |

When describing a map, use compass directions and named oceans and continents, note that activity forms **narrow belts** along boundaries, and point out **anomalies** such as Hawaii, far from any boundary.

### What happens at each boundary

| Boundary | Movement | Volcanoes | Earthquakes | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Divergent** | plates move apart; magma rises to fill the gap, forming new crust | yes: **shield volcanoes** and fissure eruptions; runny basaltic lava | frequent, small, shallow | Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland, East African Rift |
| **Convergent** (oceanic meets continental, or oceanic meets oceanic) | the denser oceanic plate is **subducted**, melting as it sinks | yes: explosive **composite volcanoes** | frequent, shallow to deep; the **largest** earthquakes on Earth; can cause **tsunamis** | Andes, Japan, Philippines |
| **Collision** (continental meets continental) | neither plate sinks, so crust crumples upwards into **fold mountains** | no | powerful, shallow | Himalayas, Nepal |
| **Conservative** | plates slide past each other, sticking and then slipping | no | can be powerful and shallow | San Andreas Fault |
| **Hotspot** | a plume of very hot mantle rises under a plate; as the plate moves, a chain of volcanoes forms | yes: shield volcanoes | some, linked to eruptions | Hawaii |

### Why volcanic hazards differ

The key chain is **boundary, then magma type, then volcano type, then explosivity, then hazards**.

| | **Basaltic** magma (divergent, hotspot) | **Andesitic** magma (convergent) |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | melted mantle rising directly | melted oceanic plate plus crust, adding silica and water |
| Silica | low | higher |
| Viscosity | low (runny) | high (sticky) |
| Gas | escapes easily | trapped, building pressure |
| Temperature | hotter, about 1,000–1,200 °C | cooler, about 800–1,000 °C |
| Eruption | **effusive**: frequent, gentle, lava fountains and flows | **explosive**: less frequent, violent |
| Volcano shape | **shield**: wide, gentle slopes, as runny lava travels far | **composite (stratovolcano)**: steep cone of alternating ash and lava layers |
| Main hazards | **lava flows** that destroy property but usually move slowly enough to escape; gases | **pyroclastic flows** (fast, very hot avalanches of gas, ash and rock), **ash fall**, **lahars** (volcanic mudflows), volcanic bombs |

So composite volcanoes at convergent boundaries are far more dangerous to life. Shield volcanoes threaten property more than people.

### Why earthquake hazards differ

An earthquake starts at the **focus** underground; the point on the surface directly above is the **epicentre**.

- **Depth.** **Shallow-focus** earthquakes (less than about 70 km deep) are much more damaging, because the seismic waves lose little energy before reaching the surface. **Deep-focus** earthquakes (up to about 700 km, only at convergent boundaries) are felt over wide areas but shake the surface less.
- **Magnitude.** The energy released. Convergent boundaries produce the largest (magnitude 9 and above), as huge areas of plate lock and then slip.
- **Tsunami.** When an undersea earthquake at a convergent boundary suddenly lifts or drops the sea floor, the whole column of water above is displaced. Waves race across the ocean at hundreds of km/h with little height, then slow and grow tall in shallow coastal water. The sea often draws back first, a natural warning.

### Skill: the Richter scale

The **Richter scale** measures earthquake magnitude. It is **logarithmic**: each whole number up the scale means about **10 times** greater ground movement (amplitude) and about **30 times** more energy. Scientists now usually use the **moment magnitude scale**, which gives similar numbers for most earthquakes.

To compare two earthquakes: amplitude difference = 10 raised to the power of the magnitude difference. A magnitude 8 is 10 times the amplitude of a 7, and 10 × 10 = **100 times** that of a 6.

::: terms
- **Plate boundary**: the line where two tectonic plates meet, where most earthquakes and volcanoes occur.
- **Convergent boundary**: a boundary where plates move towards each other; also called a destructive boundary.
- **Divergent boundary**: a boundary where plates move apart and new crust forms; also called a constructive boundary.
- **Conservative boundary**: a boundary where plates slide past each other with no crust created or destroyed.
- **Subduction**: the sinking of a denser oceanic plate beneath another plate into the mantle.
- **Hotspot**: an area of volcanic activity far from a plate boundary, above a rising plume of hot mantle.
- **Shield volcano**: a wide, gently sloping volcano built from runny basaltic lava.
- **Composite volcano**: a steep-sided cone of alternating ash and lava layers, with explosive eruptions.
- **Pyroclastic flow**: a fast-moving, extremely hot avalanche of gas, ash and rock from an explosive eruption.
- **Lahar**: a volcanic mudflow of ash, rock and water.
- **Focus**: the point underground where an earthquake starts.
- **Tsunami**: a series of large waves caused by the sudden displacement of water, usually by an undersea earthquake.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the boundary** from the movement or diagram: apart, together or sliding past. Use Pearson's term.
2. **Volcano questions:** build the full chain: boundary, magma type (silica, viscosity, gas), volcano type, eruption style, hazards.
3. **Earthquake questions:** use depth (shallow is more damaging), magnitude, and the boundary type; for tsunamis, the vertical displacement of the sea floor.
4. **Richter calculations:** find the difference in magnitude, then 10 to that power; show working.
5. **Distribution:** name belts and boundaries, give directions, and note anomalies such as hotspots.
6. **8-mark assess:** compare boundaries, explain the magma chain, weigh other factors (population, eruption frequency, warning), and judge.
:::

::: example Identify the boundary
A diagram shows two plates moving apart beneath an ocean, with magma rising between them. Identify this type of plate boundary. A: Collision. B: Conservative. C: Convergent. D: Divergent. (1)

D (1). The plates are moving apart, not together or past each other.
:::

::: example Explain two: volcano characteristics
Explain two reasons why volcanoes have different characteristics. (4)

Magma type (1): basaltic magma at divergent boundaries is low in silica and runny, so it flows far and builds wide, gently sloping shield volcanoes (1). Gas content (1): andesitic magma at convergent boundaries is sticky and traps gas, so pressure builds until it explodes, forming steep composite cones (1).
:::

::: example Richter comparison
Sample data: Earthquake A measured 9.0 and Earthquake B measured 7.0. Calculate how many times greater the ground movement (amplitude) was in A than in B. (2)

9.0 − 7.0 = 2, so 10² (1) = 100 times greater (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: volcanic hazards
Assess the reasons why some plate boundaries produce more hazardous volcanic eruptions than others. (8)

The main reason is magma type, which depends on the boundary. At convergent boundaries, the subducted oceanic plate melts and the magma picks up silica and water. This andesitic magma is viscous and traps gas, so pressure builds until composite volcanoes erupt explosively, producing pyroclastic flows, ash clouds and lahars. These hazards travel fast and far, so they kill many people.

At divergent boundaries and hotspots, basaltic magma rises directly from the mantle. It is low in silica and runny, so gas escapes and eruptions are effusive. Lava flows destroy property but usually move slowly enough for people to escape. Conservative and collision boundaries have no volcanoes at all.

Other factors also matter. Long gaps between eruptions let pressure build and lull people into settling nearby. Population density on fertile volcanic slopes raises the risk, and monitoring and warnings can reduce it.

Overall, plate boundary type is the most important reason, because it controls magma type and explosivity. Human factors determine how many people are affected by an eruption, but they do not change how hazardous the eruption itself is.

(Level 3: full chain from boundary to hazard, other factors weighed, and a clear judgement.)
:::

Name the type of plate boundary found along the San Andreas Fault. (1)
---
Conservative (1).

::: q
Explain one reason why there are no volcanoes at conservative boundaries. (2)
---
The plates slide past each other, so no crust is destroyed or created (1); there is no subduction or gap for magma to form or rise through (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why shallow-focus earthquakes cause more damage than deep ones. (2)
---
Seismic waves travel only a short distance to the surface (1), so they lose less energy and the shaking is stronger (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how a hotspot forms a chain of volcanic islands. (3)
---
A plume of hot mantle rises beneath the middle of a plate (1), melting through to form a volcano (1). As the plate moves over the fixed hotspot, older volcanoes are carried away and new ones form, creating a chain such as Hawaii (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way an undersea earthquake causes a tsunami. (3)
---
At a convergent boundary the sea floor is suddenly lifted or dropped (1). This displaces the whole column of water above it (1), creating waves that grow in height as they reach shallow coastal water (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two hazards produced by composite volcanoes. (4)
---
Two from: pyroclastic flows (1), very hot, fast avalanches of gas and ash that destroy everything in their path (1); ash fall (1) collapses roofs, ruins crops and grounds aircraft (1); lahars (1), mudflows that follow river valleys and bury towns (1); volcanic bombs (1), large rocks thrown out (1).
:::

::: watch
- Using only "constructive/destructive". Pearson's papers say **convergent/divergent**; recognise both.
- Saying collision or conservative boundaries have volcanoes. They do not.
- Naming a volcano type without explaining **why**: shields need runny, low-silica lava; composites need sticky, gas-rich magma.
- Getting the Richter maths wrong: one whole number is about 10 times the amplitude, two is 100 times, not 20.
- Calling a tsunami a tidal wave, or a primary hazard of the sea. It is caused by sea-floor displacement.
- Forgetting hotspots on distribution questions: Hawaii is the classic anomaly.
:::
