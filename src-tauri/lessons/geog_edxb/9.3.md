## What it is

This key idea is part of **Topic 9, Consuming energy resources**, examined in **Paper 3 Section C** (resource-based questions with two 8-mark questions) and often feeding the **Section D decision** (12 marks plus 4 SPaG). It says that **global demand for oil is increasing, but supplies are unevenly available**. It covers:
- how **oil reserves and production are unevenly distributed**;
- why **oil consumption is growing**: **rising GDP per capita** and **rapid industrialisation in emerging economies**;
- how **oil supply and price** are affected by **international relations** (conflicts, diplomatic relations) and **economic factors** (recession against boom, over-supply against under-supply);
- the skill of using **oil price and production data to graph trends over time**.

Any real events and countries of the right type earn the marks. This course uses OPEC and Saudi Arabia, the USA's shale boom, China and India as growing consumers, and five price events: the 1973 oil crisis, the 2008 spike and crash, the 2014–16 fall, the 2020 pandemic collapse and the 2022 rise after Russia's invasion of Ukraine.

Prices are quoted in **US dollars per barrel** (a barrel is about 159 litres). Learn the shape of the price story rather than exact numbers.

### Where the oil is: reserves and production

**Proven reserves** are oil known to exist that can be extracted profitably with current technology. They are highly concentrated:
- The **Middle East** holds the largest share: **Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait and the UAE**.
- **Venezuela** reports the largest single total, mostly heavy oil that is costly to extract.
- **Canada** has huge reserves in the Alberta **oil sands**; **Russia**, the **USA**, **Libya** and **Nigeria** also have large reserves.
- Many large consumers, such as Japan, South Korea, most of Europe and India, have very little oil of their own.

**Production** is not the same as reserves. The biggest producers are the **USA** (top since the late 2010s, thanks to **shale oil**), **Saudi Arabia** and **Russia**, followed by Canada, Iraq and China. A country can have big reserves and low production if it lacks investment or faces sanctions, as Venezuela does.

**OPEC** (the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, founded in 1960) is a group of oil exporters, mostly in the Middle East and Africa, plus Venezuela. Its members hold most of the world's proven reserves. By agreeing **production quotas** they can raise or lower world supply and so influence the price. Since 2016 OPEC has cooperated with other exporters, including Russia, as **OPEC+**.

### Who uses the oil: consumption is growing

Oil is used mainly for **transport** (petrol, diesel, jet fuel, shipping fuel), and also for **petrochemicals** (plastics, fertilisers), heating and some electricity. The largest consumers are the **USA**, **China** and **India**. The **mismatch** between where oil is produced and where it is used drives a huge global trade by tanker and pipeline.

Why consumption keeps rising:

| Cause | How it raises demand | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Rising GDP per capita** | richer people buy cars, fly more and buy more goods that are transported and made from plastics | car ownership and air travel rising fast in China and India |
| **Rapid industrialisation** in **emerging economies** | factories, construction and freight transport all need fuel and petrochemicals | China became the world's largest importer of crude oil |
| **Population growth and urbanisation** | more people and bigger cities need more transport and goods | fast-growing cities across Asia and Africa |
| **Globalisation** | goods shipped and flown around the world | container ships and air freight burn oil |

In many **developed** countries oil demand has levelled off or fallen, because of efficiency, electric vehicles and slower population growth. So almost all **growth** in world demand now comes from emerging economies.

### What moves the oil price

The price is set by **supply and demand**. Every price event in the exam comes back to one of two mechanisms:
- **Supply falls or is threatened, or demand rises** → oil is scarcer → **price rises**.
- **Supply rises, or demand falls** → oil is in surplus → **price falls**.

**International relations (political factors)**
- **Conflict:** war in or near producing regions cuts output or threatens shipping routes. Around a fifth of the world's oil passes through the **Strait of Hormuz**, so tension in the Gulf raises prices.
- **Diplomatic relations:** **sanctions** on exporters such as Iran, Venezuela or Russia reduce the oil they can sell; **embargoes** stop sales to particular countries; agreements within **OPEC** or **OPEC+** to cut or raise output change supply deliberately.

**Economic factors**
- **Boom:** fast global growth raises demand, so prices rise.
- **Recession:** factories slow down and people travel less, so demand and prices fall.
- **Over-supply:** new sources (such as US shale) or producers refusing to cut output flood the market, so prices fall.
- **Under-supply:** too little investment in new fields, or disruption, leaves supply short of demand, so prices rise.

### Five events to know

| Event | What happened | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **1973 oil crisis** | Arab OPEC members imposed an **embargo** on countries supporting Israel in the Yom Kippur War and cut output; the price roughly **quadrupled** within months | political: diplomatic action cut supply |
| **2008 spike and crash** | booming demand, especially from China, pushed prices to record highs of nearly 150 US dollars a barrel in mid-2008; the **global financial crisis** then caused a deep **recession** and prices crashed by the end of the year | economic: boom, then recession |
| **2014–16 fall** | the **US shale boom** added large new supply; OPEC, led by Saudi Arabia, chose not to cut output to defend its market share; prices fell from over 100 to below 30 US dollars a barrel by early 2016 | economic over-supply, plus an OPEC political choice |
| **2020 pandemic collapse** | lockdowns stopped travel and industry, so demand fell sharply; storage filled up and one US benchmark price briefly fell **below zero** in April 2020; OPEC+ then agreed record cuts | economic: sudden demand collapse |
| **2022 rise** | after **Russia's invasion of Ukraine**, sanctions and fears of lost Russian supply pushed prices above 100 US dollars a barrel | political: conflict and sanctions threatened supply |

### Graphing oil price and production data

The integrated skill is drawing and reading **line graphs** of oil price or production over time.
- Put **time on the x-axis** and price or production on the y-axis, with an even scale.
- Plot points accurately and join them with straight lines.
- Describe trends with **data**: the overall trend, the highest and lowest values with their dates, and any sudden changes ("rose from about 40 to over 100 US dollars between 2009 and 2011, then fell by more than half in 2014–16").
- Use **percentage change** = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
- On a dual graph of price and production, look for links: a production surge (US shale) followed by a price fall.

::: terms
- **Proven reserves**: the amount of oil known to exist that can be extracted profitably with current technology.
- **OPEC**: the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, a group of oil exporters that coordinates production to influence prices.
- **Production quota**: a limit on how much oil a country agrees to produce, used by OPEC to control supply.
- **Embargo**: an official ban on trade with a particular country, such as refusing to sell it oil.
- **Sanctions**: penalties, such as trade bans, placed on a country by others to pressure it politically.
- **Recession**: a period when an economy shrinks, with falling output and spending, which lowers demand for oil.
- **Over-supply**: when more oil is produced than is demanded, so stocks build up and prices fall.
- **Energy security**: having reliable access to enough affordable energy to meet a country's needs.
- **Shale oil**: oil trapped in fine-grained shale rock, extracted by horizontal drilling and fracking.
- **Emerging economy**: a country with rapid economic growth and industrialisation, such as China, India or Brazil.
- **Chokepoint**: a narrow sea passage through which a large share of world oil is shipped, such as the Strait of Hormuz.
:::

::: method
1. **Distribution questions:** name regions and countries for reserves, production and consumption, and point out the mismatch between them.
2. **Why demand is rising:** link GDP per capita and industrialisation to a **use** of oil (cars, freight, plastics) and name an emerging economy.
3. **Price questions:** always give the **mechanism**: event → supply or demand changes → price rises or falls. Naming an event alone scores one mark.
4. **Classify the cause** as political (conflict, embargo, sanctions, OPEC decisions) or economic (boom, recession, over- or under-supply). Some events are both.
5. **Graph questions:** quote values and dates, describe the overall trend, the peak, the low and any sudden change; calculate percentage change accurately.
6. **For 8-mark assess:** use the resource (AO4), weigh political against economic causes using at least two events (AO3), and conclude which matters more and why.
:::

::: example Identify: largest reserves
Study a world map of proven oil reserves. Identify the region with the largest proven oil reserves. (1)

The Middle East (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: percentage change in price
Sample data: the oil price was 110 US dollars a barrel in June 2014 and 33 US dollars a barrel in January 2016. Calculate the percentage change. Give your answer to one decimal place. (2)

(33 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100 (1) = −70.0%, a fall of 70.0% (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: rising consumption
Explain one reason why global oil consumption is increasing. (2)

Emerging economies such as China and India are industrialising rapidly (1), so factories, freight transport and construction need more diesel, fuel and petrochemicals each year (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: political effects on supply
Explain two ways international relations can affect the supply of oil. (4)

Conflict in producing regions can damage oil facilities or threaten tanker routes such as the Strait of Hormuz (1), reducing supply and pushing prices up (1). Sanctions on exporters such as Iran or Russia restrict how much oil they can sell (1), removing supply from the world market (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: political against economic factors
Study a resource showing a line graph of the oil price from 2000 to 2023, annotated with the 2008 financial crisis, the US shale boom, the 2020 pandemic and Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. Assess the importance of political factors compared with economic factors in changing oil prices. (8)

The graph shows the biggest falls were caused by economic factors. The 2008 financial crisis caused a deep recession, so demand fell and the price crashed from its peak. The US shale boom created over-supply and the price more than halved in 2014–16. The pandemic collapsed demand in 2020, giving the lowest point on the graph.

Political factors cause sharp rises. The 2022 invasion of Ukraine and the sanctions that followed raised fears over Russian supply, and the price rose above 100 US dollars. In 1973, an OPEC embargo quadrupled prices.

However, the two are linked: the 2014–16 fall happened partly because OPEC chose not to cut output, a political decision, and the 2020 recovery came when OPEC+ agreed cuts.

Overall, economic factors explain the long-term trend and the largest falls, but political factors cause the most sudden spikes, and OPEC decisions often decide how far prices move.

(Level 3: uses the annotated graph throughout (AO4), weighs both sets of factors with linked mechanisms, and reaches a supported judgement (AO3).)
:::

International sanctions may stop it selling oil abroad (1), so there is little reason to produce it (1); and without foreign investment it cannot afford the technology to maintain or develop its fields, as in Venezuela (1). Explain two reasons why oil demand is growing faster in emerging economies than in developed countries. (4)
---
Two from: rising incomes in emerging economies (1) mean many people are buying their first cars and flying for the first time (1); rapid industrialisation (1) needs fuel for factories, freight and petrochemicals (1); developed countries already have high car ownership (1), and efficiency and electric vehicles are slowing their demand (1).
:::

::: watch
- Naming an event without the **mechanism**: always say what happened to supply or demand, and then to price.
- Confusing **reserves** (what is in the ground) with **production** (what is extracted each year).
- Getting percentage change wrong: divide by the **old** value, and say whether it is a rise or a fall.
- Describing a graph with no **data or dates**. Quote values from the axis.
- Writing a bare dollar sign. Write "US dollars".
- In an 8-marker, treating political and economic factors as separate when events such as 2014–16 involved both.
:::
