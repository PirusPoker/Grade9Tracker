## What it is

This key idea explains why there is such a variety of river landscapes in the UK and which processes shape them. It covers:
- how river landscapes **contrast** between the **upper, middle and lower courses** of a **named UK river**, and why **channel shape** (width, depth), **valley profile**, **gradient**, **discharge**, **velocity** and **sediment size and shape** change along its course;
- how **erosion** (hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution), **transport** (traction, saltation, suspension, solution) and **deposition** form **meanders**, **interlocking spurs**, **waterfalls**, **flood plains**, **levees**, **oxbow lakes** and **deltas**;
- how **climate**, **geology** and **slope processes** influence river landscapes and sediment load, and how **storm hydrographs** and **lag times** are explained by physical factors: **geology**, **soil type**, **slope**, **drainage basin shape** and **antecedent conditions**.

Integrated skills: drawing a **valley cross-section from contours**, using **BGS geology maps** to link a river's **long profile** to its rocks, and **recognising river landforms on OS maps**. Paper 2 Question 3 is the rivers question: often "For a named UK river, explain one difference between its upper and lower course (2)" and an "Explain two…" (4). Any named UK river earns the marks; this course uses the **River Tees**.

### The River Tees: source to mouth

- **Source**: high on the slopes of **Cross Fell** in the **North Pennines**, in wet, peaty moorland.
- It flows **east** through **Teesdale**, past **Barnard Castle**, then across the lowlands past **Yarm** and **Stockton-on-Tees**, reaching the **North Sea** through a wide **estuary** at **Teesside** (Middlesbrough).
- **Upper course**: narrow, steep **V-shaped valley** and **interlocking spurs**. Waterfalls and rapids include **Cauldron Snout**, **High Force** and **Low Force**. At High Force the river plunges over the hard igneous **Whin Sill** (dolerite) onto softer limestone, shale and sandstone below, with a **gorge** downstream.
- **Middle course**: the valley widens; the river **meanders** across a developing **flood plain** around Barnard Castle and Darlington.
- **Lower course**: large **meanders** loop across a wide, flat **flood plain**; Yarm sits inside a meander loop. Old meander scars and **oxbow lakes** lie on the flood plain, and some meanders near Stockton were cut through by people in the past to shorten the route for shipping. The **Tees Barrage** at Stockton controls the tidal water upstream. The estuary has mudflats and is lined by port and industrial land.

### The Bradshaw model: downstream changes

The **Bradshaw model** summarises how a river changes from source to mouth.

| Characteristic | Upper course | Lower course | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gradient** | steep | gentle | the river is cutting down from high land towards sea level |
| **Discharge** | low | high | **tributaries** join and the drainage basin gets bigger |
| **Channel width and depth** | narrow, shallow | wide, deep | more water needs a larger channel; lateral erosion widens it |
| **Velocity** | slower on average | **faster** on average | a deeper, smoother channel has less **friction** (less water touches the bed and banks), so more energy goes into flow |
| **Valley profile** | steep V-shape | wide and flat, with a flood plain | vertical erosion upstream; lateral erosion and deposition downstream |
| **Sediment size** | large, angular boulders | small, rounded particles (sand, silt) | **attrition** and **abrasion** break load down as it travels |
| **Load quantity** | small | large | material is added from tributaries, banks and slopes |
| **Channel roughness** | high (boulders on the bed) | low | the bed is smoother downstream |

Velocity is the trap. The upper course **looks** fast because of turbulent water around boulders, but much of its energy is lost to friction, so the **average** velocity usually rises downstream.

### Erosion and transport

**Erosion processes**
- **Hydraulic action**: the force of the water hits the bed and banks and forces air into cracks, breaking rock away.
- **Abrasion**: load carried by the river scrapes and grinds against the bed and banks.
- **Attrition**: stones in the load knock against each other, becoming smaller and rounder.
- **Solution**: the water dissolves soluble rocks, such as limestone.

In the upper course, erosion is mainly **vertical** (downwards), deepening the valley. Downstream it is increasingly **lateral** (sideways), widening the valley.

**Transport processes**
- **Traction**: large boulders roll along the bed.
- **Saltation**: pebbles bounce along the bed.
- **Suspension**: fine clay and silt are carried within the water, making it look brown.
- **Solution**: dissolved minerals are carried invisibly in the water.

**Deposition** happens when the river loses energy and velocity falls: on the inside of bends, when discharge drops after a flood, where the river spills over its banks, and where it enters a lake or the sea.

### River landforms

**Interlocking spurs** (upper course): the young river has little energy to erode sideways, so it winds around ridges of harder rock, cutting down vertically. The spurs of high land on alternate sides look like the teeth of a zip.

**Waterfalls** (upper course), for example **High Force**:
1. A band of **hard, resistant rock** (the Whin Sill) lies over **softer rock**.
2. The soft rock is eroded faster by hydraulic action and abrasion, **undercutting** the hard rock and leaving an **overhang**.
3. A deep **plunge pool** forms at the base, deepened by swirling water and rocks.
4. The overhang becomes unsupported and **collapses** into the plunge pool.
5. The waterfall **retreats upstream**, leaving a steep-sided **gorge**.

**Meanders** (middle and lower course):
- On the **outside of the bend**, water flows fastest and deepest, causing **lateral erosion** by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a steep **river cliff**.
- On the **inside of the bend**, water is slower and shallower, so material is **deposited**, forming a gently sloping **slip-off slope** (point bar) of sand and gravel.
- Water flows in a corkscrew (**helicoidal flow**) that carries eroded material from the outside of one bend to the inside of the next. Over time, meanders grow larger and migrate across the flood plain.

**Oxbow lakes**:
1. Erosion on the outsides of two neighbouring bends narrows the **neck** of the meander.
2. During a **flood**, the river cuts through the neck, taking the shorter, straighter route.
3. Deposition seals off the old bend from the new channel.
4. The cut-off loop becomes a horseshoe-shaped **oxbow lake**, which slowly fills with sediment and vegetation to become a **meander scar**.

**Flood plains**: wide, flat areas of land either side of the river in the lower course. Meanders **migrate** across the valley, eroding the valley sides laterally. When the river floods, it spreads over the land and loses velocity, depositing fine **alluvium** in layers, which builds up the flood plain.

**Levees**: raised natural embankments along the river banks.
1. When the river floods, water spreads out of the channel and **loses velocity** suddenly.
2. The **heaviest, coarsest** material is deposited first, right next to the channel; finer silt travels further across the flood plain.
3. Repeated floods build these deposits into raised banks.

**Deltas**: where a river meets a sea or lake with little current or tide, it slows sharply and deposits its load faster than the water can remove it. Layers of sediment build out from the mouth, and the river splits into many channels (**distributaries**). Large deltas, such as the Nile's, need a big sediment load and a calm sea, so true deltas are rare on the UK's tidal coasts. The Tees ends in an **estuary** instead, but small deltas are common where rivers enter UK lakes.

### Climate, geology and slope processes

- **Climate**: the Tees rises in the wet North Pennines, where relief rainfall is high and snow can melt suddenly in spring, so upland discharge rises quickly. More water means more erosion and more load transported. Frost on exposed slopes supplies angular debris.
- **Geology**: hard rocks (the Whin Sill) form waterfalls, rapids and steep reaches; soft rocks erode into wider valleys. On the **long profile** (a section of the river's height from source to mouth), breaks of slope often match bands of hard rock shown on a **BGS geology map**. Impermeable rocks increase surface runoff; soluble limestone adds dissolved load.
- **Slope processes**: soil creep, landslides and rockfall on valley sides deliver sediment to the river, adding to its load; peat erosion on upland moors colours the water brown.

- **Rising limb**: discharge increases as rainwater reaches the river.
- **Peak discharge**: the highest discharge.
- **Lag time**: the time between **peak rainfall** and **peak discharge**.
- **Falling limb**: discharge decreases as less water reaches the river.
- **Base flow**: the normal level of discharge, fed slowly by groundwater.

A **flashy** hydrograph has a short lag time, steep rising limb and high peak: flooding is more likely. A **subdued** hydrograph has a long lag time, gentle limbs and a low peak.

**Physical factors**

| Factor | Short lag time, high peak (flashy) | Long lag time, low peak (subdued) |
|---|---|---|
| **Geology** | **impermeable** rock (granite, clay, shale): water runs off over the surface | **permeable** rock (chalk, sandstone): water soaks in and moves slowly as groundwater |
| **Soil type** | thin soils or clay soils with low infiltration | deep, sandy soils with high infiltration |
| **Slope** | steep slopes: water runs off fast | gentle slopes: water has time to infiltrate |
| **Basin shape** | **circular** basin: water from all parts arrives at the same time | **elongated** basin: water arrives over a longer period |
| **Antecedent conditions** | ground already **saturated** or **frozen** from earlier rain or frost | dry ground that can absorb water |

Rainfall itself matters too: an intense downpour gives a flashier response than steady drizzle, and snowmelt can add water suddenly.

### Skills

- **Valley cross-section from contours**: lay a strip of paper across the map between two points, mark where each contour crosses and its height, then plot these on a graph with height on the vertical axis. A steep V-shape suggests the upper course; a wide, flat floor suggests the lower course with a flood plain.
- **Long profile**: plot height against distance from source; it is usually concave, steep near the source and gentle near the mouth, with steps where hard rock crosses the river.
- **OS map recognition**: waterfalls are labelled "Waterfall" or "Force" in northern England; meanders show as winding blue lines; oxbow lakes as curved blue lakes beside the river; flood plains as wide areas without contours next to the river; interlocking spurs as contours that alternate from each side.

::: terms
- **Discharge**: the volume of water flowing past a point in a river each second, measured in cumecs.
- **Bradshaw model**: a model showing how river characteristics such as discharge, width, gradient and sediment size change from source to mouth.
- **Hydraulic action**: erosion by the force of moving water against the bed and banks, which can force air into cracks and break rock apart.
- **Saltation**: transport of small pebbles that bounce along the river bed.
- **Interlocking spurs**: alternating ridges of high land that the river winds around in its upper course.
- **Plunge pool**: a deep pool at the base of a waterfall, eroded by the falling water and rocks.
- **Meander**: a bend in a river, with erosion on the outside bend and deposition on the inside.
- **Oxbow lake**: a horseshoe-shaped lake formed when a meander is cut off from the main river.
- **Levee**: a raised bank along the edge of a river channel, formed by deposition of coarse sediment during floods.
- **Lag time**: the time between peak rainfall and peak discharge on a storm hydrograph.
- **Antecedent conditions**: how wet the ground already is before a storm, which affects how much rain can soak in.
- **Long profile**: a section showing how a river's height above sea level changes from source to mouth.
:::

::: method
1. **Named river**: write the name (River Tees) and use a real place for each course: High Force (upper), Barnard Castle (middle), Yarm or Stockton (lower).
2. **Differences**: compare both ends in one sentence, using a characteristic from the Bradshaw model, then say **why**.
3. **Landforms**: give every stage in order and name the processes (hydraulic action, abrasion, deposition).
4. **Hydrographs**: read the scale carefully; lag time is from **peak rainfall** to **peak discharge**, in hours.
5. **Physical factors**: link each factor to infiltration and surface runoff, then to lag time and peak.
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: quote values from both hydrographs, explain each difference with a factor, decide which factor matters most, and judge.
:::

::: example Named river: upper and lower course
For a named UK river you have studied, explain one difference between its upper course and its lower course. (2)

On the River Tees, the upper course near High Force is narrow and shallow with large angular boulders, while the lower course at Stockton is wide and deep (1), because tributaries add water downstream and lateral erosion widens the channel (1).
:::

::: example Explain how: waterfall
Explain how a waterfall is formed. (3)

A band of hard rock, such as the Whin Sill at High Force, lies over softer rock (1). The soft rock is eroded faster by hydraulic action and abrasion, undercutting the hard rock and forming a plunge pool and an overhang (1). The overhang collapses, and the process repeats, so the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a gorge (1).
:::

::: example Hydrograph reading
Sample data: rainfall peaked at 08:00 on Monday. The river's discharge rose from a base flow of 12 cumecs to a peak of 58 cumecs at 22:00 the same day. Calculate the lag time and the rise in discharge. (2)

Lag time = 22:00 − 08:00 = 14 hours (1). Rise in discharge = 58 − 12 = 46 cumecs (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: erosion processes
Explain two ways in which rivers can erode the landscape. (4)

Hydraulic action: the force of the water hits the banks and forces air into cracks (1), so rock breaks away and the bank on the outside of a meander is undercut (1). Abrasion: rocks carried by the river scrape against the bed (1), wearing it down and deepening the channel in the upper course (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: basin shape
Explain one way in which drainage basin shape affects a storm hydrograph. (2)

In a circular basin, all points are a similar distance from the main channel, so water arrives at the river at about the same time (1). This gives a shorter lag time and a higher peak than in an elongated basin (1).
:::

::: watch
- Saying the river **slows down** downstream because the gradient is gentler. Average velocity usually **increases**, because the channel is deeper and smoother.
- Measuring lag time from the **start** of rainfall. It runs from **peak rainfall** to **peak discharge**.
- Writing about "the river" without naming it. Q3 often says "a named UK river": give the name and real places.
- Skipping stages in landform formation, or giving no process names.
- Mixing up erosion (hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution) and transport (traction, saltation, suspension, solution). Solution appears in both lists.
- Saying levees form from fine silt. The **coarsest** material is dropped first, next to the channel.
- In comparison questions, describing only one course. Every difference must refer to both.
:::
