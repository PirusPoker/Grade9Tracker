## What it is

This key idea asks what the challenges are for coastal landscapes and communities, and **why people disagree** about how to manage them. It covers:
- why the **risk of coastal flooding** is **increasing**: climate change affects marine erosion and deposition, with **more frequent storms** and **rising sea level**, and the **threats to people and the environment**;
- the **costs, benefits and conflicting views** of managing the coast by **hard engineering** (**groynes** and **sea walls**), **soft engineering** (**beach replenishment**, **slope stabilisation**) and more **sustainable approaches** (**do nothing** and **strategic realignment**) linked to **Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM)**.

Integrated skills: using **OS maps and GIS** to see what is threatened by erosion and how policy decisions affect places, and **simple cost-benefit analysis** of defence options. Any real UK examples earn the marks. This course uses **Medmerry** in West Sussex (strategic realignment) and **Happisburgh** in Norfolk (no active intervention), with Holderness from 4.4 for hard engineering.

### Why coastal flood risk is increasing

**Rising sea level**
- Global sea level has risen by about **20 cm since 1900**, and the rise is speeding up.
- Two causes: **thermal expansion** (sea water expands as it warms) and **melting land ice** (glaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets).
- In south-east England the land is also **sinking slightly**, because the north of Britain is still rising after the weight of the ice sheets was removed and the south tilts down. So relative sea level rises faster there.
- Higher sea level means waves reach further up beaches and cliffs, and **storm surges** start from a higher base.

**More frequent and powerful storms**
- Climate change is expected to bring **more intense storms**, with stronger winds and bigger destructive waves.
- A **storm surge** happens when low pressure lets the sea surface rise and strong winds pile water against the coast. When this coincides with a high tide, sea defences can be overtopped. The **1953 North Sea surge** killed more than 300 people in eastern England; the surge of **December 2013** was the largest for decades, but much better defences and warnings meant far fewer losses.

**Changes to erosion and deposition**
- Higher, more powerful waves increase **cliff erosion** and strip **beaches**, which removes the natural buffer that absorbs wave energy.
- **Coastal squeeze**: salt marshes and sand dunes would naturally move inland as sea level rises, but where sea walls or development block them, they are squeezed and lost. That removes another natural defence.

### Threats to people and the environment

| People | Environment |
|---|---|
| homes and businesses flooded or lost to erosion | loss of **salt marsh**, **mudflats** and **sand dunes**, important bird and plant habitats |
| roads and railways cut, e.g. the main line at **Dawlish**, Devon, was severed when its sea wall collapsed in the 2014 storms | **salt water** flooding **freshwater** wetlands and lagoons, killing freshwater species |
| farmland ruined by **salt water** for years after a flood | loss of beaches used by nesting birds and seals |
| higher **insurance** costs, falling house prices, stress and anxiety | historic and archaeological sites lost to erosion |
| loss of tourism income for seaside towns | pollution if landfill sites or industry at the coast are eroded |

### Managing the coast: three types of approach

**Hard engineering**: building artificial structures to stop the sea.

| Method | How it works | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sea wall** | concrete wall at the back of the beach, often with a **recurved** top that reflects waves back out to sea | very effective protection for towns; promenade for tourists; lasts decades | very expensive (thousands of pounds per metre); reflected waves scour the beach in front, lowering it; ugly; needs maintenance |
| **Groynes** | timber or rock barriers at right angles to the beach, trapping sediment moved by longshore drift | build a wider beach that absorbs wave energy and is good for tourism; relatively cheap | starve beaches **downdrift**, increasing erosion there; can look unattractive; timber rots |
| **Rock armour (rip-rap)** | large boulders piled at the foot of cliffs or sea walls | absorbs wave energy; fairly cheap; quick to place | rocks are often imported from elsewhere; does not suit the local landscape; can be dangerous to climb |

**Soft engineering**: working with natural processes.

| Method | How it works | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beach replenishment** (nourishment) | sand or shingle is dredged from the seabed or brought from elsewhere and added to the beach | a wider beach absorbs wave energy; looks natural; good for tourism | must be **repeated** every few years; dredging can harm seabed habitats; costly over time |
| **Slope stabilisation** | drainage pipes remove water from cliffs; slopes are **regraded** to a gentler angle; vegetation is planted; nets or pins hold loose rock | reduces slumping and landslides; cheaper than a sea wall | does not stop wave erosion at the cliff foot; land at the top may be lost when the slope is regraded |
| **Dune regeneration** | fencing and marram grass planting trap sand and rebuild dunes | cheap; creates habitat | areas must be closed to the public; slow |

**Sustainable approaches**: accepting that the coast will change.
- **Do nothing (no active intervention)**: no money is spent on defences, so erosion and flooding happen naturally.
  - **Example: Happisburgh, Norfolk.** The cliffs are soft sands and clays. An old wooden revetment was not replaced as it decayed, and the shoreline policy became no active intervention. Houses on Beach Road were lost or demolished as the cliff retreated. Around 2010 a government-funded pilot scheme helped some residents move and their homes were demolished before they fell. Residents argued strongly that they were being abandoned.
  - Benefits: no cost; natural sediment supply continues to other parts of the coast; habitats can adapt.
  - Costs: people lose homes, land and businesses, often with **no compensation**; communities break up.
- **Strategic (managed) realignment**: deliberately allowing the sea to flood an area of **low-value land**, usually farmland, and building new defences further inland.
  - **Example: Medmerry, West Sussex**, opened in **2013**. The old shingle bank, which needed constant repair, was allowed to breach, and new embankments were built further inland. The flooded land became **intertidal habitat** (salt marsh and mudflats), now an RSPB nature reserve. It cost tens of millions of pounds, and now protects hundreds of homes and the road to Selsey far better than before.
  - Benefits: salt marsh absorbs wave energy, cutting long-term defence costs; creates habitat to replace areas lost to coastal squeeze; new tourism (birdwatching, walking).
  - Costs: farmland and some homes lost; farmers need compensation; some local people dislike losing familiar land.

### Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM)

**ICZM** manages the **whole coastal zone** together, considering all the people, uses and environments involved and the processes linking one stretch to another, instead of each town defending itself and pushing the problem along the coast.
- The coast of England and Wales is divided into **sediment cells**, stretches within which sediment mostly circulates. Defending one part of a cell affects the others.
- Each cell has a **Shoreline Management Plan (SMP)** that gives every stretch one of four policies: **hold the line** (keep it where it is), **advance the line** (build out to sea; rare), **managed realignment**, or **no active intervention**.
- Decisions are based on **cost-benefit analysis**, environmental impact and long-term sustainability, over periods of up to 100 years.

### Why there is conflict

Different groups value the coast differently, and money is limited.

| Group | Usual view |
|---|---|
| **Homeowners and residents** | want their homes protected; feel unfairly treated if their stretch is left to erode, especially with no compensation |
| **Farmers** | lose land and income in managed realignment or do nothing; want compensation |
| **Tourism businesses** | want wide beaches and defences for the seafront, but some favour natural landscapes |
| **Conservation groups** (RSPB, Natural England) | favour realignment and natural processes that create habitats; oppose hard defences that cause coastal squeeze |
| **Environment Agency and councils** | must spend public money where the benefits are greatest; follow the SMP |
| **Taxpayers** | do not want to pay for defences that protect a few valuable homes |
| **Communities downdrift** | object to groynes or walls that starve their beaches |

### Skill: cost-benefit analysis

Compare the **costs** of a scheme (building and maintenance) with its **benefits** (the value of damage avoided: property, land, roads, tourism). A **benefit-cost ratio** = total benefits ÷ total costs. A ratio above 1 means the scheme is worth more than it costs. Remember that some costs and benefits are hard to put a price on: habitats, landscape, people's stress.

**OS and GIS skills**: use maps to show what lies within the zone predicted to erode or flood (homes, roads, caravan parks), and compare maps before and after a policy, such as the new lagoon and embankments at Medmerry.

::: terms
- **Sea level rise**: an increase in the average height of the sea, caused by thermal expansion of warming water and melting land ice.
- **Storm surge**: a temporary rise in sea level caused by low air pressure and strong winds pushing water towards the coast.
- **Coastal squeeze**: the loss of coastal habitats such as salt marsh when they cannot move inland because of sea walls or development.
- **Hard engineering**: building artificial structures such as sea walls and groynes to control natural processes.
- **Soft engineering**: working with natural processes, for example beach replenishment or slope stabilisation, to reduce erosion and flooding.
- **Beach replenishment**: adding sand or shingle to a beach to make it wider so that it absorbs more wave energy.
- **Slope stabilisation**: reducing mass movement on cliffs by draining water, regrading the slope or planting vegetation.
- **Strategic realignment**: deliberately letting the sea flood an area of low-value land and building new defences further inland.
- **No active intervention**: a policy of spending nothing on defences and letting natural erosion and flooding happen.
- **Integrated Coastal Zone Management**: managing the whole coastal zone together, balancing all users and the natural processes between places.
- **Sediment cell**: a stretch of coast within which sediment is mostly moved around and kept, used as the unit for Shoreline Management Plans.
- **Benefit-cost ratio**: the value of a scheme's benefits divided by its costs; above 1 means it is worth doing.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify the approach**: hard, soft or sustainable. Name a method and, if possible, a real place.
2. **Explain how it works**: what it does to waves or sediment.
3. **Give a cost and a benefit**, each developed: who gains, who loses, what happens downdrift.
4. **Bring in people**: conflict questions need named groups with opposing views.
5. **Calculations**: benefit-cost ratio = benefits ÷ costs; check whether it is above 1.
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: use the figure's options and data, explain each group's position, weigh the reasons for conflict, and judge which matters most.
:::

::: example Multiple choice: soft engineering
Which one of the following is a soft engineering method? A: Sea wall. B: Groynes. C: Beach replenishment. D: Rock armour. (1)

C: beach replenishment, which works with natural processes by widening the beach (1).
:::

Explain one reason why the risk of coastal flooding in the UK is increasing. (2)

::: example Explain one advantage and one disadvantage: groynes
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using groynes to manage the coast. (4)

An advantage is that groynes trap sediment moved by longshore drift (1), building a wider beach that absorbs wave energy and protects the cliff or seafront behind it (1). A disadvantage is that less sediment reaches the coast further along (1), so beaches downdrift become narrower and erode faster, as at Cowden south of Mappleton (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: benefit-cost ratio
Sample data: a sea wall would cost £12 million to build and £3 million to maintain over 50 years. It would prevent damage worth £45 million. Calculate the benefit-cost ratio. (2)

Total cost = 12 + 3 = £15 million (1); 45 ÷ 15 = 3, a ratio of 3:1, so benefits are three times the costs (1).
:::

::: example Explain how: strategic realignment
Explain how strategic realignment can reduce coastal flood risk. (3)

New defences are built further inland and the old ones are allowed to breach, so the sea floods low-value land (1). The flooded area becomes salt marsh and mudflat, which absorbs wave energy before it reaches the new defences (1). This means the new defences are smaller and cheaper to maintain, and they protect homes more reliably, as at Medmerry (1).
:::

::: watch
- Writing "do nothing" as if it means nobody decided anything. It is a deliberate **policy** (no active intervention) chosen after cost-benefit analysis.
- Treating soft engineering as cost-free. Beach replenishment must be **repeated**; realignment floods **farmland**.
- Conflict answers with no **people** in them. Name groups and say what each wants.
- Confusing managed realignment with retreat by individual residents. Realignment is **planned**, with **new defences inland**.
- Forgetting the downdrift effect of groynes and the beach scour of sea walls.
- Mixing up the causes of sea level rise. Learn **thermal expansion** and **melting land ice**.
- In the 8-marker, listing costs and benefits without explaining why they lead to **disagreement**.
