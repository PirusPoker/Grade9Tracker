## What it is

Cities do not change evenly: while one district declines, another booms. This key idea asks you to explain:
- how parts of the city have experienced **decline** through **de-industrialisation** and **depopulation**, and through **decentralisation** (out-of-town shopping centres, retail and business parks), **e-commerce** and **developments in transport**;
- how other parts have experienced **economic and population growth** through **sprawl on the rural-urban fringe**, **financial and business services**, **investment by TNCs**, **gentrification and studentification**, and **culture and leisure**.

Integrated skill: using **1:25 000 and 1:50 000 OS maps** to identify land-use types (skill 3). It is examined in **Paper 2, Section B**: short items on described maps, photos and graphs, "Explain one/two…", and possibly the 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question, which may carry **4 SPaG marks**. This course uses **London**; any major UK city you studied earns the marks.

### The cycle of decline

**De-industrialisation: the docks and factories**
- For centuries London was the busiest port in the world, with miles of docks in the **East End** and along the Thames, plus factories processing imported goods (sugar, timber, food) and industry along the **Lea Valley** and in west London.
- From the 1960s, ships grew larger and cargo moved into **containers**. The old docks were too small and too far upstream, so trade moved downstream to deep-water ports such as **Tilbury**. The upstream docks closed one by one, the last in **1981**.
- Manufacturing also declined, as elsewhere in the UK: global competition, cheaper production overseas and automation.
- **Effects**: tens of thousands of dock and factory jobs were lost; derelict warehouses and polluted land; high unemployment; shops and services closed as spending fell.

**Depopulation**
- Greater London's population **peaked at over 8.5 million around 1939**, then **fell below 7 million by the 1980s**.
- **Why**: bomb damage and slum clearance; government moved people to **New Towns** beyond the green belt (Stevenage, Harlow, Basildon, later Milton Keynes); **counter-urbanisation** as families sought space and gardens; and the loss of inner-city jobs.
- **Effects**: empty housing, falling school rolls, a shrinking tax base, and a population left behind that was older, poorer and less skilled. This is the **cycle of decline**: job losses, out-migration, falling spending and investment, more job losses.

**Decentralisation: shops and offices move out**
- **Out-of-town shopping centres**: **Brent Cross** (north London), then **Lakeside** (Thurrock) and **Bluewater** (Kent), both close to the **M25**. They offer free parking, everything under one roof, and easy access by car from a huge catchment.
- **Retail parks** (big-box stores on the edge of town) and **business parks** such as **Stockley Park** near Heathrow, where land is cheaper, there is space to expand and the motorway and airport are close.
- **Effects on the centre**: town-centre and high-street shops lose trade, leading to empty units, charity shops and fewer visitors in some suburban centres.

**E-commerce**
- Online shopping lets people buy without visiting shops at all. It grew steadily and jumped during the Covid-19 pandemic.
- **Effects**: high-street chains closed or shrank, leaving empty shops; but large **distribution warehouses** have grown on the fringe near motorways, creating logistics jobs. Home working has also reduced footfall in parts of the CBD.

**Developments in transport**
- Rising **car ownership** and the **M25** orbital motorway (completed in the 1980s) made the edge of London more accessible than the congested centre, encouraging firms and shops to decentralise.
- Commuter railways let people live further out, adding to depopulation of the inner city.
- But transport can also cause **growth**: new links such as the **Jubilee line extension**, the **Docklands Light Railway (DLR)** and the **Elizabeth line** made former industrial areas accessible again.

### The cycle of growth

**Sprawl on the rural-urban fringe**
- Between the wars, London spread rapidly outwards along new railway and Tube lines, creating huge suburbs.
- The **Metropolitan Green Belt** largely stopped sprawl after the mid-20th century, so outward growth now happens mainly **beyond** the green belt (commuter towns) or on the few sites released on the edge.
- Pressure continues: housing shortages lead to debate about building on the green belt, and fringe land is used for retail parks, warehouses and roads.

**Financial and business services**
- After financial **deregulation** in **1986** (the "Big Bang"), the City of London boomed as a global financial centre.
- Finance spread east to **Canary Wharf**, built on the former West India Docks, now home to major banks and tens of thousands of office jobs.
- Business services (law, accountancy, consultancy, advertising) cluster near finance.

**Investment by TNCs**
- Global companies choose London for its skills, connectivity and status. Tech firms such as **Google** have built major headquarters at **King's Cross**, and **Apple** moved its London offices into the redeveloped **Battersea Power Station**.
- Foreign investors also fund large office and housing developments and buy property, driving regeneration and higher land values.

**Gentrification**
- **Gentrification** is when wealthier people move into a poorer, often run-down area and improve the housing, which raises prices and changes the area's character.
- Examples: **Islington** from the 1960s; more recently **Shoreditch**, **Hackney** and parts of **Brixton**, where Victorian terraces and old warehouses were converted into expensive homes, offices, cafés and galleries. Shoreditch's tech and creative firms earned the area the nickname "Silicon Roundabout".
- **Positive**: improved buildings, new businesses, lower crime, more investment.
- **Negative**: rents and house prices rise, pushing out long-standing, lower-income residents and local shops; social tension between old and new residents.

**Studentification**
- Large numbers of students concentrate near universities, in shared houses and new **purpose-built student blocks**, e.g. around Queen Mary in Mile End and the new university campuses at Stratford.
- **Positive**: spending supports cafés, bars and shops; derelict sites are redeveloped.
- **Negative**: fewer family homes; empty areas in holidays; noise; higher rents.

**Culture and leisure**
- Former industrial buildings and land reused for culture: **Tate Modern** in a former power station on the South Bank; the **O2** (the former Millennium Dome) on the Greenwich peninsula; the **Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park** at Stratford, with stadiums, parks and a new cultural quarter.
- **Effects**: tourism, jobs in hospitality and the arts, and a new image that attracts residents and investors.

### Decline and growth side by side

| Part of London | What happened | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Docklands (1960s–1981) | decline: docks closed, jobs and people lost | containerisation; trade moved downstream |
| Docklands (1980s onward) | growth: Canary Wharf, new homes, DLR | regeneration, finance, TNC investment, transport |
| Some suburban high streets | decline: empty shops | out-of-town centres, e-commerce |
| Shoreditch and Hackney | growth: tech, creative firms, cafés, high prices | gentrification, culture, nearness to the City |
| M25 fringe | growth: warehouses, retail and business parks | road access, cheap land, e-commerce |

### Skill: OS maps and land use

On a **1:25 000** or **1:50 000** OS map you can identify land use from symbols and patterns:
- **CBD**: dense grey blocks, many roads, stations, museums, churches.
- **Inner city**: tightly packed street grids, railway lines, industrial sites, docks or canals.
- **Suburbs**: curving streets and cul-de-sacs, schools, parks, playing fields.
- **Fringe**: large retail or business buildings, motorway junctions, golf courses, farmland, woods.
Use **six-figure grid references** to locate features and the **scale** to measure distances.

::: terms
- **De-industrialisation**: the decline of manufacturing and other industry in a place, with the loss of jobs.
- **Depopulation**: a fall in the number of people living in an area.
- **Containerisation**: carrying cargo in standard-sized metal containers on very large ships.
- **Decentralisation**: the movement of shops, offices and people away from the city centre to the edge.
- **Out-of-town shopping centre**: a large shopping complex on the edge of a city, near main roads, with free parking.
- **E-commerce**: buying and selling goods and services online.
- **Urban sprawl**: the unplanned outward spread of a city into the surrounding countryside.
- **Gentrification**: the improvement of a run-down area by wealthier newcomers, which raises property prices and can push out poorer residents.
- **Studentification**: the concentration of students in an area near a university, changing its housing and services.
- **Brownfield site**: land that has been built on before and can be redeveloped.
:::

::: method
1. **Decline or growth?** Check which the question asks for, and which part of the city.
2. **Name the process** from the spec list (de-industrialisation, depopulation, decentralisation, e-commerce, transport; sprawl, finance, TNCs, gentrification, studentification, culture).
3. **Give the cause and the effect**: e.g. containerisation, so docks closed, so jobs were lost, so people left.
4. **Locate it**: name a real part of London (Docklands, Canary Wharf, Shoreditch, Stratford, Bluewater, Lakeside, Brent Cross).
5. **Balance**: gentrification and decentralisation have winners and losers.
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: use the figure in every paragraph, compare reasons for decline and growth, and reach a judgement on which matter most.
:::

::: example Multiple choice: decentralisation

::: example Explain one: why the docks closed
Explain one reason why London's docks closed between the 1960s and 1981. (2)

Cargo began to be carried in containers on much larger ships (1), which could not reach the small, narrow upstream docks, so trade moved downstream to deep-water ports such as Tilbury (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: e-commerce
Explain two ways e-commerce has affected different parts of London. (4)

Online shopping has taken trade from high-street shops (1), so some suburban and town centres have more empty units and fewer visitors (1). Retailers need large distribution warehouses (1), which have been built on cheap land near the M25 on the fringe, creating logistics jobs there (1).
:::

::: example Gentrification: advantage and disadvantage
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of gentrification in an inner-city area. (4)

Advantage (1): wealthier residents renovate run-down houses and spend money locally, so new cafés, shops and businesses open and the environment improves, as in Shoreditch (1). Disadvantage (1): house prices and rents rise sharply, so long-standing lower-income residents and small shops are priced out of the area (1).
:::

::: watch
- Writing about decline or growth in general without saying **where** in London. Name the area.
- Treating gentrification as wholly good. It **displaces** poorer residents through rising rents.
- Forgetting that transport works **both ways**: the M25 encouraged decentralisation, but the DLR and Jubilee line helped regenerate Docklands.
- Explaining dock closure as "the docks got old". The key cause is **containerisation** and larger ships.
- Mixing up **decentralisation** (moving out of the centre) and **de-industrialisation** (loss of industry).
- In calculations, leaving out the sign or the word "decrease" when the population fell.
- In the 8-marker, writing two separate lists. **Link** decline and growth: the same area can do both at different times.
:::
