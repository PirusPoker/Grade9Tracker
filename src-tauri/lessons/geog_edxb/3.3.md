## What it is

Cities are never fixed: their population, the places people live and the way land is used all change over time. This key idea covers:
- how **urban population numbers, distribution and spatial growth** change through five processes: **urbanisation**, **suburbanisation**, **de-industrialisation**, **counter-urbanisation** and, in some cases, **regeneration**;
- the characteristics of the main **urban land uses**: **commercial**, **industrial** and **residential**;
- the **factors** that decide which land use goes where: **accessibility**, **availability**, **cost** and **planning regulations**.

Its integrated skill is using **satellite images** to identify land-use zones. In the exam you will see a satellite image; here they are described in words.

Any accurate real examples earn the marks. UK cities show the full sequence most clearly, but the processes apply worldwide.

### The five processes of urban change

Many cities in developed countries have passed through these processes in roughly this order. Cities in emerging and developing countries are often going through the early ones now, sometimes several at once.

| Process | What happens | Effect on numbers | Effect on distribution and spatial growth | Typical UK timing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Urbanisation** | people move from the countryside into towns for work | city population rises fast | dense housing packed around factories near the centre; the city spreads outwards | 19th century, with the Industrial Revolution |
| **Suburbanisation** | people move from the inner city to new housing on the **edge** of the city | inner city loses people; the urban area as a whole may keep growing | the built-up area spreads outwards along roads and railways (**urban sprawl**) | 1920s–1960s, with buses, trains, cars and council estates |
| **De-industrialisation** | factories, docks and mills close | inner-city jobs and population fall | derelict land and empty buildings in the inner city; decline spreads | 1960s–1990s |
| **Counter-urbanisation** | people move **out of the city entirely** to small towns and villages in the countryside | the whole city's population falls | growth of commuter villages beyond the green belt | 1970s onwards |
| **Regeneration** (re-urbanisation) | run-down areas are improved and people and businesses move back in | the city, especially its centre, grows again | brownfield sites redeveloped; city-centre flats; offices replace docks | 1980s onwards |

**Why each happens**
- **Urbanisation:** factory jobs and higher wages pulled people from farming, which was becoming mechanised.
- **Suburbanisation:** better public transport and car ownership, slum clearance, a wish for gardens, space and cleaner air, and cheaper land on the edge.
- **De-industrialisation:** **global shift** of manufacturing to cheaper countries, automation, and old inner-city sites too cramped for modern factories.
- **Counter-urbanisation:** commuting by car and rail, working from home and the internet, a wish for a quieter, safer life, and retirement to the countryside.
- **Regeneration:** government investment and partnerships with private developers, cheap brownfield land near the centre, the growth of service and knowledge jobs, and young professionals wanting to live centrally. London Docklands is the classic UK example.

**Linking the processes**
- De-industrialisation and suburbanisation both **drain the inner city**, so they often combine to create **inner-city decline**.
- Regeneration is a **response** to that decline.
- Suburbanisation stays within the urban area; counter-urbanisation leaves it. This difference is a common exam trap.

### Urban land uses

| Land use | Characteristics | Typical location |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial** | shops, offices, banks, hotels, restaurants; tall buildings; high density; busy by day | the **CBD** at the centre; also out-of-town retail parks and business parks near motorway junctions |
| **Industrial** | factories, warehouses, workshops, distribution centres; large buildings with yards and car parks | old industry in the **inner city** beside rivers, canals and railways; modern industry and warehousing on the **urban-rural fringe** near motorways |
| **Residential** | housing: terraces, flats, semi-detached and detached houses; density falls with distance from the centre | inner-city terraces and high-rise flats; suburban semis; large detached houses near the fringe; informal settlements on spare land in developing cities |

Other land uses include **open space** (parks, playing fields), **transport** (stations, roads, airports) and **services** (schools, hospitals).

### Factors that influence land use

| Factor | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Accessibility** | land that is easy to reach is valuable to shops and offices, which need many customers and workers | the CBD, where transport routes meet; retail parks at motorway junctions |
| **Availability** | some uses need large plots, which are only available on the edge of the city or on cleared brownfield sites | warehouses and distribution centres on the fringe; new housing estates on greenfield land |
| **Cost** | land is most expensive at the centre, so only uses that earn a lot per square metre can afford it; cheaper land further out suits housing and industry | the **bid-rent** pattern: offices and shops in the centre, housing further out |
| **Planning regulations** | local authorities decide what can be built where, through zoning, green belts, conservation areas and height limits | a **green belt** stops cities sprawling into the countryside; zoning separates heavy industry from housing |

**Bid-rent theory** explains the overall pattern:
- Shops and offices **bid** most for central land, because accessibility gives them the most customers.
- Industry bids less, and housing least, so they occupy rings further out.
- Land values (and building height and density) **fall** with distance from the centre.
- Values rise again at **secondary peaks** such as ring-road junctions and suburban centres.

**Models of land use**
- In developed countries, a common pattern runs from the **CBD**, through the **inner city** (old terraces and industry, some regenerated), the **suburbs** (inter-war and post-war housing) and out to the **urban-rural fringe** (new estates, retail parks, business parks, golf courses).
- In many developing and emerging cities the pattern is partly **reversed**: the rich live near the centre, with good services and security, while the poor live in **informal settlements** on the edge, on steep slopes, flood plains or beside railways, where land is unwanted.

### Skill: identifying land use on satellite images

What to look for:

| Zone | How it appears from above |
|---|---|
| **CBD** | very tall buildings casting long shadows; dense blocks; few green spaces; a road grid converging on the centre |
| **Industrial** | large flat-roofed (often pale or metal) buildings; railway sidings, canals or a river; lorry parks; little greenery |
| **Inner-city residential** | dense rows of small roofs in long, regular lines (terraces); high-rise blocks |
| **Suburban residential** | curving roads and cul-de-sacs; houses with gardens; more green space; schools with playing fields |
| **Urban-rural fringe** | mix of fields and new estates, retail parks with huge car parks, motorway junctions |
| **Informal settlement** | very dense, irregular, small roofs of varied colours with almost no roads or green space |

When describing an image: name the land use, give the **evidence** you can see, and use a **location** (north-west, near the river, along the railway).

::: terms
- **Suburbanisation**: the movement of people from the inner city to new housing on the edge of the urban area.
- **Counter-urbanisation**: the movement of people out of towns and cities to live in smaller settlements in rural areas.
- **De-industrialisation**: the decline of manufacturing industry, with the closure of factories and loss of jobs.
- **Regeneration**: improving a run-down area through new investment in buildings, jobs and services, bringing people back.
- **Urban sprawl**: the uncontrolled spread of a city's built-up area into the surrounding countryside.
- **Central Business District (CBD)**: the commercial centre of a city, with shops, offices and the highest land values.
- **Brownfield site**: land that has been built on before and is available for redevelopment.
- **Greenfield site**: land that has not been built on before, usually on the edge of a city.
- **Green belt**: a protected ring of open land around a city where new building is strictly limited.
- **Bid-rent theory**: the idea that land values fall with distance from the centre, so different uses can afford different locations.
- **Accessibility**: how easily a place can be reached by people and goods.
:::

::: method
1. **Process questions:** name the process, give the direction of movement (into the city, to the edge, out of the city, back to the centre) and the reason, then its effect on population or spatial growth.
2. **Keep processes distinct:** suburbanisation is to the edge of the city; counter-urbanisation is out into the countryside.
3. **Land-use questions:** describe the land use, say where it is, then explain why with one of the four spec factors.
4. **Satellite images:** name the zone, quote the visible evidence and give a direction or landmark.
5. **Factors questions:** use the exact factor words (accessibility, availability, cost, planning regulations) and link each to a land use with "so".
6. **8-mark assess:** weigh the factors, show how they interact (for example cost depends on accessibility), and judge which matters most for each land use.
:::

::: example Define: counter-urbanisation
Define the term counter-urbanisation. (1)

The movement of people out of towns and cities to live in smaller settlements and rural areas (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: suburbanisation
Explain one reason why suburbanisation took place in UK cities in the 20th century. (2)

Better public transport and rising car ownership (1) meant people could live on the edge of the city and still travel to work in the centre (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: commercial land use
Explain one reason why commercial land use is concentrated in the CBD. (2)

The CBD is the most accessible part of the city, where transport routes meet (1), so shops and offices can reach the largest number of customers and workers, which justifies the high land cost (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: industrial location
Explain two reasons why modern industry is often located on the urban-rural fringe. (4)

Large plots of land are available there (1), so firms can build big single-storey factories and warehouses with car and lorry parks (1). Land is cheaper than near the centre (1), so costs are lower for uses that need a lot of space (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: land-use factors
Assess the factors that influence the location of different urban land uses. (8)

Cost is often the most important factor. Land is most expensive at the centre, so only commercial uses that earn high returns per square metre, such as offices and shops, can afford the CBD. Housing and industry are pushed to cheaper land further out, which creates the rings of bid-rent theory.

Cost is closely tied to accessibility. Central land is expensive precisely because it is the most accessible point, where routes meet. Retail parks show the same logic in a new form: motorway junctions are highly accessible by car, so commercial land use has spread to the fringe.

Availability matters for uses that need space. Distribution centres and modern factories need large, flat plots, which are only available on greenfield land at the edge or on big brownfield sites such as former docks.

Planning regulations can override all three. A green belt stops housing spreading into the countryside even where land is cheap, and zoning keeps heavy industry away from homes. Regeneration schemes like London Docklands show planners can change land use by choice.

Overall, cost is the main factor because it reflects accessibility and decides which uses can outbid others. But planning regulations are the final control, especially in developed countries; in developing cities with weaker planning, availability matters more, which is why informal settlements occupy any unwanted land.

(Level 3: all four factors understood and linked, applied to land uses and places, with a supported judgement.)
:::

---

::: q
Explain two ways planning regulations influence urban land use. (4)
---
Two from: green belts limit building on the edge of the city (1), so new housing goes on brownfield land instead (1); zoning separates land uses (1), keeping heavy industry away from housing (1); conservation areas protect historic buildings (1), restricting redevelopment of the CBD (1); regeneration plans allocate land for new uses (1), e.g. docks turned into offices and flats (1).
:::

::: watch
- Mixing up **suburbanisation** (to the edge of the city) with **counter-urbanisation** (out to the countryside). This is the commonest error.
- Defining de-industrialisation as "fewer industries" without mentioning **manufacturing** and **job losses**.
- Explaining land use without the spec's four factors: **accessibility, availability, cost, planning regulations**.
- Describing satellite images vaguely ("there are buildings"). Name the zone and the **visible evidence**.
- Assuming every city follows the UK sequence. Developing cities often have the rich near the centre and informal settlements on the edge.
- Forgetting that regeneration happens only **in some cases**, and is a response to decline.
:::
