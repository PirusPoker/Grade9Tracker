## What it is

A tropical cyclone becomes a **hazard** when it threatens people and property. This key idea covers the five **physical hazards** a cyclone brings, their **impacts on people and environments**, and why some countries are more **vulnerable** than others: **physically, socially and economically**. Two storms of the same strength can have completely different death tolls, and vulnerability is the reason.

### The five physical hazards

| Hazard | How it happens | Impacts on people | Impacts on environments |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High winds** | sustained winds above 119 km/h, gusting much higher in the eyewall | buildings unroofed or destroyed; flying debris injures people; power lines and phone masts brought down | trees flattened; crops destroyed; habitats damaged |
| **Intense rainfall** | warm, moist air rises and condenses; storms can drop hundreds of millimetres in a day or two | river and flash flooding inland; drowning; homes, roads and bridges damaged; drinking water contaminated | soil erosion; rivers carry sediment and pollution into the sea |
| **Storm surge** | very low pressure lets the sea surface bulge upwards, and strong onshore winds pile water against the coast; worst at high tide, on shallow coasts and in funnel-shaped bays | usually the **biggest killer**: a wall of seawater floods coastal towns, sweeping away homes and people | salt water contaminates farmland and fresh water for months or years; mangroves and coral reefs damaged |
| **Coastal flooding** | surge plus large waves and high tides overtop defences | homes and businesses flooded; ports and tourist resorts closed | beaches and dunes eroded; wetlands altered |
| **Landslides** | heavy rain saturates steep slopes until they fail | villages buried; roads blocked, cutting off aid | hillsides stripped of soil and vegetation; rivers dammed by debris |

Secondary impacts follow: disease from contaminated water, food shortages, loss of jobs and income, and homelessness lasting months.

### Skill: the Saffir-Simpson scale

The **Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale** classifies a storm by its **maximum sustained wind speed**.

| Category | Wind speed (km/h) | Wind speed (mph) | Typical damage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 119–153 | 74–95 | some roof and tree damage; power cuts |
| 2 | 154–177 | 96–110 | major roof damage; many trees uprooted |
| 3 | 178–208 | 111–129 | **major hurricane**; well-built homes damaged; power and water lost for days |
| 4 | 209–251 | 130–156 | severe damage; most of the area uninhabitable for weeks |
| 5 | 252 or more | 157 or more | catastrophic; a high share of homes destroyed |

The scale does **not** measure rainfall or storm surge. A slow, weak storm can still cause catastrophic flooding, and a large storm at Category 3 can bring a bigger surge than a compact Category 5. Pearson may give weather and surge data alongside the scale and ask you to classify a storm or judge its impact.

### Skill: assessing impact from different sources

- **Satellite images** show the size and track of the storm and, after it, the extent of flooding or flattened forest.
- **Social media** gives instant, local reports and photos, and helps rescuers find people, but can be inaccurate, exaggerated or unrepresentative, as poorer and rural areas may post less.
- **Socio-economic data** (GDP per capita, HDI, housing quality, population density) helps explain why the impact was large or small.

### Why some countries are more vulnerable

**Vulnerability** is how easily people and places are harmed by a hazard. It is separate from the **magnitude** of the storm.

**Physical vulnerability**
- **Low-lying coasts and deltas** flood easily from surges. Much of Bangladesh is on the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta, only a few metres above sea level.
- **Shallow coastal seas and funnel-shaped bays** make surges higher. The Bay of Bengal funnels water northwards.
- **Island nations** in cyclone tracks, such as the Philippines, are hit repeatedly, and everywhere is close to the coast.
- **Steep, deforested slopes** are prone to landslides.

**Social vulnerability**
- **High population density** on coasts and floodplains puts more people in harm's way.
- **Poor-quality housing** of wood, corrugated iron or mud collapses in strong winds.
- **Low education or awareness** means warnings may not be understood.
- **Age and health**: the elderly, the very young and people with disabilities struggle to evacuate.

**Economic vulnerability**
- **Poverty**: people cannot afford stronger homes, cars to evacuate or insurance.
- **Government funds** for forecasting, shelters, sea walls and emergency services are limited.
- **Dependence on farming and fishing** means a storm destroys livelihoods as well as homes.

Developed countries are **less vulnerable to loss of life**, but they often suffer greater **economic losses**, because they have more valuable property and infrastructure to damage. Vulnerability can also fall over time. Bangladesh suffered hundreds of thousands of deaths in a 1970 cyclone, but since then thousands of raised cyclone shelters and a volunteer warning network have greatly reduced deaths from later storms of similar strength.

::: terms
- **Natural hazard**: a natural event that threatens people, property and the environment.
- **Storm surge**: a rise in sea level caused by very low pressure and strong onshore winds pushing water against the coast.
- **Coastal flooding**: flooding of land along the coast by the sea, often from a surge combined with high tide and waves.
- **Landslide**: a rapid movement of soil and rock down a slope, often triggered by heavy rainfall.
- **Saffir-Simpson scale**: a scale from 1 to 5 that classifies hurricanes by their maximum sustained wind speed.
- **Vulnerability**: how easily people and places can be harmed by a hazard, because of where and how they live.
- **Physical vulnerability**: risk caused by the natural setting, such as low-lying land or steep slopes.
- **Social vulnerability**: risk caused by people's circumstances, such as housing quality, age, health or awareness.
- **Economic vulnerability**: risk caused by lack of money, for individuals or governments, to prepare and recover.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the hazard and its mechanism.** "A storm surge happens because low pressure raises the sea and winds push it onshore."
2. **Then the impact,** split into people and environment if the question asks. Be specific: "salt water ruins farmland for years".
3. **Saffir-Simpson questions:** find the wind speed, match it to the band, and show units. Note what the scale leaves out.
4. **Vulnerability questions:** sort reasons into physical, social and economic, and make each one a **chain**: factor, so what, therefore more deaths or damage.
5. **Compare countries** using both places in each sentence.
6. **8-mark assess:** weigh physical against human factors and judge which matters more for loss of life and for economic loss.
:::

::: example Explain one: storm surge impact
Explain one impact of tropical cyclones on coastal areas. (2)

Storm surges can raise the sea several metres above normal (1), flooding coastal towns and drowning people who have not evacuated (1).
:::

::: example Classify with Saffir-Simpson
Sample data: a storm has maximum sustained winds of 215 km/h and a storm surge of 4 m. Using the Saffir-Simpson scale, identify its category and explain one limitation of the scale for judging its impact. (3)

215 km/h lies in the 209–251 km/h band, so it is Category 4 (1). The scale only measures wind speed (1), so it does not reflect the 4 m storm surge, which may cause more deaths than the wind (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: vulnerability
Explain two reasons why some countries are more vulnerable than others to the impacts of tropical cyclones. (4)

Physical: some countries, such as Bangladesh, have large, densely populated, low-lying deltas (1), so a storm surge can flood huge areas of homes and farmland (1). Economic: poorer countries have less money for warning systems, shelters and sea walls (1), so people are less protected and more die (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: vulnerability
Assess the reasons why some countries are more vulnerable to tropical cyclones than others. (8)

Physical factors set the level of exposure. Low-lying deltas such as Bangladesh's, shallow seas that build up surges, and island nations such as the Philippines that sit across typhoon tracks are hit more often and more severely. Without these, vulnerability would be lower whatever the wealth.

However, human factors decide how exposure becomes loss. Poverty means homes of wood or corrugated iron, few cars to evacuate and no insurance. Social factors matter too: crowded coastal settlements, low awareness of warnings, and the elderly or sick who cannot move. Weak governments may lack forecasting, shelters or emergency services.

The best evidence that human factors matter most is change over time: Bangladesh's physical setting has not changed, but cyclone shelters and warnings have cut deaths dramatically since the 1970s. Rich countries also show that wealth reduces deaths but not damage, as their economic losses are huge.

Overall, physical factors explain where storms are dangerous, but economic and social factors explain how many people die, so human factors are more important.

(Level 3: physical, social and economic factors explained and weighed with evidence; a supported judgement.)
:::

Seawater can rise several metres in a short time (1), giving people little time to escape, so many drown (1).

::: q
Explain one environmental impact of a tropical cyclone. (2)
---
Salt water from the surge contaminates farmland (1), making soils infertile for months or years (1). Or: strong winds flatten forests (1), destroying habitats (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest one advantage and one limitation of social media for assessing the impact of a tropical cyclone. (4)
---
Advantage (1): it gives instant, local reports and photos that help rescuers find people (1). Limitation (1): reports may be inaccurate or exaggerated, and poorer or remote areas may be under-represented (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why developed countries often have higher economic losses but fewer deaths from tropical cyclones. (4)
---
Fewer deaths (1) because of accurate forecasts, evacuation plans and strong buildings (1). Higher economic losses (1) because there is more expensive property and infrastructure to damage, much of it insured (1).
:::

::: watch
- Confusing **magnitude** with **vulnerability**. "The storm was stronger" is magnitude; low-lying land and poverty are vulnerability.
- Explaining a storm surge as "a big wave". It is a rise in sea level from low pressure and onshore winds.
- Thinking the Saffir-Simpson scale measures rainfall or surge. It measures sustained wind only.
- Listing vulnerability factors without the "so" chain.
- Writing impacts only on people when the question asks about environments too.
- Saying developed countries are "not affected". They suffer big economic losses.
:::
