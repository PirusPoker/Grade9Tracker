## What it is

Coasts are not shaped by nature alone. This key idea covers:
- how **human activities**, namely **development**, **agriculture**, **industry** and **coastal management**, have **direct** and **indirect** effects on coastal landscapes;
- how **physical and human processes interact** to cause change on **one named coastal landscape**, including the **significance of its location**.

Any named UK coastal landscape earns the marks, as long as you know its physical processes, its human activities, and why its location matters. This course uses the **Holderness coast** in the East Riding of Yorkshire, one of the fastest-eroding coastlines in Europe. Paper 2 can ask "For a named coastal landscape you have studied, explain…" (2–4 marks), and the 8-marker can ask how far people have changed a coast.

### Direct and indirect effects of human activity

A **direct effect** happens at the place where people act (a sea wall stops erosion behind it). An **indirect effect** happens somewhere else or later, often unintended (that sea wall starves the beach further along the coast).

| Activity | Direct effects | Indirect effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Development** (housing, holiday homes, caravan parks, roads) | adds weight and drainage water to cliff tops, encouraging slumping; replaces natural habitats | creates pressure for expensive defences because property is now at risk; defences then affect other places |
| **Agriculture** | farmland right up to the cliff edge; ploughing and field drains change how water reaches the cliff | loss of farmland and income as cliffs retreat; drainage water can speed slumping |
| **Industry** (ports, gas terminals, power stations, dredging, offshore aggregate extraction) | land reclaimed or built on; hard defences protect key sites | dredging and offshore extraction can reduce the sediment supply to beaches, increasing erosion elsewhere |
| **Coastal management** (groynes, sea walls, rock armour) | stops or slows erosion at the protected site | groynes trap sediment that would have moved along the coast by longshore drift, so beaches downdrift become narrower and erosion there **speeds up** |

### The Holderness coast: location and context

- Holderness is a stretch of about **60 km** of coast on the **east coast of England**, facing the **North Sea**.
- It runs from the hard chalk headland of **Flamborough Head** in the north to **Spurn Head**, a spit at the mouth of the **Humber estuary**, in the south.
- Its cliffs are made of **boulder clay (till)**, a soft mix of clay, sand and stones dumped by ice sheets at the end of the last glacial period.
- The land behind is low, flat and mostly farmed, with small towns (Hornsea, Withernsea), villages, holiday caravan parks and the **Easington gas terminal**, where gas pipelines from the North Sea come ashore.

### Physical processes: why it erodes so fast

The coast retreats on average by about **1.5–2 metres a year**, with much higher losses at some sites and in stormy years. Many villages recorded in medieval times have been lost to the sea.

1. **Soft rock**: boulder clay is weak and unconsolidated, so it is easily eroded by hydraulic action and abrasion.
2. **Slumping**: when heavy rain soaks the clay, it becomes saturated and heavy and slumps down curved slip planes. Waves then quickly remove the slumped material.
3. **Long fetch**: storm winds from the **north and north-east** blow across the North Sea, producing powerful destructive waves, especially in winter.
4. **Narrow beaches**: most eroded clay is very fine and is carried away in suspension, not left on the beach. Flamborough Head also stops sand moving south from further up the coast. Narrow beaches give the cliffs little protection, and in low sections of beach (**ords**) waves reach the cliff foot directly.
5. **Longshore drift** moves sand and shingle **southwards**, away from each section of cliff.
6. **Rising sea level** lets waves reach the cliffs more often.

**Where the sediment goes.** Some material moves south by longshore drift to build **Spurn Head**, and some is carried into the Humber, where it settles as **mudflats and salt marsh**, or out to sea and down the Lincolnshire coast. Holderness's erosion is the **sediment supply** for these landforms.

### Human activities and their effects on Holderness

**Coastal management.** Only a small part of the coast is protected; most is left to erode.
- **Hornsea** and **Withernsea**: sea walls, groynes and rock armour protect the town centres and seafronts, which hold most of the coast's population, shops and tourism.
- **Mappleton** (1991): about **£2 million** was spent on **two rock groynes** and a **rock revetment** at the foot of the cliff, after erosion threatened the village and the coastal road. It **worked locally**: the beach widened and the village and road are still there.
- **Indirect effect at Mappleton**: the groynes trapped sand that would have moved south by longshore drift. South of Mappleton, at **Cowden**, the beach is narrower and erosion **speeded up**, threatening farms and homes. This is known as **terminal groyne syndrome**.
- **Easington gas terminal**: rock armour has been placed at the foot of the cliffs to protect this nationally important industrial site.

**Development and tourism.** Caravan parks, such as those near Skipsea and Ulrome, and isolated homes sit on the retreating cliff top. As the cliff approaches, caravans are moved back and roads are closed; some houses have been abandoned and demolished.

**Agriculture.** Most of the cliff-top land is farmland. Farmers lose fields every year and receive no compensation for unprotected land. Field drains can concentrate water onto cliff faces, increasing slumping.

**Industry and the Humber.** The Humber ports (Hull, Grimsby and Immingham) depend on stable navigation channels. Spurn Head shelters the estuary mouth, and sediment from Holderness feeds the estuary's mudflats.

### Interaction of physical and human processes

The Holderness story is about **interaction**: physical processes create the threat, people respond, and their response changes the physical processes.
- Defences at towns cut off part of the **sediment supply**, so less material reaches beaches downdrift and Spurn Head.
- Protected sites stick out as the coast either side retreats, becoming **artificial headlands** that face more wave attack and need more maintenance.
- Spurn Head is naturally mobile. It was partly protected for a long time, but in the **December 2013 storm surge** the narrow neck of the spit was breached and the road washed away; the tip is now cut off at high tides. Natural processes are now largely left to run their course there.
- The **Shoreline Management Plan** for the area mostly chooses **no active intervention**, with **hold the line** only at the main towns and key sites. This creates **conflict**: residents and farmers on unprotected stretches feel abandoned, while protecting everywhere would cost far more than the land is worth and would starve Spurn and the Humber of sediment.

### The significance of Holderness's location

- **East-facing, North Sea coast**: exposed to north-easterly storms with a long fetch.
- **Between Flamborough Head and the Humber**: the chalk headland limits sediment arriving from the north, so beaches are narrow; the Humber mouth is where much of the sediment ends up.
- **Sediment source for a whole system**: erosion here builds Spurn Head and supplies the Humber's mudflats and salt marshes, which are protected habitats for wading birds. Stopping erosion everywhere would damage these.
- **Strategic industry**: the Easington gas terminal is on this coast, so national energy security is involved.
- **Low-lying land behind**: as the coast retreats, low land and the Humber shores face greater flood risk.

::: terms
- **Boulder clay (till)**: soft, unsorted clay with sand and stones dumped by ice sheets, which forms the Holderness cliffs.
- **Direct effect**: a change caused at the place where a human activity happens, such as a sea wall stopping erosion behind it.
- **Indirect effect**: a change caused elsewhere or later by a human activity, often unintended, such as faster erosion downdrift of groynes.
- **Rock revetment**: large boulders placed at the foot of a cliff to absorb wave energy and reduce erosion.
- **Groyne**: a barrier built at right angles to the coast to trap sediment moved by longshore drift and widen the beach.
- **Terminal groyne syndrome**: increased erosion downdrift of the last groyne, because the groynes have trapped the sediment that would have protected that stretch.
- **Sediment supply**: the amount of sand, shingle and mud reaching a stretch of coast, from cliff erosion, rivers or the seabed.
- **Ord**: a low, narrow section of beach on the Holderness coast that lets waves reach the cliff foot.
- **Shoreline Management Plan**: a plan that sets a policy for each stretch of coast, such as hold the line or no active intervention.
- **Hold the line**: a policy of keeping the coastline where it is now, using defences.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the coast and locate it**: Holderness, East Riding of Yorkshire, between Flamborough Head and Spurn Head, facing the North Sea.
2. **Physical processes**: soft boulder clay, slumping, long fetch, narrow beaches, longshore drift south.
3. **Human activity**: name a place and an action (Mappleton rock groynes 1991; Hornsea sea wall; Easington rock armour; caravan parks).
4. **Direct then indirect**: say what changed at the site, then what changed elsewhere (Cowden, Spurn).
5. **Location**: explain why the position matters (fetch, sediment system, Humber, gas terminal).
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: use the figure's data, weigh physical against human change, and judge "to what extent".
:::
