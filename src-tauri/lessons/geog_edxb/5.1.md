## What it is

Topic 5 opens with an overview of the whole UK's **human landscape**: where people live, how old they are, what work they do and what kind of settlements they live in. This key idea asks you to:
- describe and explain the differences between the **urban core** and the **rural periphery** in **population density**, **age structure**, **economic activities** and **settlement**;
- explain how **UK and EU government policies** have tried to reduce those differences, through **enterprise zones**, **investment in transport infrastructure** and **regional development**;
- use and interpret **UK population pyramids** from different dates (integrated skill 1).

It is examined in **Paper 2, Section B**. Expect short data questions on a map, pyramid or table, "Explain one…" and "Explain two…" items, and an 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question, which may be the one that carries **4 extra marks for SPaG**. Any accurate UK region or policy earns credit; this lesson uses well-known examples.

### Core and periphery

The **urban core** is the most densely populated, most urbanised and usually most economically powerful part of a country. In the UK it is the **London and South East** region, plus the big conurbations of the **Midlands** and the **North of England** (Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds, Liverpool, Newcastle) and Scotland's **Central Belt** (Glasgow and Edinburgh).

The **rural periphery** lies further from the core, often in upland or remote coastal areas: the **Scottish Highlands and Islands**, **mid and north Wales**, **Cornwall and Devon**, **Cumbria and Northumberland**, and much of rural **Northern Ireland**. "Periphery" means edge: these areas are further from the main markets, decision-makers and transport routes.

| Feature | Urban core | Rural periphery |
|---|---|---|
| **Population density** | very high: London has more than 5,000 people per km²; England as a whole over 400 | very low: parts of the Highlands have under 10 people per km²; Scotland as a whole about 70 |
| **Age structure** | young adults and working-age people; many students and young families | older; many retirees and fewer people aged 18–30 |
| **Economic activities** | tertiary and quaternary: finance, business services, media, research, government, retail | more primary (farming, fishing, forestry), tourism, public services; many jobs seasonal, part-time or low-paid |
| **Settlement** | large cities and conurbations, continuous built-up areas, high-rise | scattered farms, hamlets, villages and small market towns |
| **Incomes and investment** | higher wages and house prices; most new investment | lower average wages; less private investment |

### Why the differences exist

**Population density** follows relief, climate and history. Lowland England has fertile soils, a mild climate and was the centre of trade and industry, so towns grew there. Uplands such as the Highlands are steep, wet, cold and have thin soils, so they have always supported few people.

**Age structure** is shaped by **migration**:
- Young adults move **from** the periphery **to** the core for university, jobs and a social life. This is sometimes called a "brain drain" from the periphery.
- Older people often move **to** rural and coastal areas to retire, for a quieter life and attractive scenery. Coastal districts of Devon, Dorset and Norfolk have some of the oldest populations in the UK.
- Cities attract international migrants, who are mostly young adults, so city populations are younger and more diverse.

**Economic activity** reflects **accessibility**. Firms that need customers, skilled workers and fast links to other cities and airports choose the core. The periphery keeps more land-based work, and its distance raises transport costs, so fewer firms locate there.

**Settlement** follows density: where few people live, settlements stay small and scattered, and services such as hospitals, secondary schools and banks are spread thinly.

These differences tend to **reinforce themselves**. Investment goes where people and skills already are, which attracts more young people, which attracts more investment. This is **cumulative causation**: the core keeps growing and the periphery falls further behind unless government steps in.

### Policies to reduce the differences

**1. Enterprise zones**
- Areas where businesses get **incentives** to locate: lower business rates (a local property tax), simpler planning rules, and in some cases allowances for investing in machinery or **superfast broadband**.
- The first wave began in **1981**; a new wave was launched across England from **2011**, with others in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
- Many are in places that lost traditional industry, but some target the periphery and specific growth sectors. For example, the **Humber** enterprise zone helped attract an offshore wind-turbine blade factory to Hull.
- **Success:** new firms and jobs in the target area. **Limits:** some firms simply move from a nearby area to get the tax breaks (**displacement**), so few jobs are truly new; jobs may be low-skilled; many zones are in the core's own regions rather than the remote periphery.

**2. Investment in transport infrastructure**
- Better links shrink the **time-distance** between periphery and core, so firms there can reach markets and people can reach jobs.
- Examples: road upgrades such as dualling of trunk roads in Scotland and Cornwall; rail electrification; **HS2**, the high-speed line from London to Birmingham (its northern legs were cancelled in 2023); **superfast broadband** roll-out to rural areas, which lets people work from home far from offices.
- **Success:** helps tourism, commuting and new businesses; broadband allows home-based digital firms in remote places. **Limits:** very expensive and slow; better links can work both ways, letting core firms serve the periphery from a distance and drawing young people away faster; the biggest schemes often benefit the core most.

**3. Regional development**
- **EU structural funds:** while the UK was in the EU, its poorest regions received money for infrastructure, training and business support. **Cornwall and the Isles of Scilly** and **West Wales and the Valleys** received the highest level of funding because their incomes were well below the EU average. EU money helped pay for broadband, roads and new university campuses in Cornwall.
- **UK schemes:** Regional Development Agencies in England (1998–2012); **Highlands and Islands Enterprise** in Scotland; the **Northern Powerhouse** (from 2014) to link and grow northern cities; the **Levelling Up** agenda (2020s); the **UK Shared Prosperity Fund**, which replaced EU funds after Brexit.
- **Success:** visible new infrastructure and some growth in average incomes. **Limits:** the gap between London and the rest has stayed large; funds are spread thinly; replacing EU money has been criticised as smaller or less certain.

**Overall judgement:** policies have created jobs and improved links in particular places, but they have **not closed the core–periphery gap**. London and the South East still dominate wages, investment and growth. A strong answer recognises partial, local success against limited national change.

### Skill: UK population pyramids from different dates

A **population pyramid** shows the percentage (or number) of males and females in each five-year age group. Comparing the UK in **1971** and **2021**:
- **Base:** 1971 had a wider base, with more children after the 1960s baby boom. By 2021 the base is narrower because birth rates have fallen.
- **Middle:** the large **baby-boom** generations born after 1945 and in the 1960s show as a bulge that moves up the pyramid over time; in 2021 it sits around ages 50–60.
- **Top:** 2021 has a much wider top. People live longer because of better health care and living standards, so there are far more people over 80. Women outnumber men at the oldest ages.
- **Meaning:** an **ageing population** with a rising **dependency ratio**: more pension and health spending, supported by a smaller share of working-age people.

Pyramids for a **core** city and a **peripheral** area look very different: a city pyramid bulges at ages 20–40, while a rural or coastal pyramid is narrow at 20–30 and wide above 60.

**Calculations you may be asked for:**
- **Population density** = population ÷ area in km².
- **Percentage change** = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
- **Difference** between two bars on a pyramid, read carefully from the scale.

::: terms
- **Urban core**: the most densely populated and economically powerful part of a country, centred on its largest cities.
- **Rural periphery**: remote, sparsely populated areas far from the core, with fewer jobs and services.
- **Population density**: the number of people living in each square kilometre of land.
- **Age structure**: the proportion of a population in each age group, often shown on a population pyramid.
- **Ageing population**: a population in which the proportion of older people is rising.
- **Enterprise zone**: an area where firms receive tax breaks and simpler planning rules to attract investment and jobs.
- **Regional development**: government or EU spending to help poorer regions grow, for example on infrastructure and training.
- **Infrastructure**: the basic networks a place needs, such as roads, railways, broadband, energy and water.
- **Cumulative causation**: the process by which a growing region attracts more investment and people, so it keeps growing faster.
- **Displacement**: when a policy simply moves jobs from one place to another instead of creating new ones.
- **Dependency ratio**: the number of young and old dependants compared with the number of working-age people.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the figure first.** For a map or table, quote two or three real values and name places; for a pyramid, name the age group and the percentage.
2. **Use the four headings** for differences: density, age structure, economic activity, settlement. Compare core **and** periphery in every point ("whereas").
3. **Explain with a cause.** Link differences to relief, accessibility and migration (young people out, retirees in).
4. **Policy questions:** name the policy (enterprise zone, HS2, broadband, EU structural funds, Highlands and Islands Enterprise), say **how** it narrows the gap, and give one limit.
5. **Calculations:** write the formula, show the working, round sensibly and give the unit.
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…":** use figure data in most paragraphs, weigh successes against limits, consider who benefits, and finish with a judgement on "how successful". If SPaG is assessed, write in full sentences with accurate terms.
:::

::: example Calculate: population density
Sample data: a district in the Scottish Highlands has 25,000 people living in an area of 4,000 km². Calculate its population density. Show your working. (2)

25,000 ÷ 4,000 (1) = 6.25 people per km² (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: an older periphery
Explain one reason why rural peripheral areas of the UK often have an older population than the urban core. (2)

Young adults move away to cities for university and better-paid jobs (1), while older people move into the countryside or to the coast to retire, so the share of people over 65 rises (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: enterprise zones
Explain two ways enterprise zones can reduce differences between regions of the UK. (4)

They offer lower business rates and simpler planning rules (1), so firms are attracted to areas they would otherwise avoid, creating jobs in poorer places (1). Some offer allowances for investing in equipment and broadband (1), which helps new high-tech or green industries, such as offshore wind manufacturing on the Humber, to grow outside the core (1).
:::

::: example Pyramid comparison
Sample data: a pyramid for the UK shows that people aged 0–4 made up 8% of the population in 1971 and 5.5% in 2021, and people aged 80 and over made up 2% in 1971 and 5% in 2021. Compare the UK's age structure in 1971 and 2021. (3)

In 2021 the base was narrower: the 0–4 group fell from 8% to 5.5%, showing lower birth rates (1). The top was wider: the 80-plus group rose from 2% to 5%, more than doubling, so life expectancy has risen (1). Overall the UK's population has aged, with a higher proportion of older dependants by 2021 (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark: assessing policy success (Level 3 extract)
Analyse Figure 1. Figure 1 is a map of UK population density with sample data: London over 5,000 people per km²; the South East about 480; the North West about 520; Cornwall about 160; the Scottish Highlands under 10. Labels show three policies: an enterprise zone on the Humber, an upgraded trunk road in Cornwall, and broadband roll-out across the Highlands. Assess the success of government policies in reducing differences between the UK's urban core and rural periphery. (8)

Figure 1 shows a huge gap in density: London has over 5,000 people per km², while the Highlands have fewer than 10, so the core concentrates people, jobs and services. Policies have tried to narrow this. The Humber enterprise zone has used tax breaks to attract new green industry, such as a wind-turbine blade factory, bringing skilled jobs to an area that lost fishing and port work. However, the Humber is a former industrial area near large towns, not the true periphery, and some firms in enterprise zones simply moved from nearby, so the net gain is smaller than it looks.

Transport and broadband investment target the remoter places on the map. A better trunk road into Cornwall cuts journey times for tourists and firms, and broadband in the Highlands lets people run online businesses or work from home far from offices. These help keep young people in the periphery. Yet better links also make it easier for young people to leave and for core firms to serve these areas from a distance, so they can deepen the gap.

Overall, the policies have been successful locally, creating jobs on the Humber and improving access in Cornwall and the Highlands, but they have not changed the national pattern. The density contrast on Figure 1, and the dominance of London in wages and investment, show that the core–periphery gap remains wide, so the policies have had only limited success.
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of EU regional development funding for the UK's peripheral regions. (4)
---
Advantage (1): money went to the poorest regions, such as Cornwall and West Wales, for broadband, roads and university campuses (1), improving skills and access so new businesses could grow. Disadvantage (1): the money was spread across many projects and the gap with London did not close; it also ended after Brexit, leaving uncertainty over replacement funding (1).
:::

::: watch
- Describing only the core or only the periphery. "Differences" questions need **both** sides in every point.
- Writing "the government gives money" with no named policy. Name an **enterprise zone**, a **transport scheme**, broadband, EU structural funds or a regional agency.
- Treating policies as wholly successful. Examiners reward **limits**: displacement, cost, links that work both ways, the gap that remains.
- Misreading pyramids: check whether the axis shows **percentages or numbers**, and read males and females separately.
- Forgetting units in calculations: density is **people per km²**; percentage change needs a **%** sign and "increase" or "decrease".
- In the 8-marker, ignoring the figure. "Analyse Figure…" questions need data quoted throughout, not one number at the start.
- Losing SPaG marks by writing in notes. If the question carries SPaG, use full sentences and specialist terms.
:::
