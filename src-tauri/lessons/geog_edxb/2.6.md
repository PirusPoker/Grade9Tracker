## What it is

Rapid economic growth (2.5) has changed far more than India's GDP. This key idea covers its **impacts on people and the environment**:
- how growth has driven **demographic change** (fertility and death rates) and **urbanisation**, and created **regions** with very different characteristics;
- **positive and negative impacts** on different **age and gender groups**;
- **environmental impacts** (air, water and land pollution, greenhouse gases) at scales from **human health** to **global climate change**.

Pearson's Section B 8-mark question, which carries the SPaG marks, often comes from here. Any real emerging-country case study earns the marks; this course uses India.

### Demographic change

| Indicator | Change since 1990 | Why, linked to growth |
|---|---|---|
| **Fertility rate** | fallen from around 4 children per woman to about 2, close to replacement level | more girls' education, women marrying later, more women in paid work, access to contraception, urban living making children costly |
| **Death rate and infant mortality** | both fallen sharply | better health care, vaccination, clean water and nutrition paid for by higher incomes and taxes |
| **Life expectancy** | risen to around 70 | as above |
| **Population structure** | still young, but the base of the pyramid is narrowing and the working-age bulge growing | a **demographic dividend**: lots of workers relative to dependants, if there are enough jobs |

Change is uneven: richer southern states such as Kerala and Tamil Nadu have low fertility, while poorer northern states such as Bihar still have much higher rates.

### Urbanisation

- Millions migrate from villages to cities each year (**rural-urban migration**), **pushed** by low farm incomes, lack of land and drought, and **pulled** by jobs in construction, factories and services, better schools and hospitals.
- About a third of Indians now live in towns and cities, and the share is rising. Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, Bengaluru and Chennai have grown into huge urban areas.
- Cities struggle to keep up: many migrants live in **informal settlements** (slums) with poor sanitation (see Mumbai, 3.4–3.7).

### Regional differences: core and periphery

Growth has concentrated in a **core** of richer states and cities, leaving a **periphery** behind.

| | Core, e.g. **Maharashtra** (Mumbai), Gujarat, Karnataka (Bengaluru), Tamil Nadu, Delhi | Periphery, e.g. **Bihar**, Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, Odisha |
|---|---|---|
| Economy | services, IT, finance, manufacturing, ports | mainly farming, much of it small-scale |
| Income per person | well above the national average | far below; Bihar is among the poorest states |
| Fertility | lower | higher |
| Literacy | higher | lower, especially for women |
| Migration | receives migrants | loses young migrants to the core |

**Why?** Core states have ports, better infrastructure, educated workers and big cities, so TNCs and Indian firms invest there, attracting more investment in a **cumulative** (virtuous) circle. Peripheral states are landlocked or remote, with weaker infrastructure and governance, so they lose their young workers to the core.

### Skill: difference from the mean

To compare regions, find the national **mean**, then subtract it from each region's value. A positive answer means above average; a negative answer means below.

Sample data (not real figures): if the mean income per person across five states is 100 units and State A has 160, State A is **+60**; State B with 40 is **−60**.

### Impacts on age and gender groups

| Group | Positive impacts | Negative impacts |
|---|---|---|
| **Young adults (urban, educated)** | well-paid jobs in IT, finance and call centres; consumer lifestyles; new careers | long hours and night shifts serving Western time zones; high city costs; competition for jobs |
| **Young adults (rural, less educated)** | some construction and factory jobs in cities | low pay, insecure informal work, poor housing in slums; graduates may be unemployed |
| **Children** | falling infant mortality; more children in school; better nutrition | some still work in the informal sector; urban air pollution harms health |
| **Elderly** | better health care and longer lives | left behind in villages when the young migrate; fewer traditional family carers; limited pensions |
| **Women** | more girls in school; more women in service jobs and business; later marriage and smaller families; falling maternal mortality | pay gaps; a relatively low share of women in paid work; many in low-paid informal work; harassment and safety concerns in cities |
| **Men** | most of the new formal jobs; migration income | rural men in declining farm work; dangerous jobs in construction and factories |

Impacts also depend on **where** people live (core or periphery) and on **class and caste**: the same group can gain in Bengaluru and lose in rural Bihar.

### Environmental impacts at different scales

| Type | Causes | Impacts |
|---|---|---|
| **Air pollution** | vehicles, coal power stations, factories, construction dust, burning of crop stubble, cooking on wood fires | Delhi and other northern cities have some of the worst air quality in the world; breathing problems, heart disease and early deaths (**human health** scale) |
| **Water pollution** | untreated sewage, industrial waste, farm chemicals | rivers such as the Ganges and Yamuna heavily polluted; waterborne disease; harm to fish and people who rely on rivers |
| **Land pollution** | rising waste from consumer lifestyles, plastics, e-waste, pesticides, mining | landfill sites, contaminated soil, toxic waste handled by informal recyclers |
| **Greenhouse gases** | coal for electricity, more cars, industry, cattle and rice | India is the world's third-largest emitter of CO₂ in total, contributing to **global climate change**, although its emissions **per person** are far below those of the UK or USA |

India is responding: clean-river programmes for the Ganges, electric buses, rapid growth of **solar power**, and targets to cut emissions intensity. But growth continues to raise total emissions.

::: terms
- **Demographic change**: changes in a population's birth, death and fertility rates and its age structure.
- **Demographic dividend**: the economic boost from having a large working-age population compared with dependants.
- **Rural-urban migration**: the movement of people from the countryside to towns and cities.
- **Urbanisation**: the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas.
- **Core region**: a richer, more developed region with investment, jobs and good infrastructure.
- **Periphery**: a poorer region on the edge of the economy, losing people and investment to the core.
- **Cumulative causation**: a cycle in which growth in a region attracts more investment, so it keeps growing.
- **Difference from the mean**: how far a value is above or below the average of a data set.
- **Air pollution**: harmful gases and particles in the air, from sources such as vehicles, coal and crop burning.
- **Emissions per capita**: a country's total greenhouse gas emissions divided by its population.
:::

::: method
1. **Name the group precisely**: "young urban graduates", "elderly people in rural Bihar", "women in garment factories". Vague groups lose marks.
2. **Positive and negative:** give both for the groups in the question, each developed with a reason.
3. **Scale:** for environment questions, link local impacts (health) to national and global ones (climate change).
4. **Difference from the mean:** calculate the mean, subtract it, keep the sign (+ or −).
5. **8-mark evaluate (with SPaG):** weigh costs against benefits, consider who is affected and where, reach a judgement, and write in clear paragraphs with specialist terms.
:::

::: example Explain one: fertility
Explain one way economic development has changed fertility rates in India. (2)

More girls stay in school and women work in cities (1), so they marry later and choose to have fewer children, lowering the fertility rate to about 2 (1).
:::

::: example Difference from the mean
Sample data: literacy rates in five states are 94%, 82%, 76%, 70% and 63%. Calculate the mean and the difference from the mean for the state with 63%. (3)

Mean = (94 + 82 + 76 + 70 + 63) ÷ 5 = 385 ÷ 5 = 77% (1+1). Difference from the mean = 63 − 77 = −14 percentage points (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: core and periphery
Explain two reasons why some regions of India have developed faster than others. (4)

Core states such as Maharashtra have ports and major cities (1), which attract TNCs and investment in a cycle of growth (1). Peripheral states such as Bihar rely on small-scale farming with poor infrastructure (1), so investors stay away and young people migrate out, slowing development further (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: age and gender
Evaluate the impacts of economic development on different age and gender groups in an emerging country. (8)

Young adults have gained most. Educated graduates in Bengaluru and Hyderabad have found well-paid jobs in IT and finance, with lifestyles their parents could not afford. However, less educated young migrants often end up in insecure informal work and slum housing, so benefits depend on education.

Women have seen real progress: more girls finish school, more women work in services and run businesses, and falling maternal mortality and later marriage give them more choices. Yet a relatively low share of women are in paid work, pay gaps remain, and many work in low-paid informal jobs, especially in poorer states.

The elderly have mixed outcomes. Better health care means longer lives, but when young people migrate to cities, many older people are left in villages with less family support and limited pensions.

Overall, economic development has been positive for most groups, but very unevenly. Educated young urban adults have benefited most, while the rural elderly and women in the periphery have gained least, so the impacts depend as much on location and education as on age or gender.

(Level 3: several named groups, positives and negatives weighed with evidence, and a substantiated conclusion.)
:::

::: q
State one reason why death rates have fallen in India. (1)
---
Better health care / vaccinations / clean water / improved nutrition (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one push factor causing rural-urban migration in India. (2)
---
Low farm incomes (1) because plots are small and crops depend on the monsoon, so families seek better-paid work in cities (1). Or: lack of services (1) such as secondary schools and hospitals in villages (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one negative impact of economic development on elderly people in India. (2)
---
Young people migrate to cities (1), leaving older relatives in villages without traditional family care (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one cause of air pollution in Indian cities. (2)
---
Rapid growth in car and motorbike ownership (1) adds exhaust fumes and fine particles to city air (1). Or: coal-fired power stations (1) release smoke and sulphur dioxide (1). Or: burning crop stubble (1) sends smoke into northern cities (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why India's greenhouse gas emissions are high in total but low per person. (3)
---
India burns large amounts of coal for electricity and industry (1), so with the world's largest population its total emissions are among the highest (1); but many people still use little energy, so divided by the population the emissions per person are far lower than in the UK or USA (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two positive impacts of economic development on women in India. (4)
---
Two from: more girls in education (1), leading to better jobs and later marriage (1); more women in service jobs (1), giving independent income (1); falling maternal mortality (1) because of better health care (1); smaller families (1), giving women more choices (1).
:::

::: watch
- Writing "some people benefit". Name the group: age, gender **and** where they live.
- Treating women or the elderly as all the same: urban and rural, core and periphery differ.
- Saying India is the biggest polluter. It is **third** in total CO₂, and low **per person**.
- Forgetting the scale in environment questions: health locally, climate change globally.
- Losing the minus sign in a difference-from-the-mean answer.
- In the SPaG 8-marker, poor paragraphing or misspelling specialist terms such as urbanisation, periphery, fertility.
:::

::: test
Evaluate the view that the environmental costs of India's economic growth outweigh the benefits. (8)

Spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terminology (4)

(12)
---
AO2 (4) / AO3 (4), then SPaG out of 4.
Level 1 (1–3): isolated understanding of costs or benefits; little evaluation; unsupported judgement.
Level 2 (4–6): some accurate understanding of both, with some evidence; imbalanced.
Level 3 (7–8): accurate understanding throughout; costs and benefits weighed at different scales; a substantiated conclusion.
Indicative content:
- AO2 costs: severe air pollution in Delhi and northern cities (vehicles, coal, crop burning) with health impacts; polluted rivers such as the Ganges and Yamuna; land pollution from waste and e-waste; third-largest total CO₂ emitter, adding to global climate change.
- AO2 benefits: rising incomes, falling infant mortality, longer life expectancy, education; money for clean-up schemes, solar power, metros and electric buses.
- AO3: costs fall on the poor and on future generations; benefits uneven between core and periphery; emissions per person still low; growth funds solutions.
- AO3: judgement, e.g. in the short term the benefits outweigh the costs for most people, but on current trends health and climate costs may come to outweigh them unless cleaner growth speeds up.
SPaG: 4 high; 2–3 intermediate; 1 threshold; 0 errors severely hinder meaning.
