## What it is

Topic 6 human fieldwork is examined in **Paper 2 Section C2** (18 marks), where you answer **either** the urban **or** the rural question. This lesson is the urban option, **Investigating dynamic urban areas**. The spec sets the focus: **how and why quality of life varies within urban areas**.

The C2 question usually has two halves:
- **Someone else's fieldwork:** a figure shows another group's data, such as an environmental quality survey or a retail land-use survey. You suggest an enquiry question, explain a method or sampling strategy, calculate, say why data might be unreliable and suggest improvements.
- **Your own fieldwork:** often an 8-mark question, such as assessing the accuracy and reliability of your results, or the importance of primary and secondary data in reaching your conclusion. Sometimes 2- and 4-mark questions on your own methods too.

Name your town or city, your sites, your sample size and your findings. The model below uses **sample data** for an unnamed UK city; replace it with your own.

### What the spec requires you to have done

| Requirement | What it means in a city |
|---|---|
| **Enquiry question** | developed from your location, about variations in **quality of life** |
| **At least one qualitative method** | collecting data on **views and perceptions** of quality of life |
| **At least one quantitative method** | collecting data on **environmental quality** |
| **Secondary sources** | **census data** from the Office for National Statistics (ONS), plus **one other** |

### Quality of life and deprivation

**Quality of life** is how good people's lives are: income, housing, health, education, safety, environment and access to services and green space. It includes **objective** measures (income, crime rates) and **subjective** ones (how people feel about where they live), which is why the spec asks for both a qualitative and a quantitative method. **Deprivation** is the lack of things most people have, such as a decent income, housing or health.

### The six enquiry stages

1. **Question**, often split into hypotheses.
2. **Methods:** qualitative and quantitative, sampling, risk assessment.
3. **Processing and presenting.**
4. **Analysing and explaining** with theory (the **Burgess model**, the cycle of deprivation, gentrification) and case studies (such as **London**: contrasts between boroughs, Docklands regeneration).
5. **Concluding** with evidence.
6. **Evaluating** accuracy, reliability and the conclusion.

### A model investigation (sample data)

**Enquiry question:** "How and why does quality of life vary between the city centre and the suburbs?"
- **Hypothesis 1:** environmental quality improves with distance from the city centre.
- **Hypothesis 2:** residents' views of quality of life are more positive in the suburbs than in the inner city.

**Site selection.** A **transect** from the CBD to the outer suburbs, with six sites at about 1 km intervals (systematic), checked against census areas so that each zone of the city was included.

### Quantitative methods: environmental quality

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Environmental quality survey (EQS)** | a fixed sheet of criteria (litter, graffiti, noise, building condition, green space, traffic, parking, smell) each scored on a **bipolar scale** from −3 (very poor) to +3 (very good); totals compared between sites |
| **Pedestrian count** | number of people passing a fixed point in **5 minutes**, at the same time of day at each site |
| **Traffic count** | vehicles passing in 5 minutes, sorted by type |
| **Building condition or house type survey** | each building scored 1–5, or classified (terraced, semi, detached, flats) |
| **Noise meter** | decibel reading taken for one minute at each site |

### Qualitative methods: views and perceptions

| Method | How | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaire** | residents asked to rate safety, services, environment and overall quality of life, with open questions on why | leading questions; who is around at that time |
| **Interview** | longer conversations with residents, shopkeepers or community workers | small numbers; hard to compare |
| **Annotated photos and field sketches** | record evidence such as derelict buildings, new developments, parks | the observer chooses what to show |

### Sampling, risk and data types

- **Random** sampling of sites from a grid of numbered squares avoids bias in choosing streets.
- **Systematic** sampling, such as every 1 km along a transect, or asking every tenth person, gives even coverage.
- **Stratified** sampling makes sure each zone (CBD, inner city, suburbs) or each age group is represented in proportion.
- **Opportunistic** sampling (whoever agrees to answer) is quick but biased.
- **Risk assessment:** traffic, getting lost, approaching strangers (stay in groups, wear identification, be polite, never enter homes), weather.
- **Ethics:** explain the purpose, keep answers anonymous, and let people refuse.

### Secondary sources

- **ONS census data** (required): the **2021 Census** gives data such as household overcrowding, car ownership, qualifications, health and employment for small areas (output areas and LSOAs).
- **Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD):** ranks small areas in England by combining income, employment, health, education, crime, housing and living environment.
- **Police.uk crime data** for each street or area.
- **House prices** from property websites; **Google Street View** to compare with past years; local council regeneration plans.

### Presenting the data (sample)

| Site | Distance from CBD (km) | EQS total (−24 to +24) | Pedestrians in 5 min | Mean resident score (1–5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 CBD | 0 | +4 | 86 | 3.2 |
| 2 inner city | 1 | −8 | 31 | 2.4 |
| 3 regenerated inner area | 2 | +6 | 44 | 3.6 |
| 4 inner suburb | 3 | +10 | 18 | 3.8 |
| 5 outer suburb | 4 | +15 | 9 | 4.4 |
| 6 edge-of-city estate | 5 | −2 | 12 | 2.9 |

- **Radar graphs** of the EQS criteria for each site.
- **Bar charts** or a **line graph** of EQS against distance.
- **Choropleth map** of IMD or census data.
- **Proportional symbols** for pedestrian counts on a map.
- **Scatter graph** of EQS against resident score, to see whether measured and perceived quality match.
- **Annotated photos** located on a base map.

### Statistics (sample)

EQS totals for the six sites: +4, −8, +6, +10, +15, −2.
- Order: −8, −2, +4, +6, +10, +15.
- **Mean** = 25 ÷ 6 = **4.2**.
- **Median** = (4 + 6) ÷ 2 = **5**.
- **Range** = 15 − (−8) = **23**.

### Analysing with theory and case studies

- **The Burgess model** shows rings of land use: CBD, an inner zone of older, high-density housing and industry, then suburbs with lower density and better environments. Quality of life tends to rise outwards, which the sample mostly supports.
- **Anomalies:** site 3 is better than expected because of **regeneration** (new flats, cleaned-up waterfront), like **London Docklands**; site 6 is worse because it is an edge-of-city **council estate** with fewer services.
- **Gentrification** raises environmental quality and house prices but can push out existing residents, so perceptions differ by group.
- **The cycle of deprivation:** low income leads to poor housing and health, lower attainment and fewer jobs, which keeps areas deprived.
- **London** shows large contrasts within one city, such as Tower Hamlets and Newham against Richmond upon Thames.

### Conclusions (sample)

Environmental quality generally increased outwards from the inner city to the outer suburbs (−8 to +15), supporting hypothesis 1, but the regenerated inner area and the edge-of-city estate did not fit the pattern. Residents' scores followed the same pattern, supporting hypothesis 2. Census and IMD data showed the most deprived areas matched the lowest EQS scores. Quality of life varies because of the **age and type of housing**, **investment and regeneration**, and **income**.

### Accuracy, reliability and improvements

- **EQS scores are subjective:** two people may score the same street differently. Reduce bias by using a **fixed scoring sheet** with descriptions for each score, scoring in pairs and agreeing a score, or averaging the group's scores.
- **Pedestrian counts** vary with time of day and day of week; one 5-minute count is unreliable.
- **Questionnaires:** small samples, only people out on the street at that time, and leading questions.
- **Secondary data** may be out of date (the census is every ten years).
- **Improvements:** more sites and repeats at different times; stratified sampling of respondents; pilot the questionnaire; compare with up-to-date IMD and crime data.

::: terms
- **Quality of life**: how good people's lives are, combining income, housing, health, safety, environment and access to services.
- **Deprivation**: a lack of things most people have, such as a decent income, housing, education or health.
- **Environmental quality survey**: a fieldwork sheet scoring criteria such as litter, noise and green space on a fixed scale.
- **Bipolar scale**: a scoring scale between two opposite statements, for example from −3 (very poor) to +3 (very good).
- **Census**: a survey of every household in the country, carried out every ten years in the UK by the ONS.
- **Index of Multiple Deprivation**: an official ranking of small areas in England that combines several types of deprivation.
- **Transect**: a line across an area along which sites are sampled to show change with distance.
- **Opportunistic sampling**: asking whoever happens to be available and willing, which is quick but biased.
- **Gentrification**: wealthier people moving into an older area and improving it, which raises prices and can displace existing residents.
- **Subjective data**: data based on personal opinion or judgement, which may differ from person to person.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the given data first:** what was measured, where, how many sites or people, and when.
2. **Suggest an enquiry question (2):** answerable with the data, focused on a place and a variable, e.g. "How does environmental quality change with distance from the city centre in X?"
3. **Method or sampling (2):** name it, then say **how** it was done and **why** it suits the question.
4. **Reliability (2):** identify the weakness (subjectivity, sample size, timing) and develop how it affects the results.
5. **Improvements (4):** two separate changes, each linked to better accuracy or reliability.
6. **Own-work 8-marker:** name your sites and methods, split **accuracy** from **reliability**, use your own results, and judge how far you trust your conclusion.
:::

::: example Suggest an enquiry question
Students carried out an environmental quality survey at eight sites between a city centre and its suburbs. Suggest a suitable enquiry question that they could have investigated. (2)

"How does environmental quality change with distance from the city centre?" (1), which can be answered by comparing the EQS totals at sites at increasing distances from the CBD (1).
:::

::: example Explain one reason: reliability of an EQS
Explain one reason why the results of an environmental quality survey might not be reliable. (2)

The scores are subjective, so different students may give the same street different scores (1), meaning the totals may reflect who did the scoring rather than real differences between sites (1).
:::

::: example Explain one sampling method
Explain one sampling method the students might have used to choose people for their questionnaire. (2)

Systematic sampling, asking every tenth person who passed (1), which avoids the students choosing only friendly-looking people and so reduces bias (1).
:::

::: example Calculation: percentage of respondents
Sample data: 18 of 45 people questioned in the inner city rated their quality of life as poor. Calculate the percentage. (2)

18 ÷ 45 × 100 (1) = 40% (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark: accuracy and reliability of your own results
You have carried out your own fieldwork investigating how and why the quality of life varies in urban areas. Assess the accuracy and reliability of the results of your investigation. (8)

Our transect of six sites from the city centre showed EQS totals rising from −8 in the inner city to +15 in the outer suburbs, and residents' scores followed the same pattern.

The accuracy of some data was limited. The EQS is subjective: in our group, scores for litter at the same site differed by up to two points. We reduced this by using a fixed sheet with descriptions for each score and agreeing scores in pairs, but some bias remains. Pedestrian counts were more objective, but only lasted five minutes.

Reliability was also limited. We visited each site once, on a weekday afternoon, so results might differ at night or at weekends, when perceptions of safety change. We only questioned 10 people per site using opportunistic sampling, so retired people and parents were over-represented. However, the pattern matched the Burgess model and IMD data, which showed the inner-city site among the most deprived areas, so our results are consistent with independent secondary evidence.

Overall, the broad pattern is reliable because three separate sources agree, but the exact scores are not accurate enough to rank sites with similar totals. Repeating at different times with stratified samples would improve both.

(Level 3: clear distinction between accuracy and reliability, specific data and methods, supported judgement.)
:::

---
Sites are spread from the centre to the edge of the city (1), so changes in quality of life with distance can be seen and compared with the Burgess model (1).

Explain how one qualitative fieldwork method helped your urban investigation. (2)

::: q
Explain two ways the students could have improved their pedestrian counts. (4)
---
Two from: count for longer, e.g. 15 minutes (1), so short bursts do not distort results (1); count at the same time at every site (1), so the comparison is fair (1); repeat on different days, including weekends (1), giving more reliable results (1); use clickers and two counters (1), reducing missed people (1).

::: watch
- Calling an EQS "objective". It is **subjective**; say how you reduced the bias.
- Forgetting the spec focus: quality of life **varies within** urban areas, and **why**.
- Using census data without saying **how old** it is or which **areas** it covered.
- Enquiry questions that cannot be answered with fieldwork, such as "Is London a good place to live?". Make it specific and measurable.
- Mixing up **qualitative** (views, perceptions) and **quantitative** (counts, scores) methods.
- In the own-work 8-marker, writing about "fieldwork in general" with no site names, sample sizes or results.
:::
