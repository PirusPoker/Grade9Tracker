## What it is

This key idea says the taiga is **increasingly threatened by commercial development**. It covers:
- **direct threats** from **logging** for **softwood**, **pulp and paper**;
- **indirect threats** from the exploitation of **minerals**, **fossil fuels** and **HEP** potential;
- how **acid precipitation**, **forest fires**, and **pests and diseases** contribute to a **loss of biodiversity**.

Any real area of taiga earns the marks. This course uses **Canada** (the **Alberta oil sands**, British Columbia, Quebec) and **Russia** (**Siberia**). Remember from 8.2 why the taiga is so vulnerable: it grows **slowly**, recovers **slowly**, and has **few species**, so losing one has a large effect on simple food webs.

### Why the taiga is under pressure

- It is huge, sparsely populated and rich in **timber, minerals, oil, gas and fast-flowing rivers**.
- Canada and Russia are among the world's largest producers of timber, paper and energy, and these exports are vital to their economies.
- Rising global demand for construction timber, packaging (for online shopping), energy and metals pushes development further north.
- New roads, railways and pipelines open up remote areas that were once protected by their isolation.

### Direct threats: logging for softwood, pulp and paper

| Use | What it is | Effects on the taiga |
|---|---|---|
| **Softwood timber** | spruce, pine and fir sawn into planks for construction and furniture | large-scale **clear-cutting** removes whole areas of forest at once |
| **Pulp** | wood broken into fibres | the raw material for paper, cardboard and tissue |
| **Paper and packaging** | newsprint, cardboard, tissue | huge demand from the USA, Europe and China; uses smaller, younger trees |

**How logging reduces biodiversity:**
- **Clear-cutting** removes habitat for species such as the **woodland caribou**, which needs large areas of old forest with lichens to feed on in winter.
- **Slow regrowth:** in the cold climate, trees can take **many decades to a century** to reach maturity, so the forest is slow to recover.
- **Replanting with one species** creates **monoculture** plantations with fewer niches and greater vulnerability to pests and fire.
- **Logging roads** fragment the forest, disturb wildlife and give access to hunters, settlers and illegal loggers.
- Bare ground is exposed to erosion, and in the north, removing trees can let the **permafrost thaw**.
- In **Siberia** and the Russian Far East, **illegal logging** is a serious problem, with much timber exported, especially to China. Remote areas are hard to police.

### Indirect threats: minerals, fossil fuels and HEP

These activities are not aimed at the trees, but forest must be cleared or flooded to reach the resources.

**Fossil fuels: the Alberta oil sands, Canada**
- Northern Alberta has one of the world's largest deposits of **bitumen** (very heavy oil mixed with sand and clay), lying under the boreal forest around **Fort McMurray** and the **Athabasca River**.
- **Surface mining** strips away forest, peat bogs and soil over large areas to dig out the sand.
- **In-situ extraction** (steam injected down wells for deeper deposits) needs well pads, pipelines, roads and cut lines that **fragment** the forest into small patches.
- **Tailings ponds** store huge volumes of toxic waste water; they can leak and are a danger to birds.
- Processing releases **sulphur dioxide** and **nitrogen oxides** (causing acid rain) and large amounts of **greenhouse gases**.
- Fragmentation helps predators such as wolves reach caribou more easily; woodland caribou herds in the region have declined.
- **Reclamation** (restoring mined land) is required by law, but rebuilding peatland and mature forest is very slow.
- In **West Siberia**, oil and gas fields cause pipeline leaks and spills across forest and wetland.

**Minerals**
- The taiga holds nickel, copper, gold, diamonds and uranium.
- Mining clears land for pits, waste heaps, roads and towns, and smelters pollute the air.
- The nickel smelters at **Norilsk** in northern Siberia have released so much sulphur dioxide that large areas of surrounding forest have died.

**HEP**
- Fast-flowing rivers make the taiga ideal for HEP.
- The **James Bay Project** in northern Quebec dammed several rivers, flooding large areas of forest and wetland and affecting the traditional lands of the **Cree**.
- Reservoirs drown habitat, change river flows and fish migration downstream, and flooded soils release **mercury** into the water, which builds up in fish.
- Upstream dams and oil sands development have been linked to changes in the **Peace–Athabasca Delta** in Wood Buffalo National Park (see 8.6).

### Acid precipitation

- Burning coal and oil, smelting metals and processing oil sands release **sulphur dioxide (SO₂)** and **nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)**.
- These dissolve in water in the atmosphere to form weak **sulphuric and nitric acids**, which fall as acid rain, snow or fog, sometimes hundreds of kilometres downwind.
- Effects:
  - **Leaches** nutrients such as calcium and magnesium from the soil, so trees are starved.
  - Releases **aluminium** from the soil, which damages roots.
  - Damages the waxy coating of **needles**, so trees lose more water and are more vulnerable to frost, pests and disease.
  - **Acidifies lakes and rivers**, killing fish and the birds and mammals that feed on them.
- Taiga **podzols** are already acidic and have little ability to neutralise extra acid, so damage builds up fast.
- In the 1970s and 1980s, forests and lakes in **Scandinavia** suffered badly from acid rain blown from industrial Britain and the rest of Europe; emissions controls since then have reduced the problem there.

### Forest fires

- Fire is a **natural** part of the taiga cycle (see 8.2), usually started by lightning, and some pines need it to release their seeds.
- But fires are becoming **more frequent, larger and more intense** because of **hotter, drier summers** and longer fire seasons linked to climate change.
- The **Fort McMurray wildfire (2016)** forced the evacuation of the whole city. Canada's **2023** fire season burned a record area, far larger than any previous season, and huge fires have also burned in **Siberia**.
- Effects: habitat loss; wildlife killed or displaced; large amounts of **carbon** released from trees and peat; if fires return too often, trees cannot reach seed-bearing age and the forest may not regrow; burned ground can speed **permafrost thaw**.

### Pests and diseases

- **Mountain pine beetle (British Columbia):** a native beetle that lays eggs under the bark of pine trees, killing them. Since the late 1990s a huge outbreak has killed a **large share of the province's lodgepole pine**, turning forests red then grey.
  - **Why it spread:** milder winters no longer had the long very cold spells (around −35 to −40 °C) that used to kill the larvae, and there were vast areas of mature pine of the same age.
  - The beetle has spread east across the Rocky Mountains into Alberta.
  - **Effects:** loss of habitat and timber, job losses in sawmill towns, and huge amounts of dead, dry wood that **fuel fires**.
- **Spruce budworm** in eastern Canada periodically defoliates large areas of spruce and fir.
- **Fungal diseases** and root rots spread more easily in stressed trees.
- Because the taiga has **few tree species**, one pest that attacks a dominant tree can affect a whole landscape.

### Linking the threats

The threats **reinforce** each other: climate change brings milder winters (more beetles) and drier summers (more fires); beetle-killed trees fuel fires; acid rain weakens trees so pests attack more easily; roads built for logging or oil open the forest to further development. Biodiversity loss in a simple ecosystem is hard to reverse, because the taiga grows and recovers so slowly.

::: terms
- **Softwood**: timber from coniferous trees such as spruce, pine and fir, used mainly in construction.
- **Pulp**: wood broken down into fibres, used to make paper, cardboard and tissue.
- **Clear-cutting**: felling all the trees in an area at once, leaving bare ground.
- **Monoculture**: a plantation of a single species, with low biodiversity and high vulnerability to pests.
- **Oil sands**: deposits of sand and clay mixed with bitumen, a very heavy oil, as found in northern Alberta.
- **Tailings pond**: a large lake that stores toxic waste water and sediment from oil sands or mining.
- **Fragmentation**: the breaking up of a continuous habitat into smaller isolated patches by roads, pipelines or clearings.
- **Acid precipitation**: rain, snow or fog made acidic by sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels and smelting.
- **Mountain pine beetle**: a native insect whose larvae kill pine trees, causing a huge outbreak in British Columbia.
- **Reclamation**: restoring land after mining or extraction so that it can support vegetation again.
:::

::: method
1. **Sort the threat**: direct (logging for softwood, pulp, paper), indirect (minerals, fossil fuels, HEP), or a biodiversity threat (acid rain, fire, pests and diseases).
2. **Name a place**: Alberta oil sands, James Bay, Norilsk, British Columbia.
3. **Explain the process**, then its effect on **biodiversity**: which species or habitat suffers, and why the taiga recovers slowly.
4. **Link threats** for higher marks: climate change → milder winters → more beetles → more dead wood → more fire.
5. **8-mark evaluate**: weigh the threats, use the resource, consider scale and how reversible each is, and reach a judgement about which is most serious.
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: threats to biodiversity
Study a resource showing a map of British Columbia with areas of pine killed by beetles, a graph of area burned by wildfires in Canada rising over time, and a photo description of an oil sands mine. Evaluate the threats to the biodiversity of the taiga. (8)

The map shows huge areas of British Columbia's pine forest killed by the mountain pine beetle. This is one of the most serious threats because the taiga has few tree species: when a pest attacks a dominant tree such as lodgepole pine, whole landscapes lose habitat. Milder winters no longer kill the larvae, so climate change is making outbreaks worse.

The fire graph shows the area burned rising. Fire is natural, but more frequent and intense fires kill wildlife, release carbon from peat and may stop the forest regrowing, especially where beetle-killed trees add fuel.

Commercial threats matter too. The oil sands mine shows forest and peat stripped completely, and wells and roads fragment habitat for caribou. However, oil sands mining affects a smaller area than fires and beetles, and is regulated with reclamation required. Logging covers wider areas, and replanting as monocultures reduces diversity. Acid rain from smelters such as Norilsk can kill forests outright, but its impact is local.

Overall, climate-linked threats (fire and pests) are the most serious because they act across vast areas and reinforce each other. Commercial development is more locally intense but more controllable through regulation.

::: watch
- Mixing up **direct** threats (logging for softwood, pulp and paper) and **indirect** ones (minerals, fossil fuels, HEP).
- Saying acid rain "burns" trees. It **leaches nutrients**, releases aluminium and damages needles.
- Treating all fire as a threat. Fire is **natural**; the threat is **more frequent and intense** fire.
- Explaining a threat without linking it to **biodiversity loss** (habitat, species, food webs).
- Forgetting **why** the taiga is vulnerable: slow growth, few species, simple food webs.
- Inventing areas burned or numbers of trees killed. "A record area" or "a large share" is safe.
:::
