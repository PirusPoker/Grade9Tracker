## What it is

Rocks are only the starting point. The UK's landscapes are distinctive because **physical processes** and **human activity** have worked on those rocks over thousands of years. This key idea covers:
- why distinctive **upland** and **lowland** landscapes result from physical processes working together: **weathering** and **climate**, and **post-glacial river and slope processes**;
- why distinctive landscapes result from **human activity** over time: **agriculture**, **forestry** and **settlement**.

It carries two skills that appear all over Paper 2: **locating key physical features** (uplands, lowland basins, rivers) on an outline map of the UK, and **recognising physical and human features on 1:25 000 and 1:50 000 OS maps**. Any accurate UK example earns the marks; learn at least one upland and one lowland example in detail.

### Climate: the UK's two halves

The UK has a mild, wet **temperate maritime** climate, but it varies from place to place:
- **West against east**: the **prevailing wind** is from the **south-west**, bringing moist air off the Atlantic. When it rises over the western mountains it cools and its moisture condenses, giving **relief rainfall**. Parts of the western uplands receive well over 2,000 mm of rain a year; parts of the south-east, in the **rain shadow**, get under 700 mm.
- **North against south**: temperatures fall northwards and with **altitude** (roughly 6–7 °C cooler for every 1,000 m of height), so upland Scotland has long, cold winters with frequent frost and snow.

Climate matters because it controls **which processes work hardest**: freeze-thaw needs frequent frosts, chemical weathering needs water, and river erosion needs high rainfall and discharge.

### Weathering

**Weathering** is the breakdown of rock **in place**, without it being moved. (Erosion is the wearing away and removal of rock by moving water, ice or wind.)

| Type | Process | Where in the UK it matters most |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanical (physical): freeze-thaw** | water enters cracks, freezes and expands by about 9%, widening the crack; repeated freezing and thawing breaks pieces off | cold, wet **uplands** with many frosts, e.g. the Lake District and Snowdonia, where angular fragments pile up as **scree** below crags |
| **Chemical: carbonation** | rain absorbs carbon dioxide, forming a weak **carbonic acid** that dissolves **calcium carbonate** in limestone and chalk | **limestone** areas, e.g. the Yorkshire Dales: pavements, caves and swallow holes |
| **Biological** | plant roots grow into cracks and prise them apart; burrowing animals; acids from decaying plants and lichens | everywhere, especially woodland and on cliffs |

On granite, weathering along **joints** over a very long time, followed by the removal of the loose material, left the **tors** of Dartmoor.

### Post-glacial river and slope processes

Since the ice sheets melted roughly 11,000–12,000 years ago, rivers and slopes have reshaped the glacial landscape.

**River processes**
- Rivers now flow along the floors of **U-shaped valleys**. A small river in a huge glacial trough is sometimes called a **misfit stream**: the valley is far too large for the river to have carved it.
- Meltwater at the end of the glacial period carried enormous loads, spreading **sand and gravel** across valley floors.
- Rivers have since cut down into glacial deposits and deposited **alluvium** (river silt), building **flood plains** in lowland valleys.
- Lakes in glacial valleys are slowly filling with sediment brought in by rivers, forming flat, marshy **deltas** at their heads.

**Slope processes (mass movement)**
- **Rockfall**: blocks loosened by freeze-thaw drop from steep crags, building **scree slopes**.
- **Landslides and slumping**: after heavy rain, saturated material slides or slumps down slopes, especially on clay or where glaciers left oversteepened valley sides.
- **Soil creep**: the very slow movement of soil downhill, shown by bent tree trunks, leaning walls and small ridges (terracettes) on grassy slopes.

**How they combine**
- **Upland example (the Lake District)**: hard volcanic rock carved into corries and U-shaped valleys by ice; freeze-thaw building scree; heavy relief rainfall feeding fast streams and waterfalls from hanging valleys; slope failures on steep sides.
- **Lowland example (the South Downs)**: chalk with rounded slopes smoothed by periglacial processes; dry valleys; soil creep on the scarp slope; little surface water because the chalk is porous; clay vales either side with slow, meandering rivers and wide flood plains.

### Human activity over time

People have been changing the UK's landscape for thousands of years; there is almost no truly natural landscape left.

**Agriculture**
- **Forest clearance for farming** began thousands of years ago, when early farmers cleared woodland for crops and grazing.
- **Upland grazing**: sheep and deer grazing stops trees regrowing, so many moorlands, such as those of the Pennines and Lake District, are open grassland and heather because of grazing, not climate alone. Grouse moors are burned in patches to manage heather.
- **Field patterns**: dry-stone walls in the uplands; hedgerows in the lowlands, many planted during **enclosure** of open fields. Many hedgerows were removed in the 20th century to create bigger fields for machinery.
- **Drainage**: the **Fens** of East Anglia were once wetlands; drainage over several centuries turned them into flat, very fertile **arable** land. The peat has shrunk as it dried out, so much of the land is now at or below sea level.
- **Lowland arable farming** dominates the drier east (cereals, oilseed rape, vegetables); **pastoral** farming dominates the wetter west and the uplands.

**Forestry**
- Most of the UK was once covered by **wildwood**. Today woodland covers only a small share of the land, low compared with most of Europe.
- In the 20th century large **conifer plantations** were planted in uplands, after the Forestry Commission was set up in 1919, to rebuild timber supplies. **Kielder Forest** in Northumberland is the largest planted forest in England.
- Plantations change the landscape: dark blocks of trees in straight lines, forest roads, drainage ditches and acidified streams.
- Recent policy plants more **native broadleaved woodland** and restores peat bogs.

**Settlement**
- Early settlements grew at **sites** with clear advantages: **bridging points** on rivers, defensive hills, dry points above flood plains, and **spring lines** at the foot of chalk escarpments, where water emerges from the permeable chalk above impermeable clay.
- **Industrial towns** grew on coalfields and in valleys with water power in the 18th and 19th centuries.
- **Urban growth** in the 20th century covered large areas of lowland with houses, roads and factories, especially in the south-east; uplands such as Snowdonia stay thinly populated.
- **Quarrying and reservoirs**: slate quarries in Snowdonia, limestone quarries in the Peak District and reservoirs that flooded upland valleys (for example in the Lake District and mid-Wales) are major human marks on upland landscapes.

### Skill: OS maps at 1:25 000 and 1:50 000

- **Scale**: at **1:25 000**, 4 cm on the map = 1 km on the ground; at **1:50 000**, 2 cm = 1 km. Grid squares are always 1 km across.
- **Four-figure grid reference**: eastings (along the bottom, numbers increasing eastwards) first, then northings (up the side). The reference names the square whose **bottom-left corner** is at those lines.
- **Six-figure grid reference**: divide each square into tenths. For example, 345 678 means five tenths of the way east from easting 34, and eight tenths of the way north from northing 67. "Along the corridor, then up the stairs."
- **Relief**: **contour lines** join places of equal height. Close contours mean steep slopes; widely spaced contours mean gentle slopes. **Spot heights** and **triangulation pillars** give exact heights.
- **Recognising features**: cliffs and crags (black symbols), scree, marsh, coniferous and deciduous woodland symbols, rivers in blue, settlements, quarries, field boundaries, reservoirs with dams.

::: terms
- **Weathering**: the breakdown of rock in place by mechanical, chemical or biological processes, without the rock being moved away.
- **Freeze-thaw weathering**: water in cracks freezes and expands, widening the crack; repeated cycles break off angular fragments.
- **Carbonation**: chemical weathering in which weak carbonic acid in rain dissolves calcium carbonate in limestone and chalk.
- **Mass movement**: the movement of rock and soil down a slope under gravity, such as rockfall, landslides, slumping and soil creep.
- **Scree**: a slope of angular rock fragments that have fallen from a cliff or crag, often after freeze-thaw weathering.
- **Relief rainfall**: rain caused when moist air is forced to rise over high ground, cools and condenses.
- **Rain shadow**: an area on the sheltered side of high ground that receives less rain because the air has already lost its moisture.
- **Misfit stream**: a small river flowing in a valley far too large for it to have created, usually a glacial trough.
- **Enclosure**: the dividing of open fields and common land into separate fields bounded by hedges, walls or fences.
- **Plantation**: a forest planted by people, often of fast-growing conifers in straight rows.
- **Spring line**: a line of springs, and often villages, where water emerges at the junction of a permeable rock above an impermeable one.
- **Contour line**: a line on a map joining places of equal height above sea level.
:::

::: method
1. **Decide physical or human**: questions often ask for one or the other, or for both to be weighed.
2. **Name the process precisely**: freeze-thaw, carbonation, rockfall, soil creep, drainage, afforestation, urban growth. Avoid "erosion" on its own.
3. **Link process to landform**: freeze-thaw produces scree; carbonation produces limestone pavements; grazing produces open moorland.
4. **Link to climate or rock**: say why the process works hardest there (frequent frosts in the uplands; calcium carbonate in limestone).
5. **OS questions**: eastings before northings, check the scale, count squares for distance, and read contours for height and slope.
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: describe what the figure shows, explain each feature with a process, weigh physical against human, and finish with "the most important factor is… because…".
:::

They have the same easting, and the northings differ by 39.5 − 37.5 = 2.0 squares (1). Each square is 1 km, so the distance is 2 km (1).

::: example Explain two: slope processes
Explain two ways in which post-glacial slope processes have changed upland valleys. (4)

Freeze-thaw weathering loosens blocks on steep valley sides, which fall as rockfall (1), so scree slopes have built up at the base of crags, partly covering the glacial valley sides (1). Oversteepened valley sides left by the glaciers are unstable (1), so after heavy rain landslides and slumps carry material down onto the valley floor, making the U-shape less sharp (1).

::: q
Explain one difference between the weathering processes in an upland granite area and a lowland chalk area. (3)
---
In granite uplands, frequent frost makes freeze-thaw the main process (1), widening joints and leaving tors and angular scree (1); in chalk lowlands, with fewer frosts, chemical weathering by carbonation dissolves the calcium carbonate, so the rock is lowered slowly and evenly into smooth rounded downs (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why rainfall is higher in the west of the UK than in the east. (4)
---
Prevailing south-westerly winds come off the Atlantic (1), so air reaching the west is moist (1). The highest mountains are in the west (1), so air is forced to rise, cool and condense, giving relief rainfall (1). The east lies in a rain shadow (1), because air has lost most of its moisture over the western uplands before it arrives (1).
:::

::: watch
- Confusing **weathering** (rock broken down in place) with **erosion** (rock worn away and moved). Say which one you mean.
- Writing "the weather erodes the rock". Name the process: freeze-thaw, carbonation, biological.
- Forgetting that many "natural" uplands are **open because of grazing**, not just climate.
- Giving northings before eastings in grid references, or using the wrong scale (1:25 000 is 4 cm per km; 1:50 000 is 2 cm per km).
- In human-activity answers, being vague. "Farming changed the land" scores little; "draining the Fens turned wetland into arable fields" scores.
- On outline maps, placing the Pennines in Wales or Dartmoor in the north. Learn the map.
- In the 8-marker, writing about physical processes only. The question wants physical **and** human, weighed.
:::

---

::: test
Explain how carbonation creates distinctive landscapes in carboniferous limestone areas. (3)
---
Rain absorbs carbon dioxide and becomes a weak carbonic acid (1). This reacts with and dissolves the calcium carbonate in limestone, especially along joints and bedding planes (1). Over time joints widen into grykes between clints on limestone pavements, and underground water dissolves caves, giving karst scenery such as the Yorkshire Dales (1).
:::
