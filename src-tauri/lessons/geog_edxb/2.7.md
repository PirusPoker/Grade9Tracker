## What it is

As India's economy has grown, so has its power in the world. This key idea covers:
- how rapid development has changed India's **geopolitical influence**: its **regional** influence and its role in **international organisations**;
- its changing relationships with the **EU and the USA**;
- the **conflicting views** of different groups on the costs and benefits of these international relations and of **foreign investment by TNCs**.

Any real emerging-country case study earns the marks; this course uses India. Keep facts general and avoid inventing figures.

### Geopolitical influence: the region

- India has the largest population, economy and military in **South Asia**.
- It was a founder member of the **South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC)**, although tensions with Pakistan have stalled it, and it works with Bay of Bengal neighbours in other groupings.
- It gives aid, loans and infrastructure to neighbours such as Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and the Maldives, partly to compete with China's growing influence there.
- Rivalries limit its influence: a long dispute with **Pakistan** over Kashmir, and border tensions with **China** in the Himalayas, including deadly clashes in 2020.

### Geopolitical influence: international organisations

| Organisation | India's role |
|---|---|
| **G20** (the world's largest economies) | member; hosted the G20 summit in New Delhi in 2023, using it to speak for developing countries |
| **BRICS** (with Brazil, Russia, China, South Africa and newer members) | founder member; promotes a bigger voice for emerging economies, including a BRICS development bank |
| **United Nations** | founder member; often elected to the Security Council as a non-permanent member and campaigning for a **permanent seat**; one of the largest contributors of troops to UN peacekeeping over the decades |
| **World Trade Organization** | member; argues for protection for its farmers in trade talks |
| **The Quad** (with the USA, Japan and Australia) | a security partnership in the Indo-Pacific, seen as balancing China |
| **Shanghai Cooperation Organisation** | member since 2017, alongside China and Russia |

Other signs of growing power: India is a **nuclear power**, has a successful **space programme** (including landing a probe near the Moon's south pole in 2023), and its culture spreads as **soft power** through Bollywood, food, yoga and its diaspora.

India's traditional foreign policy is **strategic autonomy**: it works with many partners (the USA, Russia, the EU, Gulf states) without joining any one bloc.

### Relationships with the USA and the EU

**USA**
- Relations were cool for much of the Cold War, but have become far closer since 2000.
- A **civil nuclear agreement** (2008) let India buy nuclear fuel and technology despite not signing the Non-Proliferation Treaty.
- Growing **defence** ties and joint exercises, and cooperation through the Quad.
- The USA is one of India's largest trading partners, and US TNCs are major investors in India's IT sector; a large and successful Indian diaspora lives in the USA.
- Tensions remain over **trade tariffs**, US visa rules for Indian IT workers, and India's continuing ties with Russia.

**EU**
- The EU as a whole is one of India's biggest trading partners and sources of FDI.
- The two sides have held long-running negotiations on a **free-trade agreement**, slowed by disagreements over farm products, cars, wine and spirits, and data rules.
- Cooperation on climate change, clean energy and technology.
- Individual European countries are also important: France supplies fighter jets; the UK has strong links through the diaspora and Indian investment such as Tata's ownership of Jaguar Land Rover.

### Conflicting views on international relations and foreign investment

| Stakeholder | View | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **Indian government** | strongly in favour of FDI and closer ties | growth, jobs, technology, tax revenue and global status; trade deals open markets for Indian exports |
| **Foreign TNCs** (e.g. Microsoft, Walmart, Hyundai) | in favour | a huge market, skilled and relatively cheap labour, government incentives |
| **Educated urban workers** | mostly in favour | well-paid jobs in IT, finance and manufacturing |
| **Small shopkeepers and traders** | often against | fear being driven out by foreign retail giants and online platforms (Walmart bought the Indian online retailer Flipkart in 2018) |
| **Small farmers** | often against | fear cheap imports under trade deals; land taken for factories and special economic zones |
| **Trade unions** | critical | low wages, long hours, insecure contracts in some TNC supply chains |
| **Environmental groups** | critical | pollution and weak regulation; the 1984 Bhopal gas leak at a US-owned pesticide plant, which killed thousands, remains a warning |
| **Nationalists and some politicians** | cautious | worry about loss of sovereignty, foreign control of key industries, and being drawn into US-China rivalry |

**Costs and benefits in summary**
- **Benefits:** capital, jobs, technology, skills, exports, infrastructure, global influence and security partnerships.
- **Costs:** profits sent abroad, competition for small local firms, environmental damage, unequal benefits between core and periphery, dependence on foreign markets, and pressure on national policy.

::: terms
- **Geopolitics**: how geography, power and international relations combine to shape a country's influence in the world.
- **Geopolitical influence**: a country's ability to shape events and decisions beyond its borders.
- **G20**: a group of the world's largest economies that meets to coordinate economic policy.
- **BRICS**: a grouping of large emerging economies, originally Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa.
- **UN Security Council**: the UN body responsible for international peace and security, with five permanent members.
- **Strategic autonomy**: India's policy of working with many partners without joining any single bloc.
- **Free-trade agreement**: a deal between countries to reduce or remove tariffs and other barriers to trade.
- **Stakeholder**: a person or group with an interest in a decision or development.
- **Sovereignty**: a country's right to make its own decisions without outside control.
:::

::: method
1. **Influence questions:** give the organisation or relationship, India's role, and what it shows about its power.
2. **EU and USA questions:** use specific links (nuclear deal, Quad, trade, FTA talks) and a tension for balance.
3. **Conflicting views:** name the stakeholder, state the view, and give their reason. Contrast at least two stakeholders.
4. **Costs and benefits:** say who gains and who loses, and where.
5. **8-mark evaluate:** weigh benefits against costs for different groups, then reach a supported overall judgement.
:::

::: example Explain one: international organisations
Explain one way India's role in international organisations has changed. (2)

India hosted the G20 summit in 2023 (1), showing it is now seen as a major economy that can lead discussions on global issues (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: relations with the USA
Explain two ways India's relationship with the USA has changed. (4)

Defence and security cooperation has grown (1), for example through the Quad with Japan and Australia, partly to balance China (1). Trade and investment have expanded (1), with US TNCs investing heavily in India's IT sector and the USA becoming one of India's largest trading partners (1).
:::

::: example Contrasting stakeholder views
Explain why different groups in India hold conflicting views about foreign investment by TNCs. (4)

The government supports TNC investment (1) because it brings jobs, technology and tax revenue that boost growth (1). Small shopkeepers often oppose it (1) because they fear foreign retail giants and online platforms will take their customers (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: TNC investment
Evaluate the costs and benefits for India of increasing foreign investment by TNCs. (8)

The benefits are large. TNCs such as Microsoft and Hyundai have brought capital, technology and millions of jobs, especially in IT hubs such as Bengaluru. They have raised wages for educated workers, increased exports, and paid taxes that fund infrastructure. Investment also strengthens India's relationships with the USA, EU and Japan.

However, there are real costs. Profits are often sent back to the TNC's home country. Small shopkeepers fear competition from foreign retailers such as Walmart, which bought Flipkart. Farmers have lost land to factories and special economic zones, and environmental regulation has sometimes been weak, as the Bhopal disaster showed. Benefits are concentrated in core states such as Maharashtra and Karnataka, widening the gap with Bihar.

Overall, the benefits outweigh the costs for India as a whole, because TNC investment has been central to its growth and global influence. But the costs fall on particular groups and regions, so the balance depends on who you are and where you live, and stronger regulation is needed to share the benefits more widely.

(Level 3: benefits and costs weighed with evidence, stakeholders and regions considered, and a substantiated conclusion.)
:::

::: watch
- Writing "people disagree" without naming **who** and **why**. Conflicting-views answers need named stakeholders.
- One-sided answers on foreign investment: give costs **and** benefits.
- Inventing figures for trade or FDI. Keep them general.
- Forgetting the EU, when the spec names both the EU **and** the USA.
- Treating India as a close US ally in all things. Its policy is **strategic autonomy**, and tensions remain.
- In 8-markers, ending without saying for whom the benefits outweigh the costs.
:::
