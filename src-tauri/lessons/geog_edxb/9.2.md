## What it is

This key idea is part of **Topic 9, Consuming energy resources**, examined in **Paper 3 Section C** (resource-based questions with two 8-mark questions) and often feeding the **Section D decision** (12 marks plus 4 SPaG). It says that **access to energy is not evenly distributed**, and that this matters for people. It covers:
- how access depends on **technology** and on **physical resources**: **geology**, **accessibility**, and the **climate and landscape** that give a place its renewable potential;
- the **global pattern of energy use per capita** (per person);
- the **causes** of the variation: **level of economic development**, **reliance on traditional fuels**, and **demand from different economic sectors**;
- the skill of using and interpreting **world maps of energy resources**.

Any real examples of the right type earn the marks. This course uses Iceland (geothermal and HEP), Kenya (geothermal in the Rift Valley and off-grid solar), the Gulf oil states, the USA, China, India and sub-Saharan Africa.

### What decides whether a place can get energy

**1. Physical resources (what nature provides)**

| Factor | How it affects access | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Geology** | fossil fuels form only in certain rocks: oil and gas in **sedimentary basins**, coal from ancient swamp forests; uranium in particular ores; **geothermal** heat is greatest at **plate boundaries** and hotspots | oil in the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Iran, Kuwait); coal in China, the USA, India, Australia, Russia; geothermal in Iceland, Kenya, New Zealand |
| **Accessibility** | a resource is only useful if it can be reached and moved: remote, deep, frozen or offshore deposits cost more; landlocked countries depend on neighbours for pipelines and ports | Arctic oil and gas; deep-water oil off Brazil; landlocked countries in Africa importing fuel by road |
| **Climate** | decides renewable potential: sunshine for **solar**, strong steady winds for **wind**, high rainfall for **HEP** | solar in desert regions such as the Sahara, the Middle East and Australia; wind on Atlantic coasts such as the UK; HEP in rainy mountains such as Norway |
| **Landscape** | relief and coastline set potential: steep valleys and large rivers for **HEP**, exposed uplands and shallow seas for **wind**, estuaries for **tidal** | HEP in Norway, Brazil and China; offshore wind in the shallow North Sea |

**Iceland** shows physical factors at their most favourable: it sits on the **Mid-Atlantic Ridge**, so it has abundant geothermal heat, and its high rainfall and glaciers feed fast rivers for HEP. Almost all its electricity comes from these two sources.

**2. Technology (what people can do with it)**

Technology turns a physical resource into usable energy. Rich countries and TNCs can afford it; poorer countries often cannot.
- **Extraction technology** opens resources that were once out of reach: deep-water drilling, horizontal drilling and **fracking** for shale gas, processing of **tar sands**.
- **Renewable technology**: cheaper solar panels, larger turbines, batteries and smart grids.
- **Infrastructure**: power stations, grids, pipelines and refineries. A country may have resources but lack the capital to use them. Many African countries have large solar and HEP potential but low electricity access because grids are limited.
- **Off-grid technology**, such as solar home systems paid for in small instalments by mobile phone (widely used in **Kenya**), can bring electricity to villages far from the grid.

**Exam tip:** "access" needs both halves. Having oil under the ground is not access unless the technology and money exist to extract it.

### Interpreting world maps of energy resources

Expect a map showing reserves of a fuel, or renewable potential, with a key. To describe a distribution:
1. Give the **general pattern** (concentrated or widespread; which continents).
2. Name **specific areas** with high and low values, using the key.
3. Give an **anomaly** (an exception to the pattern).
4. Only explain if the command word asks for it.

| Resource | Typical distribution on a world map |
|---|---|
| **Oil** | highly concentrated: the **Middle East** holds the largest share of proven reserves; also Venezuela, Canada (oil sands), Russia, the USA, Nigeria |
| **Natural gas** | concentrated in **Russia**, **Iran** and **Qatar**; also the USA (shale) |
| **Coal** | more widespread: **USA, Russia, Australia, China, India** |
| **Solar potential** | highest in the **subtropical deserts** (Sahara, Arabia, Australia, southwest USA), lowest at high latitudes |
| **Wind potential** | highest on **west-facing coasts in the mid-latitudes** and in shallow seas (North Sea, Patagonia) |
| **Geothermal** | along **plate boundaries**: Iceland, the East African Rift (Kenya, Ethiopia), Indonesia, the Philippines, New Zealand |

### The global pattern of energy use per person

Energy use per capita is usually measured in kilograms of oil equivalent, gigajoules or kilowatt-hours per person per year. The pattern is very unequal: a person in the USA or Canada uses many times more energy than a person in most of sub-Saharan Africa.

| Level | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Very high** | **North America** (USA, Canada), the **Gulf states** (Qatar, UAE, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia), Iceland, Norway, Australia | wealth, large homes and cars, cold winters or air-conditioning, cheap or subsidised fuel |
| **High** | most of **Europe**, Japan, South Korea, Russia | developed economies, but efficiency and public transport keep use below North America |
| **Medium and rising fast** | **China**, Brazil, South Africa, Mexico | industrialisation and rising incomes |
| **Low** | **India**, much of South and Southeast Asia | rising, but hundreds of millions still use little commercial energy |
| **Very low** | most of **sub-Saharan Africa**, Afghanistan, Haiti | many people without electricity; cooking with wood and charcoal |

### Why energy use varies

**1. Level of economic development.** The single strongest factor. As GDP per capita rises, people buy cars, appliances, heating and air-conditioning; homes get bigger; they travel and fly more. Developed countries have more energy-hungry services (offices, data centres, hospitals). So energy use per person rises with wealth.

**2. Reliance on traditional fuel sources.** In many developing countries, **fuelwood, charcoal, crop waste and dung** are the main fuels for cooking and heating. Around two billion people still lack clean cooking fuels. These fuels:
- are often gathered rather than bought, so they barely show in national energy statistics;
- are inefficient, so little useful energy is gained;
- cause **indoor air pollution**, which harms health, especially for women and children;
- take hours to collect, usually by women and girls, reducing time for school or paid work;
- can cause **deforestation** around towns where charcoal demand is high, as in parts of the Sahel and East Africa.

**3. Demand from different economic sectors.**
- **Primary** (farming, fishing, mining): low energy per worker in subsistence farming; higher in mechanised farming and mining.
- **Secondary** (manufacturing): the most energy-intensive, especially steel, cement and chemicals. **China's** rapid rise in energy use came largely from heavy industry as it became the world's main manufacturer.
- **Tertiary and quaternary** (services, IT): high energy use in buildings, transport and data centres, but less per unit of GDP than heavy industry.

**Other factors (useful in 8-mark answers):**
- **Climate:** cold countries need heating (Canada, Russia, Iceland); hot ones need air-conditioning (Gulf states).
- **Resource availability and price:** oil-rich states often subsidise fuel, so it is cheap and used heavily.
- **Population density and distance:** large, spread-out countries such as the USA, Canada and Australia rely on cars and long-distance transport.
- **Energy efficiency and policy:** Japan and many EU countries use less per person than the USA at similar wealth.

### Implications for people

- **Energy poverty** limits development: without electricity, clinics cannot refrigerate vaccines, children cannot study after dark and businesses cannot use machines.
- Expensive energy pushes poorer households into **fuel poverty**, even in developed countries.
- High users have high **carbon footprints**, adding to climate change, which hits low users hardest.
- Countries with large reserves can earn export income, but may become dependent on it.

::: terms
- **Energy access**: the ability of people to obtain reliable, affordable energy, especially electricity and clean cooking fuel.
- **Energy use per capita**: the average amount of energy used by each person in a country in a year.
- **Traditional fuels**: fuels such as fuelwood, charcoal, crop waste and dung, usually gathered and burned for cooking and heating.
- **Energy poverty**: lack of access to modern energy services such as electricity and clean cooking fuels.
- **Geothermal energy**: heat from inside the Earth, used to make electricity or heat water, greatest at plate boundaries.
- **Renewable potential**: how much solar, wind, water or geothermal energy a place could produce, given its climate and landscape.
- **Proven reserves**: the amount of a fuel known to exist that can be extracted profitably with current technology.
- **Energy-intensive industry**: manufacturing that uses large amounts of energy, such as steel, cement and chemicals.
- **Off-grid**: an energy supply not connected to the national electricity network, such as a solar home system.
- **Energy subsidy**: government spending that keeps fuel or electricity prices below their real cost.
:::

::: method
1. **Access questions:** split your answer into **physical** factors (geology, accessibility, climate, landscape) and **human** factors (technology, money, infrastructure). Give a named place for each.
2. **Describe a map:** general pattern, then named high and low areas with values from the key, then an anomaly. No reasons unless asked.
3. **Explain variations in use:** for each cause give the point, the mechanism and an example ("China's heavy industry uses huge amounts of coal, so its energy use per person has risen fast").
4. **Calculations:** energy per capita = total energy ÷ population. Check units and give the answer the question asks for.
5. **For 8-mark assess:** use the resource (AO4), weigh development against traditional fuels, sectors and climate (AO3), and decide which factor is most important.
6. **For the 12-mark decision:** use the physical and human factors from this lesson to judge whether each option suits the place.
:::

::: example Describe: a map of energy use
Study a world map showing energy use per person in five categories. Describe the global pattern of energy use per person. (3)

Energy use is very unequal (1). It is highest in North America and the Gulf states such as Qatar, in the top category (1), and lowest across most of sub-Saharan Africa, in the lowest category (1). Europe is high but below North America, and China is in the middle category.
:::

::: example Explain one: geology and access
Explain one way geology affects a country's access to energy resources. (2)

Oil and gas are only found where suitable sedimentary rocks have trapped them (1), so countries over large basins, such as Saudi Arabia, have abundant cheap oil while others must import it (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: traditional fuels
Explain two impacts on people of relying on traditional fuels. (4)

Burning wood or dung indoors produces smoke (1), causing breathing problems and illness, especially for women and children who spend most time near the fire (1). Collecting fuelwood can take several hours a day (1), so girls may miss school and adults have less time for paid work (1).
:::

::: example Calculate: energy per capita
Sample data: a country uses 1,800 million gigajoules of energy a year and has a population of 60 million. Calculate its energy use per capita. (2)

1,800 ÷ 60 (1) = 30 gigajoules per person per year (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: why energy use varies
Study a resource showing a scatter graph of GDP per capita against energy use per capita for 30 countries (a strong positive correlation, with Qatar and Iceland well above the trend line) and a quote from a Kenyan villager who cooks with firewood. Assess the reasons for the global variation in energy use per person. (8)

The scatter graph shows a strong positive correlation: richer countries use far more energy per person, because wealth brings cars, appliances, larger homes and energy-hungry services. This makes development the main reason for the variation.

However, the anomalies show other factors matter. Qatar lies well above the line because it has huge gas reserves, subsidised fuel and needs air-conditioning in a hot desert climate. Iceland is high because cheap geothermal and HEP power energy-intensive aluminium smelting and heating in a cold climate. Sector demand matters too: China's heavy industry raised its use rapidly.

At the low end, the villager's quote shows reliance on firewood, which is gathered rather than bought and inefficient, so it barely appears in statistics and keeps measured use very low.

Overall, economic development explains most of the pattern, but resources, climate and economic structure explain why some countries sit far above or below what their wealth predicts.

(Level 3: uses both resources (AO4), weighs development against other factors, and reaches a supported judgement (AO3).)
:::

::: q
State two physical factors that affect a country's access to energy resources. (2)
---
Any two: geology (1); accessibility of the resource, e.g. remote, deep or offshore (1); climate, e.g. sunshine or wind (1); landscape, e.g. steep valleys for HEP (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why many people in sub-Saharan Africa have low energy use. (2)
---
Many rural households are not connected to an electricity grid because it is expensive to extend to scattered villages (1), so they rely on small amounts of fuelwood and charcoal for cooking and have little commercial energy (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why energy use per person in Qatar is very high. (2)
---
It has huge gas and oil reserves, so energy is cheap and often subsidised (1), which encourages heavy use, for example of air-conditioning in its hot desert climate and of large cars (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest one reason why Iceland can produce almost all its electricity from renewable sources. (3)
---
It lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a plate boundary (1), so hot rock lies close to the surface and geothermal energy is easy to tap (1); high rainfall and glacial meltwater also feed fast rivers in steep valleys, which are ideal for HEP (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways technology affects access to energy resources. (4)
---
Two from: deep-water drilling and fracking (1) allow oil and gas that were once out of reach to be extracted (1); cheaper solar panels and batteries (1) let remote villages get electricity without a grid (1); lack of capital for power stations and grids (1) means some countries cannot use their own HEP or solar potential (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why China's energy use per person has risen rapidly. (4)
---
Rapid industrialisation (1): heavy industries such as steel and cement use huge amounts of energy, much of it from coal (1). Rising incomes (1): more people own cars, appliances and air-conditioning, and live in cities, so household demand has increased (1).
:::

::: watch
- Describing a map with reasons when the question only says **describe**, or with no named places or values from the key.
- Treating "access" as only physical. **Technology, money and infrastructure** decide whether a resource can be used.
- Saying poorer countries "use no energy". They use **traditional fuels**, which are hard to measure.
- Giving only "rich countries use more" in an 8-marker. Add **traditional fuels**, **sectors** and **climate**, and use the anomalies.
- Forgetting the **implications for people**: health, education and work, not just energy statistics.
- Dividing the wrong way round in per-capita calculations: it is total ÷ population.
:::

::: test
Sample data: Country A uses 9,000 million gigajoules of energy a year and has a population of 30 million. Country B uses 2,000 million gigajoules and has a population of 100 million. Calculate how many times more energy per person Country A uses than Country B. Show your working. (2)
---

::: test
Explain one way climate affects a country's potential to use renewable energy. (2)
---
Countries in subtropical deserts receive strong sunshine almost every day (1), so solar panels produce large amounts of electricity, giving high solar potential (1). Or: windy, exposed coasts in the mid-latitudes (1) give steady strong winds for wind farms (1).

::: test

::: test

::: test
