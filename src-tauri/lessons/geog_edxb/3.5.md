## What it is

The second part of the megacity case study explains why the city has grown so fast and how that growth has reshaped it. This key idea covers:
- the reasons for **past and present trends in population growth**: rates of **natural increase**, **national and international migration**, and **economic investment and growth**;
- how population growth has affected the pattern of **spatial growth** and **changing urban functions and land use**.

Its integrated skill is using **GIS, satellite images, historic images and maps** to investigate spatial growth. In this lesson the maps and images are described in words.

The spec allows any megacity in a developing or emerging country, and any real example earns the marks. This course uses **Mumbai**. Population figures for Mumbai vary with the boundary used (the city, the urban agglomeration or the wider metropolitan region), so keep them rounded.

### Mumbai's population over time

| Period | Approximate population of the urban area | What was happening |
|---|---|---|
| **Around 1900** | under 1 million | colonial port and cotton-mill city |
| **1950s** | about 3 million | migration after Independence (1947) and Partition; industrial growth |
| **1970s–1990s** | rising rapidly to over 10 million | mass rural-urban migration; mills, port and services; megacity status reached |
| **2011 census** | about 18 million in the urban area | growth shifting to the suburbs and outer region |
| **Today** | the wider metropolitan region holds more than 20 million | slower growth in the city, fastest growth on the fringe |

**The trend:** very fast growth in the second half of the 20th century, then **slowing** growth in the city itself. In the 2000s the **old island city** in the south actually **lost** population, while the **suburbs** and outer areas such as Thane, Navi Mumbai and Vasai-Virar grew fastest. Overall the region keeps growing.

### Reason 1: national migration

Most migrants come from **within India**:
- from rural **Maharashtra**, especially drought-prone areas;
- from poorer states such as **Uttar Pradesh** and **Bihar**, and from Gujarat, Karnataka and further away.

**Push factors in rural India**
- Small plots of land, divided further each generation.
- Farming that depends on the monsoon, with **droughts** and crop failures.
- Debt and low incomes; few jobs outside farming.
- Poor access to schools, hospitals and clean water.

**Pull factors in Mumbai**
- **Jobs**, formal and informal, with wages far above rural incomes.
- Better **education and health care**.
- The image of Mumbai as the **city of dreams** spread by Bollywood and by migrants' stories.
- **Chain migration**: relatives and friends already there provide a place to stay and help find work.

### Reason 2: international migration

- International migration is a **much smaller** part of Mumbai's growth.
- Historically, communities such as the Parsis and traders from the Middle East and Europe settled in the port.
- Today, international arrivals include **TNC staff**, professionals and some migrants from neighbouring countries such as Nepal and Bangladesh.
- Mumbai also **sends** migrants abroad, for example to the Gulf states, whose remittances support families in the city.

### Reason 3: natural increase

- Migrants are mostly **young adults**, so they have children in the city. This adds a **second wave** of growth after migration.
- Death rates in Mumbai are low because of better health care, clean water (in most areas) and vaccination, so **births exceed deaths**.
- Over time, **fertility has fallen** across India and especially in Maharashtra, as women's education rises and families choose to have fewer children. So the **rate** of natural increase is falling, though the city's large young population means it still adds many people each year.
- As migration slows and the migrants' children grow up in the city, natural increase is now thought to provide a **similar or larger share** of growth than migration. Estimates differ, so describe this as a change in balance rather than giving exact shares.

### Reason 4: economic investment and growth

- **Colonial era:** investment in the port, the railways and cotton mills.
- **After Independence:** Mumbai became India's commercial and financial centre and a hub for industry: chemicals, engineering, textiles and film.
- **Since 1991:** India's **economic reforms** opened the country to foreign investment. Mumbai, as the financial capital, attracted banks, TNCs, IT and outsourcing firms, media and retail.
- **Infrastructure:** new ports (Jawaharlal Nehru Port opened across the harbour in 1989), the Metro, sea bridges and business districts such as **Bandra-Kurla Complex**.
- Each wave of investment created jobs, which attracted more migrants, which created more demand: a **positive multiplier**.

### Effects on spatial growth

**The pattern of growth**
1. **The southern tip** around the fort and harbour was the original city.
2. **Reclamation** joined the islands and extended land into the sea, for example at **Nariman Point** and Back Bay, to make room for offices and housing.
3. Growth spread **northwards along the railway lines**: the Western line and the Central line created **linear** suburbs, with stations as local centres.
4. When the peninsula filled up, growth jumped to the **mainland**: **Thane** to the north-east, **Navi Mumbai** across the harbour (planned from the 1970s to take pressure off the old city), and **Vasai-Virar** to the north.
5. Today, informal and formal housing presses against the **edge of the national park**, onto **mangroves** and wetlands along the creeks, and onto steep hillsides.

**Why growth took this shape**
- The **sea** and the narrow peninsula prevented growth east and west, so the city could only spread north.
- **Railways** carried commuters from the north into the southern CBD, so housing followed the lines.
- **Land prices** in the south pushed people and firms outwards.
- **Planning**: the state government built Navi Mumbai and new business districts to create new centres.

### Effects on urban functions and land use

| Change | What happened | Effect on land use |
|---|---|---|
| **Decline of the cotton mills** | mills closed after a long strike in the early 1980s and competition from modern factories | large inner-city mill sites (around Lower Parel) redeveloped into offices, malls, luxury apartments and hotels |
| **Growth of services** | finance, IT, media, retail and business services expanded after 1991 | new office districts at Bandra-Kurla Complex and in the suburbs; shopping malls |
| **Port relocation** | container traffic moved from the old docks to Jawaharlal Nehru Port | old dock land in the south available for new uses; port-related industry grew on the mainland |
| **Industry moves out** | factories moved to cheaper land in Thane, Navi Mumbai and beyond | industrial estates on the fringe; old industrial land in the city converted to housing and offices |
| **Housing demand** | millions of new residents | high-rise towers replace low-rise buildings; informal settlements on any spare land, along railways and creeks; new townships on the fringe |
| **Environmental pressure** | building on the edge of the city | loss of mangroves and wetlands; encroachment on the national park; more flood risk |

Overall, Mumbai's **functions** have changed from **port and manufacturing** towards **finance, services and consumption**, and its **land use** has become denser in the centre and more sprawling on the fringe.

### Skill: GIS, satellite images and historic maps

How to use them, in words:
- **Historic maps** of the 19th century show seven islands and a small fort at the southern tip; later maps show the islands joined, mills in the centre and railways running north.
- **Satellite images** today show a continuous built-up area from the southern tip to the northern suburbs and across the harbour; the dark green of the national park in the north; grey informal settlements beside railways and creeks; and new towers on former mill land.
- **GIS layers** can overlay population density, land use and dates of building to show the direction and speed of growth.

When you describe change from maps: state the **direction** of growth, its **extent** (distance, area), the **timing**, and link it to a **reason** such as railways or reclamation.

::: terms
- **Natural increase**: the birth rate minus the death rate; it adds people when births exceed deaths.
- **National migration**: the movement of people within the same country, such as from rural Bihar to Mumbai.
- **International migration**: the movement of people from one country to another.
- **Chain migration**: when migrants follow relatives or friends who have already moved to the same place.
- **Spatial growth**: the increase in the area covered by a city as it spreads outwards.
- **Linear growth**: urban growth that follows a line, such as a railway or main road.
- **Fertility rate**: the average number of children born to each woman in a population.
- **Economic investment**: spending by governments or firms on factories, offices or infrastructure to create future income.
- **Urban function**: the economic or social role of a part of a city, such as finance, industry, housing or trade.
- **Land-use change**: when an area is converted from one use to another, such as a mill site becoming offices.
- **New town**: a planned settlement built to relieve pressure on a crowded city, such as Navi Mumbai.
:::

::: method
1. **Reasons for growth:** use the spec's four headings: natural increase, national migration, international migration, economic investment. Develop each with a Mumbai-specific detail.
2. **Past against present:** say how each reason has changed over time, such as migration slowing and fertility falling.
3. **Spatial growth:** describe the direction (north, then onto the mainland), the reason (peninsula, railways, land prices, planning) and named places.
4. **Functions and land use:** name the old function, the new function and the place where the change happened.
5. **Maps and images:** direction, extent, timing, reason.
6. **8-mark evaluate:** weigh the causes against each other and over time, and give a supported conclusion about which matters most now.
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: migration against natural increase
Evaluate the view that migration is now less important than natural increase in Mumbai's growth. (8)

Migration was the main cause of Mumbai's rapid growth in the 20th century. Millions of people were pushed out of rural Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar by drought, debt and tiny plots, and pulled by jobs in the mills, the port and the informal economy. Without this migration the city would never have reached megacity size.

There are good reasons to think the balance has shifted. The migrants who arrived as young adults have had children in the city, so natural increase now continues even when migration slows. Death rates are low because of health care and vaccination. Estimates suggest natural increase now provides a similar or larger share of growth than migration.

However, migration is still important. People still arrive from poorer states every day, often through chain migration, and much of the growth on the fringe in places such as Vasai-Virar and Navi Mumbai is driven by newcomers. Fertility in Maharashtra has fallen sharply, which reduces natural increase over time. Economic investment since 1991 also keeps attracting skilled migrants to new jobs in finance and IT.

Overall, the view is broadly correct for the city itself, where natural increase from an established young population now sustains most growth. But migration remains essential for the wider metropolitan region, and because falling fertility will reduce natural increase further, migration may become relatively more important again. The two causes are linked, since today's natural increase is the result of yesterday's migration.

(Level 3: both causes explained with Mumbai detail, change over time weighed, and a substantiated conclusion.)
