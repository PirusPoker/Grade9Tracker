## What it is

This is the first part of the **megacity case study**. It covers:
- the significance of the megacity's **site**, **situation** and **connectivity** in a **national** context (environmental and cultural), a **regional** context and a **global** context;
- the megacity's **structure**: the **CBD**, **inner city**, **suburbs** and **urban-rural fringe**, described by their **functions** and **building age**.

The spec allows any megacity in a developing or emerging country, and any real example earns the marks. This course uses **Mumbai**, India, which also links to the India case study in Topic 2. Keep figures rounded.

Pearson has asked "Explain two ways this megacity's location has been important for its economic development" (4 marks). Be ready to answer it with two developed points.

### Site, situation and connectivity: what the words mean

- **Site**: the physical land the city was built on, such as relief, water supply, harbour, defence.
- **Situation**: the city's position relative to the surrounding area and other places, such as the coast, trade routes, neighbouring regions.
- **Connectivity**: how well the city is linked to other places by transport and communications: ports, airports, railways, roads, internet.

### Mumbai's site

- Mumbai lies on India's **west coast**, on the **Arabian Sea**, and is the capital of the state of **Maharashtra**.
- The original city stood on **seven small islands**. From the 17th to the 19th centuries these were joined into one **peninsula** by causeways and **land reclamation** from the sea.
- On the sheltered **eastern side** of the peninsula is a large **natural deep-water harbour**, protected from the open sea. This was the key reason the city grew.
- The site has drawbacks: much of the land is **low-lying and reclaimed**, so it floods during the **monsoon** (heavy rain from about June to September), and the narrow peninsula leaves **little room to expand**, so land in the south is extremely expensive.

### History in brief

| Period | What happened |
|---|---|
| **16th–17th century** | the islands were held by the Portuguese, then passed to England in 1661 and to the East India Company |
| **18th–19th century** | islands joined by reclamation; the port grew as a British trading base |
| **1853** | India's first passenger railway ran from Bombay to Thane, linking the port to the interior |
| **1860s** | a cotton boom, as Bombay supplied cotton to Britain; textile mills spread across the centre |
| **1869** | the Suez Canal opened, cutting the sea route to Europe and boosting Bombay's trade |
| **1995** | the city was officially renamed Mumbai |
| **Since 1991** | economic reforms made Mumbai the main gateway for foreign investment and services |

### Significance in a national context

**Environmental**
- The harbour and the flat reclaimed land made it ideal for **port industry** and docks.
- Its position on the coast gives **easy access to the sea**, but exposure to **monsoon flooding** and, in the long term, **sea-level rise**.
- The **Western Ghats** hills inland supply water from lakes and reservoirs, but also made routes to the interior harder to build.
- **Sanjay Gandhi National Park**, a large forested area, lies inside the city's limits, and mangroves fringe its creeks.

**Cultural**
- Mumbai is India's most **cosmopolitan** city: Marathi, Gujarati, Hindi and many other languages are spoken, and Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Parsi and Jain communities live there.
- It is the home of **Bollywood**, the Hindi film industry, which spreads Mumbai's image across India.
- It is seen across India as the **city of dreams**, a place where migrants hope to make their fortune, which pulls people from every state.

**Economic**
- Mumbai is India's **financial and commercial capital**. It has the **Bombay Stock Exchange** (Asia's oldest), the **National Stock Exchange**, the **Reserve Bank of India**, and the headquarters of many Indian firms and banks.
- It produces a large share of India's tax revenue and handles a large share of its foreign trade.

### Significance in a regional context

- Mumbai is the core of the **Mumbai Metropolitan Region**, which includes Thane, **Navi Mumbai** (a planned city built across the harbour from the 1970s), Kalyan and Vasai-Virar.
- It is linked by an expressway to **Pune**, a major industrial and education city.
- It is the main market, port and service centre for **Maharashtra** and western India, and draws migrants from poorer states such as Uttar Pradesh and Bihar.
- Its port and financial services serve a large **hinterland** of factories and farms.

### Significance in a global context

- **Sea**: Mumbai Port and **Jawaharlal Nehru Port** (Nhava Sheva) across the harbour handle a large share of India's container traffic, on trade routes to the Middle East, Europe and Africa.
- **Air**: Mumbai's international airport is one of India's busiest, with flights to every continent.
- **Finance and business**: global banks, TNCs and outsourcing firms have offices in Mumbai; it is India's main link to the global economy.
- **Culture**: Bollywood films are watched worldwide.
- Mumbai is classed as a **world city**, though below London or New York in influence.

### Connectivity within the city

- The **suburban railway** carries millions of passengers every day along the Western, Central and Harbour lines.
- The **Mumbai Metro** opened its first line in 2014 and is expanding.
- Sea bridges, such as the **Bandra-Worli Sea Link** (2009) and a trans-harbour link to Navi Mumbai (2024), reduce journey times.

### Mumbai's structure

| Zone | Location | Functions | Building age and type |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CBD** | the **southern tip** of the peninsula: Fort, Nariman Point, Colaba | banks, the stock exchange, government offices, TNC headquarters, hotels, tourism | the **oldest** area: 19th-century colonial buildings such as the main railway terminus (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), alongside 20th-century office towers |
| **Second business district** | **Bandra-Kurla Complex**, further north | offices, the National Stock Exchange, consulates, banks | **new**: glass office towers built since the 1990s on reclaimed marshland |
| **Inner city** | central Mumbai, e.g. the old mill district around **Lower Parel**, and **Dharavi** between the two main railway lines | formerly cotton mills; now mixed: offices, shopping malls, luxury flats, and dense informal housing and workshops | **late 19th to mid-20th century** mills and **chawls** (old tenement blocks); many mills replaced by **new** high-rise since the 1990s; informal settlements of all ages |
| **Suburbs** | the **western suburbs** (Bandra, Andheri, Borivali) and **eastern suburbs** (Kurla, Ghatkopar, Mulund) | mainly residential, with shopping centres, film studios, industrial estates, the airport | **mid to late 20th century** apartment blocks; newer high-rise towers; informal settlements along railways and creeks |
| **Urban-rural fringe** | the north and the mainland: Thane, **Navi Mumbai**, Vasai-Virar, Kalyan | new townships, factories, warehouses, ports, new business parks, and informal settlements; farmland being built over | the **newest**: late 20th and 21st century blocks and estates |

**The pattern to remember**
- **Age:** buildings get **younger** from south to north, because the city started at the southern tip and grew north along the peninsula and then onto the mainland.
- **Function:** offices dominate the south; old industry in the centre is being replaced by services; housing dominates the suburbs; new industry and housing spread on the fringe.
- **Wealth is mixed:** unlike a UK city, rich and poor often live **side by side**, with informal settlements next to luxury towers in almost every zone.

::: terms
- **Site**: the physical characteristics of the land on which a settlement is built, such as relief, water and harbour.
- **Situation**: the position of a settlement in relation to the surrounding area and other places.
- **Connectivity**: how well a place is linked to other places by transport and communication networks.
- **Land reclamation**: creating new land from the sea, marshes or creeks by draining or filling them.
- **Natural harbour**: a sheltered area of deep coastal water where ships can safely anchor and dock.
- **Hinterland**: the area around a port or city that it serves and trades with.
- **Chawl**: a type of old multi-storey tenement building in Mumbai, with one-room homes off a shared corridor.
- **Mumbai Metropolitan Region**: the wider urban area of Mumbai and its neighbouring cities, such as Thane and Navi Mumbai.
- **Urban-rural fringe**: the zone at the edge of a city where built-up land meets the countryside.
- **Monsoon**: the seasonal reversal of winds that brings very heavy rain to India from about June to September.
:::

::: method
1. **Location questions:** pick a feature (harbour, coast, rail link, airport, financial institutions), then develop how it helped the economy with "so" or "which meant".
2. **Context questions:** say whether your point is **national** (environmental or cultural), **regional** or **global**, and use named places: Maharashtra, Navi Mumbai, Pune, Jawaharlal Nehru Port.
3. **Structure questions:** for each zone give its location, its function and its building age.
4. **Describing a map:** use compass directions and distances from the southern tip, and link age to the direction of growth.
5. **8-mark assess:** weigh site against situation and connectivity, and the advantages against disadvantages, then judge which mattered most and whether that has changed over time.
:::

:::

::: example Explain two: location and economic development
Explain two ways Mumbai's location has been important for its economic development. (4)

Its position on India's west coast facing the Arabian Sea (1) puts it on direct trade routes to the Middle East and Europe, especially after the Suez Canal opened, so it became India's main port for foreign trade (1). It was connected by India's first railway (1), so goods such as cotton could be brought from the interior to the port, which led to textile mills and industry growing in the city (1).
:::

::: example Describe: building age
Describe how the age of buildings changes across Mumbai. (3)

The oldest buildings, from the 19th-century colonial period, are in the CBD at the southern tip (1). Further north, the inner city has late 19th and early 20th-century mills and chawls, many now replaced by new towers (1). The newest buildings are in the northern suburbs and on the urban-rural fringe, such as Navi Mumbai (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: the urban-rural fringe
Explain one reason why the urban-rural fringe of Mumbai is growing. (2)

Land in the south of the peninsula is scarce and very expensive (1), so new housing, factories and business parks are being built on cheaper land in the north and on the mainland, such as Navi Mumbai (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: site and situation
Assess the importance of Mumbai's site and situation for its growth. (8)

Mumbai's site was decisive at the start. Its sheltered deep-water harbour made it the best natural port on India's west coast, and the British developed it as a trading base. Reclaiming land joined the seven islands, giving room for docks, mills and the colonial city.

Its situation then multiplied this advantage. Facing the Arabian Sea, Mumbai was the nearest major Indian port to Europe once the Suez Canal opened, and the railway from 1853 linked it to the cotton-growing interior. Cotton exports and textile mills drew hundreds of thousands of workers, laying the base for the city's industry and finance.

However, the site has also limited growth. The narrow peninsula leaves little space, making land in the south among the most expensive in Asia and pushing growth north and onto the mainland at Navi Mumbai. Low-lying reclaimed land floods in the monsoon. These problems show the site is now a constraint as well as an asset.

Other factors are now as important. Mumbai's role as India's financial capital, its stock exchanges, Bollywood and the 1991 reforms that brought foreign investment explain its recent growth more than the harbour does. Connectivity through the airport and Jawaharlal Nehru Port keeps it a global gateway.

Overall, site and situation were the most important reasons for Mumbai's early growth, but today its growth depends more on economic investment, financial institutions and connectivity, while its site increasingly limits how and where it can expand.

(Level 3: site and situation explained with detail, weighed against other factors and over time, with a substantiated conclusion.)
