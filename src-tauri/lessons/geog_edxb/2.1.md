## What it is

**Development** means improving people's lives. Geographers argue about how to define and measure it. This key idea covers:
- **definitions**: economic, and broader social and political;
- **measures**: GDP per capita, the Human Development Index (HDI), measures of inequality and indices of political corruption;
- **demographic differences** between developing, emerging and developed countries.

### Ways of defining development

| Type | Focus | Examples of criteria |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | wealth and economic growth | GDP per capita, GNI per capita, share of jobs in industry and services, energy use |
| **Social** | quality of life and wellbeing | life expectancy, literacy, access to clean water, infant mortality, gender equality |
| **Political** | freedom and how a country is governed | free elections, freedom of speech, human rights, rule of law, low corruption |

An economic definition is easy to measure but narrow: a country can grow richer while most people stay poor, or while rights are denied. Broader definitions see development as people having the health, education, freedom and choices to live the lives they value.

### Pearson's three categories

The spec groups countries by their **HDI category** (from the United Nations Development Programme):

| Pearson term | HDI category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Developing country** | low human development | Niger, Chad, Afghanistan |
| **Emerging country** | medium and high human development | India, the Philippines, Brazil, China |
| **Developed country** | very high human development | UK, Japan, USA, Norway |

Use these terms in answers rather than "LIC" or "MEDC".

### Measures of development

**GDP per capita**
- Gross Domestic Product is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year. Divide by the population to get **GDP per capita**, usually given in US dollars and often adjusted for the cost of living (purchasing power parity, PPP).
- Strengths: widely available; easy to compare and rank.
- Limitations: it is an **average**, so it hides inequality; it misses the **informal economy** and subsistence farming; it says nothing about health, education or freedom.

**Human Development Index (HDI)**
- A **composite** measure that combines three things:
  1. **health**: life expectancy at birth;
  2. **education**: mean years of schooling for adults and expected years for children;
  3. **income**: GNI per capita (PPP).
- It gives a score from 0 to 1; above 0.8 counts as very high.
- Strengths: broader than income alone; shows that money is not everything.
- Limitations: still a national average; ignores inequality, political freedom, gender gaps and the environment; data in some countries is poor.

**Measures of inequality**
- The **Gini coefficient** measures how evenly income is shared, from 0 (everyone has exactly the same) to 1, or 100 (one person has everything).
- South Africa has one of the highest Gini coefficients in the world; Nordic countries such as Norway and Denmark have among the lowest.
- It shows that two countries with the same GDP per capita can be very different places to live.
- **Income quintiles** (see 2.2) are another way to show inequality.

**Indices of political corruption**
- The **Corruption Perceptions Index** (Transparency International) scores countries from 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean), based on how corrupt the public sector is believed to be.
- Corruption slows development because aid, tax and investment are stolen or wasted, and businesses lose trust.
- Limitation: it measures **perceptions**, not proven corruption.

### Skill: single against composite rankings

A **single** measure (such as GDP per capita) and a **composite** index (such as HDI) can rank the same country very differently.
- A country rich from oil but with poor education or health ranks **higher on GDP per capita than on HDI**.
- A country with good public health and schooling despite modest income ranks **higher on HDI than on GDP per capita**.

Pearson may give a table of ranks and ask you to identify the country with the biggest difference, calculate it, or suggest why. The answer is usually that one measure leaves out something the other includes.

### Demographic differences

| Indicator | Developing | Emerging | Developed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fertility rate** (average births per woman) | high, often 4–6 | falling, around 2–3 | low, often below 2 |
| **Death rate** | higher, but falling | low | low, may rise as the population ages |
| **Infant mortality** (deaths under age 1 per 1,000 live births) | high | falling | very low |
| **Maternal mortality** (deaths per 100,000 live births) | high | falling | very low |
| **Life expectancy** | lower | rising | high, often 80 or more |
| **Population structure** | young, many children | growing share of working age | ageing, many over 65 |

Why the differences? In developing countries children may be needed to work on farms and to support parents in old age; infant deaths encourage more births; contraception and girls' education are limited. As countries develop, health care cuts infant and maternal deaths, girls stay in school longer and marry later, and urban living makes large families costly, so fertility falls.

### Skill: population pyramids

A pyramid shows the percentage of males and females in each five-year age group.
- **Developing**: wide base, steeply narrowing sides, narrow top. High births, high deaths, short lives.
- **Emerging**: base beginning to narrow, wide middle. Falling births, longer lives, a large working-age group.
- **Developed**: narrow base, straight sides, wide top. Low births, long lives, an ageing population.

You may need the **dependency ratio**: (people aged 0–14 + people aged 65 and over) ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100.

::: terms
- **Development**: the process of improving people's standard of living, quality of life and freedom.
- **GDP per capita**: the total value of goods and services a country produces in a year divided by its population.
- **Human Development Index (HDI)**: a composite measure combining life expectancy, education and income per person into a score from 0 to 1.
- **Gini coefficient**: a measure of income inequality from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (complete inequality).
- **Corruption Perceptions Index**: a ranking of countries from 0 to 100 by how corrupt their public sector is believed to be.
- **Composite index**: a measure that combines several indicators into a single score.
- **Fertility rate**: the average number of children born to each woman in a country.
- **Infant mortality rate**: the number of babies who die before their first birthday per 1,000 live births.
- **Maternal mortality rate**: the number of women who die from pregnancy or childbirth per 100,000 live births.
- **Dependency ratio**: the number of young and elderly dependants compared with every 100 people of working age.
:::

::: method
1. **Define:** give the measure and what it includes, e.g. "HDI combines life expectancy, education and income".
2. **Evaluate a measure:** one strength and one limitation, each developed with "so" or "because".
3. **Rank tables:** find the difference with working, then explain it by what one measure includes that the other ignores.
4. **Pyramids:** describe the shape with percentages from the figure, then explain with births, deaths and life expectancy.
5. **Compare** developing, emerging and developed countries with figures for each.
6. **8-mark assess:** weigh the strengths and limits of each measure against the other, then judge which is more useful **for what purpose**.
:::

Define the term Human Development Index. (1)

A composite measure of development combining life expectancy, education and income per person (1).
:::

::: example Single against composite
Sample data: Country P ranks 30th in the world for GDP per capita but 60th for HDI. Calculate the difference in rank and suggest one reason for it. (3)

60 − 30 = 30 places lower on HDI (1). Country P may earn a lot from exports such as oil (1), but spend little on education or health, so life expectancy or schooling is lower than its income suggests (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: fertility
Explain one reason why fertility rates are higher in developing countries than in developed countries. (2)

Many families rely on farming, so children are needed to work on the land (1) and to look after parents in old age when there are no pensions (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: HDI against GDP
Assess the usefulness of the Human Development Index compared with GDP per capita for measuring development. (8)

GDP per capita is simple and widely available, so it is easy to compare countries and track growth over time. But it is only an average of income: it hides inequality, misses the informal economy, and says nothing about whether people are healthy or educated. An oil-rich country can rank highly while many people remain poor.

The HDI is more useful for most purposes because it combines health, education and income. It shows that countries with similar incomes can have very different life chances, and it reflects the broader social definition of development. Pearson uses HDI categories to define developing, emerging and developed countries.

However, the HDI has limits too. It is still a national average, so it hides gaps between regions, genders and rich and poor. It ignores political freedom and corruption, and data in poorer countries may be unreliable.

Overall, the HDI is more useful than GDP per capita because it measures quality of life, not just income. The best picture comes from using it alongside inequality and corruption measures.

(Level 3: both measures weighed with strengths and limits, and a supported judgement.)
:::

::: q
State the three components of the Human Development Index. (3)
---
Life expectancy / health (1); education / years of schooling (1); income / GNI per capita (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one limitation of GDP per capita as a measure of development. (2)
---
It is an average (1), so it hides inequality between rich and poor people in the same country (1). Or: it excludes the informal economy (1), so incomes in poorer countries are underestimated (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why corruption can slow development. (2)
---
Money from taxes or aid is stolen by officials (1), so less is spent on schools, hospitals and infrastructure (1). Or: foreign investors lose trust (1), so less FDI arrives (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the shape of a population pyramid for a developing country. (2)
---
A wide base showing many children / a high birth rate (1); sides that narrow quickly to a small top, showing few elderly / short life expectancy (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why infant mortality falls as countries develop. (2)
---
Better health care, vaccinations and clean water (1) reduce deaths from diseases such as diarrhoea and measles in babies (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two differences in the population structure of an emerging and a developed country. (4)
---
Children (1): an emerging country has a larger share aged 0–14 because fertility, though falling, is higher (1). Elderly (1): a developed country has a larger share aged 65+ because life expectancy is higher (1). Working age (1): an emerging country often has a growing working-age bulge as earlier high birth rates feed through (1).
:::

::: watch
- Missing the **education** part of HDI. It is health, education **and** income.
- Using "LIC/HIC" instead of Pearson's **developing, emerging, developed**.
- Saying GDP per capita "shows how rich people are". It shows the **average**, which hides inequality.
- Getting the Gini scale backwards: **0 is equal**, 1 (or 100) is completely unequal.
- Reading pyramid bars as numbers of people when they are **percentages**.
- In assess questions, praising HDI without any limitation, which caps the answer at Level 2.
:::
