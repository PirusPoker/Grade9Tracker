## What it is

This key idea starts the **Dynamic UK cities** case study. You study **one major UK city**; this course uses **London**, but any major UK city you studied earns the marks if you know it in detail. You need to explain:
- the **significance of the city's site, situation and connectivity** in a **national** (cultural and environmental), **regional** and **global** context;
- the city's **structure**: the **Central Business District (CBD)**, **inner city**, **suburbs** and **urban-rural fringe**, and how they differ in **function**, **building age and density**, **land use** and **environmental quality**.

It is examined in **Paper 2, Section B**: short items on a described map or photo, "Explain one…", "Explain two…", and possibly the 8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…" question, which may carry **4 SPaG marks**.

### Site: where London was built

The **site** is the actual land a settlement is built on.
- London grew on the **River Thames**, about 80 km from the open sea. The river is **tidal** as far as Teddington, so ships could sail far inland on the tide.
- The Romans founded **Londinium** almost 2,000 years ago at the **lowest bridging point**: the furthest point downstream where the river was narrow enough to bridge, with firm **gravel** banks above the marshy flood plain.
- The surrounding **London Basin** is a broad, low bowl of clay and gravel over chalk, so there was flat land for the city to spread across, water from the river and wells, and fertile farmland around it.
- The site's drawbacks: the low flood plain is at risk of **tidal flooding**, which is why the **Thames Barrier** (see 4.8) was built.

### Situation: where London is in relation to other places

The **situation** is a settlement's position relative to surrounding places and routes.
- London lies in **south-east England**, the part of Britain **closest to continental Europe**, so it was well placed for trade with Europe and, through the Thames estuary, the rest of the world.
- Roads, and later railways and motorways, **radiate** from London to every part of the country, so it became the hub of national transport.
- These advantages made London the **capital**, the centre of government, trade, and later the British Empire, and allowed it to grow far larger than any other UK city. Greater London has about **9 million** people.

### Connectivity in three contexts

**National context: cultural and environmental significance**
- **Capital city**: Parliament, government departments, the Supreme Court and the monarchy are based here.
- **Cultural centre**: national museums and galleries (British Museum, Natural History Museum, Tate Modern), West End theatres, national media (the BBC and many newspapers), major sports venues (Wembley, Lord's, Wimbledon, the Olympic Park).
- **Environmental significance**: the Thames and its estuary marshes, the **Royal Parks**, Kew Gardens (a World Heritage Site), and the **Metropolitan Green Belt**, which limits sprawl and protects countryside for millions of people.
- **National transport hub**: main-line stations such as King's Cross, Euston and Paddington link it to all UK regions; the M25 orbital motorway connects the motorways that radiate from London.

**Regional context: London and the South East**
- London is the **centre of a commuter region**: hundreds of thousands of people travel in daily by rail and road from towns across the South East and East of England.
- It provides **specialist services** for the whole region: major hospitals, universities, head offices, shopping and entertainment.
- The **Underground**, Overground, buses, and the **Elizabeth line** (opened in 2022, linking Reading and Heathrow in the west with Shenfield and Abbey Wood in the east) connect the region to the centre.

**Global context: a world city**
- **Finance**: the **City of London** and **Canary Wharf** form one of the world's leading financial centres: banking, insurance, currency trading and law.
- **Air**: **Heathrow** is one of the world's busiest international airports, with Gatwick, Stansted, Luton and London City adding more links.
- **Rail and sea**: **St Pancras International** sends **Eurostar** trains through the **Channel Tunnel** to Paris, Brussels and beyond; **London Gateway** and Tilbury on the Thames estuary are major container ports.
- **Communications and time zone**: fibre-optic cables, data centres, and a time zone that lets traders deal with Asia in the morning and the Americas in the afternoon.
- **People and language**: English, a highly skilled and international workforce, and universities with global reputations.
- **Culture and tourism**: one of the most visited cities in the world; global brands, fashion, music and film.

### London's structure

Most UK cities have a broadly **concentric** pattern: the oldest, densest, most expensive land in the centre, and newer, lower-density land further out. London follows this pattern, with variations along rivers, transport routes and around older villages it has swallowed up.

| Zone | Function and land use | Building age and density | Environmental quality | London examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **CBD** | offices, banks, shops, theatres, government, tourism; little housing | mix of historic buildings and modern high-rise; very high density | busy, noisy, high air pollution from traffic; some parks and squares | the City of London (the "Square Mile"), the West End, Westminster; the Shard and other towers |
| **Inner city** | dense housing, old industry and docks (many now converted or regenerated), corner shops, markets, new offices | Victorian and Edwardian terraces, post-war council estates and tower blocks, new flats; high density | mixed: some cramped housing and little green space, some regenerated areas with improved environments | Tower Hamlets, Hackney, Southwark, Newham, Lambeth |
| **Suburbs** | mainly housing, local shopping parades, schools, parks | 1920s–30s semi-detached houses with gardens, some newer estates; medium to low density | generally good: quieter, more green space and gardens, cleaner air | Bromley, Harrow, Kingston upon Thames, Sutton |
| **Urban-rural fringe** | green belt farmland, golf courses, country parks, retail and business parks, motorways, airports, landfill and sewage works | scattered modern buildings, low density | mostly high, but noise and traffic near the M25 and airports | the Metropolitan Green Belt; Heathrow on the western edge; Bluewater (Kent) and Lakeside (Thurrock) shopping centres just beyond the boundary |

**Why the pattern exists**
- **Land values (bid-rent)**: land is most expensive in the CBD, where accessibility is highest, so only offices and shops can afford it and they build upwards. Land gets cheaper outwards, so houses get bigger and gardens appear.
- **Age**: the city grew outwards over time. The inner city was built in the 19th century for workers near docks and factories; the suburbs spread in the 1920s–30s as the Underground and railways extended (the **Metropolitan line** advertised "Metro-land"); the fringe is the newest edge.
- **Planning**: the green belt stopped further outward growth after the mid-20th century, so new development since has mostly been on **brownfield** land inside the city, which helps explain new high-rise flats in the inner city.

### Reading figures in this topic

- **Photos described in words**: identify the zone from building type, height, age, density and green space.
- **Maps**: use the scale to estimate distances from the centre; note how transport routes run outward from the CBD.
- **Transects** from the centre outward: building height, density and land values fall; green space and environmental quality usually rise.

::: terms
- **Site**: the physical land on which a settlement is built, such as a river bank or gravel terrace.
- **Situation**: the position of a settlement in relation to surrounding places, routes and features.
- **Connectivity**: how well a place is linked to other places by transport and communication networks.
- **Lowest bridging point**: the furthest point downstream at which a river could be bridged, often the site of a town.
- **Global city**: a city with worldwide influence in finance, business, culture and transport.
- **Central Business District (CBD)**: the commercial centre of a city, with offices, shops and the highest land values.
- **Inner city**: the older, densely built zone around the CBD, often with former industry and terraced housing.
- **Suburbs**: the mainly residential outer areas of a city, with lower-density housing and gardens.
- **Urban-rural fringe**: the zone at the edge of a city where urban and rural land uses mix.
- **Green belt**: a protected ring of open land around a city where building is strictly controlled.
- **Bid-rent**: the idea that land values and rents fall with distance from the city centre.
:::

::: method
1. **Site or situation?** Site is the land itself (river, bridging point, gravel); situation is the position relative to other places (south-east England, near Europe, transport hub).
2. **Connectivity questions**: name real **links** (Heathrow, St Pancras and Eurostar, the Channel Tunnel, the M25, main-line stations, the Elizabeth line, ports, fibre-optic cables), then say what each link **does** for London.
3. **Context**: match the scale asked: national (capital, culture, environment), regional (commuting, services) or global (finance, airports, tourism).
4. **Structure questions**: use the four headings: function, building age and density, land use, environmental quality. Name the zone and a real London area.
5. **Compare** two zones point by point, using "whereas".
6. **8-mark "Analyse Figure… Assess…"**: use the figure throughout, weigh connectivity against other factors (history, government, finance, culture), and give a supported judgement.
:::

::: watch
- Confusing **site** with **situation**. "Near Europe" and "in the South East" are situation; "on the Thames at the lowest bridging point" is site.
- Answering a connectivity question with London's wealth or size. **Connectivity** means transport and communication links: name them.
- Missing the **scale** asked: national, regional or global context need different examples.
- Describing a zone with only one feature. Use **function, building age and density, land use, environmental quality**.
- Saying the suburbs are the "outskirts" with no detail. Give the building type (1920s–30s semis, gardens) and density.
- Putting Bluewater and Lakeside inside London. They are just **outside** the Greater London boundary, near the M25.
- In the 8-marker, failing to weigh connectivity against other factors such as history, government and finance.
:::
