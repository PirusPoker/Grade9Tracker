## What it is

This key idea is examined in **Paper 3 Section A**, using the resource booklet, and its ideas run through the whole paper: the Section D decision (12 marks plus 4 SPaG) almost always asks you to balance people's need for resources against protecting the environment.

It covers:
- how the biosphere provides **goods** for **Indigenous and other local people** (food, medicine, building materials and fuel), and how it is increasingly **exploited commercially** for **energy**, **water** and **minerals**;
- the **services** the biosphere provides: regulating the **atmosphere**, maintaining **soil health** and regulating water in the **hydrological cycle**;
- the **trends increasing demand** for food, energy and water (population growth, rising affluence, urbanisation and industrialisation) and the theories of **Malthus** and **Boserup** on population and resources.

Integrated skill: reading **line graphs of future population projections** and population against available resources. Any accurate real examples earn the marks.

### Goods and services

The biosphere is a **life-support system**. It provides:
- **goods**: physical products that people take and use, such as food, timber and medicine;
- **services**: natural processes that keep the planet habitable, such as cleaning air and water, which people often get for free and do not notice until they are lost.

### Goods for Indigenous and local people

**Indigenous people** have lived in an area for many generations and have traditional ways of life closely tied to the land, such as many groups in the Amazon and First Nations peoples in Canada's boreal forest. They, and other rural people, often depend directly on the biosphere.

| Good | How it is used | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Food** | hunting, fishing, gathering fruit, nuts and honey; small-scale shifting cultivation | Brazil nuts and fish in the Amazon; caribou hunting and fishing in the boreal forest and tundra |
| **Medicine** | plants used in traditional remedies; knowledge passed down generations | many modern drugs have been developed from plant compounds; the rosy periwinkle of Madagascar gave drugs used to treat childhood leukaemia |
| **Building materials** | timber, bamboo, palm leaves for thatch, vines for rope | wooden houses on stilts; thatched roofs |
| **Fuel** | fuelwood and charcoal for cooking and heating | in many developing countries, wood and charcoal are still the main cooking fuel for rural households |

Use by local people is usually **small-scale and sustainable**, because they take only what they need and have strong reasons to keep the ecosystem healthy. Problems arise when population grows fast or when outsiders take over the land.

### Commercial exploitation

The biosphere is increasingly used by companies and governments to make money, often on a much larger scale.

| Resource | Exploitation | Examples and problems |
|---|---|---|
| **Energy** | dams for hydroelectric power (HEP); biofuels such as sugar-cane ethanol and palm oil; timber and wood pellets; oil and gas beneath forests | the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu, a tributary of the Amazon; palm-oil plantations in Indonesia and Malaysia; oil sands under Canada's boreal forest. Dams flood forest and displace communities |
| **Water** | reservoirs, river diversions, groundwater pumping and bottled water | supplies cities and irrigation, but lowers rivers and water tables, damaging wetlands downstream |
| **Minerals** | open-pit mines for iron ore, bauxite, gold and copper | the Carajás iron-ore mine in the Brazilian Amazon; illegal gold mining that pollutes rivers with mercury |

Commercial exploitation brings **jobs, exports and tax income**, but it is often **unsustainable**: forests are cleared, rivers polluted and Indigenous people lose land they depend on.

### Services: how the biosphere keeps the planet working

**1. Regulating the atmosphere**
- Plants take in **carbon dioxide** and release **oxygen** through **photosynthesis**.
- Forests, soils, peat bogs and oceans are huge **carbon stores**; growing forests act as **carbon sinks**, removing CO₂ from the air and slowing climate change.
- When forests are burned or peat drained, the stored carbon is released, adding to the enhanced greenhouse effect.

**2. Maintaining soil health**
- Leaf litter and dead organisms are broken down by **decomposers**, returning **nutrients** to the soil (the nutrient cycle).
- **Roots bind** the soil, and the canopy and litter protect it from heavy rain, reducing **erosion**.
- Earthworms and other organisms mix and aerate the soil.

**3. Regulating the hydrological cycle**
- The canopy **intercepts** rain, slowing it down, so more **infiltrates** into the soil and less runs off. This reduces **flooding** and **soil erosion** downstream.
- Forests return moisture to the atmosphere by **transpiration**; in the Amazon a large share of rainfall is recycled this way, so clearing forest can reduce rainfall far away.
- Wetlands store water and **filter pollutants**, releasing clean water slowly to rivers.

These services are **globally important**: carbon stored in the Amazon or the taiga affects the climate everywhere, and rainfall generated by forests supports farming in other regions.

### Why demand for resources is rising

| Trend | Why it increases demand |
|---|---|
| **Population growth** | world population passed **8 billion** in 2022 and is still rising, fastest in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia; more people need more food, water and energy |
| **Rising affluence** | as incomes rise, people eat more meat and dairy (which need much more land and water per calorie), buy more goods, own cars and use more electricity |
| **Urbanisation** | city dwellers use more energy and piped water per person; cities need food brought in; growth takes over farmland |
| **Industrialisation** | factories use large amounts of energy and water and need minerals; manufacturing in emerging countries such as China and India has driven up global demand |

Demand is rising fastest in **emerging and developing** regions, while consumption per person remains highest in **developed** countries. A small rise in consumption by billions of people has a big effect on the biosphere.

### Population projections (skill)

The **United Nations** publishes **projections** for future population. These are usually shown as a line graph with several **variants**:
- a **medium** projection, which rises to around 10 billion later this century and then levels off;
- a **high** projection, which keeps rising if fertility stays higher;
- a **low** projection, which peaks earlier and then falls.

The lines **diverge** (spread apart) further into the future, because small differences in birth rates add up. When interpreting, describe the **trend**, quote **values** with years, comment on the **range** between projections, and say why the future is **uncertain** (birth rates, health, education of women, migration, policy).

Graphs that compare **population** with **likely resources** show the core question of this key idea: will resources keep up?

### Malthus and Boserup

| | **Thomas Malthus** (1798) | **Ester Boserup** (1965) |
|---|---|---|
| **Main idea** | population grows **geometrically** (1, 2, 4, 8, 16) but food supply grows only **arithmetically** (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) | **population growth drives innovation**: when food runs short, people find new ways to produce more |
| **Outcome** | population eventually **outstrips** food, so it is reduced by **positive checks** (famine, disease, war) unless people use **preventive checks** (later marriage, fewer children) | farming **intensifies** through new technology and methods, so food supply keeps pace with population |
| **Summary** | pessimistic: resources limit population | optimistic: "necessity is the mother of invention" |

**Evidence for Boserup**
- The **Green Revolution** from the 1960s (high-yielding seeds, fertilisers, irrigation) greatly increased food output in Asia, so food production has broadly kept pace with population.
- In **Machakos, Kenya**, a well-known study found that as population grew, farmers built terraces and improved farming, so land degradation was reduced rather than increased.
- New technology continues: genetically modified crops, drip irrigation, vertical farming, renewable energy.

**Evidence for Malthus (and neo-Malthusians)**
- Famines still occur, and hundreds of millions of people are undernourished.
- Intensification has environmental costs: soil degradation, falling water tables, deforestation and climate change, which may limit future food supply.
- Some resources, such as fossil fuels and fresh groundwater, are **finite** or being used faster than they are replaced.

**The balanced view.** Most modern famines are caused by **poverty, conflict and unequal distribution** rather than a global lack of food, which neither theory fully explains. Boserup describes the past better at the global scale; Malthus's warning about **limits** may apply to the biosphere's capacity to absorb pollution and supply water.

::: terms
- **Biosphere**: the living layer of the Earth, including all plants, animals and other organisms and the environments that support them.
- **Goods**: physical products obtained from the biosphere, such as food, timber, fuel and medicine.
- **Services**: natural processes provided by the biosphere, such as regulating the climate, soil and water, that keep the planet habitable.
- **Indigenous people**: people whose ancestors have lived in an area for many generations, with traditional ways of life tied to the land.
- **Commercial exploitation**: large-scale use of natural resources by companies or governments to make a profit.
- **Carbon sink**: a store, such as a growing forest, that absorbs more carbon dioxide than it releases.
- **Transpiration**: the loss of water vapour from plant leaves to the atmosphere.
- **Affluence**: wealth; rising affluence leads people to consume more food, energy, water and goods.
- **Population projection**: an estimate of future population based on assumptions about birth, death and migration rates.
- **Positive checks**: in Malthus's theory, events such as famine, disease and war that reduce population.
- **Intensification**: increasing the output from the same area of land, for example through irrigation, fertilisers or terracing.
:::

::: method
1. **Goods or services?** Goods are products taken; services are processes. Match the right word to the question.
2. **Local against commercial:** local use is small-scale (food, medicine, building materials, fuel); commercial use is large-scale (energy, water, minerals).
3. **Explain (2 or 4):** state the good, service or trend, then develop **how** or **why** ("so…", "which means…").
4. **Projection graphs:** describe the trend with years and values, compare the variants, explain the uncertainty.
5. **Malthus against Boserup:** know which is pessimistic (Malthus) and which is optimistic (Boserup), and give evidence for each.
6. **8-mark:** use the resource and your knowledge, weigh both sides, and reach a judgement.
7. **12-mark decision:** pick one option, justify with the resource, compare with the other two, and consider people and environment in the short and long term.
:::

::: example 8-mark: Malthus or Boserup
A resource booklet extract shows: world population has risen since 1960 and food production has risen faster; the number of undernourished people has fallen as a share of the population but remains in the hundreds of millions; and water tables are falling in several major farming regions. Using the resource and your own knowledge, evaluate whether Malthus's or Boserup's theory better explains the relationship between population and resources today. (8)

The resource gives strong support for Boserup. Since 1960, world population has more than doubled, yet food production has risen even faster. This matches her idea that population pressure leads to innovation. The Green Revolution's high-yielding seeds, fertilisers and irrigation, and examples such as Machakos in Kenya, where farmers built terraces as population grew, show farming intensifying to meet demand. Malthus's prediction that population would outstrip food and be cut by famine has not happened at the global scale.

However, the resource also supports some of Malthus's concerns. Hundreds of millions of people are still undernourished, so food supply has not met everyone's needs. Falling water tables show that intensive farming is using groundwater faster than it is replaced. Boserup's innovations may therefore borrow from the future, and if soils, water and the climate are damaged, food supply could fall.

But hunger today is mainly about poverty, conflict and distribution: there is enough food globally, but not everyone can afford or reach it. Neither theory explains this well, because both focus on total population and total resources.

Overall, Boserup better explains the past 60 years at a global scale, but neo-Malthusian warnings about environmental limits, such as water, are increasingly relevant. The best explanation combines Boserup's view of human ingenuity with Malthus's warning that the biosphere's capacity is finite.

(Level 3: uses every part of the resource, applies both theories accurately with evidence, and reaches a substantiated conclusion.)
:::
