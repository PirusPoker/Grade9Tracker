## What it is

This key idea is part of **Topic 9, Consuming energy resources**, examined in **Paper 3 Section C** (resource-based questions with two 8-mark questions) and often feeding the **Section D decision** (12 marks plus 4 SPaG). It says that **reducing reliance on fossil fuels presents major technical challenges**. It covers:
- the role of **energy efficiency** and **energy conservation**, in **transport** and in **the home**, in reducing demand, making finite supplies **last longer** and **reducing carbon emissions**;
- the **costs and benefits** of **alternatives to fossil fuels** (biofuels, wind, solar and HEP) and a **future technology** (hydrogen), judged by three aims: reducing **carbon footprints**, improving **energy security** and **diversifying the energy mix**.

Any real examples of the right type earn the marks. This course uses UK home insulation and heat pumps, BedZED in south London, London's congestion charge and ULEZ, Brazil's sugar-cane ethanol, UK offshore wind, Norway's HEP, Dinorwig pumped storage in Wales, and the end of coal power in the UK in 2024.

### Efficiency and conservation: using less

The cheapest and cleanest energy is the energy that is **never used**. Two ideas, often confused:
- **Energy efficiency** means getting the **same result using less energy**: an LED bulb gives the same light as an old bulb using a fraction of the electricity.
- **Energy conservation** means **choosing to use less**: turning the heating down, walking instead of driving, switching off lights.

Both **reduce demand**, so finite fossil fuel stocks **last longer**, fewer power stations are needed, **carbon emissions fall**, and households and countries **save money** and import less.

**In the home** (heating is the largest use of energy in a typical UK home)

| Measure | How it saves energy |
|---|---|
| **Loft and cavity-wall insulation** | traps heat inside, so the boiler runs less |
| **Double or triple glazing** and draught-proofing | cuts heat loss through windows and gaps |
| **Smart meters and thermostats** | show real-time use and heat only occupied rooms at the right times, encouraging conservation |
| **LED lighting and high-rated appliances** | the same service for less electricity |
| **Heat pumps** | move heat from the air or ground into the home, giving several units of heat per unit of electricity, replacing gas boilers |
| **Solar panels and solar water heating** | generate energy on site |
| **Passive design** | south-facing windows, thick walls and natural ventilation reduce the need for heating and cooling |

**BedZED** (Beddington Zero Energy Development, south London, completed in the early 2000s) is an eco-village designed to cut energy use: heavy insulation, south-facing glass, rooftop wind cowls for ventilation, solar panels, water recycling and limited parking with a car club.

**In transport** (the largest source of carbon emissions in the UK)

| Measure | How it saves energy |
|---|---|
| **Public transport** | one bus or train carries many people, using far less fuel per passenger than cars |
| **Electric and hybrid vehicles** | electric motors are much more efficient than petrol engines, and can run on renewable electricity |
| **More efficient engines and lighter cars** | more kilometres per litre |
| **Cycling and walking** schemes, such as hire bikes and cycle lanes | replace short car trips completely |
| **Car sharing** and car clubs | fewer cars on the road |
| **Congestion and emission charges** | London's congestion charge (from 2003) and Ultra Low Emission Zone discourage car use and older polluting vehicles |
| **Working from home** and online services | remove journeys altogether |

**Limits:** efficiency measures have upfront costs (insulating old houses is expensive); people may not change behaviour; and the **rebound effect** means savings are sometimes spent on extra use, such as driving further in a more efficient car.

### Alternatives to fossil fuels

Each alternative should be judged against the spec's three aims: lower **carbon footprint**, better **energy security**, and a more **diverse energy mix**.

| Alternative | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Biofuels** (ethanol from sugar cane or maize; biodiesel; wood pellets) | can be used in existing engines and power stations; carbon released is roughly balanced by carbon absorbed while growing; home-grown, so improves security; **Brazil** has used sugar-cane ethanol since the 1970s and most new cars there run on ethanol blends | needs large areas of land, competing with food and sometimes driving deforestation; fertilisers, machinery and processing use fossil energy; can raise food prices |
| **Wind** | no emissions while running; the UK is one of the windiest countries in Europe and has shallow seas, making it a world leader in **offshore wind**; costs have fallen sharply | **intermittent**; visual impact and local opposition onshore; offshore farms are costly to build and maintain; needs grid connections and back-up |
| **Solar** | no emissions while running; panels on rooftops use no extra land; costs have fallen dramatically; ideal in sunny regions and for off-grid villages | no power at night and less in winter or cloud; large solar farms take land; panels need mined materials and eventually recycling |
| **HEP** | reliable and quick to switch on, so it can meet peaks in demand; very low emissions once built; long lifespan; **Norway** gets most of its electricity from HEP; **pumped storage** such as **Dinorwig** in Wales stores surplus electricity by pumping water uphill | large dams flood valleys and displace people; high building cost; depends on rainfall, so droughts reduce output; few suitable new sites in the UK |
| **Hydrogen** (future technology) | when burned or used in a **fuel cell**, the only waste product is **water**; could power lorries, buses, ships and steelworks and store surplus renewable electricity | most hydrogen today is made from natural gas, releasing CO₂; **green hydrogen**, made by **electrolysis** using renewable electricity, is still expensive; hard to store and transport (very light, needs compressing or cooling); few filling stations |

**Energy security and diversification.** Relying on one source, especially an imported one, is risky. When Russia cut gas supplies to Europe after invading Ukraine in 2022, countries dependent on Russian gas faced soaring prices. A **diverse energy mix** (some wind, solar, nuclear, HEP, gas and storage) spreads the risk: if one source fails, others fill the gap. The UK closed its last coal power station, **Ratcliffe-on-Soar**, in **2024**, after coal's share fell rapidly as wind, gas and other sources replaced it.

### The major technical challenges

The key idea's title stresses the difficulty. Replacing fossil fuels is hard because:
- **Intermittency:** wind and solar output varies, so **storage** (batteries, pumped storage, hydrogen) and back-up are needed.
- **Grid capacity:** renewables are often far from cities (offshore, uplands), so new transmission lines are needed.
- **Transport and heating** are harder to decarbonise than electricity: aviation, shipping and heavy lorries need dense fuels.
- **Cost and time:** new infrastructure takes years and large investment.
- **Existing investment:** power stations, pipelines and car fleets built for fossil fuels last for decades.

::: terms
- **Energy efficiency**: getting the same service, such as light or heat, while using less energy.
- **Energy conservation**: reducing energy use by choosing to use less, such as lowering the thermostat.
- **Carbon footprint**: the total greenhouse gases released by a person, product or activity, measured in tonnes of CO₂ equivalent.
- **Energy security**: having reliable access to enough affordable energy to meet a country's needs.
- **Energy mix**: the combination of different energy sources that a country uses, usually shown as percentages.
- **Diversification**: increasing the number of different energy sources used, to spread risk.
- **Hydrogen fuel cell**: a device that combines hydrogen with oxygen to make electricity, with water as the only waste.
- **Green hydrogen**: hydrogen made by splitting water with renewable electricity, so no carbon is released.
- **Pumped storage**: storing surplus electricity by pumping water uphill, then releasing it through turbines when needed.
- **Heat pump**: a device that moves heat from the air or ground into a building, using a little electricity to give several times as much heat.
- **Rebound effect**: when savings from efficiency are partly cancelled because people use more of the cheaper service.
:::

::: method
1. **Efficiency or conservation?** Efficiency = same result, less energy (technology). Conservation = using less (behaviour). Name the measure, say how it saves energy, then the benefit (lower demand, supplies last longer, lower emissions).
2. **Home or transport:** give a specific measure for the right setting and a real example (BedZED, London's ULEZ).
3. **Alternatives:** for each, give a **benefit and a cost**, tied to carbon footprint, energy security or diversification.
4. **Technical challenges:** intermittency, storage, grid, transport and heating, cost and time.
5. **Calculations:** percentage change, payback period (cost ÷ annual saving), or share of the energy mix.
6. **For 8-mark assess:** use the resource (AO4), weigh what renewables can and cannot yet do (AO3), and decide "to what extent" with a clear judgement.
:::

::: example Explain one advantage and one disadvantage: biofuels
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using biofuels instead of petrol. (4)

Advantage (1): the crops absorb CO₂ as they grow, which roughly balances the carbon released when the fuel is burned, so carbon footprints are lower (1). Disadvantage (1): growing fuel crops needs large areas of land, which can push up food prices or lead to forests being cleared (1).

::: example 8-mark assess: can renewables replace fossil fuels?
Study a resource showing a line graph of the UK's electricity mix from 2010 to 2024 (coal falling to zero, wind rising to rival gas as the largest source, gas still large), and a pie chart of total UK energy use showing that transport and heating still rely mainly on oil and gas. Assess the extent to which renewable energy can replace fossil fuels. (8)

The line graph shows renewables can replace fossil fuels in electricity. Wind has grown to rival gas as the UK's largest source of electricity and coal has fallen to zero, with the last coal plant closing in 2024. Wind, solar and HEP emit almost no carbon while running and improve energy security by reducing imports.

However, the pie chart shows electricity is only part of energy use. Transport and heating still depend on oil and gas, and these are harder to switch: aviation and heavy lorries need dense fuels, and replacing gas boilers with heat pumps is expensive. Gas also remains large in the electricity mix because wind and solar are intermittent, so back-up is needed on still, dark days until storage such as batteries, pumped storage or hydrogen grows.

Overall, renewables can largely replace fossil fuels in electricity in the medium term, but only partly in transport and heating, where efficiency, electrification and future technologies such as green hydrogen will be needed as well.

(Level 3: uses both parts of the resource (AO4), separates sectors, weighs benefits against technical limits and reaches a judgement on "extent" (AO3).)

::: q
State the difference between energy efficiency and energy conservation. (2)
---
Efficiency means getting the same service using less energy, for example an LED bulb (1); conservation means choosing to use less energy, for example turning the heating down (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one way transport can be made more energy efficient. (2)
---
Encouraging people onto buses and trains (1) means one vehicle carries many passengers, so far less fuel is used per person than in cars (1). Accept electric vehicles, efficient engines, cycling schemes or car sharing, developed.
:::

::: q
Explain one reason why HEP is useful in a country's energy mix. (2)
---
HEP stations can be switched on within minutes (1), so they can meet sudden peaks in demand or fill gaps when wind and solar output falls (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest one reason why green hydrogen is not yet widely used. (3)
---
Making it by electrolysis needs large amounts of renewable electricity (1), so it is currently much more expensive than hydrogen made from natural gas or than fossil fuels (1); it is also hard to store and transport, and there are very few filling stations (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways diversifying the energy mix improves energy security. (4)
---
Two from: if one source fails or becomes expensive (1), others can fill the gap, so supply is not cut off (1); using home-grown renewables (1) reduces reliance on imports from unstable regions, as shown when Russian gas supplies to Europe were cut in 2022 (1); a mix of intermittent and reliable sources (1) means demand can be met whatever the weather (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of offshore wind farms. (4)
---
Advantage (1): stronger, steadier winds at sea and no visual conflict with homes allow very large farms that generate lots of low-carbon electricity (1). Disadvantage (1): they are expensive to build and maintain in rough seas, need long undersea cables, and still produce nothing when the wind drops (1).
:::

::: watch
- Mixing up **efficiency** (technology, same service) and **conservation** (behaviour, using less).
- Treating renewables as perfect. Level 3 needs **costs** such as intermittency, land use and expense.
- Forgetting the spec's three aims: **carbon footprints**, **energy security** and **diversifying the mix**. Tie each point to one.
- Describing a measure without saying **how** it saves energy.
- Assuming hydrogen is always clean. Most hydrogen today is made from **natural gas**.
- In "to what extent" answers, giving no clear judgement about how far.
:::
