## What it is

Topic 6 human fieldwork is examined in **Paper 2 Section C2** (18 marks), where you answer **either** the urban **or** the rural question. This lesson is the rural option, **Investigating changing rural areas**. The spec sets the focus: **how and why deprivation varies within rural areas in the UK**.

The C2 question usually has two halves:
- **Someone else's fieldwork:** a figure shows another group's rural data, such as a service survey, an environmental quality survey or a land-use survey. You suggest an enquiry question, explain a method or sampling strategy, calculate, criticise reliability and suggest improvements.
- **Your own fieldwork:** usually an 8-mark question, such as assessing the accuracy and reliability of your results, or the importance of primary and secondary data in reaching your conclusion.

Name your villages, sites, sample sizes and findings. The model below uses **sample data** for unnamed villages; replace it with your own.

### What the spec requires you to have done

| Requirement | What it means in the countryside |
|---|---|
| **Enquiry question** | developed from your location, about variations in **rural deprivation** |
| **At least one qualitative method** | collecting data on **views and perceptions** of the quality of rural life |
| **At least one quantitative method** | collecting data on **environmental quality** |
| **Secondary sources** | **census data** from the ONS, plus **one other** |

### What rural deprivation looks like

Rural areas often look attractive, so deprivation can be **hidden**. It tends to show up as:
- **poor access to services:** closing shops, post offices, pubs, banks, schools and GP surgeries, so people must travel;
- **poor public transport:** infrequent or cut bus services, a problem for the elderly, young people and anyone without a car;
- **unaffordable housing:** high prices, often pushed up by commuters, retirees and **second homes**, so young local people move away;
- **low-paid or seasonal jobs** in farming and tourism;
- **isolation**, especially for older people living alone.

Deprived households are often **scattered** among wealthy ones, so averages for a whole parish can hide them. This is a strong evaluation point: small-area data may not reveal individual hardship.

### The six enquiry stages

1. **Question**, often split into hypotheses.
2. **Methods:** qualitative and quantitative, sampling, risk assessment.
3. **Processing and presenting.**
4. **Analysing and explaining** with theory (the **settlement hierarchy**, **threshold population**, the **cycle of rural decline**, counter-urbanisation) and case studies (such as **the Chilterns**: high house prices, ageing population, service decline).
5. **Concluding** with evidence.
6. **Evaluating** accuracy, reliability and the conclusion.

### A model investigation (sample data)

**Enquiry question:** "How and why does deprivation vary between villages at different distances from a market town?"
- **Hypothesis 1:** villages further from the town have fewer services.
- **Hypothesis 2:** residents of more remote villages are less satisfied with rural life.

**Site selection.** Four villages chosen by **stratified** sampling: two large and two small, at different distances from the town. In each village, environmental quality was scored at three points chosen systematically along the main street.

### Quantitative methods

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Service provision count** | every service in the village recorded (shop, post office, school, GP, pub, bus stop, church, village hall) and given a score; totals compared |
| **Environmental quality survey (EQS)** | fixed criteria (building condition, litter, traffic, noise, green space, upkeep of verges) scored from −3 to +3 |
| **House type or condition survey** | each house along a stretch classified (detached, terraced, holiday let, social housing) or scored for condition |
| **Traffic count** | vehicles passing in 10 minutes, to show reliance on cars and through traffic |
| **Bus frequency** | buses per day counted from the timetable at the village stop (can be primary observation or secondary) |

### Qualitative methods: views and perceptions

| Method | How | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaire** | residents rate access to services, transport, housing cost and community, with open questions on why | small numbers of people in a village at one time |
| **Interview** | longer conversations with a shopkeeper, parish councillor or older resident | one person's view; hard to compare |
| **Annotated photos and field sketches** | record closed shops, "for sale" signs, new housing, bus shelters | the observer chooses what to show |

### Sampling, risk and ethics

- **Stratified** sampling makes sure villages of different sizes or distances are included, or that residents of different ages are represented.
- **Systematic** sampling along a main street, or asking every fifth person, gives even coverage.
- **Random** sampling avoids choosing only the prettiest or the most run-down spots.
- **Sample size** is a big problem in rural fieldwork: there may be few people around, so questionnaire samples are small.
- **Risk assessment:** roads with no pavements and fast traffic; farm animals and machinery; weather; poor phone signal; stay in groups and wear high-visibility clothing.
- **Ethics:** explain the purpose, keep answers anonymous, respect private land.

### Secondary sources

- **ONS census data** (required): age structure, car ownership, household composition, qualifications, health and employment for each parish or small area.
- **Index of Multiple Deprivation:** its **barriers to housing and services** domain is especially useful for rural areas, measuring distance to services and housing affordability.
- **Bus timetables** for frequency of services.
- **House prices** from property websites, compared with local incomes.
- **Parish plans and local news** about closures of schools, shops and surgeries.

### Presenting the data (sample)

| Village | Distance from town (km) | Population (approx.) | Service score | Mean EQS | Buses per day | Mean satisfaction (1–5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 3 | 2,500 | 18 | +9 | 24 | 4.1 |
| B | 6 | 900 | 11 | +7 | 10 | 3.6 |
| C | 10 | 350 | 4 | +8 | 3 | 2.8 |
| D | 14 | 150 | 1 | +6 | 0 | 2.3 |

- **Bar charts** of service scores; a **scatter graph** of distance against service score.
- **Proportional symbols** on a map for village service totals.
- **Radar graphs** of EQS criteria.
- **Divided bars** of questionnaire answers.
- **Choropleth map** of IMD barriers-to-services rank.
- **Annotated photos** located on a map.

### Statistics (sample)

Service scores: 18, 11, 4, 1.
- **Mean** = 34 ÷ 4 = **8.5**.
- **Median** = (4 + 11) ÷ 2 = **7.5**.
- **Range** = 18 − 1 = **17**.

### Analysing with theory and case studies

- **Settlement hierarchy and threshold:** a service needs a minimum number of customers (its **threshold population**) to survive. Small, remote villages fall below the threshold for shops and surgeries, so they lose them.
- **The cycle of rural decline:** young people leave, services lose customers and close, the village becomes less attractive, and more people leave.
- **Counter-urbanisation and commuting:** villages close to towns or railway lines gain wealthy commuters. House prices rise, but commuters shop in the town, so village services may still decline. In the **Chilterns**, high house prices and an ageing population sit alongside rural service decline.
- **The EQS can mislead:** village D scored well on environment (+6) but had almost no services and no bus. Environmental quality and deprivation are not the same thing in rural areas.

### Conclusions (sample)

Service scores, bus frequency and satisfaction all fell with distance from the town, supporting both hypotheses. Environmental quality changed little, showing that rural deprivation is mainly about **access** (services, transport, housing cost), not appearance. Census data showed the most remote villages had the oldest populations, and IMD barriers-to-services ranks were worst there.

### Accuracy, reliability and improvements

- **Small samples:** only a handful of residents could be questioned in villages C and D, so their satisfaction means are unreliable. Name the sample size and say why it limits the conclusion.
- **Timing:** a weekday visit misses commuters, who are at work, so the sample over-represents retired people.
- **Subjectivity:** EQS and condition scores depend on the observer; fixed scoring sheets and paired scoring reduce this.
- **Hidden deprivation:** parish-level census data averages wealthy and deprived households together.
- **Improvements:** more villages; questionnaires at weekends and evenings, or online and by post; stratified sampling by age; compare with up-to-date IMD and timetable data.

::: terms
- **Rural deprivation**: a lack of access to services, transport, affordable housing and well-paid work in countryside areas.
- **Hidden deprivation**: deprived households scattered among wealthier ones, so area averages do not reveal them.
- **Threshold population**: the minimum number of people needed to keep a service or shop in business.
- **Settlement hierarchy**: the ranking of settlements by size and number of services, from hamlet to city.
- **Service provision count**: a survey recording all the services found in a settlement, often turned into a score.
- **Counter-urbanisation**: the movement of people from towns and cities into rural areas.
- **Second home**: a property owned as a holiday home rather than lived in all year, which can raise local house prices.
- **Census**: a survey of every UK household carried out every ten years, published by the ONS.
- **Index of Multiple Deprivation**: an official ranking of small areas in England combining income, employment, health, education, crime, housing and services.
- **Stratified sampling**: choosing samples in proportion to sub-groups, such as villages of different sizes or age groups.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the given data first:** what was measured, in how many villages, with how many people, and when.
2. **Suggest an enquiry question (2):** specific, measurable and about deprivation or quality of rural life in named places.
3. **Method or sampling (2):** name it, say **how** it was done, then **why** it suits a rural deprivation question.
4. **Reliability (2):** small samples, timing, subjectivity, hidden deprivation; develop the effect on results.
5. **Improvements (4):** two separate changes, each linked to accuracy or reliability.
6. **Own-work 8-marker:** name villages, methods and **sample sizes**; separate accuracy from reliability; use your results; judge how far your conclusion can be trusted.
:::

::: example Explain one reason: unreliable questionnaire results

Very few people were available to answer, sometimes fewer than ten per village (1), so one or two unusual answers have a big effect on the mean and the results may not represent the whole village (1).

::: example 8-mark: importance of primary and secondary data
You have carried out your own fieldwork investigating how and why deprivation varies in rural areas. Assess the importance of your own primary data and secondary data in reaching your conclusion. (8)

Our primary data showed clear differences between four villages. Service scores fell from 18 in village A to 1 in village D, and mean satisfaction fell from 4.1 to 2.3. Questionnaires were vital because the spec focus is people's views, and open answers told us why: residents in D complained about having no bus and having to drive to the town for a doctor.

But our primary data had limits. We questioned only six people in village D on a weekday, mostly retired, so the sample was small and not representative. The EQS hardly varied and could not show deprivation at all.

Secondary data filled these gaps. Census data showed D had a much older population and fewer young adults, explaining the low demand for services. The IMD barriers-to-services rank was much worse for D than A, which independently confirmed our pattern over a larger area. Bus timetables gave exact frequencies. However, census data was a few years old and covered the whole parish, which may hide individual deprived households.

Overall, primary data was most important for showing perceptions and the pattern on the ground, but secondary data was essential to explain why and to make the conclusion more reliable. The conclusion depended on both agreeing.

(Level 3: assesses both types with specific own data, weighs their strengths and limits, and reaches a judgement.)

::: watch
- Treating rural deprivation as "looks run-down". It is mainly about **access**: services, transport, housing cost and jobs.
- Not naming the **sample size**. Rural samples are small, and saying how small and why it matters earns evaluation marks.
- Forgetting the focus is **variation within** rural areas and **why**.
- Mixing up qualitative (views) and quantitative (scores, counts) methods.
- Using census data without noting it may be **out of date** or cover a whole parish.
- In the 8-marker, writing about fieldwork in general with no villages or results.
:::
