## What it is

The third part of the megacity case study looks at what life is like for the people who live there. This key idea covers:
- the **opportunities** for people in the megacity: **education**, **health services**, and **formal and informal employment**;
- the **challenges** caused by rapid population growth: **housing shortages**, **poor-quality housing**, **insecure property rights**, **inadequate water supply and waste disposal**, **poor employment conditions**, **limited service provision** and **traffic congestion**;
- the **pattern** of areas of extreme wealth contrasted with areas of poor housing; the **reasons for differences in quality of life**; and the **political and economic challenges** of managing the city.

Its integrated skill is using **quantitative** (numbers) and **qualitative** (descriptions, photos, interviews) information to judge how much quality of life varies.

Any real megacity in a developing or emerging country earns the marks. This course uses **Mumbai**. Figures such as Dharavi's population vary hugely between sources, so keep them general.

### Opportunities

**Education**
- Mumbai has far more schools, colleges and universities than rural India, including the **University of Mumbai** and **IIT Bombay**, one of India's top engineering institutes.
- Literacy is higher than in most of rural India, and girls are more likely to stay in school.

**Health**
- Large public and private hospitals, including specialist cancer and teaching hospitals, serve people from across India.
- Vaccination, maternity care and clinics are more available than in villages, so life expectancy is higher.
- However, the best private care is expensive, and public hospitals are badly overcrowded.

**Formal employment**
- India's financial capital: banks, insurance, the stock exchanges, TNC offices.
- IT and outsourcing, media and **Bollywood**, the port, retail, hotels and tourism, government jobs.
- Formal jobs bring regular wages, contracts, sick pay and pensions, but usually need qualifications.

**Informal employment**
- Street vendors, rickshaw and taxi drivers, construction labourers, domestic workers, waste pickers and small workshops.
- **Dharavi** has a huge informal economy: **recycling** (especially plastics), **leather goods**, pottery, garment making and food production. Its total annual turnover is very large, and goods are sold across India and abroad.
- Informal work is easy to enter, so it gives migrants an income within days.

### Challenges caused by rapid population growth

| Challenge | What it looks like in Mumbai | Why rapid growth causes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Housing shortages** | roughly two-fifths or more of the population live in informal settlements (slums), depending on the source; others live on pavements | far more migrants arrive than formal homes are built; land on the peninsula is scarce and expensive |
| **Poor-quality housing** | one-room homes of brick, tin or plastic; old crumbling chawls; buildings that collapse in the monsoon | the poor build with whatever they can afford on unwanted land: railway edges, creeks, hillsides |
| **Insecure property rights** | many residents do not own the land they live on and can be evicted; only those who can prove residence before a **cut-off date** qualify for rehousing | settlements grew without permission on government or private land |
| **Inadequate water supply** | water piped for only a few hours a day in many areas; long queues at standpipes; some buy expensive water from tankers; leaks and illegal connections | supply from lakes outside the city has not kept pace with demand |
| **Waste disposal** | much household waste goes to huge dumping grounds such as **Deonar**, which has suffered large fires; untreated sewage flows into creeks and the Mithi River; too few toilets in informal areas | collection and treatment have not grown as fast as the population |
| **Poor employment conditions** | long hours, low pay, no contracts, dangerous workplaces, child labour in some workshops | more workers than formal jobs, so many accept informal work |
| **Limited service provision** | overcrowded government schools and hospitals; patchy electricity and sanitation in informal areas | services were planned for a much smaller city |
| **Traffic congestion** | gridlocked roads; suburban trains carrying far more than their design capacity, with many deaths each year from falls and track crossings; air pollution from vehicles | rapid growth in commuters and car ownership on a narrow peninsula with few north-south routes |

**Environmental effects** link several challenges: polluted creeks, loss of mangroves, poor air quality (traffic, construction dust, waste fires) and increased flood risk when drains are blocked by rubbish, as in the severe monsoon floods of July 2005.

### The pattern of wealth and poverty

**Areas of extreme wealth**
- **Malabar Hill**, Cuffe Parade and parts of **Bandra** have luxury apartments, sea views, private security, clubs and private schools.
- One of the world's most expensive private homes, **Antilia**, a skyscraper built for the Ambani family, stands in south Mumbai.

**Areas of poor housing**
- **Dharavi**, between the Western and Central railway lines, is one of Asia's largest informal settlements, with hundreds of thousands of residents packed into roughly 2 km².
- Smaller settlements line the railway tracks, creeks and the edge of the airport; pavement dwellers sleep on the streets.
- Many of these areas sit **next to** wealthy districts, so the contrast can be seen within a few hundred metres. Mumbai is a city of **juxtaposition**, not neat zones.

**Life in Dharavi: not only a problem**
- Challenges: very high density, shared toilets used by large numbers of people, open drains, disease, fire risk, insecure tenure.
- Positives: a strong **community**, low rents, homes close to work, a huge **informal economy**, many small businesses, and residents who are often highly skilled and enterprising.

### Reasons for differences in quality of life

- **Income and type of job:** formal workers can afford better housing and services; informal workers cannot.
- **Land and property rights:** owners invest in their homes; those who might be evicted do not.
- **Access to services:** wealthy areas have piped water, sewers and good schools; informal areas rely on standpipes and shared toilets.
- **Education and skills:** these decide who gets formal jobs.
- **When people arrived:** long-established residents often have better housing and rights than recent migrants.
- **Location:** settlements on flood-prone or polluted land suffer more from hazards.

### Political and economic challenges of managing the megacity

**Political**
- **Many authorities**: the city corporation, the Maharashtra state government, a regional development authority and the national railways all have a say, so planning is poorly coordinated.
- **Corruption** and powerful builders and landowners can block or distort schemes.
- **Politics of slums**: informal settlements contain many voters, so politicians may promise to protect them rather than redevelop them; clearances cause protests.
- **Rapid change** means plans are out of date before they are finished.

**Economic**
- The city corporation has one of the largest budgets of any Indian city, but the scale of need is enormous.
- Much economic activity is **informal**, so it pays little tax.
- **Land is extremely valuable**, so there is pressure to use it for profitable offices and luxury flats rather than affordable housing.
- Infrastructure (metro lines, sewage treatment, flood defences) costs billions and takes years.

### Skill: judging variation in quality of life

**Quantitative data** gives a scale: income, people per room, hours of water per day, distance to the nearest toilet, literacy, infant mortality.

**Qualitative data** gives meaning: interviews, photos, blogs, newspaper reports, residents' views of safety and community.

| Indicator (sample data) | Wealthy district | Informal settlement |
|---|---|---|
| People per room | 1 | 6 |
| Hours of piped water a day | 24 | 2 |
| People per toilet | 2 | 150 |
| Adult literacy | 98% | 70% |

Use both: numbers show **how big** the gap is; qualitative sources show **how people feel** about it, which can be surprising (many Dharavi residents value their community and jobs). Always comment on **reliability**: sample size, who collected it, and whether it is up to date.

::: terms
- **Quality of life**: how good people's lives are, including income, housing, health, safety, environment and wellbeing.
- **Informal settlement**: an area of housing built without permission on land the residents do not own, often lacking services.
- **Property rights**: legal ownership or the right to occupy land and buildings without risk of eviction.
- **Insecure tenure**: when residents have no legal right to their homes and can be evicted.
- **Formal employment**: registered, taxed work with contracts and legal protection.
- **Informal employment**: work that is not registered, taxed or regulated by the government.
- **Service provision**: the supply of public services such as water, sanitation, schools, health care and electricity.
- **Traffic congestion**: when the number of vehicles exceeds road capacity, causing slow movement and delays.
- **Juxtaposition**: rich and poor areas existing side by side in the same part of a city.
- **Quantitative data**: information in the form of numbers that can be measured and compared.
- **Qualitative data**: descriptive information such as opinions, interviews, photographs and written accounts.
:::

::: method
1. **Opportunities:** use the spec's three headings (education, health, formal and informal jobs) and develop each with a Mumbai detail and a "so" for the benefit.
2. **Challenges:** pick from the spec's list, explain **how rapid growth causes it**, and give a Mumbai example (Dharavi, Deonar, the suburban railway).
3. **Pattern questions:** contrast named wealthy areas (Malabar Hill) with named poor areas (Dharavi) and note that they sit side by side.
4. **Reasons for differences:** link quality of life to income, jobs, property rights, services and location.
5. **Management challenges:** separate **political** (many authorities, corruption, voters) from **economic** (cost, informal economy, land values).
6. **Data questions:** quote figures from both areas, calculate the gap, and add a qualitative point.
7. **8-mark evaluate:** weigh challenges against opportunities, for different groups, then reach a supported judgement.
:::

::: example Define: insecure property rights
Define the term insecure property rights. (1)

When people have no legal ownership or right to occupy the land or home they live in, so they can be evicted (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: an opportunity
Explain one opportunity for people living in Mumbai. (2)

There are many informal jobs, for example in Dharavi's recycling and leather workshops (1), so new migrants can earn an income quickly even without qualifications (1).
:::

::: example Explain one: traffic congestion
Explain one reason why traffic congestion is increasing in Mumbai. (2)

Mumbai sits on a narrow peninsula with few north-south routes (1), so as the population and car ownership grow, more vehicles and commuters are squeezed onto the same roads and trains (1).
:::

::: example Explain two: political challenges
Explain two political challenges of managing Mumbai. (4)

Many different authorities, such as the city corporation, the state government and the railways, control parts of the city (1), so plans for transport or housing are poorly coordinated and slow (1). Informal settlements contain many voters (1), so politicians may resist clearing or redeveloping them, which delays improvements (1).
:::

::: example 8-mark evaluate: population growth and challenges
Named megacity: Mumbai

Evaluate the statement 'Rapid population growth in a megacity creates more challenges than opportunities for its residents'. (8)

Rapid growth has created severe challenges in Mumbai. Housing has not kept pace, so roughly two-fifths or more of residents live in informal settlements such as Dharavi, with insecure property rights and the risk of eviction. Water is piped for only a few hours a day in many areas, toilets are shared by large numbers of people, and sewage pollutes the creeks. The suburban railway is so overcrowded that many people die every year.

However, the same growth has created real opportunities. Mumbai offers education at every level, from local schools to IIT Bombay, and far better health care than rural India. The economy provides formal jobs in finance, IT and Bollywood, and a vast informal economy, such as Dharavi's recycling and leather trades, gives newcomers an income within days. Migrants keep coming because, for most, life in Mumbai is better than in their villages.

The balance depends on who you are. For wealthy residents of Malabar Hill and skilled formal workers, opportunities clearly outweigh challenges. For the poorest, especially recent migrants and pavement dwellers, the challenges dominate daily life, although even they often earn more than before.

Overall, rapid growth has created more challenges than the city can manage, but not more than opportunities for most residents, because the jobs and services explain why people choose to stay. The real problem is that management has not kept pace with growth, so the opportunities are shared very unequally.

(Level 3: challenges and opportunities weighed with Mumbai detail, different groups considered, and a substantiated conclusion.)
:::

::: watch
- Seeing informal settlements **only** as failures. Dharavi has a large economy and a strong community, which the "opportunities" half of a question needs.
- Writing a list of challenges without explaining **how rapid population growth causes** each one.
- Ignoring the spec's exact list: housing shortages, poor-quality housing, insecure property rights, water, waste, employment conditions, services, congestion.
- Describing the wealth pattern as neat zones. In Mumbai, rich and poor live **side by side**.
- Mixing up political and economic challenges of management.
- Quoting precise populations for Dharavi or slum shares. Sources disagree, so keep them general.
- Using only numbers in quality-of-life questions. Add a **qualitative** point and comment on reliability.
:::
