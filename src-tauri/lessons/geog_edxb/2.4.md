## What it is

Topic 2's case study is **one emerging country**. This course uses **India**, which has medium human development and so fits Pearson's definition of an emerging country. Any real emerging country studied in class earns the marks. Lessons 2.4–2.7 build one connected picture: where India is (2.4), how its economy has changed (2.5), the impacts on people and environment (2.6), and its changing world role (2.7).

This key idea covers India's **site, situation and connectivity** and why they matter at **national, regional and global** scales, plus its broad **political, social, cultural and environmental context**.

Three location words come up every year:
- **Site**: the physical land a place occupies: relief, rivers, climate, resources.
- **Situation**: its position relative to other places: neighbours, seas, trade routes.
- **Connectivity**: how well it is linked to other places by transport and communications.

### Site: India's physical geography

| Region | Features | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Himalayas** (north) | the world's highest mountains; glaciers and snowfields | a barrier to the north; source of meltwater for great rivers; earthquake and landslide hazards |
| **Indo-Gangetic Plain** (north) | flat, fertile floodplains of the Ganges, Indus and Brahmaputra systems | some of the most densely populated farmland on Earth; major cities such as Delhi and Kolkata |
| **Thar Desert** (north-west) | hot, dry, sparsely populated | limited farming; potential for solar power |
| **Deccan Plateau** (south) | a large upland plateau between the Western and Eastern Ghats | farming, minerals; tech cities such as Bengaluru and Hyderabad |
| **Coastline** | thousands of kilometres of coast on the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal | ports, fishing, trade; exposure to cyclones and sea level rise |

**Climate.** Most of India has a **monsoon** climate. The south-west monsoon brings most of the year's rain between about June and September. Farming depends on it: a weak monsoon brings drought and poor harvests, while an intense one brings floods.

### Situation: India's position

- India lies in **South Asia**, on the Indian subcontinent, jutting into the **Indian Ocean**.
- **Neighbours**: Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Myanmar; Sri Lanka and the Maldives lie offshore.
- It sits **between the Middle East and Europe to the west and East and South-East Asia to the east**, beside some of the world's busiest shipping lanes carrying oil and goods.
- Its time zone (about five and a half hours ahead of the UK) means Indian offices can work while UK and US offices are closed, or overlap with them, which helps outsourcing.

### Connectivity

- **Ports**: Mumbai and its neighbouring container port, Chennai, Kolkata and fast-growing ports in Gujarat handle most trade.
- **Airports**: Delhi and Mumbai are major international hubs.
- **Railways**: one of the world's largest rail networks, carrying millions of passengers a day.
- **Roads**: the **Golden Quadrilateral** highway links the four biggest cities: Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata.
- **Communications**: fibre-optic cables and cheap mobile data support the IT and outsourcing industries.
- **People**: a large **diaspora** (Indians living abroad) in the Gulf states, the UK, the USA and Canada sends remittances home and builds business links.

### Why location matters at different scales

| Scale | Significance |
|---|---|
| **National (environmental)** | the monsoon and rivers support farming for hundreds of millions; the Himalayas provide water but also hazards; contrasts between fertile plains and dry or mountainous regions shape where people live and how rich regions are |
| **National (cultural)** | great diversity: many languages (Hindi and English are used by the central government, and the constitution lists 22 scheduled languages), and many religions, with a Hindu majority, a large Muslim minority and Sikh, Christian, Buddhist and Jain communities |
| **Regional** | the largest population and economy in South Asia; a natural leader in the region, but with tense relations and border disputes with Pakistan and China |
| **Global** | on major sea routes between Europe, the Middle East and East Asia; an English-speaking workforce and convenient time zone attract outsourcing; diaspora links worldwide |

### Broad context

**Political**
- Independent from British rule since **1947**, when the subcontinent was partitioned into India and Pakistan.
- A **federal parliamentary democracy**, often called the **world's largest democracy**, with national elections and 28 states that have their own governments.
- Has nuclear weapons, and seeks a bigger role in world affairs (see 2.7).

**Social**
- The **world's most populous country** (UN estimate, from 2023), with a young population.
- Most people still live in rural areas, but cities are growing fast.
- Large inequalities between rich and poor, urban and rural, and between states; the legacy of the **caste** system still affects opportunities, although caste discrimination is illegal.

**Cultural**
- Great diversity of languages, religions, food and festivals.
- Cultural exports such as **Bollywood** films, music, food and yoga give India global influence ("soft power").

**Environmental**
- Hazards: monsoon floods and droughts, cyclones on the east coast, Himalayan earthquakes, heatwaves.
- Pressures: some of the world's worst urban air pollution, water shortages, and melting glaciers that threaten river flows.

::: terms
- **Site**: the physical characteristics of the land a place is built on, such as relief, rivers and climate.
- **Situation**: the position of a place in relation to surrounding places, seas and routes.
- **Connectivity**: how well a place is linked to other places by transport and communication networks.
- **Emerging country**: in Pearson's terms, a country with medium or high human development, such as India.
- **Monsoon**: a seasonal reversal of winds bringing a very wet season, in India mainly from June to September.
- **Diaspora**: people who have left their home country and live in communities around the world.
- **Federal democracy**: a system where power is shared between a national government and elected state governments.
- **Golden Quadrilateral**: the highway network linking Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata.
- **Soft power**: influence gained through culture and values rather than military or economic force.
:::

::: method
1. **Keep site, situation and connectivity separate**, and use the right word for each.
2. **Explain significance:** feature, then why it matters: "a long coastline with ports such as Mumbai, so goods can be exported cheaply by sea".
3. **Use scales:** national (environmental and cultural), regional (South Asia) and global, as the spec does.
4. **Context questions:** use the four headings: political, social, cultural, environmental.
5. **8-mark assess:** weigh location against other factors (government policy, workforce, history), and judge how far location explains India's development.
:::

::: example 8-mark assess: location
Assess the importance of location in India's development. (8)

Location has clearly helped. India's long coastline and its situation on shipping lanes between Europe, the Middle East and East Asia make trade cheaper, and its ports handle most exports. The fertile Indo-Gangetic Plain and monsoon rains support farming for hundreds of millions. Its time zone allows Indian IT and call-centre workers to serve UK and US clients around the clock.

But location also creates problems. Dependence on the monsoon brings drought and flood; the Himalayas bring earthquakes; and tensions with neighbouring Pakistan and China lead to high defence spending.

Other factors have probably mattered more. India had the same location before 1991, when growth was slow. The 1991 reforms that opened the economy, government investment in education and technology institutes, an English-speaking workforce, and the huge domestic market explain why TNCs arrived and Indian firms grew.

Overall, location is an important foundation, giving India access to trade routes and resources, but government policy and human resources explain why development speeded up when it did, so they are more important.

(Level 3: location's benefits and costs weighed against other factors, with a supported judgement.)
:::

::: watch
- Swapping **site** and **situation**. Site is the land; situation is the position relative to other places.
- Listing features without **significance**. Always add "so…".
- Describing India as "developing" in Pearson's terms. It is an **emerging** country (medium human development).
- Forgetting the scales: the spec asks for national, regional and global significance.
- Overclaiming figures. Keep population and area general: the world's most populous country.
- Saying location alone explains growth. Location changed little, but growth took off after 1991.
