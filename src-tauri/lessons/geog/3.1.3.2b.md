## What it is

Distinctive coastal landforms are the result of **rock type, geological structure and physical processes**. The spec asks for: how **geological structure and rock type** influence coastal forms; the characteristics and formation of **landforms of erosion** (headlands and bays, cliffs and wave-cut platforms, caves, arches and stacks); the characteristics and formation of **landforms of deposition** (beaches, sand dunes, spits and bars); and **an example of a section of UK coastline** to identify its major landforms of erosion and deposition — here, the **Dorset coast around Swanage**.

### Rock type and geological structure

- **Rock type** means how **resistant** the rock is. **Hard (resistant)** rocks such as **granite, chalk and limestone** erode slowly and form **headlands** and high, steep cliffs. **Soft (less resistant)** rocks such as **clay, sand and gravel** erode quickly and form **bays** and lower, gentler, slumping cliffs.
- **Geological structure** means how the rocks are arranged: the pattern of different rock bands, and their **joints, faults, bedding planes and dip**. Joints and faults are lines of weakness that waves exploit to form caves; rocks that dip towards the sea tend to slide.

| Coast type | Rock arrangement | Result | Dorset example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Discordant coast** | bands of hard and soft rock run **at right angles** to the coastline | soft rock erodes back into **bays**, hard rock is left sticking out as **headlands** | **Swanage** area |
| **Concordant coast** | bands of rock run **parallel** to the coastline | the outer hard band protects the softer rock behind, giving a straight coast; if the hard band is breached, the soft rock behind is scooped out into a **cove** | **Lulworth Cove**, where the sea broke through the Portland limestone and hollowed out the soft clays behind |

### Landforms of erosion

**Headlands and bays** — form on discordant coasts. Soft rock (clay, sands) is eroded faster by hydraulic power and abrasion, forming a **bay** — a curved inlet, usually with a beach because it is sheltered. Hard rock is eroded more slowly and is left jutting out into the sea as a **headland**, with steep cliffs. Once formed, **wave refraction** concentrates wave energy on the headlands and spreads it out in the bays, so the headlands then erode faster and the bays become sites of deposition.

**Cliffs and wave-cut platforms** — a sequence to learn in order:

1. Destructive waves attack the base of a cliff **between the high and low tide marks**.
2. Hydraulic power and abrasion erode a **wave-cut notch** there.
3. The notch deepens, leaving an **overhang** of unsupported rock above.
4. Weathering weakens the overhang and it **collapses** under gravity; waves remove the debris.
5. The process repeats, so the **cliff retreats** inland and becomes higher.
6. Left behind at the base is a gently sloping, rocky **wave-cut platform**, exposed at low tide, often with rock pools. Waves lose energy crossing it, so as the platform widens, erosion of the cliff slows.

On the Dorset coast, the ledges at **Kimmeridge Bay** are wave-cut platforms.

**Caves, arches, stacks and stumps** — on headlands:

1. Waves exploit a **line of weakness** — a joint or fault — in the headland.
2. **Hydraulic power** forces air into the crack and **abrasion** widens it into a **cave**.
3. The cave is eroded right through the headland (often back-to-back caves meet), forming an **arch**.
4. The base of the arch is eroded while **weathering** (freeze-thaw, carbonation) weakens its roof, until the roof **collapses**.
5. The isolated column of rock left standing is a **stack**.
6. The stack is undercut at its base and eventually collapses to leave a low **stump**, covered at high tide.

Drawn as a four-stage diagram: a headland with a crack; a cave; an arch; a stack beside the headland with a stump further out.

### Landforms of deposition

**Beaches** — accumulations of sand and shingle between the low tide mark and the highest point reached by storm waves. They form where **constructive waves** deposit material, usually in **sheltered bays**. **Sand beaches** are usually wide and gently sloping, because sand is fine and the backwash drains down the surface; **shingle (pebble) beaches** are steeper and narrower, because water soaks into the gaps between the pebbles so the backwash is weak. Beaches often have ridges called **berms** marking high tides and storm levels.

**Sand dunes** — hills of sand behind a beach, formed where there is:

- a **large supply of sand** and a wide beach,
- a **large tidal range**, so a big area of sand is exposed at low tide and dries out,
- a strong **onshore wind** to blow the dry sand inland.

Formation: sand is blown inland and trapped by an obstacle such as driftwood or seaweed, forming **embryo dunes**. **Pioneer plants** such as **marram grass** colonise them — marram has long roots that bind the sand, tolerates salt and being buried, and actually grows faster when covered in sand. The dunes grow into **foredunes** and taller **yellow dunes**. Further inland, older dunes gain more plants and dead organic matter, forming soil — these are **grey (mature) dunes**. Hollows between dunes that reach the water table are wet **dune slacks**. This sequence of change over time is called a **succession**.

**Spits** — a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end and extending out into the sea or across an estuary.

1. **Longshore drift** carries sediment along the coast.
2. Where the **coastline changes direction** (for example at an estuary or a bend), the sediment continues to be carried forward into open water.
3. The water is deeper and more sheltered there, so waves lose energy and **deposit** the sediment, which builds up above sea level.
4. The spit grows. Occasional changes in wind and wave direction curve the end — a **recurved end** or hook.
5. The sheltered, calm water behind the spit traps fine mud, and a **salt marsh** develops.
6. The spit cannot extend all the way across a river estuary because the river's current carries the sediment away.

UK examples: **Spurn Head** (Holderness, East Yorkshire), **Hurst Castle Spit** (Hampshire), **Dawlish Warren** (Devon).

**Bars** — form when a spit grows right **across a bay**, joining two headlands. The water trapped behind becomes a **lagoon**. Examples: **Slapton Sands**, with the freshwater lagoon Slapton Ley behind it (Devon), and **Loe Bar** (Cornwall). (A ridge joining the mainland to an island is a **tombolo** — Chesil Beach, joining the Isle of Portland to the Dorset mainland, is the famous example.)

### Example: the Dorset coast around Swanage

The coast between **Studland** and **Durlston Head**, in the Isle of Purbeck, is the eastern end of the **Jurassic Coast**, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since **2001** that runs about 150 km from Exmouth in Devon to Old Harry Rocks. It is a textbook **discordant** coast: bands of rock run west–east, meeting the coast at right angles.

From north to south:

| Location | Rock | Landforms |
|---|---|---|
| **Studland Bay** | soft sands and clays | a wide, gently sloping **sandy beach**; a large **sand dune** system (National Trust nature reserve) with embryo, yellow and grey dunes; a freshwater lake, **Little Sea**, trapped behind the growing dunes; the **South Haven Peninsula**, a spit-like sand ridge built across the mouth of **Poole Harbour** (with the Sandbanks spit on the opposite side) |
| **Handfast Point (The Foreland)** | hard **chalk** | a **headland** with steep chalk cliffs, caves and arches; **Old Harry**, a **stack**, and **Old Harry's Wife**, a **stump** — the "wife" was a stack until it largely collapsed in 1896; a wave-cut platform at the base |
| **Ballard Down / Ballard Point** | chalk | cliffs forming the northern edge of Swanage Bay |
| **Swanage Bay** | soft **Wealden clays and sands** | a **bay** eroded into the soft rock, with a sandy beach; slumping clay cliffs in places |
| **Peveril Point** | harder **Purbeck limestone** | a small headland at the southern end of the bay |
| **Durlston Head / Durlston Bay** | hard **Purbeck and Portland limestone** | a **headland** with high limestone cliffs |

So along a few kilometres you can see the whole pattern: **hard chalk → headland (Handfast Point); soft clay → bay (Swanage Bay); hard limestone → headland (Durlston Head)** — with depositional landforms (beach, dunes, spit) in the sheltered area of Studland.

A few kilometres west on the same coast: **Lulworth Cove** (concordant cove), **Durdle Door** (a limestone **arch**) and the **Kimmeridge** ledges (**wave-cut platforms**) — useful extra examples.

::: terms
- **Discordant coast**: a coastline where bands of hard and soft rock run at right angles to the coast, forming headlands and bays.
- **Concordant coast**: a coastline where bands of rock run parallel to the coast, giving a straight coastline or coves.
- **Headland**: a section of resistant rock that juts out into the sea, with bays on either side.
- **Wave-cut notch**: an indentation eroded at the base of a cliff between high and low tide by hydraulic power and abrasion.
- **Wave-cut platform**: a gently sloping rocky surface left at the base of a retreating cliff, exposed at low tide.
- **Stack**: an isolated column of rock left standing in the sea after the roof of an arch collapses.
- **Spit**: a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end, formed by longshore drift where the coast changes direction.
- **Bar**: a ridge of sand or shingle that has grown across a bay, joining two headlands and trapping a lagoon behind it.
- **Sand dune**: a hill of wind-blown sand behind a beach, stabilised by plants such as marram grass.
- **Wave refraction**: the bending of waves as they approach an irregular coast, concentrating energy on headlands and spreading it in bays.
:::

::: method
1. **Identify from a photo or map (1–2 marks)**: name the landform exactly — "stack", "stump", "wave-cut platform", "recurved spit". Use grid references when asked.
2. **Describe characteristics (2–3 marks)**: shape, size, material, position — "a narrow ridge of sand extending into the estuary, with a curved end and salt marsh behind".
3. **Explain formation (4–6 marks)**: a **sequence in order**, naming the **processes** at each stage (hydraulic power, abrasion, weathering, longshore drift, deposition). Missing the process names caps you at Level 1. A labelled sequence diagram can earn marks too — describe the stages in words alongside it.
4. **Rock type and structure (3–4 marks)**: hard vs soft rock → different rates of erosion → headlands and bays; mention joints and faults for caves.
5. **Named coastline (6 marks)**: name the stretch and the landforms **with their locations and rock types** — "Old Harry stack at Handfast Point in chalk; Swanage Bay in soft Wealden clay; dunes at Studland". A generic answer with no Swanage names cannot reach Level 3.
6. **9-mark "to what extent / assess" questions**: weigh the influence of rock type and structure against processes (waves, weathering, longshore drift), use the Dorset evidence, and finish with a clear judgement. SPaG may be attached.
:::

::: example Formation of a stack — 6 marks
Explain how a stack is formed. (6)

A stack forms on a headland of resistant rock, such as the chalk of Handfast Point in Dorset. Waves attack lines of weakness in the headland — joints and faults. Hydraulic power forces water and air into the cracks, and abrasion throws pebbles against them, so the crack is widened into a cave.

The cave continues to be eroded, often from both sides of the headland, until it breaks through to form an arch. Waves then erode the base of the arch, while weathering such as freeze-thaw and carbonation weakens the roof from above.

Eventually the roof becomes too heavy and unsupported and collapses, leaving an isolated column of rock separated from the headland — a stack, such as Old Harry. Continued erosion at the base of the stack will eventually make it collapse to form a stump, as happened to Old Harry's Wife.

(Level 3: correct sequence, processes named at each stage, located example.)
:::

::: example Wave-cut platform — 4 marks
Explain the formation of a wave-cut platform. (4)

Destructive waves erode the base of a cliff between high and low tide by hydraulic power and abrasion (1), forming a wave-cut notch (1). The notch deepens until the overhanging rock above collapses, and the waves remove the debris (1). This repeats, so the cliff retreats, leaving a gently sloping rocky platform at its base that is exposed at low tide (1).
:::

::: example Spit — 4 marks
Explain how a spit is formed. (4)

Longshore drift carries sediment along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind (1). Where the coastline changes direction, for example at an estuary, the sediment is carried out into more sheltered, deeper water, where waves lose energy and deposit it (1). Over time the deposits build up above sea level into a long ridge (1). Changes in wind direction curve the end into a hook, and a salt marsh forms in the sheltered water behind it — as at Spurn Head (1).
:::

::: example Named coastline — 6 marks
For a named UK coastline, describe the major landforms of erosion and deposition. (6)

The Dorset coast around Swanage is a discordant coast, with bands of hard and soft rock meeting the sea at right angles. At Handfast Point, hard chalk forms a headland with steep cliffs, caves and arches; off its tip stands Old Harry, a stack, and Old Harry's Wife, a stump that collapsed in 1896.

To the south, the soft Wealden clays and sands have been eroded into Swanage Bay, which has a sandy beach. The harder Purbeck and Portland limestones form headlands at Peveril Point and Durlston Head, with high cliffs.

North of Handfast Point, sheltered Studland Bay is dominated by deposition: a wide, gently sloping sandy beach backed by a large dune system stabilised by marram grass, with Little Sea, a lake trapped behind the dunes, and a spit-like peninsula growing across the mouth of Poole Harbour.

(Level 3: several named landforms of both erosion and deposition, located accurately, with rock types.)
:::

::: watch
- Formation answers must **name the processes** (hydraulic power, abrasion, freeze-thaw, longshore drift) at each stage — a description of shapes alone stays in Level 1.
- Keep stages **in order**: crack → cave → arch → stack → stump. Arches collapse because the **roof** falls, after the base is eroded and the top weathered.
- The wave-cut notch forms **between high and low tide**, not at the top of the cliff.
- A spit forms where the **coastline changes direction** — not "where the sea ends".
- In the named coastline question, locate each landform and link it to its **rock type**: chalk headland, clay bay, limestone headland.
- Do not call Chesil Beach a spit or bar in an answer about Swanage — keep your example focused and correct.
:::
