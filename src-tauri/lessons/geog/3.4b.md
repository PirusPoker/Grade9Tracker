## What it is

**Geographical skills** are worth about a quarter of the whole GCSE (assessment objective **AO4**, 25%), and they are tested in **all three papers** — in short "Use Figure 3" questions, in the fieldwork section of Paper 3, and inside longer answers. This lesson covers the **graphical, numerical and statistical skills**, how you use **qualitative and quantitative data**, and how you build a written **argument** and conclusion. (Maps, grid references and photographs are in the map skills lesson.)

### Choosing and drawing graphs

You must be able to **select and construct** the right graph for the data, using **appropriate scales**, and **suggest** a suitable graph for data you are given.

| Graph | Use it for | Key drawing rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Line chart** | Continuous change over **time** (population growth, river discharge, temperature) | Time on the **x-axis**; plot points accurately and join with straight lines or a smooth curve |
| **Bar chart** | Separate (**discrete**) categories — land uses, types of shop, countries | Equal-width bars with **gaps** between them |
| **Histogram (equal class intervals)** | **Continuous** data grouped into classes — pebble lengths 0–9 mm, 10–19 mm… | Bars **touch**, because the classes run into each other |
| **Pie chart** | Parts of a **whole** (percentages that add up to 100) | Each 1% = **3.6°**; start at 12 o'clock and go clockwise, largest segment first |
| **Divided (compound) bar** | Parts of a whole, especially for **comparing** several places or years side by side | One bar = 100%; segments in the same order and shading in every bar |
| **Pictogram** | Simple totals shown with symbols (e.g. one car symbol = 100 cars) | Include a key; part-symbols show part-values |
| **Scattergraph** | The **relationship between two variables** (bivariate data) — distance downstream and river width | Independent variable (the one that causes change) on the x-axis |
| **Population pyramid** | Age and sex structure of a population | Males on the **left**, females on the **right**; age bands (usually 5 years) up the middle; % or numbers along the bottom |

**Combined graphs**: a **climate graph** uses **bars** for monthly rainfall (right or left axis in mm) and a **line** for mean monthly temperature (°C) on the same chart.

Rules for every graph: a **title**, **labelled axes with units**, a **key** if there is more than one set of data, an **even scale** that uses most of the paper, and **accurate plotting** (the examiner allows only a small tolerance). If you are given the axes and scale, you only need to **plot** the missing information — check the scale divisions carefully before you start.

**Suggesting a graph**: say *which* graph and *why*. "A scattergraph, because it shows whether there is a relationship between two sets of data" earns the mark; "a graph" does not.

### Completing and reading maps

| Map | What it shows | How to read / complete it |
|---|---|---|
| **Choropleth** | Areas shaded according to a value (population density by country, deprivation by ward) — **darker = higher** | Shade using the key's density of shading; describe the pattern (where the highest and lowest values are) |
| **Isoline** | Lines joining places of **equal value**: contours (height), isobars (pressure), isotherms (temperature), isohyets (rainfall) | Lines **close together** = a steep **gradient** (rapid change); far apart = gentle change. **Interpolate** a value between two lines |
| **Dot map** | Distribution — each dot represents a fixed number (1 dot = 1,000 people) | Dense clusters show concentration; count dots × value to estimate a total |
| **Desire lines** | **Straight** lines from where people start to where they go (e.g. where shoppers travel from) | Show the **sphere of influence**; thicker lines or more lines = more people |
| **Proportional symbols** | Circles, squares or bars **drawn in proportion** to the value at each place (e.g. size of circle = number of tourists) | Read the value from the scale; bigger symbol = bigger value |
| **Flow lines** | Movement **along a route** — arrow width is proportional to the amount (traffic, migrants, trade) | Measure the width against the scale; follow the real route (unlike desire lines, which are straight) |

**Weaknesses of a choropleth**: it suggests the value is the same everywhere inside a boundary and that it changes suddenly at the boundary, and the choice of class intervals can make a pattern look stronger or weaker.

**Gradient on an isoline map** is the rate of change between lines: difference in value ÷ distance. **Value** at a point between isolines is estimated by **interpolation** — a point halfway between the 20 °C and 22 °C isotherms is about 21 °C.

**Dispersion graphs** plot each value as a dot along a single vertical scale, often side by side for two sites, so you can see the spread and read off the **median and quartiles** and compare two sets of data.

**Interpreting any graph or map** — use the same checklist every time:
1. **Overall trend or pattern** ("population increased steadily").
2. **Data with units** — quote figures from the graph, including the start, end, highest and lowest.
3. **Changes in rate** ("the fastest rise was between 1990 and 2000").
4. **Anomalies** — points that do not fit.

On a **population pyramid**, describe the **base** (high or low birth rate), the **sides** (steep sides = high death rate; straight sides = low death rate), the **top** (many elderly = long life expectancy) and any bulges or indents (a baby boom, migration of young workers, war losses).

### Numerical skills

**Number, area and scale — know the unit relationships:**
- 1 km = 1,000 m; 1 m = 100 cm; 1 cm = 10 mm.
- 1 km² = 1,000,000 m² = **100 hectares**; 1 hectare = 10,000 m² (a square 100 m × 100 m).
- On an OS map each grid square is 1 km², so counting squares estimates an area.
- **Density** = amount ÷ area. 12,000 people living in 4 km² gives a population density of 3,000 people per km².
- Large numbers: 1 billion = 1,000 million. Write "7.8 billion", not a string of zeros.

**Proportion and ratio**:
- A **proportion** is a part of the whole, often given as a fraction or percentage: 30 out of 120 people surveyed = 1/4 = 25%.
- A **ratio** compares two quantities: 60 cars to 20 lorries = 60:20 = **3:1** (simplify by dividing both sides by the same number).
- The **dependency ratio** is an example used in population geography: the number of dependants (children and older people — usually taken as under 15 and 65 and over) compared with the working-age population.

**Magnitude and frequency** — big events happen **less often**. A small flood may happen most years, while a flood of much greater magnitude may be expected only once in 100 years (its **recurrence interval**). Earthquake magnitude is measured on a **logarithmic scale**: each whole number up the scale means about ten times greater ground movement and roughly 32 times more energy released.

**Designing a data collection sheet** — a table with a title, date, time and location, a column for each thing measured, **units** in the headings, a row for each site or person, space for **repeat readings** and averages, and a column for notes (weather, anything unusual).

Collecting good data depends on:
- **Accuracy** — how close a measurement is to the true value: use the right equipment, read it carefully and use sensible units (velocity to 0.01 m/s, not "fast").
- **Sample size** — the larger the sample, the more representative it is and the less effect one odd result has.
- **Sampling method** — **random** (every item has an equal chance; e.g. random number tables), **systematic** (at regular intervals, e.g. every 10 m or every fifth person), **stratified** (the sample is split in proportion to groups in the population, e.g. age groups).
- **Procedures** — a clear, repeatable method: the same equipment, the same time of day, and the same person taking readings if possible.
- **Control groups** — a comparison that has *not* been affected by the thing you are testing (a beach section without groynes compared with one with groynes; an area that has not been regenerated compared with one that has).
- **Reliability** — whether you would get similar results if you repeated the investigation; improved by repeat readings and larger samples.

**Drawing informed conclusions from numbers**: state what the data shows, quote the figures, give the size of the difference (how much bigger, what percentage change) and say how confident you can be (sample size, anomalies).

### Statistical skills

**Measures of central tendency** (the "average"):
- **Mean** — add all the values and divide by how many there are. Affected by extreme values.
- **Median** — the **middle value** when the data is put **in rank order**. Its position is the **(n + 1) ÷ 2**th value. Not affected by extremes.
- **Mode** — the value that occurs **most often**. For grouped data, the **modal class** is the class with the highest frequency (the tallest bar on a histogram).

**Measures of spread** (dispersion):
- **Range** — highest value minus lowest value. Easy, but distorted by one extreme value.
- **Quartiles** — rank the data. The **lower quartile (LQ)** is the (n + 1) ÷ 4th value from the bottom; the **upper quartile (UQ)** is the 3(n + 1) ÷ 4th value (the (n + 1) ÷ 4th value counting down from the top).
- **Interquartile range (IQR)** = UQ − LQ. It shows the spread of the **middle 50%** of the data, so it ignores extremes. A **small IQR** means the values are bunched closely around the median; a large IQR means they are spread out.

**Worked set** — 11 pebble lengths (mm) from a beach, in rank order: 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 27, 30, 34, 38, 45.

| Measure | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | 286 ÷ 11 | **26.0 mm** |
| Median | (11 + 1) ÷ 2 = 6th value | **25 mm** |
| Range | 45 − 12 | **33 mm** |
| Lower quartile | (11 + 1) ÷ 4 = 3rd value | **18 mm** |
| Upper quartile | 3 × 3 = 9th value | **34 mm** |
| Interquartile range | 34 − 18 | **16 mm** |

**Cumulative frequency** is a running total of the frequencies. Plotted as a curve against the upper limit of each class, it lets you read off the median (the value at half the total) and quartiles (a quarter and three-quarters of the total).

**Percentiles** divide ranked data into 100 equal parts. The **90th percentile** is the value that 90% of the data lies below. The median is the 50th percentile, the LQ the 25th and the UQ the 75th. Percentiles are used, for example, to compare one place with all others (a ward in the top 10% most deprived areas).

**Percentage increase or decrease**:

**Percentage change = (new value − original value) ÷ original value × 100**

- A town grows from 40,000 to 52,000 people: (52,000 − 40,000) ÷ 40,000 × 100 = **+30%**.
- Traffic falls from 250 to 200 vehicles an hour: (200 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = **−20%** (a 20% decrease).
- Always divide by the **original** (starting) value, not the new one.

**Relationships in bivariate data** (scattergraphs):
- **Positive correlation** — as one variable increases, so does the other (river width increases with distance downstream).
- **Negative correlation** — as one increases, the other decreases (pebble size decreases with distance along a beach in the direction of longshore drift).
- **No correlation** — points scattered with no pattern.
- A **line of best fit** (trend line) is a straight line drawn **through the middle** of the points with roughly equal numbers on each side. It does not have to pass through the origin or through the first and last points.
- **Interpolation** — estimating a value **within** the range of the data using the line. **Extrapolation** — extending the line **beyond** the data to predict; less reliable, because the relationship may not continue.
- **Anomalies (residuals)** — points well away from the line; suggest a reason (a measuring error, or a local factor such as a weir or a tributary).
- **Correlation does not prove causation** — two things can change together because a third factor affects both.

**Weaknesses in selective statistical presentation** — be ready to spot how data can mislead:
- A **vertical axis that does not start at zero** (a truncated axis) exaggerates small changes.
- **Cherry-picked time periods** — choosing start and end years that show the trend you want.
- **Small or unrepresentative samples** — for example, a questionnaire of ten people on a weekday morning.
- **Averages that hide variation** — a national average hides regional differences.
- **Totals instead of rates** — a large country will have more of almost everything; per person figures are fairer.
- **Uneven scales, 3-D charts or distorted symbols** that make one value look bigger.
- **Missing source or date**, or data from a source with a reason to be biased.

### Qualitative and quantitative data

- **Quantitative data** is numerical — traffic counts, river velocity, census figures.
- **Qualitative data** is descriptive — photographs, field sketches, interview quotes, environmental quality scores based on judgement, newspaper articles.
- **Primary data** is collected yourself (fieldwork); **secondary data** is collected by someone else (census, Environment Agency, Met Office, maps, websites).

You must be able to **obtain, illustrate, communicate, interpret, analyse and evaluate** information from: maps; fieldwork data; **geo-spatial data in a GIS framework** (a geographical information system stores data in **layers** on a digital map — for example flood-risk zones laid over housing and roads, or census data by area); **satellite imagery** (showing change over time, such as deforestation or urban growth); **written and digital sources** (reports, newspapers, websites — check for bias and date); **visual and graphical sources**; and **numerical and statistical information**.

Evaluating a source means asking: **who** produced it, **when**, **why** (is it biased?), how the data was **collected**, and whether it is **complete**.

### Enquiry, argument and literacy

- **Identify questions and sequences of enquiry** — turn a topic into a focused question or hypothesis ("Does pebble size decrease along the beach?") and plan the steps: question → data collection → presentation → analysis → conclusion → evaluation.
- **Write descriptively** (what the data or place is like), **analytically** (why — explaining patterns and links) and **critically** (how reliable the evidence is, what the other side of the argument is).
- **Develop an extended written argument** — for 9-mark questions: a clear line of argument, points developed with evidence, the other side considered, and a **well-evidenced conclusion** that answers the question.
- **Literacy** — communicate in a way that suits the audience. Specialist terms, spelling, punctuation and grammar earn **SPaG** marks: Papers 1 and 2 each include **3 SPaG marks** and Paper 3 includes **6**, attached to named extended questions. The top level needs consistent accuracy and a **wide range of specialist terms**.

::: terms
- **Choropleth map**: a map in which areas are shaded according to a value, with darker shading showing higher values.
- **Isoline**: a line on a map joining places of equal value, such as contours, isobars or isotherms.
- **Flow line**: an arrow drawn along a route whose width is proportional to the amount of movement.
- **Desire line**: a straight line drawn from a starting point to a destination, showing where people travel from and to.
- **Proportional symbol**: a symbol such as a circle drawn at a size in proportion to the value it represents.
- **Median**: the middle value of a data set when it is arranged in rank order.
- **Interquartile range**: the difference between the upper and lower quartiles, showing the spread of the middle 50% of the data.
- **Modal class**: the group or class of grouped data that has the highest frequency.
- **Percentile**: a value below which a stated percentage of the ranked data lies, such as the 90th percentile.
- **Correlation**: a relationship between two variables on a scattergraph, which may be positive, negative or absent.
- **Interpolation**: estimating a value within the range of known data, for example from a line of best fit or between two isolines.
- **Extrapolation**: extending a trend beyond the range of the data to make a prediction, which is less reliable.
- **Reliability**: how far similar results would be obtained if an investigation were repeated in the same way.
- **Qualitative data**: descriptive, non-numerical information such as photographs, sketches and interview comments.
:::

::: method
**What the questions look like.** Mostly short, precise questions (1–4 marks) attached to a figure: "Complete Figure 2", "Calculate the median", "Use Figure 4 to describe the trend", "Suggest one appropriate way to present this data". Paper 3's fieldwork section asks you to process and present data, justify a presentation method and evaluate results. Skills also appear inside 6- and 9-mark answers as your **evidence**.

**Command words:**
- **Calculate** — show your **working** and give **units**; round sensibly (usually one decimal place).
- **Complete / plot / shade** — follow the key and scale exactly; the examiner allows only a tiny tolerance.
- **Describe** (a graph, map or pattern) — **trend → figures with units → changes in rate → anomalies**. No explaining unless asked.
- **Explain** — give reasons for the pattern.
- **Suggest** — a sensible, justified choice or reason, e.g. a suitable graph **and why** it suits the data.
- **Compare** — use comparative words (higher, whereas, both) and give figures for **both** things.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** — weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgement.

**Levels-of-response answers (4, 6 and 9 marks):**
- **4 marks** (e.g. "justify your choice of presentation method", "describe the pattern on the choropleth map"): two developed points or four precise points, each backed by data from the figure.
- **6 marks** (e.g. "evaluate the reliability of your data collection"): three developed points — **point → develop (why it matters) → link to the actual data or enquiry** — covering strengths and weaknesses.
- **9 marks** (e.g. "To what extent did your results support your hypothesis?"): a sustained argument using **numbers from the data**, the other side (anomalies, limitations of sample size or method), and a clear **judgement** that answers the question. Where SPaG is attached, **+3 marks** for accurate spelling and a wide range of specialist terms (median, correlation, anomaly, reliability).

**Golden rules:**
1. **Quote data from the figure every time**, with **units**.
2. For percentage change, divide by the **original** value.
3. Put data in **rank order** before finding a median or quartiles.
4. A **line of best fit** goes through the middle of the scatter, not dot to dot.
5. When you name a graph, say **why** it suits the data.
:::

::: example Calculate a percentage change
The population of a town was 64,000 in 2001 and 80,000 in 2021. Calculate the percentage increase. Show your working. (2)

Increase = 80,000 − 64,000 = 16,000 (1). 16,000 ÷ 64,000 × 100 = **25%** (1).
:::

::: example Median and interquartile range
River velocity (m/s) at nine sites: 0.42, 0.18, 0.35, 0.27, 0.51, 0.30, 0.22, 0.39, 0.46. Calculate the median and the interquartile range. (3)

Rank order: 0.18, 0.22, 0.27, 0.30, 0.35, 0.39, 0.42, 0.46, 0.51. Median = (9 + 1) ÷ 2 = 5th value = **0.35 m/s** (1). LQ = (9 + 1) ÷ 4 = 2.5th value, halfway between 0.22 and 0.27 = 0.245 m/s; UQ = 7.5th value, halfway between 0.42 and 0.46 = 0.44 m/s (1). IQR = 0.44 − 0.245 = **0.195 m/s** (about 0.20 m/s) (1).
:::

::: example Describe a scattergraph
A scattergraph shows river width (m) against distance downstream (km) for ten sites. Width rises from 1.2 m at 0.5 km to 7.8 m at 9 km, with one site at 4 km only 1.5 m wide. Describe the relationship shown. (3)

There is a **positive correlation**: as distance downstream increases, river width increases (1). Width rises from 1.2 m at 0.5 km to 7.8 m at 9 km, an increase of 6.6 m (1). There is one anomaly at 4 km, where the river is only 1.5 m wide — narrower than the trend would predict (1).
:::

::: example Suggest and justify a graph
Students counted pedestrians at eight sites at increasing distances from the town centre. Suggest a suitable graph and justify your choice. (2)

A **scattergraph** (1), because it shows whether there is a relationship between two sets of numerical data — distance from the centre and pedestrian count — and allows a line of best fit to be drawn (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a bar chart and a histogram. (2)
---
A bar chart shows separate (discrete) categories, so the bars have gaps between them (1). A histogram shows continuous data grouped into classes, so the bars touch (1).
:::

::: q
In a survey of 120 shoppers, 42 travelled by bus. What percentage is this, and how many degrees would it take up on a pie chart? (2)
---
42 ÷ 120 × 100 = 35% (1). 35 × 3.6 = 126° (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the mean, mode and range of these daily rainfall totals (mm): 4, 0, 7, 4, 12, 3, 5. (3)
---
Mean = 35 ÷ 7 = 5 mm (1). Mode = 4 mm (1). Range = 12 − 0 = 12 mm (1).
:::

::: q
Give two differences between flow lines and desire lines. (2)
---
Flow lines follow the actual route taken, whereas desire lines are straight lines from origin to destination (1). The width of a flow line is proportional to the amount of movement, whereas a desire line usually represents one journey or a set number of people (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the interquartile range can be a better measure of spread than the range. (2)
---
The range uses only the highest and lowest values, so one extreme value or anomaly distorts it (1). The IQR measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data, so it is not affected by extremes (1).
:::

::: q
A graph showing the number of homes flooded in a town has a vertical axis starting at 400 rather than 0. Explain why this could be misleading. (2)
---
Starting the axis above zero (a truncated axis) stretches the scale (1), so small changes look much larger than they really are, exaggerating the trend (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by a control group in fieldwork, and give an example. (2)
---
A control is a comparison site or group not affected by the factor being investigated, so any difference can be linked to that factor (1). Example: comparing a stretch of beach with groynes to a similar stretch without groynes, or a regenerated area with a similar area that has not been regenerated (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why extrapolating a trend line is less reliable than interpolating. (2)
---
Interpolation estimates within the range of data you actually collected, where the relationship is known (1). Extrapolation predicts beyond the data, where the relationship may change or stop (1).
:::

::: watch
- Dividing by the **new** value instead of the **original** in percentage change.
- Finding the median **without putting the data in rank order** first.
- **No units** or **no working** on "calculate" questions — working often earns a mark even if the answer is wrong.
- Drawing a **line of best fit dot to dot** or forcing it through the origin.
- Describing a graph **without quoting figures** from it, or explaining when the question only says describe.
- Plotting **carelessly** — misreading the scale divisions on the axis you are given.
- Naming a graph in a "suggest" question **without saying why** it suits the data.
- Using histograms with gaps or bar charts with touching bars.
- Saying a scattergraph "proves" one thing causes another — **correlation is not causation**.
:::

---
C (1).

::: test
Visitor numbers at a national park fell from 1.6 million to 1.2 million. Calculate the percentage decrease. Show your working. (2)
---
Decrease = 1.6 − 1.2 = 0.4 million (1). 0.4 ÷ 1.6 × 100 = 25% decrease (1).

::: test
Give two features of a population pyramid that would suggest a country has a high birth rate and a high death rate. (2)
---
A wide base, showing many young children (high birth rate) (1). Sides that narrow quickly / concave sides and a narrow top, showing few people survive to old age (high death rate) (1).
:::

::: test
Suggest two ways a student could improve the reliability of a questionnaire survey of shoppers. (4)
---
Increase the sample size (1) so that one unusual answer has less effect and results are more representative (1). Survey at different times and days, or use systematic sampling such as every tenth person (1), to avoid bias towards one type of shopper (1). Also credit: same questions asked in the same way by each surveyor; pilot the questionnaire first.
:::

::: test
Describe how you would construct a choropleth map of population density for the wards of a city, and state one weakness of this type of map. (4)
---
Calculate the population density for each ward (population ÷ area) (1). Divide the values into a small number of equal or sensible classes (1) and choose shading that gets darker as values increase, shown in a key (1). Weakness: assumes density is the same across the whole ward and changes suddenly at boundaries / class choice can distort the pattern (1).
:::

::: test
Students measured pebble length at ten sites along a beach in the direction of longshore drift. Evaluate the use of a scattergraph and line of best fit to analyse their results. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a balanced evaluation of strengths and limitations, well developed and applied to the pebble data; reaches a supported conclusion. Level 2 (3–4): some strengths and/or limitations with some development. Level 1 (1–2): simple statements about scattergraphs with little application.
Indicative content: strengths — shows whether pebble size and distance are related (positive, negative or no correlation); line of best fit shows the trend clearly and allows interpolation between sites; anomalies stand out and can be investigated (e.g. a groyne trapping larger material); easy to compare with the hypothesis. Limitations — ten sites is a small sample, so one anomaly strongly affects the line; line drawn by eye is subjective; extrapolation beyond the last site is unreliable; correlation does not prove longshore drift caused the change (e.g. cliff falls, beach nourishment, storm events); a scattergraph using mean lengths hides the spread at each site — dispersion graphs or IQR could add this. Conclusion: a scattergraph is the most suitable method for testing a relationship, but results should be supported with measures of spread and a larger sample.
:::

::: test
"Statistics and graphs always give an accurate picture of geographical issues." Using examples, to what extent do you agree with this statement? (9 + 3 SPaG)
---
Level 3 (7–9): detailed, well-developed argument considering both sides, with accurate use of statistical terms and specific examples; clear, substantiated judgement. Level 2 (4–6): some development and examples, judgement partly supported. Level 1 (1–3): simple points, little development, no clear judgement. SPaG (3): consistent accuracy and a wide range of specialist terms.
Indicative content: agree — numerical data is objective and can be compared (e.g. percentage change in population, median and IQR comparing two sites); graphs show trends and anomalies clearly; large official datasets such as the census are reliable; GIS layers overlay data to reveal links, e.g. flood risk and housing. Disagree — selective presentation: truncated axes, cherry-picked years, totals rather than rates; averages hide variation (national GDP per head hides regional inequality); choropleth maps imply uniform values inside boundaries; small or biased samples in fieldwork; correlation does not prove causation; qualitative data (interviews, photographs) captures views and experiences that numbers miss; the source may be biased or out of date. Judgement: e.g. statistics give an accurate picture only when the sample is large, the method is clear and the presentation is fair — they are best combined with qualitative evidence and read critically.
:::
