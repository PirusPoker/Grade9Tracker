## What it is

**Issue evaluation** is **Section A of Paper 3 (Geographical applications)**. It is worth **37 marks** (including 3 SPaG marks) out of Paper 3's 76, and the whole of Paper 3 is 30% of the GCSE. It tests **critical thinking and problem-solving**: you use **secondary sources** about a real, **contemporary geographical issue** to analyse it, weigh up the options and **make and justify a decision**.

### How it works — the pre-release booklet

- A **resource booklet** is released **twelve weeks before** the Paper 3 exam. It sets out an issue — typically a proposed development or a choice between options (a new dam, a coastal defence scheme, a mine in a rainforest, a new town, a transport project, an energy scheme).
- It can contain **maps at different scales, diagrams, graphs, statistics, photographs, satellite images, sketches, extracts from published materials and quotes from different interest groups**.
- You study it in class and at home for twelve weeks. You **cannot take your copy into the exam**; you are given a **clean copy** there. So all your preparation has to be in your head.
- The issue can come from **any compulsory part** of the course (units 3.1 and 3.2 — hazards, ecosystems, rivers and coasts, urban issues, the economic world, resources) and it can **extend beyond** the content you have studied, using unseen contexts. It is **synoptic**: you must draw on **more than one theme** — for example, a dam proposal needs rivers, ecosystems, development and energy.

### What you are assessed on

The spec asks you to:

- **Interpret, analyse and evaluate** the information in the booklet and the question paper.
- Use **geographical skills** (maps, graphs, statistics) to set the issue in **context**, at a **range of scales** (local, national, global).
- **Examine conflicting viewpoints** — the **stakeholders** and what each wants.
- Develop a **critical perspective**: appraise the **advantages and disadvantages** of each option and **evaluate the alternatives**.
- Consider **physical and human interrelationships** — how the option affects both **people** and the **physical environment**, and how each affects the other.
- **Select an option** and make **reasoned justifications** for it, in terms of its likely impact on people and the environment.

### The shape of Section A

Questions follow a **sequence of enquiry**, building from skills to the decision:

1. **Context questions** (1–4 marks) — use a map, graph or table from the booklet: "Describe the location of…", "Calculate the percentage increase…", "Complete the graph…", multiple choice.
2. **Understanding questions** (4–6 marks) — explain why the issue has arisen, how a process works, or why stakeholders disagree: "Explain why the area is at risk of…", "Suggest why some people oppose…".
3. **Evaluation question** (6 marks) — weigh up impacts: "Assess the likely impacts of the proposal on the environment."
4. **The decision** (**9 marks + 3 SPaG**) — "Which option do you think is the best? Justify your decision" or "Should the project go ahead? Use evidence from the resource booklet and your own understanding."

### Tools for analysing an issue

**1. Stakeholders and viewpoints**

A **stakeholder** is a person or group with an interest in the issue — affected by it or able to influence it. For every issue, list them: local residents, farmers, businesses, workers, the national government, local councils, environmental groups (NGOs), developers, TNCs, tourists, indigenous people, future generations.

For each, ask: **What do they want? Why? How much power do they have?** A **conflict matrix** is a grid with the stakeholders along both axes; in each cell you mark whether the two groups' views **agree** (compatible), **conflict**, or are **neutral**. It shows quickly which groups will clash — usually developers and the national government against environmental groups and some local people.

**2. SEE (or SEEP) — the three pillars of sustainability**

Sort every impact into:
- **Social** — people's health, homes, jobs, safety, culture, quality of life.
- **Economic** — money, jobs, income, costs, investment, tax.
- **Environmental** — wildlife, habitats, pollution, landscape, climate, physical processes.
- (**Political** — laws, government, international relations — sometimes useful.)

**3. Scale and time**

- **Scale**: who gains and who loses **locally, regionally, nationally, globally**? A mine may harm a local village but boost national exports.
- **Time**: **short-term** vs **long-term**. Construction jobs last a few years; a reservoir lasts a century; cleared forest may never recover.

**4. Physical–human interrelationships**

The examiners want you to show the **links**: a new dam (human) changes the river's flow and sediment (physical), which erodes the delta downstream (physical), which floods farmland and harms fishing communities (human). Chains like this are the mark of a top answer.

**5. Sustainability**

A **sustainable** option meets the needs of people today **without** compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Ask: does it protect the environment? Is it affordable in the long term? Does it involve local people and benefit them fairly?

**6. Winners and losers**

Every option has **winners and losers**. Name them. The best decision is often the one with the **most winners and the least serious losses**, or where losers can be **compensated** or impacts **reduced** (mitigated).

### Using your twelve weeks well

1. **Read everything** and **identify the issue** in one sentence: "Should X be built at Y?" or "Which of three options is best for Z?"
2. **Make a glossary** of every term in the booklet, and **learn the location** — describe it with direction, distance, scale and named features.
3. **Practise the skills** on every figure: describe each map and graph, work out percentages, means, ranges and rates of change from the tables.
4. **Link the booklet to the spec**: which parts of the course does it draw on? Revise those topics — the processes, the case studies, the key terms.
5. **Build a stakeholder table and a conflict matrix**, and a **SEE table of advantages and disadvantages** for every option.
6. **Research similar real examples** that you can use as **your own understanding** — for example, another dam, another coastal scheme, another rainforest development.
7. **Decide in advance** which option you would choose and **why** — but be ready for the exam to add a new figure or twist, and prepare a case for **every** option. There is **no "right" answer**: marks come from the quality of the justification.
8. **Practise timed answers**, especially the 9-marker, and **learn key facts** from the booklet (you will have a clean copy, but knowing it well saves time).

### How to structure the 9-mark decision

1. **State your decision clearly** at the start: "I think Option 2 is the best because…" This sets up the whole answer.
2. **Justify it** with two or three developed reasons, using **specific evidence from the booklet** (quote figures, figure numbers, stakeholder quotes) **and your own knowledge** (similar examples, geographical processes).
3. **Cover people and the environment**, and the **links** between them; mention **social, economic and environmental** impacts; consider **short- and long-term** effects and **different scales**.
4. **Acknowledge the weaknesses** of your chosen option, and say how they could be **reduced** (mitigation, compensation, planning conditions).
5. **Explain why you rejected the other options** — briefly, with evidence.
6. **Conclude** with a final judgement that goes back to the question, for example about sustainability or who benefits most.

Write in clear paragraphs, use specialist terms, and check spelling and punctuation — **3 SPaG marks** ride on this answer.

::: terms
- **Issue evaluation**: analysing a geographical issue using resources, weighing up the options and making a justified decision.
- **Pre-release resource booklet**: the booklet of sources about the issue released twelve weeks before Paper 3; a clean copy is given in the exam.
- **Stakeholder**: a person or group with an interest in an issue, who is affected by it or can influence it.
- **Conflict matrix**: a grid showing whether the views of each pair of stakeholders agree, conflict or are neutral.
- **Synoptic**: drawing together knowledge, understanding and skills from different parts of the course.
- **Sustainability**: meeting the needs of people today without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- **Mitigation**: action taken to reduce the negative impacts of a decision or development.
- **Secondary source**: information collected by someone else, such as maps, statistics, articles and photographs in the booklet.
- **Justify**: support a decision or argument with evidence and reasons, explaining why it is better than the alternatives.
:::

::: method
**Command words in Section A:**
- **Describe / use Figure X** — say what the resource shows, with specific evidence (figures, place names, grid references). No explanation needed.
- **Calculate** — show your working and give units.
- **Explain / suggest** — reasons, developed; "suggest" means apply your understanding to the unfamiliar situation in the booklet.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** — weigh up both sides; reach a judgement.
- **Justify your decision** — state a choice, back it with evidence and reasons, and show why it is better than the alternatives.

**4-mark answers**: two developed points, each linked to the booklet. "Figure 3 shows the area floods every winter (1), so building homes there would put residents at risk (1)…"

**6-mark levels answers** (Level 1: 1–2, Level 2: 3–4, Level 3: 5–6): two or three clear points, each developed into a chain, with **evidence from the booklet** and a short **judgement** if the command is "assess". Answers with no booklet evidence cannot reach Level 3.

**9-mark decision** (Level 1: 1–3, Level 2: 4–6, Level 3: 7–9, then +3 SPaG):
- **Level 3**: a clear decision; a **thorough, well-supported justification** that uses **detailed evidence from the resources and your own knowledge**; considers **people and the environment** and the **links** between them; evaluates the alternatives and the **drawbacks** of the chosen option.
- **Level 2**: a decision with **some** justification and some use of evidence; may be one-sided or generic.
- **Level 1**: a basic decision with **simple** reasons and little evidence.
- Any option can reach Level 3 — **what counts is the justification**.

**Timing**: Section A is about 45 minutes. Spend around 15 minutes on the 9-marker, including a quick plan (decision → 3 reasons with evidence → drawbacks and mitigation → why not the others → conclusion).
:::

::: example Stakeholder viewpoints
A resource booklet describes a proposed open-pit copper mine in a rainforest in an LIC. Suggest why the national government and a local environmental group might have different views about the mine. (4)

The national government is likely to support the mine because copper exports would earn foreign currency and tax revenue (1), which could be spent on schools, hospitals and roads, helping the country develop (1). The environmental group is likely to oppose it because an open pit involves clearing large areas of rainforest, destroying habitats and reducing biodiversity (1), and waste from the mine could pollute rivers used by local people and wildlife downstream (1).
:::

::: example 6-mark — assess impacts
The booklet proposes building a large dam across a river in an NEE to provide hydroelectric power. Assess the likely impacts of the dam on people and the environment. (6)

The dam would bring major benefits for people. Hydroelectric power is renewable and reliable, so it could supply electricity to factories and homes, attracting investment and jobs, and the reservoir would store water for irrigation in the dry season, increasing food supply.

However, people living in the valley would lose their homes and farmland when it is flooded, and may not be fairly compensated. Downstream, the dam would trap sediment in the reservoir, so less silt would reach farmland on the floodplain; farmers would need to buy fertiliser instead. This is a clear link between the physical change and human impacts.

For the environment, the reservoir would flood forest and habitats, and the dam would block fish migration. Rotting vegetation in the reservoir can release methane.

Overall, the benefits are mainly national and economic, while the costs fall mostly on local people and the environment, so the dam would only be acceptable with fair compensation and measures such as fish passes.

(Level 3: impacts on people and environment, physical–human link, a judgement.)
:::

::: example 9-mark decision — model structure
A resource booklet describes an eroding stretch of coastline with a small town, farmland and a nature reserve. Three options are proposed: Option 1 — build a sea wall and rock armour along the whole coast ("hold the line"); Option 2 — protect only the town and allow the farmland to flood to create salt marsh ("managed realignment"); Option 3 — do nothing. Which option do you think is the best? Justify your decision. (9 + 3 SPaG)

I think Option 2, protecting the town while allowing the farmland to flood, is the best option because it balances the needs of people, the economy and the environment.

First, it protects the most valuable land. The town contains homes, shops and the main road, so defending it with rock armour prevents the greatest economic and social losses, and residents would not face falling house prices or the stress of losing their homes. The farmland is lower in value, so it is more cost-effective to protect only the town.

Second, the new salt marsh would be more sustainable. Salt marsh absorbs wave energy, so it acts as a natural defence and reduces the need for expensive maintenance of hard engineering. It also creates new habitat for birds and wildlife, adding to the nature reserve, and could attract visitors, supporting local businesses. This shows how a physical change — allowing the sea to flood the land — can bring human benefits.

However, the farmers would lose their land and their livelihoods, so they would oppose the plan. To reduce this impact, they should be paid fair compensation, and some might be employed managing the reserve.

Option 1 would protect everything, but a sea wall along the whole coastline would be extremely expensive, would need maintenance for decades, and would starve beaches further along the coast of sediment, increasing erosion there. Option 3 costs nothing now but would leave the town to be lost to the sea, which would devastate the community.

Overall, Option 2 is the best because it protects the most people and property at a reasonable cost, creates a natural defence that will adapt to rising sea levels, and improves the environment, even though the farmers lose out.

(Level 3: clear decision; developed reasons for people and environment with physical–human links; weaknesses acknowledged and mitigated; alternatives rejected with reasons. In the real exam you must also quote specific evidence — figures and data — from the booklet.)
:::

State two types of source that may appear in the resource booklet. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): maps at different scales; diagrams; graphs; statistics; photographs; satellite images; sketches; extracts from published materials (newspapers, reports); quotes from interest groups.

::: q
Explain what is meant by a "conflict matrix" and why it is useful. (3)
---
A grid with the stakeholders along both axes, showing for each pair whether their views agree, conflict or are neutral (1). It helps you see quickly which groups are likely to clash (1), so you can explain the conflict and choose an option that satisfies the most stakeholders or reduces the conflict (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why issue evaluation is described as synoptic. (2)
---
It requires you to draw together knowledge, understanding and skills from different parts of the course (1) — e.g. a dam issue involves rivers, ecosystems, development and energy — rather than a single topic (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why it is important to consider both short-term and long-term impacts when evaluating a proposal. (3)
---
Some benefits, such as construction jobs, only last a few years (1), whereas some costs, such as habitat loss or a reservoir flooding a valley, are permanent (1). An option that looks good now may not be sustainable, so considering the long term shows whether future generations will benefit or suffer (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how you would plan an answer to the final 9-mark "justify your decision" question. (4)
---
State the chosen option clearly at the start (1). Give two or three developed reasons, each supported with specific evidence from the resource booklet and own knowledge, covering people and the environment (1). Acknowledge the weaknesses of the chosen option and how they could be reduced (1). Explain briefly why the other options were rejected, then conclude with a final judgement (1).
:::

::: watch
- **No booklet evidence** in the 9-marker — without specific figures, quotes or data from the resources you cannot reach Level 3.
- **Sitting on the fence** — you must make a clear decision. "It depends" alone is not a decision.
- Only writing about your chosen option — Level 3 needs you to say **why the others are worse** and to admit your option's **drawbacks**.
- Covering people **or** the environment, not both — the spec demands both, and the **links** between them.
- Simply repeating what the booklet says — **analyse** it: who wins, who loses, over what time scale, at what scale.
- Assuming there is a "correct" option. There is not — the marks go to the quality of the justification.
- Rushing the SPaG: 3 marks for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms.
:::

::: test
A proposal will create 2,000 construction jobs for three years and 150 permanent jobs. Suggest why this information is important when evaluating the proposal. (3)
---
Most jobs are temporary (1), so the economic benefit to local people is short-term (1); only 150 permanent jobs may not outweigh any long-term environmental or social costs, so the long-term benefit is limited (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how considering physical and human interrelationships can improve an evaluation of a proposal. (4)
---
Changes to the physical environment often affect people, and vice versa (1). For example, a dam (human) traps sediment (physical), so less silt reaches farmland downstream and farmers need fertiliser (human) (1). Showing these chains means you identify indirect and long-term impacts that a simple list of pros and cons would miss (1), making the judgement better supported and more likely to be sustainable (1).
:::

::: test
Using the ideas of sustainability, explain how you would assess whether a proposed development is a good idea. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): clear, developed explanation of using social, economic and environmental criteria, time scales and stakeholders, reaching a judgement. Level 2 (3–4): some explanation of sustainability criteria. Level 1 (1–2): simple points.
Indicative content: consider social impacts (homes, health, jobs, culture), economic impacts (income, costs, investment) and environmental impacts (habitats, pollution, processes); short- versus long-term; local, national and global scales; winners and losers among stakeholders; whether future generations can still meet their needs; whether negative impacts can be mitigated; weigh them to decide whether benefits outweigh costs.
:::

::: test
A resource booklet presents two options for a growing city in an NEE: Option A — clear an area of forest on the edge of the city to build new housing; Option B — upgrade the existing informal settlements with water, sanitation and electricity. Which option do you think is better? Justify your decision. (9 + 3 SPaG)
---
Level 3 (7–9): a clear decision with a thorough, well-supported justification; considers social, economic and environmental impacts on people and the environment and the links between them; recognises drawbacks of the chosen option and why the other was rejected; uses own knowledge of real examples (e.g. Rio's Favela Bairro project). Level 2 (4–6): a decision with some justification and some consideration of both options; may be one-sided. Level 1 (1–3): a basic decision with simple reasons.
Indicative content — Option A: new homes with services; room for growth; but loss of forest and biodiversity, more flooding and landslides from cleared slopes, long commutes, residents moved away from jobs and communities, costly new infrastructure. Option B: keeps communities and informal jobs; cheaper per person; improves health (sanitation reduces disease), safety and quality of life quickly; examples such as Favela Bairro show upgrading can work; but overcrowding remains, land may be at risk of hazards, and more migrants will keep arriving. Either option can gain full marks if well justified.
SPaG: 3 marks for accurate spelling, punctuation, grammar and a wide range of specialist terms.
