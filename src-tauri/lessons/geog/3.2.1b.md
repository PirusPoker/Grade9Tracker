## What it is

The specification requires **a case study of a major city in an LIC or NEE** showing how urban growth creates **opportunities** and **challenges**, plus **an example of urban planning improving the quality of life for the urban poor**. This lesson uses **Rio de Janeiro** in **Brazil** (an **NEE**) and the **Favela Bairro Project**, especially in the **Complexo do Alemão**.

### Location and importance

**Location**: Rio de Janeiro is on the **south-east coast of Brazil**, on the **Atlantic Ocean**, on the western shore of **Guanabara Bay**, close to the Tropic of Capricorn. The city is squeezed between the sea and steep forested mountains (including the **Tijuca Forest**, one of the world's largest urban forests), which is why many **favelas** are built on steep hillsides.

**Size**: about **6.2 million** people in the city itself (2022 census) and about **12 million** in the wider metropolitan area — Brazil's **second-largest city** after São Paulo.

| Scale | Why Rio is important |
|---|---|
| **Regionally** | capital of **Rio de Janeiro state**; the main centre for jobs, shopping, hospitals and universities in the region; its port and industries employ people from surrounding towns |
| **Nationally** | Brazil's **capital until 1960** (when it moved to Brasília); Brazil's second-largest urban economy; headquarters of major companies including **Petrobras** (the national oil company) and **Vale** (mining); a major centre for **finance, media and tourism**; textbooks quote Rio as having about **6% of all employment in Brazil** |
| **Internationally** | one of the most visited cities in the southern hemisphere — **Christ the Redeemer** statue, **Sugarloaf Mountain**, **Copacabana** and **Ipanema** beaches, **Carnival**; a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** (its landscape "between the mountain and the sea", listed 2012); hosted the **2014 FIFA World Cup final** (at the Maracanã) and the **2016 Olympic and Paralympic Games** — the first in South America; one of Brazil's busiest **ports**, with offshore oil fields nearby attracting international oil companies |

### Causes of growth

Rio grew rapidly through the 20th century and continues to grow through both processes of urbanisation:

- **Rural–urban migration**. **Push factors** in rural Brazil — especially the poor, drought-prone **north-east (the Nordeste)** — include poverty, lack of services, drought and crop failure, and mechanised commercial farming that needs fewer workers. **Pull factors** in Rio include jobs in industry, services and tourism, higher wages, and better access to schools, hospitals, water and electricity. Some migrants also come from the Amazon region and from other countries in South America.
- **Natural increase**. Most migrants are **young adults**, so they have children in the city, keeping the birth rate relatively high, while better healthcare has lowered the death rate. This means the population grows even without new migrants.

### Opportunities created by urban growth

**Social opportunities — access to services**

- **Health**: Rio has far more hospitals and clinics than rural areas. The **Family Health Programme** sends community health teams into favelas to give home visits, check-ups and treatment, improving care for the poorest residents.
- **Education**: city schools, colleges and universities give young people the chance of qualifications that are unavailable in rural areas. The national **Bolsa Família** programme pays poor families on condition that their children attend school.

**Social opportunities — access to resources**

- **Water supply**: most of the city now has mains water; new water treatment works and pipes have extended supply into more favelas.
- **Energy**: electricity supply has improved. New power lines were built, and the **Simplício hydroelectric complex** on the Paraíba do Sul river (operational **2013**, cost about **USD 2 billion**) increased electricity for Rio and the south-east; the **Angra** nuclear power stations are also in Rio de Janeiro state. Legal connections are replacing many dangerous illegal ones.

**Economic opportunities — urban industrial areas as a stimulus for development**

- Rio's growth has created large **industrial areas**, e.g. around **Duque de Caxias** (oil refining, chemicals) and **Santa Cruz** (steel). **Petrobras** has its headquarters in Rio, and **offshore oil** from the fields off the coast supports refining, shipbuilding and engineering.
- These industries create jobs directly and in suppliers and services — a **multiplier effect**: workers' wages are spent in shops and services, and companies pay taxes that fund infrastructure.
- Rio's economy is now dominated by **services** — finance, retail, tourism, media and government — which employ many more people than manufacturing.
- Many people in the favelas work in the **informal economy** (street selling, building, domestic work), which is unregulated and poorly paid but gives people a first foothold in the city economy.

### Challenges created by urban growth

**Managing urban growth — slums and squatter settlements**

- Rio's poorest residents live in **favelas** — informal settlements built illegally on land the residents do not own, often on steep hillsides or marshland at risk of **landslides** and **flooding**.
- The **2010 census** counted **763 favelas** with about **1.4 million** people — roughly **22%** of the city's population. **Rocinha**, on a hillside in the South Zone, is the largest.
- Houses begin as makeshift shacks and are gradually rebuilt in brick; they are densely packed, with narrow alleys and poor access for emergency services.

**Providing clean water, sanitation and energy**

- Commonly quoted textbook figures for the favelas: about **12%** of homes had no running water, about **30%** no electricity and about **50%** no sewerage connection.
- Many homes take electricity through **illegal connections** to the grid, which are dangerous and cause fires and power cuts.
- Much sewage flows untreated into open drains, rivers and **Guanabara Bay**, spreading disease.

**Providing access to services — health and education**

- Health care in favelas is limited; textbooks report **infant mortality** as high as **50 per 1,000** in some favelas, far above the city average. Water-borne diseases spread where sanitation is poor.
- Only around **half of children** stayed in school beyond age 14; schools are overcrowded, teachers are poorly paid, and many children leave to earn money.

**Reducing unemployment and crime**

- **Unemployment** in favelas is high (textbooks quote **over 20%**), and many adults rely on low-paid informal work.
- Some favelas have been controlled by **drug gangs** and militias, with high rates of violent crime. Since **2008** (starting in **Santa Marta**) the state has placed **Pacifying Police Units (UPPs)** in favelas to take back control, with mixed success and complaints of police violence.

**Managing environmental issues**

- **Waste disposal**: many favelas are built with no proper roads, so rubbish lorries cannot enter; waste is dumped and blocks drains, causing flooding and disease. **Jardim Gramacho**, one of the largest open landfill sites in the world, was closed in **2012** after 34 years.
- **Water pollution**: untreated sewage and industrial waste pour into **Guanabara Bay** and its rivers; pollution in the bay was a major concern for the 2016 Olympic sailing events. A Petrobras pipeline leak in 2000 spilled oil into the bay.
- **Air pollution**: heavy traffic and industry produce smog, trapped by the surrounding mountains.
- **Traffic congestion**: Rio has some of the worst congestion in the Americas, because the city is stretched along a narrow coastal strip between mountains and sea. Responses include the **Bus Rapid Transit (BRT)** corridors, the **Metro Line 4** extension to Barra da Tijuca (opened **2016**), and a new light rail (tram) in the city centre.

### Urban planning improving the quality of life for the urban poor: the Favela Bairro Project

**What it is**: launched by Rio's city government in **1994–95**, with funding from the **Inter-American Development Bank (IDB)**. "Bairro" means neighbourhood — the aim was to **upgrade favelas into proper neighbourhoods** of the city rather than bulldoze them, **integrating** them with the formal city. It is a **site-and-service / self-help** approach: the authorities provide infrastructure and services, and residents improve their own homes. The IDB reports that by the late 2000s it had reached around **500,000 people**.

**What it provided**:

| Type | Improvements |
|---|---|
| **Social** | day-care centres, adult education classes, sports and leisure areas, health posts, community centres |
| **Economic** | **legal ownership (land titles)** for residents, so they could invest in their homes; job training; access to small loans (**microcredit**) to buy building materials or start businesses |
| **Infrastructure / environment** | paved and widened roads so rubbish lorries and ambulances can enter; piped water, sewers and drainage; street lighting; rubbish collection; relocating houses from the highest-risk slopes |

**Example: the Complexo do Alemão**

- A group of favelas in Rio's **North Zone** where **more than 60,000 people** live; long controlled by drug gangs. In **2010** police and troops occupied the area and a UPP was installed.
- The **Teleférico do Alemão** cable car opened in **2011**: **3.5 km** long, **6 stations** and **152 gondolas** carrying 10 people each, linking the hilltop communities to the train station at Bonsucesso. Residents received **one free return journey a day**, cutting a steep walk up and down the hills to a few minutes and making it easier to reach jobs elsewhere in the city.
- Other works included new homes, paved roads, sanitation, schools and health centres.

**Evaluation**

| Successes | Problems |
|---|---|
| better roads, water, sewerage and lighting; lower risk of disease and flooding | the **cable car closed in 2016** because of lack of funding and maintenance problems |
| legal land titles gave residents security and encouraged home improvements; house values rose | rising values and rents pushed some poorer residents out |
| residents stayed in their communities instead of being evicted | too little **job training**, and credit schemes were hard for many to access |
| easier access to jobs and services across the city | maintenance of new infrastructure was often poor; some residents said money should have gone on sanitation first |
| became a model copied in other cities | violence and police–gang conflict returned in some favelas after the Olympics; a larger follow-up programme (**Morar Carioca**, announced 2010) was scaled back |

**Overall**: Favela Bairro showed that **upgrading** favelas in place is cheaper and fairer than demolishing them, and it improved quality of life for many residents. But improvements last only if they are **maintained and funded**, and physical infrastructure alone cannot solve poverty, unemployment and crime.

::: terms
- **NEE**: a newly emerging economy that is industrialising rapidly and raising incomes, such as Brazil.
- **Favela**: an informal (squatter) settlement in Brazil, built illegally on land the residents do not own.
- **Squatter settlement**: an area of poor-quality housing built illegally by people who do not own the land.
- **Informal economy**: unregulated, untaxed work such as street selling, usually low-paid and insecure.
- **Multiplier effect**: when new jobs and spending create further jobs and spending in the local economy.
- **Site and service scheme**: a project in which authorities provide land and basic services and residents build or improve their own homes.
- **Pacifying Police Unit (UPP)**: police units placed permanently in Rio's favelas from 2008 to take control from drug gangs.
- **Urban planning**: the organised design and management of land use, housing and services in a city.
:::

::: method
1. **Location and importance**: split into **regional, national and international** — one or two specific facts for each (e.g. state capital; Petrobras headquarters, former capital; 2016 Olympics, UNESCO site). Location should use direction and physical features (south-east coast, Guanabara Bay, Atlantic).
2. **Opportunities and challenges** must match the spec wording: social (health, education, water, energy), economic (industrial areas as a stimulus), and challenges (slums, water/sanitation/energy, services, unemployment and crime, waste, pollution, congestion). Each point: **what the issue is → Rio-specific evidence (name, number, date) → effect on people**.
3. **Favela Bairro**: "an example of how urban planning is improving the quality of life for the urban poor" — describe what was done, then say **how** each measure improved life (e.g. paved roads let rubbish lorries and ambulances in, reducing disease and saving lives).
4. **Command words**: *Describe* — facts, no reasons. *Explain* — why/how. *Assess / evaluate / to what extent* — weigh successes against problems and give a judgement.
5. **Levels**: 4-mark — Level 2 (3–4) needs developed points with case detail. 6-mark — Level 3 (5–6) needs detailed, accurate, place-specific evidence. **9-mark + 3 SPaG** (Paper 2 Section A) — Level 3 (7–9) needs thorough, well-developed points about Rio, **both sides**, and a **supported judgement**; generic answers with no Rio facts stay in Level 1–2.
:::

::: example Evaluate — urban planning (9 + 3 SPaG)
Using an example, evaluate how successful urban planning has been in improving the quality of life for the urban poor. (9 + 3 SPaG)

The Favela Bairro Project, launched in Rio de Janeiro in 1994–95 with Inter-American Development Bank funding, aimed to upgrade favelas into proper neighbourhoods rather than demolish them. In the Complexo do Alemão, a group of favelas in the North Zone with more than 60,000 residents, roads were paved and widened so that rubbish lorries and ambulances could enter, and water pipes, sewers and street lighting were installed. This reduced disease and made the area safer at night, directly improving health and quality of life.

Economic measures were also important. Residents were given legal ownership of their land, which gave them security and encouraged them to invest in improving their homes, and some were offered job training and small loans. The cable car, opened in 2011 with six stations over 3.5 km, gave residents a free daily return trip to the Bonsucesso train station, making it much easier to reach jobs elsewhere in the city.

However, there were serious limitations. The cable car closed in 2016 because of a lack of funding, and much new infrastructure was not maintained. Job training was limited, credit was hard for many residents to access, and rising property values pushed some poorer people out. Violence also returned to some favelas after the Olympics, showing that physical improvements cannot solve deep problems of poverty and crime on their own.

Overall, Favela Bairro was partly successful: it clearly improved living conditions and showed that upgrading favelas is better than bulldozing them. However, its long-term success was limited because funding and maintenance did not continue, so the gains in quality of life were not fully sustained.

(Level 3: detailed, accurate place-specific evidence; successes and problems weighed; clear, supported judgement; high SPaG.)
:::

::: example Explain — economic opportunity
Explain how urban industrial areas can be a stimulus for economic development. (4)

Industrial areas such as Duque de Caxias near Rio, with its oil refinery, create thousands of jobs directly (1). Workers spend their wages in local shops and services, creating more jobs — a multiplier effect (1). Companies such as Petrobras also buy from local suppliers and pay taxes (1), which the government can spend on roads, schools and water supply, improving the city further and attracting more investment (1).
