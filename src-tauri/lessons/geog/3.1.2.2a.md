## What it is

Tropical rainforests grow in a belt along the Equator — the Amazon basin in South America (the largest), the Congo basin in central Africa, and South-East Asia (Malaysia, Indonesia, Papua New Guinea). They cover only about 6% of the Earth's land surface but are home to more than half of the world's plant and animal species.

The spec needs four things: the **physical characteristics** of a tropical rainforest; the **interdependence** of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people; how **plants and animals adapt** to the physical conditions; and **issues related to biodiversity**.

### Physical characteristics

**Climate**
- **Hot all year**: average temperatures of about 27 °C every month, because the Sun is high in the sky all year near the Equator. The **annual temperature range is tiny** — often only 1–3 °C — and there are no real seasons. The daily range (day to night) is bigger than the annual range.
- **Very wet**: over 2,000 mm of rain a year, falling in most months. Manaus, in the Brazilian Amazon, gets over 2,000 mm a year with temperatures of about 26–28 °C every month.
- **Daily pattern of convectional rain**: the Sun heats the ground in the morning → warm, moist air rises → it cools and condenses into towering cumulonimbus clouds → heavy thunderstorms fall in the afternoon → the sky clears in the evening. The air is very **humid**.

On a **climate graph** for a rainforest, the temperature line is almost flat at about 27 °C and every rainfall bar is tall.

**Soils** — **latosols** (also called oxisols)
- Deep (weathering is fast in the heat and wet) and **red**, because they are rich in iron oxides.
- But **infertile**: heavy rain washes nutrients down through the soil and out of reach of plants (**leaching**), and most nutrients are held in the vegetation, not the soil.
- A **thin layer of humus** at the surface is where most nutrients are; dead leaves decompose within weeks in the hot, damp conditions.

**Vegetation structure** — layers, from top to bottom:

| Layer | Height | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergents** | up to about 50 m | The tallest trees poke above the canopy; exposed to sun and wind; thick, straight trunks with buttress roots |
| **Canopy** | about 30 m | A continuous roof of tree crowns; absorbs around 70% of the sunlight and intercepts much of the rain; most of the animal life lives here |
| **Under-canopy** | about 10–20 m | Younger trees that have not yet reached the canopy; lianas and epiphytes |
| **Shrub layer** | up to about 5 m | Dark (only a few per cent of sunlight reaches the floor); shrubs and ferns with large leaves to catch what light there is |
| **Forest floor** | ground | Dark, damp, covered by a thin layer of leaf litter that decomposes rapidly |

To draw it: a cross-section with a few tall emergents above an unbroken canopy line, a patchy under-canopy, then a shrub layer, with labels for each height.

### Interdependence

All the parts of the rainforest depend on each other — change one and the others are affected.

- **Climate → plants**: constant heat, rain and sunlight give a year-round growing season, so plants grow fast and tall, producing huge amounts of biomass.
- **Plants → climate and water**: trees intercept rain and release water vapour through **transpiration**. This moisture rises, condenses and falls as rain again — up to half of the rain in the Amazon is recycled by the forest itself. Remove the trees and the local climate becomes drier.
- **Plants → soil**: leaf fall provides the litter that decomposes into nutrients; roots bind the soil together and protect it from heavy rain.
- **Soil → plants**: the thin humus layer supplies nutrients; because the soil itself is poor, plants depend on rapid recycling of dead matter — which is why rainforest trees have shallow roots.
- **Plants ↔ animals**: plants provide food and habitats; animals pollinate flowers (bees, bats, hummingbirds) and disperse seeds (monkeys, birds, bats) in their droppings. Decomposers (fungi, termites, bacteria) recycle dead matter.
- **People**: indigenous peoples — e.g. the **Yanomami** in the Amazon and the **Penan** in Borneo — rely on the forest for food, building materials, medicine and their way of life; many practise **shifting cultivation**, clearing small plots and moving on so the forest can regrow. Their actions affect the forest in turn: large-scale clearance by logging or farming breaks the links.

**Nutrient cycling in a rainforest** — on a **nutrient cycle (Gersmehl) diagram**, the **biomass** store is by far the largest circle, and the **litter** and **soil** stores are small, with thick, fast flows between them. Nutrients are held in the living plants, not the soil. That is why, when the forest is cleared, the nutrient store is removed with it and the soil quickly becomes infertile.

### Adaptations of plants

| Adaptation | How it helps |
|---|---|
| **Buttress roots** | Huge ridges at the base of tall emergents and canopy trees support them, because the shallow roots in thin soil cannot anchor such tall trees |
| **Drip tips** | Pointed, waxy leaves let heavy rain run off quickly, stopping the growth of algae and fungi and preventing the leaf from rotting or breaking |
| **Lianas** | Woody vines root in the ground but climb up tree trunks to reach sunlight in the canopy |
| **Epiphytes** (e.g. orchids, bromeliads) | Plants that grow on branches high in the canopy to reach the light; they take moisture and nutrients from the air and rain, not the soil |
| **Thin, smooth bark** | There is no need for protection against cold; smooth bark also stops other plants climbing up |
| **Large leaves in the shrub layer** | Capture as much of the little light that reaches the forest floor as possible |
| **Tall, thin trunks** with branches only at the top | Put all their energy into growing up quickly to reach sunlight |
| **Strangler fig** | Starts life as an epiphyte, sends roots down to the ground, and gradually surrounds and kills its host tree |

### Adaptations of animals

| Animal | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Sloth** | Moves very slowly and sleeps most of the day, saving energy on a low-energy leaf diet; algae growing on its fur give it green camouflage |
| **Spider monkey** | Long limbs and a prehensile (gripping) tail for moving through the canopy |
| **Toucan** | Large, lightweight beak to reach and cut fruit on branches too thin to hold its weight |
| **Flying frog / flying squirrel** | Webbed feet or flaps of skin let it glide between trees |
| **Jaguar** | Spotted coat as camouflage in the dappled light of the forest floor; strong swimmer |
| **Poison dart frog** | Bright colours warn predators that its skin is poisonous |
| **Orangutan** (Borneo, Sumatra) | Long, strong arms (longer than its legs) to swing between branches; eats fruit that ripens all year round |
| **Leafcutter ants** | Cut leaves and carry them to their nests, where they farm fungus on them for food |

### Issues related to biodiversity

**Biodiversity** is the variety of life in an area. Rainforests have the highest biodiversity of any land biome because the stable, warm, wet climate allows year-round growth and the many layers provide a huge range of niches (habitats). Millions of species there have still not been identified.

Issues:
- **Loss of species**: deforestation destroys habitats; species found only in small areas become endangered or extinct before they are even discovered. The orangutan in Borneo and Sumatra is critically endangered because its forest is being cleared for palm oil plantations.
- **Loss of medicines and foods**: many medicines were first found in rainforest plants — for example, the **rosy periwinkle** from Madagascar gave drugs used to treat childhood leukaemia. Foods such as bananas, cocoa, coffee and rubber came from rainforest plants; wild relatives of crops may hold genes for disease resistance.
- **Knock-on effects**: losing one species can affect many others through interdependence — e.g. if a seed-dispersing bird disappears, the trees that depend on it cannot spread.
- **Loss of indigenous knowledge and cultures** when forest peoples are displaced.
- **Climate**: fewer trees means less carbon stored and less rainfall recycled.

These losses are the reason for the management strategies in the next lesson (3.1.2.2b).

::: terms
- **Tropical rainforest**: a dense, layered forest biome along the Equator with a hot, wet climate all year and very high biodiversity.
- **Convectional rainfall**: rain formed when the Sun heats the ground, warm moist air rises, cools and condenses, giving afternoon thunderstorms.
- **Latosol**: the deep, red, iron-rich but infertile soil of the tropical rainforest.
- **Leaching**: the washing of nutrients down through the soil by heavy rain, out of reach of plant roots.
- **Interdependence**: the way the parts of an ecosystem, living and non-living, rely on one another, so a change to one affects the others.
- **Transpiration**: the loss of water vapour from plant leaves into the atmosphere, which returns moisture to form rain.
- **Adaptation**: a feature of a plant or animal that helps it survive in its environment.
- **Epiphyte**: a plant that grows on another plant, high in the canopy, taking moisture and nutrients from the air and rain.
- **Biodiversity**: the variety of plant and animal species, and the genetic differences within them, in an area.
- **Buttress roots**: large ridged roots at the base of tall rainforest trees that support them in shallow soil.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State / name** (1–2): a fact — "State one characteristic of rainforest soil" — "infertile / leached".
- **Describe** (2–4): what it is like — climate graph questions often ask you to describe temperature and rainfall with figures (range, total, pattern).
- **Explain** (2–6): how or why. For adaptations, you must link the **feature** to the **condition** it deals with: "drip tips let heavy rainfall run off, so algae cannot grow on the leaf".
- **Suggest** (2–4): e.g. suggest how a photographed plant is adapted.
- **Assess / to what extent** (6–9): e.g. "To what extent are the parts of the rainforest interdependent?" or "Assess the importance of biodiversity issues".
- **Use Figure X**: climate graphs — quote the maximum and minimum temperature, the annual range, the wettest and driest months and the total.

**Levels**
- **4-mark**: Level 2 needs developed links.
- **6-mark interdependence answers**: three clear chains, e.g. climate → fast growth → leaf litter → rapid decomposition → nutrients in biomass not soil. Use specific terms (transpiration, leaching, nutrient cycling).
- **9-mark**: develop several links and reach a judgement (e.g. which link is most important, or how far one change affects the whole system).

**Adaptation answers: feature → condition → benefit.**
- Feature: "buttress roots"
- Condition: "shallow soils and very tall trees"
- Benefit: "support the tree so it does not fall over".
Without the condition or the benefit, it is only a description.

**Climate graphs**: say "the temperature is high all year (26–28 °C), with a small annual range of about 2 °C" — always calculate the range.
:::

::: example Describe a climate graph
Figure 1 is a climate graph for Manaus: temperatures of 26–28 °C every month and over 2,000 mm of rain a year, with the lowest rainfall (about 60 mm) in August. Describe the climate shown. (3)

Temperatures are high all year, between about 26 °C and 28 °C (1), so the annual range is small — only about 2 °C (1). Rainfall is high, over 2,000 mm a year, with rain in every month, though there is a drier period with the lowest total of about 60 mm in August (1).
:::

::: example Explain — plant adaptations
Explain how plants are adapted to the physical conditions in a tropical rainforest. (6)

Emergent trees have buttress roots because the soils are thin and nutrients are near the surface, so roots are shallow; the large ridges support trees that can be 50 m tall so they do not fall over. Many leaves have drip tips and a waxy surface because rainfall is over 2,000 mm a year; water runs off quickly, so algae and fungi cannot grow on the leaves and block the light. Because the canopy absorbs most sunlight, very little reaches the ground, so lianas climb up tree trunks and epiphytes such as orchids grow high on branches to reach the light, taking moisture from the humid air rather than the soil. Plants in the dark shrub layer have large leaves to catch as much of the little light as possible.
:::

::: example Explain interdependence
Explain the interdependence between climate, soils and plants in a tropical rainforest. (4)

The hot, wet climate all year allows plants to grow fast and constantly, producing lots of leaf litter (1). The heat and moisture mean decomposers break down this litter within weeks, releasing nutrients into the thin humus layer, which plants quickly take up with their shallow roots (1). Heavy rain leaches nutrients from the soil, so most nutrients are held in the plants, not the soil (1). Plants return moisture to the climate through transpiration, which helps to form the next day's convectional rain, so the parts depend on each other — clear the trees and both the soil and the rainfall decline (1).
:::

::: q
State two characteristics of the climate of a tropical rainforest. (2)
---
Any two: hot all year (about 27 °C); small annual temperature range; high rainfall (over 2,000 mm); rain in most months / daily afternoon thunderstorms; humid (1 each).
:::

::: q
Explain why rainforest soils are infertile despite the lush vegetation. (3)
---
Heavy rainfall leaches nutrients down through the soil, out of reach of roots (1); most nutrients are stored in the vegetation (biomass) rather than the soil (1); leaf litter decomposes and is taken up by plants so fast that nutrients do not build up in the soil (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the layers of a tropical rainforest. (4)
---
Emergents up to about 50 m, the tallest trees poking above the canopy (1); canopy at about 30 m, a continuous layer of tree crowns where most animals live (1); under-canopy of younger trees, lianas and epiphytes (1); shrub layer and dark forest floor, receiving only a few per cent of the sunlight (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one adaptation of an animal to the rainforest. (2)
---
e.g. the sloth moves slowly and sleeps most of the day (1) to save energy because its diet of leaves provides little energy; algae on its fur camouflage it from predators (1). Or spider monkey's prehensile tail for moving through the canopy; toucan's beak to reach fruit on thin branches.
:::

::: q
Explain why tropical rainforests have high biodiversity. (3)
---
The hot, wet climate all year allows plants to grow continuously (1), producing huge amounts of food; the many layers of vegetation provide a wide range of different habitats and niches (1); the forest has been stable for millions of years, so many species have evolved (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the role of people in the interdependence of the rainforest. (3)
---
Indigenous people such as the Yanomami depend on the forest for food, shelter and medicine (1); shifting cultivation clears small plots and moves on, allowing the forest to regrow, so the balance is kept (1); large-scale clearing for logging or farming breaks the links — removing trees reduces leaf litter and transpiration, so soils become infertile and rainfall decreases (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why losing biodiversity in the rainforest matters to people living far away. (4)
---
Level 2 (3–4): developed points — many medicines came from rainforest plants (rosy periwinkle, leukaemia drugs), so undiscovered species may hold future cures; foods and raw materials (cocoa, coffee, rubber) and genes for disease-resistant crops; fewer trees means less carbon stored, contributing to climate change worldwide; less transpiration affects rainfall patterns. Level 1 (1–2): simple points.
:::

::: watch
- **Adaptation answers that only describe** the feature ("sloths are slow") — link it to the **condition** and the **benefit**.
- Saying the soil is **fertile** because the forest is lush — it is **infertile**; the nutrients are in the plants.
- Climate-graph answers without **figures** — always quote values and calculate the range.
- Writing about deforestation when the question asks about **characteristics** or **interdependence**.
- Interdependence answers that list parts rather than showing **how one affects another** — use "which means", "so".
:::

Explain why convectional rain falls most afternoons in the rainforest. (3)
---
The Sun heats the ground strongly during the morning (1); warm, moist air rises and cools, and water vapour condenses to form cumulonimbus clouds (1); by the afternoon the clouds release heavy rain and thunderstorms (1).

::: test
Suggest how the canopy affects conditions on the forest floor. (2)
---
The canopy absorbs most of the sunlight, so the forest floor is dark (1); it intercepts rain and keeps the air still and humid, so the floor is damp (1).
:::

Explain how animals are adapted to the physical conditions of the rainforest. (4)
---

Level 1 (1–2): features named but not linked to conditions.
:::

::: test
Explain the interdependence of the different parts of a tropical rainforest ecosystem. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): clear, detailed explanation of several links, using specific terms (transpiration, nutrient cycling, leaching, pollination, seed dispersal) and showing how a change to one part affects others.
Level 2 (3–4): some links explained; limited use of terms.
Level 1 (1–2): parts listed with little explanation of links.
Indicative content: climate → fast plant growth; plants → transpiration → rainfall; leaf litter → rapid decomposition → nutrients → plants (biomass store largest); roots protect soil from erosion; animals pollinate and disperse seeds; people depend on the forest and affect it through clearance.
:::

::: test
"Loss of biodiversity is the most serious issue facing tropical rainforests." To what extent do you agree? (9)
---
Level 3 (7–9): detailed evaluation of biodiversity loss against other issues, with specific examples, and a clear, supported judgement.
Level 2 (4–6): some evaluation and examples; judgement not fully supported.
Level 1 (1–3): simple points, little evaluation or judgement.
Indicative content: biodiversity — over half of the world's species; many undiscovered; extinction of endemic species (e.g. orangutans endangered in Borneo); loss of potential medicines (rosy periwinkle) and crop genes; knock-on effects through interdependence. Other issues — soil erosion and infertility after clearance; climate change from lost carbon stores; reduced rainfall through less transpiration; loss of indigenous cultures and livelihoods. Judgement e.g. biodiversity loss is the most serious because it is irreversible — soils and forests can regrow, but extinct species cannot return — although climate effects are the most serious for the world as a whole.
:::
