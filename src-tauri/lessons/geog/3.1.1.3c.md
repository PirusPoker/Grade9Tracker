## What it is

The UK does not get tropical storms or big earthquakes, but its weather is still hazardous. It sits where warm, moist air from the south-west meets cold air from the north, on the path of the **jet stream**, so it gets a stream of **depressions** (low-pressure systems) off the Atlantic, with occasional long spells of **high pressure** (anticyclones) that bring heatwaves in summer and freezing weather in winter.

The spec needs: an **overview of UK weather hazards**; one **recent extreme weather event** in the UK showing its **causes**, its **social, economic and environmental impacts**, and how **management strategies can reduce risk**; and **evidence that UK weather is becoming more extreme**.

### An overview of UK weather hazards

| Hazard | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong winds / storms** | Deep Atlantic depressions, mostly in autumn and winter | Storm Eunice, February 2022 — a gust of 122 mph at the Needles, Isle of Wight, a record for England; the Great Storm of October 1987 |
| **Heavy rain and flooding** | A series of depressions, or slow-moving thunderstorms; saturated ground | Boscastle, Cornwall, August 2004 (flash flood); Storm Desmond, Cumbria, December 2015 |
| **Snow and ice** | Cold air from the Arctic or Siberia with high pressure | The "Beast from the East", February–March 2018; December 2010, one of the coldest Decembers on record |
| **Drought** | Long spells of high pressure blocking rain-bearing depressions | 1976; summer 2022, when hosepipe bans were introduced in several regions |
| **Heatwave** | A blocking anticyclone drawing in hot air from continental Europe or Africa | August 2003 (about 2,000 extra deaths in the UK); July 2022, when 40.3 °C was recorded at Coningsby, Lincolnshire — the first 40 °C in UK records |
| **Thunderstorms, hail, lightning, fog** | Intense heating of the ground in summer; calm, clear nights for fog | Summer thunderstorms causing surface-water flooding in cities |

These hazards affect **human activity**: transport stops (roads, railways and airports close), power fails, crops are damaged, businesses lose trade, people are injured or killed, and the NHS comes under pressure in heatwaves and cold snaps.

### Named example: the Somerset Levels floods, winter 2013–14

The **Somerset Levels** are a flat, low-lying area of farmland and wetland in south-west England, between the Mendip and Quantock Hills. Much of the land is only a few metres above sea level — some is below the level of high tides in the Bristol Channel. Rivers such as the **Parrett** and the **Tone** flow slowly across the Levels to the sea, and the land has been drained for centuries by ditches ("rhynes"), embankments and pumping stations.

**Causes**

Physical causes:
- **Persistent rainfall**: between December 2013 and February 2014 a powerful jet stream steered a series of Atlantic depressions over southern England. **January 2014 was the wettest January on record** for southern England, and the winter was the wettest in the England and Wales records, which go back to 1766.
- **Saturated ground**: after weeks of rain the soil could hold no more water, so rain ran off quickly into the rivers.
- **High tides and storm surges**: very high spring tides, raised further by storm surges, pushed sea water up the tidal River Parrett, so river water could not drain out to the sea and backed up across the Levels.
- **Low, flat land**: the Levels have very gentle gradients, so floodwater drains away extremely slowly.

Human causes:
- **Rivers not dredged**: the Parrett and Tone had not been dredged for about 20 years. Silt had built up in the channels, reducing the amount of water they could carry, so they overflowed sooner.
- Some argued that changes in farming upstream (more maize fields, compacted soils) increased runoff into the rivers.

**Social impacts**
- Over **600 homes** were flooded and **16 farms** evacuated; many people lived in temporary accommodation for months.
- Villages such as **Muchelney** and **Thorney** were cut off for weeks — Muchelney effectively became an island, reachable only by boat.
- Power supplies were cut off to some homes; schools closed; people suffered stress and the loss of possessions.
- Floodwater contaminated with sewage created health risks.

**Economic impacts**
- Somerset County Council estimated the cost of flood damage at **over £10 million**.
- More than **14,000 hectares** of farmland (crops and grazing) were under water for weeks; farmers had to move **over 1,000 livestock**, buy in feed, and lost income; fields took months to recover.
- Many roads were closed, including the **A361** between Taunton and Street; the **railway between Bridgwater and Taunton** was closed.
- Local businesses lost trade as customers could not reach them; some villages lost income from visitors.

**Environmental impacts**
- Floodwater was contaminated with **sewage, oil and farm chemicals**, which polluted fields and ditches.
- Large amounts of **debris** had to be cleared from fields and roads.
- **Stagnant floodwater** lost its oxygen; it had to be **re-oxygenated before being pumped back into the rivers**, or it would have killed fish.
- Grass and soils were damaged by weeks under water, and some habitats were altered.

**Management strategies to reduce risk**
- A **£100 million, 20-year Flood Action Plan** was launched in 2014, with around £20 million of early funding from government and the council.
- **Dredging**: about **8 km** of the Rivers Tone and Parrett were dredged, starting in March 2014, to increase channel capacity.
- **River banks raised and strengthened**, and some rivers and drains (such as the River Sowy and King's Sedgemoor Drain) enlarged to carry more water.
- **Pumping**: during the flood, high-capacity pumps were brought in, some from the Netherlands; pumping stations were then upgraded.
- **Roads raised** — for example the road to Muchelney — so villages are not cut off in future.
- **Community resilience**: grants for flood barriers and door guards on homes; flood-warning schemes; local flood groups.
- The **Somerset Rivers Authority** was set up in 2015 to coordinate flood work across the county.
- A **tidal barrier on the River Parrett at Bridgwater** is being built, with opening planned for about 2027, so high tides and surges can be kept out of the river.

**How effective?** Dredging was popular locally but experts (including the Environment Agency) warned that dredging alone would not have prevented a flood on this scale, because the rivers could not drain against high tides. The combination of dredging, raised banks, pumping and the planned tidal barrier reduces risk; raising roads and protecting homes means that when floods happen, they cause less disruption.

### Evidence that UK weather is becoming more extreme

- **Temperature records**: all of the UK's ten warmest years on record have been since 2000 (Met Office, records back to 1884). 2022 was the UK's warmest year on record at the time and the first time 40 °C was recorded (40.3 °C, Coningsby, 19 July 2022).
- **Rainfall records**: Storm Desmond (December 2015) brought **341.4 mm of rain in 24 hours** at Honister Pass in Cumbria, a UK record. Winter 2013–14 was the wettest in the England and Wales series since 1766, and October 2023 to March 2024 was the wettest winter half-year on record. February 2020 (Storms Ciara and Dennis) was the UK's wettest February on record.
- **More frequent extremes**: the Met Office reports that in the last decade the number of very hot days (well above average) has increased sharply, and records are being broken more often.
- **Flooding**: major floods have come in quick succession — 2007, 2009, 2013–14, 2015–16, 2020 — and many rivers have recorded their highest ever flows.

**Caution**: one extreme event does not prove a trend — the UK has always had variable weather (e.g. the 1976 drought, the harsh winter of 1962–63). The evidence is strongest when it comes from **long-term records** showing extremes becoming **more frequent and more intense**. Scientists link this to climate change: a warmer atmosphere holds more moisture (heavier rain), and heatwaves become hotter.

::: terms
- **Weather hazard**: a weather event, such as a storm, flood, drought or heatwave, that threatens people, property or the environment.
- **Depression**: an area of low atmospheric pressure bringing cloud, rain and strong winds, usually moving in from the Atlantic.
- **Anticyclone**: an area of high atmospheric pressure bringing settled weather — heatwaves and drought in summer, cold and frost in winter.
- **Jet stream**: a fast band of high-altitude wind that steers depressions towards or away from the UK.
- **Dredging**: removing silt and sediment from a river bed to deepen the channel so it can carry more water.
- **Saturated**: describes ground whose soil pores are full of water, so further rain runs off over the surface.
- **Storm surge**: a rise in sea level caused by low pressure and strong winds pushing water towards the coast.
- **Extreme weather**: weather that is much more severe than usual for a place, such as record rainfall or temperature.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State / outline** (1–2 marks): short facts — "State one UK weather hazard" — "heatwave".
- **Describe** (2–4): what happened, with detail and numbers. For impacts, give facts: "over 600 homes flooded".
- **Explain** (2–6): reasons. For causes, link cause → effect: "the rivers had not been dredged for 20 years, so silt reduced their capacity and they overflowed sooner".
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** (6–9): weigh up and judge — e.g. "physical causes were more important than human causes" or "management strategies have reduced the risk".
- **Use Figure X**: quote values and trends from the graph or map (e.g. rainfall totals, temperature records).

**Levels**
- **4-mark**: Level 2 (3–4) needs developed points — each fact explained.
- **6-mark**: three developed points, each using Somerset detail. Level 3 is "detailed and accurate" — named places (Muchelney, Parrett, Tone, A361) and figures (600 homes, 14,000 ha, £10 million).
- **9-mark** (+3 SPaG where shown): develop both sides (e.g. physical vs human causes; how well management reduces risk vs its limits), then a **judgement** that answers the question and says *why*.

**Point → Develop → Link**
1. **Point**: "An economic impact was the flooding of farmland."
2. **Develop**: "Fields were under water for weeks, so farmers could not graze animals or plant crops..."
3. **Link**: "...over 14,000 hectares flooded and over 1,000 livestock had to be moved."

**Classify impacts correctly.** Social = people's lives, homes, health, communities. Economic = money, jobs, businesses, transport, farming income. Environmental = pollution, habitats, land and water. A question on environmental impacts earns nothing for "homes were flooded".

**"Evidence" questions** need data — record temperatures, rainfall totals, dates — not just "it feels hotter".
:::

::: example Describe — UK weather hazards
Describe two types of weather hazard that affect the UK. (4)

Heatwaves (1): long spells of high pressure bring very hot weather, such as July 2022 when 40.3 °C was recorded at Coningsby (1). Strong winds (1): deep Atlantic depressions bring gales, such as Storm Eunice in February 2022, which brought a 122 mph gust and brought down trees and power lines (1).
:::

::: example Explain — causes
Using a named example, explain the causes of an extreme weather event in the UK. (6)

In the Somerset Levels floods of 2013–14, the main physical cause was prolonged rainfall: a strong jet stream steered depression after depression over southern England, making January 2014 the wettest January on record, so the ground became saturated and rain ran straight into the rivers. At the same time, high spring tides and storm surges pushed water up the tidal River Parrett, so river water could not drain to the sea and backed up across the very flat, low-lying Levels, some of which is barely above sea level. A human cause made it worse: the Rivers Parrett and Tone had not been dredged for around 20 years, so silt had reduced the size of their channels and they overflowed sooner than they would have done.
:::

::: example Evaluate — management
To what extent can management strategies reduce the risk from extreme weather in the UK? (9 + 3 SPaG)

After the Somerset Levels floods of 2013–14, a £100 million, 20-year Flood Action Plan was introduced. About 8 km of the Rivers Tone and Parrett were dredged, increasing their capacity so they overflow less often, and river banks were raised and strengthened. Roads such as the one to Muchelney were raised, so villages should not be cut off for weeks again.

Management also makes communities more resilient: grants for door guards and flood barriers reduce damage to homes, and the Somerset Rivers Authority now coordinates flood work. The tidal barrier at Bridgwater, due to open around 2027, will tackle one of the key causes — high tides preventing the rivers from draining.

However, management cannot remove the risk. The Levels are naturally low and flat, and experts warned that dredging alone would not have prevented the 2014 flood, because water cannot drain against a high tide. If UK weather is becoming more extreme — records such as 341.4 mm of rain in 24 hours at Honister Pass in 2015 are being broken more often — defences designed for past events may be overwhelmed.

Overall, management can reduce the risk to a large extent, especially by protecting homes and keeping transport open, but it cannot stop extreme events happening, so the aim is to reduce impacts rather than eliminate flooding.
:::
