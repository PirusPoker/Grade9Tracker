## What it is

**Cartographic skills** — using maps and photographs — are tested in **all three papers**, and an **Ordnance Survey (OS) map extract** may appear in any of them. These are some of the most reliable marks on the paper: learn the techniques once and they work every time.

### Atlas maps

**Latitude and longitude** locate any place on Earth using **degrees (°)**:
- **Latitude** — lines running east–west, measuring how far **north or south of the Equator** (0°) a place is, up to 90°N (North Pole) and 90°S. The **Tropics of Cancer** (about 23.5°N) and **Capricorn** (about 23.5°S) and the **Arctic and Antarctic Circles** are key lines.
- **Longitude** — lines running north–south, measuring how far **east or west of the Prime Meridian** (0°, through Greenwich, London), up to 180°.
- Always give **latitude first**: London is about 51.5°N, 0°.

**Describing distributions and patterns** — the most common map skill. Use this checklist:
1. **General pattern** — where most of the feature is; is it **clustered**, **scattered (dispersed)**, **linear** (along a line such as a coast, river or road) or **even**?
2. **Specific evidence** — name continents, countries, oceans, lines of latitude, directions; quote values from the key.
3. **Exceptions (anomalies)** — places that do not fit.

Maps at **global and other scales** may show population distribution, population movements (migration arrows), transport networks, settlement layout, relief (height and shape of land) and drainage (rivers and lakes).

**Inter-relationships and associations**: compare two thematic maps and describe how the patterns are **linked** — for example, population density is high where the land is low and flat and the climate is mild (the lowlands of south-east England), and low in high, steep, wet uplands (the Scottish Highlands). Use phrases such as "areas with… also tend to have…" and give evidence from both maps. Remember that an **association** does not prove one thing **causes** the other.

### Ordnance Survey maps

**Scales**

The **scale** tells you how much smaller the map is than real life.

| Scale | Series | Map distance | Real distance | Grid square |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1:50 000** | Landranger | **2 cm** | **1 km** | 1 km square = 2 cm across |
| **1:25 000** | Explorer | **4 cm** | **1 km** | 1 km square = 4 cm across |

A 1:25 000 map is **larger scale** — it shows a smaller area in **more detail** (field boundaries, individual buildings). Grid lines on both are **1 km apart**, so each grid square is 1 km × 1 km = **1 km²**.

**Grid references**

- **Eastings** are the vertical lines, numbered **increasing to the east**; **northings** are the horizontal lines, numbered **increasing to the north**.
- **Always read eastings first**, then northings — "**along the corridor, then up the stairs**".
- **Four-figure grid reference** — identifies a **whole 1 km square**, using the easting and northing of its **bottom-left corner**: e.g. **4523** is the square east of line 45 and north of line 23.
- **Six-figure grid reference** — pinpoints a feature to within **100 m**. Imagine each square divided into tenths. Take the easting (45), add how many tenths across (7) → **457**; take the northing (23), add how many tenths up (2) → **232**. Result: **457232**.
- Check: a six-figure reference must have its **third and sixth** digits as the tenths; the first two and fourth and fifth digits match the four-figure square.

**Distance**

- **Straight-line distance**: measure with a ruler, then convert using the scale (on 1:50 000, divide centimetres by 2 to get km; on 1:25 000, divide by 4). Or mark the distance on the edge of a piece of paper and hold it against the **scale bar**.
- **Curved distance** (along a road, river or footpath): lay the edge of a piece of paper along the route, pivoting at each bend and marking as you go, or lay a piece of **string** along the route; then straighten it against the scale bar.
- Give answers in the unit asked for — **km or m** — and don't forget the unit.

**Direction**

- Use the **eight points of the compass** (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW) — or **16 points** (NNE, ENE, etc.) for precision. **North is at the top** of an OS map.
- "What direction is B **from** A?" — put your pencil at **A** and look towards B.
- A **bearing** is measured clockwise from north in degrees (east = 090°).

**Height and relief**

- **Contour lines** (brown on OS maps) join points of **equal height** above sea level. The **contour interval** is usually **10 m** on 1:50 000 maps and **5 m** on 1:25 000 maps in lowland areas (10 m in mountains).
  - **Contours close together** = **steep** slope; **far apart** = **gentle** slope; **no contours** = **flat** land.
  - Read the numbers — they are written so they face **uphill**.
- **Spot heights** — a black **dot** with the height in metres next to it.
- **Triangulation pillars (trig points)** — a blue **triangle** with a dot, height in metres; usually on hilltops.
- **Layer colouring** on some maps shades land by height.

**Recognising relief features from contours**

| Feature | What the contours look like |
|---|---|
| **Hill / summit** | closed rings of contours getting higher to a centre |
| **Ridge** | a long, narrow area of high land, with contours elongated in one direction |
| **Valley** | contours form **V shapes pointing uphill** (upstream), usually with a river in the middle |
| **Spur** | contours form **V or U shapes pointing downhill** — a finger of high land sticking out into a valley |
| **Plateau** | a large, flat area at the top of steep slopes — few contours on top, many around the edges |
| **Escarpment (scarp)** | a steep slope on one side (close contours) and a gentle slope on the other |
| **Floodplain** | wide, flat valley floor with few or no contours either side of a river |

**Gradient**

Gradient is the **steepness** of a slope:

**gradient = vertical difference in height ÷ horizontal distance** (both in the **same units**)

Example: a path rises from 120 m to 220 m (a difference of 100 m) over a horizontal distance of 1 km (1,000 m). 100 ÷ 1,000 = 1/10 → a gradient of **1 in 10**, or **10%**.

**Cross-sections**

A **cross-section** shows the shape of the land along a line, as if the ground were sliced through.

1. Lay the straight edge of a piece of paper along the line between the two points.
2. Mark where each **contour** (and river, road or other feature) crosses the edge, and write its height.
3. Draw axes: horizontal = the length of your paper strip; vertical = height, with a sensible scale.
4. Transfer each mark to the graph at the correct height and **join the points with a smooth curve** — not straight lines.
5. **Label** features (river, valley, summit, road) and give the cross-section a title and the grid references of each end.

The vertical scale is usually exaggerated so slopes look steeper than in reality. A **transect** is similar but can also record land use or human features along the line.

### Reading the landscape from an OS map

**Landscape features** — describe their **characteristics** with map evidence: height (contours, spot heights), slope (contour spacing), shape, size (grid squares), and grid references.

**Rivers (fluvial landscapes) on large-scale maps**:
- **Upper course**: high land, contours close together forming **V shapes** → a steep-sided **V-shaped valley**; **interlocking spurs**; **waterfalls** (labelled "Waterfall", or "Force" in northern England); narrow, fast streams, often many tributaries (a high **drainage density**).
- **Middle and lower course**: river **meanders** (curves), wider flat **floodplain** with few contours, **oxbow lakes** (crescent-shaped lakes beside the river), **levées** or embankments, **marsh** symbols, and finally an **estuary** with **mudflats** where the river meets the sea.
- **Direction of flow**: rivers flow **downhill** — from higher contours to lower ones. Look at the heights either side.

**Coasts on large-scale maps**:
- **Cliffs** — shown by a black jagged (hachured) cliff symbol and contours bunched at the coastline.
- **Headlands and bays** — land jutting out to sea next to curved indentations.
- **Stacks, arches and stumps** — often named ("Old Harry Rocks"), shown as small rocky areas offshore.
- **Wave-cut platform** — rock symbols in the intertidal area at the foot of cliffs.
- **Beaches** — sand (yellow/stippled) and shingle; **spits** — narrow ridges of sand extending into the sea or across an estuary; **sand dunes**, **salt marsh** (marsh symbols behind a spit).
- **Coastal defences** — sea walls, groynes (thin lines across the beach), breakwaters.

**Human landscape** — draw **inferences** (reasoned conclusions) from:
- **Settlement**: **nucleated** (clustered around a point), **dispersed** (scattered farms), **linear** (along a road or valley). Look at **site** factors — a bridging point, dry land above a floodplain, shelter, a spring.
- **Communication**: main roads, railways and motorways follow **valleys and low, flat land**, avoiding steep slopes.
- **Land use**: woodland symbols (coniferous vs deciduous), farmland, industry (works, quarries), CBD (dense buildings, road junctions).
- **Tourism** — infer it from **blue tourist symbols**: **camp and caravan sites, picnic sites, viewpoints, visitor centres, information centres, parking, public toilets, nature reserves, museums, golf courses, footpaths and National Trail routes**, plus beaches, marinas and piers. Many of these together suggest a **tourist honeypot**.

Link human and physical: "The village is sited on the flat floodplain at 20 m, but above the river, and the main road follows the valley floor because it is the only flat route through the hills."

### Maps with photographs

**Comparing maps** — e.g. two maps of the same place at different dates: describe **what has changed** (new housing estates, a bypass, loss of farmland) and **what has stayed the same**, with grid references.

**Types of photograph**:
- **Ground** photographs — taken at ground level; show detail and what the landscape looks like.
- **Aerial** photographs — taken from the air. **Oblique** aerial photos are taken at an angle (show height and a wide area; features at the back look smaller). **Vertical** aerial photos look straight down (like a map; good for measuring and layout).
- **Satellite** images — from space; show large areas, often in false colour; useful for land use, deforestation, floods, storms.

**Describing photographs** — describe **landforms, natural vegetation, land use and settlement**, and any geographical phenomena (a landslide, a flood, a storm). Use the **foreground, middle ground and background** and **left, centre, right** to locate things. Match a photo to a map by finding **clues** (a bridge, a church, the coastline shape) and working out the direction the camera was pointing.

**Sketch maps and field sketches**:
- A **sketch map** is a simplified map (drawn from an OS map or from memory) showing only the important features, with a title, north arrow, key and approximate scale.
- A **field sketch from a photograph**: draw a **frame**, sketch the main lines (skyline, coastline, river, field boundaries) — not every detail — and **label or annotate** the key features. Add a **title** and **direction** if known.

**Labelling vs annotating**:
- A **label** simply names a feature: "wave-cut notch".
- An **annotation** is a **label with an explanation**: "wave-cut notch — formed by hydraulic action and abrasion at the base of the cliff, between high and low tide". Annotation questions need the **explanation**.

::: terms
- **Latitude**: the distance in degrees north or south of the Equator, shown by lines running east to west.
- **Longitude**: the distance in degrees east or west of the Prime Meridian through Greenwich, shown by lines running north to south.
- **Scale**: the ratio between a distance on the map and the real distance on the ground, such as 1:50 000.
- **Four-figure grid reference**: a reference identifying a 1 km grid square, using the eastings and northings of its bottom-left corner.
- **Six-figure grid reference**: a reference pinpointing a feature to within 100 m, by dividing the grid square into tenths.
- **Contour line**: a brown line on a map joining points of equal height above sea level.
- **Spot height**: a dot on a map with a number giving the exact height of that point in metres.
- **Gradient**: the steepness of a slope, calculated as the difference in height divided by the horizontal distance.
- **Cross-section**: a side view of the shape of the land along a straight line, drawn from contour heights.
- **Relief**: the height and shape of the land.
- **Oblique aerial photograph**: a photograph taken from the air at an angle, showing a wide area and the height of features.
- **Annotation**: a label that adds explanation or description to a feature on a diagram, map or photograph.
:::

::: method
**What the questions look like.** Short questions (1–4 marks) on an OS extract, atlas map or photograph, spread across all three papers, sometimes as **multiple choice**. Occasionally a 4- or 6-mark "Use Figure X and your own knowledge" question combines map evidence with understanding — e.g. "Using the OS map, describe the landforms of the river valley" or "Suggest why the area attracts tourists".

**Command words:**
- **Give / state / identify** — a single piece of information (a grid reference, a feature, a height).
- **Measure / calculate** — show working and give **units** (km, m, 1 in x).
- **Describe** — what is there, using **map evidence**: grid references, heights, distances, directions, names.
- **Suggest / explain** — reasons: apply your knowledge to the map.
- **Complete / draw / label / annotate** — do it neatly and accurately; annotation needs explanation.

**Golden rules:**
1. **Quote map evidence every time** — grid references, spot heights, contour values, distances, place names. "The land is high" earns nothing; "The land rises to 245 m at 457232" does.
2. **Eastings before northings.** Check your six-figure reference by finding it again.
3. Convert units correctly — 1:50 000 → 2 cm = 1 km; 1:25 000 → 4 cm = 1 km.
4. For patterns: **overall pattern → evidence → exception**.
5. For photos, use **foreground / background** and **left / right** to locate what you describe.

**Longer answers (4–6 marks)**: two or three points, each with map or photo evidence **and** a geographical explanation. For "suggest why tourists visit": beach at 2345, viewpoint at 3456 shows attractive scenery, camp sites and parking show facilities, footpaths for walking — each with a reason why that attracts visitors.

**9-mark questions** do not usually test map skills alone, but in a 9-mark question on a river or coast you can **use map evidence** from an extract (landforms with grid references) as your supporting detail, and the evaluation must still end in a judgement.
:::

::: example Grid references
On an OS map, a church lies in the grid square east of easting 45 and north of northing 23. It is seven-tenths of the way across the square and two-tenths of the way up. Give its four- and six-figure grid references. (2)

Four-figure: 4523 (1). Six-figure: 457232 (1).
:::

::: example Gradient
On a 1:50 000 map, a footpath climbs from a spot height of 85 m to a summit of 335 m. The horizontal distance between them measures 5 cm on the map. Calculate the average gradient. (3)

Height difference = 335 − 85 = 250 m (1). Horizontal distance = 5 cm × 0.5 km = 2.5 km = 2,500 m (1). Gradient = 250 ÷ 2,500 = 1/10, so 1 in 10 (or 10%) (1).
:::

::: example Describe a river valley from a map
Use the OS map extract to describe the physical features of the river valley in the upper course. (4)

The river flows in a narrow, steep-sided valley: the contours are very close together on both sides, rising from about 200 m at the river to over 400 m within 1 km (1). The contours form V shapes pointing upstream, showing a V-shaped valley (1). Spurs of high land project into the valley from alternate sides, forming interlocking spurs, for example around grid square 4523 (1). There is a waterfall marked at 457232, and many small tributary streams join the main river, giving a high drainage density (1).
(Marks awarded only where map evidence — heights, grid references — supports the description.)
:::

::: example Suggest human activity from map evidence
Suggest two pieces of map evidence that the area is popular with tourists. (4)

There is a camp site and a caravan site close to the beach (1), which provide cheap accommodation for visitors staying near the coast (1). There is a viewpoint and a visitor centre on the headland (1), showing that people come to see the attractive coastal scenery and that facilities have been built for them (1).
(In an exam, add grid references for each.)
:::

::: q
State the scale of an OS Landranger map, and how many centimetres represent 1 km on it. (2)
---
1:50 000 (1); 2 cm represents 1 km (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between a four-figure and a six-figure grid reference. (2)
---
A four-figure reference identifies a whole 1 km grid square (1); a six-figure reference pinpoints a location within that square to the nearest 100 m, by dividing the square into tenths (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how contour lines show a steep slope and a gentle slope. (2)
---
Steep: contours close together (1). Gentle: contours far apart (1).
:::

::: q
On a 1:25 000 map, a river measures 14 cm along its curves between two bridges. What is the real distance? (2)
---
On 1:25 000, 4 cm = 1 km (1), so 14 ÷ 4 = 3.5 km (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between labelling and annotating a photograph. (2)
---
A label just names a feature (1); an annotation adds a description or explanation of the feature, e.g. how it was formed (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how you would draw a cross-section between two points on an OS map. (4)
---
Place the straight edge of a piece of paper between the two points (1) and mark where each contour crosses it, noting the heights (1). Transfer the marks onto a graph with a suitable vertical scale at the correct heights (1), then join the points with a smooth curve and label features such as rivers and roads (1).
:::

::: watch
- Giving **northings before eastings** in a grid reference — the whole mark is lost.
- Using the wrong **scale conversion** (1:25 000 is 4 cm per km, not 2 cm) or leaving out **units**.
- Describing a map with **no map evidence** — no grid references, heights, names or distances.
- Gradient with **mixed units** (metres over kilometres) — convert both to metres first.
- Joining cross-section points with straight lines, or forgetting labels and title.
- **Labelling** when the question says **annotate** — annotations need an explanation.
- Describing a pattern by listing places without the **overall pattern** or the **exception**.
- Mixing up **valleys** (contour Vs point **uphill**) and **spurs** (contour Vs point **downhill**).
:::

::: test
What does a triangulation pillar show on an OS map? A — a church; B — a height at a surveyed point, usually on a hilltop; C — a viewpoint; D — a camp site. (1)
---
B (1).
:::

::: test
Using lines of latitude, state what is meant by the Equator. (1)
---
The line of latitude at 0°, dividing the Earth into the northern and southern hemispheres, halfway between the poles (1).
:::

::: test
On a 1:50 000 map, the straight-line distance between two villages is 7 cm. Calculate the distance in kilometres. (2)
---
2 cm = 1 km, so 7 ÷ 2 (1) = 3.5 km (1).
:::

::: test
State the difference between an oblique and a vertical aerial photograph. (2)
---
Oblique: taken from the air at an angle, showing a wide view and the height of features (1). Vertical: taken looking straight down, like a map (1).
:::

::: test
A road rises from 60 m to 140 m over a horizontal distance of 800 m. Calculate the gradient. Show your working. (3)
---
Height difference = 140 − 60 = 80 m (1). Gradient = 80 ÷ 800 = 1/10 (1) = 1 in 10 or 10% (1).
:::

::: test
Describe the map evidence that would show a river is in its lower course. (4)
---
Any four (1 each): wide, flat floodplain with few or no contours; low heights (spot heights near sea level); meanders; oxbow lakes; levées or embankments; marshland symbols; wide river channel; estuary or mudflats; settlements and roads on the flat land. Credit use of example grid references.
:::

::: test
Using map and photographic evidence, suggest how physical features have influenced human activity in a coastal area you have seen in an OS extract. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): clear links between named physical features and human activities, supported by specific map/photo evidence (grid references, heights, symbols). Level 2 (3–4): some links with some evidence. Level 1 (1–2): simple points with little evidence.
Indicative content: settlement on flat land near sea level rather than on steep cliffs; main roads follow valleys and flat coastal plain; beaches and bays attract tourists — camp sites, car parks, visitor centres, viewpoints on headlands; cliffs and headlands with viewpoints and footpaths (coast path); harbour or marina in a sheltered bay; sea walls and groynes protecting settlements from erosion; marshland left undeveloped or used as a nature reserve.
:::

::: test
Study an OS map extract of a river valley with a small town on its floodplain. "The physical landscape is the most important influence on land use and settlement in the area." Using map evidence and your own knowledge, to what extent do you agree? (9)
---
Level 3 (7–9): detailed and accurate use of map evidence (grid references, heights, contour patterns, symbols); well-developed explanation of how relief, drainage and landforms influence land use and settlement; recognition of human factors (roads, bridging points, planning, tourism); a clear, supported judgement. Level 2 (4–6): some map evidence and explanation, judgement partly supported. Level 1 (1–3): simple description with little map evidence and no clear judgement.
Indicative content: the town is on flat land above the floodplain or on a river terrace, avoiding steep slopes and flood risk; a bridging point may explain its site; roads and railway follow the flat valley floor; steep valley sides left as woodland (contours close together); high, exposed land used for rough grazing or forestry, with few settlements (only scattered farms); floodplain used for pasture or recreation, not housing, because of flooding; human influences: main roads and junctions attract growth, embankments or flood defences allow building on low land, tourism facilities (camp sites, car parks) near attractive scenery, planning controls in a National Park. Judgement: e.g. physical landscape sets the limits (where people can build and farm) but human factors decide how land within those limits is used.
:::
