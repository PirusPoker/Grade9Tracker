## What it is

This topic is the compulsory start of **The challenge of resource management** (Paper 2, Section C, question 3). Everyone answers it, whatever option they study afterwards. It asks two things: why **food, water and energy** matter, and how the **demand for and supply of** those three resources is changing in the **UK**.

### Why food, water and energy matter

Food, water and energy are **fundamental to human development** — without them nothing else is possible.

| Resource | Economic well-being (jobs, wealth, production) | Social well-being (health, education, quality of life) |
|---|---|---|
| **Food** | a healthy, well-fed workforce is productive; farming employs a large share of workers in LICs; food exports earn foreign currency | prevents undernutrition and disease; children who eat well grow properly and learn better at school |
| **Water** | needed for farming (irrigation), industry (cooling, processing) and energy (hydroelectric power, cooling power stations) | clean water and sanitation prevent diseases such as cholera and typhoid; time not spent fetching water can be spent at school or work — often by women and girls |
| **Energy** | powers factories, transport and services; reliable electricity attracts investment | lighting lets children study after dark; clean cooking fuel avoids smoke-related lung disease; heating and refrigeration improve health |

The three are **linked**: growing food needs water and energy (pumping, fertiliser, machinery); supplying clean water needs energy (pumping, treatment); producing energy needs water (cooling, hydroelectric power). A shortage of one creates pressure on the others.

### Global inequality in supply and consumption

Resources are **not shared evenly**. In general, **HICs consume far more** than their share of the world's population, while many people in LICs cannot get enough.

- **Consumption**: the United Nations Development Programme found (1998) that the richest fifth of the world's people accounted for about **86%** of all private consumption spending. The average person in the USA or UK uses many times more energy than a person in sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Food**: average calorie supply is well above 3,000 kcal per person per day in North America and Europe, but hundreds of millions of people — around **700–800 million** in recent UN estimates — are undernourished, most in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
- **Water**: about **2.2 billion people** lack safely managed drinking water (WHO/UNICEF). HIC households use large amounts; in parts of the Sahel people may manage on under 20 litres a day.
- **Energy**: several hundred million people, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, have **no electricity**, and around 2 billion still cook with wood, charcoal or dung.

**Why the inequality?** Supply depends partly on **physical factors** (climate, rainfall, geology — where oil, gas and coal are found) and partly on **human factors**: wealth (rich countries can import what they lack), technology (dams, desalination, pipelines), infrastructure, conflict and political stability. Rich countries can buy resources from anywhere; poor countries often cannot even use the resources they have.

### Resources in the UK — the overview

The UK is a wealthy country with a growing population (over 68 million) and high expectations: all-year choice of food, water on tap and constant electricity. This **changing demand**, combined with **changing supply**, creates **opportunities** (new jobs in renewables, new markets for local food) and **challenges** (reliance on imports, water stress in the south-east, energy security and costs).

### UK food

The UK produces only around **60%** of the food it eats (Defra figures); the rest is imported — from the EU (especially fruit and vegetables from Spain and the Netherlands) and from further afield.

**1. Growing demand for high-value exports from LICs, all-year seasonal food and organic produce**

- **High-value foods** are crops that sell for a high price per kilogram — out-of-season vegetables, exotic fruit, coffee, tea, cocoa. Examples: green beans and mangetout from **Kenya**, avocados and asparagus from **Peru**, pineapples from **Ghana** and Costa Rica.
- **Why the demand?** Rising incomes, multicultural tastes, foreign travel, and supermarkets that promise the same products all year. UK consumers want strawberries in December, when they cannot be grown here without heated greenhouses.
- **Advantages for the LIC**: jobs (Kenyan horticulture employs many thousands, many of them women), foreign currency, investment in packing houses and cold stores.
- **Disadvantages for the LIC**: land and water used to grow food for export instead of for local people — in dry Kenya, irrigation for export crops competes with local water needs; low wages; dependence on UK supermarkets that can switch suppliers.
- **Organic produce** is grown without artificial fertilisers or pesticides. Demand has grown because of health and environmental concerns, and UK sales are now worth around £3 billion a year, but organic food costs more and yields are lower, so some of it is imported.

**2. Food miles and carbon footprints — and the move to local sourcing**

- **Food miles** are the distance food travels from where it is grown to the consumer. Beans flown from Nairobi to London travel roughly 6,800 km.
- More food miles, especially by **air freight**, mean more fuel burned and more carbon dioxide — a larger **carbon footprint** (the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a product or activity).
- **But** food miles are not the whole story. Crops grown outdoors in a warm country and shipped by sea can have a smaller footprint than the same crop grown in a heated UK greenhouse. Transport is only one part of a food's footprint.
- **Local sourcing** — buying food produced near where it is eaten — is growing: farmers' markets, farm shops, vegetable box schemes, "pick your own", allotments and supermarkets labelling British produce. It cuts transport emissions, supports local farmers and keeps money in the local economy, though choice is narrower and some food costs more.

**3. The trend towards agribusiness**

**Agribusiness** is farming run as a large, intensive, industrial business: big farms, heavy machinery, chemical fertilisers and pesticides, and close links to food processors and supermarkets (often through contracts). East Anglia's large arable farms are the classic UK example.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| economies of scale — cheaper food | hedgerows removed for bigger fields, destroying habitats |
| high yields and efficient production | chemicals pollute rivers (nitrates) and harm wildlife and pollinators |
| reliable supply for supermarkets | fewer farm jobs because machines replace workers |
| investment in new technology | small family farms squeezed out; power held by supermarkets |

The number of UK farms has fallen while the average farm has grown larger — a sign of the trend.

### UK water

**1. Changing demand**

Demand for water in the UK is **rising**, because of:

- **Population growth** — more people, and population is growing fastest in the already dry south-east.
- **More households** — people living alone or in smaller households use more water per person (each home has its own washing machine, dishwasher and garden).
- **Lifestyles** — more showers, power showers, dishwashers, washing machines, garden watering and car washing. Average use is about **140 litres per person per day**.
- **Industry and farming** — irrigation of crops such as potatoes in dry summers; energy production (cooling power stations).

Some things **reduce** demand: water meters (people pay for what they use), water-efficient appliances, dual-flush toilets and hosepipe bans in droughts.

**2. Water quality and pollution management**

Clean water needs careful management because rivers and groundwater can be polluted by:

- **agricultural run-off** — fertilisers (nitrates, phosphates) and pesticides washed into rivers, causing eutrophication (algal blooms that starve rivers of oxygen);
- **sewage** — storm overflows release untreated sewage into rivers and the sea after heavy rain;
- **industrial waste** and **urban run-off** (oil and chemicals from roads);
- **old mines** leaking acidic, metal-rich water.

**Management**:

- The **Environment Agency** monitors water quality and can prosecute polluters; water companies must treat water to strict drinking standards.
- **Nitrate Vulnerable Zones** limit when and how much fertiliser farmers can spread; farmers are encouraged to leave buffer strips beside rivers.
- **Sewage treatment works** clean waste water before it returns to rivers. The **Thames Tideway Tunnel**, a 25 km "super sewer" under the River Thames in London, was built to capture storm overflows that used to pour into the river, and was fully connected in 2024–25.
- **Education and laws** — for example, campaigns about not pouring oil or chemicals down drains.

**3. Matching supply and demand — areas of deficit and surplus**

- A **water surplus** is where supply is greater than demand; a **water deficit** is where demand is greater than supply. **Water stress** is when demand exceeds the available supply during a period, or poor quality limits its use.
- The UK has a **mismatch**:
  - **North and west** (Scotland, Wales, north-west England, the Lake District): high rainfall — over 1,000 mm a year and much more in the mountains — on high, impermeable rock, with low population density. **Surplus.**
  - **South and east** (London, the south-east, East Anglia): low rainfall — around 600 mm a year — high population density and the most growth. **Deficit.** The Environment Agency classes much of the south-east as **seriously water stressed**.
- So most rain falls where fewest people live, and most people live where least rain falls. Describe this on a map as "a north-west to south-east divide".

**4. The need for transfer to maintain supplies**

A **water transfer** moves water from an area of surplus to an area of deficit, using rivers, pipelines, tunnels or aqueducts.

- **Kielder Water**, Northumberland (opened 1982), is one of the largest artificial lakes in northern Europe. The **Kielder transfer scheme** can release water into the River Tyne and pump it through a tunnel to the Rivers **Wear** and **Tees**, supplying the industry and people of north-east England in dry spells.
- **Elan Valley** reservoirs in mid-Wales have supplied **Birmingham** since 1904 through an aqueduct about 118 km long, flowing downhill by gravity.
- **Thirlmere** and **Haweswater** in the Lake District supply **Manchester**.
- A **national water grid** linking the wet north and west to the dry south-east has been proposed many times, and new transfers (such as a Severn–Thames link) and reservoirs are being planned.

| Advantages of transfers | Disadvantages of transfers |
|---|---|
| secure supply for areas of deficit | very expensive to build; pumping uphill uses lots of energy |
| reservoirs can also provide HEP, leisure and tourism (Kielder has forest, cycling and an observatory) | building reservoirs floods valleys, farms and homes — Capel Celyn in Wales was drowned in the 1960s to supply Liverpool, causing lasting anger |
| reduce over-abstraction from rivers in the south-east | moving water between rivers can move pollutants and invasive species and change river temperatures, harming ecosystems |
| can help during droughts | the donor area may object — "our water" leaving the region |

The alternative is to **manage demand** instead: fixing leaks (water companies lose around a fifth of treated water to leaks), metering, and water-saving appliances. A strong answer says transfers and demand management are both needed.

### UK energy

**1. The changing energy mix**

The **energy mix** is the combination of sources a country uses (coal, oil, gas, nuclear, renewables).

- In **1970** the UK relied mainly on **coal and oil**. From the 1990s the "**dash for gas**" replaced much coal with North Sea gas.
- **Coal** has almost vanished: the last deep coal mine (Kellingley, Yorkshire) closed in **2015**, and the last coal-fired power station, **Ratcliffe-on-Soar**, closed on **30 September 2024**.
- **Renewables** have grown fast. In **2024** renewables generated **about half** of the UK's electricity for the first time, with **wind** alone providing around **29%**; fossil fuels (almost all gas) fell to about 31%. Nuclear supplies much of the rest.
- **But** electricity is only part of energy use. Most **transport** (petrol, diesel) and most home **heating** (gas boilers) still use fossil fuels, so fossil fuels still supply most of the UK's **total** energy. The UK is legally committed to **net zero** greenhouse gas emissions by 2050.

**2. Reduced domestic supplies of coal, gas and oil**

- **North Sea oil and gas** production peaked around **1999–2000** and has declined since as fields run out. The UK became a **net importer** of energy in 2004.
- The UK now imports gas by pipeline (mainly from **Norway**) and as liquefied natural gas (LNG) by ship (for example from **Qatar** and the **USA**); coal was imported from countries such as Russia until 2022.
- This raises **energy security** concerns: when Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, world gas prices soared and UK household energy bills rose sharply, even though the UK buys little Russian gas directly.

**3. Economic and environmental issues of exploiting energy sources**

| Source | Economic issues | Environmental issues |
|---|---|---|
| **Fossil fuels (gas, oil, coal)** | reliable and flexible; jobs in the North Sea industry around Aberdeen; but remaining fields are harder and costlier to reach, and prices swing with world events | burning releases carbon dioxide (climate change); oil spills; coal mining scars landscapes |
| **Fracking (shale gas)** | could have added domestic gas and jobs; but test drilling was small and uncertain | uses large volumes of water that can be contaminated; small earthquakes — drilling by Cuadrilla at **Preston New Road, Lancashire** caused a magnitude 2.9 tremor in August 2019, and a moratorium on fracking in England followed in November 2019; it is still a fossil fuel |
| **Nuclear** | reliable, low-carbon baseload power; but extremely expensive and slow to build — **Hinkley Point C** in Somerset has seen its costs and completion date rise repeatedly | radioactive waste must be stored safely for thousands of years; risk of accidents; uses coastal sites |
| **Wind (onshore and offshore)** | cheap once built; jobs in manufacturing and maintenance (e.g. turbine blades made in Hull); the **Hornsea** and **Dogger Bank** wind farms in the North Sea are among the world's largest; but output varies with the wind, so back-up or storage is needed | no emissions when running; but onshore turbines are seen as spoiling views and are noisy; offshore farms can affect seabirds and seabed habitats |
| **Solar** | falling costs; homeowners can cut bills | large solar farms use farmland; output low in winter |

**The big picture**: the UK faces an "energy trilemma" — keeping energy **secure**, **affordable** and **sustainable** (low-carbon) at the same time. Renewables help sustainability and, in the long run, security, but need big investment in the grid and storage.

::: terms
- **Resource**: a stock or supply of something that has value or a purpose, such as food, water or energy.
- **Food miles**: the distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is eaten.
- **Carbon footprint**: the total amount of greenhouse gases, measured as carbon dioxide, released by a person, product or activity.
- **Local sourcing**: buying food and other produce from nearby producers to cut transport distances and support the local economy.
- **Agribusiness**: large-scale, intensive, industrial farming run as a business, often linked to food processors and supermarkets.
- **Organic produce**: food grown without artificial chemical fertilisers or pesticides.
- **Water surplus**: where the supply of water is greater than the demand for it.
- **Water deficit**: where the demand for water is greater than the supply.
- **Water stress**: when demand for water exceeds the available supply during a period, or poor quality restricts its use.
- **Water transfer**: moving water from an area of surplus to an area of deficit by pipelines, tunnels, aqueducts or rivers.
- **Energy mix**: the combination of different energy sources a country uses to meet its needs.
- **Energy security**: having reliable, affordable access to enough energy to meet a country's needs.
- **Renewable energy**: energy from sources that will not run out, such as wind, solar, tidal and hydroelectric power.
:::

::: method
**What the questions look like.** This topic is Paper 2, Section C, question 3 — compulsory. Expect short questions on a figure (a map of UK rainfall and population, a pie chart of the energy mix, a graph of food imports) and one levels-marked question of 6 marks.

**Command words:**
- **State / identify / give** — one fact, no explanation. One word or phrase per mark.
- **Describe** — say what is there or what a figure shows. For a figure: overall trend or pattern → figures from the resource with units → an anomaly or change in trend.
- **Explain** — give reasons. Use "because", "this leads to", "which means".
- **Suggest** — apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar situation; any sensible, geographical reason earns credit.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** — weigh up both sides and reach a judgement.
- **Use Figure X** — you **must** quote data or details from the figure, or you cap your marks.

**Building a 4-mark answer**: two developed points (point + develop) or one point developed into a chain. "Demand for water is rising because population is growing (1), especially in the south-east (1), which is already an area of deficit (1), so more water must be transferred or abstracted from rivers (1)."

**Building a 6-mark levels answer** (Level 1: 1–2, Level 2: 3–4, Level 3: 5–6):
1. Make two or three clear points that answer the exact question.
2. **Develop** each one — why, how, with what effect.
3. **Use named UK examples** — Kielder, Elan Valley, Preston New Road, Ratcliffe-on-Soar, Kenyan green beans. Specific places lift you into Level 3.
4. If the command is "assess" or "evaluate", finish with a **judgement** that weighs the points ("The biggest challenge is… because…").

**Building a 9-mark answer** (this topic is usually tested at 6 marks, but the food/water/energy option questions that follow can be 9 marks with +3 SPaG): Level 3 (7–9) needs thorough, well-developed points, detailed place-specific evidence and a clear, justified judgement that answers "to what extent". Use specialist terms throughout for the SPaG marks.

**Tips**: always say **which** resource you mean; separate **economic** and **environmental** issues when asked; for water, always link to the **north-west (surplus) vs south-east (deficit)** pattern.
:::

::: example Describe a UK water pattern
Figure 1 shows average annual rainfall and population density in the UK. Use Figure 1 to describe the relationship between rainfall and population density. (3)

There is a negative relationship: areas with high rainfall tend to have low population density (1). For example, north-west Scotland and the Lake District receive over 2,000 mm a year but have few people per square kilometre (1), while London and the south-east receive around 600 mm but have the highest population densities (1), so most people live where least rain falls.
:::

::: example Explain a trend in food
Explain why the UK imports high-value food from low income countries. (4)

UK consumers now expect seasonal food, such as green beans and strawberries, to be available all year round (1), but these cannot be grown in the UK in winter without expensive heated greenhouses (1). Countries such as Kenya have a warm climate and a long growing season (1), and lower labour costs, so supermarkets can buy there cheaply and fly the produce to the UK, meeting demand that UK farmers cannot supply (1).
:::

::: example 6-mark assess — energy
Assess the economic and environmental issues of exploiting one energy source in the UK. (6)

Offshore wind has major economic advantages. Once turbines are built the "fuel" is free, so electricity from wind farms such as Hornsea off the Yorkshire coast is now among the cheapest to generate, and the industry has created jobs in ports such as Hull, where turbine blades are made. It also improves energy security because the UK is no longer relying on imported gas, whose price soared after Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022.

However, there are economic problems. Wind output varies — on calm days turbines generate little — so the UK still needs back-up power stations or expensive battery storage, and building new grid connections from the North Sea costs billions.

Environmentally, wind produces no carbon dioxide when running, which has helped renewables reach about half of UK electricity in 2024 and supports the net zero target. On the other hand, turbines can kill seabirds and building foundations disturbs seabed habitats.

Overall the environmental benefits of cutting emissions far outweigh the local impacts, and the main issue is economic: paying for the storage and grid needed to cope with calm days.

(Level 3: both economic and environmental issues developed, named UK examples, a judgement.)
:::

::: example Explain water transfer
Explain why water transfer schemes are needed in the UK. (4)

Most rain falls in the north and west, for example the Lake District and Wales, where population density is low, giving a surplus (1). Most people live in the south and east, where rainfall is only around 600 mm a year, giving a deficit (1). As the population of the south-east grows, demand will rise further, so supply cannot meet demand locally (1). Transfers such as the Elan Valley aqueduct, which carries Welsh water to Birmingham, move water from surplus to deficit areas to maintain supplies (1).
:::

::: q
To what extent is local sourcing of food better for the environment than importing food? (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): a balanced, developed answer with a clear judgement. Level 2 (3–4): some developed points, perhaps one-sided. Level 1 (1–2): simple points.
Indicative content: local sourcing cuts food miles and air freight, so carbon emissions from transport fall; less packaging and refrigeration; supports local farmers and seasonal eating. ::: watch
- Mixing up **deficit** and **surplus** — deficit means demand is **greater** than supply. The south-east is the deficit area.
- Saying "the UK gets most of its energy from renewables" — renewables are about half of **electricity** (2024), not of total energy; transport and heating still mostly use fossil fuels.
- "Use Figure 1" questions answered without quoting any figures from the resource — this caps the marks.
- Treating food miles as the whole carbon footprint — examiners reward the point that greenhouse-grown UK food can have a bigger footprint than imported food.
- Listing economic and environmental issues without saying which is which, or without naming a UK place or scheme.
- Writing about water, food and energy in general with no **UK** focus — this part of the spec is about the UK.
:::
