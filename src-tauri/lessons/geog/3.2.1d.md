## What it is

This topic finishes the UK city case study. The specification asks for three things: **an example of an urban regeneration project** (why the area needed it, and its main features), **the features of sustainable urban living** (water and energy conservation, waste recycling, green space), and **how urban transport strategies reduce traffic congestion**. Bristol supplies the regeneration and transport examples; Freiburg in Germany is the classic model of sustainable urban living.

### Urban regeneration: Bristol Temple Quarter

**Urban regeneration** means the long-term improvement of a run-down urban area — its economy, environment and social conditions — usually on **brownfield** land (previously built on). It is more than rebuilding: the aim is new jobs, homes and a better image so that private investment keeps coming.

**Where it is.** Temple Quarter is the area around **Bristol Temple Meads** station, on the eastern edge of the city centre beside the Floating Harbour and the River Avon. It is the main gateway into Bristol for rail passengers, including those from London.

**Why the area needed regeneration**

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Dereliction** | The land was dominated by old railway goods yards, a diesel depot and a large **Royal Mail sorting office that had stood derelict since 1997**. |
| **Brownfield, contaminated land** | A century of railway and industrial use left polluted ground that developers avoided because clean-up was costly. |
| **Poor image as a gateway** | Visitors arriving at Temple Meads walked out past a busy roundabout, car parks and empty buildings — a poor first impression of the city. |
| **Poor connections** | Busy roads and the river cut the station off from the city centre and from deprived neighbouring districts to the east, such as Lawrence Hill and Barton Hill. |
| **Under-used land near the centre** | Prime land a few minutes' walk from the centre was producing almost no jobs while the city was short of offices and homes. |

**Main features of the project**

- **Enterprise Zone (2012).** Announced in the 2011 Budget, the **Temple Quarter Enterprise Zone** covers **70 hectares**. Firms that locate there get business-rate discounts, simpler planning and superfast broadband. Over its 25-year life it was expected to attract **over 17,000 jobs**.
- **Engine Shed (opened December 2013).** Part of **Brunel's original station building** was converted into a business hub run with the University of Bristol, hosting start-up incubators such as SETsquared. It re-uses a historic building rather than demolishing it.
- **University of Bristol Temple Quarter Enterprise Campus.** Built on the site of the derelict sorting office, the flagship **38,000 square metre** academic building opened in **September 2026**, bringing around **4,600 students and 650 staff** into the area, with a new station entrance opening straight onto the campus.
- **Temple Meads station upgrade.** Around **£95 million** of first-phase public money funds the station's first major improvements in about a century, including **new entrances**, a public square in place of the old road layout, and better bus, walking and cycling links.
- **Brock's Bridge (2015)** links the station area to the former arena site ("Temple Island") across the river. Plans for an arena there were later dropped in favour of mixed-use development.
- **The wider programme.** The Temple Quarter and St Philip's Marsh regeneration now covers more than **130 hectares** of brownfield land over 25 years, aiming for **up to 10,000 new homes and up to 22,000 jobs**.

**Evaluation.** Successes: derelict land re-used; high-value digital and creative jobs; heritage buildings saved; a better gateway. Criticisms: much of the new housing has been purpose-built student accommodation rather than affordable family homes; the jobs created are mostly skilled and may not go to people from nearby deprived wards; progress has been slow — the full programme runs to the late 2030s and beyond.

### Sustainable urban living

A **sustainable city** meets the needs of people today without damaging the environment or reducing the ability of future generations to meet their needs. The specification lists three features.

| Feature | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|
| **Water conservation** | Rainwater harvesting (collecting roof water for toilets and gardens); green roofs and permeable surfaces that let water soak in; swales (grassy channels) instead of drains; low-flush toilets and water meters so people pay for what they use. |
| **Energy conservation** | Well-insulated **passive houses** that need almost no heating; solar panels; combined heat and power (CHP) district heating; renewable-powered public transport; homes built close together and close to jobs so less travel is needed. |
| **Waste recycling** | Separate collection of paper, glass, metals and plastics; food and garden waste composted or used in anaerobic digestion to make biogas; residual waste burnt to generate electricity instead of going to landfill. |
| **Creating green space** | Parks, green corridors and urban forests cool the city, absorb rainwater (reducing flood risk), store carbon, support wildlife and improve mental health; allotments give space for local food. |

### Case example: Freiburg, Germany

Freiburg (population around 230,000) in south-west Germany on the edge of the Black Forest is often called Germany's "green city".

- **Green space**: over **40% of the municipal area is forest** — Germany's largest communal forest — and around two-thirds of the land is in green uses.
- **Transport and energy**: over **400 km of cycle paths**; about **70% of residents live within 500 m of a tram stop**; since **2009 the trams have run on 100% renewable electricity**.
- **Waste**: waste levels have been cut by almost **two-thirds since 1988** through waste minimisation, recycling, anaerobic digestion of organic waste and burning residual waste for energy.
- **Water**: householders pay for storm-water drainage according to how much of their plot is sealed (roofs, paving), which rewards green roofs and permeable surfaces; rainwater is collected and allowed to soak into the ground.

**Vauban district.** A former French army barracks (the army left in 1992) was rebuilt from 1998 as a sustainable district of about **5,300 people on 38 hectares**.

- Streets are **"parking-free"**: residents who own a car must buy a space in a garage on the edge of the district (around **€17,500** in 2006), so most households have chosen to live **without a car**. Most journeys are made on foot, by bike or by the **tram** that runs through the district.
- Around **100 homes were built to Passivhaus standard**, and a **wood-chip combined heat and power station** supplies district heating.
- The **Solar Settlement**, designed by architect Rolf Disch, has **59 "Plus-Energy" homes** whose rooftop solar panels produce more energy than the homes use; the surplus is sold to the grid.
- Rainwater is collected in open channels and swales and green spaces sit between the housing blocks for children to play safely off the streets.

### Urban transport strategies to reduce congestion

**Traffic congestion** happens when there are too many vehicles for the road space, so journeys slow down. Its costs: lost working time, higher fuel use, air pollution (nitrogen dioxide and particulates, linked to asthma), carbon emissions and stress. Strategies either **make public and active transport more attractive** ("carrot") or **make driving less attractive** ("stick").

**Bristol**

- **MetroBus**: a bus rapid transit network with **dedicated bus lanes and bus-only roads** (about **50 km**), off-bus ticketing and real-time information, opened in stages from **May 2018** at a cost of about **£230 million**. Faster, more reliable buses tempt drivers out of cars.
- **Park and ride**: car parks on the edge of the city with frequent buses into the centre, so cars stay out of the narrow central streets.
- **Cycling**: Bristol was named England's first **Cycling City in 2008**, funding cycle lanes, parking and training; the Bristol and Bath Railway Path is a traffic-free commuter route on an old railway line.
- **Clean Air Zone (November 2022)**: the most polluting vehicles are charged to enter the central zone, discouraging older diesel vehicles.
- **Residents' parking zones** stop commuters parking free in inner suburbs.

**London**

- **Congestion Charge (February 2003)**: drivers pay a daily charge to enter central London on weekdays. It began at **£5** and rose to **£18 in January 2026**. In its first year traffic in the zone fell by about **15%** and bus use in central London rose sharply; the money raised is spent on public transport.
- **Ultra Low Emission Zone**: started in central London in 2019 and was extended to all London boroughs in August 2023.
- **Integrated public transport**: Oyster and contactless payment across Tube, bus, rail and tram; the **Elizabeth line** (opened May 2022) added a high-capacity east-west route; **Santander Cycles** hire bikes.

**Judging strategies.** Charging schemes work quickly but are unpopular and can hit poorer drivers and small businesses hardest; new public transport is popular but expensive and slow to build; cycling schemes are cheap but depend on safe, connected routes. Most cities combine several ("integrated transport").

::: terms
- **Urban regeneration**: the long-term investment in and improvement of a run-down urban area's economy, environment and social conditions.
- **Brownfield site**: land that has been built on before, often derelict or contaminated, and is available for redevelopment.
- **Dereliction**: the state of buildings and land that have been abandoned and left to fall into disrepair.
- **Enterprise Zone**: an area where the government offers business-rate discounts, simpler planning and better infrastructure to attract firms and jobs.
- **Sustainable urban living**: living in a city in ways that meet present needs without damaging the environment or the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Rainwater harvesting**: collecting and storing rain from roofs so it can be used for toilets, washing and gardens instead of treated mains water.
- **Passive house**: a building so well insulated and airtight that it needs very little energy for heating or cooling.
- **Green space**: parks, gardens, woodland and other vegetated land in a city that cools the air, absorbs rainwater and supports wildlife.
- **Traffic congestion**: too many vehicles for the road space, causing slow journeys, pollution and lost working time.
- **Congestion charge**: a fee drivers pay to enter a busy central area, designed to reduce traffic and raise money for public transport.
- **Park and ride**: car parks on the edge of a city linked to the centre by frequent buses or trams, keeping cars out of central streets.
- **Integrated transport**: different types of public transport linked together by shared tickets, timetables and interchanges so journeys are easy without a car.
:::

::: method
1. **Read the command word.** *State/identify* (1 mark each) needs a fact. *Describe* needs what it is or what it looks like — no reasons. *Explain* needs **why** or **how**, using "because", "so", "which means". *Suggest* asks you to apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar place or figure. *Assess/evaluate/"to what extent"* needs both sides and a judgement.
2. **Regeneration questions have two halves.** The spec wants the **reasons** the area needed regeneration and the **main features** of the project. Keep them separate: reasons are problems (derelict sorting office, contaminated brownfield land, poor gateway); features are solutions (Enterprise Zone, Engine Shed, university campus, station upgrade).
3. **Sustainable living: link each feature to a benefit.** Do not just list "solar panels, recycling". Say what it saves or reduces: "solar panels on the Solar Settlement produce more electricity than the homes use, so less fossil-fuel power is needed and emissions fall".
4. **Transport: name the strategy, explain the mechanism, give evidence.** "London's Congestion Charge makes driving into the centre expensive (£18 a day), so some drivers switch to buses or the Tube; traffic fell by about 15% in the first year."
5. **4-mark answers** (levels: Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2): two developed points, or one point developed several times, with a place detail.
6. **6-mark answers** (Level 3 = 5–6, Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2): three developed points, each **point → explain → link to the named place**. The top level needs accurate case-study detail throughout.
7. **9-mark answers** (Level 3 = 7–9, Level 2 = 4–6, Level 1 = 1–3): arguments on both sides, well developed and supported with case-study facts, ending in a clear **judgement** that answers the question ("to a large extent, because…"). Some 9-markers carry **+3 marks for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms** — write in full sentences and use the key terms.
8. **"Use Figure X"**: quote data or features from the figure (with units) before adding your own knowledge.
:::

::: example Reasons for regeneration
Explain why Temple Quarter in Bristol needed regeneration. (4)

The area contained large amounts of derelict land (1), such as a Royal Mail sorting office that had been empty since 1997 and old railway goods yards (1). Because the land was contaminated brownfield left from railway use, private developers avoided it as clean-up was costly (1), so prime land a few minutes from the city centre produced almost no jobs, and visitors arriving at Temple Meads saw a poor image of the city (1).
:::

::: example Sustainable urban living
Describe how one city has made urban living more sustainable. (4)

In Freiburg's Vauban district most streets are parking-free and residents must buy a garage space on the edge of the district, so most households live without a car (1) and travel by tram, bicycle or on foot (1). The Solar Settlement's 59 homes have solar panels that generate more energy than they use (1), and the wider city has cut its waste by nearly two-thirds since 1988 through recycling and composting (1).
:::

::: example Transport strategy — 6 marks
Explain how transport strategies have been used to reduce traffic congestion in a UK city. (6)

London introduced the Congestion Charge in 2003, making drivers pay to enter central London on weekdays. Because driving became more expensive, some people switched to buses and the Tube, and traffic in the zone fell by about 15% in the first year; the money raised was spent on improving buses, so the alternative became more attractive too.

London has also made public transport easier to use through integration. Oyster and contactless payment work across the Tube, buses and trains, so journeys that need several modes are simple and cheaper than driving. The Elizabeth line, opened in 2022, added a fast east-west route that takes pressure off roads and older Tube lines.

The Ultra Low Emission Zone, extended to all boroughs in 2023, charges the most polluting vehicles. This mainly targets air quality, but it also encourages people to replace old cars with public transport or cycling, which reduces vehicle numbers further.

(Level 3: three strategies, each with a clear mechanism and accurate London detail.)
:::

::: example Evaluate regeneration — 9 marks
"Urban regeneration projects bring more benefits than problems." Using an example, evaluate this statement. (9 + 3 SPaG)

Bristol's Temple Quarter shows clear benefits. Derelict brownfield land, including a sorting office empty since 1997, has been brought back into use, so the city grows without building on greenfield land at the rural-urban fringe. The Enterprise Zone offers business-rate discounts, and the Engine Shed, in Brunel's original station building, has helped create over 700 jobs through its start-up incubators in digital and creative industries. The University of Bristol's new campus, opened in 2026, brings about 4,600 students and 650 staff whose spending supports local cafés and shops, and the £95 million station upgrade gives visitors a far better first impression of the city.

However, there are problems. Much of the new housing is student accommodation rather than affordable homes for local families, and the skilled jobs in technology and research may not go to residents of nearby deprived areas such as Lawrence Hill, so inequality could widen. Rising land values can push up rents. The project is also slow: the full programme, aiming for up to 10,000 homes and 22,000 jobs, will take around 25 years, and parts of the plan, such as the arena, were abandoned.

Overall I largely agree with the statement. The environmental and economic benefits — re-using derelict land and creating thousands of high-value jobs next to the country's main rail links — are already visible, whereas the problems are about who benefits. These could be reduced if more affordable housing and local training schemes are included, so the benefits outweigh the problems provided the project is managed to include local people.

(Level 3: both sides developed with accurate detail and a supported judgement.)
:::

::: watch
- Mixing up **reasons for regeneration** (the problems) with **features of the project** (the solutions) — the spec asks for both separately.
- Listing sustainable features without saying **what each one saves or reduces**.
- Naming a transport strategy without explaining **how** it reduces congestion or giving **evidence** that it has worked.
- Vague case studies ("they built new offices") — use named features: Enterprise Zone, Engine Shed, the university campus, Temple Meads upgrade.
- No judgement at the end of a 9-marker, or a judgement that ignores the other side.
- Writing "Freiburg is car-free" — Vauban is car-*reduced*: cars are allowed but parking is only in edge-of-district garages.
:::
