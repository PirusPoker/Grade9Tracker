## What it is

This is the opening idea of Paper 1, Section A. Everything later in the section (earthquakes, volcanoes, tropical storms, UK weather, climate change) is an example of it, so the vocabulary here is used in almost every hazards answer.

### Definition of a natural hazard

A **natural hazard** is a natural event or process that has the **potential to threaten people's lives and property**. The key word is *people*: a huge eruption on an uninhabited island is a natural *event*, not a hazard. It only becomes a hazard when people or the things they value are exposed to it.

When a hazard actually causes serious loss of life, damage and disruption, beyond what the community can cope with on its own, it becomes a **disaster**. So:

- **natural event** → happens whether or not anyone is there (a volcano erupts);
- **natural hazard** → the event threatens people or property;
- **disaster** → the hazard has caused major damage and loss of life that the community cannot cope with using its own resources.

### Types of natural hazard

AQA expects you to group hazards by what causes them.

| Type | Caused by | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Tectonic (geological) hazards** | movement of the Earth's plates and processes in the crust | earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis (usually triggered by an undersea earthquake) |
| **Atmospheric (weather / climatic) hazards** | processes in the atmosphere | tropical storms, tornadoes, drought, heatwaves, extreme cold and snow |
| **Hydrological hazards** | water, often following weather events | river and coastal flooding, storm surges |
| **Geomorphological hazards** | movement of material on slopes | landslides, mudflows, avalanches |

Many hazards are **linked**: an earthquake can trigger landslides and a tsunami (both happened in Chile 2010 and Nepal 2015); a tropical storm brings high winds, a storm surge and flooding at once (Typhoon Haiyan, 2013). This is why exam questions talk about **primary** effects (caused directly by the hazard) and **secondary** effects (caused by the primary effects), which you meet in the next topics.

Hazards also differ in their **characteristics**, which affect how dangerous they are:

- **Magnitude**: the size or strength (magnitude 8.8 in Chile, Category 5 storm).
- **Frequency**: how often events of that size happen. Small earthquakes are very frequent; very large ones are rare.
- **Speed of onset**: an earthquake strikes in seconds with almost no warning; a tropical storm can be tracked for days; a drought builds over months.
- **Duration**: seconds for an earthquake, weeks for a flood, years for a drought.
- **Area affected** (spatial extent): a tornado is a few hundred metres wide; a drought can cover a whole region.
- **Predictability**: tropical storm tracks can be forecast; earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely.

### Hazard risk

**Hazard risk** is the probability, or chance, that a natural hazard will take place and affect people. Geographers often express it as a simple relationship:

**Risk = hazard × vulnerability ÷ capacity to cope**

- **Hazard**: how big, frequent and sudden the event is.
- **Vulnerability**: how exposed and susceptible people are, e.g. living in weak buildings, on a floodplain or near a fault line.
- **Capacity to cope**: the ability of a community to prepare for, respond to and recover from a hazard (money, emergency services, warning systems, insurance, education).

So risk rises when the hazard is bigger or more frequent, or people are more vulnerable, and falls when capacity to cope improves. The same magnitude of hazard can therefore cause very different effects in different places.

A classic comparison: in January 2010 a magnitude 7.0 earthquake in **Haiti**, one of the poorest countries in the Americas, killed well over 100,000 people (estimates vary widely). Six weeks later a far stronger **magnitude 8.8** earthquake in **Chile**, a much richer country with strict building codes, killed about 500 people. The hazard was bigger in Chile, but vulnerability was far higher and capacity to cope far lower in Haiti.

### Factors affecting hazard risk

The spec names the factors you should be able to explain. Learn them as a list with a *mechanism* and an *example* for each.

| Factor | How it increases risk | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Urbanisation** | Rapid growth of cities crowds more people and buildings into hazard zones, so one event affects more people. Rapid growth often means poorly built, unplanned housing. Hard, impermeable surfaces increase flood risk. | Kathmandu's crowded, poorly built districts in the Nepal earthquake 2015; Tokyo sits on a highly active plate boundary with over 35 million people in its metropolitan area. |
| **Poverty** | Poor people live in cheaper, higher-risk locations (steep slopes, floodplains) in weaker buildings, have less access to healthcare, education and insurance, and governments have less money for prediction, protection and emergency services. | Nepal 2015: many houses were built of stone or brick with mud mortar and collapsed. |
| **Farming** | Some of the most fertile land is in hazardous places, so people choose to live and work there. | Rich volcanic soils on the slopes of Mount Etna; fertile river floodplains and deltas such as in Bangladesh, which flood regularly. |
| **Climate change** | Rising temperatures may make some weather hazards more intense or more frequent: more intense tropical storms, heavier rainfall and flooding, longer droughts and heatwaves; rising sea levels increase coastal flooding and storm surges. | Low-lying coasts and small island states face higher storm-surge risk as sea level rises. |

Other factors examiners credit (useful for developing answers):

- **Level of development and governance**: richer countries can afford monitoring, earthquake-resistant buildings and trained emergency services; weak or corrupt governments may fail to enforce building codes.
- **Population density**: the more people per km², the more people exposed.
- **Education and awareness**: people who have practised drills (as in Japan) know what to do.
- **Time of day and season**: an earthquake at night catches people asleep indoors; one in daytime may hit crowded schools and offices.
- **Remoteness**: isolated mountain villages (as in Nepal) are hard to reach, so help arrives late.

### Why the risk is rising overall

Many geographers argue that global hazard risk is increasing, not because the Earth is more active but because **more people** are living in hazardous places: the world population keeps growing, cities are growing fastest in lower-income countries (many of them on coasts, floodplains or plate margins), and climate change is altering weather hazards. At the same time, better **prediction, warnings and building standards** have reduced the death toll from some hazards, especially in richer countries. A strong answer shows both sides.

::: terms
- **Natural hazard**: a natural event or process that has the potential to threaten people's lives and property.
- **Disaster**: a hazard that causes serious loss of life, damage and disruption that the community cannot cope with using its own resources.
- **Hazard risk**: the probability or chance that a natural hazard will take place and affect people.
- **Vulnerability**: how exposed and susceptible people and property are to damage from a hazard.
- **Capacity to cope**: the ability of a community to prepare for, respond to and recover from a hazard.
- **Tectonic hazard**: a hazard caused by movement of the Earth's plates, such as an earthquake, volcanic eruption or tsunami.
- **Atmospheric hazard**: a hazard caused by processes in the atmosphere, such as a tropical storm, drought or heatwave.
- **Urbanisation**: the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in towns and cities.
- **Magnitude**: the size or strength of a hazard event, such as an earthquake measured on the moment magnitude scale.
:::

::: method
**Know the command words.**
- **State / name / identify / define** (1–2 marks): a precise fact or definition. No explanation needed, but use geographical terms ("threaten people's lives and property", not "something bad in nature").
- **Describe** (2–4 marks): say what something is like or what a pattern shows. No "because".
- **Explain** (2–6 marks): give reasons. Every point needs a **because / so / this means that** chain.
- **Suggest** (2–4 marks): apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar situation, usually a photo or map. There is no single right answer: anything sensible and geographical earns credit.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** (6–9 marks): weigh up and reach a judgement.
- **Use Figure X**: you must quote from the figure (a place, a number, a feature) or you will be capped.

**How levels-of-response answers are built.**
- **4 marks** (two levels: 1–2, 3–4): two well-developed points, or one point developed into a full chain. "Poverty increases hazard risk (point) because people can only afford cheap homes made of weak materials (develop), so the buildings collapse in an earthquake, as happened in Nepal in 2015 (link to example)."
- **6 marks** (three levels: 1–2, 3–4, 5–6): two or three developed points, each linked to a real example, covering the full range of the question (if it says "factors", use more than one).
- **9 marks** (three levels: 1–3, 4–6, 7–9): developed points on **both sides**, detailed place-specific evidence, and a **supported judgement** at the end ("I agree to a large extent because…"). Some 9-markers carry **+3 marks for SPaG** (spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms), flagged on the paper, so write in full paragraphs.

**Tips for this topic.**
- For "factors affecting hazard risk", give the mechanism (why it increases risk), not just the factor's name.
- Use the risk relationship (hazard, vulnerability, capacity to cope) to structure longer answers.
- Pull in named examples from later topics: Chile and Nepal, Etna, Typhoon Haiyan, the Somerset Levels.
:::

::: example Define and classify
Define the term "natural hazard" and give one example of an atmospheric hazard. (2)

A natural event or process that has the potential to threaten people's lives and property (1). For example, a tropical storm (or drought, heatwave, tornado) (1).
:::

::: example Explain one factor
Explain how urbanisation can increase hazard risk. (4)

Urbanisation concentrates large numbers of people into cities (1), many of which are in hazardous locations such as coasts, floodplains and plate margins (1). In lower-income countries rapid growth often means informal, poorly built housing on the least safe land, such as steep slopes, so buildings are more likely to collapse or be swept away (1). For example, in the 2015 Nepal earthquake many crowded, poorly built buildings in Kathmandu collapsed, so more people were killed than in a rural area hit by the same shaking (1).
:::

::: example Assess the most important factor
"Poverty is the most important factor affecting hazard risk." Do you agree? (9)

Poverty is very important because it increases both vulnerability and lowers the capacity to cope. Poor households can only afford cheap, weak buildings: in the 2015 Nepal earthquake many homes were built of brick or stone with mud mortar and collapsed, contributing to about 9,000 deaths. Poor governments also have less money for monitoring, building codes and emergency services, so help is slower. The contrast between Haiti in January 2010 (magnitude 7.0, well over 100,000 deaths) and Chile in February 2010 (magnitude 8.8, about 500 deaths) shows that wealth can matter more than the size of the hazard.

However, other factors also matter. Urbanisation means that even rich places carry high risk because so many people are exposed: Tokyo is well prepared but a huge earthquake there would still affect millions. Farming draws people onto floodplains and volcanic slopes such as Mount Etna whatever their wealth, because the land is so fertile. Climate change is increasing the intensity of weather hazards, which raises risk for everyone, especially on low-lying coasts.

Overall I largely agree. Poverty is the most important factor because it makes every other factor worse: rapid urbanisation is most dangerous where people are poor, and poor communities have the least ability to adapt to climate change. Rich countries still face risk, but their capacity to cope keeps the death toll lower, as Chile shows.

(Level 3: both sides developed with named evidence and a justified judgement. Up to +3 SPaG if flagged.)
:::

::: q
What is the difference between a natural hazard and a natural disaster? (2)
---
A natural hazard is a natural event that has the potential to threaten people's lives and property (1); it becomes a disaster when it actually causes serious loss of life, damage and disruption that the community cannot cope with using its own resources (1).
:::

::: q
Name one tectonic hazard and one atmospheric hazard. (2)
---
Tectonic: earthquake / volcanic eruption / tsunami (1). Atmospheric: tropical storm / drought / heatwave / tornado / extreme cold (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how farming can increase hazard risk. (2)
---
Fertile soils are found in hazardous places, such as volcanic slopes or river floodplains (1), so farmers choose to live and work there and are exposed when the volcano erupts or the river floods, e.g. Mount Etna's vineyards and orchards (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how climate change might increase the risk from natural hazards. (4)
---
Warmer oceans could make tropical storms more intense, with stronger winds and heavier rain (1), increasing damage and flooding (1). Rising sea levels make coastal flooding and storm surges more likely (1), so low-lying coastal cities and islands face greater risk (1). Also credit: more frequent heatwaves or droughts; heavier rainfall causing river flooding.
:::

::: q
Suggest why two earthquakes of similar magnitude can cause very different numbers of deaths. (4)
---
Credit any developed points: differences in wealth, so quality of buildings and whether building codes are enforced (1+1); population density (a quake near a city versus a remote area) (1); time of day (people asleep indoors) (1); capacity to cope, e.g. speed and quality of emergency response and healthcare (1); depth of focus and distance from the epicentre (1). An example such as Haiti 2010 versus Chile 2010 can be credited.
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by "hazard risk", and describe two things that reduce it. (4)
---
Hazard risk is the probability that a natural hazard will take place and affect people (1). It is reduced by lowering vulnerability, e.g. building earthquake-resistant buildings or not building on floodplains (1+1 developed), and by increasing capacity to cope, e.g. warning systems, trained emergency services, insurance, drills (1+1 developed). Max 4.
:::

::: watch
- A hazard must threaten **people or property**. An event with nobody around is not a hazard.
- "Factors affecting hazard risk" answers that just list urbanisation, poverty, farming and climate change score Level 1. Each needs a **mechanism**: *why* it increases risk.
- Do not mix up **hazard risk** (the chance of being affected) with the **effects** of a hazard.
- Tsunamis are tectonic hazards, triggered usually by undersea earthquakes, not weather hazards.
- Always add a **named example**: Nepal, Chile, Haiti, Etna, Bangladesh, Tokyo. Unsupported points rarely reach the top level.
:::
