## What it is

**Development** is the process of a country improving the lives of its people. It has several strands: **economic** (wealth, jobs, industry), **social** (health, education, housing, access to water), **political** (stable, fair government) and **environmental** (managing resources sustainably). **Quality of life** is how well people's wants and needs are met — it includes things money cannot always buy, such as freedom, safety and a clean environment. The **development gap** is the difference in levels of development between the richest and poorest countries.

This topic covers four spec points: ways of **classifying** countries; the **economic and social measures** of development; their **limitations**; and the **link between the Demographic Transition Model and development**.

### Ways of classifying the world

| Classification | How it works | Strengths and weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **HIC / NEE / LIC** (the terms AQA uses) | **High-income countries** (e.g. UK, USA, Japan); **newly emerging economies** — countries whose economies are growing rapidly, often through industrialisation (e.g. Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, Mexico, Nigeria); **low-income countries** (e.g. Chad, Niger, Afghanistan). | Recognises the fast-changing middle group. But the boundaries are fuzzy — "NEE" is a judgement about growth, not a fixed number. |
| **World Bank income groups** | Four groups by **GNI per head**. In the classification used from July 2026 (based on 2025 data): **low income** up to about 1,175 US dollars; **lower-middle** about 1,176–4,635; **upper-middle** about 4,636–14,375; **high income** above 14,375. Thresholds are adjusted each year for inflation. | Clear, objective and updated annually. But uses one economic measure only and says nothing about health, education or inequality. |
| **HDI categories** (UN) | Countries grouped as very high (0.800 and above), high, medium and low (below 0.550) human development. | Includes health and education, not just wealth. |
| **The Brandt Line (1980)** | A line on the world map dividing the rich "global North" from the poorer "global South". | Simple, but now out of date: Singapore, South Korea and the Gulf states are rich but lie "south" of the line, while Australia and New Zealand were always placed above it despite being in the southern hemisphere. |

### Measures (indicators) of development

The spec names eight measures. An **economic** measure is about wealth; a **social** measure is about people's health, education and living conditions.

| Measure | Type | Definition | Typical HIC | Typical LIC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **GNI per head** | Economic | Gross national income (all income earned by a country's people and firms, including from abroad) divided by population, in US dollars | above 14,375 USD — the UK is around 50,000 | below 1,175 USD |
| **Birth rate** | Social | Live births per 1,000 people per year | about 8–12 | about 35–45 |
| **Death rate** | Social | Deaths per 1,000 people per year | about 8–11 (older populations) | about 8–12 |
| **Infant mortality** | Social | Deaths of babies under 1 year per 1,000 live births | under 5 | often 40–70 |
| **Life expectancy** | Social | Average number of years a person born now can expect to live | over 80 | often 55–65 |
| **People per doctor** | Social | Population divided by number of doctors | a few hundred | many thousands |
| **Literacy rate** | Social | Percentage of adults who can read and write | about 99% | in some countries under half |
| **Access to safe water** | Social | Percentage of people with safe drinking water | almost 100% | in some countries under two-thirds |

**Why these indicators link to development.** Higher income pays for hospitals, vaccinations and clean water, so **infant mortality falls and life expectancy rises**. As women become better educated and work outside the home, they marry later and have fewer children, so **the birth rate falls**. Wealthier governments can afford schools, so **literacy rises**. That is why these measures tend to move together.

**The Human Development Index (HDI)** is a **composite** measure created by the UN. It combines three things:

1. **Health** — life expectancy at birth.
2. **Education** — mean years of schooling for adults and expected years of schooling for children.
3. **Standard of living** — GNI per head (adjusted for purchasing power, i.e. what money actually buys locally).

It gives a score between 0 and 1. In the 2025 Human Development Report (2023 data): **Iceland ranked first (0.972)**, the **UK scored 0.946 (joint 13th)**, **Nigeria 0.560 (166th)** and **South Sudan was last of 193 countries (0.388)**.

### Limitations of the measures

| Measure | Limitation |
|---|---|
| **GNI per head** | An **average** — it hides huge **inequality** within a country (a rich elite can raise the average while most people are poor). Does not count the **informal economy** or subsistence farming, which are large in LICs. Converting currencies to US dollars ignores what money actually buys locally. Says nothing about health, freedom or environment. |
| **Birth rate** | Affected by **religion and culture** (e.g. attitudes to contraception) and government policy, not only development. |
| **Death rate** | Misleading on its own: HICs such as the UK have **ageing populations**, so their death rate can be **higher** than that of a poor country with a very young population. |
| **Infant mortality / life expectancy** | National averages hide big differences between rich and poor areas, urban and rural, and between regions. |
| **People per doctor** | Doctors cluster in cities, so rural people may have far worse access than the national figure suggests. Does not count nurses or traditional healers. |
| **Literacy rate** | Countries define "literate" differently, and data are often estimates. |
| **Access to safe water** | "Access" may mean a standpipe a long walk away. |
| **HDI** | Better because it is composite, but it is still made of **averages**; the data can be **out of date or inaccurate** in countries with poor record-keeping or conflict; it leaves out political freedom, inequality and environmental quality. |

**General limitations**: single indicators can mislead, so geographers use **a range of indicators** or a **composite** like HDI. Data may be unreliable where censuses are rare, and all national figures hide variation *within* countries. Oil-rich states can have a high GNI per head but a lower ranking on social measures than their wealth suggests, while a country like Cuba has a modest GNI per head but life expectancy close to many HICs.

### The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) and development

The **DTM** shows how the birth rate, death rate and total population of a country change over time as it develops. Picture a graph with time along the bottom: the birth-rate line starts high and falls later; the death-rate line starts high and falls earlier; the gap between them (natural increase) makes the population line rise steeply in the middle, then level off.

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Population | Why | Level of development / example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 High fluctuating** | High | High, fluctuating | Low, stable | No contraception; children needed for farm work; disease, famine and poor hygiene | No country today — only some remote tribes, e.g. in the Amazon rainforest |
| **2 Early expanding** | High | **Falling rapidly** | **Rising rapidly** | Better healthcare, vaccinations, clean water and food supply cut deaths; attitudes to family size have not yet changed | LICs, e.g. Niger, Afghanistan |
| **3 Late expanding** | **Falling** | Falling more slowly | Rising, but more slowly | Contraception, women's education and jobs, lower infant mortality (fewer children needed), urbanisation | NEEs, e.g. India, Kenya |
| **4 Low fluctuating** | Low | Low | High, stable | Family planning; children expensive; women have careers | HICs, e.g. UK, USA |
| **5 Declining** | **Very low** | Low (rising slightly as population ages) | Slowly falling | Ageing population; people choose small families or none | Some HICs, e.g. Japan, Germany, Italy |

**The link to development**: a country's position on the DTM reflects its development. **LICs** tend to be in **stage 2** (high birth rate, falling death rate); **NEEs** are in **stage 3** (birth rate falling as incomes and education improve); **HICs** are in **stages 4–5**. Development *causes* movement through the stages: improvements in healthcare and sanitation lower the death rate first; later, education (especially of women), access to contraception, urbanisation and falling infant mortality lower the birth rate.

**Limitations of the DTM**: it is based on the experience of Western Europe; LICs today are moving through the stages faster thanks to imported medicine; some countries are held in stage 2 or 3 by culture, religion or conflict; HIV/AIDS and war can raise death rates again; migration is ignored.

::: terms
- **Development**: the progress of a country in improving the economic, social, political and environmental wellbeing of its people.
- **Development gap**: the difference in standards of living and wellbeing between the world's richest and poorest countries.
- **Newly emerging economy (NEE)**: a country that has begun to experience rapid economic growth, usually through industrialisation, such as Brazil, India or China.
- **Gross national income (GNI) per head**: the total income earned by a country's people and businesses, including from abroad, divided by the population, usually in US dollars.
- **Infant mortality rate**: the number of babies who die before their first birthday per 1,000 live births in a year.
- **Life expectancy**: the average number of years a person born today can expect to live.
- **Human Development Index (HDI)**: a UN composite measure from 0 to 1 combining life expectancy, education and GNI per head.
- **Composite indicator**: a measure that combines several different indicators into one score, such as the HDI.
- **Demographic Transition Model (DTM)**: a model showing how birth rates, death rates and total population change as a country develops, in five stages.
- **Natural increase**: the birth rate minus the death rate, showing how fast a population grows without migration.
- **Quality of life**: how well people's wants and needs are met, including things such as freedom, safety and a clean environment.
:::

::: method
1. **Know which measures are economic and which are social.** Only GNI per head is purely economic; HDI mixes both. Questions often ask for "one economic and one social measure".
2. **Explain the link, not just the definition.** "Infant mortality is low in HICs *because* health care, vaccinations and clean water are widely available." The "because" earns the explain mark.
3. **Limitations = why the figure might mislead.** Averages hide inequality; death rates are distorted by ageing; data may be unreliable; single measures miss other aspects of development. Always say *what* the measure fails to show.
4. **DTM questions:** name the stage, describe the birth and death rates, then explain *why* using development factors (healthcare, education of women, contraception, urbanisation). Link each stage to LIC/NEE/HIC.
5. **"Use Figure X" (data tables, scatter graphs):** quote figures with units ("infant mortality of 4 per 1,000 live births"), compare (higher/lower, how many times), and spot anomalies — e.g. a country whose death rate is higher than expected because of an ageing population.
6. **4-mark answers** (Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2): two developed points. **6-mark answers** (Level 3 = 5–6): three developed points with examples or data. **9-mark answers** (Level 3 = 7–9): both sides (e.g. strengths and limitations of HDI) and a justified judgement; some 9-markers add **+3 SPaG**.
:::

::: example Define and classify
State two social measures of development. (2)

Any two: life expectancy (1); infant mortality (1); birth rate, death rate, people per doctor, literacy rate or access to safe water.
:::

::: example Limitation of GNI per head
Explain one limitation of GNI per head as a measure of development. (2)

It is an average, so it hides inequality within a country (1) — a small wealthy elite can raise GNI per head even though most people remain very poor, so it may make a country look more developed than most of its people's lives suggest (1).
:::

::: example Explain the DTM link — 4 marks
Explain why countries in stage 2 of the Demographic Transition Model tend to be low-income countries. (4)

In stage 2 the death rate falls quickly because basic healthcare, vaccinations and cleaner water are introduced (1), but the birth rate stays high (1). This is because in LICs children are still needed to work on farms and to support parents in old age, and infant mortality is still high enough that families have many children to ensure some survive (1). Contraception and education for women are limited, so a high birth rate continues and the population grows rapidly, as in Niger (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate HDI — 9 marks
"The Human Development Index is the best way to measure development." Do you agree? (9)

The HDI is better than any single indicator because it is a composite. It combines life expectancy, years of schooling and GNI per head, so it reflects health, education and wealth together. A single measure such as GNI per head only shows wealth and can hide poor health or education; the HDI would reveal that an oil-rich country with a high income has weaker social development than its wealth suggests. The HDI also allows easy comparison — on a 0 to 1 scale, Iceland scored 0.972 while South Sudan scored 0.388 in the 2025 report.

However, the HDI has limitations. It is still built from national averages, so it hides inequality between rich and poor, men and women, or urban and rural areas — Nigeria's HDI of 0.560 hides a large gap between the richer south and the poorer north. It ignores political freedom, human rights and environmental quality, which also affect quality of life. The data can be several years out of date and unreliable in countries with conflict or weak record-keeping.

Overall I largely agree: the HDI is the best single measure because it combines economic and social development. But it is not a complete picture, so it is best used alongside other indicators, such as inequality measures and access to safe water, to understand how development varies within a country.

(Level 3: balanced, accurate detail, supported judgement.)
:::

::: q
What is meant by the "development gap"? (1)
---
The difference in levels of development (standards of living, wealth, health) between the richest and poorest countries (1).
:::

::: q
Name the three components of the Human Development Index. (3)
---
Life expectancy / health (1); education — years of schooling (1); standard of living — GNI per head (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the death rate can be a misleading measure of development. (2)
---
HICs such as the UK have ageing populations with many elderly people (1), so their death rate can be as high as or higher than that of an LIC with a very young population, even though health care is much better (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the changes in birth rate and death rate in stage 3 of the Demographic Transition Model. (2)
---
The birth rate falls quickly (1); the death rate continues to fall but more slowly, so the population still grows but at a slower rate (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why the birth rate falls as a country develops. (4)
---
Any developed points, e.g.: women's education and employment increase, so they marry later and choose smaller families (1+1); contraception becomes widely available (1); infant mortality falls, so parents no longer need many children to ensure some survive (1); urbanisation means children cost more to raise and are no longer needed for farm work (1); pensions reduce reliance on children in old age (1). Max 4.
:::

::: q
Explain one limitation of the Brandt Line as a way of classifying the world. (2)
---
It is out of date (1): countries such as Singapore, South Korea and the Gulf states lie in the "South" but are now wealthy, so the line no longer separates rich from poor accurately (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why using several indicators gives a better picture of development than using one. (4)
---
Level 2 (3–4): developed explanation. Level 1 (1–2): basic points.
Indicative content: single indicators show only one aspect (GNI = wealth only); a country may score well on one and badly on another (high GNI but poor literacy or healthcare); averages hide inequality; combining economic and social measures (as in HDI) shows health, education and wealth; using several reduces the effect of inaccurate data for one measure.
:::

::: watch
- Calling birth rate or life expectancy an **economic** measure — they are **social**. Only GNI per head is purely economic.
- Defining a measure but never **explaining its limitation** — say what it fails to show (inequality, informal economy, ageing).
- Writing "HICs have low death rates" — ageing populations can give HICs *higher* death rates than LICs.
- DTM answers that describe the rates but do not say **why** they change using development factors.
- Forgetting units: infant mortality is **per 1,000 live births**; birth and death rates are **per 1,000 people per year**.
- Quoting HDI as a percentage — it is a score from **0 to 1**.
:::

"Classifying countries as HICs, NEEs and LICs is too simple to be useful." Do you agree? (9)
---
