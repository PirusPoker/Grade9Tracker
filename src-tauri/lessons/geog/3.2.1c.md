## What it is

This topic has two parts. First, an **overview of where people live in the UK** and where its **major cities** are. Second, **a case study of a major UK city** showing its location and importance, the impact of migration, and the **opportunities and challenges** that urban change has created. This lesson uses **Bristol**, the city in the standard AQA (Oxford) textbook. (Bristol's **Temple Quarter** regeneration, sustainable urban living and transport strategies are covered in the next lesson.)

### The distribution of population and major cities in the UK

The UK had about **69.3 million** people in mid-2024 (ONS), with an average density of roughly **280 people per km²** — but population is **very unevenly distributed**. Over four-fifths of people live in urban areas.

| **Densely populated** | **Sparsely populated** |
|---|---|
| **London and the south-east** — Greater London alone has almost **9 million** people | the **Scottish Highlands and Islands** — often under 10 people per km² |
| the **West Midlands** conurbation (Birmingham) | **mid-Wales** and the Welsh uplands |
| the **north-west** (Manchester, Liverpool) and **Yorkshire** (Leeds, Sheffield, Bradford) | the **Pennines**, the **Lake District** and the **North York Moors** |
| **Tyneside** (Newcastle), **South Wales** (Cardiff, Swansea) | upland south-west England (**Dartmoor**, **Exmoor**) |
| Scotland's **central belt** (Glasgow, Edinburgh); **Belfast** | much of rural Northern Ireland and East Anglia's farmland is fairly sparse |

**Why the pattern?**

- **Physical factors**: lowland areas with gentle relief, fertile soils and a milder climate attracted farming and settlement; **uplands** (high, steep, cold, wet, thin soils) are sparsely populated.
- **Historical and economic factors**: during the **Industrial Revolution** cities grew on the **coalfields** (Manchester, Leeds, Sheffield, Newcastle, Glasgow, South Wales) and at **ports** (Liverpool, Bristol, London). Today the **service economy** concentrates jobs — especially finance and government — in **London and the south-east**, which keeps attracting migrants.

**Major cities**: London (capital, by far the largest), then Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds, Glasgow, Liverpool, Sheffield, **Bristol**, Edinburgh, Newcastle, Cardiff and Belfast. Most major cities are in lowland England or at the coast.

### Bristol: location and importance

**Location**: Bristol is in **south-west England**, on the **River Avon** close to where it meets the **Severn Estuary**, about **170 km west of London**. The **M4** (east–west) and **M5** (north–south) motorways meet just north of the city, and it has two main railway stations, **Temple Meads** and **Bristol Parkway**, with fast links to London, Wales, the Midlands and the north.

**Size**: about **472,500** people in the city (2021 census) — the **largest city in south-west England** and one of England's **Core Cities**.

| Scale | Why Bristol is important |
|---|---|
| **In the UK** | the main regional centre for the south-west — shopping, hospitals, culture and two universities (**University of Bristol** and the **University of the West of England**); a major centre of **aerospace** at **Filton** (Airbus wing design, Rolls-Royce engines; Concorde was built there); home of **creative and media** industries such as the **BBC Natural History Unit** and **Aardman Animations**; high-tech and digital firms |
| **In the wider world** | a historic **port** — once one of Britain's leading trading ports, including a major role in the **transatlantic slave trade** in the 18th century (the statue of slave trader Edward Colston was pulled down in 2020); today **Avonmouth and Royal Portbury** docks handle cars and bulk goods; **Bristol Airport** carried about **10.5 million** passengers in 2024; aerospace companies export worldwide; it was the UK's first **European Green Capital** (2015) |

### Impacts of national and international migration

**National migration** (from elsewhere in the UK):

- Tens of thousands of **students** come to the two universities, and many stay after graduating — giving Bristol a relatively **young** population.
- People and businesses have moved from **London**, attracted by cheaper property (though still expensive), a good quality of life and fast rail links; remote working has increased this.

**International migration**:

- In the **1950s–60s**, migrants from the **Caribbean** (the Windrush generation) settled in inner-city areas such as **St Pauls**. The **Bristol Bus Boycott (1963)** against racial discrimination by the bus company helped lead to the UK's first race relations laws.
- From the **1990s**, a large **Somali** community settled, many fleeing civil war, especially in **Easton** and **Lawrence Hill**.
- After **2004**, when **Poland** and other eastern European countries joined the EU, many came for work.
- Other large groups include people with Indian, Pakistani, Chinese and Jamaican heritage.

**Effects on growth and character**:

- Migration has been a major cause of Bristol's **population growth** since 2000; in the 2021 census about **18.8%** of residents were born outside the UK.
- Bristol's people come from at least **187 countries**, speak at least **91 main languages** and follow at least **45 religions** (Bristol City Council).
- Migrants are concentrated in **inner-city east Bristol** (St Pauls, Easton, Lawrence Hill), shaping those areas' shops, restaurants, places of worship and festivals.
- Migration has kept the city's population **young**, providing workers for health care, transport, universities and services, but it has also increased demand for **housing and school places**.

### Opportunities created by urban change

**Social and economic**

- **Cultural mix**: the **St Pauls Carnival**, started in **1968** by the African-Caribbean community, is one of the UK's largest street carnivals; a huge range of food, music and festivals; Bristol's famous music scene and street art (Banksy's early work, the Upfest street-art festival) come from this mix.
- **Recreation and entertainment**: the regenerated **Harbourside** with the **M Shed** museum, the **SS Great Britain**, the Watershed and Arnolfini arts centres, and waterside bars and restaurants; **Cabot Circus** shopping centre (opened **2008**, cost about **£500 million**); the **Bristol International Balloon Fiesta** at Ashton Court; professional football and rugby at Ashton Gate.
- **Employment**: aerospace at Filton, finance, creative and digital industries, universities and health care; the **Temple Quarter Enterprise Zone** around Temple Meads station attracts new businesses. Unemployment in Bristol is generally **below the national average**.
- **Integrated transport systems**: linking different types of transport so people can easily switch between them —
  - **MetroBus** rapid bus routes (opened **2018**) using dedicated lanes, linking the centre with Cribbs Causeway, Emersons Green, Hengrove and a park-and-ride site near Long Ashton;
  - **park-and-ride** sites on the edge of the city;
  - rail links at Temple Meads and Parkway, with plans (**MetroWest**) to reopen local lines such as the one to **Portishead**;
  - cycle routes such as the **Bristol and Bath Railway Path**; Bristol was named the UK's first **Cycling City** in 2008;
  - a **Clean Air Zone** (from **2022**) charges the most polluting vehicles to enter the centre.

**Environmental — urban greening**

- **Urban greening** means increasing the amount of green space and vegetation in a city.
- Bristol has large green spaces such as the **Downs**, **Ashton Court** and **Brandon Hill**, and many local parks and nature reserves.
- Being named **European Green Capital 2015** brought funding and projects such as tree planting (the **One Tree Per Child** scheme), green roofs and wildlife corridors along rivers and railways.
- Green spaces improve air quality, reduce flood risk by soaking up rain, cool the city in summer and improve people's physical and mental health.

### Challenges created by urban change

**Social and economic — urban deprivation and inequality**

**Urban deprivation** is when people have a low standard of living and lack access to good housing, jobs, services and a healthy environment. Bristol is a prosperous city overall, but it has some of the **most deprived** areas in England. The textbook comparison is **Filwood** (south Bristol) and **Stoke Bishop** (north-west Bristol):

| | **Filwood** (south) | **Stoke Bishop** (north-west) |
|---|---|---|
| **Deprivation** | among the **most deprived 10%** of areas in England | one of the most **affluent** areas in the country |
| **Housing** | large **1930s council estate**, many homes poorly insulated | large detached and semi-detached private houses, high house prices |
| **Education** | much lower GCSE results and few young people going to university | very high GCSE results; many graduates |
| **Health** | lower life expectancy — textbook figures about **78** years | higher life expectancy — about **83** years |
| **Employment and crime** | higher unemployment, especially among young people; much more crime | low unemployment; little crime |

Across the whole city, Bristol City Council reports that **men in the most deprived areas live about 10 years less** than men in the least deprived areas.

- **Housing inequality**: rapid population growth and demand from students and people moving from London have pushed up **house prices and rents**, making housing unaffordable for many; homelessness and overcrowding are problems, while wealthier areas have large, spacious homes.
- **Education inequality**: attainment is much lower in deprived parts of south and east Bristol, and fewer young people go on to university, limiting their job prospects.
- **Health inequality**: poorer diets, more smoking, damp housing and stress in deprived areas mean higher rates of illness and lower life expectancy.
- **Employment inequality**: well-paid jobs in aerospace, finance and tech often need qualifications that people in deprived areas lack, so unemployment and low pay are concentrated there.

**Environmental**

- **Dereliction**: when shipping moved downstream to Avonmouth and Royal Portbury, Bristol's **city-centre docks** declined, leaving empty warehouses and polluted land (now largely regenerated as Harbourside). Derelict buildings remain in places such as **Stokes Croft**, and old railway land around **Temple Meads** lay unused for years.
- **Building on brownfield and greenfield sites**: Bristol needs thousands of new homes.

| | **Brownfield** (previously developed land) | **Greenfield** (land never built on, usually farmland) |
|---|---|---|
| **Bristol examples** | **Harbourside** (Canon's Marsh), **Temple Quarter**, Wapping Wharf; the former **Filton airfield** (closed 2012), now being redeveloped | **Bradley Stoke** (building began 1987, about 8,000 homes — one of Europe's largest private housing developments), **Harry Stoke**, **Lyde Green** on the northern and eastern edge |
| **Advantages** | reuses derelict land, regenerates run-down areas, close to jobs and public transport, protects countryside | cheaper and quicker to build on; no clean-up needed; space for gardens and larger homes; good road access |
| **Disadvantages** | expensive to clear and decontaminate; sites can be small and awkward; often high-density flats | loses farmland and wildlife habitats; creates urban sprawl and car dependence; more traffic and pollution |

- **Waste disposal**: a growing population produces more waste. Bristol recycles about **45%** of household waste (2023/24) — the highest rate of England's core cities since 2014 — and much of the rest goes to **energy-from-waste** plants at Avonmouth instead of landfill. Fly-tipping and contamination of recycling remain problems.

**The impact of urban sprawl on the rural–urban fringe**

**Urban sprawl** is the uncontrolled outward growth of a city into the surrounding countryside. The **rural–urban fringe** is the zone at the edge of the city where urban and rural land uses mix.

- To the **north** of Bristol, near the M4/M5 junction, farmland has been built over for **Bradley Stoke**, **Cribbs Causeway** (The Mall out-of-town shopping centre, opened 1998), the **Aztec West** business park and new estates at Harry Stoke; to the **east**, **Emersons Green** and **Lyde Green**.
- **Impacts**: loss of farmland and wildlife habitats; more traffic, noise and air pollution on the M4, M5 and M32; pressure on the **green belt** that is meant to stop sprawl; out-of-town shopping draws trade away from the city centre; but new jobs, homes and services are created.

**The growth of commuter settlements**

**Commuter settlements** are towns and villages where many residents live but travel to work in the city. Around Bristol they include **Portishead**, **Keynsham**, **Nailsea**, **Yate** and **Thornbury**.

- **Portishead** grew rapidly after its former docks and power station site were redeveloped with thousands of new homes and a marina; with no passenger railway, commuters drive into Bristol via the **A369** and the **M5**, causing serious congestion — the reason for the planned reopening of the Portishead rail line.
- **Impacts on the settlements**: house prices rise, so young local people cannot afford to stay; villages become quiet in the daytime ("dormitory" settlements); some local shops, pubs and bus services close because residents shop and socialise in Bristol, while others gain customers; traffic and pressure on schools increase; the traditional village character changes.

::: terms
- **Core City**: one of England's largest regional cities outside London, including Bristol, Birmingham, Manchester and Leeds.
- **Integrated transport system**: linking different types of transport, such as bus, rail and cycling, so journeys are easy and switching between them is simple.
- **Urban greening**: increasing and protecting green spaces and vegetation in a city, such as parks, trees and green roofs.
- **Urban deprivation**: a low standard of living in part of a city, with poor housing, low incomes, poor health and few opportunities.
- **Dereliction**: land and buildings that have been abandoned and left to decay.
- **Brownfield site**: land that has previously been built on and is available for redevelopment.
- **Greenfield site**: land that has not been built on before, usually farmland on the edge of a city.
- **Urban sprawl**: the unplanned outward spread of a city into the surrounding countryside.
- **Rural–urban fringe**: the zone at the edge of a city where urban and rural land uses meet and mix.
- **Commuter settlement**: a town or village where many residents live but travel daily to work in a nearby city.
:::

::: method
1. **UK overview questions** (often "Use Figure X", a population density map): describe the pattern with **named areas** (high: London and south-east, West Midlands, north-west, central belt; low: Highlands, mid-Wales, Pennines), then explain with physical (relief, climate) and human (industrial history, jobs) reasons.
2. **Location and importance**: give **location** (south-west England, River Avon, M4/M5) and then **importance in the UK and in the wider world**, each with specific facts (Filton aerospace, universities, port, airport, European Green Capital 2015).
3. **Migration**: separate **national** and **international** migration, and say how each has affected the **growth** (numbers) and the **character** (culture, diversity, age structure) of the city.
4. **Opportunities and challenges**: use the spec headings — cultural mix, recreation, employment, integrated transport, urban greening; deprivation and inequalities in housing, education, health and employment; dereliction, brownfield/greenfield, waste; urban sprawl and commuter settlements. Each point: **what → Bristol evidence → effect on people**.
5. **Command words**: *Describe* — facts only. *Explain* — reasons. *Evaluate / assess / to what extent* — weigh up both sides, then judge.
6. **Levels**: 4-mark — Level 2 (3–4) needs developed, case-specific points. 6-mark — Level 3 (5–6) needs detailed Bristol evidence throughout. **9-mark + 3 SPaG** (Paper 2 Section A) — Level 3 (7–9) needs thorough, developed evidence on both sides and a **clear, supported judgement**; generic answers with no named places cannot reach Level 3.
:::

::: example Describe — UK population distribution
Describe the distribution of population in the UK. (3)

Population is unevenly distributed (1). It is densest in south-east England, especially London with almost 9 million people, and in conurbations such as the West Midlands, the north-west and Scotland's central belt (1). It is sparse in upland areas such as the Scottish Highlands, mid-Wales and the Pennines (1).
:::

::: example Explain — migration and character
Explain how migration has affected the character of a UK city you have studied. (4)

In Bristol, Caribbean migrants settled in St Pauls in the 1950s and 60s (1), and in 1968 the community started the St Pauls Carnival, which is now one of the UK's largest street festivals (1). More recently, Somali and Polish migrants have settled in areas such as Easton and Lawrence Hill, opening shops, restaurants and places of worship (1), so the city now has residents from at least 187 countries speaking at least 91 languages, giving it a diverse, multicultural character (1).
:::

::: example Assess — inequality (6)
Assess the extent of social and economic inequality in a UK city you have studied. (6)

Bristol shows large inequalities. Filwood in south Bristol is in the most deprived 10% of areas in England, with a 1930s council estate of poorly insulated homes, while Stoke Bishop in the north-west is one of the most affluent areas in the country, with large private houses.

These housing differences are linked to health and education. Textbook figures suggest life expectancy is about 78 in Filwood compared with about 83 in Stoke Bishop, and the council reports that men in the most deprived parts of the city live about 10 years less than those in the least deprived. GCSE results and university entry are much lower in Filwood, so young people there have fewer chances of well-paid jobs in Bristol's aerospace and tech industries, and unemployment is higher.

Overall the inequality is severe: although Bristol is prosperous and has below-average unemployment, the benefits are unevenly shared, and the gap between areas like Filwood and Stoke Bishop affects every part of life, from housing to how long people live.

(Level 3: specific Bristol evidence, several dimensions of inequality linked together, judgement on extent.)
:::

::: example Explain — urban sprawl
Explain the impact of urban sprawl on the rural–urban fringe. (4)

Around Bristol, farmland on the northern fringe near the M4/M5 has been built over for Bradley Stoke and Cribbs Causeway (1), so habitats and farmland are lost and the countryside is pushed further away (1). More people living and shopping on the edge of the city increases car use, causing congestion and air pollution on roads such as the M5 and M32 (1); however, sprawl also creates new homes, jobs and shops that people need (1).
:::

::: watch
- Generic city answers — use **Bristol** facts: Filton, Temple Meads, St Pauls Carnival 1968, 187 countries and 91 languages, Filwood vs Stoke Bishop, Bradley Stoke, Portishead, European Green Capital 2015.
- Mixing up **brownfield** (previously built on) and **greenfield** (never built on).
- Describing migration without saying how it changed the city's **growth** and **character**.
- Treating the **rural–urban fringe** and **commuter settlements** as the same thing — sprawl is building **on** the fringe; commuter settlements are separate towns **beyond** it whose residents travel into the city.
- "Opportunity" and "challenge" answers with no **effect on people** — always finish the chain.
- Losing the **3 SPaG marks** on the 9-marker — spell place names and specialist terms correctly.
:::
