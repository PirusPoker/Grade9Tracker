## What it is

This lesson covers the first half of **3.1.1.3 Weather hazards**: the **general atmospheric circulation model** (pressure belts and surface winds), the **global distribution** of tropical storms and how it relates to that circulation, the **causes and sequence of formation** of a tropical storm, its **structure and features**, and **how climate change might affect** tropical storms' distribution, frequency and intensity. The effects of and responses to a named storm (Typhoon Haiyan) follow in the next lesson.

### Why the atmosphere circulates

The Sun heats the Earth **unequally**. At the **equator**, the Sun's rays hit the surface almost vertically, so energy is concentrated on a small area and the ground and air become very hot. Towards the **poles**, the rays arrive at a low angle and spread over a larger area, passing through more atmosphere, so it is much colder. The atmosphere constantly moves heat from the equator towards the poles to balance this. That movement, plus the Earth's spin, produces a pattern of **pressure belts** and **surface winds**.

Two rules underpin everything:

- **Rising air creates low pressure** at the surface. As it rises it cools, water vapour condenses, clouds form and it rains.
- **Sinking air creates high pressure** at the surface. As it sinks it warms, so clouds evaporate, giving clear skies and dry weather.
- **Wind** is air moving from **high pressure to low pressure** at the surface.

### The general atmospheric circulation model: three cells

Each hemisphere has **three cells** (loops of circulating air). Imagine a diagram of the Earth with the equator across the middle and three loops stacked from the equator to each pole, with arrows showing air rising, flowing towards the poles high up, sinking and returning along the surface.

| Latitude | Air movement | Surface pressure | Typical climate | Cell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0° (equator)** | warm air **rises** | **low pressure** (the Intertropical Convergence Zone, ITCZ) | hot, wet, heavy rain every day: tropical rainforests | Hadley cells rise here |
| **30° N and S** | cooler air **sinks** | **high pressure** (subtropical high) | clear skies, very little rain: **hot deserts** such as the Sahara | Hadley cell sinks; Ferrel cell sinks |
| **60° N and S** | air **rises** where warm and cold air meet | **low pressure** | wet and changeable: the UK's frequent **depressions** (low-pressure systems) | Ferrel and polar cells rise |
| **90° (poles)** | cold, dense air **sinks** | **high pressure** | cold and dry | polar cells sink |

- **Hadley cell** (0°–30°): the strongest and most important cell. Hot air rises at the equator, travels towards 30° high in the atmosphere, cools, sinks, and returns to the equator along the surface.
- **Ferrel cell** (30°–60°): the middle cell, driven by the other two, like a gear between them.
- **Polar cell** (60°–90°): cold air sinks at the poles and flows towards 60° along the surface.

### Surface winds

Surface winds blow **from high to low pressure**, but they do not blow straight north or south. Because the Earth spins, moving air is deflected: to the **right in the northern hemisphere** and to the **left in the southern hemisphere**. This is the **Coriolis effect**.

| Wind | Blows from → to | Direction (northern hemisphere) | Direction (southern hemisphere) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade winds** | 30° high → equatorial low | from the **north-east** | from the **south-east** |
| **Westerlies** (the prevailing westerlies) | 30° high → 60° low | from the **south-west** | from the north-west |
| **Polar easterlies** | polar high → 60° low | from the north-east | from the south-east |

This is why the **UK's prevailing wind is from the south-west**: we lie at about 50°–60° N, in the westerlies, and they bring mild, moist air from the Atlantic. Where the trade winds from both hemispheres meet near the equator they **converge**, forcing air upwards: this is the **ITCZ**.

The ITCZ is not fixed. It **moves north and south with the overhead sun** through the year: north of the equator in June to August, south of it in December to February. This seasonal shift is key to understanding when tropical storms form.

### Global distribution of tropical storms

A **tropical storm** is a very large, intense low-pressure weather system with sustained winds of at least **119 km/h (74 mph)**. The same kind of storm has different names in different oceans:

| Name | Where |
|---|---|
| **Hurricane** | North Atlantic (Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico, US east coast) and north-east Pacific (off Mexico) |
| **Typhoon** | north-west Pacific (the Philippines, Japan, China, Taiwan) |
| **Cyclone** | Indian Ocean (Bay of Bengal: Bangladesh, India, Myanmar; south-west Indian Ocean: Madagascar, Mozambique) and around northern Australia and the South Pacific |

**Describe the pattern** (as you would from a world map):
- Tropical storms form **over warm oceans** in the **tropics**, mostly **between about 5° and 30° north and south of the equator**.
- They form in the **late summer and autumn** of each hemisphere, when sea temperatures peak. The Atlantic hurricane season runs from **1 June to 30 November**.
- The **north-west Pacific** has the most storms each year.
- **None form on the equator** (within about 5°) and almost none in the **South Atlantic** or south-east Pacific.
- They generally travel **westwards** at first, then often curve **away from the equator** (north-west, then north-east in the northern hemisphere), and **die out over land or cooler water**.

### The relationship between tropical storms and atmospheric circulation

This is a spec point on its own, so be ready to explain it:

1. **Where they form**: storms begin in the **Hadley cell** zone near the **ITCZ**, where the **low pressure** and **rising air** encourage the huge upward movement of warm, moist air that a storm needs.
2. **When they form**: the ITCZ follows the overhead sun, so it moves over the warmest oceans in late summer. That is when and where sea temperatures pass the critical threshold.
3. **Why not on the equator**: the **Coriolis effect** is almost zero at the equator, so air flows straight into the low pressure and fills it rather than spinning. Storms need to be at least about 5° from the equator to spin.
4. **Why they move westwards**: the **trade winds** (north-easterly and south-easterly) steer them westwards across the oceans, which is why Atlantic hurricanes head towards the Caribbean and typhoons towards the Philippines.
5. **Why they curve and die**: as they drift away from the equator they reach the **westerlies**, which push them back eastwards; they then move over cooler water or land and lose their energy supply.
6. **Why not beyond 30°**: sinking air in the **subtropical high-pressure** belts, and cooler seas, stop storms forming there.

### Causes: the conditions needed

| Condition | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Ocean temperature at least 26.5–27 °C** | warm water evaporates rapidly, supplying the warm, moist air that is the storm's fuel |
| **Warm water to a depth of about 50–60 m** | provides enough energy; shallow warm layers are quickly churned up and cooled |
| **Location between about 5° and 30° from the equator** | enough Coriolis effect to make the storm spin, and warm enough seas |
| **Low wind shear** (winds at different heights blowing at similar speed and direction) | lets the tall storm grow vertically instead of being torn apart |
| **Moist air and an existing area of low pressure** | a cluster of thunderstorms gives the storm something to grow from |

### The sequence of formation and development

1. **Warm, moist air rises** from the sea surface in an area of low pressure. It is replaced by air drawn in from the surrounding area, which also warms and rises.
2. As the air rises it **cools and condenses**, forming towering **cumulonimbus clouds**. Condensation releases **latent heat**, which warms the air further, making it rise faster: this is the **energy that powers the storm**.
3. More air is sucked in at the surface, pressure falls further, and **winds strengthen**. Several thunderstorms merge into one organised system.
4. The **Coriolis effect** makes the system **spin** (anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, clockwise in the southern).
5. **Stages of growth**, by sustained wind speed: **tropical depression** (under 63 km/h), **tropical storm** (63–118 km/h), then **hurricane / typhoon / cyclone** (119 km/h or more).
6. Cool, dry air **sinks in the centre**, forming the calm **eye**.
7. The storm is **steered** across the ocean by the prevailing winds, gaining strength while over warm water.
8. When it reaches **land or cooler water** it loses its supply of warm, moist air; friction with the land also slows it. It weakens and **dissipates**, but it can still cause massive flooding inland from rainfall.

Storm strength is measured on the **Saffir–Simpson scale** from **Category 1** (119–153 km/h) to **Category 5** (252 km/h or more).

### Structure and features

Picture a cross-section through a storm: a wide spiral of cloud up to several hundred kilometres across, with a clear hole in the middle.

| Feature | What it is like |
|---|---|
| **Eye** | the centre, typically about 30–65 km across; **calm, clear skies, no rain**, very light winds; **sinking air**; **lowest pressure** (Typhoon Haiyan's centre fell to about 895 hPa, compared with normal sea-level pressure of about 1,013 hPa) |
| **Eyewall** | a ring of towering cumulonimbus clouds around the eye, up to about 12–15 km high; the **strongest winds**, **heaviest rain** and most intense **rising air** |
| **Rain bands** | spiralling bands of cloud and heavy rain outside the eyewall, separated by calmer gaps |
| **Outflow** | at the top, air spreads outwards, forming a canopy of thin, high cirrus cloud |

Moving through a storm from one side to the other you would experience: increasing wind and rain in the rain bands → extreme winds and torrential rain in the eyewall → a sudden calm with clear skies in the eye (dangerous, because people think it is over) → extreme winds again from the **opposite direction** as the other side of the eyewall arrives → rain bands and gradually easing weather.

Pressure is lowest in the eye and rises towards the edge; wind speed is highest in the eyewall. Very low pressure also lets the sea surface rise, which, combined with winds pushing water ashore, creates a **storm surge**.

### How climate change might affect tropical storms

Climate change is warming the oceans. The spec asks about three things:

- **Distribution**: as more of the ocean passes 26.5 °C, storms may form, or stay strong, **further from the equator** than today, threatening places that rarely see them now. The latitude at which storms reach peak strength has already shifted polewards in the north-west Pacific.
- **Frequency**: scientists (the IPCC) expect the **total number** of tropical storms worldwide may **stay about the same or even fall slightly**, but the **proportion of the most intense storms (Categories 4 and 5) is likely to increase**.
- **Intensity**: warmer seas give storms **more energy**, so **stronger winds**; warmer air holds more moisture, so storms bring **heavier rainfall** and more flooding; **rising sea levels** make **storm surges** reach further inland.

Be careful: the link is a **probability**, not a certainty. Individual storms cannot be blamed on climate change alone, and records of tropical storms before satellites (the 1970s) are incomplete, which makes long-term trends harder to prove. A top answer shows this uncertainty.

::: terms
- **General atmospheric circulation**: the global pattern of air movement in three cells in each hemisphere, which transfers heat from the equator to the poles.
- **Hadley cell**: the circulation cell between the equator and about 30°, where warm air rises at the equator and sinks at 30°.
- **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)**: the belt of low pressure near the equator where the trade winds meet and air rises.
- **Trade winds**: surface winds blowing from the subtropical high-pressure belts at 30° towards the equator.
- **Coriolis effect**: the deflection of moving air caused by the Earth's rotation, to the right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern.
- **Tropical storm**: an intense low-pressure weather system with sustained winds of at least 119 km/h, called a hurricane, typhoon or cyclone depending on location.
- **Eye**: the calm, clear centre of a tropical storm, where air sinks and pressure is lowest.
- **Eyewall**: the ring of tall cumulonimbus clouds around the eye, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
- **Latent heat**: heat released when water vapour condenses, which provides the energy that powers a tropical storm.
- **Storm surge**: a rise in sea level caused by low pressure and strong winds pushing water onto the coast.
:::

::: method
**Atmospheric circulation questions.**
- A labelled diagram of the three cells often appears as a "complete the figure" task: label the equator (low pressure, rising air), 30° (high pressure, sinking air), 60° (low pressure) and the poles (high pressure), and the surface winds.
- In "explain" answers, link pressure to **weather**: "air sinks at 30°, so it warms and clouds cannot form, which is why hot deserts are found there".

**Distribution questions.**
- **Describe**: latitude band (5–30° N and S), over warm oceans, named regions (the Caribbean, the Bay of Bengal, the north-west Pacific), anomalies (none on the equator or in the South Atlantic). Quote any figure given.
- **Explain**: use the conditions (27 °C sea, Coriolis, low wind shear) and the circulation (ITCZ, trade winds).

**Formation questions (4–6 marks).**
- Tell it as a **sequence** using connectives: *first… as a result… this causes… eventually*.
- Must include: warm sea (at least 26.5–27 °C), rising moist air and low pressure, condensation releasing **latent heat** (the energy source), Coriolis spin, the eye forming, dying over land or cooler water.
- 6-mark Level 3 answers are accurate, in the right order and use the key terms.

**Climate change (6–9 marks).**
- Cover **all three**: distribution, frequency, intensity. The spec names them.
- Explain **why** (warmer seas = more energy and more of the ocean above 26.5 °C) and show the **uncertainty** (frequency may not rise; records are short).
- A 9-mark judgement: "climate change is likely to make storms more intense rather than more frequent". Some 9-markers carry **+3 SPaG**.
:::

::: example Explain formation
Explain how tropical storms form. (6)

Tropical storms form over oceans where the sea is at least 26.5–27 °C, between about 5° and 30° from the equator. Warm, moist air rises rapidly from the sea surface, creating an area of very low pressure, and more air is drawn in to replace it (2). As the air rises it cools and condenses into towering cumulonimbus clouds. Condensation releases latent heat, which warms the air so it rises even faster; this is what powers the storm and makes the winds stronger (2). The Coriolis effect makes the system spin, and cool air sinking in the centre forms the calm eye. The storm is carried westwards by the trade winds, strengthening over warm water, until it reaches land or cooler water, where it loses its supply of warm, moist air and dies out (2).

(Level 3: complete, accurate sequence using key terms.)
:::

::: example Link to circulation
Explain the relationship between tropical storms and the general atmospheric circulation. (4)

Tropical storms form in the Hadley cell near the ITCZ, where the trade winds converge and air is rising, creating the low pressure a storm needs (1). The ITCZ moves with the overhead sun, so it lies over the warmest oceans in late summer and autumn, which is when storms form (1). The trade winds steer the storms westwards across the oceans (1), and when they drift away from the equator into the westerlies they are pushed east, over cooler water or land, and they weaken; they do not form in the subtropical high-pressure belt at 30°, where air is sinking (1).

::: example Evaluate climate change
"Climate change will make tropical storms more dangerous in the future." Do you agree? (9)

Climate change is warming the oceans, and warm water is the energy source of tropical storms. Warmer seas mean more evaporation and more latent heat released when water vapour condenses, so storms are likely to become more intense, with stronger winds. The IPCC expects the proportion of Category 4 and 5 storms to increase. Warmer air holds more moisture, so storms will bring heavier rainfall and more flooding, and rising sea levels will allow storm surges to reach further inland, as happened with Typhoon Haiyan's surge in the Philippines.

Distribution may also change. As a larger area of ocean passes 26.5 °C, storms may reach further from the tropics, affecting places with little experience of them and fewer defences. However, the total number of storms each year may not increase and may even fall slightly, so storms may not become more frequent. Records from before satellite observations began in the 1970s are incomplete, so it is hard to be sure how much storms have already changed, and the danger to people also depends on wealth, warnings and preparation, not just on the storm.

Overall I agree to a large extent. Even if storms do not become more frequent, the most dangerous ones are likely to become stronger and wetter, and sea-level rise makes storm surges worse, so the risk to coastal people will rise, especially in poorer countries that cannot afford defences.
:::

::: q
Explain why hot deserts are found at about 30° north and south of the equator. (3)
---
Air that rose at the equator travels towards the poles high in the atmosphere, cools and sinks at about 30° (1). Sinking air creates high pressure and warms as it descends (1), so clouds cannot form and there is very little rain, giving desert conditions such as the Sahara (1).
:::

::: q
Name the surface winds that blow towards the equator, and explain why they blow from the north-east in the northern hemisphere. (3)
---
Trade winds (1). They blow from high pressure at 30° to low pressure at the equator (1), and are deflected to the right by the Coriolis effect caused by the Earth's rotation, so they come from the north-east (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why tropical storms do not form within 5° of the equator. (2)
---
The Coriolis effect is too weak close to the equator (1), so the air does not spin and the low pressure fills up instead of developing into a rotating storm (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the conditions in the eye of a tropical storm. (3)
---
Calm, with very light winds (1); clear skies and no rain because air is sinking (1); the lowest atmospheric pressure in the storm (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how climate change might affect the intensity of tropical storms. (4)
---
Warmer oceans provide more energy through evaporation and the latent heat released by condensation (1), so storms can reach higher wind speeds and more may reach Categories 4 and 5 (1). Warmer air holds more moisture, so rainfall and flooding increase (1). Rising sea levels make storm surges bigger and reach further inland (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Pressure mix-ups**: rising air = **low** pressure (wet); sinking air = **high** pressure (dry). Reversing these ruins circulation answers.
- Formation answers must name the **energy source**: latent heat released by condensation. "Warm air rises and it spins" is Level 1.
- Give the sea temperature: **at least 26.5–27 °C**. "Warm water" alone is too vague.
- The eye is **calm and clear**. The **strongest winds** are in the **eyewall**, not the eye.
- Climate change answers must cover **distribution, frequency and intensity** separately, and should not claim storms will definitely become more frequent.
:::
