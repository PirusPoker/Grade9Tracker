## What it is

**Fieldwork** is **Section B of Paper 3**, worth **39 marks** (including 3 SPaG). You must carry out **two geographical enquiries**, each using **primary data** collected on fieldwork **outside the classroom and school grounds**, on at least two occasions. The two enquiries must be in **contrasting environments** and together show **physical and human geography**; at least one must show how **physical and human geography interact**.

The exam tests your understanding of the **enquiry process** in two ways:
1. questions using **fieldwork materials from an unfamiliar context** — someone else's data, methods and results; and
2. questions on **your own enquiries** — you will be asked to **write the titles** of your two enquiries in the answer booklet, then answer about them.

So you must **know your own two enquiries in detail**: the question, the location, the theory, every method, the results, the conclusion and the evaluation.

### The six stages of a geographical enquiry

| Stage | What the spec says you must understand |
|---|---|
| **1. Suitable question** | the factors in choosing a suitable question or **hypothesis**; the **theory or concept** behind it; appropriate **primary and secondary sources**, including **locations**; the **risks** of fieldwork and how to reduce them |
| **2. Selecting, measuring and recording data** | the difference between **primary and secondary data**; identifying and selecting appropriate **physical and human data**; measuring and recording using different **sampling methods**; **describing and justifying** data collection methods |
| **3. Processing and presenting data** | that a range of **visual, graphical and cartographic** methods exists; **selecting and accurately using** appropriate methods; **describing, explaining and adapting** presentation methods |
| **4. Describing, analysing and explaining data** | describing, analysing and explaining **results**; **linking data sets**; using appropriate **statistical techniques**; identifying **anomalies** |
| **5. Reaching conclusions** | drawing **evidenced conclusions** related to the original **aims** |
| **6. Evaluation** | **problems** with the data collection methods; **limitations** of the data; **other data** that would be useful; the **extent to which conclusions were reliable** |

### Stage 1 — choosing a suitable question

**A good enquiry question or hypothesis**:
- is **clear and focused** and can be **answered with data** you can actually collect (measurable);
- links to **geographical theory** in the spec — a model to test;
- is **manageable** in the time, with the equipment and at a **safe, accessible location** (permission, transport, distance);
- has enough **variation** in the site to show a pattern (for a river, sites far enough apart downstream).

A **hypothesis** is a statement that can be tested and proved or disproved: "River width increases with distance downstream." A **question** is open: "How do the channel characteristics of the river change downstream?"

**Theory behind enquiries** — examples:
- **Rivers**: the **Bradshaw model** predicts that downstream, **width, depth, velocity, discharge and cross-sectional area increase**, while **gradient, bedload particle size and angularity decrease** (attrition makes stones smaller and rounder).
- **Coasts**: **longshore drift** moves sediment along the beach in the direction of the prevailing wind, so sediment builds up against the updrift side of groynes and may get smaller along the beach.
- **Urban**: the **Burgess model** (concentric zones) predicts that land use, building age and density change with distance from the CBD; urban **regeneration** should improve **environmental quality** and **quality of life**.

**Sources**:
- **Primary** — data you collect yourself (measurements, counts, questionnaires, photos, sketches).
- **Secondary** — data collected by someone else: OS maps, census data (e.g. from the Office for National Statistics), Environment Agency river-level data, the Index of Multiple Deprivation, geology maps, historical photos, newspaper articles, Google Earth.

**Risk assessment** — identify each **risk**, how likely and serious it is, and how to **reduce** it:

| Environment | Risks | How they are reduced |
|---|---|---|
| **River** | drowning in deep or fast water; slipping on wet rocks; cold; waterborne disease (Weil's disease) from contaminated water | check the weather and river levels beforehand; never enter water above knee depth; work in groups with a teacher nearby; wear wellies or waders; cover cuts, use gloves and wash hands |
| **Beach** | incoming tides cutting you off; rockfalls from cliffs; slippery rocks; waves | check tide times; stay away from cliff bases; work in groups; stay on the beach above the waterline |
| **Urban** | traffic; getting lost; unfriendly members of the public; theft | work in groups; wear hi-vis; use pedestrian crossings; carry a map and phone; stay in public places; agree a meeting point and time |
| **Any** | bad weather; sunburn; allergies | suitable clothing, sun cream, water; teachers carry first-aid kit and medication details |

### Stage 2 — collecting data

**Sampling** — you cannot measure everything, so you take a **sample** that should be **representative**.

| Sampling method | How it works | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Random** | every point or person has an equal chance of being chosen, e.g. using random number tables | no bias from the researcher | may miss parts of the area by chance; sites may be inaccessible |
| **Systematic** | samples at **regular intervals** — every 5 m along a transect, every 10th person | easy; covers the whole area evenly | may miss or over-represent a pattern that repeats at that interval |
| **Stratified** | the area or population is divided into groups (**strata**) and each is sampled **in proportion** — e.g. equal numbers of men and women, or by land use | all groups represented fairly | needs prior knowledge of the proportions; more complicated |
| **Opportunistic (convenience)** | whoever or whatever is available — people willing to answer | quick and easy | very biased; not representative |

Sampling can be at **points**, along **lines** (transects) or within **areas** (quadrats).

**Physical data (river example)**:
- **Width** — a tape measure stretched from bank to bank at the water's edge.
- **Depth** — a metre rule at regular intervals across the channel (e.g. every 50 cm) → **mean depth** and a **cross-section**.
- **Velocity** — time a **float** (a dog biscuit or orange) over a measured distance (e.g. 10 m) and repeat three times; velocity = distance ÷ time. Or use a **flowmeter**, which is more accurate.
- **Cross-sectional area** = width × mean depth. **Discharge** = cross-sectional area × velocity (m³/s, cumecs).
- **Wetted perimeter** — a tape laid along the bed and banks from one water's edge to the other.
- **Bedload** — pick up (say) 10 stones at random across the channel; measure the long axis with **callipers**; judge roundness using the **Powers' roundness index**.
- **Gradient** — **clinometer** or **ranging poles** over a set distance.

**Physical data (beach example)**: beach profiles with **ranging poles and a clinometer** at breaks of slope; pebble size and shape; groyne heights on the updrift and downdrift sides to show sediment build-up; wave frequency (waves per minute).

**Human data (urban regeneration / quality of life)**:
- **Environmental quality survey (EQS)** — a **bipolar** scoring sheet (e.g. −3 to +3 or 1 to 5) for litter, graffiti, noise, building condition, greenery, traffic.
- **Questionnaires** — closed questions (easy to process) and open questions (richer opinions) about quality of life, safety, services.
- **Pedestrian and traffic counts** — for a set time (e.g. 5 minutes) at each site, at the same time of day.
- **Land-use mapping** — recording the function of each building on a base map.
- **Photographs and field sketches**, annotated; building age and condition surveys.

**Justifying a method** means explaining **why** it was **appropriate** for your question — how it helped you test the hypothesis, why it was accurate or practical. "We measured depth every 50 cm across the channel because the bed is uneven, so several readings gave an accurate mean depth to calculate cross-sectional area and test whether it increased downstream as the Bradshaw model predicts."

### Stage 3 — processing and presenting data

| Method | Best for |
|---|---|
| **Line graph** | continuous change over distance or time |
| **Bar chart / histogram** | comparing categories / frequency of grouped data |
| **Scattergraph with line of best fit** | testing a relationship between two variables (distance downstream vs velocity) |
| **Cross-section (scaled)** | river channel shape at each site; beach profiles |
| **Located bar charts or pie charts on a map** | showing how values change from place to place |
| **Radar (spider) diagram** | comparing EQS scores for several criteria at different sites |
| **Choropleth map** | data shaded by area, e.g. deprivation by ward |
| **Proportional symbols** | quantities at points — pedestrian counts |
| **Flow lines / desire lines** | movement — where shoppers come from |
| **Annotated photographs and field sketches** | showing features and conditions |

**Adapting** a method means improving it — adding a line of best fit to a scattergraph, placing bar charts on a map to show location, using a logarithmic scale for very varied data.

### Stage 4 — analysing and explaining

- **Describe** the results: the **pattern or trend**, with **figures** — "Width increased from 1.2 m at Site 1 to 6.8 m at Site 5."
- **Analyse**: use **statistics** — mean, median, mode, range, interquartile range; percentage change; a line of best fit to show the **correlation** (positive, negative, none). (Some schools also use Spearman's rank correlation.)
- **Link data sets**: does velocity rise with depth? Is EQS score higher where pedestrian counts are higher?
- **Explain** the results using **theory**: velocity increased downstream because the wetted perimeter is smaller relative to the channel's area, so there is less friction.
- **Identify anomalies** — results that do not fit the pattern — and **suggest reasons** (a tree fallen into the channel; a measurement error; a counting mistake; an unusual event on the day).

### Stage 5 — conclusions

Go back to the **original aim**: was the hypothesis **proved, partly proved or disproved**? Support the conclusion with **specific evidence** from the results, and link to the theory. "The hypothesis is largely proved: width increased at four of the five sites, from 1.2 m to 6.8 m. Site 3 was anomalous because…"

### Stage 6 — evaluation

- **Problems with the methods** — the float got stuck; the metre rule sank into soft sediment; stones chosen were not truly random; questionnaire questions were leading; the EQS is **subjective**.
- **Limitations of the data** — small sample size; one day only, one season; different groups measured differently; weather on the day (heavy rain raised river levels).
- **Other data that would help** — more sites; measurements at different times of year; Environment Agency river data; census data; a larger sample of people.
- **Reliability of conclusions** — how far can you **trust** them? Key words:
  - **Accuracy** — how close measurements are to the true value.
  - **Reliability** — whether the results would be the **same if repeated** (consistency).
  - **Validity** — whether the method actually measures **what it claims to** and answers the question.

### Model enquiries

**Physical: "How do the channel characteristics of a river change downstream?"**
Five sites along a small river, chosen by **systematic sampling** every 1 km; width, depth, velocity (float), bedload (callipers, Powers' index) at each; presented as scaled cross-sections, located bar charts and scattergraphs of distance against each variable. Typical findings: width, depth and velocity increase and stones become smaller and more rounded — supporting Bradshaw — with anomalies where a weir or bridge changes the channel. Evaluation: floats were blown by the wind; only one day in summer, when discharge was low; more sites would improve reliability.

**Human: "To what extent has regeneration improved the quality of life in [an area]?"**
An EQS, pedestrian counts and questionnaires at sites inside and outside the regenerated zone (**stratified** by area, **systematic** timing), plus secondary data (Index of Multiple Deprivation, census). Presented as radar diagrams, proportional symbols on a map and bar charts of questionnaire responses. Typical findings: higher EQS scores in the regenerated area, but residents' views mixed — some feel priced out. Evaluation: EQS is subjective; small opportunistic sample of residents; only one weekday visited.

**Interaction of physical and human geography**: e.g. how coastal defences (human) affect sediment movement (physical) along a beach, or how flood defences affect land use.

::: terms
- **Hypothesis**: a statement that can be tested with data and then proved, partly proved or disproved.
- **Primary data**: information collected first-hand by the investigator through fieldwork.
- **Secondary data**: information collected by someone else, such as census data, maps or Environment Agency records.
- **Sampling**: selecting a smaller, representative set of sites, people or items to measure instead of measuring everything.
- **Systematic sampling**: taking samples at regular intervals in space or time, such as every 5 m or every 10th person.
- **Stratified sampling**: dividing the population into groups and sampling each in proportion to its size.
- **Random sampling**: selecting samples so that each has an equal chance of being chosen, removing researcher bias.
- **Risk assessment**: identifying the hazards of fieldwork, how likely and serious they are, and how to reduce them.
- **Anomaly**: a result that does not fit the general pattern or trend of the data.
- **Accuracy**: how close a measurement is to the true value.
- **Reliability**: how consistent results are — whether the same results would be obtained if the enquiry were repeated.
- **Environmental quality survey**: a bipolar scoring sheet used to rate the quality of an area, such as litter, noise and building condition.
:::

::: method
**What the questions look like.** Section B has two parts: questions on **unfamiliar fieldwork** (figures showing someone else's methods and data — 1 to 6 marks each), then questions on **your own enquiries**, which open by asking you to **write the titles** of your physical and human enquiries. The section usually ends with a **9-mark** question on your own enquiry, with **+3 SPaG**.

**Command words:**
- **Describe** a method: say exactly what was done — equipment, where, how often, how recorded.
- **Justify** a method: explain **why it was appropriate** for your question — link to the hypothesis and to accuracy or practicality.
- **Explain** — reasons, developed with "because" and "so".
- **Suggest** — apply your understanding to the unfamiliar data.
- **Evaluate / assess / to what extent** — strengths **and** weaknesses, then a judgement.

**Unfamiliar fieldwork questions**: read the figure carefully; use its data; suggest sensible improvements (more sites, repeated readings, better equipment, different times).

**Own-enquiry questions — the golden rule**: every sentence must be **specific to your enquiry** — name the **place**, the **sites**, the **equipment**, the **numbers** you found. Generic answers ("we measured the river") are held at Level 1.

**6-mark levels answer** (Level 1: 1–2, Level 2: 3–4, Level 3: 5–6): describe → justify → link to aim, with specific detail from your enquiry. For "evaluate" questions, give strengths and weaknesses and a judgement.

**9-mark levels answer** (Level 1: 1–3, Level 2: 4–6, Level 3: 7–9, +3 SPaG):
- Level 3 needs **detailed, specific** knowledge of your enquiry, **thorough** evaluation (both sides), and a **clear, justified judgement** that answers the question ("to what extent were your conclusions reliable?").
- Structure: what you set out to do → evidence for reliability (strengths) → evidence against (problems, limitations) → what would improve it → final judgement.
- Use the vocabulary: accuracy, reliability, validity, sample size, anomaly, bias, representative.
:::

::: example Justify a method
For one of your enquiries, justify one primary data collection method you used. (3)

Enquiry: "How do the channel characteristics of the river change downstream?" We measured depth with a metre rule every 50 cm across the channel at each of five sites (1). This was appropriate because the riverbed is uneven, so taking several readings gave an accurate mean depth (1), which we needed to calculate cross-sectional area and discharge and test whether they increase downstream as the Bradshaw model predicts (1).
:::

::: example Unfamiliar data — calculate and suggest
Figure 4 shows that a float took 20, 24 and 22 seconds to travel 10 m at one site. Calculate the mean velocity, and suggest one reason why the three times were different. (3)

Mean time = (20 + 24 + 22) ÷ 3 = 22 seconds (1). Velocity = 10 ÷ 22 = 0.45 m/s (1). The float may have been caught in an eddy or against a rock, or wind may have blown it, or it may have been released at a different place across the channel where the water flows at a different speed (1).
:::

::: example 9-mark — reliability of conclusions
To what extent were the conclusions of your human geography enquiry reliable? (9 + 3 SPaG)

My enquiry asked "To what extent has regeneration improved the quality of life in the city-centre waterfront area?" I concluded that regeneration had improved environmental quality but that residents' views were mixed.

Some aspects of my conclusion are reliable. We used an environmental quality survey at ten sites, five inside and five outside the regenerated area, chosen by stratified sampling so both areas were represented equally. The regenerated sites scored a mean of +9 compared with −4 outside, a large and consistent difference across all ten sites, so the finding that the environment has improved is likely to be repeatable. Secondary data from the Index of Multiple Deprivation supported this, showing the regenerated area was less deprived.

However, other parts are less reliable. The environmental quality survey is subjective, and different groups scored the same site differently, so the scores are not fully accurate. We only visited on one weekday morning, so pedestrian counts do not show weekends or evenings. Our questionnaire used opportunistic sampling and only 30 people answered, many of them visitors rather than residents, so the sample was small and biased, and the question "Do you agree the area is much better now?" was leading.

To improve reliability, we should visit at different times and days, use a larger stratified sample of residents, and average scores from several groups.

Overall, my conclusion about environmental quality is fairly reliable because it rests on a clear, consistent pattern supported by secondary data, but my conclusion about quality of life for residents is much less reliable because the questionnaire sample was small and biased.

(Level 3: specific enquiry detail, strengths and weaknesses with numbers, improvements, and a clear, differentiated judgement.)
:::

::: q
State the difference between primary and secondary data. (2)
---
Primary data is collected first-hand by the investigator (1); secondary data has been collected by someone else, e.g. census data or maps (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how systematic sampling could be used to measure pebble size across a beach. (2)
---
Lay a tape measure from the sea to the back of the beach (1) and measure pebbles at regular intervals, e.g. every 5 m, along the transect (1).
:::

::: q
Explain one risk of river fieldwork and how it can be reduced. (2)
---
Risk of slipping on wet rocks and falling into deep or fast water (1); reduce by wearing wellies, never entering water above knee depth, working in groups and checking river levels first (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest one advantage and one disadvantage of using an environmental quality survey. (2)
---
Advantage: quick and easy to compare several sites using a numerical score (1). Disadvantage: subjective — different people score the same site differently (1).
:::

::: q
A river is 4 m wide with a mean depth of 0.5 m, and the water flows at 0.6 m/s. Calculate the discharge. Show your working. (2)
---
Cross-sectional area = 4 × 0.5 = 2 m² (1). Discharge = 2 × 0.6 = 1.2 m³/s (cumecs) (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why it is important to identify anomalies in fieldwork data. (3)
---
Anomalies are results that do not fit the pattern (1). They may reveal errors in measurement or recording, which reduce accuracy (1), or a real local factor (such as a weir or a fallen tree) that needs explaining, so identifying them makes conclusions more reliable (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Generic own-enquiry answers** — without your place, sites, equipment and results you stay in Level 1.
- **Describing instead of justifying** — "justify" needs **why the method suited your question**.
- Confusing **accuracy** (closeness to the true value) with **reliability** (would repeating it give the same result).
- Forgetting the **title** of each enquiry, or writing about the physical enquiry when the question asks about the human one.
- Risk answers that name a risk but not how it was **reduced** (or vice versa).
- Conclusions that do not go back to the **original hypothesis** and do not quote **evidence**.
- Evaluations that only list problems — say **how they affected the results** and what would **improve** them.
- Calculations with no working or no units (m/s, m², cumecs).
:::

::: test
Which sampling method involves choosing sites at regular intervals? A — random; B — stratified; C — systematic; D — opportunistic. (1)
---
C (1).
:::

::: test
State two factors to consider when choosing a location for fieldwork. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): safety / risks; accessibility and permission; distance and transport; whether it is suitable to test the hypothesis (shows enough variation); cost; time available.
:::

::: test
Suggest why a student used stratified sampling for a questionnaire about a regenerated area. (2)
---
To make sure different groups were represented in proportion — e.g. residents and visitors, or different age groups (1) — so the sample was representative and the results less biased (1).
:::

::: test
Using Figure 1, which shows the river is 3 m wide with depths of 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.4 m across the channel, calculate the cross-sectional area. Show your working. (2)
---
Mean depth = (0.2 + 0.4 + 0.6 + 0.4) ÷ 4 = 0.4 m (1). Cross-sectional area = 3 × 0.4 = 1.2 m² (1).
:::

::: test
Explain one geographical theory or concept that underpinned one of your enquiries. (3)
---
Enquiry title required. E.g. the Bradshaw model (1) predicts that width, depth, velocity and discharge increase downstream while bedload size and angularity decrease (1); the enquiry tested whether the river followed this pattern by measuring these variables at five sites (1). Accept Burgess model, longshore drift, regeneration/quality of life concepts, developed.
:::

::: test
For one of your enquiries, evaluate the effectiveness of one method you used to present your data. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): specific to the enquiry; clear strengths and weaknesses of the method with a judgement; may suggest an adaptation. Level 2 (3–4): some evaluation, partly specific. Level 1 (1–2): generic description of a method.
Indicative content: e.g. scattergraph of distance downstream against velocity — showed clear positive correlation, line of best fit highlighted trend and anomaly at Site 3; but only five points so the trend is uncertain; did not show location. Located bar charts — showed how width changed along the river on a map; but hard to read exact values. Radar diagrams for EQS — compared several criteria at once; cluttered with many sites. Judgement on how well it helped answer the question.
:::

::: test
Evaluate the methods you used to collect primary data in your physical geography enquiry. (9 + 3 SPaG)
---
Level 3 (7–9): detailed and specific knowledge of the enquiry's methods (place, sites, equipment, sampling); thorough evaluation of strengths and weaknesses and their effect on accuracy and reliability; suggestions for improvement; a clear, supported judgement. Level 2 (4–6): some specific detail and evaluation, may be unbalanced; judgement partly supported. Level 1 (1–3): generic description with little evaluation.
Indicative content (river example): systematic sites every 1 km covered the river evenly; tape measure for width was accurate; depth every 50 cm gave a good mean; float method cheap and simple but affected by wind and eddies — three repeats improved reliability, a flowmeter would be more accurate; callipers accurate for long axis but stones chosen may not be random; Powers' index subjective; one day in summer with low flow limits reliability; access problems meant some sites moved. Judgement on how effective the methods were overall.
SPaG: 3 marks for accurate spelling, punctuation, grammar and a wide range of specialist terms.
