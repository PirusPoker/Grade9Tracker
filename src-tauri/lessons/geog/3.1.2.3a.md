## What it is

A **hot desert** is an ecosystem where rainfall is **below 250 mm a year** and temperatures are high for most of the year. Deserts cover about a fifth of the Earth's land surface. The spec asks for four things: the **physical characteristics**, the **interdependence** of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people, **how plants and animals adapt**, and **issues related to biodiversity**.

### Where hot deserts are, and why

Most hot deserts lie roughly **between 15° and 35° north and south of the Equator**, close to the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn: the **Sahara** and **Arabian** deserts in the north, the **Kalahari** and **Namib** in southern Africa, the **Great Victoria** and **Great Sandy** deserts in Australia, the **Thar** in India and Pakistan, and the **Sonoran** and **Mojave** in the south-west USA and Mexico.

The main reason is **global atmospheric circulation**. Air that rises at the Equator cools, spreads out and **sinks at about 30° north and south** (the descending limb of the Hadley cell). Sinking air warms, which stops cloud forming, so these latitudes have **high pressure, clear skies and very little rain**. Other factors add to it:

- **Distance from the sea (continentality)** — the centre of the Sahara is over 1,000 km from the ocean, so winds have lost their moisture.
- **Rain shadow** — mountains force moist air to rise and rain on the windward side; the leeward side stays dry (the Mojave lies in the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada).
- **Cold ocean currents** — cold water offshore cools the air above it, so it cannot hold much moisture (the Namib and the Atacama).

### Physical characteristics

| Feature | What it is like | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Rainfall** | under 250 mm a year, very **unreliable**; some places go years without rain, then a storm brings a **flash flood** | sinking air, high pressure |
| **Temperature** | very hot by day (often over 40 °C in summer), but can fall close to or below **0 °C at night** — a huge **diurnal (daily) range** | no cloud: strong sunshine by day, heat escapes quickly at night |
| **Humidity and evaporation** | very low humidity; evaporation is far greater than rainfall | heat and dry air |
| **Soils** | thin, sandy or stony, grey-brown, **low in organic matter**; often a **salty crust** on the surface | little vegetation, so little leaf litter; evaporation draws salty water up through the soil |
| **Landscape** | sand seas (ergs) are only part of it — much of the Sahara is rocky plateau (hamada) and gravel plains (reg); dry river beds (wadis) | wind and occasional flash floods shape it |
| **Vegetation** | sparse — cacti, shrubs, thorny bushes, grasses after rain; plants widely spaced | competition for water |

### Interdependence

Every part of the desert ecosystem depends on the others. The chain to learn:

- **Climate → water**: very low, unreliable rainfall and high evaporation mean little surface water; what exists is in **oases**, wadis after storms, and underground **aquifers**.
- **Water → plants**: plants can only grow where they can reach water, so they are sparse and spaced out; ephemeral plants appear only after rain.
- **Plants → soils**: sparse plants drop little leaf litter, so soils stay thin and infertile; plant roots are one of the few things binding the soil together, so removing plants lets **wind and flash floods erode** it.
- **Soils → plants**: poor, salty soils limit which plants can grow — only specialised species cope.
- **Plants → animals**: plants give food, shade and moisture to animals (the kangaroo rat gets its water from seeds; camels browse thorny shrubs).
- **Animals → plants**: animals **disperse seeds** (in droppings or on fur) and pollinate flowers — bats pollinate the saguaro cactus; their droppings add nutrients to soil.
- **People → everything**: nomadic herders such as the **Tuareg** and **Bedouin** move their animals between grazing areas and oases, which lets vegetation recover. But if people **overgraze** or cut shrubs for **fuel wood**, plants disappear, the soil erodes and the land becomes less productive — the start of desertification (next lesson). At oases, people plant **date palms** that shade smaller crops beneath them, a human-made interdependence.

A change to one part ripples through the rest: a drier climate means fewer plants, which means fewer animals, less food for people, and more soil erosion.

### How plants adapt

| Plant | Adaptation | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Cacti** (e.g. **saguaro**, Sonoran desert) | thick, fleshy stems that **store water**; stems swell after rain | survive months without rain |
| Cacti | **spines** instead of leaves | tiny surface area cuts **transpiration**; spines deter animals from eating the plant for its water; spines also shade the stem and trap a thin layer of still air |
| Cacti | **shallow, very widespread roots** close to the surface | soak up rain quickly from a wide area before it evaporates |
| **Mesquite, acacia** | very **long tap roots** reaching down tens of metres | reach groundwater deep underground |
| Many desert plants | **thick waxy cuticle**; small leaves | reduce water loss |
| Many cacti and succulents | **stomata open at night** instead of by day | take in carbon dioxide when it is cool, so less water is lost |
| **Ephemerals** (desert wildflowers) | seeds lie **dormant** for years; after rain they germinate, flower and set seed in a few weeks | complete the whole life cycle while water is available |
| **Creosote bush** | small waxy leaves; releases chemicals that stop other plants growing nearby | less competition for scarce water |
| **Date palm** (oases) | deep roots; tough leaves | draws on groundwater at oases; provides food and shade for people |

### How animals adapt

| Animal | Adaptation | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Camel** | hump stores **fat, not water** — fat can be turned into energy and water; can drink a very large amount at once | survives long periods without food or water |
| Camel | wide, padded feet; long eyelashes and nostrils that can close | walks on soft sand; keeps sand out in storms |
| Camel | body temperature can rise several degrees in the day before it starts sweating | saves water |
| **Fennec fox** | very **large ears** full of blood vessels | radiate heat away; also hear prey under the sand |
| Fennec fox | **nocturnal**; lives in burrows; furry soles | avoids daytime heat; protects feet from hot sand |
| **Kangaroo rat** | never needs to drink — gets water from seeds it eats; produces very concentrated urine | minimises water loss |
| **Lizards and snakes** | cold-blooded; bask in the morning, shelter under rocks or burrow at midday | control body temperature without using water |
| **Thorny devil** (Australia) | grooves in its skin channel dew and moisture towards its mouth | collects water without drinking |
| Many small animals | **nocturnal** or **burrowing** | escape the heat of the day |

### Issues related to biodiversity

**Biodiversity** is the variety of plant and animal species in an area. Hot deserts have **low biodiversity** compared with tropical rainforests, but many species are **endemic** (found nowhere else) and highly **specialised**, which makes them vulnerable.

- **Fragile food webs**: with few species, the loss of one — a key plant or a predator — affects everything that depends on it.
- **Slow recovery**: plants grow slowly and rain is rare, so damaged vegetation can take decades to regrow.
- **Overgrazing and fuel wood collection** remove the plants that bind the soil and feed animals.
- **Hunting and poaching**: the **addax**, an antelope of the Sahara, is critically endangered, with fewer than 100 thought to survive in the wild; the dama gazelle and Saharan cheetah are also critically endangered.
- **Water extraction**: pumping groundwater for farms, towns and tourism lowers water tables and can dry up oases and the life they support.
- **Tourism and off-road vehicles**: 4×4s and quad bikes crush slow-growing plants and disturb animals.
- **Climate change**: higher temperatures and less reliable rain may push species beyond what they can tolerate — many are already at their limits.
- **Desertification** on the fringes turns semi-arid grassland into desert, reducing habitats.

Protection includes **national parks and reserves** (for example, the Termit and Tin Toumma reserve in Niger was set up to protect the addax and dama gazelle), captive breeding and reintroduction programmes, and controls on hunting and off-road driving.

::: terms
- **Hot desert**: an ecosystem with less than 250 mm of rain a year and high temperatures for most of the year.
- **Diurnal range**: the difference between the highest daytime and lowest night-time temperature in a single day.
- **Interdependence**: the way the parts of an ecosystem (climate, water, soils, plants, animals, people) rely on each other, so a change in one affects the others.
- **Transpiration**: the loss of water vapour from a plant, mainly through the stomata in its leaves.
- **Ephemeral plant**: a plant whose seeds lie dormant until rain, then germinate, flower and set seed within a few weeks.
- **Oasis**: a fertile place in a desert where groundwater reaches the surface, allowing plants to grow and people to settle.
- **Aquifer**: porous rock underground that stores water which can be pumped up through wells.
- **Biodiversity**: the variety of plant and animal species living in an area.
- **Endemic species**: a species found naturally in only one place or region in the world.
- **Nocturnal**: active at night and resting during the day, which lets desert animals avoid the heat.
:::

::: method
1. **State / identify (1–2 marks)**: one precise fact each — "rainfall below 250 mm a year", "spines instead of leaves". No explanation needed.
2. **Describe a climate graph or figure (2–4 marks)**: give the **pattern** (when hottest, driest), then **figures with units** from the graph ("maximum 38 °C in July, rainfall never above 10 mm in any month"), then an **anomaly** or the range. Do not explain unless asked.
3. **Explain an adaptation (2–4 marks)**: name the feature → say what it does → say why that matters in a desert. "The fennec fox has large ears (1) which contain many blood vessels that radiate heat (1), keeping it cool in temperatures above 40 °C (1)." The mark is in the **"so that / which means"**.
4. **Explain interdependence (4–6 marks)**: build a **chain** linking at least three components — climate → plants → soil → people. Use connectives: "this means… which leads to… as a result…". A list of components with no links stays in Level 1.
5. **6-mark levels of response**: Level 1 (1–2) simple points; Level 2 (3–4) some developed explanation; Level 3 (5–6) thorough, well-developed chains with specific detail (named species, figures). Two fully developed points beat four thin ones.
6. **9-mark "to what extent / assess" questions**: argue both sides, use specific detail (named species, named desert, the addax), then make a **clear judgement** that answers the question and says why. 9-markers in Paper 1 Section B often carry **+3 SPaG** — use specialist terms (transpiration, diurnal range, endemic) spelled correctly.
7. **"Use Figure X"**: you must quote the figure (a number, a place, a feature). An answer that never refers to the figure is capped.
:::

::: example Describe climate
Figure 1 shows a climate graph for In Salah, Algeria. Maximum temperature 45 °C in July, minimum 20 °C in January; total rainfall 17 mm. Describe the climate shown. (3)

Very hot all year, with temperatures between about 20 °C in January and 45 °C in July (1) — a seasonal range of about 25 °C (1). Rainfall is extremely low, only 17 mm a year, well below the 250 mm that defines a desert (1).
:::

::: example Explain a plant adaptation
Explain one way plants are adapted to hot desert conditions. (3)

Cacti have spines instead of leaves (1). Spines have a far smaller surface area than leaves, so much less water is lost through transpiration (1), which lets the cactus survive long dry periods when there is no rain to replace the water it stores in its stem (1).
:::

::: example Explain interdependence — 6 marks
Explain how the components of a hot desert ecosystem are interdependent. (6)

The climate controls everything else: with under 250 mm of rain a year and high evaporation, water is scarce, so plants are sparse and widely spaced. Because there are few plants, little leaf litter falls, so the soil has little organic matter and stays thin and infertile — which in turn limits which plants can grow.

Plants and animals depend on each other. Plants such as acacia and cacti provide food, moisture and shade for animals — the kangaroo rat gets all its water from seeds — while animals disperse seeds and bats pollinate the saguaro, allowing plants to reproduce.

People are part of the system too. Nomadic herders such as the Tuareg rely on grazing and oases; if they keep too many goats, the animals strip vegetation, so the soil is no longer held by roots and is eroded by wind and flash floods, which reduces the grazing available for the herders. A change to one component therefore affects the whole ecosystem.

(Level 3: three developed chains linking climate, soil, plants, animals and people, with specific examples.)
:::

::: example Explain an animal adaptation — use two features
Explain how the camel is adapted to the hot desert. (4)

The camel's hump stores fat, not water (1); the fat can be broken down for energy when food is scarce, so it can travel long distances between oases (1). Its nostrils can close and it has long double eyelashes (1), which keep out blowing sand during sandstorms (1).
:::

State the maximum annual rainfall of a hot desert. (1)
---
250 mm a year (accept "under 250 mm").

::: q
Explain why hot deserts are found at around 30° north and south of the Equator. (3)
---
Air rising at the Equator spreads out and sinks at about 30° N and S (1). Sinking air warms and creates high pressure (1), so clouds do not form and there is very little rain (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why temperatures in hot deserts can fall close to freezing at night. (2)
---
There is very little cloud cover (1), so heat absorbed by the ground during the day escapes quickly into the atmosphere at night (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the characteristics of hot desert soils. (3)
---
Thin / shallow (1); sandy or stony (1); low in organic matter because there is little leaf litter (1); often salty on the surface because evaporation draws salts upwards (1). Any three.
:::

::: q
Explain one adaptation of the fennec fox. (2)
---
Large ears with many blood vessels (1) radiate heat away from the body, keeping it cool (1). Or: nocturnal (1) so it avoids the extreme daytime heat (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why biodiversity in hot deserts is vulnerable to human activity. (4)
---
Few species, so food webs are simple and losing one species affects many others (1). Species are specialised/endemic, so they cannot move elsewhere or adapt quickly (1). Plants grow slowly with rare rain, so damage from overgrazing or off-road vehicles takes decades to recover (1). Hunting has left species such as the addax critically endangered, with under 100 in the wild (1). Water extraction lowers water tables and dries up oases (1). Any four developed.
:::

::: q
Explain how ephemeral plants survive in hot deserts. (2)
---
Their seeds lie dormant for long dry periods (1); when rain falls they germinate, flower and produce new seeds within a few weeks, completing their life cycle while water is available (1).

::: watch
- **Camel humps store fat, not water.** Saying "water" loses the mark every time.
- Adaptations need the **"so that"** — "cacti have spines" is 1 mark; "…which reduces transpiration, so the plant loses less water" earns the rest.
- Interdependence answers must **link** components (climate → plants → soil → people). A list of separate facts stays in Level 1.
- Deserts are not "always hot" — mention **cold nights** and the large **diurnal range**.
- When describing a climate graph, quote **figures with units** (°C, mm) and the month.
- Do not confuse **hot deserts** with the **Sahel** — the Sahel is the semi-arid fringe at risk of desertification, not the desert itself.
:::
