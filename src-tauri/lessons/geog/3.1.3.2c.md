## What it is

Different management strategies can be used to protect coastlines from the effects of physical processes. The spec asks for the **costs and benefits** of three groups of strategy — **hard engineering** (sea walls, rock armour, gabions, groynes), **soft engineering** (beach nourishment and reprofiling, dune regeneration) and **managed retreat** (coastal realignment) — plus **an example of a UK coastal management scheme** showing the **reasons for management**, the **management strategy**, and the **resulting effects and conflicts**. The scheme here is **Lyme Regis, Dorset**; **Medmerry, West Sussex** is the managed retreat example.

### Why manage the coast at all?

Coasts are eroded and flooded, and the risks are growing as **sea level rises** and storms may become more intense. People manage the coast to protect **homes, businesses, roads, railways, farmland, and heritage and habitats**. But defences are expensive, so in England each stretch of coast has a **Shoreline Management Plan (SMP)** that chooses one of four policies: **hold the line** (keep the coast where it is), **advance the line** (build new defences seaward), **managed realignment** (allow the coast to move back in a controlled way), or **no active intervention**. Decisions weigh the **value of what is protected** against the **cost** — a **cost–benefit analysis**.

### Hard engineering

**Hard engineering** uses **artificial structures** to control natural processes. It is usually effective but **expensive** and **unnatural-looking**, and it often shifts the problem along the coast.

| Strategy | What it is and how it works | Benefits | Costs / problems |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sea wall** | A concrete wall along the base of a cliff or the back of a beach. Often **curved (recurved)** so it **reflects** wave energy back out to sea. Usually has a promenade on top | Very effective at stopping erosion and flooding; long-lasting (decades); the promenade is popular with people and tourists | **Very expensive** to build and maintain — among the costliest defences per metre; reflected waves scour the beach in front, so the beach lowers and the wall can be undermined; looks artificial; restricts access to the beach |
| **Rock armour (rip-rap)** | Large boulders, often of hard rock such as granite brought in by barge, piled at the foot of a cliff or in front of a sea wall. Waves break on them and energy is absorbed by the gaps | Cheaper than a sea wall and quick to put in place; absorbs rather than reflects energy; can be fishing platforms | Rock is often imported from elsewhere (e.g. from Norway), so it does not match the local geology and looks out of place; can be dangerous to climb on; can shift in big storms |
| **Gabions** | Steel wire **cages filled with rocks**, stacked at the foot of a cliff or at the back of a beach. They absorb wave energy | **Cheap** and easy to build; absorb wave energy; vegetation can grow over them in time | Short lifespan — the wire **rusts and breaks** within a few decades; look unattractive; less effective in major storms |
| **Groynes** | **Timber or rock barriers built at right angles** to the beach, at intervals. They **trap sediment** moved by **longshore drift**, building up a wider beach | A wider beach **absorbs wave energy** and protects the cliff or town behind; a bigger beach is good for tourism; fairly cheap per unit | **Starve beaches further down the coast** (downdrift) of sediment, so erosion increases there; they look unnatural; timber rots and needs replacing; beach becomes a zigzag of high and low levels |

The classic example of the downdrift problem is **Mappleton on the Holderness coast**, where rock groynes and rock armour built in 1991 protected the village but increased erosion of cliffs to the south.

### Soft engineering

**Soft engineering** works **with** natural processes. It is usually **cheaper** and **more natural-looking**, but needs **regular maintenance** and gives less protection against big storms.

| Strategy | What it is and how it works | Benefits | Costs / problems |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beach nourishment** | Adding sand or shingle to a beach, usually **dredged from the sea bed offshore** and pumped or dumped onto the beach. A wider, higher beach absorbs wave energy before it reaches the land | Looks natural; a bigger beach benefits tourism; relatively cheap per year | Has to be **repeated** as waves and longshore drift remove the sediment; dredging can damage sea-bed ecosystems and sometimes causes erosion elsewhere; the sediment must match the beach |
| **Beach reprofiling** | Moving **existing** sediment from the lower beach back to the upper beach — usually with bulldozers after winter storms have dragged it down — to restore a profile that absorbs energy | Cheap and simple; uses the beach's own sediment; natural look | Only a **short-term** fix; must be done repeatedly, often every year; disrupts beach users while work is done |
| **Dune regeneration** | Creating new dunes or restoring damaged ones — **planting marram grass**, putting up **fences** to trap sand and keep people off, laying **boardwalks** for walkers, sometimes thatching bare areas. Dunes act as a natural barrier between the sea and the land | Cheap; natural; creates habitats for wildlife; dunes absorb wave energy and store sand | Takes time for plants to establish; areas must be fenced off, restricting access; can be destroyed by a big storm; needs ongoing management of visitors |

### Managed retreat (coastal realignment)

**Managed retreat** (**managed realignment**) means **allowing the sea to flood or erode an area of low-value land in a controlled way**, instead of defending the existing line. Old defences may be deliberately **breached**, and a **new line of defence is built further inland**. The flooded land becomes **salt marsh** and mudflats.

- **Benefits**: salt marsh **absorbs wave energy** and acts as a natural, self-maintaining defence; it is **cheaper in the long term** than maintaining old sea walls; it creates valuable **wetland habitats** for birds and fish; it adapts to sea-level rise.
- **Costs**: farmland, and sometimes buildings or roads, are lost; landowners may need **compensation**; local people may oppose "giving land to the sea"; it takes years for the habitat to develop.

**Example — Medmerry, West Sussex (completed 2013)**: the old shingle bank near Selsey was breached repeatedly by storms, flooding homes, caravan parks and the main road. The **Environment Agency** built about **7 km of new clay embankments inland** and then deliberately opened a gap in the shingle bank, letting the sea flood behind it. The scheme cost about **£28 million**, created about **183 hectares of new intertidal habitat** (now an **RSPB nature reserve**), and protects **348 properties**, a sewage works and caravan parks; it was the largest open-coast managed realignment scheme in Europe at the time. Conflicts: farmland was lost to the sea, and some local people opposed deliberately letting the sea in.

### Case study: Lyme Regis, Dorset

**Location**: a small seaside town in **West Dorset** on the **Jurassic Coast World Heritage Site**, at the mouth of the **River Lim**. It is famous for its **fossils** and for **the Cobb**, its historic curved harbour wall. Tourism is the main source of income.

**Reasons for management**

- **Geology**: the cliffs are layers of **clay** and **limestone** (Blue Lias). Clay becomes saturated after heavy rain and **slumps**; the town is built on **unstable slopes** and has suffered many **landslides** over the centuries — the **Black Ven** landslide complex just east of the town is one of the largest active landslides in Europe.
- **Waves**: the coast faces south-west, into the prevailing wind, and receives **high-energy waves** with a long fetch across the Atlantic and English Channel.
- **Erosion and old defences**: old sea walls were being undermined; the beach had become thin, so waves reached the walls.
- **What was at risk**: hundreds of homes, roads, the harbour and seafront businesses. Loss of the beach meant **fewer tourists**, damaging the economy.

**The management strategy — the Lyme Regis Environmental Improvements scheme (run by West Dorset District Council, now Dorset Council)**

| Phase | When | What was done |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase I** | completed **1995** | A new **sea wall and promenade** to the east of the mouth of the River Lim |
| **Phase II** | **2005–2007**, about **£22 million** | New **sea walls** and promenade; **beach nourishment** — a wide **sand and shingle beach** built with imported sediment to absorb wave energy and attract tourists; **rock armour** extended and strengthened to protect the Cobb and harbour; **slope stabilisation** of the town's cliffs using **soil nails**, **piling** and improved **drainage** to reduce landslides |
| **Phase III** | cancelled | Planned protection to the west of the Cobb; **cancelled because the costs outweighed the benefits** (useful elements were folded into Phase II) |
| **Phase IV** | **2013–2015**, about **£20 million** | A **390 m sea wall** built in front of the old, failing wall at **East Cliff** and Church Cliff; cliffs stabilised with nailing, piling and drainage, protecting up to **480 homes** from damage or loss of access |

So the scheme combines **hard engineering** (sea walls, rock armour, cliff stabilisation) with **soft engineering** (beach nourishment).

**Resulting effects**

- **Positive**: homes, roads and infrastructure are protected; landslide movement in the town has been greatly reduced; the new beaches and promenade have **boosted tourism** — more visitors and thriving seafront businesses; the harbour is better protected; property values and insurance are more secure.
- **Negative**: **very high cost**, much of it paid by national government (Defra) and so by taxpayers; the new structures change the natural look of a World Heritage coastline; stabilising the cliffs means **fewer fossils** are exposed, affecting fossil hunters and scientists; more visitors bring **traffic congestion**, parking problems and litter; defending Lyme Regis may **reduce the sediment** reaching beaches further along the coast.

**Conflicts**

- **Residents and businesses** wanted maximum protection; **conservationists and geologists** worried that stopping erosion interferes with a World Heritage Site valued precisely because it is naturally eroding and exposing fossils.
- **Taxpayers** elsewhere may question spending tens of millions of pounds to protect one small town.
- **Tourists vs residents**: more visitors support the economy but bring congestion.
- **Neighbouring areas**: a hard-defended town can starve nearby beaches of sediment, so the benefits are local and the costs may be felt elsewhere.

::: terms
- **Hard engineering**: using artificial structures such as sea walls, rock armour, gabions and groynes to control natural processes.
- **Soft engineering**: strategies that work with natural processes, such as beach nourishment, reprofiling and dune regeneration.
- **Sea wall**: a concrete wall, often curved, built along the coast to reflect wave energy and prevent erosion and flooding.
- **Rock armour**: large boulders piled at the foot of a cliff or sea wall to absorb wave energy.
- **Gabion**: a wire cage filled with rocks, used to absorb wave energy at the foot of a cliff.
- **Groyne**: a timber or rock barrier built at right angles to the beach to trap sediment moved by longshore drift.
- **Beach nourishment**: adding sand or shingle, usually dredged from offshore, to widen a beach so it absorbs wave energy.
- **Beach reprofiling**: moving sediment from the lower to the upper beach to restore a profile that absorbs wave energy.
- **Dune regeneration**: creating or restoring sand dunes by planting marram grass, fencing and boardwalks.
- **Managed retreat**: allowing low-value land to be flooded or eroded in a controlled way, with new defences built further inland.
- **Shoreline Management Plan**: a plan that sets the policy for each stretch of coast — hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment or no active intervention.
:::

::: method
1. **Describe a strategy (2 marks)**: what it is **and** how it works — "rock armour: large boulders piled at the cliff foot (1) that absorb wave energy in the gaps between them (1)".
2. **Costs and benefits (4–6 marks)**: for each strategy give a benefit **and** a cost, each **developed**: "Groynes build a wider beach that absorbs wave energy (benefit), but they starve beaches downdrift of sediment, increasing erosion there (cost), as at Mappleton". Balance matters — a list of only benefits stays low.
3. **Hard vs soft**: the comparison is usually **effectiveness and lifespan vs cost, appearance and impact elsewhere**. Say which suits a **high-value** place (town) and which a **low-value** one (farmland).
4. **Case study (6 marks)**: structure it with the spec's three headings — **reasons**, **strategy**, **effects and conflicts** — and use named facts (Phase II 2005–2007, about £22 million; Phase IV, 390 m sea wall, 480 homes).
5. **9-mark "evaluate / to what extent"**: e.g. "Evaluate the success of a named coastal management scheme". Give successes and problems with case-study evidence, then a **justified judgement** — successful for whom? At what cost? Consider sustainability and effects elsewhere. SPaG (+3) may be attached.
6. **"Use Figure X"**: identify the strategies shown in the photo (a curved wall, a line of boulders, a timber groyne with sand piled on one side) before explaining them.
:::

::: example Describe and explain a strategy
Explain how groynes protect the coast. (3)

Groynes are timber or rock barriers built at right angles to the beach (1). They trap sediment carried by longshore drift, so a wider, higher beach builds up on the updrift side (1). The wider beach absorbs wave energy before the waves reach the cliffs or sea wall, reducing erosion (1).
:::

::: example Costs and benefits — 4 marks
Outline the costs and benefits of beach nourishment. (4)

Beach nourishment widens the beach by adding sand dredged from offshore, so waves lose energy before reaching the land (1). It looks natural and the bigger beach attracts tourists (1). However, it must be repeated regularly because waves and longshore drift remove the sediment (1), and dredging can damage sea-bed habitats (1).
:::

::: example Case study — 6 marks
For a named UK coastal management scheme, explain why the coast needed to be managed. (6)

At Lyme Regis in Dorset, the cliffs are made of clay and limestone. After heavy rain, the clay becomes saturated and heavy and slumps, so the town, which is built on unstable slopes, has suffered repeated landslides — the Black Ven landslide nearby is one of the largest in Europe.

The town faces south-west, into the prevailing wind, so it is hit by high-energy waves with a long fetch. These were undermining the old sea walls, and the beach had become thin, so waves reached the walls directly.

Hundreds of homes, roads and the Cobb harbour were at risk — up to 480 homes at East Cliff alone. Tourism is the town's main income, and the loss of the beach was reducing visitor numbers, so protecting the seafront was vital to the local economy.

(Level 3: several developed reasons — physical and human — with specific Lyme Regis detail.)
:::

::: example Evaluate managed retreat — 4 marks
Explain why managed retreat is becoming more common on UK coasts. (4)

Sea levels are rising, so maintaining old defences everywhere is becoming more expensive (1). Where the land is low value, such as farmland, the cost of defending it is greater than the value protected (1). Allowing it to flood creates salt marsh, which absorbs wave energy and acts as a natural defence that maintains itself (1). It also creates habitats — Medmerry (2013) created about 183 hectares of intertidal habitat, now an RSPB reserve, while protecting 348 properties with new embankments inland (1).
:::
