## What it is

Major changes in the UK economy have affected — and will keep affecting — where people work and which regions grow. The specification ("Economic futures in the UK") covers seven points: the **causes of economic change**; the move to a **post-industrial economy**; **impacts of industry on the physical environment** with an example of **sustainable modern industry**; **rural change** in one area of population growth and one of decline; **improvements to road, rail, port and airport infrastructure**; the **north–south divide** and strategies to reduce it; and the **UK's place in the wider world**.

### 1. Causes of economic change

**De-industrialisation** is the decline of a country's traditional manufacturing and heavy industry. The UK's industrial base — coal mining, iron and steel, shipbuilding and textiles, concentrated in northern England, South Wales, central Scotland and Northern Ireland — has shrunk dramatically.

| Cause | Explanation and examples |
|---|---|
| **De-industrialisation and decline of the traditional industrial base** | Easily worked coal seams were used up and costs rose; machines replaced workers (mechanisation); older factories could not compete. The miners' strike of 1984–85 was followed by mass pit closures; the last deep coal mine, **Kellingley** in Yorkshire, closed in **December 2015**. Steelmaking has continued to shrink: Redcar steelworks on Teesside closed in 2015, and **Port Talbot**'s blast furnaces shut in **2024**. |
| **Globalisation** | The world's economies have become more connected through trade, investment and communication. Manufacturing moved to countries with **lower labour costs** (e.g. China, India), and cheaper imports undercut UK products. Some services, such as call centres, were also moved abroad. But globalisation also brought **foreign investment** into the UK — e.g. Nissan's car plant at Sunderland (opened 1986) — and made London a global finance centre. |
| **Government policies** | In the 1980s the government stopped subsidising loss-making industries and **privatised** state-owned firms (coal, steel, telecoms, energy), leading to closures and job losses. Deregulation of the City of London (the "Big Bang", 1986) boosted financial services. Joining the EU (1973) opened a large market; leaving it (2020) created new trade barriers. More recent policies support growth sectors through **Enterprise Zones**, **Freeports** and investment in research. |

### 2. Moving towards a post-industrial economy

A **post-industrial economy** is one where manufacturing has declined and most people work in **services** (tertiary) and **knowledge-based** jobs (quaternary — research, IT, information). Today services make up about **81% of UK economic output and 83% of employment**; manufacturing is only about **8.5% of output**, employing about **2.6 million people**.

- **Information technology**: fast broadband and the internet let firms locate almost anywhere and allow home working. Software, gaming and digital media have grown (e.g. clusters in London, Cambridge, Manchester and Bristol).
- **Service industries**: retail, tourism, health, education, leisure and the creative industries (film, music, TV, advertising).
- **Finance**: the **City of London** and **Canary Wharf** form one of the world's leading financial centres; Edinburgh, Leeds and Manchester are important regional centres.
- **Research**: universities and research institutes drive growth in medicine, biotechnology, artificial intelligence and engineering.
- **Science and business parks**: groups of offices, research labs and light industry, usually on the edge of towns with good road access, parking and pleasant landscaped surroundings.

**Example: Cambridge Science Park**

- Founded in **1970 by Trinity College, Cambridge** — Europe's longest-established science park. It is now home to **over 100 companies** employing **more than 7,000 people** in fields such as biotechnology, pharmaceuticals, computing and electronics.
- **Why there?** Close links with the **University of Cambridge** (research, spin-off companies and a supply of highly qualified graduates); good transport — the A14 and M11, rail links to London and Stansted Airport nearby; an attractive environment that appeals to skilled workers; and **clustering** — firms benefit from being near each other (the "Silicon Fen" high-tech cluster).

### 3. Impacts of industry on the physical environment

Industry can cause **air pollution** (factory emissions, traffic), **water pollution** (effluent, run-off), **noise and dust**, **scarring of the landscape** (quarries, spoil heaps), **loss of habitats** and, after closure, **derelict and contaminated land**. Modern industry tries to reduce these impacts.

**Example of more sustainable modern industry: Torr Quarry, Somerset**

- A large **Carboniferous limestone quarry** at East Cranmore near **Shepton Mallet** in the **Mendip Hills**, run by **Aggregate Industries**. It produces several million tonnes of crushed stone a year for construction, especially road building, and employs a few hundred people.
- **Environmental problems** of quarrying: a huge scar on the landscape, noise and vibration from blasting, dust, heavy lorry traffic, and possible effects on water quality.
- **How it is managed more sustainably**
  - **Rail transport**: the quarry has had its own rail link since **1970**, and most of its output (around three-quarters) leaves by train, taking thousands of lorries off local roads and cutting emissions.
  - **Landscaping**: around **60 hectares** have been landscaped, with screening banks and tree planting to hide the quarry from view.
  - **Monitoring** of noise, dust, vibration and water quality.
  - **Restoration**: in 2012 permission was given to deepen the quarry rather than spread it sideways into more countryside, extending working to around **2040**; afterwards it is planned to become a **lake** and wildlife habitat for recreation and nature.

### 4. Social and economic changes in the rural landscape

**Area of population growth: South Cambridgeshire**

- Population rose **8.9%** between 2011 and 2021 (from about 148,800 to about 162,000) — faster than England's 6.6%.
- **Why**: high-tech jobs in and around Cambridge (science parks, the university, biotech); good transport links for commuters to Cambridge and London; attractive villages; new housing.
- **Social changes**: new settlements such as **Cambourne** (about 12,000 people in 2021) and **Northstowe** (planned for about 10,000 homes); an influx of young families and commuters; pressure on schools, GP surgeries and roads; **house prices** so high that young local people struggle to afford homes; villages becoming **commuter (dormitory) settlements**, with quieter daytimes and some loss of traditional character.
- **Economic changes**: fewer people work in farming; more work in professional and high-tech jobs; farm buildings converted to offices or homes; more traffic and congestion — the **A14 Cambridge to Huntingdon** upgrade (opened 2020) was built partly to cope.

**Area of population decline: the Outer Hebrides (Na h-Eileanan Siar)**

- A chain of islands off north-west Scotland (Lewis and Harris, the Uists, Barra); the main town is **Stornoway**.
- Population fell from about **27,700 (2011) to about 26,200 (2022)** — a **5.5% drop**, the biggest proportional fall among Scotland's island councils. More than a quarter of people are aged **65 or over**, and the number of children and working-age adults is falling fastest.
- **Why**: young people leave for university and jobs on the mainland; few well-paid jobs; remoteness and costly, weather-dependent ferry links; high cost of living; limited housing.
- **Social changes**: an **ageing population**; schools and shops close or merge; services such as health care become harder and costlier to provide; fewer people to keep the Gaelic language and community life going; some houses become holiday homes.
- **Economic changes**: traditional **crofting** (small-scale farming) and **fishing** have declined; the economy relies more on public-sector jobs, **tourism**, and niche products such as **Harris Tweed**; improved broadband allows some remote working; **community land buyouts** (local people buying estates to run for the community) and renewable energy offer new opportunities.

### 5. Improvements and new developments in infrastructure

Good infrastructure moves people and goods efficiently, attracts investment and connects regions.

| Type | Developments |
|---|---|
| **Road** | Upgrades to congested routes such as the **A14** near Cambridge (opened 2020); "smart motorways" using the hard shoulder as a traffic lane (new all-lane-running schemes were halted in 2023 over safety concerns); the **Lower Thames Crossing**, a road tunnel under the Thames between Kent and Essex, given planning consent in **March 2025**. |
| **Rail** | **HS2**, a high-speed line from London to Birmingham, to increase capacity and cut journey times; its legs to Manchester and the East Midlands/Leeds were cancelled, and after a 2026 review trains between Old Oak Common (west London) and Birmingham are not expected before the second half of the 2030s. The **Elizabeth line** (opened 2022) added a fast east–west route across London. Electrification of main lines and plans for **Northern Powerhouse Rail** between northern cities. |
| **Ports** | About 95% of UK trade by volume goes by sea. **London Gateway**, a deep-water container port on the Thames estuary in Essex, opened in **2013**, able to handle the world's largest container ships and linked to a large logistics park, and is being expanded. **Liverpool2** (2016) lets bigger ships use Liverpool. Felixstowe remains the busiest container port. |
| **Airports** | **Heathrow** is one of the world's busiest international airports but is near capacity; in **January 2025** the government backed a **third runway** and in **June 2026** published a draft national policy statement for it — opponents cite noise, air pollution and carbon emissions. **Gatwick** was given approval in **September 2025** to bring its standby runway into regular use as a second runway. |

**Arguments**: supporters say infrastructure creates jobs, attracts investment and helps rebalance the economy; critics point to huge costs and overruns (HS2), damage to countryside and wildlife, noise, and the carbon emissions of more flights and road traffic.

### 6. The north–south divide

The **north–south divide** describes the economic and social differences between the richer **South East** (especially London) and the poorer regions of the **North**, the Midlands, Wales and Northern Ireland.

| Indicator | South (London and South East) | North |
|---|---|---|
| Wages | higher average earnings | lower average earnings |
| Jobs | growth in finance, tech and professional services | legacy of lost industry; more reliance on public-sector jobs |
| Health | higher life expectancy | lower life expectancy, higher rates of illness |
| Housing | very high house prices (London's average is more than three times that of the North East) | cheaper housing but less economic growth |
| Education | higher proportion with degrees | lower |

**Why**: de-industrialisation hit the North hardest; London's finance sector, government and global links attract investment; transport spending has historically been higher in London; skilled young people migrate south. (The divide is not simple — there are deprived areas in London and wealthy areas in the North, e.g. parts of Cheshire and North Yorkshire.)

**Strategies to reduce regional differences**

- **Enterprise Zones** (from 2012): tax breaks and simpler planning to attract firms to areas that need jobs.
- **Northern Powerhouse** (launched **2014**): investment in transport, science and innovation to link northern cities (Manchester, Leeds, Liverpool, Sheffield, Newcastle, Hull) so they can compete together with London.
- **Devolution**: elected **metro mayors** with powers over transport, housing and skills (e.g. Greater Manchester from 2017).
- **Levelling Up** (White Paper **2022**): funds for towns and high streets, aiming to narrow gaps in pay, health and education by 2030.
- **Freeports** (from **2021**), e.g. **Teesside**, with tax and customs advantages to attract investment.
- **Relocating government jobs**, e.g. the Treasury's campus in **Darlington** (opened 2021).
- **Evaluation**: some success (Manchester's growing media and tech sector, MediaCityUK in Salford), but the gap remains wide; funding is small relative to the problem, schemes change with each government, and transport projects for the North (HS2's northern legs) have been cut.

### 7. The UK's place in the wider world

| Link | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Trade** | The UK trades globally. In 2025 the **USA was its largest single trading partner** (about 17.5% of total trade), but the **EU as a whole** accounted for around 45% of UK trade. Main exports: services (finance, insurance, education, consulting), cars, machinery, medicines. |
| **Culture** | The English language; music, film and TV (the BBC, the Harry Potter and James Bond films); the Premier League, watched worldwide; museums and theatres that attract tourists; universities that attract international students. The UK's diverse population links it to cultures around the world. |
| **Transport** | Heathrow connects the UK to hundreds of destinations; the **Channel Tunnel** (opened 1994) links to mainland Europe by Eurostar and freight trains; major ports handle global shipping. |
| **Electronic communication** | Undersea fibre-optic cables link the UK to North America and Europe; London is a major hub for data centres and internet exchange; its **time zone** allows trading with Asia in the morning and the Americas in the afternoon. |
| **European Union** | The UK joined in **1973**, voted to leave in the **2016 referendum** (52% to 48%), and left on **31 January 2020**. Trade now runs under the **Trade and Cooperation Agreement** (from 1 January 2021), with more paperwork and checks for businesses; the EU is still the UK's largest trading bloc, and in 2025 the two sides agreed to strengthen co-operation. |
| **Commonwealth** | A voluntary association of **56 countries**, most of them former British colonies, with about a third of the world's population. Links through trade, sport (the Commonwealth Games), education, migration and shared language and legal traditions. |

::: terms
- **De-industrialisation**: the decline of a country's traditional manufacturing and heavy industries, such as coal, steel and shipbuilding.
- **Globalisation**: the growing interconnection of the world's economies, cultures and people through trade, investment, travel and communication.
- **Post-industrial economy**: an economy in which manufacturing has declined and most people work in services and knowledge-based industries.
- **Quaternary sector**: the part of the economy based on information and knowledge, such as research, IT and consultancy.
- **Science park**: a group of research-based, high-tech businesses located together, usually linked to a university.
- **Business park**: an area of offices and light industry, usually on the edge of a town with good road access and parking.
- **Rural depopulation**: the decline in the population of a rural area, as people (especially the young) move away.
- **Commuter settlement**: a village or town where many residents travel to work in a nearby city each day.
- **Infrastructure**: the basic physical systems a country needs to function, such as roads, railways, ports, airports and communications.
- **North–south divide**: the economic and social differences between the wealthier south-east of England and the poorer north.
- **Northern Powerhouse**: a government strategy launched in 2014 to boost the economy of northern England through investment in transport, science and innovation.
- **Commonwealth**: a voluntary association of 56 countries, most of them former territories of the British Empire.
:::

::: method
1. **Know which spec point is being asked.** Causes of change, post-industrial economy, environmental impact/sustainable industry, rural change, infrastructure, north–south divide or UK in the wider world — each has its own named example.
2. **Causes**: split into **de-industrialisation**, **globalisation** and **government policies**, and explain *how* each changed employment ("cheaper imports from China undercut UK steel, so plants closed").
3. **Sustainable industry (Torr Quarry)**: name the environmental problem, then the specific management step that reduces it — rail transport cuts lorry traffic; landscaping hides the scar; lake restoration after 2040.
4. **Rural change**: the spec says **social AND economic** changes in **one area of growth AND one of decline**. Use two clear paragraphs per area, with population figures.
5. **Infrastructure and north–south strategies are "evaluate" favourites**: give the benefits, the costs (money, environment, who gains) and a judgement.
6. **4-mark answers** (Level 2 = 3–4): two developed points with examples. **6-mark answers** (Level 3 = 5–6): three well-developed points with place detail. **9-mark answers** (Level 3 = 7–9): both sides, detailed evidence and a justified judgement; some carry **+3 SPaG**.
7. **"Use Figure X"** (e.g. a map of unemployment by region or a graph of employment by sector): quote figures with units, describe the pattern (highest, lowest, trend), then explain with your own knowledge.
:::

::: example Causes of change — 4 marks
Explain how globalisation has contributed to economic change in the UK. (4)

Manufacturing has moved to countries such as China where labour costs are lower (1), so cheaper imports undercut UK-made goods like steel and textiles and many UK factories closed (1). At the same time globalisation has brought foreign investment into the UK, for example Nissan's car plant in Sunderland (1), and London has grown as a global finance centre because it is connected to markets around the world (1).
:::

::: example Sustainable industry — 6 marks
Using an example, explain how modern industrial development can be more environmentally sustainable. (6)

Torr Quarry in the Mendip Hills is a large limestone quarry run by Aggregate Industries. Quarrying normally causes heavy lorry traffic, but Torr has had its own rail link since 1970 and most of its output — around three-quarters — leaves by train. This takes thousands of lorries off narrow rural roads, reducing congestion, noise and carbon emissions.

The quarry is a scar on the landscape, so about 60 hectares have been landscaped with screening banks and tree planting, hiding it from nearby villages and creating habitats for wildlife. Noise, dust, vibration and water quality are monitored to keep pollution within limits.

Planning is also long-term. In 2012 permission was given to deepen the quarry rather than extend it into more countryside, and when extraction ends, around 2040, the site is planned to become a lake for wildlife and recreation. So although quarrying damages the environment, careful management reduces the impact during and after operation.

(Level 3: three developed points with specific, accurate detail.)
:::

::: example North–south divide — 9 marks
"Strategies to reduce the north–south divide in the UK have been unsuccessful." To what extent do you agree? (9 + 3 SPaG)

There is evidence that strategies have had some success. The Northern Powerhouse, launched in 2014, directed investment into northern transport, science and innovation, and cities like Manchester have grown strongly, helped by MediaCityUK in Salford and devolution to an elected mayor with powers over transport and housing since 2017. Enterprise Zones and Freeports such as Teesside use tax advantages to attract firms, and moving government jobs, such as the Treasury campus in Darlington, brings well-paid work north.

However, the divide remains wide. Average wages, life expectancy and house prices are still much higher in London and the South East; London's average house price is more than three times the North East's. Funding for Levelling Up has been small compared with the scale of the problem, and policies change with each government. Crucial transport links have been cut: HS2's legs to Manchester and Leeds were cancelled, while the Elizabeth line in London was completed in 2022. Skilled young people still migrate south for jobs.

Overall I largely agree. Strategies have produced growth in some northern cities, so they have not been a complete failure, but they have not closed the gap between regions as a whole. Long-term, consistent investment in northern transport and skills would be needed to make a real difference.

(Level 3: both sides, detailed evidence, supported judgement.)
:::

::: watch
- Describing de-industrialisation without explaining **why** it happened (competition, mechanisation, exhausted resources, policy).
- Rural change answers that cover only one area or only social (or only economic) changes — the spec needs **both areas** and **both kinds** of change.
- Sustainable industry answers that list problems but not the **specific solutions** at Torr Quarry (rail, landscaping, restoration).
- Out-of-date infrastructure facts — HS2 no longer goes to Manchester or Leeds; Heathrow's third runway is backed but not yet built.
- Treating the north–south divide as absolute — credit comes from recognising exceptions (deprived parts of London, wealthy parts of the North).
- 9-markers without a judgement, or with a judgement that ignores the evidence given.
