## What it is

The second half of the **Food** option (Paper 2, Section C). The key ideas: **different strategies can be used to increase food supply**, and the world is **moving towards a sustainable resource future**. You need an overview of the strategies, **one large-scale agricultural development** with its advantages and disadvantages (here: the **Indus Basin Irrigation System, Pakistan**), the ways food can be supplied sustainably, and **one local scheme in an LIC or NEE** (here: **sand dams in Makueni County, Kenya**).

### Strategies to increase food supply — the overview

**1. Irrigation** — artificially watering crops where rainfall is too low or unreliable.

| Type | How it works | Pros and cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Surface (flood or furrow)** | water flows by gravity from canals along channels between crops | cheap and simple; but wasteful — much is lost to evaporation and seepage, and it can cause waterlogging and salinisation |
| **Sprinkler** | pipes spray water over the crops | even coverage; but costly and loses water to evaporation and wind |
| **Drip (trickle)** | pipes with small holes drip water directly onto each plant's roots | very efficient — little is wasted; but expensive to install and maintain |

Irrigated land is only about a fifth of the world's cultivated land but produces around **40% of its food** (FAO). Irrigation raises yields, allows two or three harvests a year and lets farming happen in dry areas — but it can **deplete rivers and groundwater**, and badly managed schemes leave salt in the soil.

**2. Hydroponics and aeroponics** — growing without soil, usually indoors or in greenhouses.

- **Hydroponics**: plant roots sit in a **nutrient-rich water solution** instead of soil.
- **Aeroponics**: roots hang in the **air** inside a chamber and are **sprayed with a mist** of water and nutrients.
- **Advantages**: use far less water than field farming (it is recycled); no soil needed, so food can be grown in cities, deserts or even underground; no pests from the soil, so few pesticides; crops grown all year under LED lights; can be stacked in **vertical farms**, cutting food miles. In London, **Growing Underground** has grown salad leaves hydroponically in old wartime air-raid tunnels under Clapham.
- **Disadvantages**: expensive to set up; need **a lot of energy** for lighting, heating and pumps (several vertical farms have closed because of high energy costs); mainly suited to high-value salad and herbs, not staple crops like wheat or rice; need skilled staff.

**3. The new green revolution and biotechnology**

- The original **Green Revolution** (1960s–70s) introduced **high-yielding varieties (HYVs)** of wheat and rice, with fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation, mainly in Mexico and Asia. India went from facing famine in the mid-1960s to being self-sufficient in grain. **But** it needed expensive inputs, so richer farmers benefited most; it polluted rivers, depleted groundwater (as in the Punjab) and reduced crop variety.
- The **new green revolution** aims to raise yields **sustainably**, especially in Africa, where the first revolution had little effect. It uses both traditional methods and new science: crop varieties bred for local conditions (such as **NERICA — "New Rice for Africa"**, a cross between Asian and African rice with higher yields and drought resistance), better soil and water management, and small-scale improvements.
- **Biotechnology** means using living organisms and genetic science to improve crops and livestock, including **genetically modified (GM) crops**:
  - **Bt cotton** in India is modified to produce its own insecticide against bollworm;
  - **drought-tolerant maize** has been developed for African farmers;
  - **Golden Rice** is modified to contain vitamin A to fight deficiency diseases; it was approved for planting in the Philippines in 2021 but remains controversial.
- **For**: higher yields, crops resistant to pests, disease and drought, extra nutrients, less pesticide spraying. **Against**: GM seed is patented and expensive, so poor farmers can become dependent on large companies; fears about effects on wildlife and cross-pollination with wild plants; consumer opposition (GM crops are hardly grown in the UK and EU).

**4. Appropriate technology** — technology suited to the needs, skills, knowledge and wealth of local people: cheap, simple, made from local materials, easy to repair and sustainable.

- **Treadle pumps** — foot-operated pumps that lift groundwater for irrigation without fuel.
- **Sand dams** — see Makueni below.
- **Magic stones (stone lines, or bunds)** in Burkina Faso — lines of stones along the contours trap rainwater and soil, raising yields.
- **Zai pits** — small planting pits filled with compost that concentrate water and nutrients around each plant.
- **Drip-irrigation kits** made from buckets or bottles; **solar-powered pumps**.

### A large-scale agricultural development: the Indus Basin Irrigation System (IBIS), Pakistan

**Location and background**

- The **River Indus** rises in the Himalayas (in Tibet), flows through India and then the length of **Pakistan** to the Arabian Sea near Karachi. Its main tributaries are the **Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej**. The Indus plains are hot and dry — much of Pakistan receives under 250 mm of rain a year — so farming depends on river water, fed by snow and glacier melt and the monsoon.
- Canal irrigation was developed by the British from the mid-1800s. After Partition (1947) the rivers were shared between India and Pakistan by the **Indus Waters Treaty (1960)**, brokered by the World Bank: Pakistan received the three **western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab)** and India the three eastern rivers. Pakistan then built huge new works to move water from the western rivers to the lands the eastern rivers had watered.

**What the system is**

- The **largest contiguous (unbroken) irrigation system in the world**, irrigating around **14 million hectares**.
- **Three major storage reservoirs** — **Tarbela** (on the Indus, completed 1976, one of the largest earth-filled dams in the world), **Mangla** (on the Jhelum, 1967) and **Chashma**.
- **16 barrages** (low dams that divert river water into canals), **12 inter-river link canals** that move water from the western rivers eastwards, **44 canal systems** and tens of thousands of kilometres of canals, feeding more than **100,000 watercourses** that carry water to individual farms.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Food supply**: the great majority of Pakistan's farm output comes from irrigated land in the Indus Basin — wheat, rice, sugar cane and cotton — feeding a population of over 240 million | **Waterlogging and salinisation**: water seeping from unlined canals and over-watered fields raises the water table; in the heat, water evaporates and leaves salt at the surface, so large areas of land have become less productive or unusable |
| **Economy and jobs**: agriculture provides about a quarter of Pakistan's GDP and over a third of its jobs; cotton supports the textile industry, Pakistan's biggest export | **Water losses**: much water is lost to seepage and evaporation from canals before it reaches the fields |
| **Exports**: Pakistan is a major exporter of rice | **Inequality**: farmers at the head of canals and large landowners take more water; "tail-end" farmers often receive too little |
| **Hydroelectric power**: Tarbela and Mangla generate a large share of Pakistan's electricity | **Sedimentation**: silt from the Himalayas fills the reservoirs, steadily reducing their storage capacity |
| **Flood control and storage**: reservoirs store monsoon and meltwater for the dry season | **Indus delta**: so little water reaches the sea that the delta is shrinking — sea water intrudes into farmland, mangroves have been lost and fishing communities have suffered |
| **Rural development**: water has allowed farming and settlement across land that would otherwise be desert | **Wildlife**: barrages split the endangered **Indus river dolphin** population into isolated sections |
| | **Displacement**: building Tarbela and Mangla dams flooded many villages and forced large numbers of people to move |
| | **Politics and future risk**: the system depends on the Indus Waters Treaty — in April 2025 India announced it was holding the treaty "in abeyance"; climate change threatens the glaciers that feed the rivers; the 2022 floods submerged a large part of the country and damaged canals |

**Judgement**: the IBIS is essential — Pakistan could not feed itself without it — but it is **not sustainable as it stands**: salinisation, silting, water loss and a shrinking delta mean it needs lined canals, more efficient irrigation (drip and sprinkler), fairer water sharing and new storage.

### Moving towards a sustainable food future

**Sustainable food supply** means producing enough food for people today without damaging the environment or the ability of future generations to feed themselves.

| Approach | What it is | Why it is sustainable |
|---|---|---|
| **Organic farming** | no artificial fertilisers or pesticides; manure, compost, crop rotation, natural pest control | protects soil, rivers and wildlife; but yields are lower and food costs more |
| **Permaculture** | "permanent agriculture" — designing farms and gardens to work like natural ecosystems, e.g. **forest gardens** with layers of trees, shrubs and ground plants; mixing crops and animals; recycling all waste | little need for outside inputs; protects soil; high variety of food from a small area |
| **Urban farming initiatives** | growing food in towns and cities — allotments, rooftop gardens, community gardens, vertical farms | cuts food miles; uses waste land; involves communities. Examples: **Incredible Edible Todmorden** (West Yorkshire, since 2008) planted free-to-pick vegetables around the town; **Havana, Cuba**, developed large numbers of organic urban gardens (*organopónicos*) after losing Soviet imports in the 1990s |
| **Fish from sustainable sources** | fishing quotas, bans on damaging methods, net sizes that let young fish escape; buying fish with the **Marine Stewardship Council (MSC)** blue label; well-managed fish farming | stops overfishing so stocks can recover |
| **Meat from sustainable sources** | free-range and high-welfare farming; eating **less** meat; grass-fed livestock on land unsuitable for crops | reduces land, water and grain used per calorie, and greenhouse gas emissions |
| **Seasonal food consumption** | eating food grown locally when it is in season | avoids air freight and heated greenhouses, cutting carbon footprints |
| **Reduced waste and losses** | roughly **a third** of the food produced in the world is lost or wasted (FAO). In **LICs**, most is **lost** after harvest through poor storage, pests and transport; in **HICs**, most is **wasted** by shops and consumers | better storage (sealed grain bags, cool stores) in LICs; campaigns such as WRAP's **Love Food Hate Waste**, apps that sell surplus food cheaply, and supermarkets selling "wonky" vegetables in HICs |

### A local scheme in an LIC or NEE: sand dams in Makueni County, Kenya

**Location and problem**

- **Makueni County** is in **south-eastern Kenya** (an LIC/lower-middle income country), south-east of Nairobi. It is **semi-arid**: rain falls in two short rainy seasons (roughly March–May and October–December) and droughts are common.
- Rivers are **seasonal (ephemeral)** — they flow for a short time after rain and then dry up, leaving **sandy riverbeds**. For most of the year, many women and children used to walk long distances — several hours a day — to collect water, often from unsafe sources.
- Without water, farmers could grow little in the dry season, so families faced **food insecurity** and depended on food aid in bad years.

**What sand dams are and how they work**

1. A reinforced **concrete wall** a few metres high is built across a **seasonal sandy riverbed**, anchored to the bedrock.
2. When the river flows in the rainy season, it carries **sand**, which builds up behind the wall. Within a few rainy seasons the dam is filled with sand.
3. **Water is stored in the spaces between the sand grains** — sand can hold water in up to around a third or more of its volume. A mature sand dam can store up to about **40 million litres**.
4. Because the water is **below the surface**, it is protected from **evaporation** and from contamination; the sand **filters** it, making it cleaner.
5. People draw the water through a **pipe and hand pump** or dig scoop holes; the dam also **raises the water table** so nearby wells and gardens have water in the dry season.

Sand dams in Makueni and the neighbouring counties of Kitui and Machakos have been built by **local self-help groups** working with charities such as the **Africa Sand Dam Foundation** (founded in 2010) and the UK charity **Excellent Development**. The community provides the **labour and local materials** (sand, stones, water); the charity provides cement, steel and technical skills.

**Benefits**

- **Water for farming**: water all year for **kitchen gardens, tree nurseries and irrigated terraces**, so families grow vegetables, fruit (such as mangoes) and fodder even in the dry season → more and more varied food, better nutrition, and a surplus to sell.
- **Time saved**: the walk to water is cut from hours to minutes, so women can farm and run small businesses and children can go to school.
- **Health**: cleaner, filtered water reduces waterborne disease.
- **Environment**: farmers build **terraces** and plant trees alongside the dams, reducing soil erosion and recharging groundwater.
- **Resilience**: communities are better able to survive drought and need less food aid.

**Why it is sustainable**

- **Appropriate technology**: simple, low-cost, built with local labour and materials; no fuel or electricity needed.
- **Community ownership**: local groups decide where to build, maintain the dams and manage the water, so they keep working.
- **Long-lasting and low maintenance**, and they improve the environment rather than damaging it.

**Limitations**: sand dams only work where there is a seasonal sandy river over solid bedrock; they need outside funding for cement; they take a few seasons to fill; and in the worst droughts even a sand dam can run low. But compared with a large-scale scheme like the IBIS, they cause **no displacement, salinisation or conflict**.

::: terms
- **Irrigation**: artificially supplying water to crops by canals, pipes, sprinklers or drips where rainfall is too low or unreliable.
- **Hydroponics**: growing plants without soil, with their roots in a nutrient-rich water solution.
- **Aeroponics**: growing plants without soil, with their roots suspended in air and sprayed with a mist of water and nutrients.
- **Green Revolution**: the introduction of high-yielding crop varieties, fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation from the 1960s to raise food output, mainly in Asia and Mexico.
- **Biotechnology**: using living organisms and genetic science, including genetic modification, to improve crops and livestock.
- **Genetically modified (GM) crop**: a crop whose genes have been altered in a laboratory to give it a useful trait such as pest or drought resistance.
- **Appropriate technology**: technology suited to the needs, skills, knowledge and wealth of local people, usually cheap, simple and sustainable.
- **Salinisation**: the build-up of salt in the soil when irrigation water evaporates, reducing fertility.
- **Permaculture**: a way of farming that copies natural ecosystems to produce food sustainably with few outside inputs.
- **Sand dam**: a concrete wall across a seasonal sandy river that traps sand, storing water between the grains below the surface.
- **Sustainable food supply**: producing enough food now without damaging the environment or future generations' ability to feed themselves.
:::

::: method
**What the questions look like.** Paper 2, Section C, Food option. Expect short questions on a photo or diagram (a hydroponic farm, a sand dam, an irrigation canal), "explain" questions of 4 marks, and the big levels-marked questions: 6 marks, and a 9-marker (often +3 SPaG) such as "Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of a large-scale agricultural development" or "To what extent can local schemes increase food supply sustainably?"

**Command words:**
- **Describe** — say what a strategy is or what a figure shows, no reasons.
- **Explain** — how the strategy increases supply, and why; use "which means", "so".
- **Suggest** — apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar scheme in a photo or text.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** — weigh advantages against disadvantages and reach a judgement, with a reason.
- **Use Figure X** — you must quote the figure.

**Building a 4-mark answer**: two developed points. "Drip irrigation delivers water straight to the roots (1), so little is lost to evaporation (1), which means more crops can be grown with the same water (1) in dry areas such as..."

**Building a 6-mark levels answer** (Level 1: 1–2, Level 2: 3–4, Level 3: 5–6):
1. Two or three points that answer the exact question.
2. Develop each into a chain of reasoning.
3. **Use your case studies** — named places, dams, dates and facts. Generic answers stay in Level 2.

**Building a 9-mark answer** (Level 1: 1–3, Level 2: 4–6, Level 3: 7–9):
1. **Both sides** — advantages and disadvantages, or evidence for and against the statement — each developed.
2. **Case-study detail**: for the IBIS — Tarbela and Mangla, the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty, around 14 million hectares, waterlogging and salinisation, the shrinking delta, the Indus river dolphin; for Makueni — sand dams, 40 million litres, kitchen gardens, time saved, community ownership.
3. A **clear, justified judgement** — "Overall the IBIS has more advantages than disadvantages because… but it is not sustainable unless…".
4. Accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar and a wide range of specialist terms for the **3 SPaG marks**.

**"Sustainable" questions**: always show **why** something is sustainable — it can go on without damaging the environment, uses local resources, involves local people, and will still work in the future.
:::

::: example Explain a strategy
Explain how hydroponics can increase food supply. (4)

Hydroponics grows plants with their roots in a nutrient-rich water solution instead of soil (1), so crops can be grown in places with poor or no soil, such as cities or deserts (1). Plants are grown indoors under artificial light, so they can be harvested all year round (1), and trays can be stacked in vertical farms, producing much more food per square metre of land (1).
:::

::: example 6-mark — a local scheme
Using an example, explain how a local scheme in an LIC or NEE has increased sustainable supplies of food. (6)

In Makueni County in semi-arid south-eastern Kenya, local self-help groups have built sand dams with the Africa Sand Dam Foundation. A concrete wall is built across a seasonal riverbed; sand builds up behind it in the rainy season and stores water between the grains, up to around 40 million litres in a mature dam.

This increases food supply because farmers have water all year to irrigate kitchen gardens, tree nurseries and terraced fields, so they can grow vegetables and fruit in the dry season, improving their diet and giving them a surplus to sell. Women who used to walk for hours to collect water have more time to farm.

The scheme is sustainable because it uses appropriate technology: local labour and materials, no fuel, and little maintenance. The water is stored under the sand, so it does not evaporate, and the community owns and manages the dam, so it keeps working long after the charity leaves.

(Level 3: named place and scheme, how it increases supply and why it is sustainable, all developed.)
:::

::: example 9-mark — large-scale development
Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of a large-scale agricultural development you have studied. (9 + 3 SPaG)

The Indus Basin Irrigation System in Pakistan is the largest contiguous irrigation system in the world. After the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 gave Pakistan the western rivers, huge dams such as Tarbela and Mangla, 16 barrages and 12 link canals were built, and the system now irrigates around 14 million hectares.

The advantages are enormous. Most of Pakistan's wheat, rice, sugar cane and cotton is grown on irrigated land in the basin, so the system feeds a population of over 240 million and supports farming, which provides over a third of jobs. Cotton supplies the textile industry, Pakistan's biggest export, and Tarbela and Mangla generate a large share of the country's electricity.

However, there are serious disadvantages. Water seeping from unlined canals has raised the water table, causing waterlogging, and as water evaporates it leaves salt behind, so large areas of farmland have been damaged by salinisation. Silt from the Himalayas is filling the reservoirs, and so little water now reaches the sea that the Indus delta is shrinking, sea water is invading farmland and mangroves have been lost. Farmers at the tail end of canals often receive too little water, so the benefits are unequal.

Overall the advantages outweigh the disadvantages, because without the IBIS Pakistan could not feed itself. However, the disadvantages are growing — salinisation and silting are reducing the system's capacity, and the treaty it depends on was put "in abeyance" by India in 2025 — so it will only remain beneficial if canals are lined, more efficient irrigation is used and water is shared more fairly.

(Level 3: detailed, place-specific advantages and disadvantages with a clear, justified evaluation.)
:::

::: q
Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using GM crops to increase food supply. (4)
---
Advantage: crops can be made resistant to pests or drought (1), so yields are higher and more reliable, e.g. Bt cotton in India (1). Disadvantage: GM seeds are patented and expensive (1), so poor farmers may become dependent on large companies and fall into debt (1). Also accept concerns about cross-pollination and wildlife.
:::

::: q
Explain how irrigation can cause salinisation. (3)
---
Over-irrigation and seepage from unlined canals raise the water table (1). In hot conditions water evaporates from the soil surface (1), leaving behind the dissolved salts, which build up and make the soil less fertile (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why reducing food waste is a sustainable way to increase food supply. (4)
---
About a third of all food produced is lost or wasted (1), so reducing waste means more food is available without farming more land or using more water and fertiliser (1). In LICs, better storage prevents losses to pests and rot after harvest (1); in HICs, campaigns such as Love Food Hate Waste and apps selling surplus food cut household and shop waste (1).
:::

::: q
Assess the extent to which urban farming initiatives can provide sustainable food supplies. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): balanced, developed assessment with examples and a judgement. Level 2 (3–4): some developed points. Level 1 (1–2): simple points.
Indicative content: uses waste land and rooftops; cuts food miles and packaging; fresh local food; builds community (Incredible Edible Todmorden); Havana's organopónicos supplied much of the city's vegetables after the 1990s; vertical farms produce all year. ::: watch
- Writing about the IBIS (or any large-scale scheme) with **only** advantages or only disadvantages — "evaluate" needs both, and a judgement.
- Vague case studies: "a dam in Pakistan". Name Tarbela and Mangla, the Indus Waters Treaty (1960), waterlogging and salinisation, the shrinking delta.
- Describing how a sand dam works but not explaining **how it increases food supply** (water for gardens and terraces all year).
- Calling a scheme sustainable without saying **why** — local materials, community ownership, no fuel, no environmental damage.
- Mixing up hydroponics (roots in water) and aeroponics (roots in mist).
- Treating the new green revolution as just "GM crops" — it also includes sustainable methods and locally bred varieties.
:::
