## What it is

This lesson covers two spec points: a **case study of a hot desert** showing the **opportunities** (mineral extraction, energy, farming, tourism) and **challenges** (extreme temperatures, water supply, inaccessibility) of development; and **desertification** on the desert fringe — its **causes** and the **strategies** used to reduce the risk. The case study here is the **Sahara**, with most detail from **Algeria** and **Morocco**; the desertification example is the **Sahel**.

### The Sahara: location and context

- The **largest hot desert in the world**, covering about **9 million km²** — roughly the size of the USA — across North Africa.
- It stretches from the **Atlantic Ocean** in the west to the **Red Sea** in the east, and from the **Mediterranean coast and Atlas Mountains** in the north to the **Sahel** in the south.
- It covers parts of **11 countries**: Algeria, Chad, Egypt, Libya, Mali, Mauritania, Morocco, Niger, Sudan, Tunisia and Western Sahara.
- Its population is small and scattered, living mostly around oases, in mining towns or as nomadic herders (Tuareg, Berbers/Amazigh).

### Development opportunities

| Opportunity | Sahara examples | Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| **Mineral extraction — oil and gas** | **Algeria**: **Hassi Messaoud** oil field (discovered 1956) and **Hassi R'Mel** gas field; **In Amenas** gas plant. Pipelines carry gas under the Mediterranean to **Italy** (Trans-Mediterranean pipeline via Tunisia) and **Spain** (Medgaz). **Libya** also has large oil reserves. | Oil and gas earn most of Algeria's export income; jobs; tax revenue for schools, roads and housing |
| **Mineral extraction — phosphates and iron ore** | **Morocco** (with Western Sahara) holds around **70%** of the world's known **phosphate** reserves, used to make fertiliser; the **Bou Craa** mine sends rock to the coast on a conveyor belt about **100 km** long. **Mauritania** mines iron ore at **Zouérat** and carries it by one of the world's longest trains to the port of Nouadhibou. | Export earnings; phosphate fertiliser feeds world food production |
| **Energy — solar** | **Noor Ouarzazate Solar Complex**, Morocco: one of the world's largest concentrated solar power plants, about **580 MW** in four phases (Noor I opened **2016**), covering about **3,000 hectares**, supplying electricity for over a million homes. Proposals to export Saharan solar power to Europe (Desertec in 2009; the Xlinks Morocco–UK cable) show the potential, though none has been built. | Clear skies and over 3,000 hours of sunshine a year; cuts Morocco's reliance on imported fossil fuels; could earn export income |
| **Farming** | **Oases** grow dates, figs and vegetables under shade from date palms — Morocco's **Tafilalt** oasis and Algeria's **Biskra** region (famous for Deglet Nour dates). Traditional underground channels (**foggaras** in Algeria, **khettaras** in Morocco) carry groundwater to fields. Modern **irrigation** from aquifers grows tomatoes and wheat. Nomadic herding of goats, sheep and camels. | Food and income; exports of dates and early vegetables to Europe |
| **Tourism** | **Morocco**: camel treks and camping in the **Erg Chebbi** dunes near **Merzouga**, 4×4 tours from Marrakech and Ouarzazate, the **Marathon des Sables** (a roughly 250 km, six-day race run since 1986). **Tunisia**: Star Wars film sets near **Tozeur**. **Egypt**: the pyramids of Giza on the desert edge. | Jobs for guides, drivers, hotel staff; income for remote communities; foreign currency |

### Development challenges

| Challenge | How it affects development | How people respond |
|---|---|---|
| **Extreme temperatures** | Daytime temperatures often above **45 °C** in summer make outdoor work dangerous; machinery and vehicles overheat; **solar panels lose efficiency** in extreme heat and **dust and sand** coat them; tourists are put off in the hottest months | Work shifts at night or early morning; air-conditioned accommodation (costly, uses energy); panels cleaned regularly — which needs water; tourism concentrated in cooler months (October to April) |
| **Water supply** | Rain is under 100 mm a year in much of the Sahara; very high evaporation; mines, farms, towns and hotels all need water; many aquifers hold **fossil water** laid down thousands of years ago that is **not replaced** — pumping lowers water tables and dries up oases; conflict over who gets water | Deep boreholes; desalination on the coast; pipelines; drip irrigation; **Libya's Great Man-Made River** pipes fossil water from the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer to coastal cities — a huge project that is ultimately unsustainable |
| **Inaccessibility** | Vast distances, few roads, shifting sand dunes cover roads, extreme heat damages tarmac; building roads, pipelines and power lines is very expensive; hard to get workers, equipment and goods in and out; remote sites are harder to protect — in **January 2013** terrorists attacked the **In Amenas** gas plant in Algeria and dozens of hostages were killed | Air links to mining towns; pipelines and conveyor belts instead of roads; the Trans-Saharan Highway (still incomplete); workers flown in on rotas |

### Desertification

**Desertification** is the **degradation of land in dry (semi-arid) areas** so that it becomes more like a desert — soils lose fertility and structure, vegetation disappears and the land can no longer support farming. It happens on the **fringes** of hot deserts, not in the middle.

The **Sahel** is a semi-arid belt over **5,000 km** long running across Africa south of the Sahara, from **Senegal** and **Mauritania** on the Atlantic to **Sudan** and **Eritrea**, with roughly 200–600 mm of rain a year falling in one short wet season. It suffered severe **droughts** from the late 1960s to the mid-1980s that caused famine; rainfall has partly recovered since the 1990s but remains unreliable.

### Causes of desertification (all six are on the spec)

| Cause | How it causes desertification |
|---|---|
| **Climate change** | Less rainfall and less reliable rainfall, plus higher temperatures increasing **evaporation**; droughts kill vegetation, leaving soil bare and dry |
| **Population growth** | The Sahel's population is growing very fast — Niger has one of the highest fertility rates in the world, around six to seven children per woman — so more food, water, fuel and grazing are needed from the same land |
| **Removal of fuel wood** | Most rural households cook on wood; trees and shrubs are cut faster than they regrow; roots no longer bind the soil and there is no shade or leaf litter |
| **Overgrazing** | Too many cattle, goats and sheep eat the grass and shrubs faster than they recover; hooves **trample and compact** the soil |
| **Over-cultivation** | Land is farmed every year without a **fallow** period; nutrients are used up, soil structure breaks down, yields fall, so even more land is cleared |
| **Soil erosion** | The **result that makes it permanent**: bare, dry soil is blown away by wind and washed away by the heavy rain at the start of the wet season; the fertile topsoil goes and cannot be replaced quickly |

These causes form a **vicious cycle**: more people → more cutting, grazing and cultivating → bare soil → erosion → lower yields → even more land used → even more erosion. Drought then tips degraded land over the edge.

### Strategies to reduce the risk of desertification

| Strategy (spec wording) | Examples | How it works | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water management** | **Zai pits** in Burkina Faso — small pits dug in hard ground, filled with manure, seeds planted in them (spread by farmer **Yacouba Sawadogo**); drip irrigation; small earth dams; rainwater harvesting | Concentrates the little rain that falls on the roots of crops; less water lost to runoff and evaporation | Labour-intensive to dig each year |
| **Soil management** | **Magic stones** (stone lines or *diguettes*) in **Burkina Faso** — lines of stones laid along the **contours** of slopes; composting; crop rotation; leaving land fallow | Stone lines slow runoff, so water soaks in and soil and seeds are trapped behind them; yields rise significantly (figures of up to 50% are often quoted) | Needs many stones and community labour; simple and cheap otherwise |
| **Tree planting** | The **Great Green Wall** — launched by the African Union in **2007**, a band of trees and restored land almost **8,000 km** long from **Senegal to Djibouti**. Aims by **2030**: restore **100 million hectares** of degraded land, store **250 million tonnes** of carbon and create **10 million** rural jobs. Also **farmer-managed natural regeneration** in Niger, where farmers protect and prune tree stumps that regrow — about 5 million hectares have been re-greened | Tree roots bind soil; canopies give shade and reduce evaporation; leaf litter adds nutrients; windbreaks reduce wind erosion; trees provide fruit, fodder and fuel | A 2020 UN review found the Great Green Wall well behind schedule — under a fifth of its target area restored — due to lack of funding, conflict and seedlings dying; it has since become a mosaic of local projects rather than a literal wall |
| **Appropriate technology** | Magic stones and zai pits themselves; **solar cookers** and **fuel-efficient clay stoves**; hand pumps; drip irrigation from simple pipes | Cheap, simple technology local people can build, use and repair; efficient stoves and solar cookers cut the amount of fuel wood needed, so fewer trees are cut | Solar cookers only work in daylight and are slower; cultural preference for traditional cooking |

**Appropriate technology** means technology suited to the needs, skills, knowledge and wealth of local people — no expensive machines or imported parts. It is sustainable because communities can keep it going themselves.

::: terms
- **Mineral extraction**: removing valuable resources such as oil, gas, phosphate or iron ore from the ground.
- **Fossil water**: groundwater stored in aquifers thousands of years ago that is not being replaced by today's rainfall.
- **Inaccessibility**: being difficult to reach because of distance, poor roads and harsh terrain, which raises the cost of development.
- **Desertification**: the degradation of land in semi-arid areas so it becomes more like a desert and can no longer support farming.
- **Sahel**: the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara, stretching across Africa from Senegal to Sudan, at high risk of desertification.
- **Overgrazing**: keeping more animals on land than the vegetation can support, so plants are stripped and soil is trampled.
- **Over-cultivation**: farming land so intensively, without fallow periods, that soil nutrients and structure are exhausted.
- **Appropriate technology**: technology suited to the needs, skills and wealth of local people, cheap to build and easy to maintain.
- **Magic stones**: lines of stones laid along slope contours in Burkina Faso to slow runoff, trap soil and help water soak in.
- **Great Green Wall**: an African Union project launched in 2007 to restore degraded land across the Sahel from Senegal to Djibouti.
:::

::: method
1. **Name your case study** in the first line — "In the Sahara, Morocco's Noor Ouarzazate solar complex…". Every developed point needs a **place, fact or figure**. Generic answers ("deserts have oil") stay in Level 1.
2. **Opportunities / challenges (4–6 marks)**: point → develop (why it is an opportunity or challenge) → case-study detail. "Extreme temperatures (point) mean equipment overheats and solar panels lose efficiency (develop); at Noor Ouarzazate dust must be cleaned off mirrors using scarce water (case study)."
3. **Causes of desertification (4–6 marks)**: show the **process chain** ending in soil erosion. "Overgrazing → vegetation stripped → roots no longer bind soil → topsoil blown/washed away → land less fertile". Linking causes together (population growth drives overgrazing) lifts you into Level 3.
4. **Strategies (4–6 marks)**: name the strategy, say **how it works** physically (slows runoff, binds soil, cuts wood use), and give the **named example**. For "evaluate", add limitations.
5. **9-mark "to what extent" / "assess" questions**: two sides, each with case-study detail, then a **justified judgement** — "the opportunities outweigh the challenges *for Morocco* because… but *only if* water use is managed". The judgement must answer the actual question. Paper 1 Section B 9-markers may carry **+3 SPaG**.
6. **"Use Figure X"**: quote data from the figure (a figure, a location, a trend) before bringing in your own knowledge.
:::

::: example Opportunity — 4 marks
Using a case study of a hot desert, explain one opportunity for development. (4)

In the Sahara, Morocco has built the Noor Ouarzazate solar complex (1). The desert has clear skies and over 3,000 hours of sunshine a year, so concentrated solar power can generate large amounts of electricity (1). Noor is about 580 MW in total, first opened in 2016 (1), which reduces Morocco's dependence on imported fossil fuels and creates construction and maintenance jobs; in future, Saharan solar power could be exported to Europe (1).
:::

::: example Challenge — 6 marks
Using a case study of a hot desert, explain the challenges of developing hot desert environments. (6)

Water supply is the greatest challenge in the Sahara. Rainfall is under 100 mm a year in many places and evaporation is very high, so farms, mines, towns and hotels must pump groundwater. Much of this is fossil water that is not being replaced — Libya's Great Man-Made River pipes water from the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer — so water tables fall and oases can dry up.

Extreme temperatures above 45 °C in summer make outdoor work dangerous, so mining and construction often happen at night, and machinery overheats. Solar plants such as Noor Ouarzazate lose efficiency and their mirrors must be cleaned of dust, which uses more of the scarce water.

Inaccessibility adds cost: distances are vast, roads are few and sand dunes drift over them. Oil and gas from Hassi Messaoud must be piped hundreds of kilometres to the coast, and remote sites are hard to protect, as the 2013 attack on the In Amenas gas plant showed.

(Level 3: three challenges, each developed and supported with specific Sahara detail.)
:::

::: example Desertification strategy — 4 marks
Explain how one strategy can reduce the risk of desertification. (4)

In Burkina Faso, farmers place "magic stones" in lines along the contours of their fields (1). When the heavy rains come, the stone lines slow the runoff (1), so more water soaks into the soil instead of washing away, and soil and seeds are trapped behind the stones rather than being eroded (1). Because the technique is cheap and uses local materials and labour, it is appropriate technology that farmers can maintain themselves, and crop yields rise significantly (1).
:::

::: example Cause chain — 3 marks
Explain how population growth can lead to desertification. (3)

A growing population needs more food and fuel (1), so farmers keep more animals and cultivate land continuously without fallow periods, and more trees are cut for cooking (1). Vegetation is removed and soil is exhausted, so it is exposed to wind and rain and the fertile topsoil is eroded, leaving land that cannot support crops (1).
:::

---
Most people in the Sahel cook with wood, so trees and shrubs are cut faster than they regrow (1). Without roots the soil is not held together and without canopies it is not shaded (1), so it dries out and is eroded by wind and heavy rain (1).

- Desertification happens on the **desert fringe (the Sahel)**, not in the Sahara itself — and it is about **land degradation**, not "the desert moving".
- Causes need the **process** — end every chain with vegetation loss and **soil erosion**.
