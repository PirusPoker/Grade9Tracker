## What it is

The spec point is short but examined often, usually with a map: **an overview of the location of major upland and lowland areas and river systems in the UK**. You need to be able to name and locate them on an outline map, describe the pattern, and explain in simple terms why the UK's landscapes differ.

### The big pattern: the Tees–Exe line

Draw an imaginary line from the mouth of the **River Tees** in north-east England to the mouth of the **River Exe** in Devon. Roughly:

- **North and west of the line — uplands**: higher land (mostly above about 600 m in the highest parts), steep slopes, thin soils, older and harder rocks (**igneous** rocks such as granite and basalt, **metamorphic** rocks such as slate and schist, and hard sedimentary rocks such as carboniferous limestone and gritstone). These areas were also heavily shaped by **glaciers** during the last ice age.
- **South and east of the line — lowlands**: land mostly below 200 m, gently rolling hills and wide flat plains, younger and softer sedimentary rocks (**chalk**, **clay**, **sandstone**), deeper, more fertile soils.

The pattern is a generalisation: there are upland areas in the south-west (Dartmoor, Exmoor) and lowland areas in the north (the Vale of York, the Central Lowlands of Scotland).

### Major river systems

Rivers rise in the **uplands**, where rainfall is highest, and flow down to the sea. The main drainage divide in England is the **Pennines**: rivers to the east flow into the **North Sea**, rivers to the west flow into the **Irish Sea**.

| River | Source → mouth | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Severn** | Plynlimon, Cambrian Mountains (Wales) → Bristol Channel | **longest river in the UK**, about 354 km |
| **Thames** | Cotswolds → North Sea via the Thames Estuary (London) | about 346 km, the longest entirely in England |
| **Trent** | Staffordshire → joins the Ouse to form the **Humber** estuary | flows north, unusually |
| **Ouse (Yorkshire)** | fed by Pennine rivers (Swale, Ure, Wharfe, Aire) → Humber | drains much of Yorkshire |

### Why the pattern matters

- **Climate**: prevailing south-westerly winds bring moist air from the Atlantic; it rises over the western uplands and gives them heavy **relief rainfall** — parts of the Lake District receive over 2,000 mm a year, while London receives around 600 mm. The east and south-east are drier and warmer in summer.
- **Rivers**: upland rivers are fast-flowing with steep gradients (waterfalls, V-shaped valleys); lowland rivers are wider and slower, with meanders, floodplains and estuaries.
- **Land use**: uplands — sheep farming, forestry, reservoirs, national parks and tourism, few large settlements; lowlands — arable and dairy farming, most of the population, big cities, transport routes.
- **Coasts**: hard rocks in the north and west produce high cliffs and rocky headlands; softer rocks in the south and east erode faster, giving rapid retreat (for example the Holderness coast) and wide beaches.

### How it is examined

Typically a **map of the UK** with shaded relief or numbered areas: "Name upland area A", "Name river B", "Describe the distribution of upland areas", or "Use Figure 1 to compare…". Practise by sketching an outline of the UK and marking: the Grampians, Southern Uplands, Lake District, Pennines, Snowdonia, Brecon Beacons, Dartmoor; the Fens, London Basin, Somerset Levels; and the Severn, Thames, Trent, Tees, Tyne, Tay.

::: terms
- **Upland**: an area of high land, generally above about 600 m in its highest parts, with steep slopes and thin soils.
- **Lowland**: an area of low, flat or gently rolling land, generally below about 200 m.
- **Tees–Exe line**: an imaginary line from the mouth of the River Tees to the mouth of the River Exe that roughly divides upland Britain (north and west) from lowland Britain (south and east).
- **River system**: a main river together with all its tributaries, draining a drainage basin.
- **Drainage basin**: the area of land drained by a river and its tributaries.
- **Watershed**: the boundary between two drainage basins, usually a ridge of high land such as the Pennines.
- **Igneous rock**: rock formed from cooled magma or lava, such as granite or basalt — usually hard and resistant.
- **Sedimentary rock**: rock formed from layers of sediment, such as chalk, clay or sandstone — often softer and more easily eroded.
- **Relief rainfall**: rain caused when moist air is forced to rise over high ground, cools and condenses.
:::

::: method
1. **Name / identify (1 mark each)**: use the exact name — "the Pennines", not "the hills in the middle". Spell river and upland names correctly.
2. **Describe the distribution (2–4 marks)**: give the **overall pattern** ("uplands are mainly in the north and west"), use **compass directions** and **named examples** ("the Grampians in northern Scotland, the Lake District in north-west England"), then an **exception** ("Dartmoor in the south-west"). Do not explain unless asked.
3. **Explain (2–4 marks)**: link to **rock type** (older, harder igneous and metamorphic rocks in the north and west resist erosion) and past **glaciation**; for rivers, link to **relief** and rainfall (rivers rise in wet uplands, flow downhill to the sea).
4. **Compare**: use "whereas" or "however" and give both sides in the same sentence.
5. **"Use Figure X"**: quote heights, names or grid references from the figure. Answers that ignore the figure are capped.
6. Longer answers on this topic are rare, but it is often the opening part of a question on coasts or rivers — link your named river or coast to its landscape.
:::

::: example Describe distribution
Describe the distribution of upland areas in the UK. (3)

Upland areas are mainly in the north and west of the UK, roughly north-west of a line from the Tees to the Exe (1). For example, the Grampian Mountains in northern Scotland, the Lake District in north-west England and Snowdonia in north Wales (1). There are some exceptions in the south-west, such as Dartmoor and Exmoor (1).
:::

::: example Explain the pattern
Suggest why the north and west of the UK are more upland than the south and east. (3)

The north and west are made of older, harder igneous and metamorphic rocks, such as granite and slate (1), which are more resistant to erosion and weathering, so they have been worn down less (1). The south and east have younger, softer sedimentary rocks such as clay and chalk, which erode more easily, leaving lower, flatter land (1).
:::

::: example Rivers
Explain why many rivers in northern England flow east towards the North Sea. (2)

The Pennines form a watershed running north–south down northern England (1); rivers rising on the eastern side, such as the Tees and the Tyne, flow downhill eastwards to the North Sea (1).
:::

::: example Compare
Compare upland and lowland areas of the UK. (4)

Upland areas are higher, often above 600 m, whereas lowland areas are mostly below 200 m (1). Uplands have steep slopes and thin, poor soils, whereas lowlands have gentle slopes and deeper, more fertile soils (1). Uplands receive more rainfall, over 2,000 mm a year in parts of the Lake District, whereas the south-east lowlands receive around 600 mm (1). Uplands are used for sheep farming, forestry and tourism, whereas lowlands have arable farming and most of the population (1).
:::

::: q
What is meant by the Tees–Exe line? (2)
---
An imaginary line from the mouth of the River Tees to the mouth of the River Exe (1) that roughly separates upland Britain to the north and west from lowland Britain to the south and east (1).

::: q
Describe the location of the Pennines. (2)
---
A chain of hills running north to south through the middle of northern England (1), from near the Scottish border down to the Peak District in Derbyshire (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why upland areas in the UK receive more rainfall than lowland areas. (3)
---
Prevailing south-westerly winds bring moist air from the Atlantic (1). The air is forced to rise over the uplands of the west, cools and its water vapour condenses (1), producing relief rainfall; by the time the air reaches the lowlands of the east it has lost much of its moisture (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Describe** means the pattern with named examples and compass directions — not an explanation.
- The **North and South Downs and the Chilterns are lowland** chalk hills, not upland areas.
- Be precise with location: "the Lake District is in north-west England (Cumbria)", not "up north".
- The Severn is the **longest** UK river; the Tay has the **greatest discharge** — do not mix them up.
- The Tees–Exe line is a **generalisation** — Dartmoor and Exmoor are exceptions worth naming.
:::

::: test
Suggest two reasons why the landscapes of the north and west of the UK differ from those of the south and east. (4)
---
Rock type (1): older, harder igneous and metamorphic rocks in the north and west resist erosion, forming uplands, while softer chalk and clay in the south-east erode into lowlands (1). Glaciation (1): ice sheets carved deep valleys and steep mountains in the north and west, while the south was less affected (1). Also accept climate/relief rainfall making upland rivers erosive (1+1).

::: test
Explain how the location of upland and lowland areas influences the UK's major river systems. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): detailed explanation with clear links between relief, rainfall and river systems; named rivers and uplands used accurately.
Level 2 (3–4): some explanation with some named examples.
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements, few or no named examples.
Indicative content: rivers rise in wet uplands where relief rainfall is heaviest (Plynlimon for the Severn and Wye; Cross Fell for the Tees); the Pennines act as a watershed — the Tees, Tyne and Yorkshire Ouse flow east to the North Sea, the Mersey, Ribble and Eden flow west; rivers flow from high to low land, so they cross the lowlands of the south and east to reach the sea — the Thames through the London Basin, the Great Ouse through the Fens to the Wash; upland stretches are steep and fast with waterfalls (High Force), lowland stretches are wide and slow with meanders, floodplains and estuaries (Humber, Thames Estuary).
:::

::: test
"The Tees–Exe line is a useful way of describing the UK's physical landscape." To what extent do you agree? (9)
---
Level 3 (7–9): detailed evaluation using named upland and lowland areas and exceptions; a clear, well-supported judgement.
Level 2 (4–6): some evaluation with named examples; judgement partly supported.
Level 1 (1–3): simple description; little evaluation or no judgement.
Indicative content — agree: most major uplands lie north-west of the line (Grampians, Southern Uplands, Lake District, Pennines, Snowdonia, Brecon Beacons); most major lowlands lie south-east (Fens, East Anglia, London Basin); it matches the rock pattern (old, hard igneous/metamorphic rocks vs young, soft sedimentary rocks) and rainfall pattern. Disagree: exceptions — Dartmoor and Exmoor uplands in the south-west; lowlands in the north such as the Vale of York, the Cheshire Plain and the Central Lowlands of Scotland; chalk hills (Downs, Chilterns) in the south-east add relief; it ignores Northern Ireland (Mourne Mountains, Lough Neagh basin). Judgement: e.g. useful as a broad generalisation for Great Britain but too simple to describe local detail.
:::
