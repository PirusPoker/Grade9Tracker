## What it is

Key idea: **management can reduce the effects of a tectonic hazard.** The spec has two points:

1. **Reasons why people continue to live in areas at risk from a tectonic hazard.**
2. **How monitoring, prediction, protection and planning can reduce the risks from a tectonic hazard.**

Hundreds of millions of people live near active volcanoes or on major fault lines. Most of them are not unaware of the danger: they have made a choice, or have little choice, and they rely on management to keep the risk acceptable.

### Why people continue to live in areas at risk

Group your reasons into **economic**, **social** and **perception** reasons. Each needs a named illustration.

| Reason | How it works | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **Fertile soils** | Weathered volcanic ash and lava produce soils rich in minerals, ideal for farming. | The slopes of **Mount Etna** in Sicily support vineyards, olive groves, citrus orchards and the famous pistachios grown around Bronte. |
| **Geothermal energy** | Hot rocks near the surface heat water and make steam, which is used to generate electricity and heat homes cheaply and cleanly. | In **Iceland** about 90% of homes are heated by geothermal energy, and geothermal plants produce around a quarter to a third of its electricity. |
| **Tourism** | Volcanoes, hot springs, geysers and dramatic landscapes attract visitors, creating jobs in hotels, guiding, transport and restaurants. | Etna has cable cars, jeep tours and guided walks to the craters; Iceland's Blue Lagoon and geysers draw large numbers of tourists. |
| **Minerals** | Volcanic activity brings valuable minerals near the surface. | Sulphur is mined by hand at Kawah Ijen in Indonesia; copper and gold are mined in the Andes. |
| **Jobs and economic opportunity** | Major cities that happen to lie on fault lines offer jobs, services and high incomes. | San Francisco and Los Angeles on the San Andreas fault system; Tokyo on a highly active plate boundary. |
| **Family, culture and home** | People have lived there for generations, own land and homes, and have strong community ties. | Villages on Etna's slopes have been rebuilt after lava flows rather than abandoned. |
| **Poverty / no choice** | Poorer people cannot afford to move, or safer land is too expensive or already occupied. | Many rural families in Nepal have nowhere else to farm. |
| **Perception of risk** | Major eruptions and earthquakes are infrequent, so people think "it won't happen in my lifetime". Some are confident that monitoring, prediction and strong buildings will protect them. | Japan's residents trust building codes and early warnings; Etna usually erupts effusively rather than explosively. |

The best answers note that the **balance of reasons differs with wealth**: in richer places people often *choose* to stay because the benefits are high and management reduces the risk (Iceland, Japan, California); in poorer places people often stay because they *cannot afford* to leave.

### How management reduces risk: the four approaches

Remember them as **MPPP**: **M**onitoring, **P**rediction, **P**rotection, **P**lanning.

#### 1. Monitoring

**Monitoring** means using scientific equipment to detect **warning signs** that an eruption or earthquake may happen. It works far better for **volcanoes** than for earthquakes, because volcanoes usually give warning signs as magma rises.

| Technique | What it detects | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Seismometers** | many small earthquakes | Magma forcing its way upwards cracks the rock, causing swarms of small tremors. |
| **Tiltmeters and GPS** | the ground bulging or tilting | Rising magma pushes the volcano's sides outwards. |
| **Satellites** (radar and thermal imaging) | ground deformation and heat changes across the whole volcano | Detects changes in remote or dangerous places without needing people on site. |
| **Gas sampling / spectrometers** | increased sulphur dioxide and other gases | Gas escapes as magma rises towards the surface. |
| **Lahar detectors, water sampling** | mudflows; changes in groundwater temperature and chemistry | Gives warning of secondary hazards. |

Mount Etna is one of the most closely watched volcanoes in the world: Italy's national institute of geophysics and volcanology (INGV) runs an observatory in Catania with a dense network of seismometers, GPS stations, gas sensors and cameras.

For **earthquakes**, seismometers record movements and scientists measure stress build-up along faults, but there is **no reliable warning sign** that an earthquake is about to happen.

#### 2. Prediction

**Prediction** means using monitoring data and past records to **forecast when and where** a hazard will happen.

- **Volcanoes** can often be forecast within days or weeks. In **1991**, scientists monitoring **Mount Pinatubo** in the Philippines forecast its huge eruption in time for tens of thousands of people to be evacuated; the US Geological Survey estimates this saved **at least 5,000 lives**.
- **Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely.** Scientists can only give **probabilities** for a region over decades, using the history of past quakes and "seismic gaps" (stretches of a fault that have not moved for a long time and so have built up stress). Folk signs such as unusual animal behaviour are unreliable.
- What *is* possible is an **earthquake early warning**: seismometers near the epicentre detect the first, faster seismic waves and send an automatic alert before the more damaging waves arrive, giving a few seconds to a minute. **Japan** broadcasts these alerts to phones, TV and radio, and bullet trains brake automatically.

#### 3. Protection

**Protection** means designing buildings and structures to **withstand** the hazard, and physically defending areas against it.

**Earthquake-resistant buildings** (a labelled sketch of a building is a useful way to show these):
- **Rubber shock absorbers / base isolation** in the foundations let the ground move without the building moving as much.
- **Steel frames** that can bend and sway without breaking; **cross-bracing** (diagonal steel beams) to stiffen walls.
- **Counterweights or dampers** at the top of tall buildings to reduce sway. Tokyo Skytree has a central concrete column inspired by traditional pagodas.
- **Reinforced concrete** and deep foundations; light, flexible materials; windows made of laminated glass so they do not shatter onto the street.
- **Retrofitting** older buildings, bridges and schools with bracing.
- **Automatic shut-off** of gas and electricity to reduce fires.

**Other protection:**
- **Tsunami defences**: after the 2011 earthquake and tsunami, which left around 18,000 dead or missing, Japan built hundreds of kilometres of higher sea walls along its north-east coast.
- **Lava diversion**: on **Etna**, explosives were used in **1983** to divert a lava flow, and in **1992** earth barriers were built and concrete blocks were dropped from helicopters to slow a flow threatening the town of **Zafferana Etnea**.
- **Cooling lava**: on the Icelandic island of **Heimaey** in **1973**, seawater was pumped onto advancing lava to cool and solidify it, helping to save the harbour on which the town's fishing industry depended.
- **Earth barriers**: in 2023–24, Iceland built large earth barriers around the town of **Grindavík** and the Svartsengi geothermal power station to divert lava from a series of eruptions on the Reykjanes peninsula.

#### 4. Planning

**Planning** means **organising** land use and people so that the impact is smaller when the hazard happens.

- **Hazard mapping and land-use zoning**: maps show the areas most at risk (from lava flows, lahars, tsunamis or strong shaking); planners ban or restrict building in the most dangerous zones and keep hospitals and schools out of them.
- **Exclusion zones and evacuation plans**: clear routes, signs and transport; in November 2023 the whole town of **Grindavík** (about 3,700 people) was evacuated as magma moved beneath it.
- **Education and drills**: in **Japan**, schools and workplaces hold regular earthquake drills, and **Disaster Prevention Day** on 1 September (the anniversary of the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake) is marked with nationwide practice.
- **Emergency supplies**: families keep emergency kits (water, food, torch, radio, first aid); governments stockpile tents, food and medicines.
- **Training emergency services** and planning how aid will be distributed.
- **Building codes** set by law (this overlaps with protection).

### How effective is management? (for evaluation answers)

| Approach | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Monitoring** | gives warning of most eruptions; data improve over time | expensive equipment and scientists; unreliable for earthquakes; some volcanoes erupt with little warning |
| **Prediction** | allows evacuation, saving thousands of lives (Pinatubo 1991) | earthquakes cannot be predicted; false alarms make people ignore future warnings; poorer countries lack the equipment |
| **Protection** | saves lives *every* time the hazard strikes, even without warning | very expensive to build and retrofit; poorer countries struggle to afford or enforce it; defences can be overtopped (Japan's 2011 tsunami overtopped many walls) |
| **Planning** | cheap and effective: drills, kits and zoning save many lives | relies on people following plans; hard to enforce zoning where land is scarce or people are poor |

A good judgement: **protection and planning** are usually most effective for **earthquakes** (because they cannot be predicted), while **monitoring and prediction** matter most for **volcanoes**. All four work best together, and wealth decides how much of each a country can afford.

::: terms
- **Monitoring**: using scientific equipment to detect warning signs that a hazard such as a volcanic eruption may happen.
- **Prediction**: using monitoring data and past records to forecast when and where a hazard will happen.
- **Protection**: designing buildings and structures to withstand a hazard, or defending areas against it.
- **Planning**: organising land use and people, through hazard maps, drills and evacuation plans, to reduce the impact of a hazard.
- **Geothermal energy**: heat from inside the Earth used to generate electricity and heat homes.
- **Hazard map**: a map showing the areas most at risk from a hazard, used to control where people build.
- **Retrofitting**: adding earthquake-resistant features such as steel bracing to existing buildings.
- **Earthquake early warning**: an automatic alert sent when the first seismic waves are detected, giving seconds of warning before strong shaking arrives.
- **Exclusion zone**: an area around a hazard that people are not allowed to enter or live in.
:::

::: method
**"Give reasons why people live near volcanoes / on fault lines" (4–6 marks).**
- Give **two or three** reasons and **develop each**: reason → why it benefits people → a named place. "Volcanic soils are fertile (point) because weathered ash is rich in minerals (develop), so farmers on Mount Etna's slopes grow vines, olives and citrus fruit (example)."
- Show variety: an economic reason, a social reason and a perception reason.

**"Explain how monitoring / prediction / protection / planning reduce risk" (4–6 marks).**
- Always finish the chain with **how lives or property are saved**: "…so people can be evacuated before the eruption" or "…so the building sways rather than collapsing".
- Keep the four approaches **separate**: monitoring (detect signs), prediction (forecast), protection (build to withstand), planning (organise people and land). Mixing them up loses marks.
- Tie each point to a real example: Pinatubo, Etna, Japan, Iceland.

**"Assess / evaluate the effectiveness of management" (9 marks).**
- Weigh strengths *and* limitations of at least two approaches.
- Bring in wealth: richer places can afford all four; poorer ones rely more on cheap planning.
- End with a **judgement** that names which approach is most effective *for which hazard*. Some 9-markers carry **+3 SPaG**.

**Level summary.** 4 marks: Level 2 is 3–4 (developed and linked). 6 marks: Level 3 is 5–6 (developed, examples, clear links to reducing risk). 9 marks: Level 3 is 7–9 (balanced, detailed, justified judgement).
:::

::: example Reasons for living near a volcano
Suggest two reasons why people continue to live close to active volcanoes such as Mount Etna. (4)

Volcanic soils are very fertile because weathered lava and ash are rich in minerals (1), so farmers on Etna's lower slopes can grow vines, olives, citrus fruit and pistachios for a good income (1). Tourism also provides jobs (1): visitors come to see the craters, so local people work as guides and in hotels, cable-car and jeep-tour businesses (1).
:::

::: example Explain how monitoring reduces risk
Explain how monitoring can reduce the risk from volcanic eruptions. (4)

Seismometers detect swarms of small earthquakes caused by magma rising and cracking the rock (1), and tiltmeters, GPS and satellites detect the ground bulging as magma collects beneath the volcano (1). Gas sensors record increases in sulphur dioxide as magma nears the surface (1). These warning signs allow scientists to forecast an eruption and authorities to evacuate people in time, as at Mount Pinatubo in 1991, when evacuation is estimated to have saved at least 5,000 lives (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate management
"Protection is the most effective way to reduce the risks from earthquakes." Do you agree? (9)

Protection is very effective because earthquakes cannot be predicted, so buildings must survive without warning. In Japan, modern buildings have rubber shock absorbers in their foundations, steel frames and cross-bracing, so they sway rather than collapse; Chile's enforced building codes help explain why its magnitude 8.8 earthquake in 2010 killed about 500 people, while weak buildings in Nepal contributed to about 9,000 deaths in 2015. Protection also works every time the ground shakes, whereas a warning may come too late.

However, protection is expensive. Building and retrofitting to high standards costs a great deal, so poorer countries such as Nepal struggle to afford or enforce it. Defences can also be overwhelmed: in 2011 Japan's tsunami overtopped many sea walls. Planning is much cheaper and also saves lives: Japan's school drills and Disaster Prevention Day teach people to shelter under desks and evacuate to high ground, and hazard maps keep schools and hospitals out of the most dangerous zones. Earthquake early warnings in Japan give seconds of warning to stop trains and shut off gas.

Overall I agree to a large extent. Because earthquakes cannot be predicted, protection is the most effective single strategy, but only where a country can afford it; for poorer countries, planning and education may give more lives saved for the money. The most effective approach combines protection and planning, as Japan does.
:::

::: watch
- **Monitoring ≠ prediction.** Monitoring collects the data (seismometers, tiltmeters, gas); prediction uses it to forecast. Keep them separate.
- Do not claim earthquakes can be predicted. Only probabilities and a few seconds of early warning are possible.
- Every management point must end with **how it reduces risk**: "so people can evacuate", "so the building does not collapse".
- "People live there because they like it" earns nothing. Give a specific benefit and a named place.
- In evaluations, bring in **cost and wealth**: the best protection is often unaffordable in LICs.
:::
