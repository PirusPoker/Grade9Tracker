## What it is

A **flood** happens when a river's **discharge** is too great for its channel to hold, so water spills over the banks onto the surrounding land (the **flood plain**). Flooding is a natural process, but it becomes a hazard when people live, work and travel on flood plains. This topic covers three things: the **physical and human factors that raise or lower flood risk**, how **hydrographs** show the link between rainfall and river discharge, and the **costs and benefits of hard and soft engineering** — illustrated by the **Banbury flood alleviation scheme** on the River Cherwell.

### How rain reaches a river

When rain falls, some is caught by plants (**interception**), some soaks into the soil (**infiltration**) and then moves slowly sideways (**throughflow**) or deeper into rock (**percolation** and **groundwater flow**), and some flows over the ground surface (**surface runoff** or overland flow). **Surface runoff is fastest**; groundwater flow is slowest. Anything that makes more water run off the surface, faster, raises the flood risk.

### Factors affecting flood risk

| Factor | Increases flood risk when… | Reduces flood risk when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Precipitation** (physical) | rainfall is **intense** (a lot in a short time — e.g. a summer thunderstorm) so it falls faster than it can infiltrate; or **prolonged** (many days) so the soil becomes **saturated** and all further rain runs off; or **snow melts** quickly, especially on frozen ground | rain is light and spread out, so it can soak in steadily |
| **Geology** (physical) | rocks are **impermeable** (e.g. granite, clay) — water cannot soak in, so it runs off quickly as surface runoff | rocks are **permeable** (e.g. chalk, limestone) — water percolates into the rock and reaches the river slowly as groundwater |
| **Relief** (physical) | valley sides are **steep** — water runs off quickly downhill with little time to infiltrate | the land is gentle or flat — water moves slowly and has more time to soak in |
| **Land use** (human) | **urbanisation**: concrete, tarmac and roofs are impermeable, and drains carry water straight to the river; **deforestation**: less interception and less water taken up by roots; ploughing **downslope** channels water; overgrazing compacts soil | the land is **forested** — trees intercept rain and take up water; fields are ploughed along contours; urban areas use permeable surfaces, green roofs and storage ponds |

The worst case combines them: heavy prolonged rain, on saturated impermeable ground, in a steep urbanised catchment. **Human factors can also make flooding worse downstream** — straightening a river upstream speeds water to the next town.

### Hydrographs

A **flood (storm) hydrograph** shows how a river's **discharge** changes over time after a period of rain. It is drawn as two graphs sharing the same time axis: a **bar chart of rainfall** (mm) and a **line graph of discharge** (cumecs).

Key parts (learn to label them):

- **Peak rainfall** — the time of heaviest rainfall (tallest bar).
- **Base flow** — the normal, steady discharge supplied by groundwater before the storm.
- **Rising limb** — the steep upward part of the discharge line as runoff reaches the river.
- **Peak discharge** — the highest discharge reached.
- **Lag time** — the time between **peak rainfall** and **peak discharge**.
- **Falling (recession) limb** — the downward part as discharge returns towards normal.
- **Storm flow** — the extra discharge above base flow caused by the storm.

**Two types of hydrograph**:

| "Flashy" hydrograph (high flood risk) | "Subdued" / gentle hydrograph (lower flood risk) |
|---|---|
| **short lag time**, **steep rising limb**, **high peak discharge** | **long lag time**, **gentle rising limb**, **lower peak discharge** |
| caused by: intense rain, saturated soil, impermeable rock, steep slopes, urban land use, few trees, small circular drainage basin | caused by: light rain, dry soil, permeable rock, gentle slopes, forest, large elongated drainage basin |

**Reading a hydrograph** in the exam: quote the times and values ("peak rainfall 12 mm at 06:00; peak discharge 45 cumecs at 18:00; lag time 12 hours"). **Lag time = time of peak discharge minus time of peak rainfall** — always give the unit (hours).

### Hard engineering

**Hard engineering** means building artificial structures to control the river. It is usually effective but **expensive**, can **damage the environment** and can move the problem **downstream**.

| Strategy | What it is | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dams and reservoirs** | a barrier across the valley holds water in a reservoir, released gradually | control discharge and reduce flood peaks downstream; reservoir can also supply drinking water, **hydroelectric power** and recreation | very expensive; flood large areas of land (farms, homes, habitats) when built; trap sediment, so rivers downstream erode more; people may need to be relocated; risk of catastrophic failure |
| **Straightening** (channelisation) | cutting through meanders to make the channel straighter (sometimes lining it with concrete) | water flows away faster, so it does not build up and flood that area; easier for boats to navigate | increases flood risk **further downstream** because water arrives faster; destroys natural habitats; faster water can increase erosion; costly to maintain |
| **Embankments** | raising the height of the river banks (artificial levées) so the channel can hold more water | river can hold more water before flooding; relatively cheap per metre; can double as footpaths | can look unnatural; if they are overtopped or breached the flooding can be sudden and severe, and water is then trapped behind them; can increase flooding downstream |
| **Flood relief channels** | an artificial channel that takes excess water around a town when discharge is high, e.g. the **Jubilee River** relief channel on the Thames near Maidenhead (opened 2002) | removes water quickly from the main channel, protecting the town; gates control the flow; can create new wetland habitats | expensive to build; if water levels rise very high, the relief channel may also flood; can pass flood risk to places downstream |

### Soft engineering

**Soft engineering** works **with** natural processes, reducing flood risk or the damage floods do rather than trying to stop the river. It is usually **cheaper and more sustainable** but may not protect places as completely.

| Strategy | What it is | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flood warnings and preparation** | the **Environment Agency** monitors rainfall and river levels and issues three levels of warning (**flood alert**, **flood warning**, **severe flood warning**) by phone, text and online; people prepare with sandbags, flood boards, raised electrical sockets and emergency kits | cheap; gives people time to move possessions upstairs and evacuate, saving lives and reducing damage | does not stop flooding; relies on people receiving and acting on warnings; some people ignore warnings or cannot respond (elderly, disabled, no phone signal); preparation does not protect land |
| **Flood plain zoning** | planners **restrict what land use is allowed** on the flood plain: high-risk areas kept for low-value uses such as parks, playing fields and farmland; housing and hospitals placed on higher ground | reduces the cost of damage; lets the flood plain flood naturally, storing water; no expensive structures | limits development where land is in demand; cannot help buildings already on the flood plain; can lower land values |
| **Planting trees** (afforestation) | planting trees in the drainage basin, especially upstream | trees **intercept** rain and take it up through roots, increasing **lag time** and lowering peak discharge; reduces soil erosion; creates habitats and absorbs carbon | takes years before trees are big enough to make a difference; land can no longer be farmed; works best for moderate floods, not extreme ones |
| **River restoration** | returning a straightened river to its **natural course** — restoring meanders, removing concrete, allowing wetlands on the flood plain to flood naturally | slows water down and stores it on the flood plain, reducing flooding downstream; improves habitats and wildlife; attractive for recreation | land on the flood plain will flood more often, so it cannot be built on; the local area may flood more; can be costly to reshape the channel |

### Example: the Banbury flood alleviation scheme

**Why the scheme was required**

- **Banbury** is a market town in north **Oxfordshire** on the **River Cherwell**, a tributary of the River Thames. Much of the town, including homes, businesses, the **railway station** and the **A361** road, lies on the flood plain.
- In **1998** (the Easter floods) Banbury flooded badly: the railway station was closed, roads were cut off, and damage was estimated at about **£12.5 million**. The town flooded again in **2007**.
- The Environment Agency judged the risk too high to leave, especially because flooding here disrupts a key rail link and a major road.

**The management strategy** (completed **2012**, cost about **£18.5 million**)

- A **flood storage area**: an earth **embankment about 3 km long and up to 4.5 m high**, built alongside the **M40 motorway** just upstream (north) of the town, creating a temporary reservoir able to hold about **3 million cubic metres** of floodwater. When the Cherwell rises, **flow-control structures** hold water back in the storage area and release it **slowly** over the following days, so the river through Banbury never exceeds what its channel can carry. The land is normally used as farmland and only floods occasionally.
- **Raising the A361** road so it stays open during floods.
- Smaller **embankments and flood walls** protecting properties, and a new **pumping station** to deal with water trapped behind the defences.
- A **biodiversity area**: new ponds, hedgerows, trees and wetland, including a pond created where earth was dug out for the embankment.
- Funded by the Environment Agency with contributions from local partners.

**Social, economic and environmental issues**

| | Positive | Negative / issues |
|---|---|---|
| **Social** | about **441 homes** now protected; less worry about flooding and better access to flood insurance; the A361 and railway stay open, so people can still get to work and school; new footpaths and green space improve quality of life | disruption and noise during construction; some residents felt the scheme took a long time to arrive after the 1998 flood |
| **Economic** | **73 commercial properties** protected; benefits estimated at over **£100 million** against a cost of £18.5 million; property values and business confidence protected; rail and road links (vital for commuters and freight) no longer cut | high up-front cost to the taxpayer; farmland in the storage area will occasionally be flooded, affecting farming |
| **Environmental** | new wetland, ponds and hedgerows created, increasing biodiversity; the flood plain upstream still floods naturally, storing water | roughly **100,000 tonnes of earth** moved to build the embankment, disturbing habitats and landscape; concrete control structures look unnatural |

**Judgement**: Banbury is widely seen as a success — a **benefit-to-cost ratio well above 5:1**, combining hard engineering (embankment, raised road, walls) with softer elements (using the flood plain for storage, habitat creation). Its limit is that it is designed for a particular size of flood; a more extreme event could still overwhelm it.

::: terms
- **Flood**: when a river's discharge exceeds the capacity of its channel and water spills onto the surrounding land.
- **Surface runoff**: water flowing over the ground surface to a river, the fastest route rain takes.
- **Impermeable**: describes rock or surfaces that do not let water pass through them, such as clay, granite and concrete.
- **Hydrograph**: a graph showing how the discharge of a river changes over time in response to rainfall.
- **Lag time**: the time between peak rainfall and peak discharge on a hydrograph.
- **Peak discharge**: the highest discharge reached by a river after a period of rain.
- **Base flow**: the normal discharge of a river, fed by groundwater, before a storm.
- **Hard engineering**: building artificial structures such as dams and embankments to control rivers.
- **Soft engineering**: working with natural processes to reduce flood risk or its effects, such as zoning or tree planting.
- **Flood plain zoning**: restricting land uses on a flood plain so that high-value buildings are kept away from the areas most at risk.
- **River restoration**: returning a modified river to a more natural state so it can flood naturally and slow the water down.
- **Flood relief channel**: an artificial channel that takes excess water away from a river around a settlement when discharge is high.
:::

::: method
1. **Hydrograph questions** ("Use Figure X"): quote exact values with units. Lag time = time of peak discharge − time of peak rainfall. For "compare" questions, contrast both graphs directly ("River A's lag time is 6 hours shorter and its peak discharge 30 cumecs higher").
2. **"Explain why the hydrograph is flashy/subdued"**: link each factor to **runoff speed** — impermeable → water cannot infiltrate → rapid surface runoff → shorter lag time. A factor alone ("it is urban") is a point; the chain is the development.
3. **Factor questions**: say whether the factor is **physical** (precipitation, geology, relief) or **human** (land use) if asked, and explain the mechanism.
4. **Costs and benefits**: for each strategy give at least one **benefit** and one **cost**, and make them *specific* ("increases flood risk downstream because water reaches the next town faster", not "bad for the environment").
5. **Scheme questions (Banbury)**: the spec asks for **why required**, **the strategy**, and **social, economic and environmental issues**. Use numbers: 1998 and 2007 floods, £12.5 million damage, 2012, £18.5 million, 3 km embankment, 3 million m³, 441 homes, 73 businesses, benefits over £100 million.
6. **Levels**: 4-mark — Level 2 (3–4) needs developed points. 6-mark ("assess", "evaluate", "to what extent") — Level 3 (5–6) needs detailed, balanced points **and a judgement** with place-specific evidence. Rivers is in Paper 1 Section C, where questions are worth 15 marks each, so 6 marks is the usual maximum; the 9-mark practice below uses the same structure: developed points on both sides, specific evidence, a judgement that answers the question ("the scheme was largely successful because…"). No SPaG in Section C.
:::

::: example Use Figure X — lag time
A storm hydrograph shows peak rainfall of 15 mm at 09:00 and peak discharge of 60 cumecs at 21:00. Calculate the lag time and suggest one reason why it might be short. (2)

Lag time = 21:00 − 09:00 = 12 hours (1). It could be short because the drainage basin has steep slopes, so rain runs off quickly into the river instead of infiltrating (1).
:::

::: example Explain — physical factors
Explain how geology and relief can increase the risk of flooding. (4)

If the drainage basin has impermeable rock such as granite or clay, rainwater cannot soak into the ground (1), so it flows over the surface as runoff and reaches the river quickly, raising discharge and shortening lag time (1). Steep relief means water flows downhill quickly (1), leaving little time for infiltration, so more water reaches the river in a short time and it is more likely to overtop its banks (1).
:::

::: example Assess — hard versus soft engineering
Assess the costs and benefits of hard engineering as a way of managing river flooding. (6)

Hard engineering can be very effective. Dams and reservoirs control discharge by storing water and releasing it slowly, and can also provide hydroelectric power and water supply. Embankments allow the channel to hold more water, and flood relief channels such as the Jubilee River near Maidenhead divert floodwater around towns, protecting homes and businesses.

However, hard engineering is expensive to build and maintain, and it can transfer the problem downstream: straightening makes water flow faster, so towns further down may flood more. Dams flood large areas of land and trap sediment, while embankments that are overtopped can cause sudden, severe floods and trap water behind them. Concrete structures also destroy natural habitats and look unnatural.

Overall, hard engineering gives the strongest protection for high-value places such as town centres, as in Banbury, where a £18.5 million scheme protected property worth far more. But because of its cost and environmental impact, it works best combined with soft engineering such as flood warnings and flood plain zoning.

(Level 3: balanced, specific costs and benefits, examples used, clear judgement.)
:::

Describe the difference between a "flashy" and a "subdued" hydrograph. (2)
