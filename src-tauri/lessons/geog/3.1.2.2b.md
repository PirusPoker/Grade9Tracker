## What it is

**Deforestation** is the cutting down and removal of forest, usually to use the land for something else. The spec needs: **changing rates of deforestation**; a **case study of a tropical rainforest** showing the **causes** (subsistence and commercial farming, logging, road building, mineral extraction, energy development, settlement, population growth) and the **impacts** (economic development, soil erosion, contribution to climate change); the **value of rainforests** to people and the environment; and **strategies to manage the rainforest sustainably** — selective logging and replanting, conservation and education, ecotourism, international agreements about tropical hardwoods, and debt reduction.

### Changing rates of deforestation

- **Globally**, forests are still being lost, but more slowly than in the late twentieth century. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimates that the **net** loss of forest fell from about 7.8 million hectares a year in the 1990s to about 4.7 million hectares a year in 2010–2020. Most of the loss is in the tropics — South America, Africa and South-East Asia.
- **Brazil (Amazon)** shows how rates rise and fall with policy and prices: deforestation peaked at about 27,800 km² in 2004, fell to about 4,600 km² in 2012 after stricter enforcement and satellite monitoring, rose again to about 13,000 km² in 2021 when protection was weakened, and fell again after 2023.
- **Malaysia** had the **highest rate of forest loss in the world between 2000 and 2012** according to satellite research (Hansen and others, 2013): it lost **14.4%** of its 2000 forest cover — about 47,000 km², an area larger than Denmark. Much of this loss was in **Sarawak and Sabah** on the island of Borneo. The rate of primary forest loss has slowed since the mid-2010s, helped by lower palm oil prices, government pledges and certification schemes.

When a question gives you a graph of deforestation rates, describe the **overall trend**, the **peak** and **lowest** values with years, and any **fluctuations** — then suggest reasons (prices, government policy, enforcement).

### Case study: Malaysia

Malaysia is a newly emerging economy in South-East Asia, made up of **Peninsular Malaysia** and the states of **Sabah** and **Sarawak** on the island of **Borneo**. Its rainforests are among the oldest in the world — **Taman Negara** is often described as about 130 million years old — and still cover over half the country. They contain orangutans, clouded leopards, Borneo pygmy elephants, rhinoceros hornbills and thousands of plant species. Malaysia's population has grown from about 8 million in 1960 to over 33 million today, and its economy has grown rapidly, putting pressure on the forest.

### Causes of deforestation in Malaysia

| Cause | What happens in Malaysia |
|---|---|
| **Commercial farming** | The biggest cause. Malaysia is the world's **second-largest producer of palm oil** (after Indonesia; together they produce well over three-quarters of the world's supply). Between 2000 and 2012 its oil palm plantations grew by about 50%, around 17,000 km², mostly on cleared forest. Rubber plantations were also important in the past. Palm oil is used in food, cosmetics and biofuels |
| **Subsistence farming** | Indigenous groups in Sarawak and Sabah practise shifting cultivation (slash and burn) — clearing a small plot, farming it for a few years, then moving on. On a small scale the forest regrows, but it has less effect than commercial farming |
| **Logging** | Malaysia is a major exporter of tropical hardwoods such as **meranti**. Commercial logging, much of it in Sarawak, removes valuable trees and damages many others as they are felled and dragged out. **Illegal logging** is also a problem |
| **Road building** | Logging roads open up remote forest to loggers, farmers and settlers. The **Pan Borneo Highway**, under construction since 2015, runs through Sarawak and Sabah |
| **Mineral extraction** | **Tin** mining (Malaysia was once the world's biggest tin producer), and more recently **bauxite** (aluminium ore) in **Kuantan, Pahang** — a boom in 2015 cleared land and turned rivers and coastal waters red with dust and runoff, leading to a government ban on mining from January 2016. Gold and other minerals are also mined |
| **Energy development** | The **Bakun Dam** in Sarawak (completed 2011) is a large hydroelectric scheme. Its reservoir flooded about 700 km² of rainforest — an area about the size of Singapore — and around **10,000 indigenous people** were relocated to Sungai Asap. More dams have been built or planned under the Sarawak Corridor of Renewable Energy (SCORE) |
| **Settlement and population growth** | From 1956 the **Federal Land Development Authority (FELDA)** resettled poor rural families on newly cleared land to farm rubber and oil palm. As the population grows, more land is needed for housing, towns and food |

### Impacts of deforestation in Malaysia

**Economic development (positive and negative)**
- Palm oil is one of Malaysia's most valuable exports and the industry employs hundreds of thousands of people, many in rural areas; it has helped Malaysia become a newly emerging economy.
- Logging and timber processing earn export income and create jobs in sawmills and furniture factories.
- FELDA schemes lifted many rural families out of poverty.
- Roads improve access for rural communities to schools, hospitals and markets; hydroelectric dams supply electricity for industry.
- **But** the forest's long-term value is lost: less ecotourism potential, loss of forest products that indigenous people rely on, and costs of flooding and pollution. Many profits go to large companies rather than local people. Displaced communities such as those moved by the Bakun Dam lost their land and traditional livelihoods, and some were still waiting for promised compensation years later.

**Soil erosion**
- Without the canopy to intercept rain and roots to hold the soil, heavy tropical downpours wash the thin topsoil away.
- Eroded soil is washed into rivers, making them muddy; the extra sediment harms fish, fills river channels and increases flood risk downstream.
- Without leaf litter the nutrient cycle is broken; the latosols quickly become infertile, so farmland may be abandoned within a few years.

**Contribution to climate change**
- Burning cleared forest releases CO₂ directly into the atmosphere.
- Fewer trees means less CO₂ is absorbed — the forest's role as a **carbon sink** is lost.
- Draining **peat swamp forests** (common in Sarawak) for plantations releases very large amounts of carbon stored in the peat, and dried peat burns easily.
- Locally, less transpiration means less rainfall, so the climate becomes drier and hotter, and fires spread more easily.

**Other impacts**: loss of biodiversity (the orangutan is critically endangered; the last Sumatran rhinoceros in Malaysia died in Sabah in 2019, and the species is now extinct in the country); loss of indigenous ways of life — the **Penan** of Sarawak have set up road blockades against loggers since the 1980s.

### The value of tropical rainforests

| To people | To the environment |
|---|---|
| Homes, food, medicine and building materials for indigenous peoples | Huge store of carbon — helps regulate the global climate |
| Timber, rubber, palm oil, fruit, nuts, coffee, cocoa | Produce oxygen through photosynthesis |
| Medicines — many drugs first came from rainforest plants | Recycle water through transpiration, keeping regional rainfall high |
| Jobs and income from forestry, farming and tourism | Protect soils from erosion and rivers from sediment |
| Hydroelectric power from forested river valleys | Habitat for more than half the world's plant and animal species |
| Genetic material for crop breeding | Reduce flooding by intercepting rain and slowing runoff |

### Managing the rainforest sustainably

**Sustainable management** means using the forest to meet people's needs today without damaging it for future generations — balancing economic development with protecting the environment.

| Strategy | How it works | Examples | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Selective logging and replanting** | Only mature trees above a certain size are felled, leaving younger trees to grow and the canopy mostly intact; felled areas are replanted, and the area is left to recover for decades before logging again | Malaysia's **Selective Management System** sets minimum tree sizes and long cutting cycles; timber certified by the **Malaysian Timber Certification Scheme** or the **Forest Stewardship Council (FSC)**. The **Greening Malaysia: 100 Million Trees** campaign, launched in 2021, aims to replant forest | Removing even a few large trees damages others and requires roads; replanted forest has less biodiversity than primary forest |
| **Conservation and education** | Protected areas where logging and farming are banned or restricted; teaching local people and visitors why the forest matters and how to use it sustainably | **Taman Negara** national park (Peninsular Malaysia); **Gunung Mulu** and **Kinabalu** national parks (Sarawak and Sabah, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites since 2000); the **Heart of Borneo** agreement (2007) between Malaysia, Indonesia and Brunei to conserve about 220,000 km² of forest; Malaysia's pledge at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit to keep at least 50% of the country forested | Protected areas need funding and enforcement to stop illegal logging; local people may lose access to land they need |
| **Ecotourism** | Small-scale, low-impact tourism that earns money from the forest standing, gives local people jobs, and funds conservation | **Sepilok Orangutan Rehabilitation Centre** in Sabah (founded 1964); the canopy walkway in Taman Negara; community-run homestays along the **Kinabatangan River** in Sabah where villagers act as guides | Only a limited number of visitors can be supported; flights add to carbon emissions; income is small compared with palm oil |
| **International agreements about tropical hardwoods** | Countries agree to trade only in legally and sustainably logged timber, reducing the market for illegal wood | The **International Tropical Timber Agreement** (first signed 1983) and its International Tropical Timber Organization; **FSC** certification (since 1993) so buyers can choose sustainable wood; the EU Timber Regulation (2013) banning illegal timber from EU markets; for palm oil, the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil and Malaysia's MSPO certification, compulsory from 2020 | Difficult to trace timber back to its source; certification can be forged; some importing countries do not take part |
| **Debt reduction** | A richer country or organisation cancels part of a poorer country's debt in return for spending on forest protection — a **debt-for-nature swap** | In 2010 the USA reduced Brazil's debt payments by about USD 21 million in return for protecting its Atlantic Forest, Cerrado and Caatinga; Indonesia has made similar agreements for its forests | Relies on the country keeping its promise; less relevant to Malaysia, which does not have a heavy debt burden; the amounts are small compared with the value of logging |

**Evaluating the strategies**: the most successful approaches combine several strategies — protected areas stop the worst clearance, certification changes what buyers will pay for, and ecotourism gives local people a reason to keep the forest standing. The biggest challenge in Malaysia is that palm oil and timber earn far more than conservation in the short term, so strategies work best when they make the standing forest valuable.

::: terms
- **Deforestation**: the cutting down and removal of forest, usually to use the land for farming, mining, settlement or other development.
- **Commercial farming**: large-scale farming, such as oil palm plantations, where crops are grown to sell for profit.
- **Subsistence farming**: farming to produce food for the farmer's own family, with little or none left over to sell.
- **Logging**: cutting down trees for their timber, either selectively or by clear-felling whole areas.
- **Soil erosion**: the removal of topsoil by rain or wind, which is much faster once the protective tree cover has been cleared.
- **Carbon sink**: a natural store, such as a forest or peat bog, that absorbs more carbon dioxide than it releases.
- **Selective logging**: felling only certain mature trees above a set size, leaving the rest of the forest to regenerate.
- **Ecotourism**: small-scale, low-impact tourism that helps conserve the environment and benefits local people.
- **Debt-for-nature swap**: an agreement in which part of a country's debt is cancelled in return for spending on conservation.
- **Sustainable management**: using a resource to meet present needs without damaging its ability to meet the needs of future generations.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State / name** (1–2): a fact — "State one cause of deforestation in a named rainforest" — "palm oil plantations in Malaysia".
- **Describe** (2–4): what happens — describing a graph of deforestation rates needs figures, years and the trend.
- **Explain** (2–6): how or why — causes need the reason and the effect on the forest; impacts need the chain (clearance → no roots → heavy rain washes soil away → rivers silt up).
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** (6–9): e.g. "Assess the extent to which economic development is more important than the environmental impacts of deforestation", or "Evaluate the success of strategies to manage rainforests sustainably". Judge.
- **Use Figure X**: quote numbers and places from the map or graph.

**Levels**
- **4-mark**: Level 2 — developed, linked points.
- **6-mark**: three developed points **with Malaysia detail**: palm oil (second-largest producer, plantations up about 50% 2000–2012), Bakun Dam (700 km², 10,000 people moved), bauxite in Kuantan, FELDA, Taman Negara, Sepilok.
- **9-mark** (+3 SPaG on Paper 1 where shown): two sides developed with the case study, then a **judgement** that answers the question — e.g. "economic development is more important in the short term for Malaysia, but the environmental costs are greater in the long term because..."

**Sustainable strategy answers**: say **what** the strategy is, **how** it reduces damage, and give a **real example**. For a "sustainable" judgement, check all three: does it protect the environment, bring economic benefit, and meet local people's needs, now and in future?

**Don't confuse the case study**: the causes list is from the spec — learn a Malaysian example for each one.
:::

::: example Explain — causes
Using a named example, explain two causes of deforestation in a tropical rainforest. (4)

Commercial farming (1): in Malaysia, large areas of rainforest have been cleared to grow oil palm, because palm oil is a valuable export used in food, cosmetics and biofuels — Malaysia is the world's second-largest producer, and its plantations grew by about 50% between 2000 and 2012 (1). Energy development (1): the Bakun Dam in Sarawak, completed in 2011, flooded about 700 km² of forest to create a reservoir for hydroelectric power, and around 10,000 indigenous people had to move (1).
:::

::: example Explain — impacts
Explain how deforestation can contribute to climate change. (4)

When forest is cleared it is often burned, releasing the carbon stored in the trees as CO₂ (1). Fewer trees are left to absorb CO₂ by photosynthesis, so the forest stops acting as a carbon sink (1). In Malaysia, draining peat swamp forests for oil palm plantations releases very large amounts of carbon from the peat (1). More CO₂ in the atmosphere enhances the greenhouse effect, trapping more heat and raising global temperatures (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — economic vs environmental impacts
"The economic benefits of deforestation outweigh the environmental costs." Using a case study, do you agree? (9 + 3 SPaG)

Deforestation has brought Malaysia clear economic benefits. Palm oil is one of its most valuable exports and the industry employs hundreds of thousands of people, many in poor rural areas; FELDA settlement schemes lifted many families out of poverty. Logging earns export income, roads give rural communities access to markets and hospitals, and the Bakun Dam provides hydroelectric power for industry. These have helped Malaysia become a newly emerging economy.

However, the environmental costs are severe. Malaysia lost 14.4% of its forest between 2000 and 2012, the highest rate in the world. Without tree cover, heavy rain washes the thin soils away, silting rivers and increasing flooding; burning and draining peat swamps release CO₂, contributing to climate change; and biodiversity is lost — the Sumatran rhino is now extinct in Malaysia and the orangutan is critically endangered.

Some economic benefits are also short-lived: soils become infertile within a few years, profits go to large companies rather than local people, and communities such as those moved by the Bakun Dam lost their livelihoods. The forest's long-term value — ecotourism at places such as Sepilok, medicines, and its role in regulating rainfall — is destroyed.

I disagree. The economic benefits are real and important for a developing country, but the environmental costs are larger because they are long-term and often irreversible — extinct species cannot return, and eroded soils take centuries to form. Sustainable management, such as certified palm oil and selective logging, can deliver much of the economic benefit without the same cost.
:::

---
An agreement where part of a country's debt is cancelled (1) in return for the country spending money on protecting its environment (1). e.g. in 2010 the USA reduced Brazil's debt payments by about USD 21 million in return for protecting the Atlantic Forest (1).

::: q
Explain how international agreements on tropical hardwoods help reduce deforestation. (3)
---
Agreements such as the International Tropical Timber Agreement and FSC certification mean timber is only traded if it comes from legal, sustainably managed forests (1); importing countries (e.g. under the EU Timber Regulation) ban illegal wood (1), so there is less profit in illegal logging and buyers pay more for certified timber (1).
:::
