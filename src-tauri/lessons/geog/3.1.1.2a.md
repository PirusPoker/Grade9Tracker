## What it is

Earthquakes and volcanic eruptions are the result of **physical processes** inside the Earth. This lesson covers the three spec points behind them: **plate tectonics theory**, the **global distribution** of earthquakes and volcanoes and how it relates to plate margins, and the **processes at each type of plate margin** (constructive, destructive and conservative).

### The structure of the Earth

| Layer | What it is like |
|---|---|
| **Crust** | thin, solid outer layer. Split into large slabs called **tectonic plates**. |
| **Mantle** | about 2,900 km thick; mostly solid rock, but hot enough to flow very slowly. The upper part is partly molten. |
| **Outer core** | liquid iron and nickel. |
| **Inner core** | solid iron and nickel under enormous pressure, with a temperature of around 5,000–6,000 °C. |

There are two kinds of crust, and the difference explains what happens at plate margins:

| | **Continental crust** | **Oceanic crust** |
|---|---|---|
| Thickness | thick: about 30–50 km, up to about 70 km under high mountains | thin: about 5–10 km |
| Density | lighter (less dense), granite-type rock | heavier (denser), basalt |
| Age | very old, some over 3 billion years | young, no more than about 200 million years |
| Can it sink? | no, too light to be subducted | yes, it sinks (is subducted) when it meets continental crust |

### Plate tectonics theory

The crust and the rigid top of the mantle (together, the lithosphere) are broken into about seven large plates and many smaller ones. The plates **move** a few centimetres a year, roughly the speed your fingernails grow.

**How the idea developed.** In 1912 Alfred Wegener proposed **continental drift**: the continents were once joined in a supercontinent (Pangaea) and have since drifted apart. His evidence was the jigsaw fit of South America and Africa, matching fossils (such as the reptile Mesosaurus) and matching rock types on continents now separated by oceans. He could not explain *how* they moved, so the idea was rejected for decades. In the 1950s and 1960s, mapping of the ocean floor revealed mid-ocean ridges, **sea-floor spreading** and matching stripes of magnetised rock on either side of the ridges, which showed new crust forming and moving outwards. This evidence turned continental drift into the theory of **plate tectonics**.

**Why plates move.** GCSE textbooks give two explanations. Know both:

1. **Convection currents**: heat from the core, much of it from radioactive decay, heats the lower mantle. Hot rock rises slowly, spreads out beneath the crust, cools and sinks again, forming circular currents that drag the plates along above them.
2. **Slab pull and ridge push** (the more recent view): where a dense oceanic plate sinks into the mantle at a subduction zone, its weight **pulls** the rest of the plate behind it (slab pull). At mid-ocean ridges, newly formed, raised crust slides away from the ridge under gravity (ridge push). Scientists now think slab pull is the main force.

A **plate margin** (boundary) is where two plates meet. Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen here because this is where plates collide, pull apart or slide past each other.

### Global distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes

On a world map, earthquakes and volcanoes are **not random**. Describe the pattern with names and directions:

- They form **narrow belts** that follow **plate margins**.
- The biggest belt is the **Pacific Ring of Fire**, a horseshoe of margins around the edge of the Pacific Ocean: down the west coast of the Americas (Alaska, the Andes in Chile), and down the eastern edge of Asia (Kamchatka, Japan, the Philippines, Indonesia) to New Zealand. Around three-quarters of the world's active volcanoes and about 90% of its earthquakes lie on it.
- A belt runs down the middle of the **Atlantic Ocean** along the **Mid-Atlantic Ridge**, surfacing in **Iceland**.
- A belt of mostly earthquakes (few volcanoes) runs east–west from the **Mediterranean** (Italy, Greece, Turkey) through Iran to the **Himalayas** (Nepal) and China.
- Along the **East African Rift Valley** there are volcanoes such as Kilimanjaro.
- **Earthquakes are more widespread** than volcanoes: there are earthquakes at all three margin types, but volcanoes only at constructive and destructive margins.
- **Exceptions**: some volcanoes lie far from any margin, above **hotspots**, where a plume of unusually hot mantle rises beneath the middle of a plate. The **Hawaiian Islands** formed as the Pacific plate moved over a hotspot, leaving a chain of older, extinct volcanoes to the north-west. Iceland is both on a ridge and above a hotspot, which is why it is so volcanically active.

When describing a distribution map in the exam, use **PQE**: **P**attern (belts along margins), **Q**uantify with names and figures (the Ring of Fire, the west coast of South America), **E**xceptions (Hawaii).

### What happens at each type of plate margin

#### Constructive (divergent) margins: plates move apart

Example: the **Mid-Atlantic Ridge**, where the North American and Eurasian plates move apart, through **Iceland**. On land, the **East African Rift Valley** is a constructive margin in the making.

- As the plates move apart, pressure is released and the mantle below partly melts to form **magma**.
- Magma rises into the gap and erupts, forming **new crust** (this is sea-floor spreading).
- The magma is **basaltic**: runny (low viscosity) and low in gas, so eruptions are relatively **gentle** and **effusive**, with lava flows rather than violent explosions. Lava spreads out to build wide, gently sloping **shield volcanoes**, or erupts from long cracks as **fissure eruptions**.
- **Earthquakes** occur as the plates crack and move apart, but they are usually **shallow and relatively small**.
- Exception: in 2010 **Eyjafjallajökull** in Iceland erupted beneath a glacier. Meltwater chilled the magma so it shattered into fine ash, and the ash cloud closed much of Europe's airspace for days.

#### Destructive (convergent) margins: plates move towards each other

**Oceanic meets continental**. Example: the **Nazca plate** moving towards and sinking beneath the **South American plate**, forming the **Andes** and the **Peru–Chile trench**. This caused the Chile earthquake of 2010.

- The **denser oceanic plate** is forced down (**subducted**) beneath the lighter continental plate, forming a deep **ocean trench** where it bends down.
- As the plates grind together, **friction** makes them stick. Pressure builds up until the rocks suddenly give way, releasing energy as **earthquakes**. These are often **very powerful**; the largest ever recorded, magnitude 9.5 in Chile in 1960, was at this type of margin.
- As the oceanic plate descends into the hot mantle, it (and the water it carries) causes rock to **melt**, forming magma.
- This magma is less dense, so it rises through cracks in the continental crust and erupts as **composite (strato) volcanoes**, such as those in the Andes or Mount Fuji in Japan. The magma is **thick (viscous) and gas-rich**, so pressure builds and eruptions are **explosive**, with ash, pyroclastic flows and lava bombs.
- The continental crust is squeezed and buckled upwards to form **fold mountains**.

**Oceanic meets oceanic**: one oceanic plate subducts beneath another, forming a trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands (an island arc), as in Japan and the Mariana Trench in the western Pacific.

**Collision margins (continental meets continental)**: example, the **Indian (Indo-Australian) plate** moving north into the **Eurasian plate**, forming the **Himalayas**. AQA sometimes counts these as destructive margins, and some textbooks give them their own name.

- Both plates are continental and light, so **neither is subducted**. The crust crumples and is forced **upwards** into fold mountains; the Himalayas are still rising.
- Huge pressure builds and is released as **powerful, often shallow earthquakes**, as in **Nepal in 2015** (magnitude 7.8).
- **No volcanoes**, because no plate sinks into the mantle to melt, so no magma is formed.

#### Conservative margins: plates slide past each other

Example: the **San Andreas Fault** in California, where the **Pacific plate** moves north-west relative to the **North American plate**. (Plates at a conservative margin can move in opposite directions, or in the same direction at different speeds; either way they slide past each other.)

- Crust is **neither created nor destroyed**.
- The plates do not slide smoothly. **Friction** locks them together and **pressure builds up** over years. When the rocks suddenly slip, the stored energy is released as an **earthquake**, which can be very destructive because the focus is often **shallow** and major cities (San Francisco, Los Angeles) lie close to the fault. San Francisco was devastated in 1906.
- **No volcanoes**, because no magma is produced.

### Summary: margin, process, hazard

| Margin | Plate movement | Example | Earthquakes | Volcanoes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Constructive** | apart | Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland | yes: shallow, usually small | yes: gentle, shield or fissure, basaltic lava |
| **Destructive** (ocean–continent) | towards; oceanic plate subducted | Nazca and South American plates, Andes | yes: very powerful | yes: explosive composite volcanoes |
| **Collision** | towards; both continental | Indian and Eurasian plates, Himalayas | yes: powerful | **no** |
| **Conservative** | sideways past each other | San Andreas Fault, California | yes: can be powerful and shallow | **no** |

### Earthquake vocabulary you need

- **Focus (hypocentre)**: the point underground where the rocks break and the earthquake starts.
- **Epicentre**: the point on the surface directly above the focus. Shaking is usually strongest here.
- **Seismic waves**: shock waves that spread out from the focus.
- **Magnitude**: measured on the **moment magnitude scale** (older books say the Richter scale). It is logarithmic: each whole number is about 10 times more ground movement and about 32 times more energy.
- **Shallow-focus** earthquakes (near the surface) usually cause more damage than deep ones, because the energy has less rock to travel through.

### Describing the diagrams you will need to draw

For a 4- or 6-mark "explain the processes" question, a labelled cross-section helps. For a **destructive margin**, draw the continental plate on the right and the oceanic plate on the left with arrows pointing towards each other; show the oceanic plate bending down beneath the continent at a trench, a zone of melting where it sinks into the mantle, magma rising in a column to a steep cone-shaped volcano on the continent, a star for the earthquake focus along the sinking plate, and fold mountains. For a **constructive margin**, draw two plates with arrows pointing apart, magma rising into the gap and a low, wide volcano or ridge above.

::: terms
- **Tectonic plate**: a large, rigid slab of the Earth's crust and upper mantle that moves slowly over the mantle.
- **Plate margin**: the boundary where two tectonic plates meet.
- **Constructive margin**: a plate margin where two plates move apart and new crust is formed from rising magma.
- **Destructive margin**: a plate margin where two plates move towards each other and the denser oceanic plate is subducted.
- **Conservative margin**: a plate margin where two plates slide past each other and crust is neither created nor destroyed.
- **Subduction**: the process in which a denser oceanic plate sinks beneath another plate into the mantle, where it melts.
- **Convection current**: a slow circular movement of heated rock in the mantle, thought to help drive plate movement.
- **Slab pull**: the pulling force created as the weight of a sinking oceanic plate drags the rest of the plate behind it.
- **Hotspot**: an area of volcanic activity away from a plate margin, above a plume of unusually hot rising mantle.
- **Epicentre**: the point on the Earth's surface directly above the focus of an earthquake.
:::

::: method
**Describe the distribution (map questions).**
- Use the figure. Name the pattern ("in narrow belts"), the places ("around the edge of the Pacific Ocean, along the west coast of South America") and an anomaly (Hawaii), and quote any figure given.
- Describe only. "Because they are on plate margins" is explanation and earns nothing on a describe question unless the question asks "describe *and* explain".

**Explain the processes (4 and 6 marks).**
- Name the margin and a real example (the Nazca and South American plates).
- Tell it as a **sequence**: plates move → what happens to the crust → how that causes the earthquake (friction, pressure builds, sudden release) → how magma forms and why it erupts in that way.
- Use the key terms: subduction, friction, pressure, magma, viscosity, trench, fold mountains.
- Always answer *why* there are or are not volcanoes: "no volcanoes because no crust is subducted, so no magma is formed."

**Levels.** 4 marks: two linked stages, or one well-developed chain (Level 2 is 3–4). 6 marks: a full, accurate sequence with correct terms and an example (Level 3 is 5–6). A labelled diagram can support the answer but you must still write the explanation.

**9 marks** rarely appear on this sub-topic alone, but if you are asked to evaluate (e.g. "destructive margins are more hazardous than constructive margins"), compare the power of earthquakes, the explosivity of eruptions and the numbers of people living nearby, then give a judgement.
:::

::: example Describe a distribution
Using a world map of earthquakes, describe the global distribution of earthquakes. (3)

Earthquakes occur in long, narrow belts rather than randomly (1). The largest belt runs around the edge of the Pacific Ocean, the Ring of Fire, for example down the west coast of South America and through Japan (1). There is another belt through the middle of the Atlantic Ocean and one from the Mediterranean eastwards to the Himalayas; very few earthquakes occur in the middle of continents such as Australia (1).
:::

::: example Explain a destructive margin
Explain why volcanoes and earthquakes occur at destructive plate margins. (6)

At a destructive margin, such as where the Nazca plate meets the South American plate, an oceanic plate and a continental plate move towards each other. The oceanic plate is denser, so it is subducted beneath the continental plate, forming a deep trench (1–2). As the plates are forced past each other, friction makes them stick; pressure builds up until the rocks suddenly give way, releasing energy as powerful earthquakes, such as the magnitude 8.8 earthquake in Chile in 2010 (2). As the oceanic plate sinks into the hot mantle it melts, forming magma. This is less dense than the surrounding rock, so it rises through cracks in the continental crust and erupts. Because the magma is thick and full of gas, pressure builds and the eruptions are explosive, forming steep composite volcanoes in the Andes (2).

(Level 3: accurate sequence, key terms, named example.)
:::

::: example Explain the absence of volcanoes
Explain why there are no volcanoes at conservative plate margins. (3)

At a conservative margin, such as the San Andreas Fault, the plates slide past each other (1). No crust is subducted or pulled apart (1), so no rock melts into magma and there is nothing to erupt (1). Earthquakes still happen because friction causes pressure to build up until the plates suddenly slip.
:::

::: example Compare two margins
Compare the volcanic activity at constructive and destructive plate margins. (4)

At constructive margins, such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge in Iceland, basaltic magma is runny and low in gas (1), so eruptions are gentle and build wide, low shield volcanoes or fissures (1). At destructive margins, such as the Andes, magma is viscous and gas-rich because it forms from melting subducted crust (1), so eruptions are explosive and build steep composite cones, making them more dangerous (1).
:::

::: q
State the difference between the focus and the epicentre of an earthquake. (2)
---
The focus is the point underground where the earthquake starts (1); the epicentre is the point on the surface directly above the focus (1).
:::

::: q
Give two differences between oceanic and continental crust. (2)
---
Any two: oceanic crust is denser/heavier; oceanic crust is thinner (5–10 km versus 30–50 km); oceanic crust is younger; oceanic crust can be subducted and destroyed but continental crust cannot; oceanic crust is basalt, continental is granite-type (1+1).
:::

::: q
Explain how convection currents are thought to move tectonic plates. (3)
---
Heat from the core warms rock in the lower mantle, which becomes less dense and rises (1); it spreads out beneath the crust, cools and sinks again, creating a circular current (1); the moving mantle drags the plates above it along (1). Credit slab pull as an alternative explanation.
:::

::: q
Explain why powerful earthquakes occur at collision margins but volcanoes do not. (4)
---
Two continental plates move towards each other, e.g. India into Eurasia (1). Both are light, so neither is subducted; the crust is forced upwards into fold mountains such as the Himalayas (1). Huge pressure builds as the plates push together and is released suddenly as earthquakes, e.g. Nepal 2015 (1). No crust sinks into the mantle to melt, so no magma forms and there are no volcanoes (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why Hawaii has volcanoes even though it is thousands of kilometres from a plate margin. (2)
---
It lies above a hotspot, a plume of very hot mantle rising beneath the middle of the Pacific plate (1); magma burns through the crust to erupt, and as the plate moves over the hotspot a chain of volcanic islands forms (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the processes at a constructive plate margin. (4)
---
Two plates move apart, e.g. the North American and Eurasian plates at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (1). Pressure is released and the mantle melts to form magma, which rises into the gap to form new crust (1). The magma is basaltic and runny with little gas, so eruptions are gentle, forming shield volcanoes or fissures, as in Iceland (1). Earthquakes occur as the crust cracks and moves, but they are usually shallow and small (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Describe** a distribution means pattern, names and exceptions. Explaining it with "because of plate margins" earns nothing on a describe question.
- Name the **plates** and the **place**: "the Nazca plate subducts under the South American plate" beats "one plate goes under another".
- Say **why** the oceanic plate subducts: it is **denser**. "Heavier" is accepted; "bigger" is wrong.
- Earthquakes need the **friction → pressure builds → sudden release** chain. "The plates rub together" alone is Level 1.
- Conservative and collision margins have **no volcanoes**. Say why: no subduction, so no magma.
- Do not say plates move "fast" or that the mantle is liquid. It is mostly solid rock that flows very slowly.
:::

::: test
"Destructive plate margins are more hazardous to people than any other type of plate margin." Evaluate this statement. (9)
---
Level 3 (7–9): detailed understanding of processes at all margin types, applied to their hazards, with named examples and a clear, justified judgement.
Level 2 (4–6): some understanding of two or more margin types, some examples, a judgement with limited support.
Level 1 (1–3): simple descriptions, largely one margin, little or no judgement.
Indicative content:
- Destructive: the most powerful earthquakes (Chile 1960 magnitude 9.5, Chile 2010 magnitude 8.8, Japan 2011); explosive composite volcanoes with pyroclastic flows and ash; subduction earthquakes under the sea trigger tsunamis; many densely populated places lie along the Ring of Fire.
- Collision: very powerful earthquakes and landslides in mountains (Nepal 2015, about 9,000 deaths), though no volcanoes.
- Conservative: no volcanoes, but shallow earthquakes close to big cities can be very destructive (San Francisco 1906, California).
- Constructive: generally gentle eruptions and small earthquakes, but exceptions (Eyjafjallajökull ash cloud 2010) and fewer people live on most mid-ocean ridges.
- Judgement: e.g. destructive margins produce the widest range and largest hazards, but the actual risk to people depends on population and wealth as much as margin type, as Nepal shows.
