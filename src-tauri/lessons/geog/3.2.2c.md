## What it is

Some LICs and NEEs are developing fast, and that growth brings big social, environmental and cultural change. The specification asks for **one case study** of an LIC or NEE covering eight points: its **location and importance**; its **political, social, cultural and environmental context**; its **changing industrial structure**; the **role of TNCs**; its **changing political and trading relationships**; **international aid**; the **environmental impacts** of development; and the **effects on quality of life**. The case study here is **Nigeria**, an NEE in West Africa.

### 1. Location and importance

**Location.** Nigeria is in **West Africa**, on the **Gulf of Guinea** (Atlantic Ocean), just north of the Equator (roughly 4°N to 14°N). It borders **Benin** to the west, **Niger** to the north, **Chad** to the north-east (across Lake Chad) and **Cameroon** to the east. The capital is **Abuja** (since 1991); the largest city and main port is **Lagos**.

**Regional importance (within Africa)**

- **Africa's most populous country** — about **237 million people** (2025 estimate), with a very young population.
- **One of Africa's largest economies.** After a 2014 statistical rebasing it became Africa's biggest economy for several years; the fall in the value of the naira from 2023 pushed it down the ranking, and a further rebasing in July 2025 valued its 2024 GDP at about **USD 243 billion**.
- A leading member of **ECOWAS** (the Economic Community of West African States, headquartered in Abuja) and the **African Union**; it has contributed troops to peacekeeping missions in Africa.

**Global importance**

- A major **oil and gas exporter** and member of **OPEC** since 1971.
- **Nollywood** is one of the world's biggest film industries by number of films; Nigerian **Afrobeats** music has a global audience.
- A growing market that attracts **TNCs**, and a large diaspora (for example in the UK and USA).

### 2. Political, social, cultural and environmental context

| Context | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Political** | A British colony until **independence on 1 October 1960**. A **civil war (the Biafran War, 1967–70)** and long periods of **military rule** followed. Civilian democracy since **1999**; in **2015** power passed peacefully from one party to another for the first time through an election. Serious insecurity remains: the **Boko Haram** insurgency in the north-east since 2009 (including the kidnap of 276 schoolgirls from Chibok in 2014), banditry in the north-west and militancy in the oil-producing Niger Delta. Corruption has deterred investors. |
| **Social** | A multi-ethnic nation of **over 250 ethnic groups**; the largest are the **Hausa-Fulani** (north), **Yoruba** (south-west) and **Igbo** (south-east). Roughly half the population is Muslim (mainly the north) and half Christian (mainly the south). The population is very young and growing fast, and is rapidly urbanising. |
| **Cultural** | A rich culture: Nollywood films, Afrobeats music, literature (Chinua Achebe, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie), football (the Super Eagles). Culture is also an export and a source of soft power. |
| **Environmental** | Nigeria has distinct zones from south to north: **mangrove swamps and tropical rainforest** along the coast and Niger Delta; **tropical savanna** grassland across the centre; and **semi-arid Sahel** in the far north, where drought and desertification are problems. The **River Niger** and **River Benue** meet at Lokoja. The wetter south has the oil, the ports and more farmland; the drier north is poorer. |

### 3. The changing industrial structure

At independence Nigeria's economy was mainly **agricultural** — exporting palm oil, groundnuts and cocoa. **Oil** was discovered at **Oloibiri in the Niger Delta in 1956** and the oil boom of the 1970s made petroleum the main export. Today the economy is **diversifying** towards services and manufacturing.

**Balance between sectors (rebased GDP, 2024)**

| Sector | Share of GDP | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** (agriculture, forestry, fishing) | about 28% | cassava, yams, maize, cocoa; many small-scale farmers |
| **Secondary** (industry — including oil and gas, manufacturing, construction) | about 17% | oil extraction, cement, food processing, refining |
| **Tertiary** (services) | about 55% | trade and retail, telecoms, banking, real estate, film and music |

Key points to remember:

- **Oil is a small share of GDP but dominates exports**: crude oil and petroleum products made up about **88% of the value of exports in 2024**, and oil and gas provide a large share of government revenue. This makes Nigeria vulnerable when oil prices fall.
- **Farming employs far fewer people than it did**: about a third of workers in 2023, compared with well over half in the early 2000s, as people move to cities and service jobs.
- **Growing services**: mobile phones and the internet have expanded rapidly; Lagos has a booming **fintech** and tech start-up scene, and banking and retail have grown with the middle class.
- **Manufacturing** is growing: **Dangote Cement** is one of Africa's largest cement producers; the **Dangote refinery** at Lekki near Lagos, one of the world's largest single-site refineries (designed to process **650,000 barrels a day**), began production in 2024 to reduce Nigeria's dependence on imported petrol.

**How manufacturing stimulates economic development**

- Provides **regular, better-paid jobs** than subsistence farming, so workers have money to spend.
- **Multiplier effect**: factories need suppliers (packaging, transport, raw materials) and workers spend wages in local shops, creating further jobs.
- **Adds value**: processing raw materials (e.g. crude oil into petrol, cocoa into chocolate) earns more than exporting them raw, and saves money on imports.
- **Tax revenue** lets the government invest in schools, hospitals and infrastructure.
- **Skills and technology** develop in the workforce, attracting more investment.

### 4. The role of TNCs

A **transnational corporation (TNC)** operates in more than one country. TNCs bring investment, technology and jobs, but their decisions are made abroad and profits may leave the country.

**Shell (oil and gas)**

- Shell's Nigerian subsidiary, the **Shell Petroleum Development Company (SPDC)**, was the largest onshore oil producer in the Niger Delta for decades, operating in a joint venture with the state-owned Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation (NNPC, 55%), Shell (30%), Total (10%) and Agip (5%).
- **Advantages**: thousands of jobs directly and through contractors; training; large tax and royalty payments; oil revenue that funds government spending; infrastructure and community projects.
- **Disadvantages**: **oil spills** from pipelines (from corrosion, sabotage and theft) polluted farmland, creeks and mangroves. Two major spills at **Bodo** in 2008 devastated fishing; in **2015 Shell agreed an out-of-court settlement of £55 million** for about 15,600 Bodo residents and the community. **Gas flaring** pollutes the air. Much profit went abroad, and anger over pollution and poverty in the Delta fed militancy.
- **Change**: in **March 2025** Shell completed the **sale of SPDC to Renaissance**, a mainly Nigerian consortium, for about **USD 2.4 billion**, exiting onshore oil but keeping its deep-water offshore and gas interests.

**Unilever (consumer goods)**

- In Nigeria since **1923** as Lever Brothers, opening a soap factory at **Apapa (Lagos) in 1925** and a factory at **Agbara in 1982**; it became **Unilever Nigeria plc** in 2001. Brands include Knorr, Lipton, Pepsodent and Close-Up.
- **Advantages**: local manufacturing jobs; buys raw materials from local suppliers and farmers; pays taxes; health and hygiene campaigns (e.g. handwashing with soap) improve public health.
- **Disadvantages**: profits go to shareholders abroad; large TNCs can out-compete small local firms; decisions are made abroad — in **2023** Unilever Nigeria stopped making home-care and skin-cleansing products such as **Omo** and **Lux**, citing difficult economic conditions, and leased the factory space to another firm.

### 5. Changing political and trading relationships

- **Colonial and Commonwealth ties**: the UK was the main trading partner at independence; Nigeria is a **Commonwealth** member (it was suspended from 1995 to 1999 during military rule).
- **Regional ties**: founding member of **ECOWAS (1975)**; member of the **African Union**; signed the **African Continental Free Trade Area** agreement in 2019, which aims to boost trade between African countries.
- **OPEC** member since 1971.
- **Changing partners**: oil and gas exports now go mainly to **India and European countries** (the USA bought much more Nigerian oil before its own shale-oil boom). **China** has become the biggest source of imports (machinery, electronics, vehicles) and a major investor and lender, building railways such as the **Lagos–Ibadan** line (opened 2021).
- A stable democracy since 1999 has improved Nigeria's relationships with the wider world and encouraged investment, although insecurity and corruption still hold it back.

### 6. International aid

**Types of aid**

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Short-term (emergency)** | Food, shelter, medical help after a disaster or conflict |
| **Long-term (development)** | Projects to improve education, health, water, farming over years |
| **Bilateral** | Government to government |
| **Multilateral** | Through international organisations such as the World Bank or UN |
| **Tied** | Must be spent on goods or services from the donor country |
| **Voluntary (NGO)** | Given by charities funded by public donations |

Nigeria receives a lot of aid in absolute terms, but it is small relative to the size of its economy.

**Impacts of aid on Nigeria**

- **Health**: aid-funded vaccination campaigns (WHO, UNICEF, Rotary, the Gates Foundation) helped end wild polio — Nigeria's last case was in **2016** and Africa was certified free of wild polio in **August 2020**. Programmes such as **Nets for Life** distributed insecticide-treated **mosquito nets** and trained local people, reducing malaria. Aid has funded HIV/AIDS treatment.
- **Emergency aid** supports people displaced by the Boko Haram conflict in the north-east.
- **Problems**: **corruption** can divert aid; aid can create **dependency**; insecurity stops aid reaching some areas; and aid can be cut suddenly — the USA's sharp cuts to foreign aid in 2025 disrupted health programmes, showing the risk of relying on donors.

### 7. Environmental impacts of economic development

- **Oil pollution in the Niger Delta**: spills from pipelines and illegal refining pollute creeks, farmland and mangroves, killing fish and destroying livelihoods. A **UN Environment Programme report (2011)** said clean-up of **Ogoniland** could take **25–30 years**.
- **Gas flaring**: burning off gas at oil wells releases carbon dioxide and toxic pollutants, causing acid rain and breathing problems.
- **Deforestation**: forests are cleared for logging, farming, fuelwood and urban growth, causing soil erosion and loss of biodiversity.
- **Desertification** in the north: overgrazing, over-cultivation and drought turn semi-arid land into desert.
- **Urban and industrial pollution**: traffic congestion and air pollution in Lagos; huge amounts of waste, much of it dumped at large open landfill sites; factory effluent in rivers.
- **Mining**: in **2010** lead poisoning from unregulated gold mining in **Zamfara** state killed over 400 children.

### 8. Effects of economic development on quality of life

**Improvements**

- **Rising incomes** and a growing **middle class** in cities such as Lagos and Abuja; more jobs in services and manufacturing.
- **Better health**: life expectancy rose to about **54.5 years (2023)**; infant mortality has fallen; Nigeria is free of wild polio.
- **Connectivity**: mobile phones and mobile banking let people access information, services and markets.
- **Education and services** are improving, especially in the south.

**Continuing problems**

- **Inequality**: the benefits are uneven — the oil-rich south and big cities have gained most while the north lags behind. The World Bank estimated about **139 million Nigerians were living in poverty** in 2025.
- **Life expectancy** remains among the lowest in the world, and the **HDI is 0.560** (166th of 193, 2025 report).
- **Slums** such as **Makoko** in Lagos lack clean water and sanitation.
- **Insecurity** in the north-east and north-west, and high **inflation** after reforms from 2023 (removing the fuel subsidy, devaluing the naira), have made life harder for the poorest.

**Overall**: economic development has improved quality of life for many Nigerians, but not for all — the gains are concentrated in the south and among urban, educated groups.

::: terms
- **Newly emerging economy (NEE)**: a country beginning to experience rapid economic growth and industrialisation, such as Nigeria.
- **Industrial structure**: the proportion of the workforce or GDP in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors of the economy.
- **Transnational corporation (TNC)**: a company that operates in more than one country, usually with its headquarters in an HIC.
- **Multiplier effect**: when investment and new jobs lead to more spending and further jobs, so the economy grows in an upward spiral.
- **Bilateral aid**: aid given directly from one government to another.
- **Multilateral aid**: aid given through international organisations such as the World Bank or the United Nations.
- **Tied aid**: aid given on the condition that the receiving country spends it on goods or services from the donor.
- **Gas flaring**: burning off unwanted natural gas at oil wells, releasing carbon dioxide and toxic pollutants.
- **Desertification**: the process by which land in dry areas becomes desert, through drought, overgrazing and over-cultivation.
- **ECOWAS**: the Economic Community of West African States, a regional trade and political group in which Nigeria plays a leading role.
:::

::: method
1. **Use the eight spec headings.** Most questions target one of them: location/importance, context, industrial structure, TNCs, trade/politics, aid, environment, quality of life. Recognise which one and stay on it.
2. **Case-study detail is what separates the levels.** Replace "a TNC caused pollution" with "Shell's pipeline spills at Bodo in 2008 devastated fishing; Shell agreed £55 million compensation in 2015". Aim for two or three hard facts per paragraph.
3. **TNC questions: advantages AND disadvantages to the host country.** Structure: one paragraph of benefits (jobs, tax, skills, infrastructure), one of costs (pollution, profits leaving, competition, decisions made abroad), then a judgement.
4. **Industrial structure**: say what share each sector has and how it has **changed** (from farming exports to oil, now to services), then explain *why* manufacturing stimulates development (jobs → spending → multiplier → tax).
5. **Aid**: name the type, then give its impact — positive (polio, bed nets) and negative (corruption, dependency, sudden cuts).
6. **4-mark answers** (Level 2 = 3–4): two developed, place-specific points. **6-mark answers** (Level 3 = 5–6): three developed points, each tied to Nigeria. **9-mark "to what extent / evaluate" answers** (Level 3 = 7–9): both sides with detailed evidence and a justified judgement; some carry **+3 SPaG**.
7. **"Use Figure X"** (e.g. a pie chart of GDP by sector or a trade graph): quote the figures with units, describe the change, then explain it with your case-study knowledge.
:::

::: example Location and importance — 4 marks
Describe the location and importance of an LIC or NEE you have studied. (4)

Nigeria is in West Africa on the Gulf of Guinea, bordered by Benin, Niger, Chad and Cameroon (1). It is Africa's most populous country, with about 237 million people (1). Regionally it is a leading member of ECOWAS, whose headquarters are in its capital, Abuja (1). Globally it is important as a major oil exporter and OPEC member, and for its Nollywood film industry (1).
:::

::: example TNC advantages and disadvantages — 6 marks
Explain the advantages and disadvantages of TNCs to an LIC or NEE you have studied. (6)

TNCs bring jobs and investment to Nigeria. Unilever has made products in Nigeria since the 1920s, with factories at Apapa and Agbara, employing local people, buying from local suppliers and paying taxes that fund public services. Shell's oil operations generated large tax and royalty payments for the government.

However, TNC activity has caused serious environmental damage. Oil spills from Shell's pipelines at Bodo in 2008 destroyed fishing grounds and mangroves, and in 2015 Shell agreed a £55 million settlement with the community. Gas flaring has polluted the air across the Niger Delta.

Much of the profit also leaves the country for shareholders abroad, and decisions are made outside Nigeria: in 2023 Unilever Nigeria stopped making products such as Omo and Lux, and in 2025 Shell sold its onshore business. This shows that TNCs can bring growth, but the host country has limited control over it.

(Level 3: advantages and disadvantages both developed with accurate Nigerian detail.)
:::

::: example Quality of life — 9 marks
To what extent has economic development improved the quality of life for people in an LIC or NEE you have studied? (9 + 3 SPaG)

Economic development has improved quality of life for many Nigerians. The growth of services — telecoms, banking, retail and the tech sector in Lagos — has created better-paid jobs and a growing middle class. Mobile phones and mobile banking have given millions of people access to information and markets. Health has improved: life expectancy has risen to about 54.5 years, and aid-funded vaccination helped Nigeria become free of wild polio, with its last case in 2016.

However, the benefits are very unevenly shared. The World Bank estimated about 139 million Nigerians were living in poverty in 2025, and life expectancy remains one of the lowest in the world. Oil wealth has not reached many people in the Niger Delta, where oil spills and gas flaring have damaged farming, fishing and health. Slums such as Makoko in Lagos lack clean water and sanitation, and inflation after the 2023 fuel-subsidy removal hit the poorest hardest. The north, affected by Boko Haram and drought, lags far behind the south.

Overall, development has improved quality of life only to a limited extent. It has benefited urban, educated Nigerians in the south, but for the majority poverty, insecurity and pollution mean life has changed little. Nigeria's HDI of 0.560 shows how far it still has to go.

(Level 3: balanced, well-evidenced, clear judgement.)
:::

::: watch
- Generic answers that could be about any country — every paragraph needs a **Nigerian fact** (a place, a date, a number, a company).
- TNC answers that give only advantages or only disadvantages — the spec says **advantages and disadvantages to the host country**.
- Saying "Nigeria's economy is mainly oil" — oil dominates **exports**, but services are more than half of **GDP**.
- Confusing types of aid (bilateral vs multilateral) or describing aid without its **impact**.
- Quality-of-life answers that ignore **inequality** between north and south, rich and poor.
- Out-of-date facts: Shell sold its onshore business (SPDC) in 2025 — describe its role in the past tense for onshore oil.
:::
