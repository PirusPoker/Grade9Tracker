## What it is

Development is **uneven**: some countries have grown rich while others remain poor, and the **development gap** between them is wide. This topic covers the **causes** of uneven development (physical, economic, historical), its **consequences** (disparities in wealth and health, international migration), an **overview of the strategies** used to reduce the gap, and **an example of how tourism in an LIC or NEE helps to reduce the gap** — Jamaica.

### Causes of uneven development

| Type | Cause | How it holds development back |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | **Landlocked position** (e.g. Chad, Niger, Mali) | No coastline, so goods must cross other countries to reach a port; trade is slower and costlier. |
| | **Extreme climate** — drought in the Sahel, unreliable rainfall | Crop failure and food shortages; farmers cannot build up savings or surplus to sell. |
| | **Natural hazards** — earthquakes, tropical storms, floods | Rebuilding swallows money that could have gone on schools and industry, e.g. Haiti after the 2010 earthquake. |
| | **Disease** — malaria, cholera, sleeping sickness in tropical climates | Sick workers cannot work; money goes on treatment; children miss school. Malaria kills hundreds of thousands of people a year, most of them young children in Africa. |
| | **Few natural resources** — lack of fuel, minerals or fertile soil | Less to sell, less energy for industry. (But having resources does not guarantee development — they may be exploited by others.) |
| **Economic** | **Trade** — many LICs export **primary products** (cocoa, coffee, minerals) with low and fluctuating prices, and import expensive manufactured goods | Their export earnings are small and unpredictable; HICs add the value (and profit) by processing. Tariffs and subsidies in rich countries can make it hard for LIC goods to compete. |
| | **Debt** — loans taken out to fund development | Interest repayments take money away from health and education. |
| | **Lack of investment and infrastructure** | Poor roads, power and water deter firms from locating there. |
| | **Poor governance and corruption** | Money for development is lost; investors are put off. |
| **Historical** | **Colonialism** — European powers ruled much of Africa, Asia and the Caribbean | Raw materials and wealth flowed to the colonial power; economies were built around exporting one or two cash crops; colonial borders ignored ethnic groups, storing up conflict; few local people were trained for skilled jobs. |
| | **Conflict** — civil wars, e.g. in South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of Congo | Destroys infrastructure, kills and displaces people, frightens off investors and diverts spending to the military. |

In reality the causes combine: a landlocked, drought-prone ex-colony that exports one primary product and has high debt faces several barriers at once — a **cycle of poverty**.

### Consequences of uneven development

**Disparities in wealth.** The gap in income between the richest and poorest countries is enormous: GNI per head in the UK is dozens of times higher than in the poorest LICs. Within countries, too, wealth is concentrated in a few cities or regions. Poor countries have less money for schools, health care and infrastructure, which keeps them poor.

**Disparities in health.** Life expectancy in Japan is about 84; in Nigeria it is about 54 — a gap of roughly **30 years**. Infant mortality in LICs can be more than ten times higher than in the UK. Causes: lack of clean water and sanitation, fewer doctors and hospitals, malnutrition, and diseases such as malaria and HIV/AIDS that are easier to prevent and treat in rich countries.

**International migration.** People move from poorer to richer places:

- **Economic migrants** move voluntarily for jobs and higher wages — e.g. from Mexico to the USA, or from poorer EU countries to the UK before Brexit.
- **Refugees** are forced to flee conflict or persecution — e.g. from Syria, Afghanistan and South Sudan.
- Effects on the **source country**: loss of young, skilled workers (the "brain drain") but **remittances** (money sent home) support families and can be a major source of income. Effects on the **host country**: workers fill labour shortages and pay taxes, but there can be pressure on housing and services and political tension.

### Strategies to reduce the development gap — overview

| Strategy | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Investment** | Foreign direct investment (FDI) by TNCs and other countries in factories, mines, infrastructure; e.g. China has invested heavily in roads, railways and ports in Africa | Jobs, skills, infrastructure, tax revenue | Profits often leave the country; can create debt and dependence |
| **Industrial development** | Governments build up manufacturing (e.g. export processing zones with tax breaks) | Manufacturing adds value, pays more than farming, and triggers the **multiplier effect** | Needs power and infrastructure; factory conditions can be poor; pollution |
| **Tourism** | Attracting visitors to beaches, wildlife, culture | Foreign currency, jobs, new infrastructure | **Leakage** of profits abroad; seasonal, low-paid jobs; environmental damage; vulnerable to hazards and recessions |
| **Aid** | Money, goods, expertise or loans given by governments (**bilateral**), via international organisations such as the World Bank (**multilateral**), or by charities (**NGOs**); **short-term (emergency)** or **long-term (development)** | Saves lives in disasters; funds schools, clinics, water supply | Can create dependency; may be **tied** (spent on the donor's goods); can be lost to corruption; donors can cut it — the UK reduced its aid from 0.7% to 0.5% of GNI in 2021 and plans 0.3% by 2027 |
| **Intermediate technology** | Simple, affordable technology that local people can build, use and repair with local materials and skills — e.g. hand-dug wells with hand pumps, small wind turbines, solar lamps, clay "pot-in-pot" fridges promoted by the charity **Practical Action** | Cheap, sustainable, uses local skills, does not need imported fuel or experts | Small-scale — improves local lives but not national GDP quickly |
| **Fairtrade** | Producers get a guaranteed **minimum price** plus a **Fairtrade premium** to invest in their community; e.g. the **Kuapa Kokoo** cocoa co-operative in Ghana, which co-owns Divine Chocolate | Stable income; premium funds schools, wells, clinics; better working conditions | Only a small share of world trade; certification costs; higher prices for consumers; benefits may not reach labourers |
| **Debt relief** | Cancelling or reducing debts, often on condition the savings go on health and education — e.g. the **Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative** from 1996; **Zambia** received debt relief in 2005 and removed charges for health care in rural areas | Frees money for services | Conditions can be strict; corrupt governments may misuse savings; countries can build up new debt |
| **Microfinance loans** | Very small loans to people too poor for normal banks, often women, to start a business; pioneered by the **Grameen Bank** in Bangladesh (Muhammad Yunus, Nobel Peace Prize 2006) | Helps people escape poverty through their own work; high repayment rates | Some borrowers fall into debt; loans too small to lift whole countries |

**Top-down vs bottom-up**: large projects run by governments and big organisations (investment, industrial development, large aid projects) are **top-down**; local, community-led schemes (intermediate technology, microfinance, many NGO projects) are **bottom-up**.

### Case example: tourism in Jamaica

**Location and background.** Jamaica is a Caribbean island of about 2.8 million people, south of Cuba, independent from Britain since 1962. It is a middle-income country with high poverty in parts of the capital, Kingston, and in rural areas.

**Why tourists go**: a warm tropical climate, white-sand beaches and coral reefs; the **Blue Mountains** (coffee, hiking); **Dunn's River Falls** near Ocho Rios; culture — reggae music and the **Bob Marley Museum**; resorts at **Montego Bay, Negril and Ocho Rios**; cruise ship ports; and short flight times from the USA.

**Scale**: about **4.3 million visitors in 2024** (stopover tourists plus cruise passengers), earning about **USD 4.3 billion**. Tourism is the island's largest earner of foreign exchange and, counting indirect effects, contributes around **a third of GDP**; it employs about **175,000 people directly**.

**How tourism helps reduce the development gap**

- **Employment**: jobs in hotels, restaurants, attractions, transport and crafts, giving a regular wage and training in skills such as languages and hospitality.
- **Foreign exchange and government revenue**: taxes on hotels, airports and tourists fund schools, health care and infrastructure.
- **Multiplier effect**: tourist spending creates jobs in farming (food for hotels), construction and local shops; those workers spend their wages, supporting more businesses.
- **Infrastructure**: improved airports (Montego Bay's Sangster International is the main tourist gateway), roads such as the **North–South Highway** (Highway 2000) linking Kingston to Ocho Rios, water and electricity supply — which also benefit local people.
- **Quality of life**: higher incomes and better services raise living standards for employees and their families.

**Limits and problems**

- **Leakage**: many hotels and **all-inclusive resorts** are foreign-owned, and much food and drink is imported, so a large share of tourist spending leaves Jamaica. Estimates suggest only around **40%** of tourism foreign exchange is retained.
- **Enclave tourism**: all-inclusive guests stay inside the resort, so little money reaches local restaurants and traders.
- **Jobs** are often low-paid and seasonal, with few managerial posts for local people.
- **Environmental damage**: building resorts and cruise terminals has cleared mangroves and damaged coral reefs; more water and waste pressure.
- **Inequality**: gated resorts sit beside poor communities, and crime can deter visitors.
- **Vulnerability**: tourism collapses in recessions, pandemics and disasters. In October 2025 **Hurricane Melissa**, a Category 5 storm and the strongest to hit Jamaica in decades, caused damage the World Bank estimated at about **USD 8.8 billion**; about half the island's hotels were damaged and dozens of resorts closed during the peak season, although the main resort areas and airports had reopened by December 2025.

**Judgement**: tourism has clearly raised incomes and funded infrastructure, so it narrows the gap — but only partly, because of leakage, low wages and dependence on one fragile industry. Jamaica's aim is to increase **linkages** (e.g. hotels buying local food) so more of each tourist dollar stays on the island.

::: terms
- **Uneven development**: the way development differs between countries and regions, so some become much richer than others.
- **Primary product**: a raw material or unprocessed good, such as cocoa, coffee or copper, usually of low value.
- **Colonialism**: the control of one country by another, often exploiting its resources and people for the ruling country's benefit.
- **International migration**: the movement of people from one country to another, as economic migrants or as refugees.
- **Remittances**: money sent home by migrants to their families in their country of origin.
- **Foreign direct investment**: investment by a company or government from one country in businesses or infrastructure in another.
- **Aid**: help in the form of money, goods, expertise or loans given by governments, international organisations or charities.
- **Intermediate technology**: simple, affordable technology that local people can build, use and maintain with local materials and skills.
- **Fairtrade**: a system that gives producers in poorer countries a guaranteed minimum price and a premium to invest in their community.
- **Debt relief**: cancelling or reducing a country's debts so that money can be spent on development instead of repayments.
- **Microfinance**: very small loans to people who cannot get normal bank loans, to help them start or grow a small business.
- **Leakage**: the proportion of money spent by tourists that leaves the host country, e.g. to foreign-owned hotels or for imported goods.
- **Multiplier effect**: when money spent in an area creates jobs and incomes that lead to further spending, so the local economy grows.
:::

::: method
1. **Causes: sort into physical, economic and historical** and explain *how* each holds development back — "Chad is landlocked, so exports cost more to transport, making its goods less competitive." A cause without the "so" is only half an answer.
2. **Consequences: use the three spec headings** — wealth, health and migration — with a figure where you can (e.g. the 30-year life-expectancy gap between Japan and Nigeria).
3. **Strategies: for any strategy, give how it works, one benefit and one problem.** "Evaluate a strategy" questions need both sides and a judgement on how far it closes the gap.
4. **Tourism case study**: learn Jamaica's attractions, the scale (visitors, earnings, jobs), the benefits (jobs, multiplier, infrastructure, foreign exchange) and the limits (leakage, low pay, environmental damage, vulnerability to hurricanes).
5. **4-mark answers** (Level 2 = 3–4): two developed points. **6-mark answers** (Level 3 = 5–6): three developed points linked to examples. **9-mark "to what extent" answers** (Level 3 = 7–9): arguments for and against, detailed Jamaica evidence and a judgement — "to a large extent / partly, because…". Some 9-markers carry **+3 SPaG**.
6. **"Use Figure X"**: when given a graph of tourist numbers or earnings, describe the trend (up/down/fluctuating), quote start and end values with units and year, and identify anomalies (e.g. a sharp fall in 2020).
:::

::: example Physical cause — 2 marks
Explain one physical cause of uneven development. (2)

Many LICs such as Chad are landlocked (1), so goods must be transported through other countries to reach a port, raising costs and making it harder to trade profitably (1).
:::

::: example Historical cause — 4 marks
Explain how historical factors have contributed to uneven development. (4)

Colonialism meant European countries took raw materials from their colonies to fuel their own industrial growth (1), so wealth flowed to the colonial power rather than staying in the colony (1). Colonial economies were often based on exporting one or two cash crops, leaving them dependent on low-value primary products after independence (1). Colonial borders ignored ethnic groups, contributing to later conflicts, such as civil wars, which destroyed infrastructure and deterred investment (1).
:::

::: example Tourism — 6 marks
Using an example, explain how tourism can help reduce the development gap. (6)

Tourism in Jamaica creates jobs: about 175,000 people are directly employed in hotels, restaurants and attractions in resorts such as Montego Bay and Ocho Rios. Regular wages allow workers to pay for better housing, food and their children's education, which raises quality of life.

Tourism earns foreign exchange — about USD 4.3 billion in 2024 — and taxes on hotels and visitors provide the government with revenue to spend on schools, health care and infrastructure. Roads such as the North–South Highway and improved airports help tourists but also benefit local people and other industries.

The multiplier effect spreads these benefits: hotels buy food from local farmers and hire builders, and those workers spend their wages in local shops. However, leakage limits the gains — many all-inclusive resorts are foreign-owned and import food — so only part of the money stays in Jamaica.

(Level 3: three well-developed benefits with Jamaica detail and a limitation.)
:::

::: example Evaluate a strategy — 9 marks
To what extent can tourism reduce the development gap in an LIC or NEE you have studied? (9 + 3 SPaG)

Tourism has done a lot to reduce the development gap in Jamaica. It is the country's biggest source of foreign exchange, earning about USD 4.3 billion from about 4.3 million visitors in 2024, and contributes around a third of GDP including indirect effects. About 175,000 people work directly in the industry, and through the multiplier effect farmers, builders and craft sellers also gain. Government revenue from tourism helps pay for schools and clinics, and infrastructure built for tourists, such as the North–South Highway, benefits everyone.

However, the benefits are limited. Many all-inclusive resorts are foreign-owned and import much of their food, so leakage means only around 40% of the foreign exchange stays on the island. All-inclusive guests rarely leave the resort, so local businesses gain little. Many jobs are seasonal and low-paid, so workers remain relatively poor, and poverty persists in parts of Kingston. Resort building has damaged coral reefs and mangroves. Above all, depending on tourism is risky: Hurricane Melissa in October 2025 caused damage estimated at USD 8.8 billion and closed dozens of resorts in the peak season.

Overall, tourism reduces the development gap to a moderate extent. It has raised incomes and funded infrastructure, but leakage, low wages and vulnerability to hazards and recessions mean it cannot close the gap on its own. The benefit would be greater if more hotels were locally owned and bought local produce, and if Jamaica diversified its economy.

(Level 3: balanced, detailed, clear judgement.)
