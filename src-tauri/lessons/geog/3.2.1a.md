## What it is

**Urbanisation** is the **increase in the proportion of a country's population living in towns and cities** (urban areas). It is not the same as **urban growth**, which is simply an increase in the *number* of people or the *area* covered by towns and cities. A country can have urban growth without urbanisation if its rural population grows just as fast. The key idea: **a growing percentage of the world's population lives in urban areas** — but the speed, causes and results of this change are very different in rich and poor parts of the world.

### The global pattern of urban change

- In **1950** about **30%** of the world's population lived in urban areas.
- Around **2007** the world passed a milestone: for the first time, **more people lived in urban areas than rural areas**.
- By **2018** the figure was about **55%**, and the UN projects about **68% by 2050** (UN *World Urbanization Prospects 2018* — the figures used in the standard textbooks).
- Nearly all future urban growth will be in **Asia and Africa**; India, China and Nigeria alone were projected to account for over a third of the growth in urban population between 2018 and 2050.

**A note on definitions**: countries define "urban" differently (in some a settlement of 2,000 counts; in others it must be much larger), so percentages are hard to compare. In **November 2025** the UN published new figures using one global method: about **45%** of the world's 8.2 billion people now live in **cities**, with most of the rest in **towns** — so urban living (cities plus towns) is still the majority. Either way, the trend is the same: more people, in bigger urban areas, especially in Asia and Africa.

Drawing the pattern (e.g. from a choropleth world map): the **highest percentages urban** are in North America, Latin America, Europe, Australia and parts of the Middle East (often **75–85%+**); the **lowest** are in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia (many under **40%**) — but these are exactly the places where urbanisation is happening **fastest**.

### Urban trends in HICs, NEEs and LICs

| | **HICs** (high-income countries, e.g. UK, USA, Japan) | **NEEs** (newly emerging economies, e.g. Brazil, China, India, Nigeria) | **LICs** (low-income countries, e.g. Ethiopia, Afghanistan, Malawi) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Level of urbanisation** | high — most people already urban (UK about 80%+) | moderate to high and rising (Brazil over 80%; China over 60%) | low but rising quickly |
| **Rate of urbanisation** | **slow** — little rural population left to move | **rapid**, driven by industrialisation | **fastest** — rural–urban migration and high natural increase |
| **When it happened** | mainly in the 19th and early 20th centuries with the **Industrial Revolution** | mainly since the 1950s–60s | mainly since the 1970s–80s, still under way |
| **Current trends** | **counter-urbanisation** (people moving out to smaller towns and the countryside, helped by cars and home-working) and **re-urbanisation** (people moving back into regenerated city centres) | fast growth of large cities, often with **informal settlements** (slums) on the edges | very fast growth of the largest city, huge squatter settlements, services unable to keep up |

Latin America urbanised earlier than Asia and Africa, which is why countries such as Brazil already have urban majorities comparable to HICs.

### Factors affecting the rate of urbanisation

Urbanisation is driven by two processes: **rural–urban migration** and **natural increase**.

**1. Migration — push–pull theory**

People decide to move because of **push factors** (negative things that drive them away from the countryside) and **pull factors** (positive things — real or perceived — that attract them to the city).

| Push factors (from rural areas) | Pull factors (to urban areas) |
|---|---|
| **poverty** and low incomes from subsistence farming | more **jobs**, especially in factories and services, with **higher wages** |
| **mechanisation** of farming — machines replace farm workers | better **healthcare** — hospitals, clinics, doctors |
| **natural hazards** such as **drought** and floods, leading to crop failure and famine | better **education** — secondary schools, colleges, universities |
| **desertification** and land degradation | access to **clean water, electricity** and sanitation |
| **conflict and war**, which force people to flee | perceived better **quality of life** and entertainment ("bright lights") |
| lack of services — few schools, clinics or clean water | family and friends already there (chain migration) |
| land being taken over by large farms or mining companies | safety and more political stability |

Pull factors are often **perceived** rather than real: many migrants end up in informal jobs and slum housing. Even so, cities in NEEs and LICs usually do offer better chances than the countryside, so migration continues.

**2. Natural increase**

**Natural increase** = **birth rate − death rate** (when births exceed deaths, the population grows).

- Rural–urban migrants are mostly **young adults (aged roughly 18–35)** — the age group most likely to have children. So cities in LICs and NEEs have **high birth rates**.
- At the same time, **better healthcare** in cities (hospitals, vaccinations, clean water) means the **death rate falls** and people live longer.
- High birth rate plus falling death rate = rapid natural increase. In many large cities of the Global South, natural increase now causes **more urban growth than migration** does.

Migration and natural increase **reinforce each other**: migration brings young people, who then raise the birth rate.

### The emergence of megacities

A **megacity** is an urban area with a population of **over 10 million**.

- In **1950** there were only **two**: New York and Tokyo — both in HICs.
- By **1975** there were **8**; by the late 2010s there were **over 30**.
- The UN (2025) counts **33 megacities**, **19 of them in Asia**, and projects **37 by 2050**. The largest are now **Jakarta** (about 42 million), **Dhaka** (about 40 million) and **Tokyo** (about 33 million).
- New megacities are emerging almost entirely in **NEEs and LICs**, especially in **Asia and Africa** (e.g. Lagos, Kinshasa, Cairo, Delhi, Karachi), while **HIC megacities** such as Tokyo and New York are growing slowly or have stopped growing.

Why megacities have emerged mostly in poorer countries:

- **Rapid rural–urban migration** into a country's largest city, where jobs and services are concentrated (**primate cities**).
- **High natural increase** among young urban populations.
- **Industrialisation and globalisation** — TNCs locate factories and offices in big cities with ports and airports, creating jobs that attract more migrants.

Megacities can be grouped by how fast they are growing: **slow-growing** (mostly in HICs, e.g. Tokyo, with low birth rates and ageing populations); **growing** (mostly NEEs, e.g. Mexico City, São Paulo); and **rapidly growing** (mostly LICs and NEEs in Africa and Asia, e.g. Lagos, Dhaka), where a large share of residents live in **slums** or **squatter settlements**.

::: terms
- **Urbanisation**: an increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas.
- **Urban growth**: an increase in the number of people living in urban areas or the area they cover.
- **Megacity**: an urban area with a population of more than 10 million people.
- **Rural–urban migration**: the movement of people from the countryside to towns and cities.
- **Push factor**: a negative feature of a place that makes people want to leave it, such as poverty or drought.
- **Pull factor**: a positive feature, real or perceived, that attracts people to a place, such as jobs or healthcare.
- **Natural increase**: the growth in population when the birth rate is higher than the death rate.
- **Counter-urbanisation**: the movement of people out of large cities to smaller towns, villages and the countryside.
- **HIC**: a high-income country, with gross national income per head above the World Bank threshold, such as the UK.
- **LIC**: a low-income country, with a low gross national income per head, such as Ethiopia or Malawi.
- **NEE**: a newly emerging economy that is industrialising rapidly, such as Brazil, India or Nigeria.
:::

::: method
1. **Define carefully**: urbanisation is about the **proportion** living in urban areas, not just the number. Half-marks are lost by mixing it up with urban growth.
2. **"Use Figure X" (maps and graphs)**: describe the **pattern** first (general trend), then give **specific evidence** (named regions or countries with figures), then an **anomaly** if there is one. For graphs: state the trend, quote start and end values, and describe the rate of change ("increased from 30% in 1950 to 55% in 2018 — a rise of 25 percentage points, faster after 1990").
3. **Explain push–pull**: pair a push with a pull and develop each — *what* it is, *why* it causes migration. "Jobs" alone is a point; "factory jobs pay regular wages, far more than subsistence farming, so young adults move to send money home" is developed.
4. **Natural increase**: always make the **link to migration** — migrants are young adults, so birth rates are high; better urban healthcare lowers death rates.
5. **Compare HICs and LICs**: use comparative language (*higher, slower, earlier*) in **both halves of each sentence**, not two separate descriptions.
6. **Levels**: 4-mark — Level 2 (3–4) needs developed explanation. 6-mark — Level 3 (5–6) needs several developed points with specific examples. **9-mark** (with **+3 SPaG** — this topic is Paper 2 Section A) — Level 3 (7–9) needs detailed, well-developed points with named examples, **both sides considered**, and a **clear, supported judgement**. SPaG marks reward accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar and a wide range of specialist terms.
:::

What is meant by the term "urbanisation"? (1)

An increase in the proportion (percentage) of a country's population living in towns and cities (1).

::: example Explain — push and pull
Explain why people migrate from rural areas to cities in LICs and NEEs. (4)

In rural areas, drought can cause crops to fail, so farmers lose their food and income (1), pushing them to leave to find another way to survive (1). Cities offer more jobs, for example in factories and services, which pay regular wages that are much higher than subsistence farming (1), so migrants are pulled by the chance to earn money and send it back to their families, as well as better access to schools and hospitals (1).
:::

::: example Assess — migration versus natural increase (9 + 3 SPaG)
"Rural–urban migration is the main cause of rapid urbanisation in LICs and NEEs." Do you agree? (9 + 3 SPaG)

Rural–urban migration is clearly an important cause. Push factors such as poverty, mechanisation of farming, drought and conflict drive people out of rural areas, while pull factors such as jobs in factories, higher wages and better healthcare and education attract them to cities. Industrialisation in NEEs, often led by TNCs, has concentrated jobs in large cities, so millions of people have moved; this is why cities such as Lagos and Dhaka have grown into megacities.

However, natural increase is also very important and in many cities now causes more growth than migration. Most migrants are young adults aged 18–35, who are at the age when people start families, so birth rates in cities are high. At the same time, better healthcare, clean water and vaccination programmes in cities lower the death rate, so the gap between births and deaths is large and the population grows rapidly.

The two causes are linked: migration brings young people to the city, and it is these young migrants who raise the birth rate. Without migration, natural increase in the city would be smaller.

Overall, I partly agree. Migration was the main cause of the initial rapid growth of cities in LICs and NEEs and still drives it where industrialisation is recent, but in many established megacities natural increase is now the larger cause. The most accurate conclusion is that migration starts rapid urbanisation and natural increase sustains it.

(Level 3: both causes developed with examples, the link between them analysed, and a clear, supported judgement; high SPaG — accurate, wide range of terms.)

::: q
Explain how natural increase contributes to urbanisation. (3)
---
Migrants to cities are mostly young adults of child-bearing age (1), so birth rates in cities are high (1); better healthcare and clean water in cities lower the death rate, so births far exceed deaths and the urban population grows quickly (1).

::: q
Compare urban trends in HICs and LICs. (4)
---
HICs have a much higher percentage urban than LICs (1), but their rate of urbanisation is slower because most people already live in cities (1). HICs urbanised earlier, during the Industrial Revolution, while LICs are urbanising now, faster than any HIC did (1). Some HICs are experiencing counter-urbanisation, whereas LICs are experiencing rapid rural–urban migration (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why most new megacities are in Asia and Africa. (4)
---
Many countries in Asia and Africa are NEEs or LICs undergoing rapid industrialisation (1), so large numbers of rural people migrate to the biggest cities for jobs and services (1). These migrants are young, so birth rates are high while healthcare lowers death rates, adding rapid natural increase (1). HIC megacities such as Tokyo have low birth rates and little rural population left to move, so they grow slowly (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why pull factors to cities are sometimes described as "perceived" rather than real. (2)
---
Migrants may expect well-paid jobs and good housing (1), but many end up in informal work and living in slums or squatter settlements without clean water or sanitation, so the city is not as good as they imagined (1).
:::

- Defining urbanisation as "cities getting bigger" — it is an increase in the **proportion** of people living in urban areas.

- Forgetting to link **natural increase** to migration (young migrants → high birth rate).
- In "Use Figure X" questions, describing without **figures** — quote values, dates and the change.
- Saying HICs are "urbanising fastest" because they are most urban — they have the **highest level** but the **slowest rate**.
- 9-markers without a **judgement** — and throwing away the **3 SPaG marks** with careless spelling of specialist terms (megacity, migration, urbanisation).
