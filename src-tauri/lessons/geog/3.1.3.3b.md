## What it is

As a river flows from source to mouth, the balance of its processes changes — **vertical erosion** in the upper course, **lateral erosion** in the middle course, **lateral erosion and deposition** in the lower course. Each part of the river therefore has its own **distinctive landforms**. The specification groups them by the processes that form them:

| Formed by | Landforms | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Erosion** | interlocking spurs, waterfalls, gorges | upper course |
| **Erosion and deposition** | meanders, ox-bow lakes | middle and lower course |
| **Deposition** | levées, flood plains, estuaries | lower course |

For every landform you need its **characteristics** (what it looks like) and its **formation** (a step-by-step sequence of processes). Formation answers are where most marks are won or lost.

### Landforms of erosion

**Interlocking spurs**

- *Characteristics*: ridges of high land (**spurs**) that stick out alternately from each side of a steep, narrow V-shaped valley, overlapping ("interlocking") like the teeth of a zip. The river winds around them. Looking upstream, you cannot see far because the spurs block the view.
- *Formation*: in the upper course the river has lots of energy for **vertical erosion** but little for lateral erosion. It is not powerful enough to cut through areas of **harder, more resistant rock**, so it swings around them, cutting down into the softer rock between. The valley sides are worn back by weathering and mass movement, forming the V shape, and the spurs of hard rock are left interlocking on either side.

**Waterfalls**

- *Characteristics*: a sudden, steep drop in the river's long profile where water falls vertically over a ledge of rock, usually with a deep **plunge pool** at the base and an **overhang** of hard rock at the top.
- *Formation*, step by step:
  1. Waterfalls form where a band of **hard, resistant rock** lies on top of **softer, less resistant rock**.
  2. The river erodes the soft rock more quickly than the hard rock (**differential erosion**), so a **step** forms in the river bed.
  3. Water falls over the step and **hydraulic action and abrasion** erode the soft rock at the base, creating a deep **plunge pool** (swirling rocks in the pool deepen it by abrasion).
  4. The soft rock behind the falling water is **undercut**, leaving an **overhang** of hard rock.
  5. With no support, the overhang eventually **collapses** into the plunge pool. The rocks that fall are used to erode further by abrasion.
  6. The process repeats, so the waterfall **retreats upstream**.

**Gorges**

- *Characteristics*: a narrow, steep-sided valley, often with near-vertical rock walls, found **immediately downstream of a waterfall**.
- *Formation*: as the waterfall retreats upstream through repeated undercutting and collapse, it leaves behind a steep-sided gorge. The length of the gorge shows how far the waterfall has retreated.

### Landforms of erosion and deposition

**Meanders**

- *Characteristics*: large, sweeping bends in the river, found in the middle and lower course. A cross-section across a meander is **asymmetrical**: a **steep river cliff** on the **outside** of the bend, where the water is deepest, and a **gently sloping slip-off slope** (a **point bar** of deposited sand and gravel) on the **inside** of the bend, where the water is shallow.
- *Formation*:
  1. Where the river flows over a gentle gradient, any small bend makes the current swing towards one bank. The line of fastest flow (the **thalweg**) moves to the **outside** of the bend.
  2. On the outside bend the water flows faster and has more energy, so **lateral erosion** by hydraulic action and abrasion undercuts the bank, forming a **river cliff**.
  3. On the inside bend the water is shallower and slower, with more friction, so it loses energy and **deposits** sediment, forming a **slip-off slope**.
  4. A spiralling current (**helicoidal flow**) carries eroded material from the outside of one bend towards the inside of the next.
  5. Over time erosion on the outside and deposition on the inside make the bend more pronounced, and meanders **migrate** across the flood plain and slowly downstream.

**Ox-bow lakes**

- *Characteristics*: horseshoe-shaped lakes on the flood plain, separated from the main channel, often slowly filling with silt and plants and eventually drying out to become a **meander scar**.
- *Formation*:
  1. Erosion on the outside bends of a meander narrows the **neck** of land between two bends.
  2. The neck gets narrower and narrower until, usually **during a flood** when the river has extra energy, the river **cuts through** the neck.
  3. The river now takes the **shortest, straightest route** and flows through the new channel.
  4. **Deposition** seals off the ends of the old loop from the new channel, because the water entering it is slow.
  5. The cut-off loop becomes an **ox-bow lake**.

### Landforms of deposition

**Flood plains**

- *Characteristics*: the wide, flat area of land on either side of a river in its lower course, which floods when the river overtops its banks. Made of **alluvium** (fertile silt). Often used for farming and — riskily — for building.
- *Formation*: meanders **migrate** across the valley floor, and lateral erosion on the outside bends **widens** the valley; deposition on the inside bends builds up the floor. When the river floods, water spreads across the land, slows down, loses energy and **deposits silt**, which builds up the flood plain layer by layer over many floods.

**Levées**

- *Characteristics*: **natural embankments** of sediment along the banks of a river, raising the banks above the level of the flood plain. They are made of coarser sediment near the channel and slope gently away onto the flood plain.
- *Formation*:
  1. When the river **floods**, water spills over its banks onto the flood plain.
  2. As it leaves the channel, the water **slows down immediately** because of friction with the land, so it loses energy and **deposits** its load.
  3. The **largest, heaviest** material is dropped first, **closest to the river bank**; finer silt and clay are carried further and dropped across the flood plain.
  4. After many floods, the coarse material builds up into raised banks — levées — along the river edge. (Between floods, deposition on the bed can also raise the channel.)

**Estuaries**

- *Characteristics*: the **tidal mouth** of a river where fresh river water meets salty sea water. Usually wide and funnel-shaped, with extensive **mudflats** and **salt marshes** exposed at low tide. Important habitats for wading birds and fish, and often sites of ports and industry.
- *Formation*: twice a day the tide comes in and the sea water meets the river, checking its flow. The river water slows and deposits its load of fine silt and clay. As the tide goes out, large areas of **mudflats** are exposed; where plants colonise them, **salt marshes** develop. The estuary floods at high tide and drains at low tide.

### Example: the River Tees

The **River Tees** in north-east England is the standard AQA textbook example of a UK river valley showing all these landforms.

- **Source**: the eastern slopes of **Cross Fell** (893 m, the highest point in the Pennines) in the **North Pennines**.
- **Length**: about **137 km**, flowing east to the **North Sea**.
- **Mouth**: an estuary at **Teesmouth**, between **Hartlepool** and **Redcar**, past **Middlesbrough**.

| Course | Location | Landforms and evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper** | Upper Teesdale, North Pennines | steep **V-shaped valley** and **interlocking spurs** in upper Teesdale (e.g. around **Cauldron Snout**, a series of cascades over hard rock); **High Force** waterfall and its **gorge**; **Low Force** waterfall a little further downstream |
| **Middle** | around **Middleton-in-Teesdale** and **Barnard Castle** | the valley widens; the river begins to **meander** as lateral erosion takes over; a small flood plain develops |
| **Lower** | **Darlington, Yarm, Stockton-on-Tees** | large **meanders** — the town of **Yarm** sits inside a big meander loop; **ox-bow lakes** and meander scars on the flood plain; **levées** along the banks (natural, and artificially raised for flood protection); wide flat **flood plain** |
| **Mouth** | Middlesbrough to Teesmouth | the **Tees estuary**, with mudflats and sandflats such as **Seal Sands**, home to wading birds and a colony of seals |

**High Force** — the key detail for exams:

- About **21 m** high, one of England's highest waterfalls.
- It falls over the **Whin Sill**, a band of very **hard dolerite** (igneous rock) formed about 295 million years ago when molten rock was forced between existing rock layers.
- Beneath the Whin Sill are **softer** layers of **Carboniferous limestone, sandstone and shale**, which are eroded more quickly.
- Hydraulic action and abrasion undercut the softer rock, the dolerite overhang collapses, and the waterfall retreats. It has left behind a steep-sided **gorge about 700 m long** downstream.

**Human change in the lower Tees**: the lower Tees once had even larger meanders near Stockton. In the 19th century two artificial channels were cut across meander necks to shorten the route for ships — the **Mandale Cut (1810)** and the **Portrack Cut (1831)** — a human version of ox-bow lake formation. The **Tees Barrage** at Stockton (built 1991–95) now controls the tidal flow in the lower river. The estuary is also heavily industrialised, with the port of **Teesport** and chemical works; much of its original mudflat has been reclaimed for industry.

**How to use the Tees in an answer**: name a landform, give its precise location on the Tees, and explain it with the process sequence — e.g. "High Force (21 m) formed where the hard Whin Sill dolerite overlies softer limestone and shale…".

::: terms
- **Interlocking spurs**: alternating ridges of hard rock that project from either side of a V-shaped valley, which the river winds around in its upper course.
- **Waterfall**: a sudden vertical drop in a river's course where hard rock lies on top of softer rock.
- **Plunge pool**: a deep pool at the base of a waterfall, eroded by hydraulic action and abrasion.
- **Gorge**: a narrow, steep-sided valley left downstream as a waterfall retreats.
- **Meander**: a large bend in a river, with a river cliff on the outside bend and a slip-off slope on the inside bend.
- **River cliff**: a steep bank on the outside of a meander bend, formed by lateral erosion.
- **Slip-off slope**: a gentle slope of deposited sediment on the inside of a meander bend, where the water is slow and shallow.
- **Ox-bow lake**: a horseshoe-shaped lake formed when a meander is cut off from the main river channel.
- **Flood plain**: the wide, flat area of land either side of a river that floods, built up of alluvium.
- **Levée**: a natural raised embankment of sediment along a river bank, formed by repeated flooding.
- **Estuary**: the tidal mouth of a river where fresh water meets sea water and mudflats form.
- **Differential erosion**: when softer rock erodes faster than neighbouring harder rock.
:::

::: method
1. **Characteristics vs formation**: "Describe the characteristics" wants what it looks like (shape, size, features, location). "Explain the formation" wants a **sequence of processes**. Do not just describe the landform when asked to explain it.
2. **Formation answers = numbered chain**. Start with the conditions (hard over soft rock; a bend; a flood), then the process by name (hydraulic action, abrasion, lateral erosion, deposition), then what that does, then what happens next, then the final landform. Use "this causes… so… eventually…".
3. **Name processes precisely**. "Erosion" alone is vague — say *hydraulic action and abrasion undercut the soft rock*. "Deposition" needs a reason: *the water slows down and loses energy*.
4. **Diagrams earn marks**. You may draw an annotated sketch (e.g. a cross-section of a waterfall showing hard rock, soft rock, overhang, plunge pool, gorge; or before-and-after plan views of an ox-bow lake). Labels must explain, not just name. Describe it in words too, in case the diagram is unclear.
5. **"Use Figure X" / OS map questions**: identify the landform from contour patterns (closely spaced contours = steep valley sides; the river crossing contours = a waterfall), give **grid references** and **distances** from the map.
6. **Named example (the Tees)**: 4- or 6-mark questions about "a river valley you have studied" need named locations — High Force, the Whin Sill, Yarm, Teesmouth. Level 3 (5–6) requires accurate place-specific detail linked to processes; generic answers cap at Level 2.
7. **Levels**: 4-mark — Level 1 (1–2) simple, Level 2 (3–4) developed sequence. 6-mark — Level 3 (5–6) needs a full, logically sequenced explanation with accurate terminology. 9-mark practice — explain several landforms, weigh up the relative importance of erosion and deposition, and give a judgement.
:::

::: example Explain — formation of a waterfall
Explain the formation of a waterfall. (4)

Waterfalls form where a band of hard rock lies over softer rock (1). The softer rock is eroded more quickly by hydraulic action and abrasion, so the hard rock is undercut, creating an overhang and a deep plunge pool at the base (1). Eventually the overhang is unsupported and collapses into the plunge pool, where the fallen rocks are swirled around and deepen the pool further by abrasion (1). The process repeats, so the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge downstream (1).
:::

::: example Explain — formation of an ox-bow lake
Explain how an ox-bow lake is formed. (6)

Ox-bow lakes form from meanders in the lower course. On the outside of each bend the fastest flow erodes the bank by hydraulic action and abrasion (lateral erosion), creating a river cliff, while on the inside bend the slower, shallower water deposits sediment as a slip-off slope. This makes the meander loop more and more pronounced.

As erosion continues on the outside bends, the neck of land between two bends becomes narrower. During a flood, when the river has much more energy and discharge, the water breaks through the narrow neck and takes the shortest, straightest route.

The river now flows along the new straight channel, and because the water entering the old loop is slow, deposition seals off its ends. The abandoned loop is left as a horseshoe-shaped ox-bow lake on the flood plain; over time it fills with sediment and vegetation to become a meander scar. On the lower River Tees near Stockton, people shortened the route artificially in the same way by cutting through meander necks (the Mandale Cut, 1810).

(Level 3: full sequence with named processes, the role of flooding and deposition sealing the loop, accurate terms, and a place-specific link.)
:::

::: example Case study — a UK river valley
Using a named example, explain how erosion has formed landforms in the upper course of a river valley. (6)

On the River Tees in the North Pennines, the upper course flows in a steep V-shaped valley because the river is high above base level and uses its energy for vertical erosion; it winds around ridges of more resistant rock, leaving interlocking spurs in upper Teesdale.

The best-known landform is High Force, a waterfall about 21 m high. It has formed where the Tees flows over the Whin Sill, a band of very hard dolerite, which lies above softer Carboniferous limestone, sandstone and shale. Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the softer rocks faster, undercutting the dolerite and creating a deep plunge pool. The unsupported overhang of dolerite eventually collapses, and the waterfall retreats upstream.

As High Force has retreated it has left a steep-sided gorge about 700 m long downstream, showing how far the waterfall has moved. Differential erosion between hard and soft rock is therefore the key reason these upper-course landforms exist on the Tees.

(Level 3: accurate named example with specific facts, processes sequenced and linked to each landform.)
:::

::: example Describe — levées
Describe the characteristics of levées. (3)

Levées are raised embankments running along both banks of a river in its lower course (1). They are higher than the surrounding flood plain, and the sediment is coarsest next to the river, becoming finer further away (1). The land slopes gently from the top of the levée down onto the flood plain (1).
:::

State two characteristics of a gorge. (2)
---
Narrow (1); steep-sided / near-vertical rock walls (1); found downstream of a waterfall (1). Any two.
:::

::: q
Explain why a meander has a river cliff on one side and a slip-off slope on the other. (4)
---
The fastest current swings to the outside of the bend, where the water is deeper and has more energy (1), so lateral erosion by hydraulic action and abrasion undercuts the bank to form a steep river cliff (1). On the inside bend the water is shallow and slow because of friction (1), so it loses energy and deposits sediment, building a gentle slip-off slope (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how levées are formed. (4)
---
When a river floods, water spills over its banks (1). As it leaves the channel it slows down because of friction, so it loses energy and deposits its load (1). The heaviest, coarsest material is dropped first, closest to the bank, while finer silt is carried further across the flood plain (1). After repeated floods, the coarse material builds up into raised banks along the river — levées (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how interlocking spurs form. (3)
---
In the upper course the river has lots of energy for vertical erosion but little for lateral erosion (1). It cannot cut through outcrops of harder rock, so it winds around them, eroding down into softer rock (1). The ridges of hard rock are left projecting alternately from each side of the V-shaped valley, overlapping like a zip (1).
:::

::: q
Explain how an estuary is formed and describe one feature found there. (4)
---
The estuary is the tidal mouth of the river where river water meets the sea (1). The tide checks the river's flow, so the water slows and deposits fine silt and clay (1). Feature: mudflats (1) — large areas of fine sediment exposed at low tide, colonised by plants to form salt marshes, e.g. Seal Sands on the Tees estuary (1).
:::

::: q
For a named UK river, identify one landform of erosion and one landform of deposition, and state where each is found. (4)
---
River Tees: landform of erosion — High Force waterfall (1), in the upper course in the North Pennines where the river flows over the Whin Sill (1). Landform of deposition — levées / estuary mudflats (1) on the lower Tees near Yarm and Stockton / at Teesmouth, e.g. Seal Sands (1).

::: q
Explain how a flood plain is formed. (4)
---
Meanders migrate across the valley floor, and lateral erosion on the outside bends widens the valley (1), while deposition on the inside bends builds up the floor (1). When the river floods, water spreads across the land, slows and deposits silt (alluvium) (1). Over many floods the silt builds up into a wide, flat, fertile flood plain (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Describing instead of explaining** formation: "a waterfall is a big drop" earns nothing on an explain question — give the sequence (hard over soft rock → undercutting → overhang → collapse → retreat).
- Forgetting the **flood** in ox-bow lake formation and the **deposition that seals off** the loop.
- Putting erosion on the **inside** of a meander bend — the fastest flow and erosion are on the **outside**.
- Levées: forgetting to say **why** the coarse material is dropped near the bank (the water slows immediately and loses energy).
- Using a named example with no place-specific facts — "the River Tees has waterfalls" is generic. Use **High Force, 21 m, Whin Sill dolerite over limestone and shale, 700 m gorge, Yarm meander, Seal Sands**.
- Calling any flat land a "flood plain" without explaining it is built from **alluvium** deposited in floods.
:::
