## What it is

A river flows from its **source** (usually in upland areas, where rain falls on hills and mountains) to its **mouth** (where it meets the sea, a lake or a larger river). As it flows, the shape of the river channel and the valley around it change in a predictable way. Geographers describe that change in two ways: the **long profile** and the **cross profile**. Both are explained by three **fluvial processes** — erosion, transportation and deposition — and by the fact that the river gains more water and more energy the further downstream it goes.

The river is usually split into three sections:

- **Upper course** — near the source, high up, steep, narrow and fast-flowing over large rocks.
- **Middle course** — lower and gentler; the river has more water and starts to swing from side to side.
- **Lower course** — close to the mouth, on low flat land; wide, deep and carrying a lot of fine sediment.

### The long profile

The **long profile** is a line showing how the **height of the river bed changes with distance** from the source to the mouth — as if you sliced along the whole river and looked at it from the side. To draw it: put *distance from source* on the x-axis and *height above sea level* on the y-axis.

The typical long profile is a **concave curve**: **steep in the upper course**, becoming **gentler in the middle course**, and **almost flat in the lower course** as the river approaches sea level. The curve can have "steps" in it — for example where a band of hard rock creates a waterfall — but the overall shape is smooth and concave.

Why concave? In the upper course the river is high above **base level** (sea level, the lowest point it can erode down to), so it has lots of potential energy and cuts **downwards**. Nearer the sea it is close to base level and cannot cut down much further, so it erodes **sideways** and deposits sediment instead.

### The cross profile

The **cross profile** is a line showing a **cross-section across the river channel and its valley** at a particular point — as if you sliced across the valley and looked at it end on.

| | Upper course | Middle course | Lower course |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Valley shape** | steep-sided **V-shaped valley** | wider, more open valley with gentler sides; a small flood plain | very wide, flat **flood plain**; valley sides far apart and gentle |
| **Channel shape** | narrow and shallow | wider and deeper | widest and deepest |
| **Gradient** | steep | moderate | gentle, almost flat |
| **Main erosion** | **vertical** (downwards) | **lateral** (sideways) begins to dominate | lateral; **deposition** increasingly important |
| **Load** | large, angular boulders | smaller, more rounded stones | fine sand, silt and clay; much in suspension |
| **Typical landforms** | interlocking spurs, waterfalls, gorges | meanders, small flood plain | large meanders, ox-bow lakes, levées, wide flood plain, estuary |

To draw a cross profile: put *distance across the valley* on the x-axis and *height* on the y-axis, and draw the channel at the bottom of the valley.

### How the river changes downstream (the Bradshaw model)

A useful summary of the changes (often shown as the **Bradshaw model**, a set of wedge-shaped bars):

- **Increase downstream**: **discharge** (the volume of water passing a point per second, in cumecs — cubic metres per second), **channel width**, **channel depth**, **cross-sectional area**, **velocity** and the **amount of load**.
- **Decrease downstream**: **gradient**, **load particle size**, **load angularity** (stones become rounder) and **channel roughness** (bed and banks become smoother).

A common surprise: the river is usually **faster in the lower course**, not the upper course. Upstream it *looks* fast because it is turbulent, but a lot of its energy is lost to **friction** against a rough, boulder-strewn bed in a shallow channel. Downstream the channel is deep and smooth, so less water touches the bed and banks and more energy goes into flow. Discharge also increases downstream because **tributaries** join the main river.

### Fluvial processes 1 — erosion

**Erosion** is the wearing away of the bed and banks of the river by the moving water and the load it carries. There are four processes:

| Process | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Hydraulic action** | the sheer force of the water hits the bed and banks; water and air are forced into cracks, compressing the air, which then expands and weakens the rock until pieces break off |
| **Abrasion** | rocks and sediment carried by the river are scraped and bumped against the bed and banks, wearing them away like sandpaper — the main way a river erodes |
| **Attrition** | rocks carried by the river knock against **each other**, breaking into smaller, rounder and smoother pieces — this erodes the **load**, not the channel |
| **Solution** | river water, which is slightly acidic, **dissolves** soluble rocks such as limestone and chalk, carrying the minerals away |

Rivers erode in two directions:

- **Vertical erosion** — downwards into the river bed. Dominant in the **upper course**, where the river is high above base level and its gradient is steep. It deepens the channel and creates **V-shaped valleys**, gorges and waterfalls.
- **Lateral erosion** — sideways into the banks. Dominant in the **middle and lower courses**, where the river is closer to base level. It widens the channel and valley and creates **meanders** and a wide **flood plain**.

Rivers erode most when they have lots of energy — after heavy rain, when **discharge** is high and the river is in flood.

### Fluvial processes 2 — transportation

The material a river carries is its **load**. It is moved in four ways:

| Process | Load moved | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Traction** | large boulders and pebbles | **rolled** along the river bed |
| **Saltation** | pebbles and gravel | **bounced** along the bed in a series of small hops |
| **Suspension** | fine sand, silt and clay | **carried within the water**, making it look brown or cloudy |
| **Solution** | dissolved minerals (e.g. from limestone) | **dissolved** and carried invisibly in the water |

Traction and saltation happen mostly in the upper course and in floods; suspension and solution carry most of the load in the lower course, where particles are small. A river needs more energy to move bigger particles, so the size of load it can carry depends on its velocity.

### Fluvial processes 3 — deposition

**Deposition** is when a river **drops the sediment it is carrying**. It happens when the river **loses energy** — when its **velocity falls** — so it can no longer carry its load. The largest, heaviest particles are dropped first; the finest clay is dropped last.

A river deposits sediment when:

- **discharge falls** — after a dry spell, or as a flood goes down;
- the river enters **shallower water** or its flow is slowed by friction (for example on the **inside bend of a meander**);
- the river **floods** over its banks — water spreading across the flood plain slows down and drops silt;
- the river reaches its **mouth** and meets the sea or a lake — the flow is checked and the load settles out (forming estuary mudflats);
- the **amount of load increases** — for example after a landslide into the river — so there is more than the river can carry.

Deposition is most important in the **lower course**, where it builds **flood plains, levées and estuaries**.

### Putting it together

The three processes work together all along the river, but the balance changes downstream: **vertical erosion and transport of large load dominate the upper course; lateral erosion takes over in the middle course; lateral erosion and deposition dominate the lower course.** That shift in processes is what produces the change in the long profile (steep to gentle) and the cross profile (narrow V to wide flat flood plain).

::: terms
- **Long profile**: a line showing how the height of a river bed changes with distance from source to mouth, usually a concave curve.
- **Cross profile**: a cross-section showing the shape of a river channel and its valley from one side to the other at a given point.
- **Source**: the start of a river, usually in an upland area.
- **Mouth**: the point where a river flows into the sea, a lake or a larger river.
- **Base level**: the lowest level to which a river can erode, usually sea level.
- **Discharge**: the volume of water passing a point in a river per second, measured in cumecs (cubic metres per second).
- **Hydraulic action**: erosion by the force of water, including air compressed in cracks in the bed and banks that forces rock apart.
- **Abrasion**: erosion when rocks carried by the river scrape and wear away the bed and banks.
- **Attrition**: erosion when rocks carried by the river knock into each other and become smaller and rounder.
- **Solution**: the dissolving of soluble rocks such as limestone by slightly acidic river water, which also carries the dissolved load.
- **Vertical erosion**: downward erosion of the river bed, dominant in the upper course, deepening the valley.
- **Lateral erosion**: sideways erosion of the river banks, dominant in the middle and lower course, widening the valley.
- **Traction**: the rolling of large boulders along the river bed.
- **Saltation**: the bouncing of pebbles and gravel along the river bed.
- **Suspension**: the carrying of fine particles such as silt and clay within the flowing water.
- **Deposition**: the dropping of sediment when a river loses energy and its velocity falls.
:::

::: method
1. **Know your command words.** *State/Name* (1 mark each — one precise fact). *Describe* — say what it is like or what happens, with no reason needed; for a profile, use shape words (steep, concave, V-shaped, wide, flat) and data if given. *Explain* — give reasons using "because", "so", "this means". *Suggest* — apply what you know to an unfamiliar river or figure. *Use Figure X* — you must quote something from the figure (a height, a distance, a label) or you cap your marks.
2. **Process questions** (2–4 marks): name the process, then describe *how it works* — e.g. abrasion is not just "erosion by rocks" but "rocks carried by the river scrape against the bed and banks, wearing them away". Always make clear whether a process erodes the **channel** (abrasion, hydraulic action, solution) or the **load** (attrition).
3. **Transport vs erosion**: *solution* appears in both lists — say which you mean. Traction = **rolled**, saltation = **bounced**, suspension = **carried in the water**, solution = **dissolved**.
4. **Profile questions**: describe from source to mouth in order, with a comparative word at each step (steeper, wider, deeper, flatter). If a figure gives heights or distances, quote them and calculate gradients or differences.
5. **4-mark "explain" (levels)**: Level 1 (1–2) — simple statements; Level 2 (3–4) — clear, developed explanation. Build it as **point → develop (how/why) → link** (e.g. to where in the course it happens or to the landform it creates).
6. **6-mark "explain" or "assess" (levels)**: Level 1 (1–2) basic; Level 2 (3–4) clear with some development; Level 3 (5–6) detailed, well-sequenced, accurate terms throughout. Write two or three developed points, each a chain of cause and effect, and use technical vocabulary (vertical erosion, base level, discharge, friction).
7. **9-mark questions** are rare on the rivers section of Paper 1 (Section C questions are worth 15 marks each, so the biggest is usually 6), but the 9-mark practice here builds the same skill as Section B: explain both sides, use examples, and finish with a **supported judgement** ("to what extent" needs "largely / partly because…"). SPaG (+3) is assessed in Section A of Papers 1 and 2, not on rivers questions.
:::

::: example Describe — the long profile
Describe the long profile of a river from its source to its mouth. (3)

The long profile is a concave curve (1). In the upper course near the source the gradient is steep and the river loses height quickly (1); the gradient becomes gentler in the middle course and almost flat in the lower course as the river approaches sea level at its mouth (1).
:::

::: example Explain — two erosion processes
Explain how a river erodes its bed and banks by abrasion and hydraulic action. (4)

Abrasion happens when stones and sediment carried by the river are dragged and bounced against the bed and banks (1), scraping and wearing them away like sandpaper, which deepens and widens the channel (1). Hydraulic action is the force of the water itself hitting the banks (1); water forces air into cracks, compressing it, and when the pressure is released the air expands, weakening the rock until pieces break off (1).
:::

::: example Explain — why the cross profile changes (6)
Explain why the cross profile of a river valley changes from the upper course to the lower course. (6)

In the upper course the river is high above base level and flows down a steep gradient, so most of its energy goes into vertical erosion. Abrasion and hydraulic action cut down into the bed, while weathering and mass movement on the valley sides wear them back, producing a narrow, steep-sided V-shaped valley with a narrow, shallow channel.

Further downstream the river is closer to base level, so it cannot cut down as much; instead lateral erosion becomes dominant. As the river swings from side to side it erodes its banks, widening the valley and creating a flat valley floor. Discharge has also increased because tributaries have joined, so the channel is wider and deeper.

In the lower course lateral erosion and deposition dominate. When the river floods it spreads out, slows down and deposits silt, building up a wide, flat flood plain, so the cross profile becomes very wide and gentle with a large, deep channel. This change from a V shape to a broad flat valley therefore reflects the shift from vertical erosion to lateral erosion and deposition as the river approaches base level.

(Level 3: detailed, sequenced explanation of all three courses, linking process to shape, with accurate terminology.)
:::

::: example Suggest — deposition
Suggest two reasons why a river deposits its load near its mouth. (4)

As the river enters the sea its flow is checked by the tide and the water slows down (1), so it loses energy and can no longer carry its load, dropping fine silt and clay (1). The gradient near the mouth is almost flat (1), so the river has little energy and deposits sediment, especially when discharge is low or at high tide when the river water backs up (1).
:::

::: q
Name the four processes of river transportation. (4)
---
Traction (1); saltation (1); suspension (1); solution (1).
:::

::: q
What is the difference between attrition and abrasion? (2)
---
Attrition is when rocks carried by the river knock against each other and become smaller and rounder — it erodes the load (1). Abrasion is when the load is scraped against the bed and banks, eroding the channel (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the difference between vertical and lateral erosion. (2)
---
Vertical erosion wears the river bed downwards, deepening the valley — dominant in the upper course (1). Lateral erosion wears the banks sideways, widening the valley — dominant in the middle and lower course (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a river usually flows faster in its lower course than in its upper course. (3)
---
In the upper course the channel is shallow and the bed is rough with large boulders (1), so much of the water is in contact with the bed and banks and energy is lost to friction (1). In the lower course the channel is deeper, wider and smoother, and discharge is greater because tributaries have joined, so less energy is lost to friction and the water flows faster (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the load of a river in the upper course compared with the lower course. (3)
---
Upper course: large, angular boulders and stones (1), mostly moved by traction and saltation (1). Lower course: fine, rounded particles such as sand, silt and clay, carried mainly in suspension and solution — the load has been worn smaller and rounder by attrition (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two conditions that cause a river to deposit its load. (4)
---
Any two, each with development: low discharge after a dry spell (1) means the river has less energy and velocity, so it cannot carry as much load (1); flooding over the banks (1) — water spreading over the flood plain slows down and drops silt (1); entering the sea or a lake (1) checks the flow so sediment settles (1); shallow water or the inside of a meander bend (1), where friction slows the flow (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what is meant by base level and why it affects the type of erosion. (3)
---
Base level is the lowest level a river can erode down to, usually sea level (1). In the upper course the river is far above base level, so it has lots of potential energy and erodes vertically (1). Near the mouth it is close to base level and cannot cut down further, so it erodes laterally and deposits instead (1).
:::

::: watch
- Mixing up **attrition** (rocks hitting each other — erodes the load) and **abrasion** (rocks hitting the bed and banks — erodes the channel).
- Saying the river is **fastest in the upper course** — velocity usually increases downstream because friction falls and discharge rises.
- Describing the long profile as "going downhill" without the key word **concave** and the change in gradient from steep to gentle.
- Confusing **solution** as erosion (dissolving the rock) with **solution** as transport (carrying dissolved minerals) — say which you mean.
- Writing "deposition happens when the river slows down" and stopping — explain *why*: less energy, so it cannot carry its load, and name the situation (low discharge, flooding, reaching the sea, inside bend).
- Ignoring **"Use Figure X"** — quote heights, distances or labels from the figure.
:::
