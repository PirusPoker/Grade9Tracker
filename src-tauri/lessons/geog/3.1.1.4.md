## What it is

**Climate change** is a long-term change in the Earth's average weather patterns — its temperature, rainfall and winds. The climate has always changed naturally, but since the Industrial Revolution (from about 1750) human activity has warmed the planet far faster than natural change usually does. The spec needs four things: the **evidence** for climate change from the start of the **Quaternary period** to today; its **natural and human causes**; an **overview of its effects** on people and the environment; and how it is **managed** by **mitigation** (reducing the causes) and **adaptation** (responding to the change).

### Evidence for climate change

The **Quaternary period** began about **2.6 million years ago** and continues today. It has been marked by repeated **glacial periods** (ice ages, when ice sheets spread across northern Europe and North America) separated by warmer **interglacial periods**. The last glacial period reached its peak about 20,000 years ago, when ice covered most of Britain; we now live in an interglacial, the **Holocene**, which began about 11,700 years ago.

| Evidence | What it shows | Timescale |
|---|---|---|
| **Ice cores** | Air bubbles trapped in layers of Antarctic and Greenland ice record past CO₂ levels and temperatures. The Vostok core in Antarctica covers about 400,000 years, and the EPICA Dome C core about 800,000 years. CO₂ and temperature rise and fall together | hundreds of thousands of years |
| **Sea-floor sediments** | The shells of tiny sea creatures in ocean sediment record the temperature of the water they lived in | millions of years |
| **Pollen analysis** | Pollen preserved in peat and lake mud shows which plants grew; each species needs a particular climate | thousands of years |
| **Tree rings** | A tree grows a thicker ring in a warm, wet year and a thinner one in a cold or dry year | up to about 10,000 years |
| **Historical records** | Diaries, paintings and harvest dates — e.g. "frost fairs" held on the frozen River Thames during the **Little Ice Age** (roughly 1300–1870) | hundreds of years |
| **Temperature records** | Thermometer readings since the mid-1800s show global average temperature has risen by about 1.1–1.2 °C since the late 1800s, with most of the rise since 1980 | about 170 years |
| **Glaciers and sea ice** | Photographs and satellite images show most mountain glaciers retreating; Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk sharply since satellite measurements began in 1979 | decades |
| **Sea level** | Tide gauges and satellites show global sea level has risen by roughly 20 cm since 1900, and the rate is speeding up — from melting ice and the expansion of warmer water | about a century |

The **strength** of the evidence is that very different methods point the same way. Its **limits**: older evidence (pollen, sediments) gives less precise dates and is usually local; thermometer records only go back about 170 years and early ones covered few places.

### Natural causes

| Cause | How it changes the climate | Evidence / example |
|---|---|---|
| **Orbital changes** (Milankovitch cycles) | The Earth's orbit changes shape from more circular to more elliptical (about every 100,000 years); the tilt of its axis varies between about 22° and 24.5° (about every 41,000 years); and the axis wobbles (precession, about every 26,000 years). These change how much sunlight reaches different parts of the Earth at different seasons | The timing of glacial and interglacial periods in ice cores matches these cycles — they explain the pattern of the Quaternary ice ages |
| **Volcanic activity** | A large eruption blasts sulphur dioxide and ash high into the atmosphere; they form a haze that reflects sunlight, **cooling** the Earth for a year or two | Tambora (Indonesia, 1815) led to 1816, "the year without a summer"; Mount Pinatubo (Philippines, 1991) cooled global temperatures by about 0.5 °C for around a year |
| **Solar output** | The Sun's energy output varies; it rises and falls in an 11-year sunspot cycle — more sunspots mean slightly more energy | Very few sunspots were seen during the **Maunder Minimum** (about 1645–1715), which coincides with the coldest part of the Little Ice Age |

Natural causes explain past changes well, but **cannot explain recent warming**: solar output has not increased since the 1950s, and orbital changes work over tens of thousands of years, not decades.

### Human causes: the enhanced greenhouse effect

Greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, water vapour) let short-wave radiation from the Sun pass through the atmosphere but absorb some of the long-wave heat radiated back from the Earth's surface, keeping the planet warm. This **natural greenhouse effect** keeps the Earth about 33 °C warmer than it would otherwise be. Human activity adds extra gases — the **enhanced greenhouse effect** — so more heat is trapped. Carbon dioxide has risen from about **280 ppm** before the Industrial Revolution to over **420 ppm** today.

- **Use of fossil fuels**: burning coal, oil and gas for electricity, industry, heating and transport releases carbon dioxide — the largest human source of greenhouse gases. Carbon locked away for millions of years is released in decades.
- **Agriculture**: cattle and sheep release **methane** as they digest food; flooded **rice paddies** release methane; nitrogen **fertilisers** release nitrous oxide. Methane traps far more heat per molecule than CO₂. Demand for meat and rice rises as population and wealth grow.
- **Deforestation**: trees absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis and store carbon. Cutting them down removes this carbon sink, and burning or rotting the wood releases the stored carbon as CO₂.

### Effects of climate change: an overview

**On the environment**
- **Rising sea levels** from melting ice sheets and glaciers and from thermal expansion of warmer sea water, flooding low-lying coasts and increasing coastal erosion.
- **Melting ice**: shrinking Arctic sea ice, retreating glaciers (e.g. in the Alps and Himalayas), loss of habitat for species such as polar bears.
- **Coral bleaching**: warmer seas make corals expel their algae and turn white; the Great Barrier Reef has suffered repeated mass bleaching since 2016.
- **Changing ecosystems**: species move towards the poles or uphill; those that cannot move, or whose food supply changes timing, may become extinct.
- **More extreme weather**: more intense heatwaves, droughts, heavy rainfall and, probably, stronger tropical storms.

**On people**
- **Coastal flooding** threatens hundreds of millions of people on low-lying coasts and deltas — e.g. **Bangladesh**, and island nations such as the **Maldives**, where most land is less than 1 m above sea level.
- **Food**: crop yields are likely to fall in hot, dry regions such as sub-Saharan Africa, but some cooler places (e.g. parts of the UK, Canada) may be able to grow new crops.
- **Water**: less reliable rainfall and shrinking glaciers threaten water supplies, e.g. for people who depend on meltwater from Himalayan glaciers.
- **Health**: more deaths in heatwaves; diseases such as malaria may spread to new areas as they warm.
- **Economy**: damage from extreme weather is costly; some industries lose (winter ski resorts, farming in drought areas), others may gain (UK vineyards, Arctic shipping routes).
- **Migration and conflict**: people may be forced to leave areas made uninhabitable by drought or flooding.

The effects are **unequal**: the poorest countries have contributed least to the problem but are often the most exposed and least able to adapt.

### Managing climate change: mitigation

**Mitigation** means reducing the causes of climate change — cutting greenhouse gas emissions or removing CO₂ from the atmosphere.

| Strategy | How it works | Example | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alternative energy production** | Wind, solar, hydro, tidal and nuclear power generate electricity without burning fossil fuels | The UK has rapidly expanded offshore wind and closed its last coal-fired power station (Ratcliffe-on-Soar) in 2024 | Wind and solar vary with the weather; some schemes are costly or unpopular locally; nuclear waste |
| **Carbon capture and storage (CCS)** | CO₂ from power stations or factories is captured, piped away and stored deep underground, e.g. in old oil and gas fields | Sleipner gas field, Norway, has stored CO₂ under the North Sea since 1996; Boundary Dam coal power station, Canada (2014) | Expensive; uses extra energy; few large schemes are running; risk of leaks |
| **Planting trees** | Trees absorb CO₂ as they grow and store carbon (afforestation and reforestation) | National tree-planting targets in the UK; China's large-scale afforestation | Takes decades to have an effect; needs land that could grow food; trees can burn |
| **International agreements** | Countries agree to cut emissions together | **Kyoto Protocol** (1997); **Paris Agreement** (2015) — nearly every country agreed to keep warming well below 2 °C and to try to limit it to 1.5 °C, setting their own targets (NDCs) | Targets are set by each country and are not legally enforced; the USA left the Paris Agreement in 2020, rejoined in 2021 and announced it would leave again in 2025 |

The UK's **Climate Change Act (2008)** made it law to cut emissions; since 2019 the target is **net zero by 2050**.

### Managing climate change: adaptation

**Adaptation** means responding to the changes climate change brings, to reduce their harm.

- **Change in agricultural systems**: growing drought-resistant or heat-tolerant crop varieties; changing planting dates; irrigation and water-saving methods; in some places switching crops entirely. In the UK, warmer summers have allowed vineyards to expand in southern England. In drier parts of Africa, farmers use drought-tolerant maize and water-harvesting.
- **Managing water supply**: building reservoirs and water transfers; installing water meters to reduce use; fixing leaks; recycling waste water; rainwater harvesting; **desalination** plants that make fresh water from sea water (e.g. the Thames Water desalination plant at Beckton, London, opened 2010).
- **Reducing risk from rising sea levels**: sea walls, flood barriers and raised defences (the **Thames Barrier** protects London, and the TE2100 plan sets out how defences will be raised to 2100); restoring mangroves and salt marshes as natural buffers; raising buildings on stilts; managed retreat from the most vulnerable coasts. The Maldives has built an artificial island, **Hulhumalé**, raised about 2 m above sea level.

**Mitigation vs adaptation**: mitigation tackles the cause and benefits everyone, but it is slow, needs global cooperation and its benefits come decades later. Adaptation helps a particular place now, but can be expensive, may not help the poorest countries, and cannot keep up if warming is not also limited. Most experts argue that **both** are needed.

::: terms
- **Climate change**: a long-term change in the Earth's average temperature and weather patterns, caused by natural and human factors.
- **Quaternary period**: the most recent geological period, from about 2.6 million years ago to today, marked by glacial and interglacial periods.
- **Glacial period**: a long cold phase within an ice age when ice sheets and glaciers advance.
- **Interglacial period**: a warmer phase between glacial periods when ice retreats — we are in one now, the Holocene.
- **Milankovitch cycles**: regular changes in the Earth's orbit, tilt and wobble that alter how much sunlight it receives and help drive ice ages.
- **Enhanced greenhouse effect**: the extra warming caused by human activities adding greenhouse gases to the atmosphere, trapping more heat.
- **Mitigation**: action that reduces the causes of climate change, such as cutting greenhouse gas emissions or removing CO₂.
- **Adaptation**: action that responds to the effects of climate change to reduce its harm, such as flood defences or drought-resistant crops.
- **Carbon capture and storage**: capturing CO₂ from power stations or industry and storing it deep underground so it cannot enter the atmosphere.
- **Thermal expansion**: the increase in volume of sea water as it warms, which contributes to rising sea levels.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State / outline** (1–2): short facts — "State one natural cause of climate change" — "volcanic eruptions".
- **Describe** (2–4): say what the evidence shows or what an effect is, with detail.
- **Explain** (2–6): give reasons and processes — how orbital change, eruptions or burning fossil fuels change temperature; how a strategy reduces emissions.
- **Suggest** (2–4): apply knowledge to a figure — e.g. a graph of CO₂ and temperature from an ice core.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** (6–9): weigh up — e.g. "Human factors are more important than natural factors" or "Mitigation is more effective than adaptation" — and give a judgement.
- **Use Figure X**: quote data from the graph (dates, values, the direction and rate of change).

**Levels**
- **4-mark**: Level 2 needs developed explanation — a process explained, not just named.
- **6-mark**: three developed points; use named examples (Pinatubo 1991, Maunder Minimum, Paris 2015, Thames Barrier).
- **9-mark** (+3 SPaG where shown): develop both sides; judge. For "mitigation or adaptation" questions, a strong judgement often argues both are needed but says which matters more *and why* (e.g. mitigation for the long term, adaptation for the poorest places now).

**Mitigation vs adaptation** — the classic mix-up. Ask: does this reduce the *causes* (mitigation) or deal with the *effects* (adaptation)? Wind farms = mitigation; sea walls = adaptation. Planting trees = mitigation (it absorbs CO₂), even though mangroves can also be adaptation (protecting coasts) — say which role you mean.

**Explaining a cause**: always give the chain. "Burning fossil fuels releases CO₂ → more greenhouse gas in the atmosphere → more long-wave radiation absorbed → global temperature rises."
:::

::: example Explain — natural cause
Explain how volcanic activity can cause climate change. (3)

A large eruption blasts sulphur dioxide and ash high into the atmosphere (1). These form a haze of tiny particles that reflect incoming sunlight back into space (1), so less solar energy reaches the ground and global temperatures fall for a year or two — for example Mount Pinatubo (1991) cooled the Earth by about 0.5 °C (1).
:::

::: example Describe — evidence
Describe how ice cores provide evidence for climate change. (4)

Ice sheets in Antarctica and Greenland build up in annual layers of snow, so a core drilled through them is a record going back hundreds of thousands of years (1) — the EPICA Dome C core covers about 800,000 years (1). Bubbles of air trapped in each layer show the level of CO₂ in the atmosphere at that time (1), and the chemistry of the ice shows the temperature, so scientists can see that CO₂ and temperature rose and fell together through glacial and interglacial periods (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — mitigation vs adaptation
"Mitigation is a more effective way to manage climate change than adaptation." Do you agree? (9 + 3 SPaG)

Mitigation tackles the cause of climate change. The Paris Agreement (2015) committed almost every country to keep warming well below 2 °C, and switching to alternative energy is cutting emissions — the UK closed its last coal power station in 2024 and relies increasingly on offshore wind. If emissions fall, every country benefits, including the poorest, which cannot afford large defences.

However, mitigation has limits. Countries set their own targets and there is no enforcement; the USA has left the Paris Agreement twice. Carbon capture is expensive and there are few large schemes, and trees take decades to absorb much CO₂. Even if emissions stopped today, some warming and sea-level rise are already locked in.

Adaptation deals with this unavoidable change and protects people now. The Thames Barrier and the TE2100 plan protect London from rising seas; drought-resistant crops protect food supplies; the Maldives has raised a new island, Hulhumalé, about 2 m above sea level. But adaptation is costly and local — it protects the places that can afford it — and it cannot keep pace if warming continues unchecked.

I partly agree. Mitigation is more effective in the long term because it is the only way to stop the problem getting worse for everyone. But adaptation is essential now, because some change is already unavoidable, and it matters most for vulnerable countries such as Bangladesh. The most effective approach is both together.
:::

::: watch
- Confusing **mitigation** (reducing causes) with **adaptation** (responding to effects) — the single most common error.
- Explaining the greenhouse effect as the **ozone hole** — they are different problems.
- Naming a natural cause without the **process** (e.g. "volcanoes" instead of "ash and sulphur dioxide reflect sunlight, cooling the Earth").
- Evidence questions answered with "it's getting hotter" — name the **method** (ice cores, tree rings, pollen, records) and what it shows.
- Forgetting that effects are **both** on people and on the environment when the question asks for both.
- 9-markers without a **judgement** — or a judgement that does not answer the exact question.
:::
