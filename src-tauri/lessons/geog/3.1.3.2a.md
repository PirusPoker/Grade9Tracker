## What it is

The coast is shaped by a set of physical processes. The spec lists them precisely, and exam questions use the same words, so learn them in this order: **wave types and characteristics**; **weathering** (mechanical and chemical); **mass movement** (sliding, slumping, rock falls); **erosion** (hydraulic power, abrasion, attrition); **transportation** (longshore drift); and **deposition** (why sediment is deposited in coastal areas).

### How waves form

Waves are created by **wind blowing over the surface of the sea**. Friction between the wind and the water makes the surface ripple, and the energy is passed forward. The **size and energy** of a wave depend on:

- **wind speed** — stronger winds make bigger waves;
- **duration** — how long the wind has been blowing;
- **fetch** — the distance of open water the wind blows over. The south-west coast of England faces the Atlantic, with a fetch of thousands of kilometres, so it receives powerful waves.

In open water, the water itself moves in a **circular orbit** and does not travel forward. As the wave approaches the shore, the sea bed creates **friction** with the bottom of the wave, slowing it; the top keeps moving, the wave grows taller, becomes unstable and **breaks**. Water rushing up the beach is the **swash**; water draining back down under gravity is the **backwash**.

### Wave types

| | **Constructive waves** | **Destructive waves** |
|---|---|---|
| Height | low (usually under 1 m) | high and steep |
| Wavelength | long | short |
| Frequency | low — about 6–8 per minute | high — about 10–14 per minute |
| Breaking | spill gently onto the beach | plunge (crash) down onto the beach |
| Swash vs backwash | **strong swash, weak backwash** | **weak swash, strong backwash** |
| Effect | carry sediment up the beach and leave it there — they **build beaches** (depositing ridges called berms) | drag sediment back down the beach and out to sea — they **remove material and erode** |
| When | calm weather, often in summer | storms, often in winter; large fetch and strong winds |

Why the swash/backwash difference matters: constructive waves spill onto the beach and much of the water soaks into the sand, so the backwash is too weak to take material back. Destructive waves arrive so frequently that each backwash interferes with the next swash, and they plunge downward, so they scour material away.

### Weathering

**Weathering** is the **breakdown of rock in place (in situ)** by the weather, plants, animals or chemical action. It happens on the cliff face above the reach of the waves and weakens the rock so it can be eroded or collapse. It is **not** erosion — nothing is moved.

- **Mechanical weathering** breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its chemistry.
  - **Freeze-thaw**: water enters cracks and joints in the rock; at night it freezes and **expands by about 9%**, forcing the crack wider; it thaws, more water enters, and after repeated cycles pieces of rock break off. Most effective when temperatures fluctuate around 0 °C.
  - **Salt weathering**: sea water evaporates in cracks, leaving **salt crystals** that grow and exert pressure, breaking the rock apart. Common on coasts because spray constantly supplies salt.
- **Chemical weathering** changes the chemical composition of the rock.
  - **Carbonation**: rainwater absorbs **carbon dioxide** from the air and becomes a weak **carbonic acid**. This reacts with **calcium carbonate** in **limestone and chalk**, dissolving it; the dissolved material is carried away in solution.
- **Biological weathering** (useful extra): plant roots grow into cracks and widen them; burrowing animals and seabirds loosen rock.

### Mass movement

**Mass movement** is the **downhill movement of material (rock, soil, mud) under the influence of gravity**. It is common on coastal cliffs because waves erode and steepen the base, removing support, while rain soaks and weights the cliff top.

| Type | What happens | Typical rock | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rock fall** | Fragments of rock **break off** a steep cliff face and fall to the base, often after freeze-thaw weathering or undercutting by waves; they pile up as **scree** | hard, jointed rock, e.g. chalk, limestone | Chalk falls at the Seven Sisters and Birling Gap, East Sussex; a sandstone cliff fall at Burton Bradstock, Dorset, killed a walker in July 2012 |
| **Sliding (landslide)** | A mass of rock or soil moves **in one block** down a **straight slip plane** — often along a bedding plane dipping towards the sea — with little rotation | layered rocks, especially where a permeable rock lies over clay | Parts of the Jurassic Coast, Dorset |
| **Slumping** | Saturated soft rock or soil moves down a **curved (concave) slip surface**, rotating as it goes, leaving a series of steps (terraces) on the cliff | **clay** and other soft, impermeable rock; after heavy rain the clay becomes saturated, heavy and weak | **Holbeck Hall Hotel, Scarborough, June 1993** — part of the hotel collapsed into the sea after a rotational slump; **Black Ven**, near Lyme Regis |

Why heavy rain triggers mass movement: water **adds weight** and **lubricates** the slip surface, and in clay it increases the water pressure between particles so the rock loses strength.

### Erosion

**Erosion** is the **wearing away and removal** of material by a moving force — here, the sea. The spec names three processes:

- **Hydraulic power (hydraulic action)**: the sheer force of waves crashing against the cliff. Water and **air are forced into cracks** and compressed; when the wave retreats the air expands explosively. Repeated, this widens cracks and breaks off pieces of rock.
- **Abrasion**: waves pick up **sand, pebbles and boulders** and hurl them at the cliff, **scraping and chipping** it like sandpaper. This is the most effective erosion process, especially in storms. (Also called corrasion.)
- **Attrition**: rocks and pebbles carried by the waves **knock against each other**, breaking into **smaller, smoother and rounder** pieces. Attrition does not erode the cliff — it wears down the sediment, which is why beach pebbles are rounded.
- (Also **solution / corrosion**: sea water dissolves soluble rock such as chalk and limestone.)

Erosion is greatest where waves are **destructive**, the fetch is long, the rock is soft or heavily jointed, and there is **no beach** to absorb wave energy.

### Transportation and longshore drift

Material is moved by waves and currents in four ways: **traction** (large boulders rolled along the sea bed), **saltation** (pebbles bounced along), **suspension** (fine sand and silt carried in the water) and **solution** (dissolved minerals).

**Longshore drift** moves sediment **along** the coastline:

1. The **prevailing wind** makes waves approach the beach **at an angle**.
2. The **swash** carries sediment **up the beach at the same angle** as the wind.
3. The **backwash** carries it **straight back down the beach**, at right angles to the coastline, under **gravity**.
4. Repeated, the sediment moves in a **zigzag** along the beach in the direction of the prevailing wind.

Examples: on the **Holderness coast** of East Yorkshire, sediment is carried **southwards** by longshore drift and forms **Spurn Head** spit; on much of England's south coast, the prevailing south-westerly wind moves sediment from **west to east**. **Groynes** built across beaches trap it — sand builds up on the **updrift** side.

When drawing: a diagram shows the beach with the sea, an arrow for the prevailing wind at an angle, arrows for swash (angled up the beach) and backwash (straight down), and the zigzag path of a pebble with a big arrow showing overall movement.

### Deposition

**Deposition** is when the sea **drops the sediment** it is carrying. It happens when waves **lose energy**. Sediment is deposited in coastal areas because:

- **constructive waves** dominate — strong swash, weak backwash, so material is left on the beach;
- the coast is **sheltered** — in **bays**, behind spits and in estuaries, waves have less energy;
- there is a **large supply of sediment** — from eroding cliffs nearby, from rivers or from longshore drift;
- the coastline **changes direction** — longshore drift continues into deeper, calmer water and drops its load (this forms spits);
- **obstacles** such as groynes slow the movement of sediment;
- **calm weather** and **low winds** reduce wave energy;
- in estuaries, fresh water meets salt water and fine clay particles stick together (flocculate) and sink, forming mudflats.

::: terms
- **Fetch**: the distance of open water over which the wind blows to form waves; the longer the fetch, the more powerful the waves.
- **Swash**: the forward movement of water up the beach after a wave breaks.
- **Backwash**: the movement of water back down the beach under gravity.
- **Constructive wave**: a low, long-wavelength wave with a strong swash and weak backwash that builds up beaches.
- **Destructive wave**: a high, steep, frequent wave with a weak swash and strong backwash that removes material from beaches.
- **Weathering**: the breakdown of rock in place by mechanical, chemical or biological processes, without it being moved.
- **Freeze-thaw weathering**: water freezes in cracks, expands by about 9% and widens them until rock breaks off.
- **Mass movement**: the downhill movement of rock, soil or mud under the influence of gravity.
- **Hydraulic power**: erosion by the force of waves, forcing water and compressed air into cracks in the rock.
- **Abrasion**: erosion caused by waves throwing sand and pebbles against the cliff, wearing it away like sandpaper.
- **Attrition**: rocks carried by the sea knock into each other, becoming smaller, smoother and rounder.
- **Longshore drift**: the zigzag movement of sediment along a beach, caused by waves approaching at an angle.
:::

::: method
1. **Define / state (1–2 marks)**: one accurate sentence using the key idea — weathering is "in place", erosion involves "removal", mass movement is "under gravity".
2. **Describe the differences (2–4 marks)**: compare **both** wave types in the same sentence with "whereas" — height, frequency, swash/backwash, effect.
3. **Explain a process (2–4 marks)**: sequence it in steps — cause → what happens → result. For freeze-thaw: water enters crack → freezes and expands by about 9% → pressure widens crack → repeated cycles → rock breaks off.
4. **Longshore drift (3–4 marks)**: four steps — prevailing wind, swash at an angle, backwash straight down under gravity, zigzag. A labelled diagram can earn full marks if it shows all four.
5. **Explain why deposition happens (2–4 marks)**: always return to **loss of energy**, and give a reason why energy is lost (shelter, constructive waves, change in coastline direction).
6. **6-mark "explain how processes combine" answers**: link processes together — weathering weakens the cliff top, erosion undercuts the base, mass movement brings material down, waves then transport it. Level 3 needs developed chains and an example (Holbeck Hall 1993).
7. **"Use Figure X"**: name what you can see in the photo or diagram (a notch, scree, slump terraces) before explaining it.
:::

::: example Compare waves
Describe the differences between constructive and destructive waves. (4)

Constructive waves are low, under 1 m high, whereas destructive waves are high and steep (1). Constructive waves are less frequent, about 6–8 a minute, whereas destructive waves arrive at 10–14 a minute (1). Constructive waves have a stronger swash than backwash, whereas destructive waves have a stronger backwash (1), so constructive waves build up beaches while destructive waves remove sediment and erode them (1).
:::

::: example Longshore drift
Explain how longshore drift moves sediment along a coastline. (4)

The prevailing wind causes waves to approach the beach at an angle (1). The swash carries sediment up the beach at that same angle (1). The backwash then carries it straight back down the beach at right angles, because of gravity (1). This is repeated, so the sediment moves in a zigzag along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind — on the Holderness coast this is southwards towards Spurn Head (1).
:::

::: example Mass movement — 6 marks
Explain how weathering, erosion and mass movement combine to cause cliff retreat. (6)

Weathering weakens the cliff from above. In chalk or limestone, rainwater containing carbon dioxide forms weak carbonic acid that dissolves the rock (carbonation), while freeze-thaw widens joints when water freezes and expands by about 9%. The rock becomes loose and cracked.

At the same time, waves erode the base of the cliff between high and low tide. Hydraulic power forces air into cracks, and abrasion hurls pebbles at the cliff, cutting a wave-cut notch. This removes the support for the rock above.

Mass movement then brings the weakened, unsupported cliff down. In hard rock this is a rock fall; in clay, heavy rain saturates the cliff, adding weight and lubricating a curved slip surface so it slumps, as at Holbeck Hall, Scarborough, in 1993. Waves then remove the fallen material, exposing the base again, so the cycle repeats and the cliff retreats inland.

(Level 3: all three processes explained and clearly linked, with a specific example.)
:::

::: example Deposition
Explain why deposition occurs in bays. (3)

Bays are sheltered between headlands (1), so wave energy is spread out and reduced as waves enter the bay (wave refraction) (1). With less energy, waves can no longer carry their sediment, so it is deposited and forms a beach (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Weathering ≠ erosion.** Weathering happens in place; erosion removes material. Mixing them up loses marks in every process question.
- **Attrition does not erode the cliff** — it wears down the sediment.
- In longshore drift, the **backwash goes straight down the beach because of gravity** — not "back out at an angle". Many students lose this mark.
- Name the **right** wave feature: constructive = **strong swash**; destructive = **strong backwash**.
- Deposition answers must mention **loss of energy** and a reason why.
- Mass movement needs **gravity**, and heavy rain works by **adding weight and lubricating** — say both.
:::
