## What it is

This is the first half of the **Food** option (Paper 2, Section C). The key idea is that **demand for food is rising globally, but supply can be insecure, which may lead to conflict**. You need to know where the world has a food **surplus** and a food **deficit**, why people are eating more, what affects how much food is produced, and what happens to people and places when there is not enough.

### Food security and insecurity

- **Food security** exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs for an active and healthy life (the United Nations' definition from the 1996 World Food Summit). Countries with food security usually produce a **surplus** or can afford to import what they need.
- **Food insecurity** is when people do **not** have reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food. Countries where large numbers of people are food insecure are in **deficit**.

Food security has four parts worth knowing: **availability** (is enough food produced or imported?), **access** (can people afford it and reach it?), **utilisation** (is it safe and nutritious, and can the body use it — clean water matters here?) and **stability** (is supply reliable all year and every year?). A country can grow plenty of food and still have hungry people if the poor cannot afford it.

### Global patterns of calorie intake and food supply

A **calorie** (strictly a kilocalorie, kcal) measures the energy in food. The NHS recommends around **2,000 kcal a day for women and 2,500 for men**. The world's average food supply is roughly **2,900–3,000 kcal per person per day** — enough in total to feed everyone, if it were shared evenly.

| Region | Typical daily calorie supply | Food security |
|---|---|---|
| North America, western Europe, Australia | over 3,300 kcal — often far more than needed | secure; high obesity levels |
| Russia, most of South America, the Middle East, China | around 2,800–3,300 kcal | mostly secure, rising |
| South Asia (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh) | around 2,500–2,700 kcal | improving but large numbers undernourished |
| Much of sub-Saharan Africa; Haiti; Yemen; Afghanistan | many below 2,400 kcal; the poorest countries, such as the Central African Republic and Madagascar, lowest | insecure — deficit |

**Describing the pattern on a world map**: the highest intakes are in HICs in the **northern hemisphere** (North America, Europe) and Australasia; the lowest are in **sub-Saharan Africa** and parts of **South Asia**, the Caribbean (Haiti) and conflict zones such as Yemen and Afghanistan. There is a close link between **wealth** (GNI per head) and calorie intake.

**The number of undernourished people** — around **700 million** people in recent UN estimates, roughly one in eleven of the world's population — is concentrated in **sub-Saharan Africa** (the highest proportion) and **Asia** (the largest number, because of Asia's huge population).

**Food supply** also varies. The main food **exporters** — countries with a surplus — include the **USA, Brazil, Canada, Australia, Argentina, France, Russia and Ukraine**. Many **LICs** are net food importers, and some depend on **food aid**.

### Reasons for increasing food consumption

**1. Rising population**

- World population passed **8 billion** in November 2022 and the UN projects around **9.7 billion by 2050**.
- Most growth is in **Africa and South Asia** — often the places that are already food insecure. More people means more food is needed just to keep consumption per person the same.

**2. Economic development**

As countries develop and incomes rise, people eat **more** food and **different** food:

- **More calories** — people who could once afford only one or two meals a day can now afford three, plus snacks.
- **More meat and dairy** — as in **China**, where meat consumption per person has risen several times over since 1980, and India, where dairy consumption has grown. Producing meat uses far more land, water and grain than growing crops for people directly: it takes **several kilograms of feed grain to produce one kilogram of beef**. So rising meat demand increases the demand for crops even faster.
- **More processed and imported food** — supermarkets, fast food and year-round choice spread with urbanisation.
- **Higher expectations** — people in HICs and NEEs expect a varied, all-year diet (see the UK: food topic).

In short: **more people × more food per person = a rapid rise in global demand.**

### Factors affecting food supply

| Factor | How it reduces (or increases) food supply | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Climate** | crops need suitable temperatures and reliable rainfall; extreme weather (drought, floods, heatwaves) destroys harvests; climate change is making weather less predictable | five failed rainy seasons in a row in the **Horn of Africa (2020–23)** — Kenya, Ethiopia and Somalia — killed millions of livestock and left millions short of food |
| **Technology** | machinery, irrigation, fertilisers, pesticides, high-yielding and GM seeds raise yields; without them, yields stay low | the **Green Revolution** from the 1960s greatly increased wheat and rice yields in India; many African farmers still work by hand |
| **Pests and disease** | pests eat crops; diseases kill crops and livestock | **desert locust** swarms in East Africa in 2019–20 were the worst in Kenya for 70 years; **potato blight** caused the Irish famine of the 1840s |
| **Water stress** | farming uses about **70%** of all freshwater withdrawn worldwide, so where water is scarce, crops fail or cannot be irrigated | the Sahel; Pakistan relies on irrigation from the Indus |
| **Conflict** | farmers flee, fields are mined or burned, trade routes are blocked, food is used as a weapon | **Sudan** — famine was confirmed in the Zamzam displacement camp in 2024; **Russia's invasion of Ukraine (2022)** blocked Black Sea grain exports and pushed world prices to record highs |
| **Poverty** | poor farmers cannot afford seeds, fertiliser, irrigation or storage; poor consumers cannot afford food even when it is available | subsistence farmers in LICs; food wasted after harvest through poor storage |

The factors **interact**: poverty makes it impossible to cope with drought; conflict stops farmers planting; climate change increases water stress and brings new pests.

### Impacts of food insecurity

**1. Famine** — a widespread, severe shortage of food leading to starvation and death. In **Somalia in 2011**, drought combined with conflict caused a famine in which an estimated **258,000 people died, half of them children under five** (UN FAO / FEWS NET study, 2013).

**2. Undernutrition** — not eating enough of the right foods (calories, protein, vitamins, minerals). It causes:
- **stunting** (children too short for their age — around 150 million children under five worldwide are stunted, according to UNICEF and WHO) and **wasting** (dangerously thin);
- weakened immune systems, so diseases such as diarrhoea and measles become deadly;
- poor concentration and learning, so children do worse at school — and adults are less productive at work. **Undernutrition keeps people in poverty**, a vicious cycle.
(**Malnutrition** is broader: it includes both undernutrition and overnutrition — obesity.)

**3. Soil erosion** — desperate farmers **overcultivate** (grow crops without letting the soil rest) and **overgraze** animals, and cut down trees for fuel and fields. The bare soil is blown or washed away, making the land less fertile, so even less food can be grown next year. In the **Sahel** this contributes to **desertification**.

**4. Rising prices** — when supply falls or demand rises, food prices rise. This hurts the poorest most, because poor households in LICs can spend over half their income on food. The United Nations' **Food Price Index** reached a record high in **March 2022** after the invasion of Ukraine. Earlier price spikes in **2007–08** led to food riots in many countries, including **Haiti** and **Egypt**.

**5. Social unrest** — hunger and rising prices lead to protests, riots and even conflict. High bread prices were one of the causes of the **Arab Spring** protests of 2010–11 in Tunisia and Egypt. Competition for scarce land and water can fuel conflict between groups — for example between herders and farmers in parts of the Sahel — so **insecurity can cause conflict, and conflict causes insecurity**.

::: terms
- **Food security**: when all people, at all times, have access to enough safe, affordable and nutritious food for an active and healthy life.
- **Food insecurity**: when people lack reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food.
- **Calorie (kcal)**: a unit measuring the energy content of food; an adult needs roughly 2,000–2,500 a day.
- **Food surplus**: where a country produces or can obtain more food than its population needs.
- **Food deficit**: where a country cannot produce or obtain enough food to meet its population's needs.
- **Undernutrition**: not eating enough calories, protein, vitamins or minerals for good health, causing stunting and wasting.
- **Malnutrition**: an unbalanced diet — too little (undernutrition) or too much (overnutrition and obesity) of the nutrients needed for health.
- **Famine**: a widespread, severe shortage of food causing starvation and death across a region.
- **Water stress**: when demand for water exceeds the available supply, or poor quality restricts its use.
- **Soil erosion**: the removal of the fertile topsoil by wind or water, often made worse by overgrazing and overcultivation.
:::

::: method
**What the questions look like.** Paper 2, Section C, Food option: short questions on a figure (a world map of calorie intake, a graph of food prices, a table of undernourishment) and levels questions of 6 and 9 marks (the 9-mark in Section C often carries +3 SPaG).

**Command words:**
- **Describe (a map or graph)** — give the **overall pattern**, then **specific evidence** (named regions or countries and values with units), then an **anomaly** or exception. Do not explain unless asked.
- **Explain** — give reasons, with connectives: "because", "this means", "as a result".
- **Suggest** — apply what you know to an unfamiliar figure; sensible, geographical reasons earn credit.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** — weigh factors or impacts against each other and decide which matters most, and why.
- **Use Figure X** — quote data from the figure or you cap your marks.

**Building a 4-mark answer**: two developed points — factor → how it reduces food supply → effect. "Conflict forces farmers to flee their land (1), so crops are not planted or harvested (1)..."

**Building a 6-mark levels answer** (Level 1: 1–2, Level 2: 3–4, Level 3: 5–6):
1. Two or three points that answer the question exactly.
2. Develop each one into a chain of reasoning.
3. **Use named examples** — Somalia 2011, the Horn of Africa drought, East African locusts, the Ukraine war, the Arab Spring.
4. For "assess", say which factor or impact is most important, and why.

**Building a 9-mark answer** (Level 1: 1–3, Level 2: 4–6, Level 3: 7–9):
1. Cover **several** factors or impacts, each developed, with place-specific detail.
2. **Show the links** between them — poverty worsens the effects of drought; conflict creates famine; soil erosion reduces next year's harvest. Linking is what separates Level 3.
3. Finish with a **justified judgement** that answers "to what extent" — agree, disagree or "it depends", with the reason.
4. Use specialist terms (undernutrition, water stress, food insecurity) and write in accurate paragraphs for the **3 SPaG marks**.
:::

::: example Describe a world pattern
Figure 1 is a world map showing daily calorie intake per person. Describe the global pattern of calorie intake. (4)

The highest calorie intakes, over 3,300 kcal per person per day, are in HICs such as the USA and the countries of western Europe, and in Australia (1). Intakes are also high in parts of South America and in Russia (1). The lowest intakes, many below 2,400 kcal, are in sub-Saharan Africa, for example the Central African Republic and Madagascar (1). An exception to the pattern is Haiti, which has low intake although it is in the Americas (1).
:::

::: example Explain rising consumption
Explain how economic development leads to increasing food consumption. (4)

As countries such as China develop, incomes rise, so people can afford to eat more food and more meals each day (1). They also change their diet, eating much more meat and dairy (1). Producing meat needs several kilograms of grain to feed the animal for each kilogram of meat (1), so the demand for crops rises even faster than the demand for food eaten directly, increasing total consumption (1).
:::

::: example 6-mark — impacts of insecurity
Explain the impacts of food insecurity on people and the environment. (6)

Food insecurity can lead to famine. In Somalia in 2011, drought and conflict combined to cause a famine in which around 258,000 people died, half of them young children, because crops failed and aid could not reach many areas.

Even without famine, undernutrition affects millions. Children who do not get enough protein and vitamins become stunted and their immune systems weaken, so diseases such as diarrhoea can kill them. Undernourished children also learn less at school, so as adults they earn less and remain poor — a cycle of poverty.

The environment is affected too. Hungry farmers overgraze and overcultivate their land and cut down trees, leaving the soil bare so it is eroded by wind and rain. This reduces soil fertility, so the next harvest is smaller and insecurity worsens. In the Sahel this contributes to desertification.

(Level 3: impacts on both people and environment, each developed into a chain, with a named example.)
:::

::: example 9-mark — factors
"Conflict is the most important factor affecting food supply." Do you agree? (9 + 3 SPaG)

Conflict can have devastating effects on food supply. In Sudan, fighting since 2023 has forced millions of farmers from their land, so crops were not planted, and famine was confirmed in the Zamzam camp in 2024. Conflict also affects supply globally: Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 blocked grain exports from the Black Sea, and world food prices reached a record high in March 2022, hitting poor importing countries in Africa and the Middle East.

However, climate affects far more farmers. Five failed rainy seasons in the Horn of Africa between 2020 and 2023 killed millions of livestock and left millions of people in Kenya, Ethiopia and Somalia short of food, with no fighting involved in much of the area. Climate change is making droughts and floods more frequent, so this factor is growing in importance.

Poverty underlies both. Poor farmers cannot afford irrigation, fertiliser or storage, so they cannot cope with drought, and poor countries cannot afford to import food when harvests fail or prices rise.

Overall, I only partly agree. Conflict causes the most severe food crises — most recent famines, such as Somalia in 2011, have involved conflict — but climate affects the largest number of people, and poverty turns a poor harvest into a crisis. Conflict is therefore the most important factor in the worst crises, but not overall.

(Level 3: several factors developed with named examples, links between them, and a clear, justified judgement.)
:::

::: q
Define "food security". (2)
---
When all people, at all times, have access (1) to enough safe, affordable and nutritious food for an active and healthy life (1).
:::

::: q
State two reasons why global food consumption is increasing. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): rising world population; economic development and higher incomes; changing diets with more meat and dairy; urbanisation and more processed food.
:::

::: q
Explain how pests and disease can affect food supply. Use an example. (3)
---
Pests eat or damage crops before harvest (1), so yields fall and less food is available (1). Example: desert locust swarms in East Africa in 2019–20 destroyed crops and pasture in Kenya (1). Also accept disease — potato blight in Ireland in the 1840s; livestock diseases.
:::

::: q
Explain why food insecurity can lead to social unrest. (3)
---
Food shortages cause prices to rise (1); the poor, who spend a large share of their income on food, cannot afford to eat (1), so anger at governments leads to protests and riots — e.g. food riots in Haiti and Egypt in 2007–08, and high bread prices contributing to the Arab Spring in 2010–11 (1).
:::

::: q
Suggest why some countries with a food surplus still have people who are food insecure. (3)
---
Food security depends on access as well as availability (1). The poorest people may not be able to afford food even when enough is produced (1), or may live far from markets, or food may be exported to earn money (1). Also accept: unequal distribution within a country; food wasted through poor storage.
:::

::: q
Explain how water stress affects food supply. (4)
---
Agriculture uses about 70% of global freshwater withdrawals (1), so where water is scarce there is not enough for irrigation (1). Crops fail or yields fall, and livestock die without drinking water (1), reducing food supply — for example, in the Sahel or during the Horn of Africa drought of 2020–23 (1).
:::

::: watch
- Describing a map by listing countries without stating the **overall pattern** first — and forgetting to quote values with units (kcal per person per day).
- Mixing up **undernutrition** (too little) with **malnutrition** (any unbalanced diet, including obesity).
- Explaining rising consumption only through population growth — the **economic development / changing diet** reason is half the marks.
- Naming factors (climate, conflict, poverty) without **explaining how** each reduces supply.
- Impact answers that say "people die" and stop — develop it: undernutrition → weak immunity → disease; poor learning → poverty.
- 9-markers with no judgement, or a judgement that just repeats the statement without a reason.
:::

::: test
Explain how economic development and population growth together increase the demand for food. (6)
---
Level 3 (5–6): both reasons explained with clear development and examples; links between them. Level 2 (3–4): both reasons with some development, or one well developed. Level 1 (1–2): simple statements.
Indicative content: world population over 8 billion (2022), projected about 9.7 billion by 2050, growth fastest in Africa and South Asia; more mouths to feed. ::: test
"Food insecurity has greater impacts on people than on the environment." To what extent do you agree with this statement? (9 + 3 SPaG)
---
Level 3 (7–9): detailed, well-developed impacts on both people and the environment, with named examples; links between them; a clear, justified judgement. Level 2 (4–6): reasonable impacts, some development and use of examples, judgement partly supported. Level 1 (1–3): simple impacts, little development, no clear judgement.
Indicative content — people: famine (Somalia 2011, about 258,000 deaths, half under five); undernutrition, stunting (around 150 million children), weakened immunity, lower productivity and learning — a poverty cycle; rising prices (record FAO index March 2022; food riots in Haiti and Egypt 2007–08); social unrest and conflict (Arab Spring; herders and farmers in the Sahel); migration. Environment: overgrazing, overcultivation and deforestation for fuel and fields; soil erosion; desertification in the Sahel; loss of biodiversity. Links: environmental damage reduces future harvests and so worsens impacts on people. Judgement: e.g. agree — impacts on people are more immediate and deadly — but the environmental damage makes future insecurity worse, so the two cannot be separated.
SPaG: 3 marks for accurate spelling, punctuation, grammar and a wide range of specialist terms.
:::
