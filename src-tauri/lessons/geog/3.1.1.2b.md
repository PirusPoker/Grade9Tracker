## What it is

The spec says: "The effects of, and responses to, a tectonic hazard vary between areas of contrasting levels of wealth." You must know **primary and secondary effects**, **immediate and long-term responses**, and **two named examples** that contrast in wealth. AQA's textbooks use **Chile 2010** (a richer country) and **Nepal 2015** (a low-income country), and that is what this lesson teaches.

### The four categories

| Category | Meaning | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary effects** | caused **directly** by the hazard itself (the ground shaking) | buildings and bridges collapse; people killed and injured by falling buildings; roads and pipes cracked |
| **Secondary effects** | the **knock-on** effects that result from the primary effects, often hours to years later | tsunamis, landslides and avalanches; fires from broken gas pipes; disease from contaminated water; homelessness; loss of jobs, income and tourism |
| **Immediate responses** | in the hours, days and weeks after, to **save lives** and meet basic needs | search and rescue; medical care; emergency food, water, tents; restoring power and roads; international aid |
| **Long-term responses** | months and years later, to **rebuild** and **reduce future risk** | rebuilding homes, schools and infrastructure; stronger building codes; restoring the economy and tourism; better preparation |

A useful test: *did the shaking do it?* If yes, it is primary. *Did it happen because of something the shaking did?* If yes, it is secondary. A tsunami is secondary (the earthquake moved the sea bed, which moved the water); a collapsed house is primary.

### Case study 1: Chile, 27 February 2010 (a richer country)

**Background.** Chile is one of the wealthiest countries in South America, with a large copper-mining industry. At the time of the earthquake the World Bank classed it as upper-middle income (it became high-income in 2013), with GNI per person more than ten times Nepal's. It has had strict **building codes** since the magnitude 9.5 Valdivia earthquake of 1960, the largest ever recorded.

**The earthquake.**
- Magnitude **8.8**, at **3.34 am** local time.
- Epicentre **just off the coast** of central Chile, in the Maule region, north of the city of **Concepción**.
- Caused by the **Nazca plate** being **subducted** beneath the **South American plate** at a **destructive margin**.

**Primary effects.**
- About **500 people killed** (the official count was 525) and around **12,000 injured**.
- About **800,000 people affected**.
- **220,000 homes**, **4,500 schools**, **56 hospitals** and **53 ports** damaged or destroyed.
- Roads, bridges and Santiago's airport damaged; power, water and communications were cut across much of central Chile.
- Estimated cost around **USD 30 billion**, roughly one-seventh of Chile's annual GDP.

**Secondary effects.**
- A **tsunami** struck the coast within minutes, devastating coastal towns such as **Constitución** and **Talcahuano**, and caused many of the deaths. Chile's navy oceanographic service was later criticised for failing to issue a clear tsunami warning in time.
- Tsunami warnings were issued right across the Pacific.
- A **fire** at a chemical plant near Santiago forced people to evacuate.
- **Landslides** blocked roads, cutting off remote communities for days.
- **Looting** broke out in Concepción in the days afterwards.

**Immediate responses.**
- **Emergency services** acted quickly; troops were sent to affected areas and a **curfew** was imposed in Concepción to stop looting.
- Temporary **repairs to the main north–south highway** were made **within 24 hours**, so aid could reach the south.
- **Power and water were restored to 90% of homes within 10 days.**
- A **national appeal** (telethon) raised about **USD 60 million**, enough to build around **30,000 small emergency shelters**.
- Chile **accepted only limited, targeted international help** (such as field hospitals, satellite phones and generators), because it had the resources to cope.

**Long-term responses.**
- One month after the earthquake the government launched a **housing reconstruction plan** to help around **200,000 households**.
- Chile's **copper-based economy** and government funds meant it did **not need much foreign aid**; the country recovered without long-term economic collapse.
- Tsunami warning systems and coastal planning were reviewed and improved.

### Case study 2: Nepal, 25 April 2015 (a low-income country)

**Background.** Nepal is a low-income, landlocked, mountainous country between India and China, with GNI per person under USD 1,000 at the time. Many people live in remote mountain villages reached only by poor roads or on foot. Many houses are built of stone or brick with mud mortar, and although a building code existed it was **poorly enforced**.

**The earthquake.**
- Magnitude **7.8**, at **11.56 am** local time on a Saturday.
- Epicentre at **Barpak** in the **Gorkha** district, about **80 km north-west of Kathmandu**, with a **shallow** focus.
- Caused by the **Indian (Indo-Australian) plate** colliding with the **Eurasian plate** (a collision / destructive margin: the same movement that builds the Himalayas).
- A major **aftershock** of magnitude **7.3** struck on **12 May 2015**, killing more people and damaging already weakened buildings.

**Primary effects.**
- About **9,000 people killed** and about **20,000 injured**.
- More than **8 million people affected**, over a quarter of the population.
- About **3 million people made homeless**; hundreds of thousands of buildings destroyed or severely damaged.
- About **7,000 schools** destroyed; hospitals overwhelmed or damaged.
- Historic buildings in the Kathmandu Valley destroyed or damaged, including temples in **Kathmandu Durbar Square**, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and the **Dharahara tower**.
- Electricity and water supplies cut; roads blocked.
- Cost estimated at **USD 5 billion or more**, a large fraction (roughly a quarter or more) of Nepal's GDP.

**Secondary effects.**
- **Avalanches** on **Mount Everest** killed at least **19 people** at base camp, the deadliest day on the mountain at that time.
- An avalanche and landslides in the **Langtang valley** buried the village of Langtang, with around **250 people missing**.
- **Landslides** blocked roads, cutting off remote villages for weeks; in May a landslide blocked the **Kali Gandaki river**, and people downstream were evacuated in case the natural dam burst.
- **Tourism**, a key source of income and jobs, fell sharply; trekking and climbing seasons were lost.
- Loss of crops, livestock and food stores in rural areas, and the **monsoon** in June brought further landslides and made life in temporary shelters harder.

**Immediate responses.**
- **Helicopters** rescued climbers trapped on Everest and people in remote areas, and delivered supplies.
- **International search and rescue teams**, medical teams and supplies came from India, China, the UK and many other countries; charities such as the Red Cross sent aid.
- **Field hospitals** supported overcrowded main hospitals.
- **Tent cities** were set up in open spaces in Kathmandu; hundreds of thousands of people left the city to stay with family in villages.
- **Social media** helped: Facebook's Safety Check let people mark themselves safe.
- Aid was **slowed** by blocked roads, remote mountain villages and congestion at Kathmandu's single international airport.

**Long-term responses.**
- At an international conference on **25 June 2015**, donors pledged **USD 4.4 billion** for reconstruction, half as grants and half as loans; India and China were among the largest donors.
- A **National Reconstruction Authority** was set up to manage rebuilding, and householders were offered grants in instalments to rebuild to earthquake-safe standards.
- Roads were repaired and landslides cleared; lakes dammed by landslides were monitored and drained.
- Heritage sites reopened to tourists in June 2015 and Everest reopened to climbers later in 2015, to bring back tourist income.
- Rebuilding was **slow**: more than a year later many families were still in temporary shelters.

### Comparing the two: why the effects and responses were so different

| | **Chile 2010** | **Nepal 2015** |
|---|---|---|
| Magnitude | **8.8** (about 30 times more energy) | **7.8** |
| Deaths | about 500 | about 9,000 |
| Homeless / affected | 800,000 affected | 3 million homeless, 8 million affected |
| Cost | about USD 30 billion (higher, because more valuable property) | about USD 5 billion (smaller in money, but a much larger share of GDP) |
| Buildings | strict, enforced building codes; most modern buildings stood | weak, unreinforced buildings; codes poorly enforced |
| Immediate response | fast, organised, mostly domestic; highway repaired in 24 hours | slowed by remoteness and blocked roads; heavily reliant on foreign help |
| Long-term response | funded mainly by Chile itself | dependent on USD 4.4 billion in international pledges; slow rebuilding |

**The key point**: Chile's earthquake was far **more powerful**, yet killed about **18 times fewer** people. The difference was **wealth** and what wealth buys:

- **Building quality**: enforced earthquake-resistant codes in Chile versus weak, unreinforced buildings in Nepal.
- **Preparedness and emergency services**: Chile has well-trained services and practised plans.
- **Infrastructure and geography**: Chile could repair its main road in a day; Nepal's mountain villages were hard to reach even before landslides blocked roads.
- **Money for recovery**: Chile could pay for reconstruction itself; Nepal depended on international aid, which took time to arrive.

Other factors also played a part. Chile's quake struck at 3.34 am, so most people were at home in houses built to code; many deaths were from the tsunami, not collapsing buildings. Nepal's quake struck at midday on a Saturday, when schools were empty (which probably saved many children's lives) but many people were outdoors or in temples. And the **economic cost** was higher in Chile in absolute terms because richer countries have more valuable buildings and infrastructure to lose; as a share of GDP, Nepal's loss was much greater.

::: terms
- **Primary effect**: an effect caused directly by the hazard itself, such as buildings collapsing from ground shaking.
- **Secondary effect**: an effect that results from the primary effects, such as a tsunami, landslide, fire or disease.
- **Immediate response**: action taken in the hours, days and weeks after a hazard to save lives and meet basic needs.
- **Long-term response**: action taken in the months and years after a hazard to rebuild and reduce future risk.
- **Aftershock**: a smaller earthquake that follows the main shock as the crust settles, such as the magnitude 7.3 aftershock in Nepal on 12 May 2015.
- **Tsunami**: a series of large sea waves caused by the sudden movement of the sea bed, usually by an undersea earthquake.
- **Building code**: legal rules setting how buildings must be constructed, for example to resist earthquake shaking.
- **International aid**: help in the form of money, goods, equipment or people given by other countries or organisations.
:::

::: method
**What the examiner wants.** Case-study questions reward **specific, accurate detail**: place names, dates, magnitudes and a few key numbers. "Lots of people died" is Level 1; "about 9,000 people died and 3 million were made homeless" is Level 2 or 3.

**Sort before you write.** Decide which of the four categories the question wants (primary, secondary, immediate, long-term). Putting a tsunami under "primary", or rebuilding under "immediate", loses credit.

**Explain the differences.** Always link the effect or response to **wealth**: "Chile's highway was repaired within 24 hours *because* it had the money, equipment and trained engineers to do so, *whereas* Nepal's remote villages waited weeks *because*…". Use comparison words: whereas, however, in contrast.

**Levels.**
- **4 marks**: two developed points, or one developed comparison with case-study detail.
- **6 marks**: developed points from **both** case studies, with specific facts, directly answering the question (e.g. "to what extent do effects differ?").
- **9 marks**: a balanced argument (e.g. wealth versus magnitude, time of day, geography), detailed facts from both examples, and a **judgement** that answers the question. Some 9-markers carry **+3 SPaG**: write in paragraphs, use terms such as "subduction", "secondary effect", "long-term response".

**Useful structure for a 9-marker ("to what extent…")**: paragraph 1 with evidence for the statement; paragraph 2 with evidence against or other factors; paragraph 3 with a judgement ("to a large extent, because…"), referring back to the facts.
:::

::: example Classify effects
Classify each of these effects of the Nepal earthquake as primary or secondary: (a) collapse of houses in Kathmandu; (b) avalanches on Mount Everest; (c) loss of tourism income. (3)

(a) Primary (1). (b) Secondary: caused by the shaking loosening snow and ice (1). (c) Secondary: a knock-on effect of the damage and danger (1).
:::

::: example Compare responses
Using named examples, compare the immediate responses to earthquakes in countries of contrasting wealth. (6)

In Chile (2010), a richer country, the immediate response was fast and largely domestic. Temporary repairs to the main highway were completed within 24 hours so that aid could reach the worst-hit areas, and power and water were restored to 90% of homes within 10 days. A national appeal raised USD 60 million, enough for 30,000 emergency shelters, so Chile needed only limited international help (3).

In contrast, Nepal (2015), a low-income country, relied heavily on international help. Search and rescue teams and supplies came from India, China and the UK, and helicopters had to rescue people trapped by avalanches on Everest and in remote villages. The response was slower because landslides blocked mountain roads and aid was held up at Kathmandu's only international airport, so many villages waited weeks for help while people lived in tent cities (3).

(Level 3: detailed facts from both examples and clear comparison.)
:::

::: example Evaluate the role of wealth
"The level of wealth of a country is the main reason why the effects of earthquakes differ." Use named examples to evaluate this statement. (9 + 3 SPaG)

Wealth clearly explains much of the difference between Chile and Nepal. Chile's magnitude 8.8 earthquake released about 30 times more energy than Nepal's magnitude 7.8 earthquake, yet killed about 500 people compared with about 9,000. Chile could afford strict building codes, enforced since the 1960 earthquake, so most modern buildings survived the shaking, whereas in Nepal many homes were built of brick or stone with mud mortar and collapsed, leaving 3 million people homeless. Wealth also shaped the recovery: Chile paid for a reconstruction plan for about 200,000 households itself, while Nepal depended on USD 4.4 billion of international pledges and rebuilding was very slow.

However, other factors also mattered. Geography made Nepal's situation worse: its mountain villages were remote, and landslides and avalanches blocked roads and killed at least 19 people on Everest, so help arrived late regardless of money. Time of day mattered in both: Chile's quake struck at 3.34 am, when people were in their homes, and many deaths were caused by the tsunami rather than collapse, while Nepal's struck at midday on a Saturday when schools were empty. Finally, the economic cost was higher in the richer country, about USD 30 billion in Chile compared with about USD 5 billion in Nepal, because there was more valuable infrastructure to damage, so wealth does not reduce every effect.

Overall I agree to a large extent. Wealth was the main reason for the difference in deaths, injuries and homelessness, because it decided building quality, preparation and the speed of response. Geography and timing made Nepal's disaster worse, but even these were partly about wealth, since a richer Nepal could have built better mountain roads. The one effect wealth increased, rather than reduced, was the monetary cost.

(Level 3 with high SPaG: balanced, detailed facts, sustained judgement.)
:::

---
Any two developed: USD 4.4 billion pledged at a donor conference in June 2015, half grants, half loans (2); a National Reconstruction Authority set up and rebuilding grants to build to earthquake-safe standards (2); roads repaired and landslide-dammed lakes drained (2); heritage sites and Everest reopened to bring back tourism (2).

::: q
Explain why the immediate response in Chile was faster than in Nepal. (4)
---
Chile had more money, equipment and trained emergency services (1), so it repaired its main highway within 24 hours and restored power and water to 90% of homes within 10 days (1). Nepal is poorer and mountainous, and landslides blocked roads to remote villages (1), so it relied on international teams and helicopters, and aid was delayed at Kathmandu's single international airport (1).

::: q
Suggest why the economic cost of the Chile earthquake was higher than that of the Nepal earthquake, even though fewer people died. (3)
---
Chile is richer, so it has more valuable buildings, ports, roads and infrastructure to damage (1); the earthquake was more powerful and affected a large, developed region including ports and the airport (1); in Nepal many destroyed buildings were cheap, simple homes of low monetary value, although the loss was a larger share of GDP (1).
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why the death toll in Nepal was much higher than in Chile. (4)
---
Two developed reasons: weak, unreinforced buildings (stone or brick with mud mortar) and poorly enforced building codes, so many collapsed (2); remoteness and landslides blocking roads, so rescue and medical help arrived late (2); limited money for emergency services and hospitals, which were overwhelmed (2); secondary hazards such as avalanches on Everest and in Langtang (2).
:::

::: watch
- **Mixing categories**: the tsunami in Chile is **secondary**, not primary; rebuilding is **long-term**, not immediate.
- **Vague facts**: "many people died" earns little. Learn a small, accurate set: 8.8, 3.34 am, about 500 deaths, 220,000 homes, USD 30 billion, highway in 24 hours, 90% power in 10 days, USD 60 million appeal; 7.8, about 9,000 deaths, 3 million homeless, 7,000 schools, 19 killed on Everest, USD 4.4 billion pledged.
- **Not comparing**: when the question says "contrasting levels of wealth", every point needs the *other* country alongside it and a reason linked to wealth.
- Do not claim Chile "had no damage" or "needed no help". It suffered USD 30 billion of damage and accepted some targeted aid.
- In 9-markers, a judgement is required. Also consider other factors (magnitude, time of day, remoteness).
:::
