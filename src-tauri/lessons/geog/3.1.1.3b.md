## What it is

A tropical storm (a hurricane in the Atlantic, a typhoon in the north-west Pacific, a cyclone in the Indian Ocean) does damage in three ways: **very strong winds**, **torrential rain** and a **storm surge** — a dome of sea water pushed onto the coast by the wind and raised by the very low pressure at the centre. In most big storms the storm surge kills the most people.

The spec asks for four things: the **primary and secondary effects** of tropical storms, the **immediate and long-term responses**, a **named example** showing both, and how **monitoring, prediction, protection and planning** can reduce the effects.

### Primary and secondary effects

**Primary effects** are caused directly by the storm itself — the wind, rain and surge. **Secondary effects** happen later, as a *result* of the primary effects.

| Primary effects (direct) | Secondary effects (knock-on) |
|---|---|
| People drowned by the storm surge or killed by flying debris | Disease spreads from contaminated water (diarrhoea, cholera) |
| Buildings, bridges and roads destroyed by wind and flooding | People made homeless and living in shelters for months |
| Power lines and phone masts brought down | Shortages of clean water, food and fuel |
| Crops flattened, boats wrecked, farmland flooded with salt water | Jobs and incomes lost; food prices rise |
| Coastal erosion; trees uprooted; landslides on steep slopes | Looting and crime where law and order break down |
| Ports and airports damaged | Aid cannot get in because transport links are cut |

A useful test: if you can ask "and what did *that* lead to?", the first thing is primary and the answer is secondary. Flooded rice paddies are primary; the resulting rise in rice prices is secondary.

### Immediate and long-term responses

**Immediate responses** happen in the hours, days and first few weeks: evacuation, search and rescue, emergency food, water and shelter, field hospitals, restoring power and clearing roads. **Long-term responses** happen over months and years: rebuilding homes, schools and infrastructure, restoring livelihoods (farms, fishing boats), and improving defences and warnings so the next storm does less harm.

### Named example: Typhoon Haiyan, Philippines, November 2013

The Philippines is a lower-middle-income country of over 7,000 islands in the western Pacific. It lies in the path of around 20 typhoons a year because it sits beside the very warm waters of the western Pacific. Haiyan (known locally as **Yolanda**) was one of the strongest tropical storms ever recorded to make landfall.

- **Landfall**: 8 November 2013, first at Guiuan in Eastern Samar, then across Leyte and the central Visayas islands.
- **Strength**: a Category 5 "super typhoon" with sustained winds estimated at about **315 km/h**.
- **Storm surge**: about **5 m** high in the city of **Tacloban** on Leyte, with some surveys finding water marks of up to 7 m. Much of Tacloban lies less than a few metres above sea level on a funnel-shaped bay, which piled the water up.
- **Rainfall**: up to 400 mm, causing flash flooding and landslides.

**Primary effects**

- **6,300** people killed (official government figure), most drowned by the storm surge; over 1,000 more missing and about 28,000 injured.
- About **1.1 million houses** damaged, around half of them totally destroyed. Around 90% of Tacloban city was destroyed.
- The airport terminal at Tacloban was badly damaged; roads were blocked by debris and fallen trees; power and communications were cut across Leyte and Samar.
- Crops (rice, sugar cane, coconuts) flattened; thousands of fishing boats wrecked; farmland flooded with salt water.

**Secondary effects**

- About **4.1 million** people displaced; around **16 million** affected in some way.
- Shortages of food, clean water and shelter led to outbreaks of disease; bodies left in the debris for days added to health risks.
- Looting and violence broke out in Tacloban while police and local government had collapsed.
- Around 6 million workers lost their source of income; coconut trees take years to grow back, so farm incomes stayed low long after the storm.
- Some areas were without electricity for weeks; damage to ports and roads slowed the arrival of aid.

**Immediate responses**

- About **800,000** people were evacuated before the storm arrived, after warnings from the national weather agency (PAGASA) — without this the death toll would have been far higher. However, many evacuation centres were themselves in the surge zone and were flooded.
- The Philippine government and army led search and rescue and restored the airport so aid flights could land.
- International aid arrived quickly: the **USA** sent the aircraft carrier USS George Washington; the **UK** sent HMS Daring (later relieved by HMS Illustrious), whose helicopters took medics and supplies to cut-off islands. The UK was the largest single government donor, giving **£77 million**.
- The UN, Red Cross and other charities supplied food, water-purification kits, tents and medicines; field hospitals were set up by several countries.
- Over 1,200 evacuation centres housed the homeless.

**Long-term responses**

- **Cash-for-work** schemes paid local people to clear debris and rebuild, putting money back into the local economy.
- Homes, roads, schools, bridges and the airport were rebuilt, many to stronger designs.
- Rice farms and fishing were re-established (new boats, seeds and tools from aid agencies); coconut plantations were replanted.
- The government's "Build Back Better" approach included more **storm shelters**, improved **storm-surge warnings** (many people in 2013 did not understand what "storm surge" meant, and officials later said it should have been described as a tsunami-like wall of water), and a proposed **no-build zone** along the most exposed coast.
- Mangroves were replanted along the coast to absorb wave energy.

**What made Haiyan so deadly?** The storm's exceptional strength; a storm surge funnelled into a shallow bay; many people living in flimsy housing on the coast; poverty (many could not afford to move or rebuild); and warnings that did not make the danger of a surge clear.

### Reducing the effects: monitoring, prediction, protection and planning

We cannot stop a tropical storm, but we can reduce how many people are killed and how much is damaged.

| Strategy | What it involves | How it reduces the effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Monitoring** | Satellites track cloud patterns and sea temperatures; aircraft ("hurricane hunters") fly into storms to measure pressure and wind; weather buoys and radar | Storms are spotted days in advance, so there is time to prepare |
| **Prediction** | Computers model the likely track and strength; forecasters issue a "cone of uncertainty" showing where landfall may occur; storms are graded (e.g. Saffir–Simpson scale 1–5) | People and governments know who is at risk and how badly, so evacuation can be targeted |
| **Protection** | Storm shelters on raised ground; sea walls and storm-surge barriers; buildings on stilts, with reinforced roofs and "hurricane straps"; mangroves and coastal forests as natural barriers | Fewer buildings collapse and people have a safe place to go |
| **Planning** | Evacuation routes and drills; land-use zoning (no building in the surge zone); education and emergency kits; clear warning messages | People know what to do and act in time; fewer people live in the most dangerous places |

**Bangladesh** shows how effective this can be. Cyclones in 1970 and 1991 killed hundreds of thousands of people. After thousands of raised concrete **cyclone shelters** were built and an early-warning system using volunteers with megaphones was set up, Cyclone Sidr (2007) killed about 4,000 — still tragic, but far fewer from a storm of similar strength.

**Limits**: monitoring and prediction are only useful if warnings reach people and they understand and trust them; protection is expensive, so poorer countries cannot build everywhere; and a very strong storm can still overwhelm defences — the shelters in Tacloban were flooded.

::: terms
- **Tropical storm**: an intense low-pressure weather system with very strong winds and heavy rain that forms over warm tropical oceans.
- **Storm surge**: a large rise in sea level pushed onto the coast by strong winds and the very low pressure of a tropical storm.
- **Primary effect**: damage or harm caused directly by the storm's wind, rain or surge, such as drowning or collapsed buildings.
- **Secondary effect**: a problem that results later from the primary effects, such as disease, homelessness or rising food prices.
- **Immediate response**: action taken in the hours and days around a disaster to save lives, such as evacuation, rescue and emergency aid.
- **Long-term response**: action taken over months and years to rebuild, restore livelihoods and reduce the risk from future storms.
- **Monitoring**: using satellites, aircraft, buoys and radar to watch a developing storm and measure its strength.
- **Prediction**: forecasting where and when a storm will make landfall and how strong it will be.
- **Protection**: building or designing structures, such as shelters, sea walls and reinforced homes, so they withstand a storm.
- **Planning**: preparing people and places before a storm, such as evacuation routes, drills and keeping building out of danger zones.
:::

::: method
**Command words (AQA)**
- **State / name / outline** (1–2 marks): a short fact. "State one primary effect of a tropical storm" — "people drowned by the storm surge".
- **Describe** (2–4 marks): say what happened, with detail. No reasons needed, but numbers and places earn credit.
- **Explain** (2–6 marks): give reasons — *why* or *how*. Use "because", "which means", "so".
- **Suggest** (2–4 marks): apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar situation or figure — often a photo of storm damage.
- **Assess / evaluate / to what extent** (6–9 marks): weigh up — how far, how well, how important — and reach a judgement.
- **Use Figure X**: you must quote from the figure (a number, a place, a feature). An answer that ignores the figure is capped.

**How the levels work**
- **4-mark** (two levels): Level 1 (1–2) is simple points; Level 2 (3–4) is developed points — each point is explained with a "so" or "which means".
- **6-mark** (three levels): aim for three developed points, each one tied to the **named example** (Haiyan: 6,300 deaths, 5 m surge, Tacloban, cash-for-work). Level 3 (5–6) needs clear, detailed, well-developed explanation with accurate case-study detail.
- **9-mark** (three levels, often +3 SPaG on Paper 1): two sides or several factors developed with Haiyan facts, then a **judgement** that answers the exact question ("the immediate responses were more effective because...").

**Structure for case-study questions: Point → Develop → Link**
1. **Point**: "A primary effect was deaths from the storm surge."
2. **Develop**: "The 5 m surge swept into Tacloban, where most houses were flimsy and low-lying..."
3. **Link to the case**: "...so most of the 6,300 deaths were drownings."

**Primary vs secondary** — the most common mix-up. Label every effect you give. If a question asks for secondary effects, a primary effect earns nothing, however well explained.

**Responses questions** — separate immediate (days/weeks) from long-term (months/years), and say *who* responded (national government, foreign governments, charities, local people). Evaluation of responses asks: did it save lives? Did it reach everyone? Did it make the next storm less damaging?
:::

::: example Describe — primary effects
Using a named example, describe the primary effects of a tropical storm. (4)

Typhoon Haiyan hit the Philippines on 8 November 2013 (1). Its storm surge of about 5 m swept into the city of Tacloban, drowning most of the 6,300 people who died (1). Around 1.1 million houses were damaged or destroyed, and about 90% of Tacloban was flattened (1). Winds of about 315 km/h brought down power lines and trees and flattened rice and coconut crops (1).
:::

::: example Explain — secondary effects
Explain two secondary effects of Typhoon Haiyan. (4)

Disease (1): the storm destroyed water supplies and sanitation, so survivors drank contaminated water, and diarrhoea and other infections spread in the crowded evacuation centres (1). Loss of income (1): about 6 million workers lost their livelihoods because fishing boats were wrecked and coconut trees destroyed, and coconut trees take years to grow back, so families stayed poor long after the storm (1).
:::

::: example Evaluate — responses
To what extent were the responses to a named tropical storm effective? (9 + 3 SPaG)

The immediate responses to Typhoon Haiyan (Philippines, 2013) saved many lives. About 800,000 people were evacuated before landfall after PAGASA's warnings, and foreign help arrived within days — the UK sent HMS Daring and HMS Illustrious, whose helicopters reached islands that were otherwise cut off, and gave £77 million.

However, the immediate response had weaknesses. Many evacuation centres were themselves in the 5 m surge zone and were flooded, and people did not understand the word "storm surge", so many stayed near the shore. Aid was slow to reach Tacloban because the airport and roads were damaged, and looting broke out while government collapsed locally.

The long-term responses were more successful at reducing future risk. Cash-for-work schemes gave people an income while clearing debris, fishing boats and rice farms were restored, and "Build Back Better" added storm shelters, clearer surge warnings and a no-build zone. But coconut farmers waited years for incomes to recover, and some families were still in temporary shelters long after.

Overall, the responses were effective to a large extent: the evacuation and international aid prevented an even higher death toll, and the long-term changes mean the Philippines is better prepared. They were least effective where poverty left people living on the exposed coast, which aid alone cannot quickly change.
:::

::: q
Explain how monitoring can reduce the effects of tropical storms. (3)
---
Satellites and aircraft track a storm and measure its wind speed and pressure (1), so forecasters can see it developing several days before landfall (1), giving time to issue warnings and evacuate people from the threatened coast, reducing deaths (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why a storm surge often causes more deaths than the wind in a tropical storm. (3)
---
A surge floods low-lying coastal land suddenly with several metres of water (1); many people live on the coast in flimsy homes and cannot escape rising water (1); drowning kills more quickly than wind damage — e.g. most of Haiyan's 6,300 deaths were drownings in Tacloban's 5 m surge (1).
:::

::: q
Describe two long-term responses to Typhoon Haiyan. (4)
---
Cash-for-work schemes paid survivors to clear debris and rebuild (1), giving them income and speeding up recovery (1). Rebuilding with more storm shelters, clearer storm-surge warnings and a no-build zone on the most exposed coast (1), so fewer people will be killed in future storms (1). (Also: replanting coconuts and mangroves; new fishing boats.)
:::

::: q
Explain how protection can reduce the effects of tropical storms. (4)
---
Level 2 (3–4): developed explanation, e.g. raised concrete cyclone shelters give people a safe place above the surge, which is why deaths in Bangladesh fell from hundreds of thousands in 1970 and 1991 to about 4,000 in Cyclone Sidr (2007); reinforced roofs, hurricane straps and houses on stilts mean fewer buildings collapse; sea walls and mangroves absorb wave energy.
Level 1 (1–2): simple statements, e.g. "build sea walls", "make stronger houses".
:::

::: q
Suggest why the effects of tropical storms are often greater in lower-income countries. (4)
---
Level 2 (3–4): developed points — houses are built of wood and corrugated iron and collapse in strong winds; less money for monitoring, shelters and sea walls; poor communications mean warnings do not reach everyone; many people live in exposed coastal areas because they depend on fishing; slower emergency services and weaker health care mean more die from secondary effects. Level 1 (1–2): simple list of points.
:::

::: watch
- Mixing up **primary and secondary** effects — disease, homelessness and rising prices are secondary. Label each effect.
- Vague case studies: "lots of people died" earns little; "6,300 died, mostly drowned by a 5 m surge in Tacloban" earns Level 3.
- Writing about the **causes** of the storm when the question asks for effects or responses.
- Not separating **immediate** from **long-term** responses — say when each happened.
- "Evaluate" questions without a **judgement** — the top level needs a clear answer to "how effective" or "to what extent".
- Listing monitoring, prediction, protection and planning without explaining **how** each one reduces deaths or damage.
:::
