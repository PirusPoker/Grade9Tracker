## What it is

An **ecosystem** is a community of plants and animals together with the non-living environment they live in, and all the links between them. Ecosystems exist at every scale: a single pond or hedgerow (small scale), a forest (medium scale), or a whole **biome** such as the tropical rainforest (global scale).

Every ecosystem has two kinds of component:
- **Biotic** (living): plants, animals, fungi, bacteria.
- **Abiotic** (non-living): climate (temperature, rainfall, sunlight), water, soil, rocks, nutrients, oxygen.

The spec needs: a **small-scale UK ecosystem** to show **interrelationships** — producers, consumers, decomposers, food chains, food webs and **nutrient cycling**; the **balance** between components and the **impact of changing one component**; and an **overview of the distribution and characteristics of global ecosystems (biomes)**.

### A small-scale UK ecosystem: a freshwater pond

A lowland pond in England is a complete ecosystem in a few square metres.

**Abiotic components**: water; sunlight (for photosynthesis — only reaches a few metres deep); temperature (warmer in summer, may freeze in winter); dissolved oxygen (from the air and from plants); nutrients such as nitrates and phosphates in the water and the mud at the bottom; the soil and rock that line the pond.

**Biotic components**

| Role | What it does | Pond examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Producers** | Convert sunlight into energy by photosynthesis — the base of every food chain | Phytoplankton (microscopic algae), pondweed, water lilies, reeds and bulrushes at the edge |
| **Primary consumers** (herbivores) | Eat producers | Water fleas (daphnia), freshwater snails, tadpoles, mayfly larvae |
| **Secondary consumers** | Eat primary consumers | Water boatmen, dragonfly nymphs, water beetles, sticklebacks, frogs, newts |
| **Tertiary consumers** (top predators) | Eat secondary consumers | Pike or perch in large ponds, grey heron, kingfisher, grass snake |
| **Decomposers** | Break down dead plants and animals and waste, releasing nutrients | Bacteria and fungi in the mud; also detritus feeders such as freshwater shrimps and worms |

**Food chain**: a single line showing how energy passes from one organism to the next when it is eaten. For example:

phytoplankton → water flea → stickleback → pike

Energy is lost at every stage (through movement, heat and waste), so there are fewer organisms at each higher level, and food chains rarely have more than four or five links.

**Food web**: in reality, most animals eat — and are eaten by — several species, so food chains join into a **web**. In a pond, the stickleback eats water fleas and mayfly larvae, and is eaten by both pike and heron; frogs eat insects and are eaten by herons and grass snakes. To draw one, write the producers at the bottom, then herbivores, then predators, with arrows pointing **from the food to the feeder** (the direction energy flows).

### Nutrient cycling

Nutrients (such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium) are recycled constantly:

1. Plants take up nutrients dissolved in the water and the mud through their roots.
2. The nutrients pass to animals when plants are eaten, and on up the food chain.
3. When plants and animals die, or produce waste, **decomposers** (bacteria and fungi) break them down.
4. This releases the nutrients back into the water and mud, where plants can use them again.

Nutrients also enter the pond from outside — rainwater, rocks weathering, leaves falling in, and runoff from surrounding fields. In a diagram, show three **stores** (biomass, litter, soil/water) linked by **flows** (uptake by plants, fall-out as leaves and dead matter, decomposition, runoff and weathering).

### Balance, and the impact of changing one component

In a healthy ecosystem the components are in **balance**: the numbers of predators and prey, and the supply of nutrients, stay roughly stable over time. Because everything is **interdependent**, changing just one component has a knock-on effect on the others — a **chain reaction**.

| Change | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Fertiliser runoff (too many nutrients)** — *eutrophication* | Extra nitrates and phosphates from farmland wash into the pond → algae grow rapidly (an **algal bloom**) and cover the surface → sunlight cannot reach pondweed, which dies → decomposers break down the dead plants and algae, using up dissolved oxygen → fish and invertebrates die from lack of oxygen |
| **Removing a top predator** (e.g. pike fished out) | Sticklebacks and other small fish increase → they eat more water fleas → fewer water fleas to graze the algae → algae increase and the water becomes murky |
| **Drought / hot summer** | The pond shrinks and warms → warm water holds less dissolved oxygen → fish and invertebrates may die; the pond edge dries and marginal plants take over |
| **Invasive species** (e.g. New Zealand pygmyweed, signal crayfish) | The newcomer outcompetes native species for light, space or food, with no natural predators to control it, reducing biodiversity |
| **Shade from overhanging trees** | Less sunlight reaches the water → fewer producers → less food for everything above them; more leaf litter adds nutrients as it decays |

Other small UK ecosystems follow the same principles: in **Epping Forest** (a deciduous woodland in Essex), oak and beech are the producers, deer, squirrels and caterpillars the consumers, and fungi decompose the thick leaf litter each autumn.

### Global ecosystems (biomes)

A **biome** is a large-scale global ecosystem — a whole region with a similar climate, vegetation and wildlife. Climate (temperature and rainfall) is the main control on where biomes are found, and it changes with **latitude**, distance from the sea and altitude.

| Biome | Distribution | Climate | Vegetation and wildlife |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tropical rainforest** | Along the Equator, roughly within 10° of it: the Amazon basin (South America), the Congo basin (central Africa), South-East Asia (Malaysia, Indonesia) | Hot all year (about 27 °C), very wet (over 2,000 mm), no seasons | Dense, layered evergreen forest with very tall trees; the richest biodiversity on Earth |
| **Tropical grassland (savanna)** | Between the rainforests and the hot deserts, roughly 5–20° from the Equator: East Africa (Serengeti), northern Australia, Brazil | Hot all year; a distinct wet season and dry season | Tall grasses with scattered drought-resistant trees such as the baobab and acacia; large grazing herds and predators |
| **Hot desert** | Around the tropics, about 15–30° north and south: Sahara, Kalahari, Arabian, Atacama, Australian deserts | Very hot days, cold nights; under 250 mm of rain a year | Sparse, drought-adapted plants (cacti, deep-rooted shrubs); few, specially adapted animals |
| **Mediterranean** | West coasts around 30–40°: the Mediterranean, California, central Chile, south-west Australia | Hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters | Evergreen shrubs and trees (olive, cork oak) with small waxy leaves |
| **Temperate deciduous forest** | Mid-latitudes about 40–60° N: western Europe including the UK, eastern USA, eastern China | Mild; rain all year; four seasons | Broad-leaved trees (oak, beech) that shed leaves in winter; rich undergrowth |
| **Temperate grassland** | Continental interiors in mid-latitudes: prairies (North America), steppes (Russia, Central Asia), pampas (Argentina) | Hot summers, cold winters, fairly low rainfall | Grasses; few trees; much now converted to farmland |
| **Boreal (coniferous) forest / taiga** | About 50–70° N across Canada, Scandinavia and Russia | Long, very cold winters; short, cool summers | Evergreen conifers (pine, spruce, fir) with needle leaves; bears, wolves, moose |
| **Tundra** | Beyond the tree line, about 60–75° N: northern Canada, Alaska, Siberia, Greenland coasts | Very cold, long dark winters; low precipitation; permafrost | Mosses, lichens, low shrubs; caribou, Arctic fox |

**Patterns to learn**: the biomes form rough bands parallel to the Equator because temperature falls and rainfall patterns change with latitude — rainforest at the Equator, hot deserts near the tropics (where air sinks in the global atmospheric circulation, giving dry conditions), temperate forest in the mid-latitudes, and tundra near the poles. Mountains and ocean currents break up the bands.

::: terms
- **Ecosystem**: a community of plants and animals and the non-living environment they live in, linked together by flows of energy and nutrients.
- **Biotic**: the living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
- **Abiotic**: the non-living parts of an ecosystem, such as climate, water, soil, rocks and sunlight.
- **Producer**: an organism, usually a green plant or alga, that makes its own food from sunlight by photosynthesis.
- **Consumer**: an organism that gets its energy by eating other organisms — herbivores, carnivores and omnivores.
- **Decomposer**: an organism such as a bacterium or fungus that breaks down dead plant and animal matter, releasing nutrients.
- **Food chain**: a single line showing how energy is passed from one organism to another as each is eaten.
- **Food web**: a diagram of many interconnected food chains, showing the complex feeding links in an ecosystem.
- **Nutrient cycling**: the continual recycling of nutrients between living things, dead matter and the soil or water.
- **Eutrophication**: excess nutrients, often from fertiliser runoff, cause an algal bloom that uses up oxygen and kills fish and plants.
- **Biome**: a large-scale global ecosystem with a similar climate, vegetation and wildlife, such as tropical rainforest or tundra.
:::

::: method
**Command words**
- **State / name / define** (1–2): e.g. "Define the term producer" — give a precise definition, not an example alone.
- **Describe** (2–4): e.g. "Describe the distribution of tropical rainforests" — use latitude, continents and named regions, and any anomaly.
- **Explain** (2–6): processes and chains of cause and effect — how nutrients are recycled; why a change affects the whole ecosystem.
- **Suggest** (2–4): apply ideas to an unfamiliar food web in a figure.
- **Use Figure X**: food-web and world-map questions are common. Quote species or places from the figure.

**Levels**
- **4-mark**: Level 2 needs linked, developed explanation.
- **6-mark**: a clear chain of consequences with the named small-scale ecosystem (the pond: phytoplankton, water fleas, sticklebacks, pike, heron).
- 9-mark questions on this section are more likely to be about rainforests or deserts, but the same ideas (interdependence, balance) are credited there.

**"Impact of changing one component" answers** — write a **chain**, step by step, with "which means... so...". Each link is a mark. Eutrophication is the model answer: fertiliser → algal bloom → blocks light → plants die → decomposers use oxygen → fish die.

**Food webs from a figure**: identify producers (at the bottom, no arrows pointing into them from animals); arrows go from food to feeder. If one species is removed, trace both **up** (its predators lose food) and **down** (its prey increase).

**Describing distribution**: pattern (where most are) → named examples (continent/country with latitude) → anomalies. Avoid "all over the world".
:::

::: example Define and give an example
Define the term "decomposer" and give one example. (2)

An organism that breaks down dead plant and animal material, returning nutrients to the soil or water (1). For example, bacteria or fungi in the mud at the bottom of a pond (1).
:::

::: example Explain — changing one component
Using an example of a small-scale UK ecosystem, explain how a change to one component can affect the whole ecosystem. (6)

In a freshwater pond, fertiliser can wash in from surrounding farmland after heavy rain. This adds extra nitrates and phosphates, so phytoplankton and algae grow rapidly into an algal bloom that covers the surface. The bloom stops sunlight reaching the pondweed below, so the pondweed cannot photosynthesise and dies. Decomposers such as bacteria then break down the dead algae and plants, and in doing so they use up the dissolved oxygen in the water. With too little oxygen, fish such as sticklebacks and invertebrates such as mayfly larvae die. This means top predators such as herons and pike lose their food supply. One change — extra nutrients — has affected producers, consumers and decomposers throughout the food web.
:::

Figure 1 is a world map of biomes. Describe the distribution of hot deserts shown. (3)

::: example Food web — use a figure
Figure 2 is a pond food web: phytoplankton → water flea → stickleback → pike; phytoplankton → mayfly larva → stickleback; stickleback → heron. Suggest what might happen if the sticklebacks were removed. (4)

Water fleas and mayfly larvae would increase because nothing is eating them (1), so they would eat more phytoplankton, reducing the amount of phytoplankton (1). Pike and herons would lose their main food (1), so their numbers would fall, or they would switch to other prey or move to another pond (1).

::: q
Explain why a food web is more realistic than a food chain. (2)
---
Most animals eat several different species and are eaten by several predators (1), so a web shows the many connections, whereas a chain shows only one route of energy flow (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the role of decomposers in nutrient cycling. (3)
---
When plants and animals die, decomposers such as bacteria and fungi break down the dead matter (1), releasing nutrients such as nitrates into the soil or water (1), where they can be taken up again by plants' roots, so the nutrients are recycled (1).
:::

Explain what happens when fertiliser runoff enters a pond. (4)
---
Level 2 (3–4): full chain — extra nutrients → algal bloom → blocks light → pondweed dies → decomposers use up oxygen breaking down dead matter → fish and invertebrates die. Level 1 (1–2): only part of the chain, e.g. "algae grow and fish die".

::: watch
- Arrows in food webs pointing the **wrong way** — they go **from the food to the feeder** (direction of energy flow).
- Defining an ecosystem as "just animals and plants" — it includes the **non-living** environment and the links between them.
- Chain-of-consequence answers that jump from "fertiliser" to "fish die" — spell out **every link** (light blocked → plants die → decomposers use oxygen).
- Describing biome distribution without **latitude** or **named regions**.
- Mixing up **decomposers** (bacteria, fungi) with consumers.
:::
