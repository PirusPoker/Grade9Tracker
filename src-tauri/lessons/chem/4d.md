## What it is

**Cracking** is the **breaking down of long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful molecules** — a shorter alkane and one or more **alkenes** (and sometimes hydrogen). It is a **thermal decomposition** reaction, carried out by passing the vaporised hydrocarbon over a hot **catalyst** (silica or alumina, aluminium oxide) at about **600–700 °C** — **catalytic cracking**.

**Why cracking is needed — supply and demand.** Fractional distillation gives the fractions in the proportions they occur in crude oil, but that does not match what customers want:

- **Demand for short-chain fractions** (petrol, kerosene, diesel) is **higher than the supply** from distillation; these are the fuels.
- **Supply of long-chain fractions** (fuel oil, bitumen) is **higher than the demand** — they are less useful as fuels because they are viscous, hard to ignite and burn with smoky flames.
- Cracking converts the surplus long-chain fractions into extra petrol-sized alkanes, and also makes **alkenes** (ethene, propene) which are the **raw material for polymers** (4i) and for making ethanol (4g). Neither alkenes nor enough petrol come directly from crude oil.

**Equations.** The long alkane splits at a random point; the products must contain the **same total number of C and H atoms** as the reactant. The **alkane** product follows CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ and each **alkene** CₙH₂ₙ. Typical equations:

- **C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄** (decane → octane + ethene)
- C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + 2C₂H₄ (dodecane → octane + two ethene)
- C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₆H₁₂ + C₂H₄ (one alkane, two alkenes)
- C₆H₁₄ → C₄H₁₀ + C₂H₄

Check: in C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄, carbon 10 = 8 + 2 and hydrogen 22 = 18 + 4. Because the reactant is saturated and only one alkane forms, the remaining carbons must go into alkenes — there is always **at least one alkene** product.

**Laboratory cracking (required practical style).** Paraffin (liquid alkane) is soaked into mineral wool at the closed end of a boiling tube; broken pot or aluminium oxide (the catalyst) is placed part-way along; the tube is heated strongly at the catalyst, then the paraffin end is warmed so the vapour passes over the hot catalyst. The gaseous products are collected over water in an inverted test tube. Testing shows the product is an **alkene**: it **decolourises bromine water** (orange → colourless), which the original paraffin does not. Safety: remove the delivery tube from the water **before** stopping heating to prevent **suck-back** (cold water drawn into the hot tube, which could crack it).

**Products of cracking** — alkanes used as fuels (petrol); alkenes used to make polymers (poly(ethene), poly(propene)), ethanol, and other chemicals; hydrogen may be produced and used in the Haber process or hydrogenation.

::: terms
- **Cracking**: breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes.
- **Catalytic cracking**: cracking using a catalyst of silica or alumina at about 600–700 °C.
- **Why crack**: there is more supply than demand for long-chain fractions and more demand than supply for short-chain ones such as petrol; alkenes made are used to make polymers.
:::

::: method
1. **Define cracking** with all three ideas: long-chain alkane → shorter alkane + alkene; thermal decomposition; catalyst (Al₂O₃/SiO₂) at ~600–700 °C.
2. **Why?** Supply vs demand: give both halves — more long chains than wanted, fewer short chains than wanted — *and* mention alkenes for polymers.
3. **Equation questions**: write the reactant; write the given product; find the missing product by subtraction of C and H; check it fits CₙH₂ₙ (alkene) or CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (alkane).
4. **Practical questions**: catalyst, heating, collection over water, bromine water test, suck-back precaution.
5. **Identify the alkene** in a mixture: bromine water orange → colourless.
:::

::: example Definition and conditions
What is cracking, and what conditions are used? (3)

Cracking is the thermal decomposition of a long-chain alkane into a shorter alkane and an alkene (1). The alkane is vaporised and passed over a catalyst of aluminium oxide or silica (1) at a high temperature of about 600–700 °C (1).
:::

::: example Why crack
Explain why oil companies crack the heavier fractions from fractional distillation. (3)

Fractional distillation produces more of the long-chain fractions such as fuel oil than there is demand for, because they are poor fuels (1), while demand for short-chain fractions such as petrol is greater than the amount obtained by distillation (1). Cracking converts the surplus long chains into short-chain alkanes for fuels and alkenes, which are needed to make polymers (1).
:::

::: example Balancing
Complete the equation for the cracking of dodecane to give octane and one other product: C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + ... (2)

Carbon left over: 12 − 8 = 4; hydrogen left over: 26 − 18 = 8, so the other product is C₄H₈ (1). C₄H₈ fits CₙH₂ₙ, so it is the alkene butene: C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + C₄H₈ (1).
:::

::: example Testing the product
A student cracks paraffin in the laboratory and collects a gas. Describe a test to show the gas contains an alkene, and explain one safety precaution taken at the end of the experiment. (3)

Shake the gas with bromine water: it turns from orange to colourless, showing a C=C double bond is present (1). Before the heating is stopped, the delivery tube is lifted out of the water (1) so that cold water is not sucked back into the hot boiling tube, which could shatter it (1).
:::

::: q
Name the two types of hydrocarbon produced by cracking a long-chain alkane. (2)
---
A shorter alkane (1) and an alkene (1).
:::

::: q
Name a catalyst used in cracking. (1)
---
Aluminium oxide (alumina) or silica.
:::

::: q
Why is there more demand for petrol than fractional distillation supplies? (1)
---
Petrol-sized short-chain hydrocarbons are the most useful fuels, but crude oil contains only a limited proportion of them.
:::

::: q
Complete: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₆H₁₄ + ... (1)
---
C₄H₈ (butene): 10 − 6 = 4 C and 22 − 14 = 8 H.
:::

::: q
Complete: C₂₀H₄₂ → C₁₂H₂₆ + 2C₃H₆ + ... (2)
---
Carbon: 20 − 12 − 6 = 2; hydrogen: 42 − 26 − 12 = 4 (1); the missing product is C₂H₄, ethene (1).
:::

::: q
Why must at least one product of cracking be an alkene? (1)
---
The alkane reactant has the maximum number of hydrogens (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂), so when it splits into two alkane-type fragments there are not enough hydrogen atoms for both — one must be unsaturated (CₙH₂ₙ).
:::

::: q
Give one use of the alkenes produced by cracking. (1)
---
Making polymers such as poly(ethene) (or making ethanol by hydration).
:::

::: q
How are the gaseous products collected in the laboratory cracking experiment? (1)
---
Over water, in an inverted test tube or measuring cylinder.
:::

::: q
What is suck-back and how is it prevented? (2)
---
Cold water being drawn back into the hot boiling tube as the gas inside cools and contracts, which could crack the glass (1); remove the delivery tube from the water before stopping heating (1).
:::

::: q
Is cracking a physical or chemical change? Explain. (1)
---
Chemical — covalent C–C bonds are broken and new substances (an alkene and a shorter alkane) are formed.
:::

::: watch
- Cracking is **thermal decomposition** with a **catalyst** and **high temperature** — all three earn marks.
- The answer to "why crack?" needs **both** supply/demand halves **and** the alkene/polymer point.
- Balance by **subtracting atoms**; check the leftover fits **CₙH₂ₙ** (alkene) — if it does not, you have miscounted.
- **At least one alkene** is always produced.
- Bromine water: **orange → colourless**, never "clear".
:::
