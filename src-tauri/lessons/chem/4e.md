## What it is

**Alkanes** are a homologous series of **saturated hydrocarbons** — every carbon–carbon bond is a **single** bond, so each carbon holds as many hydrogens as it can. General formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**.

| Alkane | Molecular formula | Structural formula | Boiling point / °C | State at 20 °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Methane** | **CH₄** | CH₄ | −162 | gas |
| **Ethane** | **C₂H₆** | CH₃CH₃ | −89 | gas |
| **Propane** | **C₃H₈** | CH₃CH₂CH₃ | −42 | gas |
| **Butane** | **C₄H₁₀** | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ | −1 | gas |
| Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | CH₃(CH₂)₃CH₃ | 36 | liquid |

In a displayed formula each carbon has **four bonds** and each hydrogen **one**: methane is a carbon with four H atoms around it; ethane is two carbons joined, each with three H. The boiling point **rises** by roughly 30–50 °C per extra CH₂ because larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces (4a, 4b).

**Chemical properties.** Alkanes are fairly **unreactive** — the C–C and C–H bonds are strong and non-polar, so alkanes do **not** react with acids, alkalis, metals or (importantly) **bromine water**, which stays **orange**. This is how they are told apart from alkenes. Their two important reactions:

**1. Combustion** (the reason they are used as fuels): complete combustion with plenty of oxygen gives **carbon dioxide and water**; incomplete combustion gives carbon monoxide or soot (4c).

- CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
- 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O
- C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

**2. Substitution with halogens in the presence of UV light.** Alkanes react with chlorine or bromine only when exposed to **ultraviolet light** (or sunlight), which supplies the energy to split the halogen molecule. A **hydrogen atom is replaced (substituted) by a halogen atom**, forming a **halogenoalkane** and a **hydrogen halide**:

- **CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl** (methane + chlorine → chloromethane + hydrogen chloride)
- CH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBr (bromomethane)
- C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl (chloroethane)

Further substitution can follow (CH₃Cl → CH₂Cl₂ → CHCl₃ → CCl₄), giving a mixture of products. This is a **substitution** reaction because one atom is *swapped* for another — contrast the **addition** reactions of alkenes (4f), where atoms are added across the double bond and nothing is lost. In the dark, nothing happens: a mixture of methane and chlorine kept in darkness stays unchanged; in sunlight the green colour of chlorine slowly fades and misty fumes of HCl appear.

::: terms
- **Alkanes**: saturated hydrocarbons with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ — methane, ethane, propane, butane, pentane.
- **Saturated hydrocarbon**: a hydrocarbon with only single carbon–carbon bonds.
- **General formula of alkanes**: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, e.g. with 5 carbons, C₅H₁₂.
- **Substitution with halogens**: in ultraviolet light, a hydrogen atom in an alkane is replaced by a halogen atom, e.g. CH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBr.
- **Ultraviolet light**: the energy source needed to start the substitution reaction between an alkane and a halogen.
:::

::: method
1. **Formulae from the general formula**: n carbons → 2n+2 hydrogens. Hexane, n = 6 → C₆H₁₄. Check displayed formulae: 4 bonds per carbon.
2. **First four names and formulae** — methane, ethane, propane, butane — instant recall.
3. **Saturated** means **only single bonds**; write it out in full when asked to explain the term.
4. **Substitution equation**: swap one H for one halogen, and always produce the hydrogen halide too; state the condition — **UV light**.
5. **Distinguish from alkenes**: bromine water stays **orange** with an alkane (no C=C to add across).
:::

::: example Formulae
Give the molecular formula of the alkane with seven carbon atoms, and draw the displayed formula of ethane. (2)

n = 7, so H = 2 × 7 + 2 = 16: heptane, C₇H₁₆ (1). Ethane: two carbon atoms joined by a single bond, each bonded to three hydrogen atoms, so every carbon has four bonds — H₃C–CH₃ drawn with all six C–H bonds shown (1).
:::

::: example Saturated
Explain what is meant by the statement "butane is a saturated hydrocarbon". (2)

Butane contains only hydrogen and carbon atoms (1), and all the bonds between its carbon atoms are single bonds — it has no C=C double bonds, so it holds the maximum number of hydrogen atoms (1).
:::

::: example Substitution
Methane reacts with bromine in the presence of ultraviolet light. Write the equation, name the organic product, and name the type of reaction. (3)

CH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBr (1). The organic product is bromomethane (1). It is a substitution reaction — a hydrogen atom in the methane is replaced by a bromine atom (1).
:::

::: example Alkane or alkene
Two unlabelled gas jars contain propane and propene. Describe a test to tell them apart and give the result for each. (3)

Add a few drops of bromine water to each jar and shake (1). In the jar containing propene the bromine water turns from orange to colourless (1); in the jar containing propane it stays orange because propane is saturated and cannot undergo addition (1).
:::

What is the molecular formula of the alkane containing 12 carbon atoms? (1)

C₁₂H₂₆.

::: q
Why are alkanes described as saturated? (1)
---
They contain only single carbon–carbon bonds and so hold the maximum number of hydrogen atoms.

::: q
Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of ethane. (2)
---
2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O.
:::

::: q
What condition is needed for methane to react with chlorine? (1)
---
Ultraviolet light (sunlight).
:::

::: q
Write the equation for ethane reacting with chlorine in UV light. (1)
---
C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl.
:::

::: q
Why is the reaction of an alkane with bromine called a substitution reaction? (1)
---
A hydrogen atom in the alkane is replaced by a bromine atom.
:::

::: q
What is observed when bromine water is added to hexane and shaken? (1)
---
No change — the bromine water stays orange (hexane is saturated).
:::

::: q
Why does the boiling point of the alkanes increase from methane to butane? (2)
---
The molecules get larger (1), so the intermolecular forces between them are stronger and need more energy to overcome (1).
:::

::: watch
- Saturated = **single C–C bonds only**; it has nothing to do with dissolving.
- Halogenation of alkanes is **substitution** and needs **UV light**; halogenation of alkenes is **addition** and needs nothing.
- The substitution equation has **two products**: the halogenoalkane **and** the hydrogen halide.
- Alkanes leave bromine water **orange** — that is the negative result, and it is still an answer.
- Formula check: hydrogens = **2n + 2**, never 2n.
:::
