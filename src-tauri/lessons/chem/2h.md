## What it is

Chemical tests identify substances by a reaction with a **visible result**. Every answer needs **two parts: the test and the positive result** — a test with no observation scores nothing.

**Tests for gases:**

| Gas | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydrogen** | hold a **lit splint** at the mouth of the tube | burns with a **squeaky pop** |
| **Oxygen** | insert a **glowing splint** | splint **relights** |
| **Carbon dioxide** | bubble through (or shake with) **limewater** | limewater turns **milky/cloudy** (white precipitate of calcium carbonate: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O) |
| **Ammonia** | hold **damp red litmus paper** in the gas | turns **blue** (ammonia is the only common alkaline gas; also has a sharp smell) |
| **Chlorine** | hold **damp blue litmus paper** (or damp universal indicator) in the gas | **bleached white** (may briefly turn red first) |

**Flame tests for metal cations** — dip a clean nichrome or platinum wire in concentrated hydrochloric acid, then in the solid sample, and hold it in a **blue (roaring) Bunsen flame**:

| Ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| **Lithium, Li⁺** | **red** (crimson) |
| **Sodium, Na⁺** | **yellow** |
| **Potassium, K⁺** | **lilac** |
| **Calcium, Ca²⁺** | **orange-red** (brick red) |
| **Copper, Cu²⁺** | **blue-green** |

**Sodium hydroxide test for cations** — add a few drops, then excess, of sodium hydroxide solution to the solution:

| Ion | Result with NaOH(aq) |
|---|---|
| **Copper(II), Cu²⁺** | **blue precipitate** (copper(II) hydroxide): Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂ |
| **Iron(II), Fe²⁺** | **green precipitate** (iron(II) hydroxide) |
| **Iron(III), Fe³⁺** | **brown** (red-brown) precipitate (iron(III) hydroxide): Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃ |
| **Ammonium, NH₄⁺** | **warm** the mixture — **ammonia gas** given off, turns damp red litmus blue |

**Tests for anions:**

| Ion | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbonate, CO₃²⁻** | add dilute **hydrochloric acid** | **fizzing**; the gas turns **limewater milky** |
| **Sulfate, SO₄²⁻** | add dilute **hydrochloric acid**, then **barium chloride** solution | **white precipitate** (barium sulfate): Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄ |
| **Chloride, Cl⁻** | add dilute **nitric acid**, then **silver nitrate** solution | **white precipitate** (silver chloride) |
| **Bromide, Br⁻** | nitric acid, then silver nitrate | **cream precipitate** (silver bromide) |
| **Iodide, I⁻** | nitric acid, then silver nitrate | **yellow precipitate** (silver iodide) |

The acid is added first to **remove carbonate ions** (and hydroxide), which would otherwise give a false white precipitate (barium carbonate / silver carbonate). Use nitric acid with silver nitrate (hydrochloric acid would add chloride ions); use hydrochloric acid with barium chloride (sulfuric acid would add sulfate ions).

**Tests for water:**

| Test | Result | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate** | turns from **white to blue** (CuSO₄ + 5H₂O → CuSO₄·5H₂O) | water is **present** |
| **Anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride** paper | turns from **blue to pink** | water is present |
| **Boiling point** | boils at exactly **100 °C** (freezes at 0 °C) | water is **pure** |

The chemical tests show that water is present, not that it is pure — only a sharp boiling point of 100 °C shows purity.

::: terms
- **Test for hydrogen**: a lighted splint gives a squeaky pop.
- **Test for oxygen**: relights a glowing splint.
- **Test for carbon dioxide**: turns limewater milky.
- **Test for ammonia**: turns damp red litmus paper blue.
- **Test for chlorine**: bleaches damp litmus paper.
- **Flame test colours**: lithium red, sodium yellow, potassium lilac, calcium orange-red, copper blue-green.
- **Sodium hydroxide test for cations**: Cu²⁺ blue precipitate, Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ brown; NH₄⁺ gives ammonia gas when warmed.
- **Test for halide ions**: add dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate — chloride white, bromide cream, iodide yellow precipitate.
- **Test for sulfate ions**: add dilute hydrochloric acid then barium chloride — white precipitate.
- **Test for carbonate ions**: add dilute acid — bubbles of carbon dioxide that turn limewater milky.
- **Test for water**: turns anhydrous copper(II) sulfate from white to blue.
- **Test for pure water**: it boils at exactly 100 °C and freezes at exactly 0 °C.
:::

::: method
1. **Test + result** in every answer — e.g. "add limewater; it turns milky". Say what you *do* and what you *see*.
2. **Gases**: match the splint — *lit* for hydrogen, *glowing* for oxygen — and the litmus: *red → blue* for ammonia, *blue → bleached* for chlorine.
3. **Unknown solid questions**: use a flame test or NaOH for the cation and an acid/precipitation test for the anion; give the colour precisely (green vs brown for iron).
4. **Explain the acid step**: "to remove carbonate ions that would also give a white precipitate".
5. **Water**: present (copper sulfate blue / cobalt chloride pink) vs pure (boiling point 100 °C) — different questions, different answers.
:::

::: example Identifying a gas
A colourless gas is collected. Describe tests that would distinguish between hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide. (3)

Hold a lit splint in the gas: hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop (1). Hold a glowing splint in the gas: oxygen relights it (1). Bubble the gas through limewater: carbon dioxide turns it milky (1).
:::

::: example Identifying a salt
A white solid dissolves in water. With sodium hydroxide there is no precipitate, but a lilac flame is seen in a flame test. With nitric acid and silver nitrate a cream precipitate forms. Identify the salt. (3)

The lilac flame shows potassium ions, K⁺ (1). The cream precipitate with silver nitrate shows bromide ions, Br⁻ (1). The salt is potassium bromide, KBr (1).
:::

::: example Sulfate test
Describe how to test a solution for sulfate ions and explain why hydrochloric acid is added first. (3)

Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then a few drops of barium chloride solution (1); a white precipitate of barium sulfate shows sulfate ions are present (1). The acid removes any carbonate ions, which would otherwise react with the barium ions to give a white precipitate of barium carbonate and a false positive (1).
:::

::: example Water
A student claims a colourless liquid is pure water. Describe two tests and what each shows. (4)

Add the liquid to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate: it turns from white to blue (1), showing that water is present (1). Measure its boiling point: pure water boils at exactly 100 °C (1); an impure sample would boil over a range or above 100 °C, so only this test shows purity (1).
:::

::: example Identify — cations with sodium hydroxide
Sodium hydroxide solution is added to three solutions. A gives a blue precipitate, B a green precipitate that turns brown on standing, and C gives no precipitate but a gas that turns damp red litmus blue when warmed. Identify the cation in each. (3)

A — copper(II) ions, Cu²⁺ (1).

B — iron(II) ions, Fe²⁺; the green iron(II) hydroxide is slowly oxidised to brown iron(III) hydroxide in air (1).

C — ammonium ions, NH₄⁺; the gas is ammonia (1).
:::

::: example Describe — test for a carbonate
Describe how to show that a white powder is a carbonate. (3)

Add dilute hydrochloric acid to the powder (1). It fizzes, giving off a gas (1). Bubble the gas through limewater: it turns milky, showing the gas is carbon dioxide, so the powder is a carbonate (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Lit splint = hydrogen (pop); glowing splint = oxygen (relights)** — do not mix the splints.
- Litmus: **ammonia turns red → blue**; **chlorine bleaches**.
- Iron(II) **green**, iron(III) **brown**, copper(II) **blue** precipitates with NaOH.
- Halides with silver nitrate: **white, cream, yellow** for Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻; acidify with **nitric** acid first.
- Copper sulfate/cobalt chloride show water is **present**; boiling point of **100 °C** shows it is **pure**.
:::

::: test
A white solid is thought to be calcium bromide. Describe tests you would carry out to identify both the cation and the anion, with the expected results. (6)
---
Any six: flame test — clean wire, hydrochloric acid, solid in the flame; orange-red (brick-red) flame shows calcium ions; dissolve the solid in distilled water; add dilute nitric acid; then silver nitrate solution; a cream precipitate shows bromide ions; (optionally) sodium hydroxide gives a white precipitate insoluble in excess, consistent with calcium.
Marking: 1 mark for each correct point, up to 6; for full marks the answer must be in a logical order.
