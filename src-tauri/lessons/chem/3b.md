## What it is

The **rate of a reaction** is how fast reactants are used up or products formed:

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\text{amount of reactant used or product formed}}{\text{time}}$$

Units are typically cm³/s (gas volume), g/s (mass lost) or mol/dm³/s. Rate is measured by following a change you can see or weigh:

| Method | Suitable for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume of gas** collected in a gas syringe (or inverted measuring cylinder) against time | reactions producing a gas | Mg + HCl; CaCO₃ + HCl |
| **Loss of mass** on a balance (cotton wool plug lets gas escape but keeps spray in) | gas given off, especially a dense one (CO₂) | marble chips + acid |
| **Time for a cross to disappear** (precipitation) | cloudy product forms | sodium thiosulfate + HCl → sulfur precipitate |
| **Colour change / pH change** with a probe | colour or acid change | iodine clock |

On a graph of amount against time, the **gradient is the rate**. The curve is **steepest at the start** (highest concentration of reactants), flattens as reactants are used up, and becomes **horizontal when the reaction stops** — the limiting reactant has run out. A faster reaction gives a steeper line that levels off sooner at the **same final amount** (if the same quantity of limiting reactant is used).

**Collision theory**: particles react only if they **collide** with **enough energy — at least the activation energy** — and in the right orientation. Rate increases when collisions become **more frequent** or a **greater proportion have enough energy**:

| Factor | Effect on rate | Explanation (say *both* parts where relevant) |
|---|---|---|
| **Concentration** of a solution (or **pressure** of a gas) increases | faster | **more particles per unit volume**, so collisions are **more frequent** |
| **Temperature** increases | faster (roughly doubles per 10 °C) | particles have **more kinetic energy**: they move faster so **collide more often**, *and* a **greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ the activation energy** — the second effect is the main one |
| **Surface area** increases (powder rather than lumps) | faster | **more particles exposed** on the surface, so more collisions per second between the solid and the solution |
| **Catalyst** added | faster | provides an **alternative route with a lower activation energy**, so a greater proportion of collisions succeed; the catalyst is **not used up** and does not change the products or ΔH |

A catalyst is **specific** to a reaction; examples: manganese(IV) oxide for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂), iron in the Haber process, vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process, enzymes in living things. On the reaction profile, the catalysed path has a **lower peak** but the same start and end levels.

**Required practicals**: (1) **Marble chips and hydrochloric acid** — measure gas volume every 10 s with a gas syringe (or mass loss on a balance); compare large and small chips (surface area) or different concentrations, keeping the mass of marble, volume and temperature the same. (2) **Sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid** — a cross under the flask disappears as a sulfur precipitate forms; time it at different temperatures or concentrations; rate ∝ 1/time. Control variables must be stated: same volumes, same concentration of the other reactant, same temperature, same starting time.

::: terms
- **Rate of reaction**: how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed.
- **Collision theory**: particles must collide, with at least the activation energy, for a reaction to happen.
- **Activation energy**: the minimum energy colliding particles need in order to react.
- **Successful collision**: a collision with at least the activation energy, which leads to a reaction.
- **Surface area**: breaking a solid into smaller pieces increases its surface area, so more particles are exposed and collisions are more frequent.
- **Concentration**: more particles in the same volume, so collisions are more frequent.
- **Temperature**: particles move faster, so collisions are more frequent and more of them have at least the activation energy.
- **Catalyst**: a substance that speeds up a reaction without being chemically changed at the end, by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy.
- **Reaction profile**: a diagram showing the energy of the reactants, the products and the activation energy.
:::

::: method
1. **Rate from a graph**: gradient of the tangent — read a change in *y* over a change in *x* and give units. Average rate = total change ÷ total time.
2. **Explain a factor** in two steps: what changes about the particles → why that gives more successful collisions per second. For temperature give **both** frequency **and** energy.
3. **Sketch curves**: faster = steeper and levels off earlier; same amount of limiting reactant = same final height; more reactant = higher plateau.
4. **Catalyst answers**: lowers activation energy via an alternative pathway; not used up; same ΔH.
5. **Practical design**: independent, dependent and *control* variables; how rate is measured; repeat and average.
:::

::: example Rate from data
In 40 s, 48 cm³ of hydrogen was collected from magnesium reacting with acid. Calculate the average rate, and explain why the rate at 5 s would be higher. (3)

Rate = $48 \div 40 = 1.2$ cm³/s (1). At 5 s the concentration of acid is at its highest, so there are more acid particles per unit volume (1) and collisions with the magnesium are more frequent, giving a faster rate than the average (1).
:::

::: example Temperature
Explain, using collision theory, why increasing the temperature increases the rate of a reaction. (3)

The particles gain kinetic energy and move faster, so they collide more frequently (1). More importantly, a greater proportion of the particles have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy (1), so a larger fraction of collisions are successful and the rate increases (1).
:::

::: example Surface area
A student repeats the marble-chip experiment using the same mass of powdered calcium carbonate instead of lumps. Describe and explain the effect on the graph of volume of gas against time. (3)

The curve is steeper at the start and levels off sooner (1), but reaches the same final volume because the same amount of carbonate and acid is used (1). The powder has a larger surface area, so more carbonate particles are exposed to the acid and collisions happen more frequently (1).
:::

::: example Catalyst
Manganese(IV) oxide speeds up the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide. Explain how a catalyst works and how you could show it is not used up. (3)

The catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy (1), so more of the collisions have enough energy to react and the rate increases (1). Weigh the manganese(IV) oxide before the reaction, filter it off and dry it afterwards — its mass is unchanged (1).
:::

::: example Explain — concentration and rate
Explain, using collision theory, why magnesium reacts faster with 2 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid than with 1 mol/dm³. (3)

The more concentrated acid has more acid particles in the same volume (1), so acid particles collide with the magnesium more often (1). More frequent collisions means more successful collisions per second, so the rate is higher (1).
:::

::: example Draw — a reaction profile with a catalyst
Describe the reaction profile for an exothermic reaction with and without a catalyst. (3)

The products are drawn lower than the reactants, and the overall energy change (ΔH) is shown as the drop from reactants to products (1).

Without a catalyst, the curve rises to a hump above the reactants — the height of the hump above the reactants is the activation energy (1).

With a catalyst the hump is lower, showing a lower activation energy, but the reactant and product levels, and so ΔH, stay the same (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways of measuring the rate of a reaction that produces a gas. (2)
---
Collect the gas in a gas syringe and record its volume at intervals (1); measure the loss of mass on a balance over time (1).
:::

::: q
On a graph of mass of gas produced against time, what does the gradient represent, and why does the line become horizontal? (2)
---
The rate of reaction (1); the reaction has finished because a reactant has been used up (1).
:::

::: q
State what is meant by activation energy. (1)
---
The minimum energy that colliding particles must have in order to react.
:::

::: q
Explain why increasing the concentration of hydrochloric acid increases the rate of its reaction with zinc. (2)
---
There are more acid particles in the same volume (1), so collisions between acid particles and zinc are more frequent (1).
:::

::: q
Why does increasing pressure speed up a reaction between gases? (1)
---
The same number of gas particles are squeezed into a smaller volume, so they collide more often.
:::

::: q
What is a catalyst? (2)
---
A substance that speeds up a reaction by lowering the activation energy (1) without being used up (1).
:::

::: q
Does a catalyst change the enthalpy change of a reaction? Explain. (1)
---
No — the reactants and products have the same energies; only the activation energy is lowered.
:::

::: q
In the sodium thiosulfate experiment, the cross disappeared in 60 s at 20 °C and 30 s at 30 °C. How does rate change? (2)
---
Rate is proportional to 1/time, so 1/60 to 1/30 (1); the rate doubles when the temperature rises by 10 °C (1).
:::

::: q
Name three variables that must be controlled when investigating the effect of concentration on rate. (3)
---
Temperature; volume of solution; mass/surface area of the solid (or concentration of the other reactant).
:::

::: q
Name a catalyst and the reaction it speeds up. (1)
---
Manganese(IV) oxide for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (or iron in the Haber process).
:::

::: watch
- Temperature increases rate mainly because **more collisions have enough energy** — not just "particles move faster".
- Concentration and surface area change **collision frequency**; temperature changes **frequency and energy**; catalysts change **activation energy**.
- A catalyst is **not used up** and **does not change ΔH** or the products.
- Faster reaction, same reactant amount → **same final volume**, reached **sooner**.
- Rate ∝ **1/time** in the disappearing-cross experiment — a shorter time means a faster rate.
:::
