## What it is

In a metal, the atoms lose their outer-shell electrons, which become **delocalised** — free to move throughout the whole structure. What remains is a **giant lattice of positive metal ions** arranged in **regular layers**, surrounded by a **"sea" of delocalised electrons**.

**Metallic bonding** is the **strong electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons**. The attraction acts throughout the lattice, holding it together.

**Properties explained by the structure:**

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Conduct electricity** (solid and molten) | the **delocalised electrons are free to move** through the lattice and carry charge; when a voltage is applied they drift towards the positive terminal |
| **Conduct heat** | the free electrons move quickly and transfer energy through the metal |
| **High melting and boiling points** | a lot of energy is needed to overcome the **strong electrostatic attraction** between the many ions and the delocalised electrons |
| **Malleable** (can be hammered into shape) and **ductile** (drawn into wires) | the ions are in **regular layers** that can **slide over each other** when a force is applied; the delocalised electrons move with them, so the **metallic bonding is not broken** and the metal changes shape without cracking |
| Shiny, dense | delocalised electrons reflect light; ions are close-packed |

Malleability is the mechanism question: it is not that metals are "soft" — the **layers slide while the sea of electrons keeps holding the ions together**. (Compare ionic compounds, which shatter when layers shift, because like charges meet.)

**Why covalent compounds do not conduct** (the contrast the spec asks for): in a covalent substance all the electrons are held in **shared pairs** between specific atoms, and the molecules (or the lattice atoms in diamond and silicon dioxide) are **neutral** — there are **no delocalised electrons and no ions** free to move, so no charge can be carried. Graphite is the one exception, because of its delocalised electrons.

**Summary of the four structures:**

| Structure | Particles | Held by | Melting point | Conducts? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic lattice | positive and negative ions | electrostatic attraction between ions | high | only molten or dissolved (ions move) |
| Simple molecular | small molecules | weak intermolecular forces (strong covalent bonds inside) | low | no |
| Giant covalent | atoms | strong covalent bonds throughout | very high | no (except graphite) |
| Metallic | positive ions + delocalised electrons | attraction between ions and electrons | high | yes, solid and molten (electrons move) |

Deciding a structure from data: high melting point + conducts only when molten → ionic; high melting point + conducts when solid → metal; very high + never conducts → giant covalent; low melting point → simple molecular.

::: terms
- **Metallic bonding**: the strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.
- **Metallic lattice**: a regular arrangement of positive metal ions surrounded by delocalised electrons.
- **Malleable**: able to be hammered into shape, because layers of ions can slide over each other.
- **Ductile**: able to be drawn into wires.
- **Electrical conductivity of metals**: the delocalised electrons can move through the structure and carry charge.
:::

::: method
1. **Describe the structure in one sentence**: a regular lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.
2. **Define the bond**: electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.
3. **Every property → the structural feature**: conduction → delocalised electrons free to move; melting point → strong attraction, lots of energy; malleability → layers slide, bonding not broken.
4. **Covalent vs metallic conduction**: metals have delocalised electrons; covalent compounds have all electrons in shared pairs and no ions.
5. **Identify a structure from properties** using melting point and when it conducts — ions move in ionic liquids, electrons move in metals.
:::

::: example Structure and bonding
Describe the structure and bonding in a metal such as copper. (3)

Copper consists of a giant lattice of positive copper ions arranged in regular layers (1). The outer-shell electrons of the atoms are delocalised, forming a sea of electrons that moves freely between the ions (1). Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons (1).
:::

::: example Conduction
Explain why copper is used for electrical wiring. (2)

Copper's delocalised electrons are free to move throughout the lattice (1), so when a voltage is applied they flow through the wire, carrying the charge — copper conducts very well (1).
:::

::: example Malleability
Explain why metals can be hammered into shape without breaking. (3)

The metal ions are arranged in regular layers (1). When a force is applied the layers slide over each other into new positions (1); the delocalised electrons move with the ions, so the attraction between ions and electrons is maintained and the metal keeps its structure rather than cracking (1).
:::

::: example Identify the structure
A substance melts at 801 °C, does not conduct as a solid, but conducts when molten. Another melts at 1,085 °C and conducts as a solid. Identify the structure of each. (2)

The first is a giant ionic lattice — high melting point, and conducts only when the ions are free to move (1). The second is a metal — high melting point and conducts when solid because of its delocalised electrons (1).
:::

::: q
Define metallic bonding. (1)
---
The strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.
:::

::: q
What are delocalised electrons? (1)
---
Electrons not attached to any particular atom, free to move throughout the structure.
:::

::: q
Explain why metals have high melting points. (2)
---
The electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is strong and acts throughout the lattice (1), so a lot of energy is needed to overcome it (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why metals conduct heat well. (1)
---
The delocalised electrons move freely and transfer energy quickly through the lattice.
:::

::: q
Explain why a covalent compound such as sugar does not conduct electricity. (2)
---
All its electrons are held in shared pairs between atoms and the molecules are neutral (1), so there are no delocalised electrons or ions free to carry charge (1).
:::

::: q
Why does molten sodium chloride conduct but solid sodium chloride does not, whereas sodium conducts in both states? (2)
---
In sodium chloride charge is carried by ions, which move only when the lattice is molten (1); in sodium metal charge is carried by delocalised electrons, which are free to move in the solid as well (1).
:::

::: q
What is meant by "ductile"? Explain why metals are ductile. (2)
---
Can be drawn out into wires (1); the layers of ions slide past each other while the delocalised electrons keep them bonded (1).
:::

::: q
A substance melts at −78 °C and does not conduct electricity. What is its structure? (1)
---
Simple molecular — low melting point, no free electrons or ions.
:::

::: q
Why is an ionic compound brittle while a metal is malleable? (2)
---
Shifting an ionic layer brings like charges together, which repel and split the crystal (1); shifting a metal layer keeps ions in a sea of electrons that still attracts them, so no repulsion occurs (1).
:::

::: watch
- Structure: **positive ions** (not atoms) in a **sea of delocalised electrons**.
- Conduction: **delocalised electrons free to move** — in metals it is electrons; in molten ionic compounds it is ions.
- Malleable = **layers slide, bonding kept** by the electron sea. "Soft" is not an explanation.
- Covalent compounds: **no delocalised electrons, no ions** → no conduction.
- Identify structures from **melting point + when it conducts**.
:::
