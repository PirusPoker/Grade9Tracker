## What it is

**Alcohols** are a homologous series with the **hydroxyl functional group, –OH**, attached to a carbon chain. General formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH** (or CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O). Because they contain oxygen they are **not hydrocarbons**.

| Alcohol | Formula | Structural formula |
|---|---|---|
| **Methanol** | **CH₃OH** | CH₃OH |
| **Ethanol** | **C₂H₅OH** | CH₃CH₂OH |
| **Propanol** | **C₃H₇OH** | CH₃CH₂CH₂OH (propan-1-ol) or CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (propan-2-ol) |
| **Butanol** | **C₄H₉OH** | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH (butan-1-ol) |

In a displayed formula the –OH must be drawn out with the **O–H bond shown**. The short alcohols are colourless liquids that **mix with water** (the –OH group bonds to water) and are used as **solvents** (methylated spirits, perfumes), as **fuels** (ethanol in petrol blends, methanol in racing cars), and ethanol is the alcohol in drinks.

**Combustion** — alcohols burn cleanly with a blue flame to give **carbon dioxide and water**:

- **C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O**
- 2CH₃OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 4H₂O

The oxygen already in the alcohol counts when balancing: ethanol has one O, so the right-hand side's 7 O atoms need only 6 from O₂.

**Oxidation to carboxylic acids.** Alcohols are **oxidised** to **carboxylic acids** (4h). Ethanol → **ethanoic acid**. Two ways:

- **Chemical oxidation** — heat with **acidified potassium dichromate(VI)** (K₂Cr₂O₇ in dilute sulfuric acid). The dichromate is the oxidising agent and turns from **orange to green** as the alcohol is oxidised. Written with [O] for the oxidising agent: **CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O**.
- **Microbial oxidation** — bacteria in the air oxidise ethanol in wine or beer left open to ethanoic acid; this is how **vinegar** is made and why open wine goes sour.

**Making ethanol — two industrial routes.**

**1. Fermentation** (from sugar): **C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂**. Glucose (from sugar cane, sugar beet or starch) is mixed with water and **yeast** in a container kept **warm (about 30 °C)** and **anaerobic** (no air/oxygen). **Enzymes** in the yeast catalyse the conversion; carbon dioxide bubbles off. The temperature matters: too cold and the enzymes work too slowly; **above about 40 °C the enzymes are denatured** and fermentation stops. Oxygen must be excluded or the ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid. Fermentation stops when the ethanol concentration reaches about 15% because it kills the yeast; the ethanol is separated from the mixture by **fractional distillation**.

**2. Hydration of ethene** (from crude oil): **C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH** — ethene and steam pass over a **phosphoric acid catalyst** at about **300 °C and 60–70 atm** (4f). A reversible reaction; unreacted ethene is recycled.

| | **Fermentation** | **Hydration of ethene** |
|---|---|---|
| **Raw material** | sugar from plants — **renewable** | ethene from crude oil — **non-renewable** |
| **Type of process** | **batch** — stop, empty, restart; slow | **continuous** — runs all the time; fast |
| **Rate** | slow (days) | fast |
| **Conditions** | mild: ~30 °C, normal pressure — cheap energy | harsh: 300 °C, 60–70 atm — expensive energy |
| **Purity of product** | impure (about 15%, aqueous) — needs fractional distillation | **pure** ethanol produced |
| **Equipment / labour** | simple, cheap equipment; labour-intensive | expensive plant; few workers |
| **Atom economy** | CO₂ is a by-product | 100% — only one product |

Choice depends on local resources: countries with lots of sugar cane and little oil (Brazil) use fermentation; oil-rich countries with a petrochemical industry use hydration.

::: terms
- **Alcohols**: a homologous series with the functional group –OH and the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH — methanol, ethanol, propanol, butanol.
- **Fermentation**: making ethanol from sugars using yeast, at about 30 °C, in the absence of air: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂.
- **Hydration of ethene**: making ethanol by reacting ethene with steam at about 300 °C, 60–70 atm, with a phosphoric acid catalyst.
- **Oxidation of ethanol**: forms ethanoic acid — by bacteria in air (souring wine to vinegar) or with acidified potassium dichromate, which turns from orange to green.
:::

::: method
1. **Formula and functional group**: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH; –OH drawn with the O–H bond; name with **-ol**.
2. **Combustion**: products CO₂ + H₂O; balance C, H, then O — remembering the alcohol's own oxygen.
3. **Oxidation**: reagent (acidified potassium dichromate), colour change (orange → green), product (ethanoic acid); or air/bacteria → vinegar.
4. **Fermentation conditions with reasons**: yeast (enzymes), ~30 °C (enzymes work best; higher denatures), no oxygen (prevents oxidation to acid).
5. **Compare the two routes** across rate, purity, cost/conditions, renewability and batch/continuous — pick the ones the question asks for and state *both* sides.
:::

::: example Combustion
Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of propanol, C₃H₇OH. (2)

C₃H₇OH + 4.5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O, so doubling: 2C₃H₇OH + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 8H₂O (2). (Oxygen on the right: 6 × 2 + 8 = 20; from the alcohol: 2, so 18 from O₂ = 9 molecules.)
:::

::: example Oxidation
Describe how ethanol can be oxidised in the laboratory, including what is seen, and name the product. (3)

Warm the ethanol with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) solution (1). The solution changes from orange to green as the dichromate is reduced (1). The ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH (1).
:::

::: example Fermentation
Describe the conditions used to make ethanol by fermentation and explain why each is needed. (4)

Glucose solution is mixed with yeast, whose enzymes catalyse the reaction C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ (1). The temperature is kept at about 30 °C — warm enough for the enzymes to work quickly, but not so hot that they are denatured (1). Air is excluded, because oxygen would allow the ethanol to be oxidised to ethanoic acid (1). When the concentration reaches about 15% the yeast is killed, so the ethanol is then separated by fractional distillation (1).
:::

::: example Comparing routes
Compare the production of ethanol by fermentation with production by the hydration of ethene. (4)

Fermentation uses sugar, a renewable resource, whereas hydration uses ethene from non-renewable crude oil (1). Fermentation is a slow batch process at about 30 °C, while hydration is a fast continuous process needing 300 °C and 60–70 atm, so it uses more energy (1). Fermentation gives an impure, dilute solution that must be distilled, whereas hydration produces pure ethanol (1). Fermentation needs cheap, simple equipment but more labour; hydration needs an expensive plant but few workers (1).
:::
