## What it is

- **Element** — a substance made of **only one type of atom** (oxygen, iron). Cannot be broken down chemically.
- **Compound** — two or more elements **chemically bonded** in a **fixed ratio** (water H₂O, sodium chloride NaCl). Its properties differ from its elements; it can be separated only by a chemical reaction.
- **Mixture** — two or more substances **not chemically bonded**, in **any proportion** (air, sea water, crude oil). Each substance keeps its own properties; mixtures can be separated by **physical methods**.

**Pure substance** — in chemistry, a **single element or compound**, not mixed with anything else. (Everyday "pure orange juice" is a mixture.) A pure substance has a **sharp, fixed melting point and boiling point**; a mixture melts **over a range** of temperatures and usually lower (impurities disrupt the lattice). So measuring the melting point tests purity.

**Separation techniques** — choose by the difference in properties:

| Technique | Separates | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Filtration** | an **insoluble solid** from a liquid | liquid passes through filter paper; solid (**residue**) is trapped; liquid (**filtrate**) collected | sand from water |
| **Crystallisation** | a **soluble solid** from its solution | heat to evaporate some solvent until saturated (crystals form on a glass rod); cool slowly; filter off crystals; dry | copper sulfate from solution |
| **Simple distillation** | a **solvent** from a solution (or two liquids with very different boiling points) | heat; the liquid with the lower boiling point boils, its vapour passes into a **condenser** (cold water in at the bottom, out at the top) and condenses; collected as **distillate** | pure water from sea water |
| **Fractional distillation** | **miscible liquids** with **close boiling points** | a **fractionating column** (glass beads give a temperature gradient — hot at the bottom, cool at the top); the liquid with the lowest boiling point reaches the top first and is collected; the thermometer at the top reads its boiling point | ethanol (78 °C) from water (100 °C); crude oil; liquid air |
| **Paper chromatography** | **soluble substances** in a mixture, e.g. dyes in ink | see below | inks, food colourings |

**Chromatography**: draw a **pencil** baseline on chromatography paper (ink would dissolve and run); put a spot of the mixture on it; stand the paper in a **solvent** with the level **below the baseline**; the solvent rises through the paper (the **mobile phase**) and carries the substances at different speeds depending on their **solubility** in the solvent and their attraction to the paper (the **stationary phase**). More soluble substances travel further. Mark the **solvent front** when the solvent nears the top. A **pure** substance gives **one spot**; a mixture gives several. Substances are identified by comparing with known samples run alongside, or by the **Rf value**:

$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the substance}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent}}$$

Measured from the baseline to the centre of the spot and to the solvent front. Rf is always between 0 and 1, has no units, and is the same for a given substance in a given solvent — so it identifies substances. Colourless substances are made visible with a **locating agent**.

::: terms
- **Element**: a substance made of only one type of atom.
- **Compound**: a substance made of two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions.
- **Mixture**: two or more substances that are not chemically combined and can be separated by physical methods.
- **Pure substance**: a single element or compound, not mixed with anything else; it has a sharp melting and boiling point.
- **Filtration**: separating an insoluble solid from a liquid.
- **Crystallisation**: obtaining a soluble solid from a solution by evaporating some solvent and leaving it to crystallise.
- **Simple distillation**: separating a solvent from a solution by boiling and condensing it.
- **Fractional distillation**: separating a mixture of liquids with different boiling points.
- **Paper chromatography**: separating the soluble substances in a mixture by how far they travel up paper with a solvent.
- **Rf value**: distance moved by the substance ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
:::

::: method
1. **Classify**: one type of atom → element; atoms of different elements bonded in a fixed ratio → compound; substances mixed, not bonded → mixture. Use "chemically bonded" and "fixed ratio" for compounds.
2. **Purity test**: sharp melting point = pure; a range and a lowered value = impure.
3. **Choose a technique from the difference**: insoluble solid → filtration; soluble solid wanted → crystallisation; solvent wanted → simple distillation; liquids with close boiling points → fractional distillation; small amounts of soluble dyes → chromatography.
4. **Describe the method fully**, including the apparatus detail that carries the mark: condenser water direction, fractionating column, pencil baseline, solvent below the baseline.
5. **Rf**: measure both distances from the **baseline**; divide; compare with known values. Same solvent and paper needed for a fair comparison.
:::

::: example Classify
Classify each as an element, compound or mixture: sea water; carbon dioxide; copper; air. (4)

Sea water — mixture (water, salts, not bonded, variable proportions) (1). Carbon dioxide — compound (carbon and oxygen atoms chemically bonded, fixed ratio 1:2) (1). Copper — element (one type of atom) (1). Air — mixture of gases (1).
:::

::: example Purity
A student measures the melting point of a white solid as 118–126 °C. The pure compound melts at 128 °C. What does this show? (2)

The solid is impure (1): it melts over a range and below the true melting point, because impurities disrupt the structure (1).
:::

::: example Fractional distillation
Describe how you would obtain ethanol from a mixture of ethanol and water. (4)

Heat the mixture in a flask fitted with a fractionating column and a condenser (1). Ethanol has the lower boiling point (78 °C), so its vapour rises through the column; the column is cooler towards the top, so water vapour condenses and runs back while ethanol vapour reaches the top (1). When the thermometer at the top reads 78 °C, ethanol vapour passes into the condenser, where it is cooled by water flowing in at the bottom and out at the top, and condenses (1). Collect the ethanol distillate; when the temperature starts to rise, stop collecting (1).
:::

::: example Rf
On a chromatogram the solvent front moved 8.0 cm from the baseline and a dye spot moved 6.0 cm. Calculate the Rf value and explain how it could identify the dye. (3)

$R_f = \dfrac{6.0}{8.0} = 0.75$ (2). Rf is constant for a substance in a given solvent, so comparing 0.75 with the Rf values of known dyes run in the same solvent identifies it (1).
:::

::: example Describe — salt from rock salt
Describe how to obtain pure, dry salt crystals from rock salt (salt mixed with sand). (4)

Crush the rock salt and stir it into warm water, so the salt dissolves but the sand does not (1).

Filter the mixture; the sand stays on the filter paper and the salt solution passes through as the filtrate (1).

Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin to evaporate some of the water until crystals start to form, then leave it to cool so more crystals form (1).

Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers or in a warm oven (1).
:::

::: q
Define "compound". (2)
---
A substance made of two or more elements (1) chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio (1).
:::

::: q
What does "pure" mean in chemistry? (1)
---
A single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance.
:::

::: q
Name the technique to separate sand from a mixture of sand and water, and name the solid and liquid collected. (2)
---
Filtration (1); the sand is the residue and the water the filtrate (1).
:::

::: q
Describe how to obtain pure dry crystals of a salt from its solution. (3)
---
Heat the solution to evaporate some water until it is saturated — a glass rod dipped in shows crystals forming (1); leave to cool slowly so crystals form (1); filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers or in a warm oven (1).
:::

::: q
In simple distillation, why does the cooling water enter the condenser at the bottom? (1)
---
So the condenser is always full of water and cools the vapour efficiently along its whole length.
:::

::: q
Why is the baseline on a chromatogram drawn in pencil? (1)
---
Ink would dissolve in the solvent and run up the paper, spoiling the result.
:::

::: q
Why must the solvent level be below the baseline? (1)
---
Otherwise the spots would dissolve into the solvent instead of moving up the paper.
:::

::: q
A chromatogram of an ink shows three spots. What does this tell you? (1)
---
The ink is a mixture of at least three substances (dyes).
:::

::: q
A substance moved 3.6 cm and the solvent 9.0 cm. Calculate the Rf value. (1)
---
$3.6 \div 9.0 = 0.4$.
:::

::: q
Explain why fractional distillation, not simple distillation, is used to separate ethanol and water. (2)
---
Their boiling points are close (78 °C and 100 °C), so both would evaporate in simple distillation (1); the fractionating column repeatedly condenses the higher-boiling water and lets only ethanol vapour through (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Pure = one substance.** Purity shows as a **sharp melting point**; mixtures melt over a **range**.
- Compounds are **chemically bonded in a fixed ratio**; mixtures are **not bonded** and separable physically.
- Distillation: name the **condenser** and the **water direction**; fractional needs the **column** and **close boiling points**.
- Chromatography: **pencil** line, solvent **below** the line, **Rf = spot ÷ solvent front**, no units.
- Filtration keeps the **residue**, collects the **filtrate** — use the words.
:::

Describe how you would separate a mixture of salt and sand to obtain dry salt. (4)

Add water and stir to dissolve the salt (1); filter — sand stays as the residue (1); heat the filtrate to evaporate most of the water (1); leave to crystallise and dry the crystals (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how simple distillation separates pure water from sea water. (3)
---
Heat the sea water: the water boils and evaporates, leaving the salt behind (1); the vapour passes into the condenser, where cold water cools it (1); it condenses and is collected as the distillate (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how fractional distillation separates ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) from water (100 °C). (3)
---
The mixture is heated; ethanol has the lower boiling point so its vapour rises through the fractionating column first (1); the column is cooler at the top, so water vapour condenses and runs back (1); ethanol vapour reaches the top at 78 °C and is condensed and collected (1).
:::

::: test
A pure substance and a mixture are heated. How do their melting behaviours differ? (2)
---
A pure substance melts sharply at one fixed temperature (1); a mixture melts over a range of temperatures (1).
:::

::: test
In paper chromatography, explain how substances become separated. (2)
---
Substances dissolve in the solvent to different extents and are attracted to the paper to different extents (1); more soluble substances travel further up the paper with the solvent front (1).
:::

::: test
A dye moved 4.5 cm while the solvent front moved 7.5 cm. Calculate the Rf value. (2)
---
Rf = 4.5 ÷ 7.5 (1) = 0.6 (1).
:::

::: test
Two food colourings give spots with the same Rf value in the same solvent. What can you conclude? (1)
---
They are likely to be the same substance (same Rf in the same solvent).
:::

::: test
Name the technique used to separate (a) an insoluble solid from a liquid, (b) two miscible liquids, (c) coloured substances in an ink. (3)
---
(a) filtration (1); (b) fractional distillation (1); (c) chromatography (1).
:::
