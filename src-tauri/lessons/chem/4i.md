## What it is

A **polymer** is a very large molecule made by joining together many small molecules called **monomers**. The process is **polymerisation**. Polymers are all around us — poly(ethene) bags, poly(propene) ropes and crates, PVC pipes, PTFE (Teflon) non-stick pans, nylon and polyester fabrics.

**Addition polymerisation.** **Alkenes** are the monomers: under **high pressure** with a **catalyst**, the **C=C double bond opens** and the molecules **join end to end** into a long chain of single-bonded carbons. **Nothing is lost** — the polymer is the **only product**, and its empirical formula is the same as the monomer's.

- n C₂H₄ → –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ– ethene → poly(ethene)
- n C₃H₆ → poly(propene); n C₂H₃Cl (chloroethene) → poly(chloroethene), PVC; n C₂F₄ → poly(tetrafluoroethene), PTFE

The polymer is named **poly(monomer)** with brackets.

**Drawing a repeat unit** from a monomer: draw the monomer's displayed formula, change the C=C to C–C, keep every other atom exactly where it was, put **square brackets** around the two carbons with **bonds sticking out through both brackets**, and write **n** at the bottom right. To go back from a repeat unit to the monomer, do the reverse: reinstate the double bond and remove the trailing bonds. Propene's repeat unit has H, H on one carbon and H, CH₃ on the other; the CH₃ is a **side group**, not part of the chain.

**Condensation polymerisation.** Monomers with **two functional groups** each join in a chain, and **each time a link forms a small molecule — usually water — is eliminated**. So there are **two products**: the polymer **and** water.

- **Polyesters** (e.g. PET, Terylene): a **dicarboxylic acid** (HOOC–□–COOH) + a **diol** (HO–□–OH) → polyester with **ester links (–COO–)** + water. Each acid group reacts with an alcohol group as in esterification (4h); because both monomers have two groups, the chain keeps growing.
- **Nylon** (a polyamide): a dicarboxylic acid + a diamine → amide links + water.
- Biological examples: **starch** from glucose; **proteins** from amino acids. Both are condensation polymers and are **biodegradable**.

| | Addition polymerisation | Condensation polymerisation |
|---|---|---|
| Monomers | alkenes (one C=C) | two different monomers, each with **two** functional groups (or one monomer with two different groups) |
| Products | polymer **only** | polymer **+ small molecule** (water) |
| Link in chain | C–C backbone only | ester (–COO–) or amide links |
| Examples | poly(ethene), poly(propene), PVC, PTFE | polyesters (PET), nylon, starch, proteins |

**Problems with polymer disposal.** Most addition polymers are **inert** — the strong C–C bonds and lack of reactive groups mean microorganisms cannot break them down: they are **non-biodegradable** and persist in landfill for centuries, and litter the oceans, harming wildlife. Disposal options:

- **Landfill** — polymers do not decompose, so sites fill up; wastes a valuable resource.
- **Burning (incineration)** — releases energy (can generate electricity) but produces **carbon dioxide** (greenhouse gas) and, for some polymers, **toxic gases** — PVC gives off **hydrogen chloride**, and incomplete combustion gives carbon monoxide.
- **Recycling** — saves crude oil and energy and reduces landfill, but polymers must be **sorted by type** (expensive, labour-intensive) and cleaned; repeated recycling degrades the polymer.
- **Biodegradable polymers** — made from plant starch or with additives so microorganisms can break them down.

Polyesters and polyamides can be **hydrolysed** (the reverse of condensation), so they are more easily broken down than addition polymers.

::: method
1. **Define**: monomer (small molecule), polymer (long chain of many monomers), polymerisation. For addition, say **"the C=C opens"** and **"the alkene molecules join together"**.
2. **Repeat unit drawing checklist**: single bond between the carbons; all side groups kept; square brackets; **bonds through both brackets**; **n** outside bottom right; every carbon still has four bonds.
3. **Monomer from a polymer**: put the double bond back between the two backbone carbons.
4. **Condensation**: two functional groups per monomer; **water eliminated**; name the link (ester); two products.
5. **Disposal**: for each method give one advantage and one disadvantage; "non-biodegradable because microorganisms cannot break the inert chains" is the key idea.
:::

::: example Addition polymerisation
Explain how poly(ethene) is formed from ethene, and draw the repeat unit. (3)

Ethene molecules are the monomers; under high pressure with a catalyst the carbon–carbon double bond in each molecule opens (1). The molecules join end to end through new single carbon–carbon bonds to form a long chain — an addition polymer, with no other product (1). Repeat unit: two carbons joined by a single bond, each carrying two hydrogens, in square brackets with bonds extending through the brackets and n outside (1).
:::

::: example Repeat unit of a substituted alkene
Chloroethene, CH₂=CHCl, is used to make PVC. Describe the repeat unit of poly(chloroethene). (2)

Two backbone carbons joined by a single bond inside square brackets, with bonds extending out through each bracket (1). One carbon carries two hydrogens; the other carries one hydrogen and one chlorine — the Cl stays as a side group exactly where it was in the monomer; n is written outside (1).
:::

::: example Condensation polymerisation
Describe how a polyester is formed and explain how this differs from addition polymerisation. (4)

A polyester is made from two monomers: a dicarboxylic acid, with a –COOH group at each end, and a diol, with an –OH at each end (1). An acid group and an alcohol group react to form an ester link, –COO–, and because each monomer has two groups the chain grows in both directions (1). Each time a link forms a molecule of water is eliminated, so water is a second product (1). In addition polymerisation the alkene monomers simply join by opening their double bonds and the polymer is the only product (1).
:::

::: example Disposal
Discuss the problems of disposing of addition polymers such as poly(ethene) and evaluate two methods. (4)

Poly(ethene) is inert and non-biodegradable — microorganisms cannot break down its unreactive carbon chains — so it persists in landfill for hundreds of years and litters the environment (1). Burning releases useful energy but produces carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, and some polymers give toxic gases such as hydrogen chloride from PVC (1). Recycling conserves crude oil and reduces landfill (1), but the polymers must be collected, cleaned and sorted by type, which is expensive and uses energy (1).
:::

::: q
Define the terms monomer and polymer. (2)
---
Monomer: a small molecule that can join to others to form a polymer (1). Polymer: a very large molecule made of many repeating monomer units joined together (1).
:::

::: q
What feature must a monomer have to undergo addition polymerisation? (1)
---
A carbon–carbon double bond (it must be an alkene).
:::

::: q
Name the polymer formed from propene and describe its repeat unit. (2)
---
Poly(propene) (1); two backbone carbons with H, H on one and H, CH₃ on the other, in brackets with bonds through both sides and n outside (1).
:::

::: q
Why is the empirical formula of poly(ethene) the same as that of ethene? (1)
---
Addition polymerisation adds the monomers together with nothing lost, so the ratio of atoms (CH₂) is unchanged.
:::

::: q
Give the monomer of PTFE, poly(tetrafluoroethene). (1)
---
Tetrafluoroethene, C₂F₄ (CF₂=CF₂).
:::

::: q
Name the small molecule usually eliminated in condensation polymerisation. (1)
---
Water.
:::

::: q
Name the two types of monomer needed to make a polyester and the link formed. (2)
---
A dicarboxylic acid and a diol (1); an ester link, –COO– (1).
:::

::: q
Give two naturally occurring condensation polymers. (2)
---
Starch (from glucose) (1); proteins (from amino acids) (1).
:::

::: q
Why are most addition polymers non-biodegradable? (1)
---
They are inert — their strong, unreactive carbon–carbon chains cannot be broken down by microorganisms.
:::

::: q
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of burning waste polymers. (2)
---
Advantage: releases energy that can be used, and reduces the volume of waste (1). Disadvantage: produces carbon dioxide and possibly toxic gases such as hydrogen chloride (1).
:::

::: watch
- Repeat unit: **single bond**, **bonds through the brackets**, **n outside**, side groups **unchanged** — miss any one and the mark goes.
- Addition polymerisation has **one product**; condensation gives the polymer **plus water**.
- Condensation monomers need **two functional groups each**.
- Polymer name = **poly(monomer)** with brackets: poly(propene), not "polypropane".
- Non-biodegradable means **microorganisms cannot break it down** — say so explicitly.
:::
