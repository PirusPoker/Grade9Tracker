## What it is

**Acids** are substances that produce **hydrogen ions, H⁺**, when dissolved in water — an acid is a **proton donor** (H⁺ is just a proton). Common laboratory acids: **hydrochloric acid HCl**, **sulfuric acid H₂SO₄**, **nitric acid HNO₃**; weaker ones include ethanoic acid (vinegar) and citric acid. In water: HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻.

**Bases** are substances that **neutralise acids** — metal oxides, metal hydroxides, ammonia and carbonates. A base is a **proton acceptor** (it takes H⁺). An **alkali is a base that is soluble in water** and produces **hydroxide ions, OH⁻**: sodium hydroxide **NaOH**, potassium hydroxide **KOH**, calcium hydroxide Ca(OH)₂ (limewater), ammonia solution. NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻. Every alkali is a base, but insoluble bases such as copper oxide are not alkalis.

**Neutralisation** is the reaction between an acid and a base; in solution it is simply hydrogen ions meeting hydroxide ions: **H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O**. The salt formed depends on the acid: hydrochloric → **chlorides**, sulfuric → **sulfates**, nitric → **nitrates**.

**Indicators** change colour depending on whether a solution is acidic or alkaline:

| Indicator | In acid | In alkali | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Litmus** | **red** | **blue** | purple when neutral; simple acid/alkali test |
| **Phenolphthalein** | **colourless** | **pink** | sharp change — good for titrations |
| **Methyl orange** | **red** | **yellow** | orange at the end-point |
| **Universal indicator** | red → orange → yellow | green (neutral) → blue → purple | gives an approximate **pH** |

**The pH scale** runs from **0 to 14** and measures **how acidic or alkaline** a solution is — it depends on the **concentration of hydrogen ions**. Lower pH = more H⁺ = more acidic.

- **pH 0–6: acidic** (pH 0–2 strongly acidic — hydrochloric acid, stomach acid; pH 4–6 weakly acidic — vinegar, rainwater).
- **pH 7: neutral** — pure water, sodium chloride solution.
- **pH 8–14: alkaline** (pH 8–10 weakly alkaline — baking soda, toothpaste; pH 12–14 strongly alkaline — sodium hydroxide, oven cleaner).

Each step down the scale is ten times more hydrogen ions; a pH probe (meter) gives a more precise reading than universal indicator.

**Neutralisation in practice**: adding alkali to an acid raises the pH from low values through 7; the colour of universal indicator moves from red through green to purple. **Excess** acid or alkali is what determines the final pH — equal amounts of moles of H⁺ and OH⁻ give pH 7. Everyday uses: **indigestion tablets** (bases such as magnesium hydroxide or calcium carbonate neutralise stomach acid), **lime** (calcium hydroxide/oxide) neutralises acidic soil, toothpaste neutralises acids made by bacteria, wasp stings (alkaline) treated with vinegar.

::: terms
- **Acid**: a source of hydrogen ions, H⁺, in water; a proton donor.
- **Alkali**: a soluble base that is a source of hydroxide ions, OH⁻, in water.
- **Base**: a substance that neutralises an acid to form a salt and water; a proton acceptor.
- **pH scale**: a scale from 0 to 14 measuring acidity: below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 alkaline.
- **Neutralisation**: the reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
- **Litmus**: red in acid, blue in alkali.
- **Phenolphthalein**: colourless in acid, pink in alkali.
- **Methyl orange**: red in acid, yellow in alkali.
- **Universal indicator**: shows the pH by a range of colours, red (strong acid) to purple (strong alkali).
- **Titration**: a method for finding the exact volume of one solution needed to react with another, using a burette, pipette and indicator.
:::

::: method
1. **Definitions with ions**: acid → H⁺ (proton donor); alkali → OH⁻ (soluble base, proton acceptor). Give the ion every time — "acids taste sour" earns nothing.
2. **Indicator colours** — learn the table, especially litmus red/blue, phenolphthalein colourless/pink, methyl orange red/yellow.
3. **pH questions**: state the number range and the word (acidic/neutral/alkaline); link low pH to *high* H⁺ concentration.
4. **Neutralisation ionic equation**: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, then name the salt from the acid used.
5. **Base vs alkali**: if asked why something is a base but not an alkali, answer "it is insoluble in water".
:::

::: example Definitions
Explain, in terms of ions, what is meant by an acid and by an alkali. (2)

An acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) when dissolved in water (1). An alkali is a soluble base that produces hydroxide ions (OH⁻) in water (1).
:::

::: example Indicator colours
Solutions of hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide are tested separately with litmus, phenolphthalein and methyl orange. Give the colours. (3)

Litmus: red in the acid, blue in the alkali (1). Phenolphthalein: colourless in the acid, pink in the alkali (1). Methyl orange: red in the acid, yellow in the alkali (1).
:::

::: example pH and neutralisation
Universal indicator is added to dilute nitric acid, and sodium hydroxide solution is slowly added until it is in excess. Describe the colour changes and write the ionic equation for the reaction. (3)

The indicator is red in the acid (pH 1–2); as alkali is added it turns orange, then yellow, then green at pH 7 when the acid is just neutralised (1); with excess sodium hydroxide it turns blue then purple (pH 12–14) (1). H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O (1).
:::

::: example Base or alkali
Copper(II) oxide and sodium hydroxide are both bases. Explain why only sodium hydroxide is an alkali. (2)

An alkali is a base that is soluble in water (1). Sodium hydroxide dissolves and releases hydroxide ions, but copper(II) oxide is insoluble, so it cannot form hydroxide ions in solution (1).
:::

::: example Describe — a titration
Describe how to find the exact volume of hydrochloric acid needed to neutralise 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution. (5)

Use a pipette to measure 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide into a conical flask (1), and add a few drops of indicator such as methyl orange (1).

Fill a burette with the acid and record the starting reading (1).

Add the acid to the flask while swirling, drop by drop near the end point, until the indicator just changes colour, and record the final reading (1).

Repeat until two titres agree within 0.10 cm³ and calculate the mean (1).
:::

::: example Explain — proton donor and acceptor
In the reaction HCl + NH₃ → NH₄Cl, identify the acid and the base and explain why. (2)

HCl is the acid because it donates a proton (H⁺) (1); NH₃ is the base because it accepts the proton, forming the ammonium ion NH₄⁺ (1).
:::

::: watch
- Acid = **H⁺ (proton donor)**; alkali = **OH⁻ in solution**; base = **proton acceptor**. All alkalis are bases; not all bases are alkalis (solubility).
- Litmus **red in acid**, blue in alkali. Phenolphthalein **colourless → pink**. Methyl orange **red → yellow**.
- pH 7 is **neutral**; **low pH = high H⁺ concentration**.
- Neutralisation is **H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O** — the spectator ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻) are left out.
- Universal indicator gives a **pH value**; litmus only says acid or alkali.
:::

::: test
In a titration, 22.50 cm³ of 0.10 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid neutralises 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution. Calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide. (3)
---
Moles of HCl = 0.10 × 22.50 ÷ 1000 = 0.00225 mol (1). NaOH + HCl, ratio 1 : 1, so 0.00225 mol NaOH (1). Concentration = 0.00225 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.090 mol/dm³ (1).
:::

::: test
Explain, in terms of proton transfer, what happens when ammonia reacts with water to form an alkaline solution. (2)
---
Water donates a proton (H⁺) to ammonia, forming NH₄⁺ (1); water acts as an acid and ammonia as a base (proton acceptor), leaving OH⁻ ions that make the solution alkaline (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how to carry out an acid–alkali titration to find the exact volume of acid needed to neutralise 25.0 cm³ of an alkali. (6)
---
Any six: rinse and fill a burette with acid and record the initial reading; use a pipette (with filler) to measure 25.0 cm³ of alkali into a conical flask; add a few drops of indicator (e.g. methyl orange or phenolphthalein); add acid from the burette while swirling the flask; add dropwise near the end point; stop when the indicator just changes colour (permanently); record the final reading; titre = final − initial; repeat until concordant results (within 0.10 cm³) and take the mean.
Marking: 1 mark for each correct point, up to 6; for full marks the answer must be in a logical order.
:::
