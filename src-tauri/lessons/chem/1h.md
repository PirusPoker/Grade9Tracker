## What it is

Atoms bond to get a **full outer shell** (the stable arrangement of a noble gas). **Ionic bonding** happens between a **metal and a non-metal**: the metal atom **loses** its outer electrons and the non-metal atom **gains** them, forming charged particles — **ions**.

- A **metal** atom loses electrons → **positive ion (cation)**: Na (2.8.1) → Na⁺ (2.8) + e⁻; Mg (2.8.2) → Mg²⁺ (2.8) + 2e⁻; Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻.
- A **non-metal** atom gains electrons → **negative ion (anion)**: Cl (2.8.7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2.8.8); O (2.6) + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ (2.8).
- The charge equals the number of electrons lost or gained; the ion has the electronic configuration of a noble gas. Group 1 → +1, Group 2 → +2, Group 3 → +3, Group 5 → −3, Group 6 → −2, Group 7 → −1.

For magnesium oxide, Mg transfers two electrons to one O: [Mg]²⁺ [O]²⁻. For calcium chloride, one Ca gives one electron to each of two Cl atoms: [Ca]²⁺ 2[Cl]⁻. For sodium oxide, two Na each give one electron to one O: 2[Na]⁺ [O]²⁻.

**The ionic bond** is the **strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions**. It is not a "shared" or "transferred" bond — the transfer creates the ions; the attraction *is* the bond. The ions pack into a **giant ionic lattice** — a regular 3-D arrangement in which each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge (in NaCl, each Na⁺ by six Cl⁻ and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺), held by attractions acting in all directions.

**Properties of ionic compounds, explained by the lattice:**

- **High melting and boiling points** — a lot of energy is needed to overcome the **many strong electrostatic attractions** between the ions throughout the lattice.
- **Do not conduct electricity when solid** — the ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move.
- **Conduct when molten or dissolved in water** — the lattice breaks down and the **ions are free to move** and carry charge. (It is the *ions* that move, never "the electrons".)
- Usually **soluble in water**; **hard but brittle** — shifting a layer brings like charges together, which repel and split the crystal.

::: method
1. **Ion formation**: write the configuration before and after, and the number of electrons lost/gained — Group number tells you the charge.
2. **Dot-and-cross**: outer shells only; metal dots, non-metal crosses; arrows; **square brackets and charges** on the ions; the right *number* of each ion (CaCl₂ needs two chloride ions).
3. **Define the bond as electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions** — always those words.
4. **Property explanations must reach the structure**: "many strong electrostatic attractions between ions need a lot of energy to overcome" for melting point; "ions free to move" for conductivity.
5. **Ionic ⇔ metal + non-metal**; and formulae of ionic compounds are empirical (ratios in a lattice).
:::

::: example Ion formation
Explain, using electronic configurations, how magnesium and oxygen atoms form ions when they react. (3)

Magnesium (2.8.2) loses its two outer electrons to form Mg²⁺ (2.8) (1). Oxygen (2.6) gains those two electrons to form O²⁻ (2.8) (1). Both ions now have full outer shells, like the noble gas neon (1).
:::

::: example Dot-and-cross
Describe the dot-and-cross diagram for calcium chloride, CaCl₂. (3)

A calcium atom with two outer electrons (dots) transfers one electron to each of two chlorine atoms, each with seven outer electrons (crosses) (1). The products are one [Ca]²⁺ ion with an empty outer shell drawn (or 2.8.8) (1) and two [Cl]⁻ ions, each with eight outer electrons — seven crosses and one dot (1).
:::

::: example Melting point
Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point. (3)

Sodium chloride is a giant ionic lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions (1) held together by strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions, acting in all directions (1). A large amount of energy is needed to overcome these many strong attractions, so the melting point is high (1).
:::

::: example Conductivity
Explain why solid magnesium oxide does not conduct electricity but molten magnesium oxide does. (3)

Electrical conduction needs charged particles that are free to move (1). In the solid the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice, so they cannot move (1). When molten the lattice has broken down and the Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions are free to move and carry charge (1).
:::

::: q
What is an ion? (1)
---
An atom (or group of atoms) that has lost or gained electrons and so carries a charge.
:::

::: q
Give the charge on the ions formed by elements in Groups 1, 2, 6 and 7. (2)
---
+1, +2 (1); −2, −1 (1).
:::

::: q
Write the electronic configuration of an aluminium atom and an aluminium ion. (2)
---
Al: 2.8.3 (1); Al³⁺: 2.8 (1).
:::

::: q
Define ionic bonding. (1)
---
The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
:::

::: q
Describe the structure of solid sodium chloride. (2)
---
A giant ionic lattice — a regular repeating 3-D arrangement of ions (1) in which each Na⁺ is surrounded by Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by Na⁺ ions (1).
:::

::: q
Why do ionic compounds conduct electricity when dissolved in water? (2)
---
Dissolving separates the ions from the lattice (1), so they are free to move and carry the charge (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why ionic compounds are brittle. (2)
---
When a layer of ions is pushed, ions of the same charge line up next to each other (1); they repel, splitting the crystal (1).
:::

::: q
In a dot-and-cross diagram of sodium oxide, how many sodium ions and how many oxide ions are shown, and why? (2)
---
Two Na⁺ and one O²⁻ (1); oxygen needs two electrons and each sodium supplies one (1).
:::

::: q
Why is the formula of magnesium chloride MgCl₂ and not MgCl? (2)
---
Magnesium loses two electrons to form Mg²⁺ (1); each chlorine gains only one, so two Cl⁻ ions are needed to balance the charge (1).
:::

::: watch
- Conduction when molten/dissolved is because **ions are free to move** — "electrons move" is wrong and costs the mark.
- The bond is **electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions**, throughout a **giant lattice**.
- Melting point: **many strong attractions, lots of energy to overcome** — three ideas.
- Dot-and-cross: **brackets, charges, and the correct number of each ion**.
- Ionic = **metal + non-metal**; electrons are **transferred**, not shared.
:::
