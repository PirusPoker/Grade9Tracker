## What it is

- **Empirical formula** — the **simplest whole-number ratio** of atoms of each element in a compound.
- **Molecular formula** — the **actual number** of atoms of each element in one molecule.

Ethene's molecular formula is C₂H₄; its empirical formula is CH₂. Glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆, empirical CH₂O. For water, both are H₂O. For ionic compounds the formula (NaCl, MgO) is always the empirical formula, since there are no molecules.

**Calculating an empirical formula from masses or percentages:**

1. Write the **mass** (or **percentage** — treat 100 g of compound) of each element.
2. **Divide each by its Ar** → moles of atoms.
3. **Divide every result by the smallest** → the simplest ratio.
4. If a ratio is not near a whole number (e.g. 1.5, 1.33, 1.25), **multiply all the numbers** by 2, 3 or 4 to make them whole.
5. Write the formula.

A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen:

| | C | H | O |
|---|---|---|---|
| mass | 40.0 | 6.7 | 53.3 |
| ÷ Ar | 40 ÷ 12 = 3.33 | 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7 | 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33 |
| ÷ smallest | 1 | 2 | 1 |

Empirical formula **CH₂O**.

**Molecular formula from the empirical formula**: find the empirical formula mass, divide the given Mr by it to get a whole number *n*, and multiply every subscript by *n*. CH₂O has mass $12 + 2 + 16 = 30$; if Mr = 180, $n = 180 \div 30 = 6$ → **C₆H₁₂O₆**.

**Required practical — finding the formula of a metal oxide:**

- **Magnesium oxide (by oxidation)**: weigh a crucible and lid, add a coil of clean magnesium ribbon, weigh again. Heat strongly, lifting the lid briefly now and then to let in oxygen without letting the white smoke escape. Heat until no further change, cool, reweigh. Mass of Mg = second − first weighing; mass of O = final − second. Convert both to moles and find the ratio (1:1 → MgO). Errors: MgO lost as smoke; magnesium not fully reacted; some magnesium nitride formed — all make the oxygen mass too low.
- **Copper oxide (by reduction)**: weigh a known mass of copper(II) oxide in a tube; pass hydrogen (or methane) over it while heating; the oxide is reduced to copper (black → pink-brown); keep the gas flowing while it cools to stop re-oxidation; reweigh. Mass of oxygen lost = mass of oxide − mass of copper. Moles of Cu and O give the ratio (1:1 → CuO).

::: method
1. **The three divisions**: mass ÷ Ar, then ÷ smallest, then (if needed) × to whole numbers. Stopping after the first division is the classic error.
2. **Lay it out as a table** — element, mass, moles, ratio. Examiners award method marks from the table.
3. **Recognise the fractions**: 1.5 → ×2; 1.33 or 1.67 → ×3; 1.25 or 1.75 → ×4.
4. **Molecular formula**: empirical mass → Mr ÷ empirical mass = *n* → multiply.
5. **Experiment questions**: state what is weighed, why the lid is lifted (oxygen in, smoke kept) or why gas keeps flowing while cooling (prevent re-oxidation), and the effect of each error on the ratio.
:::

::: example From masses
2.4 g of magnesium burns to form 4.0 g of magnesium oxide. Calculate the empirical formula. (Ar: Mg 24, O 16) (3)

Mass of oxygen $= 4.0 - 2.4 = 1.6$ g (1). Moles: Mg $= 2.4 \div 24 = 0.1$; O $= 1.6 \div 16 = 0.1$ (1). Ratio 1 : 1, so MgO (1).
:::

::: example From percentages with a fraction
An oxide of iron contains 70% iron and 30% oxygen by mass. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: Fe 56, O 16) (3)

Moles: Fe $= 70 \div 56 = 1.25$; O $= 30 \div 16 = 1.875$ (1). Divide by 1.25: Fe 1, O 1.5 (1). Multiply by 2: Fe₂O₃ (1).
:::

::: example Molecular formula
A hydrocarbon has the empirical formula CH₂ and a relative molecular mass of 56. Find its molecular formula. (2)

Empirical mass $= 12 + 2 = 14$; $56 \div 14 = 4$ (1). Molecular formula C₄H₈ (1).
:::

::: example Experiment
Describe how you would find the formula of magnesium oxide by experiment, and explain one source of error. (4)

Weigh a crucible with its lid, add a coil of magnesium ribbon and reweigh; heat strongly, lifting the lid briefly at intervals to let oxygen in (1). When the glowing stops, cool and reweigh; the gain in mass is the mass of oxygen combined (1). Divide the mass of magnesium by 24 and the mass of oxygen by 16, and find the simplest ratio of the moles — 1 : 1 gives MgO (1). Some magnesium oxide escapes as white smoke when the lid is lifted, so the mass of oxygen measured is too low and the ratio of O to Mg comes out below 1 (1).
:::

::: q
Define empirical formula. (1)
---
The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
:::

::: q
Give the empirical formula of (a) C₄H₁₀ (b) C₆H₆ (c) N₂O₄. (3)
---
(a) C₂H₅; (b) CH; (c) NO₂.
:::

::: q
A compound contains 3.2 g of copper and 0.8 g of oxygen. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: Cu 64, O 16) (2)
---
Cu $= 3.2 \div 64 = 0.05$; O $= 0.8 \div 16 = 0.05$; ratio 1 : 1 → CuO.
:::

::: q
A compound is 75% carbon and 25% hydrogen by mass. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: C 12, H 1) (2)
---
C $= 75 \div 12 = 6.25$; H $= 25$; ÷ 6.25 → 1 : 4 → CH₄.
:::

::: q
A compound contains 5.6 g of iron and 2.4 g of oxygen. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: Fe 56, O 16) (3)
---
Fe $= 0.1$; O $= 0.15$; ÷ 0.1 → 1 : 1.5 → ×2 → Fe₂O₃.
:::

::: q
The empirical formula of a compound is NO₂ and its Mr is 92. Find the molecular formula. (2)
---
Empirical mass 46; $92 \div 46 = 2$; N₂O₄.
:::

::: q
In the copper oxide experiment, why is the gas kept flowing while the tube cools? (1)
---
To stop the hot copper reacting with oxygen in the air and forming copper oxide again, which would make the mass of copper too high.
:::

::: q
Why do ionic compounds only have an empirical formula? (1)
---
They form giant lattices, not molecules, so the formula can only show the ratio of ions.
:::

::: q
Hydrogen peroxide has the molecular formula H₂O₂. State its empirical formula and explain why the two are different. (2)
---
HO (1); the molecule contains twice the simplest ratio of atoms (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Three steps**: ÷ Ar → ÷ smallest → × to whole numbers. Most lost marks come from stopping at step one.
- **1.5 means ×2, 1.33 means ×3** — do not round 1.5 to 2.
- Percentages: treat as **grams in 100 g**.
- Molecular formula = empirical × **n**, where n = Mr ÷ empirical mass.
- Experiments: **lid lifted for oxygen, smoke kept in**; **gas flowing while cooling** — and the direction of each error.
:::

A hydrocarbon contains 4.8 g of carbon and 1.0 g of hydrogen. Find its empirical formula. (3)
---
C: 4.8 ÷ 12 = 0.4; H: 1.0 ÷ 1 = 1.0 (1); ratio 0.4 : 1.0 = 1 : 2.5 = 2 : 5 (1); C₂H₅ (1).
:::

The empirical formula of a compound is CH₂ and its Mr is 56. Find its molecular formula. (2)
---
CH₂ has mass 14; 56 ÷ 14 = 4 (1); C₄H₈ (1).
:::

::: test
A compound contains 40% sulfur and 60% oxygen by mass. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: S 32, O 16) (3)
---
S: 40 ÷ 32 = 1.25; O: 60 ÷ 16 = 3.75 (1); ratio 1 : 3 (1); SO₃ (1).
:::

::: test
Describe how you would find the empirical formula of magnesium oxide by experiment. (4)
---
Weigh a crucible with lid, then with a coil of clean magnesium ribbon (1); heat strongly, lifting the lid occasionally to let air in but not smoke out, until no further change (1); reweigh; mass of oxygen = increase in mass (1); convert masses of Mg and O to moles and find the simplest ratio (1).
:::

::: test
In the magnesium oxide experiment, a student's result gives a formula of MgO₀.₈ instead of MgO. Give one reason. (1)
---
Some magnesium oxide escaped as smoke, or not all the magnesium reacted, so too little oxygen was recorded.
:::

::: test
Glucose has the molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆. Give its empirical formula and explain the difference between the two formulae. (2)
---
CH₂O (1); the molecular formula gives the actual number of each atom in a molecule, the empirical formula the simplest whole-number ratio (1).
:::

::: test
Hydrated copper sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, loses 3.6 g of water when 10.0 g is heated to leave 6.4 g of anhydrous salt. Find x. (Mr: CuSO₄ 160, H₂O 18) (3)
---
CuSO₄: 6.4 ÷ 160 = 0.04 mol; H₂O: 3.6 ÷ 18 = 0.2 mol (2); ratio 1 : 5, so x = 5 (1).
:::

::: test
A compound contains 2.4 g of carbon, 0.4 g of hydrogen and 3.2 g of oxygen; its Mr is 60. Find its molecular formula. (3)
---
Moles: C 0.2, H 0.4, O 0.2 → ratio 1 : 2 : 1, empirical formula CH₂O (2); CH₂O mass 30, so molecular formula C₂H₄O₂ (1).
:::
