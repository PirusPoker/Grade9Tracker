## What it is

Atoms are too small to count, so chemists count them in **moles**. **One mole** of any substance contains **6.02 × 10²³** particles (the **Avogadro constant**) and has a mass in grams equal to its **relative formula mass**. So the mole is the unit for **amount of substance**, and it links the balanced equation (which counts particles) to masses you can weigh.

$$\text{moles} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{M_r} \qquad \text{mass} = \text{moles} \times M_r$$

12 g of carbon (Ar 12) is 1 mol; 9 g of water (Mr 18) is 0.5 mol; 2 mol of CO₂ (Mr 44) has mass 88 g.

Two other conversions that appear: for **gases at room temperature and pressure (rtp)**, 1 mol occupies **24 dm³** (24,000 cm³), so moles = volume ÷ 24; for **solutions**, moles = concentration (mol/dm³) × volume (dm³).

**Reacting masses** — the coefficients in a balanced equation give the **ratio of moles**, not the ratio of masses:

1. Write the balanced equation.
2. Convert the mass you are given into **moles** (÷ Mr).
3. Use the **ratio** from the equation to find the moles of the substance asked for.
4. Convert back to **mass** (× Mr).

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. What mass of magnesium oxide forms from 12 g of magnesium? Moles Mg $= 12 \div 24 = 0.5$; ratio Mg : MgO = 2 : 2, so 0.5 mol MgO; mass $= 0.5 \times 40 = 20$ g.

**Limiting reactant** — when two reactants are given, one runs out first and stops the reaction; the other is **in excess**. Convert both to moles, compare with the equation ratio, and the one that would need more than is available is limiting. All product calculations are then based on the **limiting** reactant.

**Percentage yield** — the **theoretical yield** is the mass the equation predicts if everything reacted perfectly; the **actual yield** is what you actually obtain, always less:

$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$$

Why yield is less than 100%: the reaction may be **reversible** and not go to completion; **side reactions** make other products; product is **lost** when transferring, filtering, evaporating or purifying; reactants may be impure.

**Required practical — the reacting-mass experiment**: heating a weighed piece of magnesium in a crucible (lid lifted occasionally to let oxygen in but keep the smoke — MgO — from escaping), reweighing after it has stopped glowing, and using the masses of Mg and O to confirm the formula MgO (see 1g). Errors: MgO escaping as smoke (mass too low), incomplete reaction, magnesium nitride forming.

::: method
1. **Always convert to moles first.** The ratio in the equation applies to moles; applying it to grams is the standard wrong answer.
2. **Set the calculation out in four lines**: moles given; ratio; moles wanted; mass wanted. Show Mr values used.
3. **Limiting reactant**: moles of each → "*x* mol A needs *y* mol B; only *z* available" → identify → use it for the yield.
4. **Percentage yield**: theoretical from the reacting-mass calculation, then actual ÷ theoretical × 100. If yield exceeds 100% you have swapped them.
5. **Gases**: moles = volume ÷ 24 dm³ at rtp; **solutions**: moles = conc × volume in dm³ (÷ 1000 from cm³).
6. **Reasons for low yield**: reversible, side reactions, losses in transfer/purification — give two specific ones.
:::

::: example Moles and mass
(a) How many moles are in 22 g of carbon dioxide? (b) What is the mass of 0.25 mol of calcium carbonate? (Ar: C 12, O 16, Ca 40) (2)

(a) Mr(CO₂) = 44; $22 \div 44 = 0.5$ mol (1). (b) Mr(CaCO₃) = 100; $0.25 \times 100 = 25$ g (1).
:::

::: example Reacting masses
Calcium carbonate decomposes: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Calculate the mass of calcium oxide produced from 50 g of calcium carbonate. (Ar: Ca 40, C 12, O 16) (3)

Moles CaCO₃ $= 50 \div 100 = 0.5$ mol (1). Ratio 1 : 1, so 0.5 mol CaO (1). Mass $= 0.5 \times 56 = 28$ g (1).
:::

::: example Limiting reactant
4.8 g of magnesium is added to a solution containing 0.3 mol of hydrochloric acid: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Identify the limiting reactant and calculate the volume of hydrogen produced at rtp. (4)

Moles Mg $= 4.8 \div 24 = 0.2$ mol (1). 0.2 mol Mg would need 0.4 mol HCl but only 0.3 mol is present, so HCl is limiting and Mg is in excess (1). 2 mol HCl gives 1 mol H₂, so 0.3 mol HCl gives 0.15 mol H₂ (1). Volume $= 0.15 \times 24 = 3.6$ dm³ (1).
:::

::: example Percentage yield
A student expected to make 12.0 g of copper sulfate crystals but obtained 8.4 g. Calculate the percentage yield and give two reasons it is below 100%. (4)

$\dfrac{8.4}{12.0} \times 100 = 70\%$ (2). Some product was lost on the filter paper or when transferring between containers (1); some solution was left behind or crystals were lost during evaporation and drying (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate the number of moles in 4 g of sodium hydroxide, NaOH. (Ar: Na 23, O 16, H 1) (2)
---
Mr = 40; $4 \div 40 = 0.1$ mol.
:::

::: q
Calculate the mass of 3 mol of water. (1)
---
$3 \times 18 = 54$ g.
:::

::: q
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. What mass of water forms from 8 g of hydrogen? (Ar: H 1, O 16) (3)
---
Moles H₂ $= 8 \div 2 = 4$ mol (1); ratio 2 : 2, so 4 mol H₂O (1); mass $= 4 \times 18 = 72$ g (1).
:::

::: q
Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. What mass of iron is produced from 160 g of iron(III) oxide? (Ar: Fe 56, O 16) (3)
---
Mr(Fe₂O₃) = 160; 1 mol (1); ratio 1 : 2, so 2 mol Fe (1); $2 \times 56 = 112$ g (1).
:::

::: q
What volume does 0.5 mol of any gas occupy at rtp? (1)
---
$0.5 \times 24 = 12$ dm³.
:::

::: q
A reaction has a theoretical yield of 25 g and an actual yield of 20 g. Calculate the percentage yield. (1)
---
$20 \div 25 \times 100 = 80\%$.
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why the actual yield of a reaction is less than the theoretical yield. (2)
---
The reaction may be reversible and not go to completion; product is lost during filtering, transferring or purifying (or side reactions occur).
:::

::: q
0.4 mol of zinc is added to 0.5 mol of hydrochloric acid: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. Which reactant is limiting? (2)
---
0.4 mol Zn needs 0.8 mol HCl; only 0.5 mol is present (1), so HCl is limiting (1).
:::

::: q
Why must you convert masses to moles before using the ratio in an equation? (1)
---
The coefficients give the ratio of particles (moles), not of masses — different substances have different masses per mole.
:::

::: q
How many moles are in 250 cm³ of 0.2 mol/dm³ sodium chloride solution? (2)
---
$0.2 \times 0.25 = 0.05$ mol.
:::

::: watch
- **Moles first, always.** Mass → ÷ Mr → moles → ratio → moles → × Mr → mass.
- The equation ratio is a **mole** ratio — never apply it to grams.
- **Limiting reactant** decides the yield; identify it by comparing available moles with the ratio.
- **% yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100** — never over 100%.
- Gas at rtp: **24 dm³ per mol**; solutions: **conc × volume in dm³**.
:::
