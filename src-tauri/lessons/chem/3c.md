## What it is

In a **reversible reaction** the products can react to re-form the reactants, so the reaction can go in **both directions**. It is shown with the symbol **⇌**. Classic examples:

- **Hydrated copper(II) sulfate**: CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue) ⇌ CuSO₄ (white) + 5H₂O. Heating drives off the water (forward reaction, **endothermic**); adding water to the white anhydrous solid turns it blue again and gets hot (backward reaction, **exothermic**). If the forward reaction is endothermic, the backward reaction is exothermic by exactly the same amount.
- **Ammonium chloride**: NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g). Heating the white solid makes it decompose into two gases (thermal dissociation); as they cool higher up the tube they recombine to form a white solid again.
- The **Haber process**: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ/mol; the **Contact process**: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃.

**Dynamic equilibrium.** If a reversible reaction takes place in a **closed system** (nothing can enter or leave), it reaches equilibrium: the **forward and backward reactions occur at the same rate**, so the **concentrations of reactants and products stay constant** — not equal, just unchanging. "Dynamic" means both reactions are still happening; it just looks as though nothing is changing. At the start the forward rate is high and the backward rate zero; as products build up the backward rate rises and the forward rate falls until they meet.

**The position of equilibrium** describes how far the reaction has gone: "to the right" (lots of product) or "to the left" (mostly reactants). Changing the conditions shifts it — the system responds to **oppose the change** (Le Chatelier's principle):

| Change | Shift | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Increase temperature** | towards the **endothermic** direction | the system absorbs the extra heat; for an exothermic forward reaction (Haber), heating **reduces the yield** |
| **Decrease temperature** | towards the **exothermic** direction | releases heat to replace what was removed |
| **Increase pressure** (gases only) | towards the side with **fewer moles of gas** | fewer molecules reduce the pressure; Haber: 4 mol → 2 mol, so high pressure **increases the yield** |
| **Decrease pressure** | towards the side with **more moles of gas** | — |
| **Increase concentration** of a reactant (or remove a product) | to the **right** | uses up the added substance / replaces the removed one |
| **Add a catalyst** | **no shift** | speeds up forward and backward reactions **equally**; equilibrium is reached **faster** with the **same** yield |

Pressure has no effect if there are **equal numbers of gas moles** on both sides (H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI).

**Compromise conditions** — the Haber process uses **450 °C, 200 atm and an iron catalyst**. A low temperature would give a higher yield (exothermic forward reaction) but too slow a rate, so a moderate temperature is a compromise between yield and rate. High pressure raises both yield and rate, but very high pressures need expensive, thick-walled equipment and more energy, so 200 atm is a compromise between yield and cost. Unreacted gases are recycled.

::: method
1. **Definitions**: reversible (can go both ways, ⇌); dynamic equilibrium (closed system, equal rates, constant concentrations). Say all three parts.
2. **Temperature questions**: decide which direction is endothermic — it is the *opposite* sign to the ΔH given. Heat → endothermic direction.
3. **Pressure questions**: count **gas moles** on each side; raising pressure favours the side with fewer.
4. **Catalyst**: no change in position or yield — just reached sooner. Say "same yield, faster".
5. **Industrial conditions = compromise**: state what would give the best yield, why it is not used (rate or cost), and the actual compromise.
:::

::: example Copper sulfate
Blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate is heated until it turns white; water is then added. Describe what happens and explain why this shows the reaction is reversible. (3)

On heating, the blue crystals turn white as water is driven off, forming anhydrous copper(II) sulfate (endothermic) (1). Adding water turns the solid blue again and the mixture becomes hot — the reaction is exothermic in the backward direction (1). The products can be turned back into the reactants, so the reaction is reversible: CuSO₄·5H₂O ⇌ CuSO₄ + 5H₂O (1).
:::

::: example Dynamic equilibrium
Explain what is meant by dynamic equilibrium. (3)

In a closed system a reversible reaction reaches a point where the forward and backward reactions take place at the same rate (1). The concentrations of reactants and products therefore remain constant (1), even though both reactions are still occurring — hence "dynamic" (1).
:::

::: example Haber conditions
N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, ΔH = −92 kJ/mol. Explain the effect on the yield of ammonia of (a) increasing the pressure, (b) increasing the temperature, and (c) why a temperature of 450 °C is used. (5)

(a) There are 4 moles of gas on the left and 2 on the right; increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium to the side with fewer gas molecules, so the yield increases (2). (b) The forward reaction is exothermic, so raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic (backward) direction and the yield falls (1). (c) A low temperature would give a high yield but the rate would be too slow (1); 450 °C is a compromise between a reasonable yield and an acceptable rate (1).
:::

::: example Catalyst
State and explain the effect of an iron catalyst on the yield of ammonia. (2)

No effect on the yield or the position of equilibrium (1), because the catalyst speeds up the forward and backward reactions equally — equilibrium is simply reached more quickly (1).
:::

::: q
What does the symbol ⇌ mean? (1)
---
The reaction is reversible — it can proceed in both directions.
:::

::: q
Heating ammonium chloride produces two gases that recombine on cooling. Write the equation and name the two gases. (2)
---
NH₄Cl ⇌ NH₃ + HCl (1); ammonia and hydrogen chloride (1).
:::

::: q
The forward reaction of a reversible change is exothermic. What can you say about the backward reaction? (1)
---
It is endothermic, taking in the same amount of energy that the forward reaction releases.
:::

::: q
Why must a system be closed for equilibrium to be reached? (1)
---
So that no reactants or products can escape — otherwise the backward reaction could not build up to match the forward rate.
:::

::: q
At equilibrium, are the concentrations of reactants and products equal? (1)
---
Not necessarily — they are constant, but the position of equilibrium may favour one side.
:::

::: q
2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g) is exothermic. What happens to the yield of SO₃ if the temperature is lowered? (2)
---
The yield increases (1) because lowering the temperature shifts the equilibrium in the exothermic (forward) direction (1).
:::

::: q
What effect does increasing the pressure have on the equilibrium H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g)? (1)
---
None — there are equal numbers of gas moles (2) on each side.
:::

::: q
In the Haber process, what happens to the equilibrium if ammonia is removed as it forms? (1)
---
The equilibrium shifts to the right to replace the ammonia, increasing the amount produced.
:::

::: q
Give the conditions used in the Haber process. (3)
---
About 450 °C; about 200 atmospheres; iron catalyst.
:::

::: q
Why is a very high pressure not used in the Haber process even though it would improve the yield? (1)
---
Very high pressures need expensive, strong equipment and a lot of energy to maintain, so the extra cost outweighs the benefit.
:::

::: watch
- Equilibrium means **equal rates**, not equal amounts.
- Heating shifts towards the **endothermic** direction — check which direction that is from the sign of ΔH.
- Pressure: count **moles of gas** only; solids and liquids do not count.
- **Catalysts do not shift equilibrium** — same yield, reached faster.
- Industrial conditions are a **compromise**: temperature trades yield against rate; pressure trades yield against cost.
:::
