## What it is

The **reactivity series** ranks metals by how readily they **lose electrons to form positive ions** — the more easily, the more reactive. Learn it with hydrogen and carbon included as reference points:

**potassium > sodium > lithium > calcium > magnesium > aluminium > (carbon) > zinc > iron > (hydrogen) > copper > silver > gold**

Reactions used to build the series:

| Reaction | Very reactive (K, Na, Li, Ca) | Moderately reactive (Mg, Al, Zn, Fe) | Unreactive (Cu, Ag, Au) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **With cold water** | fizz, form **hydroxide + hydrogen** (Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂) | very slow or none with cold water; Mg burns in **steam** to form the **oxide + hydrogen**: Mg + H₂O(g) → MgO + H₂ | no reaction |
| **With dilute acid** | too violent to try safely | fizz, form **salt + hydrogen**: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ — fastest for Mg, slowest for Fe | **no reaction** (below hydrogen — cannot displace it) |

Metals **above hydrogen** in the series react with acids to release hydrogen; those below do not. Aluminium reacts more slowly than its position suggests because of a protective oxide layer.

**Displacement reactions** — a **more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt** (or from its oxide when heated):

- Zinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper: **Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu** — the blue solution fades, a pink-brown solid forms, the zinc dissolves. Ionic: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu; zinc is oxidised (loses electrons), copper ions are reduced.
- Copper + zinc sulfate → **no reaction** (copper is less reactive).
- Iron + copper sulfate → iron sulfate + copper (an iron nail becomes coated with copper).
- Magnesium + copper oxide (heated) → magnesium oxide + copper; the thermite reaction: 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe.

To place an unknown metal, add it to solutions of the salts of known metals: it displaces those below it and not those above. Oxidation/reduction in terms of oxygen: the metal that gains oxygen is oxidised (Mg + CuO → MgO + Cu: magnesium oxidised, copper oxide reduced); the metal ion that gains electrons is reduced. A **reducing agent** removes oxygen or donates electrons (the reactive metal); an **oxidising agent** supplies oxygen or accepts electrons.

**Rusting** is the corrosion of **iron** (and steel): iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust), 4Fe + 3O₂ + 2H₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·H₂O. It needs **both water and oxygen** — the classic experiment: nail in boiled water under oil (no oxygen) → no rust; nail in dry air with calcium chloride (no water) → no rust; nail in tap water open to air → rusts. Salt and acid speed it up. Rust is flaky and falls off, exposing fresh iron, so rusting continues right through.

**Preventing rust:**

- **Barrier methods** — keep out water and oxygen: **painting**, **oiling/greasing** (moving parts), **coating with plastic**, **plating with tin** (food cans) or **chromium**. Fail if scratched.
- **Galvanising** — coating with **zinc**. Acts as a barrier, *and* zinc is more reactive than iron, so even if scratched the **zinc corrodes instead** of the iron.
- **Sacrificial protection** — attaching blocks of a **more reactive metal** (zinc or magnesium) to the iron (ships' hulls, pipelines): the more reactive metal **loses electrons in preference to** the iron and corrodes sacrificially; the blocks are replaced periodically.
- **Alloying** — stainless steel (iron with chromium and nickel) does not rust.

::: method
1. **Series order** — learn it; place hydrogen and carbon.
2. **Predicting reactions**: with water, acid or a salt solution, ask "is this metal above the other element in the series?" Above → reacts/displaces; below → no reaction. Say so, with the reason.
3. **Displacement observations**: colour change of the solution (blue fades), solid deposited (pink-brown copper), metal dissolving; then the ionic equation and which species is oxidised/reduced.
4. **Rusting needs water AND oxygen** — name both, and be able to describe the three-tube experiment with its controls.
5. **Rust prevention**: name the method, then *how* it works — barrier (keeps out water/oxygen) or sacrificial (more reactive metal loses electrons instead).
:::

::: example Placing a metal
Metal X reacts slowly with dilute acid, displaces copper from copper sulfate solution but does not displace zinc from zinc sulfate. Place X in the reactivity series. (2)

X is above hydrogen and above copper (it reacts with acid and displaces copper) (1) but below zinc (it cannot displace zinc), so X lies between zinc and hydrogen — around iron (1).
:::

::: example Displacement
A strip of zinc is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution. Describe what is seen, write the ionic equation and identify what is oxidised. (4)

The blue solution turns paler/colourless and a pink-brown solid (copper) forms on the zinc, which slowly dissolves (1). Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu (1). Zinc atoms lose electrons to form Zn²⁺, so zinc is oxidised (1); Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons and are reduced (1).
:::

::: example Rusting experiment
Describe an experiment to show that both water and oxygen are needed for iron to rust. (4)

Tube 1: iron nail in tap water open to the air (water and oxygen present) (1). Tube 2: nail in boiled water with a layer of oil on top — boiling removed the dissolved oxygen and the oil keeps air out (water, no oxygen) (1). Tube 3: nail in a dry tube with anhydrous calcium chloride to absorb moisture, stoppered (oxygen, no water) (1). After a few days only the nail in tube 1 rusts, showing both water and oxygen are needed (1).
:::

::: example Sacrificial protection
Explain how attaching blocks of zinc to a steel ship's hull prevents rusting. (3)

Zinc is more reactive than iron (1), so it loses electrons more readily and corrodes in preference to the iron — it is oxidised instead (1). The iron stays protected as long as zinc remains; the blocks are sacrificed and replaced periodically (1).
:::

::: q
Write the reactivity series from potassium to gold, including hydrogen and carbon. (2)
---
K, Na, Li, Ca, Mg, Al, (C), Zn, Fe, (H), Cu, Ag, Au.
:::

::: q
Write the equation for calcium reacting with water. (1)
---
Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂.
:::

::: q
Why does copper not react with dilute hydrochloric acid? (1)
---
Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it cannot displace hydrogen from the acid.
:::

::: q
Write the equation for magnesium reacting with steam. (1)
---
Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂.
:::

::: q
Will iron displace magnesium from magnesium sulfate solution? Explain. (1)
---
No — iron is less reactive than magnesium.
:::

::: q
Name the two substances needed for iron to rust and the chemical name of rust. (2)
---
Oxygen and water (1); hydrated iron(III) oxide (1).
:::

::: q
Why does painting prevent rust, and what is its weakness? (2)
---
The paint is a barrier that keeps water and oxygen away from the iron (1); if scratched, the exposed iron rusts (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why galvanised iron stays protected even when the zinc coating is scratched. (2)
---
Zinc is more reactive than iron (1), so it corrodes in preference to the exposed iron — sacrificial protection (1).
:::

::: q
In the reaction Mg + CuO → MgO + Cu, which substance is reduced, and what is the reducing agent? (2)
---
Copper oxide is reduced (loses oxygen) (1); magnesium is the reducing agent (1).
:::

::: q
Why is aluminium less reactive in practice than its position in the series suggests? (1)
---
It is covered by a thin, unreactive layer of aluminium oxide that protects the metal beneath.
:::

::: watch
- Rusting needs **water and oxygen** — both, every time; the experiment has one control for each.
- **Sacrificial protection = more reactive metal loses electrons instead.** Galvanising is barrier *plus* sacrificial.
- Displacement: **more reactive displaces less reactive** — and no reaction if it is below.
- In displacement the metal is **oxidised** (loses electrons); the metal ion is **reduced**.
- Metals **below hydrogen** (Cu, Ag, Au) do not react with acids.
:::

::: test
Describe how you could use displacement reactions to place magnesium, copper and iron in order of reactivity. (3)
---
Add each metal to solutions of the other metals' salts (e.g. sulfates) (1); a reaction (colour change, solid deposited, temperature rise) shows the added metal is more reactive (1); Mg displaces both Fe and Cu, Fe displaces Cu only, Cu displaces neither — Mg > Fe > Cu (1).
:::

::: test
Explain the difference between how calcium and magnesium react with cold water. (2)
---
Calcium reacts steadily, fizzing to give hydrogen and calcium hydroxide (1); magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water (but rapidly with steam) (1).
:::

::: test
Describe an experiment to show that both air and water are needed for iron to rust. (3)
---
Three test tubes with iron nails: one in boiled water covered with oil (water, no air) (1); one in dry air with anhydrous calcium chloride (air, no water) (1); one in water open to the air — only this nail rusts (1).
:::

::: test
Explain how sacrificial protection stops a steel ship's hull rusting. (2)
---
Blocks of a more reactive metal such as zinc or magnesium are attached to the hull (1); the more reactive metal loses electrons/corrodes in preference to the iron (1).
:::

::: test
Write the word equation for rusting and give the chemical name of rust. (2)
---
Iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide (1); hydrated iron(III) oxide (1).
:::

::: test
Explain why gold does not need protecting from corrosion. (1)
---
It is very unreactive — at the bottom of the reactivity series — so it does not react with oxygen or water.
:::
