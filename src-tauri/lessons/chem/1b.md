## What it is

A **solution** is a mixture of a **solute** (the substance that dissolves, e.g. salt) in a **solvent** (the liquid it dissolves in, e.g. water). A solution is **saturated** when no more solute will dissolve at that temperature — undissolved solid remains.

**Solubility** is the **mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent** (usually water) **at a given temperature** to make a saturated solution. Units: **g per 100 g of water**. It must be stated with a temperature, because for most solids solubility **increases with temperature** (for gases it decreases).

$$\text{solubility} = \frac{\text{mass of solute dissolved}}{\text{mass of water}} \times 100$$

- **Point on the curve** = saturated solution. **Below** the curve = unsaturated (more will dissolve). **Above** = not possible — the excess would be undissolved solid.
- **Mass that crystallises on cooling** = (solubility at the higher temperature − solubility at the lower temperature), for 100 g of water — then **scale to the actual mass of water**. From the curve, potassium nitrate: 63 g at 40 °C, 32 g at 20 °C, so cooling a saturated solution made with 100 g of water from 40 °C to 20 °C deposits $63 - 32 = 31$ g of crystals; with 50 g of water, $31 \times \frac{50}{100} = 15.5$ g.
- Steep curve = solubility changes a lot with temperature (potassium nitrate); flat = little change (sodium chloride).

**Required practical — solubility at different temperatures**: put a known mass of water (e.g. 10 g) in a boiling tube; add the solid in small weighed amounts, warming and stirring, until no more dissolves (or dissolve a weighed excess, then cool the solution in a water bath and record the temperature at which crystals first appear). Repeat at several temperatures. Calculate solubility per 100 g by scaling. Errors: judging the exact point of saturation; heat loss; supercooling; weighing.

::: method
1. **Definition** must contain: mass of solute, 100 g of water (solvent), saturated, at a stated temperature.
2. **Scale to 100 g**: solubility = mass dissolved ÷ mass of water × 100. Then, going the other way, mass dissolved in *x* g water = solubility × *x* ÷ 100.
3. **Crystallisation**: read both solubilities off the curve, subtract, then scale to the actual mass of water. Show the reading, the subtraction and the scaling.
4. **Reading a curve**: draw the lines from the axes; give the value with units g/100 g.
5. **Practical**: what is measured (mass of water, mass of solute, temperature of saturation), how saturation is judged, and one source of error.
:::

::: example Calculate solubility
A saturated solution at 30 °C is made by dissolving 15 g of a salt in 40 g of water. Calculate the solubility of the salt at 30 °C. (2)

$\dfrac{15}{40} \times 100 = 37.5$ g per 100 g of water (2).
:::

::: example Read and scale
The solubility of copper sulfate is 40 g/100 g at 50 °C. What mass dissolves in 25 g of water at 50 °C? (2)

$40 \times \dfrac{25}{100} = 10$ g (2).
:::

::: example Crystallisation
A saturated solution of potassium nitrate is made in 200 g of water at 40 °C and cooled to 20 °C. Using the curve (63 g/100 g at 40 °C; 32 g/100 g at 20 °C), calculate the mass of crystals that form. (3)

Per 100 g of water: $63 - 32 = 31$ g crystallises (1). For 200 g of water: $31 \times \dfrac{200}{100} = 62$ g (2).
:::

::: example Practical
Describe how you would measure the solubility of potassium chloride at 40 °C. (4)

Weigh 10 g of water into a boiling tube in a water bath at 40 °C (1). Add potassium chloride in small weighed portions, stirring until each dissolves, until some solid remains undissolved — the solution is saturated (1). Record the total mass added that dissolved (1). Solubility = mass dissolved ÷ 10 × 100, giving g per 100 g of water at 40 °C; repeat and average (1).
:::

::: q
Define solubility. (2)
---
The mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent (water) to form a saturated solution (1) at a stated temperature (1).
:::

::: q
What is meant by a saturated solution? (1)
---
A solution in which no more solute can dissolve at that temperature.
:::

::: q
8 g of a salt saturates 20 g of water at 25 °C. Calculate its solubility. (2)
---
$\dfrac{8}{20} \times 100 = 40$ g/100 g.
:::

::: q
The solubility of a salt is 24 g/100 g at 60 °C. What mass saturates 150 g of water? (2)
---
$24 \times 1.5 = 36$ g.
:::

::: q
Why must a temperature be stated with a solubility value? (1)
---
Solubility changes with temperature — for most solids it increases as temperature rises.
:::

::: q
The solubility of a salt is 50 g/100 g at 70 °C and 20 g/100 g at 20 °C. A saturated solution in 100 g of water is cooled from 70 °C to 20 °C. What mass crystallises? (1)
---
$50 - 20 = 30$ g.
:::

::: q
Repeat the previous question for a solution made with 40 g of water. (2)
---
$30 \times \dfrac{40}{100} = 12$ g.
:::

::: q
On a solubility curve, what does a point below the curve represent? (1)
---
An unsaturated solution — more solute could dissolve at that temperature.
:::

::: q
How does the solubility of a gas in water change with temperature? (1)
---
It decreases as temperature rises (the opposite of most solids).
:::

::: q
Give one source of error in a solubility experiment. (1)
---
Judging exactly when the solution is saturated (or heat loss changing the temperature, supercooling before crystals appear).
:::

::: watch
- Solubility is **per 100 g of water** — always scale to the actual mass of water in the question.
- Crystallisation = **difference in solubilities**, then scale. Two steps, both shown.
- A **temperature** must accompany every solubility value.
- Read curves with dotted lines and give **units (g/100 g)**.
- Gases get **less** soluble when warmed — the exception worth a mark.
:::

::: test
Describe how you would find the solubility of potassium nitrate at 40 °C. (4)
---
Warm water to 40 °C and add potassium nitrate, stirring, until no more dissolves (saturated) (1); filter off the excess and weigh a sample of the solution in an evaporating basin (1); evaporate the water and weigh the dry solid (1); solubility = mass of solid ÷ mass of water × 100, in g per 100 g water (1).
:::

::: test
The solubility of copper sulfate is 20 g/100 g water at 20 °C. A solution contains 15 g in 50 g of water at 20 °C. Is it saturated? Explain. (2)
---
50 g of water can hold 10 g (1); 15 g exceeds this, so it is saturated and 5 g would be undissolved (1).
:::

::: test
A saturated solution of a salt in 200 g of water at 80 °C (solubility 110 g/100 g) is cooled to 20 °C (solubility 35 g/100 g). Calculate the mass of crystals that forms. (3)
---
Dissolved at 80 °C: 220 g (1); at 20 °C only 70 g can stay dissolved (1); crystals = 150 g (1).
:::

::: test
State how the solubility of most solids changes with temperature, and how the solubility of gases changes. (2)
---
Solids: solubility increases with temperature (1); gases: solubility decreases with temperature (1).
:::

::: test
A solubility curve is drawn for two salts, A and B, which cross at 50 °C. What does the crossing point mean? (1)
---
At 50 °C both salts have the same solubility.
:::

::: test
Explain why warm lakes in summer contain less dissolved oxygen than cold ones, and why this matters. (2)
---
Gases are less soluble in warmer water (1); fish and other organisms may not get enough oxygen (1).
:::

::: test
Give two reasons why a measured solubility might come out too high. (2)
---
Some undissolved solid passed through the filter (1); the solution was not fully saturated / cooled during filtering so solid crystallised out and was weighed with the solution — or water was not fully evaporated (1).
:::

::: test
Define the term 'saturated solution', and explain how you could show a solution is saturated. (2)
---
A solution that cannot dissolve any more solute at that temperature (1); add a crystal — if it does not dissolve (some solid remains), the solution is saturated (1).
:::
