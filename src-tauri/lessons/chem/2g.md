## What it is

A **salt** is the compound formed when the **hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal (or ammonium) ion**. The four general reactions of acids all make a salt:

| Reaction | General equation | Example | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acid + metal** | → salt + **hydrogen** | Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ | fizzing (squeaky pop), metal dissolves |
| **Acid + metal oxide** (base) | → salt + **water** | CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O | black powder dissolves on warming to give a blue solution |
| **Acid + metal hydroxide** (alkali) | → salt + **water** | NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O | no visible change — needs an indicator |
| **Acid + metal carbonate** | → salt + water + **carbon dioxide** | CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ | fizzing (gas turns limewater milky), solid dissolves |

Naming: the metal (or ammonium) comes first; **hydrochloric acid → chloride**, **sulfuric acid → sulfate**, **nitric acid → nitrate**. Ammonia also neutralises acids: NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃ (ammonium nitrate, a fertiliser).

**Solubility rules** — needed to choose the preparation method:

| Compound type | Soluble | Insoluble |
|---|---|---|
| **Sodium, potassium, ammonium** salts | **all** | — |
| **Nitrates** | **all** | — |
| **Chlorides** | most | **silver chloride, lead chloride** |
| **Sulfates** | most | **barium sulfate, calcium sulfate, lead sulfate** |
| **Carbonates** | sodium, potassium, ammonium | **all others** |
| **Hydroxides** | sodium, potassium, ammonium (calcium slightly) | **all others** |

**Preparing a soluble salt from an insoluble base, carbonate or metal — required practical** (e.g. copper(II) sulfate from copper(II) oxide):

1. Warm dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker (speeds the reaction; do not boil).
2. Add copper(II) oxide a spatula at a time, stirring, until some **remains undissolved** — the base is in **excess**, so **all the acid is used up**.
3. **Filter** to remove the excess, unreacted solid; the filtrate is the salt solution.
4. **Evaporate** the filtrate gently (evaporating basin over a water bath) until about half the volume remains / crystals begin to form on a glass rod — the point of crystallisation.
5. Leave to **cool and crystallise** slowly (large crystals form on slow cooling), then filter off the crystals and **dry** them between filter papers or in a warm oven.

The same method works with an insoluble carbonate or a metal: zinc + sulfuric acid → zinc sulfate (fizzing stops when the acid is used up).

**Preparing a soluble salt from an alkali — titration** (sodium chloride from sodium hydroxide): both reactants are soluble, so there is no excess solid to filter off. Use a **pipette** to measure 25.0 cm³ of the alkali into a conical flask, add a few drops of indicator (phenolphthalein), and add acid from a **burette** until the indicator just changes colour (pink to colourless); note the volume. Repeat **without indicator** using exactly the same volume of acid, then evaporate and crystallise as before.

**Preparing an insoluble salt — precipitation**: mix two **soluble** solutions that contain the required ions. For barium sulfate: barium chloride solution + sodium sulfate solution → **barium sulfate precipitate** + sodium chloride: BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq); ionic equation Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s). Then **filter**, **wash** the residue with distilled water to remove soluble impurities, and **dry**. For lead iodide: lead nitrate + potassium iodide → yellow lead iodide.

**Choosing a method**: is the salt soluble? No → precipitation. Yes → is the starting base soluble? No → excess solid, filter, evaporate. Yes (alkali) → titration.

::: method
1. **Complete the general equation first** — identify the reaction type (metal / oxide / hydroxide / carbonate), write the products, then balance.
2. **Name the salt** from the acid: chloride, sulfate, nitrate; metal first.
3. **Practical questions**: excess solid → *why* (to use up all the acid) → filter → *why* (remove unreacted solid) → evaporate → *why stop early* (avoid decomposing the salt; large crystals from slow cooling) → dry.
4. **Precipitation**: pick two soluble compounds — usually a **nitrate** for the metal and a **sodium** salt for the anion — write the equation with state symbols and include filter/wash/dry.
5. **Solubility**: check the rules table before deciding the method; the marks are for the reasoning as well as the choice.
:::

::: example Reactions of acids
Write balanced symbol equations for (a) zinc with hydrochloric acid, (b) magnesium carbonate with nitric acid. (4)

(a) Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ (2). (b) MgCO₃ + 2HNO₃ → Mg(NO₃)₂ + H₂O + CO₂ (2).
:::

::: example Soluble salt from an insoluble base
Describe how to prepare pure, dry crystals of copper(II) sulfate from copper(II) oxide and dilute sulfuric acid. (6)

Warm the sulfuric acid and add copper(II) oxide, stirring, until no more dissolves and some solid remains (1) — the excess oxide makes sure all the acid is used up (1). Filter to remove the unreacted copper(II) oxide (1). Heat the blue filtrate gently in an evaporating basin until crystals start to form on a glass rod, then stop heating (1). Leave the solution to cool so that crystals form slowly (1). Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers or in a warm oven (1).
:::

::: example Insoluble salt
Describe how to make a pure sample of lead(II) sulfate, giving an equation for the reaction. (4)

Mix solutions of lead(II) nitrate and sodium sulfate (both soluble) (1): Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → PbSO₄(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq) (1). A white precipitate of lead sulfate forms; filter it off (1), wash the residue with distilled water to remove soluble sodium nitrate, and dry it in a warm oven (1).
:::

::: example Choosing a method
A student wants to make potassium chloride. Explain why the excess-solid method cannot be used and outline the method they should use. (3)

Potassium chloride is made from potassium hydroxide, which is soluble — so any excess would stay in the solution and could not be filtered off (1). Use a titration: pipette 25.0 cm³ of potassium hydroxide into a flask with indicator and add hydrochloric acid from a burette until the indicator changes colour, recording the volume (1). Repeat without indicator with the same volumes, then evaporate the solution to obtain crystals (1).
:::

::: q
Name the products when an acid reacts with a metal carbonate. (1)
---
A salt, water and carbon dioxide.
:::

::: q
Name the salt formed when calcium hydroxide reacts with nitric acid, and write the equation. (2)
---
Calcium nitrate (1); Ca(OH)₂ + 2HNO₃ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O (1).
:::

::: q
Which gas is produced when a metal reacts with an acid, and how is it tested? (2)
---
Hydrogen (1); a lit splint gives a squeaky pop (1).
:::

::: q
Are the following soluble or insoluble: sodium carbonate, silver chloride, barium sulfate, copper nitrate? (2)
---
Sodium carbonate soluble, copper nitrate soluble (1); silver chloride insoluble, barium sulfate insoluble (1).
:::

::: q
Why is the base added in excess when making a soluble salt? (1)
---
So that all the acid is used up and none remains to contaminate the salt.
:::

::: q
Why is the solution not evaporated to dryness when preparing salt crystals? (2)
---
Heating to dryness can decompose the salt or drive off water of crystallisation (1); stopping early and cooling slowly gives larger, purer crystals (1).
:::

::: q
Write the ionic equation for the precipitation of silver chloride. (1)
---
Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
:::

::: q
Why is the precipitate washed with distilled water before drying? (1)
---
To remove the soluble salt (e.g. sodium nitrate) left in solution on the solid.
:::

::: q
Which two solutions could be mixed to make insoluble lead(II) iodide? (2)
---
Lead(II) nitrate solution (1) and potassium (or sodium) iodide solution (1).
:::

::: q
Copper does not react with dilute sulfuric acid. How could copper(II) sulfate be made instead? (2)
---
React the acid with copper(II) oxide or copper(II) carbonate (1), which are bases that neutralise the acid to form the salt (1).
:::

::: watch
- Acid + carbonate gives **three** products — do not forget the water.
- **Excess solid → filter → evaporate partly → cool → dry**: every step has a reason, and examiners mark the reasons.
- Copper is **below hydrogen** — use the **oxide** or **carbonate**, never the metal.
- For precipitation choose **two soluble salts**: nitrates and sodium/potassium salts are always soluble.
- Titration method for salts from **alkalis** — repeat **without indicator** so the crystals are not contaminated.
:::
