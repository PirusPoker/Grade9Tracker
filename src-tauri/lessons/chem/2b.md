## What it is

**Group 7** — fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine (astatine) — the **halogens**. They are **non-metals** that exist as **diatomic molecules** (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂), have **seven outer electrons** and **gain one electron** to form a **−1 halide ion** (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻). Their compounds with metals are ionic salts (sodium chloride); with non-metals covalent (hydrogen chloride).

| Halogen | State at room temperature | Colour | Melting/boiling point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fluorine** | gas | pale yellow | lowest |
| **Chlorine** | **gas** | **green** (yellow-green) | ↓ |
| **Bromine** | **liquid** | **red-brown** (orange-brown vapour) | ↓ |
| **Iodine** | **solid** | **grey-black** crystals (purple vapour on heating) | highest |

**Trend in physical properties**: going down the group the molecules get **larger** with more electrons, so the **intermolecular forces are stronger** → melting and boiling points **increase** (gas → liquid → solid) and colours get **darker**. Astatine would be a dark, solid, very rare element.

**Trend in reactivity — decreases down the group** (fluorine most reactive, iodine least): a halogen reacts by **gaining one electron**. Going down, the atoms are **larger**, the outer shell is **further from the nucleus** and **more shielded**, so the nucleus **attracts an incoming electron less strongly** — the electron is gained less easily. (Opposite to Group 1, where the trend is about *losing* an electron.)

**Displacement reactions** — a **more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its salt**. This is how the order of reactivity is shown experimentally:

- Chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine: **Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂** — the colourless solution turns **orange/brown** (bromine formed).
- Chlorine + potassium iodide → potassium chloride + iodine: Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂ — turns **brown** (iodine).
- Bromine + potassium iodide → potassium bromide + iodine: Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂ — turns **brown**.
- Bromine + potassium chloride → **no reaction** (bromine is less reactive than chlorine); iodine displaces nothing.

Ionic equation, showing that the halide ions are oxidised and the halogen reduced: **Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂**. The bromide ions lose electrons (oxidation); the chlorine molecules gain them (reduction) — a redox reaction.

| Added to → | KCl(aq) | KBr(aq) | KI(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chlorine** | — | orange (Br₂) | brown (I₂) |
| **Bromine** | no change | — | brown (I₂) |
| **Iodine** | no change | no change | — |

**Other reactions**: halogens react with metals to form halides (2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl; 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃, vigorous for chlorine, less so for bromine and iodine); with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides (H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, explosive in light; H₂ + I₂ reacts slowly and reversibly). **Test for chlorine**: bleaches damp litmus paper.

::: terms
- **Halogens**: the Group 7 elements — fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine.
- **Chlorine**: a pale yellow-green gas at room temperature.
- **Bromine**: a red-brown liquid that gives off an orange vapour.
- **Iodine**: a grey-black solid that forms a purple vapour.
- **Displacement reaction**: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its salt.
- **Reactivity trend in Group 7**: reactivity decreases down the group, because the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so an electron is gained less easily.
:::

::: method
1. **Learn the state-and-colour table** — two facts per halogen, at room temperature.
2. **Physical trend**: bigger molecules → stronger intermolecular forces → higher boiling points, darker colour.
3. **Reactivity trend and reason**: decreases down; incoming electron further from the nucleus and more shielded → attracted less → gained less easily.
4. **Displacement**: more reactive displaces less reactive from its salt; write the word equation, the symbol equation and the colour change (colourless → orange for bromine, → brown for iodine). "No reaction" is a valid answer and must be justified by reactivity.
5. **Ionic and redox**: halide ions lose electrons (oxidised); halogen gains (reduced).
:::

::: example Colours and states
State the colour and physical state of chlorine, bromine and iodine at room temperature. (3)

Chlorine — green gas (1). Bromine — red-brown liquid (1). Iodine — grey-black solid (1).
:::

::: example Displacement
Chlorine water is added to potassium bromide solution. Describe what is seen, write a balanced equation, and explain why the reaction occurs. (4)

The solution turns from colourless to orange (1). Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ (1). Chlorine is more reactive than bromine because its atoms gain an electron more easily (1), so it takes the electrons from bromide ions, displacing bromine from the solution (1).
:::

::: example Trend reason
Explain why chlorine is more reactive than iodine. (3)

Halogens react by gaining an electron into their outer shell (1). Chlorine atoms are smaller, so the outer shell is closer to the nucleus and shielded by fewer inner shells (1); the nucleus attracts an extra electron more strongly, so chlorine gains it more readily than iodine (1).
:::

::: example Redox
Write the ionic equation for bromine reacting with potassium iodide solution, and identify which species is oxidised. (3)

Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂ (2). The iodide ions lose electrons to form iodine, so they are oxidised; the bromine molecules gain electrons and are reduced (1).
:::

---
Two atoms share a pair of electrons so that each has a full outer shell.

Reactivity decreases down the group.

::: watch
- Group 7 reactivity **decreases** down — the opposite of Group 1. Reason: **gaining** an electron gets harder as the atom grows.
- Colours and states: **green gas, red-brown liquid, grey-black solid**.
- Displacement: **more reactive displaces less reactive from its salt** — give the colour change (orange = bromine, brown = iodine).
- Halide ions are **oxidised** (lose e⁻); the halogen is **reduced**.
- Boiling points **rise** down the group because of **stronger intermolecular forces** between larger molecules.
:::
