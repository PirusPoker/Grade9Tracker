## What it is

**Covalent bonding** happens between **non-metal atoms**. Instead of transferring electrons, two atoms **share a pair of electrons**, one from each atom, so that both get a full outer shell. A **covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons**; the bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between the shared electrons and the nuclei of both atoms.

- A **single bond** shares one pair (H–H, C–H); a **double bond** shares two pairs (O=O, C=C, C=O); a **triple bond** shares three (N≡N).
- An atom forms as many bonds as it needs electrons: hydrogen 1, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3, carbon 4, halogens 1.

**Dot-and-cross diagrams** — show the outer shells overlapping, with the shared pair in the overlap (one dot, one cross). You must be able to draw: **H₂, Cl₂, HCl, H₂O, NH₃, CH₄, O₂, N₂, CO₂, ethane, ethene**.

**Simple molecular substances** — small molecules (H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, Cl₂, ethanol) with **strong covalent bonds inside** each molecule but only **weak intermolecular forces between** molecules. This explains their properties:

- **Low melting and boiling points** — many are gases or liquids at room temperature — because only the **weak forces between molecules** need to be overcome to melt or boil; the covalent bonds are **not broken**.
- **Do not conduct electricity** — the molecules are neutral and there are **no free electrons or ions** to carry charge.
- Often **insoluble** in water (unless they can interact with water molecules).

The distinction the exam hunts for: melting ice breaks the weak forces **between** water molecules, not the O–H bonds. If the covalent bonds broke you would get hydrogen and oxygen, not steam.

**Trend down a homologous series** (alkanes, alcohols…): as molecules get **bigger** (longer carbon chains) the **intermolecular forces get stronger**, so **more energy** is needed to separate the molecules and the **boiling point rises** — methane is a gas, octane a liquid, C₂₀H₄₂ a waxy solid. Same reason: relative molecular mass up → intermolecular forces up → boiling point up.

::: method
1. **Define**: a covalent bond is a **shared pair of electrons** between two non-metal atoms.
2. **Dot-and-cross**: count the outer electrons of each atom; each atom must end with 8 (hydrogen 2); shared pairs sit in the overlap; show **lone pairs** too (water's oxygen has two).
3. **Two levels of force, always named**: **strong covalent bonds within** molecules; **weak intermolecular forces between** them. Property answers say which one is overcome.
4. **Melting/boiling point low** → weak intermolecular forces → little energy. **No conduction** → no free electrons or ions.
5. **Trend in boiling point**: larger molecule → stronger intermolecular forces → more energy → higher boiling point.
:::

::: example Define and draw
Describe the bonding in a molecule of ammonia, NH₃, and how a dot-and-cross diagram would show it. (3)

Nitrogen has five outer electrons and needs three more; each hydrogen has one and needs one more (1). Nitrogen shares one pair of electrons with each of three hydrogen atoms — three single covalent bonds (1). The diagram shows the nitrogen circle overlapping three hydrogen circles, a dot and a cross in each overlap, and one lone pair (two crosses) left on the nitrogen (1).
:::

::: example Boiling point
Explain why chlorine, Cl₂, is a gas at room temperature even though its covalent bond is strong. (3)

Chlorine consists of small simple molecules (1). Between the molecules there are only weak intermolecular forces, which need very little energy to overcome, so it boils at a low temperature (1). The strong covalent bond within each molecule is not broken when chlorine boils — the molecules stay intact and simply separate (1).
:::

::: example Conductivity
Explain why methane does not conduct electricity in any state. (2)

Methane molecules are neutral, so there are no ions (1), and all the electrons are held in covalent bonds, so there are no free (delocalised) electrons to carry charge (1).
:::

::: example Homologous series
Explain why the boiling points of the alkanes increase from methane to butane. (3)

The molecules become larger, with more atoms and higher relative molecular mass (1), so the intermolecular forces between molecules become stronger (1). More energy is needed to overcome the stronger forces and separate the molecules, so the boiling point rises (1).
:::

::: q
Define a covalent bond. (1)
---
A shared pair of electrons between two atoms.
:::

::: q
How many covalent bonds do carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and hydrogen atoms form? (2)
---
Carbon 4, nitrogen 3 (1); oxygen 2, hydrogen 1 (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the bonding in an oxygen molecule, O₂. (2)
---
Each oxygen atom has six outer electrons and needs two more (1), so the two atoms share two pairs of electrons — a double covalent bond, O=O (1).
:::

::: q
Describe the bonding in carbon dioxide. (2)
---
The carbon atom shares two pairs of electrons with each of two oxygen atoms (1): two double bonds, O=C=O, giving every atom a full outer shell (1).
:::

::: q
What type of forces exist between water molecules, and how strong are they? (1)
---
Weak intermolecular forces — much weaker than the covalent O–H bonds within each molecule.
:::

::: q
Explain why simple molecular compounds have low melting points. (2)
---
Only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules must be overcome to melt them (1), which needs little energy; the strong covalent bonds inside the molecules stay intact (1).
:::

::: q
A student says "ice melts when the bonds between hydrogen and oxygen break." Explain why this is wrong. (2)
---
Melting overcomes the weak forces between water molecules, which stay as H₂O (1); breaking the O–H covalent bonds would be a chemical change producing hydrogen and oxygen (1).
:::

::: q
Which has the higher boiling point, ethanol (C₂H₅OH) or butanol (C₄H₉OH)? Explain. (2)
---
Butanol (1): its molecules are larger, so the intermolecular forces are stronger and need more energy to overcome (1).
:::

::: q
Give two properties of simple molecular substances. (2)
---
Low melting and boiling points; do not conduct electricity (or often gases/liquids at room temperature, insoluble in water).
:::

::: watch
- Two forces, two names: **strong covalent bonds within**, **weak intermolecular forces between**. Say which is overcome.
- Low boiling point is **not** because covalent bonds are weak — they are strong; it is the forces **between molecules** that are weak.
- No conduction: **no free electrons or ions** — both.
- Dot-and-cross: shared pairs in the **overlap**, one dot + one cross; show **lone pairs**.
- Bigger molecules → **stronger intermolecular forces** → higher boiling point.
:::
