## What it is

Every chemical reaction involves an **energy change**, usually as heat. The **enthalpy change, ΔH**, is the heat energy transferred at constant pressure, measured in **kJ/mol**.

- **Exothermic** reactions **give out heat** to the surroundings, so the **temperature rises**; **ΔH is negative**. Examples: combustion, neutralisation, respiration, metals with acids, hand-warmers (oxidation of iron).
- **Endothermic** reactions **take in heat** from the surroundings, so the **temperature falls**; **ΔH is positive**. Examples: thermal decomposition of carbonates, photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate (cold packs), citric acid + sodium hydrogencarbonate.

**Energy level (reaction profile) diagrams** show the energy of reactants and products. In an exothermic reaction the **products are lower** than the reactants (energy released); in an endothermic reaction the **products are higher**. The hump is the **activation energy, Eₐ** — the minimum energy the colliding particles need to react (the energy to start breaking bonds). ΔH is the vertical gap between the reactant and product levels; it is not affected by the height of the hump.

**Bond energies explain the sign of ΔH.** **Breaking bonds is endothermic** (energy must be put in); **making bonds is exothermic** (energy is released). Overall:

$$\Delta H = \text{(energy to break bonds in reactants)} - \text{(energy released making bonds in products)}$$

If more energy is released making new bonds than was needed to break the old ones, the reaction is exothermic (ΔH negative). Bond energies are given in kJ/mol for each bond, so count every bond: for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, broken = H–H (436) + Cl–Cl (242) = 678 kJ; made = 2 × H–Cl (2 × 431) = 862 kJ; ΔH = 678 − 862 = **−184 kJ/mol**.

**Measuring energy changes — calorimetry (required practical).** The heat given out or taken in is found from the temperature change of a known mass of water (or solution):

$$Q = m \times c \times \Delta T$$

*Q* in **J**, *m* = mass of water or solution in **g** (1 cm³ ≈ 1 g), *c* = **4.2 J/g/°C** (specific heat capacity of water), ΔT = temperature change in °C. Then the **molar enthalpy change** is $\Delta H = Q \div \text{moles}$, converted to kJ/mol and given a **negative sign if the temperature rose**.

- **Combustion of a fuel**: a spirit burner containing the fuel is weighed, the flame heats a known mass of water in a copper calorimeter (or metal can), the temperature rise is measured, and the burner is reweighed to find the mass of fuel burned. Heat is lost to the air and the can, and combustion may be incomplete, so the result is **smaller than the data-book value**; improve with a draught shield, a lid, and placing the flame close to the can.
- **Displacement, neutralisation, dissolving**: mix measured volumes in a **polystyrene cup** with a lid (a good insulator), stir, record the maximum (or minimum) temperature; *m* is the total mass of solution.

::: terms
- **Exothermic reaction**: a reaction that gives out heat energy to the surroundings, so the temperature rises; ΔH is negative.
- **Endothermic reaction**: a reaction that takes in heat energy from the surroundings, so the temperature falls; ΔH is positive.
- **Enthalpy change (ΔH)**: the heat energy change in a reaction, in kJ/mol.
- **Calorimetry**: measuring the heat energy change of a reaction from the temperature change of water or a solution.
- **Heat energy change**: Q = m × c × ΔT, where m is the mass of water or solution, c = 4.2 J/g/°C and ΔT the temperature change.
- **Molar enthalpy change**: ΔH = −Q ÷ moles of the reactant, in kJ/mol.
- **Bond breaking**: an endothermic process — energy is needed to break bonds.
- **Bond making**: an exothermic process — energy is released when bonds form.
- **Energy level diagram**: a diagram showing the energy of the reactants and products and the overall energy change.
:::

::: method
1. **Sign convention**: temperature up → exothermic → ΔH negative. Write the sign every time.
2. **Q = mcΔT**: *m* is the mass of the **water/solution** (not the fuel), *c* = 4.2, ΔT is the change. Answer in J, ÷1000 for kJ.
3. **Per mole**: moles of the substance in the question (fuel burned, or the limiting reactant) → ΔH = Q ÷ mol; add the sign.
4. **Bond energies**: list every bond broken and every bond made — use the displayed formulae — then broken − made.
5. **Energy level diagram**: label axes (energy / progress of reaction), reactants, products, Eₐ (to the peak) and ΔH (level to level), with the correct direction.
:::

::: example Neutralisation
50 cm³ of 1.0 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid at 21.0 °C is mixed with 50 cm³ of 1.0 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide in a polystyrene cup. The maximum temperature is 27.5 °C. Calculate the molar enthalpy change of neutralisation. (4)

Total mass of solution = 100 g; ΔT = 27.5 − 21.0 = 6.5 °C (1). $Q = 100 \times 4.2 \times 6.5 = 2730$ J (1). Moles of acid (and of water formed) = $1.0 \times 50/1000 = 0.050$ mol (1). $\Delta H = -2730 \div 0.050 = -54\,600$ J/mol = **−54.6 kJ/mol** (negative because the temperature rose — exothermic) (1).
:::

::: example Combustion of a fuel
Burning 0.46 g of ethanol (Mr = 46) raised the temperature of 100 g of water by 20.0 °C. Calculate the enthalpy of combustion of ethanol and explain why the value is lower than the accepted −1367 kJ/mol. (5)

$Q = 100 \times 4.2 \times 20.0 = 8400$ J = 8.4 kJ (1). Moles of ethanol = $0.46 \div 46 = 0.010$ mol (1). $\Delta H = -8.4 \div 0.010 =$ **−840 kJ/mol** (1). Much of the heat from the flame is lost to the surrounding air and to the calorimeter itself rather than the water (1), and some ethanol may burn incompletely, releasing less energy (1).
:::

::: example Bond energies
Use the bond energies C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805 and O–H 464 kJ/mol to calculate ΔH for CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. (3)

Bonds broken: 4 C–H + 2 O=O $= 4 \times 413 + 2 \times 498 = 2648$ kJ (1). Bonds made: 2 C=O + 4 O–H $= 2 \times 805 + 4 \times 464 = 3466$ kJ (1). $\Delta H = 2648 - 3466 =$ **−818 kJ/mol**, exothermic — more energy is released forming the new bonds than is needed to break the old ones (1).
:::

::: example Energy level diagram
Draw and label an energy level diagram for the endothermic thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate. (3)

Axes: energy (vertical) against progress of reaction (1). Reactants (CaCO₃) drawn at a lower level than the products (CaO + CO₂), with ΔH shown as an upward arrow from reactants to products, labelled positive (1). A curve rising from the reactants over a peak to the products, with the activation energy marked from the reactant level to the top of the peak (1).
:::

::: q
Define an exothermic reaction and give the sign of ΔH. (2)
---
A reaction that gives out heat energy to the surroundings, so the temperature rises (1); ΔH is negative (1).
:::

::: q
Give two examples of endothermic changes. (2)
---
Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate; dissolving ammonium nitrate in water (also photosynthesis).
:::

::: q
Is bond breaking exothermic or endothermic? Explain. (2)
---
Endothermic (1) — energy must be supplied to overcome the attraction and pull the atoms apart (1).
:::

::: q
State the equation used to calculate the heat energy change in calorimetry, with units. (2)
---
Q = m × c × ΔT (1); Q in J, m in g, c = 4.2 J/g/°C, ΔT in °C (1).
:::

::: q
When 50 g of water has 4.0 g of ammonium nitrate dissolved in it, the temperature falls by 8.0 °C. Calculate the energy change. (2)
---
$Q = 50 \times 4.2 \times 8.0 = 1680$ J (1); endothermic, so +1680 J (+1.68 kJ) (1).
:::

::: q
Calculate ΔH for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl using H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431 kJ/mol. (3)
---
Broken: 436 + 242 = 678 kJ (1); made: 2 × 431 = 862 kJ (1); ΔH = 678 − 862 = −184 kJ/mol (1).
:::

::: q
What is activation energy? (1)
---
The minimum energy colliding particles need for a reaction to occur.
:::

::: q
Why is a polystyrene cup used in a neutralisation calorimetry experiment? (1)
---
It is a good insulator, so less heat is lost to the surroundings.
:::

::: q
Give two reasons why a measured enthalpy of combustion is less exothermic than the data-book value. (2)
---
Heat is lost to the air and the apparatus (1); incomplete combustion of the fuel (or evaporation of the fuel) (1).
:::

::: q
On an energy level diagram, what does the height from the reactants to the top of the curve represent? (1)
---
The activation energy of the reaction.
:::

::: watch
- **Temperature rise → exothermic → ΔH negative.** Students lose the mark by dropping the sign.
- In Q = mcΔT, ***m* is the mass of water/solution** being heated, never the mass of fuel.
- Bond energies: ΔH = **broken − made**; count *all* bonds (4 C–H in methane, 2 O=O for 2O₂).
- ΔH is the gap **between the levels**; Eₐ is **up to the peak** — they are different arrows.
- Endothermic products are **higher** than reactants on the diagram.
:::

::: test
Describe an experiment to measure the temperature change when zinc powder reacts with copper(II) sulfate solution, and explain how the result would be used to calculate ΔH. (6)
