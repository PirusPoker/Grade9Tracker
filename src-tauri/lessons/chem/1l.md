## What it is

**Electrolysis** is the **decomposition of an ionic compound by passing an electric current through it** when it is **molten or in aqueous solution** — the states in which its ions are free to move. The liquid is the **electrolyte**; the electric current is carried through it by the **ions**, and through the wires by electrons.

- **Cathode** — the **negative** electrode; attracts **positive ions (cations)**. Cations **gain electrons** there: **reduction**.
- **Anode** — the **positive** electrode; attracts **negative ions (anions)**. Anions **lose electrons** there: **oxidation**.
- **OIL RIG**: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain. Electrolysis is a **redox** reaction.
- Electrodes are usually **inert** (graphite or platinum) so they do not react.

**Molten compounds** — only two ions present, so the products are simply the **metal at the cathode** and the **non-metal at the anode**:

- Molten lead(II) bromide: cathode Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb; anode 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ (brown bromine vapour).
- Molten sodium chloride: Na at the cathode, Cl₂ at the anode. Molten aluminium oxide: Al at the cathode, O₂ at the anode (see 2e).

**Aqueous solutions** — water is also present, supplying **H⁺ and OH⁻ ions**, so there is a competition at each electrode:

- **Cathode**: the **less reactive** of the metal ion and hydrogen is discharged. If the metal is **below hydrogen in the reactivity series** (copper, silver), the **metal** forms: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. If the metal is **more reactive** (Na, K, Mg, Zn…), **hydrogen** forms instead: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂.
- **Anode**: if the solution contains a **halide** (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) in reasonable concentration, the **halogen** forms: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻. Otherwise (sulfates, nitrates), **oxygen** forms from the hydroxide ions: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻.

| Electrolyte (inert electrodes) | Cathode | Anode | Left in solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Molten PbBr₂ | lead | bromine | — |
| Aqueous NaCl (concentrated) | hydrogen | chlorine | sodium hydroxide |
| Aqueous CuSO₄ | copper (pink-brown coating) | oxygen | sulfuric acid (solution turns paler blue) |
| Dilute H₂SO₄ (acidified water) | hydrogen | oxygen | — (volume of H₂ is twice O₂) |
| Aqueous CuCl₂ | copper | chlorine | — |

**Copper electrodes in copper sulfate** (not inert): the anode copper dissolves (Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻) and copper is deposited on the cathode; the anode loses mass, the cathode gains it, the solution stays blue — used to **purify copper**.

**Half-equations** must balance for **atoms and for charge**: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ has 4− on each side. Diatomic gases need two ions: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.

**Required practical** — electrolysis of aqueous solutions (CuSO₄, NaCl, KI, dilute H₂SO₄) with graphite electrodes in a beaker, connected to a low-voltage DC supply; gases collected in inverted test tubes over the electrodes and identified with tests (2h): hydrogen — squeaky pop; oxygen — relights a glowing splint; chlorine — bleaches damp litmus.

::: terms
- **Electrolysis**: the breakdown of an ionic compound, molten or in solution, by passing an electric current through it.
- **Electrolyte**: the molten or dissolved ionic compound that conducts electricity and is decomposed.
- **Electrode**: a conductor through which the current enters or leaves the electrolyte.
- **Inert electrode**: an electrode that does not react, such as graphite or platinum.
- **Cathode**: the negative electrode, where cations gain electrons (reduction).
- **Anode**: the positive electrode, where anions lose electrons (oxidation).
- **Half-equation**: an equation showing what happens at one electrode, including electrons.
- **Oxidation**: loss of electrons. **Reduction**: gain of electrons. (OIL RIG)
:::

::: method
1. **Electrode facts**: cathode negative, attracts cations, reduction; anode positive, attracts anions, oxidation. Learn as one line.
2. **Molten → metal at cathode, non-metal at anode.** Aqueous → apply the two rules: metal only if less reactive than hydrogen; halogen only if a halide is present, else oxygen.
3. **Half-equations**: write the ion and the product; add electrons to the side that makes charge balance (cathode: electrons on the left; anode: electrons on the right); diatomic products need 2 ions.
4. **Observations**: gas bubbles at electrodes; pink-brown copper coating; blue solution fading; brown bromine; bleached litmus for chlorine.
5. **Explain why it must be molten or dissolved**: ions must be **free to move** to carry current and reach the electrodes.
:::

::: example Molten compound
Molten zinc chloride is electrolysed with graphite electrodes. Give the product at each electrode and write the half-equations. (4)

Cathode: zinc (1) — Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn (1). Anode: chlorine (1) — 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (1).
:::

::: example Aqueous solution
Predict the products at each electrode when aqueous sodium chloride is electrolysed, and explain why sodium is not produced. (4)

Cathode: hydrogen — 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ (1). Anode: chlorine — 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (1). Sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen ions from the water are discharged in preference to sodium ions (1); sodium ions and hydroxide ions remain, leaving sodium hydroxide solution (1).
:::

::: example Copper sulfate
Describe what is observed when aqueous copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed with inert electrodes, and write the anode half-equation. (4)

A pink-brown solid (copper) coats the cathode (1); bubbles of a colourless gas (oxygen) form at the anode (1); the blue colour of the solution fades as Cu²⁺ ions are removed (1). Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ (1).
:::

::: example Why molten
Explain why solid lead bromide does not conduct electricity but molten lead bromide does, and name the type of reaction at the cathode. (3)

In the solid the ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot move to carry charge (1); when molten the Pb²⁺ and Br⁻ ions are free to move to the electrodes (1). At the cathode Pb²⁺ gains electrons — reduction (1).
:::

::: q
Define electrolysis. (2)
---
The breaking down (decomposition) of an ionic compound, molten or in solution (1), by passing an electric current through it (1).
:::

::: q
Which electrode is negative, and which ions move towards it? (2)
---
The cathode (1); positive ions (cations) (1).
:::

::: q
What is oxidation, in terms of electrons? Where does it occur in electrolysis? (2)
---
Loss of electrons (1); at the anode (1).
:::

::: q
Write the half-equation for the formation of hydrogen at a cathode. (1)
---
2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂.
:::

::: q
Write the half-equation for the formation of bromine at an anode. (1)
---
2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻.
:::

::: q
Predict the products of electrolysing aqueous potassium iodide. (2)
---
Cathode: hydrogen (potassium is more reactive than hydrogen) (1); anode: iodine (a halide is present) (1).
:::

::: q
Predict the products of electrolysing dilute sulfuric acid, and the ratio of gas volumes. (2)
---
Hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode (1); twice as much hydrogen as oxygen by volume (1).
:::

::: q
Why are graphite electrodes used? (1)
---
Graphite conducts electricity and is inert, so it does not react with the products.
:::

::: q
In the electrolysis of copper sulfate with copper electrodes, what happens to each electrode's mass? (2)
---
The anode loses mass as copper dissolves into Cu²⁺ ions (1); the cathode gains mass as copper is deposited (1).
:::

::: q
How would you test the gas collected at the anode during the electrolysis of aqueous sodium chloride? (1)
---
Hold damp blue litmus paper in it — chlorine bleaches it white.
:::

::: watch
- **Anode: oxidation (loss); cathode: reduction (gain).** OIL RIG.
- Aqueous cathode rule: **metal only if below hydrogen** in the reactivity series, else **hydrogen**. Anode: **halogen if a halide**, else **oxygen**.
- Half-equations balance **charge as well as atoms**; diatomic gases need **2 ions**.
- Electrolyte must be **molten or dissolved** so **ions are free to move**.
- Copper electrodes are **not inert** — anode dissolves, cathode gains: copper purification.
