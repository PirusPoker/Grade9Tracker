## What it is

**Crude oil** is a **mixture of hydrocarbons** — mostly **alkanes** of many different chain lengths — formed over millions of years from the remains of marine organisms buried under sediment; it is a **finite, non-renewable** resource. Because it is a mixture, the components keep their own properties and can be separated physically by **fractional distillation**, which uses the fact that **boiling point increases with the number of carbon atoms**.

**How fractional distillation works.** Crude oil is **heated** (to about 350 °C) in a furnace so that most of it **vaporises**, and the vapour is fed into the bottom of a **fractionating column** which is **hot at the bottom and cooler towards the top**. The vapours **rise** through the column and **cool**; each hydrocarbon **condenses when it reaches the level where the temperature is just below its boiling point**. **Long-chain** hydrocarbons have **high boiling points**, so they condense **near the bottom**; **short-chain** hydrocarbons have **low boiling points** and condense **near the top**; the very shortest (refinery gases) never condense and leave as gas. The liquids collect on trays and are tapped off. Bitumen does not vaporise and is collected at the base. The products are **fractions** — still mixtures, but of hydrocarbons with **similar numbers of carbon atoms and similar boiling points**.

| Fraction (top → bottom) | Approx. carbons | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Refinery gases** | 1–4 | bottled gas for heating and cooking (propane, butane) |
| **Gasoline (petrol)** | 5–10 | fuel for cars |
| **Kerosene (paraffin)** | 10–16 | aircraft fuel; heating |
| **Diesel (gas oil)** | 14–20 | fuel for lorries, buses, trains, some cars |
| **Fuel oil** | 20–50 | ships, power stations, heating |
| **Bitumen** | 50+ | surfacing roads and roofs |

**Trends with increasing chain length** (from top to bottom of the column):

- **Boiling point increases** — larger molecules have **stronger intermolecular forces** between them (more electrons, larger surface contact), so more energy is needed to separate them.
- **Viscosity increases** — longer chains tangle and flow less easily (bitumen is almost solid; petrol is runny).
- **Volatility decreases** — short-chain hydrocarbons evaporate easily (petrol vapour), long ones do not.
- **Flammability decreases** — short-chain hydrocarbons ignite easily and burn with a **clean blue flame**; long-chain ones are harder to ignite and burn with a **smoky, sooty flame** (incomplete combustion).
- **Colour darkens** — from colourless gases to black bitumen.

These trends are why the light fractions are prized as fuels and why heavier fractions are **cracked** (4d) into more useful smaller molecules.

::: terms
- **Crude oil**: a mixture of hydrocarbons, mostly alkanes, formed from the remains of ancient sea creatures.
- **Fractional distillation of crude oil**: separating crude oil into fractions by heating it and letting the vapours condense at different heights of a column with a temperature gradient.
- **Fraction**: a group of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points, collected together.
- **Main fractions**: refinery gases, gasoline (petrol), kerosene, diesel, fuel oil and bitumen.
- **Viscosity**: how thick and slow-flowing a liquid is; it increases with the size of the molecules.
- **Trends down the column**: larger molecules, higher boiling point, darker colour, more viscous, harder to ignite.
:::

::: method
1. **Describe the process in order**: heat → vaporise → column hot at the bottom and cool at the top → vapours rise and cool → condense at the level where temperature = boiling point → collected as fractions.
2. **Link position to chain length and boiling point** explicitly: "long chains, high boiling points, condense low down".
3. **Name the fractions top to bottom** with one use each — "gases, petrol, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil, bitumen".
4. **Trend questions**: state the trend *and* the molecular reason (stronger intermolecular forces for boiling point; longer chains for viscosity).
5. **"Why is it a physical process?"** — no bonds broken; the hydrocarbons are separated, not changed.
:::

::: example The process
Describe how crude oil is separated into fractions by fractional distillation. (4)

The crude oil is heated in a furnace until it vaporises and the vapour enters the bottom of a fractionating column (1). The column has a temperature gradient — hot at the bottom, cool at the top (1). The vapours rise and cool, and each hydrocarbon condenses at the level where the temperature falls below its boiling point (1). Long-chain hydrocarbons with high boiling points condense near the bottom, short-chain ones with low boiling points near the top, and each fraction is tapped off as a liquid (1).
:::

::: example Boiling point trend
Explain why bitumen has a much higher boiling point than petrol. (3)

Bitumen consists of very long hydrocarbon chains, while petrol has short chains of 5–10 carbons (1). Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces between them (1), so much more energy is needed to overcome these forces and separate the molecules, giving a higher boiling point (1).
:::

::: example Fraction properties
Compare the viscosity and flammability of the refinery gas fraction and the fuel oil fraction, and explain the difference in flammability. (3)

Refinery gases have very low viscosity (they are gases) and are highly flammable; fuel oil is viscous and much harder to ignite (1). Short-chain hydrocarbons vaporise easily so they mix readily with oxygen and ignite (1); long-chain molecules do not vaporise easily and burn with a smoky flame (1).
:::

::: example Fractions and uses
Name the fraction used as aircraft fuel and the fraction used for road surfaces, and state where in the column each is collected. (3)

Kerosene is used as aircraft fuel and is collected in the middle-upper part of the column (1). Bitumen is used for road surfaces and is collected at the very bottom (1) because its molecules are the longest, with the highest boiling points, so it does not vaporise (1).
:::
