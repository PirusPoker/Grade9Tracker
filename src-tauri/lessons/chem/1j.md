## What it is

Some covalent substances are not made of small molecules at all. In a **giant covalent structure** every atom is joined to its neighbours by **strong covalent bonds** in a continuous 3-D **lattice** containing millions of atoms. There are no separate molecules and no weak intermolecular forces — so the properties are completely different from simple molecular substances.

**Common properties of giant covalent structures:**

- **Very high melting and boiling points** — melting means **breaking many strong covalent bonds** throughout the lattice, which needs a great deal of energy (diamond does not melt below about 3,500 °C).
- **Insoluble** in water and other solvents.
- Usually **do not conduct electricity** — no free electrons or ions (graphite is the exception).
- Often **very hard** (diamond, silicon dioxide).

**Diamond** (carbon): each carbon atom is covalently bonded to **four** other carbon atoms in a rigid **tetrahedral** lattice. Properties: **extremely hard** (the rigid network of strong bonds resists any distortion) → used for cutting tools and drill bits; very high melting point; **does not conduct** — all four outer electrons of every carbon are used in bonds, so there are **no free electrons**.

**Graphite** (carbon): each carbon atom is covalently bonded to only **three** others, in flat **hexagonal layers**. The layers are held together by only **weak intermolecular forces**. Properties:

- **Soft and slippery** — the layers can **slide over each other** because the forces between them are weak → pencil "lead", lubricants.
- **Conducts electricity** — each carbon uses only three of its four outer electrons for bonds; the **fourth electron is delocalised** and free to move along the layers → electrodes.
- Very high melting point — the covalent bonds within the layers are strong and must be broken to melt it.

**Silicon dioxide** (silica, sand, quartz, SiO₂): a giant covalent lattice in which each **silicon** atom is bonded to **four oxygen** atoms and each **oxygen** to **two silicon** atoms (hence the 1:2 ratio), in a tetrahedral arrangement similar to diamond. Properties: very high melting point, hard, does not conduct, insoluble — used in glass and as an abrasive.

| Atoms | C | C | Si and O |

::: terms
- **Giant covalent structure**: a very large network of atoms all joined by strong covalent bonds, e.g. diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide.
- **Diamond**: each carbon atom bonded covalently to four others in a rigid lattice — very hard, very high melting point, does not conduct.
- **Graphite**: each carbon atom bonded to three others in layers, with delocalised electrons and weak forces between the layers — soft, slippery, conducts electricity.
- **C₆₀ fullerene**: a simple molecular form of carbon made of 60 carbon atoms in a ball; weak intermolecular forces, so it is soft with a low melting point.
- **Delocalised electrons**: electrons not tied to any one atom, free to move through the structure.
:::

::: method
1. **Giant covalent melting point**: "many strong covalent bonds must be broken, needing a lot of energy" — contrast with simple molecular, where only intermolecular forces are overcome.
2. **Diamond**: 4 bonds per carbon → rigid lattice → hard; all electrons in bonds → no conduction.
3. **Graphite**: 3 bonds per carbon → layers; weak forces between layers → slide → soft; one delocalised electron per carbon → conducts. The "3 bonds, 4th electron delocalised" sentence is the mark.
4. **Silicon dioxide**: Si 4 bonds, O 2 bonds, tetrahedral, like diamond.
5. **Diamond and graphite are both carbon** — the difference is entirely in structure; link every property to a structural feature.
:::

::: example Melting point
Explain why silicon dioxide has a much higher melting point than carbon dioxide. (3)

Silicon dioxide is a giant covalent lattice in which every silicon atom is bonded to four oxygen atoms and every oxygen to two silicon atoms (1); melting it requires breaking many strong covalent bonds, which needs a very large amount of energy (1). Carbon dioxide consists of small separate molecules with only weak intermolecular forces between them, which need little energy to overcome, so it is a gas at room temperature (1).
:::

::: example Graphite conducts
Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. (3)

In graphite each carbon atom forms only three covalent bonds, so the fourth outer electron of each atom is not used in bonding (1); these electrons are delocalised and free to move along the layers, carrying charge (1). In diamond every carbon uses all four outer electrons in bonds, so there are no free electrons (1).
:::

::: example Graphite is soft
Explain why graphite is used as a lubricant. (2)

Its carbon atoms are bonded in flat layers with only weak forces between the layers (1), so the layers slide easily over each other, making graphite soft and slippery (1).
:::

::: example Diamond is hard
Explain why diamond is used in cutting tools. (2)

Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional lattice (1); the strong bonds in every direction resist deformation, making diamond extremely hard (1).
:::

::: q
Why does graphite have a very high melting point even though it is soft? (2)

::: q
Why is silicon dioxide insoluble in water? (1)
---
The strong covalent bonds of the lattice cannot be broken by water molecules.
:::

::: q
Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Explain why their properties are so different. (2)
---
Their atoms are bonded differently: diamond's carbons form four bonds in a rigid 3-D lattice; graphite's form three bonds in layers (1). Properties depend on structure, so the rigid lattice is hard and non-conducting while the layered structure is soft and conducts (1).
:::

- Graphite conducts because each carbon bonds to **three** others, leaving **one delocalised electron**. That exact detail scores.
- Graphite is soft because of **weak forces between layers**, not weak bonds.
- Silicon dioxide: **Si–4, O–2**, tetrahedral.
- Diamond's hardness: **four bonds per atom, rigid 3-D lattice**.
