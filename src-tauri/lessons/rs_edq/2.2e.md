## What it is

This lesson covers Christian **worship** and **prayer**. You need: the **nature and significance of liturgical, informal and individual worship** (**Matthew 18:20**); the **nature and importance of prayer**, including the **Lord's Prayer**; **set prayers and informal prayers**; and the different forms of worship across Christian traditions, with reference to the **Society of Friends (Quakers)** and **Evangelical worship**.

This is part of Question 2 (Practices) of Component 2. The (d) on Practices has **no SPaG**, but the examiners still reward accurate specialist terms. In June 2025 the 2(d) asked about the main role of the church, and many candidates simply wrote out a learnt essay on types of worship. Use this topic's knowledge only where the question asks for it.

### Why Christians worship

**Worship** is showing love, respect and devotion to God. Christians worship to **praise** and **thank** God, to say sorry and receive **forgiveness**, to ask for help for themselves and others, to hear God's word in the **Bible**, and to grow in faith. Worship can be **public** (with others) or **private** (alone).

**Matthew 18:20**: Jesus said, "**For where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them.**" Christians take from this that:

- Christ is **present** whenever believers meet to worship, even in a tiny group;
- worship is meant to be **shared**: Christians support one another as the body of Christ;
- the size or grandeur of the building does not matter; what matters is meeting **in Jesus' name**.

### Liturgical worship

**Liturgical worship** follows a **set order** of service with **set words** and actions, usually written in a book and led by a **priest** or minister.

- **Examples**: the Catholic **Mass**; the Orthodox **Divine Liturgy**; Anglican services from the **Book of Common Prayer** or **Common Worship**.
- **Features**: set prayers and responses said by the congregation; Bible readings fixed by a calendar (the **lectionary**); the **Eucharist** at its centre; a priest in **vestments** whose colours follow the church year; ritual actions such as making the **sign of the cross**, kneeling and genuflecting; incense, candles and icons (especially Orthodox).
- **Significance**: it links Christians with **tradition** going back centuries; Catholics can join the same Mass anywhere in the world, so it expresses **unity**; familiar words help people pray when they cannot find their own; the liturgical year takes worshippers through the whole story of Jesus; the solemnity expresses awe at God's holiness.
- **Criticisms**: some Christians find it repetitive, so the words can be said without thought; it can feel formal to newcomers.

### Informal (non-liturgical) worship

**Informal worship** has **no fixed order**. The leader, often a minister or pastor, plans each service, and much of it can be **spontaneous**.

- **Examples**: many **Evangelical**, **Pentecostal**, Baptist and Methodist churches, and many newer independent churches.
- **Features**: **spontaneous (extempore) prayer**; modern worship songs led by a band; **Bible reading** and a long **sermon** as the focus; members may share a **testimony** of what God has done in their lives; services may be held in halls, schools or homes.
- **Significance**: worshippers feel free to respond to the **Holy Spirit**; language and music connect with everyday life and young people; personal faith is emphasised.
- **Criticisms**: services can depend heavily on one leader; some feel it lacks the depth and reverence of tradition.

### Individual (private) worship

**Individual worship** is worship a Christian does **alone**, usually at home.

- **Forms**: personal prayer; Bible reading and study; **meditation** or quiet reflection; daily devotional notes or apps; saying the **rosary** (Catholic); praying before **icons** in a home "prayer corner" (Orthodox); keeping a prayer journal.
- **Significance**: it builds a **personal relationship** with God; it can happen anywhere and at any time; Jesus taught "when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father" (Matthew 6:6).
- **Balance**: most churches teach that private prayer and shared worship belong together, because of Matthew 18:20 and the early Christians who met together regularly (Acts 2:42).

### Prayer

**Prayer** is **communicating with God**: speaking and listening to him. Christians believe God hears and answers prayer, though not always in the way people expect. A common way of remembering its kinds is **ACTS**:

| Kind | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Adoration** | praising God for who he is |
| **Confession** | saying sorry for sin and asking forgiveness |
| **Thanksgiving** | thanking God for his gifts |
| **Supplication** | asking for help, for oneself (petition) or for others (**intercession**) |

**Why prayer matters**: it is the heart of a relationship with God; Jesus himself prayed often (for example in Gethsemane); it brings comfort, strength and guidance; through **intercession** Christians care for others; it helps believers know and do God's will.

### The Lord's Prayer

The **Lord's Prayer** (**Our Father**) is the prayer **Jesus taught** his disciples when they asked him how to pray (**Matthew 6:9-13**, **Luke 11:2-4**). It is the one prayer shared by **almost all Christians**.

| Line | What it teaches |
|---|---|
| "Our Father in heaven" | God is a loving parent, and "our" makes all Christians one family. |
| "Hallowed be your name" | God is **holy**; his name must be honoured (**adoration**). |
| "Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as in heaven" | Christians long for God's rule of justice and peace and commit to doing his will now. |
| "Give us today our daily bread" | Dependence on God for everyday needs (**supplication**); a simple request, not luxury. |
| "Forgive us our sins, as we forgive those who sin against us" | **Confession**; receiving forgiveness means forgiving others. |
| "Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil" | A request for protection and strength. |

Many churches end with a **doxology**: "For the kingdom, the power and the glory are yours, now and for ever. Amen." It is said at the Eucharist, at baptisms, weddings and funerals, in school assemblies and in private.

**Significance**: it is a **set prayer** (said word for word) and a **model** for informal prayer (praise first, then God's will, then needs, forgiveness and protection). It unites Christians across denominations.

### Set prayers and informal prayers

| Set prayers | Informal prayers |
|---|---|
| Fixed words, written down and repeated. | The person's **own words**, spontaneous. |
| e.g. the Lord's Prayer; the **Hail Mary** (Catholic); the **Jesus Prayer** (Orthodox: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me"); the **collect** of the day (Anglican). | e.g. praying aloud in a prayer meeting, quiet personal prayer, prayer for a friend in need. |
| **Strengths**: words of great depth and beauty; unite people saying them together; help when you do not know what to say. | **Strengths**: personal, heartfelt, honest; fit the exact situation. |
| **Weakness**: can be said without thinking ("vain repetition", Matthew 6:7). | **Weakness**: can lack focus; some find it hard to know what to say. |

**Aids to prayer**: the **rosary** (beads used by Catholics to pray the Hail Mary while meditating on events in the lives of Jesus and Mary); **icons** (Orthodox images through which believers feel drawn into God's presence; they venerate, not worship, them); candles; crucifixes; the simple chants of **Taizé** (2.2g).

### Worship in the Society of Friends (Quakers)

The **Religious Society of Friends** began in England in the 17th century with **George Fox**. Quaker worship is very different from most churches.

- **Meeting for worship** takes place in a plain **meeting house**, with chairs arranged in a **circle or square** around a central table, which may have a Bible, Quaker writings and flowers.
- It is held mostly in **silence**: Friends wait quietly to be open to God ("expectant waiting"). It usually lasts about an hour.
- There is **no priest or minister**, **no set liturgy**, no hymns and usually no outward **sacraments**.
- **Anyone** who feels moved by the Spirit may stand and speak briefly (giving **ministry**), share a reading or pray aloud; the meeting then returns to silence.
- The meeting ends when appointed Friends **shake hands**, and then everyone does.
- **Beliefs behind it**: there is "**that of God in everyone**", so all are equal and need no one to stand between them and God; the Spirit can speak through anyone; the whole of life is sacred, so no special rituals are needed. Quaker **testimonies** to peace, equality, simplicity and truth come from this.

### Evangelical worship

**Evangelical** Christians stress the authority of the **Bible**, a personal relationship with Jesus, being "**born again**" (John 3:3) and sharing the gospel (**evangelism**, 2.2h).

- Worship is usually **informal**: lively modern songs led by a **worship band**, often for a long block of time; people may clap, raise their hands or dance.
- The **sermon** is central, explaining a Bible passage and applying it to life, often with a call to commitment.
- **Spontaneous prayer**, testimonies and small-group Bible study during the week.
- In **charismatic** and **Pentecostal** churches, gifts of the **Holy Spirit** (Acts 2) such as **speaking in tongues**, prophecy and prayer for healing (2.2c).
- The Eucharist (often called the **Lord's Supper**) is usually less frequent and seen as a **memorial** (2.2f).

### Diversity: a quick comparison

| | Catholic / Orthodox | Anglican | Evangelical / Pentecostal | Quaker |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Order | fixed liturgy | set but flexible | informal, spontaneous | silent waiting |
| Leader | priest | priest/vicar | pastor, band, members | none |
| Focus | Eucharist | Word and sacrament | Bible, sermon, Spirit | inner light, silence |
| Music | chant, hymns | hymns, choirs | worship bands | usually none |

::: terms
- **Worship**: showing love, respect and devotion to God, publicly or privately.
- **Liturgical worship**: worship that follows a set order of service, with set words and actions, often led by a priest.
- **Informal worship**: non-liturgical worship with no fixed order, often including spontaneous prayer and modern songs.
- **Individual worship**: worship a Christian does alone, such as personal prayer, Bible reading or meditation.
- **Prayer**: communicating with God, through praise, confession, thanksgiving and asking.
- **The Lord's Prayer**: the prayer Jesus taught his disciples (Matthew 6:9-13), beginning "Our Father".
- **Set prayer**: a prayer with fixed words that is repeated, such as the Lord's Prayer or the Hail Mary.
- **Informal prayer**: a spontaneous prayer in the person's own words.
- **Intercession**: praying to God on behalf of other people.
- **Society of Friends (Quakers)**: a Christian denomination whose worship is mainly silent waiting, with no priest or set liturgy.
- **Evangelical**: Christians who stress the Bible, personal faith in Jesus, being born again and spreading the gospel.
- **Rosary**: a string of beads Catholics use to count prayers while meditating on the lives of Jesus and Mary.
:::

::: example (b) Quaker worship
Describe worship in the Society of Friends (Quakers). (5)

Quakers meet for worship in a plain meeting house, sitting on chairs arranged in a circle or square around a table that may hold a Bible and flowers. The meeting is held mainly in silence, as Friends wait expectantly to be open to God, usually for about an hour. There is no priest, no set liturgy and no hymns. Anyone who feels moved by the Spirit may stand and speak, read or pray aloud, and then the silence returns. The meeting ends when appointed Friends shake hands. Quakers worship like this because they believe there is "that of God in everyone", so all are equal and anyone can be inspired by the Spirit.

::: example (c) forms of worship
Explain different forms of Christian worship. (8)

Many Christians, including Catholics, Orthodox and Anglicans, use liturgical worship, which follows a set order with set prayers, such as the Mass or the Divine Liturgy. It links them to centuries of tradition and to Christians worldwide, and it centres on the Eucharist. Evangelical and Pentecostal Christians usually prefer informal worship with no fixed order: modern songs, spontaneous prayer, testimonies and a long sermon. They believe this lets the Holy Spirit guide worship, and some speak in tongues as at Pentecost. Quakers sit mostly in silence, with no priest, and anyone may speak if moved by the Spirit, because there is "that of God in everyone". Most Christians also worship individually through personal prayer and Bible reading, following Jesus' teaching to pray to the Father in private (Matthew 6:6). Shared worship matters too, because Jesus promised, "where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them" (Matthew 18:20).

[Band 4, 8 marks: three forms plus Quaker and Evangelical; sources explained; reasons given.]

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Private prayer is more important than worshipping in church.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some Christians would agree, since Jesus taught his followers to go into their room, shut the door and pray to the Father in secret (Matthew 6:6), warning against praying to be seen by others. Private prayer builds a personal relationship with God and can happen every day, whereas church may be only weekly. However, others argue that worship in church is just as important, because Jesus promised to be present "where two or three gather in my name" (Matthew 18:20), and the first Christians devoted themselves to fellowship, breaking bread and prayer together (Acts 2:42). For Catholics, attending Mass on Sundays is an obligation, as the Eucharist can only be received in community. Even Quakers, who value silence, meet together to wait on God. Overall, the strongest view is that they are not rivals: private prayer sustains faith day by day, but shared worship keeps it rooted in the Church.
:::
