## What it is

This lesson covers what Muslims believe about **the nature of Allah**: **Tawhid** (the oneness of Allah, **Qur'an 3:18**); the attributes of Allah that the spec names: **immanence**, **transcendence**, **omnipotence**, **beneficence**, **mercy**, **fairness and justice** (**Qur'an 46:33**); and **Adalat**, the belief in divine justice that is one of the roots of faith in **Shi'a Islam**.

It is examined in Question 1 (Beliefs and Teachings) of Component 3: (a) 2, (b) 5, (c) 8, (d) 15 = 30 marks, with **no SPaG** on this paper. **Tawhid** is one of the eight (a) concepts, and the June 2025 paper asked exactly "What do Muslims mean by 'tawhid'?". This paper is marked **inside Islam**: comparisons with Christianity earn nothing, but differences between **Sunni** and **Shi'a** Muslims are credited.

### Tawhid: the oneness of Allah

**Tawhid** means "making one" or "oneness". It is the belief that **Allah is One**: there is only one God, who is unique, has no partners, no equals, no parents and no children, and cannot be divided. Every other Muslim belief and practice grows from it, so Muslims often call it the **foundation** of Islam.

- **Qur'an 3:18**: Allah Himself bears witness that there is no god but Him, and so do the angels and those who have knowledge, and He upholds justice. The verse links Allah's oneness with His **justice**, which is why both appear in this lesson.
- **Surah al-Ikhlas (Surah 112)**, often said to be worth a third of the Qur'an in meaning: Allah is One; He is the Eternal, on whom all depend; He does not give birth and was not born; and there is nothing comparable to Him.
- **The Shahadah** begins "There is no god but Allah", so a Muslim declares Tawhid every time they pray (see 2.3f).
- **Qur'an 42:11**: "There is nothing like unto Him." Allah cannot be compared to anything in creation.

**Shirk** is the opposite of Tawhid: associating partners with Allah, treating anything (an idol, a person, money, power, one's own desires) as equal to Him. Muslims regard shirk as the **gravest sin**; the Qur'an says Allah does not forgive association with Him, though He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills (Qur'an 4:48). Scholars explain this as meaning the person who dies committing shirk without repenting; a person who repents can be forgiven.

**How Tawhid affects Muslims' lives**:

| Belief | What it leads to in practice |
|---|---|
| Allah alone is God | Worship (salah, du'a) is directed to Allah alone, never to prophets, saints or angels |
| Allah cannot be pictured | **No images** of Allah or the prophets; mosques are decorated with **calligraphy** and geometric patterns instead |
| Allah is the only Creator | The whole world belongs to Him; humans are **khalifah** (stewards), not owners |
| One God, one humanity | The **ummah** (worldwide Muslim community) is united, regardless of race or nation |
| Allah's will comes first | Muslims say "insha'Allah" (if Allah wills) and try to submit their whole life to Him; "Islam" means submission |

Many scholars describe Tawhid in three parts: the oneness of Allah's **lordship** (He alone creates and controls everything), the oneness of **worship** (He alone deserves to be worshipped), and the oneness of His **names and attributes** (His qualities are unique and perfect).

### The 99 names of Allah

The Qur'an says "to Allah belong the best names, so call on Him by them" (Qur'an 7:180). Muslim tradition lists **99 names** (al-Asma' al-Husna), such as ar-Rahman (the Most Gracious), ar-Rahim (the Most Merciful), al-Khaliq (the Creator), al-'Adl (the Just), al-Qadir (the All-Powerful) and al-Wadud (the Loving). Some Muslims use **prayer beads** (subhah or misbaha, often in 33 or 99 beads) to recite the names. The names describe **what Allah is like** without making Him like creation: they tell humans about Him while He remains beyond full understanding.

### The attributes of Allah

| Attribute | Meaning | Source or example |
|---|---|---|
| **Immanence** | Allah is **close to** and **active within** the world and each person's life | "We are closer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16) |
| **Transcendence** | Allah is **beyond** the world, outside time and space, greater than anything humans can imagine or picture | "Vision perceives Him not, but He perceives all vision" (Qur'an 6:103); Qur'an 42:11 |
| **Omnipotence** | Allah is **all-powerful**: He created everything and can do anything | Qur'an 46:33 (below); Allah says "Be" and it is |
| **Beneficence** | Allah is **all-loving and good**, and gives generously to His creation | Rain, food, life itself; the name ar-Rahman |
| **Mercy** | Allah is **compassionate and forgiving** towards those who turn to Him | Bismillah; "do not despair of the mercy of Allah" (Qur'an 39:53) |
| **Fairness and justice** | Allah treats every person **fairly** and will judge everyone justly | Qur'an 3:18; Allah does not wrong anyone by even an atom's weight (Qur'an 4:40) |

**Qur'an 46:33** asks whether people do not see that Allah, who created the heavens and the earth and was not wearied by creating them, is able to **give life to the dead**; it answers that He is indeed able to do **all things**. The verse shows Allah's **omnipotence** (creating the universe without effort), and links it to the **Akhirah**: the God who made everything can raise the dead for judgement. It also implies **justice**, because resurrection leads to a fair judgement of every life.

**Immanence and transcendence together**: Muslims believe Allah is both. He is utterly beyond creation (so He must never be pictured or compared to anything), and yet He is closer to each person than their own jugular vein, hearing every prayer and knowing every thought. Muslims see no contradiction: an all-powerful God is not limited to being either near or far. In practice, transcendence leads to **awe and humility** (for example, prostration in salah), while immanence leads to **personal prayer** (du'a) in any language at any time, and to the awareness that Allah sees everything (**taqwa**, God-consciousness).

**Beneficence and mercy**: every surah of the Qur'an except one (Surah 9) begins with the **Bismillah**: "In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious (ar-Rahman), the Most Merciful (ar-Rahim)". Muslims say it before eating, travelling, studying and many other daily actions, so Allah's mercy is remembered constantly. In a well-known hadith qudsi (a saying of Allah reported by the Prophet), Allah says that His **mercy prevails over His anger**. Mercy shapes behaviour: Muslims try to be merciful to others, to forgive, to give charity (zakah, sadaqah) and to care for animals and the environment.

**Fairness and justice**: Allah is **al-'Adl**, the Just. He gives every person the guidance they need, judges each by their intentions and actions, and never punishes anyone for someone else's sin (Islam does not teach inherited original sin). Belief in justice gives Muslims hope that wrongs will be put right on the **Day of Judgement**, and it calls them to act justly themselves: the Qur'an commands believers to stand firm for justice even against themselves or their relatives (Qur'an 4:135).

**Mercy and justice in balance**: Muslims believe Allah's mercy and justice work together. Justice means every deed is weighed fairly; mercy means Allah can forgive the sincere person who repents (tawbah), and multiply rewards for good deeds. Some Muslims emphasise mercy more (it "prevails"); others warn against relying on mercy while ignoring Allah's commands. Both views are found among Sunni and Shi'a Muslims.

### Adalat in Shi'a Islam

**Adalat** (from 'adl, justice) is the belief that **Allah is perfectly just** and **never acts unfairly or wrongly**. It is the **second of the five roots of Usul ad-Din** in **Shi'a Islam** (Tawhid, Adalat, Nubuwwah, Imamah, Mi'ad; see 2.3b).

- Sunni Muslims also believe Allah is just; what is distinctive is that Shi'a Muslims make Adalat a **separate root of faith**, which shows how central it is to them.
- **Reason and goodness**: Shi'a theology teaches that good and evil are real and can be recognised by human reason, and that Allah always does what is good and wise. Allah would never command something evil or punish someone unjustly.
- **Free will and responsibility**: because Allah is just, He would not punish people for actions they were forced to do. So Shi'a scholars stress that humans have **genuine free will** and are **responsible** for their own choices. A saying attributed to **Imam Ja'far as-Sadiq** describes the human situation as neither total compulsion nor total independence, but "a matter between the two" (see 2.3e).
- **Judgement and suffering**: Adalat assures Shi'a Muslims that the Day of Judgement will be completely fair, and that suffering in this life is not pointless: it may be a test, and those who suffer unjustly will be compensated.
- **Influence**: Adalat inspires Shi'a Muslims to **work for justice** on earth. The stand of **Imam Husayn** against injustice at Karbala (remembered at **Ashura**, see 2.3i) is seen as an example of this.

### Shariah: the straight path

**Shariah** (often translated "the straight path" or "the path to water") is **Islamic law and guidance**, drawn from the **Qur'an** and the **Sunnah** (the example of the Prophet Muhammad), and interpreted by scholars. It is one of the eight (a) concepts. It flows from Tawhid: because Allah is the one Creator and the Just, His guidance shows humans how to live rightly. Shariah covers worship (prayer, fasting), family life, business, food (**halal** and **haram**) and justice. Muslims see it as a mercy, a path to lead them safely to Allah. Sunni Muslims follow one of four main schools of law (madhhabs); Twelver Shi'a Muslims follow the Ja'fari school and the rulings of a living senior scholar (marja').

::: terms
- **Tawhid**: the belief in the oneness of Allah; there is only one God, unique and without partners.
- **Shirk**: associating partners with Allah, regarded by Muslims as the gravest sin.
- **Immanence**: Allah being close to and active within the world and each person's life.
- **Transcendence**: Allah being beyond the world, outside time and space, and beyond human understanding.
- **Omnipotence**: Allah being all-powerful, the Creator who is able to do all things (Qur'an 46:33).
- **Beneficence**: Allah being all-loving and generous, giving good things to His creation.
- **Mercy**: Allah's compassion and forgiveness towards those who turn back to Him.
- **Justice**: Allah's perfect fairness in His treatment and judgement of every person.
- **Adalat**: the Shi'a root of faith that Allah is perfectly just and never acts wrongly, so humans are responsible.
- **Shariah**: the straight path; Islamic law and guidance drawn from the Qur'an and the Sunnah.
- **Bismillah**: the phrase "In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful".
- **99 names**: the beautiful names of Allah (al-Asma' al-Husna) that describe His qualities.
:::

::: example (c) the importance of Tawhid
Explain the importance of Tawhid for Muslims. (8)

Tawhid, the oneness of Allah, is the most important belief in Islam because everything else depends on it. Qur'an 3:18 says Allah Himself bears witness "that there is no god but He", and Surah al-Ikhlas teaches that He neither begets nor is begotten and that nothing is comparable to Him. This is why the opposite, shirk, is the gravest sin: putting anything in Allah's place, whether an idol, money or one's own desires, breaks the basis of faith (Qur'an 4:48).

Tawhid shapes worship. Muslims pray only to Allah, and the first words of the Shahadah, "There is no god but Allah", are repeated in every salah and whispered to newborn babies, so a Muslim's life begins and ends with Tawhid. Because Allah is transcendent and nothing is like Him (Qur'an 42:11), there are no images of Him, so mosques use calligraphy and patterns instead.

Tawhid also shapes how Muslims live. Since one God created everything, humans are khalifah, stewards responsible for His world, and all people are equal before Him, which unites the ummah across races and nations. For Shi'a Muslims Tawhid is the first of the five roots of Usul ad-Din, and Sunni Muslims place belief in Allah first among the six articles of faith; so both traditions agree that it is the foundation. A Muslim who truly believes in Tawhid tries to submit every part of life to Allah, which is the meaning of "Islam".

[Band 4, 8 marks: detailed, sources explained, influence on individuals and community, Sunni and Shi'a.]

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Allah's mercy is more important than His justice.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Many Muslims would agree, because almost every surah begins by calling Allah "the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful", and in a hadith qudsi Allah says His mercy prevails over His anger. This is a strong argument because it comes from Allah's own words, and it gives sinners hope: "do not despair of the mercy of Allah" (Qur'an 39:53). However, others would argue that mercy without justice would make the Day of Judgement meaningless; the Qur'an says Allah upholds justice (3:18) and wrongs no one by an atom's weight (4:40), and Shi'a Muslims make Adalat a root of their faith. This argument is also strong, because if wrongdoing were simply ignored, victims would never be treated fairly. Therefore the best view may be that the two are not rivals: Allah's justice is merciful, since it weighs every intention and multiplies good deeds, and His mercy is just, since it is given to those who sincerely repent.
