## What it is

This lesson covers Christian beliefs about **Jesus Christ** and **salvation**. You need: the **incarnation** (**John 1:14**, **Luke 1:28-33**); the **crucifixion** (**Matthew 27:28-50**); **salvation and atonement** (**Matthew 26:26-29**, **Leviticus 16:20-22**, **Isaiah 53:3-9**); the **resurrection** (**Luke 24:1-9**, **1 Corinthians 15:3-8, 12-14**); the **ascension** (**Luke 24:50-53**); **sin** as preventing salvation; and **grace and the Spirit** (**Acts 2:1-6**), including the role of the **Holy Spirit in Evangelical worship**.

This is Question 1 (Beliefs) of Component 2, with **6 SPaG marks** on the (d). Three of the eight (a) concepts come from here: **incarnation**, **atonement** and **resurrection**.

### The incarnation

The **incarnation** means God becoming **human** ("in the flesh") in **Jesus**. Christians believe Jesus is **fully God and fully human**: not half of each, and not God pretending to be human.

- **John 1:14**: "The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us." The eternal Word (the Son, through whom all things were made, John 1:1-3) took on a real human life.
- **Luke 1:28-33, the Annunciation**: the angel **Gabriel** greeted **Mary** as highly favoured, with the Lord with her. He told her she would conceive and give birth to a son, to be named **Jesus**, who would be great and called the **Son of the Most High**; God would give him the throne of his ancestor **David**, and his kingdom would never end.
- Christians believe Jesus was conceived by the power of the **Holy Spirit** and born of the **Virgin Mary** (Luke 1:35 follows straight on).
- **Why it matters**: God is not distant: he shares human life, temptation and suffering; Jesus reveals what God is like ("anyone who has seen me has seen the Father", John 14:9); only someone both God and human could bring God and humanity back together.
- **Divergent views**: Catholics and Orthodox give **Mary** great honour as **Mother of God**; most Protestants respect her but focus on Christ alone. A minority of liberal Christians read the virgin birth symbolically while keeping the belief that God was uniquely present in Jesus.

### The crucifixion

**Matthew 27:28-50**: the soldiers stripped Jesus, put a **scarlet robe** on him, twisted a **crown of thorns** onto his head, put a staff in his hand, knelt and mocked him ("Hail, king of the Jews!"), spat on him and struck him. On the way out, they forced **Simon of Cyrene** to carry the cross. At **Golgotha** ("the place of the skull") they offered him wine mixed with gall, crucified him, and divided his clothes by casting lots. Above his head was the charge: "This is Jesus, the King of the Jews." Two rebels were crucified with him. Passers-by, the chief priests and elders mocked him: he saved others but cannot save himself. From noon until three there was **darkness** over the land. Jesus cried out, "**My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?**" Someone offered him wine vinegar on a sponge; then he cried out again and **gave up his spirit**.

**Meaning for Christians**:

- Jesus' suffering was real: he was fully human.
- His cry of abandonment shows he took on the full weight of human sin and separation from God; it also echoes Psalm 22, which ends in trust.
- He died **willingly** for others; the cross, once a symbol of shame, became the central Christian symbol of God's love (John 3:16).
- Christians remember it on **Good Friday** and every time they share the **Eucharist**.

### Salvation and atonement

**Salvation** is being **saved** from sin and its consequences (separation from God, death) and brought into eternal life with God. **Atonement** (at-one-ment) is the **repairing of the relationship** between God and humans through Jesus' death and resurrection.

- **Leviticus 16:20-22, the scapegoat**: on the **Day of Atonement**, the high priest Aaron laid both hands on a live goat, **confessed** over it all the sins of the Israelites, and sent it away into the **wilderness**, carrying their sins away. Christians see this as a picture of Jesus **bearing** humanity's sins.
- **Isaiah 53:3-9, the suffering servant**: a servant **despised and rejected**, familiar with suffering, who was **pierced for our transgressions** and crushed for our iniquities; by his wounds we are **healed**. Like a **lamb led to the slaughter**, he did not open his mouth, and he was assigned a grave with the wicked and the rich, though he had done no wrong. Christians believe this prophecy was fulfilled in Jesus.
- **Matthew 26:26-29, the Last Supper**: Jesus took bread, gave thanks, broke it and said, "Take and eat; this is my body." He took the cup and said it was his "**blood of the covenant**, which is poured out for many for the **forgiveness of sins**." He would not drink wine again until he drank it new in his Father's kingdom. Christians see his death as a **sacrifice** sealing a **new covenant**, remembered in the Eucharist.

**Different understandings of the atonement**:

| Theory | Idea | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Sacrifice / substitution** | Jesus took the punishment for sin in humanity's place, as the perfect sacrifice; the view many evangelicals hold as **penal substitution**. | Isaiah 53:5; Leviticus 16 |
| **Ransom** | Jesus' death paid the price to free humans from slavery to sin and death. | Mark 10:45 ("a ransom for many") |
| **Victory** (Christus Victor) | Through the cross and resurrection Jesus defeated sin, death and evil. | 1 Corinthians 15 |
| **Moral example** | Jesus' self-giving love moves people to repent and to love in return. | John 15:13 |

Most Christians see truth in more than one theory. Catholics and Orthodox also stress that salvation is lived out through the Church and the **sacraments**.

### Sin as preventing salvation

**Sin** is any thought or action that goes against God's will. Christians believe sin **separates** humans from God, who is perfectly holy. **Original Sin** (from the Fall, Genesis 3) is the inherited tendency to sin; **personal sin** is what each person chooses. Paul teaches that "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23) and that "the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus" (Romans 6:23). Humans cannot remove sin by their own efforts, which is why they need Jesus' saving work.

### Grace

**Grace** is God's **free, undeserved love and gift** of salvation. "It is by grace you have been saved, through faith… it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8). Protestants since the Reformation stress salvation by **grace through faith** alone; Catholics agree that salvation is God's gift of grace, received especially through the **sacraments**, and say that faith should be shown in good works. All agree that salvation cannot be earned.

### The resurrection

- **Luke 24:1-9**: very early on the first day of the week, the women went to the tomb with spices. They found the **stone rolled away** and the **body gone**. Two men in gleaming clothes asked, "Why do you look for the living among the dead? He is not here; he has risen!" and reminded them that Jesus had said he would be crucified and rise on the third day. The women went and told the Eleven.
- **1 Corinthians 15:3-8**: Paul passes on what he received: Christ **died for our sins** according to the Scriptures, was **buried**, was **raised on the third day**, and **appeared** to Peter (Cephas), then the Twelve, then more than **five hundred** at once (most still alive when Paul wrote), then James, all the apostles, and last of all to Paul himself.
- **1 Corinthians 15:12-14**: if there is no resurrection of the dead, then Christ has not been raised; and if Christ has not been raised, Christian **preaching is useless** and so is **faith**.
- **Why it matters**: it shows Jesus is the Son of God and that his sacrifice was accepted; it defeats **death**; it promises believers their own resurrection and eternal life; it is celebrated every **Easter** and every Sunday.
- **Divergent views**: most Christians believe in a **bodily** resurrection, the empty tomb being literal; some liberal Christians understand it as a **spiritual** reality, the disciples' experience that Jesus was alive with them, while still calling it central.

### The ascension

**Luke 24:50-53**: Jesus led the disciples out to the vicinity of **Bethany**, lifted up his hands and **blessed** them; while he was blessing them, he left them and was **taken up into heaven**. They **worshipped** him and returned to Jerusalem with **great joy**, and stayed continually at the temple praising God. (Acts 1 describes the ascension too, after forty days.)

Christians believe the ascension shows Jesus returning to the **Father** in glory, ruling with him ("seated at the right hand of the Father" in the creeds); it ends his earthly appearances, prepares for the coming of the **Holy Spirit**, and points to his promised **return** (the Second Coming).

### The Holy Spirit and Evangelical worship

- **Acts 2:1-6, Pentecost**: the disciples were together when a sound like a **violent wind** filled the house, and what looked like **tongues of fire** rested on each of them. They were all **filled with the Holy Spirit** and began to speak in other **tongues** (languages). A crowd of Jews from every nation gathered, amazed that each heard them speaking in his own language. Christians see Pentecost as the **birthday of the Church**: the Spirit gives courage and power to spread the gospel.
- **The Spirit and grace**: Christians believe the Spirit brings God's grace to believers, helps them to pray, guides them, and produces "fruit" such as love, joy and peace (Galatians 5:22-23).
- **Evangelical and charismatic worship**: in many Evangelical, **Pentecostal** and **charismatic** churches, the Holy Spirit is central to worship. Services are often **informal** and lively, with modern music, raised hands, and spontaneous prayer. Believers may experience **baptism in the Spirit**, **speaking in tongues** (glossolalia), **prophecy** and prayer for **healing**, which they see as the same gifts given at Pentecost.
- **Divergent views**: Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican Christians also believe in the Spirit's work, often seen in the sacraments (e.g. confirmation) and in calm, liturgical worship; some Christians believe the more dramatic gifts were mainly for the early Church.

::: terms
- **Incarnation**: God becoming human in Jesus Christ, who is fully God and fully human.
- **Crucifixion**: Jesus' execution by being nailed to a cross, which Christians believe was for the sins of the world.
- **Atonement**: the repairing of the relationship between God and humans through Jesus' death and resurrection.
- **Salvation**: being saved from sin and its consequences and brought into eternal life with God.
- **Resurrection**: Jesus rising from the dead on the third day after his crucifixion.
- **Ascension**: Jesus being taken up into heaven forty days after his resurrection.
- **Sin**: any thought or action that goes against the will of God and separates people from him.
- **Grace**: God's free, undeserved love and gift of salvation to humans.
- **Pentecost**: the day the Holy Spirit came upon the disciples (Acts 2), seen as the birth of the Church.
- **Speaking in tongues**: praying or praising in a language unknown to the speaker, a gift of the Spirit.
:::

::: example (c) salvation
Explain Christian beliefs about salvation. (8)

Christians believe sin separates humans from God, since "all have sinned" (Romans 3:23), and humans cannot save themselves. Salvation comes through Jesus' death and resurrection, which bring atonement, repairing the relationship with God. Christians see this foreshadowed in the scapegoat that carried Israel's sins into the wilderness (Leviticus 16:20-22) and in the suffering servant "pierced for our transgressions" (Isaiah 53:5). At the Last Supper Jesus called the wine his "blood of the covenant… for the forgiveness of sins" (Matthew 26:28). Christians explain this differently: many evangelicals believe Jesus took the punishment in our place; others stress his victory over death or his example of love. Salvation is a gift of grace (Ephesians 2:8). Protestants stress faith alone, while Catholics teach that grace is received through the sacraments and shown in good works. The resurrection confirms that death is defeated.

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'The resurrection is the most important event in Jesus' life.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Many Christians would agree, because Paul writes that if Christ has not been raised, Christian preaching and faith are useless (1 Corinthians 15:14). The resurrection shows that Jesus' sacrifice was accepted and that death is defeated, and it gives believers hope of their own resurrection; this is why Easter is the greatest Christian festival. This argument is strong because without it the crucifixion would look like a defeat. However, others argue that the crucifixion is more important, as it is the moment of atonement, when Jesus bore the sins of the world (Isaiah 53:5). Still others say the incarnation comes first, since without God becoming human there would be no saving death or resurrection at all. Overall, the resurrection may be the most decisive event, but it only has meaning as part of a single story of incarnation, cross and new life.
