## What it is

This lesson covers Muslim beliefs about **angels** (**malaikah**): the **significance of angels** in Islam (**Qur'an 2:97-98**, **2:285**); Muslim beliefs about **angels and free will**; the significance of **Jibril**'s revelation of the Qur'an to Muhammad (**2:97-98**); **Mika'il**, placed in charge of plants and rain; and **Israfil**, who will announce the Day of Resurrection.

It is examined in Question 1 (Beliefs and Teachings) of Component 3: (a) 2, (b) 5, (c) 8, (d) 15, with **no SPaG**. The June 2025 (b) was "Describe Muslim beliefs about Jibril"; the report said candidates who knew Jibril's roles, and added general beliefs about angels that also apply to him, reached the top band. Stay inside Islam; Sunni and Shi'a views are credited.

### What angels are

- **Created by Allah from light** (nur), according to a hadith recorded by Muslim; the **jinn** were created from smokeless fire, and **Adam** from clay.
- **Invisible** to humans unless Allah allows; they can take human form, as Jibril did when he questioned the Prophet in the Hadith of Jibril, and when he appeared to Maryam (Qur'an 19:17).
- They are described as having **wings**, "two, three or four" (Qur'an 35:1), and they do not eat, drink, sleep or marry. They are neither male nor female.
- They are **countless** and **constantly worship** Allah, praising Him without tiring.
- They are **servants and messengers** of Allah: the word malak means messenger. They carry out His commands in the universe.

### Angels and free will

Muslims believe angels have **no free will** in the way humans do. They always **obey Allah** perfectly: the Qur'an describes angels who "do not disobey Allah in what He commands them, but do what they are commanded" (Qur'an 66:6). They have no desires that pull them towards sin.

This makes **humans different**:

| | Angels | Humans | Jinn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Created from | Light | Clay (earth) | Smokeless fire |
| Free will | **No**: always obey | **Yes**: can choose good or evil | **Yes**: can choose good or evil |
| Judged on the Last Day | No | Yes | Yes |
| Example | Jibril, Mika'il, Israfil | Adam | **Iblis** (Shaytan) |

- **Iblis** refused to bow to Adam when Allah commanded (Qur'an 2:34). Muslims do **not** believe he was a fallen angel: the Qur'an says he was **of the jinn** (Qur'an 18:50), which is why he could choose to disobey. (A minority of earlier scholars thought he was among the angels; the common view is that he was a jinn who lived among them.)
- When Allah told the angels He would place a **khalifah** on earth, they asked whether He would place there someone who would cause corruption (Qur'an 2:30). Muslims understand this as a **question** seeking understanding, not disobedience; once Allah showed Adam's knowledge, they bowed.
- Because humans have free will and angels do not, humans who choose to obey Allah despite temptation can, in the view of many scholars, reach a **higher rank** than angels; humans who choose evil fall lower. Humans carry the **trust** (amanah) of choice and **responsibility**.

**Diversity**: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims agree that angels have no free will and always obey. Belief in angels is one of the **six articles of faith** in Sunni Islam; Shi'a Muslims also believe in angels as part of belief in the unseen (al-ghayb) taught by the Qur'an and the prophets, although angels are **not one of the five roots** of Usul ad-Din.

### The significance of angels: Qur'an 2:285 and 2:97-98

- **Qur'an 2:285**: the Messenger believes in what was revealed to him from his Lord, and so do the believers; all believe "in Allah and His angels and His books and His messengers", making no distinction between any of the messengers. This verse makes belief in angels **part of faith itself**: rejecting angels means rejecting part of Islam.
- **Qur'an 2:97-98**: whoever is an enemy to **Jibril**, it is he who brought the Qur'an down **to your heart by Allah's permission**, confirming what came before it, as guidance and good news for believers; whoever is an enemy to Allah, His angels, His messengers, **Jibril** and **Mika'il**, Allah is an enemy to the disbelievers. So angels are honoured as part of Allah's work: opposing them is opposing Allah. These verses are the only place in the Qur'an where **Mika'il** is named.

**Why angels matter to Muslims**:

1. **Revelation**: without Jibril, there would be no Qur'an and no prophets' messages.
2. **Accountability**: angels record every deed, so Muslims are aware they are watched and will be judged.
3. **Allah's care**: angels carry out His providence (rain, protection), showing His mercy and immanence.
4. **The Akhirah**: angels take souls, question the dead, and will announce the resurrection.
5. **Humility and worship**: angels' constant obedience is a model for Muslims' own submission.

### Jibril: the angel of revelation

**Jibril** (Gabriel) is the most important angel, the **messenger** between Allah and the prophets. The Qur'an also calls him the **Holy Spirit** (Ruh al-Qudus) and the **trustworthy spirit**.

- **Revealed the Qur'an**: in 610 CE, on the **Night of Power** (Laylat al-Qadr) in Ramadan, Jibril appeared to Muhammad in the cave of **Hira** and commanded him to "Read!" (or "Recite!"). Muhammad replied that he could not read; Jibril squeezed him and repeated the command, and then revealed the first verses (Surah 96). Over about **23 years**, Jibril brought the whole Qur'an, piece by piece, as events required.
- Jibril **checked the Qur'an** with the Prophet: according to hadith, he reviewed the revelation with Muhammad every Ramadan, and twice in the last year of his life.
- **Taught the Prophet** how to pray (in hadith) and accompanied him on the **Night Journey and Ascension** (al-Isra wal-Mi'raj) from Makkah to Jerusalem and through the heavens.
- **Appeared to Maryam** to announce the birth of **Isa** (Qur'an 19:17-19); supported Isa (Qur'an 2:87).
- **Brought revelation to earlier prophets**: many Muslims believe he brought the earlier kutub.
- In the **Hadith of Jibril** he came in human form to teach the companions the meaning of Islam, iman (faith, including the six articles) and ihsan (worshipping Allah as if you see Him).

**Significance of Jibril's revelation**:

- It shows the Qur'an is **the word of Allah**, not Muhammad's own words: it came "by Allah's permission" through a trustworthy angel (2:97).
- It is the beginning of Muhammad's **prophethood** and the completion of revelation.
- It is why the **Night of Power** is the holiest night of the year (see 2.3i), and why Ramadan is the month of the Qur'an.
- It shows Allah **communicates** with humanity: He is transcendent but chooses to guide.

### Mika'il: the angel of provision

**Mika'il** (Michael) is placed in charge of **plants and rain**: sending rain, making crops grow and providing **sustenance** (food) for all creation, by Allah's command. He is named alongside Jibril in **Qur'an 2:98**.

**Significance**:

- Shows Allah's **beneficence and mercy**: He cares for His creation and provides for every living thing.
- Shows Allah's **control** of nature: rain and harvests are not random but part of His will (al-Qadr).
- Encourages **gratitude**: Muslims thank Allah for food (Bismillah before eating) and pray for rain in times of drought (a special prayer, salat al-istisqa').
- Reminds Muslims of their role as **khalifah**: caring for the natural world that Allah sustains.
- Some traditions describe Mika'il as also protecting believers and praying for forgiveness for them.

### Israfil: the angel of the trumpet

**Israfil** will blow the **trumpet** (as-sur) to announce the **Day of Resurrection**. The Qur'an speaks of the trumpet being blown (e.g. Qur'an 39:68, 36:51); Israfil's name comes from the hadith tradition.

- At the **first blast**, everything in the heavens and earth will die or fall unconscious, except whom Allah wills.
- At the **second blast**, all the dead will be **resurrected** and stand for **judgement** (39:68).
- Traditions say Israfil is ready with the trumpet at his lips, waiting for Allah's command; only Allah knows when the Day will come.

**Significance**:

- A reminder that the **Day of Judgement** is certain and could come at any time.
- Encourages Muslims to **prepare** by living rightly, praying, repenting and helping others.
- Shows Allah's **power** over life and death (Qur'an 46:33) and His **justice**, since everyone will be raised to be judged.

### Other angels (useful for (c) and (d))

| Angel(s) | Role |
|---|---|
| **Kiraman Katibin** (the noble recorders) | Two angels with each person, one on the right recording good deeds, one on the left recording bad deeds (Qur'an 50:17-18; 82:10-12); the record is shown on the Day of Judgement |
| **Angel of death** (malak al-mawt, often called **Izra'il** in tradition) | Takes the soul at death at its appointed time (Qur'an 32:11) |
| **Munkar and Nakir** | Question the dead in the grave about their Lord, their religion and their prophet (hadith) |
| **Guardian angels** | Protect each person by Allah's command (Qur'an 13:11) |
| **Malik** | Guardian of hell (Qur'an 43:77) |
| **Ridwan** | Guardian of paradise (in tradition) |

**Influence on daily life**: some Muslims end their prayer by turning their head to the right and left, saying "peace be upon you", which many understand as greeting the recording angels (and fellow worshippers). Knowing that two angels record every deed encourages honesty, kindness and avoiding sin even when alone; knowing that Munkar and Nakir will question them encourages Muslims to keep their faith strong.

::: terms
- **Malaikah**: angels; beings created by Allah from light who serve and obey Him perfectly.
- **Jibril**: the angel who brought the Qur'an to Muhammad and revelation to the prophets.
- **Mika'il**: the angel placed in charge of plants and rain, providing sustenance for creation.
- **Israfil**: the angel who will blow the trumpet to announce the Day of Resurrection.
- **Kiraman Katibin**: the two noble angels who record each person's good and bad deeds.
- **Munkar and Nakir**: the angels who question the dead in the grave about their faith.
- **Jinn**: beings created from smokeless fire who, like humans, have free will.
- **Iblis**: the jinn who refused to bow to Adam out of pride; also called Shaytan.
- **Revelation**: Allah making His will known, as when Jibril brought the Qur'an to Muhammad.
- **Free will**: the ability to choose between good and evil, which humans and jinn have but angels do not.
:::

::: example (c) beliefs about angels
Explain Muslim beliefs about angels. (8)

Belief in angels (malaikah) is one of the six articles of faith: Qur'an 2:285 says the believers believe "in Allah and His angels and His books and His messengers". Muslims believe angels were created from light and, unlike humans and jinn, have no free will; they "do not disobey Allah" but do what they are commanded (66:6). This means they are perfect servants and messengers of Allah, and their obedience is a model for Muslims.

Each of the main angels has a role that shows something about Allah. Jibril brought the Qur'an down to Muhammad's heart by Allah's permission (2:97), so the Qur'an is trusted as Allah's own word; whoever is an enemy to Jibril and Mika'il is an enemy to Allah (2:98). Mika'il is in charge of rain and plants, showing Allah's mercy in providing for creation, so Muslims are grateful for their food. Israfil will blow the trumpet to announce the Day of Resurrection, reminding Muslims that judgement is certain.

Angels also affect daily life. Two recording angels, the Kiraman Katibin, write down every good and bad deed (50:17-18), so Muslims try to act well even when alone, knowing their record will be shown on the Last Day. The angel of death takes each soul at its time (32:11), and Munkar and Nakir question the dead. Shi'a Muslims share these beliefs, although angels are not listed among the five roots of Usul ad-Din. Belief in angels therefore reminds Muslims that Allah is close and that every action matters.

[Band 4, 8 marks: detailed, sources explained, influence on believers, diversity.]

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Belief in angels is the most important Muslim belief.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some Muslims might argue that belief in angels is vital, because without Jibril there would be no Qur'an: he brought it to Muhammad's heart by Allah's permission (2:97), so every other belief Muslims learn from the Qur'an depends on him. The recording angels also shape daily behaviour more directly than many beliefs, since Muslims know every deed is written down. However, this argument is weaker than it first seems, because the angels only act on Allah's command and have no free will; their importance comes entirely from Allah. Tawhid is the foundation on which every other article rests, and for Shi'a Muslims angels are not even among the five roots of Usul ad-Din. Therefore belief in angels is an essential part of faith, as 2:285 shows, but it is not the most important belief, because it points beyond itself to Allah.
:::
