## What it is

This lesson covers **the worldwide Church**. You need:

- the work of **Tearfund**, as Christian beliefs in action;
- the **persecution of Christians**, past and present;
- **working for reconciliation**, through the **World Council of Churches** and the **ecumenical movement**.

This is part of Question 2 (Practices) of Component 2. In June 2025, 2(b) asked candidates to "describe two ways Christians might work for reconciliation". The examiners said it was answered poorly, and over 12% of candidates did not attempt it. Many confused reconciliation with the work of **Tearfund** or **Open Doors**.

### Tearfund: Christian beliefs in action

**Tearfund** is a UK-based **Christian relief and development charity**, founded in **1968** by Evangelical Christians. It works through **local churches** and Christian partners in many of the world's poorest countries. It helps people of **all faiths and none**.

**What it does**:

- **Disaster response**: after earthquakes, floods, droughts and conflict, it provides emergency **food, clean water, shelter** and health care, often through local churches that are already on the ground.
- **Long-term development**: clean water and **sanitation**; better farming methods; **self-help and savings groups**, especially for women, so families can start small businesses; health education. It aims to help communities lift **themselves** out of poverty rather than depend on aid.
- **Church and community mobilisation**: Tearfund trains local churches to work with their whole community. Together they identify needs and use their own skills and resources to meet them, for example building a well, a school or a clinic.
- **Campaigning (advocacy)**: it speaks up for the poor on issues such as **climate change**, which hits the poorest hardest, **waste and plastic pollution**, and fair treatment. It encourages governments and businesses to change.
- **In the UK**: it asks churches and individuals to **pray**, **give** and **act**, for example through simpler lifestyles and campaigning.

**Why: Christian beliefs in action**:

- **Love your neighbour** (Mark 12:31); the **Good Samaritan** helped a stranger in need (Luke 10).
- The **Sheep and the Goats**: feeding the hungry and caring for the stranger is serving Christ (Matthew 25:35-40).
- God asks his people "to act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly" (Micah 6:8).
- Humans are made in the **image of God** (Genesis 1:27), so every person has dignity.
- **Stewardship** of the earth (Genesis 2:15) motivates its climate work.
- Faith must be shown in **action** (James 2:17).

### Persecution of Christians: past

**Persecution** is hostility and ill-treatment of people because of their beliefs. It ranges from discrimination and threats to imprisonment, violence and death.

- **Jesus' warning**: "If the world hates you, keep in mind that it hated me first" (John 15:18). He also said, "Blessed are those who are persecuted because of righteousness" (Matthew 5:10).
- **The early Church**: **Stephen** was stoned to death, becoming the first Christian **martyr** (Acts 7). **Saul** (later Paul) persecuted Christians before his conversion (Acts 8-9). Paul himself was later beaten and imprisoned many times.
- **The Roman Empire**: after the great fire of Rome in AD 64, the emperor **Nero** blamed the Christians and had many killed. Tradition holds that **Peter** and **Paul** were martyred in Rome. Persecution came in waves for about 250 years, with the most severe under **Diocletian** in the early fourth century. It ended when **Constantine** granted Christians freedom of worship (313).
- **Christians persecuting Christians**: during the **Reformation**, both Protestants and Catholics were executed in England, for their beliefs, under different monarchs.
- **The twentieth century**: Christians were imprisoned and killed under **Soviet communism** and in **Nazi Germany**. The Lutheran pastor **Dietrich Bonhoeffer** was executed in 1945 for resisting Hitler. Archbishop **Oscar Romero** was shot in 1980, while celebrating Mass in El Salvador, after speaking out for the poor.

A saying from the early Church (Tertullian) is that the **blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church**: persecution has often made the Church grow.

### Persecution of Christians: present

- Charities that monitor religious freedom, such as **Open Doors** (which publishes an annual **World Watch List**) and **Aid to the Church in Need**, report that millions of Christians face high levels of persecution.
- **Examples** that are well documented: in **North Korea**, owning a Bible or meeting to pray can lead to labour camps. In parts of **northern Nigeria**, armed groups have attacked churches and villages. In **China**, unregistered churches face restrictions, and some buildings have been closed or crosses removed. Some countries have **blasphemy** or **anti-conversion** laws that are used against Christians. On **Easter Sunday 2019**, suicide bombers attacked churches and hotels in **Sri Lanka**.
- Other religious groups are also persecuted in many of these places. Christians are not the only victims.

### How Christians respond to persecution

- **Prayer**: "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). Many churches mark an annual **International Day of Prayer for the Persecuted Church**.
- **Solidarity**: "Remember those in prison as if you were together with them in prison" (Hebrews 13:3). If one part of the body of Christ suffers, every part suffers with it (1 Corinthians 12:26).
- **Practical support**:
  - **Open Doors**, founded by Brother Andrew, who smuggled Bibles into communist countries, supplies Bibles, training, trauma care and emergency help.
  - **Aid to the Church in Need**, a Catholic charity, rebuilds churches and supports priests and families. Its "**Red Wednesday**" lights up buildings in red to raise awareness.
- **Campaigning**: writing to governments, lobbying for religious freedom for **all** faiths, and helping persecuted **refugees** to settle.
- **Forgiveness and courage**: many persecuted Christians respond by forgiving their attackers, following Jesus on the cross (Luke 23:34), and by staying faithful.

### Reconciliation

**Reconciliation** means **restoring harmony and friendship after a relationship has broken down**. It is more than forgiving a crime. For Christians it has several levels:

- **With God**: through Jesus, God "reconciled us to himself" and gave believers "the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18). Catholics also celebrate the sacrament of **reconciliation** (confession).
- **Between people**: Jesus taught that if you remember someone has something against you, you should first go and be reconciled with them, and only then offer your gift at the altar (Matthew 5:23-24). "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9).
- **Between communities in conflict**: Christians work to heal divisions after war, racism and violence.
- **Between churches**: healing the divisions between denominations, through the **ecumenical movement**.

**Examples of Christians working for reconciliation** (learn two well for the (b)):

1. **Coventry Cathedral**: after the cathedral was destroyed by bombing in 1940, the words "**Father Forgive**" were written on the wall of the ruins. It now runs a worldwide ministry of reconciliation through the **Community of the Cross of Nails**, with links to Dresden and other cities bombed in the war, and helps groups in present-day conflicts.
2. **Corrymeela** in **Northern Ireland**: an ecumenical community founded in the 1960s that brings **Catholics and Protestants**, and others divided by conflict, together to meet, talk and build trust.
3. **Desmond Tutu**, the Anglican archbishop, chaired South Africa's **Truth and Reconciliation Commission** after apartheid. Victims told their stories, and those responsible could confess and seek amnesty.
4. **Local and personal**: churches holding services and joint events with other denominations or faiths; Christians mediating between neighbours; restorative justice projects.

### The World Council of Churches (WCC)

- **Founded in 1948** in Amsterdam; based in **Geneva**.
- A **fellowship of churches** from more than a hundred countries, including **Orthodox**, **Anglican**, **Lutheran**, **Methodist**, **Reformed**, **Baptist** and some **Pentecostal** churches. Together they represent hundreds of millions of Christians.
- The **Catholic Church is not a member**, but it works closely with the WCC, for example on theology and on preparing the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity.

**Its work**:

- seeking **visible unity** between the churches, through **dialogue** on beliefs that divide them, such as baptism, the Eucharist and ministry;
- **peace and reconciliation** in conflict zones, and work for **justice**: it opposed **apartheid** in South Africa;
- **humanitarian aid** and care for refugees;
- care for **creation** and climate justice;
- **dialogue with other faiths**;
- holding an **Assembly** of all member churches every few years.

### The ecumenical movement

The **ecumenical movement** is the movement to bring **Christian denominations together** in **unity**. It is based on Jesus' prayer for his followers "that all of them may be one" (**John 17:21**), and on Paul's teaching of "one Lord, one faith, one baptism" (Ephesians 4:5). Its modern start is often traced to a missionary conference in Edinburgh in **1910**, where missionaries saw that division between Christians hindered their witness. The Catholic Church committed itself to ecumenism at the **Second Vatican Council** (1960s).

**Examples**:

- **Churches Together in Britain and Ireland**, and local **Churches Together** groups that hold joint services, Lent courses and community projects.
- The **Week of Prayer for Christian Unity**, held each January.
- **Local ecumenical partnerships**, where different denominations share a building or minister.
- **Taizé** (2.2g), where Catholic and Protestant brothers pray together with young people from every tradition.
- Formal dialogues, such as between Anglicans and Catholics, and leaders such as the Pope and the Archbishop of Canterbury praying together.

**Why it matters**: Jesus prayed for unity; division weakens the Church's witness; together the churches can do more for the poor and for peace; unity reflects the oneness of God the **Trinity**.

**Obstacles and diverse views**:

- Real differences remain, on the **Eucharist** (not all churches share communion with each other), the authority of the **Pope**, and **women's ordination**.
- Some conservative Evangelicals and some Orthodox worry that unity could mean **compromising truth**.
- Many Christians aim for **unity in diversity** rather than one single church.

::: terms
- **Tearfund**: a UK Christian relief and development charity working through local churches to tackle poverty and injustice.
- **Development**: long-term work that helps communities lift themselves out of poverty, such as clean water and farming projects.
- **Persecution**: hostility and ill-treatment, from discrimination to violence, because of a person's religious beliefs.
- **Martyr**: a person who is killed because of their faith.
- **Reconciliation**: restoring harmony and friendship after a relationship has broken down, with God or between people or groups.
- **World Council of Churches**: a worldwide fellowship of churches, founded in 1948, working for unity, justice and peace.
- **Ecumenical movement**: the movement to bring different Christian denominations together in unity.
- **Ecumenism**: efforts to promote unity and co-operation between Christian denominations.
- **Denomination**: a distinct branch of the Christian Church, such as Catholic, Anglican, Methodist or Baptist.
- **Open Doors**: a Christian charity that monitors persecution and supports persecuted Christians worldwide.
- **Churches Together**: groups in Britain and Ireland through which different denominations worship and work together.
:::

::: example (b) reconciliation
Describe two ways Christians might work for reconciliation. (5)

Reconciliation means restoring harmony after a relationship has broken down. One way is through local and national ecumenical work: in Churches Together groups, Catholics, Anglicans, Methodists and others hold joint services and projects, and during the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity each January they pray together, following Jesus' prayer "that all of them may be one" (John 17:21). A second way is peace-building between divided communities. After Coventry Cathedral was bombed in 1940, the words "Father Forgive" were written on the ruins, and today its Community of the Cross of Nails links partners worldwide, including Dresden, to bring former enemies and people in conflict together. Christians do this because Jesus taught "blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9).

::: example (b) Tearfund
Describe the work of Tearfund. (5)

Tearfund is a UK Christian charity that tackles poverty through local churches in some of the world's poorest countries. When disasters strike, it provides emergency food, clean water and shelter. It also does long-term development work, such as clean water and sanitation projects, and savings groups that help women start small businesses, so that communities can lift themselves out of poverty. It trains local churches to work with their whole community to identify needs and meet them with their own resources. It campaigns on issues such as climate change, which hurts the poorest most. ::: example (c) ecumenical movement
Explain the importance of the ecumenical movement for Christians. (8)

The ecumenical movement aims to bring divided Christian denominations together. It is important because Jesus prayed that his followers "may be one" (John 17:21), and Paul taught "one Lord, one faith, one baptism" (Ephesians 4:5), so division goes against God's will. The World Council of Churches, founded in 1948, brings together Orthodox, Anglican and many Protestant churches for dialogue on issues that divide them, such as the Eucharist, and for shared work on peace and justice. The Catholic Church is not a member but works closely with it, having committed itself to ecumenism at the Second Vatican Council. In Britain, Churches Together groups hold joint services, and Taizé shows Catholics and Protestants praying side by side. Unity strengthens the Church's witness and lets churches do more together for the poor. However, some Evangelicals and Orthodox worry about compromising truth, and differences on the Eucharist and the Pope remain, so many aim for unity in diversity.

[Band 4, 8 marks: sources explained, WCC and examples, diverse views.]

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Christians should do more to help persecuted Christians.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Many Christians would agree, because the Church is one body and "if one part suffers, every part suffers with it" (1 Corinthians 12:26). Hebrews 13:3 tells Christians to remember prisoners as if they were in prison with them, and charities such as Open Doors report that millions face persecution, yet many Christians in Britain know little about it. However, others argue that Christians must help all who suffer, not just fellow believers. The Good Samaritan helped a stranger from another community, and Tearfund helps people of all faiths and none. Christians already pray for the persecuted, and Jesus' own command was to "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44), not to take revenge. Overall, Christians should do more, especially through prayer, campaigning for religious freedom for everyone and supporting refugees, but without neglecting others in need.
:::
