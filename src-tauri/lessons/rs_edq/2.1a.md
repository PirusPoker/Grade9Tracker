### Families in the twenty-first century

A **family** is a group of people related by birth, marriage, civil partnership or adoption who care for one another. Britain now has many kinds:

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Nuclear** | Two parents and their children in one home |
| **Extended** | Grandparents, aunts, uncles or cousins living with or close to the nuclear family |
| **Single-parent** | One parent bringing up children, after divorce, death, separation or by choice |
| **Blended (step-)family** | A couple bringing together children from earlier relationships |
| **Same-sex parents** | Two men or two women bringing up children |

**Christianity.** The family is the basic unit of society and the "domestic church", where children first learn to pray, love and forgive. Its purposes are **procreation** (having children), **stability**, **nurturing faith** and caring for the old and the young. The commandment "Honour your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12) gives children **responsibilities** too. Most churches support every kind of family pastorally, while many still teach that the ideal is a married mother and father.

**Islam.** The family is the foundation of the **ummah** (the worldwide Muslim community). The hadith in **Sahih Bukhari 9:89:252** teaches that every person is a **guardian** (shepherd) responsible for those in their care: a ruler for the people, a man for his family, a woman for her husband's home and children, a servant for his master's property, and each will be asked about that charge. So family life is a set of **responsibilities** given by Allah, not just a personal arrangement. Extended families are common, and caring for elderly parents is a religious duty.

### Roles of women and men in the family

- **Traditional Christian view**: husband and wife are **equal in worth** (both made in God's image, Genesis 1:27) but may have **complementary** roles; some evangelical Christians read Ephesians 5 as giving the husband a role of loving, self-giving leadership.
- **Modern Christian view** (most Anglicans, Methodists, many Catholics): roles are shared according to the couple's gifts; both may work and both care for children. Paul's call to "submit to one another out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:21) is read as mutual.
- **Islam**: men and women are equal before Allah, with **different but complementary roles**. Traditionally the husband must provide financially for the family, and the wife's earnings are her own; the mother has a special honour (a hadith places paradise "at the feet of mothers"). Many British Muslim women work, study and lead in community life, and many Muslim couples share roles; the Guardian hadith gives both partners real authority.

### Cohabitation

**Cohabitation** means a couple living together in a sexual relationship without being married. It is now common in Britain, and many couples cohabit before marrying.

| View | Teaching |
|---|---|
| **Catholic** and many evangelical Christians | Sex belongs only in marriage, which is a lifelong, public **commitment** before God. Cohabitation is a "trial run" that lacks that commitment, so it is wrong. |
| **Church of England** | The **General Synod** has said that marriage is the ideal and the right setting for sex, but its reports have recognised that many cohabiting couples are faithful and committed, and that for many it is a step towards marriage. Parishes welcome cohabiting couples who come to be married. |
| **Liberal Christians** | What matters is love, faithfulness and commitment, not the certificate. |
| **Islam** | Not allowed. Sex outside marriage is **zina** (Qur'an 17:32), so a couple may not live together before nikah. The family and community help young people to marry instead. |

### The nature and purpose of marriage: Christianity

Jesus quoted Genesis when asked about marriage: God made them "male and female", so a man leaves his parents and is united to his wife, and "the two will become one flesh" (**Mark 10:6-8**). Christians take from this that marriage is **God's design**, a **lifelong**, **exclusive** union of a man and a woman.

- **Catholics and Orthodox** call marriage a **sacrament**: an outward sign through which God gives grace to the couple.
- Most **Protestants**, including the Church of England, see it as a holy **covenant**: a binding promise made before God.

The Church of England's marriage service (its teaching is agreed through the **General Synod**) gives the purposes of marriage as: a gift of God in creation; the couple's **mutual comfort and help**, in prosperity and adversity; the **joy of sexual union**; and a secure setting for **the birth and nurture of children**.

**A Christian wedding in Britain and what it shows:**

| Feature | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Held in church, "in the presence of God" | Marriage is holy and God is part of it |
| Declarations ("I will") and **vows** — to love and cherish "for better, for worse… till death us do part" | A free, public, lifelong **commitment** |
| Exchange of **rings** | Unending love and faithfulness |
| Bible readings (often 1 Corinthians 13, "love is patient…") and a sermon | Christian love (**agape**) is the model |
| The priest declares them husband and wife and **blesses** them | God joins the couple (Mark 10:9) |
| Signing the register; Catholic weddings often include a **Nuptial Mass** | Legal as well as religious; Catholics see it as a sacrament |

### The nature and purpose of marriage: Islam

**Nikah** (marriage) is a **contract** before Allah, strongly encouraged as the **sunnah** (example) of the Prophet Muhammad. Its purposes are summed up in **Qur'an 30:21**: Allah created spouses "from yourselves" so that you may find **tranquillity** in them, and placed between them **affection and mercy**. Marriage is also the only right setting for sex and for bringing up children as Muslims.

**A Muslim wedding in Britain and what it shows:**

| Feature | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Both partners must **freely consent**; often there is a **wali** (guardian) for the bride | Marriage needs consent: forced marriage is forbidden |
| The **mahr**, a gift from groom to bride that is hers to keep | Respect for the wife and her rights |
| The **nikah** contract is agreed and signed before **two witnesses** | Marriage is a public contract with duties on both sides |
| A short **khutbah** (sermon) and Qur'an recitation, often by an imam | Marriage is an act of worship |
| The **walima**, a celebration meal | The community shares the couple's joy |
| A **civil registration** as well | In Britain a nikah alone is not usually a legal marriage, so many couples also marry at a register office |

### Marriage outside the religious tradition

- **Catholic**: a Catholic may marry a baptised non-Catholic with permission, or a non-Christian with a **dispensation**; the Catholic partner promises to do all they can to bring the children up as Catholics.
- **Church of England and other Protestants**: generally allow it, and the wedding can take place in church; some evangelicals cite "do not be yoked together with unbelievers" (2 Corinthians 6:14) to discourage it.
- **Islam**: a Muslim man may marry a Christian or Jewish woman (the "People of the Book", Qur'an 5:5), but not an idolater (2:221). The great majority of scholars say a Muslim woman may marry only a Muslim man, because the children follow the father's religion. Many families worry about the faith of the children in any mixed marriage.

### Temporary marriage: Sunni and Shi'a diversity

- **Mut'ah** is a temporary marriage for a fixed period agreed in the contract, with a mahr. **Twelver Shi'a** scholars hold it is lawful under strict conditions, because it was allowed in the Prophet's time and they believe it was never cancelled by him.
- **Sunni** scholars reject mut'ah: they believe the Prophet later forbade it, and that a valid marriage must be meant to last and must be **announced publicly** with witnesses. A secret, **unannounced** marriage is therefore not valid for most Sunnis.
- Both traditions agree that marriage protects people from zina and must be fair to the woman.

### Arranged marriage in Britain

In an **arranged** (or **assisted**) marriage, parents or relatives suggest a suitable partner, often of the same faith and culture, and the couple then decide. It is common among some British Muslim families (and Hindu and Sikh families). Supporters say families know their children well and that shared faith and values give stability. A **forced marriage**, where either person does not freely agree, is a **crime** in England and Wales and is condemned in Islam: the Prophet allowed a woman to cancel a marriage made without her consent. Muslims stress that consent is the line between arranged and forced.

::: terms
- **Family**: a group of people related by birth, marriage, civil partnership or adoption who care for one another.
- **Roles**: the parts or jobs a person is expected to take on in a family or community, such as provider or carer.
- **Responsibilities**: duties a person must carry out, such as parents raising children and children honouring parents.
- **Commitment**: a sworn promise to stay faithful and loyal to someone, as in marriage vows made before God and witnesses.
- **Cohabitation**: a couple living together in a sexual relationship without being married.
- **Sacrament**: in Catholic and Orthodox teaching, an outward sign through which God gives grace; marriage is one.
- **Covenant**: a binding agreement made before God, the usual Protestant way of describing marriage.
- **Nikah**: the Muslim marriage contract, freely agreed by both partners before witnesses.
- **Mahr**: the gift the groom gives the bride in a Muslim marriage, which belongs to her.
- **Mut'ah**: a temporary marriage for a fixed period, accepted by Twelver Shi'a scholars and rejected by Sunni scholars.
- **Arranged marriage**: a marriage in which families suggest the partner, but both people freely choose to accept.
:::

::: example (c) cohabitation, two religions
Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, attitudes to cohabitation. (8)

**Christianity.** Christian attitudes vary. Catholics and many evangelicals believe cohabitation is wrong, because sex is meant only for marriage, where a couple have made a lifelong commitment before God. Jesus taught that in marriage "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8), which they take to mean that the union of bodies belongs with the union of lives in marriage. So these Christians encourage couples to marry rather than live together first. The Church of England teaches through its General Synod that marriage is the ideal, but it recognises that many cohabiting couples are faithful and committed and often go on to marry, so it welcomes them pastorally rather than condemning them. Liberal Christians stress that love and faithfulness matter more than a ceremony.

**Islam.** Muslims believe cohabitation is not allowed. Any sexual relationship outside nikah is zina, and the Qur'an commands believers not even to "approach" it (17:32). Marriage is the sunnah of the Prophet and brings "tranquillity", "affection and mercy" (30:21), so Muslims see marriage, not living together, as the proper start of a shared life. Families often help young people to marry, for example through assisted marriage, so that they do not face that temptation.

[Band 4, 8 marks: two religions, diversity within Christianity, sources interpreted, influence on believers.]

::: example (d) paragraph that evaluates
'Marriage is out of date.' Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religion and belief in your answer.) (15)

One developed paragraph: Some people argue marriage is out of date because many couples in Britain now cohabit and are just as committed. A liberal Christian might agree that love and faithfulness matter more than a certificate. However, this argument is weaker than it looks, because it confuses what is *common* with what is *valuable*. For Catholics, marriage is a sacrament, a channel of God's grace, and Jesus rooted it in creation itself — "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8) — so for them it cannot go out of date, since God's design does not change with fashion. Muslims would agree for a different reason: nikah is the Prophet's sunnah and Qur'an 30:21 says Allah placed "affection and mercy" between spouses, so marriage is a sign of God, not a social trend. Therefore, for religious believers the statement fails, although the rise in cohabitation shows that for many non-religious people its legal and social role has weakened.
