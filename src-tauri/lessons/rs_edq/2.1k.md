### Human rights and social justice

- **Human rights** are the basic rights and freedoms that every person is entitled to simply because they are human: life, freedom from torture and slavery, a fair trial, freedom of thought, religion and expression, education.
- The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (UDHR) was adopted by the United Nations in **1948**, after the horrors of the Second World War. In the UK, the **Human Rights Act 1998** brought the rights of the European Convention on Human Rights into British law.
- **Social justice** means fairness in society: making sure wealth, opportunities and rights are shared fairly and that the vulnerable are protected.
- Rights come with **responsibilities**: to respect the rights of others and obey just laws.

### Christian beliefs about the dignity of human life

- **Genesis 1:26-27**: God created humankind **in his own image**; male and female he created them. Christians call this the **imago Dei** (image of God): every person, of any race, gender, ability or status, has equal and God-given **dignity**.
- Because every person reflects God, Christians believe they should be treated with respect and never used, enslaved or tortured. Jesus' command to "love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31) and the parable of the **Good Samaritan** (Luke 10), who helped a stranger from a hated group, show that this love has no boundaries.
- Jesus said that whatever is done for "the least of these" is done for him (Matthew 25:40), so serving the poor, the sick and prisoners is serving Christ.
- **Diversity**: Catholics express this in **Catholic Social Teaching**, which puts human dignity at the centre; Evangelicals stress sharing the gospel and practical care together; liberal Christians and Quakers often stress campaigning for rights and equality.

### Muslim beliefs about the dignity of human life

- **Qur'an 5:32**: killing one innocent soul is as if killing **all mankind**, and saving one life is as if saving **all mankind**. Every individual life has immense worth.
- Allah created all humans and made them His **khalifah** (stewards) on earth, with **fitra** (a natural goodness). Allah has honoured the children of Adam (17:70).
- The **Prophet Muhammad's Last Sermon** taught that all people are equal, and that no one is superior to another except in piety and good action.
- Muslims must stand for **justice**, even against themselves or their families (4:135). Rights in Islam come with duties to Allah and to others.

### Promoting human rights: agape in action

**Agape** is the selfless, unconditional love that Christians believe God shows and that Christians should show to all. Examples of agape in action:

- **Food banks**: many local food banks are run by churches and Christian volunteers, offering emergency food and help to people in crisis.
- **Supporting refugees and asylum seekers**: churches offer welcome centres, English classes and legal advice; Catholic and Anglican leaders have spoken up for refugees' rights.
- **Campaigning**: Christian Aid and other Christian groups campaign against injustice, slavery and poverty; many Christians support Amnesty International's work for prisoners of conscience.
- Quakers have a long history of working for **equality** and **peace**, from opposing slavery to prison reform.

### Promoting human rights: ummah in action

The **ummah** is the worldwide community of Muslims, united as brothers and sisters in faith. Ummah in action means Muslims caring for one another and for all humanity:

- **Zakah** (compulsory annual giving of 2.5% of savings above a minimum) and **sadaqah** (voluntary charity) redistribute wealth to those in need.
- **Mosques** in Britain run food banks, community kitchens and support for the homeless, open to people of any faith.
- **Islamic Relief** and **Muslim Aid** provide emergency aid and long-term development worldwide.
- Muslims campaign for the rights of persecuted Muslims and others, seeing injustice to one member of the ummah as harm to the whole body.

### Conflict between personal conviction and the law

**Personal conviction** is a strongly held belief that guides a person's actions. Sometimes it clashes with a country's law. Christians and Muslims generally believe they should obey the law (Romans 13; Qur'an 4:59 on obeying those in authority), but many also believe God's law comes first if a human law is clearly unjust.

| Example | Conflict | Belief |
|---|---|---|
| **Martin Luther King** (Baptist minister, USA) | Broke segregation laws through non-violent protest and was jailed in Birmingham, Alabama in 1963. | An unjust law is no true law; all are equal before God; non-violence follows Jesus. |
| **Dietrich Bonhoeffer** (Lutheran pastor, Germany) | Opposed the Nazi regime and joined resistance plans against Hitler; executed in **1945**. | Christians must resist evil, even at personal cost. |
| **Muhammad Ali** (Muslim boxer, USA) | Refused to be drafted into the US army in **1967** during the Vietnam War; convicted and stripped of his boxing titles. The Supreme Court overturned the conviction in **1971**. | His Muslim faith meant he would not fight in that war; he accepted the consequences rather than abandon his conviction. |

These people usually broke the law **openly** and **accepted the punishment**, which many believers see as the right way to show conviction.

### Censorship and freedom of religious expression

- **Censorship** is the control or suppression of what people can say, write, publish or view, usually by a government or other authority. Examples in the UK: film age ratings, laws against **hate speech** (e.g. stirring up religious hatred), and online safety rules protecting children.
- **Freedom of religious expression** is the right to practise, show and talk about one's religion in public: worship, wearing religious symbols or dress, preaching, and changing religion (UDHR Article 18).
- **Arguments for some censorship**: protects children; prevents hatred and violence; stops extremist propaganda; some believers want protection from deeply offensive attacks on their faith.
- **Arguments against**: freedom of speech is a basic right; censorship can be abused by governments to silence religious minorities or critics; truth is tested by open debate. In England and Wales the old **blasphemy laws** were abolished in **2008**.
- **Cases about religious dress** show the balance: **Nadia Eweida**, a Christian British Airways employee, won her case at the European Court of Human Rights (2013) to wear a small cross at work; **Shabina Begum**, a Muslim pupil, lost her case (2006) to wear a jilbab at a school with a uniform policy that already allowed Muslim dress such as the hijab. Courts weigh religious freedom against the rights and rules of others.
- **Christian views**: most support freedom of expression, including the freedom to preach and to criticise; some campaign against content they find blasphemous. **Muslim views**: Muslims value freedom of belief ("There is no compulsion in religion", 2:256), but many find insulting depictions of the Prophet deeply offensive and believe respect should limit expression; mainstream Muslims say any protest must be peaceful and lawful.

### Religious extremism and Islamophobia

- **Extremism** is holding extreme political or religious views far outside the mainstream, which may lead a person to support or use violence or to deny others their rights.
- Extremists have claimed to act for many religions, including Christianity (e.g. some violent attacks in the USA on abortion clinics) and Islam (e.g. terrorist groups such as Islamic State). **Mainstream** Christians and Muslims overwhelmingly **condemn** this. Muslims point to 5:32 (killing one innocent is like killing all humanity) and 2:256; Christians point to Jesus' teaching on loving enemies.
- **Islamophobia** is fear, prejudice or hatred directed at Muslims or Islam. It includes verbal and online abuse, attacks on mosques, and discrimination in jobs; Muslim women who wear the hijab are often targeted. It can grow when the actions of a few extremists are wrongly blamed on all Muslims.
- **Responses**: mosques hold open days so neighbours can meet Muslims; Muslim and Christian leaders issue joint statements after attacks; organisations record and challenge anti-Muslim hatred; British law protects people from religious hatred and discrimination.

::: terms
- **Human rights**: the basic rights and freedoms to which every person is entitled simply because they are human.
- **Social justice**: fairness in society, so that wealth, opportunities and rights are shared fairly and the vulnerable protected.
- **Dignity**: the worth and value of every human being, which means they should be treated with respect.
- **Imago Dei**: the Christian belief that humans are made in the image of God (Genesis 1:26-27).
- **Agape**: selfless, unconditional love for others, which Christians believe God shows to all.
- **Ummah**: the worldwide community of Muslims, who share a duty to care for one another and for all.
- **Personal conviction**: a strongly held belief or principle that guides how a person acts.
- **Censorship**: the control or suppression of what people can say, publish or view, usually by an authority.
- **Freedom of religious expression**: the right to practise, display and speak about one's religion in public.
- **Extremism**: holding extreme views far outside the mainstream, which may lead to violence or denying others' rights.
- **Islamophobia**: fear, prejudice or hatred directed towards Muslims or Islam.
:::

::: example (b) conviction and law
Describe an example of a conflict between personal conviction and the laws of a country. (5)

In 1967, during the Vietnam War, the boxer Muhammad Ali was called up to serve in the US army. As a Muslim, he refused to be drafted, saying his religious beliefs would not allow him to fight in that war. Refusing the draft was against US law, so he was arrested, convicted and stripped of his world heavyweight title, and banned from boxing for over three years at the peak of his career. He chose to accept these consequences rather than act against his conscience. In 1971 the US Supreme Court overturned his conviction. His stand made him a symbol of acting on personal conviction even at great cost.

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Religious believers should obey the teachings of their religion instead of the laws of a country.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some believers would agree when a law is clearly unjust. Martin Luther King broke segregation laws because he believed all are equal before God, and Muhammad Ali refused the draft for his faith; history now sees both as right. This argument is strong because laws are made by fallible humans, and obeying an evil law, as many did in Nazi Germany, can make believers complicit, which Bonhoeffer refused to be. However, both the Bible and the Qur'an also teach respect for lawful authority (Romans 13; Qur'an 4:59), and in a democracy most laws protect rights and can be changed peacefully. If every believer followed only their own reading of religious teaching, extremists could justify anything. So believers should normally obey the law, and only disobey openly and non-violently when it forces them to do something seriously wrong.
