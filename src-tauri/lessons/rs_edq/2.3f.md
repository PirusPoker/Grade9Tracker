## What it is

This lesson covers the **Five Pillars of Sunni Islam** and how they are practised in **Britain and elsewhere**: **Shahadah** (the statement of faith, **Qur'an 3:18**); **salah** (prayer) in the **mosque** and at home, including **Jummah** (**Qur'an 15:98-99**, **29:45**); **zakah** (how charity tax and alms are paid and how zakah money may be spent); **sawm** in Ramadan (**Qur'an 2:184**) and issues of fasting in Britain; and **Hajj** (**Qur'an 2:125**) and issues for British Muslims undertaking it.

It is examined in Question 2 (Practices) of Component 3: (a) 2, (b) 5, (c) 8, (d) 15, with **no SPaG**. Four of the eight (a) concepts belong here: **mosque** (asked June 2025), **halal**, **haram** and **ummah**. The June 2025 (b) was "Describe how fasting during Ramadan could be difficult for a Muslim in Britain"; the report noted that some candidates drifted into halal food or prayer without linking it to fasting. Shi'a practice of the same duties is in 2.3g.

### The pillars as a whole

The Prophet taught that Islam is "built on five": the Shahadah, salah, zakah, sawm in Ramadan and Hajj (hadith in Bukhari and Muslim). Like the pillars of a building, they **hold up** a Muslim's faith; together they cover **belief, daily worship, wealth, the body and the whole of life**. They are **acts of worship** (**'ibadah**) that show submission to Allah, unite the **ummah** and train Muslims in self-discipline and care for others.

### Key concepts

- **Mosque** (masjid, "place of prostration"): a building where Muslims gather to pray, especially for Jummah; also a community centre for learning, celebrations and support.
- **Halal**: what is **permitted** by Allah, e.g. food prepared according to Islamic law, honest work.
- **Haram**: what is **forbidden** by Allah, e.g. pork, alcohol, gambling, theft, lying.
- **Ummah**: the worldwide **community of all Muslims**, united by faith regardless of race, nation or language; most visible when all pray towards Makkah and at Hajj.

### Shahadah: the statement of faith

"**There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah**" (in Arabic: la ilaha illallah, Muhammadur rasulullah).

- The first half declares **Tawhid**; **Qur'an 3:18** shows Allah, the angels and people of knowledge bearing witness that there is no god but Him. The second half declares **Risalah**, accepting Muhammad's message and Sunnah.
- Saying it sincerely, in front of witnesses, is how a person **becomes a Muslim** (reverts or converts).
- It is whispered into a **newborn baby's ear** (as part of the call to prayer), so it is the first thing a Muslim hears.
- It is said in the **adhan** (call to prayer) and in every **salah**, so it is repeated many times a day.
- Muslims hope it will be their **last words** before death, and others may recite it to a dying person.
- It is the **foundation** of the other pillars: without belief, worship has no meaning.
- Shi'a Muslims accept the same Shahadah; many add, in the call to prayer, a testimony that Ali is the friend (wali) of Allah, as an expression of belief rather than part of the obligatory words.

### Salah: prayer

**When**: five times a day at set times: **Fajr** (dawn), **Zuhr** (just after midday), **Asr** (afternoon), **Maghrib** (just after sunset), **Isha** (night). In Britain the times change through the year, so mosques publish **timetables**; phone apps are widely used.

**How**:

1. The **adhan** (call to prayer) is given by the **mu'adhdhin** (muezzin), often from the minaret in Muslim-majority countries; in Britain usually inside the mosque or by app.
2. **Wudu** (ritual washing) of the hands, mouth, nose, face, arms, head, ears and feet, in a set order, to be clean in body and mind. If no water is available, tayammum (dry washing with clean earth) is allowed.
3. Face the **qiblah**, the direction of the **Ka'bah** in Makkah (south-east from Britain).
4. Pray on a clean surface such as a **prayer mat**, with the body covered modestly.
5. Make the **niyyah** (intention), then perform the set number of **rak'ahs** (units): 2 at Fajr, 4 at Zuhr, 4 at Asr, 3 at Maghrib, 4 at Isha. Each rak'ah includes standing and reciting **Surah al-Fatihah** in Arabic, **bowing** (ruku), **prostrating** (sujud) twice, and sitting.
6. End by turning the head to the right and left saying "peace be upon you".
7. Personal prayers (**du'a**) may follow, in any language.

**Qur'an 15:98-99**: glorify your Lord with praise, be among those who **prostrate**, and **worship your Lord until certainty** (death) comes; so prayer is a lifelong duty. **Qur'an 29:45**: establish prayer, because **prayer restrains from shameful and evil deeds**, and the remembrance of Allah is greatest.

**In the mosque**: praying together (**jama'ah**) is believed to bring greater reward. Worshippers stand **shoulder to shoulder** in straight rows, rich and poor together, behind the **imam** who leads. Men and women usually pray in **separate rows or areas**, for modesty and concentration.

**Features of a mosque**: the **prayer hall** with carpet marked in rows and no chairs or images; the **mihrab** (an alcove in the wall showing the qiblah); the **minbar** (raised pulpit from which the imam gives the sermon); the **minaret** and **dome** (on many purpose-built mosques); the **wudu area** with running water; a place for shoes; calligraphy of Qur'an verses; often a **madrasah** (school) for children to learn Arabic and the Qur'an, and rooms for community events.

**Jummah prayer**: the Friday midday prayer, which replaces Zuhr. Men are expected to attend the mosque; the Qur'an tells believers, when the call is made on Friday, to hasten to the remembrance of Allah and leave trade (62:9). The imam gives a **khutbah** (sermon), often in English and Arabic in Britain. Women may attend or pray at home. In Britain Jummah falls in the working or school day, so Muslims may need to use their lunch break or ask for time off; some schools and workplaces provide prayer rooms.

**At home**: Muslims may pray anywhere clean, at home, school or work. Families often pray together, and many homes have a prayer mat and a compass or app showing the qiblah. Women more often pray at home, especially with young children.

**Importance**: direct contact with Allah five times a day; discipline; keeps Allah central (taqwa); purifies the person (29:45); equality and unity of the ummah, all facing Makkah.

**Issues in Britain**: finding time and a clean, quiet place at school or work (the Equality Act 2010 protects against religious discrimination, and many employers allow breaks, but it is not always easy); short winter days put Zuhr, Asr and Maghrib close together; few public calls to prayer.

### Zakah: charity tax and alms

**Zakah** (purification, growth) is the **compulsory** giving of **2.5%** of a Muslim's **savings and wealth** held for a full lunar year, above a minimum amount called the **nisab** (traditionally the value of 85 grams of gold or 595 grams of silver). It also applies, at different rates, to crops, livestock and business goods.

- It **purifies** the giver's wealth and heart from greed, and reminds them that all wealth belongs to Allah; humans are trustees.
- It is a **right of the poor**, not a favour: it is due, like a tax, though paid as worship.
- **How it is paid**: in some Muslim-majority countries it is collected by the state; in Britain Muslims calculate it themselves (often using online calculators) and give it through the mosque or charities such as **Islamic Relief**, **Muslim Aid** or the **National Zakat Foundation**, which distributes zakah to people in need in the UK. Many pay during **Ramadan**, when rewards are believed to be multiplied.
- **Zakat al-fitr** is a small separate payment, per person, made at the end of Ramadan before the Id prayer so the poor can celebrate (see 2.3i).
- **Sadaqah** is **voluntary** charity of any amount, at any time, including kind acts and a smile; it is **not** the same as zakah.

**How zakah may be spent**: Qur'an 9:60 names **eight categories** of people who may receive it:

| | Recipients |
|---|---|
| 1 | The **poor** |
| 2 | The **needy** |
| 3 | Those **employed to collect and distribute** zakah |
| 4 | Those whose **hearts are to be reconciled** (e.g. new Muslims needing support) |
| 5 | To **free captives** (and, historically, slaves) |
| 6 | Those in **debt** |
| 7 | **In the cause of Allah** |
| 8 | **Travellers** in need |

Zakah may not be used for the giver's own family whom they must support anyway, or, according to many scholars, for building mosques.

### Sawm: fasting in Ramadan

**Ramadan** is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, when the Qur'an was first revealed (2:185). During it, adult Muslims **fast from dawn until sunset**: no food, drink (including water), smoking or sexual relations during daylight. They also try to avoid bad thoughts, words and actions.

- **Suhur**: a meal before dawn. **Iftar**: breaking the fast at sunset, traditionally with **dates** and water, then Maghrib prayer and a meal, often shared with family, friends and the mosque.
- **Tarawih**: extra night prayers in the mosque during Ramadan, in which many mosques recite the whole Qur'an over the month.
- **Qur'an 2:184**: fasting is for a limited number of days; anyone who is **ill or on a journey** should make up the same number of days later; those who can only fast with great hardship may instead **feed a poor person** (fidyah) for each day.
- **Exemptions**: children before puberty, the elderly, the ill, pregnant or breastfeeding women, women during menstruation, and travellers. Missed days are made up later where possible.

**Purposes**: obedience to Allah; **self-discipline** and control of desires (a form of Greater Jihad); **empathy** with the hungry, leading to generosity; spiritual focus and **taqwa**; closeness to the Qur'an; unity of the **ummah**, all fasting together.

**Issues for Muslims fasting in Britain**:

- **Long summer fasts**: because the Islamic calendar is lunar, Ramadan moves about 11 days earlier each year. When it falls in summer, the fast in Britain can last **18 hours or more**, longer in Scotland than in southern England.
- **Exams**: in some years Ramadan falls during the GCSE and A-level exam season (it did for several years up to about 2021); students must then fast while revising and sitting papers.
- **School and work**: most people around them are not fasting; food is everywhere (canteens, adverts, colleagues eating); PE and sport may be hard; there is no public signal when the fast begins or ends.
- **Isolation** in areas with few Muslims, with no shared iftar or late tarawih nearby.
- **Responses**: suhur apps and mosque timetables; employers and schools increasingly accommodating; community iftars; open iftar events that invite non-Muslims (e.g. the **Ramadan Tent Project**'s Open Iftar); a Muslim may make up days if illness or genuine hardship occurs. Most British Muslims follow **local** sunrise and sunset times.

### Hajj: pilgrimage to Makkah

**Hajj** is the pilgrimage to **Makkah**, which every adult Muslim who is **physically and financially able** must make **once** in their lifetime. It takes place from the 8th to the 12th or 13th of **Dhul-Hijjah**, the last month of the Islamic year. (Umrah is a shorter pilgrimage that can be made at any time but does not replace Hajj.)

**Qur'an 2:125**: Allah made the House (the **Ka'bah**) a place of return for people and a place of **safety**, and told them to take the **standing place of Ibrahim** as a place of prayer; Ibrahim and Isma'il were told to purify the House for those who walk around it, stay there, and bow and prostrate.

**The main rites**:

| Rite | What happens | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Ihram** | At the boundary (miqat) pilgrims enter a state of purity; men wear two plain white sheets, women simple modest clothing | **Equality** of all before Allah; leaving the world behind |
| **Tawaf** | Walking seven times anticlockwise around the **Ka'bah** | Allah at the centre of life; the unity of the ummah |
| **Sa'i** | Walking or running seven times between **Safa** and **Marwah**; drinking **Zamzam** water | Remembering **Hajar**'s search for water and trust in Allah |
| **Mina** | Pilgrims stay in a vast tent city | Preparation and prayer |
| **Arafat** | Standing in prayer from midday to sunset on 9 Dhul-Hijjah, at the Mount of Mercy | The **most important** rite; asking forgiveness; a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement |
| **Muzdalifah** | Spending the night in the open, collecting pebbles | Humility, equality |
| **Jamarat** | Throwing pebbles at three pillars in Mina | Rejecting **Shaytan**, who tempted Ibrahim |
| **Sacrifice** | An animal is sacrificed (now usually by voucher), beginning **Id-ul-Adha** | Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son |
| **Hair** | Men shave or cut their hair; women cut a lock | A new beginning |
| **Final tawaf** | Return to the Ka'bah before leaving | Farewell |

**Importance**: fulfils a pillar; follows **Ibrahim**, **Hajar** and **Isma'il**; brings **forgiveness** of sins (a hadith says an accepted Hajj returns a person as pure as a newborn); shows the **unity and equality** of the ummah, millions from every nation dressed alike; strengthens faith; the pilgrim returns with the respected title **hajji** (or hajjah).

**Issues for British Muslims undertaking Hajj**:

- **Cost**: Hajj packages from the UK typically cost several thousand pounds per person, so many save for years.
- **Quotas and booking**: Saudi Arabia limits the number of pilgrims from each country, and British pilgrims must travel through **approved** arrangements; there have been fake or unlicensed Hajj packages, so pilgrims are warned to book carefully.
- **Time off** work or school (Hajj takes about two weeks with travel) and leaving family.
- **Heat and crowds**: in recent years Hajj has fallen in early summer, with extreme heat; large crowds make it physically demanding, especially for older pilgrims.
- **Health**: required vaccinations; fitness to walk long distances.
- **Responses**: saving schemes; trusted operators; some perform **Umrah** first; those who cannot afford Hajj are excused, and Allah judges their intention.

::: terms
- **Shahadah**: the Muslim statement of faith: there is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah.
- **Salah**: the five daily prayers, performed at set times facing Makkah.
- **Wudu**: ritual washing before prayer, in a set order.
- **Qiblah**: the direction of the Ka'bah in Makkah, which Muslims face to pray.
- **Rak'ah**: one unit of prayer, made up of standing, bowing, prostrating and sitting.
- **Jummah**: the Friday midday congregational prayer at the mosque, with a sermon (khutbah).
- **Mosque**: masjid, the "place of prostration"; a building for communal prayer and community life.
- **Zakah**: the compulsory payment of 2.5% of savings above the nisab, given to those in need.
- **Sadaqah**: voluntary charity of any amount, given at any time.
- **Sawm**: fasting from dawn to sunset during the month of Ramadan.
- **Hajj**: the pilgrimage to Makkah in Dhul-Hijjah, required once of every able Muslim.
- **Ihram**: the state of purity, and the simple white clothing, of pilgrims on Hajj.
- **Halal**: what is permitted by Allah, such as food prepared according to Islamic law.
- **Haram**: what is forbidden by Allah, such as alcohol, pork and gambling.
- **Ummah**: the worldwide community of all Muslims, united by faith.

::: example (c) Hajj
Explain the importance of Hajj for Muslims. (8)

Hajj is the fifth pillar: every adult Muslim who is healthy and can afford it must go to Makkah once. Qur'an 2:125 says Allah made the Ka'bah a place of return and safety, and told believers to pray at the standing place of Ibrahim, so Hajj connects Muslims to Ibrahim and Isma'il, who built the House.

Each rite has meaning. In ihram, every man wears the same white sheets, showing that all are equal before Allah, whatever their wealth or nation, which makes the unity of the ummah visible. Tawaf around the Ka'bah shows that Allah is at the centre of life. Sa'i between Safa and Marwah recalls Hajar's trust in Allah as she searched for water. Standing at Arafat in prayer is the heart of Hajj, a time to seek forgiveness and a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement. Stoning the pillars at Mina rejects Shaytan, and the sacrifice recalls Ibrahim's obedience and begins Id-ul-Adha.

Hajj changes the pilgrim: an accepted Hajj is believed to wipe away sins, and pilgrims return with renewed faith and the title hajji. For British Muslims it is also a great sacrifice, costing several thousand pounds and needing time off work, often in extreme heat; many save for years, which shows how important it is to them.

[Band 4, 8 marks: rites explained, source, ummah, British issues, influence.]

::: example (c) zakah
Explain how zakah is used. (8)

Zakah is the compulsory payment of 2.5% of a Muslim's savings held for a year above the nisab. It purifies wealth and the heart from greed and reminds Muslims that all wealth belongs to Allah. Qur'an 9:60 sets out exactly who may receive it: the poor, the needy, those who collect it, those whose hearts are to be reconciled such as new Muslims, freeing captives, people in debt, the cause of Allah, and travellers in need. So zakah is not a general donation but a right of particular people.

In Britain Muslims calculate their own zakah and usually pay it through the mosque or charities such as Islamic Relief or the National Zakat Foundation, which gives it to people in need in the UK, for example helping families with rent or debts. Much is also sent abroad for famine and disaster relief. Many pay during Ramadan, believing the reward is greater, and pay zakat al-fitr before the Id prayer so everyone can celebrate. Zakah is different from sadaqah, which is voluntary charity of any amount. Using zakah in these ways reduces poverty, strengthens the ummah, and helps the giver grow spiritually.

[Band 4, 8 marks: accurate rules, 9:60 categories, British practice, influence.]
:::

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'It is too difficult to keep the Five Pillars in Britain today.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some would agree, because Britain is not a Muslim-majority country: summer fasts can last over eighteen hours, Jummah falls in the working day, there is rarely a call to prayer, and Hajj costs several thousand pounds. These are real difficulties, especially for students fasting during exams. However, this argument is weaker than it seems, because Islam itself allows for difficulty: Qur'an 2:184 lets the ill and travellers make up fasts, Hajj is only required of those able to go, and salah can be prayed anywhere clean. Many British workplaces and schools now provide prayer rooms, and charities make zakah easy to pay. Many Muslims also argue that difficulty increases the reward, as the pillars are meant to train self-discipline. Therefore keeping the pillars in Britain is harder in some ways, but not too difficult, because Islam is designed to be practised everywhere.
