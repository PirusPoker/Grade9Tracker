## What it is

This lesson covers the **Ten Obligatory Acts** of **Shi'a Islam** (in Arabic **Furu ad-Din**, the "branches of religion"): how Shi'a Muslims perform **salah**, observe **sawm**, pay **zakah** and make **Hajj** to Makkah (**Qur'an 2:125**); **khums** (the one-fifth payment on surplus income); pilgrimage to **Shi'a shrines**; **jihad**; **amr-bil-maroof** (encouraging good); **nahi anil munkar** (discouraging evil; the spec spells it "Nahil Anril Munkar"); **tawalla** (loving the friends of Allah); and **tabarra** (disapproving of evil-doers).

It is examined in Question 2 (Practices) of Component 3: (a) 2, (b) 5, (c) 8, (d) 15, with **no SPaG**. The June 2025 (c) was "Explain the importance of the Ten Obligatory Acts for Shi'a Muslims". The examiners' report said it had the **lowest attempt rate and the lowest mean mark on the whole paper**, even though it covers a full section of the spec. The best answers made a general point about why the Acts matter, such as teaching Muslims how to treat others, and then used individual Acts as evidence. Weak answers wrote only about the Sunni Five Pillars. So learn this lesson as thoroughly as 2.3f.

### Who are Shi'a Muslims?

Shi'a Muslims are the second-largest branch of Islam, roughly 10-15% of Muslims worldwide. Most live in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan, Bahrain and Lebanon, with large communities in Pakistan, India and the Gulf states. In Britain there are Shi'a communities of Iraqi, Iranian, Pakistani, Indian, East African and Lebanese heritage, among others, with their own mosques and centres (often called **imambargahs** or **Husayniyyas**) in cities such as London, Birmingham and Manchester.

Shi'a and Sunni Muslims share the Qur'an, the Shahadah, belief in Tawhid and the Prophet, and almost all the same worship. The main difference began over **leadership after the Prophet's death** in 632 CE. Shi'a Muslims ("Shi'at Ali", the party of Ali) believe the Prophet appointed his cousin and son-in-law **Ali** as his successor, and that leadership passed through a line of **Imams** from the Prophet's family (the **Ahl al-Bayt**). Most Shi'a Muslims (the **Twelvers**) follow twelve Imams, the last of whom, they believe, is hidden and will return. Today, Shi'a Muslims follow the rulings of senior living scholars, each called a **marja'** (source of emulation).

### Roots and branches

Shi'a teaching divides Islam into **roots** and **branches**, like a tree:

| | Roots: **Usul ad-Din** (beliefs, see 2.3b) | Branches: **Furu ad-Din** (practices, this lesson) |
|---|---|---|
| What | The five things a Shi'a Muslim must **believe** | The ten things a Shi'a Muslim must **do** |
| List | Tawhid, Adalat, Nubuwwah, Imamah, Mi'ad | Salah, Sawm, Zakah, Khums, Hajj, Jihad, Amr-bil-Maroof, Nahi anil Munkar, Tawalla, Tabarra |
| Image | The roots hold the tree in the ground | The branches show the faith in action and bear fruit |

The roots must be accepted through personal understanding; the branches are **obligatory** (wajib) acts of obedience. A tree without roots dies; roots without branches bear no fruit. So belief and practice belong together.

### Overlap with the Five Pillars

| Ten Obligatory Acts | Also a Sunni pillar? |
|---|---|
| 1 Salah (prayer) | Yes |
| 2 Sawm (fasting in Ramadan) | Yes |
| 3 Zakah (charity on set wealth) | Yes |
| 4 Khums (one fifth of surplus income) | No: a distinctively Shi'a duty |
| 5 Hajj (pilgrimage to Makkah) | Yes |
| 6 Jihad (struggle) | Not a pillar, but taught by Sunnis too |
| 7 Amr-bil-Maroof (encouraging good) | Not a pillar, but a Qur'anic duty for all Muslims |
| 8 Nahi anil Munkar (discouraging evil) | Not a pillar, but a Qur'anic duty for all Muslims |
| 9 Tawalla (love for the friends of Allah) | No: a distinctively Shi'a emphasis |
| 10 Tabarra (disapproval of evil-doers) | No: a distinctively Shi'a emphasis |

Four of the Acts are four of the pillars. The **Shahadah** is not listed as an Act because, for Shi'a Muslims, belief is covered by the roots; they say the same Shahadah, and many add in the call to prayer a testimony that Ali is the friend (wali) of Allah. The extra Acts turn faith **outwards**: towards the community, justice and right relationships.

### 1. Salah: how Shi'a Muslims pray

The five obligatory prayers are the same as for Sunni Muslims (Fajr, Zuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha), with wudu, the qiblah towards the Ka'bah, rak'ahs, recitation of al-Fatihah, bowing and prostration. Some differences in practice:

- **Three prayer times**: Shi'a Muslims usually **combine** Zuhr with Asr, and Maghrib with Isha, so they pray at **three** times a day (dawn, midday-afternoon and evening) while still performing all five prayers. They base this on hadith reporting that the Prophet sometimes combined prayers when he was not travelling. (Sunni Muslims also allow combining, but mainly when travelling or in difficulty.) In Britain this can make prayer easier to fit into a school or working day.
- **The turbah** (or mohr): a small tablet of clean clay or earth, often from **Karbala**, on which the forehead is placed in prostration. Shi'a teaching holds that prostration should be on earth or natural things, not on manufactured cloth, as a sign of humility.
- **Arms by the sides** while standing, rather than folded across the body (the Sunni Maliki school also prays with arms at the sides).
- The **adhan** includes "come to the best of deeds", and in wudu the feet are usually **wiped** rather than washed.
- **Jummah** prayer is observed in Shi'a mosques, though scholars differ on whether it is obligatory while the twelfth Imam is hidden.

### 2. Sawm: fasting

Shi'a Muslims fast during **Ramadan** from dawn to sunset with the same rules and exemptions (**Qur'an 2:184**). A visible difference: many Shi'a Muslims break the fast a little **later**, when the redness of the sky after sunset has passed, at the time of Maghrib prayer. The **Night of Power** (see 2.3i) and the anniversary of **Imam Ali's death** (in Ramadan, on the 21st) are kept with special prayers. The same British issues apply as for Sunnis (long summer days, exams, work).

### 3. Zakah

Shi'a Muslims pay **zakah** as a duty that purifies wealth. In Shi'a law it is traditionally due on a **set list of goods**: certain crops (wheat, barley, dates, raisins), livestock (camels, cattle, sheep and goats) and gold and silver. It goes to the same eight groups listed in **Qur'an 9:60** (the poor, the needy, and so on). Because cash savings are largely covered by **khums**, Shi'a giving is a **two-part** system. Shi'a Muslims also pay **zakat al-fitr** at the end of Ramadan.

### 4. Khums: one fifth

**Khums** means "**one fifth**". Shi'a Muslims pay **20%** of their **surplus income**: whatever is left over at the end of their financial year after paying for themselves and their family's reasonable needs (home, food, bills, education). Each person sets a "khums year" date and calculates it annually.

- **Source**: **Qur'an 8:41** says that a fifth of what is gained belongs to Allah, the Messenger, near relatives, orphans, the needy and travellers. Sunni scholars apply this verse to spoils of war (and things like buried treasure); Shi'a scholars understand "what is gained" to include **all profit**, so khums applies to everyday income.
- **How it is divided**: half is the **Sahm al-Imam** (the Imam's share). While the twelfth Imam is hidden, it is paid to, or with the permission of, a **marja'** or his representatives, and it funds religious work: mosques, seminaries, Islamic schools, scholars, publishing, and help for the poor. The other half is the **Sahm as-Sadat**, given to **needy descendants of the Prophet** (sayyids), who by tradition may not receive zakah.
- **In Britain**: Shi'a Muslims calculate khums themselves (many use guides from their marja's office) and pay it through the UK offices or representatives of their marja', or through approved charities and centres.
- **Why it matters**: it purifies wealth, supports the community's religious life and teaching, honours the Prophet's family, and keeps Shi'a scholarship independent of governments.

### 5. Hajj and pilgrimage to Shi'a shrines

**Hajj** to Makkah is obligatory once in a lifetime for every Shi'a Muslim who is able, with the same main rites as for Sunnis (ihram, tawaf, sa'i, Arafat, Muzdalifah, Jamarat, sacrifice). **Qur'an 2:125** describes the Ka'bah as a place of return and safety, linked to Ibrahim and Isma'il.

Shi'a Muslims also make **ziyarah** (visiting) to the **shrines** of the Imams and their families. This is **recommended**, not obligatory like Hajj, but it is deeply loved:

| Shrine | Who is buried there |
|---|---|
| **Karbala** (Iraq) | **Imam Husayn**, the Prophet's grandson, killed in 680 CE (see Ashura, 2.3i) |
| **Najaf** (Iraq) | **Imam Ali**, the first Imam |
| **Mashhad** (Iran) | **Imam Ali al-Ridha**, the eighth Imam |
| **Kadhimiya** (Baghdad) and **Samarra** (Iraq) | Other Imams |
| **Madinah** (Saudi Arabia) | The Prophet, and some Imams in the al-Baqi cemetery |

- **Arba'een**, forty days after Ashura, sees **millions** of pilgrims walk to Karbala, many on foot from Najaf; local people offer free food and rest along the way. It is among the largest annual gatherings in the world.
- **Purpose**: showing love for the Ahl al-Bayt (tawalla); remembering their sacrifice and example; prayer and asking Allah for forgiveness; strengthening faith and community.
- **From Britain**: organised group trips to Iraq and Iran; issues of cost, safety advice for travel to the region, and time off. Many British Shi'a who cannot travel hold **majlis** gatherings at home or in their centre instead.

### 6. Jihad: struggle

**Jihad** means **struggle** or **striving** in the way of Allah. **Greater Jihad** is the inner struggle to live as a good Muslim every day; **Lesser Jihad** is the outer struggle to defend Islam, including, under strict conditions, by force. Both are covered in **2.3h**. For Shi'a Muslims, Imam Husayn's stand against an unjust ruler at Karbala is a powerful example of struggling for justice whatever the cost.

### 7 and 8. Amr-bil-Maroof and Nahi anil Munkar

- **Amr-bil-Maroof**: **encouraging** others to do what is **good** (ma'ruf, "what is known to be right").
- **Nahi anil Munkar**: **discouraging** others from what is **wrong** (munkar, "what is rejected").

**Qur'an 3:104** calls for a community among the believers that invites to good, enjoins what is right and forbids what is wrong. A well-known hadith (in Sahih Muslim) teaches that whoever sees a wrong should change it **with their hand**; if they cannot, **with their tongue**; and if they cannot, **in their heart**, which is the weakest faith.

So there are **stages**: first disapproving in the heart; then speaking out kindly and wisely; then taking action, where that is lawful and right. Shi'a scholars set **conditions**: the person must know the act really is right or wrong, there must be a reasonable hope that speaking will help, the wrong must be continuing, and acting must not cause serious harm. It must be done with **wisdom and gentleness**, starting with oneself and one's family.

**Examples in Britain**: encouraging a friend to pray, be honest or revise; setting a good example; volunteering, such as helping prisoners reform through charities; teaching at the madrasah; speaking out against bullying, racism, drug-dealing or injustice; campaigning on poverty and climate; giving to charity, because poverty can drive people to crime. These Acts make every Muslim **responsible for the moral health of society**, not just for themselves.

### 9 and 10. Tawalla and Tabarra

- **Tawalla**: the duty to **love the friends of Allah**: Allah, the Prophet, his family (the Ahl al-Bayt), the Imams, and all righteous people. Love is shown by **following their example**, not just by feeling. Shi'a Muslims link this to **Qur'an 42:23**, where the Prophet asks for no reward except love for his near kin.
- **Tabarra**: the duty to **disassociate from**, and **express disapproval of**, the **enemies of Allah** and **evil-doers**: rejecting injustice, oppression and wrongdoing, and refusing to imitate those who do it. The Qur'an praises Ibrahim, who declared himself free of the idolatry of his people (**60:4**).

The two go together: loving goodness and rejecting evil are two sides of one coin. Shi'a scholars stress that tabarra is about **rejecting wrong actions** and not supporting injustice; it is **not** permission for hatred or insults, and senior Shi'a scholars have ruled against insulting figures whom Sunni Muslims revere, for the sake of Muslim unity. In practice, tawalla is seen in mourning and celebrating the Imams' lives, and tabarra in standing against oppression, as Husayn did at Karbala.

### Why the Ten Obligatory Acts matter

A general point, then evidence from individual Acts: this is what the 2025 report praised.

- **They show faith in action**: the roots (belief) must bear fruit in the branches (practice).
- **Obedience and devotion**: each Act is wajib, done for Allah; they make life revolve around serving Allah.
- **They teach how to treat others**: amr-bil-maroof, nahi anil munkar, zakah and khums are about other people, so a Shi'a Muslim cannot live only for themselves.
- **Justice**: tabarra and jihad reject oppression, reflecting Adalat (Allah's justice).
- **Community and identity**: khums funds mosques, schools and scholars; ziyarah and tawalla bind the community to the Ahl al-Bayt; they help preserve **Shi'a identity**, especially as a minority in Britain.
- **Leadership**: they keep Shi'a Muslims connected to their **marja'** and imams, who teach the Acts and guide a whole way of life.
- **Reward and the Akhirah**: keeping them prepares a Muslim for judgement.

::: terms
- **Ten Obligatory Acts**: the ten practical duties of Shi'a Islam, known as Furu ad-Din, the branches of religion.
- **Furu ad-Din**: the "branches of religion": what Shi'a Muslims must do, growing from the roots of belief.
- **Ahl al-Bayt**: the "people of the house": the Prophet's family, including Ali, Fatimah, Hasan, Husayn and the Imams.
- **Marja'**: a senior Shi'a scholar whose rulings Shi'a Muslims follow; khums is often paid through his office.
- **Turbah**: a small tablet of clay, often from Karbala, on which Shi'a Muslims place the forehead in prostration.
- **Khums**: the Shi'a payment of one fifth (20%) of surplus annual income, based on Qur'an 8:41.
- **Sahm al-Imam**: the Imam's half of khums, used for religious work through a marja'.
- **Sahm as-Sadat**: the half of khums given to needy descendants of the Prophet.
- **Ziyarah**: a visit or pilgrimage to the shrine of an Imam or holy figure, such as Karbala or Najaf.
- **Amr-bil-Maroof**: the duty to encourage others to do what is good and right.
- **Nahi anil Munkar**: the duty to discourage others from doing what is wrong or evil.
- **Tawalla**: the duty to love the friends of Allah, especially the Prophet and his family, and follow their example.
- **Tabarra**: the duty to disassociate from and disapprove of evil-doers and injustice.
:::

::: example (b) khums
Describe how Shi'a Muslims pay khums. (5)

Khums, meaning "one fifth", is one of the Ten Obligatory Acts. Each year, on their own "khums year" date, a Shi'a Muslim works out their surplus income, the money left after paying for their own and their family's reasonable living costs, and gives 20% of it. This is based on Qur'an 8:41, which says a fifth of what is gained belongs to Allah, the Messenger, his near relatives, orphans, the needy and travellers. The payment is divided in two: half, the Imam's share, is paid to or with the permission of a marja', a senior scholar, and funds mosques, seminaries and Islamic education; the other half goes to needy descendants of the Prophet. In Britain Shi'a Muslims often use their marja's guidance to calculate it and pay through his UK office or an approved centre. Paying khums purifies their wealth and reminds them that everything they own comes from Allah.

::: example (c) importance of the Ten Obligatory Acts
Explain the importance of the Ten Obligatory Acts for Shi'a Muslims. (8)

The Ten Obligatory Acts, or Furu ad-Din, are the branches of Shi'a Islam that grow from the roots of belief, Usul ad-Din. They are important because they turn belief into action: a Shi'a Muslim who believes in Tawhid and Allah's justice must show it by obeying Allah in everyday life. Acts such as salah, prayed at three times a day with the forehead on a turbah, and sawm in Ramadan keep Allah at the centre of their life.

The Acts also teach Shi'a Muslims how to treat others. Amr-bil-Maroof and Nahi anil Munkar mean encouraging good and discouraging evil, as Qur'an 3:104 commands. A Shi'a Muslim in Britain might encourage friends to be honest, volunteer with prisoners to help them reform, or speak out against racism. Zakah and khums, one fifth of surplus income, support the poor and fund mosques and Islamic education, so the community is strengthened.

Finally, the Acts preserve Shi'a identity. Tawalla means loving the Prophet's family and following their example, and tabarra means rejecting evil and injustice, which is shown by Imam Husayn's stand at Karbala. Pilgrimage to Makkah and ziyarah to shrines such as Karbala and Najaf unite Shi'a Muslims worldwide. Keeping the Acts also prepares them for the Day of Judgement.

[Band 4, 8 marks: general points backed by named Acts, sources, influence on individuals and community.]

::: example (b) amr-bil-maroof and nahi anil munkar
Describe how Shi'a Muslims might carry out amr-bil-maroof and nahi anil munkar. (5)

Amr-bil-Maroof means encouraging others to do good, and Nahi anil Munkar means discouraging them from evil; Qur'an 3:104 calls for a community that enjoins right and forbids wrong. A hadith teaches that a wrong should be changed with the hand, then the tongue, and at least hated in the heart. So a Shi'a Muslim starts with themselves and their family, setting a good example, then speaks out gently and wisely, for example encouraging a friend to pray or to stop bullying someone. Scholars say they should only speak if they know the act is wrong and it may help, and not if it would cause serious harm. In Britain this might mean volunteering in prisons, campaigning against injustice, or giving to charities that tackle poverty, which can lead to crime.

[Band 3, 5 marks: both Acts, source, stages and conditions, practical British examples.]
:::

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Encouraging others to do good is the most important of the Ten Obligatory Acts.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some Shi'a Muslims would agree, because amr-bil-maroof affects the whole community, not just the individual: Qur'an 3:104 calls for a community that invites to good, and if Muslims encourage each other, the other Acts, such as salah and khums, are more likely to be kept. However, this argument depends on the Muslim being good themselves; scholars teach that amr-bil-maroof starts with oneself, so salah, which keeps a Muslim close to Allah five times a day, may be more basic. Others would say tawalla is the foundation, because love for the Prophet's family shapes every other act. On balance, encouraging good is especially important for Shi'a Muslims as a minority in Britain, because it builds a strong and caring community, but it cannot be "the most important" on its own, since the Ten Acts are branches of one tree and depend on each other.
:::
