## What it is

This part of **Theme 1: Issues of Relationships** covers what happens when marriages break down and what religions teach about sex. You need: varying Christian attitudes to **adultery, divorce, annulment, separation and remarriage** (with **Matthew 19:8-9** and **Mark 10:9**); Muslim attitudes to **adultery, divorce, separation and remarriage** (with **Qur'an 4:35, 4:128-130 and 2:229**); Christian teaching on the **nature and purpose of sex** and **contraception**, including **Thomas Aquinas's Natural Law** and its **five primary precepts**, especially the **second** (reproduction); and Muslim teaching on sex and contraception (**Qur'an 17:32**).

### The law in Britain (background)

- Since April 2022 divorce in England and Wales is **"no-fault"**: a spouse (or both) states that the marriage has broken down irretrievably, and nobody has to prove adultery or bad behaviour.
- **Adultery** is not a crime in Britain. A religious divorce (e.g. an Islamic divorce from a Shari'ah council) does not end a civil marriage; a civil divorce is also needed.

### Adultery

**Adultery** is sex between a married person and someone who is not their spouse. Christians and Muslims agree it is a serious sin, because it breaks **commitment**, betrays trust and harms children.

- **Christianity**: "You shall not commit adultery" is the seventh of the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:14). Jesus went further: even looking at someone lustfully is adultery "in the heart" (Matthew 5:27-28). Yet he did not condemn the woman caught in adultery; he told her to "go and sin no more" (John 8:1-11), so Christians combine strong disapproval with **forgiveness**.
- **Islam**: adultery and all sex outside marriage are **zina**. "Do not approach unlawful sexual intercourse; it is an immorality and an evil way" (Qur'an **17:32**). Classical Shari'ah gives a severe punishment, but only on very strict evidence (four witnesses to the act, 24:4), so in practice it was hard to prove. In Britain Muslims follow British law, and the emphasis is on **repentance** (tawbah) and avoiding situations that lead to temptation: modesty and not being alone with someone of the opposite sex.

### Christian attitudes to divorce, annulment, separation and remarriage

**Mark 10:9**: "What God has joined together, let no one separate." **Matthew 19:8-9**: Jesus says Moses allowed divorce only "because your hearts were hard", "but it was not this way from the beginning"; anyone who divorces his wife, "except for sexual immorality", and marries another commits adultery.

| Tradition | Divorce | Remarriage |
|---|---|---|
| **Catholic** | A valid sacramental marriage **cannot be dissolved** by anything except death. A couple may **separate** (live apart) if staying together is harmful, e.g. in abuse, and a civil divorce may be tolerated for legal protection, but they remain married in God's eyes. | Not allowed in church while the former spouse lives, unless there has been an **annulment**. |
| **Church of England** | Marriage is meant to be lifelong, but when it has truly broken down divorce may be the lesser evil. Reconciliation should be tried first. | Since **2002** a divorced person may remarry in church **at the priest's discretion**; otherwise a civil wedding followed by a service of prayer and dedication. |
| **Orthodox** | Divorce may be allowed in some cases (e.g. adultery), seen as a pastoral concession to human weakness. | Up to **three** marriages; a second or third wedding has a more penitential tone. |
| **Many Protestants** (e.g. Methodists) | Divorce is accepted when a marriage has failed. | Generally allowed, with the emphasis on God's forgiveness and a new start. |

**Annulment** is **not** a Catholic divorce. It is a declaration by a Church tribunal that a **valid marriage never existed**, because something essential was missing at the wedding: for example one partner did not give **free consent**, lacked the maturity to understand the commitment, never intended to be faithful or to have children, or was already married. After an annulment both people are free to marry in church.

**How the sources are interpreted:**
- **Catholics** read Mark 10:9 literally: God makes the bond, so no human authority can break it. They read the "sexual immorality" clause in Matthew 19:9 as referring to unions that were never lawful marriages in the first place, not as permission to divorce.
- **Many Protestants** read Matthew 19:9 as a real **exception**: adultery breaks the marriage, so divorce (and remarriage) is allowed in that case.
- **Liberal Christians** point out that Jesus was protecting women, who could be cast out easily in his time; his deeper teaching is **love and compassion**, and it cannot be God's will for someone to stay in an abusive marriage.

### Muslim attitudes to divorce, separation and remarriage

Divorce is **permitted but disliked**: a well-known hadith calls it the most hated to Allah of all lawful things. The Qur'an lays out steps to save a marriage first.

1. **Reconciliation through arbitration**: if a breach is feared, "appoint an arbitrator from his family and an arbitrator from her family"; if they want reconciliation, Allah will bring it about (**4:35**).
2. **Settlement**: if a wife fears ill-treatment or desertion, there is no blame if the couple make peace, and "reconciliation is best" (**4:128**). The passage warns that perfect fairness is hard, and urges people not to leave a spouse in limbo (4:129). If they do separate, Allah will provide for each of them from His abundance (**4:130**), so divorce is not a disaster beyond God's care.
3. **Talaq** (divorce pronounced by the husband) is followed by a waiting period, the **'iddah** (about three months), during which the couple may reconcile, the wife stays in the home, and it becomes clear whether she is pregnant. This is a time of **separation** with a door left open.
4. **Twice, then a decision**: "Divorce is twice. Then either keep [her] in an acceptable manner or release [her] with good treatment" (**2:229**). A third divorce is final. The same verse says the husband may not take back the mahr he gave.
5. A wife can end the marriage by **khul'** (usually returning the mahr), or ask a judge or, in Britain, a **Shari'ah council** to dissolve it.

Diversity: **Shi'a** law requires the talaq to be said in set words before **two just witnesses**, and does not count three talaqs said at one time as more than one. Many Sunni scholars today take the same view on the "triple talaq", though historically Sunni schools counted it as three.

**Remarriage** is allowed and often encouraged, so that no one is left alone or without support. The Prophet's own wives included widows and a divorcee.

### The nature and purpose of sex

**Christianity**: sex is a **gift from God**, good in itself, with two purposes inside marriage: **unitive** (it binds the couple together as "one flesh", Genesis 2:24) and **procreative** (it can bring new life, "be fruitful and increase", Genesis 1:28). Traditional teaching is that sex belongs only in marriage. Many liberal Christians accept sex in a committed, faithful relationship.

**Islam**: sex is a gift from Allah, to be enjoyed **within nikah**, where it is even an act of reward: a hadith says that in a man's lawful relations with his wife there is **sadaqah** (charity). Both spouses have a right to sexual fulfilment. Outside marriage it is **zina**, and Qur'an **17:32** forbids even "approaching" it, so Muslims avoid anything that leads towards it.

### Natural Law and contraception

**Thomas Aquinas** (13th century) taught that God made everything with a purpose, and that human **reason** can discover it. Acting in line with our God-given purposes is good; acting against them is wrong. His **five primary precepts** are:

1. Preserve **life**
2. **Reproduce** (continue the species)
3. **Educate** the young
4. Live in an ordered **society**
5. **Worship** God

From these come **secondary precepts** (more specific rules). Natural Law is usually **absolutist**: some acts are always wrong whatever the consequences.

**The second primary precept applied to contraception: varied interpretations**

| Interpretation | Reasoning | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **Catholic teaching** (*Humanae Vitae*, Pope Paul VI, 1968) | The natural purpose of sex includes reproduction, so **every sexual act** must stay open to new life. Artificial contraception deliberately blocks that purpose. | Artificial contraception is wrong. **Natural family planning** (using the infertile times of the woman's cycle) is allowed for serious reasons, because it does not block the act itself. |
| **Wider reading** (many Anglicans and other Protestants, some Catholic theologians) | The precept is fulfilled by the **marriage as a whole** being open to children, not by each act. Other precepts matter too: spacing births helps **educate** the young; condoms **preserve life** by preventing disease. | Contraception can be compatible with Natural Law. |
| **Rejecting absolutism** | Situation ethics and many liberal Christians say the most loving action in the situation matters more than a fixed rule. | Contraception is a matter of **conscience** for a married couple. |

**Other Christian views**: the Church of England's bishops first accepted contraception in some circumstances at the **1930 Lambeth Conference**; most Protestant churches now leave it to the couple's conscience. Many Christians reject methods that may work **after** fertilisation, because of the sanctity of life.

### Muslim teaching on contraception

- Some companions of the Prophet practised withdrawal (**'azl**) and it was not forbidden, so **most scholars** allow reversible contraception within marriage, provided **both spouses agree** and it is not used to avoid children altogether.
- Good reasons include spacing births, the mother's health and serious hardship.
- **Permanent** methods (sterilisation) are generally not allowed except for medical reasons; methods that may act after conception are rejected by many as close to abortion.
- Some Muslims oppose contraception, citing "do not kill your children for fear of poverty; We provide for them and for you" (17:31), and trusting Allah to provide.
- Contraception cannot make sex outside marriage acceptable: 17:32 still applies.

::: terms
- **Adultery**: sexual intercourse between a married person and someone who is not their husband or wife.
- **Divorce**: the legal ending of a marriage.
- **Annulment**: a Catholic Church ruling that a valid marriage never existed, so both people are free to marry.
- **Separation**: a married couple deciding to live apart without ending the marriage.
- **Remarriage**: marrying again after a divorce or the death of a spouse.
- **Contraception**: artificial or natural methods used to prevent pregnancy.
- **Natural Law**: Aquinas's theory that God gave everything a purpose that human reason can discover, and that acting in line with it is good.
- **Primary precepts**: Aquinas's five basic purposes of human life: preserve life, reproduce, educate the young, live in society, worship God.
- **Absolutism**: the view that some actions are always right or wrong, whatever the situation or consequences.
- **Talaq**: divorce in Islam pronounced by the husband, followed by a waiting period.
- **'Iddah**: the waiting period of about three months after talaq, when the couple may still reconcile.
- **Khul'**: divorce in Islam begun by the wife, usually by returning the mahr.
:::

::: example (c) divorce in two religions
Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, attitudes to divorce. (8)

**Christianity.** Catholics believe divorce is impossible for a valid sacramental marriage, because Jesus said "What God has joined together, let no one separate" (Mark 10:9) and taught that Moses allowed divorce only because of "hard hearts" (Matthew 19:8). So a divorced Catholic cannot remarry in church while their spouse is alive, unless the marriage is annulled. The Church of England agrees that marriage should be lifelong, but sees divorce as the lesser of two evils when a marriage has failed, so a priest may remarry divorced people in church. This shows Christian diversity: both value the vows, but the Anglican approach puts more weight on compassion.

**Islam.** Muslims believe divorce is allowed but is the most disliked of lawful things, so it must be a last resort. The Qur'an tells families to appoint an arbitrator from each side to try to reconcile the couple (4:35), and teaches that "reconciliation is best" (4:128). If talaq is pronounced there is a waiting period, the 'iddah, in which the couple can still reunite. Qur'an 2:229 says divorce may happen twice, after which the husband must "keep [her] in an acceptable manner or release [her] with good treatment", so even divorce must be done kindly. A wife may also seek khul'. Remarriage is allowed.

::: example Natural Law and contraception
Describe how Aquinas's Natural Law has been used to argue against contraception. (5)

Thomas Aquinas taught that God created everything for a purpose and that human reason can work out what that purpose is. He set out five primary precepts: preserve life, reproduce, educate the young, live in an ordered society and worship God. The second precept, reproduction, shows that one natural purpose of sex is to create new life. The Catholic Church, in Humanae Vitae (1968), applies this absolutely: every sexual act must remain open to life, so artificial contraception, which deliberately blocks that purpose, is always wrong. Catholics who need to space births may use natural family planning instead, because it works with the body's natural cycle rather than against it.

[Band 3, 5 marks.]
:::

::: example (d) weighing an argument
'Religious believers should never use contraception.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

The strongest support for the statement comes from Natural Law. If the natural purpose of sex includes reproduction, as Aquinas's second primary precept says, then blocking it is acting against God's design, which is why the Catholic Church teaches in Humanae Vitae that artificial contraception is wrong. However, this argument depends on reading the precept as applying to **every act**. Many Anglicans argue that a marriage that welcomes children as a whole still fulfils it, and that other precepts — preserving life (protecting the mother's health) and educating the young (spacing children so they can be cared for) — actually support family planning. Most Muslim scholars agree from a different source, the hadith on 'azl, provided both spouses consent. So the word "never" is hard to defend even within a Natural Law framework, because the theory itself can be read in more than one way.

[A full answer adds further views and ends with a clear judgement.]
