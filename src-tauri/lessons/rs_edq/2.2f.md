## What it is

This lesson covers the **sacraments**. You need: **diverse beliefs regarding sacraments**; the **role, meaning and celebration of baptism and the Eucharist** (**John 3:3-6**); and **diverse interpretations** of baptism and the Eucharist, with reference to the beliefs of the **Catholic and Protestant** Churches.

This is part of Question 2 (Practices) of Component 2. **Sacraments** is one of the eight (a) concepts. In June 2025, 2(c) asked candidates to "explain different Christian beliefs about the Eucharist", and over 15% did not attempt it because they did not recognise the word. **Eucharist** = Holy Communion = Mass = Lord's Supper = Divine Liturgy. The best answers used terms such as **transubstantiation** and **consubstantiation** accurately.

### What is a sacrament?

A **sacrament** is a **religious rite or ceremony** in which an **outward, visible sign** (water, bread, wine, oil) shows and brings an **inward, invisible grace** from God. The Anglican catechism's phrase "an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace" is widely used. Catholics add that the sacraments were **instituted by Christ** and actually **give** the grace they signify.

| Tradition | Number | Which |
|---|---|---|
| **Catholic** | **seven** | baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, reconciliation (confession), anointing of the sick, marriage, holy orders (ordination) |
| **Orthodox** | **seven** "holy mysteries" (some say more) | the same seven, e.g. chrismation instead of confirmation, given straight after baptism |
| **Anglican** | **two** "sacraments of the Gospel" | baptism and Eucharist; the other five are honoured as **sacramental rites** |
| **Most Protestants** (Baptist, Methodist, Evangelical) | **two**, often called **ordinances** | baptism and the Lord's Supper, because **Jesus himself commanded** them (Matthew 28:19; Luke 22:19) |
| **Quakers, Salvation Army** | **none** | no outward rituals are needed; grace is received inwardly, and the whole of life can be sacramental |

**Why sacraments matter**: they mark key moments in a Christian's life (birth, growing up, marriage, illness, death); they bring God's **grace** and strengthen faith; they unite believers as the Church; they obey Jesus' commands.

### Baptism: role and meaning

**Baptism** is the sacrament of **initiation**: it makes a person a member of the **Church**, the body of Christ. Water symbolises **washing away sin** and **new life**.

- **Jesus' example**: Jesus was baptised by John in the River Jordan, and the Spirit came down on him like a dove (Matthew 3:13-17).
- **Jesus' command**: "go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
- **John 3:3-6**: the Pharisee **Nicodemus** came to Jesus at night. Jesus told him that no one can see the kingdom of God unless they are **born again**. Nicodemus asked how a grown person could be born a second time. Jesus replied that no one can enter the kingdom of God unless they are **born of water and the Spirit**: flesh gives birth to flesh, but the **Spirit gives birth to spirit**. Christians link this to baptism: **water** and the **Spirit** bring a spiritual **rebirth**.

### Infant baptism

Practised by **Catholics, Orthodox, Anglicans, Methodists** and others.

**How it is celebrated** (Catholic and Anglican pattern):

1. The family, with **godparents**, bring the baby to the church, usually at the **font** near the entrance (a sign that baptism is the way in).
2. The priest asks the name; the baby is **signed with the cross** on the forehead.
3. Bible readings and prayers.
4. Parents and godparents **reject sin and evil** and **profess faith** in Father, Son and Holy Spirit on the child's behalf, promising to bring the child up as a Christian.
5. The priest pours **water** over the baby's head **three times**, saying "I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit".
6. Catholics **anoint** the baby with **chrism** (holy oil); the child may be dressed in a **white garment** (purity, new life in Christ); a **candle** lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle is given (Christ the light of the world).
7. The congregation welcomes the child into the Church.

In the **Orthodox** Church, the baby is fully **immersed** three times and receives **chrismation** and first **communion** at the same service.

**Reasons for infant baptism**:

- Catholics teach that it washes away **original sin** and brings the child into God's grace from the start.
- Whole **households** were baptised in the early Church (e.g. Acts 16:33).
- Grace is God's **gift**, not something a person earns by understanding; a baby is loved by God before it can respond.
- The child grows up within the Christian community; they later make the promises for themselves at **confirmation**.

### Believers' baptism

Practised by **Baptists**, **Pentecostals** and many **Evangelical** churches.

**How it is celebrated**:

1. The candidate is old enough to **choose** for themselves (a teenager or adult) and has usually attended preparation classes.
2. They often give a **testimony**, explaining how they came to faith.
3. They are asked whether they repent of sin and **trust in Jesus** as Lord and Saviour.
4. They go down into a **baptistry** (a pool in the church floor), or a river or the sea, and the minister **fully immerses** them in the water "in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit".
5. Coming up from the water, they are welcomed; there may be songs and prayer for the Holy Spirit.

**Reasons for believers' baptism**:

- In the New Testament people were baptised after they **repented and believed** (Acts 2:38).
- **Jesus** was baptised as an adult.
- Full immersion pictures **dying and rising with Christ**: going under is burial of the old life, coming up is new life (Romans 6:4).
- Baptism is a **public declaration** of a personal faith, which a baby cannot make. Babies are instead **dedicated** (blessed, with parents promising to raise them in faith).

### The Eucharist: role and meaning

The **Eucharist** ("thanksgiving") remembers and re-enacts the **Last Supper**. Jesus took bread, gave thanks, broke it and said, "Take and eat; this is my body"; he took the cup and called it his "blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (Matthew 26:26-28). Paul records the command "do this in remembrance of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24).

**Names**: **Mass** (Catholic); **Divine Liturgy** (Orthodox); **Holy Communion** or Eucharist (Anglican, Methodist); the **Lord's Supper** or **Breaking of Bread** (many Protestants).

**Why it matters to all who celebrate it**: it remembers Jesus' **sacrifice** and the new covenant; it brings believers into **communion** with Christ and with each other; it gives spiritual strength; it looks forward to the heavenly banquet.

### Diverse interpretations of the Eucharist

| View | Held by | Belief about the bread and wine |
|---|---|---|
| **Transubstantiation** | **Catholics** | At the **consecration** by the priest, the **substance** of the bread and wine becomes the **real body and blood** of Christ, while the appearance, taste and texture remain. Christ is **truly present**. |
| **Real presence (a mystery)** | **Orthodox** | Christ is truly present, but **how** is a holy mystery that should not be defined. |
| **Consubstantiation** | **Lutherans** (and some Anglicans) | Christ's body and blood are present **"in, with and under"** the bread and wine, which remain bread and wine. |
| **Spiritual presence** | many Anglicans, Reformed churches | Christ is **spiritually** present and received by faith; Anglicans range from Catholic-like to memorial views. |
| **Memorial** (Zwinglian) | **Baptists**, many **Evangelicals** | The bread and wine are **symbols**; the meal is a **remembrance** of Jesus' death, "do this in remembrance of me". |
| **No outward Eucharist** | **Quakers**, Salvation Army | Communion with Christ is inward and can happen at every meal. |

### How it is celebrated

- **Catholic Mass**: celebrated daily, and Catholics are expected to attend on **Sundays** and holy days. Liturgy of the **Word** (readings, homily), then Liturgy of the **Eucharist**: offertory, the **Eucharistic prayer** in which the priest repeats Jesus' words, consecration, the **Lord's Prayer**, sign of peace, and **Holy Communion**, usually a wafer called the **host** (sometimes with the cup too). The Mass is seen as a **re-presentation** of Christ's **one sacrifice** on the cross, not a new one. Consecrated hosts are kept in the **tabernacle**. Children usually make their **first Holy Communion** at about seven or eight, after confession.
- **Orthodox Divine Liturgy**: much is sung; the bread is **leavened**; the priest prepares the gifts behind the **iconostasis** (screen of icons); communion is given on a **spoon**, bread soaked in wine.
- **Anglican Holy Communion**: from **Common Worship** or the Book of Common Prayer; people usually kneel or stand at the altar rail to receive bread and wine from the priest; weekly in many churches.
- **Protestant Lord's Supper / Breaking of Bread**: simpler and often **monthly**; ordinary bread and often **non-alcoholic wine** in small cups, passed along the rows so believers serve one another; emphasis on the Bible words and personal faith; in some churches any member may lead.

### Catholic and Protestant views compared

| | Catholic | Protestant (most) |
|---|---|---|
| Number of sacraments | seven | two |
| Baptism | infant; removes original sin | infant (Anglican, Methodist) or believers' only (Baptist) |
| Eucharist | transubstantiation; sacrifice re-presented; priest needed | memorial or spiritual presence; meal of remembrance |
| Frequency | daily Mass; Sunday obligation | weekly to monthly, or less |
| Grace | received through the sacraments | received through faith; sacraments are signs of it |

::: terms
- **Sacrament**: a religious rite in which an outward, visible sign brings an inward, invisible grace from God.
- **Grace**: God's free, undeserved love and gifts, which Christians believe the sacraments bring or show.
- **Baptism**: the sacrament of initiation into the Church, using water to symbolise cleansing from sin and new life.
- **Infant baptism**: baptism of babies, with parents and godparents making promises on the child's behalf.
- **Believers' baptism**: baptism, usually by full immersion, of people old enough to declare their own faith.
- **Godparents**: adults who promise at an infant baptism to help bring the child up in the Christian faith.
- **Eucharist**: the sacrament remembering the Last Supper with bread and wine; also called Mass, Holy Communion or Lord's Supper.
- **Transubstantiation**: the Catholic belief that the bread and wine become the real body and blood of Christ at consecration.
- **Consubstantiation**: the Lutheran belief that Christ is present in, with and under bread and wine that remain unchanged.
- **Memorial view**: the belief that the bread and wine are symbols, and the Eucharist is a remembrance of Jesus' death.
- **Ordinance**: a Protestant term for baptism and the Lord's Supper as practices commanded by Jesus.
:::

::: example (c) the Eucharist
Explain different Christian beliefs about the Eucharist. (8)

The Eucharist, also called Mass, Holy Communion or the Lord's Supper, remembers the Last Supper, when Jesus said of the bread "this is my body" and of the wine "my blood of the covenant… poured out for the forgiveness of sins" (Matthew 26:26-28). Catholics believe in transubstantiation: at the consecration the bread and wine become the real body and blood of Christ, though they still look and taste the same, so the Mass re-presents his sacrifice on the cross and Catholics receive Christ himself. The Orthodox also believe Christ is truly present but call it a mystery that should not be explained. Lutherans believe in consubstantiation, that Christ is present "in, with and under" unchanged bread and wine. Many Anglicans believe in a real but spiritual presence received by faith. Baptists and many Evangelicals hold a memorial view: the bread and wine are symbols, and they obey Jesus' command, "do this in remembrance of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24). Quakers have no outward Eucharist at all.

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Baptism should only be for adults.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Baptists would agree, because in the New Testament people were baptised after they repented and believed (Acts 2:38), and Jesus himself was baptised as an adult. Baptism is a personal declaration of faith and a picture of dying and rising with Christ (Romans 6:4), which a baby cannot understand. However, Catholics, Orthodox and Anglicans argue that infant baptism is right, because it washes away original sin and shows that grace is God's free gift, not a reward for understanding. Jesus said no one can enter the kingdom without being "born of water and the Spirit" (John 3:5), and whole households were baptised in the early Church. The child can confirm the promises later. The infant-baptism view seems stronger in showing God's love comes first, but the believers' view rightly stresses personal commitment, which confirmation also provides.
