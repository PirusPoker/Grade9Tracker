## What it is

This short lesson completes Theme 3 with **forgiveness**. You need: **Christian teaching** on forgiveness (**Matthew 18:21-22**, **Matthew 6:14-15**); **Muslim teaching** (**Qur'an 42:30**, **64:14**); and **examples of forgiveness arising from personal beliefs**. Questions often link forgiveness to punishment, so keep the two ideas clearly separate.

### What forgiveness is, and is not

- **Forgiveness** means letting go of anger, resentment and the wish for revenge against someone who has wronged you, and pardoning them.
- **Reconciliation** goes further: restoring a broken relationship so that both sides can live or work together again. Forgiveness can happen without reconciliation (for example, if the offender is not sorry).
- Forgiveness does **not** mean pretending the wrong did not matter, and it does **not** mean the offender escapes **punishment**. A victim can forgive personally while the law still requires justice. Many believers say forgiveness and punishment work together: punishment deals with justice for society, forgiveness heals the victim and offers the offender a fresh start.

### Christian teaching on forgiveness

- **God forgives**: Christians believe God forgives the sins of all who truly repent, because of Jesus' death on the cross. Jesus prayed for those crucifying him: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34).
- **Matthew 18:21-22**: Peter asked how many times he should forgive someone who sins against him, "up to seven times?" Jesus replied not seven times but **seventy-seven times** (some translations say "seventy times seven"). Christians interpret this as meaning there should be **no limit** to forgiveness. Jesus followed it with the parable of the **unforgiving servant**, who was forgiven a huge debt but refused to forgive a small one.
- **Matthew 6:14-15**: straight after the **Lord's Prayer** ("forgive us our sins, as we forgive those who sin against us"), Jesus taught that if you forgive others, your heavenly Father will forgive you; but if you do not forgive others, your Father will not forgive your sins. Christians interpret this as making forgiveness a **condition** of receiving God's forgiveness: someone who refuses to forgive has not understood God's mercy.
- **Diversity**:
  - **Catholics** receive God's forgiveness in the **sacrament of reconciliation** (confession), where a priest gives absolution after the person confesses and repents, and they are asked to make amends.
  - **Protestants** usually confess directly to God in prayer and in services, and stress forgiveness through faith and grace.
  - **Quakers** and many liberal Christians emphasise **restorative justice**: bringing offenders and victims together so the offender understands the harm and the victim can find healing.
  - Most Christians agree that forgiving does not cancel the need for justice: Christians still support lawful punishment (Romans 13).
- **Hard cases**: some Christians honestly find certain wrongs, such as the murder of a child, very hard to forgive, and see forgiveness as a long process that relies on God's help.

### Muslim teaching on forgiveness

- **Allah forgives**: among the names of Allah are **al-Ghafur** (the Most Forgiving) and **al-Rahim** (the Most Merciful). Almost every surah opens "In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful". Allah forgives those who sincerely **repent**; this is **tawbah**: regretting the sin, stopping it, resolving not to repeat it, and putting right any wrong done to others.
- **Qur'an 42:30**: whatever misfortune happens to people is because of what their own hands have earned, but Allah **pardons much**. Muslims interpret this as teaching that humans are responsible for wrongdoing, yet Allah's mercy is greater than strict justice: He does not punish every sin.
- **Qur'an 64:14**: even among a believer's spouses and children there may be some who act as enemies, so be careful; but if you **pardon, overlook and forgive**, Allah is **Forgiving and Merciful**. Muslims interpret this as teaching that forgiving even those closest to us who have hurt us is the way to receive Allah's mercy, and that forgiveness should start at home.
- **Forgiveness in the Shari'ah**: under **qisas** (equal retaliation), the family of a murder victim may choose to **forgive** the killer and accept **diyah** (compensation) instead of the death penalty; the Qur'an presents this as a mercy (2:178). So forgiveness has a place even in the law.
- **Limits**: Muslims believe some wrongs must still be punished for the sake of justice (16:90). Some sins against other people can only be forgiven once the person wronged has forgiven, so believers try to settle wrongs with others. **Shirk** (associating partners with Allah) is described in the Qur'an as unforgivable if a person dies without repenting.
- **The Prophet's example**: when Muhammad returned to **Makkah** as a victor, many Muslims recall that he pardoned the people of the city who had persecuted him and his followers, rather than taking revenge. Muslims see this as the model of forgiveness.

### Examples of forgiveness arising from personal beliefs

| Person | What happened | Belief behind it |
|---|---|---|
| **Gee Walker** (Christian) | Her son **Anthony Walker**, a black teenager, was murdered in a racist attack in Liverpool in **2005**. Soon after the trial she said publicly that she forgave the killers. | She explained that her Christian faith, and Jesus' words on the cross, meant she could not hate; refusing to forgive would let bitterness destroy her family too. She later set up a foundation in Anthony's name to promote racial harmony. |
| **Pope John Paul II** (Catholic) | He was shot and seriously wounded in St Peter's Square in **1981**. He later visited his attacker in prison and spoke with him privately. | He said he had forgiven the man, following Christ's teaching to love enemies and forgive without limit. |
| **Azim Khamisa** (Muslim) | His son **Tariq**, a student delivering pizza in San Diego, was shot dead by a 14-year-old gang member in **1995**. | Drawing on his Muslim faith, he forgave the boy, befriended the boy's grandfather, and together they set up a foundation that teaches young people non-violence and forgiveness. |

In each case, the person still accepted that the law should punish the offender: their forgiveness was personal, not a demand that the crime be ignored.

### Why forgiveness matters to believers

- It follows the example of God, who forgives (Christianity) and of Allah, al-Ghafur (Islam).
- It is a **condition** of being forgiven (Matthew 6:14-15; 64:14).
- It **breaks the cycle** of revenge and hatred, and can bring peace to the victim.
- It gives the offender a chance to **reform**, which links forgiveness to the aims of punishment.

**Non-religious views** (useful as an alternative view in (d)): many people, including Humanists, value forgiveness because it helps victims move on and supports restorative justice, though they may feel there is no duty to forgive someone who is not sorry.

::: terms
- **Forgiveness**: letting go of anger and the wish for revenge against someone who has wronged you, and pardoning them.
- **Reconciliation**: restoring a broken relationship so that people who were in conflict can live in peace again.
- **Repentance**: being truly sorry for wrongdoing and turning away from it, with the intention not to repeat it.
- **Tawbah**: repentance in Islam: regret, stopping the sin, resolving not to repeat it and putting wrongs right.
- **Al-Ghafur**: one of the names of Allah, meaning the Most Forgiving.
- **Sacrament of reconciliation**: the Catholic sacrament of confession, in which a priest gives absolution to a repentant person.
- **Restorative justice**: bringing offender and victim together so the offender understands the harm and makes amends.
- **Diyah**: compensation paid to a victim's family in Shari'ah, which they may accept instead of qisas.
:::

::: example (c) teachings in two religions

**Christianity.** Christians believe God forgives all who truly repent, through Jesus' death on the cross. When Peter asked whether to forgive seven times, Jesus said seventy-seven times (Matthew 18:21-22), meaning forgiveness should have no limit. Jesus also taught that if we do not forgive others, God will not forgive us (Matthew 6:14-15), which is why Christians pray "forgive us our sins, as we forgive those who sin against us". Qur'an 64:14 tells believers that if they pardon and forgive, even within their own families, Allah is Forgiving and Merciful. Even in the Shari'ah, a murder victim's family may forgive the killer and accept diyah (2:178), following the Prophet's example of pardoning the people of Makkah.
