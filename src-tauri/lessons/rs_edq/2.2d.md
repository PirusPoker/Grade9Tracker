## What it is

This lesson covers Christian beliefs about **the afterlife**. You need: **eschatological beliefs** (**John 11:25-26**, **John 14:2-7**); **judgement** (**Matthew 25:31-46**, the Sheep and the Goats, and **Luke 16:19-31**, the Rich Man and Lazarus); **resurrection** (**1 Corinthians 15:42-55**); and **traditional and contemporary beliefs about heaven and hell**.

This is part of Question 1 (Beliefs) of Component 2, which carries **6 SPaG marks** on the (d). One of the eight (a) concepts, **resurrection**, belongs here as well as in 2.2c. In June 2025, 1(b) asked candidates to "describe traditional Christian beliefs about heaven". The examiners credited what heaven is like and how Christians believe people get there, but gave **nothing** for writing about hell or purgatory. Answer the question that is set.

### Eschatology: the "last things"

**Eschatology** (from the Greek word for "last") is the study of the **last things**: death, judgement, heaven and hell, and the end of the world as we know it. Christian eschatology centres on Jesus:

- **The Second Coming (parousia)**: Christians believe Jesus will **return in glory** at the end of time. The creeds say he "will come again to judge the living and the dead".
- **The general resurrection**: the dead will be raised.
- **The final judgement**: every person will be judged by God through Christ.
- **The new creation**: God will make "a new heaven and a new earth" (Revelation 21:1), where there will be no more death, mourning or pain (Revelation 21:4).

**John 11:25-26.** Jesus speaks to **Martha**, whose brother **Lazarus** has died, just before he raises Lazarus from the tomb. Jesus says, "**I am the resurrection and the life.** The one who believes in me will live, even though they die." Whoever lives and believes in him will **never die**. For Christians this means:

- eternal life is found **in Jesus himself** and through **faith** in him, so physical death is not the end. Many Christians read this passage at funerals.

**John 14:2-7.** At the Last Supper, Jesus comforts his disciples: "My Father's house has **many rooms**." He is going to **prepare a place** for them, and he will come back and take them to be with him. When **Thomas** asks how they can know the way, Jesus answers, "**I am the way and the truth and the life.** No one comes to the Father except through me." Knowing Jesus means knowing the Father. Christians take from this that:

- heaven is a real home with God, with room for all who follow Christ;
- Jesus has gone ahead and will return (the Second Coming);
- Jesus is the **only way** to the Father. Some Christians read this **exclusively**: only those with explicit faith in Christ are saved. Others read it **inclusively**: Christ saves people in ways known to God, perhaps including some who never heard of him.

### Judgement

Christians believe God is a **just judge** who sees everything, and that people are accountable for how they live.

**Particular and final judgement.** Catholic teaching describes a **particular judgement** of each soul immediately after death, and a **final (general) judgement** at the end of time, when Christ returns and all are raised. Many Protestants speak mainly of the one final judgement; some believe the soul "sleeps" until then, while others believe believers go to be with Christ at once.

**The Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31-46).** When the **Son of Man** comes in glory with his angels, all the nations are gathered before him. He separates them as a shepherd separates **sheep** (on his right) from **goats** (on his left).

- He welcomes the sheep into the kingdom prepared for them. When he was **hungry** they fed him; **thirsty**, they gave him drink; a **stranger**, they welcomed him; he needed **clothes**, and they clothed him; he was **sick**, and they looked after him; in **prison**, they visited him.
- Surprised, they ask when they did this. He replies that whatever they did "for one of the **least of these** brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me".
- The goats failed to do these things, so they failed to do them for him. They go to **eternal punishment**, and the righteous to **eternal life**.
- **Meaning**: judgement is based on **love shown in action**, especially to the poor and outcast; serving others is serving Christ. This motivates Christian charities and volunteering.

**The Rich Man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31).** A **rich man**, dressed in purple and fine linen, feasted every day. At his gate lay a beggar named **Lazarus**, covered in sores, longing for scraps; even the dogs licked his sores.

- Both died. Lazarus was carried by the **angels to Abraham's side**; the rich man was in **torment in Hades**.
- He begged Abraham to send Lazarus to dip his finger in water and cool his tongue. Abraham reminded him of the good things he had received in life, and said a **great chasm** had been fixed between them that no one could cross.
- The rich man then asked for Lazarus to be sent to warn his **five brothers**. Abraham replied that they have **Moses and the Prophets**. If they will not listen to them, they will not be convinced **even if someone rises from the dead**.
- **Meaning**: ignoring the needy has eternal consequences, and choices in this life are final.

**Diverse views on what judgement is based on.**

| View | Held by | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| **Faith and grace** | many Protestants | Salvation is a free gift received by **faith in Christ** (Ephesians 2:8); good works are the **fruit** of real faith, not the way to earn heaven. |
| **Faith shown in works** | Catholic, Orthodox | Faith must be lived out in love; Matthew 25 shows deeds matter; grace comes through the **sacraments**. |
| **Self-judgement** | some contemporary and liberal Christians | God offers love to all; people "judge themselves" by accepting or refusing it. |

### Resurrection

Christians believe in the **resurrection of the body**, not just the survival of a soul. The pattern is **Jesus' own resurrection** (2.2c), "the firstfruits" of those who have died (1 Corinthians 15:20).

**1 Corinthians 15:42-55.** Paul compares the body to a **seed**: what is sown is **perishable**, but it is raised **imperishable**. It is sown in **dishonour** and raised in **glory**; sown in **weakness**, raised in **power**. It is sown a **natural** body and raised a **spiritual body**.

- The first Adam became a living being; the "last Adam" (Christ) is a life-giving spirit. "Flesh and blood" cannot inherit the kingdom of God, so the body must be **transformed**.
- Not everyone will die before the end, but all will be **changed**, "in the twinkling of an eye, at the **last trumpet**".
- Death is "swallowed up in **victory**". Paul taunts death: "Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your **sting**?"

**Diverse views on resurrection.**

- **Bodily resurrection** (Catholic, Orthodox, most Protestants): a real, physical but **glorified** body, recognisably the same person, raised at the end of time. Catholic teaching says the **soul** survives death and is reunited with the body at the general resurrection.
- **Spiritual resurrection**: some Christians stress Paul's "spiritual body" and believe the person is raised in a non-physical form, perhaps straight after death.
- **Influence**: hope at funerals; respect for the body (traditionally burial, though cremation is now accepted by most churches, including the Catholic Church); courage in the face of death.

### Heaven

| Traditional beliefs | Contemporary beliefs |
|---|---|
| A real **place** of perfect happiness where God is, often pictured above the sky. | A **state of being** in perfect relationship with God, not a location in space. |
| Images from **Revelation 21**: a holy city, gates of pearl, streets of gold, no more death, mourning, crying or pain (21:4). | The images are **symbols** of a joy beyond human words. |
| The **beatific vision**: seeing God face to face. | Heaven can **begin now**, in a life lived with God (John 11:25-26, "will never die"). |
| Reserved for those who have faith in Christ and/or lived good lives. | Many hope that God's love reaches far more people than traditional views allowed. |

Pope John Paul II taught that heaven is neither an abstraction nor a physical place in the clouds, but a living relationship with God. Many Protestants agree that its essence is being **with Christ**.

**Purgatory** is a **Catholic** belief: a state of **cleansing** after death for those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully purified, before they enter heaven. Catholics pray for the dead and have Masses said for them for this reason. **Most Protestants reject purgatory**, saying Christ's death is sufficient and it has no clear basis in the Bible. The **Orthodox** pray for the dead but do not define purgatory as Catholics do. Never present purgatory as something all Christians believe.

### Hell

| Traditional beliefs | Contemporary beliefs |
|---|---|
| A real place of **eternal punishment** for the wicked. | A **state of separation** from God, chosen by those who reject him. |
| Images of **fire** ("eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels", Matthew 25:41), darkness and torment (Luke 16). | The fire is a **symbol** of the pain of losing God, not literal flames. |
| Ruled by Satan; eternal and unchangeable (the great chasm, Luke 16:26). | Some believe the wicked are **destroyed** rather than tormented for ever (annihilationism). |
| Taught clearly by many **conservative evangelicals** and in traditional preaching. | **Universalists** hope that in the end all will be saved and hell will be empty. |

The Catholic Catechism describes hell as a state of **definitive self-exclusion** from communion with God: God does not want anyone to be lost, but he respects human **free will**. Many Christians find this answers the objection "how can a loving God send people to hell?": God does not force anyone into his presence.

**Why the debate matters**: traditionalists argue that removing hell makes Jesus' warnings meaningless and ignores God's **justice**. Liberal Christians argue that eternal torment for finite sins is incompatible with God's **omnibenevolence** (2.2a). Both use the Bible.

::: terms
- **Eschatology**: beliefs about the last things: death, judgement, heaven, hell and the end of the world.
- **Second Coming (parousia)**: the belief that Jesus will return in glory at the end of time to judge the living and the dead.
- **Resurrection**: Jesus rising from the dead on the third day, and the belief that the dead will be raised to new, transformed life.
- **Spiritual body**: Paul's term in 1 Corinthians 15:44 for the glorified, imperishable body of the resurrection.
- **Judgement**: God assessing each person's life and faith, either at death or at the end of time.
- **Particular judgement**: in Catholic teaching, the judgement of each soul immediately after death.
- **Heaven**: eternal life in the presence of God, seen as a place (traditional) or a state of being with God (contemporary).
- **Hell**: eternal separation from God, seen as a place of punishment (traditional) or a state of self-exclusion (contemporary).
- **Purgatory**: in Catholic belief, a state of cleansing after death before entering heaven; rejected by most Protestants.
- **Universalism**: the hope or belief that all people will eventually be saved.
- **Annihilationism**: the view that the wicked are destroyed rather than punished for ever.
:::

::: example (b) traditional beliefs about heaven
Describe traditional Christian beliefs about heaven. (5)

Traditionally, Christians believe heaven is a real place where God lives and where the faithful go after death, to enjoy eternal life in his presence. Jesus told his disciples, "My Father's house has many rooms", and promised to prepare a place for them (John 14:2). The Book of Revelation pictures heaven as a holy city with streets of gold, where God "will wipe every tear from their eyes" and there is no more death or pain (Revelation 21:4). Heaven is the beatific vision: seeing God face to face. It is for those who have faith in Christ, who said, "I am the way and the truth and the life" (John 14:6), and who show that faith by caring for others, as in the Sheep and the Goats. This hope comforts Christians when someone dies and encourages them to live faithfully.

[Band 3, 5 marks: only heaven described; accurate sources; influence on believers.]
:::

::: example (c) judgement
Explain different Christian beliefs about judgement. (8)

All Christians believe God will judge every person, and the creeds say Jesus "will come again to judge the living and the dead". In the parable of the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31-46), the Son of Man separates people according to whether they fed the hungry, welcomed strangers and visited the sick and prisoners, because "whatever you did for one of the least of these… you did for me". The Rich Man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31) shows that ignoring the poor has eternal consequences, as a "great chasm" separates the rich man in torment from Lazarus at Abraham's side. However, Christians differ. Catholics believe in a particular judgement at death and a final judgement at the end of time, with purgatory for those who need cleansing. Many Protestants reject purgatory and stress that salvation is by grace through faith (Ephesians 2:8), so good works are the fruit of faith, not a way to earn heaven. Some liberal Christians believe people judge themselves by accepting or rejecting God's love. This belief encourages Christians to live justly and help the needy.

[Band 4, 8 marks: sources explained, Catholic, Protestant and liberal views, influence.]
:::

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Belief in hell is out of date.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some Christians would agree, arguing that the image of eternal fire belongs to a time when people pictured the universe as a three-storey world, and that a God who is omnibenevolent (John 3:16) would not punish finite sins with endless torment. Universalists hope that God's love will finally win everyone over. However, many Christians argue that hell is not out of date, because Jesus himself warned of "eternal punishment" (Matthew 25:46) and the great chasm in Luke 16 shows that choices matter. Without hell, God's justice and human free will would mean little. A middle view, found in Catholic teaching, is that hell is real but is a state of self-exclusion from God rather than a place of flames. This seems the strongest position: the old images may be dated, but the belief that people can reject God for ever is still taught by most churches.
:::
