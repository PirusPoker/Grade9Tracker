## What it is

The last part of **Theme 2: Issues of Life and Death** asks what happens when we die. You need: Christian beliefs and teachings about **life after death**, the **soul**, **judgement**, **heaven** and **hell** (**John 11:24-27**, **1 Corinthians 15:42-44**) and the **diversity** of Christian beliefs about the afterlife; Muslim beliefs about life after death, the soul, judgement, **Akhirah**, heaven and hell (**Qur'an 46:33**, **3:16**); how **Christian, Muslim and Humanist funerals in Britain** reflect beliefs about the afterlife; and the **diversity of views about Muslim worship at graves**.

As with all of Theme 2, the (d) question must use **religious and non-religious** beliefs, such as those of Humanists and Atheists. Present each view as its holders would.

### The soul

- The **soul** is the non-physical, spiritual part of a person: their true self, which many religious people believe is created by God and lives on after the body dies.
- Christians believe each person has a soul made by God, which makes humans unique in creation (made "in the image of God", Genesis 1:27). Many Christians believe the soul is **immortal**.
- Muslims call the soul the **ruh**. Allah breathes it into each person; at death it leaves the body and continues to exist until the Day of Resurrection.
- Most **Atheists** and **Humanists** do not believe in a soul: the mind is what the brain does, so when the brain dies, the person ends.

### Christian beliefs about life after death

**The key sources**

- **John 11:24-27**: after Lazarus has died, his sister Martha says she knows he will rise "at the last day". Jesus replies that he is **"the resurrection and the life"**: whoever believes in him will live, even though they die. Martha declares that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God. Christians take this to mean that **eternal life comes through faith in Jesus**, and that Jesus' own resurrection is the guarantee of theirs. The words are read at the start of many Christian funerals.
- **1 Corinthians 15:42-44**: Paul compares the body to a seed. It is sown **perishable** and raised **imperishable**; sown in weakness, raised in power; sown a **natural body**, raised a **spiritual body**. So Christians believe in the **resurrection of the body**, but a transformed body, not the same flesh brought back to life.

**Judgement, heaven and hell**

- **Judgement**: God will judge each person. Many Christians believe in a **particular judgement** straight after death and a **final judgement** when Christ returns at the end of time (Matthew 25:31-46, the sheep and the goats, judged on how they treated the hungry, the stranger and the prisoner).
- **Heaven**: eternal life in the presence of God, with no more suffering or death.
- **Hell**: eternal separation from God.
- Christians believe salvation is a gift of God's **grace** received through faith in Jesus; many also stress that faith shows itself in how a person lives.

**Diversity of Christian beliefs about the afterlife**

| View | What believers hold |
|---|---|
| **Traditional** | Heaven and hell are real places; heaven a paradise, hell a place of fire and punishment. The dead are raised bodily at the last day. |
| **Contemporary / liberal** | Heaven and hell are **states of being**: being with God or cut off from God. Images of fire and golden streets are symbols, not literal descriptions. |
| **Catholic** | Teaches **purgatory**: a state of purification after death for those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully purified. Prayers and Masses for the dead can help them. Protestants generally reject purgatory as not taught in the Bible. |
| **Immediate or at the end?** | Some believe the soul goes to God straight away; others that the dead "sleep" until the resurrection at Christ's return. |
| **Universalism** | A minority believe a loving God will in the end save everyone, so hell may be empty. |
| **Annihilationism** | Some evangelicals believe the unsaved simply cease to exist rather than suffer forever. |

### Muslim beliefs about life after death

**Akhirah** (life after death) is one of the central beliefs of Islam. Belief in the Day of Judgement is one of the six articles of faith in Sunni Islam, and **Mi'ad** (resurrection and judgement) is one of the five roots of Usul ad-Din in Shi'a Islam. This life is a **test**; the next life is permanent.

- **Death and barzakh**: at death the angel of death takes the **ruh**. The soul then waits in **barzakh**, a barrier or in-between state, until the Day of Resurrection. Many Muslims believe the dead are questioned in the grave by two angels (often named **Munkar** and **Nakir**) about their faith.
- **Resurrection**: the angel **Israfil** will blow the trumpet, and all people will be raised. **Qur'an 46:33** asks whether people do not see that Allah, who created the heavens and the earth without being wearied, is able to **give life to the dead**, and answers that He has power over all things. If Allah could create the universe, raising the dead is no difficulty for Him.
- **Judgement (Yawm ad-Din)**: each person is given the **book of their deeds**, recorded by angels; receiving it in the right hand is a sign of salvation. Deeds are weighed on the **scales**. Allah is perfectly just but also merciful, and judges intentions (niyyah) as well as actions.
- **Jannah (paradise)**: gardens with flowing rivers, peace and closeness to Allah, for those who believed and did good. **Qur'an 3:16** gives the prayer of the righteous: they say they have believed, and ask their Lord to **forgive their sins** and **protect them from the punishment of the Fire**. The verse shows that even the faithful rely on Allah's forgiveness, not only on their own merit.
- **Jahannam (hell)**: a place of fire and suffering for those who rejected Allah and did evil. Many scholars teach that believers who sinned may be punished for a time and then admitted to paradise, while those who rejected faith remain.
- **The bridge (as-Sirat)**: many Muslims believe all must cross a narrow bridge over hell; the righteous cross safely.

**Influence on Muslims**: belief in Akhirah encourages **accountability** in daily life: praying, giving zakah, being honest and kind, because every deed is recorded. It also comforts the bereaved: death is a return to Allah. At news of a death Muslims say, "Truly we belong to Allah and to Him we shall return" (Qur'an 2:156).

### Non-religious beliefs

- **Atheists** and **Humanists** generally believe **death is the end** of the person. There is no evidence for a soul or an afterlife, and consciousness depends on the brain.
- Humanists believe this makes **this life** more valuable: people should live fully and well now, and they live on in the **memories** of others, in their influence, their children and their achievements, and sometimes through **organ donation**.
- Some non-religious people are open to an afterlife, pointing to **near-death experiences** or reports of the paranormal; most scientists explain these in terms of the brain.

### How funerals in Britain reflect beliefs about the afterlife

The skill is to **link each feature to a belief**. "There are hymns" earns little; "the hymns give thanks that the person has gone to be with God, showing the belief in heaven" earns marks.

**Christian funerals**

| Feature | Belief it shows |
|---|---|
| Opening words: "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25) | Eternal life through Jesus; hope of resurrection |
| Prayers **commending the soul** to God | The soul survives death and goes to God for judgement |
| Readings, hymns and a sermon of hope | Death is not the end; comfort that the person is with God |
| **Catholic Requiem Mass**: white pall over the coffin, **Paschal candle**, holy water, incense; prayers for the dead | The pall and water recall baptism (new life in Christ); the Paschal candle stands for the risen Christ; prayers for the dead reflect belief in **purgatory** |
| Committal: "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust", followed by words of "sure and certain hope of the resurrection" | The body returns to the earth, but Christians hope for resurrection |
| **Burial or cremation** | Most Christians accept both, because God can raise a transformed spiritual body (1 Corinthians 15:42-44). The Catholic Church has permitted cremation since the 1960s but prefers burial and asks that ashes be kept in a sacred place rather than scattered. |

Mourners may wear black, but many families now ask for bright clothes, showing that the funeral is also a thanksgiving for the life and a statement of hope.

**Muslim funerals**

| Feature | Belief it shows |
|---|---|
| The dying person is encouraged to say, or hear, the **Shahadah** | Dying in faith, ready to meet Allah |
| **Ghusl**: the body is washed (usually by family members of the same sex) | Purity before meeting Allah; respect for the body, which will be raised |
| **Kafan**: wrapped in simple white cloths | Everyone is **equal** before Allah; wealth is left behind |
| **Salat al-janazah**: the funeral prayer, said standing, asking Allah to forgive the dead person | Belief in judgement and Allah's mercy (compare 3:16) |
| **Burial**, as soon as possible (often within 24 hours), with the body laid on its right side **facing Makkah** | Waiting in barzakh for resurrection; the body will be raised (46:33). Muslims do **not cremate**, out of respect for the body. |
| Simple graves; mourning usually three days, a longer period for a widow | Humility; life goes on; trust in Allah's plan |

In Britain, Muslims often need **Muslim burial sections** in council cemeteries, and quick burial can clash with post-mortem rules or weekend registration; many councils and coroners now try to make arrangements for fast burial.

**Humanist funerals**

| Feature | Belief it shows |
|---|---|
| Led by a **humanist celebrant**, often at a crematorium or other non-religious venue | No God or afterlife; a ceremony centred on the person |
| A **celebration of the person's life**: tributes, stories, photographs, music they loved | The person lives on in memories and influence, not in an afterlife |
| No prayers or hymns; often a **moment of quiet reflection** in which religious mourners may pray privately | Respect for the different beliefs of mourners |
| Committal words accept the end of life and the return of the body to nature | Death is the natural end of a life |

**Humanists UK** trains and accredits celebrants who lead such funerals.

### Muslim worship at graves: diversity of views

The spec asks for the **diversity** of Muslim views about worship at graves.

- **Visiting graves** is widely encouraged in both Sunni and Shi'a Islam: a well-known hadith reports that the Prophet first forbade it, then allowed it because it **reminds people of the hereafter**. Visitors greet the dead, pray **for** them (asking Allah to forgive them) and recite the Qur'an.
- **Shi'a Muslims** place great importance on **ziyarah**: pilgrimage to the shrines of the Imams and the Prophet's family, such as the shrine of **Imam Husayn at Karbala** in Iraq. They pray there, recite special prayers of greeting, and many ask the Imams to **intercede** with Allah for them (they stress that worship itself is only for Allah).
- Many **Sunni Muslims**, especially in Sufi traditions, also visit the tombs of respected saints and scholars.
- **Salafi** and other strict Sunni scholars **reject** building shrines over graves, praying towards or at graves, and asking the dead for help. They argue this risks **shirk** (associating others with Allah), the gravest sin, and point to hadith that forbid taking graves as places of worship. Some also discourage elaborate gravestones.
- Common ground: all agree that **worship belongs to Allah alone**; the disagreement is about what counts as worship and what counts as remembrance and respect.

::: terms
- **Afterlife**: the belief that a person's existence continues in some form after physical death.
- **Soul**: the non-physical, spiritual part of a person, which many believe is God-given and survives death.
- **Ruh**: the Arabic word for the soul, which Muslims believe Allah breathes into each person.
- **Akhirah**: the Muslim belief in life after death, including resurrection, judgement and the eternal life that follows.
- **Barzakh**: the barrier or waiting state between death and the Day of Resurrection in Islam.
- **Judgement**: the belief that God will assess each person's beliefs and actions and decide their eternal destiny.
- **Resurrection**: being raised to life after death; Christians believe in a transformed spiritual body.
- **Purgatory**: in Catholic teaching, a state of purification after death before entering heaven.
- **Jannah**: paradise in Islam, a place of gardens and closeness to Allah for the faithful.
- **Jahannam**: hell in Islam, a place of fire and punishment for those who reject Allah and do evil.
- **Ziyarah**: visiting a holy site or shrine, especially the shrines of the Imams in Shi'a Islam.
- **Humanist celebrant**: a person trained to lead non-religious ceremonies such as funerals.
:::

::: example (c) funerals in two religions
Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, how funerals reflect beliefs about the afterlife. (8)

**Christianity.** A Church of England funeral often opens with Jesus' words "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25), showing the belief that eternal life comes through faith in Jesus. Prayers commend the person's soul to God, reflecting belief that the soul survives death and faces judgement. At the committal the minister speaks of a "sure and certain hope of the resurrection", because Christians believe the body will be raised as a spiritual body (1 Corinthians 15:42-44); this is also why most Christians accept cremation. In a Catholic Requiem Mass, the white pall and holy water recall baptism and new life in Christ, the Paschal candle represents the risen Jesus, and prayers for the dead reflect belief in purgatory.

**Islam.** The body is washed (ghusl) and wrapped in simple white cloths, showing purity before meeting Allah and that all are equal at judgement. The funeral prayer (salat al-janazah) asks Allah to forgive the dead person, echoing the prayer of the righteous in Qur'an 3:16. Muslims bury quickly, facing Makkah, and do not cremate, because the body will be raised on the Day of Resurrection; Allah, who created the heavens and earth, can give life to the dead (46:33). The soul waits in barzakh until then.

::: example (d) paragraph with a non-religious view
'Death is the end.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Humanists and many Atheists would agree. They argue that the mind depends on the brain, so when the brain stops, the person stops; there is no reliable evidence of a soul or an afterlife. This is a strong argument because it rests on what can be observed, and humanists add that it gives life its value: we should live well now and live on only in memories and influence, which is why a humanist funeral celebrates a life rather than praying for a soul. However, Christians and Muslims reply that the afterlife is known by revelation, not experiment. For Christians, Jesus' resurrection is the proof that death is defeated (John 11:25; 1 Corinthians 15:42-44); for Muslims, the God who created the heavens and earth can surely give life to the dead (Qur'an 46:33). The non-religious view is stronger on evidence, but science cannot prove a negative, so the question finally depends on whether a person accepts revelation as a source of knowledge.
:::

::: example (b) diversity at graves
Describe different Muslim views about worship at graves. (5)

Most Muslims, Sunni and Shi'a, believe visiting graves is good because the Prophet allowed it as a reminder of the hereafter; visitors greet the dead, pray for their forgiveness and recite the Qur'an. Shi'a Muslims make ziyarah to the shrines of the Imams, such as Imam Husayn's shrine in Karbala, where they pray and ask the Imams to intercede with Allah. Many Sufi Sunnis also visit the tombs of saints. However, Salafi scholars teach that praying at graves, building shrines over them or asking the dead for help risks shirk, the worst sin, because worship and requests belong to Allah alone. All sides agree that only Allah may be worshipped, but disagree about where remembrance becomes worship.
